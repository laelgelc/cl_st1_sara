The Shadow of Dr. Dorrimore

It was on a night of peculiar darkness that I once again crossed paths with Dr. Dorrimore. The road from Newcastle to Auburn, already treacherous with its shifting banks and rushing creek, seemed to me that evening like a passage through some infernal chasm. My horse shied suddenly, and there, almost under its hooves, stood a man. His voice, rich and insinuating, left no doubt—it was he.

Years had passed since our first, unforgettable encounter in San Francisco, when, in the pale moonlight, he had revealed to me illusions so dreadful that my reason faltered at their recollection. Yet here he was again, unaltered by time, his smile at once courteous and cruel. Against my inclination I offered him a seat beside me, and we rode into Auburn together. A chill seemed to attend his presence; the very town, as we entered it, appeared lifeless and hollow, as though abandoned by its inhabitants.

At the Putnam House, where I lodged, I learned with dismay that Dorrimore was also a guest. My unease deepened the next day when fortune, which I thought most kind, brought to me Miss Margaret Corray and her mother, newly arrived from Oakland. Margaret was my promised wife, and in her presence all shadows should have fled. But I soon observed with tormenting jealousy that she and her mother were captivated by Dorrimore’s polished manners and exotic charm. To them he appeared a man of the world; to me, he was a living curse.

I cannot recount without shame the depth of my suspicion. Often I saw Margaret walking with him in the sunlit streets, and though she smiled upon me as tenderly as ever, I nursed the poison of doubt. Words of protest passed my lips, and her glance—cold, incredulous—stung me more sharply than any rebuke. At last, in bitterness of spirit, I resolved to quit Auburn and abandon the field to him.

It was that very evening I wandered into the old cemetery at the town’s heart. The place was a desolation: headstones toppled, graves sunken, trees twisting their roots through the resting places of the dead. In such a scene my dark thoughts found congenial company. There, among broken shadows and pale shafts of moonlight, I saw him. Dorrimore advanced slowly, and at his side—O God!—was Margaret herself, clinging to his arm as though in trust.

Something fierce and unreasoning possessed me. I leapt forward with murderous intent, yet what followed is veiled from my memory. I awoke days later, battered and delirious, in the hotel. The clerk, when questioned, swore solemnly that no guests named Corray had ever been there. Margaret, he said, was unknown to the place. As for Dorrimore, he had departed abruptly the morning after my madness.

The truth, dreadful in its simplicity, revealed itself later. Margaret, in fact, had never left Oakland. She knew nothing of Auburn, nor of my ravings and jealous fancies. All had been the devil’s work of Dorrimore, whose arts of suggestion and hypnosis—learned, as I afterwards read in a public account, from the jugglers of India—could bind the senses of his victim for weeks, ensnaring him in a world of phantoms.

Thus it was that I, believing myself betrayed, fought against nothing but shadows. And though Margaret is now my wife, and the horror belongs to memory, I confess that in lonely hours I still shudder to recall the smile of Dr. Dorrimore, and to wonder whether even now I am free of his spell.

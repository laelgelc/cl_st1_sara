In the city of Surat there stood, near the quay where merchants landed from every quarter of the earth, a coffee-house much frequented by travellers. There, beneath a roof darkened by smoke, men of many nations rested upon divans, drank coffee from little cups, and discoursed concerning trade, government, war, and the nature of the Deity.

One evening, when the heat of the day had abated, a Persian theologian entered the coffee-house, followed by an African slave. The Persian had spent his life studying the First Cause of all things. He had read so many books, compared so many doctrines, and devised so many arguments that at last his mind became confused. Having sought to prove God by reason alone, he ended by persuading himself that there was no God.

For these opinions he had been driven from his native country, and now wandered from place to place. He seated himself upon a cushion, called for coffee, mixed opium with it, and, while the drug was beginning to cloud his thoughts, looked toward his slave, who sat upon the threshold.

The slave had taken from beneath his garment a small wooden figure. It was dark, polished by handling, and rudely carved in the shape of a man. Before this figure he laid a few grains of rice.

“Tell me, foolish one,” said the Persian, “is there a God?”

The slave immediately concealed the idol in his bosom.

“Certainly there is,” he answered.

“And how do you know it?”

“Because here He is,” said the slave, bringing forth the little image. “This is the god who has guarded me since childhood. He was carved from the sacred tree in my own country, and no journey do I undertake without Him.”

The men in the coffee-house laughed. The Persian smiled contemptuously.

“Behold,” said he, “how ignorance invents its Deity! This man worships a piece of wood, while I, who have examined the arguments of the wisest philosophers, know that there is no sufficient proof that any God exists.”

A Brahmin, who had been listening near by, rose angrily.

“You speak falsely,” said he. “How can a man deny Him by Whose will all things are sustained? Yet your slave is no less mistaken than you. God cannot be enclosed in a wooden figure carried in the folds of a garment. The true God is Brahma, the eternal and incomprehensible. He has revealed His law to our holy teachers, and the Brahmins alone preserve that law in purity beside the sacred river. Kingdoms rise and fall; conquerors come with fire and sword; but the knowledge entrusted to us endures. Therefore it is plain that our worship alone is acceptable.”

So spoke the Brahmin, and he expected that none could answer him.

But a Jewish broker, who had come to Surat to purchase precious stones, struck the table with his hand.

“No,” said he, “you also are wrong. The one true God chose not your people, but ours. He called our fathers, gave them His commandments, and promised them an inheritance. Though we have been scattered among the nations, mocked, plundered, and driven from city to city, we remain His people. Our sufferings are a chastisement, not a rejection; and when the appointed time arrives, He will gather us again and establish His kingdom.”

“You wait in vain,” said a Catholic missionary seated opposite him. “The promise has already been fulfilled, yet you refused to recognise it. Salvation is given through Christ, and the authority to preserve His truth was committed to the holy Church. Outside that Church men may possess fragments of goodness, but they do not possess the complete means of salvation. Her sacraments, her priesthood, and her unbroken authority bear witness that she alone is the vessel ordained for mankind.”

A Protestant minister, who had been turning the pages of a small book, closed it sharply.

“What you are saying is untrue,” he declared. “No earthly institution may stand between the soul and God. Authority belongs not to ceremonies, councils, or priests, but to divine truth received in faith. Your traditions have obscured the simple teaching with pomp and human invention. The man who trusts in outward forms may kneel before a thousand altars and yet remain far from God.”

At this a Turkish customs officer, a Sunni and a zealous defender of his creed, drew the mouthpiece of his pipe from between his lips.

“How Christians dispute among themselves!” said he. “Each condemns the other, though both have departed from the final revelation. God sent prophets to many peoples, but the last and clearest message was delivered to His Messenger. The faithful have carried it across Asia, Africa, and Europe; mighty kingdoms acknowledge it; learned men expound it; and countless worshippers turn their faces each day toward the holy city. Therefore cease your vain contentions and submit to the one true faith.”

A Persian merchant of another Mohammedan sect interrupted him, maintaining that the succession had been wrongly understood. An Armenian spoke for the antiquity of his Church. A fire-worshipper praised the pure flame, which rises upward and consumes corruption. A traveller from the islands declared that the spirits of his ancestors still guided their descendants. Even the keeper of the coffee-house offered an opinion, though no two guests agreed what that opinion meant.

Soon every one spoke and no one listened.

Only one man remained silent. He was a Chinese student of Confucius, who had travelled through many lands in order to observe the customs of men. He sat in a corner with his hands hidden in his wide sleeves, drinking no opium and taking little part in the noise. While the others shouted, he watched them calmly.

The Turkish officer at last noticed him.

“You are silent, traveller,” said he. “The Chinese are reputed to be wise, though they have not received the true teaching. Tell us which man is right.”

All turned toward the student. He placed his cup upon the table and remained thoughtful for a little while.

“I cannot decide among you by adding another angry claim,” said he. “But if you will hear a story told me during my travels, perhaps the story may answer better than I.”

The disputants consented, each hoping that the conclusion would favour himself. The student therefore began:

“I once sailed in an English vessel across the eastern seas. Our ship anchored beside the island of Sumatra to take in fresh water. As the sun stood high, several of us went ashore and sat beneath cocoanut palms near a fishing village.

“Among the islanders was a blind man. He had not been born blind, but had lost his sight while attempting to discover the nature of the sun. He had been told that the sun was fire. ‘If it is fire,’ he reasoned, ‘then it may be caught and preserved.’ He exposed his eyes to its splendor, watched it upon the water, and tried by many experiments to seize its rays. The light destroyed his sight, but he did not discover what he sought.

“Having become blind, he concluded that the sun did not exist.

“‘If the sun were a body,’ said he, ‘it would fall like other bodies. If it were fire, water would extinguish it. If it were a spirit, it could not be seen. Men contradict one another concerning it, and I myself can neither see nor touch it. Therefore the sun is an invention of idle minds.’

“His slave heard him. The slave had split a cocoanut, poured oil into the shell, and placed a wick in it, for darkness would soon come. He lit this lamp and said:

“‘It is plain that my master has grown blind not only in his eyes but in his understanding. The sun certainly exists. Here it is.’

“‘What do you call the sun?’ asked the blind man.

“‘The sun is fire,’ replied the slave. ‘It gives light and enables me to see. I carry it in this shell. By it I shall prepare your food, find your mat, and lead you home.’

“So spoke the slave, and he believed that no one could know more of light than he, because he possessed a lamp.

“A lame villager, resting upon crutches, laughed at him.

“‘You have lived all your life in bondage,’ said he, ‘and know nothing beyond your master’s hut. The flame in your cocoanut is not the sun. I cannot travel far, but I have eyes and have watched the sun every day. It is a bright ball that rises from the sea beyond those trees, passes above our island, and descends behind the western hills. If you were not ignorant, you would know this.’

“Then a fisherman who had drawn his boat upon the sand said:

“‘You also are wrong. The sun does not rise from the sea beyond those trees, nor does it descend behind your hills. I go out before dawn and often remain upon the water after evening. I see it come from the far eastern ocean and sink into the ocean in the west. Your hills merely hide it from you.’

“An old man of the village, leaning upon a stick, drew a circle in the sand.

“‘The fisherman speaks with the pride of one who has sailed a few miles and thinks he has measured creation,’ said he. ‘Our fathers taught us that the sun travels around Sumatra. It rises on one side of the island and sets on the other, because our island stands at the centre of the world. Thus it has always been, and no honest man should abandon the wisdom of his ancestors for the tales of sailors.’

“A traveller from India heard him and shook his head.

“‘How can an aged man repeat such childish errors?’ said he. ‘The sun does not circle your island. It passes around the sacred mountain, above which dwell the powers that govern day and night. At times a dark being attacks and swallows it; then the heavens are obscured until prayers and holy ceremonies drive the monster away. Learned men among my people have known these things for generations.’

“An Egyptian merchant, who had traded along the Red Sea, answered him:

“‘I have visited India and heard such stories, but they are not believed by men acquainted with the world. The sun shines not only upon Sumatra and India. It shines also upon Arabia, Egypt, and many countries beyond. It circles neither this island nor your sacred mountain, but travels across a far greater portion of the earth than you suppose.’

“Then an English sailor, who was mending a rope, laughed aloud.

“‘You all talk as though the sun wandered wherever your fancy directs it,’ he said. ‘I have sailed around the world. I have seen the sun in these seas, in China, off Africa, and beyond America. It serves no nation alone. Every child in England knows that it rises in the east and sets in the west, passing over the whole earth.’

“He took a stick from the sand and tried to show by gestures how the sun moved, but though he was well travelled, he was not learned. His circles crossed one another, and the islanders understood nothing.

“Finally the pilot of our vessel, who had listened without interruption, spoke.

“‘You have each observed something,’ said he, ‘but you have mistaken the boundary of your experience for the boundary of truth. The sun does not circle the slave’s lamp, the villager’s hills, Sumatra, India, or even the whole earth. Nor does it rise from the ocean and sink into it. The earth upon which we stand is itself in motion. It turns continually, bringing first one land and then another into the light, while it also travels around the sun. The sun is vastly greater than this island and greater than the earth. It shines at once upon regions unknown to us; and when darkness covers our own dwelling, we must not therefore imagine that the light has been extinguished.’

“The men upon the shore became quiet. Those who had travelled least found his words hardest to believe; yet none could answer him, because his account embraced what was true in their observations without accepting the errors their pride had joined to them.”

The Chinese student paused. In the coffee-house no one now interrupted him. He then continued:

“Is it not so with the question you dispute? Each man desires to carry the Deity in his own cocoanut shell. One says, ‘God dwells in this image.’ Another says, ‘He dwells only beside this river.’ A third says, ‘He chose my nation and rejected all others.’ A fourth says, ‘He may be approached only through my Church.’ A fifth says, ‘He hears only those who pronounce the words I pronounce and turn toward the place to which I turn.’

“Yet can He Who made all nations belong to one nation? Can He Who gave men reason be confined within one man’s reasoning? Can the Eternal be enclosed in a temple, a book, an image, a ceremony, or a name?

“Consider the world which pride divides but Providence sustains. Is not the whole earth God’s temple? Its pavement is the land on which all peoples walk; its font is the ocean, from which clouds rise and waters descend; its roof is the immeasurable heaven; its lamps are the sun, moon, and stars. The winds are its messengers, carrying life from land to land; the mountains are its altars; living hearts are the offerings laid upon them.

“Human temples are built of stone, wood, and gold. Their walls separate one worshipper from another, and their doors may be shut. But the temple of God has no boundary. No priest opens it, and no ruler can close it. Men may exile one another from cities, yet no man can be exiled from the earth while he lives, nor from the mercy of Heaven while he seeks what is good.

“You ask whose worship is true. Perhaps the truest worship is not to praise God loudly while despising those whom He has made. Perhaps it is to feed the hungry, forgive an enemy, speak honestly, restrain cruelty, visit the afflicted, and sacrifice one’s own ease for another’s need. He who loves only those who repeat his creed loves himself in them; but he who shows mercy to the stranger begins to love something greater than himself.

“The blind man in my story denied the sun because he could no longer see it. Yet the sun continued to shine upon him. Therefore do not hate those who doubt. Their blindness may be the result of proud inquiry, suffering, confusion, or the fault of those who claimed to teach them. Lead them, if you can, but do not strike them for stumbling.

“The slave mistook his lamp for the sun, yet the lamp gave some light. Therefore do not despise the simplest image by which an ignorant heart seeks the Highest. Better a small flame that leads a man to kindness than a vast doctrine that inflames him to persecution.

“The islander, the fisherman, the merchant, and the sailor each possessed part of the truth. Their error began when each said, ‘Beyond what I have seen, nothing can be.’ Travel enlarged their knowledge; silence might have enlarged their wisdom. For he who has crossed many seas learns how large the earth is, but he who has examined his own pride learns how small a man is.

“No mortal eye beholds the whole of the Deity. One perceives justice, another mercy; one reveres unity, another divine love; one treasures law, another forgiveness; and still another, unable to speak of heavenly things, performs goodness without naming its source. These may be separate rays, but the light from which they come is not divided.

“Therefore keep what is holy in your own faith, but do not suppose that your possession of a ray makes you master of the sun. Do not extinguish another man’s lamp because it is smaller than yours. Do not declare that night covers the universe merely because darkness has entered your own bosom. Above all, do not dishonour God by cruelty committed in defence of His honour. The Almighty needs no man’s anger; but every weak creature needs man’s compassion.”

So spoke the Chinese student.

The Persian theologian looked at his slave, who still held the wooden figure against his breast, and he no longer smiled. The Brahmin ceased to boast of his sacred river. The Jewish broker folded his hands. The missionary and the minister did not renew their quarrel. The Turk allowed his pipe to go out, while the fire-worshipper gazed silently at the dying coal.

Outside, evening had fallen over Surat. Lamps appeared along the quay, small and wavering beneath the heavens; and above them, unseen beyond the darkness, the sun continued to illumine other lands.

No one in the coffee-house spoke again of possessing the whole truth. Each remained silent, considering how much he had seen, and how much might lie beyond his sight.

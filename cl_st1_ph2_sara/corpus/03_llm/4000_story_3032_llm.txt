Adventure is commonly supposed to favour the bold, but this is one of those agreeable theories which owe their survival to the bold having written most of the memoirs. The cautious also encounter adventures; they merely experience them as administrative errors.

John James Abbleway was cautious by upbringing, profession, and private inclination. He had been born in a town where the principal excitements were municipal elections and the annual rumour that a second bank might open. At thirty-four he represented an English firm that sold agricultural machinery to countries whose agriculture he regarded as picturesque but whose machinery he regarded as regrettably foreign. His business journeys carried him through places in which emperors, languages, and railway timetables seemed equally liable to alteration without notice.

At the moment he was travelling from Vienna to Fiume, where he was to meet his fiancée, Millicent Sowerby.

Millicent was a placidly lovable English girl, and John James was placidly in love with her. Their engagement had been undertaken with every precaution which could reasonably be applied to so hazardous an enterprise. The consent of both families had been obtained; incomes had been compared; religious, dietary, and seaside preferences had been discussed. Nothing remained except marriage, and even that had been fixed for a Thursday, on the ground that railway accommodation was easier toward the end of the week.

John James therefore had a future, a timetable, and a second-class compartment almost to himself. These are three possessions which generally produce serenity in an Englishman abroad. The fourth occupant of his happiness was a luncheon basket containing ham, bread, cheese, two pears, and a bottle of mineral water. By Agram, however, the pears had vanished, the ham had been reduced to a memory, and the mineral water had proved insufficiently mineral to justify its price.

Snow had begun falling shortly after noon. At first it decorated the landscape with the conscientious prettiness of a Christmas card. Then the sky lowered, the flakes thickened, and the telegraph wires became great shining cords stretched between white, stooping poles. Fields disappeared; cottages sank to their chimneys; the forest ahead took on the muffled, secretive appearance of a crowd that has witnessed something and does not intend to give evidence.

The train moved more slowly. It stopped, advanced with offended snorts, stopped again, and finally entered the forest with the reluctant dignity of an elderly diplomat entering a public bath.

John James consulted his watch whenever nothing happened and the window whenever something did. Neither source was reassuring. The carriage contained, besides himself, only an elderly peasant woman in a black woollen shawl. She had broad red hands, a basket, and that complete indifference to discomfort which civilised travellers find faintly insulting.

A violent jerk threw John James against the armrest. There followed a grinding sound, a metallic crack, and a succession of diminishing clatters in front of them. For several seconds he expected the carriage to overturn, collapse, or burst into flame; it did none of these things. It merely stopped.

The engine and the rest of the train continued without it.

Through the whitened glass John James saw the rear lamps dwindling between the trees. They became two red sparks, one red spark, and nothing. The abandoned carriage stood in a silence made more impressive by the fading roar of its desertion.

“Our coupling has broken,” he announced.

The woman nodded. She had apparently been aware that the train was no longer present.

“They will discover it,” he added.

“Perhaps.”

“They must discover it.”

The woman considered this proposition with the reserve of one who had known railway officials longer than he had.

“Perhaps at the next station,” she said.

John James looked out again. Snow drove between the fir trunks in slanting sheets. There was no house, signal box, road, or human footprint. The carriage, stranded on the single line, seemed suddenly a very small article in a very large forest.

“It will grow dark in less than an hour,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Is this district inhabited?”

“By wolves.”

The answer was delivered without emphasis. Had she said woodcutters or Croats, John James might have asked a supplementary question in the interests of geographical knowledge. Wolves belonged to another department of his imagination, one already well staffed.

“Wolves?” he repeated.

“In winter they come close to the railway.”

John James stared at the frosted window. In England, wolves had the decency to be extinct, which made them suitable for heraldry and improving books for boys. On the Continent they were apparently allowed to interfere with commercial travellers.

“Do they attack people?”

“When they are hungry.”

“And are they hungry now?”

“It is winter.”

This relentless logic was followed by silence.

John James examined the door fastenings. They appeared designed to discourage fare evasion rather than carnivorous invasion. He pictured a grey multitude pouring from the forest, surrounding the carriage, leaping against the panels, smashing the windows, and converting him into a brief but memorable interruption in the dietary routine of Central European wildlife. Millicent would receive a telegram. His firm would send a wreath. Somebody else would marry her, probably a man who considered Thursday inconvenient.

The woman opened her basket and took out a packet wrapped in cloth.

“Have you food?” John James asked.

“A little.”

The word exposed two thick slices of bread, a piece of cheese, and three dark sausages.

“I should be glad to purchase some.”

“They are not for sale.”

Under ordinary conditions John James disliked bargaining. In the present circumstances he discovered that civilisation rests less securely upon fixed prices than he had supposed.

“I will pay you two kronen for one sausage.”

“They cost more than that in Agram.”

“I bought sausages in Agram yesterday.”

“Then you should have kept them.”

She cut a slice from the loaf and ate it with tranquil thoroughness.

“Three kronen,” said John James.

“No.”

“Four.”

The woman glanced toward the window, where darkness was assembling among the trees.

“Eight kronen for a sausage and bread.”

“Eight! That is outrageous.”

“They may be cheaper in Agram,” she said. “They may be free in Paradise. Here they are eight kronen.”

John James paid.

The sausage was tough, heavily seasoned, and profoundly comforting. Hunger subdued his indignation but did not abolish it. He reflected that the peasantry, though much praised in poetry, had been insufficiently examined by commercial auditors.

“Aren’t you afraid?” he asked.

“No.”

“But if wolves come?”

“They will not eat me today.”

“Why not today?”

“It is my name day.”

John James waited for an explanation belonging to the ordinary world. None arrived.

“What difference can that make?”

“My patron saint protects me on my name day.”

“From wolves?”

“From everything.”

The woman spoke with a certainty which, in a boardroom, would have been considered evidence of unsound management. In a darkening forest it acquired an uncomfortable grandeur.

“But the wolves cannot know what day it is,” he protested.

“The saint knows.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is enough.”

John James wished he possessed her faith, though he would have preferred it supported by statistics. He asked whether the protection might extend to fellow travellers.

“No,” she said. “It is not your name day.”

The distinction seemed theologically narrow.

Darkness now pressed against the windows, and the snow had nearly covered the rails. Once there came a distant sound which might have been a branch breaking or an animal moving through undergrowth. John James stopped chewing. The woman continued eating.

A second sound followed, nearer than the first.

“There!” he whispered.

“Yes.”

“What was it?”

“Something in the forest.”

He found this accurate but unhelpful. The carriage lamp had gone out when the coupling broke, and only a pallid reflection from the snow entered the compartment. The woman became a dark triangular shape opposite him. Her basket remained on her knees, guarded with the instinctive vigilance of finance.

Minutes passed. John James counted them by heartbeats, an extravagant method. Then a pale head appeared at the lower corner of the window.

It vanished almost at once.

John James rose so violently that his knee struck the opposite seat.

“There is one outside.”

The woman turned her head.

Another shape came level with the glass: pointed ears, a long muzzle, bright eyes. A second head appeared behind it. Their breath clouded the pane.

“Wolves,” said John James, with the hushed conviction of a man whose worst expectations have at last shown proper respect for him.

The animals moved away. He heard them circling the carriage, their paws crunching the crusted snow. Once something scratched at the door.

“We must barricade it.”

“It is shut.”

“They may break the glass.”

“Then shutting it again will not help.”

John James pulled two leather straps from the luggage rack and attempted to fasten the handle to the seat. His hands trembled, which made the operation resemble an intricate religious ceremony performed by an unbeliever.

The woman gathered her shawl around her and lifted the basket.

“What are you doing?”

“I am going.”

“Going where?”

“To the inn.”

For a moment his fear surrendered to scandalised astonishment.

“What inn?”

“The inn beyond the trees.”

“You said nothing about an inn.”

“You did not ask if there was an inn. You asked if the district was inhabited.”

“And you said wolves!”

“There are wolves.”

“How far away is this inn?”

“Ten minutes. Perhaps fifteen in the snow.”

John James stared at her. A nearby inn altered the situation so completely that he felt personally deceived by geography.

“We must go there at once.”

“You may come if you like.”

“But the wolves are outside.”

“They will not eat me.”

“Because it is your name day.”

“Yes.”

“And what about me?”

The woman regarded him with calm sympathy.

“It is unfortunate.”

She opened the carriage door. Snow whirled in, along with a gust of air so cold that it seemed to have travelled expressly from Russia. John James caught her sleeve.

“You cannot seriously intend to cross a wolf-infested forest in darkness.”

“My cousin Karl keeps the inn. He will have soup.”

The invocation of soup overcame whatever objections her theology had left unresolved. She stepped down onto the buried track.

John James remained in the doorway, divided between the wolves outside and the solitude within. Both alternatives possessed grave disadvantages; the carriage at least had walls, while the inn had soup. He was still calculating the relative value of these features when two large animals burst from behind the fir trees.

They rushed at the woman.

John James gave a warning cry which was neither wholly articulate nor entirely English. The animals sprang around her, rearing against her skirts and thrusting their muzzles into her hands. She scolded them, laughed, and struck one lightly with the edge of her basket.

They were dogs—great shaggy, delighted dogs wearing broad leather collars.

“Karl’s,” she called back. “They come when they hear the train.”

John James descended carefully, retaining one hand on the carriage rail. The animals bounded toward him with sociable violence. He retreated at once.

“You knew they were dogs?”

“When I saw them.”

“You let me think they were wolves.”

“You thought it without help.”

This was unjust, but not demonstrably so.

The woman started along a narrow path between the trees, the dogs escorting her with ceremonial leaps. John James prepared to follow.

“Why did you not tell me at once that there was an inn nearby?” he demanded.

She turned.

“Karl charges strangers too much.”

“And you wished to spare me?”

“No. I wished to sell the sausage.”

There are revelations which illuminate character, and others which merely confirm the darkness already suspected. John James looked at the basket, the retreating shawl, and the dogs that had lately represented death. He began to understand that the forest contained dangers subtler than wolves.

“How much does Karl charge?” he asked.

“For soup?”

“For everything.”

“Too much.”

Before this disturbing estimate could be tested, a whistle sounded from the direction in which the train had vanished. A red light grew among the trees, followed by the laboured approach of a relief engine pushing a guard’s van. Railway men appeared with lanterns, shovels, chains, and the important expressions of officials arriving after a catastrophe has safely failed to occur.

John James waved with an enthusiasm he would later consider excessive.

The woman paused, calculated, and returned toward the carriage. The inn lost its customer; the railway recovered its passenger; and the wolves, if any were present, remained professionally silent.

Within half an hour the carriage had been coupled to the relief engine. John James resumed his journey to Fiume, to Millicent, and to a marriage arranged with every possible safeguard against surprise.

He never discovered whether Karl’s prices justified his cousin’s warning. This ignorance troubled him occasionally in later life, though not so much as the suspicion that they probably did.

## Cock-eye Blacklock and Sloppy Weather

After supper the cook flung his dish-water beyond the fire, where it fell with a sound like a brief shower upon the sage, and Bunt and I saddled for the middle watch.

The cattle had been uneasy since noon. A dry wind had come down from the Lost River country, carrying with it the smell of distant rain that never arrived, and the herd had lifted its many heads to that deceptive promise. All afternoon the animals had drifted against the riders, lowing, milling, and pressing their horns together. Once after dark they had risen almost as one beast, four thousand legs under a single hide, and stood listening toward the north. There had been no sound that we could hear. But cattle possess alarms beyond the reach of men, and for a long while the herd remained standing, troubled by some whisper in the earth.

Bunt rode ahead of me, shapeless in an old buffalo coat, his shoulders hunched and his hat brim touching the collar behind. He had been a cowboy since the word meant more than a hired man on a fenced ranch. He had seen the gold camps flare up and go black; he had seen the buffalo in such numbers that their dust browned the noon; he had seen the first thin herds driven north out of Texas, and had watched the last great herds broken against barbed wire. He remembered when a man might ride three days without crossing a road, and he regarded a railroad fence as a personal insult.

He had seen wheat come where antelope had fed, towns rise where wagon trains had buried their dead, and irrigation ditches draw green squares upon the desert. The country had changed around him while he remained wonderfully the same—brown, narrow, patient, and permanently surprised that civilization should have gone to so much trouble.

We rode without speech until the red eye of the campfire had sunk behind us. The night drew near, not empty and immeasurable, but crowded with familiar lights. Venus hung low over a juniper like a yellow lamp placed there by a careful hand. Mars burned above the eastern ridge, the red lantern of some celestial switchman. Orion stepped between two peaks with his belt shining, and the Dipper tilted as if an invisible camp-tender were about to pour darkness upon the plains.

The herd lay below us in a broad black patch. Now and then a horn clicked against another horn. A steer sighed. Hooves stamped softly in the dust. Far off, a coyote sent one thin cry across the flats, and from somewhere in the sage came the crisp snap of a cooling twig. The earth itself appeared to settle under the stars, contracting after the heat of day.

Bunt began to sing.

His song concerned a young lady in Charleston who had rejected a faithful gentleman, whereupon the gentleman went to sea and the lady died of remorse beneath a magnolia tree. How a song equipped with magnolias, harps, ocean voyages, and a ruined Southern mansion had reached an Idaho cow camp I never learned. Bunt sang it in a high, small voice of great earnestness. The cattle accepted it. Their breathing grew regular, and the last standing animals folded their legs and sank down.

When the lady had been buried for the fourth time—Bunt being uncertain of the verses—he turned in his saddle.

“You ever hear,” he said, “how Cock-eye Blacklock come to his reward?”

I told him that I had not.

“Well,” said Bunt, “he come to it powerful sudden. He didn’t have no notice served on him, nor no time allowed for prayer, repentance, change of venue, nor clean underclothes. The jedgment overtook him runnin’, which was his customary gait whenever honesty got within speakin’ distance.”

He shifted his reins into one hand.

“Blacklock was a bad man, but he was worse’n that—he was an irritatin’ man. There’s bad men you can get along with comfortable, providin’ you don’t dispute ’em, lend ’em money, look at their cards, admire their wives, or set with your back toward ’em. But Cock-eye would create a difficulty in an empty room. He’d cheat at solitaire and then shoot the lamp for knowin’ it. He stole horses from men that hadn’t got but one, and if a fellow owned two he’d take both, account of not wishin’ to separate a family.

“He was called Cock-eye because one eye looked at you and t’other kept case on the exits. This give him a broad field of observation. He could watch the marshal, study a poker hand, and locate a back door simultaneous. Nature had built him special for crime.”

The story, as Bunt told it, belonged to his California years, when he had worked in a mountain camp called Prosperity. The town had begun with a gold discovery and survived its disappointment by mining quartz, selling whiskey, and arguing about its future. It occupied a narrow gorge between timbered slopes. A stamp mill thudded night and day below the mine, and the river, clear and cold from the snow fields, ran past the cabins with a noise that made silence impossible.

“The place was called Prosperity,” Bunt explained, “because there wasn’t none in sight. Them names works by opposition. I knowed a town called Temperance that maintained seven saloons and no pump, and another called Harmony where they buried three men after an election for dog-catcher.

“Prosperity had a mine boss named Colonel Jim Sturgis. He wasn’t no colonel, but he’d bought a gray coat with brass buttons from a military gentleman who had got reduced by poker, and the title come with the garment. Colonel Jim was built thick and square, like somebody had started to make a courthouse and quit at the first story. He didn’t scare worth a cent, and he hated a thief the way a wet cat hates everything.

“The marshal was old Dad Weaver. Dad had a badge, a shotgun, rheumatism, and a theory that every citizen was innocent till caught with the property still warm. This theory give Blacklock considerable freedom.”

Blacklock had come to Prosperity with a pack mule, two revolvers, and thirty-seven dollars. By the end of his first month the mule had become two mules, the revolvers had become four, and the thirty-seven dollars had multiplied mysteriously while the money of other men underwent a corresponding decline.

“He dealt faro,” Bunt continued, “and the cards was affected by his personality. They’d behave for anybody else, but soon as Cock-eye took hold of the box they become dishonest. He could turn a king into a trey merely by rubbin’ his thumb on it. He won so steady that luck got ashamed and left town.

“Then one night a miner named Oregon Pike observed a ace emerge from Blacklock’s sleeve. Oregon spoke about it—not loud, but definite—and Blacklock shot him through the shoulder. He claimed self-defense, sayin’ Oregon had been approachin’ him with a hostile expression. Dad Weaver locked him up, but the jail window come loose before mornin’. Curious thing. The screws was all found in Blacklock’s pocket afterward, but the evidence wasn’t what Dad called consecutive.”

Blacklock remained because leaving would have resembled retreat, and his vanity was as pronounced as his lawlessness. He was suspected of robbing the mine payroll, of altering the brands on six horses, of burning a stable whose owner had declined him credit, and of shooting a man called Lily Morgan in the leg during a difference over the rules of draw poker.

“Every time,” said Bunt, “we knowed he done it, but knowin’ and provin’ is two separate horses. The law would climb onto one and justice onto the other, and Blacklock would slap both behind and watch ’em run opposite ways.

“Finally Colonel Jim calls him into the office. I was there, for I was teamin’ ore and had come in to collect seventy-six dollars, which the company treasured so affectionate they couldn’t bear to part with it.

“‘Blacklock,’ says the colonel, ‘I can’t hang you legal, and I’m too busy to hang you informal. But you’re through in Prosperity.’

“Cock-eye leans against the wall. One eye studies the colonel and the other examines a calendar.

“‘What for?’ says he.

“‘For bein’ a plague, a scandal, a public expense, and a general misapplication of meat,’ says Colonel Jim.

“‘That ain’t law,’ says Blacklock.

“‘No,’ says the colonel. ‘It’s mining regulations.’

“Then he gives Cock-eye till sundown to move outen camp. Blacklock argued some, but there was eight men gathered outside with rifles, all persuaded in the same direction. So he loaded his blankets, two fryin’ pans of doubtful ownership, and a spotted deck of cards onto his mule and departed up the river.”

Five miles above Prosperity the gorge opened into a high meadow, and there Blacklock established himself in an abandoned trapper’s cabin. He was out of the camp, but not out of communication with it. He came down every few days to buy flour, coffee, cartridges, and expensive cigars. Since he was known not to work, his prosperity attracted attention.

“No honest man could live up there on the scenery,” Bunt said. “It was handsome scenery, but it lacked nutrition. Yet Cock-eye kept fat. He’d come into the store with gold pieces when everybody knowed the last dust he owned had departed in a poker game. Colonel Jim says to me, ‘Bunt, that reptile’s found some new villainy.’

“‘Likely,’ says I. ‘He’s got a genius that suffers if unemployed.’

“We figured he was high-gradin’ ore from the mine, but he wasn’t. We watched the trail nights. Nobody come or went. Dad Weaver searched his cabin while Cock-eye was in town and found blankets, bacon, cards, cartridges, and a book called The Young Man’s Guide to Moral Improvement. Dad said the book looked untouched and was probably stolen.”

At that time the mountain river had been closed to fishing for part of the summer. The mine had muddied some of its lower stretches, and the county authorities, newly awakened to the existence of nature, had declared the upper pools protected. They posted notices on trees and promised a fine of one hundred dollars to anyone taking trout by net, trap, poison, giant powder, or other unlawful means.

This ordinance was unpopular in Prosperity. The miners considered fish a form of loose property, like firewood or unattended whiskey. Still, Colonel Jim supported it, partly because he liked the river and partly because he owned the company store that sold hooks.

“Now and again,” said Bunt, “Cock-eye would ride into camp with money. Then he’d stay broke three-four days and appear sudden with another roll. We watched him, but his cabin didn’t produce no mint. Finally the cook at the boardinghouse mentions that a feller has been sellin’ him trout—fine big trout, twenty and thirty at a lick.

“‘Who?’ says Colonel Jim.

“The cook got embarrassed. He says it was a private commercial transaction.

“Colonel Jim takes away his whiskey ration.

“‘Blacklock,’ says the cook immediate.

“That commenced to shape the thing. But hooks and lines wouldn’t account for such quantities. Cock-eye was a lazy man. He wouldn’t willingly deceive one fish at a time when he could assassinate a congregation.”

The next week Colonel Jim sent Bunt to look around the upper river. Bunt found no nets and no fish traps. What he found was a scattering of dead minnows along the bank, two trout with their air bladders ruptured, and a piece of fuse caught in willow roots.

“Dynamite,” Bunt said solemnly. “That was Cock-eye’s financial institution. He’d swipe powder from the mine, toss it in a pool, collect what floated up, and sell it. He was robbin’ Colonel Jim, violatin’ the fish law, destroyin’ public resources, and introducin’ trout to sudden death. There wasn’t hardly an offense he couldn’t crowd into one transaction.”

They knew, but again they could not prove. Blacklock’s customers denied buying fish. The powder clerk admitted that dynamite was missing, but his records had been kept on loose scraps of paper, some of which had served to light the office stove. Dad Weaver searched the cabin a second time and discovered nothing except six trout heads buried behind the woodpile.

“‘Coyotes,’ says Blacklock.

“‘Coyotes don’t generally bury heads in a straight line,’ says Dad.

“‘Educated coyotes might,’ says Cock-eye.

“And there it stood. Blacklock was guilty enough to poison the air around him, but technical he remained pure as snow.”

During this period Prosperity possessed a dog named Sloppy Weather. No one knew who owned him. The camp had acquired him as it had acquired its name, its marshal, and several debts—by gradual acceptance. He was yellow, long-legged, narrow in the ribs, and decorated with patches of hair that pointed in conflicting directions. His tail curled upward, his ears folded downward, and his expression suggested that he had just remembered a joke.

“He was a fool pup,” said Bunt, with affectionate gravity. “I don’t mean he lacked sense complete. He had sense enough for ordinary purposes, but all of it was arranged wrong. He’d bark at a wagon after it passed, hide bones in the blacksmith’s fire, and go to sleep under horses he hadn’t been introduced to.

“We called him Sloppy Weather because when he shook hisself it rained in every direction, whether he was wet or not.

“That dog had one grand passion: fetchin’ sticks. If you picked up anything long, he become inspired. You could throw a broom, a shovel handle, a table leg, a crutch, or a section of stovepipe, and Sloppy would chase it with religious devotion. Once the parson points his arm toward salvation, and Sloppy jumps through the church window lookin’ for where it landed.

“He wouldn’t quit, neither. A man could throw a stick for him till his shoulder wore out, and Sloppy would stand there waggin’, askin’ if that was the full extent of human entertainment.”

Blacklock disliked the dog. This was partly because Sloppy liked him. The dog followed him through camp, leaned against his trousers, entered the saloon when he entered, and waited outside the store while he transacted business.

“Animals sometimes take to wicked men,” Bunt observed. “Maybe they’re missionary-minded. Anyway Sloppy admired Cock-eye scandalous. Blacklock kicked him considerable, which the dog mistook for attention. You couldn’t discourage him without killin’ him, and nobody but Blacklock desired to go that far.”

One afternoon Sloppy followed Blacklock out of town. At the bridge Blacklock threw a branch into the river to be rid of him. Sloppy plunged from the bank, swam through the current, seized the branch, overtook Blacklock a quarter mile up the trail, and laid it proudly across his boots.

“Cock-eye throwed it again. Sloppy fetched it again. Blacklock pitched it down a ravine. Sloppy clumb down, found it, clumb up, and fetched it. By the time they reached the cabin, the dog believed a wonderful friendship had been established.

“That evening Cock-eye come into Prosperity special to complain. He says, ‘If that idiot hound follows me again, I’ll shoot him.’

“‘That would be unlawful,’ says Dad Weaver.

“‘So’s stealin’ dynamite,’ says Colonel Jim, keepin’ his eye on Cock-eye.

“Blacklock smiles. ‘You ought to catch whoever does it.’

“‘We aim to,’ says the colonel.”

Two nights later another case of dynamite vanished from the mine magazine.

Colonel Jim made his plans. He told Blacklock publicly that the marshal intended to search his cabin at dawn. The announcement was bait. If Blacklock had stolen powder there, he would move it or use it before the search. Shortly after midnight the colonel, Dad Weaver, and Bunt rode quietly out of Prosperity, left their horses in timber, and approached the cabin on foot.

“The moon was about half full,” said Bunt, “and the river shone in pieces between the trees. We laid behind a deadfall where we could see the cabin door. Dad’s rheumatism objected to the ground, and Colonel Jim objected to Dad’s objections, so there was some whisperin’ not properly suited to detective work.

“We waited.

“About three in the mornin’ the door opens and Cock-eye steps out with a sack. He stands listenin’. His honest eye looks up the trail and his other eye watches the cabin, maybe to see if he’s followin’ hisself. Then he starts downstream.

“We trail him. Slow work. Dad kept steppin’ on dry limbs that hadn’t been there till he put his foot down. Cock-eye stops twice, but the river covers us. Finally he comes to a pool below a ledge—a deep black hole where the water turns once before runnin’ out through a chute.

“There he takes from the sack a stick of dynamite.”

Bunt paused. Below us a steer rose heavily, turned in a circle, and lay down again. Along the eastern horizon the blackness had thinned by the smallest degree, though dawn was still far away.

“Colonel Jim whispers, ‘Now we’ve got him.’

“Dad says, ‘Wait till he lights it.’

“Cock-eye lays the stick on a rock, cuts his fuse, and fits the cap. He was neat about such work. A man may be morally loose and still object to dynamite goin’ off in his fingers. Then he strikes a match on his pants.

“The match flares up. We see his face plain, mean and pleased.

“He touches it to the fuse.

“‘Hold!’ yells Dad Weaver, rising up with the shotgun. ‘You’re under arrest!’

“Now, this surprised Cock-eye. It also surprised Dad’s rheumatism, because his knees straightened for the first time in six years.

“Blacklock whirls around. The dynamite’s in his hand with the fuse spittin’. For one fraction he looks ready to throw it at us, which would have concluded the legal proceedings informal. But Colonel Jim puts a rifle on him.

“‘Drop it in the river!’ he shouts.

“Cock-eye calculates. If he holds it, he explodes. If he throws it toward us, he gets shot. So he swings back and pitches it far out over the pool.”

At this point Bunt leaned toward me, and though we sat under an Idaho sky years and hundreds of miles from Prosperity, his voice dropped as if the men behind the deadfall might still be discovered.

“What none of us knew,” he said, “was Sloppy Weather had followed us.”

The dog had slept beneath the porch of the boardinghouse until the three men rode out. He then awakened, examined the vacant camp, and went after them. Somewhere on the trail he had found their scent. He arrived at the pool just as Blacklock cast the dynamite.

“He sees a man throw a stick,” said Bunt. “That was enough. He didn’t ask who owned the river, whether fish was protected, nor what county regulations applied. He give one glad yelp and went past us like a yellow streak.

“Colonel Jim says, ‘Good Lord!’

“Dad says, ‘Call him back!’

“We all call. ‘Sloppy! Sloppy Weather! Come outen that!’

“But the fool pup hits the water with all four feet spread, comes up swimmin’, and heads for that burnin’ stick.

“The fuse is hissin’ on the surface. Sloppy reaches it. For a second we hope he’ll be scared by the sparks. He sneezes, opens his mouth, grabs the dynamite in the middle, and turns for shore.”

Bunt’s horse lifted its head, perhaps hearing in the animated voice some promise of motion.

“Now everybody’s disposition changes. Us behind the log, we commence crawlin’ backward. Dad forgets the arrest. Colonel Jim forgets the fish. I forgets everything except a strong desire to occupy some other geography.

“But Cock-eye—he’s standin’ right on the bank where Sloppy aims to land.

“‘Git away!’ he yells.

“Sloppy swims harder. He’s proud. He’s fetched the stick, and he sees his friend waitin’.

“Blacklock runs downstream.

“Sloppy climbs out, shakes half the river over the county, picks up the dynamite again, and trots after him.

“Cock-eye looks back and sees the pup comin’, tail curled, eyes bright, fuse smokin’ under his jaw. He runs faster.

“Sloppy runs faster.

“‘Drop it!’ screams Blacklock.

“Sloppy thinks this means he’s to lay it down for another throw. So he drops it.

“Cock-eye sees it on the trail behind him and stops, likely thinkin’ he can dodge past. Sloppy sees him stop, snatches the stick up again, and comes joyful.

“Then Cock-eye goes over a fallen tree, through a willow patch, and up the bank like sin itself had heard the sheriff. Sloppy goes under the tree, through the willows, and up after him. Every time Blacklock turns, the pup wags harder. He considers this the finest game ever arranged by man.

“Dad Weaver is yellin’, ‘Shoot the dog!’

“Colonel Jim shoots. But the light is poor, the dog’s movin’, and Colonel Jim is naturally reluctant to fire into a creature carryin’ sufficient powder to reorganize the landscape. He misses.

“Blacklock hears the shot and thinks maybe we’re shootin’ at him. He angles away from us and starts up the slope. Sloppy angles with him.

“The fuse is gettin’ short. You could see its red point jumpin’ along in the dark.

“Cock-eye reaches a big pine and circles it. Sloppy circles the opposite direction. They meet. Blacklock kicks at him. Sloppy dodges, delighted. Cock-eye turns and runs back toward the river. Sloppy follows.

“For a moment it looked like that chase might continue till Judgment Day—Blacklock runnin’ from the law, Sloppy Weather runnin’ after Blacklock, and the dynamite runnin’ after all of ’em.

“Then Cock-eye’s boot catches in a root.”

Bunt removed his hat, not out of reverence, I thought, but to give his conclusion more room.

“He falls. Sloppy catches up and puts the stick against his chest.

“And the mountain comes apart.”

The explosion went into Bunt’s account with the spaciousness of apocalypse. There were rocks in the air, and branches, roots, water, trout, pinecones, smoke, gravel, one boot, two hats, and portions of the trail. The pool rose upright, silver in the moon, and seemed for an instant to stand on its lower end. A young fir crossed the river without touching the bridge. Dad Weaver sat down involuntarily in a patch of nettles. Colonel Jim lost his rifle and found it later thrust halfway through a rotten stump. Bunt himself, according to Bunt, was lifted gently but firmly from one side of the deadfall to the other.

“For several minutes,” he said, “the air was occupied. Then the heavier parts of California returned to their accustomed level.

“We got up cautious. Dad asked if he was dead, and Colonel Jim told him no such luck. The river come down. Smoke drifted off. Rocks quit fallin’, though now and then a trout would arrive late.

“Where the pine had stood there was a hole maybe twelve feet wide and six feet deep. The tree was distributed over three counties. Of Sloppy Weather we found his tail some distance away, still curled up cheerful. Of Cock-eye there wasn’t enough left to serve papers on.”

They spent the remainder of the night collecting what could be collected. At daylight, Prosperity came up the trail, led by the cook and followed by every man able to walk. A jury was assembled, though the need for deliberation appeared slight. Dad Weaver addressed them from the edge of the crater.

“He says, ‘Gentlemen, this deceased come to his death while resistin’ arrest.’

“Colonel Jim says, ‘No, he come to his death while runnin’ from a dog.’

“‘That don’t sound dignified,’ says Dad.

“‘It’s accurate,’ says the colonel.

“So the jury brought in a verdict that Cock-eye Blacklock had been removed by an act of Providence workin’ through a fool pup.”

There was no coffin. They gathered Blacklock’s identifiable remains into a blanket and placed them in the crater, since the crater was available and no one wished to dig. Sloppy Weather’s tail was buried with him.

“The parson come up,” Bunt said, “but he couldn’t settle on a text. Every virtuous passage seemed too complimentary, and every severe one was unnecessary. Finally Colonel Jim made a speech.

“He says, ‘Here lies Cock-eye Blacklock, gambler, thief, fish destroyer, and disturber of the public peace. He lived outside the law whenever possible and inside it whenever convenient. He was difficult to catch and impossible to improve. Let his fate be a warning to all men who fish with giant powder.’

“Then Dad Weaver says, ‘A hook and line is fish-tackle enough for any honest citizen.’

“That was the moral. We piled rocks over ’em both, but not too many, the ground bein’ already well supplied.”

For a while no one spoke. The herd rested, broad-backed and shadowy. Somewhere beyond the flats the coyote cried again, but now another answered from the hills. The two calls seemed to mark the boundaries of the dark.

“What became of Prosperity?” I asked.

Bunt replaced his hat.

“Burned once, flooded twice, and got respectable. That finished it. Mine shut down, mill machinery was sold, and a railroad come through six miles away, which was near enough to take the business and too far to bring any. Last I heard there was a summer hotel standin’ where the saloon used to be. Folks pay money to sit on the porch and admire the scenery we endured free.”

“And Blacklock’s grave?”

“County put a road through it. Automobile road. They likely spread him considerable thinner than he was accustomed.”

The eastern ridge had become distinct. Its edge, formerly blended with the sky, now ran sharp and black from north to south. The stars nearest it faded first. Mars went out like a lantern extinguished by a watchman; Orion grew pale and stepped backward into heaven. Frost silvered the sage. When our horses moved, their hooves made small crushing sounds upon the whitened grass.

Bunt looked toward the morning.

“Hello,” he said. “There’s the sun most.”

Night closed quietly behind his story, like a great book shut by an unseen hand. The enchanted distances withdrew. Mountains became rock and timber again; the constellations surrendered their spears and lamps; the mysterious black field below us resolved itself into cattle, each animal separate, practical, hungry, and worth a stated sum of money.

One steer rose, then another. Along the edge of the herd a hundred backs lifted. Horns moved in the gray light. The cattle shook dew from their hides and began to bawl for the day. From camp came the faint knock of the cook’s axe, followed by a thread of blue smoke that climbed straight into the windless air.

Bunt turned his horse downhill. His buffalo coat, touched by sunrise, showed bald patches, broken seams, and repairs made in several colors of twine. He was old, though until that hour I had not thought of him as such. He rode with the easy slouch of men who had spent half their lives in a saddle, carrying with him vanished camps, dead trails, forgotten songs, stampeded herds, lawless gamblers, impossible dogs, and all the good old, foolish old, violent old days that had gone under wire and wheat.

We descended toward the stirring cattle.

Behind us the frost began to melt. Ahead, the campfire brightened in the clean morning, coffee steamed beside it, and the practical world waited to be ridden.

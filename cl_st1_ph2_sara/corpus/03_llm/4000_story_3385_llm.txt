## Mrs. Laughton’s Man Under the Bed

For seven years Mrs. Laughton had looked beneath her bed every night for a man, and on the only night when she neglected this precaution there was one there.

This is not so unreasonable as it sounds. Mrs. Laughton was a coward par eminence; a pretty little person, pale and fine as a rain-lily, with soft brown hair, two anxious blue eyes, and a conviction that Providence had scattered burglars, snakes, runaway horses, mad dogs, and hostile Indians over the earth chiefly for her alarm. Before retiring she inspected the closet, shook the curtains, peered behind the washstand, and knelt to examine the darkness under the bed. After this ceremony she locked the door, pushed a chair beneath the knob, and asked Tom whether he was quite certain he had fastened the kitchen windows.

Tom usually replied that a regiment could not enter the kitchen without upsetting the tin boiler, two pans, and a patent churn, and that, if a burglar survived the noise, he deserved anything he could carry away.

But upon this night of which I speak Tom was absent.

The Laughtons lived in a small frame house on the prairie, twelve miles from a town which possessed one church, three saloons, and a deputy sheriff who was generally elsewhere when needed. Tom was superintendent of a grading camp farther west. He had ridden out after supper to settle a dispute concerning mules, powder, and two Irishmen, promising to return by eleven.

Before leaving, he entrusted his wife with a long envelope.

“There’s the payroll,” he said. “Six thousand four hundred dollars. Benson was to take it, but he’s sick. I’ll fetch it in the morning.”

“Six thousand—Tom!”

“Don’t look as if it were dynamite.”

“It is worse. Dynamite does not tempt wicked men.”

“Hide it, then.”

“Where?”

“Anywhere a wicked man wouldn’t look.”

This advice seemed to Mrs. Laughton to cover very little territory. Nevertheless, she placed the envelope in the lowest drawer of her little writing-desk, beneath a collection of paid bills, ribbon samples, old letters, crochet patterns, and a pair of pink silk mitts which no person in full possession of his senses could have wished to investigate.

Tom kissed her, laughed at her solemnity, and rode away.

For an hour she sewed. The lamp burned pleasantly; the clock ticked; the wind rubbed the dry grass against the house with a whispering sound. At half-past ten she examined the door, the windows, the kitchen, and the pantry. At a quarter before eleven she performed the same tour. At eleven she told herself Tom would arrive at any instant, and began preparing for bed.

Now whether she was tired, or preoccupied by the payroll, or merely betrayed by that malign chance which delights in making fools of the prudent, she did not look beneath the bed. She had unbuttoned her collar and removed one shoe when her thimble slipped from her fingers, rolled across the floor, and disappeared under the valance.

Mrs. Laughton knelt.

At first she saw nothing. Then two boots shaped themselves out of the darkness.

They were large boots, muddy boots, boots that did not belong to Tom. Beyond them were trouser legs. Beyond the trouser legs, though merciful shadow concealed the details, was unmistakably a man.

She rose without her thimble.

It may require five minutes to tell what Mrs. Laughton thought during the fraction of a second in which she stood with one shoe on and one shoe off, but thought travels faster than lightning when terror oils its wheels. There was a burglar beneath the bed. He had come for the payroll. He would steal it. Tom’s men would not be paid. They would desert. The contract would fail. Tom would be ruined, disgraced, perhaps imprisoned, for timid little Mrs. Laughton did not know precisely what the law did to superintendents whose money was stolen by men under beds. The burglar would then murder her. Tom would return and find her dead, with one shoe off, which seemed an especially humiliating condition in which to be discovered. Her mother would say she had always opposed the marriage. The newspapers would describe her as “the unfortunate female.” The house might be burned. The prairie might catch. The entire county might perish through her carelessness.

What was she to do?

Scream?

The nearest human ear was perhaps four miles away.

Run?

The door stood on the other side of the bed.

Faint?

This was attractive, but postponed rather than solved the difficulty.

She looked toward the desk. The burglar, following her gaze from beneath the valance, could not have failed to look also. At that instant Mrs. Laughton understood that the money was as good as lost unless she could move it.

She put on her shoe.

This trivial act represented the first victory of mind over matter. Her fingers trembled so violently that she fastened two buttons in the wrong holes, but she fastened them. Then, humming a hymn in a voice like a mosquito inside a bottle, she approached the bed.

The bed was mounted on small wooden rollers, for Mrs. Laughton believed dust to be nearly as dangerous as burglars. She seized the footboard and pulled.

The bed came forward.

The man came with it for perhaps six inches, clutching vainly at the floor. Then the valance lifted, a black hat appeared, followed by an unshaven face and two exceedingly angry eyes.

Mrs. Laughton stopped pulling.

The man crawled out, rose, and seemed to continue rising until his shoulders were near the ceiling. He wore a red handkerchief at his throat. A pistol hung in his right hand.

“Don’t you holler,” he said.

“I was not going to,” replied Mrs. Laughton.

“You was.”

“No. I was going to ask you to move. You are on a piece of my lace.”

The man glanced down involuntarily. One muddy boot did indeed rest upon a strip of lace which had fallen from the work-basket.

“Never mind no lace. Where’s the money?”

“What money?”

“None of your lip!”

“I beg your pardon?”

“The pay money. Laughton brought it here.”

“Oh, that money.”

“Yes, that money.”

“I thought you meant my housekeeping money. I have fifteen dollars and sixty cents in the blue teapot, but if you are not asking for it there is no need to mention it.”

“I don’t want no fifteen dollars!”

“Then you should be more particular. People cannot answer accurately when questions are put in such a general way.”

The burglar stared at her. Mrs. Laughton stared back, though her knees had become as weak as wet paper. The pistol’s muzzle appeared as large as a railway tunnel. Her heart did not beat; it fluttered like a hen-sparrow caught in a stove-pipe.

“Where’s the payroll?” he demanded.

“I do wish you would point that somewhere else. It may go off.”

“It will, too, if you fool me.”

“I am the last person in the world to fool anyone. Tom says I have no humour.”

“Open that desk.”

The command struck her like cold water. For one dreadful instant she saw herself obeying. Then she clasped her hands and gave him a look of pained surprise.

“You cannot mean this minute?”

“This minute.”

“But I am not dressed to receive callers.”

“What?”

“My collar is unbuttoned.”

The man’s eyes narrowed.

“You’re either a dumb fool,” he said, “or you’re bluffing.”

“Tom often calls me foolish. I suppose a husband ought to know. But I cannot imagine what bluffing is, unless it has something to do with cards. My cousin Laura’s husband played cards in Denver and lost a silver watch and nearly all his hair, though Laura says the hair was not connected with the cards except morally.”

The burglar made a sound which was not quite a word.

“Open—the—desk.”

Mrs. Laughton moved toward it with the reluctant grace of a fairy-queen being led to execution. She touched the little brass handle, then withdrew her hand.

“It is locked.”

“Unlock it.”

“I do not know where the key is.”

“You locked it.”

“Yes, but that does not prove I know where the key is. I put things away very safely. Sometimes too safely. Last Christmas I hid Tom’s present and did not find it until April.”

The man stepped close enough for her to smell tobacco and cold night air upon him.

“I’ll count three.”

“Oh, please don’t. Counting makes me nervous. At school I never could do fractions when the teacher stood over me.”

“One.”

“It may be in my pocket.”

“Two.”

“No, this is a peppermint. Would you care for one? They are very soothing.”

He caught her wrist.

“Three.”

Mrs. Laughton uttered a little cry; not because his grip hurt, though it did, but because at that moment she remembered the key was lying in full view beside the lamp.

The burglar saw it.

He released her, snatched it, and turned to the desk.

Now courage is often represented as a splendid person in armour, blowing a trumpet and advancing without a tremor. In Mrs. Laughton it was a very small, frightened creature, no bigger than a wren, which desired passionately to hide in a corner but could not because there was work to be done.

The burglar fitted the key into the lock.

“Please be careful,” she said. “That desk belonged to Aunt Emmeline.”

He raised the sloping lid.

It stuck.

“You must press it in first.”

He pressed. The lid flew downward and struck him smartly on the forehead.

The pistol discharged.

The bullet entered a framed engraving of Washington crossing the Delaware. Washington remained calm, but Mrs. Laughton’s composure nearly followed the bullet through the wall.

The burglar staggered back, clutching his head, and expressed himself with an energy which caused even the clock to seem shocked.

“Oh, swear if it relieves you,” said Mrs. Laughton faintly. “Tom does when he hits his thumb. Only I should prefer that you not use that particular word. My father was an elder.”

“You done that!”

“I warned you it stuck.”

“You done it a-purpose!”

“I assure you I did not. If I had known the pistol would fire, I should have covered Washington. It is our only good engraving.”

The man bent over the desk again, half blinded by pain and fury. He dragged open the upper drawer, flinging its contents upon the floor.

While he searched it, Mrs. Laughton saw the long envelope in the lower drawer. The fall of the lid had jarred the drawer partly open.

She was beside the desk, he between her and the door. There was no time to think; fortunately, perhaps, because she had always found thinking beforehand an impediment to boldness. She stooped as though rescuing her lace, slid two fingers into the drawer, drew out the envelope, and tucked it beneath the loose cushion of the chair behind her.

The operation consumed three seconds.

To Mrs. Laughton it appeared to occupy the whole of her natural life.

The burglar threw out letters, mitts, patterns, bills, sealing wax, a photograph of Tom as a child, and a little box containing three teeth which Mrs. Laughton’s mother had preserved for reasons sentiment alone could justify.

“Ain’t here,” he growled.

“Of course not.”

He wheeled upon her.

“What d’you mean, ‘of course not’?”

“You are looking in the ribbon drawer.”

“You said this was the desk.”

“It is the desk. But a desk has several drawers. That is the difficulty with furniture. One can so seldom judge from the outside.”

He dragged open the lower drawer.

The envelope was gone.

He thrust both hands among the crochet patterns, shook every folded paper, and examined the pink mitts with profound suspicion.

“Where is it?”

“I suppose Tom took it.”

“You’re lying.”

“I never lie,” said Mrs. Laughton, which, as she had begun to discover, was not the same thing as never producing an erroneous impression.

“He brought it here. I seen him.”

“Did you? Where were you?”

“Never mind.”

“Were you behind the stable? Tom thought he heard something there. He said it was probably a coyote, but I remarked at the time that coyotes do not generally wear boots.”

The burglar looked down at his muddy boots and then back at her. His eyes had the disagreeable appearance of two augers heated red.

“You hid it.”

“How could I? You have been watching me.”

His expression altered. This was plainly true, and his mind was not constructed to hold two suspicions comfortably at once.

“You got another box?”

“There is Tom’s strongbox.”

“Where?”

“In the kitchen.”

He seized her shoulder.

“Show me.”

Mrs. Laughton’s thoughts leaped like grasshoppers. The kitchen offered no exit except a window fastened by a nail. There was no strongbox there. Worse, the chair beneath which lay the money stood directly behind her. If she moved, the cushion might shift; if the cushion shifted, one corner of the envelope would show.

She sat down.

“I cannot.”

The man lifted the pistol.

“Git up.”

“I would rather not.”

“Why?”

At this point her ingenuity, having performed beyond all reasonable expectation, suddenly deserted her. She looked toward the kitchen door.

The kitchen was dark. The wind made a dry scratching at its window. Somewhere near the stove a pan settled with a metallic click.

Mrs. Laughton’s terror, searching wildly for assistance, seized upon the most terrible thing it knew.

“Because,” she whispered, “there is an Apache asleep in the kitchen.”

The burglar’s jaw dropped.

“A what?”

“An Apache.”

The word seemed to improve by repetition.

The man glanced at the dark doorway.

“You’re crazy.”

“I hope so,” said Mrs. Laughton earnestly. “I have been hoping so ever since I found him.”

“Found who?”

“The Apache. He came just after Tom left. At least, I think he is an Apache. He has feathers and a blanket, and his face is painted. I did not like to ask, because he seemed tired.”

The burglar looked from her to the kitchen and back again.

“He’s asleep?”

“So he said.”

“Said?”

“Well, not in words I understood. He pointed to the floor and then to his knife. I took it to mean that he wished to sleep and did not desire interruption.”

“You let an Injun sleep in your kitchen?”

“What else could I do? He had a knife.”

“I got a gun.”

“Yes,” said Mrs. Laughton, looking admiringly at it. “But it appears to go off unexpectedly.”

The burglar’s hand tightened.

“You’re bluffing.”

“I thought you said I was a dumb fool.”

“You’re both.”

“Is that possible?”

A small crash came from the kitchen.

The burglar jumped.

Mrs. Laughton jumped also, but recovered first.

“There,” she whispered. “You have awakened him.”

In truth, the crash had been caused by the tin boiler, insecurely balanced after her last inspection, slipping from the stove and falling against the coal scuttle. Yet no war-whoop could have served her half so well.

The burglar backed toward the window.

“He ain’t there.”

“I hope you are right. Perhaps he has gone outside.”

The man stopped backing.

“Outside?”

“He may have companions. I believe Apaches generally travel with companions, do they not? I know so little about their customs. Tom says my education has been sadly neglected.”

A second sound followed—a slow scrape, then a bump.

This time it was the patent churn settling against the pantry wall, but Mrs. Laughton turned pale with perfect sincerity. Her invention had become so vivid that she herself now saw, in the black square of the kitchen doorway, feathers, painted cheeks, and a lifted knife.

The burglar saw something too, though doubtless less definite.

“Tell him I never come in here,” he said.

“He might not believe me.”

“Tell him you was alone.”

“But I cannot lie.”

“You been lying ever since I crawled out!”

Mrs. Laughton drew herself up with offended dignity.

“I have tried to be courteous under very trying circumstances.”

A gust rattled the kitchen window.

The burglar went through the bedroom window without troubling to raise the sash.

There was a magnificent confusion of glass, curtain, frame, long legs, and red handkerchief; then he landed outside, rolled once in the dust, sprang up, and ran across the moonlit prairie. He ran with the ardour of a man pursued by the whole Apache nation. The open country received him, diminished him, and finally swallowed him among the pale folds of grass.

Mrs. Laughton watched until he disappeared.

Then she closed the curtain over the broken window with great precision. She lifted the chair cushion and took out the envelope. She counted the money twice, replaced it in the envelope, and held it tightly against her breast.

“All safe,” she said.

After that she considered the kitchen.

It remained black and silent.

The woman who had faced a pistol, cheated a robber, and invented an armed visitor on half a second’s notice was all but annihilated by the possibility that her invention might have come true. Surely the boiler had not fallen by itself. Surely something had moved. She remembered stories of figures seen at windows, moccasin tracks in gardens, camps surprised at midnight. She remembered that the kitchen door had seemed not quite as she left it. She remembered, indeed, a great many things which had never happened.

“Who is there?” she called.

No answer.

She took one step toward the doorway.

The churn creaked.

Mrs. Laughton sat down abruptly. Her hand released the envelope. Six thousand four hundred dollars slid to the floor in a dignified heap.

Then, being no longer required to exhibit courage, she fainted.

Tom arrived twenty minutes later. He saw the broken window, flung himself from his horse, and came through the door with his revolver drawn. His heart beat like a trip-hammer. The bedroom looked as if a cyclone had developed literary tastes: papers covered the floor, drawers hung open, Washington had been shot, and his wife lay white and motionless beside the scattered payroll.

“Milly!”

He dropped the revolver, lifted her, called her name, rubbed her hands, and dashed water upon her forehead. At length she sighed. Her eyelids fluttered. She looked at him without recognition, then clutched his coat.

“Tom!”

“I’m here. What happened? Who did this? Are you hurt?”

“The money—”

“It’s here. Never mind the money.”

“Count it.”

“I’ll count nothing till you tell me—”

“Count it, Tom!”

He obeyed because he had been married seven years. The notes were all there.

“Every dollar,” he said. “Now, who broke the window?”

“The burglar.”

“What burglar?”

“The man under the bed.”

Tom looked beneath it with commendable promptness.

“He’s gone,” Mrs. Laughton explained. “I frightened him away.”

“You frightened him?”

Her lips trembled.

“I talked to him.”

Tom surveyed the ruined desk, the bullet hole, the shattered window, and the payroll.

“What did you say?”

“I do not remember everything. I offered him a peppermint. He was very rude about Aunt Emmeline’s desk. Then it struck him.”

“What struck him?”

“The lid. After that he searched the drawers, but I hid the money under the chair. Oh, Tom, I was so frightened! I never knew anyone could be so frightened and continue speaking.”

He gathered the pretty little creature into his arms, though she still held herself stiffly and listened past his shoulder.

“You saved the payroll,” he said. “You faced an armed man and saved every cent.”

“I told you I was a coward.”

“You’re the bravest woman west of the Missouri.”

“No, Tom. Truly, I am not. I merely could not think of a proper way to stop.”

From the kitchen came a sudden clatter.

Mrs. Laughton stiffened.

Tom turned his head.

“It’s that confounded boiler again,” he said. “I’ve told you the shelf isn’t level.”

She gripped both lapels of his coat. Her blue eyes widened, and the last remnant of colour left her face.

“Tom,” she whispered, “before you go in there—are you quite certain there really isn’t an Apache asleep in the kitchen?”

# Beyond the Golden Gate

When I shut my eyes I can still see Uncle Jesse Boyd sitting in the doorway of his little gray house above the harbour, his hands folded over the knob of his stick and his mild blue eyes fixed upon the Golden Gate.

He was not truly our uncle. All the children of the shore called him so, and before I had known him a week I could not have called him anything else. He was old even then—how old I never certainly knew—with a stoop in his shoulders, a fringe of white hair about a bald, brown crown, and a face so weather-beaten that it seemed to have been carved out of driftwood and afterward left through innumerable winters for the rain and salt wind to finish. He had an absurd little twist in his nose, and one eye was larger than the other. No one could honestly have called him handsome.

Yet I have never remembered Uncle Jesse as ugly.

His face was lighted from within. Good humour dwelt in every wrinkle; kindness looked out through those unequal eyes; and when he smiled, which was often, his whole countenance became beautiful with that beauty which neither youth nor symmetry can confer. It seemed to me that his rough body was no more than a storm-battered lantern through which a steadfast and cheerful flame shone.

Always, when he was alone, he looked toward the Gate—the narrow blue passage between two dark headlands by which our harbour opened to the sea. Beyond it the water spread to the far horizon, silver in the morning, sapphire at noon, and purple when the westering sun laid a royal road across it. I used to wonder what Uncle Jesse sought beyond that shining threshold.

In time I learned.

Mother and I went to Golden Gate in May, when I was eighteen, because Mother had been ill through the winter and the doctor prescribed sea air and idleness. The latter was a medicine Mother found bitter, but the sea air she loved from the first. We rented a small cottage on the eastern curve of the harbour, where wild roses climbed about the kitchen window and a bent spruce harped continually to every wandering wind.

Golden Gate was hardly a village. It consisted of a score of houses scattered along the shore, two wharves, a store, a weather-stained church and more boats than gardens. Behind it rose low green hills, patterned with gray stone walls and sheep paths; before it the harbour sparkled and dimpled, laughed beneath the sun, or sobbed under rain. At night the breakers boomed beyond the Gate like great bells rung in some drowned cathedral.

We arrived in a cold silver drizzle. The carrier deposited our trunks, promised to send provisions from the store, and drove away. We discovered that the cottage chimney smoked, the kindling was damp, and the kitchen pump had to be coaxed like a contrary child.

Mother sat upon a trunk and laughed until she cried.

“This,” she said, “is the simple life, Mary.”

At that moment there came a tapping at the door. On the step stood Uncle Jesse, wearing a patched blue coat and an ancient sou’wester from whose rim rain streamed in merry rivulets. In one hand he held a kettle; in the other, a basket covered with a spotless cloth.

“Evenin’, neighbours,” he said. “Seein’ smoke come down your chimbley ’stead of goin’ up, I reckoned you might be havin’ an argyment with it. Chimbleys is like folks—some draws natural, an’ some has to be humoured. I’ve brung dry chips, a loaf, an’ a mite of tea. Tea’ll hearten anybody except a dead man, an’ I ain’t sartin it wouldn’t hearten him if it was made strong enough.”

In ten minutes he had conquered the chimney, started the fire, primed the pump, and put the kettle on. The basket contained bread, butter, six brown eggs, a pot of strawberry preserves and a bunch of pale mayflowers.

“Now don’t you go thinkin’ I baked the bread,” he warned Mother. “I can bake, after a fashion, but my loaves is useful mostly for ballast. Mis’ Burns made this one. The butter’s mine, though—or, leastways, the cow that started it belongs to me.”

When he had made tea he would not sit down until Mother insisted. Even then he perched on the edge of a chair as if ready to obey an instant summons.

“I live up yander,” he said, pointing with his pipe stem toward the gray house on the hill. “Name’s Jesse Boyd. Sailor once, fisherman afterward, an’ now, by the look of it, general interference with other folks’ business.”

“Providential interference,” Mother said warmly.

Uncle Jesse’s eyes twinkled.

“Providence gets blamed for a sight of things done by old men with too much time. But if you want bait, fish, kindlin’, weather opinions, or a hand with anything that’s too heavy, holler. I ain’t so spry as I was, but I’m still considerable useful in spots.”

From that day he belonged to our summer.

He came down each morning with some offering—a string of trout shining like dark jewels, a basket of clams, a handful of early roses, or merely a new story. He repaired the loose shutter, showed Mother where the sweetest raspberries grew, and taught me to row without splashing half the harbour into the boat. He knew the coast as another man might know his own room. Every reef had a name, every cove a history, every change in wind a meaning.

“There’s weather brewin’ outside,” he would say, although the sky was clear and the harbour asleep.

Before evening the clouds came marching over the headlands, and the wind ran crying through the grass.

He could not pass a child with a torn toy or a woman carrying a heavy basket. I once saw him walk half a mile out of his way to lift a gate for old Mrs. Bell, who had called him a shiftless vagabond in their youth and had never wholly revised her opinion.

“She doesn’t like you,” I said after we had gone on.

“Mebbe not,” said Uncle Jesse. “But the gate was heavy jest the same. ’Tain’t neighbourly to let a body’s opinions strain her back.”

We spent many evenings at his house. It had four small rooms, clean as a ship’s cabin and nearly as crowded. Models of schooners stood on the mantel; shells from southern seas filled a cabinet; a Chinese idol grinned beside the clock; and over his bed hung a faded red scarf which, he said, had once belonged to a Spanish dancer at Valparaíso.

“She give it to you?” I asked.

“No,” said Uncle Jesse gravely. “She give it to Bill Cates, an’ Bill owed me four dollars. Bill never paid debts in money if he could help it.”

But the treasure of the house was a great sea chest scarred by travel. From it he brought carved ivory, foreign coins, a silver-mounted pistol, a cocoanut cup and a little sandalwood box that filled the room with a ghostly sweetness when opened.

Each object unlocked a story.

He told us of a typhoon in the China Sea, where waves towered like moving mountains and the ship climbed them with “every timber prayin’ separate.” He told of mutiny, fever and shipwreck; of a midnight race around Cape Horn beneath stars bright as frozen sparks; of a coral island where birds clouded the sunrise; of an African harbour smelling of spices, tar, fruit and hot rain. His tales were not polished in the literary sense, but they lived. He saw everything again as he spoke, and therefore we saw it too.

Sometimes he was the hero of his own adventures, though never by his telling.

“Captain Marsh said I saved the brig,” he would conclude. “But captains has to say something at such times. Truth was, the old Mercy Jane saved herself. She was a better Christian than any aboard her, an’ did her duty without braggin’.”

Sometimes his stories ended in laughter; sometimes in a brief silence during which he looked out at the darkening Gate. There were locked chambers in his memory which even his careless speech did not open.

One wet afternoon he showed us his life-book.

Rain blurred the harbour and beat with soft fingers upon the panes. Mother was knitting, I was sorting shells, and Uncle Jesse had been telling us how he deserted from a whaler at Honolulu because the captain flogged a sick boy.

“Did you ever write these things down?” I asked. “You should.”

He scratched his chin.

“I made a kind of log, off an’ on. ’Tain’t fit for company.”

After much persuasion he went to the sea chest and dragged out a thick packet of paper tied with tarred cord. Some sheets were foolscap, some wrapping paper, some the blank backs of old bills. The writing sprawled over them in a large uncertain hand; words ran together, spelling proceeded according to impulse, and punctuation appeared chiefly where Uncle Jesse’s pencil had broken or his thought had run aground.

Yet from the first page I knew it was no common document.

I read of storms and strange ports, hunger, peril, rough comradeship and little mercies. Facts which would have made another man boast were recorded almost apologetically. Of his own courage he said least; of the courage of others, much. Humorous remarks had been squeezed into margins, and theological reflections appeared among accounts of cargo and weather.

“Why, Uncle Jesse,” I cried, “this is your life!”

“Some of it,” he said. “The outside part. A man can’t log everything. There’s voyages no ink’ll foller.”

I looked up. He had gone to the window and stood watching rain veils drift across the Gate.

“What voyage couldn’t ink follow?” I asked lightly.

He did not answer for a moment.

“The one a heart makes an’ never comes back from,” he said at last.

Mother glanced at me, warning me not to question him. But later that summer the closed door opened of itself.

It was an August evening, golden and still. The day had been warm, and after supper Mother and I walked up the hill to Uncle Jesse’s house. We found him sitting outside, looking toward the harbour, where every anchored boat had its long bright double beneath it. Beyond the Gate the sea was a path of flame.

He had a small bunch of white clover in his hand.

“Set down,” he said. “Fine evenin’. Too fine, mebbe. Days like this makes an old man remember.”

We sat on the doorstep. For some time none of us spoke. The tide whispered among the stones; far off a gull cried with the lonely cadence of a voice calling from another world.

At length Uncle Jesse held out the clover.

“She liked that better’n roses,” he said. “Said roses was too proud, but clover smelled of home.”

“Who liked it?” I asked, though something in his face made the question unnecessary.

“Margaret.”

He spoke the name softly, as one handles a relic.

“She was Margaret Blair, an’ we was to be married come September, fifty year ago. I was twenty-two, an’ she was nineteen. Purty? Well, I couldn’t tell you. Folks said her sister Jane was the purty one. Jane had pink cheeks an’ curls. Margaret was little an’ brown, with gray eyes that changed like the sea. I never seen anybody like her, afore or since.”

His voice had lost its usual comic turns. It was still homely, but through its simplicity ran an old grief, deep and tranquil as the tide under summer moonlight.

“I’d made two voyages an’ thought I knowed most everything worth knowin’. Margaret cured me of that without ever tellin’ me I was a fool. She had a quiet way. When she laughed, you wanted to be better jest to keep the laugh in the world.

“One afternoon in June she went across the harbour with her brother Tom to fetch a calf from the north pasture. They took old Blair’s dory. The day was fair when they started, but fog come in sudden from outside—a white wall rollin’ through the Gate. Then the wind riz.

“Tom’s body washed ashore next mornin’, over by Black Ledge. The dory was found bottom up. Margaret—”

He stopped. His gnarled fingers closed about the clover.

“Margaret was never found.”

The harbour before us was still golden, but I seemed to see it as it had been that day, smothered in fog and tossed by a cruel wind. I saw a little dark-haired girl in a drifting boat; I heard through fifty silent years the cry that had found no answering voice.

“You never married,” Mother said.

Uncle Jesse shook his head.

“Couldn’t. There was nobody else. Folks told me I was foolish. Mebbe I was. But if you’ve once heard the right tune, Mary, you can’t make do with another jest because the fiddle keeps playin’.”

“Did you think she drowned?” I asked.

“Her body drowned, likely. But Margaret herself? No.”

He looked beyond the Gate, and the old light returned to his eyes.

“I’ve sailed seas that wasn’t on the school maps, but there’s one sea no livin’ man has charted. The Captain knows it. When my orders come, I reckon I’ll put out, an’ somewhere beyond the last fog-bank Margaret’ll be waitin’. She won’t be old, neither. Souls don’t count years the way bodies does.”

He smiled then, with a tenderness that transformed him.

“I’d like to go at sunrise. Always favoured sailin’ with the dawn. There’s enough light to show the channel, an’ all the day ahead.”

After that night we understood why Uncle Jesse watched the Golden Gate. It was not merely the harbour mouth to him. It was the door through which Margaret had vanished and through which, in God’s hour, he believed he should follow.

September took us away. On our last morning Uncle Jesse brought Mother a basket of apples and me the cocoanut cup from his cabinet.

“You’ll come back?” he asked, striving to make the words careless.

“We shall,” Mother promised.

“Well, I’ll keep an eye on the cottage. Houses gets lonesome. An’ people too, though they makes more fuss hidin’ it.”

All winter his letters came—laborious, blotted epistles filled with weather, village news and solemn advice. He told us that Mrs. Bell’s cow had eaten the minister’s washing, that the harbour had frozen “tight enough for sinners to walk over, though none trusted it,” and that he was adding to his life-book.

When May returned, so did we.

Uncle Jesse met us at the station, wearing his blue coat and a bunch of white clover in his buttonhole. He looked older and thinner, but his welcome was as warm as sunlight.

“Golden Gate ain’t been conductin’ itself proper without you,” he declared. “Harbour’s been dull, gulls has lost their conversation, an’ I’ve had to drink my tea alone, which is bad for the disposition.”

That second summer remains in memory like a long bright day. We picnicked on the islands, sailed in Uncle Jesse’s boat, gathered wild strawberries and listened to tales under the stars. Yet beneath our happiness ran a current of anxiety. Uncle Jesse tired easily. A cough troubled him, and sometimes, halfway up the hill, he stopped with one hand pressed against his side.

“Old hull’s leaky,” he said when Mother urged him to see the doctor. “Doctor can caulk here an’ patch yander, but he can’t make new timber grow.”

It was in July that my cousin Robert Kennedy came to visit us.

Robert had published two novels and a book of travel sketches, and therefore Golden Gate regarded him with mingled pride and suspicion. He was thirty, lean, keen-eyed, and possessed of the unhappy habit of observing people as if they were unpublished manuscripts. I feared Uncle Jesse might dislike him.

Instead they became friends before tea was over.

Robert asked no foolish questions and did not patronize. He listened. That was the sure road to Uncle Jesse’s heart. By sunset the two were sitting on an overturned boat, arguing whether a man showed more courage by climbing a mast in a hurricane or apologizing to his wife.

“Mast, every time,” said Uncle Jesse. “A mast can’t remind you of it ten year afterward.”

The next day I showed Robert the life-book.

He read until noon, asked for food without looking up, and continued through the afternoon. At dusk he came out to the veranda with his eyes alight.

“Mary,” he said, “there is a book here.”

“I knew it.”

“Not in its present condition,” he added. “The chronology is mutinous, the spelling has been wrecked with all hands, and three different captains bear the same name. But the life—heavens, what a life!”

“Could you make it into a real book?”

Robert paced the veranda.

“Perhaps. Not an autobiography. The material wants shaping. It needs unity—a thread through all the voyages.”

I thought of white clover and a girl lost in fog.

“There is a thread,” I said.

Uncle Jesse had never written Margaret’s story in his life-book. That belonged to the inward voyage which ink could not follow. I hesitated to betray a confidence, but after Robert had talked with him, Uncle Jesse gave permission.

“Use it if it’ll serve,” he said. “Only don’t go makin’ Margaret flighty or foolish, same as women in some books. She was neither. An’ don’t say she was the purtiest girl in the county, because likely she wasn’t.”

“She shall be herself, as far as I can make her,” Robert promised.

“Well, no man can do more’n that. The Lord Himself lets most of us be ourselves, though it must try His patience.”

The book began.

Robert rented a room at the inn and stayed at Golden Gate after his intended fortnight was over. Every morning he climbed to Uncle Jesse’s house with notebooks under his arm. They worked by the open window, where the harbour wind stirred their papers and the Gate shone blue between the cliffs.

Their collaboration was a curious and delightful thing. Robert demanded dates, motives, names and sequences. Uncle Jesse supplied incidents, contradicted himself, wandered into unrelated adventures and suddenly uttered some phrase so vivid that Robert wrote it down word for word.

“No, no,” Robert would say. “You cannot have reached Barbados before leaving Boston.”

“Can’t I?” Uncle Jesse would reply. “Then shift Barbados. Islands is handier moved on paper than in water.”

At times they quarrelled over style.

“I did not ‘stride undaunted to the rail,’” Uncle Jesse protested when Robert read a passage aloud. “I crawled on my belly, bein’ scared stiff. If you make me too brave, nobody’ll believe the rest.”

“But you cut the burning rope.”

“So would any man with sense. Powder was below it.”

“Very well. You crawled to the rail.”

“Don’t say I whimpered, though. I was scared, but I had standards.”

Robert gradually saw what I had seen—that Uncle Jesse’s humility was not a pose but the natural modesty of a man who measured himself against high things. And Uncle Jesse, for his part, discovered the magic of literary craft. He marvelled at the way Robert could take a broken recollection, clean it of confusion, set it in order and clothe it with colour without falsifying its soul.

“’Tis like buildin’ a ship,” he told me. “I fetches the timber, an’ Robert shapes it. Some of my timber’s uncommon crooked.”

The lost Margaret became the heart of the story. Robert invented little, but he wove her memory through the voyages: a sprig of clover pressed in a Bible, a gray-eyed girl remembered beneath foreign moons, a name whispered when storms closed over the ship. What had been a sequence of adventures became the history of a man who wandered the earth carrying one faithful love.

When Robert read those chapters aloud, Uncle Jesse sat motionless.

“Is it wrong?” Robert asked once.

“No,” Uncle Jesse said after a long silence. “It’s truer’n what happened, somehow. Things don’t always show their meanin’ while they’re happenin’. You’ve put in the meanin’.”

That, I think, was the finest praise Robert ever received.

By September the first draft was finished. Robert returned to the city to revise it and seek a publisher. He insisted that the title page should bear the words:

**THE UNCHARTED SEA**  
**By Robert Kennedy and Jesse Boyd**

Uncle Jesse objected strenuously.

“I ain’t wrote it. I jest remembered.”

“Remembering was the greater part,” Robert said.

“No, sir. I’d as soon claim I made the ocean because I sailed on it.”

But Robert prevailed, and from the hour he did, Uncle Jesse’s life seemed to gather itself around the promised book. It gave him a future toward which to look. He spoke of “when our book comes out” with shy pride, then pretended the pride belonged wholly to Robert.

Mother and I left Golden Gate in October. The hills were russet, the sea iron-gray, and white gulls blew over it like scraps of torn paper. Uncle Jesse looked frail in the autumn light.

“You must take care of yourself,” Mother told him.

“I’ll hold together till launchin’ day,” he said. “A shipbuilder ought to see his vessel leave the ways.”

Through the winter Robert sent proofs, questions and messages. The manuscript had been accepted. Publication was set for April.

Uncle Jesse’s letters became shorter. In February Mrs. Burns wrote that he had suffered “a weakness of the heart.” He refused to leave his house, saying a sailor should finish his voyage in his own berth. Mother and I went to Golden Gate in March, earlier than we had planned.

We found him in his chair by the window.

He was terribly changed. His face had grown fine and transparent, the roughness smoothed away as if some inward tide were already wearing the earthly man to spirit. But his blue eyes were cheerful.

“Knew you’d come,” he said. “The Captain’s keepin’ me waitin’, seems like. Mebbe on account of the book. He knows a man hates to sail afore the mail arrives.”

We stayed with him daily. Spring stole softly over the coast. Snow vanished from the hollows; the grass brightened; tiny white clover stars appeared beside the path. The harbour changed from lead to blue, and at dawn the Gate was filled with golden mist.

Still the book did not come.

Uncle Jesse asked for it each morning. By evening he would say, “Mebbe tomorrow,” with a patience that hurt us more than complaint could have done.

At last, on an April afternoon, the carrier brought a square parcel from Robert.

I carried it up the hill myself. Uncle Jesse saw it through the window and tried to rise, but could not. His hands trembled as I cut the cord.

The book lay within, bound in deep sea-blue cloth. Upon the cover a little ship sailed toward a golden sunrise. I opened to the title page and showed him both names.

He touched his own with one finger.

“Jesse Boyd,” he whispered. “Looks uncommon respectable in print.”

There was a letter from Robert.

> Dear partner,  
> Here is our ship at last. Whatever praise she earns belongs first to the man who supplied her keel and timbers. I have only set the sails. May she carry your stories farther than either of us can travel.

Uncle Jesse read it twice.

“Robert’s a fine lad,” he said. “Too fine a lad to tell the truth about who done the work.”

He turned the pages slowly. When he came to the dedication, he stopped.

**To Margaret, who waited beyond the fog.**

For a moment I feared the emotion would be too much for him. He closed his eyes. Then he smiled.

“He understood,” he said.

I offered to read aloud, but he shook his head.

“No, Mary. I want to make this voyage alone first. I’ve been waitin’ a long spell to see where I went.”

We arranged him comfortably by the window. The declining sun cast a red-gold path across the harbour, and the printed pages glimmered in his hands. When Mother and I left at dusk, he was reading the chapter in which young Jesse first met Margaret among the clover fields.

“Don’t stay up too late,” Mother cautioned.

“Can’t promise,” he said. “Book’s tolerable interestin’. Seems like I know some of the folks.”

I looked back from the path. He lifted one hand in farewell, the book open upon his knee. Behind him, through the window, the Golden Gate was a deepening arch of violet, and one star burned above it.

In the morning I awoke before sunrise.

Why I did so I cannot say. No sound had disturbed me. Yet I rose with the feeling that someone had called my name—not loudly, but from a great distance. The eastern sky was pearl-gray, and the harbour lay still beneath a low white mist.

I dressed and went up the hill.

Uncle Jesse’s door was unlocked. The lamp had burned out. In the pale room the shells, carved idols and little ships waited silently for morning.

He sat by the window.

For one instant I thought he slept. His head rested against the chair; his face was turned toward the Gate. The book was clasped against his heart, both hands folded over it. There was no pain in his expression. Rather, he looked as if he had heard good news and was keeping it quietly until the proper time to tell.

The book had fallen open near the end. On the last page Robert had written of an old sailor standing at sunset beside an unknown sea, listening for the voice of the girl he had lost.

Uncle Jesse had reached the end.

As I stood there, the sun rose.

Its first beam struck through the Golden Gate and entered the room, touching his white hair, his peaceful face and the blue cover of the book. Outside, the mist lifted in shining folds. The harbour woke in ripples of gold, and beyond the headlands the sea stretched radiant and immeasurable.

Perhaps it was only the cry of a gull that I heard then. Perhaps it was merely the wind beginning among the grasses. Yet it seemed to me that the far water called as a familiar voice calls to one who has lingered long upon the shore.

Uncle Jesse had received his sailing orders.

We buried him on the hill above the harbour, where he could face the Gate. Robert came, bringing white clover for the grave. The book was already being praised, but none of us spoke much of praise that day. Fame seemed a small and noisy thing beside the quiet victory of that faithful heart.

Years have passed since then. Golden Gate has changed. There are more houses now, and motorboats disturb the coves where our little sail once drifted. The gray cottage is gone, and strangers live where Uncle Jesse’s house stood. But the Gate remains. At sunrise it is still filled with gold; at evening it still opens upon mystery.

The book remains also. New editions have followed the first, and readers who never knew Uncle Jesse have loved his courage, his humour and his Margaret. Robert shaped the tale, certainly; I helped him understand its ending; but the living soul of it belonged to the old sailor who claimed that he had merely remembered.

And sometimes, when I open my worn copy, a faint fragrance rises from the white clover pressed between its pages. Then I see again the small room at dawn, the book clasped to Uncle Jesse’s breast, and the long glory of sunlight reaching through the harbour mouth.

No one can know with certainty what lies beyond that last bright threshold. Yet somehow I think Uncle Jesse knew when he saw it. I think that, as the sun rose and the earthly shores grew dim behind him, his spirit went out like a little ship upon an uncharted sea—past the reefs of pain, beyond the fog of years, through the Golden Gate.

And somewhere in the morning Margaret was waiting.

# Popopo and the Fashionable Birds

Popopo was a knook, and knooks, as every properly instructed child knows, are the especial guardians of birds. They watch the nests in spring, teach the fledglings which berries may safely be eaten, and keep the north wind from blowing too sharply upon late travellers. They are immortal, or nearly so, and have plenty of time in which to perform these duties.

But even an immortal may sometimes become bored.

One evening Popopo sat beneath an elder bush, counting the stars that shone between its leaves. He had counted them seventeen times, always with a different result, and at last he flung away the twig with which he had been making figures in the earth.

“This is wearisome,” he said. “The owls are awake, the larks are asleep, and not one of my birds requires assistance. I shall visit the city and discover what mortals do at night.”

Therefore he put on his green cap, buttoned his coat of brown velvet, and walked toward the distant glow above the rooftops.

The city greatly astonished him. Though it was late, lamps burned along the streets, carriages rattled over the stones, and people hurried about as if daylight were too precious to waste upon business. Popopo peeped into restaurants, theatres, bakeries, and shops. He understood none of them very well. Immortal beings neither buy nor sell, for whatever they require is usually obtained by wishing for it; so barter and exchange appeared to him a complicated method of getting into quarrels.

Finally he came to a millinery establishment.

In the broad window were hats of every shape and color: hats trimmed with ribbon, hats covered with lace, hats bearing artificial flowers, and hats that looked rather like overturned baskets. Upon many of them were birds.

There were bluebirds and linnets, orioles and tanagers, a little green hummingbird, two scarlet wings without a body, and one solemn owl whose glass eyes stared into the street. All were motionless.

Popopo stopped so suddenly that a gentleman behind him stumbled over his heels.

“What wickedness is this?” cried the knook.

The gentleman supposed the remark referred to him and walked away indignantly. Popopo gave him no attention. He pressed his face against the glass and gazed at the unfortunate birds.

“Captive!” he murmured. “Slain, perhaps! And displayed upon these monstrous head coverings as though suffering were ornamental!”

Of course, Popopo had never seen a stuffed bird. When a bird under his protection died, it was buried beneath moss while the ryls sang a small green song over it. That mortals should preserve dead birds, wire them into stiff attitudes, and fasten them to women’s hats was beyond his experience and nearly beyond his belief.

The shop was closed, but locks are chiefly intended for mortals. Popopo touched the door, passed through it, and stood among the milliner’s wares.

The birds appeared even more pitiful at close range. Their feathers were dusty, their claws were bound with thread, and their little bodies were fixed in unnatural positions. Popopo lifted the hummingbird from a dainty hat and stroked its head.

“Awake,” he commanded.

Instantly the hummingbird shivered, opened its eyes, and darted upward so rapidly that it struck the ceiling.

“Halloa!” it cried, for in fairy tales birds speak whenever there is anything important to say. “Where is the sky?”

“On the other side of the window,” replied Popopo.

He waved his hand, and the glass became, for a moment, as yielding as water. The hummingbird flew through it and vanished into the night.

Popopo then walked from hat to hat, touching every stiffened creature. The linnet sneezed three times when life returned to it. The owl blinked and said, “This is highly irregular.” The bluebirds fluttered gayly about the gas chandelier, while the oriole tore loose the thread around its feet and scolded the entire human race.

“What has happened to us?” asked a tanager.

“You were held prisoners by mortals,” answered Popopo, “but I have set you free.”

“Then we shall waste no time in accepting the gift,” said the linnet.

He opened the window by magic, and out they flew--the bluebirds first, the owl last, for an owl dislikes appearing eager. Within five minutes every hat was bare.

Popopo regarded his work with satisfaction.

“There,” he said. “That is the advantage of having a knook nearby.”

He returned to the forest before dawn, ate a breakfast of dew and hazelnuts, and slept beneath a fern.

The following morning Miss Prindle, who owned the millinery shop, arrived with her younger sister, Clarabel. Miss Prindle was a thin lady who wore spectacles on a silver chain. Clarabel was plump and romantic, and spent much of her time trying on hats intended for customers.

Miss Prindle unlocked the door, drew up the shades, and uttered a shriek.

“My birds!”

Clarabel ran to her.

“Burglars!” she cried. “They have stolen the merchandise!”

“They have stolen only the birds,” said Miss Prindle, surveying the shelves with horror. “See! The ribbons remain. The lace remains. Even the jet buckles remain. But every bird is gone!”

She sank upon a chair.

“The Countess of Pumpernickel is to collect her heron hat today. Mrs. Dobb requires three swallows for a garden party. The Mayor’s wife ordered the owl. I am ruined!”

Clarabel began to sob. Miss Prindle also sobbed, although she did so with more dignity. Their lamentations were so loud that a sparrow upon the gutter heard them and carried the news to the forest, where birds are even more fond of gossip than people are.

By noon Popopo knew all that had occurred.

“I had not considered the milliner,” he said uneasily. “She is a dealer in captive birds and ought to know better; yet if she is ruined, I shall have caused it. One must repair the trouble one makes, even when the trouble begins with a good deed.”

He pondered until sunset. Then an idea came to him.

“Mice!” he exclaimed.

This may not appear to you a particularly brilliant idea, but Popopo knew little of fashions. He had seen mice wearing sleek gray fur, and he supposed they would be quite as decorative as birds. Moreover, mice were plentiful, quick to learn, and willing to enter shops whenever they found an opportunity.

Popopo called twelve field mice and explained that they were to take the places of the missing ornaments.

“You will sit upon the hats,” he told them, “and remain perfectly still while people admire you.”

“Will there be cheese?” asked the eldest mouse.

“Certainly,” said Popopo, who wished to be fair.

He led them to the city after midnight. In the shop he arranged a mouse upon each hat, curling the tails into graceful loops and tying tiny bows around their necks. The mice objected to sitting still, so Popopo enchanted them into immobility until morning.

“You look very handsome,” he assured them. “And this will teach you patience, which improves the morals.”

At nine o’clock Miss Prindle entered. For a moment she did not notice the change. Then the mouse upon the Countess’s hat twitched its whiskers.

The enchantment had ended.

“Eeeee!” screamed Clarabel.

At this the mice became frightened and leaped from the hats. Some ran beneath the counter, some climbed the curtains, and one sprang directly into Miss Prindle’s open handbag. Miss Prindle mounted a stool. Clarabel mounted the counter. The mice, deciding that fashionable employment was disagreeable, rushed in twelve different directions.

One climbed Miss Prindle’s skirt.

She fainted.

Another ran across Clarabel’s shoe.

Clarabel fainted also.

When Popopo heard of this second disaster, he was deeply mortified.

“It seems,” he admitted, “that mice are not fashionable.”

The birds had gathered in an old orchard outside the city. There Popopo found them eating cherries and discussing their remarkable restoration. He explained the milliner’s distress and asked them to return for a little while.

“You need only sit on the hats until her business is secure,” he said. “Afterward I shall free you again.”

The oriole flew into a rage.

“Return to those pins and wires!” it cried. “Never!”

“But Miss Prindle may lose her livelihood,” urged Popopo.

“Did she ask whether we might lose our lives?” demanded a bluebird.

“She purchased you according to mortal custom,” said Popopo, though he did not find this argument convincing even while he made it.

“Can a custom turn wrong into right?” asked the owl gravely.

The linnet perched upon Popopo’s shoulder.

“Suppose,” it said, “that a giant killed you, stuffed you with sawdust, and fastened you to his wife’s bonnet. Would you return because the bonnet-maker had promised delivery on Thursday?”

“No,” replied Popopo.

“Suppose he tied a charming ribbon around your knees?”

“No.”

“Suppose you were the very newest style?”

“I should still object.”

“Then,” said the linnet, “you understand us perfectly.”

Popopo sighed. He did understand. Birds were created to fly in the sky, to sing in trees, to build nests, and occasionally to steal fruit which does not belong to them. They were not created to sit eternally upon hats.

Yet the milliner had spent money on her wares and depended upon selling them. Popopo could not restore the birds without doing them an injustice, nor leave them free without harming Miss Prindle. The problem befuddled him until his cap slipped sideways from thinking.

At last he went to the King of the Knooks.

His Majesty lived inside a hollow hill. The entrance was guarded by two rabbits in brass helmets, and the throne room was lighted by glowworms arranged in chandeliers. The King wore a crown of acorns and was sorting complaints from creatures under his protection.

“A robin says a cat has been watching her nest,” announced the royal secretary.

“Send the cat three miles away,” said the King.

“A woodpecker complains that an oak is too hard.”

“Send him to a birch.”

“Popopo has animated an entire stock of millinery birds, substituted mice for them, and caused two women to faint.”

The King looked up.

“Send him in.”

Popopo entered and told the whole story. He did not omit the mice, although he would have preferred to do so.

When he finished, the King frowned.

“You acted kindly,” said His Majesty, “but not thoughtfully. Kindness without thought often limps into mischief.”

“I know that now,” replied Popopo.

“The birds must remain free.”

“I agree.”

“The milliner must not be ruined because you interfered with her property.”

“I agree to that also.”

“Then why do you stand there?”

“Because,” confessed Popopo, “I cannot agree with both at once.”

The King leaned back upon his mossy throne.

“You are trying to mend one hat when you should alter the fashion,” he said. “Mortals do not desire objects because they are beautiful. They often desire them because someone has announced that everyone else desires them. Discover who makes these announcements, and your task will be simple.”

“The newspapers!” exclaimed Popopo.

“And the magazines,” added the King. “Never neglect the magazines.”

That night Popopo travelled over the whole country. He entered newspaper offices, printing houses, railway bookstalls, and the private rooms of editors. Wherever he found printed words concerning women’s headgear, he touched them with his little finger.

The magic was instantaneous.

The next morning every fashion page proclaimed:

BIRDS UPON HATS ARE NOW IMPOSSIBLE.

RIBBONS AND LACE REPRESENT THE HIGHEST ELEGANCE.

THE TRULY REFINED WOMAN WEARS NO FEATHERS.

In one magazine an article declared that bird-trimmed hats had not been seen in civilized society for at least six months, although they had been seen everywhere the afternoon before. Another announced that ribbons were “startlingly novel.” A third contained portraits of twelve duchesses wearing lace bows.

Mortals read these statements and obeyed them, for such is the mandate of fashion.

Before noon ladies began carrying their bird-covered hats back to the shops.

“My dear Miss Prindle,” said the Countess of Pumpernickel, “surely you did not expect me to wear a heron! Herons are hopelessly old-fashioned.”

“Remove the swallows at once,” ordered Mrs. Dobb. “Replace them with violet ribbon.”

The Mayor’s wife would not even look at her owl.

Miss Prindle and Clarabel worked briskly. They stripped feathers from old hats and decorated them with bows, rosettes, lace, silk cords, and artificial cherries. Since all these things could be sold at excellent prices, the business prospered. Indeed, it prospered better than before, because ribbon wears out sooner than a stuffed bird and must be replaced more frequently.

“Who would ever have imagined,” said Miss Prindle, counting the day’s receipts, “that birds could be so unfashionable?”

Clarabel shuddered.

“Or mice,” she added.

Popopo then visited every millinery shop in the land. In storerooms, boxes, windows, and dusty cupboards he found thousands of birds. He touched each one and restored its life. Through doors, chimneys, keyholes, and enchanted panes they flew away, until the skies seemed filled with a feathery festival.

Some birds returned to their native forests. Others, having forgotten where they belonged, settled wherever the trees appeared friendly. A white tropical bird chose a northern park and had to be given a warm scarf by the fairies; but aside from this, the rescue succeeded perfectly.

Popopo was never again bored. He had learned that the customs of mortals create a vast deal of work for anyone who undertakes to correct them. Still, he performed his duties cheerfully, and whenever fashion threatened some harmless creature, he kept a close watch upon the newspapers.

As for the revived birds, they remained grateful to him, although several had been so long stuffed that they never entirely recovered their former habits. When alarmed, they sometimes became rigid in the middle of a branch and stared straight ahead with glassy solemnity.

This explains why a hunter may aim carefully at a bird, pull the trigger, and yet find that it has suddenly escaped him. The bird has merely remembered its life upon a hat and has kept still until the final instant, thereby confusing the hunter.

You may have supposed the hunter simply missed. But, having read this story, you will understand the matter better.

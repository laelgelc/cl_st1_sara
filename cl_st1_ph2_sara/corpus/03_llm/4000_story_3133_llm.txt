### Elsbeth’s Christmas Stocking

I was not, upon our first acquaintance, prepossessed in Elsbeth’s favour. This is an odious confession for a godmother to make, but I have reached that austere age when one may tell the truth and suffer little diminution of character, having already lost whatever character for wisdom one possessed.

She was a small, brown child, with straight shoulders, a wide mouth, and hair of no distinguishable colour, which escaped perpetually from its ribbon. Her nose turned upward; one tooth crossed another; and her frocks, though selected with maternal devotion, became disorderly within ten minutes of being put on. Yet presently these defects vanished. One saw only the pellucid grey of her eyes, the light upon her face when she was happy, and a certain air of secret wisdom which made grown people seem, by comparison, both noisy and unfinished.

I adored her.

Her two little brothers, Robin and Kit, were honest, round creatures, prodigal in their expenditures of jam and shoe-leather, but without Elsbeth’s acquaintance with the Unknown. They believed whatever she told them. So, for that matter, did I.

One morning in June she came to me carrying a hazel switch from which she had peeled the bark in spirals.

“This is a wand,” she said.

“It has every appearance of one.”

“You must come at once. They won’t wait after noon.”

“Who will not?”

She regarded me patiently.

“The fairies, godmother.”

I am an old maid and was even then approaching that condition with unbecoming rapidity; nevertheless, I put on my hat. Elsbeth led the expedition, bearing her wand. The tiny boys followed, Robin with a wooden sword and Kit with one stocking sagging over his shoe. I brought up the rear, a foolish old creature with a parasol, a pocketful of biscuits, and a materialistic gaze which Elsbeth had undertaken to correct.

We left the lawn, crossed the brook by three uncertain stones, and entered the wood. It was one of those mornings when the earth seems to have awakened before mankind and prepared a festival for its own pleasure. Dew trembled upon the fern; the tulip tree flaunted its glossy leaves; wild roses breathed from the thickets; and somewhere, beyond the green shadow, a thrush poured out his silver cup of song and filled it again before the last bright drops had fallen.

Elsbeth walked very softly.

“You mustn’t break anything,” she whispered. “The Queen does not like crashing people.”

Robin put his sword under his coat. Kit lifted his fallen stocking and trod with such ceremony that he twice sat down among the violets.

A little green snake ran coquettishly across the wee path. A squirrel, seated upon a branch, stroked his whiskers with a complaisant air. The children accepted both as courtiers. I, being educated, recognised the first as a reptile and the second as a rodent; but my advantage gave me remarkably little satisfaction.

At last Elsbeth parted two curtains of elder and motioned us through.

The clearing beyond was no larger than a chamber. Velvet grass covered it; foxgloves stood round it like rosy towers; and a single shaft of sunlight descended through the leaves and lay upon the ground, golden and palpable, as though some celestial door had been left ajar. The air was full of warm perfume and minute wings.

“They’re here,” said Elsbeth.

“Where?” asked Robin, opening his eyes until they were nearly circular.

“Everywhere. You mustn’t look too hard.”

This injunction was plainly meant for me.

“What do they wear?” I asked.

“Green, mostly. But the Queen has white, with a red jewel. There—under the fern.”

“I see her,” said Robin loyally.

“Me too,” said Kit.

Now I observed only a fern, a blue moth, and three midges; but Elsbeth’s face was so illuminated that I became ashamed of my poverty.

“Does she carry a wand?” I asked.

“No. Queens don’t need them. Hark! They are blowing the horns.”

The thrush had ceased. From very far off came a faint, tremulous note, perhaps the wind in a hollow bough, perhaps a bee imprisoned in foxglove bells.

“I hear them,” I said.

Elsbeth slipped her hand into mine. It was warm, slight, and fragrant with crushed pennyroyal.

“They like you now,” she whispered. “You can always come back, even when I’m not here.”

Alack! The roads to Arcady are easily lost, and no map marks the turning.

Soon afterward I went abroad. I travelled among solemn persons who discoursed of galleries and dynasties, and I wrote Elsbeth letters upon thin foreign paper. From Cairo I sent her a little blue scarab, assuring her that it had belonged to a princess. She replied that she knew it had wings at night, although it behaved like a stone when watched.

Then came her mother’s telegram.

Elsbeth had been ill scarcely a week. At first no one feared; then suddenly everyone feared. The child herself remained tranquil. On the morning before she died she asked whether Christmas were near.

“Not very near, darling,” her mother said.

“I should like to stay till Christmas.”

“Yes, dear child.”

“I’ve nearly finished Robin’s reins, and Kit’s rabbit has only one ear. And perhaps”—here she smiled—“perhaps I shall have an autoharp.”

She had desired an autoharp with that intense but ceremonious desire peculiar to children who have been taught not to petition too often. Her father had bought it already. It lay hidden in the upper cupboard, wrapped in brown paper.

But Elsbeth did not stay till Christmas.

I received the news in a hotel room overlooking a sea too blue to be compassionate. For several hours I read about the Ptolemies. There is no grief, I had once maintained, which cannot be held at a decorous distance by Egyptian history. I read of Ptolemy Philadelphus, of Ptolemy Euergetes, of queens who married their brothers and murdered their sons. At midnight I discovered that the book was upside down.

What was, was.

I did not return to England that winter. Perhaps I was cowardly; perhaps her parents, in their first agony, preferred no witness who had loved her in another fashion than their own. They wrote little. The house, they said, was very quiet. The boys were well. No one mentioned the cupboard or the brown-paper parcel.

On Christmas Eve the father and mother made such Christmas as they could for Robin and Kit. Before the nursery fire they placed two stockings and two piles of toys. Two tin engines, two boxes of soldiers, two sugar pigs. Everything in twos. Their sorrow had become painfully exact.

Elsbeth’s mother paused with a toy rabbit in her hand.

“It seems so little,” she said.

“Two is very little,” answered her husband.

But there were only two children now, and so they put the rabbit down and went away.

The boys slept in the adjoining chamber. Near midnight Robin awoke and heard Kit whispering.

“Who’s there?”

A feeble candle burned upon the landing, and by its light they saw Elsbeth standing beside the fireplace.

So they told it afterward.

She wore her white nightdress and had bare feet. Her hair fell over her shoulders, and her face was paler than they remembered, but it was Elsbeth. She bent above the stockings and counted them.

“One,” she said. “Two.”

Then she counted again.

“One. Two.”

She looked behind the chair and beneath the table. After that she began to cry—not loudly, but with the desolate astonishment of a child who has found herself forgotten.

Robin called, “Elsbeth!”

She turned toward them. Kit said she smiled a little, so that they should not be frightened.

“Hadn’t you no stockin’?” Robin asked.

She shook her head.

Then the candle flickered.

“She jus’ went out,” said Kit. “Like the candle does when Nurse pinches it.”

The parents believed the boys had dreamed. What else could sensible, heartbroken people believe? Yet Robin and Kit had never agreed in any account for more than half a minute, and on this they agreed always: Elsbeth had counted the two stockings; Elsbeth had wept; Elsbeth had gone out like a light.

When I came home in spring, her mother told me the story. She apologised while telling it, as if the dead child had committed some small breach of manners.

I went alone to the woodland clearing. The foxgloves were not yet in flower. Rain had darkened the trees, and last year’s leaves lay sodden over the path. I could not find the elder doorway. I called once—softly, foolishly—but there was only the ticking of rain among the branches.

The lost path to Arcady!

For the next Christmas I invited myself to Elsbeth’s home. Her parents protested that I should find it melancholy. I answered that melancholy and I were old companions and understood one another’s habits.

On the afternoon of Christmas Eve I went into the village and returned with parcels enough to compromise my reputation for common sense. I had a doll in a scarlet cloak, a paint-box, a silver thimble, candied violets, a book of fairy tales, and a little pair of fur-lined slippers. From my trunk I took the blue scarab returned to me after Elsbeth’s death. Last of all, her father brought down the autoharp from the cupboard.

The brown paper had grown dusty.

Before the fire we hung three stockings.

Three stockings, three piles of gifts.

Her mother turned away, but presently came back and laid upon Elsbeth’s heap the unfinished rabbit with one ear.

“No,” I said, putting my arm round her. “It is not reasonable. But love has never been reasonable, and I see no profit in beginning tonight.”

We determined that no childish trick should deceive us. The nursery windows were fastened. The chimney was too narrow for anything stouter than smoke. The door was locked, and the key placed in the father’s pocket. Robin and Kit, now grave with the importance of the affair, were locked into their chamber by another door opening upon the passage. Their nurse slept within.

The household went to bed. No one slept.

At eleven we examined the nursery. Three stockings. Three piles of gifts. The autoharp shone darkly beneath the candle.

At twelve we examined it again.

Only two stockings.

Only two piles of toys.

Elsbeth’s stocking was gone. The doll, the slippers, the scarab, the fairy book, the one-eared rabbit—all gone. The autoharp was gone also.

The windows remained fastened. The chimney was empty. The key had not left her father’s pocket. In the boys’ chamber Robin and Kit slept heavily, one on each side of their astonished nurse.

We searched. Of course we searched. Rational people are most energetic when reason has deserted them. We looked in cupboards, beneath beds, behind curtains, inside boxes scarcely large enough to contain the thimble. Nothing.

Only the empty place before the fire.

Only two stockings.

Elsbeth’s mother sank into a chair and began to tremble. Her husband covered his face. As for me, I felt suddenly neither terror nor surprise, but the old warmth of that small hand in mine and the remembered fragrance of pennyroyal.

Very late, when the house was still, I opened my bedroom window.

The night was frosty and clear. The garden lay silver beneath the moon; beyond it the wood rose in purple shadow. For a while there was no sound. Then, from an immeasurable distance—or from the clearing beyond the elder trees—came the frail chiming of strings.

One chord. Another.

The uncertain music of little fingers discovering delight.

After it came a child’s laugh, clear as water, light as wings.

I leaned into the cold darkness.

“Farewell, Elsbeth,” I said. “You have your Christmas now. Take your presents, dear child, and be happy. But you must not come again. Do you hear? Rest now. Rest.”

The strings sounded once more, tenderly, obediently. Then the wood grew very still.

Elsbeth never returned.

I have sometimes wondered whether we acted wisely, though wisdom is a dreary lamp to carry into such places. The worldly may say the gifts were hidden, the locks contrived, the music imagined by an old maid whose affections had disturbed her judgement. Let them. I shall not quarrel with the worldly; they have so little, and guard it so fiercely.

I know only that there were three stockings, and afterward two. I know that the autoharp was never found. And I know that, for one crystalline moment, laughter crossed the winter darkness from that secret country where children see what we have forgotten how to see.

She did not come again.

Elsbeth was always an obedient little thing.

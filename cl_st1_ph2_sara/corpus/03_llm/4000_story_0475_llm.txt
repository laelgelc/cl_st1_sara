I am an old woman now, and there are some as say an old woman’s memory is no better than a cracked looking-glass, which gives you a face, to be sure, but with a line through the middle of it. Yet I remember Sir Jasper’s last days as if they was yesterday; and if you’ll believe a foolish old woman, I can hear his voice this minute, gentle and laughing, though the rain is beating against my panes and him lying these twenty years in the family vault.

I had served at the Hall from the time I was fifteen. First I was under-nurse, and afterward nurse proper to Master Jasper, and then housekeeper when Mrs. Bell married the coachman and went to live at York. So I knew the family’s ways, both good and bad, and more besides than servants ought to know, though not so much as servants commonly pretend.

Sir Jasper, he had been wild in his youth. Nobody denied it, least of all himself.

“Nelly,” he would say, when some gentleman praised his sober habits, “you should have known me before I learned wisdom.”

“Indeed, Sir Jasper,” I would answer, “I have heard sufficient.”

Then he would laugh, for he never took offence where another man would have rung the bell and ordered a servant from the room.

He had a way with every one, high or low. The little children ran beside his horse; the tenants came to him with their troubles; and there was not a widow on the estate who went without coal at Christmas. His smile was a kind of sunshine, though God forgive me for saying it of any mortal creature. When he entered the servants’ hall, all faces turned toward him, and when he left it there seemed less light than before.

Young Jasper was different.

I had nursed him from his birth, and for that reason I loved him long after others had ceased to find much in him to love. A woman may see a man’s faults plain enough and still remember the little warm head that once lay against her breast. He was handsome, with his mother’s dark eyes, but proud and sharp in speech, and forever reckoning what would be his when Sir Jasper died.

“The west farms will want draining,” he said once, standing before the library fire. “And I shall sell that useless timber by the lake.”

Sir Jasper looked up from his book.

“Wait until the trees belong to you, my boy.”

“They must belong to me one day.”

“So must the churchyard, in the same fashion,” said Sir Jasper. “But I would not be in haste to take possession.”

Young Jasper coloured and walked out. I told myself it was no more than youth and thoughtlessness. Ah, well! We shut our eyes when love bids us, and afterward complain that the room was dark.

There was another young man about the place, Robert Dale, the gamekeeper. Robert had been born in the village and brought up by his mother’s sister, a decent woman who kept the post-office. His mother had died when he was an infant, and of his father nobody spoke. There had been tongues wagging once, I believe, but I was away nursing Lady Jasper at Brighton, and by my return the matter had gone quiet.

Robert was tall and fair, with grey eyes and a pleasant turn of the mouth. Many remarked on his likeness to the portraits in the long gallery, particularly to Sir Jasper’s grandfather, who had fought under Wellington. I noticed it myself, but likenesses are strange things, and a housemaid will discover the Queen’s nose in a kitchen-boy if she stares long enough.

Sir Jasper was fond of Robert. He had paid for his schooling, put him under old Harris the keeper, and afterward promoted him over men twice his age.

“He is honest,” Sir Jasper said to me. “That is a scarce quality, Nelly.”

“And grateful, sir.”

“Gratitude is a heavier debt than money. I would rather he were happy.”

There was something sorrowful in the way he said it, but I did not question him. A servant who pries into her master’s conscience is like a cat in the dairy: however soft her step, she has no business there.

The accident came in November. The day began clear, but before noon the sky went black and the daylight was put out like as if it had been a bedroom candle. Sir Jasper had ridden with the hounds. Near Bracken Hollow his horse took fright at a fallen branch and threw him against the stone wall.

Robert brought him home.

I shall never forget the sight: Robert walking beside the cart, white as linen, and Sir Jasper lying under two coats, with rain upon his face. Dr. Marsh came within the half-hour. He examined him and sent us all away except myself, but I knew from his eyes that there was little hope.

Sir Jasper lingered two days, and then he died.

On the second evening he called me to him. His voice was weak, but his smile was the same.

“Well, Nelly,” he said, “you have packed my boxes often enough. I must ask you to pack one more.”

“Do not speak so, sir.”

“My dear soul, if speech could kill me, Dr. Marsh would have murdered half the county.”

The doctor, who stood by the curtains, grunted and turned away.

Sir Jasper pointed toward the old escritoire beside the window.

“In the small drawer at the back there is a spring. Press it.”

I did so, and a narrow panel flew open. Inside lay a green leather case, no longer than my hand, tied with a faded ribbon. The brass corners was black with age.

“Bring it here,” he said.

He held it for some moments against his breast. Then he gave it to me.

“Nelly, when I am in my coffin, place that beside me. Let no one see you. Do you understand?”

I began to cry.

“Yes, sir.”

“Not Jasper. Not Robert. No one.”

“Not for the Queen’s crown would I disobey you.”

“I know that.” His hand closed over mine. “You have done your duty by us all.”

Those were almost the last words he spoke to me. Before dawn his mind wandered, and he called once for a woman named Mary. I had never heard the name from his lips before. Dr. Marsh heard it too, but said nothing.

When the end came, young Jasper stood at the foot of the bed. He did not weep. I do not blame any man because his grief runs inward, but his first question, asked before the curtains was drawn, concerned the will.

“Did my father make any alteration?”

Dr. Marsh looked at him in a way I did not like.

“You may ask the lawyer.”

“I ask whether he spoke of it.”

“He spoke of dying with less fear than many speak of living,” said the doctor. “That is all I shall tell you.”

Robert waited outside the room. When I opened the door, he saw my face and covered his own with both hands. That did more to break my heart than all the black cloth and tolling bells that followed.

I carried out Sir Jasper’s request. On the night before the funeral, when the watchers had gone down to supper, I entered the great chamber where the coffin stood upon trestles. Four tapers burned round it, and the shadows moved on the walls as though the painted people in the family portraits was stirring in their frames.

I slipped the green case beneath the folds of the winding-sheet, close against his left side.

“There, my dear master,” I whispered. “Nelly has done her duty.”

As I turned, I fancied the door moved. I went into the passage, but saw nobody. Young Jasper’s room was at the opposite end of the house, and a line of light showed beneath his door.

The funeral took place next day. Rain fell steadily, and the road to the church was deep with mud. All the village came. Old men stood bareheaded; women sobbed; and Robert walked behind the coffin with such misery in his face that several wondered at it.

The family vault had been opened, but because repairs was needed to one wall, the coffin was left that night in the little chapel adjoining the Hall. Dr. Marsh objected, yet the mason swore all would be ready by morning.

That night I could not sleep. Whether it was grief or Providence that kept me wakeful, the Lord above knows. Near one o’clock I heard a sound in the corridor—a light scrape, then a pause, then another scrape.

I rose, put on my wrapper, and opened my door.

A candle glimmered on the lower stair.

At first I thought one of the maids was walking in her sleep, but the light moved toward the chapel. Then I saw a man’s shoulder and the edge of a dark dressing-gown.

It was young Jasper.

My heart leapt into my mouth. I followed without slippers, holding the rail lest my knees give way. At the chapel door I heard metal turn against metal. There came a little grinding noise, dreadful in the stillness.

The door stood ajar.

Inside, two candles burned upon the altar. The coffin lid had been lifted off and laid across a bed which had been brought there for the watcher. The silver screws lay in a row upon the bedside table. Young Jasper bent over his father’s body, and in his hand was the green leather case.

For one moment I could neither breathe nor cry out. He had cut the ribbon and was forcing the rusty lock with a penknife.

“Master Jasper!”

He wheeled round. I shall never forget his face. It was not fear alone, nor shame. It was the devil looking out of his eyes because honest daylight had found him.

“What are you doing here?” he demanded.

“What am I doing? Good Lord, bless your soul, what are you doing?”

“Be silent.”

He caught my wrist. I had dandled him as a baby, watched his first step, sat beside him through the measles; but all that love died in me beneath his fingers. There are things affection cannot live through, and the violation of him as gave him life was one.

“You opened your father’s coffin!”

“He concealed this from me. It may contain papers affecting the estate.”

“It was his last wish.”

“His last wish may have been made under weakness or deception.”

“Let go of her,” said a voice behind us.

Dr. Marsh stood in the doorway, fully dressed, carrying a lamp. He had been sleeping in the west room because of the funeral arrangements, and my cry had brought him.

Young Jasper released me.

“This is no concern of yours, doctor.”

“Desecration of the dead is the concern of any Christian man who witnesses it. Put down that case.”

“I shall do no such thing.”

The lock, weakened by age, sprang apart as he spoke. Papers slid upon the floor.

Dr. Marsh set down his lamp and gathered them before Jasper could prevent him. There was a certificate folded in parchment, several letters, and a narrow miniature of a young woman with fair hair.

The doctor read the first paper. His expression changed.

“What is it?” Jasper asked.

Dr. Marsh did not answer. He opened a letter, glanced at the signature, and then looked across the coffin toward the man who had broken it open.

“You wished to know whether these documents affected the estate,” he said. “You shall have your answer. They do.”

Jasper’s lips went pale.

“How?”

“This is the certificate of Sir Jasper’s marriage, thirty-two years ago, to Mary Dale.”

“Dale?”

“Robert’s mother.”

The chapel seemed to rock beneath me. I caught hold of the bedpost.

Dr. Marsh continued, stern as judgment.

“Here is the record of Robert’s birth eleven months later. Here are letters from the clergyman who performed the marriage, and a written acknowledgment in Sir Jasper’s own hand. Mary left him after a quarrel, believing herself abandoned. Her death was falsely reported to him, and years passed before he learned that both she and the child had lived. By then he had married again.”

“My mother—” Jasper began.

“His marriage to your mother was valid,” said Dr. Marsh, “for Mary Dale had truly died before it took place. But Robert is Sir Jasper’s legitimate firstborn son.”

Jasper stared at the papers.

“Why conceal them?”

“Perhaps because your father loved you despite your faults. Perhaps because public proof of Robert’s birth would displace you. Perhaps shame kept him silent so long that courage failed him at the end. The dead man meant to bury the truth. You have dug it up.”

“He cannot inherit merely upon these scraps.”

“That is for the law. Yet these ‘scraps’ are clear enough, and there are living witnesses named in them.”

Jasper snatched at the certificate, but the doctor thrust him back.

“Touch it, and I shall call every servant in the house.”

For all his pride, Jasper feared scandal. He stood trembling while Dr. Marsh replaced the papers in the case. Then the doctor ordered him to restore the lid. He refused.

So Robert was summoned.

Ah, that was bitter. He came half-dressed and bewildered, and when he saw the coffin open he went down on one knee beside it. Dr. Marsh told him only that a wrong had been done and must be repaired. Robert asked no questions. With his own hands he lifted the lid into place and turned each silver screw, though his tears fell upon the polished wood.

The will was read three days later in the library. Mr. Hargreaves, the solicitor, sat behind the great desk. Young Jasper stood by the window, pale and stiff. Robert had been required to attend and looked as if he would rather face a firing party. Dr. Marsh was there, and so was I, for Sir Jasper had remembered me.

The will left Robert ten thousand pounds and all Sir Jasper’s personal property not otherwise assigned—horses, plate, books, pictures, and furniture. To me he left an annuity and the portrait painted in his thirtieth year. The estate, being entailed, was not named as a gift.

At this young Jasper breathed freely.

“The Hall is mine, then.”

Mr. Hargreaves folded his spectacles.

“It would have been yours, sir, had you been Sir Jasper’s eldest legitimate son.”

Jasper turned on Dr. Marsh.

“You have spread this lie.”

“I have placed the documents in lawful custody,” said the doctor. “The lie was silence, and you ended it.”

Mr. Hargreaves explained the matter, though Jasper would hardly listen. Robert, as firstborn lawful son, was heir to the title and entailed lands. Had the green case remained in the coffin, no claim might ever have been made. Robert himself knew nothing of his birth. Sir Jasper had chosen secrecy, perhaps wrongly, perhaps mercifully. But young Jasper’s greed had exposed what his father sought to hide.

“You rob me!” he cried at Robert.

Robert rose.

“I asked for nothing. If there is any honourable means by which—”

“There is no honour in you!”

“Enough,” said Dr. Marsh. “Do not accuse the innocent because your own hand has punished you.”

Jasper left the room, striking the door so hard that a pane cracked in the corridor. He challenged the succession, naturally. There was rows among the lawyers for near two years, with witnesses fetched from distant parishes and old registers searched; but truth, though it may limp, comes to its own at last. The marriage was proved, the birth was proved, and Robert became Sir Robert Dale—though in my heart he was always Master Robert for a long while after.

Young Jasper went abroad. I heard he gambled in Paris, then lived in Italy, forever talking of the inheritance stolen from him. God forgive me, I pitied him sometimes. I remembered the child he had been. But I never loved him again.

Sir Robert took possession of the Hall without pride. His first act was to keep every old servant who wished to remain. His second was to cancel three harsh notices young Jasper had ordered sent to tenants before Sir Jasper’s death. He repaired the school roof, restored the widow’s coal allowance, and would stop in the rain to hear a labourer’s complaint, just as his father had done.

The people loved him. Blood has strange workings, but kindness is the truer inheritance.

As for me, I stayed housekeeper until my legs would no longer carry me up the back stairs. Then Sir Robert gave me this cottage, where I sit now with Sir Jasper’s portrait hanging above the mantel. He is young in it, younger than I ever knew him, with laughing eyes and one hand upon his horse’s neck.

Sometimes, when the fire burns low, I look up and wonder whether I did right. I obeyed him, certainly. Yet obedience could not protect his secret from another man’s wicked curiosity. Perhaps no truth can be buried forever, not even beneath oak and lead and consecrated stone.

Still, I believe Sir Jasper knew the hearts of both his sons. One he tried to shield from loss, and the other from painful knowledge. Human wisdom made a knot of it, and selfishness pulled the knot tight; but Providence cut through it clean.

That is how Robert came to his own.

And that is why, though I am only Nelly, an old servant with little learning, I say a house belongs not merely to him whose name stands first in a register, but to him who carries its goodness forward. Sir Robert did that. He made the Hall bright again.

Yet none ever made it quite so bright as the man in the portrait above me.

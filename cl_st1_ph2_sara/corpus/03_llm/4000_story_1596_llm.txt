### The Footwear of Fortune

In Coralio the sea had the color of a green bottle held up against the sun, and the sun had the manners of a creditor. It called early, remained late, and collected interest in perspiration.

Along the beach stood white houses with blue shadows, a customs shed, a market roofed with rusty tin, a cuartel containing eleven soldiers and a melancholy bugle, and the consulate of the United States. Above the consulate hung an eagle of cast iron that looked as if it regretted the appointment.

Within, John Atwood, consul, representative, seal keeper, fee taker, and defender of American dignity, lay in a hammock and endeavored to forget Rosine Hemstetter.

He had been forgetting her for eighteen months.

The process required brandy.

At nine each evening Billy Keogh came in from the hot, guitar-tinkling dark and found John stretched beneath the eagle’s shadow, with a glass on the floor and a photograph turned face downward upon the desk.

Billy was a lean, freckled man whose good humor had survived mining companies, mule contracts, revolutions, and three Central American laundries. He owned no visible business, but knew the affairs of everybody who did. When the consul neglected his correspondence, Billy answered it. When the consul neglected his meals, Billy ordered them. When the consul neglected himself, Billy sat nearby and let him do it sociably.

“Thinking about Rosine?” Billy would ask.

“No,” John would say.

“Forgot her entirely?”

“Absolutely. What was the name?”

“Hemstetter.”

“Never heard it.”

Then John would drink, and Billy would grin with the patient cheerfulness of a man watching a parrot pretend it cannot talk.

Rosine belonged to Alabama, which is a state richly supplied with pine trees, politics, and young women capable of making a man leave home under the impression that he is acting from choice. Her hair was brown, her eyes were gray, and her refusal of John had been delivered with such gentleness that he had needed two oceans and a consular commission to recover from it.

The recovery, as already stated, was progressing nightly.

There had been another man in the case—Pinkney Dawson, called Pink by his friends and several other colors by John. Pinkney owned land, wore clean collars, and possessed that dreadful steadiness which mothers admire in a possible son-in-law.

“How’s Dink Pawson?” John sometimes asked.

“Pink Dawson,” Billy corrected.

“That’s what I said. Still hanging around the Hemstetter place?”

“Likely.”

“Let him hang. Rosine may marry a signpost if she likes. Pour me one.”

“Thought you’d forgotten her.”

“Who?”

“Hemstetter.”

“Never heard the name.”

Thus Love, expelled by the front door, returned each evening disguised as a question about Pink Dawson.

One afternoon, while Billy was discharging the duties of the United States and John was discharging a bottle, the mail steamer brought a letter from Alabama. It was addressed to the consul in the careful handwriting of a man who respects government stationery.

Billy slit it open.

“Here’s commercial inquiry,” he announced. “The postmaster at Bledsoe wants to know whether a first-class shoe store would pay in Coralio.”

John raised himself in the hammock.

“A what?”

“A shoe store.”

Outside, a barefoot mozo trotted past carrying a mahogany table on his head. Two brown children, also barefoot, chased a pig that maintained the same opinion of shoes. Across the plaza, barefoot market women sat among baskets of plantains. Even the policeman wore sandals borrowed, by their appearance, from the Punic wars.

John stared through the door.

“How many shoes,” he asked solemnly, “would you estimate Coralio consumes in the course of a year?”

“Counting both feet?”

“Be statistical.”

Billy took a pencil.

“There’s you.”

“I don’t own any,” said John, whose white canvas pair stood under the hammock.

“There’s me. The British merchant. The German druggist. Captain Beasley when his steamer’s in. The commandante has boots, but they belong to the army and squeak too loud to wear. Padre Esteban puts on shoes for mass. Madame Ortiz wears French slippers at the baile. Two customs officers have one pair between them, according to alternate Tuesdays.”

“An untouched field,” said John. “A golden opportunity.”

“A barefoot El Dorado.”

John got out of the hammock. Mischief can achieve in a moment what conscience fails to accomplish during an administration. He drew the official letter paper toward him, dipped the pen, and wrote with the stately gravity that governments employ when saying nothing.

“‘Sir,’” he read aloud, “‘in response to your esteemed communication concerning the commercial possibilities of this district in the line of boots, shoes, gaiters, slippers, brogans, congresses, and other coverings for the human foot, I have the honor to report that no establishment devoted exclusively to these indispensable articles exists within one hundred miles.’”

“True,” said Billy.

“Truth is the foundation of diplomacy. Listen. ‘The population is numerous, the climate favorable to pedestrian activity, and competition practically unknown.’”

“Also true, in a lopsided way.”

“‘A well-selected stock, particularly in durable grades, should command immediate attention. I may add that responsible local opinion regards such an enterprise as one of the most urgent commercial needs of Coralio.’”

“Whose responsible opinion?”

“Mine. I am bonded.”

John flourished his signature.

“‘Your obedient servant, John Atwood, United States Consul.’ There. The republic has spoken.”

“You ought to mention,” said Billy, “that the population regards leather on its feet the way a cat regards a bath.”

“The postmaster asked about opportunity, not prejudice. Enterprise must educate its market.”

Billy folded the letter.

“What other businesses does Coralio urgently need?”

“Fur overcoats. Snow shovels. A skating rink. A factory for woolen underwear. Send the letter.”

Billy sent it.

There are jokes that die upon utterance, poor little wingless things incapable of crossing a room. Others possess locomotion, appetite, and a passport. They board trains, change boats, enlist innocent persons, consume capital, and return months later with trunks.

John’s letter was of the traveling kind.

By the next mail he and Billy had forgotten it. Coralio resumed its usual occupations: sleeping, bargaining, playing guitars, holding revolutions in conversation, and waiting for rain. John continued to forget Rosine with improving quantities of brandy. The iron eagle rusted. The tree frogs rattled in the dark like bones in a wooden cup.

Then, one morning, the little coastal steamer *Pajaro* entered the harbor under a plume of smoke. Her deck was heaped with cases. Her captain came ashore and sent a boy to the consulate.

“Señor Atwood,” reported the boy, “there is on the ship an American señor, an American señorita, and many boxes marked zapato.”

The glass in John’s hand stopped halfway to his mouth.

“What name?”

“Hem—Hem—”

“Hemstetter?” said Billy.

“Sí, señor. That one.”

John set down the glass with care. A man may receive a bullet, bankruptcy, or matrimony with composure if properly warned. Surprise is the ingredient that turns misfortune into drama.

Billy looked through the window toward the harbor.

“You haven’t forgotten her too completely to go down and meet her, have you?”

John found his coat, rejected it, found another, and put it on inside out.

“Why is she here?”

“Maybe Dink Pawson sent her.”

“Pink Dawson,” said Billy. “And I suspect the shoe business has come to town.”

John stood motionless.

“The letter,” he said.

“The official commercial report.”

“Great bunions.”

They hurried to the wharf.

Mr. Abner Hemstetter came first down the gangplank—a tall, mild man with white whiskers, a black alpaca coat, and the pleased expression of one who has again escaped stability. He had successively cultivated peaches, edited a weekly paper, bred trotting horses, represented a patent churn, and opened an academy of practical elocution. Each enterprise had been approached with faith and left with debts. Change was not his habit; it was his profession.

Behind him came Rosine.

The tropics, which fade ribbons, reputations, and political promises, could do nothing at once with Rosine. She wore gray, with a narrow traveling hat, and held herself as coolly as if Alabama shade trees accompanied her. John saw the same calm eyes, the same brown hair, the same mouth that had once said, “I’m sorry, John,” and upset his geography.

For eighteen months he had rehearsed indifference. At sight of her the whole production closed before the curtain rose.

“Why, John,” she said, offering her hand, “father told me you were consul here.”

John took the hand and forgot every diplomatic formula in his possession.

“I am,” he said. “More or less.”

Mr. Hemstetter embraced him.

“My boy! Providence has arranged everything. When our postmaster showed me your letter, I knew opportunity was knocking. I sold the house, the orchard, the two lots on Jefferson Street, and my interest in the feed warehouse. Every cent is in those cases.”

John looked toward the steamer.

Sailors were lowering box after box: women’s button shoes, men’s brogans, children’s boots, patent-leather pumps, russet walking shoes, slippers, gaiters, laces, polish, horns, trees, and enough durable American leather to shod an infantry division through a granite campaign.

“How much?” John whispered.

“Nearly four thousand dollars wholesale,” said Mr. Hemstetter triumphantly. “I followed your advice about durable grades. The people here are active pedestrians, you said.”

At that instant a porter carrying a two-hundred-pound case stepped upon a stone with his bare foot, shifted the box, and continued without inconvenience.

Billy coughed into his hand.

Rosine regarded John.

“You advised father to come?”

There are moments when truth presents itself as a noble steed ready to bear a man into honor. John looked at Rosine, at her father, and at the mountain of shoes. He allowed the steed to pass.

“I supplied certain information,” he said.

“He wrote the finest business letter I ever read,” declared Mr. Hemstetter. “No competition! Urgent need! Immediate attention!”

Rosine smiled.

“I hope you were right, John.”

“So do I,” he said, with feeling.

The Hemstetters rented an empty building on the plaza. By sunset a sign announced:

**HEMSTETTER’S AMERICAN SHOE EMPORIUM**  
**FOOT COMFORT, ELEGANCE AND CIVILIZATION**

The next morning the doors opened.

Coralio attended magnificently.

Citizens crowded the windows, admired the shining merchandise, touched the leather, smelled it, discussed it, and called in their relatives. Children begged for the bright boxes. A fisherman offered two parrots for a pair of yellow shoes, not to wear but to hang over his doorway. An old woman bought six feet of laces for her goat. One young caballero tried on patent-leather pumps, strutted before the mirror, learned the price, and departed barefoot with his dignity only slightly pinched.

At the end of the day the cash drawer contained one peso and twenty centavos.

Mr. Hemstetter remained hopeful.

“They are cautious buyers,” he said. “That is sound commercial character.”

On the second day sales reached eighty centavos. On the third, nothing was sold except a shoehorn, which a boatman mistook for a device for scraping fish.

Billy summoned the foreign colony.

He bought two pairs of boots, three pairs of slippers, and shoes intended for a boy of twelve, though he had no boy and no immediate intention of acquiring one. The British merchant purchased heavy walking shoes and complained that Americans confused architecture with footwear. The German druggist bought four pairs at cost and tried to resell them beside his quinine. Captain Beasley ordered sea boots, then discovered none were sea boots. The padre accepted a pair on credit. Madame Ortiz bought satin slippers after a discount that caused Mr. Hemstetter to sit down abruptly.

John purchased fourteen pairs.

“You have only two feet,” Rosine observed.

“Official requirements,” he answered. “A consul must be prepared.”

“For what?”

“Changes in the weather.”

There was no change in the weather.

By the end of the week the foreign population had shoes under its beds, shoes in its cupboards, and shoes pledged for birthdays extending into the next decade. The stock in the Emporium seemed undiminished. Cases rose against the walls like the fortifications of a leather kingdom.

Meanwhile John’s torment developed departments.

He loved Rosine. This was inconvenient but not new.

He had helped ruin her father. This was new.

She believed him a wise and generous friend. This was unbearable.

Every evening she sat outside the shop while the plaza cooled. The lamps shone among the orange trees; guitars murmured from courtyards; cigarettes moved through the dark like restless red insects. John came to talk with her and remained to condemn himself.

“Father thinks business will improve,” she said one night.

“It must.”

“You sound as if you could order it.”

“I am the American consul.”

“Does that give you authority over feet?”

“Only American feet, and they have done their duty.”

She laughed, then became grave.

“We risked everything, John. Father does not understand worry until it is too late. I do the worrying in advance.”

John wanted to tell her. Confession rose to his lips, encountered pride, fear, love, and cowardice there, and was turned back for lack of documents.

Instead he said, “I won’t let you lose.”

Rosine looked at him steadily.

“You used to make promises like that in Alabama.”

“I used to mean them there, too.”

“And then you went away.”

“I had been refused.”

“You had been asked to wait.”

“For Pink Dawson?”

Her eyebrows lifted.

“Pink had nothing to do with it.”

“Dink Pawson always had something to do with everything.”

“His name is Pink.”

“So I’ve heard.”

She smiled faintly and went inside.

John returned to the consulate and attacked the brandy. It tasted suddenly like medicine prescribed by an enemy. He poured the glass out of the window.

Billy, entering at that moment, watched the sacrifice fall upon a hibiscus.

“Plant sick?” he asked.

“I’m through.”

“With the brandy?”

“With being a fool.”

“That’s a larger contract.”

John paced the room.

“I wrote that letter. Hemstetter sold his home because of me. Rosine trusted me because she thinks I’m something I’m not. If I tell her, she’ll despise me.”

“Likely.”

“If I don’t tell her, I despise myself.”

“Cheaper, but uncomfortable.”

“We must sell those shoes.”

Billy counted on his fingers.

“We’ve shod every foreigner, the padre, one goat indirectly, and Beasley’s cabin boy. The natives prefer the arrangement furnished by nature.”

“They walk over stones, thorns, shells—”

“From infancy. Their soles could sand furniture.”

John seized his hat.

“Get out.”

“It’s my hat.”

“Then leave it and get out. I’ve got to think.”

Billy surrendered the hat.

“When you begin, pull out the full stop. Half thinking has done enough damage.”

All night John walked.

He walked through the plaza, past the shuttered Emporium, along the beach where the surf came in with a white edge and withdrew as if dissatisfied with the country. He considered false advertising, government contracts, a compulsory shoe ordinance, a religious decree, and importing a thousand American tourists by force. He imagined setting fire to the stock, insuring it afterward, and fleeing to Peru. He imagined confessing to Rosine and saw her calm eyes grow cold.

Near dawn he sat beneath a thorn tree beyond the cuartel.

A burr fastened itself to his trouser leg.

John pulled it off, cursed it, and stared.

The burr was dry, hard, and furnished with more points than a politician’s platform. Another clung to his sock. He stepped back and found six more waiting with impartial hostility.

Then the great machinery of his mind, long idle and somewhat rusted by brandy, made one complete revolution.

At sunrise Billy found him at the consulate desk surrounded by paper, maps, a shipping schedule, and three cups of black coffee.

“You look sober,” Billy said suspiciously.

“I am.”

“Then something serious has happened.”

John held up a cockleburr.

“What is this?”

“A cockleburr.”

“What does it do?”

“Clings.”

“To bare feet?”

Billy’s grin widened slowly until it resembled the expression of a shark receiving good commercial news.

“I begin to hear the wheels,” he said.

“How many pounds would it take to distribute an educational sample through the streets, paths, plaza, market ground, beach approaches, and neighboring roads of Coralio?”

“Johnny, that is wicked.”

“It is commerce. The people have no need of shoes. We establish the need. Civilization has often begun with discomfort.”

“Where do you get cockleburrs in wholesale quantities?”

John tapped a name in an Alabama newspaper.

Pinkney Dawson had recently advertised seed, fodder, and farm supplies from his warehouse outside Bledsoe.

“Pink Dawson,” Billy said.

“Dink Pawson,” said John. “He owns low fields full of the things. I remember picking them off my trousers when I visited his place. He’ll think I’m crazy, which gives us an advantage.”

“What will five hundred pounds cost?”

“Not enough. Therefore we offer too much. A low price invites questions; a high price commands packing.”

John drew a cable form toward him and wrote:

> PINKNEY DAWSON, BLEDSOE, ALABAMA.  
> SHIP IMMEDIATELY FASTEST ROUTE FIVE HUNDRED POUNDS DRY COCKLEBURRS CLEAN AND SOUND. WILL PAY FIFTY CENTS POUND PLUS FREIGHT. URGENT GOVERNMENT COMMERCIAL PURPOSE. NO QUESTIONS. WIRE ACCEPTANCE.  
> JOHN ATWOOD, CONSUL, CORALIO.

Billy read it twice.

“Fifty cents a pound! He’ll strip the county.”

“I want promptness.”

“What if he asks what for?”

“Jealous men don’t ask when they believe a rival has lost his mind.”

“And when the burrs arrive?”

“We employ boys at night. One sack to the market road, two to the plaza, three around the baile, a generous line before the church, and a light but persuasive scattering everywhere else. By noon Coralio will discover podiatry. By sunset Hemstetter will be selling salvation by the pair.”

Billy sat down and laughed soundlessly.

“You’d injure an entire republic to save one girl’s opinion of you.”

“And her father’s four thousand dollars.”

“Of course. Business first.”

John leaned back. His face was pale from the night, but his eyes had recovered something that brandy had been trying to drown.

“If it works,” he said, “I’ll tell Rosine everything.”

“And if it doesn’t?”

“I’ll tell her and take the next steamer.”

Billy picked up the cable.

“And Pink Dawson?”

John looked toward the Emporium. Across the plaza Rosine had opened the shutters. Morning light touched her hair, and beside her Mr. Hemstetter arranged a hopeful row of unsold shoes.

“Let Dink Pawson,” said John, “perform one useful act in his life.”

Billy carried the message to the cable office.

At ten minutes past nine, the instrument began to click. The operator wrote the reply and sent it by a barefoot boy across the plaza.

John tore open the paper.

> FIVE HUNDRED POUNDS ASSURED. CAN SUPPLY THOUSAND. SHIPPING FRIDAY. PAYMENT EXPECTED. PURPOSE PROBABLY IDIOTIC.  
> PINKNEY DAWSON.

Billy read over his shoulder.

“Well?”

John folded the telegram, looked at the barefoot messenger, and gave him a silver coin.

“Go buy yourself something, muchacho.”

The boy glanced toward the shoe store, then ran directly to the sweetmeat stall.

John sighed.

“Education,” he said, “is always slow at the beginning.”

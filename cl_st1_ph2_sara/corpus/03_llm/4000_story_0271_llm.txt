## What the Wind Remembered at Borreby

Have you heard the wind when it runs round an old house at night? Have you heard it put its mouth to the keyhole, groan in the chimney, and shake the loose window-frame as though some one stood without and begged admittance?

Then stir the fire, draw your chair nearer, and listen.

The wind knows much. It has looked beneath crowns and cottage-thatch; it has played with bridal ribbons and burial weeds; it has turned the leaves of books that no living hand opens now. It remembers what men forget.

I am the wind! I ran over Borreby Hall when the roofs were new and the weathercocks shone like flames against the blue. I run there still, though the gilding is gone and the names once spoken with reverence have dropped from memory like leaves into a ditch.

Whew!—whew!—fare away!

In the old days Borreby stood broad and proud among fields, gardens and deep woods. Its walls were red, its gables stepped high, and its windows caught the morning sun. There were carved chairs from foreign lands, tapestries where kings hunted stags that never escaped, cups of silver, flagons of German ale, polished floors upon which silk shoes whispered from door to door. Black horses stamped in the stables, and the gilded coach rolled out beneath the gatehouse whenever Herr Waldemar Daa and his lady went to church.

I knew every chimney. I had counted every tree in the forest. I had rocked the rooks in their high nests, shaken acorns upon the wild boars, and blown the fragrance of lime blossom across the moat.

Herr Waldemar Daa was highborn and learned. He could speak of wars, stars, metals and kings. His brow was broad, his beard carefully trimmed, and his neck held stiffly, as if an invisible collar of iron kept his head forever lifted above other men. His ancestors looked down from the walls in armour and lace, and he never passed them without thinking, “I am of your blood.”

His lady was mild and beautiful. She moved through Borreby as the evening moves through a summer garden, softening all that is hard. There were three daughters.

The eldest, Ida, was a rose—warm of cheek, tender of heart, and easily bent by love or sorrow.

The second, Johanna, was a lily—white, upright and proud. Her glance was sharp, and she would sooner break than bow.

The youngest, Anna Dorothea, was a pale hyacinth, delicate and grave, with eyes that seemed always to listen for something beyond the sound of speech.

I played with their curls when they ran beneath the trees. I filled their little aprons with fallen blossom. I snatched the ribbons from their caps and carried them into the shrubbery, so that they must laugh and chase me.

“Give it back!” cried Ida.

“Come and take mine, if you dare!” cried Johanna.

But Anna Dorothea stood beneath the old elm where a stork had built its nest upon a broken chimney-stack. A servant boy had thrown a stone and loosened the dry branches. The nest tilted; the young birds clattered their beaks and beat their unfledged wings.

“Do not hurt them!” Anna Dorothea cried.

She fetched men from the stable, and begged until they brought a ladder and bound the nest securely with rope and willow withes. Herr Waldemar laughed when he heard of it.

“A Daa troubling herself for marsh birds!”

“They have a house, father,” said the child, “and they were afraid to lose it.”

The lady kissed her. I went circling above the chimney and rustled the stork’s wings.

Remember that nest. Men call little deeds little because their eyes are little. I have blown over the world, and I tell you: no kindness is so light that it cannot outlast a stone tower.

Then sorrow entered Borreby Hall.

It did not come with trumpet or drum. It came softly, in a closed coach, with a physician who shook his head; it came in covered mirrors, whispered steps and a bell tolling through rain.

The lady died.

I had breathed against her window all night. Within, the candles burned low, the daughters knelt, and Herr Waldemar stood with his head high. When morning came, he had not bowed it. Not even grief could bend that stiff neck.

The coffin passed between lines of tenants. Ida wept without restraint. Johanna bit her lip until it bled. Anna Dorothea clasped her hands and looked toward the clouds. Herr Waldemar walked behind them as if he followed a queen to her throne rather than a wife to the grave.

Afterward the great rooms grew louder and emptier. The lady’s footstep was gone, and no music, no hunting party, no feast could fill the silence it left.

That was when Waldemar began to build his dream.

A war was spoken of. Men told of fleets and cannon, treaties broken and kingdoms watchful. Waldemar had read of shipbuilding; he had studied drawings brought from Holland. He walked among the ancient oaks and struck their trunks with his glove.

“Here lies a man-of-war,” he said. “Here lie masts, ribs, decks and gun-carriages. The king shall buy her. Borreby shall gain both honour and gold.”

The master shipbuilder, a square, weather-browned man, bowed and ventured a warning.

“Your Honour, the trees are worth a fortune standing. A ship costs twice in the building what a gentleman expects, and half again before she touches water.”

Waldemar looked above him, not at him.

“You know how to shape timber. I know what becomes my estate.”

So the axes came.

Oh, how they rang! Blow after blow went through the forest. The deer fled. The fox left her earth. Owls rose blinking at noon, and squirrels leaped from branches that crashed beneath them. The rooks wheeled black against the sky, crying as if a city burned.

I rushed among the leaves. I tugged at the woodmen’s coats; I threw chips into their eyes; I made the tallest oak groan before it fell. But steel bit, ropes tightened, and one by one the old trees bowed lower than Waldemar had ever bowed.

A forest grows by rain, sun, frost and patience. A fool may lay it low in a season.

Whew!—whew!—fare away!

At the inlet beyond the fields, the ship took form. Her ribs rose like the bones of a great beast. Hammers sounded from dawn to dusk. Carts sank axle-deep beneath timber. Pitch smoked; sawdust covered the shore; wages, iron, cordage and provisions ate through chests of coin.

Waldemar came daily on his black horse.

“She shall carry sixty guns.”

“Forty would better suit her breadth,” said the shipbuilder.

“Sixty.”

“She sits deep already.”

“Then make her higher.”

The ship grew high, broad and splendid. Her stern was carved with lions, shields and the proud device of the Daa family. Her figurehead wore a crown. Flags fluttered from her masts when she was launched, and all the countryside came to see.

Barrels of ale were opened. Musicians played. Ida wore crimson silk; Johanna wore white satin; Anna Dorothea had a blue ribbon at her throat. Waldemar stood upon the quay in gold brocade.

The wedges were knocked free. The vessel trembled, slid, struck the water and rolled so heavily that sailors crossed themselves. Yet she floated. The people cheered, because people will cheer a costly thing when the ale is free.

I filled the flags and whistled through the rigging.

“Now,” Waldemar said, “the king shall see what service a loyal nobleman can render.”

Messengers rode out. Letters went to court. Weeks passed.

The king’s officers came at last. They measured, inspected and whispered. They struck the beams, examined the keel, counted the ports and looked long at one another.

“She is strongly built,” said the chief officer.

Waldemar smiled.

“But she is too heavy for our purpose, too costly to arm, and ill-suited to the waters where she must serve.”

The smile remained, but hardened.

“The king refuses her?” Waldemar asked.

“The Crown does not require the vessel.”

The officers departed.

There stood the man-of-war—magnificent, useless, consuming money even at anchor. Rain streaked her painted sides. Weed gathered beneath her hull. The carved lions stared over muddy water while creditors began to knock at Borreby’s gate.

The shipbuilder had a son, Niels, a quiet young man with clear eyes and strong hands. He had often seen Ida among the gardens, and Ida had often seen him, though silk curtains and ancient names stood between them.

One autumn evening Niels came to Borreby in his best coat. He asked to speak with Herr Waldemar.

“I love Frøken Ida,” he said. “My father’s yard prospers. I can provide her an honest house.”

Waldemar rose so quickly that his chair fell backward.

“A shipwright’s son?”

“An honest man’s son.”

“A sparrow does not marry into a crane’s nest.”

Niels flushed, bowed and went away.

Ida stood in the passage, white as linen. Her father passed her without speaking. That night I heard her weeping behind the tapestry, while the portraits in the hall looked down with painted pride and offered no comfort.

The useless ship was sold at last for less than the timber had cost. Part became a warehouse, part was broken for beams, and the crowned figurehead lay for years in a nettled yard, staring up at rain.

But Waldemar did not count the loss as other men counted it. Pride has strange arithmetic. He said the king’s officers were ignorant, the shipbuilder timid, the age unworthy of great designs.

Then he turned from timber to gold.

He had long possessed books on alchemy: thick volumes with black-letter pages, signs of planets, dragons eating their tails, kings buried in furnaces and suns rising from vessels of glass. He read them first for learning, then for hope, and at last for hunger.

“Gold is not created,” said the steward. “It is earned, inherited or taken.”

“Gold sleeps in baser matter,” Waldemar replied. “The learned man awakens it.”

A chamber beneath the west tower became his laboratory. There he built furnaces and set retorts, crucibles, coils and alembics upon brick tables. He bought salts, acids, sulphur, mercury and powders whose names impressed those who did not understand them. He melted plate, coin and ornaments to feed experiments that promised more than they consumed.

At first the daughters brought him meals.

“Father, come into the garden,” Ida begged. “The roses are open.”

“Roses become dust.”

Johanna said, “The tenants wait for judgment in the boundary dispute.”

“Let the steward decide.”

Anna Dorothea placed a hand upon his sleeve.

“You have not slept.”

“I shall sleep when Borreby’s chests are filled again.”

The furnace roared. Smoke blackened the ceiling. Sharp vapours stung the eyes. I crept through cracks and scattered papers, but Waldemar shut the casements and stuffed the keyhole with cloth.

“Keep out!” he cried. “The balance must not be disturbed.”

Keep out the wind? Keep out time? He might as well have ordered winter to halt at the gate.

Silver dishes disappeared from the dining hall. Then goblets, candlesticks and serving trays. The gilded coach was sold. Two black horses went, then four. Servants were dismissed.

Fewer servants; more mice.

The tapestries gathered dust. Rain entered through a cracked pane and spotted the silk wall-covering. The clocks stopped one by one because no one wound them. In the barns, empty stalls echoed. In the kitchen, the cook measured flour with anxious hands.

Still the laboratory glowed night and day.

“Another trial,” Waldemar said. “One more purification.”

A meadow was mortgaged.

“One more.”

The southern farm was sold.

“One more.”

The forest was gone already; now the fields followed it, acre by acre, signature by signature.

Ida’s gowns were turned and remade. Johanna refused to mend hers until the sleeves frayed. Anna Dorothea learned to patch linen so finely that the repair seemed a pale flower in the cloth.

Winters came. Snow fell upon Borreby and melted from the roofs. Springs came. Apple trees blossomed and shed their petals into unmown grass. Summer burned the lawns yellow. Autumn shook the last leaves through broken casements.

Whew!—whew!—fare away!

Then came the night of triumph.

Rain drove against the west tower. The daughters sat in a room where only one candle burned, for tallow cost money. Ida stitched. Johanna stared into the dead hearth. Anna Dorothea read a psalm under her breath.

Suddenly a bell rang below.

Once, twice, wildly!

They ran to the laboratory.

Waldemar stood before the furnace. His hair was grey and disordered; his face was hollow; his velvet coat was stained with ash. Yet his eyes shone as they had shone in the days of feasts.

In his hands he held a glass vessel. At the bottom lay a glowing lump, yellow-red, bright as fire beneath water.

“Gold!” he cried. “Do you see? I have conquered it! Borreby is saved. The forests will rise again; the farms will return; the king himself shall hear of Waldemar Daa!”

Ida clasped her hands.

Johanna stepped nearer. “Is it truly gold?”

“Truly? You ask me? I have weighed, washed and proved it!”

Anna Dorothea looked not at the vessel but at his trembling fingers.

“Set it down, father.”

“Set down my victory? No! I shall carry it through the hall. Let my forefathers behold what their son has done.”

He strode toward the door, head high, neck stiff.

A furnace draws air. A chamber has cracks. I had waited outside all those years.

Now I entered.

Not fiercely—no. Only a breath through the ill-fastened casement, a small cold finger upon the flame. The candle leaped. A paper flew. Waldemar turned.

His sleeve caught the iron stand.

The glass slipped.

It struck the stone floor and shattered.

There came a bright flash, a hiss, a burst of bitter smoke. The yellow lump broke into black grains and greenish scum. Acid ran between the stones. What had looked like treasure smoked and vanished with the fumes.

For a moment no one spoke.

Waldemar knelt among the shards. He touched the stained floor with both hands.

“No,” he whispered.

Johanna turned away.

Ida began to sob.

Anna Dorothea knelt beside him, though the vapour made her cough.

“Father.”

He lifted his head. His face had become very old.

“The vessel was impure,” he said. “The final conjunction was disturbed. It can be done again.”

But there was no money for another trial. No fuel. No metals. No credit.

Nothing to melt, and nothing to burn!

The creditors came in winter. Their boots left snow and mud upon the floor where princes had once danced. They opened cupboards, counted chairs, lifted lids and wrote in ledgers. One took the carved chest from the blue chamber; another marked the great dining table. The portraits were valued by the frames.

Waldemar walked from room to room while strangers handled his possessions.

“This is entailed,” he said.

“It was pledged.”

“That cup belonged to my grandfather.”

“It was listed among the securities.”

“You cannot take Borreby.”

But Borreby had been taken already—field by field, coin by coin, flame by flame.

On the morning of eviction, frost silvered the empty courtyard. A cart stood at the gate. Upon it lay three trunks, bedding, a kettle and a bundle of clothes. That was all.

Ida wore a brown cloak too thin for the weather. Johanna carried herself as if she departed by choice. Anna Dorothea wrapped a shawl around her father’s shoulders.

Waldemar paused beneath the archway. Above him the stone shield of his house remained set in the wall. He looked at it a long while.

“Come, father,” Anna Dorothea said.

He did not answer.

A gust crossed the courtyard. I lifted dead leaves from the corners, whirled straw round his boots and slammed the great door behind him.

So Herr Waldemar Daa left Borreby Hall.

No trumpets. No black horses. No tenants kneeling by the road.

Only a groaning cart and four figures walking beside it through the frost.

Whew!—whew!—fare away!

They found shelter in a poor cottage beyond the marshes. Its roof sagged, its door hung loose, and the smoke preferred the room to the chimney. Rain crept through cracks; rats scratched behind the boards. In winter the water froze in the pail.

Waldemar still spoke of restoration. He kept one alchemical book beneath his pillow and covered its pages with notes.

“When I obtain a little copper,” he said, “and a proper furnace—”

“There is no bread,” Johanna answered.

He frowned as though hunger were an impertinence.

Ida worked in a farmhouse, first sewing, then milking and churning. There Niels found her again. He was no longer merely a shipwright’s son; he had his own yard, a modest house and work enough for willing hands.

“I asked once,” he said. “I ask again.”

Ida looked toward the cottage where her father sat among ashes.

“If I say yes, I wound him.”

“If you say no, whom do you save?”

She wept, for the rose bends beneath both rain and sun. Then she said yes.

They were married in the village church. Ida wore no crimson silk, only a plain woollen kirtle and a sprig of rosemary. Niels stood beside her, honest and steady. The peasants feasted them with rye bread, smoked fish and ale.

Waldemar did not attend.

When Ida came afterward to ask his blessing, he remained seated.

“You have made yourself a peasant’s wife.”

“I have made myself the wife of a good man.”

“The blood of the Daa family—”

“Blood does not warm a cold house, father.”

He looked at her then, and the words struck deeper because she had spoken gently.

“Go,” he said.

She knelt and kissed his hand. He did not bless her. Yet when she had gone, I saw him press that hand to his eyes.

Ida’s cottage was small, but there was bread in it. Children came. Their voices were loud, their feet muddy, their cheeks red. They knew nothing of gold brocade or silver flagons, and they laughed as freely as princes.

Waldemar never entered their home.

His pride was now the last estate he owned, and he guarded it fiercely.

Johanna could not endure the marsh cottage. She who had walked upright through the halls of Borreby would not bow her head under a peasant’s doorway.

“I shall seek service abroad,” she said.

“As a governess?” Anna Dorothea asked.

“As myself.”

But the world had little use for a highborn maiden without dowry, reference or skill in submission.

Johanna cut her hair. She bound her breast, put on breeches and a sailor’s coat, and called herself Johan. Her hands blistered at the ropes, but she did not complain. She went aboard a merchant brig bound southward, and no one guessed that the proud young deckhand was Waldemar Daa’s second daughter.

I went with the ship.

I filled the sails beneath fair skies. I carried salt over the rails and made the rigging sing. Johanna climbed higher than many men and looked toward the horizon with fierce joy.

“At sea,” she thought, “no one asks the age of your ancestors.”

That is nearly true. The sea asks only whether you can stand.

Beyond the northern cape, clouds gathered black and low. The captain shortened sail. Waves rose; rain struck like shot; the deck pitched beneath running feet.

Johanna was sent aloft.

“Hold fast!” the mate shouted.

She held fast. Her fingers were strong. Her courage was stronger.

But I was strongest.

Why did I strike her? Ask the storm why it breaks one tree and spares another. Ask time why the good die before the foolish. I had pitied her, mocked her, admired her; yet I rushed from the dark water and caught the sail broadside.

The yard swung.

Johanna lost her footing.

For one instant she hung above the deck, white-faced, still proud, one hand clutching the rope. A sailor reached for her.

Then I tore her away.

She fell between two waves. Her cap vanished. Her cut hair streamed, and perhaps in that final moment the men saw that Johan was Johanna. Perhaps not.

The sea closed.

The brig turned, ropes were cast, men searched until darkness hid the foam. Nothing returned.

No grave received her. No bell tolled at Borreby.

I sang in the shrouds all night, and my song was not laughter.

Whew!—whew!—fare away!

Herr Waldemar heard the news months later from a sailor who had shipped on the brig. The old man stood outside the marsh cottage, staring over the reeds.

“She died bravely,” Anna Dorothea said.

“She died nameless,” he answered.

Soon afterward he took to his bed. There was no physician in a closed coach now, only an old woman from the village with herbs and warm cloths. Snow blew beneath the door. Ida came and sat beside him, though he had never crossed her threshold. Her eldest child waited outside, afraid of the stern old man who was his grandfather.

Waldemar’s alchemical book lay open upon the blanket.

Near dawn he murmured, “The proportion was correct.”

Anna Dorothea held his hand.

“Think now of another kingdom, father.”

He looked at her, and for once his head lay low.

“Borreby,” he whispered.

Then he died.

They buried him in the village churchyard. There was no marble monument, no armoured figure, no long procession of tenants. A small stone bore his name. Moss covered the lower letters. Frost split the upper edge.

Ida returned to her husband and children. She lived, worked, aged and was loved. Her descendants carried other names. The rose had rooted in common earth, and common earth proved kind.

Anna Dorothea remained alone.

Years passed over her.

Snow, blossom, leaves. Snow, blossom, leaves.

She spun flax, mended clothes and gathered reeds. Sometimes Ida sent food. Sometimes there was none to send. Anna Dorothea grew thin; her hands became knotted; her pale hair turned white. Yet she shared crumbs with birds and set broken-winged creatures in a basket by the hearth.

The marsh cottage fell at last, and she moved into a hovel near the old road—a wretched place with clay walls, one dim window and a roof patched with straw. It had once been a herdsman’s shelter. No one knew who owned it. No one cared.

A stork built its nest upon the chimney.

When men came to clear the ruin, one raised his pole toward the roof.

“Leave it until autumn,” said another. “There are young birds above.”

Autumn came, but by then the magistrate had forgotten the order. In spring the storks returned. Again the roof was spared.

Year after year, the nest grew broad and heavy. Children pointed to it from the road.

“That is the stork-house,” they said. “The old woman lives beneath.”

Did the descendants of the birds remember the child at Borreby who saved a falling nest? Birds do not keep ledgers as men do. Heaven may.

Half a century fled.

I had crossed oceans, rattled palace windows, driven mills and scattered battle-smoke before I returned one winter evening to the marshes.

Borreby Hall still stood, but other feet crossed its floors. The old forest was pasture. The inlet where the man-of-war had floated was choked with reeds. Of Waldemar’s ship there remained not one beam known by its history.

I sought the hovel.

The stork’s nest, empty for winter, crowned the broken chimney like a dark, rough diadem. Beneath it the roof held, though barely. I slipped through a crack.

Anna Dorothea sat beside a hearth where three pieces of peat smouldered. She was very old. Her dress was patched upon patches; her shoes were wrapped with cord. A bowl held water. On the table lay half a crust.

Nothing to bite, and little to burn!

Yet the room was clean. A wooden cross hung on the wall. Beside it lay a faded blue ribbon, folded carefully—the ribbon she had worn at the launching of the great ship.

She heard me in the chimney.

“Is that you?” she asked.

Perhaps she spoke to me. Perhaps to death.

I stirred the ash gently.

She smiled. “You were loud at Borreby.”

Yes, I had been loud there. I had shaken the banners, roared through the felled forest, filled the useless sails, broken the hope in the glass, slammed the gate, and swept Johanna from the rope.

I had done much.

Anna Dorothea clasped her hands.

“Lord,” she said, “I have had little, but more than I deserved. Take care of Ida’s children. Take care of Johanna in the deep water. Forgive my father his pride. He was lonely after mother died.”

No reproach for herself. That is the way with the gentle: they carry other people’s burdens and call their own light.

The peat sank into ash. Moonlight entered through the patched pane. Above us the old nest creaked.

She lay down upon her straw bed and drew the blanket to her chin.

By morning she was dead.

The parish buried her near the outer wall of the churchyard. The clerk misspelled her second name in the register. No stone was raised. Grass covered the mound; then the boundary was altered, and a cart-track passed near it.

The hovel stood one more spring because the storks returned. When they departed in autumn, rain broke the roof. The clay walls dissolved into the earth. Children found the blue ribbon in the mud and tied it round a stick for play. Before winter, I had carried it away.

Whew!—whew!—fare away!

Now new roads cross the country. Steam whistles where the heron once stood in quiet water. Iron wheels hurry past with merchants, soldiers, farmers and fine ladies who know nothing of Waldemar Daa. They see Borreby’s distant gables and ask, perhaps, “Who lived there?”

Names are offered, names of later owners, newer fortunes, other prides.

No one speaks of the forest that became a ship which never fought. No one remembers the alchemist bending over false gold, or Ida in her woollen kirtle, or Johanna beneath the black wave, or Anna Dorothea praying under the stork’s nest.

The churchyard stones lean. Rain eats the letters. Roads widen. Ditches fill. Men lay gravel over places where the forgotten sleep.

But I know.

I blow over the hall and the cottage, over cradle and grave. I enter the rich man’s chamber and the poor woman’s hovel. I turn the mill and scatter the ash. I lift crowns from carved shields and seeds from humble weeds, and I carry both into the same earth.

Can you hear me at your window?

The fire burns warmly; the room is safe; your name is spoken and your place is known. So it seemed at Borreby Hall. So it seemed beneath the banners and beside the silver plate.

Listen, then, before the flame sinks.

Pride builds high, but time climbs higher. Gold glitters, glass breaks, forests fall, and rank is a garment the grave does not keep. Yet a child’s mercy to a trembling bird may remain when heraldry, wealth and learned folly have all fared away.

That is what I remember.

That is what the wind told.

Whew!—whew!—fare away!

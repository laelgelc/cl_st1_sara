## THE BRASS BEAST

The corvette *Claymore* had left the English coast at dusk, under a sky of iron.

She was an old French vessel, captured, renamed, altered, disguised, and at last restored to her first allegiance by those secret transformations which war calls stratagem and peace calls treason. Her hull was black; her sails were brown with age; no flag flew at her peak. She carried thirty guns, a crew chosen for silence, and a single passenger whose name had not been spoken aloud since they weighed anchor.

Toward midnight the wind freshened.

The coast vanished. The sea grew white. The corvette entered that narrow and sinister wilderness between Brittany and the Channel Islands, where rocks lie in ambush beneath the foam, currents pull four ways at once, and the compass itself seems to hesitate. Jersey was somewhere to larboard, Guernsey somewhere ahead; but darkness had swallowed both.

Below, the men slept beside the guns.

A ship of war is a town constructed over an abyss. Its streets are decks, its houses hammocks, its walls oak, its foundations waves. It carries fire in its belly, thunder along its sides, and death in orderly rows. So long as every bolt holds, every breeching remains fast, every sailor obeys, this floating city is obedient to man. Let one knot fail, and the abyss remembers its rights.

Something terrible had just happened.

On the lower gun-deck, a carronade had broken loose.

The weapon was a short thirty-six-pounder, squat, broad-mouthed, heavy as an elephant and almost as mobile when its chains were gone. Its breeching had not been properly secured. During the first violent roll the ring-bolt held; during the second, the rope slipped; during the third, the hook sprang free.

Then the cannon moved.

At first it advanced only half a foot, as if awakening. Its truck-wheels creaked. The deck inclined. It rolled backward, struck the ship’s side, and rebounded. A sailor, roused from sleep, raised himself upon one elbow.

The next roll flung the carronade across the deck.

The man disappeared beneath it.

There was a cry, a cracking of bones, and then a broad red stain travelled slowly with the slope of the planks.

The gun was free.

Nothing more terrible can happen on board a vessel.

A loose cannon is no longer a machine. It is an animal. It has the blind life of matter set at liberty. The ship gives it movement; the sea gives it caprice; the storm gives it fury. It starts, pauses, rushes, turns; it seems to choose its victims, to calculate its leaps, to conceal its intention. It is the thunderbolt made cunning. It is a mastodon in a chamber. It is a demon of brass playing at ball with men.

The *Claymore* rolled to starboard.

The carronade charged.

Five sailors were running toward the companion-way. The gun overtook two of them. One was thrown against a beam and remained there, folded in two like a garment. The other had only time to turn his head. The wheel passed over his chest. Blood burst from his mouth.

“Up! All hands up!” shouted the lieutenant.

The order was useless and necessary. Men scrambled for the ladders, slipped, caught at ropes, climbed over one another. A gunner attempted to throw a chock before the wheels. The carronade struck the chock, leaped like a panther, and landed upon him. When it rolled away, nothing human remained except one hand still closed around the block of wood.

The lanterns swung madly from the beams. Their circles of yellow light crossed, separated, climbed the walls, fell upon faces, vanished. Between those flashes the gun seemed to multiply. Now it was at the bow, now at the stern; now it crouched in darkness, now it sprang into fire.

It smashed the carriage of a twelve-pounder. The second gun, still secured, resisted; the carronade struck it again, broke one wheel, bent the axle, and tore loose the tackles. Iron assaulted iron. Sparks flew. The deck rang like a forge.

Then the sea lifted the corvette upon its shoulder and let her fall.

The carronade bounded.

For one instant its four wheels left the deck. It seemed suspended beneath the beams, a black and monstrous insect. Then it came down upon the muzzle of another gun, overturned it, tore away a portion of the bulwark, and drove the shattered wood outward through the hull.

The ocean showed itself through the wound.

A black wave thrust in its hand.

The carpenter saw the water and turned pale.

“Hole below the line!” he cried.

But his voice was lost in the tumult.

The cannon backed, advanced, struck right, struck left, wheeled, hesitated, rushed; it demolished a rack of muskets, cut a hammock in two, snapped a stanchion, tore loose planking, crushed shot-boxes, and scattered round-shot across the deck. Those iron balls joined the dance. They rolled in every direction around their enormous mother, little planets obeying a mad sun.

A sailor fell. Shot rolled over his fingers. He screamed. At once the cannon descended upon him, and the scream ceased.

Five men were dead.

The survivors had reached the upper deck. They gathered around the open hatchway, looking down.

To describe their terror is impossible.

They were brave men. Several had stood under English broadsides; two had boarded a frigate through burning rigging; one had been buried beneath a fallen battery at Toulon and had dug himself out with a bayonet. Yet all shrank before this thing. An enemy can be faced. A blade has a hand behind it; a bullet has a direction; even fire, which devours, can be anticipated. But this mass had all directions and no hand. It was chance armed with thirty-six hundred pounds of bronze.

“What is to be done?”

No one answered.

To descend was death. To remain above was perhaps shipwreck. Each blow damaged the vessel. The gun had already stove in two ports, loosened a knee, and grazed the foot of the mizzenmast. If it struck that mast squarely, it might bring down the rigging. If it burst another plank beneath the water-line, the corvette would fill. If it reached the powder-room—

There are thoughts which men do not finish.

The captain ordered the hatch covered with a grating. The first lieutenant protested.

“And leave it below?”

“And keep it from coming above.”

At that moment the carronade struck the ladder. The whole companion-way started upward. Men recoiled.

The captain looked around him. Faces were grey under the rain. The gale tore words from mouths. Above them, the sails cracked like musketry; beneath them, the gun continued its appalling promenade. The ship had two tempests: one outside, made of wind and water; one within, made of brass.

Then a man stepped forward.

He was the gun captain responsible for the carronade.

His name was Le Goff. He came from Paimpol. He was broad-shouldered, short, red-haired, and about forty years of age. Fifteen minutes earlier he had been merely a sailor. Now five corpses accused him.

He had neglected the breeching.

Why? Fatigue, haste, confidence, carelessness—those four cracks by which disaster enters. Before sailing he had inspected the guns. At this carronade he had found a frayed rope, had intended to replace it, had been called away, had returned, had forgotten. Afterwards, fearing reprimand, he had passed the hook rapidly and trusted to calm weather.

The sea had examined his work.

The sea is an incorruptible inspector.

Le Goff took an iron handspike from the rack and a coil of rope from a peg.

“I will go down,” he said.

The lieutenant seized his arm.

“You will be killed.”

Le Goff looked at him.

“That will not fasten the gun.”

He knotted one end of the rope around his waist. The other he gave to the sailors.

“Pay it out. Do not pull unless I call.”

A voice behind the officers said:

“Let him descend.”

The speaker was the passenger.

Until then he had remained apart beneath the quarterdeck awning. He was an old man, tall, lean, and erect, dressed in a dark travelling coat without ornament. His hair was white; his eyes were clear and cold. During the whole catastrophe he had neither cried out nor offered counsel. He watched.

There are immobilities which command.

The captain obeyed without knowing why.

Le Goff swung himself through the hatch.

The battle began.

The gunner landed behind an overturned carriage. Ten paces away the carronade rested near the larboard ports. The deck at that moment was almost level. The monster had stopped.

Man and cannon contemplated one another.

The lantern above Le Goff threw his shadow before him. It stretched across the planks and touched the wheels. The carronade’s mouth, black and round, seemed to stare. A wisp of broken tackle hung from its cascabel like a severed chain upon the neck of an escaped galley slave.

Le Goff held the handspike with both hands.

He knew his enemy. He knew the weight of the barrel, the play of the trucks, the probable sweep of each roll, the fraction of a second during which an axle might be checked. Technical knowledge is intelligence touching matter. Yet what is intelligence when the floor itself betrays the feet?

The corvette leaned.

The carronade moved.

Slowly at first, then faster, it rolled toward Le Goff.

He waited.

The men above held their breath. They saw him below through the grating: a small human figure in a chamber devastated by a god. Around him were broken beams, mutilated bodies, streaming blood, loose shot, splintered carriages. Beyond the hull roared the unseen sea.

The gun came on.

At three paces Le Goff sprang aside and thrust the handspike between the rear wheel and the deck. The iron bar caught. The wheel rose a finger’s breadth. The bar bent. Le Goff was flung against the side.

The cannon passed him, so close that its breeching touched his coat.

It smashed into the stern-post.

The corvette rolled back.

“Look out!” cried twenty voices.

The gun returned more swiftly than before.

Le Goff dropped flat. The axle swept over him. One wheel grazed his shoulder and tore away cloth and skin. He rose at once.

Blood ran down his arm.

“Come!” he said.

It was the word one says to a dog, a bull, a man, or death.

The carronade accepted.

For several minutes—or several centuries—the duel continued. The monster rushed; the man escaped. The man attacked; the monster recoiled. Le Goff attempted to cast a loop over the cascabel; the barrel swerved and snapped the rope. He drove a wedge before a wheel; the wedge was crushed. He hooked the handspike through the carriage; a sudden roll tore the bar from his grasp and sent it spinning against the beams.

Frailty struggled against the invulnerable.

The gladiator of flesh attacked the beast of brass.

Sometimes the cannon seemed furious; sometimes crafty. It hid behind smoke and swinging shadow. It waited at the lower end of the deck as though asleep. Then, when Le Goff approached, a movement of the sea awoke it and it sprang. Once it chased him from bow to stern, striking alternately at both sides, a chariot of the Apocalypse galloping through a tomb. Once it spun almost in place, its broken tackle whipping around it like a tail. Once it mounted the wreck of a carriage and remained there, tilted, monstrous, ready to fall.

Le Goff approached on his knees.

The ship shuddered.

The carronade crashed down.

He escaped by the width of two fingers.

The spectators groaned. The old passenger did not move.

Suddenly a more violent sea than the others struck the corvette beneath the quarter. The vessel heeled until the tips of the yards seemed to enter the waves. Men on deck were thrown against the rail. Below, corpses, shot, fragments, ropes, and pools of blood slid together toward starboard.

The carronade, at the opposite side, began its longest charge.

Le Goff had fallen.

His foot was caught beneath a broken tackle-block.

He pulled. The foot remained imprisoned.

The cannon descended upon him.

It came without haste, with the majesty of certainty. Its wheels passed over splinters and crushed them. The muzzle dipped and rose. The distance diminished: eight paces, six, four.

Le Goff wrenched at his leg.

Nothing.

The rope around his waist had tangled beneath a gun-carriage. Those above pulled, but the knot tightened and held him down.

Three paces.

He raised his hands, not in fear, but as if to seize the mass which was about to annihilate him.

Two paces.

Then the old passenger acted.

Near him lay a square package wrapped in oilskin, the same which he had guarded since embarkation and which no sailor had been permitted to touch. It contained papers, seals, maps, lists of names, military instructions—perhaps the future of an army. The old man lifted it and threw it through the hatch.

The package fell exactly before the forward wheels.

The carronade mounted it.

That insignificant obstacle changed the world.

One wheel rose. The axle inclined. The gun’s direction altered by half a hand’s breadth. Instead of crushing Le Goff’s chest, it passed beside him. Its carriage struck the broken tackle-block and paused, balanced for an instant between two movements.

The instant belonged to the man.

Le Goff tore his foot free, seized the handspike, thrust it beneath the elevated axle, and bore down with all the strength of his back, arms, rage, shame, and soul.

The carronade resisted.

Matter is enormous; man is nothing. But nothing possesses will.

The gun tilted.

Le Goff roared.

The gun rose farther, trembled, appeared to reflect, then overturned with a crash so tremendous that the lanterns went out.

Darkness followed.

In that darkness all heard the gunner shouting:

“Rope!”

A line was thrown. He passed it around the cascabel, then around a ring-bolt; another followed; a third crossed the carriage. Hands above hauled. Knots tightened. Hooks bit. When a lantern was relighted, the carronade lay upon its side, bound, defeated, still formidable, but once more a thing.

It was ended.

The crew descended.

They found Le Goff standing beside the cannon. His face was white; his shoulder bled; his hands were torn. He looked neither proud nor relieved. He looked at the five dead men.

The package lay burst beneath the wheel. Papers had scattered into blood and bilge-water. The old passenger came down, gathered them calmly, wiped one seal upon his sleeve, and returned them to their wrapping.

Le Goff approached him.

“You saved my life, monsieur.”

The old man replied:

“You saved the ship.”

These two debts were not equal, but both were real.

There was no time for more. Victory revealed disaster.

The carpenter’s report was grave. Two planks had been stove in below the water-line. The temporary collision-mats slowed the entry but did not stop it. Three frames were cracked; a deck-beam had shifted; the larboard battery was unusable; the mizzenmast had been deeply wounded at the partners. The pumps must be worked without interruption.

Above, matters were worse.

During the struggle the corvette had drifted north-west. The gale had strengthened; the compass jumped; the clouds had descended until sea and sky formed one black revolving wall. No observation could be taken. The pilot believed they were between Jersey and Guernsey; the captain believed they had been carried west of both; a seaman who knew the waters swore that he had heard breakers off the Écréhous.

To be uncertain by a mile in those seas is to be uncertain between life and death.

The pumps began their monotonous lament.

Day appeared without light.

The horizon became a circle of ash. Great waves pursued the corvette, lifted her stern, showed her for an instant the valleys of water ahead, then hurled her downward. The wounded mizzenmast groaned. Men laboured at the braces. Others plugged the shot-holes with oakum, sailcloth, hammocks, even coats. The dead were laid beneath a tarpaulin.

The five shapes under it moved with every roll.

At seven o’clock the captain assembled the officers on the quarterdeck. The old passenger stood near the mainmast. Rain ran down his coat. He seemed not to feel it.

The captain removed his hat.

“General,” he said, “the crew awaits your orders.”

Thus the passenger was revealed.

A murmur passed among the officers. Some knew the face; others knew only the reputation. He was the Marquis de Lantenac, lieutenant-general of the royal armies, sent secretly to the coast of France to raise the Vendée, unite the scattered bands, bring discipline to zeal, and turn peasant revolt into war.

The papers he had thrown beneath the cannon contained his commissions.

He had risked the rebellion to save a sailor.

Now he looked at Le Goff.

The gunner had been brought forward. His wound was bandaged. He stood barefoot, having lost one shoe below. Behind him, the sea opened and closed like a gulf.

The general turned to the captain.

“Have you the Cross of Saint-Louis?”

The captain started.

“I have mine, General.”

“Give it to me.”

There was hesitation. A cross worn for twenty years is not a trinket; it is memory made visible. Nevertheless, the captain unfastened the red ribbon from his coat and placed it in the general’s hand.

The Marquis beckoned Le Goff.

“Kneel.”

The gunner knelt upon the wet deck.

The old man fixed the cross to his torn shirt.

“In the name of the King,” he said, “I make you Chevalier of Saint-Louis.”

The sailors uncovered their heads.

Even the storm appeared to pause between two gusts.

The general continued:

“Men, this sailor has shown extraordinary courage. Alone he descended where all death was gathered. He fought the danger, mastered it, and saved the vessel. Courage must be honoured. Whoever forgets that weakens brave men and insults France.”

A subdued acclamation rose from the crew. Le Goff looked at the cross. For the first time his face changed. Something childlike appeared in it. A Breton peasant, hidden beneath the gunner, remembered churches, processions, banners, and saints glittering above altars.

The general said:

“Rise.”

Le Goff rose.

The Marquis turned toward the captain.

“Now assemble a court-martial.”

No one moved.

The general’s voice became colder.

“This man was captain of the gun. He neglected its breeching. Through that negligence five men have died; the battery is ruined; the hull is pierced; the mast is damaged; the mission entrusted to us is endangered. Negligence must be punished. Whoever forgets that destroys discipline and betrays France.”

The captain stared at him.

“General—”

“Do your duty.”

There are moments when severity resembles madness because ordinary men cannot look directly at principle.

The court consisted of the captain and two lieutenants. It stood beneath the torn mizzen rigging while the pumps worked and the sea struck the hull. The charge was read. Le Goff admitted it.

“Did you know the breeching was defective?”

“Yes.”

“Why did you not replace it?”

“I intended to.”

“That is not an answer.”

“I forgot.”

“Did you report the defect?”

“No.”

“Did you understand the danger?”

“Yes.”

The officers looked at one another. The law at sea was clear. A warship cannot pardon the fault which nearly destroys her merely because the offender afterwards saves her. If remorse cancelled negligence, discipline would be made of emotion; and emotion is a rope which parts in bad weather.

Yet the Cross of Saint-Louis shone upon the accused man’s breast.

The court condemned him to death.

The captain pronounced the sentence with a shaking voice.

Le Goff saluted.

“May I keep the cross?”

The question struck every man there more violently than the cannon.

The captain could not answer. The general did.

“Yes.”

“Thank you, General.”

The marine guard formed at the stern. Six muskets were loaded. The deck was cleared. The crew stood in two lines, bareheaded in the rain. Beyond them, grey water rose and fell; now it hid the horizon, now revealed it. The corvette, leaking and dismasted, continued toward an uncertain coast.

Le Goff was placed before the taffrail.

The chaplain was absent; the *Claymore* carried guns, powder, soldiers, and conspiracy, but no priest. An old quartermaster murmured a prayer. Le Goff listened, then shook his head gently.

“Pray for the five,” he said.

The general stood ten paces away.

Not a muscle moved in his face.

Was he cruel? No. Cruelty takes pleasure. He took none. Was he just? Justice itself would perhaps have trembled. He did not tremble. He was something more useful and more terrible than either judge or executioner: he was command.

The officer of marines approached Le Goff with a strip of cloth.

“No blindfold,” said the gunner.

He looked once toward the hatchway. Beneath that deck the brass beast lay tied. Above, the man stood free.

A strange equality had been established between them. Both had caused death. Both had been subdued. The cannon by rope; the man by law.

Le Goff touched the cross upon his breast.

“Long live the King,” he said.

The officer raised his sword.

“Present!”

Six muskets descended.

The ship rolled. The firing party adjusted their feet, sailors to the last.

“Aim!”

At that instant a cry came from the foretop.

“Land!”

Every head turned except the general’s and Le Goff’s.

Through a rent in the mist a black ridge showed itself, then disappeared beneath rain. Was it Jersey? Guernsey? A reef? Salvation or destruction? No one knew.

The general’s eyes remained upon the condemned man.

“Proceed.”

The sword fell.

A volley broke across the storm.

Le Goff was thrown against the taffrail and slipped to the deck. The red ribbon moved once in the wind. Blood flowed beneath him, mingled with rain-water, and found its way toward the scuppers.

The general approached, looked down, and saluted the dead.

Then he turned to the captain.

“What land?”

“We cannot yet tell, General.”

“Find out.”

He went forward.

The officers remained silent. Behind them the marines lifted the body. Below them the pumps beat on; ahead, the unknown coast waited; around them the storm assembled its full strength.

One lieutenant, very young, whispered:

“He rewarded him, then shot him.”

The captain answered:

“He rewarded courage. He shot negligence.”

“That is a hard man.”

The captain watched the Marquis standing alone in the bows, rigid against the wind, while spray broke over him and the wounded corvette entered the whitening sea.

“No,” he said. “That is a leader.”

And the officers understood that the Vendée, which until then had possessed anger, faith, forests, muskets, bells, priests, peasants, and blood, had just received the one thing it lacked.

It had received a will.

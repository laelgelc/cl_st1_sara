## I. THE CITY

There was once a little lad named Dick Whit´ting-ton, who had neither father nor mother, nor any friend rich enough to care for him. He lived in a poor country village, where he earned a crust now and then by minding sheep, carrying bundles, or sweeping a stable. Often he went hungry, and at night he slept on straw.

Now Dick had heard many wonderful tales about London. A travelling pedlar told him that it was the greatest city in the world, and that its streets were paved with gold.

“Then no one can be poor there,” thought Dick. “I have only to pick up a little gold from the road, and I shall soon have food and a warm coat.”

So one bright morning he set out for London. He had no bundle, for he had nothing to put into one, and he had no shoes worth speaking of. Still, he walked bravely on, begging a little bread at farmhouses and drinking from the brooks.

At last, after many weary days, he saw the towers and steeples of the great city. His heart jumped for joy.

“London at last!” he cried. “Now my troubles are over.”

But when he entered the crowded streets, he found no gold beneath his feet. There were only stones, mud, straw, and rubbish. Carts rattled past him, merchants hurried about their business, and no one noticed the poor country lad.

Dick looked carefully in every gutter, thinking that perhaps the gold had been swept to the sides. He found a bent nail, two buttons, and a piece of broken pot, but not one golden coin.

By evening he was very hungry. He asked a baker for work, but the baker sent him away. He offered to carry a gentleman’s bag, but the gentleman had a servant. He begged a woman for a crust, and she said she had five children of her own to feed.

Poor Dick slept under an arch that night. The next day he wandered farther through the city, growing weaker and weaker. At last he sank down beside the door of a large house in Leadenhall Street.

The house belonged to a wealthy and honourable merchant named Mr. Fitz-war´ren. He owned ships which sailed to far countries and returned with spices, silk, fine woods, and other costly goods.

That morning Mr. Fitzwarren happened to look from his window.

“What poor little fellow is lying at my door?” he asked.

A servant went out and called sharply, “Get away with you! The master does not wish beggars here.”

Dick tried to rise, but he was too faint.

“Please, sir,” he whispered, “I am not unwilling to work. I have eaten nothing since yesterday.”

Mr. Fitzwarren had followed the servant, and when he heard these words, he felt sorry for the lad.

“Bring him inside,” said the good merchant. “No hungry child shall be turned from my door while there is bread in the house.”

Dick was given warm broth and a thick slice of bread. He ate so eagerly that Alice, Mr. Fitzwarren’s gentle daughter, smiled and filled his bowl again.

“What can you do?” Mr. Fitzwarren asked him.

“Anything you please, sir,” replied Dick. “I can sweep and scrub, fetch wood, clean boots, and make myself useful. Only try me.”

“You speak honestly,” said the merchant. “You may help in the kitchen. Work well, and you shall have food, clothing, and a place to sleep.”

Dick could scarcely believe his good fortune.

“Oh, thank you, sir!” he cried. “I will serve you faithfully.”

And so the little lad who had expected to gather gold from the streets began his London life by carrying coal and scouring pots.

## II. THE KITCHEN

The head cook in Mr. Fitzwarren’s house was a cross and ill-tempered woman. She did not like Dick, because he was small, slow at lifting heavy pails, and too humble to answer her angry words.

“Make haste, you lazy boy!” she would cry. “Turn the meat! Wash those dishes! Fetch more wood! Must I do everything myself?”

Dick worked from early morning until late at night. If a spoon was missing, the cook blamed Dick. If the fire smoked, she blamed Dick. If the pudding burned because she had forgotten it, she blamed Dick for that too.

Sometimes she struck him with a wooden spoon, and sometimes she pulled his ears. Poor Dick often hid behind the pantry door to wipe away his tears.

Alice saw this unkindness and spoke to the cook.

“You must not treat the boy so cruelly,” she said. “He is willing and industrious.”

“He is a troublesome little wretch,” answered the cook.

“He is an orphan,” said Alice, “and that is all the more reason to be kind to him.”

The cook behaved better whenever Alice was near, but when Alice went away, she became as cross as ever.

Dick’s sleeping-place was a tiny garret at the top of the house. Its roof sloped so low that he could not stand upright near the wall, and the wind came through the cracks. Yet Dick would have been thankful for it if it had not been filled with rats and mice.

As soon as he lay down, they began to scamper.

Scratch, scratch! Rustle, rustle! Squeak, squeak!

They ran across the floor. They climbed the bedposts. They nibbled his candle and even pulled at his blanket. One bold rat ran over Dick’s pillow and whisked its cold tail across his nose.

“Oh, dear!” cried poor Dick. “The cook torments me by day, and the rats torment me by night. How shall I ever sleep?”

One afternoon a man came to the kitchen door carrying a cat under his arm. She was a small grey-and-white puss with bright eyes and quick paws.

“A fine mouser!” called the man. “Who will buy a fine mouser for one penny?”

Dick had earned a penny by carrying a gentleman’s parcel. It was the only money he possessed, and he had meant to buy himself a little break-fast. But when he saw the cat, he thought of the rats.

“Will she truly catch them?” he asked.

“Catch them?” said the man. “Why, she would clear a palace before morning!”

Dick took out his penny.

“Then I will buy her,” he said.

That night he carried puss to the garret and gave her part of his supper.

“Here is your new home,” he told her. “It is poor enough, but if you rid it of rats, I shall love you dearly.”

The cat purred and purred, as if she understood every word.

Before long there came a scratching beneath the boards. Puss became as still as a stone. Then—spring!—she caught a mouse. Soon she caught another, and after that a large rat which had long ruled the garret like a wicked king.

For several nights there was a great scampering and squeaking. Then the few rats that remained fled from the room, and Dick could sleep in peace.

He loved his little cat better every day. When he called, “Pussy, pussy, pussy!” she came running to him. She sat upon his shoulder, followed him up the stairs, and rubbed against his legs whenever the cook scolded him.

“You are my only possession,” Dick would say, stroking her soft head, “and almost my only friend.”

## III. THE VENTURE

One day Mr. Fitzwarren prepared a ship called the *Unicorn* for a trading voyage. She was to sail over distant seas with cloth, metal tools, glass, and many other goods.

It was the custom of this kind merchant to let every person in his household send something upon his ships. If the goods were sold at a profit, the owner received the money. Thus even a humble servant might share in a successful venture.

Mr. Fitzwarren called everyone into the great hall.

“My friends,” he said, “the *Unicorn* sails tomorrow. Let each of you bring what you can spare, and Captain Morris will trade it in foreign lands.”

The steward brought a roll of fine cloth. The cook sent a string of beads. The butler offered two silver cups, and Alice sent a little parcel of embroidered work.

“Where is Dick?” asked Mr. Fitzwarren. “He must have a share also.”

The cook laughed.

“Dick has nothing, sir. Shall he send an old broom or a cracked saucepan?”

Alice frowned at her, but Dick, who stood near the door, hung his head.

“It is true, sir,” he said. “I own nothing in the world except my cat, and I cannot send her. She is my friend.”

Mr. Fitzwarren spoke gently.

“No one will force you, my boy. Yet your cat is a good mouser, and she may prove useful aboard the ship. A venture asks us to part with something now in hope of good fortune later.”

Dick looked at puss, and tears filled his eyes. He remembered the quiet nights she had given him and the way she came when he called.

At last he said, “If you think it right, sir, she shall go. I owe everything to your kindness, and I will trust your judgment.”

He carried the cat to Captain Morris.

“Take good care of her, please,” Dick begged. “Do not let the sailors tease her, and give her a little milk when there is any.”

“Aye,” said the captain. “She shall be treated as one of the crew.”

Puss licked Dick’s hand and began to purr. Then she was taken aboard the *Unicorn*.

When the ship sailed down the river, Dick stood watching until the last white sail disappeared.

The cook came behind him.

“There goes your grand fortune!” she mocked. “Perhaps your cat will send you a golden collar.”

Poor Dick went back to work with a heavy heart.

After that, the cook grew more un-pleas-ant than ever. She scolded him morning, noon, and night, and without his cat the garret seemed lonely. At last, after she had struck him for dropping a dish which another servant had pushed from the table, Dick could bear no more.

“I will leave London,” he said. “The streets are not paved with gold, and there is no happiness here for me.”

Before sunrise he tied his few clothes in a cloth and slipped out of the house. He walked northward until he reached the top of Highgate Hill. There he sat upon a stone to rest.

Far behind him, the city bells began to ring.

Ding, dong! Ding, dong!

As Dick listened, they seemed to speak:

“Turn again, Whit´ting-ton,  
Thrice Lord Mayor of London!”

Dick sat upright.

“That cannot mean me,” he said.

Again the bells rang over the morning air:

“Turn again, Whit´ting-ton,  
Thrice Lord Mayor of London!”

“If I am ever to be Lord Mayor,” cried Dick, “I must not run away because a cook is cross. I will return and do my duty.”

So he turned back at once. He entered the kitchen before anyone knew he had gone, stirred the fire, filled the kettles, and began his work.

The cook came downstairs and snapped, “Why are you staring about? Make haste!”

“Yes, cook,” said Dick cheerfully.

For he was thinking of the bells.

## IV. THE CAT

Meanwhile, the *Unicorn* sailed for many weeks. She met strong winds and rolling seas, but at last she reached a kingdom where English merchants had seldom been seen.

The king of that country received Captain Morris with great honour. He ordered a splendid feast to be prepared, and the Englishmen were seated upon cushions beside the king and queen.

Golden dishes were placed before them. There were roasted birds, sweet cakes, fruits, rice, and sauces of many colours.

But no sooner had the food appeared than hundreds of rats and mice rushed into the hall.

They swarmed over the tables. They snatched cakes from the dishes, drank from the cups, and dragged away pieces of meat. One fat rat sat boldly upon the king’s plate and began eating his dinner.

The king struck the table.

“Drive them away!” he shouted.

Servants beat sticks upon the floor, but the rats only scattered for a moment. As soon as the servants withdrew, back they came—rats from behind the curtains, mice from beneath the cushions, and more rats from holes in the walls.

The queen lifted her feet and cried, “Oh! Oh! One has pulled my slipper!”

Captain Morris stared in astonishment.

“Your Majesty,” he said, “have you no cats in this country?”

“Cats?” asked the king. “What creatures are those?”

Captain Morris smiled.

“We have one aboard our ship. She belongs to a poor lad in my master’s household. With your permission, I will fetch her.”

“Bring this wonderful creature at once!” commanded the king.

A sailor hurried to the *Unicorn* and returned carrying Dick’s grey-and-white puss. The cat looked around the hall. Her eyes grew round. Never had she seen such a great, great many rats.

The queen leaned forward.

“What is the creature called?” she asked.

“A pussy, Your Majesty,” replied the captain.

“A putty?” said the queen.

“Pussy,” said Captain Morris.

“Putty, putty, putty,” called the queen.

The cat took no notice, for just then a mouse ran past her paws.

Spring! She caught it.

Then she leaped upon a rat. She chased three more beneath the table, darted behind the king’s throne, and came out with another in her mouth. The rats squeaked, the mice scattered, and the courtiers shouted with delight.

The king jumped so high that his turban fell off.

“Wonderful!” he cried. “There has never been such a hunter in all my kingdom!”

For several days puss was allowed to roam through the palace. She cleared the dining hall, the kitchens, the royal bed-chambers, and the storerooms. Soon the remaining rats fled from the palace, and meals could be served without a single whiskered thief appearing.

The queen took the cat upon her lap and stroked her.

“Dear putty!” she said. “I cannot part with her.”

The king offered Captain Morris a chest of gold for the animal.

“Your Majesty,” said the honest captain, “she is not mine to sell cheaply. She belongs to a poor serving lad named Dick Whittington.”

“Then Dick Whittington shall be rich,” declared the king.

He gave ten times the first sum, with jewels besides, for he said that the cat was worth more than half his kingdom. Captain Morris carefully recorded the bargain, and the treasure was loaded aboard the *Unicorn*.

Puss remained in the palace, where she slept on a silk cushion, drank cream from a golden bowl, and was treated with as much respect as any royal minister.

## V. THE FORTUNE

Some time after that, the *Unicorn* returned to London. Mr. Fitzwarren was sitting in his office when Captain Morris entered with the account of the voyage.

“Sir,” said the captain, “every venture has done well, but one has brought a fortune beyond belief.”

He told the story of the foreign king, the palace rats, and Dick’s cat. Then he showed the gold and jewels paid for her.

Mr. Fitzwarren was amazed, but he never thought of keeping the treasure.

“Send for Dick immediately,” he said. “The money belongs to him.”

The cook found Dick in the yard, black with soot from cleaning a great kettle.

“Come to the master’s office,” she said. “No doubt you are to be punished.”

Dick trembled as he entered.

“I am sorry, sir,” he began, “if I have done wrong.”

Mr. Fitzwarren rose and held out his hand.

“Indeed, Mr. Whittington, you have done no wrong. I rejoice to tell you that you are a wealthy man.”

Dick looked behind him, thinking some gentleman must have entered.

“Mr. Whittington?” he said. “Do you mean me, sir?”

Captain Morris laughed and poured gold coins upon the table. Then he told the whole wonderful tale.

“The cat was yours,” said Mr. Fitzwarren, “and therefore all this payment is yours. Not one penny belongs to me.”

Poor Dick began to cry.

“Oh, sir,” he said, “surely half should be yours.”

“No,” replied the merchant. “Right is right, whether the owner be a prince or a kitchen boy.”

Dick thanked him again and again. Then, though he had known poverty all his life, he did not become proud or selfish.

He gave handsome presents to Captain Morris and every sailor aboard the *Unicorn*. He rewarded the servants and made sure the poor of the neighbourhood had bread and warm clothing. He gave Alice a beautiful necklace, and he offered Mr. Fitzwarren a share of the treasure, though the merchant would accept only a small token.

Last of all, Dick went to the cook.

She expected angry words, for she remembered every blow and insult.

But Dick placed a purse in her hand.

“You were not always kind to me,” he said, “but I will not remember old injuries now that good fortune has come.”

The cook burst into tears.

“Forgive me, Master Dick,” she said. “I was cross and cruel, and you are kinder than I deserve.”

Dick forgave her freely.

He now dressed as a young gen-tle-man, but he did not waste his money. Mr. Fitzwarren taught him the merchant’s business, and Dick learned quickly. He was honest in his bargains, careful in his ventures, and generous to those in need. Before many years had passed, he became one of the most respected merchants in London.

He also married kind Alice, who had befriended him when he was only a hungry kitchen lad. Their wedding was a joyful one, and even the old cook danced until she lost a shoe.

By and by Dick was chosen sheriff of London. After that he became Lord Mayor, not once, nor twice, but three times, just as the bells had promised:

“Turn again, Whittington,  
Thrice Lord Mayor of London!”

He served the city wisely. He built places for the poor, helped prisoners, encouraged honest trade, and never forgot what it was to be cold and hungry. King Henry the Fifth valued his counsel and, in reward for his faithful service, made him a knight.

Thus the orphan boy who had entered London barefoot became Sir Richard Whittington, merchant, sheriff, and three times Lord Mayor. Yet of all his honours, none pleased him more than helping a poor child, for he would always say:

“Kindness is a better treasure than gold, and an honest heart is the best venture of all.”

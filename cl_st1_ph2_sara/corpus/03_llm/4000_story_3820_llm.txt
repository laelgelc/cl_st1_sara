There are moments in life which announce themselves with trumpets, telegrams, and the agitated entrance of relatives; and there are others, no less decisive, which pass with the faint click of an insect’s wing. It is our habit to remember the trumpets. We say, “At this instant my destiny changed,” because a door slammed, a physician frowned, or a railway train departed without us. But destiny, being older and more experienced than we are, seldom conducts its business so theatrically. It prefers to turn the switch while we are looking out of the window.

I have often reflected upon this fact, particularly when I have missed a train, a fish, or an opportunity to remain silent. Opportunities, indeed, swarm around a man like gnats in June; yet he commonly spends his time slapping at those which merely annoy him and allowing the important one to settle unnoticed upon his nose.

The particular crisis of which I am about to speak occurred on the last day of the ouananiche season, beside a flooded river in Quebec, and was embodied in a grasshopper.

We had come down that morning—Ferdinand, our guide; Blackwood, my companion in many innocent disasters; and I—to make a final assault upon the Grande Chute pool. The phrase “final assault” was Blackwood’s. He had served for three months in the militia and considered military language applicable to everything, including breakfast.

The night had been one of steady rain. It was not the cheerful patter which encourages sleep and improves the mushrooms, but a deliberate, official rain, as though the Department of Rivers had resolved to increase its output before the end of the fiscal year. It beat upon the camp roof, drummed on the birches, and filled the darkness with a thousand runnels. Ferdinand listened to it from his blanket and remarked at intervals, “Beaucoup d’eau demain.” Then he slept again, having transferred the responsibility to Providence.

At dawn the clouds withdrew, leaving the whole country washed and brilliant. Crimson maples flamed along the hillsides; the spruces stood dark and solemn among them; and every twig carried a drop that burned for a moment like a diamond before falling into the soaked earth. The air had that sharpened sweetness of a northern autumn which makes a man think noble thoughts until he discovers that his boots are wet.

We reached the pool shortly after eight and found no pool.

The river had risen four feet. The smooth dark basin in which we had taken five fish during the week was obliterated beneath a yellow, furious torrent. Water rushed over the ledges, climbed the alders, tore at exposed roots, and hurled entire trees downstream with an energy that seemed personal. The place where I had stood the previous afternoon was now occupied by a whirlpool large enough to transact business.

Blackwood surveyed it and said, “Retreat in good order.”

Ferdinand shrugged. “No fish to-day, m’sieu’.”

Now I am not naturally despondent. I acquire despondency only under special circumstances and then display it with unusual completeness. The last day of a fishing season is such a circumstance. It has the melancholy of Sunday evening, the final act of a tragedy, and the closing of a bank at which one has neglected to cash a cheque. I sat upon a wet stone and contemplated the river with bitterness.

Ferdinand and Blackwood went upstream to examine a smaller reach. They did this, I believe, less from hope than from reluctance to remain near me. A disappointed angler should either be left alone or placed in charge of a competent nurse.

For twenty minutes I watched the flood. Then, on the far side of the torrent, where a submerged rock divided the current, something rose.

It was not the nervous flick of a small trout, nor the heavy roll of a log. A broad silver side turned in the tea-coloured water; a tail as wide as my hand struck the surface; and the fish sank behind the rock.

At once the landscape changed. The river, which had been an insult, became a problem. The rain had not ruined the day; it had merely arranged a test of character, skill, and foolhardiness, three qualities between which the enthusiastic angler makes very little distinction.

The salmon lay perhaps sixty yards below me and thirty yards from the bank. Between us rose a cliff, steep, damp, and ornamented with shrubs apparently designed to come loose when grasped. Beneath the cliff the flood ran close and deep. Beyond it a fallen spruce stretched diagonally from the shore toward a narrow shelf of rock. The butt was wedged between two boulders, but the upper end moved in the current with the slow, comfortable wallowing of a playful hippopotamus.

From the shelf, I calculated, one might cast to the fish.

Precipitation is a fault. But deliberation, for a person of precipitate disposition, is a vice. I therefore considered the matter carefully for nearly half a minute and began to climb.

The first twenty feet were easy. The next twenty induced a warmer appreciation of level ground. I edged along cracks, embraced a cedar, and entrusted my weight successively to three plants whose botanical names I did not know and whose moral character I mistrusted. Once my foot slipped, and a stone bounded into the river.

I looked down after it and recalled that a large family was dependent upon me—large, at least, in its expectations. This consideration should have sent me back. Instead, by one of those curious processes of reasoning familiar to sportsmen, it persuaded me to go forward, since to retreat over the worst passage appeared dangerous.

Near the end of the cliff a small purple flower grew from a fissure. Under other conditions I might have repeated Tennyson’s lines about the flower in the crannied wall and the mystery of God and man. Under existing conditions I reflected that if I pulled it out, root and all, I should probably follow it.

At last I reached the fallen spruce. Its bark had been stripped away, and the trunk shone with spray. The shoreward end lay six feet below me. The farther end, twenty feet out, dipped, rose, and twisted under pressure of the flood.

It was the only chance.

So I jumped.

I landed astride the trunk, felt it sink, scrambled upright, ran three steps while it rolled beneath my boots, dropped to my knees, recovered, and leaped to the rock shelf just as the top of the spruce swung downstream. The whole performance occupied perhaps four seconds. During the first I was an athlete, during the second an acrobat, during the third a penitent, and during the fourth a man profoundly surprised to be alive.

The shelf was barely wider than a dining-table and had none of a dining-table’s stability. Water washed over its lower edge. Yet from there I could see the salmon distinctly, holding behind the submerged rock, his fins moving just enough to balance the current. He was a magnificent ouananiche, heavy and deep, with that burnished silver which no photograph and few fishermen’s adjectives can reproduce.

I assembled my rod, tested the leader, and opened the fly-book.

The selection of a fly is a branch of moral philosophy. There is the conservative school, which maintains that a fish should be offered what has succeeded before; the progressive school, which insists upon novelty; and the psychological school, which selects the fly least likely to interest the angler, on the ground that it may therefore intrigue the fish. I have belonged to all three schools, sometimes during the same cast.

I began with a Silver Doctor. The salmon moved slightly, inspected it, and resumed his position.

I offered a Jock Scott. He did not move.

A Parmacheene Belle produced what I interpreted as disdain. A Professor received the indifference too often accorded to professors. The Montreal, the Grizzly King, the Brown Hackle, and a small unnamed creation purchased from an optimistic dealer in Quebec passed above him like arguments before a judge who had already written his decision.

I changed leaders. I lengthened the cast. I shortened it. I allowed the fly to sink, skated it across the current, dropped it delicately above the rock, and caused it—less delicately—to strike the fish upon the nose. He endured these attentions with the composure of a bishop hearing a long sermon by a curate.

Meanwhile the sky brightened. The river continued to rise. My rock grew smaller, my arm tired, and the lawful season approached its conclusion with indecent speed.

I had reached the bottom row of the fly-book and the bottom layer of hope when a sound came from the cliff behind me:

“KRI-KAREE-KAREE-KRI!”

I turned.

A grasshopper clung to a tuft of faded grass not two feet from my shoulder. How he had reached that aqueous neighbourhood I cannot say. Perhaps he had been washed from the meadow above; perhaps, like myself, he had miscalculated a jump. He was large, olive-brown, and vigorous, with legs constructed upon the principle of immediate departure.

At that instant the ouananiche rose again, and a second fact entered my mind. In late September, grasshoppers fall into the rivers. Salmon eat them. The season for grasshoppers, like the season for ouananiche, was nearly ended. The fish, I imagined, might have conservative sentiments regarding this natural arrangement.

The insect said, “Kri,” and shifted his position.

I laid down the rod and extended my hand.

There are contests whose issue depends upon armies, money, diplomacy, or the endurance of nations. This one depended upon the reflex action of two sets of neural ganglia, mine and the grasshopper’s. His had been perfected through generations of escape; mine had been impaired by civilization, office work, and the wearing of gloves.

My hand descended.

He jumped.

So did I.

My fingers closed in empty air; then, by a movement so swift that reason disclaims all credit for it, my other hand intercepted him against my coat. For a moment he buzzed indignantly between my palms. I had him.

I confess that I felt no immediate revelation. Bells did not ring in heaven. No inward voice cried, “This is the turning-point.” I merely congratulated myself upon possessing bait.

With some difficulty—for the grasshopper strongly objected to fulfilling his destiny—I placed him upon the hook. Then I cast above the submerged rock.

The current carried him down, kicking and revolving. The salmon moved six inches.

The grasshopper drifted past.

I cast again, farther upstream. This time the bait sank in the broken water and came across the fish’s line of vision.

There was a flash, a swirl, and a pull that nearly took the rod from my hands.

I struck.

For one second the salmon hung motionless, as if astonished by the interruption of his luncheon. Then he went downstream.

The reel screamed. The line cut a white mark through the surface. I lifted the rod and shouted, not words but a primitive noise common, I suppose, to hunters, railway brakemen, and persons who have sat upon pins. The fish ran forty yards, leaped clear of the flood, and showed his silvery sides against the dark spruces. He fell with a crash and ran again.

My tackle was not intended for such water. The leader was fine, the rod light, and the reel held less line than I had previously considered sufficient. I could not follow along the bank; I could not descend the cliff; and the movable spruce now swung almost parallel with the current. I was attached to a powerful fish in a flooded river while standing upon a vanishing rock. It was the happiest moment of the day.

“FERDINAND!” I shouted. “LE FILET! VITE! FERDINAND!”

No answer came except the river.

The salmon turned and rushed upstream, forcing me to reel with frantic speed. He bored beneath the ledge, came out, rolled, and shook his head. Each shake travelled along the line into my wrist with a brutal, living force. Then he leaped again, higher than before.

“FERDINAND! BLACKWOOD! AU SECOURS! THE NET!”

Far above, a small figure appeared between the trees. It was Blackwood. He waved both arms, a gesture which conveyed sympathy but offered no practical assistance. Then Ferdinand broke from the alders with the landing-net and came down the bank at a speed extraordinary in a man who usually regarded haste as an Anglo-Saxon infirmity.

He stopped at the cliff. I pointed downstream.

The fish had begun to tire, but the current fought for him. Each time I drew him toward the rock, the torrent swept him away. Ferdinand studied the situation, crossed himself—whether for me, for himself, or for the salmon I never learned—and descended by a route which made my own appear like the front steps of a hotel.

He reached a gravel tongue below the cliff and waded out until the water rose above his knees. The fish came in slowly, broadside to the current.

“Doucement, m’sieu’,” he called. “Doucement!”

Advice to be gentle is never more plentiful than when one’s tackle is about to break.

I lowered the rod. The salmon drifted toward him. Ferdinand extended the net.

A fish should be netted head first, with one calm forward motion. This is the established principle. Ferdinand, confronted by a flood, a short reach, and an excitable employer, abandoned principle. He swung the net sideways like a mower cutting thistles.

By miracle, the salmon entered it.

“Bon!” cried Ferdinand.

At that instant the wooden handle snapped.

Net and fish dropped into the river and began to float downstream.

All seemed to be lost.

The line remained attached, but the weight of salmon, net, and current dragged the rod down. Ferdinand lunged after them, stumbled, recovered, and waded waist-deep through the racing water. With his left hand he seized an alder branch. With his right he snatched up a crooked stick from the shallows and reached for the drifting mesh.

Once he missed. The net turned over, and the salmon’s tail beat the surface.

He reached again.

The crooked end caught.

Ferdinand braced himself, the alder bent, the stick bowed, and for several seconds the whole arrangement—guide, bush, branch, net, salmon, rod, and myself—formed a chain of dependencies so absurd and so precarious that laughter would have been appropriate if breathing had not seemed more urgent.

Then Ferdinand stepped backward. Once. Twice. The net scraped upon the gravel. He caught its rim and dragged it ashore.

The ouananiche lay there, magnificent and exhausted, the grasshopper’s hook fixed in the corner of his jaw.

He weighed a little more than seven pounds, though Blackwood, after dinner, made him eight, and Ferdinand, by the following summer, had raised him to nine and a half. I do not object. A fish which has passed through so much water is entitled to grow in recollection.

People to whom I have told the story generally suppose that its crisis was my leap to the spruce, the salmon’s strike, or the breaking of the net handle. These events were noisy and picturesque. They resembled crises. Yet each followed naturally from what had gone before. The true turning-point occurred quietly, when a grasshopper cried “KRI-KAREE” upon the cliff and my hand closed one fraction of a second before he escaped.

Had he jumped farther, had I looked away, had my civilized neural ganglia delayed their answer, there would have been no battle, no broken net, no seven-pound ouananiche, and no story.

So it is, I suspect, with larger matters. We recognize the flood, the leap, and the shout; we overlook the small winged instant on which everything depends. Life rarely labels its opportunities. It lets them chirp beside us while we are changing flies.

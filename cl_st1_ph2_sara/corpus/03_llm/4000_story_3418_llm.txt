### THE SEVEN UNDISMISSED SPIRITS

In the settlement of Little Cedar, which lay between two low Virginia ridges and consisted of a store, a mill, thirty-seven cabins, and a church with one shutter missing, Brother Peter Jasper enjoyed a reputation considerably larger than his education. He could neither read nor write, except that he had learned to recognize his own name when somebody pointed it out to him; but he possessed a deep voice, a grave manner, an excellent memory, and that confidence which, in public affairs, is often accepted as a substitute for every other qualification.

For nearly fourteen years he had occupied the pulpit of Mount Moriah Church. During this period he had baptized the doubtful, married the willing, buried the inevitable, and preached upon every important subject from Pharaoh’s hardness of heart to the proper conduct of hogs on Sunday. If he did not always quote Scripture with exactness, he did so with abundance; and, since few of his hearers could have corrected him without borrowing spectacles and losing half a day, his authority had remained substantially undisturbed.

Brother Peter worked during the week wherever employment could be obtained. Sometimes he cut timber; sometimes he helped repair the county road; and during the tobacco season he was often absent from home from Monday morning until Saturday night. On Sunday, however, he appeared in a long black coat, polished at the elbows, and conducted himself with such dignity that strangers commonly supposed he owned land.

His wife, Aunt Rebecca, was a short, energetic woman, whose opinions were never concealed by any weakness of voice. She had supported Peter through poor crops, wet winters, two church divisions, and a prolonged controversy concerning whether the pulpit cushions ought to be red or purple. She was, therefore, entitled to respect, and received it from everyone except Peter, who, like many men of commanding public presence, found it necessary to reserve a portion of his authority for home consumption.

On the Saturday which gave rise to the events here recorded, Brother Peter returned after a week spent hauling stone for a new bridge. He was tired, hungry, and disposed to receive attention as his natural due. Aunt Rebecca, on the other hand, had passed the week hoeing beans, nursing a sick grandchild, and defending the smokehouse against a wandering sow. Her own sense of merit was consequently not inferior to his.

“Supper ready?” asked Peter, setting down his bundle.

“It been ready,” said Rebecca, “an’ it been waitin’. Ef you’d come when you said you was comin’, you’d ’a’ got it hot.”

“A man cain’t pull a stone bridge up de hill jes’ to git home when de skillet say so.”

“An’ a woman cain’t keep bread from dryin’ up whilst a man sets at de store talkin’ ’bout politics.”

Brother Peter had, in fact, stopped at the store, though only for an hour and a half. He considered the reference unnecessary.

“I ain’t been talkin’ no politics,” he replied. “I been discussin’ de condition er de country.”

“De country’s condition ain’t called you husband,” said Rebecca. “It kin wait.”

This answer, containing both truth and disrespect, wounded him severely. He ate in silence for some minutes, but Aunt Rebecca, whose temper did not cool merely because the fire had found no fuel, continued to mention his shortcomings in a clear and orderly catalogue. Before the meal ended she had recalled the broken gate, the unpaid meal bill, his missing axe, the hole in the chicken fence, and a promise made at Christmas to mend the roof.

“Woman,” said Peter at last, “you is got mo’ contrary sperits in you dan dey is fleas in July.”

“Maybe I has,” returned Rebecca. “But none of ’em is lazy.”

Peter pushed back from the table. A reply occurred to him, but it lacked both force and safety. He therefore took his hat and went out to the woodpile, where he sat for some time, considering the general nature of womankind. Since Aunt Rebecca was the woman best known to him, his conclusions were naturally influenced by the available evidence.

The next morning Mount Moriah Church was unusually full. The weather was clear, the tobacco had been planted, and there had lately been little amusement in the neighborhood. Brother Peter entered the pulpit with a solemn face and announced that the Lord had laid a special message upon him.

The choir sang with vigor, the prayer lasted twenty-seven minutes, and after the customary preliminaries Peter rose, opened the large pulpit Bible at random, and placed one hand upon it. This gesture had always been effective, though he could not have said whether he had opened at Genesis or the Acts.

“Brethren an’ sisters,” he began, “my tex’ dis mawnin’ is a mighty deep tex’. It is so deep dat some folks kin look down in it an’ git dizzy. But de Lawd done give me a rope, an’ I aims to let you down easy.”

Several deacons nodded. Aunt Rebecca, seated in the second row, looked straight before her.

“De subjec’,” continued Peter, “is dis: Eve an’ de Seven Apples; or, Why Every Woman Got Seven Devils.”

At this announcement a slight movement passed over the congregation. The men shifted comfortably; the women sat upright.

“You all knows,” said Peter, “how de serpent come unto Eve in de gyarden. He come slippin’ an’ slidin’, wid his mouf full er sweet talk, an’ he says, ‘Sister Eve, won’t you have a apple?’ An’ Eve say, ‘No, I thank you; de Lawd done tol’ me not to eat.’”

Here Peter paused and looked toward Aunt Rebecca, as if to emphasize that there had once been a woman who began by acknowledging instructions.

“But de serpent,” he resumed, “was a perseverin’ creetur. He ain’t quit. He reach up an’ pull down a big red apple—one er dem early kinds, mighty pretty outside an’ sour clean through—an’ Eve took it. Dat was de fust.”

Brother Peter lifted one finger.

“Den he give her a yaller apple. Dat was de secon’. Den a green apple, de third. Den a russet apple, de fo’th. Den one er dem streakedy apples, de fifth. Den a sweet apple, de sixth. An’ den, brethren, he give her a great big shiny apple, look like it’d fetch ten cents at de county fair, an’ dat was de seventh.”

He now held both hands before him, laboriously displaying the proper total.

“An’ every apple she et put a devil in her. De fust was de devil er curiosity. De secon’ was de devil er argument. De third was de devil er contradiction. De fo’th was de devil er fault-findin’. De fifth was de devil er havin’ de las’ word. De sixth was de devil er rememberin’ everything a man ever done. An’ de seventh—”

He stopped. The men leaned forward.

“De seventh,” said Peter, with mournful emphasis, “was de devil er remindin’ him of it at supper-time.”

A number of the brethren coughed into their hands. Deacon Willis bent to pick up a hymnbook which he had not dropped. Aunt Rebecca’s eyes narrowed.

“Now, brethren,” Peter continued, encouraged by these signs, “Eve is de mother er all women. What goes in de spring comes down de branch. What de ole tree got, de young shoots don’t git shet of easy. An’ dat is how come every woman borned into dis worl’ is nachally furnished wid seven separate an’ partic’lar devils.”

Had Peter stopped at this point, the disturbance might have been no greater than that produced by many other sermons. But success is dangerous to a speaker who has not prepared his conclusion. He proceeded to illustrate the seven devils by examples so local and recognizable that nearly every married woman present suspected her husband of having supplied information.

He described a woman who hid her husband’s Sunday shoes because he had tracked mud across the floor; Sister Eliza Morton had done this the previous week. He spoke of another who counted the eggs and accused her husband of selling three for tobacco; Sister Hannah Price flushed visibly. He then referred to a wife who could remember, after twelve years, the exact words used by her husband during a quarrel over a mule. At this, Aunt Rebecca rose.

“Set down, Rebecca,” said Peter, forgetting for the moment where he was.

“I ain’t spoke yit,” she answered.

“No, but you is standin’ like you gwine to.”

“I’m standin’ ’cause I don’t choose to set under no sech foolishness.”

She walked from the church, followed first by Sister Price, then by Sister Morton, and finally by nearly every adult woman in the congregation. The younger girls remained long enough to look interested, but were removed by their mothers. The men, suddenly deprived of the protection afforded by a mixed audience, found Peter’s doctrine less amusing than before.

Brother Peter concluded in some haste. His final proposition—that women’s seven devils could be subdued by obedience to their husbands—was delivered to twelve uneasy men, three old ladies too deaf to understand him, and a boy asleep behind the stove.

By Monday morning the sermon had been reported throughout Little Cedar. It had also been improved, as reports commonly are. Peter was said to have declared that women had tails, that wives ought not to own shoes, and that Eve had stolen seven apples from a white man’s orchard. No two accounts agreed, but all were considered offensive.

The women met Tuesday afternoon at Sister Susan Henry’s house. Susan was a tall, sharp-featured widow, who owned six acres, a cow, and a family Bible. Her material independence gave weight to her moral convictions. She had never liked Brother Peter, partly because he had once forgotten her name while announcing a donation, and partly because, at a church supper, he had praised another woman’s biscuits.

The gathering was large. Chairs were borrowed from three houses, benches were brought from the church shed, and the younger children were sent outdoors under threat of punishment.

“Sisters,” Susan began, standing behind a table upon which the Bible had been placed, “we is come together on serious business. Brother Peter done scandalized de female sex, insulted Eve, an’ preached apples dat ain’t in de Word.”

“Dat’s right,” said several voices.

“He say every woman got seven devils.”

“Ain’t nobody got seven but his wife,” observed Sister Morton.

Aunt Rebecca, who sat near the window, turned slowly.

“Ef I got seven,” she said, “Pete done give employment to every one of ’em.”

This was received with approval.

The meeting then considered what steps should be taken. Some favored locking the church door. Others proposed that Peter be compelled to apologize from the pulpit while standing on the floor, which would deprive him of the advantage of elevation. One sister suggested throwing him into the millpond; but this motion was ruled out, the pond being shallow in summer and the dignity of the church being involved.

Susan Henry favored constitutional methods.

“We ain’t gwine act disorderly,” she declared. “We gwine call him befo’ de congregation an’ make him show chapter an’ verse. Ef de Bible say women got seven devils, den we got to bear it. But ef de Bible don’t say it, he got to come down.”

“Down out de pulpit?” asked someone.

“Down out de pulpit, down out de church office, an’, ef Sister Rebecca got any sense, down out her house.”

Aunt Rebecca sniffed. “I kin tend to my own house.”

A committee of five was appointed to examine Scripture. This task proved more difficult than anticipated. Sister Susan could read, but slowly; Sister Price could read the New Testament if the print was large; the others knew certain passages by location, appearance, or family association. Nevertheless, after three evenings of investigation, they established beyond reasonable doubt that Genesis mentioned neither seven apples nor seven devils.

The men, meanwhile, were not united. A few believed that Peter should be sustained as a matter of masculine solidarity. Others, after observing the atmosphere of their homes, decided that doctrine ought never to interfere with peace. Deacon Willis remarked privately that a preacher should preach about the sins of people who lived farther away. By Saturday public opinion had turned strongly against Brother Peter.

Peter knew nothing of these developments. Early Monday he had gone twelve miles up the county to work in a sawmill camp and did not return until late Saturday night. Aunt Rebecca received him with such cold ceremony that he suspected trouble, but she refused explanation.

“You’ll find out to-morrer,” she said.

“What I gwine find out?”

“Whether seven devils is enough.”

Peter slept poorly.

On Sunday the road to Mount Moriah was crowded before the bell rang. People came from neighboring settlements, some in support of the women, some in support of Peter, and many from that respectable human desire to witness difficulty without being held responsible for it. Every bench was filled. Men stood by the walls; children occupied the windows; two white farmers stopped their wagon beneath a tree and remained within hearing distance.

When Peter entered, the usual call of “Mawnin’, Reverend” was noticeably absent. Several persons said, “Howdy, Uncle Pete,” which, though friendly in form, represented a serious reduction in rank.

Susan Henry rose before he could reach the pulpit.

“Peter Jasper,” she said, “dis congregation wants a answer.”

Brother Peter placed his hat upon a peg with care. “A answer to what, Sister Susan?”

“You knows what. You preached dat every woman is p’essed of seven devils. We searched de Scriptures, an’ we don’t find no sech doctrine.”

“Maybe you ain’t searched deep enough.”

“We searched Genesis, Matthew, Mark, Luke, John, an’ most er Romans. We went clean through Revelations, too, lookin’ for dem apples.”

Peter glanced at the pulpit Bible. It appeared larger than usual.

“An’ now,” continued Susan, “you is called on to prove it. Chapter an’ verse. Ef you proves it, you stays. Ef you don’t, you quits dis pulpit to-day.”

A murmur of approval filled the church.

Peter looked at Deacon Willis, who became interested in a crack in the floor. He looked at Aunt Rebecca, whose expression suggested that domestic asylum would not be immediately available. He then looked over the congregation, and, strange to say, his uneasiness began to diminish.

Brother Peter’s gifts were not scholarly, but they were real. He had spent fourteen years observing faces from a pulpit. He knew when an audience was hostile, when it was doubtful, and when it was merely hungry for entertainment. He saw that the women wanted victory, the men wanted safety, and everyone wanted to hear what he would say next. These were not encouraging circumstances to a theologian, but they were useful to an orator.

He mounted the pulpit.

“Sister Susan,” he began, “you ask me to prove what I preached.”

“Dat’s what I asks.”

“An’ you gwine let de Bible settle it?”

“De Bible an’ nothin’ else.”

“Will dis whole congregation abide by de Word?”

A general “Yes” arose.

Peter opened the Bible, not because he expected assistance from the page, but because public reasoning is improved by appropriate furniture.

“Den we gwine proceed calm,” he said. “No confusion, no disputin’, no millpond. I gwine ask some questions, an’ de congregation gwine answer according to Scripture.”

Susan folded her arms. “Go on.”

“Fust question: Does de Bible speak of Mary Magdalene?”

“It does,” said Sister Susan.

“Was Mary Magdalene a man or a woman?”

“A woman,” replied the congregation, with some impatience.

“Be particular,” Peter warned. “Dis is deep doctrine. Was she a sho’-’nough woman?”

“Yes.”

“Not no figure er speech? Not no city? Not no mountain? A woman same as any woman?”

“Yes, Peter,” called Aunt Rebecca. “Git on with it.”

Peter bowed slightly. “I’m gittin’ on, Sister Rebecca. Slow wheels carry heavy loads.”

The men smiled, though cautiously.

“Now, next question. Does de Bible say de Lawd Jesus cast devils outen Mary Magdalene?”

“It does,” said Susan.

“How many?”

There was a pause. Susan turned to Sister Price, who had brought the family Bible. Sister Price found the passage with some assistance and placed her finger beneath the line.

“Seven,” she admitted.

Peter rested both hands upon the pulpit.

“Speak up, Sister, so de brethren by de door kin hear.”

“Seven devils.”

“An’ who cast ’em out?”

“Jesus.”

“Did He cast out six?”

“No. Seven.”

“Did He cast out eight?”

“No. Seven.”

“Was dey little devils or big devils?”

“The Book don’t say,” Susan snapped.

“Dat’s right. We ain’t gwine put nothin’ in de Book dat ain’t dar.”

This rebuke, being directed against the very fault of which Peter stood accused, produced a thoughtful silence.

He continued: “Now, when dem seven devils was cast outen Mary Magdalene, was dey cast out complete?”

“Yes.”

“Did one hide in her pocket?”

“No.”

“Did one climb back when nobody was lookin’?”

“The Bible don’t say so.”

“Den we takes it dey was gone?”

“Yes.”

Peter drew a long breath and looked over the church. The congregation had followed him thus far without difficulty. Susan’s expression showed impatience, but not alarm.

“Now,” he said, “I comes to de las’ question, an’ I wants everybody to listen. Does de Bible say Jesus cast seven devils outen any other woman?”

Susan opened her mouth, then closed it. Sister Price turned pages. Another woman called for Luke; a third suggested Mark. The rustle of paper spread through the church like dry leaves in October.

Peter waited.

“Take yo’ time,” he said generously. “I don’t want nobody say Brother Peter hurried ’em.”

Five minutes passed. Sister Susan searched a concordance borrowed from the schoolteacher, but, since she had not previously understood the use of one, it gave her no immediate comfort. Several men whispered. One child laughed and was pinched into silence.

At last Susan said, “It don’t say they was.”

“Say it louder.”

“It don’t say seven devils was cast out of no other woman.”

Peter shut the Bible with great gentleness.

“Den, brethren an’ sisters, de matter is plain.”

The congregation leaned forward.

“Mary Magdalene had seven devils, an’ de Bible says dey was cast out. But de Bible don’t say dey was cast outen no other woman. What follows? Mary got shet of hers.”

He paused and lifted one finger.

“De rest of ’em still got theirs.”

For several seconds nobody moved. The argument had entered the church in so respectable a dress that its infirmity was not at once detected. The women knew they had been tricked, but could not immediately discover at which question the trick had occurred. The men, recognizing danger in every direction, maintained expressions of judicial gravity.

Susan Henry looked down at her Bible as though it had betrayed her. Sister Price turned back to Genesis, where the matter seemed no clearer. Aunt Rebecca gazed at Peter with an expression in which outrage contended unwillingly with admiration.

Deacon Willis was the first to act. He rose, cleared his throat, and said, “Seems to me de preacher done answered from Scripture.”

A few men said, “Amen,” softly and from behind other people.

Susan whirled upon them. “He ain’t proved no apples!”

“No,” said Peter, “but de apples was only de introduction. De devils is de doctrine.”

This distinction, though newly invented, possessed the advantage of being stated from the pulpit. No one had a ready reply.

Brother Peter observed that the moment had come when continued discussion could only weaken his position. He therefore lifted both hands.

“Brethren an’ sisters,” he said, “we has searched de Word, we has established de facts, an’ we has come through tribulation. Let us now put away disputin’ an’ worship in peace. De choir will please rise an’ sing number forty-seven: ‘Amazing Grace.’”

The choir rose by habit. Sister Susan remained seated, but the woman beside her began the hymn, and in another moment the congregation joined. Brother Peter sang louder than anyone.

He retained his pulpit. Nevertheless, for several months he confined his preaching to the sins of Pharaoh, Nebuchadnezzar, and other persons safely dead. At home he also spoke less often of devils, for Aunt Rebecca informed him that whatever the Scripture might omit, she knew precisely where all seven of hers were, and could produce them whenever the demands of theology required it.

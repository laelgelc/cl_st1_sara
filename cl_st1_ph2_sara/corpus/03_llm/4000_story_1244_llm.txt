### The Long Courtship

Leyden was a slow village. It had been built slowly, after one of two patterns, and it lived as it had been built. There were forty-seven natty little white cottages with spring-green blinds, narrow piazzas, and front yards divided by paths as mathematically as copy-book diagrams; and there were twenty-three other cottages, also white, also green-blinded, which differed from the first forty-seven chiefly in having their kitchens set to the north instead of the south. Behind them stretched bean-poles, currant bushes, clothes-lines, and patches of corn. Beyond these rose the great shoe factories, black and grimy, with a multitude of staring windows and tall chimneys which looked, on gray mornings, as if they upheld the sky.

Everybody in Leyden worked in shoes, or worked for somebody who did. The men cut, lasted, pegged, and finished; the women stitched uppers in the factories, bound them at home, or boarded the operatives. Even the children knew leather by smell and quality before they knew all their letters. At six o’clock the factory bells rang, at twelve they rang again, and at six in the evening they released the village to its suppers, its gardens, its piazzas, and its gossip.

The factory bells were the only things in Leyden that were always in haste.

Between the white cottages wound footpaths which were shorter than the road and much more social. A woman going to borrow saleratus might learn on the way that the minister’s wife had a new bonnet, that old Mis’ Crane’s rheumatism had “took a turn,” and that somebody’s cow had eaten somebody else’s early cabbages. News did not travel fast, according to the standards of the world, but it traveled thoroughly. Nothing escaped it except, now and then, the thing nearest at hand.

Maria Brewster lived in one of the first pattern of cottages, and David Emmons in one of the second. The distance between them by the road was a quarter of a mile; by the path across the factory meadow it was considerably less. David had walked that path every Sunday evening for twenty-five years.

People had long ago ceased to wonder whether he was going to Maria’s. They wondered only whether he would get there before the lamps were lighted.

David was slow. It was not mere laziness, nor weakness, nor even indecision, though it bore some relation to all three. His motions appeared to wait respectfully upon one another. When he rose from a chair, he first placed both hands upon his knees and seemed to consider the proposition. When he crossed a room, every floor-board had its separate share of him. When he answered a question, he examined it as a shoemaker examines a doubtful piece of leather, turning it over to see whether it can safely be used.

Leyden said that once, when David was walking to the factory, the meeting-house had passed him; but Leyden was imaginative when there was no danger of being contradicted. It also said that if Maria waited for him to propose, she had better put yeast into her patience and set it to rise.

Maria heard such remarks. There was very little said in Leyden which the person concerned did not hear before bedtime. She would laugh, color slightly, and reply that folks had better tend to their own affairs. Then she would go home and bake David a pie.

She was not herself slow. She was quick without being fluttering, capable without display, and orderly by a natural instinct which made disorder seem almost immoral. Her bread rose to the proper height; her preserves never worked in their jars; her geraniums blossomed when other people’s merely considered it. While her mother lived, Maria washed, ironed, cooked, sewed shoes, tended the garden, and still found time to carry broth to sick neighbors. A practical nature can dry its tears quickly, because there is generally something on the stove which will burn if it does not.

Years before, when both were young, nobody had thought David’s slowness serious. It was regarded as a harmless peculiarity, like a cast in the eye or a dislike of onions. Maria was a handsome girl then, with clear brown eyes, smooth dark hair, and a way of holding her head which made the ribbons under her bonnet look important. David, though never handsome, was tall and mild-faced. He had a little yellow mustache and wore his Sunday coat with an air of apologizing for its newness.

He first walked home with Maria from singing-school. At her gate he stood for so long with his hand upon the latch that Maria’s mother, watching through the sitting-room blind, thought it had caught his mitten. He said at last that it looked like snow. Maria said yes, she believed it did. Then he said he hoped she would not take cold. She said she hoped not. After another interval, during which the snow actually began, he told her good-night.

The next Sunday he came to see her.

From that time his courtship proceeded with the regularity of the moon and rather less variation. Every Sunday evening he washed at the kitchen sink, combed his thin, light hair before the square glass over the mantel, put on his best coat, and set forth. In summer he brought Maria radishes, green peas, or a bunch of pinks from his garden. In autumn he brought apples. Once he brought a crooked cucumber which he had preserved upon the vine because Maria had remarked that she liked them young.

Maria received these offerings with proper seriousness. In exchange she provided materials for an innocent and delicious repast: brown bread, sponge cake, seed cookies, a flaky mince pie, or doughnuts with a faint flavor of nutmeg. David sat on the slippery horsehair sofa and ate slowly. Maria sat near the lamp with her sewing. Her mother knitted in the corner and went to bed at nine.

At ten precisely Maria would say, “Ain’t it ’most time you was gittin’ along, David?”

“I s’pose likely,” David would answer.

Then he would sit another five minutes, looking toward his hat.

After he had risen and placed the hat upon his head, there remained the matter of farewell. Sometimes Maria fancied that he was about to speak. His lips moved; his pale eyes became troubled and earnest; one hand wandered toward the top button of his coat.

“Well, Maria—” he would begin.

“Well, David?”

“I guess—maybe—it’ll be fair weather to-morrow.”

“Shouldn’t wonder.”

Then he went home.

At first Maria did not doubt the end of the matter. Other girls had suitors who came irregularly, brought nothing but themselves, and proposed within three months. It seemed reasonable that a man who came every Sunday, carried vegetables in summer and apples in fall, and had been observed to look at wedding furniture in Turnbull’s store, must possess serious intentions. Maria was not romantic in her language, but there was a chamber in her heart which she kept as neatly as her spare bedroom, ready for the expected guest.

When she had been courted seven years, old Mr. Emmons died and left David the cottage. This was held throughout Leyden to remove the final obstruction.

“David’ll speak now,” said Mrs. Tolman. “A man don’t know what he’s goin’ to do till he knows where he’s goin’ to do it.”

Maria herself thought so. She helped David settle the house. The elder Emmons had been no housekeeper, and his kitchen cupboards contained cracked teacups, rusty nails, garden seeds, and a dried piece of cheese, all distributed with democratic indifference. Maria scrubbed the shelves, washed the curtains, aired the feather bed, and put the kitchen to rights. She did not move into the cottage, but order entered before her like a deputy.

That spring she bought the wedding dress.

It was pearl-colored silk, subdued and lustrous, with a faint shifting light upon its folds like sunshine under water. Maria bought it at Calvert’s store in the county town. She had gone in for black thread and a paper of pins. The silk lay upon the counter, and when the clerk unrolled it, it seemed to Maria that all the dim possibilities of her life had suddenly taken color and substance.

She asked the price, went home, counted her savings, returned the following week, and bought twelve yards.

No one in Leyden knew of it except her mother and the dressmaker. The dress was made with a fitted basque, narrow sleeves, and a skirt which could be altered if the fashion changed before it was needed. This last provision was Maria’s. Hope may soar, but prudence takes measurements.

When the finished gown was brought home, Maria tried it on in her bedroom. Her mother fastened it and stood back admiringly.

“You look real pretty, Maria,” she said. “I shouldn’t wonder if David—”

Maria turned quickly toward the glass. A tear had gathered in each eye, but she wiped them away with the corner of an old handkerchief.

“Mercy,” she said, “I near about spotted the silk.”

The dress was wrapped in a sheet and laid in the long drawer of the spare-room bureau.

That was a long time ago.

David did not speak that spring, nor the next. His cottage remained in a condition of partial domesticity. Maria went over every Wednesday evening to sweep and dust. On Saturdays she sent a loaf of bread or a plate of baked beans. If she found a button gone from his shirt or the lining loose in his coat, she repaired it. David brought her early lettuce, late squashes, and all the middle portions of the garden year. Each thanked the other with the sober courtesy of people exchanging favors, though in fact they were maintaining between two cottages the scattered pieces of one household.

Leyden watched them with affectionate impatience.

“Maria’s as good as married now,” said one neighbor.

“She’s got all the work and none of the name,” replied another.

“It’s David’s natur’.”

“Natur’ ain’t any excuse for keepin’ a woman waitin’ till her hair turns.”

Still, no one disliked David. He was too gentle for resentment. He never spoke ill of anybody, never refused a favor, and never hurried enough to jostle another human creature. At the factory he worked carefully and turned out excellent shoes, though his bench-mate declared that David could cord one boot so long the mate of it went out of fashion. When work was slack, he made up the loss by staying later. Time did not appear to David as an enemy. It was merely an immense field through which he expected, eventually, to arrive.

Maria’s mother died. David came to the funeral in his black coat and stood beside Maria at the grave. That evening he remained after the neighbors had gone, carrying empty plates to the kitchen and bringing in wood. Maria thought again that he might speak. Grief had stripped life of its small disguises, and she felt a painful tenderness for his awkward figure moving about the familiar rooms.

At the door he paused.

“Maria,” he said, “if there’s ever anything I can do—anything—”

“I know, David.”

“I allus calc’lated—”

He looked at her, then at the floor.

“You’d better take care not to set up too late,” he concluded. “You’re wore out.”

After he had gone, Maria folded her mother’s shawl and laid it away. She cried then, but not long. There were dishes to wash, food to distribute, and the black dress to be brushed for the next morning. Sorrow, with Maria, was never allowed to leave tracks through the whole house.

The years went on.

The green blinds faded and were painted again. New windows were put into one factory, and another factory burned to its brick walls and was rebuilt, uglier than before. Children who had shouted after David when he walked to Maria’s grew up, married, and sent their own children to the gate to see him pass. The minister died; his successor resigned; the successor’s successor preached against undue attention to worldly amusements, though there had never been any in Leyden.

Every Sunday night David came. Every Sunday night Maria made tea. At ten she reminded him of the hour. Sometimes a sentence approached the subject which lay between them and then moved aside, like a timid traveler who sees a difficult crossing.

Maria ceased, by degrees, to expect. Expectation did not die dramatically; it wore thin. There was no morning on which she said, “He will never ask me,” but there came a morning when she realized that she had not thought he would.

Her hair had threads of gray in it then. David’s mustache had faded almost white, and his shoulders had acquired a stoop from years over the bench. The pearl-colored silk still lay in the bureau drawer. Once every spring Maria took it out, shook the folds, inspected it for moths, and wrapped it afresh. At first she had imagined occasions upon which she might wear it. Later she preserved it because she had preserved it before.

Then Ellen Pike was to be married.

Ellen was the daughter of a widow who lived near the lower factory. She was a pretty, industrious girl, and her marriage to young Henry Lord was universally approved. There was only one trouble: she had no money for a gown. Mrs. Pike mentioned this to Maria while they were cording boots together one rainy afternoon.

“Ellen says she’ll be married in her brown cashmere,” said Mrs. Pike, “and it’s neat enough, but she’s wore it two winters. A girl kind of hankers for something different when she’s married. Not that she complains. Ellen ain’t that sort.”

Maria drew her thread through the leather and fastened it.

“I’ve got a piece of silk,” she said.

She brought out the pearl-colored dress that evening. It had been made for a younger and fuller fashion, but the silk remained beautiful. Maria held it against herself before the glass. Its mild radiance softened her face and made the gray in her hair look almost silver.

For one instant she saw the girl she had been: the clear brown eyes, the dark smooth hair, the quick hand brushing away tears lest they spot the silk. Then the image was gone. A looking-glass is sometimes more honest than a friend.

She carried the dress to Ellen Pike the next day.

There was enough sound silk to make a wedding gown with narrower sleeves and a plain skirt. Ellen cried, Mrs. Pike cried, and Maria nearly cried herself; but she began at once to consider how the basque could best be altered.

“It ain’t doin’ anybody any good in my bureau,” she said.

Leyden heard of the gift and praised her. Some said it was just like Maria; others said they wondered how she could bear it; a few believed she had never seriously expected to wear the dress at all. Public opinion generally finds a comfortable explanation for sacrifices which it has not made.

David heard, too. The next Sunday he sat longer than usual with his teacup in his hand.

“I hear you give Ellen Pike that silk dress,” he said.

“Yes.”

“It was a handsome piece.”

“It made over real nice.”

“I recollect when you bought it.”

Maria looked at him. “Do you?”

“I seen you bring the parcel from the stage.”

He set the cup carefully in the saucer. His fingers trembled a little, though whether from age or feeling Maria could not tell.

“I allus meant—” he said.

She waited.

David looked toward the clock. “I allus meant to tell you that was a handsome color.”

Maria gathered the cups and carried them into the kitchen.

After that she felt something had ended. It had not ended with anger, because she could no more be angry with David than with the slow winter for delaying spring. Yet winter, however slow, did finally relinquish the earth. David had held his season for a lifetime.

Still she cared for him.

As he grew older, his cottage became more desolate. A bachelor’s disorder has a pathetic look when nobody can suppose it temporary. Ashes whitened the hearth; a pan stood where it had been left; the tablecloth slipped toward one corner; the clock stopped and remained at twenty minutes past four for three months. Maria put things to rights, but order brought from another house cannot strike deep root. It lasted until David began to look for something.

One Sunday she noticed a tear in his best coat under the arm. The lining had frayed, and the cloth itself was thin as paper.

“David Emmons,” she said, “you ain’t fit to be seen.”

“I guess it don’t show much when my arm’s down.”

“What if you want to lift it?”

“I don’t know as I shall.”

Maria made him take off the coat. He sat in his shirtsleeves while she threaded a needle and examined the damage. The collar was shiny, one cuff was unraveling, and a button hung by a single thread. The poor old garment spoke more plainly of his loneliness than David had ever done.

Maria bent over it. Her eyes blurred.

“You ought to have somebody to see to you,” she said.

“You see to me considerable.”

“I don’t live there.”

“No,” said David.

The needle went through the cloth and pricked her finger. She put the finger quickly to her lips.

“You can’t go on so,” she said.

David looked as if he were preparing to answer, but by the time the coat was mended the preparation had come to nothing.

His health failed with the same quiet persistence that had marked his courtship. At first he tired on the walk from the factory. Then he gave up factory work and attended only to his garden. He raised excellent beans and cabbages, but the rows became less straight, the weeds bolder. His gifts to Maria diminished from baskets to handfuls. One July evening he brought three peas in a pod and a little bunch of wilted mint.

Maria wanted him to have a doctor.

“I ain’t sick,” David said. “I’m jest gittin’ along.”

“That’s what ails you. You ain’t gittin’ along enough.”

The old joke sounded different then.

She sent him nourishing dishes: chicken broth, custards, baked apples, and soft bread. David returned the plates washed, but not always clean. Sometimes he forgot to eat what she sent. Once she found a bowl of broth untouched upon his shelf and scolded him until he smiled faintly.

“You take on as if you was my wife, Maria.”

The words silenced them both.

David turned toward the window. Maria covered the bowl and carried it away. Neither referred to the matter again.

That autumn came early. Frost blackened the bean vines before the middle of September, and smoke lay low over the village. The factory whistles sounded farther away in the cold air. Maria saw that David wore his thin coat buttoned tightly, and she made him a comforter from a piece of dark wool.

Every Sunday he continued to come.

His journey took longer now. Maria watched from the window and saw him stop by the elm, then at the meadow bars, then beside the lilac hedge. He would not use a cane until she bought one and left it in his entry without discussion. Thereafter he carried it, though apologetically, as if it belonged to somebody else.

One Sunday night in November he came later than usual. The meeting-house clock had struck eight before his hand fumbled at the latch. Maria hurried to the door.

“You had no business comin’ out,” she said. “It’s raw enough to freeze the breath in you.”

“I wanted to come.”

She took his coat and seated him near the stove. His face was gray, and there was a bluish shade around his mouth. He ate almost nothing, though Maria had made a delicate little pudding because his teeth troubled him.

The room was very still. Wind brushed dead leaves along the piazza, and once a factory shutter banged in the distance. The clock ticked loudly between them. Maria sewed a patch upon one of David’s shirts, but she had to hold the cloth closer to the lamp than formerly.

At half-past nine David spoke.

“Maria.”

“Yes, David?”

“You’ve been real good to me.”

“I ain’t done anything worth mentionin’.”

“Yes, you have. You’ve done—everything.”

Maria folded the shirt over her hand. David’s voice was strange, but she would not look frightened.

“I’ve been slow,” he continued.

“You allus was.”

“A good many things I calc’lated to do—I didn’t git ’round to ’em.”

“Well,” said Maria, with forced briskness, “there’s plenty of things most folks don’t git ’round to.”

“I don’t want you to lay up anything ag’in me.”

She looked at him then. His pale eyes had the helpless, questioning look of a child who has broken something precious and cannot say how.

“Why, David,” she said softly, “I don’t lay up anything ag’in you. I never did.”

He breathed deeply, as if some weight had shifted.

“I’m glad,” he said. “I wanted to be sure.”

At ten she insisted upon walking partway home with him, but David would not allow it.

“You’ll ketch cold.”

“And you won’t, I s’pose?”

“I’ve got the comforter.”

He smiled a little. At the gate he turned, leaning on the cane. Maria stood in the doorway with the lamp behind her.

“Good-night, Maria.”

“Good-night, David. You send for me if you feel poorly.”

“I will.”

She watched until his bent figure disappeared beyond the lilac hedge.

Maria slept badly. Several times she woke thinking she had heard a knock. At daylight she rose, kindled the fire, and made coffee, but she could not eat. The eastern sky was the color of cold ashes. Factory operatives passed her window in groups, dinner pails swinging, their voices sharp in the frosty air.

At seven there came a hurried pounding at the kitchen door.

It was Jimmy Crane, the neighbor’s boy, bareheaded and panting.

“Mis’ Brewster—Mr. Emmons is took awful bad. They want you right off.”

Maria did not stop for her bonnet. She threw a shawl over her head and followed the boy across the factory meadow.

There were people before David’s cottage. Mrs. Tolman stood on the step; old Mr. Pike was bringing water; two factory women whispered near the gate. Their faces turned toward Maria with the solemn recognition which tells news before speech can shape it.

She went through them without a question.

The kitchen was crowded and confused. A kettle boiled over; the clock still pointed to twenty minutes past four; somebody had placed David’s muddy boots upon a chair. Maria took command because it was her nature to do so.

“Open that window a crack. Don’t let so many stand around. Mrs. Tolman, fetch a clean towel. Has the doctor been sent for?”

Then she entered the little bedroom.

David lay high upon the pillows, breathing with terrible difficulty. His face had sunk and sharpened since the night before. The doctor bent over him, then stepped aside when he saw Maria.

“He’s been asking for you,” he said.

Maria sat on the edge of the bed and took David’s cold hand. The room cleared behind her. She heard the door close, the murmur in the kitchen, and far away the factory bell beginning its morning summons.

“David,” she said, “I’m here.”

His eyelids lifted. Recognition came slowly, but it came. His fingers moved within hers.

“Maria—”

“Yes, David.”

“I was—goin’ to—”

“Don’t try to talk.”

“I’ve got—to. I meant—Sunday nights—all these years—”

His breath failed. Maria bent nearer, holding his hand between both of hers.

“I allus meant to ask you, Maria—if you’d marry me.”

## THE TESTIMONY OF AMASA D. KEATING

There are seasons when the Pacific, that ancient and treacherous companion of our western shore, appears to repent of her immensity and lie, blue, smooth, and speechless, beneath the benediction of the sun; but there are other hours in which she renews her immemorial war against the continent, striking with all her leagues of water at the granite barriers that deny her dominion. It was during such an hour, in the winter of 1879, that there befell me the singular adventure which I am about to relate.

All day the south wind had marched upon the peninsula. Toward evening it strengthened to a gale; the cypresses upon Point Lobos groaned as though some giant hand twisted their black trunks, while the rain, driven nearly level, effaced the familiar headlands and reduced the world to a narrow confusion of gray water, gray air, and whitening foam. Carmel Bay, ordinarily a place of melancholy tranquillity, had become a vast cauldron. The waves came in ranks, lifted their plumed heads, and fell with detonations that shook the sand beneath my feet.

Why I had gone abroad in such weather I can scarcely say. There are men whom a storm repels, and others whom it summons. To me the voice of the sea in anger has ever possessed a dreadful fascination; and thus, wrapped in a heavy oilskin and leaning against the wind, I descended by the dunes to the beach, where the advancing tide had strewn kelp, timber, dead birds, and wreckage of every description.

The evening was not yet dark, but no distinct boundary remained between earth and heaven. A leaden twilight hung over all. At intervals the rain thinned, and through its moving veil I saw the cypresses contending with the gale upon the heights, the black rocks continually appearing and vanishing beneath the surf, and, farther out, a flock of gulls whirled helplessly down the wind like scraps of torn paper.

I had walked perhaps half a mile when the sea, which had already drenched and buffeted me, armed one of its waves with a more substantial bludgeon. The breaker overtook me while I was stooping to examine a fragment of carved wood. Something struck my ankle with such violence that I was thrown upon one knee. I caught at the object before the retreating water could reclaim it, and found it to be a dark, thick-sided bottle, incrusted with minute shells and wrapped about the neck with several turns of copper wire.

The bottle was not empty.

Within it lay a compact roll of what appeared to be paper or very thin parchment. Its presence might, under ordinary circumstances, have excited no more than a passing curiosity; sailors, fishermen, and idle visitors to the coast have cast thousands of such messages into the water. Yet this bottle had an aspect of extraordinary age and travail. Its surface was scarred, its glass dimmed almost to opacity, and the wire about its stopper had corroded until it resembled a greenish growth. The sea seemed not merely to have carried it, but to have labored with it through unnumbered years, and at last, in the violence of that evening, to have delivered it at my feet.

I thrust it beneath my coat and returned with some difficulty to my lodging.

The cork, though shrunken at its upper end, had been sealed below with pitch. I broke the neck rather than risk injury to the contents. The roll proved to consist of nine narrow leaves covered on both sides with writing in a brown ink, much faded and in many places wholly destroyed. The outer leaf was brittle; several lines crumbled while I unfolded it. By drying the sheets between blotting papers, examining them under a lens, and comparing doubtful letters with clearer specimens of the same hand, I obtained, after many weeks, a tolerably connected account.

I have supplied no event which the manuscript did not warrant. Where a word was lost but its meaning obvious, I restored it; where both word and meaning had vanished, I have indicated the omission as gently as possible, lest too frequent interruptions should impair a narrative already sufficiently broken. The dates, names, and geographical conjectures are given precisely as I found them.

The document bore no formal title. It began thus:

---

My name is Amasa D. Keating, formerly of Windham County, in the State of Connecticut, and latterly a passenger aboard the American brig *Hopewell*, Captain Elias Campbell, which vessel sailed from New Bedford on the 17th day of September, in the year of our Lord 1853, bound ultimately for the islands of the South Pacific.

If this writing be found, I entreat the finder, in the name of Christian mercy and our common humanity, to place it without delay in the hands of some naval officer, magistrate, missionary society, or other person capable of procuring assistance for those whose situation I shall here set down. I write in a small boat upon the open sea, without chart, compass, food, water, or proper clothing. My companion, Lemuel Arthur, lies in the bottom of the boat, being wounded and at times insensible. Our own preservation is doubtful; nevertheless, the rescue of others may yet be accomplished, should Providence carry this testimony where we cannot go.

I am sensible that violent mental disturbance has rendered my hand uncertain and my recollection in some particulars confused. Yet the principal facts are true. Of this I call Almighty God to witness.

The *Hopewell* was a brig of two hundred and eighty tons, built at Bath, Maine, and commanded by a man whose skill and sobriety were acknowledged by all who knew him. We were not traders in the customary sense, though certain implements and articles of manufacture had been placed aboard for barter. Our company consisted chiefly of persons who had resolved to establish an American settlement upon one of several islands recommended to us by a commercial association in Boston. There were farmers, carpenters, coopers, blacksmiths, two schoolmasters, a physician, a milliner, a printer, and a clergyman of the Congregational persuasion. Including women and children, we numbered seventy-three passengers. The officers and crew added eighteen more.

Our purpose may now seem rash; at that time it appeared neither extraordinary nor imprudent. We had been assured that the proposed place of settlement possessed abundant water, fertile soil, a healthful climate, and a harbor adequate for small vessels. Captain Campbell carried letters to certain chiefs reputed friendly to Americans, together with gifts calculated to secure their goodwill. We had tools, seed, medicines, arms, books, and sufficient provisions for a long passage. Several among us, myself included, had disposed of nearly all property in the United States and looked upon the voyage not as an experiment, but as the opening of a new life.

For many months nothing occurred which it is necessary to relate. We rounded Cape Horn after severe but not unusual weather, touched at Valparaíso, and thence stood westward. In February of 1854—on the exact date there was afterward some disagreement—we encountered a succession of calms, variable winds, and violent squalls which carried us considerably north of the course intended by Captain Campbell. The sun was obscured for several days; the chronometer, having received an injury during the passage round the Horn, could not be wholly relied upon; and when at last an observation was obtained, our longitude remained a matter of dispute between the captain and Mr. Hobart, his mate.

At two o’clock on the morning of the 3rd of March, while rain fell with extreme violence and the wind blew from the east-southeast, the *Hopewell* struck upon a reef.

I was awakened by the concussion and thrown from my berth. Before I reached the deck she struck again, slewed broadside to the sea, and began to fill. The darkness was complete except when lightning disclosed, at a little distance, a line of breakers and beyond them what appeared to be high land. The foremast went by the board within ten minutes, carrying three sailors with it. The longboat was stove before it could be lowered. The quarter boat, though damaged, was got away; rafts were made of spars, hatches, and casks; and by these means, and by swimming, the greater part of our company reached the reef.

I deem it wholly unnecessary to describe the lamentations of those who lost husbands, wives, children, or friends during that passage of less than two hundred yards. Sixteen persons were drowned or killed by wreckage. Of the remainder, many were bruised, cut, or nearly senseless from exhaustion. We crouched upon the coral until daylight, while the brig broke behind us and every wave carried some portion of our property into the lagoon.

With dawn the storm diminished. We then perceived that the reef enclosed a broad sheet of comparatively quiet water, beyond which rose an island of uncommon beauty. Its eastern shore was mountainous and clothed to the summit with dark forest; toward the west the land descended into gentle hills and valleys, where groves of palms were interspersed with cultivated fields. Smoke ascended from several villages. A river entered the lagoon approximately three miles from the place where we had been cast away.

The surviving boat made repeated journeys between the reef and shore. Certain inhabitants, having assembled upon the beach, came out in canoes and assisted us. Their conduct was orderly and compassionate. They carried the injured, supplied us with fruit and water, and expressed grief—by countenance and gesture, for we did not yet comprehend their speech—when bodies were brought from the reef.

Among the passengers was a man named Josiah Bell, formerly a sailor, who had lived for some years among the Marquesas and could make himself understood in several island dialects. The language spoken here differed from those known to him, but not so greatly as to prevent an imperfect communication. By his aid we learned that the island was called either Malakato or Malekata—the pronunciation cannot be represented precisely in English—and that its ruler, who resided inland, had already been informed of our misfortune.

Before noon there arrived a procession of nearly one hundred persons, at whose head walked the king.

His name, as Bell rendered it, was Koro Manu. He was a man of perhaps fifty years, tall, grave, and somewhat corpulent, with hair already touched by gray. Unlike many of his attendants, he wore a mantle woven of fine pale fiber, and around his neck hung a disk of polished shell. His manner had neither the theatrical violence nor the childish curiosity which some writers attribute indiscriminately to the inhabitants of those seas. He greeted Captain Campbell with dignity, listened attentively to Bell’s explanation, and surveyed our wretched company with every appearance of concern.

He caused shelters to be erected near the beach, ordered food in great abundance, and placed several houses at the disposal of the women and children. Persons acquainted with the treatment of wounds washed and bound our injuries with preparations of leaves. Fires were lighted; mats and coverings were distributed; and guards were stationed, as we supposed, to protect us from theft or intrusion.

For these benefits we were profoundly grateful. Captain Campbell, in the presence of us all, thanked the king and declared that the people of the United States would remember his generosity. Koro Manu replied through Bell that no gratitude was necessary; strangers whom the sea had delivered into his country were sacred, and while they remained they should be fed as his children.

This expression, afterward so dreadful in our remembrance, then affected many persons to tears.

During the next fortnight we recovered what could be saved from the wreck. A quantity of tools, arms, soaked powder, seed grain, clothing, and provisions was brought ashore. The king’s people assisted zealously, though they examined each object with great interest and appeared especially pleased by iron utensils. Nothing of consequence was taken without permission, so far as we could ascertain.

When Captain Campbell proposed that we should establish ourselves temporarily near the coast and rebuild the quarter boat, Koro Manu advised another course. The season of great winds, he said, had begun; the shore was exposed; and sickness commonly visited those who slept near the lagoon. At a distance of six miles inland lay a valley where the air was better, the soil richer, and unoccupied houses sufficient for our use. There we might dwell until a vessel called, or until we constructed one capable of carrying a party to the nearest frequented island.

The advice appeared sound. We accordingly removed to the valley.

No place could at first have seemed more suited to our necessities. It was an oval plain, perhaps four miles in length and two in breadth, enclosed on all sides by wooded heights. A clear stream crossed it from north to south. Breadfruit, coconut, plantain, orange, and other trees grew profusely; yams and sweet potatoes were cultivated in neighboring fields; and upon the eastern slope stood twenty or thirty houses of timber, reed, and thatch, clean, elevated above the damp ground, and commodious beyond our expectation. We were told that the village had been abandoned after a fever some years earlier. No trace of disease remained.

Under these favorable circumstances we applied ourselves to regular occupation. The carpenter, Mr. Croft, examined the tools and timber. Captain Campbell established watches, appointed persons to hunt, fish, cook, and tend the sick, and caused a daily record to be kept. Mr. Pritchard, the clergyman, held service beneath a great tree. Several children resumed their lessons. Gardens were planted, and for a brief period it appeared possible that our misfortune might prove no more than a severe interruption of our original design.

There were, however, restrictions.

At each of the three paths leading out of the valley, the king had placed armed men. Bell understood them to be guides, stationed lest we should enter sacred ground, become lost in the mountains, or offend villages whose customs we did not know. We were permitted to go to the river, the lower fields, and a grove to the west, but not beyond certain carved posts. If any person approached those limits, the guards courteously turned him back.

Captain Campbell remonstrated. Koro Manu, who visited us every fourth or fifth day, answered with patience. He said that a ceremony of national importance was being prepared in the northern district; strangers must not witness it; after the next moon we should have greater liberty. He smiled frequently during this explanation, and presented the captain with hogs, baskets of fruit, and a carved staff as tokens of friendship.

The king’s gentleness disarmed opposition. Some among us even maintained that his precautions were reasonable. We were dependent upon his people; we knew nothing of local disputes or religious prohibitions; and to provoke our benefactors over a temporary inconvenience would have been both foolish and ungrateful.

Meanwhile sickness appeared.

Mrs. Pritchard, who had never wholly recovered from exposure upon the reef, developed a fever. An elderly farmer named Samuel Voss suffered swelling in the legs and difficulty of breathing. Two children had dysentery. Our physician, Dr. Hale, possessed medicines rescued from the brig, but much of his supply had been injured by seawater.

When the king next visited, he examined the sick with particular attention. He then informed us that, on the southern side of the island, there existed a station frequented by foreign vessels. A schooner from Tahiti was expected within a few days. If we wished, he would send the most infirm persons there, where they might receive better treatment and perhaps obtain immediate passage to a civilized port.

The proposal occasioned considerable discussion. Captain Campbell asked permission for Dr. Hale and several others to accompany the invalids. The king replied that the road was difficult, the southern villages unsafe for strangers, and the canoe intended for their ultimate transportation small. His own attendants, being acquainted with the route, could convey them more rapidly. Letters might be sent, and upon reaching the station the patients could notify the first vessel of our situation.

Mrs. Pritchard herself begged to go. She believed she would die if she remained. Mr. Pritchard, though unwilling to leave the larger company, resolved to accompany her as far as permitted. At dawn she and Samuel Voss were carried away in litters, attended by twelve islanders. Mr. Pritchard went with them to the western boundary of the valley, where the guards compelled him to turn back.

No letter or message was afterward received.

At first this caused no alarm. The king explained that the schooner had sailed unexpectedly, taking the sick with it, and that our letters had by some accident been carried away also. When pressed for the name of the vessel, he gave one which Bell could not clearly understand. He assured us, however, that Mrs. Pritchard and Voss were safe and already many leagues nearer home.

Ten days later one of the children, little Abigail Croft, was removed in the same fashion. Then went Thomas Avery, who had broken two ribs during the wreck and whose breathing had become painful. After him were taken Mrs. Bell, a sailor named Haines, and old Mr. Satterlee, the printer.

The frequency of these removals produced uneasiness. None who departed returned; no communication came from the alleged station; and no ship, though men watched the heights whenever they could do so unobserved, was seen approaching or leaving the island.

We also began to perceive that our condition was one of complete isolation. The quarter boat, which had been hauled to the mouth of the river for repair, disappeared. Koro Manu said that a high tide had carried it away. Mr. Croft disputed this, for the boat had been secured above the reach of any ordinary tide with a new rope of great strength. The firearms recovered from the wreck were kept in a locked house, but one morning the lock was found broken and every musket removed. The king expressed indignation and promised an inquiry; the weapons were not restored.

Our guards, formerly twelve, increased to thirty. They remained polite, but carried clubs and spears and no longer pretended to be guides.

At length Captain Campbell called the adult men together. His speech, so nearly as I recollect it, was as follows:

“Friends, we have desired to attribute every doubtful circumstance to our ignorance of this country; but there comes a time when confidence ceases to be charity and becomes folly. We were received with kindness. We have therefore endured restraint without violence. Yet our boat and arms are gone; our sick are carried away; and we are forbidden to discover whither. I will not accuse the king of a crime for which we possess no proof, but I say plainly that we must obtain proof of their safety or refuse to surrender another person.”

All agreed.

Dr. Hale then made known a circumstance which he had hitherto concealed. While attending Avery on the morning of his removal, the doctor had seen an attendant press the patient’s arm and thigh, as a butcher might examine an animal. The man had spoken to another, who laughed and replied with a word signifying, according to Bell, “thin,” “poor,” or “not ready.” Bell was reluctant to attach importance to this. Similar words, he said, might refer to health or strength.

Nevertheless, from that hour the possibility of cannibalism was whispered among us.

The islanders appeared, in most respects, further removed from such a practice than many peoples among whom it was known to exist. Their fields were carefully cultivated; fish, hogs, and domestic fowl were plentiful; their villages were orderly; and their ceremonies, so far as we had observed them, possessed gravity and even refinement. Koro Manu frequently condemned warfare. He had punished a man for striking one of our children. It seemed monstrous that a ruler capable of justice and compassion should secretly destroy helpless strangers.

Thus reason, gratitude, and fear contended within us, and uncertainty became almost more oppressive than knowledge.

On the 11th day of May, Lemuel Arthur was taken.

Arthur was twenty-four years of age, unmarried, and by occupation a wheelwright. He had suffered no illness since the wreck. Indeed, among all our company there was scarcely a man more vigorous. He stood nearly six feet in height, had broad shoulders, a cheerful disposition, and a complexion so fair that exposure to the island sun caused him continual discomfort.

Shortly before dusk, four men entered the valley with a message that the king desired Arthur’s assistance in repairing a vehicle used for the transport of timber. The request was credible, for he had previously examined certain native sledges and suggested improvements. Yet Captain Campbell refused to let him go unless others accompanied him.

An argument followed. The messengers withdrew; within an hour nearly sixty armed men appeared at the eastern path. Their leader stated that Arthur had been invited by the king and that refusal would constitute a grave insult. Captain Campbell answered that the young man was not a servant and could not be commanded.

The islanders then advanced.

We had knives, axes, hammers, and several iron bars, but no firearms. The women and children had gathered behind us. A struggle at that place must have ended in general slaughter. Arthur, perceiving this, stepped forward and declared that he would go willingly, provided he returned by the following noon.

He endeavored to smile as he left. I shall never forget the manner in which that expression failed upon his face when two guards placed themselves on either side of him.

The taking of a healthy man destroyed every reassuring interpretation we had maintained. It was resolved that someone must follow and learn the truth. The attempt appeared nearly hopeless. All paths were watched; our clothing and features distinguished us immediately; and Bell, the only person able to speak more than a few words of the language, was lame from an injury received at the reef.

It happened, however, that I had spent much of my youth at sea and possessed some knowledge of the customs of Pacific peoples. My hair was black, my skin darkened readily, and I was of smaller stature than most of the other men. I therefore offered myself.

Near midnight I rubbed my body with a mixture of soot, nut oil, and brown clay, stained my lips, tied my hair close to my head, and put on a short garment which Bell had obtained by barter. Around my neck I hung several strings of shell. The disguise could not have survived careful examination, but in darkness, among a crowd, it might suffice.

The southern outlet of the valley followed the stream through a narrow ravine. Here the guards kept a small fire and ordinarily sat together upon the eastern bank. Shortly after midnight, Mr. Croft and two others released a number of hogs from an enclosure and drove them toward the western fields, while Captain Campbell raised an outcry that thieves had entered one of the houses. As we hoped, several guards abandoned their place. I crept through the stream beneath overhanging reeds, passed within twenty feet of the remaining men, and gained the forest.

For perhaps two hours I followed a path that climbed toward the north. The moon had not yet risen. More than once I lost the way and was compelled to feel with my hands for footprints in the damp earth. At intervals I saw lights moving among the trees and heard voices. The whole island appeared awake.

Upon reaching the crest of the ridge, I perceived a broad red illumination beyond the next descent. I advanced with greater caution and presently came to the edge of a clearing where perhaps three hundred persons had assembled.

At the center stood a large building of stone or hardened clay, shaped somewhat like a baker’s oven, with a high chimney from which issued sparks and black smoke. Before it burned several fires. Men carried baskets of wood to an opening at the base, while others raked a quantity of pale ash from a side chamber.

The nature of this structure did not at first occur to me. I supposed it connected with the ceremony of which Koro Manu had spoken.

I concealed myself behind a pile of fuel, where two elderly islanders soon approached and began conversing. Their dialect differed slightly from that used in the valley, but I understood enough to follow the greater part of their speech.

One complained of the labor required to keep the kiln hot. The other answered that after that night there would be no need to heat it again until the strangers became weak or troublesome. The first said that the last old man had burned badly because his bones were large. The second replied that the child had made almost no ash and that the woman with the swollen legs had required twice the expected wood.

I cannot adequately express the effect of these words. My first thought was of some funerary custom; I told myself that our sick had died naturally and been burned according to the practice of the country. I clung to this explanation even when one of the men asked whether the strangers suspected.

“No,” said the other. “They believe the weak ones go over the water.”

“And the strong?”

“They eat, and sleep, and become fat.”

“When will they be given?”

“After the great feast. The king will keep some for the eastern houses. The priests choose first. Those with much white flesh will be divided among the high men.”

The old men moved away before I heard more.

I remained unable to stir. The heat of the kiln beat upon my face; a sweetish odor, previously unnoticed, now seemed to mingle with the smoke. Near the opening lay several fragments which might have been calcined bone. Among the ashes I saw something round and metallic. When no one watched, I crawled nearer and recovered it.

It was a brass button from Samuel Voss’s coat.

All doubt ended.

The sick and infirm, being unfit for the purpose designed for the rest of us, had been destroyed in the kiln. Those who remained were fed generously, guarded from exertion, and preserved for consumption. The valley was not a refuge but an enclosure.

I thought immediately of returning to warn the others; but Arthur was still alive, and the words I had overheard suggested that his death was near. Presently a drum sounded in the forest. The people around the kiln formed into lines and moved northward. I joined the last of them.

The path descended to a plain enclosed by enormous trees. In its midst had been prepared an extraordinary arrangement of fires, stones, mats, and shallow pits. One fire, larger than the rest, burned beneath a platform of green timber upon which lay many rounded stones. Men tended these with long poles. Others carried clubs composed of several flexible rods bound together, resembling agricultural flails. At the northern end of the clearing stood a raised seat beneath a canopy. Koro Manu occupied it, surrounded by chiefs, priests, and women wearing elaborate ornaments.

I kept to the outer portion of the crowd.

The ceremony proceeded with a deliberation more terrible than frenzy. There was singing, but no shouting; movements were directed by drums; the fires were tended as calmly as though a communal meal were being prepared. Men spread broad leaves over the ground, filled wooden bowls with herbs and oil, and laid out knives of sharpened shell. The heated stones were examined one by one and rejected if cracked or insufficiently smooth.

Near me two young men discussed the distribution of the victim.

“The king has the heart,” said one.

“He gave it to the priest of the north.”

“No, the priest has the right thigh.”

“That belongs to the harbor chief.”

They continued in this manner, disputing not whether a man should be eaten, but who possessed a claim upon each portion. One remarked that the stranger must be beaten carefully, since flesh bruised before heating became tender. The other laughed and said the pale men required much beating because they were tougher than hogs.

A procession entered from the eastern side.

Arthur walked in its midst.

They had stripped him. His hands were tied behind his back, and a rope encircled his neck. Blood ran from a wound above his left eye, but he held himself erect. In the red light his body appeared almost luminous against the darker multitude, and this contrast, together with the perfect composure of those preparing to kill him, produced in me a peculiar sense of unreality. I seemed to look not upon an event occurring before my eyes, but upon some infernal picture.

He was brought to a post near the fire. The king descended and spoke to him through an interpreter whom I had not previously seen. This man knew a little English.

“The king says you are honored,” he told Arthur. “You are first because you are beautiful and strong. If you do not cry, your spirit will be great.”

Arthur looked toward the canopy.

“Tell your king,” he replied, “that if my friends come here, they will hang him from that tree.”

The interpreter did not translate this exactly. I saw Koro Manu smile.

Four men carrying the bound flails took their places. Two attendants poured oil upon Arthur’s shoulders and breast. A priest raised a shell trumpet.

I had no plan. To attempt his rescue among several hundred people, most of them armed, was manifestly impossible; yet there are moments when the mind, having exhausted reason, commits itself to action with a clarity almost peaceful.

Beside the king’s seat stood a rack containing spears and two heavy clubs. Between myself and the canopy were perhaps thirty persons. The larger fire cast much smoke toward the west. I moved through the crowd until I had approached within twenty paces of Koro Manu.

The trumpet sounded.

The men with flails advanced.

At that instant one of the heated stones burst apart with a loud report. Sparks flew among the attendants. Several persons cried out and turned their faces. I snatched a spear from a man beside me, leaped upon the lower step of the platform, and drove the weapon beneath the king’s ribs.

He stared at me, not in pain at first, but in astonishment. I wrenched the spear free and struck again. He fell against the canopy, tearing it down upon the priests behind him.

I shouted in English, “Run, Arthur! Run!”

The crowd recoiled from the sound of the foreign tongue. I sprang to the post, caught up a shell knife, and cut the rope at Arthur’s wrists. One of the executioners struck at me with his flail; Arthur seized the man by the throat and flung him into the fire. Another raised a club. I thrust the spear into his face.

Then the whole assembly moved.

Arthur snatched a knife. I overturned a bowl of oil upon the nearest mats and kicked burning wood after it. Flame ran at once across the dry leaves. Smoke, cries, and confusion covered us. We fled westward, where the trees grew thickest.

Behind us drums sounded rapidly. Men crashed through the undergrowth on either side. Arthur, though wounded and barefoot, ran with extraordinary strength. I had retained the spear, but soon cast it away because the branches impeded me. We descended a stony slope, crossed a stream, and climbed again. Twice we heard pursuers pass so near that we threw ourselves beneath ferns and scarcely breathed.

Before dawn we reached a village overlooking the western shore. The houses were deserted, all inhabitants having gone to the ceremony. Below lay a narrow inlet in which three canoes and a small outrigger boat had been drawn upon the sand.

The larger canoes were too heavy. We chose the outrigger, pushed it into the water, and found within a paddle, a coil of cord, and a mat sail. As we launched, voices sounded from the hill. Spears fell around us. One struck Arthur in the shoulder and remained there until, having gained deeper water, I drew it out. Another pierced the mat sail.

We paddled beyond the reef. A fresh wind then carried us westward.

The island at sunrise presented an appearance of perfect tranquillity. Its mountains rose green and luminous; white birds circled above the forest; smoke from the villages ascended vertically in the still air. No sign of the night’s horrors could be discerned. I could see, however, several canoes putting out from the northern shore.

We continued until the island had sunk below the horizon.

Since then—I do not know how many hours have passed—the wind has failed. Arthur has lost much blood. The sun burns us by day, and at night we suffer from cold. We have neither water nor food. The paddle was lost while I slept. The sail is useless without wind, and the current carries us whithersoever it will.

I write upon leaves torn from the small memorandum book which, by a fortunate accident, remained tied beneath my garment. The pencil is nearly gone. I have placed with this account such names as I can remember, in order that those left behind may be identified.

They are Captain Elias Campbell; Dr. Nathaniel Hale; Reverend Gideon Pritchard; Josiah Bell; Ezra Croft, carpenter, with his wife and surviving son; William Hobart, mate; Deborah Satterlee, milliner; Lemuel Arthur—who is here with me—and many others whose names appear upon the next leaf.

[Here followed a list, much injured by damp. Forty-two names could be read wholly or in part.]

The island lies, according to Captain Campbell’s last conjecture, between the equator and twenty degrees south latitude, and west of the Marquesas, though our position after the storms was uncertain. It has a great reef on the eastern side, a double-peaked mountain toward the north, and a smaller bare hill resembling a broken tooth near the western inlet. The king was Koro Manu, whom I believe I killed. Whether his death will hasten the destruction of the prisoners or throw the island into disorder, I cannot know.

The guarded valley is inland from the eastern lagoon and south of the double mountain. Those who go to rescue our companions must come armed and in force. They must place no confidence in protestations of friendship. They must search the kiln north of the valley, where the remains of the sick will be found.

Arthur has revived. He asks for water, and I have none.

Clouds gather in the east. If rain comes, we may live another day. If wind comes, it may carry us toward land or farther into the waste. I shall seal these pages in the bottle which lay beneath the forward seat, stopper it with cloth and pitch from the boat’s seams, and cast it overboard before darkness. Haply the current that destroys us may preserve our testimony.

To whoever receives it: do not delay. The people in the valley believe Arthur and I have abandoned them, or that we are dead. They are without weapons. Their guards are numerous. They are fed continually.

This last circumstance is a matter of some moment.

I can write no more. The sea is rising, and Arthur calls to me.

God preserve them.

AMASA D. KEATING.

---

Here the manuscript ended.

I made inquiries, extending them over several years, concerning the brig *Hopewell*, her passengers, and any expedition which might have followed her disappearance. In New Bedford I found a shipping notice confirming that a vessel of that name, commanded by Elias Campbell, had sailed in September of 1853 with emigrants for the Pacific. She was never afterward reported in any recognized port.

Of Amasa D. Keating I discovered only that a man of that name, aged thirty-one, had sold a small property in Connecticut shortly before the *Hopewell* sailed. Lemuel Arthur appeared in a passenger list preserved by the family of one of the lost. No record of either man’s return exists.

Whether the island called Malakato was ever identified, I cannot say. Charts of the period contain many uncertainties, and reefs or islands reported by one navigator are omitted by another. The coordinates in Keating’s manuscript were too indefinite to guide a vessel through so wide an ocean. It is possible that the place bore another name; possible that the words heard by Bell signified a district rather than an island; possible, also, that subsequent disease, warfare, or visitation altered it beyond recognition.

There remains only the bottle.

For twenty-five years, if the date upon the testimony be accepted, it wandered through the circulations of the Pacific. It may have drifted westward among archipelagoes, turned south in some cold current, approached Australia, escaped again into the vast revolving waste, and at last been taken by that northward stream which moves along the American coast. Through calm and typhoon, beneath tropic suns, amid whales, ships, floating forests, and the drowned possessions of men, it carried its small burden; not swiftly, not mercifully, but with the patience of the sea.

And then, upon that storm-dark evening in Carmel Bay, the old ocean lifted it, armed a wave with it, and struck me down.

Whether this was accident or revelation, the reader may determine. I know only that whenever the winter wind renews its war against the peninsula, and the cypresses writhe upon the heights, I seem to hear, beneath the tumult of the breakers, a multitude crying from some fertile and forgotten valley; while farther off, already faint with thirst and exposure, two naked men drift under an empty heaven, watching the last bottle vanish among the waves.

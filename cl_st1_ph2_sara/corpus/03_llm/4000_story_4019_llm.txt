### The Five Fires of Benbo

In the year when Sir Frederick Hamilton carried sword and torch through the northern counties, there stood beside the dark water of Lough Gill an abbey of the White Friars, whose bell had sounded over wood and mountain for more generations than any man could number. The people called it the House of Saint Berach; but the stones of it are now hidden beneath thorn and elder, and the hollow wherein its altar stood is known as the Burnt Choir.

Hamilton came against it at dawn, with musketeers in buff coats and iron caps, and demanded that the brethren deliver unto him certain Irish captains whom he believed they had concealed. The abbot, being an old man, came forth holding a crucifix of black wood, and his monks followed in their white habits, and he answered that no armed man dwelt beneath that roof, but only servants of Christ and two poor pilgrims who had sought bread.

Then five troopers, who had drunk through the night, cried that the friars lied, and one discharged his musket, and the ball struck a young brother beneath the throat. At once the quiet court became a place of thunder. The soldiers smote the gates with axes, and the monks fled among the cloisters, and white garments reddened upon the stones. Some were slain beside the well, some beneath the image of the Virgin, and some before the altar where the holy candles yet burned in the grey light of morning. Musket smoke rolled beneath the arches, and the wounded called upon saints and angels, and the five who had begun the slaughter laughed and thrust with their swords wherever a white hood moved.

When no living friar remained save the abbot, those five dragged brushwood into the chapel and piled broken benches upon it, and they tore down the embroidered cloths and cast them into the heap. One set fire to the dry ivy at the eastern window, another kindled the vestry, and presently yellow tongues ran along the rafters and red light leaped from pillar to pillar. The shadows of the soldiers, stretched by the flames, climbed the walls and bowed and sprang in a fantastic dance, until the painted saints seemed surrounded by black demons.

The abbot had been wounded twice, yet he rose upon his knees before the altar. Blood ran over his folded hands, and behind him the great crucifix stood amid smoke and fire.

“Woe unto those,” he said, “who have slain the faithful in their sanctuary and given holy things unto the flame. The earth shall refuse their tread, and the waters shall deny them passage. They shall wander among ungovernable shadows and follow ungovernable fires. The dead shall show them their own faces, and music not made for mortal dancing shall lead them unto judgment.”

The oldest of the five troopers lifted his sword.

“Your saints cannot save you,” said he.

“The saints have no need to save me,” answered the abbot. “Save thou thyself, if thy sword be strong enough.”

The trooper struck him down, and at that moment the burning roof fell over the altar, scattering the holy candles and sending a tower of flame into the morning sky.

Before noon word came to Hamilton that two Irish messengers had escaped from the district and were riding toward the western passes to seek aid. Being angered, he called for the five who had fired first upon the friars, for they were reputed bold riders and without mercy, and commanded them to overtake the men before night. They mounted willingly, boasting that they would return by moonrise, and one spoke of the wine awaiting him, and another of a sweetheart at Manorhamilton, while a third, whose wife was lately buried, said that no woman living or dead had power to keep him from his cup.

They entered the woods while a red glow yet stained the sky behind them, for the abbey continued burning, and its smoke lay across the country like a mourning veil. The road was at first broad and firm, but gradually the oaks pressed closer, and wet branches struck their steel caps, and roots rose beneath the mud like knotted hands. Clouds covered the moon; then the moon broke forth again, white and cold, and its light danced among the trees in such manner that every trunk appeared to move and every shadow to pursue them.

Presently the youngest trooper saw something gliding beside the path. It was longer than a hound and pale as silver, and upon its raised head shone a little crown.

“Look there!” he cried.

All five beheld it pass without stirring leaf or grass, and when their horses snorted and reared, the crowned worm turned eyes as red as altar coals upon them.

“A device of Satan,” said the oldest. “He takes the serpent’s shape because it is written that his head shall be bruised.”

Yet while he spoke, his sword hand trembled, and two of the others almost made the holy sign upon their breasts, but remembered their doctrine and clenched their fists instead.

They rode onward, though none boasted now. The road divided where no division had been before, and they chose the wider track, but it ended at a pool dark with reeds. They returned and chose the other, and after an hour came again to the same pool. They tried again and again, now turning north and now west, but the trees altered their places behind them, and the moon shifted from shoulder to shoulder, and still the black water waited.

Then they heard a splashing beyond the reeds, slow and regular, as though a woman beat linen upon a stone.

They found her at the farther edge of the pool, an ancient woman with hair hanging grey to her knees. She washed a soldier’s coat in the water, and though the pool was black, the water streaming from the cloth was red. Beside her lay five naked corpses.

The first corpse bore the face of the youngest trooper. The second had the oldest man’s beard. The third wore about its throat the little chain which one of them had received from his sweetheart. The fourth had mud upon its boots and a broken wine cup in its hand. The fifth, though swollen and pale, smiled like the man whose wife was dead.

The washer lifted one body after another, saying, “This one is washed, and this one is washed, and this one waits but a little.”

The troopers shouted and drew their pistols. At the report she vanished, and the smoke spread low upon the pool; but through the smoke came laughter, and the splashing continued, louder and louder, though no living shape remained.

They fled until the trees opened before a river swollen with rain. The ford should have been shallow, yet the water rushed there deep and furious, and pale hands seemed to turn beneath its foam. The oldest drove his horse forward, but the beast planted its feet and would not enter. He beat it with the flat of his sword until blood shone upon its flank, and still it refused.

“The earth refuses our tread, and the waters deny us passage,” whispered the youngest.

“Speak no friar’s nonsense,” said the oldest. “Would you fear a dead old man?”

“I fear what knew our faces.”

They followed the river in search of another crossing, and for a time the treetops moaned above them like men dying far away. At length they perceived a red spark among the alders, and beside it sat a piper dressed in a ragged green coat. His hair was red, his cap was red, and his face was so brown and wrinkled that it might have been cut from bog oak. A small torch burned at his feet, though rain fell upon it, and beneath his arm he held black pipes bound with silver.

“Old man,” called the leader, “show us the road across the river and over the mountain.”

The piper looked up and smiled.

“There are many roads over the mountain,” said he, “but few that come down.”

“We seek two Irish riders.”

“I have seen two riders.”

“Then guide us, or I shall hang you from this tree.”

“You burned the tree on which I was hanged long ago,” said the piper; and he laughed softly.

The troopers looked at one another, but the oldest set his pistol against the stranger’s brow.

“Lead on.”

The piper rose, tucked the pipes beneath his arm, and took up the torch. At once they discovered stepping-stones where moments before there had been only deep water. The stranger crossed lightly, and the five followed, their horses shaking so violently that steel and bridle rang together.

Beyond the river the path climbed. The woods became thin, and black rocks appeared among the heather, and the wind smelled of frost and smoke. Sometimes the piper walked far ahead, his torch no larger than a firefly, and sometimes he was suddenly beside the last horse, though none had seen him return. Whenever the troopers questioned him, he pointed upward and said, “They are before us. They are before us.”

The youngest looked toward the valley and saw, through a rent in the clouds, the burning abbey many miles away. Its flames seemed no larger than five red candles. Then the clouds closed, and the darkness was complete save for the piper’s torch, which bent beneath the wind but would not go out.

“Why do you carry fire in rain?” asked the trooper whose sweetheart waited.

“To light men home.”

“And where is your home?”

“My wife keeps it,” said the piper. “You saw her by the pool. She was washing! She was washing!”

He gave a shrill cry, and the horses started forward. The men cursed him and threatened him, but he only laughed, and from the valley there answered the beating of wet cloth upon stone.

They had now reached the bare mountain, which is called Benbo, and the slope grew more and more severe. Above them the clouds broke suddenly, revealing a multitude of stars. They hung low and white over the summit, and to the guilty men they resembled the holy candles that had stood upon the friars’ altar before the soldiers cast them down.

“There is the chapel,” murmured one.

“There is no chapel here,” said another.

“I see the brothers in white.”

Those were only stones touched by moonlight, yet they appeared to kneel in long ranks along the ridge. The piper’s torch flared red, and its flame divided into many flames which ran over the heather without consuming it. They danced beside the riders, now before and now behind, and every horse rolled its eyes and foamed at the mouth.

“Ungovernable fires,” said the youngest.

Then the oldest struck him across the face.

“We are Christian soldiers,” he cried, though his voice broke. “No popish curse can harm us, nor any phantom of the Sidhe. Steel is steel, and rock is rock, and the devil is a liar.”

The piper turned upon them, walking backward without stumbling, and his eyes shone yellow.

“The messengers are escaping,” he said. “Ride, brave soldiers! Ride, or Hamilton will hang you all!”

He put the pipes to his mouth.

The first note was low, like wind entering a grave; the second was the cry of a woman over a slain son; and then there burst forth such a wild measure that the rocks seemed to leap and the stars to whirl. The horses sprang into a gallop. The troopers dragged at the reins, but the leather stiffened in their hands as though held by other hands stronger than theirs. They shouted, they prayed, they cursed, and they called upon God, upon Hamilton, upon their mothers and wives; but faster and faster the horses ran.

The slope rose beneath them, and the moon-fires streamed at their sides. Branches no longer barred the way, for there were no trees; yet unseen things struck their helmets, and unseen riders thundered behind. The piper ran before them, though no mortal feet could have matched that speed, and his red cap burned like a coal upon the wind.

“Turn us!” screamed the youngest. “For Christ’s wounds, turn us!”

“Straight onward!” cried the piper. “The two riders are before you!”

They reached the summit. For one moment the world opened beneath them: the silver lake, the black woods, the distant burning abbey, and the pale road winding harmlessly far below. Before them there was no path, but only the great western precipice and the abyss that is now called the Five Troopers’ Fall.

The oldest man saw it first, and at last he made the sign of the cross.

Then the piper played a note sharp as judgment, the five horses bounded together into the empty air, and five screams mingled with the music. There followed a dull crash from the darkness below, and after it silence.

At dawn the two Irish messengers crossed the mountain by another road and met no enemy. Shepherds later found five broken bodies in the ravine, each washed clean by a stream that had not flowed there the day before; but neither horses nor weapons were ever discovered.

And for many years, when red light shone over the ruins of the White Friars and the moon made moving fires among the trees, men heard pipes upon Benbo, and hoofbeats climbed where no road was, and a woman beside the black pool beat bloody garments upon a stone. The people then closed their doors and quenched their candles, for they knew that the five were wandering still among ungovernable shadows, and following unto the end of time the ungovernable fires.

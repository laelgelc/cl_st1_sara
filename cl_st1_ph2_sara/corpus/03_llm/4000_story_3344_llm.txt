## THE TWO TREASURES

### SCENE FIRST

“Contentment is better than wealth, William.”

Thus spoke Robert Gray, as he stood beneath the ancient elm which had shaded their father’s door before either brother had learned to pronounce the sacred name of home. Evening was descending upon the valley. The windows of the low, brown homestead glowed with the last beams of the sun; the cattle moved slowly through the meadow; and from the garden came the mingled fragrance of roses and newly turned earth.

William rested one hand upon his travelling trunk. He was the younger by two years, handsome in person, ardent in temper, and ambitious beyond the narrow bourne of prudence.

“Contentment!” he repeated, with a smile in which affection struggled with contempt. “A proverb for drones, Robert. Contentment may keep a man poor with a cheerful face; but wealth gives him power, honour, and the homage of the world.”

“Or makes him their slave,” answered Robert. “You possess health, youth, a fair patrimony, and hearts that love you. Why cast away certainties for shadows?”

“Because I was not born to wear out my life upon these few acres. I will return rich, Robert. You shall see my carriage upon the hill; you shall hear men speak my name with respect.”

Robert shook his head.

“I would rather see your face at my table, and hear children call you uncle.”

At this William laughed; yet his laugh was not free from pain, for across the little orchard, beside the white gate, stood Ellen May. Pale was her face, and motionless her slender figure. She had not come to persuade him; that task had been attempted, and attempted in vain. She had come only to say farewell.

William approached her.

“Ellen, why do you look upon me thus? Ten years hence you will bless the resolution which makes me leave you now.”

“Ten years!” she murmured. “They are easily spoken.”

“They will quickly pass.”

“To the prosperous, perhaps.”

“Do you doubt me?”

“I doubt the world, William. It promises with a smiling face, and pays with an empty hand.”

He took hers.

“When I have gained fortune, I will return and claim you.”

“And if fortune refuses to be gained?”

He turned impatiently away.

“You speak like Robert. Must every one preach contentment to me?”

“No,” replied Ellen, with meek dignity; “go, if your soul commands it. I ask no promise which your heart cannot keep. But remember, should the world grow cold, the old home will still have a hearth; and there will be one heart, at least, which will pray for you.”

Poor maiden! Her tears fell upon the hand he had placed within her own; but Ambition stood between them, dazzling his eyes with golden attractions.

The coach horn sounded from the road.

“Farewell, Robert!”

“Farewell, brother. Prosper, if prosperity may leave you good; but if adversity teach you truth, do not despise the teacher.”

“Farewell, Ellen!”

She attempted to speak, but could only whisper his name.

He sprang into the coach. The wheels rolled rapidly onward; the old elm vanished behind the turn; the church spire sank beneath the hill. There—his face is toward the restless world, and his back upon peace!

And Robert?

He remained. He tilled the fields which his father had tilled; repaired the mossy roof; planted young trees beside the old; and, in due season, married gentle Mary Lane, whose wealth was a cheerful spirit and whose jewels were affectionate thoughts. Children gathered about their board. Winter found them beside a bright hearth; summer beneath the elm. They had sickness, for none are exempt; losses, for earth contains no perfect paradise; but love divided sorrow and doubled joy. Their wants were few, their gratitude abundant.

Men sometimes called Robert unambitious. Ah! they knew not that he had reached the highest summit—peace of soul—and looked down with pity upon those still climbing amid clouds and precipices.

Meanwhile William sought the glittering prize.

Year followed year. Hope ran before him, beckoning; disappointment followed, scourging. He entered trade, and a dishonest partner ruined him. He speculated in distant lands, and the ship which bore his venture went down beneath a midnight sea. He sought office, but another possessed stronger influence. Yet each defeat, instead of curing his fever, increased it. Ambition is a fiery draught: the more deeply a man drinks, the more fiercely he thirsts.

Ten years passed, and no letter reached the homestead.

Robert still spoke kindly of his brother. Ellen still listened whenever wheels sounded upon the road. But the wheels rolled onward, and the traveller was never William.

### SCENE SECOND

Behold him now in a gilded mansion!

Has he won the prize? Is happiness seated beside him? Look again.

The room is spacious; costly pictures cover the walls; silver glitters upon the table. But gilded bars may make a prison, and luxury cannot warm an unloved heart. William had married Augusta Bellmont, the only daughter of a wealthy merchant. She possessed beauty, accomplishments, and pride; he brought youth, polished manners, and concealed poverty. Each deceived the other. She believed him rising toward distinction; he believed her fortune already secure. Their vows, uttered before Heaven, were bargains secretly made with mammon.

Ellen’s image had arisen before him on the eve of that marriage.

“Too late!” he cried. “Too late! Poverty has no right to love.”

False heart! Poverty had not betrayed Ellen—ambition had betrayed her.

Augusta soon discovered the motive which he vainly endeavoured to hide. From that hour contempt poisoned every word between them. She reproached him with dependence; he answered with bitterness. Her father, Mr. Bellmont, watched him with cold, suspicious eyes.

“You married my daughter for my money,” said the old man one day.

William started.

“That is an accusation which no gentleman should endure.”

“And no fortune hunter can refute.”

“Sir!”

“You shall have shelter while Augusta lives. Beyond that, expect nothing. My property remains mine, and no smooth-tongued adventurer shall count the days until it becomes his.”

William quitted the room with rage in his bosom; but conscience followed him, repeating the accusation more terribly than Bellmont had spoken it.

For three miserable years the marriage endured. Then illness entered the mansion. Time, with invisible finger, wrote Death upon Augusta’s forehead. Pride left her as strength declined; and the woman who had lived without tenderness stretched forth her hands for it when all earthly vanities were fading.

“William,” she said, on the last night, “did you ever love me?”

He stood beside her couch, unable to answer.

“Not even now?”

“I pity you, Augusta. I forgive all.”

“Forgive!” she repeated. “Then there was something to forgive. And you—have you nothing for which forgiveness is needed?”

He bowed his head.

“Yes.”

“Was there another?”

A long silence followed.

“There was.”

“And she loved you?”

“Better than I deserved.”

The dying woman turned her face toward the wall.

“Then we have both been punished. I purchased admiration, and you sold love. Neither received the thing desired.”

Ere morning, she was dead.

Poor stricken heart! Whatever her errors, that pale form had passed beyond reproach. William wept beside it—not with the tears of a husband mourning beloved companionship, but with the bitter grief of one who beholds in death the solemn result of wasted life.

After the funeral, Mr. Bellmont summoned him.

“You will leave this house to-day.”

“Surely,” said William, “Augusta made some provision—”

“She made none.”

“But as her husband—”

“She possessed only what I bestowed upon her, and what I bestowed has returned to me.”

“Am I to be cast forth penniless?”

“You came to us nearly so. You depart precisely as you came.”

William’s face burned.

“You triumph over me!”

“No. I protect my own from one who would have converted affection into gold. Go! If you have talents, use them; if honour, preserve it—if any yet remains.”

The door closed. Thus vanished the mansion, the silver, the homage, the dream! Poor, humiliated, broken in spirit, William stood once more amid the streets with scarcely enough money to purchase bread.

Yet even then he did not immediately turn homeward. Pride, that stern gaoler, held him back. How could he meet Robert’s tranquil eyes? How speak Ellen’s name? Had she married? Did children gather at her knee while she taught them to forget the faithless wanderer?

Again he struggled. He accepted mean employment, lost it, starved, and struggled anew. One winter night, after two days without food, he entered the counting-room of a merchant who had once employed him. The place was vacant. Upon the desk lay a purse; beside it, an open ledger. A few figures altered, a purse removed—and hunger would be silenced.

His hand advanced.

Then, from the gulf of memory, arose the old homestead: his mother’s Bible; Robert beneath the elm; Ellen at the gate, saying, “Should the world grow cold, the old home will still have a hearth.”

His fingers touched the purse.

“Take it,” whispered Despair. “The world has cheated you.”

“Leave it,” answered Conscience. “Will you complete poverty with crime?”

He snatched back his hand as though burned.

“No!” he cried. “I have sacrificed love to wealth; I will not sacrifice my soul also.”

He fled into the snowy street. The tempest smote his face, but within there had come a sudden calm. Ambition was dead. At last—after failure, perfidy, humiliation, bereavement, and temptation—Robert’s simple words shone forth as verity:

“Contentment is better than wealth.”

The prodigal turned toward home.

### SCENE THIRD

It was autumn when a solitary traveller ascended the familiar hill. His garments were worn; silver mingled with his hair; his step, once proud and elastic, was slow and uncertain.

Below him lay the valley. The river flashed through yellow reeds. Smoke curled from the homestead chimney. The old elm had lost a mighty branch, but still stretched its sheltering arms above the roof.

William paused.

Would the doors indeed remain open? Ten silent years and more had passed. Affection, neglected, may perish; memory itself may grow weary.

Children’s voices reached him. A little girl ran through the gate, pursued by two laughing boys. Behind them came Robert, broader of frame, browned by labour, peace written upon his countenance.

William drew back; but the child had seen him.

“Father, there is a stranger!”

Robert looked toward the road. For one moment he stood motionless. Then he came quickly forward.

“William!”

The wanderer’s head sank upon his breast.

“Robert, if you can forgive—”

But the sentence was never finished. Robert’s arms were around him.

“My brother! My brother has come home!”

No reproach, no recital of warnings fulfilled, no proud display of superior wisdom! True affection keeps no ledger wherein repentance must balance sorrow.

Mary welcomed him as a sister; the children gazed with wondering tenderness upon the uncle whose name had long been familiar to them. William crossed the threshold and saw the same clock, the same broad fireplace, the same worn Bible upon its shelf. He covered his face.

“I went forth,” he said, “believing this place poor. I return knowing it to be richer than palaces.”

A light step sounded in the passage.

“And Ellen?” asks the reader. Has she forgotten? Has some worthier man received the hand which William cast away?

The door opened. A woman entered, bearing flowers for the supper table. Time had softened the brightness of youth into a graver beauty, but William knew her before she raised her eyes.

The flowers fell.

“William!”

“Ellen!”

She trembled and grasped the chair beside her. He approached, then stopped, for shame held him as with an iron chain.

“I have no right to come near you,” he said. “I broke faith without one word. I sold the remembrance of your love for a promise of gold, and the gold became ashes. Ellen, I ask not that you should restore what I destroyed. Tell me only that you forgive me.”

Her eyes filled.

“I forgave you long ago.”

“And you are—happy?”

“I have been useful, I hope. Robert’s children have loved me.”

“But no husband—?”

“No.”

He uttered a cry in which remorse and joy were strangely mingled.

“You waited?”

“I trusted that you would one day remember the road home.”

Then did the proud man kneel—not in worship of beauty, as in former days, but in repentance before faithful love.

“I return poor.”

“You return William.”

“Dishonoured.”

“Not if sorrow has made you true.”

“Broken.”

“Then home must make you whole.”

He rose and clasped her to his heart. Robert turned away to conceal his tears; Mary gathered the wondering children close. Outside, the autumn wind moved through the elm; within, the hearth sent forth its cheerful light, touching alike the faces of the steadfast and the restored.

And here we leave them, reader. William never possessed the carriage of which he had boasted, nor did men bow before his name. He worked beside his brother; he married Ellen; he learned to receive daily bread with gratitude and nightly rest with peace. His scars remained, for repentance cannot make the past unborn; but they guarded him against the old temptation.

Two treasures are offered to mankind. One glitters afar, ever-attractive and ever-fading; its apples are fair to sight, but ashes within. The other lies near—duty, affection, a peaceful conscience, and hearts united around a humble hearth. William rejected the second and pursued the first; through misery he learned their value.

May you learn it more gently! For wealth without contentment is poverty wearing jewels; but contentment, enriched by love, makes even a cottage a palace.

## The White Sea Below

I had gone up to Mount Saint Helena, not in pursuit of solitude, though I found it there in a measure sufficiently austere, nor from any particular affection for rocks, rattlesnakes, and ruined mining works, but upon the plain advice of my lungs. These, being delicate instruments and somewhat ill-tuned, had long protested against the coast, where the Pacific fogs came ashore nightly like an army of cold ghosts, entered by every cranny, and sat upon my breast until morning. At Silverado the air was commonly dry, aromatic with pine and heated stone, and swept clean each afternoon by a wind of our own—a brisk domestic tempest which shook the boards, worried the chimney, and went sighing off among the ravines as though disappointed that the house had survived it.

Thus I lived high above the vineyards and warm roads of Napa, lodged among the scars of an abandoned enterprise. The miners had departed; their tunnels remained, their dump-heaps glittered deceitfully in the sun, and here and there some gaunt piece of timber stood up from the chaparral like the gallows of a forgotten kingdom. Our dwelling, such as it was, occupied a shelf of the mountain. Behind us the summit rose in stony stages; before us the country descended by ridge, forest, and tawny hollow to the broad valley, where farms, towns, and avenues of trees lay arranged with the pleasant exactness of a map.

In clear weather I could follow the roads for miles. Calistoga sat among its greenery; white houses shone and disappeared as the sun altered; the vineyards made squares of dull and vivid green; and beyond all, enclosing the inhabited plain, the hills stood one behind another in colours that passed from brown through purple into blue. It was a prospect to encourage long thoughts, and perhaps to shorten them also; for the mere act of looking became occupation enough, and a man might forget his philosophy while counting shadows on the opposite slopes.

One Sunday morning, before the sun had fairly entered the room, I woke with an impression that the world outside had undergone some noiseless calamity. There was no sound to justify the fancy. The boards were still; no branch scraped the roof; no bird called from the neighbouring pines. Yet the light upon the wall was strange—not dim, but purified and cold, as though reflected from snow.

I rose, made my toilet in two or three heroic movements, and went out.

Napa Valley had vanished.

From immediately beneath our station to the farther mountains there extended a level whiteness, immaculate, shining, and apparently without motion. The nearer ridges descended into it and were cut short as cleanly as islands at high tide. Farther off, detached summits rose darkly from the surface; one sharp peak appeared to float unsupported, while a low range, severed from all earthly connexion, lay like a long cloud upon a cloud. Above was a sky of dazzling blue, already warming toward gold in the east; below was this second heaven, pale and opaline, on which no sail moved and no sun glittered. The valley, with its vineyards, fences, roofs, dusty roads, and waking inhabitants, was not merely concealed: it had been abolished.

I knew well enough what I beheld. It was fog, no more; the same maritime vapour from which I had fled, travelling inland during the night and filling the lower country. Reason made the explanation promptly, and stood satisfied with its little triumph. But the eye rejected so meagre a report. To the eye it was an ocean—a sea mysteriously lifted from its proper bed, poured among the mountains, and arrested there in supernatural calm.

The deception was the more complete because of the silence. Had those white reaches been water, waves must have broken along every wooded cape; a surf must have thundered in the ravines; and the narrow straits between the hills should have roared with tide. Yet not the least murmur ascended. A jay, flashing blue across the foreground, uttered one harsh syllable, and his cry seemed not to disturb but rather to establish the quiet. He flew out over the apparent gulf, diminished, and was lost against a projecting ridge; and I was absurdly relieved to see him gain the farther shore.

At first the fog seemed to be withdrawing. A dark line showed beneath one islanded hill; then a row of treetops pricked through the white, each surrounded by a little stain of grey. I congratulated the sun on so early a victory and went indoors, where the necessities of breakfast, though humbler than meteorology, made the stronger claim. Through the window I continued to glance at the spectacle. The upper surface shone more brightly; bars of rose and pale amber travelled across it; and now and again some submerged promontory disclosed a shoulder, as if rising refreshed from the bath.

When I returned outside, perhaps half an hour later, the dark line had disappeared. The treetops were drowned anew. An isolated knoll which had stood clear at sunrise was reduced to a narrow reef, then to one black tuft, and then to nothing.

The sea was rising.

There was no mistaking the fact. The apparent surface, so level and tranquil when regarded broadly, had advanced upon every slope. It entered the gullies first, sending long white arms up channels where winter torrents ran; it mounted among the woods; it detached grove from grove and ridge from ridge. A road visible a little before, winding yellow upon the opposite hillside, ended suddenly at the whiteness, reappeared above it, and was again cut off. While I watched, the lower portion vanished; presently the middle also; and at last only one pale thread remained close beneath the crest, like a path along the rampart of a besieged town.

This was no retreating vapour under sentence of the sun. It was an invasion.

The oceanic image, which until then had lain smooth and spacious in my fancy, took on violence without sacrificing silence. The fog poured through a notch in the hills as water pours over a weir, except that the cataract gave no voice. It toppled across the crest in slow folds, divided round the pine trunks, reunited beneath them, and descended on our side in torn streamers. Elsewhere a whole white mass heaved itself above a ridge and slid downward, deracinating nothing, bending no branch, yet swallowing forest after forest with a thoroughness more dreadful than destruction. Fire would have crackled; a flood would have smitten the rocks and carried trees before it; this pale conqueror made no proclamation and left no ruin to mark its path. It simply removed the country.

The sight delighted me. I must confess so much, although the enemy then advancing was one to whom I owed no hospitality. Here was the very fog whose wet fingers had searched my chest upon the coast and found every weakness; here, transfigured by sunlight and distance, was my old persecutor become magnificent. Beauty is a notorious accomplice of danger. I watched with the admiration of a mouse for the measured approach of a handsome cat.

Presently a current of air climbed from below. It was cold enough to seem a material thing, and it bore a smell unlike the usual dry fragrance of the mountain. There was in it damp cloth, wet stone, and something sharp and saline—the breath of distant beaches. The Pacific had sent its visiting-card.

I coughed.

One cough invited another, and the second was followed by that narrow, metallic sensation in the breast which I had climbed three thousand feet to avoid. Prudence, who had been silent while the landscape performed for me, now spoke with the plain accent of a physician. The fog was no longer a picture. The rarefied sea had reached out of its basin and touched me.

Still, I did not immediately retire indoors. A wall of boards, loosely joined and variously perforated, promised little defence if the mist should climb to Silverado; moreover, the summitward trail lay open behind us, and there are occasions when safety gains charm by masquerading as adventure. I therefore resolved to ascend. This was retreat in the military sense, but in my own account it became exploration. A child who flies from an imaginary bear knows very well that the bear is imaginary, yet runs with genuine ardour; and I, who understood the harmless chemistry of condensation, fled uphill from a deluge.

The path rose steeply among manzanita, pines, and pale masses of volcanic rock. In places it narrowed to a ledge above a ravine; in others it lost itself among stones and had to be recovered by guesswork. The sun already struck hot upon my shoulders, while from below came that cold exhalation, so that I seemed divided between two climates. A lizard lay upon a rock, flattened in luxury, and watched my passage with an expression of ancient disapproval. Nothing else moved.

At each opening in the trees I turned to look. The spectacle enlarged as I climbed, but the habitable world diminished. Hill after hill had gone down. What had been a varied country of folds, valleys, and settlements was now one universal field of white, out of which the highest points emerged at vast and deceptive distances. Their familiar proportions were destroyed. A hill which yesterday had seemed a near and inconsiderable neighbour now stood as a lonely island worthy of a name, a history, and perhaps a race of kings. The farther ranges made continents; the nearer spurs became capes; and Mount Saint Helena, on whose flank I crept like an industrious ant, appeared to be the last considerable land before the end of things.

The rising sun altered the colour but did not check the advance. Along the southern quarter the vapour shone like pearl; toward the west it was a colder white, touched here and there with blue; beneath the sun it flashed so brilliantly that the eye could scarcely endure it. Shadows of clouds—true clouds, far above their earthbound brethren—passed over the surface in broad violet stains. For a few seconds one such shadow disclosed the movement of the fog, and I saw that the supposed sea was running in enormous currents, curling round promontories and streaming northward through the valley.

Then the shadow passed, and all became deceptively still again.

There is something tyrannical in a level surface. A forest offers innumerable particulars: trunk and bough, opening and recess, bird, leaf, and changing light. A town relieves the gaze with roofs, chimneys, gardens, and human purposes. But this whiteness denied variety; it reduced a hundred square miles to one expression. The eye travelled over it and found no foothold. I began to understand, not the terror of a flood—for nothing before me could wet a shoe—but the imaginative oppression of the Deluge, when every known landmark had been covered and the survivors looked abroad for one green sign of the familiar earth.

Had an ark appeared below me, painted perhaps a practical Californian red and carrying two specimens of every local politician, I believe I should scarcely have been astonished.

From a higher shoulder I could look back upon Silverado. Our roofs lay beneath the summit of a crooked spur which projected from the mountain and divided two ravines. That spur, insignificant in ordinary weather, was now a breakwater. On either side the vapour had climbed high; in one ravine it surged within a few hundred feet of the settlement, while in the other it pressed against the wooded slope and sent thin scouts among the trunks. Yet the ridge itself held clear, and our little habitation remained in a pocket of sunlight, protected less by architecture than by geography.

I sat upon a warm rock to observe the siege.

Below the spur the fog made repeated advances. A white tongue would lengthen through the lower pines, hesitate, fray into separate wisps, and dissolve; then a denser body followed, occupying the ground which the first had reconnoitred. The process possessed an almost military intelligence. Ravines served it for covered approaches; saddles in the ridge were assaulted; every depression was tried. Where the sun struck full upon it, the foremost ranks perished, but the host behind supplied them without haste and apparently without limit.

Meanwhile the valley beneath remained wholly submerged. Not a house, barn, field, or road could be seen. I thought of the people down there going about their Sunday occupations under that low grey roof. To them the morning would be no more than chilly and obscure. A farmer might complain of damp hay; a woman might shut a window; a child, sent to fetch wood, might draw figures in the moisture on a gate. None could behold the prodigy of which he formed a part. They were inhabitants of a drowned country and did not know themselves drowned.

This is one privilege of mountains: they reveal arrangements hidden from the plain. The dweller below experiences weather as an immediate inconvenience—rain on his hat, dust in his eyes, fog at his door; but he who looks from above sees armies, oceans, the circulation of vast invisible powers. The inconvenience remains, particularly if his lungs are poor, yet it is admitted into a grander society.

By ten o’clock the mist had reached the lower edge of the spur. A group of tall pines, formerly clear from root to crown, stood knee-deep in it. Soon they were waist-deep; then only their upper branches showed, agitated by a wind unfelt at my station. They resembled drowning men who lift their arms above the flood. One after another the lesser trees disappeared, while the tallest endured for a time in melancholy isolation. At last the vapour closed over them, and the smooth brightness was restored.

Still it rose.

I climbed again, though slowly now, for excitement is a bad steward of breath. The trail entered a region of broken stone where the heat came fiercely from the ground. I paused often, ostensibly to admire the view, and in truth because my heart and lungs demanded negotiation. Each pause showed some new conquest. A round hill to the east became first a crescent, then a black button upon the white, and finally ceased to exist. Two distant summits, long separated by a low ridge, were joined into one island when the ridge went under. A saddle near us filled from the farther side until the vapour spilled across in soft, heavy coils, as though some celestial washerwoman had emptied suds over the mountain.

With the spill came a stronger wind. It rushed upward through the brush with a strident sighing, cold at the core despite the sun. The manzanita leaves turned their lighter faces; dust ran in little eddies across the stones; and a hawk, caught while sailing above the ravine, tilted sharply and was borne away. The fog itself, though urged by the same current, appeared ponderous. It rolled rather than flew, clinging to hollows, wrapping boles and boulders, and leaving behind detached shreds that faded in the heated air.

My cough returned, and this time it brought a warning pain. I fastened my coat, though the sun was warm, and moved farther uphill. It amused me that so vast an enemy should be hunting so small a fugitive. All Napa lay in its possession; forests had submitted; roads, farms, and villages were whelmed; yet the campaign seemed incomplete until it had discovered one thin invalid among the rocks. I had become its Jonah, and Mount Saint Helena the ship on which I vainly sought concealment.

Near midday I gained a lookout from which the whole defensive line above Silverado could be examined. The natural barrier had grown narrow. Fog filled both ravines to an equal height and pressed against either side of the spur; only the crest remained, a green and golden strip with our buildings perched upon it like toys. For a moment the scene held. The air at my station was brilliant; insects hummed about a flowering shrub; the rock under my hand was almost too hot to touch. Yet scarcely two hundred feet below, winter and the sea waited together.

Then the mist crossed.

It came at first by a shallow notch, a mere overflow, thin enough for the trees to show through it. But the stream deepened quickly. White vapour poured over the crest, descended on the protected side, and spread along the slope. Another breach opened nearer the settlement; then a third. The breakwater had been topped. Silverado stood upon an island no longer, but upon a reef which the next hour must cover.

For all my imaginative pleasure, I felt genuine apprehension then. Our refuge was primitive; the upper workings offered no wholesome lodging; and a night in saturated air might exact a heavier payment than the spectacle deserved. I considered the remaining trail, the distance to a dry camp, the food we could carry, and all those practical matters which enter with muddy boots when poetry has kept the house too long. There was nothing heroic in the calculation. A weak chest is an excellent schoolmaster of humility.

The fog continued across the spur. It touched the lower roofs. A pine beside the house faded from the trunk upward until its crown floated alone; the dump-heaps lost their sharp outlines; and the road by which we had come to the mountain was erased. The siege was nearly done.

Then, without trumpet or visible signal, the wind changed.

A breeze struck my cheek from the north—dry, warm, and scented with resin. The manzanita leaves swung back. The fog at the crest paused; its flowing edge trembled; and the stream which had poured over the notch began to thin. For some minutes the forces appeared balanced. White fragments advanced among the trees, recoiled, advanced once more, and spun upward in tatters. Sunlight fell through them in long bars, turning their edges to silver and their inner folds to pearl.

The northern breeze freshened.

At once the whole vast fabric showed its frailty. The smooth sea broke into provinces, channels, and drifting islands. Holes opened in it, at first no larger than ponds, through which the dark green of the submerged forest appeared at an immeasurable depth. These widened rapidly. A ridge rose entire from the whiteness, streaming vapour from its trees; a road flashed out upon a slope; the top of a barn emerged, disappeared under one last gust of mist, and emerged again.

The retreat, once begun, became general. Vapour drew back from the spur in long banners. It lifted out of the ravines, uncovering pine after pine from the crown downward, so that the drowned forest was restored in reverse order. Across the valley the currents turned southward. What had advanced with the deliberation of an empire now fled like a routed host, abandoning hill, pass, and woodland. Detached companies lingered in hollows, but the main body streamed away, torn by sunlight and harried by the dry wind.

The silence was broken at last—not by the fog, which remained mute in defeat as in victory, but by the returning world. Birds called from the recovered trees. Somewhere below, an axe rang. The afternoon gale began its accustomed business among the ridges, and from Silverado came the faint clap of a loosened board. These small sounds had an extraordinary cheerfulness. They were the voices of the earth after enchantment.

Within an hour the transformation was nearly complete. Napa Valley returned piece by piece: first the upper vineyards, then the roads and clustered houses, then the long floor of the valley itself, washed green and bright beneath the declining vapour. Calistoga reappeared with an air of innocence, as though it had never been submerged. The hills resumed their proper sizes; islands became knolls; continents dwindled into familiar ridges. The world, having been mythological all morning, settled again into geography.

Only in the south did the defeated sea retain its grandeur. There it travelled in enormous white masses, filling the lower passes and trailing streamers along the hills. Sunlight struck upon its retreating surface and kindled reaches of gold and rose; above it the sky deepened into a triumphant blue. Presently the mountains closed behind it, and what remained looked no more formidable than a bank of cloud.

I descended toward Silverado in the afternoon. The path, which during my ascent had seemed an escape route from universal inundation, was once again a dusty mountain trail, troublesome with loose stones and decorated by lizards. At the spur I passed through patches of cold air where the fog had lain. Drops trembled on the pine needles; spider-webs shone among the bushes; and the soil gave forth a dark, damp smell uncommon at that season. These were the high-water marks of our aerial flood.

Our house had escaped. The roofs were wet, the windows clouded, and the boards chilled to the touch; but already the private gale was at work, blowing through every seam with such zeal that before evening we had reason to desire a little of the fog’s protection. Thus do our enemies exchange masks.

At sunset I looked down once more. Small remnants of mist had collected in the deepest hollows. They lay there meekly, no broader than ponds, and received the red light without menace. It was difficult to believe that these quiet scraps belonged to the power which had erased a valley, drowned forests, stormed the mountain passes, and driven me upward among the rocks. Yet the salt remained upon my lips, and the soreness in my chest supplied a less poetic testimony.

During the following weeks other fogs came inland. I saw them from afar, white companies moving under the coast hills, and sometimes at dawn a thin lake occupied the lower fields. Once a narrow streamer climbed a ravine and vanished before noon; once the valley towns awoke beneath a grey ceiling while our heights stood clear in sunshine. But none mounted to our refuge. The season had spent its great assault.

So Silverado remained above the sea, though the sea itself was many miles away; and I, who had fled the fog for life and welcomed it for beauty, was left with the memory of that silent Sunday when the familiar earth sank from sight, the mountains became an archipelago, and the Pacific, transformed into air, came searching for me among the heights.

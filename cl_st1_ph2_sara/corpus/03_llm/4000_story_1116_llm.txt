SISTER JOSEPHA’S DOOR

Sister Josepha was tired of trimming lamps.

There were seventeen in the little chapel, and each lamp appeared to possess a separate and most exasperating disposition. One smoked; another sputtered; a third devoured oil with a greed unbefitting a consecrated object; and the smallest, which hung before the image of the Blessed Virgin, would persist in going out at vespers. Then Sister Angélique would whisper, “The wick, ma sœur—the wick!” as though poor Josepha had never heard of wicks.

This evening the rain crept down the brown convent walls and pressed its wet fingers against the windows. The chapel smelled of wax, damp stone and faded roses. From the street came the roll of wheels, the clatter of a mule-car, a burst of laughter quickly lost beneath the solemn tolling of the Cathedral bell.

Josepha stood upon a stool, her small brown hands stained with oil, her black veil drawn severely about a face that had not been made for severity. Her eyes were too large, her mouth too red, and rebellious little curls escaped at the temples in spite of pins, prayers and holy discipline.

“Oh, cher Seigneur!” she murmured, as the lamp smoked anew. “If patience be the price of heaven, I shall never arrive.”

“Sister Josepha!”

It was Sister Angélique’s shocked voice.

“C’est ma très grande faute,” replied Josepha meekly; but there was no meekness in the quick motion with which she snipped the wick.

She had been three years in the convent, and she was eighteen.

At eighteen, three years are not a portion of life; they are an eternity. The days had slipped into weeks, the weeks into months, the months into a gray procession of bells, beads, coarse bread, hard beds, sour coffee, lessons with sleepy children and lamps—always lamps. In summer the garden magnolias leaned over the wall, heavy with perfume, and whispered of the world beyond. In winter the north wind rattled the shutters like an impatient visitor. Josepha listened to both.

Yet she had chosen the convent.

Before Sister Josepha there had been Camille—Camille without another name.

No one knew precisely whence she came. One wet November morning, years ago, a fisherman’s wife had found her sleeping beneath an arch near the river, wrapped in a shawl of yellow silk. The child could say “Camille,” and nothing more. There was no medal upon her neck, no written word, no mark by which charity might trace the vanished mother. Her skin held the warm tint of the Gulf; her hair curled black and fine; her eyes seemed at one moment French, at another Spanish, and at another to inherit some remoter, unspoken ancestry. New Orleans, which asks many questions concerning blood and answers few, shook its head over her.

The Sisters received her.

She grew among the brown corridors and white coifs as a bright weed grows between paving-stones—unexpectedly, stubbornly, with a certain tropical grace. At seven she climbed the fig-tree. At nine she stole sugar from the kitchen and confessed before she was accused. At twelve she sang hymns in a voice so sweet that Père Laurent wept; and afterward she mimicked the squeak of the organ until the younger pupils choked behind their prayer-books.

“Camille this, Camille that!” cried Sister Angélique. “Camille, descend! Camille, be silent! Camille, where are thy shoes?”

Her shoes were generally where Camille was not.

When she was fifteen, a respectable couple from Canal Street offered to adopt her. Madame Varron was pale, soft-spoken and childless; she wore violet ribbons and smelled delicately of orris. Monsieur Varron had rings upon three fingers, a heavy gold chain across his waistcoat and eyes that lingered too long.

“My dear,” said Madame, “you shall have a room with blue curtains.”

“And a piano,” said Monsieur Varron, smiling.

Camille looked at his smile, at the fleshy hand resting upon his knee, at the narrow nails polished like little shells. Some instinct, swift and voiceless, fled through her like a frightened fawn.

“No,” she said.

The Mother Superior drew herself up. “Camille, you do not comprehend the generosity—”

“I comprehend enough, ma mère.”

Madame Varron began to weep. Monsieur Varron ceased smiling.

That night Camille knelt before the Mother Superior and begged to take the veil.

“Child,” said the old woman, examining her over folded hands, “a religious life is not a hiding-place.”

“It is safe here.”

“Safety is not vocation.”

“I love the Blessed Virgin.”

“So do many ladies who wear diamonds.”

“I shall obey.”

At this the Mother Superior’s brows rose a little, for Camille’s obedience had never been her most conspicuous jewel.

But the girl persisted. What was outside? Men with smooth nails; women who wept; a city that knew her face and not her name. Within were walls, rules, bread, a bed, and the familiar white wings of the Sisters’ coifs. Fear sometimes disguises itself so prettily as devotion that wiser eyes than Camille’s have been deceived.

In time they clothed her in black, cut away her curls, placed the veil upon her bowed head and called her Sister Josepha.

She wept throughout the ceremony. Everyone believed the tears holy. Perhaps some were.

For a little while all was beautiful. The bell seemed the voice of God; the rosary beads were pearls of peace; each small duty shone like a star upon the ladder to Paradise. Then the beads became beads, the bell became a bell, and duty—well, duty has an unfortunate habit of looking very much like work when examined every morning.

The Mother Superior observed Josepha’s unrest and prescribed additional prayer. Sister Angélique prescribed humility. Sister Céleste prescribed herb tea. None prescribed youth, sunlight, music, ribbons, or the liberty to walk as far as Canal Street and buy a lemon ice.

Now lemon ices are not commonly numbered among the instruments of salvation. Neither are bonbons, bright parasols and military bands. Yet Providence, which made the rainbow quite unnecessarily beautiful, must surely understand why human creatures turn their heads when colour passes.

The great celebration at the Cathedral came in May.

For days the convent trembled with preparation. Vestments were aired; lace was mended; brass candlesticks were polished until they reflected long, astonished faces. Baskets of roses arrived from gardens below the city. Children practised hymns in thin, earnest voices. Even the chapel lamps behaved better, as if conscious that an Archbishop was expected.

On the morning of High Mass the sky spread blue and stainless above Jackson Square. The trees glittered after an early shower; balconies blossomed with ladies; carriages crowded Chartres Street; bells flung their silver tumult over roofs, courtyards and the broad brown river.

The Sisters went out two by two.

Josepha walked between Sister Angélique and Sister Céleste, with lowered eyes as obedience required. But lowered eyes may see much. They saw the scarlet edge of a carpet, satin slippers, a child’s green sash, the polished wheel of a carriage, orange-peel shining upon the pavement. Perfumes floated past—rose, musk, tobacco, wet earth, coffee, horse and river—all the complicated incense of the city.

At the Cathedral doors she lifted her head.

Ah, the colour of it!

Purple and crimson flowed from the sanctuary; gold burned upon the altar; candles rose in ranks, their flames tremulous with importance. White lace foamed over scarlet cassocks. Flowers crowded the steps—roses smiling, lilies pale with ecstasy. The censers clinked upon their chains, scattering blue clouds which climbed toward the painted roof. The organ, which on common days coughed like an old aunt, opened its mighty throat and pealed.

Josepha forgot to be tired.

She forgot the brown walls, the sour coffee, the lamp that smoked. She drank splendour through her great black eyes. The procession entered: acolytes, priests, dignitaries, officers in bright uniform. Swords flashed; epaulettes glittered; boots struck the pavement with a sound firm, free and worldly.

Then she saw him.

He was young—scarcely more than twenty-three—with a sun-browned face and a straight soldierly figure. His uniform was blue, its buttons gold; one white glove rested upon the hilt of his sword. He should have looked toward the sanctuary. Instead, for the briefest instant, he looked at Sister Josepha.

Perhaps he noticed only her youth beneath the imprisoning veil. Perhaps her startled eyes reminded him of someone far away. Perhaps he thought nothing at all. But his brown eyes softened; compassion passed through them, warm and quick, like sunlight over water.

Then he turned.

That was all.

Briefly, Sister Josepha was in love.

Not with a name, for she did not know it; nor with a character, for he had spoken no word; nor even altogether with the young officer himself. She loved the pity in his eyes, the gold upon his shoulders, the air that seemed to move freely about him. She loved doors opening, streets lengthening, boats departing, music playing under balconies. She loved everything forbidden and called it him.

During the Credo she imagined his voice. During the Sanctus she imagined his hand. When the censer swung, its silver note became the delicate clink of his sword. At the elevation she bowed her head, frightened by her own heart.

“Cher Seigneur, forgive me!” she prayed.

But when she raised her eyes, she sought him again.

After that day the convent altered—not in truth, but truth is often helpless before desire. The walls drew closer. The food grew coarser. The dormitory windows seemed barred, though they were not. Josepha heard the city at all hours: market cries at dawn, bells at noon, laughter at dusk, distant music after nightfall. Each sound called, “Camille!”

She had thought Camille dead.

Camille was only sleeping.

For a week Josepha struggled. She counted rosaries until the beads seemed little black tears beneath her fingers. She scrubbed the refectory tables; she taught catechism; she trimmed every wick with murderous precision. Still the tender, pitying brown eyes followed her.

What if she left?

The thought came first as a whisper, then as a companion, then as a command.

On Sunday the Sisters would attend Mass again. Afterward the crowd in Chartres Street would be dense. She could fall behind at the Cathedral steps, turn beneath the arcade, cross Jackson Square and disappear. Her novice’s cloak, folded in an old chest, might cover the habit. By noon she could be on Canal Street. By evening—anywhere!

Anywhere is a splendid country when viewed through a locked door.

She possessed two small coins, a comb and a medal of Saint Joseph. These seemed sufficient. Surely she might find work. She could sew a little, sing very well, and read both French and English. Some respectable lady would employ her. Perhaps the officer would see her in a shop. He would recognize her eyes. He would say—

Here imagination became generous, as it always does when paying with money not its own.

On Saturday night Josepha lay awake upon her narrow bed. Rain had begun, soft and warm. It whispered in the courtyard fountain and drummed lightly upon the tiles. Around her the Sisters breathed in tranquil rhythm.

She rose, dressed noiselessly and went to the linen-room, where her cloak was hidden. As she reached for the latch, voices sounded in the passage. She drew back behind a cupboard.

Sister Céleste and the Mother Superior entered carrying folded altar-cloths.

“The child is restless,” said Sister Céleste.

“She is eighteen,” replied the Mother Superior. “At eighteen the heart considers rest an insult.”

“Do you fear she may run away?”

There was a pause. Josepha pressed both hands against her breast.

“I fear the world would receive her too gladly.”

“She is clever.”

“Clever? Yes. But what will cleverness answer when they ask her name?”

“Josepha—”

“Is a convent name. Camille—what? She has no surname, no family, no country that will claim her, no father whose hand may be raised in her defence. She does not know whether she is French, Spanish, Creole, or a child cast here from some island ship. The city is kind to beauty when beauty is protected. Alone—”

The Mother Superior sighed.

Sister Céleste lowered her voice. “She is very beautiful.”

“That is the danger.”

The rain filled the silence.

“She thinks the street means liberty,” continued the old woman. “She does not know that a young girl without money may pass from one prison into another before sunset. Who would believe her story? Who would employ her? And if some gentleman offered protection—”

Josepha remembered polished nails, a gold chain, Monsieur Varron’s smile.

“She refused him once,” said Sister Céleste, as though following the same thought.

“She was a child then, and under our roof. Outside, refusal is not always heard.”

The Sisters gathered the linen and went away. Their steps faded down the corridor.

Josepha remained behind the cupboard, holding the old cloak against her face.

Until that moment the unknown had been bright—blue as a soldier’s coat, golden as a button, sweet as lemon ice. Now shadows entered it. She saw herself questioned at a lodging-house: “Camille who?” She saw curious eyes measure her colour, her speech, her ignorance. She saw doors shut; other doors open too eagerly. She saw the officer pass without recognition, his compassionate glance bestowed upon some beggar, some child, some wounded dog.

No surname. No family. No nationality. No friend.

A person may step into the world with empty hands; but it is terrible to step there without even a name.

Josepha returned the cloak to the chest.

She did not pray. There are griefs too small for heaven and too large for speech; they sit beside the heart until morning.

At Mass she moved like one in a dream. The city shone after the rain. Jackson Square glittered; pigeons wheeled about the roofs; vendors cried flowers and pralines; carriages flashed along Chartres Street. Every balcony seemed hung with invitation.

The young officer was not there.

When the service ended, the Sisters formed their quiet procession and crossed the square. At the convent entrance Josepha stopped.

Before her lay the street, bright, noisy, dangerous and free. A mule-cart lumbered past. Two girls in pink ribbons laughed beneath one parasol. Farther away, beyond roofs and spires, a steamboat whistle uttered its long departing cry.

Sister Angélique touched her arm.

“Come, ma sœur.”

Josepha looked once more toward the city. Her lips parted, as if Camille might answer that whistle and fly. But no sound came. Two tears slipped beneath her veil, round and dark as rosary beads.

Then little Sister Josepha bowed her head and followed the others within.

Behind her the heavy door closed; and the sunbeams, left outside, danced laughing upon Chartres Street.

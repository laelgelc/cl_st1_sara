The August sun had already whitened the road when Madame de Cadour came down the steps of the hotel at Fécamp. Her husband, seated beneath the awning with a newspaper spread over his stomach, watched her fasten the ribbons of her bonnet.

“You are really going?” he asked.

“Certainly.”

“In this heat? You will return cooked like a lobster.”

She did not answer. Monsieur d’Apreval appeared at the door, carrying a cane and wearing the grave, ceremonious air which, forty years earlier, had seemed to her the very expression of love. He bowed.

“I am at your service, madame.”

Monsieur de Cadour laughed.

“Take care of her, d’Apreval. She has been restless since yesterday. At breakfast she said she must see the country, as though fields and trees had never been invented before our arrival.”

His wife turned toward him with an impatient movement.

“You would not come.”

“I have reached an age when I do not march six leagues to admire a hedge. Besides, I know Normandy. It is green when it is not yellow, wet when it is not dusty, and full of cows in every season.”

“You will be here when we return?”

“Where should I go? I shall wait for you with a bottle of cider and a physician.”

D’Apreval offered his arm. Madame de Cadour did not take it until they had crossed the square and passed the last houses of the town. Then her fingers closed upon him so suddenly that he looked at her.

“You are ill?”

“No. Let us go on.”

They followed a rising road between banks scorched by the heat. Dust floated beneath their feet and clung to the hem of her dress. On either side, fields stretched toward low wooded hills, and the grasshoppers filled the air with a dry, incessant crackling. There was not a cloud. The whole countryside lay motionless beneath a blaze which seemed to press roofs, trees, animals, and people toward the earth.

D’Apreval walked slowly, measuring his pace to hers.

“We can still turn back,” he said.

She stopped.

“Turn back! After coming here? After forty years?”

“I did not mean—”

“Yes, you did. You are afraid.”

He glanced along the empty road.

“One must always consider what may happen. Suppose he has been told? Suppose he guesses? People in villages remember everything. A word passes from one house to another and remains there for generations.”

“He cannot know.”

“His foster parents knew.”

“They are dead.”

“They may have spoken before dying. There are also papers, dates, payments—”

She released his arm.

“You think only of exposure.”

“I think of you.”

“No. You think of scandal, of my husband, of your daughters, of money perhaps. You imagine that he may demand something.”

D’Apreval reddened.

“It is not unreasonable. A man learns that wealthy people abandoned him in infancy; he may not greet the discovery with gratitude.”

She resumed walking.

“If he asked for all I possess, I should give it.”

“You say that because you do not know him.”

“That is why I am here.”

For several minutes they heard only the brittle cries in the grass and the distant ringing of a blacksmith’s hammer. Madame de Cadour bent her head. Her breath had grown short, but she walked with obstinate haste, as though the road itself were stealing what little time remained to her.

She had been twenty-two then. How clearly everything returned on that burning road: the winter visits, the closed drawing-room, the sound of carriage wheels beneath the windows, d’Apreval’s letters hidden in a box lined with blue satin. She had been married three years to Monsieur de Cadour, who was already stout, already cheerful, already certain that his house, his wife, and his position had been arranged forever.

And then the terror.

The physician’s lowered voice. Her mother’s hard stare. A journey announced on account of her health. A lonely house near Rouen, where the shutters remained closed though spring had come. Weeks of waiting in shame and fear, while respectable relatives invented explanations for her absence.

And the night!

A small cry in the dark room; a cry so feeble and yet so piercing that it had never ceased within her. She had asked to see the child. They had refused. She had begged. At last a nurse had brought him, wrapped tightly, his little face red and wrinkled, his eyes closed. She had touched his forehead with one finger.

“My child!” she had whispered. “My child!”

The next morning he was gone.

They told her he had been placed with good people, honest Norman farmers, that money would be sent, that later a farm should belong to him. Everyone had praised the arrangement. It saved her name, her husband’s honor, d’Apreval’s future, two families, perhaps three. It was reasonable. It was necessary. It was perfect.

Only the mother had not been saved.

For forty years she had imagined him at every age. At five, running barefoot through an orchard; at twelve, grave and handsome; at twenty, tall like d’Apreval had been, with the same dark eyes and delicate hands. Later she imagined him married, bending over a cradle. Sometimes, in a street, a stranger’s face made her tremble. Sometimes she followed a young man for several minutes, knowing it was absurd, yet unable to stop.

Never a letter. Never a portrait. Never a word beyond the annual assurance that Pierre Benedict lived, worked, and caused no trouble.

Now she was sixty-two. Her hair had turned white. She suffered at night from a pressure near the heart which the physician called insignificant but which she regarded as a warning. She could not die without seeing him. She could not.

“At last!” she murmured.

D’Apreval looked at her.

“What did you say?”

“Nothing.”

The road divided near a cluster of cottages. A man was shoeing a horse under an open shed, striking the nails with short, regular blows. D’Apreval approached him.

“Can you direct us to Pierre Benedict’s farm?”

The blacksmith raised his head and examined the two strangers.

“Benedict? Which one?”

“Pierre Benedict.”

“There’s old Pierre, but he’s bedridden. You mean his son?”

“The farmer who owns the place at La Haie?”

“That’s him. Follow the left road as far as the elm trees. Then take the sunken lane. You’ll see a pond and a broken cart. His yard is beyond.”

“Is it far?”

“A good half-hour.”

Madame de Cadour uttered a faint sound.

The blacksmith glanced at her flushed face.

“Madame ought not to be walking at this hour.”

“She wished to see the countryside,” d’Apreval replied.

The hammer began again behind them.

The lane was narrow and shaded, but no cooler. High banks shut out the wind, and branches tangled overhead. The ground had been cut into deep ruts, hardened like iron. Madame de Cadour stumbled twice. D’Apreval tried to support her.

“You cannot arrive in this state. Sit down for a moment.”

“No.”

“You are trembling.”

“No.”

“What will you say to them?”

“What we agreed.”

“And if you lose control?”

“I shall not.”

“You are losing it already.”

She faced him. Tears had gathered in her eyes, but her voice remained low.

“If you prevent me now, I shall call out his name in every house until someone brings me to him.”

D’Apreval became pale.

“I am not preventing you.”

“Then come.”

They found the pond, its green surface covered with flies. Beside it stood a cart without wheels, half buried in nettles. Beyond a row of apple trees appeared the farm.

It was larger than Madame de Cadour had expected, but poorer. A low dwelling stood opposite a stable with a sagging roof. The yard was scattered with broken tools, bundles of straw, hens, wooden pails, and heaps of manure drying in the sun. A sow slept against a wall. Two thin calves were tied beneath a shed. Everything bore the marks of labor without rest and repair forever postponed.

A black dog rushed from behind the stable, barking furiously.

Madame de Cadour seized d’Apreval’s wrist.

A woman appeared in the doorway. She was perhaps fifty, though the weather had made her look older. Her arms were bare to the elbows; a faded kerchief covered her hair. She shouted at the dog, then stood watching the visitors without welcome.

D’Apreval removed his hat.

“Good day, madame. We have walked from Fécamp and hoped you might sell us a little milk.”

The woman’s eyes traveled from his boots to Madame de Cadour’s dress.

“We’ve no milk to sell.”

“Only two glasses. Madame is overcome by the heat.”

“The milk is for the house.”

Madame de Cadour stepped forward.

“I beg you. I am very thirsty.”

Her voice shook so much that the peasant woman’s expression changed. Suspicion remained, but she called the dog away and pointed to a bench beneath a pear tree.

“Sit there. I’ll see what there is.”

Madame de Cadour sat. Through the open door she could see a dark room, a table, a cupboard, and a child’s wooden shoe near the hearth.

A child’s shoe.

She stared at it, unable to breathe. Had her son worn such shoes? Had he cried in that room? No, the house might have been rebuilt. The old people were dead. Everything she saw belonged to a life from which she had been absent: meals, winters, illnesses, quarrels, harvests, weddings, births. Forty years contained in worn stones and smoke-darkened beams.

The woman returned with a brown pitcher and two thick glasses.

“It is warm,” she said. “The cow has been troublesome.”

D’Apreval drank and praised the milk. Madame de Cadour raised the glass but could not swallow.

“You have a fine farm,” she said.

The woman shrugged.

“Fine enough to work oneself dead.”

“Does it belong to you?”

“To my husband.”

“Pierre Benedict?”

The woman looked sharply at her.

“You know him?”

“No. We were told the name when we asked our way. My companion has lost a watch somewhere along the road, and we thought perhaps one of your people might have found it.”

D’Apreval glanced at her, astonished by the invention.

“What sort of watch?” the woman asked.

“A gold one,” he answered quickly. “An old watch.”

“No one here found it.”

“Your husband has been on the road today?”

“He’s in the lower meadow.”

“Will he return soon?”

“When he has finished.”

Madame de Cadour placed the untouched glass upon the bench.

“You have children?”

“Three living. Two married. The youngest is with his brother near Bolbec.”

“And your husband—is he well?”

“Well? He works.”

“Is he—” She stopped. “Is he like his father?”

The woman frowned.

“His father has been dead twelve years.”

“I meant—one sometimes resembles one’s parents.”

“I never saw his mother. She died before I came here.”

A cock crowed from the dung heap. Somewhere inside the stable a horse struck the floor with its hoof. Madame de Cadour heard these sounds with unnatural distinctness.

“What age is your husband?”

“Forty.”

“Forty,” she repeated.

“Forty-one in November.”

In November. Yes. The date pierced her with the certainty of a blade.

“Was he brought up here?”

The woman folded her arms.

“You ask many questions for people wanting milk.”

D’Apreval rose.

“Forgive us. Madame is interested in farms. We have land ourselves.”

“Near Fécamp?”

“Near Paris.”

The woman’s mistrust increased, as though Paris were itself an accusation.

Then a man’s voice burst out beyond the orchard.

“Pull, you brute! Pull, then!”

A cow appeared between the trees, advancing sideways, its head twisted by a rope. Behind it came a man bent almost double, dragging with both hands. His blouse, darkened with sweat, clung to his back. Mud covered his trousers to the knees. He jerked the rope, swore at the animal, slipped, recovered himself, and struck it on the flank with his wooden shoe.

Madame de Cadour stood up.

The man came into the yard without seeing her. He was broad, short-necked, and heavy. A yellow beard grew unevenly across his face. His skin had the baked, cracked look of earth after drought. One eyelid drooped, perhaps from fatigue or an old injury. His hands were enormous.

Her son.

She searched his face with terrified hunger. Somewhere beneath that rough forehead, behind those dull eyes, there must be the infant she had kissed. Somewhere there must be d’Apreval’s youth, her own fine features, the life she had dreamed for him. She found nothing. Or rather she found what forty years of labor, bad food, cold rooms, debt, animals, rain, and exhausted sleep had made.

“My child!” rose within her.

Her lips moved, but no sound came.

Pierre Benedict pulled the cow toward the byre.

“Did you give her anything?” he asked his wife.

“She wouldn’t take it.”

“She’ll take the stick.”

“We have visitors.”

He glanced toward them for one instant. His gaze rested first on d’Apreval, then on Madame de Cadour, without curiosity and without recognition.

“Who are they?”

“Travelers. They lost a watch.”

“Not here.”

He disappeared into the darkness behind the cow. They heard the rope scrape against wood, a pail overturn, and another oath.

That was all.

Madame de Cadour took several uncertain steps toward the gate.

“We must go,” she said.

The peasant woman picked up the glasses.

“You haven’t paid for the milk.”

D’Apreval gave her a coin. She examined it, bit it, and put it into her pocket.

“It is too much.”

“Keep it.”

She looked at him more closely.

“For warm milk?”

“For the trouble.”

They left the yard. The dog followed them to the pond, barking at their heels.

As soon as the farm had disappeared behind the trees, Madame de Cadour stopped. Her face seemed to have collapsed. D’Apreval reached for her, but she pushed his hand away.

“That man!” she said.

He remained silent.

“That man was my son.”

“Our son.”

“No. Do not say it.”

She leaned against the bank. Tears ran down her cheeks, though she made no sound.

“I had imagined—Oh, I was foolish! But could I imagine that? Bent like an animal, swearing, covered with mud! He looked at me. He looked at me as one looks at a stone in the road.”

“What else could he do?”

“You made him that.”

D’Apreval drew himself up.

“I?”

“You arranged everything. You chose those people. You said the country would make him strong and honest. You said he would be happier than children raised in drawing rooms. That was your happiness!”

“You consented.”

“I had just given birth. I was ill. They would not let me keep him.”

“And what was possible? Tell me. Should we have placed him in your husband’s arms? Should I have announced him to my wife? We did what everyone in our position would have done.”

“Yes. Everyone. That is what destroyed him.”

D’Apreval struck the dust with his cane.

“I bought this farm for him. It cost a considerable sum. The old Benedicts were considered respectable. He inherited land, cattle, a house. Many legitimate sons received less.”

“You gave him a farm and took away his mother.”

“You would have been ruined.”

“Perhaps I should have been.”

“You say that now, after forty years of comfort.”

She stared at him. The truth of the words made them crueler.

“Yes,” she whispered. “After forty years.”

They resumed the road. The heat had not diminished. Each step raised a little cloud of pale dust. Madame de Cadour walked slowly now, with lowered head, and d’Apreval no longer offered his arm.

Once she said:

“I might have spoken.”

“And then?”

“I do not know.”

“He might have laughed. He might have called his wife. He might have asked for proof.”

“Be quiet.”

Later she murmured:

“He has three children.”

“Apparently.”

“I am their grandmother.”

D’Apreval gave no answer.

At the edge of Fécamp they stopped to remove the dust from their clothes. He brushed her skirt with his handkerchief; she straightened his collar. These gestures, trivial and careful, restored the appearance required of them. By the time they reached the hotel, their faces were composed.

Monsieur de Cadour still sat under the awning. A bottle stood beside him, empty.

“Well!” he cried. “Here are the explorers. You look as though you had crossed Africa.”

Madame de Cadour sank into a chair.

“I am tired.”

“Tired? You are half dead. What madness to go tramping over hills at your age! Did you at least discover something worth seeing?”

She closed her eyes.

Her husband laughed and turned to d’Apreval.

“And you, my poor friend? Was the expedition agreeable?”

D’Apreval looked at the woman he had once loved. On her dusty glove lay one tear, bright in the afternoon light. He bowed with the old politeness which had survived passion, terror, separation, and remorse.

“A delightful walk,” he said.

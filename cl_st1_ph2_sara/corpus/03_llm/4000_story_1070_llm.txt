There were twelve of us in Anton Antonitch’s drawing room that evening, though why we had gathered no one could precisely say. It was neither a name day nor a farewell supper; no cards had been announced, and the samovar, having hissed itself into exhaustion, stood forgotten beside a plate of sugared biscuits. Yet everyone remained. In provincial towns people often remain simply because to go home is to confess that nothing awaits them there.

At first we discussed the new inspector, then a fire in the merchants’ quarter, then whether a certain lieutenant had really lost three thousand roubles at billiards. Toward eleven o’clock the conversation grew louder and less charitable. It was then that Osip Mihalitch Polzunkov, who had spent the entire evening darting from one group to another, laughing before anyone had completed a joke and once upsetting a cup with his coat-tail, sprang into the middle of the room.

He was a small, sharp-faced man of thirty-five or perhaps forty. His age varied with his expression. When he laughed he looked like a mischievous schoolboy; when silence caught him unprepared, he seemed old and frightened. His dress was respectable at a distance. Near at hand one noticed that his cuffs were frayed and that the brass buttons on his coat did not belong to the same regiment.

“Gentlemen!” he cried, lifting one finger. “Gentlemen, I will tell you something exquisite.”

Someone groaned.

“Not the colonel and the soup again,” said Pavel Semionitch.

“No, no, not soup! I swear it is new. New, gentlemen, absolutely virginal. An April story!”

“Then wait till April.”

“It happened in April.”

“What year?”

“What does the year matter? Years are administrative details. The soul has no calendar.”

“The soul perhaps not,” Anton Antonitch said, “but your creditors have.”

There was laughter. Polzunkov laughed louder than anyone, bent almost double, slapped his knees, straightened, glanced around to make sure we had observed his good humour, and then suddenly became solemn.

“You laugh,” he said. “Excellent. That is what I desire. I offer myself, gentlemen, as a sacrifice upon the altar of social merriment. Laugh! Only let me warn you that this is not an ordinary story. It is a tragedy in a fool’s cap, an official epic, a provincial Iliad—”

“Without an Achilles?”

“With two Achilleses! No, one Achilles and one—one wooden horse. I was the wooden horse. Or perhaps I was Troy. Wait, you shall decide. Only don’t interrupt.”

“Begin.”

“I am beginning.”

“You have said that three times.”

“Because you interrupted me three times. There, you see already the fatal influence of society upon individual intention! Well, then.”

He drew a chair forward, sat on its edge, leapt up again, and began walking between the card table and the window.

“You all knew Fedosey Nikolaitch?”

A chorus answered that we had.

“Knew him!” Polzunkov repeated. “No, gentlemen, you knew his waistcoat, his civil rank, his grey whiskers, his habit of rubbing two fingers together when he said, ‘We are all Christians here.’ But the man himself—the statesman in slippers, the domestic Machiavelli, the father of a family and feeder at the public trough—you did not know him. I knew him. I served under him six years.”

“You also dined with him six years.”

“True. Why conceal it? On Thursdays. Sometimes Sundays. His cabbage soup was excellent, and I am not a hypocrite. A man may despise the cook’s master and still esteem the cook. Indeed, gentlemen, civilization rests upon such distinctions.

“I was then in the seventh department, in accounts. A miserable post, you will say, but one of trust. Figures, gentlemen, are innocent only in arithmetic books. Once they enter an office, they become political. A seven may conceal a widow’s tears; a zero may build a councillor’s summer house. I had quick eyes, quicker fingers, and—why not admit it?—a certain talent for discovering where official ink had been mixed with private butter.”

“Meaning you knew who stole what.”

“I dislike that naked mode of expression. It catches cold. But yes.

“Fedosey Nikolaitch feathered his nest. Not greedily, not vulgarly, not like some brute who grabs the whole goose and runs. No, delicately! A feather today, a feather tomorrow; a downy little voucher here, a soft supplementary estimate there. He never stole a cart; he merely arranged that the government should continually pay for wheels which no cart possessed.

“And I helped him.”

At this there was less laughter.

Polzunkov noticed it. His smile became strained.

“Yes, yes, write it down against me. I helped. Not at first knowingly; then half knowingly; then knowingly while assuring myself that I did not know. There are three degrees of official innocence, gentlemen, and the last is the most comfortable. He would summon me.

“‘Osip Mihalitch,’ he would say, ‘there appears to be a slight discrepancy.’

“‘How slight, Fedosey Nikolaitch?’

“‘One cannot measure morality by quantity,’ he would answer. A sublime phrase! He had phrases as a spider has thread: always enough to lower himself safely from any height.

“I corrected discrepancies. I shifted columns, resurrected dead expenditures, buried living receipts. I did not take money. That is my boast and my shame. I committed rascalities gratuitously, from affection.”

“Affection for him?”

“For the family,” Polzunkov said quickly. “For his house, his welcome, his— Well, for Marya Fedoseyevna.”

Someone whistled softly.

“Yes! Now we approach the sentimental portion. Put away your handkerchiefs, gentlemen; you may need them, though perhaps only to stuff into your mouths.

“Marya Fedoseyevna was his daughter. She was not beautiful in the almanac sense. Her nose was a little too decided and her eyes could become sharp when contradicted. But when she sat at the piano in a pale dress, with the lamp behind her hair—ah! A man who spends his days among ledgers may mistake any illuminated object for an angel. I loved her.

“Did she love me? Gentlemen, never ask that question of a man in love; he will answer from grammatical habit, not evidence. She allowed me to carry wool for her mother. She once asked whether I had a headache. She laughed at my imitation of the police superintendent. On these three granite pillars I constructed a temple of mutual passion.

“Fedosey Nikolaitch encouraged me. Not openly. Oh, no! A look, a sigh, a paternal hand upon the shoulder. ‘You are one of us, Osip Mihalitch,’ he would say. Or at dinner, when his wife complained that Marya was pale, he would turn to me: ‘Young people have secrets.’ Then everybody smiled and I melted.

“I melted, gentlemen! Remember that expression. It explains my biography. Wax has no principles in warm rooms.

“But there was another suitor: Lieutenant Verevkin, cavalry, moustaches, spurs, debts—the complete military bouquet. Whenever he entered, the furniture itself seemed to salute. Marya looked at him as she had never looked at me, except once when I swallowed a fish bone. He borrowed money from Fedosey Nikolaitch and promises from Marya. Then his regiment was transferred; he departed with trumpet blasts, unpaid bills and, as events proved, more consequences than luggage.”

Polzunkov stopped.

“Well?” said Anton Antonitch.

“Well, he abandoned her.”

“After compromising her?”

“I am telling it delicately.”

“Tell it plainly.”

“Plainly, then: she was with child.”

A brief silence followed. Polzunkov rubbed his palms together and looked at the carpet.

“I did not know at first. I knew only that the lieutenant was preferred and that I had been pushed aside. The Thursday dinners ceased. Fedosey Nikolaitch became official with me. Instead of ‘my dear fellow,’ it was ‘Mr. Polzunkov.’ Marya was not at home. Her mother had headaches. The door which had opened like the gates of paradise now behaved like an office door on a holiday.

“I was wounded. Vanity, love, jealousy, righteous indignation—all the great powers assembled in my breast and elected spite as chairman.

“Meanwhile an inspection was rumoured. Not one of our friendly inspections, where the inspector arrives with a letter, eats sturgeon, praises order and departs with a carpet. A real inspection. From the capital. Sharp men, it was said, who could smell an altered date through sealing wax.

“I had papers. Copies, memoranda, private instructions in Fedosey Nikolaitch’s hand—enough to make his whiskers tremble. I had preserved them, originally, from caution. Later I preserved them from philosophy. At last I preserved them from revenge.

“One evening I placed them in a portfolio, put on my best cravat and went to him.

“‘Fedosey Nikolaitch,’ I said, ‘I have come tête-à-tête.’

“He was in his study, wearing a dressing gown embroidered with green leaves. That dressing gown, gentlemen, had more moral dignity than I. He looked at the portfolio and understood everything. Such men understand a sealed envelope before it is written.

“‘Sit down, Osip Mihalitch.’

“‘I prefer to stand.’

“‘As you please. Tea?’

“‘No.’

“‘You are agitated.’

“‘I have reason.’

“‘Then perhaps tea would be wise.’

“You see his method? He offered tea to my vengeance. Had I accepted, I should have been lost at once. Fortunately I remained heroic for nearly four minutes.

“I laid out the papers. ‘These concern the accounts,’ I said.

“‘Naturally.’

“‘They concern certain irregularities.’

“‘All human affairs are irregular.’

“‘The inspector may take a less theological view.’

“He looked at me. I looked at him. My knees trembled, but the table concealed them.

“‘What do you want?’ he asked.

“There it was, gentlemen—the naked question! What did I want? Justice? Marya? Revenge? Promotion? I had rehearsed a speech on wounded dignity, but at that instant dignity demanded a numerical expression.

“‘Two thousand roubles,’ I said.

“You see? Why two thousand? Because one thousand sounded petty and three thousand incredible. Man’s conscience has its tariffs.

“Fedosey Nikolaitch sighed. ‘I expected better from you.’

“‘And I expected better from you.’

“Good answer, eh?”

“Stolen from a play,” said Pavel Semionitch.

“All good answers are stolen from plays. Life is incapable of dialogue without assistance.

“He rose, went to the window, and gazed into the yard where his servant was beating a carpet. Thump, thump! At each blow my courage rose. I became Brutus. I became the incorruptible avenger of public virtue.

“Then he turned. There were tears in his eyes.”

“Real tears?”

“What is a real tear? Chemically, I daresay, they were genuine.

“‘Osip Mihalitch,’ he said, ‘you have eaten my bread.’

“Immediately I felt the cabbage soup of six years rise in accusation.

“‘You entered my home.’

“His preserves, his tea, the little pies on Thursdays—all appeared as witnesses.

“‘My children trusted you. My wife called you son.’

“She had called me ‘sir,’ but perhaps motherhood had been implicit.

“‘And now, because my daughter could not command her heart, you would destroy us.’

“‘I am not discussing hearts,’ I said. ‘I am discussing documents.’

“‘Documents! Always documents! Is there no heavenly document, Osip Mihalitch? No record kept above?’

“That was powerful. He pointed at the ceiling. I glanced up involuntarily and noticed a crack in the plaster. Even heaven seemed to have opened an account.

“Still, I held firm. I was magnificent. ‘Two thousand,’ I repeated.

“He rang. I almost fled, thinking he had summoned the police, but he merely ordered tea. Then began bargaining. He pleaded poverty, family expenses, Marya’s health, his wife’s nerves, the price of firewood, the ingratitude of the age. I invoked the inspector. He invoked God. We met at fifteen hundred roubles.

“He brought the money from a small iron box: notes in a packet tied with blue thread. My hands went cold.

“‘Count them,’ he said.

“‘I trust you.’

“‘Do not insult me with trust now.’

“I counted them. Fifteen hundred. He held out his hand for the papers. I gave them. Then—what do you think happened?”

“He kicked you out.”

“He blessed me.”

Laughter burst out. Polzunkov laughed too, though his lower lip twitched.

“Yes, blessed me! He placed both hands upon my head. ‘May this money bring you happiness equal to that which your conduct has brought me.’ Elegant, was it not? A curse in a paternal overcoat.

“I went into the street with fifteen hundred roubles inside my coat. Snow was melting. Water dripped from roofs; the moon shone in puddles; somewhere a dog barked. Every thief expects nature to mark his crime with thunder, but nature had a cold and merely dripped.

“At first I exulted. ‘I am rich!’ I thought. Then, ‘I am independent!’ Then, ‘I am a blackmailer.’ By the next corner the notes had grown hot. By the bridge they weighed a pood. Near my lodging I fancied every policeman knew. I slept with the packet under my pillow and dreamed that blue thread wound round my throat.

“Three days passed. Fedosey Nikolaitch did nothing.

“That frightened me more than prosecution. In the office he was polite, calm, even kind. Once he asked whether the stove smoked in my room. What did he mean? Why the stove? Was smoke an allegory? I became ill with interpretations.

“On the fourth evening his servant arrived.

“‘Fedosey Nikolaitch asks you to call.’

“‘Officially?’

“‘He did not say.’

“‘Is there company?’

“‘The family.’

“Family! That word struck me here.”

Polzunkov thumped his chest.

“I went. Of course I went. You would have gone. Do not shake your heads. Curiosity pulls harder than honour.

“The drawing room was lighted. His wife came toward me weeping. Marya stood beside the piano, pale—truly pale this time, not lamp-pale, not romantic, but as though someone had erased her. Fedosey Nikolaitch took my hand.

“‘Osip Mihalitch, let us forget.’

“‘Forget what?’

“‘Human weakness. Yours, mine, hers. We have all suffered.’

“Then they told me about Verevkin. The gallant lieutenant had departed, ceased writing, and, when pressed, declared that marriage was impossible because his aunt disapproved. His aunt, apparently, commanded greater forces than his colonel.

“Marya was ruined. Fedosey Nikolaitch spoke the word softly, almost respectfully, as if ruin were an illness requiring quiet. Her mother sobbed. Marya hid her face.

“Then he said, ‘There remains one man whose heart, though clouded by resentment, is fundamentally noble.’

“I looked behind me.

“‘You, Osip Mihalitch.’

“Gentlemen, I melted. I melted from boots to collar.

“He did not ask outright whether I would marry her. No, he allowed nobility to propose to itself. He spoke of forgiveness, of a child needing a name, of the prodigal son returning to his Penates—though strictly speaking I was neither a son nor did the Penates belong to me. He said Marya had always esteemed me, that girlish vanity had deceived her, that suffering had revealed the solid merits of my character.

“Solid merits! No one had ever called me solid. I had been quick, amusing, useful, occasionally trustworthy, but never solid. The word conquered me.

“I approached Marya. ‘Can you forgive me?’

“She looked up. There were tears on her eyelashes.

“‘It is I who must ask forgiveness,’ she whispered.

“I fell on one knee.”

“In front of all of them?”

“Both knees, if you insist upon exactitude. I kissed her hand. Her mother blessed us. Fedosey Nikolaitch embraced me. We all wept. It was a deluge. Noah himself could not have kept the accounts.

“That evening I returned home engaged.

“And the money?” someone asked.

“Still under my pillow.”

“Did you tell them you had it?”

“Fedosey Nikolaitch knew I had it.”

“Marya?”

“Not then. Why spoil a sacred moment with bookkeeping? Besides, I intended to return it.”

“You intended?”

“I intended so intensely that for several days the intention had all the moral flavour of the act.

“I became again a visitor at the house. More than a visitor—a son. Fedosey Nikolaitch called me ‘our Osip.’ His wife discussed curtains with me. Marya permitted me to sit beside her. She was quiet, ashamed, grateful. I mistook gratitude for love, but gratitude, gentlemen, is love wearing mourning; at dusk one may be deceived.

“I brought her gifts beyond my means. A shawl, a little silver cross, French bonbons. Then I found a starling in the market which had been taught to say, ‘Bonjour, ma belle!’ At least the seller assured me it could. In my room it said nothing except a noise like an unpaid creditor. Still, I bought a brass cage and presented it to Marya.

“She smiled for the first time. ‘What shall we call him?’

“‘Osip,’ I suggested.

“She laughed. Ah, that laugh! Had the bird eaten my heart through the bars I should have thanked it.

“Meanwhile the first of April approached. Among us clerks it was customary to play little jokes—exchange inkstands, seal empty envelopes, send a junior to inquire after a nonexistent prince. Harmless demonstrations of intellectual life.

“On the morning of April first I prepared a masterpiece. I wrote a formal petition requesting dismissal from service on account of ill health and domestic circumstances. Perfectly phrased, all margins observed, stamped paper, respectful conclusion. I carried it into Fedosey Nikolaitch’s office with a funeral face.

“‘What is this?’ he asked.

“‘My resignation.’

“He stared at me. ‘Resignation?’

“‘I can no longer serve.’

“‘Why?’

“‘My conscience.’

“At that he nearly smiled, but controlled himself.

“‘You have reflected?’

“‘Deeply.’

“‘And this decision is irrevocable?’

“‘As fate.’

“He read the petition twice. His face changed. He rose and embraced me.

“‘I understand,’ he said. ‘After recent events you cannot remain my subordinate. You wish to enter our family as an equal, not a dependent. Delicate! Noble! Osip Mihalitch, you constantly shame me.’

“I almost confessed the joke then, but his praise tasted too pleasant. I bowed.

“‘Still,’ he continued, ‘do not be hasty. Marriage requires income. We shall keep the petition between ourselves. After the wedding we will arrange a transfer, perhaps a promotion elsewhere. Meanwhile, there is work only you can do.’

“Then I cried, ‘April fool!’

“He blinked.

“‘April fool, Fedosey Nikolaitch! The petition is a joke. First of April! I have taken you with my hook!’

“He looked at me for three seconds. Then he laughed.

“Such laughter! Homeric, paternal, administrative. He rang for the chief clerk and made me repeat the joke. The chief clerk laughed. Two copyists were summoned; they laughed. Even the doorkeeper heard and laughed in the corridor. I bowed to right and left like a celebrated comic actor.

“‘Give it back,’ I said, reaching for the petition.

“Fedosey Nikolaitch held it behind him.

“‘No, no! I shall preserve it as evidence of your wit.’

“‘But it is on stamped paper.’

“‘All the better. Posterity respects stamps.’

“I let him keep it.

“Why are you groaning? You, Pavel Semionitch—do not pretend superior wisdom. At that moment it was a joke. Everybody had laughed. A document that provokes laughter ceases, does it not, to be a document?”

“No,” said Anton Antonitch.

“I know that now! I am explaining what I knew then.

“That same afternoon Fedosey Nikolaitch called me privately. His gaiety had vanished.

“‘Osip Mihalitch, the inspection is confirmed.’

“‘When?’

“‘Within a fortnight. The accounts are in terrible disorder.’

“‘You have the papers.’

“‘Those papers are no longer the difficulty. There are discrepancies from previous years, neglected entries, balances carried twice. Nothing criminal, perhaps, but appearances—appearances destroy honest men.’

“He always became honest when threatened.

“‘You alone can put matters right,’ he said. ‘Will you help your future father?’

“What could I answer? I plunged in.

“For twelve days I worked like a galley slave chained to an abacus. I arrived before dawn and left after midnight. Columns marched before my eyes in sleep. I balanced sums that had no desire to balance, persuaded missing vouchers to exist in duplicate, and made expenditures leap from one fiscal year to another like trained fleas. Fedosey Nikolaitch visited me every evening.

“‘Courage, my son.’

“He brought tea with rum. Once Marya sent a note: ‘Do not tire yourself for our sake.’ I kissed it and repaired three thousand roubles before supper.

“But the shortage remained—one thousand four hundred and fifty roubles.

“Notice the figure, gentlemen. Precisely one thousand four hundred and fifty.”

“Your fifteen hundred minus fifty.”

“Exactly. You are quick when another man is falling into a pit.

“The shortage belonged, Fedosey Nikolaitch explained, to a cashier named Akim Petrovitch, an elderly clerk with nine children and a chronic cough. The poor man had used government funds because his house was to be sold for debt. If discovered, he would go to trial; his children would beg; his wife would die on the courthouse steps—a detailed catastrophe.

“‘I have promised to save him,’ Fedosey Nikolaitch said.

“‘How?’

“‘By replacing the money.’

“‘Then replace it.’

“He lowered his eyes.

“‘I cannot.’

“‘But—’

“‘My resources are exhausted. The sum I gave you—’

“He stopped. Delicacy itself! He would not mention our transaction. He allowed the fifteen hundred roubles to enter the room invisibly and sit between us.

“I understood. My heart began talking. My vanity joined it. Then Marya’s pale face appeared, followed by the nine children of Akim Petrovitch, whom I had never seen but who immediately became barefoot.

“‘You wish me to lend you the money?’

“‘Never!’ he cried. ‘I have no right even to think it. After the wound I received from you, and the wound I inflicted upon you—no. Let the old man perish. God sees my helplessness.’

“He covered his face.

“I walked about. The blue-threaded packet was still hidden in my room. I had spent perhaps thirty roubles on gifts, ten on the cage, some on dinners and cabs. Fifty altogether.

“‘I have fourteen hundred and fifty,’ I said.

“He did not move.

“‘Fedosey Nikolaitch, do you hear?’

“‘Do not tempt me.’

“‘I will bring it tomorrow.’

“Then he seized me. ‘No! You are beginning life. You need furniture. Marya—’

“‘Marya will approve.’

“‘You would save an entire family.’

“‘I will.’

“‘You would return good for evil.’

“‘I will!’

“‘You would heap burning coals upon my head.’

“‘Let them burn!’

“We embraced. Again tears. Always moisture with that family! If a man had entered unexpectedly, he would have thought us two saints competing for martyrdom.

“The next morning I brought the money. He counted it slowly.

“‘Fourteen hundred and fifty.’

“‘All I have.’

“‘But I gave you fifteen hundred.’

“‘I spent fifty.’

“A cloud passed over his face—not reproach, no; concern.

“‘That creates a small difficulty.’

“‘What difficulty?’

“‘The accounts must show the entire sum.’

“‘I can repay fifty after salary.’

“‘Naturally. A formality only. We shall enter fifty roubles as advanced to you for departmental expenses. You sign a report acknowledging receipt, and next month the account is closed.’

“He pushed a paper toward me. It stated that I, Collegiate Registrar Osip Mihalitch Polzunkov, had received fifty roubles in advance and accepted responsibility for their proper expenditure.

“‘But I did not receive them now.’

“‘You received them previously.’

“‘From you, privately.’

“‘And now that private sum acquires official character. Merely a bridge between columns.’

“I hesitated.

“He looked wounded. ‘You trust me so little?’

“That was enough. A man may blackmail another and still be unable to endure the accusation that he lacks trust. I signed.

“Fedosey Nikolaitch folded the report and put it into his drawer. The notes disappeared into the iron box.

“‘Akim Petrovitch’s children will bless you,’ he said.

“I left feeling stripped but radiant. I had bought back my honour for fourteen hundred and fifty roubles and sold it again for fifty, though naturally I did not understand the arithmetic then.

“The inspection came.

“For three days men with pale faces and clean collars turned our office inside out. They asked quiet questions, which are the most terrible kind. Fedosey Nikolaitch remained calm. The accounts I repaired were praised. One inspector even remarked upon the ingenuity of certain adjustments. I blushed like an artist hearing his first favourable review.

“Then nothing happened. No arrest, no scandal. The storm passed.

“I went to Fedosey Nikolaitch to speak about the wedding.

“He received me standing.

“‘Mr. Polzunkov,’ he said.

“Mr. Polzunkov! The old door had closed again.

“‘Is Marya Fedoseyevna at home?’

“‘My daughter is indisposed.’

“‘Then I will call tomorrow.’

“‘That will be unnecessary.’

“My stomach vanished.

“‘What do you mean?’

“He went to his desk and produced a paper. My petition. My comic resignation, stamped, dated, signed.

“‘Your request has been granted.’

“I laughed. What else could I do? ‘Excellent! Another April joke.’

“‘It is no longer April.’

“‘But you know I was joking.’

“‘An official petition cannot joke.’

“‘You promised to keep it.’

“‘I promised nothing. You submitted it voluntarily before witnesses.’

“‘The witnesses heard me say “April fool.”’

“‘They heard you behave with regrettable levity after presenting a serious request.’

“‘I withdraw it.’

“‘Too late. The order has been issued.’

“He showed me the order. Dismissed at my own request.

“I could not breathe. ‘And the engagement?’

“‘What engagement?’

“Yes, gentlemen: what engagement! A single question erased dinners, tears, blessings, kisses, the pale dress, the nine barefoot children—everything.

“‘You gave me your daughter’s hand.’

“‘You presumed upon our compassion. Marya is in no condition to marry a man without position or prospects.’

“‘I had a position yesterday!’

“‘Which you resigned.’

“‘As a joke!’

“‘A man who jokes with his livelihood is not fit to support a family.’

“Observe the logic. Perfectly polished. Not a crack in it except the truth.

“I rushed to the drawer where he had placed my report.

“‘Then return my money.’

“‘What money?’

“‘The fourteen hundred and fifty roubles.’

“‘You mean the sum contributed to cover an official deficiency?’

“‘Yes.’

“‘That sum has been duly entered.’

“‘And the fifty?’

“He took out my signed statement.

“‘You acknowledge receiving it.’

“‘You gave me nothing.’

“‘Your signature says otherwise.’

“‘I signed because you asked me!’

“‘That is generally why people sign.’

“I threatened him. I shouted. I said I would go to the inspector, the governor, the Senate, the emperor. He listened with melancholy patience.

“‘Go wherever you please. You extorted money from me in exchange for stolen papers. Shall I explain that too?’

“I stopped.

“‘The papers!’ I cried. ‘You admitted—’

“‘What papers? You returned to me certain private memoranda unlawfully taken from my desk. Out of pity, and to avoid ruining a misguided subordinate, I gave you financial assistance. You later restored most of it voluntarily. The remaining fifty roubles you received officially. Everything is documented.’

“Documented! There stood my whole soul, gentlemen, naked and shivering; and against it he set three papers in a drawer.

“At that moment the door opened and Akim Petrovitch entered—the ruined cashier, the father of nine, the man whose wife was to die on the courthouse steps.

“He bowed cheerfully.

“‘The deed is ready, Fedosey Nikolaitch.’

“‘What deed?’ I demanded.

“Akim Petrovitch looked surprised. ‘For my house.’

“‘Your house?’

“‘Fedosey Nikolaitch has purchased it. A generous price. I can now move to my brother’s farm.’

“I looked at Fedosey Nikolaitch. He did not blink.

“My fourteen hundred and fifty roubles had not saved the house. They had bought it—for him. Akim Petrovitch had no shortage. The nine children, if they existed, were probably wearing boots.

“I became calm. There is a point beyond rage where a man begins noticing furniture. I remember a fly walking across the inkstand. I remember that one green leaf on his dressing gown—he had put it on over his clothes because the room was cold—was coming loose at the hem.

“‘You bought his house with my money,’ I said.

“‘You have no money,’ he replied. ‘You resigned your post and owe the department fifty roubles.’

“I left.

“Wait, the finale is still to come.

“For a week I wandered. I wrote complaints and tore them up. I composed speeches. I imagined entering his office before everyone and saying: ‘Fedosey Nikolaitch, you are a scoundrel!’ Just that. No rhetoric. A clean, honest word.

“Meanwhile Marya sent nothing.

“Then a servant brought a package. The brass bird-cage. Inside sat the starling, looking offended by transportation. Tied to the cage was a sealed envelope.

“I opened it. There was no letter, no explanation. Only a small card on which Fedosey Nikolaitch had written: ‘April 1st.’

“That was all.”

For several seconds no one spoke. The starling, the sealed envelope and the two words seemed to hang in Anton Antonitch’s smoky drawing room. Then Pavel Semionitch gave a short laugh; another guest followed; soon the company broke into noisy merriment.

Polzunkov laughed with us. He flung himself into a chair, kicked up his heels, mimicked the starling, then leapt up and bowed as if summoned before the curtain.

“Excellent, is it not? The joker joked! The fisherman took his own hook! April first—structural unity, gentlemen, like in the finest literature. He returned the bird but kept the cage of circumstances—no, wait, he returned the cage too; the metaphor fails. I warned you my eloquence outruns accuracy.”

“What happened to Marya?” someone asked.

“She went away to an aunt. The child was born there. Later, I heard, she married a widowed assessor in another province.”

“And Fedosey Nikolaitch?”

“Retired honourably. Sold Akim Petrovitch’s house at a profit. He now has roses in his garden and a pew in church.”

“And you never denounced him?”

“I wrote seven denunciations.”

“Sent any?”

“One.”

“To whom?”

“To myself. I read it aloud and burned it.”

“But did you ever call him a scoundrel to his face?”

Polzunkov’s smile sharpened. He strode to the middle of the room and planted his feet wide apart.

“That, gentlemen, is the finest scene! Two years later I met him in the street. Broad daylight. No office, no papers, no daughter, no cabbage soup—man against man. I saw him approaching. I prepared myself.

“‘Now,’ I thought, ‘now I shall restore moral order. I shall stop before him, look straight into his eyes and say, “Fedosey Nikolaitch, you are a scoundrel.” Nothing more. History does not require long speeches.’

“He approached. My heart beat, but nobly. Ten steps—five—three. I raised my head. He recognized me.

“‘Ah, Osip Mihalitch,’ he said, ‘how are you getting on?’

“I opened my mouth.”

“Well?” cried several voices.

Polzunkov looked around at us. He still wore his comic smile, but it seemed suddenly to have been pinned to his face by someone else.

“I said, ‘Very well, thank you, Fedosey Nikolaitch. And how is your health?’”

The room exploded in laughter.

He laughed too—once, twice, louder than all the rest. Then he bowed, knocked over the empty cup beside Anton Antonitch’s chair, apologized elaborately, and began talking and talking and talking about the fragility of porcelain, though no one was listening.

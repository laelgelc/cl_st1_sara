# THE IMPONDERABLE CAPTIVE

I am not ignorant of the reception which awaits this narrative. There are truths whose very nakedness protects them better than secrecy; for the mind, startled by their magnitude, refuses them admission, and condemns the witness before it will examine the testimony. I have seen men accept, with an air of philosophic composure, frauds that flattered their reason, while they laughed at facts because the facts exceeded it. Knowing this, I have delayed for eleven years to record the circumstances which follow. I now set them down, not in expectation of belief, but because silence has become more intolerable to me than ridicule.

The principal witness besides myself was Doctor Elias Hammond, formerly of Bleecker Street, whose professional reputation needs no defence from my pen. Three other persons touched the object. Two assisted in the experiments. A plaster reproduction of it remains, unless recently removed, in the anatomical museum attached to the College of Physicians in —— Street. Thus much can be verified. Names less intimately connected with the extraordinary business I shall either suppress or indicate by initials, for the terror excited at the time was so profound that several persons exacted from me a promise of discretion.

In October of 18— I occupied two rooms in a boardinghouse situated in Twenty-Fourth Street, near the western edge of the built city. Beyond it were vacant lots, stone yards, low wooden dwellings, and patches of market garden where cabbages grew under the smoke of New York. The house, which had once been the suburban residence of a merchant enriched by the China trade, was an edifice of brown brick, four stories in height, with a broad stoop, a fanlight over the door, and at the rear a garden of unexpected dimensions. Two pear-trees, ancient and rheumatic, leaned over the paths; a grape-arbor, half fallen, extended from the kitchen wing; and in summer the place was filled with fireflies, whose wandering lamps gave to the shrubbery a fantastic animation.

The mistress of the establishment, Mrs. M——, was a widow of excellent character and determined temper. She had obtained the house at a low rent because it enjoyed the reputation of being haunted.

The story was not singular. A former owner, a Mr. Atherton, had disappeared from an upper chamber during a snowstorm, leaving his clothes upon a chair, his watch beneath his pillow, and his door locked on the inside. A servant declared that for many nights thereafter she heard a heavy body dragged along the corridor. A child had seen, or imagined that she saw, a hand without an arm upon the stairs. Bells rang when no wire moved. A pale woman appeared in the garden and vanished beside the old well. Such manifestations, multiplied by gossip, had rendered the house unprofitable to its proprietor and irresistibly attractive to Mrs. M——, who combined a prudent regard for economy with a contempt for the spiritual world.

“If a ghost pays board,” she was accustomed to say, “I shall treat him civilly. If he does not, I shall not trouble myself about him.”

We boarders entered into the humor of the thing. There were ten of us: a broker; two young gentlemen attached to a newspaper; a French music-master; Mr. and Mrs. S——, with their daughter; an aged clerk from the Custom House; Doctor Hammond; myself; and a student of divinity whose faith in apparitions varied curiously with the hour of night. Each new sound was discussed at breakfast. The creaking of timber became Atherton’s groan; rats were identified as dismembered hands; and when the cook, descending before dawn, discovered a luminous shape in the passage and overturned a lamp in her flight, the spectre proved to be the Frenchman’s white overcoat hanging upon a peg.

For nearly a year we were disappointed. Nothing occurred which could not be referred to mice, plumbing, wind, indigestion, or servants. The house possessed no more mystery than any other old dwelling in which beams shrink, doors settle, and water-pipes complain of changes in temperature. Even the divinity student grew audacious. He offered, for five dollars, to sleep in Atherton’s chamber, but withdrew the proposal when informed that it was already occupied by the cook’s married sister.

I confess that I was less skeptical than my companions supposed. My imagination had been nourished upon the Arabian narrators, the darker scenes of Shakespeare, the fantastic tales of Hoffmann, and those medieval chronicles in which the Devil, with a pedantic attention to legal forms, affixes his claw to contracts. Yet my credulity, if such it may be called, demanded evidence. I had attended spirit circles and detected the action of knees beneath tables. I had watched clairvoyants and found their celestial perception embarrassed by a folded handkerchief. I neither denied nor accepted. I waited.

Doctor Hammond, who occupied the room above mine, was of a different constitution. He was a materialist in speech, though, like many physicians, he retained in secret a profound reverence for mysteries. His profession had taught him that the visible man is but an exterior diagram, imperfectly representing innumerable concealed processes. He had placed his hand upon living organs which few men see; he had watched thought perish under pressure upon the brain; and he knew too well the insolence of human knowledge to believe that nature had submitted all her laws to our inspection.

“The supernatural,” he said to me one evening, “is commonly only the natural not yet ticketed. Lightning was a god until Franklin drew it down by a string. Madness was possession until we opened the skull. If a phantom ever allows me to feel its pulse, I will prescribe for it.”

This remark, uttered in jest, returned to us under circumstances which stripped it of all pleasantry.

The night was that of the seventeenth of May. The day had been unusually warm, and toward sunset a violet haze hung over the river. We had dined early. Most of the boarders had gone to hear an Italian singer at the Academy, while Mrs. M—— and the aged clerk played backgammon in the parlor. Hammond and I carried our chairs into the garden, where the evening breeze moved faintly through the grape leaves.

There was at that time among cultivated men a deplorable but not uncommon curiosity regarding opium. I had used the drug occasionally—not from bodily need, but from that rash desire to push consciousness beyond its appointed frontier which has tempted men into deserts, laboratories, and tombs. That night I possessed a small quantity of excellent Smyrna opium. Hammond, after some protest on medical grounds which neither of us regarded as final, consented to join me.

We smoked in a little summerhouse at the extremity of the garden. The moon, not yet risen, left the upper air crowded with stars. Through openings in the wall I saw the windows of the city kindle one by one; heard wheels rattle remotely over stone; caught the bells of ferryboats on the river. Fireflies moved among the currant bushes with slow, green pulsations. The tobacco smoke and the heavier perfume of the drug mingled beneath the roof, until our retreat seemed less a wooden structure than a pavilion suspended in some dim province of dream.

The opium produced in me no violent hallucination. Objects preserved their identity, but acquired significance. Every leaf appeared to contain a history; every star seemed an aperture through which an inconceivable intelligence regarded us. Hammond’s face, illuminated by the red point of his pipe, receded and advanced like a portrait seen beneath moving water.

Our conversation turned, by what transition I cannot remember, upon fear.

“You have been in battle,” I said. “You have seen cholera wards and dissecting rooms. Tell me, Hammond, what is the ultimate terror?”

“Death,” he answered.

“No. Men seek death. Some greet it with calmness.”

“Pain, then.”

“Pain can be measured; expectation can prepare for it. I mean a terror beneath all particular terrors—the root from which they grow.”

He smiled. “You ask for the anatomy of a shadow.”

“Perhaps the final fear is not of injury, but of contradiction. We are secure only while the universe keeps its habits. A dead man walking would not be terrible because he was dead, nor because he walked, but because the two facts could not coexist. Suppose that something should seize you—something heavy, warm, breathing—and that you could not see it. Would the invisibility add to the danger?”

“It would add to the inconvenience of hitting it.”

“You evade me. The eye is our despot. We believe touch, hearing, smell, only after vision has signed the warrant. What if the hand swore there was a body where the eye found nothing?”

“Then,” said Hammond, knocking the ashes from his pipe, “I should suspect the hand, examine the eye, and afterward look for the body.”

“And if all three remained obstinate?”

“I should call in witnesses.”

“Materialist!”

“Romancer!”

We laughed, though I remember that the sound appeared curiously small in the great obscurity around us. Soon afterward Hammond rose. The effects of the opium had begun to fade. We entered the house through the kitchen, took candles, and ascended the staircase together. At his door he laid his hand on my shoulder.

“Sleep soundly,” he said. “If the ultimate terror visits you, pull the bell.”

I entered my chamber at a few minutes after midnight.

The room was large and agreeably furnished. A mahogany bed stood with its head against the inner wall. Opposite were two windows looking upon the garden. Between them hung a steel engraving of Fuseli’s *Nightmare*, which I had often considered in bad taste and had never taken the trouble to remove. There was also a wardrobe, a washstand, a writing-table, and near the fireplace a deep armchair covered in green damask.

I undressed slowly. The night remained oppressive. I opened both windows, extinguished the candle, and lay down with only a sheet over me. For some time I watched the faint oblongs of the windows and the vague tremor of leaves beyond them. My mind was tranquil. I was conscious of no fantastic images, no confusion of places or persons. I emphasize this because the opium must inevitably be invoked against my testimony. I had used a moderate quantity; its sensible operation was past; and from the moment I entered my chamber until sleep overtook me, my faculties were as orderly as they are while I write these lines.

I slept perhaps two hours.

I awoke instantly, without dream or gradual return to consciousness. Something had fallen upon the foot of the bed.

The mattress sank. The wooden frame uttered a sharp complaint.

I remained motionless, supposing that a cat had entered through the window; but the depression was too great. It was as if a child, or a dense sack of grain, had been dropped upon the coverlet. Before I could rise, the Thing moved.

Two heavy impressions advanced over the bed toward me.

There was no visible shape. The windows showed their pale rectangles; the sheet lay whitely over my limbs; the room contained only its accustomed shadows. Yet the mattress yielded in successive hollows. I heard a low, rapid breathing.

Then hands closed upon my throat.

Hands! I use the word deliberately. They possessed fingers and opposing thumbs. Their palms were hard, warm, and hideously powerful. At the same instant a weight flung itself upon my chest, forcing the breath from my lungs, while knees—or structures corresponding to knees—pressed against my sides.

I saw nothing.

The pressure increased. I tore at the wrists which were strangling me, and my fingers encountered flesh: smooth, hairless, moist with exertion, and stretched over cords of muscle that seemed disproportionate to the size of the limbs. There was a body above me—a solid, panting, furious body—and between my face and the vacant ceiling there was not so much as a stain upon the darkness.

For perhaps five seconds I was paralysed, not by weakness but by the monstrous incompatibility of my sensations. My eyes denied what my hands embraced. The bed shook beneath an assailant that vision pronounced nonexistent. It breathed. I felt its warm breath. It had weight. It had sinews. It had fingers. It was invisible!

The need of air saved my reason. Terror became combat.

I released one of the wrists, drove my fist upward, and struck what seemed a face. My knuckles met something broad and flat; a hiss broke from the emptiness. The fingers at my throat relaxed. Twisting violently, I rolled from beneath the creature, seized it around the middle, and dragged it with me to the floor.

We fell together.

The carpet received my shoulder, and something struck the boards with a concussion that shook the washstand. It was lighter than I—perhaps ninety pounds—but its strength was prodigious. Its legs wound about me with the tenacity of serpents. Teeth snapped near my cheek. I caught a jaw under my left hand, and, thrusting it backward, felt the mouth open, the hot tongue, the irregular teeth. One tooth entered the flesh below my thumb.

No man who has not grappled with an unseen enemy can conceive the confusion of such a struggle. Against a visible antagonist the eye directs every effort; it anticipates the blow, selects the vulnerable point, and converts the body’s many motions into one intelligent act. I had only touch. I fought as a blind man fights in a pit. When the creature’s limbs slipped from my grasp, they ceased, for that instant, to exist. A stroke came from nowhere. A mouth materialized only when it bit me. I seemed not to wrestle with one body, but with separate malignities—hands, knees, teeth, a heaving breast—each born beneath my fingers and annihilated when contact ended.

We rolled beneath the table. A chair overturned. The water-pitcher fell and shattered. I shouted Hammond’s name, but one hand again covered my mouth. I bit into it. A bitter, animal taste filled my lips, though when I afterward examined them by candlelight there was no visible blood.

The Thing gave a harsh, sobbing cry.

By good fortune my right hand encountered its throat. I closed upon it with all my power. The skin was extraordinarily loose there; under it fluttered a pulse as rapid as that of a terrified bird. The creature struggled more violently, then changed its method. Instead of striking, it attempted to escape. It wriggled backward, compressing its chest in a manner scarcely human, and nearly freed itself from my knees. I tightened my grip, forced it face downward, and planted my weight across its loins.

At that moment feet sounded in the corridor.

My door opened. A candle blazed into the room, and Hammond, dressed only in trousers and shirt, stood upon the threshold.

“What has happened?”

“Shut the door!” I cried. “Come here—quickly!”

He stared at me. I must have presented the appearance of a madman: half naked, bleeding from the hand, kneeling upon an empty space in the disordered carpet, my arms rigid around nothing.

“Are you ill?”

“Come here, I tell you! There is something under me.”

“Something?”

“An animal—a man—I do not know. It is invisible.”

His face altered, though he did not move.

“Hammond, for God’s sake! It is getting loose!”

The creature writhed beneath me with renewed desperation. My body rose and fell visibly upon the vacant air. That spectacle overcame his hesitation. He set down the candle, crossed the room, and extended one hand cautiously.

“Lower—near my left knee!”

His fingers touched the creature’s back.

I shall never forget his expression. Curiosity, incredulity, and horror passed across it like shadows driven by wind. He snatched his hand away, then deliberately put it forth again. This time he traced the spine, the shoulder, the arm.

“Good God,” he whispered. “There is a body!”

“Take its wrists.”

“Where?”

I guided him. The Thing snapped at his hand, and Hammond recoiled with an oath.

“It has a mouth!”

“Did you imagine I was embracing a pillow? Take the wrist. Hold it fast.”

He mastered himself instantly. Whatever his metaphysical confusion, his practical faculties became admirable. We turned the creature upon its back. Hammond placed his knee upon one arm while I held the other and controlled the head.

“Cord,” he said. “Have you cord?”

“In the wardrobe—a leather trunk-strap.”

He fetched it without relaxing his pressure. We secured the wrists behind the creature’s back, though the knots seemed to float unsupported about two feet above the floor. A clothesline served for the ankles. We passed another cord around the waist and tied it to the iron fender.

When at last we stood away, the sight—or, rather, the absence which constituted the sight—was more appalling than the struggle. The carpet was indented by a body. A loop of strap encircled invisible wrists; rope knotted itself around invisible ankles; the line from the fender rose obliquely and disappeared into an apparently vacant point. The captive breathed in short, savage gasps. Now and then it strained, and the cords grew taut.

Hammond looked at me. His face was colorless.

“How much opium did you take?” he asked.

“You smoked from the same pipe.”

“Not so much.”

“Do hallucinations leave tooth-marks? Do two men share them by touch?”

He made no reply. Kneeling again, he placed his ear where he supposed the chest to be. Presently he raised his head.

“A heart,” he said. “Very rapid. Regular enough. The respiration is mammalian.”

“Mammalian! Hammond, what is it?”

“I know what it does. I do not know what it is.”

He fetched his medical case and examined my throat and hand. While he dressed the bite, the captive lay comparatively still, though from time to time a tremor passed through the rope.

“We must have light,” I said.

“We have light.”

“More light. Every lamp in the house.”

“What purpose would that serve?”

“I do not know. This darkness seems part of it.”

We lit two additional candles and the oil lamp. The chamber became brilliantly illuminated. Nothing was revealed. No refraction marked an outline; no shadow lay beneath the Thing; light passed, or seemed to pass, wholly through the space it occupied. Yet when Hammond held a candle beyond it and I inserted my hand between the flame and my eyes, my hand vanished where it passed behind the creature. There was no darkness, no blur—simply an interruption, as though that portion of my hand had been cut away.

This experiment so disturbed us that we did not repeat it.

By then our noise had awakened the household. Knuckles beat upon the door, and Mrs. M—— demanded admission. After a hurried consultation, we allowed her to enter, together with Mr. S—— and the newspaper gentlemen. I cannot dwell upon the confusion that followed. They first believed that I had suffered an opium delirium and that Hammond, through friendship or professional caution, humored me. Mrs. M—— surveyed the broken pitcher and overturned furniture with greater indignation than alarm.

“An invisible man,” said one reporter, laughing uneasily. “Has he given his name?”

“Put your hand there,” Hammond answered.

The young man did so.

His laughter ended in the middle of a breath.

The other reporter touched the captive’s foot, uttered a cry, and backed against the wall. Mr. S—— refused all proof and declared that we had constructed a mechanical deception. The Thing then struggled, jerking the clothesline from his hand. He fled from the room, and before noon he and his family had quitted the house.

Mrs. M—— behaved with remarkable fortitude. After touching the creature’s shoulder once, she crossed herself—a practice foreign to her denomination—and asked whether it would damage the carpet.

The remainder of the night passed in examination. We moved the captive to the bed and bound it to the wooden frame. By touch we estimated its height at four feet six or seven inches. The head was large, the chest deep, the limbs short but enormously developed. There were five fingers upon each hand and five toes upon each foot. The skin was smooth except along the spine, where Hammond detected a narrow ridge of coarse bristles. The ears lay close to the skull. The mouth was wide, the chin retreating, and the nose so depressed that at first we believed it absent. Its sex was male.

At dawn we attempted to weigh it. A platform scale stood in the cellar for household provisions. We carried the creature downstairs wrapped in a blanket—not that the covering concealed it, but because the spectacle of ropes enclosing vacancy had become intolerable. Its struggles caused us great difficulty. Finally, with the assistance of the reporters, we placed it upon the scale.

It weighed eighty-seven pounds and four ounces.

The figure remains fixed in my memory with an absurd distinctness. That a being wholly outside the known order of creation should weigh eighty-seven pounds and four ounces seemed at once grotesque and blasphemous. Infinity had entered our boardinghouse and submitted to the grocer’s scale.

By nine o’clock Hammond had formed a plan.

“We cannot study what we cannot see,” he said. “But we may give it an exterior. Wax would be too hot. A coating of paint might poison it. Plaster offers the best chance.”

“You propose to make a mould?”

“Precisely.”

“It will struggle.”

“We shall anesthetize it.”

“With chloroform?”

“If it breathes air, chloroform should affect it. We must proceed cautiously.”

The undertaking occupied the greater part of the day. Hammond summoned, under a pledge of secrecy, Doctor Whitcomb, a surgeon of scientific tastes, and Mr. Laird, a modeller employed by an anatomical establishment. Their disbelief followed the same course as that of the others: smiles, remonstrance, touch, silence. Laird, who was a small, dry man, sat down after laying his palm upon the creature and declared that he would not continue for one hundred dollars. Ten minutes later curiosity overcame fear, and he consented for nothing.

We removed the captive to an unused room at the rear of the third story, where the floor could be protected with canvas. Hammond saturated a sponge with chloroform and held it near the invisible nostrils. The creature resisted furiously. Its head swung from side to side; its jaws closed upon the sponge; the straps cut into flesh we could feel but not see. Gradually its movements weakened. The breathing became deep and stertorous. The muscles relaxed.

“It is under,” said Hammond.

The words affected me strangely. All the common procedure of surgery—the bottle, sponge, measured pulse, professional whisper—surrounded a vacancy. Science had extended its instruments into nightmare and found nightmare obedient.

Laird worked rapidly. We first anointed the body with oil, judging its distribution by touch. Then plaster of Paris, mixed thin, was applied in successive layers. At every stroke of the brush a fragment of form came into existence. A shoulder whitened in the air. The curve of a flank appeared. Knotted arms emerged, not from shadow, but from nothingness. The face was left until last.

I had believed myself prepared. I was not.

As the plaster accumulated upon the head, the physiognomy of our prisoner declared itself by degrees: first the enormous arch of the jaw; then cheekbones placed high and far apart; a low, backward-sloping brow; nostrils like transverse wounds; and eyes—or the sealed spaces above the eyes—so disproportionately large that the whole countenance acquired an expression of vigilant malignity even in insensibility. The mouth stretched nearly from cheek to cheek. The neck was thick, the ears pointed, and from the crown backward ran the low serration which Hammond had mistaken for bristles.

No creation of fevered art—not the ghouls of Eastern legend, not the demons crouching in illuminated missals, not the obscene familiars etched by forgotten masters—ever united such deformity with such formidable coherence. It was grotesque, but not accidental. Every monstrous feature appeared adapted to a purpose. The short limbs were loaded with muscle; the fingers ended in blunt, horny nails; the chest had the depth of an ape’s; the feet were broad and prehensile. It resembled man as a nightmare resembles daily life: all the elements were recognizable, yet their relation was an outrage.

When the shell hardened, Laird divided it with extreme care, removed it in sections, and joined these to form a hollow negative. From that mould he afterward produced the solid cast now preserved in the museum. During the removal the creature began to recover consciousness. A hand moved under ours. Plaster cracked. The Thing uttered a sound deeper than any we had yet heard—less a cry than a vibration, which seemed to pass through the boards into the house.

We rebound it and carried it to the rear room.

What were we to do with it?

This question, simple in form, contained an abyss. To release the creature was impossible. It had attacked me without provocation and possessed strength sufficient to strangle a man. It might escape into the city, enter houses, murder sleepers, and remain forever beyond detection. Every locked chamber would be open to suspicion; every unexplained death might be laid at our consciences.

To kill it appeared scarcely less terrible. It was living, warm-blooded, susceptible to pain and anesthesia. However hideous its form, we could not prove it irrational. Its attack might have arisen from fear or hunger. Were we entitled to destroy a being merely because nature had concealed it from our eyes?

“To the law, it does not exist,” Hammond said. “We could not charge it, imprison it, or execute it. We could not even present it in court without being committed as lunatics.”

“Then we are its judges.”

“And its jailers.”

“And perhaps,” I said, “its murderers.”

He turned away.

We resolved to confine it temporarily while seeking some means of safe disposition. The room contained a massive bedstead fixed to the wall. To this we attached a broad leather collar, wrist manacles borrowed from a police acquaintance without explanation, and chains strong enough to hold a large dog. The windows were nailed shut. Food and water were placed within reach.

Here began the most melancholy portion of the affair.

The Thing would eat nothing.

We first offered bread, beef, milk, and water. The bowls remained untouched. Thinking that the creature might feed only in darkness, we extinguished the lamp and withdrew for several hours. Nothing had changed upon our return. Raw meat, cooked meat, fish, fruit, roots, sugar, eggs, grain, and spirits were successively tried. Hammond obtained a live pigeon and placed it in the room, but the bird survived, though it beat itself nearly senseless against the window in its terror. A rat, introduced in a cage, shrieked continuously until we removed it.

We attempted to force milk between the creature’s lips. It spat the liquid back upon us. Water produced the same result. Yet it must have required nourishment; its pulse weakened, and its weight declined.

During the first three days it remained violent. Whenever we approached, the chain lifted, the mattress sank, and invisible hands clutched at us. At night its efforts shook the bedstead and awakened persons on the floor below. The remaining boarders departed one by one. Mrs. M—— alone refused to abandon her property, though she would no longer ascend the upper staircase.

On the fourth day the captive ceased to struggle continually. It lay for hours in one position, revealed only by a depression in the bedding and the slow rise of a sheet laid over it. That sheet became our sole method of observing its posture. Draped upon the body, it presented a rude, moving statue: the head, shoulder, bent knees, and feet outlined beneath the linen. But the folds concealed the anatomical horror and imparted to it something pitiably human. Seen thus, the Monster became a patient; the Enigma, a prisoner.

Once, as I entered with broth, I found it sitting upright. The sheet hung from its head like a shroud. Its hands were extended toward the fading sunlight at the window. Whether it saw that light, or merely felt its warmth, I cannot say. It remained motionless while I approached. Then one hand moved over the air, slowly, as though following the flight of something invisible even to itself.

At that instant an idea, too vast and dreadful for calm consideration, entered me. Suppose the creature belonged to an order of matter existing beside ours, ordinarily imperceptible, as the microscopic world had existed around mankind before the lens disclosed it. Suppose countless such beings moved among us, touched us, watched us, passed through our dwellings, while some accident of appetite or atmosphere had rendered this one tangible. Was it blind in our world as we were blind to its form? Had it fallen among us from a medium in which it was as natural as we? Were we the apparitions by which it was tormented?

The thought caused me to set down the broth and leave the room.

Hammond rejected these speculations, though not with his former confidence.

“We possess facts enough,” he said, “without breeding cosmologies. It occupies space. It has weight. It breathes oxygen. Chloroform affects it. These are our footholds.”

“And its invisibility?”

“A property.”

“Of what?”

“Of matter.”

“Then why is the plaster upon it visible?”

“Because the plaster reflects light.”

“And its body does not?”

“Apparently.”

“Does light pass through it?”

“You know that it does not, or your hand would have remained visible behind it.”

“Then what becomes of the light?”

He was silent.

Our tests multiplied and explained nothing. A thermometer placed beneath the creature’s arm registered one hundred and three degrees. Its heartbeat, at first one hundred and forty, descended as weakness increased. The chest sounded resonant upon percussion. A needle inserted into the forearm emerged wet, though no stain could be seen until the drop touched a strip of white paper, where it appeared dark violet and rapidly faded. Under the microscope the residue showed oval bodies unlike human blood-corpuscles, but the sample was too poor for certainty.

The urine—if I may introduce so ordinary a detail into this unparalleled relation—was colorless and faintly phosphorescent in darkness. Whitcomb analyzed it and found compounds he could not identify. These results, which should have delighted men of science, filled us only with confusion. Each fact was a door opening upon another locked chamber.

The creature’s silence troubled me most. It uttered grunts under pain and the deep vibration already described, but no articulate sound. I spoke to it in English, French, and such fragments of Latin as I possessed. Hammond tried tones of reassurance, command, and anger. The Thing never responded. Yet I sometimes felt, when the sheet turned toward us, that it understood more than we imagined.

On the seventh day it permitted me to touch its hand without resistance.

The muscles had already wasted. The powerful fingers lay open upon the coverlet, and when I placed my palm against them they closed feebly—not in attack, but with the instinctive pressure of a sick child. I experienced then a revulsion of feeling for which I was wholly unprepared. The bite in my hand still ached; bruises darkened my throat; I remembered the jaws snapping beside my face. Nevertheless, pity entered into me and remained.

“We are starving it,” I told Hammond.

“It is starving itself.”

“Because we have not discovered its food.”

“We have offered every substance we can obtain.”

“Every substance known to us.”

“What would you have me do?”

“Release it.”

He looked toward the bed, where the sheet barely stirred.

“And if it recovers?”

“Perhaps it cannot live in captivity.”

“Neither can a tiger, indefinitely. Yet one does not loose it in Broadway from benevolence.”

“It may be intelligent.”

“Then it may also be malicious.”

“Is ugliness proof of malice? Is an attack committed in darkness proof of guilt? It may have awakened in my room as terrified as I was.”

Hammond’s expression hardened.

“You did not feel its hands on your throat.”

“I did.”

“Then remember them.”

I remembered them—and could not answer.

We considered conveying the captive to some isolated place, but every method involved danger. A covered wagon could not be trusted; a ship’s captain would demand explanation; a public institution would expose us to notoriety and perhaps to criminal proceedings. Moreover, the creature was now too weak to survive rough transport. Thus hesitation became a decision. We kept it where it was, and every day its substance diminished beneath our hands.

The plaster cast was completed on the ninth day. Laird brought it after midnight, wrapped in sacking. We placed it upright in the locked room adjoining the prisoner’s.

No description can reproduce the impression made by that white effigy. It stood four feet seven inches high, but its breadth and muscular development gave it a compressed power. The head thrust forward; the long arms hung almost to the knees; the huge, closed eyelids swelled beneath the receding forehead. Laird had preserved every surface faithfully—the wrinkled throat, the corded abdomen, the strange ridge along the spine, even the scars or natural markings which crossed the breast.

I passed from the cast to the bed and laid my hand upon the invisible body. The correspondence was exact, but altered by starvation. The cast represented strength; the captive had become bone and loose skin. Between them lay only a wall, yet they seemed memorial and corpse.

On the eleventh night I remained alone with the Thing. Hammond had been called to a difficult confinement, and Whitcomb had ceased his visits, alleging professional obligations, though I believe horror had conquered curiosity. Rain struck the nailed windows. The city was muffled in mist, and the house, emptied of boarders, seemed to listen to itself.

Near two in the morning the chain moved.

I approached the bed. The sheet had slipped to the floor, leaving the creature once more entirely absent. I could hear its breathing, shallow and irregular.

“Do you suffer?” I asked.

The question was absurd, yet the darkness made speech necessary.

A hand touched my wrist.

I did not withdraw. The fingers moved upward over my sleeve, reached my face, and rested there. They were cold. For several moments the invisible palm covered my cheek, not violently, but with an incomprehensible deliberation. Then the arm fell.

The deep vibration sounded within the room.

This time it seemed neither anger nor pain. It possessed cadence. It might have been a word.

I bent close. “What are you?”

The vibration came again, weaker.

“Where did you come from?”

Only breath answered me.

I moistened its lips with water. A little was swallowed. Encouraged, I tried again, but the jaws closed. I sat beside it until dawn, holding a hand I could not see. Toward morning I slept in the chair.

Hammond awakened me shortly after seven.

One glance at my face caused him to go to the bed. He examined the captive, counting the pulse with his watch. After a minute he looked up.

“It will not live through the day.”

“Can nothing be done?”

“Nothing, unless we know what sustains it.”

“Try injection.”

“Of what? Beef extract? Saline solution? We might prolong the agony.”

At noon the breathing became convulsive. The chain rattled at intervals, not from struggling but from spasms in the limbs. We covered the body with the sheet. Its outline had become so attenuated that the ribs showed plainly beneath the linen. The monstrous head moved from side to side. The hands opened and closed.

Mrs. M—— came to the threshold but would not enter. Laird arrived and stood by the window, weeping silently, though he had once called the creature an infernal vermin. Hammond listened to the chest.

At seventeen minutes past two, according to his watch, the Thing raised itself halfway from the mattress.

The sheet formed, for one instant, the outline of that impossible face. The mouth opened. A long breath passed through it—a sigh, almost human. Then the figure collapsed.

The cloth became still.

Hammond waited, applied his ear, and touched the throat.

“It is dead,” he said.

Death did not render the body visible.

That fact, though we should have anticipated it, produced a final and peculiar horror. We had unconsciously expected dissolution to restore the creature to the common laws of matter, as if invisibility were a vital action which the heart had maintained. But the corpse remained a vacancy: cold, ponderable, palpable, and unseen.

A post-mortem examination was discussed. Hammond desired it on scientific grounds; I opposed it with an emotion I cannot justify. The captive had endured enough at our hands. To cut that defenseless body—watching instruments enter empty air, seeing cavities opened where no wound appeared—seemed an indignity from which every instinct recoiled. Hammond yielded, perhaps because his curiosity, like mine, had become contaminated by remorse.

We resolved upon secret burial.

After midnight we wrapped the body in oilcloth, placed it in a narrow wooden box, and carried it down the back stairs. The weight had fallen to fifty-eight pounds. Rain had softened the garden soil. Beneath the oldest pear-tree, near the ruined wall, Hammond and I dug a grave four feet deep.

No prayer was spoken. What ritual could have applied? We did not know whether we buried beast, man, demon, accident, or exile. Yet when the box was lowered, and the first shovelful of earth struck its lid, I felt that we had entombed not merely a creature, but a reproach.

We filled the grave, replaced the turf as carefully as possible, and scattered dead leaves over the ground. By dawn no casual eye could have detected disturbance.

The official story was that experiments upon a deformed ape had been conducted in the house and that the animal died. Those who knew more preserved silence. Mrs. M—— remained there until the property was demolished six years later. I asked her, before the garden was destroyed, whether anything unusual had occurred near the pear-tree.

“Nothing,” she answered. Then, after reflection, she added: “Except that no grass ever grew properly on the grave.”

The cast passed into Hammond’s possession and afterward into the museum already mentioned. It is labeled *Congenital Anthropoid Monstrosity—Origin Unknown*. Students regard it as a fraud, a pathological exaggeration, or the caprice of some sculptor familiar with the grotesque. I have stood unrecognized while young men joked before it. Once I heard a professor explain, with admirable precision, why no such organism could have existed.

I made no contradiction.

What remains to be said? I have presented the facts with as much exactitude as memory, notes, and surviving witnesses permit. Opium may explain my readiness for terror, but it cannot explain the marks upon my throat, the broken furniture, the testimony of Hammond and the others, the reading of the scale, the action of chloroform, the cast, the chain that rose from the floor, or the grave beneath the pear-tree. Fraud may be alleged; insanity may be alleged; collusion, mesmeric influence, collective hallucination, and every convenient phrase by which ignorance disguises itself may be alleged. I have neither the power nor the desire to compel conviction.

There are nights, however, when conviction compels me.

I awake and hear the timber settle. The curtain sways where no wind enters. A cushion, released from pressure, seems slowly to regain its shape. Then the old question returns—not what the creature was, but whether it was alone.

We inhabit a world translated for us by five imperfect senses. Of the immensities surrounding and perhaps penetrating our existence, we perceive only those fragments which our organs have been fashioned to receive. The air may be crowded with forms for which the eye has no obedience. The locked room may contain its population. A hand may hover an inch from ours and remain beyond all knowledge until anger, hunger, accident, or some rare conjunction of physical laws gives it weight.

Vision is not truth. Silence is not emptiness.

I learned this upon a May night in New York, when something without color, shadow, or name laid its warm fingers upon my throat—and breathed.

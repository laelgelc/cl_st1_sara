On Christmas Eve, after the stockings had been hung where they could most conveniently be pulled down and examined every nine minutes, Ned and Lucy got into their beds and called for their father.

They did not call timidly. They had brought their father up with considerable care, and had taught him that when they were in bed he must come promptly, sit between them, tuck in the blankets twice, and tell a story until they were sleepy. Whether they ever became sleepy was uncertain; but he generally did.

“I’ve told all the stories,” he said, appearing in the doorway.

“You haven’t told tonight’s,” said Ned.

“And it must be a Christmas story,” said Lucy.

“With a moral?” their father asked cautiously.

“No,” said both children.

“Then I don’t know one.”

“You can make one.”

“Oh, anybody can make a story without a moral,” he said, sitting down. “Once there was a—”

“Not a bear,” said Lucy.

“Nor a prince,” said Ned. “Princes are silly.”

“Once there was a little Pony Engine,” their father began, “and it lived in a Boston railway depot with its mother.”

“Where was its father?” Lucy asked immediately.

“He was dead.”

Lucy’s lip moved.

“That isn’t Christmasy,” Ned objected.

“It gets Christmasy,” said their father. “The father engine had been drowned while crossing a bridge in a freshet. He was a strong, handsome freight locomotive, with a black chimney, brass bands, and the largest, kindest cow-catcher you ever saw. One dark night he found the bridge washed away, and before he could put on his air-brakes—”

“Engines don’t put them on,” said Ned. “The engineer does.”

“This engine put on his own. He had been carefully educated. But it was too late, and he went into the river with twenty-seven cars of flour.”

“Was the flour spoiled?” Lucy asked.

“Just perfectly spoiled. The water made it into bread before morning.”

“It couldn’t,” said Ned.

“Very well. Once there was a little Pony Engine—”

“No, no!” they cried. They knew that beginning again was one of his worst habits.

“So the poor mother engine had to bring up her child all alone. She had once been a beautiful passenger locomotive, but she had injured herself pulling an excursion train up a hill, and now she wheezed in her chest and had to remain about the depot doing little jobs. She was very tender—”

“She had a tender,” Ned corrected.

“She was both kinds. She loved her little engine, and on cold nights she used to draw close, lay her buffer against his, and tell him about his brave father. The little Pony Engine would stand under the great glass roof, with the snow sliding down outside and the red and green signal lamps shining through it, and wish he were grown up.

“‘Mother,’ he would say, ‘when shall I have a cow-catcher?’

“‘When you are older,’ she would answer, sighing softly through her air-brakes.

“‘And brass bands?’

“‘If you are good.’

“‘And may I pull the Pacific Express?’

“Then his mother would whistle very sadly, because she knew what ambition had done to several members of the family.

“‘My dear little Pony,’ she would say, ‘you are built for light work. You may carry milk, or take a lady’s private car to the seaside, or perhaps pull three picnic coaches if the weather is fine. But never, never race with the Pacific Express.’

“‘Why not?’

“‘Because it is the greatest engine in the country. Its driving-wheels are as tall as houses, it eats twelve tons of coal before breakfast, and when it comes through the depot the windows shake and all the small engines pretend they were not looking.’

“Now the little Pony Engine loved his mother, but he did not care for milk trains, and he did not intend to spend his life taking picnics out and bringing them home sunburnt. He wanted to rush across the whole country with bells ringing and passengers saying, ‘What a splendid engine!’”

“I’d rather have an electric train,” Ned said.

“There weren’t any.”

“When was this?”

“Long ago.”

“How long?”

“On Christmas Eve.”

“That’s not how long.”

“It was the longest Christmas Eve ever known,” their father replied, and went on quickly. “That night the mother engine was ill. She had taken cold from standing beside an open freight door, and the doctor came and examined her boiler.

“‘You must stay in the roundhouse,’ he said, ‘and eat nothing but very soft coal.’

“So she kissed the little Pony with her front buffer and told him not to leave the depot. Then she went to sleep, coughing little clouds of steam.

“But about eleven o’clock the Pony Engine heard a deep roaring far down the line. The rails began to hum, the signal lamps winked, and a switch engine cried:

“‘Clear the track! The Pacific Express is coming!’

“The Pony Engine looked westward. He could see the Express’s headlight, large and white as the moon, and he thought, ‘If I start now, I shall reach San Francisco before it, and then everybody will know that I am a real passenger locomotive.’

“He remembered his mother’s warning. He remembered it extremely well, which made what he did much worse. Then he slipped out of the depot and hurried away.”

“Without an engineer?” Ned demanded.

“The engineer was asleep in the cab.”

“Then he had an engineer.”

“He had one, but he was of no use. He was asleep so thoroughly that he did not wake even when his cap blew off in Worcester. The Pony Engine ran faster and faster through the snowy Massachusetts night, past villages with candles in their windows, past farmhouses where children had gone to bed, and past church steeples ringing Christmas bells.

“Presently he heard, a long way behind:

“‘Whooooo—whooooo!’

“It was the Pacific Express.

“‘You can’t catch me!’ whistled the Pony Engine.

“And the Express kept gaining on him.

“He rushed through Springfield and on toward the Berkshire hills. The grade grew steeper, his little wheels slipped, and his breath came in short puffs, but he would not turn back. Then he saw the black mouth of a tunnel.

“He had never been through a tunnel alone. It looked as if the mountain had opened its mouth to swallow locomotives, and it was so dark inside that he could not see his own headlight except where it shone, which is the worst kind of darkness.

“‘Pacific Express!’ he cried bravely, and plunged in.

“The sound struck the stone walls and came back: ‘Express! Press! Ess! Ss!’

“He thought other engines were chasing him. Sparks flew from his chimney and bats flew into his cab, and the engineer remained asleep, though a bat sat on his nose for seventeen miles.”

“Bats don’t sit on noses,” said Ned.

“This one had been up all day and was tired.

“At last the Pony Engine came out into moonlight, and he was so glad that he laughed with his whistle. But behind him the tunnel shone red, and the great Pacific Express burst out with smoke rolling above it.

“And the Express kept gaining on him.

“By morning the Pony had crossed New York State. Lake Erie lay gray and cold beside the track, and ice covered the telegraph wires. He was hungry, but there was no time to stop for coal. He picked up lumps that freight engines had dropped, and once he swallowed a red-hot cinder by mistake and hiccuped sparks all the way through Ohio.

“‘Go home!’ called the sensible engines on the side tracks.

“‘I am going to San Francisco!’ he answered.

“‘You are going to pieces!’ said an old locomotive with spectacles around its headlight.

“But the Pony Engine dashed on through Indiana and Illinois, across flat country where the wind had room to run beside him. The snow had disappeared, and the prairie grass bent in silver waves under the moon. Whenever he looked back, he saw the headlight of the Express, larger than before.

“‘Pacific Express!’ he panted.

“And the Express kept gaining on him.

“When he reached the Mississippi, the bridge was open.”

“Why?” Lucy asked.

“To let a steamboat through.”

“In winter?”

“It was an all-season steamboat.”

“He would have to stop,” Ned said.

“That was precisely what the bridge keeper thought, and he waved a red lantern. But the Pony Engine was too frightened to stop. He was going sixty miles an hour, then seventy, then eighty, and when he reached the river he shut his eyes, held his breath, and jumped.”

“Engines can’t jump.”

“Not ordinary engines. A Pony Engine can. That is why it is called a pony.”

“It isn’t,” Ned said.

“Once there was a little—”

“All right, he jumped,” Ned said hurriedly.

“He sailed over the Mississippi with his wheels turning in the air and the engineer sleeping backward in the cab. Under him the steamboat captain looked up and said, ‘Merry Christmas!’ because he could think of nothing else. The Pony landed on the farther bank, bounced twice, and ran on without spilling more than half the water in his boiler.

“Then the Pacific Express came to the open bridge.”

“It had to stop,” said Lucy.

“It jumped too.”

“But you said it wasn’t a Pony.”

“No, but it had been practicing.”

“And the Express kept gaining on him.

“Now the little engine began to feel lonely. He thought of the Boston depot and the warm roundhouse, and of his mother asleep with her injured chest, expecting to find him beside her when she woke. He wished he had obeyed. He would gladly have carried milk for the rest of his life and never complained, not even when the cans leaked. But he was too far away to turn back, for the Express was behind him, and he believed it meant to catch him and drag him home by the tender.”

“That would have been good,” said Lucy.

“He didn’t think so. He hurried across Iowa, but toward evening a pack of prairie wolves began to follow the track. Their eyes were green, their teeth were white, and they ran beside him, biting at his wheels.”

“They’d break their teeth,” Ned said.

“They did. You never heard such a cracking. But there were more wolves behind those wolves, with entirely new teeth, and the Pony had to go faster. He blew steam at them and threw coal, and the sleeping engineer’s lunchbox fell out and struck the largest wolf on the head.

“‘Pacific Express!’ cried the Pony.

“‘Express!’ howled the wolves.

“‘Press!’ echoed the hills.

“And the Express kept gaining on him.

“Night fell again, and the Pony Engine was so tired that he began to dream while running. He dreamed that the track curled itself around him like a snake, and that out of the darkness came a monstrous locomotive with one enormous wheel in the middle of its boiler and six chimneys arranged like teeth. Its headlight was an eye, its cow-catcher was a beard, and it rolled along without rails.

“‘I am the Engine That Catches Disobedient Engines,’ it said.

“‘There is no such engine,’ whispered the Pony.

“‘That is what makes me so terrible,’ said the monster.

“It rolled nearer on its one wheel, which was square.”

“A square wheel couldn’t roll,” Ned observed.

“That made it roll awfully severe. Bump! Bump! Bump! It opened its furnace door, and inside were all the lost whistles and broken bells of naughty little locomotives. Then it reached out a long coupling chain—”

Lucy pulled the blanket to her chin.

“Don’t make it too horrid,” she said.

“I shall make it mild. Just as the chain touched him, the Pony woke and found that he had run into a water tank. The spout poured water over him, cold enough to put out his fire, and for a minute he thought he was dead.

“But he wasn’t?”

“Not yet. He shook himself, built up steam, and hurried on. The wolves had gone, and the nightmare engine had gone, and the prairie stretched empty behind him—except for one great white light.

“And the Express kept gaining on him.

“By the time he reached Nebraska, he could no longer say the whole name. He puffed, ‘Pacific ’Press!’ and when he reached Wyoming he could only say, ‘’Press!’ The country climbed toward the sky. The rails twisted along cliffs, snow covered the cuts, and the wind came down from the mountains and tried to push him backward.

“Big engines waited to help trains up the grade.

“‘Where is your train?’ one asked.

“‘I haven’t any,’ gasped the Pony.

“‘Then what are you running from?’

“He looked back at the approaching headlight.

“‘’Press!’

“The big engines laughed until their bells rang, but the Pony did not stop. He climbed through the Rocky Mountains, over trestles so high that the rivers looked like blue threads below, and around bends so sharp that he could see his own tender coming after him. Once an eagle flew alongside and asked if he had any mail for Denver.”

“Eagles don’t talk,” Lucy said.

“This one was in the postal service.

“The Pony Engine had lost his cap, although he had never worn one, and his paint was scratched, his little brass bell hung sideways, and his coal was nearly gone. Yet every time he thought he could rest, he heard the thunder of the Pacific Express.

“And the Express kept gaining on him.

“He crossed Utah, where the salt flats glittered like frozen ponds, and Nevada, where the desert was so dry that his steam evaporated before he could use it. Lizards sat beside the rails and warmed their hands at his wheels. He begged a cactus for water, but the cactus said it was saving what it had.

“Then he came to the Sierra Nevada.

“The mountains stood shoulder to shoulder, all white with snow, and the track went up and up until it seemed fastened to the stars. The Pony’s wheels turned, but he scarcely moved. Behind him came the Express, roaring louder than an avalanche.

“‘Ess!’ he cried, for he had no breath for more.

“He tried one slope and slipped down. He tried another and slipped farther. Then he left the rails altogether and began to leap from ledge to ledge like a mountain goat.”

“With cars?” asked Ned.

“He had no cars.”

“Or rails?”

“No rails.”

“Then he couldn’t steer.”

“He steered with his cow-catcher.”

“You said he hadn’t grown one yet,” Ned said triumphantly.

Their father looked troubled. “It grew on the journey.”

“Cow-catchers don’t grow.”

“This was Christmas Eve.”

“It was Christmas Eve when he started,” Lucy said. “It must be two days later.”

“It was still Christmas Eve in California,” said their father. “The time is different there.”

“Not two days different.”

“Would you like me to begin?”

“No!”

“So the Pony Engine bounded over the Sierra Nevada, from rock to rock, with snow flying from his wheels. He frightened a family of goats, who had never seen such poor climbing. He jumped a ravine, landed in a pine tree, ran down the trunk, and came upon the track again on the western side.

“Below him lay California, green and shining, and far away was San Francisco, with the Pacific Ocean beyond it.

“‘I shall win!’ he whispered. Or rather he said, ‘’Ss!’ which meant the same thing by then.

“He tore down the mountain. The rails sang, the telegraph poles flew past like fence pickets, and his bell rang without being asked. He crossed valleys and vineyards, rushed past stations where men waved Christmas newspapers, and smelled the sea for the first time.

“But the Pacific Express came behind him, closer and closer, its headlight filling the whole track.

“And the Express kept gaining on him.

“The Pony could see the San Francisco depot. He could see people waiting on the platform, and porters with trucks, and boys selling oranges, and a stationmaster holding up both hands. He thought they had come to cheer him.

“‘Stop!’ shouted the stationmaster.

“But the Pony’s brakes were worn out, his whistle was only a squeak, and terror had made him foolish. He went through the depot doors, across the waiting room, under the clock, between two rows of astonished passengers, and out through the opposite wall.”

“Was the engineer still asleep?” Ned asked.

“He woke in the waiting room and stepped off.”

“That was convenient.”

“Very. The Pony Engine rushed down the wharf. He saw the black water ahead and tried to stop. His little wheels struck sparks, his brakes screamed, and he cried one last, tiny ‘’Ss!’

“Then he plunged into the Pacific Ocean.

“There was a great hiss, a cloud of steam, and a few bubbles. His bell rang once beneath the water—ding!—and the little Pony Engine was never seen again.”

Lucy’s eyes filled, and Ned looked firmly at the ceiling.

“What did the Express do?” he asked.

“It stopped at the depot, precisely on time. It had never been racing at all.”

There was silence.

“And his mother?” Lucy whispered.

“When she woke in Boston and found him gone, she suffered a complete breakdown.”

“Her machinery?”

“Also her feelings. They took her to the repair shop, but she had brain fever and whistled for him every night.”

“Engines haven’t brains,” Ned said, though less confidently.

“This one had. That is the moral.”

“What is?”

“Children, obey your parents.”

“You said there wouldn’t be a moral,” said Ned.

“I said I didn’t know a story without one. This moral slipped in while I wasn’t looking.”

“But it wasn’t Christmasy,” Lucy complained. “There wasn’t any Christmas except bells and newspapers.”

“The Pony was going to San Francisco to buy his mother a Christmas present.”

“You never said so.”

“I was saving it.”

“What present?”

“A new chest.”

“You said he wanted to beat the Express,” Ned persisted.

“He had two reasons.”

“And he crossed the country in two days.”

“One day,” said their father.

“He couldn’t.”

“Then perhaps it took a year, and he reached San Francisco on the next Christmas Eve.”

“But you said the Express was behind him all the time.”

“It was an unusually patient Express.”

The children stared at him. He rose and moved toward the door.

“And how could his mother have brain fever?” Ned called.

“And why did the Pony’s cow-catcher grow?” Lucy demanded.

“And how could bread bake under water?”

Their father ran.

They sprang from their beds and chased him down the hall, their bare feet pattering after him while he cried that it was Christmas Eve and all well-brought-up children should be asleep. They caught him at the stairs, where he surrendered and promised that next year he would tell a true story about a reindeer who operated an elevator.

Later, when Lucy had been carried back sniffling and Ned had been tucked in for the third time, Ned said into the darkness:

“I knew the Pony Engine wasn’t real.”

Lucy made no answer.

“And I didn’t cry,” he added.

“You put up your lip,” she said sleepily.

“I did not.”

“You did.”

“I was only thinking.”

“What about?”

Ned listened to the winter wind, which sounded a little like a far-off whistle crossing the country.

“Nothing,” he said.

Into Teloth, the granite city, there came at evening a youth in a robe of faded purple, carrying a silver-stringed lute beneath his arm. Dust lay pale upon his sandals, and burrs clung to the hem of his garment; yet his face was fair and unwearied, and his hair shone yellow in the last light, as though the west had crowned him with a remnant of its fire. The gate-wardens, who were square of beard and iron-grey of raiment, asked his name and craft.

“I am Iranon,” said the youth, “a singer and a prince in exile. From Aira, the city of marble and beryl, was I driven in childhood, and through many lands have I wandered seeking the road home.”

But the wardens knew no Aira, nor cared they greatly for princes who wore tattered robes. They led him before the archons of Teloth, who sat in a chamber without hangings, under lamps whose flames were small and useful.

“Wherefore hast thou come?” asked the eldest archon.

“To sing,” answered Iranon, “and peradventure to learn from travellers what path leadeth unto Aira.”

Then the archon frowned, for in Teloth every man wrought with stone, metal, grain, or law, and no man was suffered to labour only at delight.

“Song buildeth no wall,” he said. “It draweth no water and grindeth no corn. All here must serve, and song is folly.”

Iranon lifted his head. “Is not joy also a service? The wall preserveth the body, but what preserveth the heart within it? The mill sustaineth life, but for what end is life sustained if there be no moonlight, no music, and no memory of fair things?”

“These are idle riddles,” said the archon. “On the morrow thou shalt work in the stone-yards.”

So Iranon went from the chamber and slept beneath a portico where the rain dripped through cracks in the roof. Above him rose Teloth’s grey domes, and beyond them the stars appeared one by one, remote and glittering. He touched the strings of his lute very softly, lest the watchmen hear, and sang of Aira.

He sang of towers whose roofs were beaten gold, and of walls built of veined marble and pale-green beryl; of the river Nithra, clear as crystal, flowing beneath balconies wreathed in roses; and of the tiny Kra, that murmured through gardens of myrrh and pomegranate. He sang of white roads beneath flowering trees, of cerulean pools wherein silver fish moved like fallen stars, and of the palace where his father had feasted kings beneath an ivory dome. There, he said, maidens in saffron veils danced upon floors of onyx, and old men smiled when young men sang, because wisdom had not yet forgotten wonder.

From an alley came a boy thinly clad, with black hair and solemn eyes. His name was Romnod, and he had long hated the clang of Teloth’s hammers and the maxims of its elders.

“Was Aira truly thus?” he whispered.

“Fairer,” said Iranon, “for no song can contain it.”

“Then why dost thou tarry here?”

“I tarry nowhere willingly. I seek the road, but men know only the paths to markets, quarries, and graves.”

Romnod sat beside him until the moon had crossed the portico. Iranon told how in childhood he had played under green vines while the Nithra flashed beyond a balustrade; how a dark usurper had entered Aira with spearmen from Sidrak; and how faithful servants had borne the prince away. Of those servants he could recall neither face nor name, for grief had veiled them, yet of Aira he remembered every stone and blossom.

On the morrow Iranon was set to labour among the quarrymen. They gave him a coarse tunic and ordered him to lay aside his purple robe, but he would not. Under a sky the colour of lead he carried blocks until his hands bled, whilst overseers mocked the delicacy of a prince. At noon he sang to make the stones less heavy, and the master struck him, saying that rhythm weakened diligence. At dusk the archons proclaimed that he must return each day and speak no more of countries which did not pay tribute to Teloth.

That night Romnod came again.

“Beyond the southern hills lieth Oonai,” said the boy. “Caravans speak of it as the city of lutes and dancing. There men cast flowers before singers, and no archon counteth every hour. Take me with thee, Iranon, for I would rather seek a city I have not seen than inherit this one.”

So before dawn they passed through a postern and went southward, Iranon in his tattered purple and Romnod in a labourer’s brown cloak. Behind them Teloth lay beneath its smoke, solid and joyless; before them stretched the stony hills, touched at their crests by a thin gold light.

For many years they wandered, though to Iranon the years seemed no more than changes in the wind. They crossed Narthos, where black pines leaned over ravines and wolves cried at sunset; they traversed the salt flats of Yath, wherein the moon made a second sky beneath their feet; and they slept in ruined temples whose gods had lost their names. In villages Iranon sang for bread, and sometimes men listened, but more often they demanded jests, war-ballads, or prophecies concerning the price of grain. Always he sang instead of Aira, and always he asked whence ran the road to the city of marble and beryl.

Romnod grew tall, and the first shadow of a beard came upon his cheeks; but Iranon remained as he had entered Teloth, slender and golden-haired, with the same clear brow and eyes wherein distant towers seemed reflected. When Romnod marvelled, Iranon answered that those born in Aira carried its youth until they came home.

At last, from a pass above a warm and fragrant valley, they beheld Oonai. Its domes were rose and amber in the evening, and its terraces descended among groves of fig and almond to a river shining blue. Bells sounded from towers of tinted marble; fountains lifted crystal plumes above gardens; and along the walls fluttered banners of scarlet, peacock-green, and cloth-of-gold.

“Surely,” cried Romnod, “this is almost Aira!”

But Iranon looked long upon the city and shook his head.

“Aira’s towers are more slender, and the Nithra is clearer than that river. Yet perhaps the people of Oonai remember beauty, and through them we may learn tidings of my home.”

At the gate stood revellers crowned with vines, and when they saw the fair stranger and his silver-stringed lute, they drew him into their procession. Thus Iranon entered Oonai beneath showers of rose petals, whilst Romnod laughed among girls whose bracelets chimed as they walked.

That night Iranon sang in the palace of the magistrate. Lamps of perfumed oil burned in chains of gold; wine glowed purple in cups of jade; and the guests reclined upon satin couches beneath painted ceilings. Iranon sang first of twilight on the Nithra, when swans passed between the reflections of towers. He sang of the little Kra beneath green branches, and of moonbeams lying white upon the palace floor. He sang of a kingly father, grave and gentle, who listened from an ivory throne; and of Aira’s people waiting through the years for their lost prince to return.

When the last chord faded, the feasters were silent. Then they shouted his name, and women cast necklaces at his feet, and the magistrate placed upon him a robe of fresh purple clasped with beryl.

“Remain in Oonai,” they said, “and sing every night, for no voice so sweet hath been heard among us.”

Iranon dwelt therefore in a chamber overlooking gardens where fountains murmured continually. Servants combed his golden hair and brought him fruits upon silver plates. Wherever he walked, men greeted him, and children followed to hear of Aira. For a season he believed that Oonai might be the vestibule of his homeland, and that some caravan master or mariner would at last recall the road.

But Romnod drank deeply of Oonai. He clothed himself in scarlet, bound vine leaves about his brow, and went nightly among dancers, dice-players, and drinkers. At first he returned before dawn and begged Iranon for one more tale of the Nithra. Later he returned at noon, wine-reddened and laughing, and asked instead for coins. Still later he ceased to return, save when feasts had ended and other doors were shut.

The years moved over Oonai like silent birds. Fashions altered, magistrates died, and children who had followed Iranon grew broad and heavy. Romnod’s beard became thick, then streaked with grey; his clear gaze clouded, his laughter coarsened, and his hands trembled when no cup was near. Iranon remained young.

“Come away,” Iranon pleaded one moonless night. “No man here knoweth Aira, and beauty without remembrance is but a flower cast into wine. Let us seek eastward, where the merchants of Sidrak travel.”

Romnod lay amid cushions, his face swollen and old beneath a wilted crown.

“Aira?” he muttered. “We have fountains, girls, and wine. What more could thy dream-city give?”

“Not dream-city,” said Iranon. “My home.”

Romnod laughed until coughing seized him. “Then seek it alone, prince. Teloth was stone, and Oonai is wine, and perhaps Aira is only the song between them.”

Before dawn Romnod died. They found him beside an overturned cup, whilst musicians played in the courtyard and green morning shone through the lattice. The people of Oonai buried him beyond the southern wall, and for one evening Iranon sang a lament above his grave. Few attended, for a troupe of masked acrobats had newly arrived from Thalarion.

Thereafter Oonai wearied of Iranon. His songs had not changed, and the revellers desired novelty. They praised shrill pipes, dancers with painted limbs, dwarfs who mocked the gods, and beasts trained to bow before drunken nobles. When Iranon sang of marble and beryl, men spoke over him; when he named the Nithra, women yawned; and when he told of the exiled prince, boys flung nutshells at his lute.

The magistrate’s steward took back the beryl clasp, then the silken chamber, and at last the purple robe. Yet Iranon found his old garment folded in a chest, faded and dusty but still his own, and he put it on gladly.

So Iranon went from Oonai, and none followed him. Behind the gates sounded cymbals, laughter, and the clatter of cups, whilst before him lay a pale road wandering between fields.

It is not known how long he journeyed thereafter. He passed through cities where men sold prayers in brass vessels, and through countries where queens slept in crystal tombs. He saw the sea beneath a green moon and asked sailors whether Aira stood beyond it; he climbed mountains whose snows were pink at dawn and questioned hermits who had forgotten speech. Ever his robe grew more ragged, yet his face remained youthful, and ever he sang the same song.

At length he came to a barren country of low hills, thorn trees, and sluggish streams. There were no marble towers, but only huts of clay, and the wind smelled of wet earth. Under an ancient willow sat a shepherd, exceedingly old, watching lean sheep crop the coarse grass.

Iranon greeted him and asked, as he had asked ten thousand others, “Knowest thou the way to Aira, city of marble and beryl, beside the clear Nithra and the murmuring Kra?”

The shepherd raised clouded eyes.

“What names hast thou spoken?”

“Aira, my homeland. The Nithra runneth beneath its rose-hung balconies, and the little Kra singeth among gardens. I am Iranon, son of Aira’s king, exiled in childhood when spearmen came from Sidrak.”

The old man stared long, and a troubled memory moved in his face.

“In my youth,” he said slowly, “there dwelt near this place a beggar named Ranon, who had a yellow-haired son. They slept in an abandoned byre and ate what the villagers gave them. The boy was mocked because he would not herd swine nor gather stones, but made songs and wore scraps of purple stolen from refuse heaps.”

Iranon’s hand tightened upon his lute.

“Thou mistakest another for me.”

“Peradventure. Yet the boy called himself Iranon, adding greatness to his poor name. He spoke of a golden city he had fashioned in thought, with a river called Nithra because a little muddy brook ran behind the byre, and a stream called Kra because frogs croaked in a ditch. His father was no king. He died by the roadside in winter. Thereafter the boy vanished.”

“No,” said Iranon, and the word was scarcely sound. “Aira hath ivory palaces, and my father wore a crown.”

“I have lived fourscore years in this land,” replied the shepherd, “and never was there an Aira. Children make kingdoms when the world giveth them only mud.”

Then the stars appeared one by one above the waste, as once they had appeared above Teloth. Moonlight fell upon Iranon’s tattered purple, and his lute slipped from his hands. No song arose when its strings struck the earth.

For the first time he looked not toward a distant tower, but backward upon the road: Teloth with its granite laws, Oonai with its wine-stained garlands, Romnod young beneath the portico and old beside the overturned cup, and all the countries traversed beneath an undiminishing hope. The Nithra darkened within his memory. The tiny Kra ceased to murmur. Marble became clay, beryl became broken green glass, and the king upon the ivory throne had no face.

Then the years which dream had held at bay came suddenly upon Iranon.

His golden hair whitened and fell thinly about his brow; his fair cheeks sank; his shoulders bent beneath an invisible burden; and the hand that reached toward the vanished city became knotted and tremulous. The shepherd cried out, for where a youth had stood there now tottered a man older than sorrow.

But Iranon’s eyes brightened once more.

“See,” he whispered, gazing beyond the willow. “The moon shineth upon Aira. The gates are open, and they have kindled golden lamps for their prince. I hear the Nithra, and my father calleth me home.”

Before him lay only a marsh, silvered by moonlight. Mist covered its black pools, and reeds bent above hidden quicksand. The shepherd warned him, but Iranon did not hear. He gathered his ruined robe about him and walked toward the imagined gates.

With each step he sank deeper. Yet he smiled, for in the trembling water he beheld crystal fountains, and in the pale mist towers of marble and beryl. The mud closed over his knees, his breast, and at last his white head; and still, faintly, there seemed to sound a song of green branches, rose-hung balconies, and moonbeams upon an onyx floor.

At dawn the shepherd found the silver-stringed lute beside the marsh. He carried it home, but none in that country could make music from it. And from that night onward men said the world had grown older, because somewhere beneath the dark mire lay the last prince of a city that had never been, and with him slept a portion of youth and beauty which no granite labour, no jeweled revel, and no truth however stern could ever restore.

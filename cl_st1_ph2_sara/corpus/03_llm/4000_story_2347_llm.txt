### Dave Regan’s Fishing Party

There was a payable reef somewhere in the range behind Regan’s claim. There is always a payable reef somewhere behind a claim, just as there is always a fortune waiting for the man who has spent his last pound proving that it isn’t where he thought it was. Dave Regan knew the reef was there because the country “made” that way; Jim Bently doubted it because Dave knew; and Andy Page said nothing, but went on sinking.

They had bottomed one shaft, abandoned two, and were baling a fourth when the water beat them. The claim lay in a narrow gully, with scrub on the ridges, a creek under the bank, and a shanty, called the Royal Hotel, a mile down the track. There were trout in the creek—or blackfish, or cod, according to the man who spoke last. The fish were generally seen by somebody who had no hook.

The three men lived in a bark camp, and with them lived Tommy.

Tommy was a curly, yellowish retriever of no particular family and every possible good intention. He had a broad grin, a wet tongue, and a tail that went like a stock-whip whenever a man looked at him. He would fetch anything. He fetched sticks that weren’t thrown, boots that were wanted, the frying-pan when it was hot, and once he fetched the publican’s trousers while the publican was bathing in the creek. This last performance had made Tommy unpopular at the Royal, though Dave maintained that no dog could be expected to know that a publican was using his trousers when he had left them on a stump.

On the afternoon when the trouble began, Jim came back from the creek with an empty line and a temper.

“There’s fish there,” he said, “but they won’t rise.”

“Too educated,” said Andy.

“They’ve had that bait before,” said Dave. “What we want is something fresh.”

Jim looked at him. “If you’re goin’ to invent another way of fishin’, invent it without me.”

Dave took no notice. He sat on an upturned bucket, filled his pipe, and studied the creek through the saplings. Dave’s ideas generally came while another man was working. Presently he slapped his knee.

“Powder,” he said.

Jim said he had expected worse.

Now, using a charge to bring fish to the surface was neither new nor respectable, but Dave did not propose to drop a common stick of blasting powder into the water and trust to Providence, for he had learned by experience that Providence was careless with fuses. His plan was scientific. He would make a waterproof cartridge large enough to stun all the fish in the hole, without, as he explained, “needlessly disturbing the district.”

Andy, who could make anything from a cradle to a coffin if somebody else supplied the measurements, undertook the manufacture.

They selected three plugs of blasting composition from the powder-box, though Jim said one was plenty, and Dave said one might merely irritate the fish. The plugs were bound together with string. Andy wrapped them in newspaper, then in a strip cut from an old flour-bag, then in oiled calico. He wound copper wire round the parcel, turned the ends neatly, painted the whole with melted candle-grease, and finally rolled it in warm tallow from the meat-tin.

The time-fuse was pushed well home under the detonator cap, and the place where it entered the cartridge was stopped with soap, grease, and a piece of chewed rag.

Jim watched from the doorway.

“Why don’t you put handles on it?” he asked.

“You’d laugh if it fizzled,” said Dave.

“I’ll laugh if it don’t.”

Andy held up the finished cartridge. It was as thick as a man’s wrist, nearly a foot long, black, greasy, and heavy enough to sink through mud.

“There,” said Dave with satisfaction. “Water couldn’t get into that if it lay in the creek till Christmas.”

“No,” said Jim. “But I’d like to know what’s goin’ to happen when the water tries to get away from it.”

They intended to light the fuse, drop the cartridge into the deepest hole, and stand behind the bank until the fish came up. That was the intention. In the bush, intentions are useful chiefly for showing afterwards how far events have travelled from them.

Andy put the cartridge on a stump while he went to find a stone and some twine for weighting it. Dave cut another length off the fuse because he thought the first too long. Jim put chops in the frying-pan. The billy boiled over. Tommy lay near the fire, watching everything with bright interest and hoping that the whole business concerned food.

Then Andy called from the creek, and Dave answered him. Jim bent over the pan. Nobody saw Tommy rise.

The dog sniffed the parcel on the stump. It smelt of tallow, soap, candle-grease, men’s hands, and adventure. He took it carefully in his mouth.

Unfortunately, the loose fuse dragged across the edge of the fire.

It caught without fuss. A little red spark ran into the black cord, hesitated, and began to eat its way along with a steady, poisonous sputter.

Jim heard it first. He turned, stared at the burning fuse, and let the frying-pan fall.

“Dave!”

Dave looked round.

Tommy stood beside the stump with the cartridge across his jaws, smiling as well as a dog can smile with his mouth full. His tail swept the dust.

“Drop it!” yelled Dave.

Tommy’s ears went up.

“Drop it, you blanky fool!”

Tommy understood that a game had begun.

Dave sprang at him. Tommy dodged neatly round the fire, scattering plates and knocking over the billy. Dave went after him, Jim came from the other side, and Tommy, delighted by this sudden attention, slipped between them and galloped towards the creek.

“Head him off!” shouted Dave.

Andy came up the bank carrying a length of twine. He saw the men running, saw the dog, saw the thin thread of smoke trailing under Tommy’s chin, and understood all that was necessary.

He dropped the twine and ran the other way.

The dog followed Andy.

Andy, hearing Tommy’s joyful breathing close behind, turned and saw the cartridge bobbing scarcely a yard from his leg. He gave a yell that brought two cockatoos out of a dead tree, doubled back, and passed Dave at speed.

“Don’t run towards me!” shouted Dave.

“Where d’you want me to run?”

“Anywhere else!”

Andy went behind the camp. Tommy swung after him. Jim seized a sapling and climbed like a native bear who had just heard dogs below. He got three yards from the ground and stopped because there were no branches higher up.

“Call him, Jim!” cried Dave.

“I’ll call him after it goes off!”

Dave got an idea.

There was a forked stick near the woodheap. He snatched it up, waited until Tommy came round the corner, and tried to pin the cartridge to the ground. He pinned Tommy’s front paw instead. Tommy yelped, dropped the parcel, and jumped back.

Dave darted in, caught the cartridge by the end clear of the fuse, and flung it with all his strength into the scrub.

For half a second the men stood still.

Then Tommy bounded after it.

“No!” said all three together.

Tommy found the cartridge among the leaves, picked it up, and came back proudly. He laid it at Dave’s feet, backed away, crouched with his forelegs stretched out, and lashed his tail. The fuse sputtered shorter.

Dave did not stop to argue. He bolted for the shaft.

Jim came down from the sapling at the same moment and bolted for the shaft too. They collided at the windlass, went through a heap of mullock together, and rose separately. Tommy chose Jim.

Jim looked over his shoulder, saw the dog gaining, and changed his plans. The working shaft was deep and had water in it; but there was an old prospecting hole farther up the slope, about six feet deep, narrow at the mouth, and dry except for a dead goanna and some tins.

Jim reached it, put both hands on the edge, and went in head first.

The last Dave saw of him was a pair of boots kicking at daylight.

Tommy stopped at the hole and peered down. Jim, crouched below, looked up into the dog’s cheerful face and saw the live cartridge hanging over him.

“Go to Dave,” he whispered.

Tommy wagged.

“Dave’s got it. Dave’s got it, boy! Foller Dave!”

Tommy knew the name. He turned his head.

Dave was already making for the creek.

The dog left Jim and tore after him.

There are times when a man runs by calculation, saving his wind, choosing his footing, and keeping something in reserve. Dave did none of these things. He ran through blackberry, over stones, under branches, and across the creek without touching much of the country between. Tommy came behind, splashing through the water with the fuse spitting beside his muzzle.

Andy, meanwhile, had taken cover behind a fallen log. He raised his head cautiously, saw Dave cross the flat, and called:

“Scatter! Don’t foller one another!”

Dave had no breath for an answer. Also there was no “one another” to follow him, except Tommy, who could not be reasoned with.

At the edge of the flat Dave glanced round. The fuse had burned down alarmingly. He saw the track to the Royal Hotel and took it because it was open and because frightened men generally run towards other men, though later they cannot explain why.

The Royal was a slab-built shanty with a bark kitchen, a crooked verandah, and a signboard showing a crown that looked like a dead crab. The publican, Clancy, stood behind the bar arguing with a carrier about a sixpence. Two shearers played cards by the window. A traveller slept on a settle. Mrs Clancy was rolling pastry in the kitchen, and her sister, a nervous woman from Sydney, was complaining of the flies.

Dave came through the front door as if the police were behind him.

“Shut it!” he gasped.

Clancy stared. “What’s up?”

“Dog—cartridge—fuse!”

The carrier laughed. One of the shearers got up slowly.

Then Tommy trotted through the doorway.

He was dusty, wet to the shoulders, and happy. The cartridge was still in his mouth, and the smoking fuse now showed only a few feet.

Clancy understood before the others. He went over the bar without opening the flap. The carrier went through the window, taking most of the blind with him. The shearers upset the table and fought each other for the back passage. The traveller woke, saw everybody leaving, and left too.

Dave ran into the dining-room.

Tommy followed Dave.

Dave ran round the table, through the kitchen, and into the yard.

Tommy followed Dave.

Mrs Clancy screamed and threw the rolling-pin. It struck the traveller, who had chosen the kitchen as the safest road out. Clancy’s sister climbed upon the stove, though the fire was in it, and remained there until her skirts began to smoke.

In the yard Dave tried another dodge. He ran behind the woodpile, jumped sideways, and doubled towards the stable. Tommy overshot him, stopped, wheeled, and came on again with his ears flying.

“Call your dog off!” roared Clancy from the cellar entrance.

“He don’t belong to me!” shouted Dave.

This was not true, but it was natural.

By now everybody who had escaped was trying to get behind something. The carrier was under the horse-trough. One shearer had shut himself in the fowl-house and was keeping the hens away from the door with his hat. The other had climbed the stable ladder. Mrs Clancy lay flat behind a water-cask, praying in a loud, practical way and reminding Clancy of several sins he had hoped she had forgotten.

Dave was nearly spent. He dodged round the chopping-block, and Tommy drew level with him. The fuse hissed and spat between them.

Then the yellow cattle dog came out from under the hotel.

This dog was lean, scarred, and bad-tempered. He had been bred to turn wild bullocks, but had long since given up cattle for travellers, Chinamen, and harmless dogs. He hated Tommy because Tommy was cheerful, well fed, and had once tried to play with him.

The yellow dog rushed in without asking what the game was about.

Tommy swerved. The cattle dog snapped at his cheek. Tommy dropped the cartridge, gave one astonished bark, and backed away.

The yellow dog stood over the parcel growling.

Dave saw it on the ground. For an instant he thought of rushing in and kicking it away. Then the fuse gave a vicious leap of sparks, and Dave thought better.

He ran behind the pigsty and lay down.

The cattle dog sniffed the cartridge. Tommy, forgetting offence almost at once, approached from one side and wagged in a conciliatory manner. Two small terriers belonging to the hotel came from beneath the verandah. A black mongrel arrived from across the road. The carrier’s old collie slipped his chain at the stable. Within seconds there was a committee of dogs round the cartridge, each anxious to know why the others were interested.

The yellow dog claimed it.

He put one paw on the parcel and showed his teeth. One terrier darted in and caught at the fuse. The yellow dog snapped at him. Tommy barked encouragement from a safe distance—safe according to Tommy.

From the fowl-house a shearer called, “How long’s left?”

Nobody answered.

The yard became very still. A horse stamped in the stable. Mrs Clancy stopped praying to listen. Dave raised his head a fraction above the pigsty wall. The yellow dog bent down, took the cartridge by the middle, and lifted it—

The explosion made one clean, tremendous crack.

Dust, bark, feathers, dogs, pieces of box, and one old wheelbarrow rose together. The kitchen jumped away from the hotel and appeared to think better of it. Every window facing the yard burst inward. A cask rolled off its blocks. The stable horses tore loose and went through the back fence as though it had been painted on the air.

The yellow cattle dog was not seen again in his previous condition. Bushmen afterwards said that he went over the stable roof, struck a grey horse, bounced into the kitchen chimney, came down with soot on him, travelled through the dust like a burning bag, upset the wheelbarrow, and finished on the far side of the fence before deciding to die. This account may include several dogs and part of a saddle.

The two terriers came out of the smoke at opposite ends of the yard. One was singed white and the other barked continuously for three days without appearing to know it. The collie reached the creek, crossed it, and was later found sitting in a hollow log with his face to the wall. The black mongrel abandoned the district.

Tommy had been far enough away to escape injury. The blast rolled him twice, filled his coat with dust, and surprised him. He stood up, sneezed, and looked round for the parcel.

The cartridge had been excellently well made.

For several seconds nobody moved. Then Clancy lifted his head from the cellar step. The carrier rose out of the trough with green slime over one eye. A hen fell from the roof. Mrs Clancy began to laugh behind the cask—not pleasantly at first, but in little jerks that became screams. The shearer in the fowl-house joined in. So did the man on the stable ladder. The traveller sat in the kitchen doorway with the rolling-pin across his knees and laughed until he cried.

Dave came out from behind the pigsty, pale and muddy.

Clancy pointed at him but could not speak. When at last he found his voice, all he said was:

“Fish!”

Then he doubled up again.

Tommy saw Dave and came over, tail swinging, tongue out, plainly satisfied with the afternoon. Dave looked at him for a long moment.

“You ought to be dead,” he said.

Tommy licked his hand.

They returned to camp by the creek track. Tommy ranged ahead, exploring, then came back every few yards to make sure Dave was following. Dave carried a piece of paling in case the dog found anything else.

Andy rose from behind the log when he saw them.

“Gone off?”

“No,” said Dave. “It turned into a pumpkin.”

Andy inspected Tommy, found him sound, and chained him to a stump with the strongest chain in camp. Then he gathered the scattered plates, washed the dirt out of the frying-pan, and cut three more chops. Bushmen cannot postpone tea merely because a hotel has shifted slightly.

They remembered Jim when the billy boiled.

He was still in the prospecting hole, cramped double, with the dead goanna under one knee. Dave lay flat and reached down while Andy pulled at his belt. They got him out slowly. Jim stood bowed for a while, rubbing his back.

“Where’s the dog?” he asked.

“Chained.”

“Where’s the cartridge?”

“Royal Hotel.”

Jim looked towards the distant shanty. A thin haze of dust still hung above it.

“Catch any fish?”

Dave said nothing. He had toothache by then, he said, with a burred edge to it, and was in no humour for foolish questions.

The reef was never found. The claim went under, as claims do, and the three mates drifted to other fields. But the story stayed in the gully. It was told at the Royal until Clancy rebuilt the kitchen, and afterwards because he had rebuilt it. Carriers added horses, shearers added dogs, and one boundary-rider swore that a cod weighing forty pounds had been blown through the bar window, though there had not been a fish within half a mile.

For years, whenever Dave Regan was recognised on a track or in a mining camp, some bushman would shade his eyes, look solemnly towards the nearest creek, and drawl across the distance:

“Da-a-ve! How’s the fishin’?”

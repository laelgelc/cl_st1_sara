I first met Cyril Harjohn when he was fourteen and I was twenty-eight; an age at which a man imagines himself old because he has begun to discover that he is young.

At that time I was a hack-journalist of the humbler sort. I furnished paragraphs to an evening paper, dramatic criticisms to a provincial weekly, and, when Providence was unusually negligent, biographical notes of eminent persons who had not yet died. My rooms were in Staple Inn, and my income, though elastic in theory, possessed in practice the rigidity of a five-pound note. I dined in Soho because it was cheap, praised Bohemia because I could not afford civilisation, and cherished a tragedy in blank verse which no manager would read. An assured income is often the grave of ambition; poverty is sometimes merely its comic funeral.

Cyril was then a pupil at Fauerberg’s, a private school in Bloomsbury where boys were prepared for everything and educated for nothing. Old Fauerberg had the conscientious air of a poulterer stuffing geese. Facts were thrust into his pupils until their natural faculties became hopelessly congested. Cyril, being constitutionally incapable of retaining anything that did not interest him, was considered backward.

Between a junior resident master at a third-rate school and a backward boy there can be little social intimacy. Between a struggling journalist and a lonely child there may exist, however, a complete republic of the affections.

I had gone to Fauerberg’s to obtain particulars of an ex-pupil who had won some scholarship. While the headmaster searched for dates, Cyril sat on a bench in the passage, nursing a damaged pigeon.

“Young Harjohn,” said Fauerberg, observing him, “has no application.”

The boy lifted his head. It was a remarkable head even then: broad-browed, dark-eyed, and surrounded by fair hair which gave it, in certain lights, the suggestion of a halo. Halos are inconvenient possessions. They attract martyrs’ crowns and women’s fingers.

“What is the matter with the bird?” I asked.

“Wing broken, sir.”

“You can’t mend it.”

“No, sir.”

“Then why keep it?”

He looked at me with grave surprise.

“Because it hurts.”

That answer began our friendship.

His father, a clergyman in Cumberland, had married late, begotten Cyril later, and died before discovering what manner of son he had produced. His mother, a gentle, ineffectual woman, lived upon a small annuity and the advice of relatives. They had sent Cyril to Fauerberg’s because an uncle knew Fauerberg’s brother. The great decisions of life are often determined by such majestic causes.

The boy came frequently to my rooms. I gave him tea, tobacco-smoke, and conversation far above his years; he gave me the reverence which a neglected child bestows upon the first adult who treats him as an equal. He read my rejected tragedy and pronounced it splendid. No critic has since equalled him in discernment.

He was idle at school, but never idle with life. A beetle, a beggar, a dead sparrow, the machinery of a watch, the tremor in an old man’s hand—each could arrest him. He had the scientific eye before he possessed scientific knowledge: he observed because he loved. When at last a humane master discovered that the dunce in Latin prose had a passion for natural science, Cyril advanced with astonishing rapidity. He went to Oxford, entered Brasenose, and emerged with distinctions which confounded Fauerberg and confirmed his mother in the belief that the school had been excellent.

During his Oxford years I saw him rarely, but his letters came to me—eager, untidy documents, crowded with schemes for the regeneration of medicine and mankind. He would discover the origin of fevers, abolish unnecessary pain, reform hospitals, carry sanitation to Asia, and provide every village with a trained nurse. Youth lays out eternity as confidently as a suburban garden.

Yet there was nothing ridiculous in Cyril’s ardour. He possessed that rarest form of ambition—the desire to achieve much and to be known little. Medicine, as he conceived it, was not a profession but a crusade. The physician was to him at once priest, soldier, and interpreter: a priest because he entered the chamber of suffering; a soldier because death must be fought foot by foot; an interpreter because Nature writes her warnings in a script legible only to patience.

After Oxford he went to Guy’s. There he worked with a kind of joyous ferocity. He took prizes, served in wards from which stronger men recoiled, and spent his nights over microscopes. His health appeared inexhaustible. His temper was sweet, his courage without advertisement, and his sympathy so immediate that pain seemed to pass through the sufferer into him and there lose half its sting.

I watched him grow from the backward schoolboy into a man whom older men consulted. He published a paper on tropical infection which attracted attention abroad. A famous physician offered him a position that would have led, by the broadest and most respectable road, to Harley Street and wealth. Cyril refused it because he wished to continue research.

“You are a damned fool,” I told him.

“Probably,” he answered. “But a comfortable fool becomes comfortable first and a fool for ever.”

“Humanity,” said I, “does not pay bills.”

“No. It presents them.”

Had I known the world better, I should have been less delighted by his answer. Martyrdom, like marriage, ought not to be entered upon unadvisedly. The man who resolves to give everything should first make certain that there is not within him some secret creditor determined to seize the whole estate.

It was at a musical evening in Kensington that Cyril met Elspeth Grant.

The Grants were Scotch, wealthy without ostentation, and religious without ill-manners. Elspeth was not beautiful in the ordinary sense, for ordinary beauty demands that one should first admire the woman and afterwards inquire whether she has a soul. With Elspeth the soul was visible at once. Her features were delicate rather than regular; her grey eyes had the serenity of deep water; and her pale hair seemed less an ornament than an atmosphere. Men lowered their voices beside her, not because she was timid, but because vulgarity became suddenly laborious in her presence.

She had nursed a brother through years of illness and lost him. Sorrow had done for her what it does for the finest natures: it had enlarged tenderness without narrowing joy. She knew suffering, yet did not worship it. She could laugh, and her laughter was the more charming because it came from one who had earned the right to be sad.

Cyril and she understood each other almost immediately. Their intimacy had none of the feverish cleverness by which young people often counterfeit sympathy. They talked quietly of work, duty, death, and the strange economy by which humanity advances upon the bodies of its best servants. Elspeth believed in God; Cyril, at that period, believed chiefly in evidence. But her faith did not offend him, because it was not an argument but a light.

I remember seeing them one summer evening in the Grants’ garden. Elspeth stood beneath a cedar, her white dress touched with green shadow, while Cyril, seated on the grass, looked up at her. There was in the attitude something at once pagan and Christian: Artemis watching over Endymion, or a young Madonna bending toward some tired pilgrim. I thought then that they loved each other. Perhaps they did. But we use one word for many mysteries, as savages use one knife for many sacrifices.

“Why do you not marry her?” I asked Cyril later.

He turned sharply.

“Has she spoken to you?”

“My dear fellow, Elspeth Grant would sooner discuss her prayers with a bookmaker.”

“Then don’t talk nonsense.”

“It is not nonsense. You worship her.”

“Yes.”

“And she loves you.”

His face changed, and for an instant I saw happiness enter it like sunrise into an empty church.

“Do you think so?”

“I know so.”

He walked to the window and stood looking down upon the small, evil-smelling street. The city moaned about us like a tired child. Presently he said:

“That is just why I cannot marry her.”

At the time I supposed this one of those moral subtleties by which noble young men contrive to make themselves and excellent women miserable. Had I known the world better, I should have asked not what he feared for Elspeth, but what he feared in himself.

Geraldine Fawley entered our circle soon afterwards.

She sang at a studio gathering in Chelsea—French songs in which love was represented as an elegant disease affecting chiefly the shoulders. She had a rich, low voice and the technical skill to make art seem a species of indolence. Her beauty was undeniable. She was tall, dark, and exquisitely formed; her mouth had the red precision of a wound; her eyes were brown, brilliant, and entirely without rest.

Her father, Septimus Fawley, had been a company promoter, racing man, and professional bankrupt. He had ruined two partners, deserted one wife, and died in a hotel at Ostend while negotiating a third marriage. Geraldine resembled him so strongly that those who had known him grew cautious in her presence. Physiognomy is an untrustworthy science, but heredity sometimes advertises.

She liked admiration as misers like gold—not for its use, but for accumulation. The devotion of a poor man pleased her vanity; only that of a rich man could satisfy her judgment. She had no hypocrisy on this point, and therefore received credit for honesty from persons who forgot that frankness may be the final luxury of the unscrupulous.

Cyril disliked her at first. I noticed that he watched her continually.

“She is a wrong ’un,” he said as we walked home.

“Then why did you spend the evening staring at her?”

“I was wondering what makes women like that.”

“Nature, dressmakers, and men like us.”

He laughed, but without amusement.

Within a month he was seeing her frequently. She consulted him about her throat; then about her nerves; then about troubles which required neither medicine nor confidence, but merely an audience. Cyril knew what she was. That fact must be insisted upon. He was not deceived by a mask, for Geraldine seldom troubled to wear one.

Once, at supper in a dingy, garlic-scented restaurant off Dean Street, she discussed marriage before us with alarming simplicity.

“I shall marry position,” she said. “Money alone is vulgar, though useful. A name alone is inconvenient. I want both if possible.”

“And love?” I asked.

“My dear man, love is what one feels while waiting to see whether the other things are possible.”

Cyril’s hand tightened upon his glass.

“Suppose a man loved you and had neither?”

“I should be touched.”

“And if he had a future?”

“I might invest.”

“You would marry him for what he could earn?”

“Certainly. Why else does a woman marry a doctor? Not for the smell of disinfectant.”

I expected Cyril to be disgusted. Instead, he looked at her with the terrible concentration I had seen him give to some obscure specimen under his microscope. But this malady he could observe, name, and fear; he could not cure it.

Elspeth knew. Women such as Elspeth always know, though they often possess too much mercy to call knowledge by its name. She neither reproached Cyril nor avoided Geraldine. Her gentleness toward him became more vigilant, and there were moments when I fancied her spirit stood before his like an angel before a dark gate, swordless but resolute.

He continued his work. Indeed, he worked more desperately. He obtained an appointment connected with an expedition to India, where his researches might have been of immense value. Papers praised his promise. Senior men spoke of him as one of the coming physicians of Europe. Yet he grew thin, irregular, and restless. Rumours reached me of nights spent in clubs and places less innocent than clubs. I dismissed them. We accept evidence against our enemies and demand proof concerning our friends.

One wet November night he came to my rooms after midnight. His coat was unbuttoned, his hair drenched, and his face so pale that for an instant I thought him ill.

“Give me a drink,” he said.

I poured whisky. He swallowed it and held out the glass again.

“No,” I said. “Sit down.”

He obeyed. For several minutes neither of us spoke. The rain ticked against the panes; a cab rattled below; somewhere a woman laughed, and the laughter rose through the darkness with an ugly, homeless sound.

At last he said:

“You think me good.”

“I think you a damned fool, which is often the same thing.”

“Don’t joke.”

“My dear Cyril—”

“You think I’m made for Elspeth. You think I ought to marry her, work, grow famous, and spend my life doing good. Isn’t that your pretty little scheme?”

“It is not especially pretty. It is reasonable.”

“Reasonable!” He leaned forward. “Do you suppose I haven’t reasoned? I have dissected this thing until there is nothing left but the poison, and still the poison works.”

“What thing?”

“Geraldine.”

I made some impatient movement.

“Yes, sneer at her. Call her cruel, greedy, common in everything but manners. You’ll be right. She wants money. She wants houses, jewels, servants, people crawling to her. She has told me so. She would take my work, my time, my strength, and turn them into dresses and dinners. She would make me give up research, take rich patients, flatter old women, humour hypochondriacs, become a successful physician—successful!” He struck the arm of the chair. “And I should do it.”

“You would not.”

“I should.”

“You know too much.”

“Knowledge!” he cried. “Knowledge tells a drunkard what is in the bottle. Does it empty it?”

I had no answer, and he went on with increasing violence.

“You see the part of me that works in hospitals, talks of service, and plays the young apostle. That part is real. I have never pretended. I want to fight disease. I want to give men a little more light, a little less pain. I would die for that.”

“Then live for it.”

“But there is another man in me. He has been there always—hungry, gross, secret. At school, at Oxford, in London, I’ve kept him down. Work kept him down. Elspeth made him ashamed. But Geraldine calls him, and he answers.”

“You are overwrought.”

“No. For once I am honest. I don’t want Geraldine’s soul. She hasn’t one that I can find. I want her beauty, her body, her cruelty—yes, her cruelty too. If she were kind, perhaps I should escape. But she holds out her hands and says, ‘Give.’ Give, give, give! And I know I should give her everything—my work, my honour, my future, my heart, and my brain, and my soul.”

He rose and paced the room.

“She would drag me down, down, down, and I should kiss the hand that dragged me. That is the truth. Your saint and your scientist are very fine in daylight. At night the animal remembers the forest.”

I caught his arm.

“Stop this melodrama. You are not the first man to desire the wrong woman.”

“No. But perhaps I am one of the few who knows precisely what obedience will cost.”

“Then don’t obey.”

He looked at me, almost pityingly.

“That is your prescription?”

“It is the only one.”

He laughed. “And you call yourself a man of letters.”

I urged travel, absence, the Indian appointment. Distance, I said, lends perspective. He replied that distance also lends imagination. I spoke of Elspeth.

At her name his anger collapsed. He covered his face.

“She knows,” he whispered.

“She will help you.”

“That is the worst of it. If she despised me, I might become respectable out of spite. But she understands. She looks at me as if this filth were a wound.”

“Perhaps it is.”

“No. A wound is received. This was born.”

We talked until dawn, if the feeble grey dilution above the roofs deserved that name. Once we quarrelled so loudly that a policeman paused beneath the window and advised two invisible gentlemen to “take theirselves quietly home.” The absurdity restored us for a moment. Cyril laughed, and I, mistaking exhaustion for recovery, believed the crisis past.

We dream our poems, not live them. In life the sublime is always being interrupted by the policeman.

Cyril agreed to go to India. His work would keep him there for six months, after which he intended to return by way of Egypt. Elspeth supported the plan. What passed between them I never knew, but on the evening before his departure I saw them together.

She came from the room first. Her face was colourless, yet composed. She gave me her hand.

“You will write to him?” she asked.

“Frequently.”

“Remind him that no defeat is final while one still wishes to be better.”

Then she added, very softly:

“And remind him that nothing he could confess would make me cease to pray for him.”

Cyril appeared after she had gone. His eyes were red, but his manner almost cheerful.

“There,” he said. “I have been shriven by a saint who refuses to believe in damnation.”

“Marry her when you return.”

“If I return as the man she loves.”

He sailed the following week.

For a time his letters were hopeful. Calcutta appalled and fascinated him. He wrote of hospitals crowded beyond endurance, of fever districts, of native assistants whose skill had been ignored by official vanity, of children lying with solemn eyes beneath slow fans. The scale of suffering steadied him. Personal passion seemed, he said, a candle-flame beneath the Indian sun.

He worked incessantly. He sent notes for a paper which, had he lived to complete it, might have changed the treatment of a particular infection. His old ardour returned. Even his handwriting became firmer.

Elspeth received longer letters than I did. She showed me none, but hope transformed her. The quiet light in her seemed to deepen, and I began to believe that goodness, though less spectacular than appetite, might prove stronger in the end.

Geraldine meanwhile obtained an engagement with a theatrical company travelling east. When I heard of it, I went to see her.

“You are going to Calcutta?”

“If the sea behaves.”

“You know Cyril is there.”

“So I am told.”

“Did you arrange the engagement for that reason?”

She smiled. “You credit me with influence over managers.”

“Leave him alone.”

“My dear friend, Cyril is not a schoolboy.”

“I knew him when he was one. He had more sense then.”

She studied me with lazy amusement.

“You all wish to preserve him for humanity. Humanity should pay better.”

“He will never marry you on your terms.”

“No?”

“No.”

“Then you may sleep peacefully.”

Her confidence frightened me more than denial would have done.

Cyril’s last letter arrived in March. He expected to leave Calcutta in ten days and hoped to be in London before June. The letter ended: “Tell E. the battle is nearly done. I do not say won. Men like me have no victories—only intervals during which the enemy is quiet.”

Two days later I learned that Geraldine had reached Calcutta.

After that, silence.

I wrote repeatedly. Elspeth cabled. No reply came. His hospital informed us that Dr. Harjohn had obtained leave and was travelling with acquaintances in the north. Geraldine’s company moved on to Rangoon without her; then we heard she had returned to Europe by another route.

Three months passed.

One afternoon, in the smoking-room of a club to which I had lately been admitted by an error of the committee, I encountered Major Densmore, a florid acquaintance who had spent much of his life going to remote countries in order to associate exclusively with Englishmen.

“Harjohn,” he said, on hearing the name. “Knew a medical chap called Harjohn. Clever fellow. Died out there.”

The room appeared to tilt slightly.

“What did you say?”

“Died. Shooting accident. Rotten business.”

“When?”

“April, I think. At a place near Darjeeling. Party after hill birds. Bullet through the head.”

“Whose bullet?”

“His own gun, apparently. Climbing a bank, or cleaning it, or some such thing. Nobody saw exactly. These accidents happen.”

“Was a woman there?”

Densmore frowned with the effort of memory.

“There was some singer staying in the neighbourhood. Handsome creature. Fawley—Geraldine Fawley. But I don’t know that she had anything to do with the party. Why?”

“Nothing,” I said.

He tapped ash from his cigar.

“Poor devil. Brilliant career before him, they said. Dear me—how very sad!”

“Very sad,” I repeated.

And in my heart, beneath horror, beneath grief, beneath the shame of understanding, another phrase formed itself.

Thank God.

I never spoke it. Even now I scarcely dare write it.

The official account was simple. Cyril had gone alone a little ahead of the others. A shot was heard. They found him beside a narrow path, his gun close to his hand and the bullet having passed through his head. The ground was uneven. The weapon had a sensitive trigger. Death was instantaneous.

It may have been an accident.

Elspeth accepted that explanation, or said she did. She wore no mourning, for she had no acknowledged right to it, but something in her withdrew from the common daylight. She continued her charities, visited hospitals, and smiled upon children. Saints do not break; they become transparent.

Geraldine married, within the year, a manufacturer twice her age and many times richer. I saw her once afterwards at the opera, blazing with diamonds. She bowed to me. Her beauty had ripened; her expression had not changed.

I have often asked myself what happened in those silent weeks. Did Cyril yield and recoil? Did Geraldine refuse him because his prospects were less immediate than she had imagined? Did she demand the sacrifice he had foretold? Or did he, seeing the road before him—the consulting-room, the rich patients, the purchased honours, the slow sale of every noble faculty—choose one swift darkness rather than a lifetime of descent?

I do not know.

That ignorance is the heart of the matter. We explain character by childhood, appetite by heredity, courage by ideals, and surrender by fatigue; but when all our explanations are assembled, labelled, and arranged, the human soul slips through them like light through prison bars. Cyril knew the good and desired it. He knew the evil and desired it also. Clear sight did not save him. Perhaps it only made the struggle intolerable.

Between the angel and the devil, moralists place a battlefield. They forget that both armies may wear the same face.

To the world Cyril Harjohn remains a young physician accidentally killed in India. To medicine he is a footnote attached to an unfinished paper. To Elspeth he was, I think, a soul not lost but released. To Geraldine he may have been only a possibility that failed to mature.

To me he is still the backward boy in Fauerberg’s passage, holding a broken pigeon because it hurt.

And sometimes, when I remember the gun upon the mountain path, I wonder whether, at the last, he held his own wounded nature with the same tenderness—or destroyed it because he could discover no gentler cure.

### The Glass Cottage and the Squirrels

Once upon a time, in the midst of a pleasant forest, where the ferns spread their green fans under the chestnut trees, and the wild strawberries hid their red cheeks beneath three leaves, and the sunshine came down in golden patches to warm the moss, a gentleman resolved to build a cottage.

Now there was nothing remarkable in building a cottage, you may say; but this one was to have so many windows that the animals declared it would be made chiefly of glass. There were windows to the east, to catch the morning; windows to the west, to keep the sunset; windows under the roof, windows beside the doors, and one long window looking into a thicket where the thrushes nested.

The gentleman admired the site exceedingly.

“There is not a quieter place in the world,” said he.

That showed how little he knew about it.

The place was quiet to human ears, perhaps; but beneath every leaf and root some business was proceeding. The ants were carrying burdens; the beetles were polishing their coats; the moles were extending their tunnels; the birds were discussing their neighbours; and the squirrels, who possessed estates in several trees, were leaping from branch to branch with the greatest possible activity.

When men arrived with axes, saws, hammers, boards, bricks, ladders, and no end of nails, the whole forest was thrown into consternation.

A brown rabbit carried the news to the hollow oak. A blue jay repeated it, with additions, near the brook. Before noon a travelling ant had written a paragraph for the *Crickets’ Gazette*, entitled, “Human Invasion of the Rural Solitudes of Nature.”

“They will cut down everything,” said Father Gray, an elderly squirrel with a ragged ear.

“They will eat everything,” said Mother Red, his wife.

“They will certainly dig up the road,” said Mr. Mole. “And if they disturb the foundations in this vicinity, underground property will fall so low that a respectable mole may as well sell out.”

The oldest chestnut tree, who had seen many generations of creatures born, married, and buried under his shade, rustled all his leaves for silence.

“My friends,” said he, “I know mankind. Not a flower opens but they desire to pluck it; not the least root pushes into the kindly earth but they are ready to tear it out; not a bird sings but they wish either to cage him or frighten him away. They bring smoke where there was sweet air, noise where there was peace, and impatience where Nature has laboured for a hundred years. In one morning they will destroy what rain and sunshine and winter snow have taken a century to prepare.”

This was considered a noble speech, especially by the birds, though Father Gray whispered that birds were flighty creatures and would applaud anything delivered from a high branch.

Mother Red had two young children named Tit-bit and Frisky. Tit-bit had a white spot under his chin and was fond of tasting whatever he found. Frisky was smaller, quicker, and so full of curiosity that his mother said she expected some day to find him inside the moon.

“Will the men cut down our tree?” asked Tit-bit.

“Will they climb to our hole?” asked Frisky.

“Will they eat squirrels?”

“Hush!” said Mother Red. “No well-bred child asks whether he is to be eaten.”

Nevertheless, she moved the family’s winter store to a more secret chamber and gave Father Gray a very long curtain lecture that night because he had not foreseen the invasion.

The building went forward. Bang went the hammers, rasp went the saws, and down came one small birch, which caused such an outcry that the woodpeckers repeated the scandal for three days. Yet, to the surprise of all, the gentleman marked the great trees with bits of ribbon and ordered the workmen not to injure them. When a wall was planned too near the chestnut’s roots, he had it moved. When a ladder disturbed a robin’s nest, he stopped the work until the young robins had flown.

“Policy,” said Father Gray darkly.

But the chestnut tree said nothing.

At last the cottage stood complete, with its gray roof and its shining panes, among which the forest appeared a hundred times over—green branches in one, clouds in another, and sometimes a squirrel’s astonished face in a third. No iron fence enclosed it. No traps were set. Moss and violets remained about the doorstep, and a climbing rose was planted where the birch had fallen.

Then the gentleman came to live there with his household, and with him came his sister, Aunt Esther.

Aunt Esther was not truly aunt to everybody, but she had such an aunt-like manner that children, servants, dogs, and even strangers soon called her so. She wore soft gray gowns, carried keys at her belt, and always seemed to have in her pocket some crumb, string, pin, seed, sweet, or other article which exactly suited the emergency.

On her first morning she scattered grain for the birds.

The sparrows descended at once, for sparrows are not troubled with too much dignity. The chickadees followed, then the jays, then a solemn crow who pretended he had come merely to inspect the roof. Aunt Esther put water in a shallow dish beneath the lilac bush, and when she discovered a toad near the kitchen steps, she warned everybody not to tread upon him.

The animals held another meeting.

“It may be a trick,” said Mother Red.

“It is a remarkably agreeable trick,” said a sparrow, swallowing his fifth crumb.

Mr. Mole reported that no poison had been placed in the soil. The robin declared that the lady had tied up a broken branch. The chestnut tree, who had watched Aunt Esther prevent a boy from carving his initials upon the bark, finally admitted that mankind might contain exceptions.

“Not all storms uproot trees,” he said, “and perhaps not all humans destroy them.”

Tit-bit and Frisky listened eagerly.

“May we go near the cottage?” asked Tit-bit.

“No,” said Mother Red.

“May we go near the path?”

“No.”

“May we look at the roof from our own branch?”

“If you keep your tails behind you.”

For several days they obeyed. Then Frisky went one branch farther, and Tit-bit followed to bring him back. The next day they descended halfway down the trunk. The day after that they crossed the path, whisk-frisk, whisk-frisk, and hid behind a stone whenever a curtain moved.

Aunt Esther saw them, of course. She saw nearly everything, though she often had the kindness to appear blind.

She laid two hazelnuts upon the window-sill and went away.

“Poison!” said Mother Red when she heard of it.

“Hazelnuts,” said Tit-bit.

“Poisoned hazelnuts.”

Tit-bit and Frisky sat at a safe distance and stared until sunset. At last Tit-bit crept forward—creep, creep, creep—seized one nut, and flew back so quickly that his brother accused him of leaving his shadow behind.

It was the finest nut he had ever eaten.

The next morning there were four. Soon there were nuts on the step, then inside the open door, then upon a chair near Aunt Esther’s work-basket. The young squirrels advanced by degrees, each day resolving to go no farther and each day going farther than before.

One afternoon Aunt Esther sat very still with a walnut upon her palm. Tit-bit came first. He stretched his body until it seemed twice its proper length, kept his hind feet ready for flight, and snatched the walnut. Frisky, unwilling to be thought less courageous, took a piece from her fingers.

After that there was no end to their boldness.

They ran over the carpet, climbed the curtains, inspected the flower-pots, and chased one another along the mantelpiece. Tit-bit once hid a chestnut in the gentleman’s hat; Frisky carried away three thimbles, though what he expected to do with them nobody ever discovered. They sprang upon Aunt Esther’s shoulder, searched her pockets, and sometimes sat on the window ledge washing their faces while she read aloud.

Mother Red was shocked when she learned of these proceedings.

“It is not respectable,” she said. “Your grandfather never sat upon a human shoulder.”

“Perhaps he was never invited,” said Frisky.

This reply brought both young squirrels to bed without supper; but the very next day they were back at the cottage, and even Mother Red gradually ceased to object when she observed that they returned sleek, cheerful, and generally full of nuts.

Those were happy days. In summer the doors stood open, and the cottage seemed only another chamber of the forest. The smell of roses came in, and the sound of spoons and laughter went out. The birds bathed under the lilac; rabbits nibbled the clover near the porch; and Aunt Esther knew each visitor, or believed she did, which came to nearly the same thing.

But happiness, whether in cottage or tree, does not always remain in one shape.

Behind the house stood a tank in which rainwater was collected. It was deep, and its smooth sides offered no foothold. One autumn morning Frisky, who had been racing along the roof, slipped from a wet gutter and fell into it. No one saw him fall. When he was found, his bright eyes were closed, and the quick little feet that had explored every room were still.

I grieve to say that Aunt Esther wept over him. She wrapped him in a piece of white linen and buried him beneath the chestnut tree, where red leaves fell softly upon the small mound. Tit-bit searched the cottage for many days. He looked behind the curtains, under the chairs, and inside the work-basket, and when he found nothing he sat upon the window ledge without play.

Even Father Gray made no speeches. Mother Red held her remaining child close, and the old chestnut dropped three burrs upon the grave, which was the only offering he could make.

In time Tit-bit grew older and visited less often. He had a family and winter stores of his own, and many important affairs among the branches. Yet he never passed the long window without pausing, and if Aunt Esther held out a nut, he came to her hand with the trust of former days.

Then, after some years, Aunt Esther also went away—away from the glass-filled cottage and the forest paths, to that land, we may hope, where the flowers never fade and the birds never die, and where perhaps a little gray squirrel came whisk-frisk to meet her.

The woodland creatures missed her. Grain was still scattered, but not with quite the same understanding. Water was set out, but no gentle voice warned the cat away. The gentleman grew older, and others came to live in the cottage, all of whom admired the squirrels, though none possessed exactly Aunt Esther’s patience.

For the squirrels, meanwhile, had discovered that a human dwelling may contain advantages which no sensible animal should despise. The eaves were dry, the chimneys warm, and the garret was a veritable palace, filled with boxes, baskets, old coats, rolls of carpet, broken chairs, and mysterious corners suited to the concealment of no end of nuts.

A young pair, descended—so Father Gray’s family maintained—from Tit-bit himself, gnawed a small entrance under the roof and moved in.

At first nobody knew it.

Then hazelnut shells appeared in a trunk. An old shawl was torn into strips. A rain of plaster fell during breakfast. Presently, every morning just before sunrise, there began overhead a patter, a scramble, a leap, and a rolling sound, as though six marbles, three brushes, and a very small broom were engaged in a dance.

“What can it be?” asked a visitor.

“Squirrels,” said the gentleman.

“Charming!”

“They are charming at eleven o’clock,” he replied.

The family increased. There were children in spring, and their education was conducted with extraordinary noise. They chased one another through the garret, quarrelled inside the walls, and occasionally dropped nuts down a pipe, where the nuts rolled and knocked in the most melancholy fashion.

“We must drive them out,” said the household.

“Certainly,” said the gentleman.

But when a ladder was brought, a little squirrel appeared under the eaves, sat up with its paws upon its breast, and looked so innocent that the ladder was taken away again.

“They will ruin the roof,” said the carpenter.

“We shall attend to it tomorrow,” said the gentleman.

Tomorrow there were two young squirrels playing upon the sill. The next day their mother came for bread, and the cook declared it would be cruel to disturb a nursing parent. A week later there was a quarrel in the chimney so violent that soot fell into the parlour, and everybody resolved that kindness had its limits.

A humane trap was obtained and baited with walnuts.

The squirrels removed the walnuts without entering the trap.

It was baited with cake.

They removed the cake.

It was baited with apple, and one young rogue sat upon the trap while eating it.

“Rapacious creatures!” cried the gentleman, though he was laughing.

Thus matters continued. The humans complained of the squirrels, and the squirrels complained of the humans. The squirrels considered the garret their property because they occupied it; the humans considered it theirs because they had paid for the house. The moles said possession depended upon the ground beneath a place; the birds said it depended upon the air above it; and the chestnut tree, older and wiser than ever, observed that nearly every quarrel began when two creatures said “mine” of the same thing.

Still, on winter mornings, when snow lay over the forest and the bare branches tapped the glass, it was pleasant to see the little family warm beneath the eaves. Still, when they raced at dawn, upset boxes, chewed papers, and awakened every sleeper, it was difficult to remember that pleasure.

So the household continues to say, “We shall turn them out tomorrow,” and tomorrow always finds another excuse.

Now tell me, what would you do about it? Would you stop the hole and give the garret entirely to the humans, who built it? Or would you leave a corner for the squirrels, whose forest stood there first?

It is not so easy a question as it appears—particularly when the culprits sit in a row upon the roof, with bright eyes, folded paws, and the most charming tails in the world.

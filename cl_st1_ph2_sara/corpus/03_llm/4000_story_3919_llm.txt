# Bismarck Anne

The monthly dance at Roaring Fork began at nine o’clock, achieved respectability at no known hour, and usually ended when the lamps went out, the whisky gave out, or somebody was carried out.

On this particular pay-night the lamps were still burning bravely at midnight. They hung from the beams in garlands of red, yellow, and smoky blue, and the dust stirred by forty pairs of boots made a luminous fog about them. A fiddler from Boise sawed at “The Girl I Left Behind Me” with the wrath of a man settling an old grudge. Beside him a melancholy Mexican picked three strings of a guitar. Neither listened to the other.

At one end of the hall stood the bar, fortified with bottles and flanked by two men whose duty was to preserve order and whose pleasure was to destroy it. At the other end, beneath a lithograph of a pink lady reclining in a seashell, six women in bright dresses received the homage of Roaring Fork.

The youngest and liveliest of these was Bismarck Anne.

She wore a red gown with black braid across the bodice. A miner had given her a red feather, another had presented her with a gilt bracelet, and a third, having nothing else, had offered her his week’s wages and a claim in the Silver Orphan. Anne had accepted the feather, lost the bracelet, drunk part of the wages, and expressed an unprintable opinion of the claim.

She was dancing now with Dutch Henry, a ponderous man who lifted his knees as if climbing stairs. Anne darted around him, pulled him after her, escaped beneath his arm, and came back laughing. She was healthy of body, hot of blood, and careless of all conclusions. Her black hair had shaken loose. Her cheeks matched her dress. When she laughed, the nearest men laughed also, though they had not heard the cause.

Two strangers stood near the door.

One was a young Easterner named Leigh, who had come to inspect a mine for gentlemen in Boston. He wore a clean collar, though it was midnight, and regarded the dance with the stern fascination of one studying vice from a safe moral elevation. The other, Bill Gannett, had lived so long among mining camps that he could distinguish a quarrel from a courtship only by the final shot.

Leigh watched Anne for some minutes. His expression grew troubled.

“Who is that girl?”

Bill followed his glance. “Her? That’s Bismarck Anne.”

“Bismarck?”

“She took the town by storm.”

“I mean, who are her people?”

“Roaring Fork, mostly.”

“You know what I mean. She cannot belong here.”

Bill considered this. “She belongs here enough to owe everybody money.”

“She looks scarcely twenty.”

“Maybe she is. Maybe she ain’t. Years don’t count straight in camps.”

At that moment Dutch Henry, having attempted a flourish beyond his architecture, fell backward through a card table. Anne planted one foot upon his waistcoat and called for a drink. The crowd applauded.

Leigh’s face flushed.

“This is damnable.”

“It’s a high old time,” Bill agreed.

“Those brutes are making sport of her.”

“Son, she’s making sport of them.”

“You cannot tell me that a girl chooses such a life.”

“I ain’t tellin’ you anything. I’m watchin’ the show.”

“There is innocence in her face.”

“Likely. There’s flour on Pete Haskins’s coat, too, but he ain’t a biscuit.”

Leigh turned angrily. “You Western men excuse everything.”

“No. We just quit explainin’ things that keep on happenin’.”

Anne had climbed upon the bar. She held a tumbler above her head while the men shouted her title.

“To Bismarck!” cried Dutch Henry from the ruins of the table.

“To war!” answered Anne, and drank.

Leigh stepped forward, but Bill caught his sleeve.

“What do you mean to do?”

“Take her away.”

“Where?”

“Anywhere out of this.”

Bill’s weathered face opened in genuine amusement. “You’ll need a sack.”

“She is intoxicated.”

“That’s her kick.”

“She is being degraded before our eyes.”

Bill looked once more at Anne, who had seized the bartender’s towel and was crowning a bald miner with it.

“Well,” he said, “if she’s goin’ downhill, she’s took the long way around.”

Leigh shook him off. Before he had gone three steps, Anne sprang down from the bar and whirled into another dance. Her new partner was Red Morris, the camp bully, who danced with the solemnity of a bear performing under compulsion. Anne drove him faster. The fiddle shrieked. The guitar surrendered. Feet beat the floor until glasses trembled along the shelves.

Then the hall began to break into pieces for Anne.

There was no longer a room, but bursts of room: a red lantern swimming near the roof; Bill Gannett’s moustache; a white hand; the pink lady in the shell; Dutch Henry’s open mouth; yellow whisky rising in a glass; black boots; red dress; smoke.

Round and round.

A face came close, vanished, returned as another face. Somebody shouted in her left ear. Somebody kissed her right cheek. The fiddle became a bright wire drawn through her head. She laughed because the wire tickled. She drank because a glass stood where her hand fell. She danced because the floor tipped and only dancing kept her upright.

Red—yellow—black.

A man shouted.

A chair lifted like a curious animal above the crowd.

The lamps swung.

There came a crash at the bar. Red Morris had objected to another man’s admiration of Anne, though Anne herself had encouraged it. The admirer replied with a bottle. Red answered with a stool. Men surged toward the pleasure of the matter; women climbed upon tables; the fiddler continued for seven seconds out of professionalism and then disappeared behind his instrument.

Leigh saw Anne between two struggling miners.

He forced his way toward her. “Come with me!”

She stared at him. His collar interested her.

“You’re too white,” she said.

A revolver cracked. One lantern burst, scattering orange flame and blue smoke. The bartender dropped behind his counter. A man near the door put both hands to his side and sat down slowly in a widening pool of blood.

Anne watched the blood. It was redder than her dress and appeared to her of immense importance. Then someone spun her around, and she forgot it.

The crowd opened, closed, and burst through the doors. Anne went with it. Outside, the night struck her coolly across the face. She laughed and ran. She heard hoofs, curses, a woman crying, the thin continuation of the fiddle from within, though perhaps the fiddle was only in her head.

She passed the assay office, the blacksmith’s shed, the last cabin, and the heap of pale tailings beyond the town. No one stopped her. No one saw that she had gone.

The trail divided. Anne chose neither branch. She climbed through sagebrush, lost one shoe in a patch of mud, and carried the other in her hand until she lost that also. The mountains moved gravely around her. The moon rolled in and out of the clouds.

At length she came upon a little dell cupped between two ridges. Grass lay deep there, watered by a spring no miner had yet troubled to name. Anne sank beside a granite boulder, pulled her red skirt over her shoulders, and fell asleep.

Behind her, Roaring Fork roared on.

---

Dawn entered the mountains by degrees.

First the high peaks received it, their snows blushing as though surprised in sleep. Then a thin gold line descended the eastern walls, touching pine after pine, while the ravines remained filled with violet shadow. Mist lifted from the creek. The breeze, tired from its night journey, lay down among the grasses. From a cedar came one clear bird-note, then another, and presently every thicket had joined the chorus of praise.

The world sketched in the picture of day with a patient hand: green willow, grey stone, silver water, blue distance, and the young sun pouring gold over all.

Anne stirred.

For a time she knew nothing but warmth. The earth held her easily. A red bird hopped near her bare foot, cocked its head, and flew to a branch where it burned like a living coal among the leaves. Anne opened her eyes.

She did not remember the dance. The thirst in her mouth, the ache behind her eyes, the torn braid on her dress—these belonged to some person who had gone away. She sat up, and the dew jewelled her loosened hair. With her cheeks pale and her hands folded about her knees, she looked younger than she had under the lanterns. Solitude removed the gestures by which the camp knew her. The hills did not call her Bismarck. The creek had heard no scandal. To bird, grass, sky, and morning she was merely another warm creature awakened in the sun.

She drank at the spring. The water was so cold that it hurt her teeth, yet she drank again and laughed softly when it ran down her chin. She washed her face. She braided her hair with wet fingers. In the clear pool she saw a woman without powder, feather, or hard gaiety, and for one innocent moment she accepted that face as her own.

Then hoofbeats sounded above the dell.

A young rider came down the trail, leading a second horse packed with books and blankets. He was perhaps twenty-five, broad-shouldered, brown from the sun, and dressed more plainly than most prosperous men of the country. His horse picked a careful path among the stones.

When he saw Anne, he stopped.

She rose quickly. Her skirt was torn, her feet bare, her hair wet. Yet the morning had arranged her so gently that he might have taken her for a child lost from some emigrant train.

“Are you hurt?” he asked.

His voice was quiet. There was no jest in it, no bargain, no familiar claim. He dismounted but did not approach too near.

Anne looked at his face.

It was not saintly. It was only clean and concerned. But goodness, when it comes unexpectedly before one who has lived without it, may be more terrible than accusation. In his eyes Anne saw what she might have been. Behind him rose the gold-lit hills; above him the red bird flashed from cedar to cedar; about him stood that unpurchased morning. Suddenly she remembered the lanterns, the mouths, the hands, Dutch Henry beneath her foot, the whisky on her lips, and the blood widening on the floor.

Her purification vanished, not because the dawn had lied, but because she had learned to compare.

She covered her face.

“Don’t look at me.”

The rider misunderstood. “I won’t harm you.”

“That’s worse.”

She began to tremble. He took one step, and she dropped beside the spring, sobbing with the violence she had previously reserved for laughter. The red bird flew away.

The young man stood helplessly. He had intended kindness and had produced despair. At last he laid his coat upon a stone near her.

“There’s a preaching camp a half-mile down,” he said. “Women there will help you.”

He mounted and rode on, looking back once. His pity remained after him like a wound.

Anne put on the coat and followed the creek.

The preaching camp consisted of two wagons, a grey tent, three tethered mules, and a white-bearded evangelist addressing the wilderness. His congregation had gone to gather wood, but he continued his rehearsal with unabated majesty.

“Behold,” he cried, one hand raised toward a granite cliff, “the wages of sin is death! The grass withereth, the flower fadeth, but the word endureth forever!”

The cliff returned a faint “forever.”

Anne stopped at the edge of the clearing.

The preacher saw her, though his gesture continued to include the mountain. “Daughter, have you come seeking the Word?”

“I want to quit.”

He lowered his arm. “Quit what?”

“All of it.”

Her face crumpled again. She told him, not her history, for she had never considered herself to possess one, but a confused catalogue of acts: drinking, fighting, dancing, waking in strange rooms, taking money, losing money, laughing at funerals, and once throwing a Bible into a stove because its owner had refused to buy champagne. As she spoke, these actions assumed a shape they had never worn while being done. They stood together and accused her.

The evangelist was a sincere man, but at that moment he was also a hurried one. His mules had strayed, breakfast was burning, and a sermon on the tribes of Israel pressed heavily upon his mind.

Anne seized his sleeve.

“Tell me what to do.”

“My child, repentance—”

“I’ve repented. It hurts. What comes next?”

He glanced toward the wagon, from which smoke had begun to issue.

“What must I do so I don’t go back?”

The preacher searched his crowded memory for an answer. Scripture presented many; urgency selected one.

“Let her labor,” he declared, “working with her hands the thing which is good.”

Anne stared.

“With my hands?”

“Yes, yes. Honest labor. Industry. The idle soul is a field of tares.” He sniffed the air. “Excuse me—the biscuits.”

He hurried away.

Anne remained beneath the pines, repeating the words. Working with her hands. They were plain words. They required no understanding, no miracle, no past. She looked at her hands as if they had just been given to her. They were strong hands, though the nails were broken and a ring cut into one finger.

The answer seemed direct. Heaven, having brought her out of the dance, washed her in the spring, shown her goodness on horseback, and stationed a prophet beside the creek, had now prescribed the road.

She returned to Roaring Fork before noon.

At the Monarch Hotel she entered by the kitchen and addressed Mrs. Doolan, who presided over three stoves, two Chinese cooks, a mountain of dirty plates, and one exhausted girl.

“I want work.”

Mrs. Doolan stared at the torn red dress and man’s coat.

“Who from?”

“God.”

“Does He give references?”

“No.”

Mrs. Doolan looked toward the plates. “Can you wash?”

“I got hands.”

“So has a clock, but it don’t clear tables. Fifty cents a day and meals.”

Anne removed the rider’s coat, folded it carefully, and laid it on a flour barrel.

“I’ll start now.”

---

The first day was glorious.

Grease became sin, hot water became tribulation, and every plate that emerged white from Anne’s dishpan shone like a little absolution. She scrubbed with fervor. She scoured kettles until her knuckles bled and welcomed the pain as proof that salvation had begun. At night she slept on a cot under the stairs and dreamed of the red bird.

The second day was less glorious, but still important. There were one hundred and eighty-seven plates after breakfast, and nearly as many after dinner. Miners left yellow ridges of egg, grey gravy, bones, tobacco, and once a dead beetle upon them. Anne washed all without complaint.

The third day the water seemed hotter, the grease more stubborn, and heaven farther from the kitchen.

On the fourth, Dutch Henry looked through the service hatch.

“Well, if it ain’t Bismarck! Heard you’d got religion.”

“I’m working.”

“So I see. Come have one.”

“No.”

He blinked, grinned uncertainly, and went away. Anne’s refusal filled her with pride for almost an hour. Then she resented him for having asked only once.

The fifth day brought rain. Mud came into the dining room on forty boots and returned to the kitchen on forty plates. Mrs. Doolan scolded Anne for breaking a cup. Anne replied that the cup had been cracked.

“Cracked cups cost money,” said Mrs. Doolan.

“So do people.”

“I can get another girl.”

Anne bent again to the dishpan.

The sixth day had no character at all. It was a long corridor of plates.

On the seventh, bells rang from the mission tent beyond town. Anne expected some warmth to rise in her. None did. Her hands were swollen, her back ached, and the smell of old fat had entered her hair. She began to suspect that honest labor differed from other hardships chiefly in being repeated at regular hours.

It is easy to renounce pleasure in the presence of mountains. It is harder beside a tub after supper.

On the eighth day Roaring Fork received another pay-roll.

By afternoon the fiddle could be heard tuning in the dance hall. Men washed, shaved, lied about their wages, and put on bright neckcloths. A wagon arrived with whisky. Someone passed the Monarch kitchen whistling the first bars of “The Girl I Left Behind Me.”

Anne dropped a plate.

Mrs. Doolan turned. “That’s two.”

Anne looked at her hands. They were red, wrinkled, and smelling of soap. These were the hands by which she had been told to save herself. They had washed perhaps two thousand dishes. Yet no gate had opened. No voice had spoken. The rider had not returned for his coat.

From down the street came laughter.

At seven Anne took the red dress from beneath her cot. At seven-ten she put it on. At seven-fifteen she painted her cheeks. At seven-twenty Mrs. Doolan found the dishpan full, the plates unwashed, and the rider’s coat lying neatly upon the flour barrel.

At nine Bismarck Anne entered the dance hall.

The miners greeted her with a roar that shook dust from the rafters. Dutch Henry lifted her onto the bar. Red Morris ordered champagne. The lamps were red, yellow, and blue; the fiddle shrieked; the guitar despaired; and Anne, drinking deeper than she had ever drunk before, flung herself into the dance as if motion could trample memory.

“Thought you’d reformed!” Bill Gannett shouted.

Anne raised her glass.

“I got tired!”

They cheered. Before midnight she was the drunkest person in Roaring Fork, which was distinction in a severe field.

Outwardly, nothing had altered. The same red dress spun beneath the same garish lamps. The same men applauded. The same whisky burned, the same boots thundered, the same laughter rose.

But once she had danced as the bird flew and the creek ran—without judgment, because she knew no other way. Now behind every lamp waited the dawn; behind every coarse face, the quiet face of the rider; beneath the fiddle, the preacher’s accidental command. She had seen another life, mistaken drudgery for its gate, and turned back when the gate proved heavy.

Thus knowledge accomplished what appetite alone had never done.

It made her revelry a mortal sin.

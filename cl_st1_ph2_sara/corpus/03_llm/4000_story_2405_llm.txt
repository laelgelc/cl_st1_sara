### THE SQUIRE’S LEG  
*Being a Relation Found among the Papers of the Reverend Michael Flannery, Late Parish Priest of Ballycroghan*

Among the miscellaneous manuscripts committed to my examination after the death of the Reverend Michael Flannery—whose antiquarian diligence was equalled only by the illegibility of his handwriting—I discovered several narratives illustrative of those popular superstitions which, notwithstanding the advance of education, agricultural improvement, and other agencies conventionally supposed to discourage commerce with the invisible world, continued to flourish in the western districts during the earlier part of the present century.

The following relation concerns Ballycroghan Castle, a building now reduced, by rain, ivy, and the indifference of its proprietor, to a condition more picturesque than profitable. The eastern tower has wholly disappeared; sheep graze in what was once the dining-hall; and the great staircase, upon which three generations of the Neil family declared they had heard the late squire descending after midnight, was removed in 1847 and converted, with commendable economy, into stalls for cattle. It should be observed, however, that since the removal of that staircase the apparition has not been seen—a circumstance interpreted by sceptics as favourable to their position, and by believers as proof that the deceased gentleman objected to passing through a cow-house.

The superstition attached principally to a portrait of Squire Cornelius Blake, who had been master of the estate in the reign of George the Third. He was represented in a red coat, with one hand resting upon a table and the other holding a riding-whip; behind him appeared a black horse, a column, and a thunder-cloud, none of which, according to parish tradition, had been present when the picture was painted. The squire’s left leg had been broken in a hunting accident, badly treated, and thereafter shorter than its companion. His temper, never distinguished for Christian meekness, deteriorated under this bodily inconvenience; and after his death it was reported that he traversed the castle on stormy nights, striking the floors with his stick and demanding liquor from rooms in which no liquor had been placed.

Father Flannery states that he took down the narrative substantially as it was delivered by Darby Neil, son of the person most intimately concerned. Darby, he adds, possessed a retentive memory, a sincere reverence for truth, and an unfortunate admiration for words of more than three syllables, which he introduced into discourse without prejudice arising from their meaning. As his peculiarities form no inconsiderable part of the evidence, I have preserved them with scrupulous fidelity.

Here the priest’s memorandum ends, and the testimony begins.

***

Well, your reverence—an’ your honour too, sir, if there’s no offence in givin’ the two titles together—it was my father, Terry Neil, that seen the sperit, an’ no other man; though there’s plenty this day’ll tell you they seen him themselves, for there’s nothin’ grows by tellin’ like a ghost, barrin’ a landlord’s rent an’ a neighbour’s misfortune.

My father was the finest bone-setter in the seven parishes. I’m not sayin’ it because he was my father, for if he’d been no relation at all he’d be the same excellent man, only I wouldn’t know so much about him. There wasn’t a joint from Ballycroghan to Kilfenora but Terry knew the constitution of it, whether it belonged to man, woman, child, horse, cow, or any other Christian. He could put a shoulder in while you’d be sayin’ “Lord preserve us,” an’ have it out again before you’d know which was the proper way; though, to be sure, puttin’ it out wasn’t his regular practice.

He was a small, broad man, with a round face an’ a hand like the root of an oak. He was sober beyond description, except whin he’d take a glass, which he did from natural weakness an’ not from intemperance; an’ his word was incredible through the whole country. If Terry said a thing happened, why, it happened—or something near it, an’ that’s as much truth as can reasonably be expected after twenty years.

He held twelve acres under the Blakes, an’ bad acres they were, full of stones that multiplied faster than the praties. If you cleared a field of them on Monday, there’d be a fresh family of rocks lookin’ at you Tuesday mornin’. But that doesn’t signify. The agent, Mister Clancy, sent for my father one wet day in November, an’ says he:

“Terry Neil, you’re wanted at the castle.”

“Is it bones, sir?” says my father.

“It may come to that,” says Mister Clancy, “if you refuse.”

Now that was the pleasant way the agents had in them times; they’d make a joke first an’ ruin you after, so there’d be entertainment mixed with business.

It seems there was to be a great hunt at Ballycroghan, an’ the new squire—young Mister Robert Blake, that lived mostly in Dublin because the roof of the castle let in the rain an’ the tenants let out no money—wanted the place aired an’ made respectable. But the servants wouldn’t stay there after dark, on account of the ould squire walkin’. Every night there’d be knockin’ in the long gallery, an’ groans in the blue room, an’ the clatter of a lame step on the stairs; an’ once the cook found the pantry door open with two bottles of port gone, which she said was supernatural, though the coachman held a different theology.

So Mister Clancy says somebody must watch in the great parlour till mornin’, to show the servants there was no danger.

“An’ why wouldn’t you do it yourself, sir?” says my father, respectful enough.

“Because,” says Mister Clancy, “I do not encourage superstition.”

“Nor me,” says Terry; “but wouldn’t that make your company all the more improvin’?”

At that Mister Clancy looked at him in the severe way of authority, an’ says:

“You’ll keep watch to-night, Neil, or I’ll reconsider the little question of your arrears.”

Now my father had only three years’ rent behind, which was moderation itself in that country; but he knew a reconsideration from Mister Clancy was generally followed by a process-server. So he touched his hat.

“I’ll do it, sir,” says he. “An’ if the sperit comes?”

“Speak to him rationally.”

“I will, sir. An’ if he won’t listen to reason?”

“Then use your own discretion.”

“Would a blackthorn be discretion, sir?”

“You may take what precautions you please.”

So my father went home to make preparations. He took his ash stick, his tobacco, two candles blessed at Candlemas, a bottle of whiskey, an’ a small bottle of holy water the priest had given my mother when the red cow was bewitched. There was a prayer-book too, but he left that behind because, says he, he couldn’t be burdened with unnecessary articles.

My mother cried, an’ said he’d never come back alive.

“Whisht, woman,” says he. “If I come back dead, you’ll have the satisfaction of sayin’ you foretold it.”

“That’s little comfort,” says she.

“It’s as much as most prophets get,” says my father.

He set out at dusk, with the rain slantin’ across the bog, an’ the wind cryin’ in the thorn bushes like a child that lost its way. Ballycroghan Castle stood black above the river, with the rookery tossin’ behind it an’ a light showin’ now an’ again in the broken windows, though there was nobody inside to carry one. My father seen that light, but he said nothin’, because he was a man of tremendous courage when there was no one near to hear him admit otherwise.

At the gate he found Lawrence Connor, the ould steward, waitin’ with a lantern. Larry was near eighty, an’ had served three squires, two agents, an’ one mistress that was worse than the whole five. He was bent like a reapin’-hook, but stiff in his opinions; an’ he knew every room in the castle, except them he’d forgotten.

“God save you, Terry,” says he.

“God save yourself, Larry. Are you to keep me company?”

“I am,” says Larry, “by the squire’s express direction.”

“That’s a comfort,” says my father.

“It needn’t be,” says Larry, “for I sleep sound.”

They went through the hall, where the flags were green with damp, an’ the armour on the walls made motions in the lantern-light, an’ the wind came under the doors with a smell of earth, soot, an’ dead fires. Up the grand staircase they went—every board cryin’ out under them—to the long parlour where the portrait hung.

It was a quare room, your honour. There was a chimney big enough to roast an ox, though the fire in it wouldn’t have warmed a snipe. There were twelve chairs with carved legs an’ faces on their backs, all grinnin’ as if they knew the joke beforehand. At one end stood a clock stopped at twenty minutes past one; at the other hung the portrait of Squire Cornelius, life-size an’ proud enough to be twice that.

He had a red coat, white breeches, a black cravat, an’ boots that came above the knee. One hand held the whip, an’ the other rested beside a silver drinking-cup. His face was long an’ yellow, with little black eyes; an’ the painter had made the bad leg shorter, which showed he was either a truthful artist or had a spite against the family.

Larry put turf on the fire an’ a candle on the table.

“There now,” says he. “That’s himself.”

“An’ a powerful bad likeness,” says Terry, hopin’, maybe, the picture would be flattered.

“It’s as like him as one curse is like another,” says Larry. “I was a boy when he died.”

“Did he walk then?”

“Not before he died,” says Larry, “except with a stick.”

“I mean after.”

“After, he walked better.”

My father didn’t relish that information, so he drew a chair close to the hearth, took a small taste from the bottle to prevent the cold from enterin’ his stomach, an’ offered the same medicine to Larry.

Larry took it, for courtesy; then another, for fear the first might stand alone; an’ after that a third, because odd numbers are lucky. They smoked their pipes an’ talked of harvests, funerals, rent-days, an’ the great frost, till the fire sank an’ the shadows grew long.

Every now an’ then my father looked at the portrait. At first the squire’s eyes appeared to be fixed on the window. Then they seemed to turn toward the table. Then they looked straight at the whiskey.

“Larry,” says Terry, “does that picture move?”

“Never by daylight,” says Larry.

“But now?”

“Ask it,” says Larry, an’ gave a yawn that shook the ashes from his pipe.

Within five minutes he was asleep, with his chin on his breast an’ a snore risin’ out of him like a sawmill workin’ under water.

“Larry,” says my father.

“Ugh,” says Larry.

“Keep awake.”

“I am awake,” says Larry, still snorin’.

My father took another little drop—not drinkin’, you understand, but preservin’ himself from the vapours of the room. The clock, though stopped, gave a small click. The wind fell. Larry ceased snorin’ all of a sudden, which was more frightenin’ than the noise. There came three taps from inside the wall:

Tap.

Tap.

Tap.

My father looked at the door. It was shut.

He looked at the windows. They were shuttered.

He looked at the portrait, an’ the silver cup painted on the table was gone.

“Terry Neil,” says a voice.

My father turned cold from the crown of his head to the buckle of his shoe; but he crossed himself an’ says:

“Who’s callin’ me?”

“Come here.”

“Certainly, your honour,” says Terry, for he knew the squire’s voice, though he’d never heard it while the man was alive.

The picture swelled outward like a sail in the wind. The squire lifted one boot over the bottom of the frame, then the other; an’ down he stepped upon the floor, solid as any gentleman, only with a faint light shinin’ through him an’ a smell about him of brimstone, wet stone, an’ old cellars.

He limped to the table.

“Your honour’s welcome,” says my father.

“You lie,” says the ghost.

“I do, your honour,” says Terry, “but only from politeness.”

“Give me a drink.”

“Would whiskey agree with your honour’s present constitution?”

“It agreed with my former one.”

“That’s sufficient medical authority,” says Terry, an’ filled him a glass.

The ghost drank it without drawin’ breath. You could see the liquor run down inside him like fire through blue glass, which my father considered highly unnatural, though he didn’t say so.

“Another,” says the squire.

Terry gave him another.

“Good whiskey,” says the ghost.

“It’s the best,” says my father, which was not strictly the case, for it was made by Widow Hynes behind her turf-stack an’ had removed the hair from a badger; but contradiction to a dead landlord would be neither manners nor safety.

The squire sat down. The chair groaned under him, though whether from his weight or his company no man can certify.

“How’s the estate?” says he.

“Flourishin’, your honour.”

“How are the tenants?”

“Flourishin’ likewise.”

“Are they paying their rents?”

“Well,” says Terry, “not so as to injure the circulation of money.”

“Rascals!” says the ghost. “In my day I’d have driven them out.”

“You did, your honour.”

“What?”

“I said you had a grand reputation for charitable an’ inhuman proceedings.”

The ghost looked pleased at the big words, an’ held out his glass.

“You always had sense, Neil.”

“Thank you, your honour.”

“Which makes your present poverty inexcusable.”

“That’s what Mister Clancy says.”

At the agent’s name the squire gave a grin so terrible the candle burned blue.

“Clancy will come to me,” says he.

“Not for many years, God grant,” says Terry.

“Soon,” says the squire.

“Well, your honour, I won’t spoil the surprise.”

The ghost drank again, then stretched his left leg before the fire an’ rubbed the knee.

“That leg,” says he, “has tormented me these forty years.”

“I heard it was broke huntin’.”

“Broke by a wall.”

“Was the horse throwin’ you?”

“No. The wall fell on me because I was pulling down a widow’s cabin.”

“Ah,” says Terry, “that would be what they call poetic jurisprudence.”

“Silence! It was badly set.”

“Doctor Philbin set it, didn’t he?”

“He did, the ignorant butcher. Look at it.”

Now my father had no wish to handle the apparition; but professional pride is a dangerous thing, an’ he bent down, keepin’ one eye on the squire’s face an’ the other, as well as he could, on the leg.

The boot was cold as winter iron. Terry felt along the shin, the ankle, an’ the knee.

“It’s a bad job,” says he.

“I know that.”

“The two ends of the bone are overlapin’.”

“I know that too.”

“The muscles have contracted.”

“Don’t display your learning. Mend it.”

“Mend it, your honour?”

“You are a bone-setter.”

“For the livin’, sir.”

“Am I not standing before you?”

“You are, your honour; but I wouldn’t like to give an opinion on the technicality.”

The ghost leaned forward. His little black eyes widened, an’ there was no white round them—only a red glimmer like turf under ashes.

“Do you know where I’ve been, Terry Neil?”

“I wouldn’t take the liberty of guessin’.”

“I am condemned,” says the squire, “to carry cold water day an’ night to thirsty souls in a hot place.”

My father bowed, not knowin’ what compliment fitted the information.

“It’s laborious work,” the ghost went on, “an’ the road is steep. There are sharp stones, burning cinders, and a superintendent with a fork who allows no rest. Every drop I carry turns to steam before it reaches the poor devils waiting for it.”

“Your honour always had bad luck with public improvements.”

“My leg makes the labour intolerable. Set it straight, an’ I may earn some alleviation.”

“I’ll do my best,” says Terry, “but a bone that’s been forty years dead is apt to be obstropolous.”

“Set it.”

“You’d require bandages, sir, an’ splints, an’ maybe six weeks’ quiet.”

“I have no six weeks.”

“A fortnight?”

“No.”

“Till Monday?”

The ghost rose, an’ the room darkened round him.

“Pull it straight this instant, or I’ll break every bone in your body one by one, and make you set them yourself afterward.”

“That’s conclusive,” says Terry. “Sit down, your honour.”

The squire sat. My father took the spectral boot between his knees an’ gripped the ankle. The cold of it ran up his arms into his teeth.

“Whin I say pull,” says the squire, “pull.”

“I’d rather your honour said stop.”

“Pull!”

Terry pulled.

The ghost caught the arms of the chair, an’ the chair gave a crack.

“Harder!”

My father pulled harder.

“Is it comin’?” says the squire.

“It’s considerin’ the matter.”

“Pull, you bog-born ape!”

“Easy, your honour. Abuse contracts the nerves.”

“Pull!”

Terry braced both feet against the chair an’ leaned back. There came a sound from the leg—not the crack of a proper Christian bone, but a long creak like a door openin’ in an empty house.

“That’s it!” cried the squire. “Again!”

“Give it a moment to reconcile itself.”

“Again, I say!”

My father pulled until his face went purple. The room shook, the shutters beat, an’ Larry snored on through it all, peaceful as a saint carved on a tomb. The squire cursed Doctor Philbin, the widow whose cabin had fallen, the horse, the wall, the tenants, an’ my father’s professional incapacity. Then, growin’ thirsty with the exertion, he reached behind him for the whiskey.

Now there were two bottles on the table, as I told you: the square black bottle of whiskey, an’ the little clear bottle of holy water. My father had moved them while makin’ room, or the squire’s sight was affected by his habitation; anyhow, instead of the whiskey he caught up the holy water.

Terry saw it.

“Beggin’ your honour’s pardon—”

The squire pulled out the cork with his teeth.

“That’s not—”

He raised the bottle.

“Don’t drink that, sir!”

“Pull!” roared the squire, an’ swallowed.

There was a silence no longer than the wink of an eye.

Then the ghost gave a scream.

It wasn’t a human scream, nor the cry of any beast upon the earth. It was the noise of iron gates tearin’ from their hinges, of a thousand kettles boilin’ dry, of the wind in all the chimneys of Ireland at once. Blue fire burst from his mouth. Smoke rolled from his ears. His red coat blazed without burnin’, an’ the windows flew open though the shutters were barred.

“Pull!” he shrieked.

My father pulled from fright, duty, an’ the impossibility of doin’ anything else.

The leg came off.

Away went Terry across the room, backward over a stool, with the limb clutched to his breast. The ghost rose to the ceilin’ in a whirl of smoke, kickin’ with the one leg that remained to him. He struck the portrait, passed into it upside down, an’ vanished with a clap that brought half the plaster down the chimney.

After that my father remembered no more.

***

At daylight Lawrence Connor woke because the fire was out and his feet were cold. He found Terry stretched beneath the window, his coat torn, his hair full of plaster, and both hands locked round the carved leg of one of the great chairs. The chair itself lay overturned near the table. The whiskey bottle was empty; the holy-water bottle was empty too; and the portrait of Squire Cornelius hung as before, except that the painted figure’s left boot appeared fainter than the right—a distinction some observers afterward denied and others perceived even before entering the room.

“Terry,” says Larry, shakin’ him, “wake up.”

“Don’t let him at me!” says Terry.

“There’s nobody here.”

“His leg! Take away his leg!”

“It’s a chair leg, man.”

“Maybe it is now,” says my father, openin’ one eye; “but it had a worse disposition in the night.”

Larry maintained he had heard nothin’. My father maintained Larry would have slept through the Last Trump if the angel played it softly. They searched the room an’ found scorch marks on the table, a crack through the portrait’s frame, and one silver buckle lying in the ashes. Whether that buckle belonged to the squire, to an ancient curtain, or to Larry’s shoe was never legally determined, because Larry threw it into the river before breakfast.

Mister Clancy said the whole business proceeded from whiskey.

“That may be,” says Terry, “but if whiskey can bring a dead landlord out of a picture, there ought to be a law against givin’ it to agents.”

The new squire laughed when he heard the account, though he ordered the portrait removed from the parlour. Two men carried it to the lumber-room in daylight; neither would go back for his tools until the priest accompanied him. It remained there till part of the roof fell in, after which it was said to have been sent to Dublin, though no one could name the carrier. Some say it was burned. Some say it hangs in another house under another title. My aunt Bridget swore she saw it once in a pawnbroker’s window in Limerick, but she also swore the Duke of Wellington asked her to dance, so there’s moderation required.

As for my father, he was never the same careless man again. From that mornin’ forward he went to confession regular, heard Mass whenever the weather, work, health, distance, sleep, or a sick cow didn’t prevent him, an’ kept holy water in every room of the house. He gave up watchin’ in castles entirely. He gave up whiskey too—for nearly three weeks—which proved the depth of his consternation.

An’ there’s one thing more, your reverence, that shows the substantiality of the occurrence. Before that night the ould squire was heard walkin’ up an’ down the gallery with a lame step: thump—drag, thump—drag, from twelve till cockcrow. But after my father set him, no one ever heard him walk there again.

You may say that’s because the ghost was laid.

You may say it’s because the portrait was removed.

You may even say, as Mister Clancy did, that there was never a ghost in it.

But my father’s explanation was the natural one.

“How could he walk,” says Terry Neil, “when I had the best leg of him under my bed?”

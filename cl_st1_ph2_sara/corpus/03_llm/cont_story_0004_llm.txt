By the time my parents began to resemble each other, I had stopped trying to persuade them to move out of the flat.

They had lived there for thirty-eight years, first as university lecturers with two children and a succession of cousins, house helps, visiting colleagues, and stranded acquaintances; later as retired people surrounded by the remains of those years. Every room was overfull. Books were stacked beneath chairs and behind curtains. My mother kept old bottles of Mateus rosé on top of the kitchen cabinets because she liked the shape of them. On the sideboard stood a family of Russian dolls, bought during a conference in Moscow, although the smallest doll had been missing since my sister was ten.

On that visit, I noticed that my father had begun to use my mother’s expressions. He said “Imagine that” with her measured indignation and narrowed his eyes before disagreeing with the television. My mother, who had once mocked his habit of rubbing Vicks VapoRub on every imaginable pain, now carried a small jar in her handbag.

Their faces, too, had drawn closer. Their cheeks had loosened in the same places. Their lips had thinned. From the side, in the evening light, their profiles seemed to overlap.

“You are looking at us as if you want to paint us,” my mother said.

“I’m only looking.”

“Because you have not seen old people before?”

My father laughed. “Leave him. Perhaps he is calculating how much the funeral will cost.”

“That is a foolish thing to say.”

“But practical.”

They spoke about death often and lightly, as though familiarity would reduce its authority. My sister disliked this and interrupted them whenever she called from Atlanta. I said nothing. I was forty-two, unmarried, and still visited three times a year with the dutiful punctuality of a man who had organized his life around absences.

My old bedroom was no longer mine. My father’s journals occupied the wardrobe; my mother’s examination scripts, tied with faded string, filled the lower shelves. I slept beneath a framed photograph of myself at eleven, wearing a school blazer too large for me. In the photograph I looked suspicious of the future.

That Sunday, after lunch, rain pressed softly against the louvres. My mother sat at the dining table, turning the pages of a newspaper. My father drank warm stout from a glass and watched a panel of men argue about corruption.

“These people enjoy hearing themselves,” he said.

“You enjoyed hearing yourself when you were on television,” my mother said.

“I had something to say.”

“They all think so.”

She flattened the newspaper with her palm. “Ifukwa? Look at this one. Armed robbers caught in Awka.”

My father did not turn. Armed robberies, kidnappings, rituals, miracle churches—such stories had become their evening entertainment and their evidence that the world had declined since their youth. My father, who had once dismissed every rumor as the product of an undisciplined mind, now believed that thieves could vanish by rubbing an egg on their foreheads. My mother, a retired professor of chemistry, accepted stories of bullets turning to water if they appeared in print.

“They say the leader used to be a houseboy in Nsukka,” she said. “Raphael Nwafor. The name is familiar.”

The name entered the room without warning.

My father looked at her. “Raphael?”

“Yes. Raphael Nwafor. From Umunnachi.” She read further. “He confessed to seven operations. They recovered two guns, charms, and a police uniform.”

“Was that not the boy who worked for us?”

My mother pushed her glasses higher. “Which boy?”

“The tall one. In the old house.”

“We had many boys.”

“The one before Sunday.”

She studied the small blurred photograph. “Ah. Perhaps. He had very large eyes.”

I remembered.

Of course I remembered Raphael.

Memory did not return gently. It came complete: the smell of wet soil behind the boys’ quarters, the slap of his bare feet on cement, the low whistle he made before striking, and the quick turn of his wrists as the pieces of wood spun around him.

I was twelve when he came to us. He was seventeen, although at first I thought he was older because he had muscles along his arms and a faint roughness on his chin. My mother brought him back from a visit to a former student in Umunnachi. She said his father was dead and his mother had too many children. In our house this explanation was considered sufficient. It accounted for his presence and, in some unspoken way, for what he could expect from us.

He slept in the boys’ quarters behind the kitchen, in a room with a narrow bed, a bucket, and a wooden box for his clothes. He washed my father’s Peugeot on Saturdays, swept the compound, carried water when the taps stopped running, and helped our cook, Beatrice, grind beans for akara. My parents called him a good boy because he learned quickly and rarely broke things.

At first I did not notice him. Houseboys came and went. Some left to learn a trade; some were sent away after money disappeared; one vanished after being told to wash the curtains. They belonged to the domestic weather of my childhood, present but not examined.

Then Raphael saw me practicing a flying kick.

It was an afternoon during the long vacation. My parents were at work. Beatrice had gone to Odenigbo Market, and I was in the living room watching a kung fu film on our video machine. The picture trembled because the cassette had been copied many times. Men in pale robes leaped from roofs and landed with their arms spread. They moved as if the air had agreed to hold them.

I rewound one fight three times. Afterward, in the corridor, I attempted a kick I had seen. My foot rose no higher than my waist, and I struck the wall with my elbow.

Behind me, Raphael said, “Your leg is too stiff.”

I turned. He was holding a basket of washed clothes.

“You know how to do it?”

He smiled. Until then, I had thought his smile was merely a courteous response to my parents. That day it altered his face. “Small.”

“Show me.”

He set down the basket. He looked toward the kitchen, then lifted one leg slowly, almost lazily, and snapped it outward. His heel stopped close to my nose.

I stumbled back.

“Watch it!” he said, laughing.

“Do it again.”

He did. The second kick was higher. He turned his body, balanced for a moment, and brought his foot down without a sound.

I demanded to know where he had learned. He told me that, in his village, he and his friends watched films through the windows of a video parlour when they had no money. Later they copied the movements in a school field. One boy had broken a tooth. Another had dislocated his shoulder. Raphael spoke of these injuries with admiration.

After that we practiced in secret.

We chose the strip of earth between the boys’ quarters and the back wall, where pawpaw trees shielded us from the kitchen window. Raphael made nunchaku from two short pieces of broom handle joined with a length of metal chain. He wrapped the handles in black electrical tape. The first time he spun them, one piece hit his shoulder, but he did not stop. Soon the wood circled his body with a soft, regular hum.

“Watch it,” he would say before a difficult move.

The words became a promise. Watch this. Watch me become someone else.

I practiced punches until my knuckles hurt. Raphael taught me to bend my knees and turn my hips. He said strength came not from the arm but from the whole body. When I lost balance, he caught my waist. When I performed a kick correctly, he slapped my back and called me Master Okenwa.

“Don’t call me Master.”

“Why? You are the master’s son.”

He said it lightly, but I heard the distance in it. I told him to call me Okenwa.

He shook his head. “If Madam hears?”

“She won’t.”

“Okenwa,” he said, testing it.

My name sounded different in his mouth, stripped of family expectation. At school, boys called me Okens or Professor because my father was well known. My mother called me Okenwa only when she wanted an immediate answer. Raphael made the name private.

I began to measure my days by the hours when we could practice. At breakfast I listened for his movements in the kitchen. At night, lying under my mosquito net, I imagined new sequences for us: I would attack, he would block, I would sweep his legs, he would fall and roll. In these scenes we were evenly matched. There was no houseboy and no master’s son. There were only two fighters who understood the same code.

My admiration grew with the simple insistence of a plant bending toward sun. I admired the ropes of muscle in his forearms, the pale scar near his left nipple, the way he could open a bottle with the edge of a spoon. I admired his calmness when my father shouted and his ability to carry a full bucket in each hand. Once, when rain flooded the back path, he lifted me across it, although I was old enough to walk around. I put my arms around his neck. He smelled of soap, sweat, and kerosene.

I did not then have a language for the excitement I felt. I understood only that his attention warmed me and its absence diminished the day.

My parents saw our friendship as evidence of my good nature. My mother sometimes stood at the kitchen door and watched Raphael show me a stance.

“It is good that you are keeping Okenwa active,” she told him. “He reads too much.”

My father disagreed. “He does not read enough.”

“He is twelve.”

“At twelve I had finished Things Fall Apart.”

“You tell that story as if you wrote it.”

They laughed, and Raphael lowered his head. None of us imagined that what existed between us could be hidden in plain sight.

The conjunctivitis began during the harmattan. At school we called it Apollo, after the American space missions, although no one knew why. It moved through classrooms quickly. Children arrived with swollen lids and eyes red as palm oil. Teachers warned us not to share towels, not to touch our faces, not even to stare too long at an infected person, as though sight itself could transmit it.

Raphael caught it first.

At breakfast my mother noticed him squinting as he served tea.

“What is wrong with your eyes?”

“Nothing, Madam.”

“Look at me.”

He raised his face. His left eye was inflamed and wet.

“Jesus,” she said, drawing back. “Apollo. Go to your room.”

My father folded his newspaper. “Have you been using our towels?”

“No, Sir.”

“You people must be careful,” my mother told Beatrice. “Disinfect everything he touched.”

She drove to the pharmacy and returned with cotton wool, antiseptic, and a bottle of eye drops. She gave Raphael instructions from the back step, holding the medicine by its cap.

“Two drops in each eye, three times a day. Do not touch the nozzle. And stay in your room until it clears.”

“Yes, Madam.”

“Okenwa, don’t go near him.”

“I won’t.”

She looked at me for an extra moment. “I mean it.”

That afternoon, I went to his room.

The door was closed but not locked. When I knocked, Raphael asked who it was. His voice sounded thick.

“It’s me.”

“Go away.”

I entered.

He was sitting on the edge of the bed in an old singlet and shorts. The room was hot. A small high window admitted a square of dusty light. His metal plate, with half a piece of boiled yam on it, rested on the floor. The bottle of medicine stood unopened beside the plate.

“Why haven’t you used it?”

“I will use it.”

“When?”

“Later.”

“Your eyes are getting worse.”

Both lids were swollen now. Tears ran down his cheeks, though he was not crying. Seeing him reduced in this way disturbed me. Raphael, who could split firewood with one stroke, could not look at the light.

“Let me put it.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“I don’t like things entering my eye.”

“It’s medicine.”

He turned his face away. “Leave me.”

I picked up the bottle and read the label, prolonging my authority. “If you don’t use it, you can become blind.”

“Who told you?”

“I know.”

This was not true, but it persuaded him. He lay back on the narrow bed. I sat beside him and told him to look upward.

When I brought the nozzle close, he shut his eye.

“Raphael.”

“I’m trying.”

“You’re not trying.”

He laughed, then winced. “Ndo. Do it.”

I rested my fingers against his cheek and pulled down the lower lid. His skin was warmer than I expected. I released one drop, then another. He jerked and gripped my wrist.

“It burns.”

“It’s supposed to.”

“How do you know?”

“I know.”

He kept hold of my wrist while I treated the other eye. His hand was rough from work. The room seemed suddenly too small for both of us, filled with his breath and the pressure of his fingers. When I finished, he did not release me at once.

“Thank you,” he said.

The next morning I returned before school. At lunchtime, I told my teacher I had forgotten a book and came home to give him the second dose. For four days, I cared for him. I brought Milo, akara wrapped in newspaper, and a bottle of Lucozade taken from my mother’s cupboard. I washed the crust from his lashes with warm water. Each time he resisted the drops less.

Sometimes we spoke. Sometimes he lay with his eyes closed and I sat beside him, listening to the sounds from the main house. Once he raised his hand and touched my face, feeling for me.

“You will catch it,” he said.

“I don’t care.”

“You will care when it starts burning.”

“I won’t.”

He smiled. “Stubborn boy.”

The name should have offended me. Instead, I carried it through the day like praise.

His eyes cleared. Mine reddened two days later.

My mother responded as if I had confirmed her lowest opinion of my judgment.

“I told you not to go near him.”

“I didn’t.”

“Then how did you catch it?”

“It is everywhere in school,” I said.

My father peered at me over his glasses. “Do not interrogate the patient.”

“You keep quiet. It is because you allow him too much freedom.”

“I allow him freedom?”

They moved me into my bedroom and closed the curtains. My mother changed my sheets, placed a flask of hot water by the bed, and arranged medicine, cotton wool, biscuits, Lucozade, and a bell on my bedside table. She brought me pepper soup. My father came in with three novels and sat at the foot of the bed to discuss one I had not read.

Their care was abundant and irritating. I waited for Raphael.

On the first day he brought my lunch on a tray. He stood near the door.

“Kedu?” he asked.

“My eyes hurt.”

“Ndo.”

I waited for him to come closer. He did not.

“Put the tray there,” I said.

He placed it on the desk.

“You can sit.”

“Madam said I should not stay.”

“You already had it. You can’t catch it again.”

He looked toward the corridor. “I have work.”

Then he left.

I told myself he would return in the afternoon. He did not. The following morning he brought boiled yam and egg sauce.

“Did you practice?” I asked.

“No.”

“We can practice when I’m better.”

“Yes.”

His answers had become smooth and empty. I wondered if he was ashamed of our days in his room, or if, now that he no longer needed me, he had put me back in my proper place.

On the third day, I heard laughter in the backyard.

My eyes were still painful, but the swelling had reduced. I left my room and followed the sound to the kitchen veranda. Raphael was there with Josephine, the new house help from the downstairs flat. She was peeling plantains over a basin. Raphael leaned against the railing, holding one of the plantains like a microphone.

Josephine was fifteen or sixteen, with narrow hips and beads at the ends of her braids. I had seen her at the tap but had never spoken to her.

“You are telling lies,” she said.

“I can show you.”

“You? Show me what?”

Raphael lifted one leg in a slow imitation of a kung fu kick. Josephine shrieked and slapped his thigh. He caught her wrist. They laughed.

The ease between them stunned me. It was not merely that he was flirting with her. He was giving away our language. The stance, the mock seriousness, the promise to show—these were things I had believed belonged to us.

He saw me.

His smile disappeared. “Okenwa. Why are you outside?”

Josephine pulled her hand free. “Your eye is bad.”

I ignored her. “I want my medicine.”

“It is in your room,” Raphael said.

“I know.”

He waited.

“Come and put it for me.”

His expression changed, not quite to embarrassment. “You can put it yourself.”

“I put yours.”

Josephine looked from him to me.

Raphael said quietly, “Go inside. Madam will be angry.”

The humiliation of being dismissed before her was physical. Heat climbed my neck.

“I said come.”

“I’m busy.”

“Doing what?”

He glanced at the basin. “Helping her.”

“She doesn’t need help.”

Josephine stood. “I will go.”

“No,” Raphael said, still looking at me. “Finish it.”

That “no” was more than refusal. It was a choice made in front of me.

I returned to my room and rang the bell until my mother came.

“What is it?”

“I need my drops.”

“Where are they?”

“On the table.”

“Then use them.”

“I can’t.”

She sighed, but she administered the drops, wiped my face, and tucked the sheet around me. I lay still beneath her care, furious at Raphael and ashamed of needing him. My mother mistook my silence for pain.

“It will clear soon,” she said. “Ndo.”

When she left, I threw the bell against the wall.

That evening I waited near the kitchen veranda. The household settled into its routines: my father listened to the news, my mother marked a postgraduate thesis, Beatrice fried onions. The generator next door coughed into life.

Raphael came from the tap carrying a bucket.

“Why did you tell me to go inside?” I asked.

He continued toward the kitchen.

“I’m talking to you.”

“I heard.”

“Then answer.”

He set down the bucket. “What should I say?”

“You were showing Josephine our moves.”

He frowned. “Our moves?”

“The kick.”

He stared at me, and I understood from his face that what had been sacred to me might have been ordinary to him.

“It is only play,” he said.

“You like her.”

He glanced toward the kitchen. “Go inside.”

“You like her.”

“Okenwa, leave me.”

“Don’t call me that.”

Now he looked genuinely confused. “You said I should call you Okenwa.”

“I can make them send you away.”

The sentence appeared between us before I understood I had spoken it.

Raphael’s body became still. “Why will you say that?”

“Because you don’t do your work.”

“What work did I not do?”

“You refused to give me my medicine.”

“You are not a baby.”

“I gave you yours.”

His expression softened for a moment. This made me angrier than if he had laughed.

“Thank you,” he said. “But nobody asked you.”

I struck him.

It was a poor punch, thrown from the shoulder, exactly as he had taught me not to do. My fist landed against his chest. He caught my wrist easily.

“Stop.”

“Leave me.”

“You are behaving like a small child.”

“I hate you.”

I tried to pull free. He released me suddenly, perhaps to prevent us from struggling, and I staggered backward. The veranda had two shallow steps, but I was too close to the edge. My heel found nothing. I fell sideways and struck my forehead against the cement border of the flower bed.

For a few seconds there was no pain, only a white surprise. Then blood ran warm past my eyebrow.

Raphael jumped down.

“Okenwa!”

He reached for me. I pushed his hand away and screamed.

My parents came at once. My mother was first, still holding her pen. She knelt, saw the blood, and shouted for my father. Beatrice appeared at the kitchen door. Josephine stood behind her.

“What happened?” my mother asked.

I looked at Raphael.

His face held fear, but also something like trust. He believed I would say that I had fallen. The truth was so simple that perhaps he could not imagine any alternative.

“He pushed me,” I said.

No one spoke.

My father turned to Raphael. “You pushed him?”

“No, Sir.”

“He pushed me,” I said again.

Raphael looked at me. “Why are you saying that?”

“How dare you question him?” my mother said.

“Madam, I did not push him.”

My father’s anger was quiet. “Go to your room. Pack your things.”

Raphael did not move.

“Did you hear me?”

“Yes, Sir.”

“He should leave tonight,” my mother said. “A boy who can attack a child in his own home—God knows what else he has done.”

Beatrice brought a towel. My mother pressed it to my forehead. Her hands trembled.

Raphael was still looking at me.

“Tell them,” he said.

My father stepped toward him. “Get out of here.”

It was then that I first understood the full size of my lie. I had imagined punishment, perhaps a scolding, perhaps my parents warning him not to neglect me. I had not imagined the wooden box dragged from beneath his bed, his clothes folded into a rice sack, my father standing at the gate with money for transport.

There was time to correct it.

There was time while my mother cleaned the cut and complained that the clinic would be closed. There was time while my father telephoned Dr. Ekwueme. There was time when Beatrice whispered, “What happened between you people?” and I turned my face away.

Raphael came into the living room carrying his sack. He had changed into a faded blue shirt. The nunchaku protruded from the top of the sack, its chain dull beneath the electric light.

My father held out some notes. “Take this.”

Raphael did not accept them.

“You will need transport.”

“I don’t want it, Sir.”

“Don’t be foolish.”

Raphael took the money.

My mother sat beside me on the sofa, one arm around my shoulders. “You should be grateful we are not calling the police.”

Raphael’s eyes moved to my bandaged forehead.

“Did I push you?” he asked.

My mother drew in her breath. “Leave this house.”

I could have answered then.

I could have said that I hit him first. I could have said he caught my wrist and let go. I could have said I stepped backward without looking.

Instead, I lowered my eyes.

At the door Raphael paused. For one foolish moment, I thought he might say “Watch it,” and that the words would release me from what I had done. But he said nothing. The gate opened, scraped over the ground, and closed.

My parents did not speak of him again. A new houseboy arrived two weeks later, a shy fourteen-year-old called Sunday who did not know kung fu and avoided looking directly at me. Beatrice told me that Raphael had returned to his village. Josephine stopped coming to our tap.

The cut healed into a pale line near my eyebrow. When people asked about it, I said I had fallen.

Now, at my parents’ dining table, rainwater gathered in the groove of the balcony railing. My mother pushed the newspaper toward me.

“Is he the one?” she asked.

The photograph was small and badly printed. A man knelt on the ground beside three rifles and bundles of cable. One side of his face was swollen. His hair had receded. Behind him stood policemen with hard, satisfied expressions.

I could not tell if it was Raphael. Then I saw the scar near his left temple, a short raised mark I remembered tracing with my eyes while he slept through the burning of the medicine.

“Yes,” I said.

My father leaned over the paper. “Terrible. Some of these boys, no matter what opportunity you give them—”

“We treated him well,” my mother said.

My father nodded. “Very well.”

They began to discuss whether wickedness was born or acquired. My mother blamed bad company. My father blamed the failure of the state. Each theory protected the room in which it was spoken.

I wanted to tell them.

Even then, after thirty years, there was something I could have corrected. Not Raphael’s dismissal, not the years that followed, not the guns placed before him in the photograph. I did not believe my lie had made him a robber. Such a belief would have been another kind of vanity, another way of placing myself at the center of his life. I knew nothing of what had happened to him after he left our gate. I did not know whom he loved, whom he feared, what work he tried, what doors closed, or what choices he made.

But I could have told my parents that they were wrong about that evening.

I could have removed one false thing from the world.

My mother folded the newspaper and asked whether I wanted tea. My father complained that the rain would spoil the satellite signal. Their old faces, alike in the dimness, waited for no confession.

“No tea,” I said.

That night I lay in my crowded childhood room, among their books and abandoned papers. Rain tapped the louvres. Somewhere beyond the estate wall a generator started, settled, and hummed.

I touched the scar near my eyebrow.

I remembered Raphael’s hand around my wrist, not hurting me, only holding me still. I remembered the warm room in the boys’ quarters and his face tilted toward mine, trusting me to bring the drops close. I remembered all the moments when I had mistaken access for devotion, kindness for possession, and my longing for a promise he had never made.

In the morning, the newspaper was gone. My mother had used it to line the cupboard beneath the sink.

I could have pulled it out. I could have found the prison named in the report. I could have written to him, although I did not know what apology could cross so many years without becoming an appeal for mercy. I could have asked him what happened after the gate closed.

I could have told him that I remembered.

But I remained in bed until I heard my father calling my name for breakfast, and then I rose, washed my face, and went out to join them.

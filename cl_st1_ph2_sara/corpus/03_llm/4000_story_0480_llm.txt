# Satterlee, Second, Makes Good

“I don’t say Don’s afraid I’ll play better than he does,” observed Satterlee, 2d, gloomily. “That would be silly, of course.”

“Of course,” agreed Tom Beverley, with suspicious readiness.

Satterlee looked at him.

“But,” he continued, “I do say he’s so everlastingly afraid of seeming partial that he isn’t fair. That’s different.”

“Quite different,” said Tom.

They were seated beneath one of the big elms beyond the third-base side of the school diamond. Practice had ended ten minutes before, and most of the St. Anselm’s players had trotted away toward the gymnasium, their gray uniforms showing now and then between the trees. Across the field the groundsman dragged a wide mat over the infield, smoothing away the scars of sliding feet. Beyond right field the river flashed in the late afternoon sunshine.

Satterlee, 2d, picked up a twig and broke it into small pieces.

“I’ve been on the squad seven weeks,” he said. “Seven weeks, mind you! And how much real baseball have I played? Two innings against Winthrop, when we were eleven runs ahead; one inning against Carlisle, when it was raining so hard nobody could see the ball; and last Saturday at Hillbury, when I went out to left in the eighth and not a thing came my way.”

“You caught that foul.”

“It was twenty yards outside the line.”

“You caught it, though.”

“Anybody could have caught it. A baby in a perambulator could have caught it.”

“Not without getting out of the perambulator,” Tom objected.

Satterlee refused to smile.

“I’m as good in center as Jimmy Ward.”

“You’re quicker coming in.”

“I’m quicker every way. And I can hit better.”

“Sometimes.”

“Well, sometimes is better than never. Jimmy hasn’t made a hit in three games.”

“He got a base on balls yesterday.”

“That isn’t hitting. Even father knows that, and father thinks the shortstop stands behind the catcher.”

Tom chuckled. Mr. Satterlee’s knowledge of baseball was indeed a favorite family subject. But the younger Satterlee’s face remained dismal.

“The worst of it is,” he went on, “mother and father are coming tomorrow.”

“To the Alden game?”

“Yes. Mother wrote that she has bought a blue parasol because blue is one of the school colors. Father says he has postponed a directors’ meeting. They think I’m going to play.”

“Did you tell them so?”

“No. I said I was on the first-team squad. That’s true. But mother’s certain to suppose I’m the captain or something.”

“There’s already one captain in the family,” Tom reminded him.

“That’s just it! Don has everything. He’s captain, he plays short, he bats third, and when he says ‘Come on, fellows!’ everybody comes on. I’m merely Satterlee, 2d, substitute outfielder. I carry bats, chase fouls, and make myself useful.”

Tom stretched his long legs and studied the toes of his shoes.

“Don has to think of the team,” he said presently.

“I know that.”

“And Alden has beaten us twice.”

“I know that too.”

“And tomorrow’s game decides the series and practically the championship.”

“You needn’t recite the school catalogue,” said Satterlee. “I’ve heard all about it.”

“Then you can see why Don doesn’t care to experiment.”

“It wouldn’t be an experiment. Curly says I cover more ground than Ward.”

“Did Curly say you ought to start?”

Satterlee hesitated.

“No. He said I was coming fast.”

“Ah!”

“Oh, you’re as bad as Don!”

He flung the remains of the twig away and rose. At that moment a tall boy in a sweater came across the infield toward them. His cap was pushed back, and he carried two bats under one arm. He was Don Satterlee, captain of the St. Anselm’s nine, and just then he looked more weary than impressive.

“Here you are, Kid,” he said. “Curly wants all the extra bats in the cage.”

Satterlee, 2d, stared at the bats.

“Why doesn’t Ward take them?”

“Because Ward has gone to the infirmary to have his thumb looked at.”

“What’s wrong with it?”

“Split nail. Nothing serious.”

“Of course not,” replied Satterlee bitterly. “Nothing ever happens to Ward.”

Don’s brows drew together.

“What’s eating you now?”

“Nothing.”

“That’s nonsense. You’ve been going about for three days like the last survivor of a shipwreck.”

Tom rose with tactful haste.

“I’ve got to see Purdy about that Latin prose,” he said. “So long.”

He vanished toward the dormitories.

Don dropped the bats on the grass.

“Now, then,” he said, “let’s have it.”

Satterlee thrust his hands into his pockets.

“Tom and I were just talking.”

“So I gathered. Tom looked as if he had been listening to a funeral oration.”

“I was saying that some fellows get a chance and others don’t.”

“You mean Ward?”

“I mean me.”

Don drew a long breath.

“Look here, Kid. We’ve had this out before. Ward’s played center all season. He knows the other men, and they know him. Tomorrow isn’t the time to shift.”

“He’s batting eighty-six!”

“He may get going.”

“He may also be struck by lightning.”

“If he is, I’ll send you in.”

“Thanks,” said Satterlee. “That’s dandy. Perhaps you’d better have Curly put a lightning rod beside the bench.”

Don’s mouth twitched, but his eyes stayed serious.

“You think I’m keeping you out because you’re my brother?”

“I think you’re keeping me out because you don’t want anyone to say you favor me.”

“Well, suppose I put you in and you muff the first fly?”

“I shouldn’t.”

“Suppose you did. Half the school would say I had risked the game to give my kid brother a show.”

“And so you risk games with Ward to prove you haven’t!”

“Ward is steady.”

“So is a hitching-post.”

For a moment the brothers faced each other. They were much alike about the eyes and chin, though Don was taller and broader and had acquired the composed manner which comes to a school captain through long practice in concealing alarm.

Then Don picked up the bats.

“You’re a good little player,” he said. “You’ll get your chance.”

“When? Next year?”

“When it comes.”

“That’s what everyone says when he doesn’t mean to do anything.”

Don’s face hardened.

“All right. Sulk if you want to. But don’t let it hurt the team.”

He turned away.

Satterlee watched him cross the field, and for one rebellious instant wished that Alden Academy might hammer Ward’s offerings—or rather, since Ward did not pitch, hammer the offerings past Ward—until Don should be compelled to make a change.

But the wish would not remain respectable even in his own mind. St. Anselm’s was St. Anselm’s, and Alden was Alden. A fellow might be injured, overlooked, and condemned to carry bats until his arms dragged on the ground, but he could not honestly wish victory to the purple.

Satterlee kicked the trunk of the elm and immediately regretted it.

The next afternoon the St. Anselm’s grounds presented a very different appearance. The long grand stand was bright with blue banners and summer dresses. Striped awnings sheltered the faculty and guests; automobiles stood in two shining rows beyond the elms; and from the dormitory windows hung blankets, flags, and one enormous blue stocking whose significance nobody could explain.

Across the diamond Alden’s supporters made a brave patch of purple. They had come by train and motor, bringing horns, bells, and a confidence which the St. Anselm’s boys considered offensive.

The sky was cloudless. A little breeze wandered up from the river, stirred the ivy on the gymnasium wall, and then died away over the hot infield. It was, according to the school paper afterward, a perfect day for baseball. To Satterlee, 2d, sitting at the far end of the bench, it was merely a perfect day to sit at the far end of a bench.

His mother and father occupied front seats near first base. Mrs. Satterlee’s blue parasol was visible at a considerable distance and was being waved with enthusiasm at every gray-clad figure who appeared.

“There’s Donald!” she cried as the St. Anselm’s team ran onto the field.

“That fellow near second?” asked Mr. Satterlee.

“No, dear. Donald is at shortstop.”

“Certainly. I meant shortstop.”

“And there’s Bobby!”

Satterlee, hearing the name he endured only at home, looked toward the stand. His mother waved the parasol. He lifted his cap, while two substitutes beside him grinned.

“Your signal flag’s working, Bob,” said Pete Ames.

“For a cent I’d brain you with the water bucket,” replied Satterlee.

Mr. Satterlee waved too. He had spent the morning studying a small baseball guide, but the effort had produced more earnestness than enlightenment.

“Robert appears to be in reserve,” he said.

“He will play presently,” Mrs. Satterlee declared.

“Yes, yes. Naturally. Fresh troops.”

The umpire called the teams in, and Alden came to bat.

Trouble began at once. Mallory, St. Anselm’s pitcher, walked the first man. The second slapped a single past third. Don took a hard grounder from the next batsman and forced the runner at second, but then Carpenter, the formidable Alden catcher, drove a double into the left-field corner.

Two runs came home amid a storm of purple cheers.

“Steady, Mal!” Don called.

Mallory steadied after a fashion. He struck out one man, but a passed ball let in the third run, and a scratch hit over second scored the fourth. By the time the side was retired, Alden had four runs and the appearance of a team prepared to acquire as many more as might be convenient.

The blue section was quiet.

Mr. Satterlee leaned toward his wife.

“I take it,” he said, “that this is unfavorable.”

“Very,” she answered.

St. Anselm’s went out in order in the first. Don hit a long fly, but Carpenter’s younger brother, who played left for Alden, caught it with one hand against the scoreboard. The purple horns made a hideous rejoicing.

In the second, Alden filled the bases with one out. Curly James, the St. Anselm’s coach, stood at the edge of the bench, arms folded, his freckled face expressionless. Mallory induced a pop fly and then struck out Carpenter with a curve that left that large young gentleman staring politely after it.

“That’s pitching!” shouted Satterlee before he remembered he was aggrieved.

Don glanced toward the bench and nodded.

St. Anselm’s gathered two hits in its half of the second, but no run. In the third, Don made a stop behind second that brought even the purple stand to applause. He threw from an awkward position, and the ball reached first a fraction before the runner.

“Donald has put him out!” exclaimed Mrs. Satterlee.

“Capital!” said her husband. “Which one was Donald?”

The game moved into the fourth with Alden still leading, four to nothing. Hope had not vanished from the blue side, but it had withdrawn to a safe distance.

Satterlee sat with his chin in his hands. Ward had received one fly—a lazy thing directly over his head—and had caught it without moving.

“Terrific range,” murmured Satterlee.

“What?” asked Pete Ames.

“I said he’s playing a dandy game.”

Ward came to bat in the fourth with one out and struck out on three pitches. He returned to the bench looking miserable.

“Keep your eye on it, Jimmy,” Don said quietly.

Satterlee waited for Don to look his way. Don did not.

The next batter, Lewis, singled. Mallory followed with another hit, and Lewis reached third. Then Alden’s pitcher lost the plate and walked the bases full.

The blue stand awoke. Boys rose, shouting and stamping. The school band, which had been saving its strength, began the first four bars of the school song and repeated them because the remaining bars had momentarily escaped the cornet player.

Don came to bat.

He took two balls, fouled a fast one into the stand, and drove the next pitch sharply between short and third. Lewis scored. Mallory rounded third, but the left fielder’s throw held him there. The bases remained full.

Four to one!

Tom Beverley, seated behind the bench with the non-playing students, leaned over the rail.

“Come on, Don! Work like thunder!”

The next batter lifted a high fly to center. Mallory tagged after the catch and scored, but that was all. The inning ended with St. Anselm’s two runs nearer.

“Now they’re pursuing them,” said Mr. Satterlee.

“They’re only two behind,” said his wife.

“Exactly. Pursuing.”

Neither team scored in the fifth. In the sixth Alden threatened, placing a man on third with two out. Carpenter hit a savage grounder toward short. Don went down, knocked it aside, recovered on one knee, and hurled to first. The runner was out by half a step.

Don trotted in through a roar of applause.

“Pretty stop,” Satterlee told him as he reached the bench.

“Thanks, Kid.”

That was all; but some of the bitterness had gone from Satterlee’s voice.

St. Anselm’s added a run in the last of the sixth. Hayes doubled, went to third on a bunt, and came home on a fly to right. Four to three!

The afternoon shadows lengthened across foul ground. The river beyond the trees had turned from silver to deep blue, and the crowd, which had begun in holiday leisure, now leaned forward on every pitch.

In the seventh Ward came to bat with two out and nobody on base. Satterlee studied Don’s face. Surely now—

Don merely said, “Meet it, Jimmy.”

Ward met it. The ball rolled feebly toward second, and he was thrown out by several yards.

Satterlee dug his nails into his palms.

Alden failed to score in the eighth. So did St. Anselm’s, though Don reached second with one out. A line drive was caught by the Alden first baseman, who doubled him off the bag.

The ninth began with the score still four to three.

Curly walked down the bench and spoke to Don. The captain looked toward center field, where Ward was flexing his injured thumb. Then he looked at Satterlee.

“Kid!”

Satterlee was on his feet so quickly that he kicked over the water bucket.

“Take center,” Don said. “You bat in Ward’s place.”

For a second Satterlee could not speak.

“All right,” he managed.

“And, Kid—”

“Yes?”

“Go get them.”

Satterlee snatched his glove and raced onto the field. The blue stand saw him and applauded, though not very loudly; most spectators were consulting score cards to discover who he was.

Mrs. Satterlee waved her parasol until its joints were endangered.

“There!” she cried. “I knew Robert would play.”

“Center forward,” said Mr. Satterlee, studying his guide.

“Center field, dear.”

“Of course. A slip of the tongue.”

Satterlee reached his position and looked in toward the diamond. From center field the bases seemed strangely close together and the crowd enormously far away. His heart beat so hard that he could feel it beneath his shirt.

The first Alden batter sent a grounder to third. One out. The next fouled to the catcher. Two out. Carpenter came up and smashed a long drive toward right center.

Satterlee sped across. For a glorious moment he thought the ball was his. Then the right fielder called, caught it chest-high, and the inning was over.

Not a chance had come to Satterlee.

He trotted toward the bench, trying not to show disappointment. Don met him near the foul line.

“You’re up third,” the captain said.

That was different.

St. Anselm’s came to bat for the last time needing one run to tie. Alden’s pitcher, who had worked steadily after the sixth, seemed tired, but he still had speed.

Lewis, first up, drove a single over second.

The stand erupted. Caps sailed into the air. The band attempted the school song again and this time reached the sixth bar before breaking down.

Mallory bunted. The pitcher fielded the ball and threw late to second. Both men were safe.

There were no outs, runners on first and second, and Don came to bat.

“Hit it out!” shouted a hundred voices.

Don did not hit it out. He tried to sacrifice, but the bunt rolled directly to the third baseman, who threw to first. The runners advanced, and Don was out.

One out. Men on second and third.

Hayes lifted a fly to left, not deep enough to score Lewis. Two out.

The noise died.

Satterlee, 2d, walked toward the plate.

He had wanted this chance for seven weeks. He had pictured it while fielding practice flies, while carrying bats, while lying awake in the dormitory. In every picture he had been calm, even careless. Now his knees felt hollow, his mouth was dry, and the bat seemed to have gained several pounds.

As he passed Don, who was coaching at third, their eyes met.

“Wait for yours, Kid,” Don said.

Satterlee nodded.

The Alden catcher, Carpenter, crouched behind the plate. He looked even larger from six feet away.

“Easy one, youngster,” he remarked genially.

“You can search me,” said Satterlee, though the reply made no particular sense.

The first pitch was a curve outside.

“Ball!”

Satterlee breathed again.

The second came straight and fast. He swung late.

“Strike!”

Alden’s infielders chattered. The purple stand stamped rhythmically.

The third pitch dipped near his knees.

“Ball two!”

Carpenter returned it to the pitcher and pushed his mask higher.

“Make him hit it,” called the Alden captain.

The pitcher wound up. Satterlee saw the ball leave his hand and, for one clear instant, saw everything else too: Lewis dancing off third, Mallory leading from second, Don standing rigid beyond the foul line, the blue banners stirring, and his mother’s absurd parasol motionless above the crowd.

The ball came over waist-high.

Satterlee swung.

There was a crack—not the dull tap of a mishit ball, but the clean, satisfying report that every batter knows before he begins to run.

The ball streaked over second base, too high for the pitcher and too far to the left for the shortstop. The center fielder charged, but it fell before him and bounded past his outstretched glove.

Lewis scored easily.

Mallory rounded third like a gray whirlwind.

“Come on! Come on!” Don roared.

The center fielder recovered and hurled to the plate. Mallory slid in a cloud of dust. Carpenter caught the throw and swept his glove down—

“Safe!”

For an instant there was silence, as if the entire field had drawn one breath.

Then the blue stands exploded.

Satterlee reached second standing. Boys were pounding one another’s backs, the band was playing three tunes at once, and Mrs. Satterlee’s parasol had become a blue blur. On the scoreboard the figures changed:

ST. ANSELM’S 6  
ALDEN 4

Satterlee stood on the bag, grinning foolishly.

Don came halfway down the line before remembering that the inning was not over.

“That’s the stuff, Kid!” he shouted.

The next batter grounded out, and St. Anselm’s took the field with a two-run lead.

Two runs! Satterlee repeated the words as he ran toward center. He had driven in two runs in the ninth inning of the Alden game. Whatever happened now, nobody could take that away.

But, as events soon proved, there was more to come.

Mallory walked the first batter on four pitches.

The blue cheering ceased abruptly.

The second Alden man hit a slow roller toward third. Hayes charged, picked it up barehanded, and threw wild. The first baseman knocked the ball down, preventing a run, but both runners were safe.

No outs. Men on first and second.

Curly walked to the mound. Don joined him, speaking quietly to Mallory. The infield gathered around, while Carpenter swung two bats beside the Alden bench.

Satterlee watched from center. The late sun was in his eyes, though not badly. He rubbed his palm in the grass and tightened his glove.

Carpenter advanced to the plate.

He had doubled in two runs in the first inning. He was Alden’s best hitter, a broad-shouldered senior with a calm expression and the disagreeable habit of hitting harder when it mattered most.

“Play deep!” Don signaled.

Satterlee moved back three steps.

Carpenter fouled the first pitch over the stand. Strike one.

The second was low. Ball one.

Mallory tried a curve. Carpenter did not offer. Ball two.

From the bench came Curly’s voice: “Put it over, Mal!”

Mallory put it over.

Carpenter swung, but he got beneath the ball. It rose only a little way above the infield, cleared second base, and began to drop into the shallow stretch of grass beyond.

Satterlee started forward.

The second baseman raced back, looking over his shoulder. The shortstop went out. Neither could reach it.

“Mine!” Satterlee shouted.

He ran harder.

The ball was falling rapidly now, spinning against the pale sky. It seemed certain to strike the turf several yards before him. The two Alden runners, equally certain, had started at the crack of the bat. The man on second was nearly at third; the man on first was rounding second.

Satterlee’s cap flew off. His breath tore in his throat. He saw the ball sink lower—lower—

He flung himself forward.

For one fraction of a second the ball hung just above the grass. Then it struck the pocket of his glove.

Satterlee slid on his chest, his arms outstretched.

He had it!

The crowd gave a single tremendous cry.

Satterlee sprang up. The runner from first, who had rounded second and was trying desperately to return, was almost upon him. Satterlee reached out and tagged him on the shoulder.

Two out!

“Second!” yelled Don. “Touch second!”

The runner who had left second was still beyond third base. Satterlee wheeled and raced to the bag. He planted one foot upon it and held up the ball.

For a moment the umpire stood like an immovable Fate, watching the wild gestures of players and coaches. Then his right arm went up.

“Out!”

Three outs.

An unassisted triple play.

The game was over.

Satterlee remained on second base, staring at the umpire. He knew he had caught the ball. He knew he had tagged a runner. He knew he had touched the bag. Yet the sum of these facts was too large to enter his mind at once.

Then Don struck him from behind with an embrace that nearly knocked him down.

“You little wonder!” the captain shouted.

The whole St. Anselm’s team came racing upon him. Mallory seized one arm, Hayes the other. Someone pounded his back. Someone else jammed his lost cap onto his head backward. Boys poured from the stands and swarmed across the diamond, shouting his name.

“Satterlee! Satterlee, Second!”

“Make way for him!”

“Up with the kid!”

He was lifted to a dozen shoulders. Above him tossed blue caps, blue banners, and the ridiculous blue stocking from the dormitory window, now borne upon a pole like the standard of a conquering army.

From that height Satterlee saw Curly laughing beside the bench. He saw Tom Beverley dancing alone on the pitcher’s box. He saw Don, dusty and breathless, looking up with a pride he made no effort to conceal.

And in the front row stood Mr. and Mrs. Satterlee.

Mrs. Satterlee was crying a little and waving the battered remains of her parasol. Mr. Satterlee had abandoned his guide and was cheering with complete disregard for dignity.

“That,” he declared to everyone within hearing, “was my younger son! He caught them all out at once!”

For practical purposes, the explanation was near enough.

Satterlee, 2d, rode from the field like a victorious Caesar, one hand gripping a teammate’s shoulder and the other still holding the ball. His grievance had vanished, his bitterness had gone, and even the duties of a substitute outfielder no longer seemed wholly contemptible.

For the substitute had received his chance at last.

And he had made fairly good use of it.

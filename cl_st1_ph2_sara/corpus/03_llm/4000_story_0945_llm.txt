At seven o’clock of a gray northern morning Euston stood beneath its famous portico with the solemnity of a temple whose god had not yet arrived.

Cabs rattled in from the wet London streets and discharged their offerings—commercial gentlemen folded into ulsters, ladies accompanied by pyramids of leather, clerks going as far as Rugby, soldiers going farther, children who regarded Scotland as a region inhabited indifferently by deer, bagpipes, waterfalls, and uncles. Porters moved through the confusion with that grave omniscience peculiar to men who know where every train is going and suspect that no passenger knows where he himself is going. The great clock looked down upon us all without sympathy.

Our express waited beside the platform.

The engine was painted a deep vermilion relieved with black and brass, and against the surrounding smoke its long boiler glowed like the flank of some heraldic beast. Behind it stood the tender, piled high with coal; then the coaches, bottle-green, varnished and stately, their compartments already warming to the human atmosphere of rugs, newspapers, luncheon baskets, impatience, and resignation. The locomotive appeared scarcely to belong to them. It was a creature of another order, fiery, narrow-minded, and athletic, harnessed by administrative compulsion to a procession of drawing rooms.

Presently the driver leaned from the cab and surveyed the platform. He was a spare man of perhaps fifty, with a brown beard beginning to whiten at the chin, and eyes which seemed neither anxious nor inattentive. One could not imagine those eyes surprised by fog, snow, cattle, broken rails, or the sudden illumination of Judgment Day between two signal posts. The fireman, much younger and built with the compact shoulders of a prizefighter, examined a gauge, opened the furnace door, and for an instant disclosed a blood-red cavern in which the coal burned with intolerable brilliance.

A guard’s whistle sounded.

“All right,” said the fireman.

The driver moved his hand.

There was no violence. The couplings tightened one after another with a succession of mild metallic complaints; the engine breathed softly; the platform began, almost imperceptibly, to move backward. At first we proceeded at the pace of a mouse considering whether it has been observed. The porters slid by, then the bookstall, then a late passenger running beneath the majestic clock with the hopeless dignity of a man who knows that his private catastrophe has become a public entertainment. The roof gathered itself above us; columns fled slowly astern; signals appeared in orderly ranks.

Then the locomotive put its will upon the train.

London broke apart.

Walls, sheds, bridges, soot-blackened gardens, windows with pale faces at them, chimneys, laundries, advertisements, semaphores, brick cuttings and sudden strips of sky flew backward into a gray confusion. The exhaust, at first separately audible, became a rapid, enormous hammering. Iron answered iron beneath our feet. The coaches swayed, resisted, submitted, and the express shot northward with the purposeful arrogance of a projectile.

In London at that hour there were undoubtedly thousands of persons imagining Scotland. The stockbroker, unfolding his paper in a suburban breakfast room, imagined grouse. The schoolboy imagined clans at perpetual war among purple mountains. The elderly lady imagined draughts. A cook, hearing that her employers were gone to Glasgow, imagined savages who consumed oats by preference and spoke only in proverbs. Meanwhile Scotland, which would have been amused by these conjectures if it had not been busy with shipbuilding, mining, weaving, bargaining, preaching, and preparing its own breakfast, lay four hundred miles ahead.

I was permitted to ride in the cab.

There are travelers who desire velvet, quiet, and a cushion between themselves and every truth. To them the first-class compartment is the crown of civilization. They sit in it with their backs to the engine and learn from the timetable that they have crossed England. But the cab is the proper observatory of an express. Here one does not merely travel; one witnesses the manufacture of travel.

The floor trembled like a deck above propellers. Brass fittings quivered. The reversing lever stood within reach of the driver’s hand; gauges watched us with round, incorruptible faces; the regulator seemed a weapon of authority. Wind rushed through the open side of the cab and drove smoke, steam, cinders, moisture, and the breath of fields against us. Ahead, the paired rails drew together to a bright point, unrolled beneath the buffer beam, and vanished. Telegraph wires dipped and rose, dipped and rose, writing an endless message which no one in the train could read.

The driver stood easily, one hand near the regulator, his body adjusting itself without thought to every movement. He looked not at the countryside but through it, beyond it, into the language of signals. A green light, a lifted arm, the glint of points, a curve half a mile away—these were his scenery. Villages might exhibit their church towers, rivers might shine under stone bridges, noble houses might present their ancestral woods: he regarded none of them unless they interfered with the road.

The fireman had another landscape. His world was coal, water, pressure, heat.

He opened the furnace door, and daylight became trivial. With a long shovel he took coal from the tender, turned, bent, and cast it into the glare. He did not throw carelessly. Each black offering had its destination: near corner, far corner, center, edge. Again the shovel entered the heap. Again it flashed toward the fire. The door clanged shut; darkness returned; he wiped his forehead with an arm already streaked in black; then, after scarcely a pause, he began once more.

Shoveling, watching, shoveling. The feeding, feeding, feeding.

The locomotive was insatiable.

A passenger in a warm compartment may complain because tea has arrived without sufficient milk. At that moment, forty feet ahead of him, a man may be laboring in a temperature fit for the discipline of medieval saints, lifting ton after ton while the floor pitches beneath him and the wind endeavors to remove his head. This arrangement is called comfort. All comfort, when traced to its cellar, has a tired man in it.

We flew through a station whose name I could not read. The platform appeared complete—lamps, benches, porter, milk cans, a woman in a blue cloak—and in the same instant collapsed into distance. The woman raised her head. Perhaps she saw only a crimson front, a storm of sound, windows filled with pale ovals, and a vanishing red lamp. To us she was a fragment of blue; to her we were London going north at sixty miles an hour.

A tunnel mouth opened before us.

The driver did not stir. The engine plunged into it.

Day was extinguished by one stroke. The roar, flung back from brick and stone, multiplied until we seemed enclosed in a cannon at the moment of discharge. Sparks whirled along the darkness; the furnace door opened and painted the two men, the gauges, the roof and the plunging shovel in red. Steam wrapped itself around the cab. The engine gasped like a giant swimmer caught beneath a wave, and every joint of the train contributed its separate clangor to the universal assault.

Then the farther mouth grew—a small gray coin, a plate, a shield—and burst over us into sky.

The English countryside resumed as though nothing had happened.

It is impossible from an express to behold a landscape in the old manner. The pedestrian receives a tree, considers it, passes it, and retains some civil acquaintance with its shape. We assaulted whole counties. Hedges became green cords; plowed fields widened and snapped shut; sheep were white punctuation scattered over a sentence too rapidly spoken. A pond flashed like a dropped mirror. Farmhouses approached with an air of permanence and were gone before permanence could be examined. In the middle distance, where speed had less power, woods moved with stately deliberation; upon the horizon, hills accompanied us like remote ships.

The railway itself was magnificent. The road-bed lay broad, firm, drained, fenced, guarded, ballasted, and apparently prepared to resist not merely weather but invasion by all the armies of Europe. Bridges went over what might have been crossed by a plank. Retaining walls upheld banks which showed no immediate intention of falling. Culverts, signal cabins, sidings, points, telegraph posts, mile boards, water columns, and trim stations followed one another with the solemn regularity of state institutions.

An American contemplates such work with mingled admiration and alarm. He admires the conscience, the masonry, the refusal to leave anything to chance; but he cannot help calculating how many hundred miles of useful track might have been laid elsewhere with the stone devoted to protecting one English embankment from a stream three feet wide. Britain builds as if each sleeper were to be inherited by a grandson. America builds because the town at the other end has already been founded, has elected a mayor, issued a newspaper, and threatens to lynch the railway board unless rails arrive by Thursday.

The American road is often a courageous suggestion. The British road is a constitutional amendment in iron and granite.

Nevertheless, the British result inspires confidence. There is comfort in observing that the bridge beneath you has been designed by men who distrusted bridges, inspected by men who distrusted designers, and maintained by men who distrust everybody. Safety is not the product of one invention. It is a habit distributed among thousands: the platelayer tapping a wheel, the signalman polishing his levers, the clerk checking a schedule, the driver refusing a risk, the porter closing a door, the inspector appearing when least desired. Night and day, day and night, this scattered army performs its minute ceremonial, and the traveler, ignorant of the whole, attributes his arrival to the engine.

At a junction we slackened. Signals stood above the converging tracks like the lances of a disciplined host. A signal box, elevated upon brick, displayed through its windows a row of shining levers—red, blue, black, white—each connected to a distant obedience. The signalman moved among them with the measured activity of an organist. He drew one lever; points changed. He returned another; a semaphore lifted. Somewhere a bell clicked; elsewhere another bell replied. No music could be heard above our own uproar, but one felt the great instrument sounding from London to the north: steel answering steel, wire calling wire, block succeeding block, the innumerable hymn of a world at work.

A slow goods train waited upon a siding. Its engine puffed with offended patience while we swept past. The goods driver looked across, and our driver lifted two fingers from the regulator. The gesture was slight, almost indolent, yet it resembled a salute exchanged by commanders whose campaigns are understood between them. The fireman, being occupied with eternity’s appetite, did not look up.

Speed returned. The exhaust hardened. The tender shook under its black cargo, and the coaches followed with the dignified reluctance of dowagers attached to a runaway horse.

The driver spoke rarely. When he did, his remarks were practical and brief. He pointed out a distant signal before I had discovered even the post. He named a gradient which the landscape concealed. Once, while the locomotive bounded through a shallow cutting and the rails appeared to pour themselves directly into the wheels, I asked whether the speed caused him any uneasiness.

He looked at me, then at the road.

“No,” he said.

This was not bravado. It was a statement of professional irrelevance. Uneasiness, like scenery, had no place in the schedule. He knew the engine, the line, the signals, the load, the weather, and his own powers. Fear begins where knowledge leaves an empty chair.

The engine driver belongs to an order not sufficiently celebrated. The public praises soldiers because their courage is displayed amid flags, and sailors because the sea has always employed poets as its press agents. But the driver conducts his battle in smoke, drizzle, darkness and routine. He must have a sober eye, a steady hand, a memory faithful to every gradient and signal, a temper not betrayed by fatigue, and that deeper courage which can remain quiet. Behind him sit hundreds who have never seen his face. They sleep, quarrel, flirt, dine, lose umbrellas, and make jokes about delays. He carries all their frivolities as gravely as their bodies.

Near noon I returned to the train.

The compartment had acquired the settled appearance of a temporary club. Newspapers had been exchanged; boots extended beneath opposite seats under treaties of uncertain legality; a child slept with its head against an aunt; two commercial travelers discussed wool as if no other substance had been created. A gentleman in a checked coat had summoned tea by pressing an electric bell.

The bell was one of those ingenious conveniences which demonstrate that civilization advances by introducing fresh ambiguities. A card explained that the same bell would call an attendant in ordinary need and assistance in emergency. Thus a passenger desiring tea and a passenger being murdered were instructed to express themselves through an identical electrical impulse. Perhaps one pressed briefly for refreshment and continuously for rescue; perhaps the distinction depended upon moral tone. The attendant, when he appeared, looked capable of discriminating. He brought tea.

Meals upon trains reveal national character more exactly than constitutions. The Englishman does not demand that a railway luncheon should resemble a festival; he asks that it should be recognizable, punctual, and accompanied by mustard. The American desires abundance, ice water, rapid service, and machinery concealed beneath the table. The continental traveler accepts three hours of delay if the sauce has a title. Each believes the others uncivilized.

Our sandwiches were stern but honorable. The tea had endured adventure. Yet while cups trembled in their saucers and the landscape streamed past the glass, even these modest provisions acquired romance. To eat while traveling at high speed is to rebuke geography. Counties labor to pass; rivers offer historical associations; ancient towns exhibit towers; and one butters bread.

Crewe announced itself first by multiplication.

Tracks appeared beside us, then more tracks beyond them. Sidings spread like a metallic delta. Engines stood in rows—black, green, crimson, some breathing, some cold, some stripped for repair. Workshops extended under serried roofs; cranes leaned above wagons; chimneys wrote upon the sky. Men in dark clothes crossed the rails with the calm of citizens in their native streets. Everywhere lay wheels, rods, boilers, axles, plates, pipes, coal, timber, steam and ordered grime. The railway, which in the countryside had seemed a path, here became a kingdom.

We entered the station under a great reverberating roof and stopped.

After the sustained violence of motion, stillness appeared unnatural. One expected the platform to slide away. Doors opened. Porters descended upon luggage. Passengers stretched their legs and consulted clocks, though the clocks were larger and better informed. A lady asked whether this was Scotland and seemed disappointed by the answer. The engine, uncoupled from its train, stood a little forward, breathing heavily after its run.

Its work was done.

There is melancholy in the detachment of a locomotive. For hours it has been the will, voice, heat, speed and foremost courage of the train; then a pin is withdrawn, a hose released, a man waves his hand, and the great creature moves away alone. The coaches remain with the composure of aristocrats changing servants.

Our driver climbed down. On the platform he appeared smaller than in the cab, as a captain does when separated from the bridge. The fireman’s face was dark with coal except where sweat had made pale channels. They had no audience, no wreath, no band. An official spoke to them; the driver nodded; the fireman drank from a tin. In a few moments both were lost among the machinery.

Another engine backed toward us with delicate puffs, touched the leading coach, and was coupled on. It was of a different build, though equally conscious of importance: large driving wheels, polished brass, a black boiler, and paint maintained with a degree of affection usually reserved for cavalry horses. Men examined connections. A hammer struck metal. The guard looked at his watch, then at the platform, as if allowing humanity one final opportunity to organize itself.

We departed Crewe.

The afternoon had cleared. Light lay upon the fields in broad silver bands, and wet roofs shone beneath retreating clouds. The country opened. Industrial towns alternated with farms; church spires rose from smoke; canals accompanied us for a time with oily resignation. At crossings, carts waited behind gates. Children waved. Dogs barked themselves into futility.

The new crew performed the same ritual with individual variations. This driver was heavier, cleanshaven, and inclined to lean forward when watching the road. His fireman, a red-haired youth, attacked the coal as though it had insulted his family. The engine answered their management with a deeper exhaust than the first. On rising grades its voice slowed and broadened—four tremendous beats, repeated, repeated, shaking the air—while on descending stretches it ran freely, the rods flashing beside the wheels.

To stand in the cab at speed is to learn that a locomotive does not glide. It struggles, strikes, catches, surges, corrects itself, and plunges onward. The apparent smoothness observed from a distant meadow is a polite fiction. Inside the mechanism there is conflict: steam thrusts the pistons, rods drive the wheels, wheels beat the rails, the boiler strains upon its frame, water tosses, coal leaps, metal complains. The machine is a treaty among explosions.

Yet no horse could answer rein more sensitively. A touch upon the regulator altered the whole temper of the train. Steam was admitted; the dragon spoke. Steam was checked; its voice softened. The brake drew upon a thousand tons with invisible fingers. Human intelligence, translated into valves, levers, rods, vacuum and pressure, governed violence without destroying it.

We ran north by stations whose names came and vanished: Warrington, Wigan, Preston, Lancaster. Some received us beneath roofs of iron and glass; others watched us pass with their lamps already kindled against the early dusk. At Preston there was a brief pause, a confusion of doors, baskets and cries. Then the guard’s whistle, the answering breath, the tightening couplings. Always the same ceremony, always another beginning.

Beyond Lancaster the land gathered seriousness.

The flat abundance of the south gave way to folds, slopes, stone walls, rough pasture, streams flashing beneath bridges. Hills rose farther ahead, blue at first, then dark and immediate. Farms crouched against weather. The sky lowered; cloud trailed along summits; rain struck the cab in hard diagonal lines. The railway entered cuttings blasted from rock and crossed valleys upon embankments whose construction must have consumed fortunes, parliaments, and several generations of argument.

Here British engineering displayed its favorite virtue—determination carried to the edge of theology. If a hill opposed the appointed line, men drilled it, fired it, removed it by wagons, walled the wound, drained the wall, fenced the drain, and appointed a person to inspect the fence. If a valley intervened, they raised an embankment broad enough for a Roman road. There are works upon these lines which represent energy poured into a rat-hole; but it is magnificent energy, and the rat-hole will be in excellent condition two centuries hence.

Gradients grew severe. The fireman’s labor increased. The furnace seemed never closed. Each opening revealed an interior white at the heart, orange along the sides, red where fresh coal fell and instantly began to writhe. Heat struck us in waves, only to be swept away by the cold wind. The young man’s shirt clung to his back. He shoveled, checked the water, glanced at the pressure, used the injector, shoveled again. Apprenticeship here was written in sweat.

The driver watched him without fuss. Once he made a quiet suggestion. The fireman altered the distribution of coal, and after a minute the pressure needle steadied. Nothing more was said. Thus skill descends—not through speeches, but through a word delivered at the correct instant by one man who knows to another who will know.

Dusk thickened as we climbed.

On one side, the hills rose close and black; on the other, valleys opened unexpectedly, containing pale rivers and farms already reduced to lights. Smoke dragged low over the train. The engine’s exhaust reverberated between slopes, making a company of locomotives out of one. At moments a retaining wall rushed so near that every stone appeared separately before dissolving into a gray torrent. Then we crossed an open height where the wind struck broadside and the whole north seemed visible beneath a torn ceiling of cloud.

There are greater mountains in America, more savage gorges, rivers that would swallow an English county and continue without remark. Scale is our hereditary argument. We bring it into every comparison like a lawyer producing a decisive document. Yet size is not the sole author of grandeur. These northern hills possessed the authority of long habitation. Roads, walls, villages, ruins, sheep paths and bridges had adhered to them through centuries. The land did not seem empty before man arrived; it seemed to have trained him.

Night met us before Carlisle.

The world outside contracted to the beam of the engine lamps, the shine of rails, the sudden white posts, and signals floating red or green in the dark. A cottage window appeared and vanished. Water flashed below a bridge. The furnace painted steam crimson. Sparks flew over the tender and died among the first coaches like a swarm of brief stars.

Carlisle came as a constellation of lamps.

The station roof received us with clangor. Voices echoed under iron. Guards called, doors slammed, trucks rattled, and from somewhere beyond the visible platforms another locomotive gave a long, melancholy shriek. Here the second English engine surrendered the train. Beyond lay the Scottish road.

The Caledonian engine backed in with an air at once martial and ceremonial. Blue livery showed dark beneath the lamps; brass bands shone upon the boiler; its number plate gleamed like a decoration. It seemed less a machine newly assigned than a northern nobleman arriving to conduct guests across his estates. The crew wore the reserve of men aware that comparison was under way and Scotland must not be disgraced before an observer.

The coupling struck. Chains tightened. Pipes were joined. A lamp moved beneath the buffer. The guard consulted his watch.

We waited only minutes, but the pause had the significance of a frontier. No customs officer appeared; no barrier marked the kingdoms; no trumpet announced transition. There were merely rails continuing into darkness. Yet every traveler felt the north ahead.

The whistle sounded, sharp and imperial.

We went out of Carlisle under stars.

Soon a dim board, a bridge, a river, and an unremarkable tract of darkness indicated that England had ended. Scotland did not rush forward in tartan to receive us. It presented a wet cutting, three signal lamps, and the rear wall of a cottage. This reticence was becoming.

The new engine ran superbly. Its exhaust possessed a crisp, ringing note, and on the long grades it took hold of the coaches with enormous certainty. We climbed through an unseen country. Shapes of hills erased portions of the sky; streams declared themselves only by momentary whiteness; isolated farms burned their small yellow squares beneath the mass of night. The driver knew each curve before the lamp found it. He eased for gradients, opened for climbs, checked speed on descents, and read the colored lights appearing miles apart like commands issued from the stars.

At the summit the engine seemed to pause within its own effort. Then came the descent.

The rails fell northward. The locomotive lengthened its stride, and the train followed, first cautiously, then with gathering impetus. Darkness streamed against us. Wheels clicked faster at the joints—click-clack, click-clack, clickclack-clickclack—until separate beats fused into a continuous metallic cry. Signals rushed forward, changed from points to blazing disks, leapt above the cab and were gone. Curves swung the coaches behind us, their windows making a luminous chain across the black hillside.

The journey became valkyric.

We were no longer a train but a fiery spear hurled through the northern night; a ship descending invisible seas; a mailed horseman whose hoofs struck sparks from the earth. Wind tore at the cab. Steam writhed white over the boiler. The furnace door opened, and the driver’s face appeared for one instant in red relief—calm, intent, almost sacerdotal—then vanished again.

Yet this grandeur depended upon minute obedience. One misread signal, one neglected gauge, one pin imperfectly set, and the epic would become an inquest. Romance rides upon inspection reports. The sublime requires good maintenance.

Behind us, passengers slept.

That fact seemed the highest tribute. Men and women who would not have entrusted one another with a parcel lay unconscious while hurtling down black gradients behind a furnace, relying upon unseen persons whose names they would never ask. A mother arranged a rug over her child. A merchant snored beside a dispatch case. Two young men played cards under a lamp, setting down trumps while bridges, ravines, and signal posts flew beneath and past them. Civilization is not confidence in mankind generally. It is confidence in particular strangers performing particular duties.

At intervals we passed little stations. Their lamps stood in orderly rows; a porter appeared; a clock declared the hour to empty benches. Sometimes a branch engine waited, breathing modestly. Sometimes a group of persons on a platform turned toward our thunder and were illuminated coach by coach, each face visible for less than a second. Then the station dropped behind, possessing again its silence, its clock, its porter and its local concerns.

Farther north the valleys widened. Towns announced themselves by a glow behind the hills. Mining settlements scattered lights over black slopes. Furnaces rose in the distance, staining the clouds rose and orange. Chimneys appeared against the reflected glare, and long sheds ran beside tracks crowded with wagons. We had entered the outer dominions of industry.

The romantic traveler may lament that factories destroy the landscape. This depends upon which landscape he has come to see. A blast furnace at night has as valid a place in human wonder as a moonlit abbey, and considerably more employment. Its towers, pipes, ladders, smoke, flames and moving figures compose a severe architecture. Here iron is persuaded, coal expended, wages earned, lungs injured, fortunes made, unions organized, children fed, rivers darkened, ships supplied, and sermons occasioned. Beauty is not innocence.

We passed goods yards vast as encampments. Lamps moved among wagons. Engines pushed black columns of freight with hoarse, laboring breaths. Signal boxes glowed above the rails, each an illuminated brain controlling a little province of points. Bells spoke from cabin to cabin. Men in caps stood beside levers, while far away in London other men, perhaps at that very moment, thought of Glasgow as a remote northern word printed beneath a weather report.

Glasgow, meanwhile, imagined London.

A girl waiting beneath the station clock imagined theaters and golden carriages. A manufacturer expected prices, contracts and delays. A young clerk dreamed of streets paved not with gold but with opportunity, which is the more dangerous paving. An old woman waiting for her son imagined only the train, and because love is a provincial geographer, London to her was the place from which he would presently step.

Houses thickened around us. Their rear windows showed kitchens, curtains, hanging shirts, women bending over tables, men reading by firelight. Bridges crossed above, crowded with spectators who looked down into our smoke. The line curved between walls stained by generations of steam. Every mile drew more tracks alongside; every track bore points, lamps, wagons, engines, and obscure metallic intentions.

The driver stood straighter. The descent and open running were done. Now came the intricate approach, where speed must submit to exactitude. Signals crowded upon one another. Green admitted us, yellow warned, red held other trains in obedient darkness. The engine, so lately a warrior of the hills, became a courtier threading an antechamber. Brakes whispered along the coaches. The exhaust softened. We crossed shining points and slipped beneath bridges, past warehouses, signal gantries, waiting locomotives, and platforms that appeared to be rehearsing for our arrival.

Then the great station opened ahead.

Its roof was a cavern of glass and iron filled with smoke, light, and expectation. The platform ran toward us crowded with porters, friends, cabmen, officials, idlers, wives, children, and those mysterious persons who are always present at the arrival of an important train though no one admits to knowing them. Lamps burned yellow in the haze. The engine whistle gave one final cry, not of alarm but proclamation.

We entered slowly.

After four hundred miles of lunging, pounding, roaring flight, the express moved with sovereign composure. The coaches followed in perfect order, their windows bright, their paint dulled by the long road. Faces leaned toward the platform. Hands waved. Porters began to pace beside us before we had stopped. The Caledonian engine advanced beneath the high roof like a victorious animal restrained from exhibiting excitement.

The platform columns slid more slowly.

A woman recognized someone and raised both arms. A child danced beside a trunk. A man removed his hat. The old mother beneath the clock stepped forward, searching each window with terrible concentration. Steam curled around the wheels. The brakes tightened. There was a final, gentle compression of buffers.

We stopped.

Immediately the spell broke into human pieces. Doors flew open. Passengers descended with rugs, baskets, grievances and embraces. Porters cried destinations. A dog emerged indignantly from a van. The commercial travelers resumed Scotland at the precise point where they had left off wool. The child who had slept through the hills awoke and demanded to know when the train would start.

At the head of the platform the engine stood breathing.

Its polished surfaces were filmed with soot; the great wheels, so lately invisible in speed, rested upon the rails; water dripped, steam sighed, and deep within the firebox the conquered coal still glowed. The driver climbed down and spoke to an inspector. The fireman stretched his shoulders. Neither looked like a hero. Heroes, when genuine, seldom do.

Around them continued the vast coordination that had brought us north: guards checking vans, porters unloading trunks, cleaners waiting, signalmen resetting roads, clerks recording minutes, another crew preparing another engine for another darkness. No single hand had carried the train from London. It had passed through a chain of vigilance extending station by station, cabin by cabin, lever by lever, from the Euston portico to this smoky northern roof.

Outside, Glasgow waited with its cabs, rain, granite, factories, suppers, arguments and beds. Behind us lay England in a long invisible line: London’s walls, the flying suburbs, green counties, tunnels, Crewe’s iron kingdom, the northern grades, Carlisle’s lamps, the black Scottish hills. The distance seemed at once immense and abolished.

The great clock marked the appointed minute.

There was no cheering, because punctuality receives no ovation. It is merely expected to perform miracles and apologize for five minutes of mortality. Yet the arrival possessed the dignity of a procession. The train had crossed a kingdom in a working day; had carried sleeping children, anxious mothers, merchants, letters, newspapers, promises, money, and private destinies; had climbed into darkness and descended through firelit valleys; and now stood quiet, as if four hundred miles were no more than the fulfillment of a modest professional engagement.

The driver glanced once along the length of the coaches.

Perhaps he was judging the stop. Perhaps he was thinking of tea. One must not burden working men with the emotions of observers.

Above him, smoke climbed among the iron ribs of the station roof and dissolved against the glass. Beneath, the engine released a final measured breath. It had no speech for triumph, no philosophy of distance, no opinion of England or Scotland, no preference between masonry and improvisation. It had fire, water, steel, and the disciplined hands of men.

That was enough.

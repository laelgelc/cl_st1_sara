## The Place on the Steps

There were three church bells in Wakefield, and on a still Sunday morning each appeared to regard the other two as instruments of error.

The Methodist bell began early and briskly, with no waste of metal or doctrine. The Baptist bell followed, deeper and more argumentative, rolling its convictions over the roofs. Last came the bell of the Congregational church, deliberate and well satisfied, as if it had allowed the others to speak first merely from Christian courtesy.

At ten o’clock the street was full of families proceeding toward one summons or another. Black coats shone at the shoulders; little girls stepped carefully in buttoned boots; elderly women carried their best silk skirts lifted clear of the dust, though now and then an inattentive partisan let hers trail when passing a rival meetinghouse. Parasols bobbed among the maples like great pale flowers. The village street, heated white under the July sun, appeared peaceful; but its peace was of that well-regulated sort beneath which every person knows exactly who has gone where, wearing what, and in company with whom.

On the middle step of the Congregational church sat Marcus Woodman.

He had sat there, during the hours of Sunday worship, for ten years.

Marcus was not an ill-looking man. His forehead was broad, his eyes gray and mild, and his brown hair, which had begun to thin, still curled a little at the temples. Had his face ended below the nose, he might have been judged easy-tempered. Unfortunately, it continued into a square jaw and a chin whose forward projection seemed almost to constitute a second declaration of character.

He wore his Sunday clothes, although he did not enter the church. His black coat was brushed, his boots were polished, and a narrow strip of white linen showed properly at his throat. Beside him, on the granite, lay his hat. He greeted certain passers soberly; others greeted him with a suppressed smile. Children, being less instructed in hypocrisy, stared.

“Marcus is at meetin’ early,” said Deacon Pritchard as he went up the steps.

“I ain’t at meetin’,” Marcus replied.

“Well, you’re nigh enough to hear the singin’.”

“Hearin’ ain’t attendin’.”

The deacon’s mouth twitched. “Mebbe not in your theology.”

Marcus looked straight before him.

The affair had begun when the Reverend Josiah Pennell was called to the Congregational pulpit. Marcus had opposed the appointment. His objections, repeated so often that no one could afterward remember their original proportion, concerned Mr. Pennell’s youth, his city training, his uncertain pronunciation of certain Scriptural names, and a sermon preached as a candidate in which Marcus believed free will had been handled with dangerous lightness.

Marcus’s mother had favored Mr. Pennell. So had Esther Barney, to whom Marcus was then engaged.

The church meeting had been long and warm. Men who commonly used no more than twelve words between sunrise and supper spoke for twenty minutes. Women sat in the rear pews, listening with a stillness that had more effect than interruption. When the vote was taken, Mr. Pennell received a decisive majority.

Marcus rose, pale about the mouth.

“If that man is set over this church,” he said, “I’ll never cross its doorsill.”

Several persons afterward declared that they had tried to stop him. This was untrue, although one man had coughed. A public vow has a fascination for its hearers, especially when they perceive instantly that the person making it will suffer more than anybody else.

Mr. Pennell was installed. The next Sunday Marcus walked to church with his mother, left her at the door, and seated himself upon the steps. He remained there until the service ended.

The second Sunday he did the same.

By the fourth Sunday the village had begun to laugh. By the twentieth it had ceased laughing aloud and had adopted him as an institution. After two years, strangers were directed toward him. After five, children who had not been born at the time of the church quarrel understood that Mr. Woodman sat outside because he had said he would. After ten years, even Mr. Pennell had lost the outlines of the original dispute and would have welcomed Marcus with a humility partly pastoral and partly fatigued.

Marcus, however, continued to sit.

Habit is sometimes only an old act with the life worn out of it; but sometimes the life of the man himself passes into the habit, until to break it seems not like changing a practice, but like destroying an identity. Marcus no longer hated Mr. Pennell. In secret he admitted that the minister preached well enough. Once, listening through an open window to a sermon on Naaman, he had been profoundly moved, though he afterward maintained that the text was an easy one. His vow had become independent of its cause. He sat upon the steps because he had sat there before, because Wakefield expected it, because Wakefield laughed, because Esther Barney had commanded him to stop, and because his own terrible chin appeared to have entered into a compact with the granite beneath it.

Esther had broken their engagement during the first autumn.

She was a small woman, erect and bony, with quick black eyes and hair drawn so tightly from her forehead that it seemed to pull her whole face toward decision. She was not pretty, except at rare moments when feeling softened her; but she was so vigorously alive that prettiness would have been superfluous. At twenty-eight she had owned a dressmaking shop. At thirty-eight she still owned it, and had added two apprentices, a glass case of trimmings, and a reputation for fitting shoulders which brought customers from three towns.

On the afternoon when she returned Marcus’s ring, rain struck the shop windows and ran down them in crooked threads. Esther stood beside the cutting table in a brown merino dress. Marcus stood near the door, holding his hat.

“You went an’ done it again,” she said.

“I said I should.”

“Set there where everybody could see you.”

“I don’t choose to go in.”

“You choose to make a fool of yourself.”

“That’s my affair.”

“No, it ain’t. Not if I’m goin’ to marry you.”

Marcus’s chin advanced slightly. “I don’t see what it’s got to do with marriage.”

“You don’t? S’pose we was married. S’pose I went into meetin’ and you set outdoors like a peddler waitin’ for trade. S’pose our children—if we had any—had to walk past their father on the steps whilst all the village looked on.”

“I ain’t asked any children to do it.”

“Marcus Woodman, don’t talk like a born idiot.”

“I won’t be drove.”

“I ain’t drivin’. I’m tellin’ you what you’ve got to do.”

There are commands which produce obedience and commands which manufacture opposition. Esther’s command was of the latter sort.

“I said I wouldn’t cross that sill,” Marcus answered.

“You said it when you was mad.”

“I said it.”

“And you’d throw away your church, and me, and everything, jest so folks can say you stuck to a wicked, senseless speech?”

“I won’t be drove,” he repeated.

Esther drew the ring from her finger. It caught at the knuckle, and for one betraying instant she twisted it desperately. Then it came free.

“Take it.”

He did not lift his hand.

“Take it, Marcus.”

“What for?”

“You know what for.”

“I don’t want it.”

“Nor I don’t, unless it means more’n your foolishness.”

He took the ring. His face had gone white, but the jaw remained fixed.

“All right,” he said.

“All right,” said Esther.

He left. She cut the next customer’s sleeve too narrow and had to piece it under the arm, a circumstance which grieved her professional pride almost as much as the loss of Marcus. For years afterward the two injuries were associated in her mind.

They did not cease speaking. Wakefield would hardly have permitted so conspicuous a silence, and both were too proud to display a broken heart by avoiding the ordinary places of life. Esther bowed to Marcus on the street. Marcus carried parcels for her when chance made refusal absurd. Once he repaired a warped shutter at the shop, and she paid him twelve cents, though neither slept well that night.

Love did not die between them. It merely became an unacknowledged resident, like a poor relation in the attic—seldom mentioned, frequently heard, and always influencing the household arrangements.

During the tenth year of Marcus’s vigil, Esther’s younger apprentice became engaged.

Margy Vale was twenty, round-faced, fair-haired, and endowed with the hopeful belief that happiness naturally followed the choosing of a blue ribbon over a green one. She could hem neatly, feather-stitch beautifully, and cut plain goods with fair success; but velvet disturbed her. It seemed to perceive her uncertainty and take advantage of it.

Her suitor, George Eastman, worked at the flour mill. He was tall, slow-spoken, and clean, with a sandy mustache he stroked whenever his thoughts became too numerous. The village approved the match. George owned half a house on Cedar Street and expected to own the other half after eight years of payments. Margy had six quilts, four sheets, a feather bed, and a set of china with pink roses. Their prospects were thus regarded as practically luxurious.

One Tuesday morning Esther entered the back room of the shop and found Margy cutting a piece of plum-colored velvet on the bias.

“For the land’s sake!” cried Esther. “What are you doin’?”

Margy started, and the shears made a fatal bite.

“Nothin’.”

“Nothin’! You’ve cut Mrs. Dalrymple’s basque trim crooked as a dog’s hind leg.”

Margy stared at the velvet. Tears gathered in her eyes and rolled over before she had time to turn away.

Esther’s anger altered its character. “What ails you?”

“Nothin’.”

“Don’t tell me nothin’. Folks don’t ruin two yards of velvet for nothin’. Is George sick?”

“No.”

“Lost his place?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

Margy pressed the back of her wrist to her eyes. “We’ve quarreled.”

Esther picked up the velvet, examined the damage, and laid it down with the solemnity due to the dead.

“What about?”

“His mother.”

“What’s she done?”

“She ain’t done anything. That’s jest it. She’s always goin’ to be there.”

“Where?”

“In our house.”

Esther drew out a chair. “Tell it sensible.”

Margy sat beside the worktable, twisting a thread around her finger.

“George says when we’re married his mother’s got to live with us. He says he promised his father on his dyin’ bed he’d look after her, and there ain’t anywhere else for her to go. I said I wa’n’t marryin’ his mother. He said he knew that. I said it didn’t appear so. Then he said she wouldn’t make trouble, and I said she wouldn’t have a chance, because I wouldn’t have her. Then he said—he said—”

“What?”

“He said any woman that could ask him to turn his mother out wa’n’t the woman he took me for.”

The last words dissolved into sobs.

Esther folded her arms. “Well, what did you say?”

“I told him p’rhaps he’d better find the woman he took me for. He said p’rhaps he had. Then he went.”

“Humph.”

Margy raised a wet face. “You think I’m right, don’t you, Miss Barney?”

Esther’s first impulse was to say yes. A young wife had a right to her home. Mrs. Eastman was rheumatic, opinionated, and noted for drying apple rings on every available surface. She had also the habit of entering a room without knocking and opening boiling pots to determine whether they had been properly salted.

Yet Esther hesitated.

“Did you know about the promise to his father?” she asked.

“No.”

“Would his mother be dependent on him?”

“I s’pose.”

“Has she treated you bad?”

“No. But she tells me how George likes his biscuits.”

“Likely he does like ’em some way.”

“He likes mine.”

“Then make ’em your way.”

Margy stared at her in wounded surprise. “But you wouldn’t want another woman runnin’ your house.”

“No.”

“Nor I don’t.”

Esther moved the ruined velvet aside. “Wantin’ ain’t always the whole of livin’.”

This statement sounded so little like her that she frowned after making it.

Margy began to cry again, more quietly. “I had it all planned. There’s a little room off the kitchen where I was goin’ to keep my sewin’ machine, and now George says she could have that. And I was goin’ to put morning-glories by the south porch, but she says vines rot the wood. It wouldn’t be my house at all.”

“Did George say she could rule it?”

“He said she wouldn’t.”

“Do you believe him?”

Margy wound the thread tighter about her finger until the tip reddened. “George don’t lie.”

“No.”

“But he took her part.”

“A man that won’t take his mother’s part when she’s old ain’t overly likely to take his wife’s when she gets so.”

Margy looked down. Esther began measuring the velvet, although there was not enough left for Mrs. Dalrymple’s trim.

After a while Margy whispered, “What would you do?”

Esther’s shears stopped.

In the bright back room, among paper patterns, spools, chalk, and folded cloth, the question appeared to assume a shape larger than the affairs of George Eastman’s half-paid house. Esther saw a church step in sunlight. She saw a man sitting there with his hat beside him, absurd, immovable, and lonely. She saw herself at twenty-eight, holding out a ring and saying, “Take it.” For ten years she had considered only Marcus’s obstinacy. It had not occurred to her that her own will, while directed toward a more reasonable object, had been equally unbending. Reasonableness is a great comfort to the stubborn, since it permits them to enjoy all the pleasures of obstinacy while believing themselves virtuous.

“What would you do?” Margy repeated.

Esther snapped the shears shut.

“I ain’t you,” she said. “An’ George ain’t—anybody I know.”

That evening George came to the shop.

The front room was warm and smelled of new cloth, lamp oil, and the faint scorched odor of an iron cooling on the stove. Esther sat by the window sewing black beads on a cape. Margy, who had been working in the rear, became immediately invisible behind a hanging curtain, though the toe of one shoe remained in sight.

George stood just inside the door and turned his hat in his hands.

“Evenin’, Miss Barney.”

“Evenin’.”

“Margy here?”

“P’rhaps.”

The shoe behind the curtain moved.

George stroked his mustache. “Could I see her?”

“That depends.”

“On what?”

“Whether she wants to see you.”

“I came to say—I came to ask—” He looked toward the curtain. “Margy, I can see your foot.”

The foot withdrew.

There was silence.

“George,” Margy called from behind the curtain, “if you’ve come to say I’m hard-hearted, you can go.”

“I didn’t.”

“Or that I want your mother in the poorhouse.”

“I didn’t say poorhouse.”

“You meant it.”

“I meant you asked what I couldn’t do.”

The curtain trembled. Esther continued sewing, though she put three beads into one stitch.

George said, “I guess there ain’t any use.”

“No,” said Margy faintly.

He turned toward the door.

Then Margy came through the curtain. Her eyes were red, and a streak of tailor’s chalk crossed one cheek.

“George.”

He stopped.

“She can have the little room.”

He stared at her.

“Your mother,” Margy said. “She can have it. I’ll put the machine in our bedroom.”

“Margy—”

“But I won’t have apple rings dryin’ on the parlor chairs.”

“You sha’n’t.”

“An’ I’ll make the biscuits my way.”

“I like ’em your way.”

“An’ the morning-glories—”

“We’ll put ’em up with wires, so’s they won’t touch the porch.”

Margy’s mouth quivered. “Do you think she’ll be satisfied?”

“No,” said George honestly. “Not all the time.”

Margy gave a little laugh that ended in a sob. George dropped his hat and caught both her hands.

Esther looked sternly at her beadwork.

“I was mean,” Margy said.

“No, you wa’n’t.”

“Yes, I was.”

“Well, I was, too.”

“You ought to be ashamed.”

“I am.”

George made as if to kneel.

“Don’t!” cried Margy. “You look ridickerlous. Get up.”

“I ain’t down yet.”

“Then don’t get.”

He kissed her instead. Esther rose, gathered her cape, needles, and dignity, and went into the back room. There she stood among the spoiled velvet and listened to the low murmur of reconciliation.

She was not sentimental by custom. She mistrusted embraces in workrooms and considered tears injurious to silk. Yet she felt something loosen painfully within her. Margy had surrendered the little room, the sewing machine, and perhaps many mornings of being told how George liked his biscuits. In return she had recovered George. The bargain might not be equal, but love rarely keeps accounts in a hand that arithmetic can read.

On Sunday Esther watched Marcus upon the church steps.

He was older than she had allowed herself to notice. There were gray hairs beside his ears; his shoulders, once square, had acquired the slight forward bend of a man accustomed to carrying lumber and private unhappiness. When the congregation sang, he sat very still. A maple leaf dropped beside his shoe. He picked it up, split the stem, and placed the two halves together again.

Esther passed him without speaking.

Inside the church, Mr. Pennell preached on mercy. It was an able sermon, but Esther heard very little. Her mind moved in a narrow circle around a disturbing possibility: Marcus had been wickedly stubborn, but perhaps she had loved her own judgment more than she had loved him.

She resisted the idea throughout the first hymn, admitted it during the prayer, rejected it during the sermon, and carried it home after the benediction.

During the following weeks Marcus’s mother failed rapidly.

Mrs. Woodman had been a spare, cheerful woman who wore lavender ribbons in her cap and defended her son without agreeing with him. For ten years she had entered the church while Marcus remained outside. Each Sunday she paused beside him.

“Come in, Marcus.”

“No, Mother.”

“Well, set straight, then. Your collar’s turned.”

When she became too weak to attend, Marcus ceased going to the steps for three Sundays. Wakefield noticed his absence and, to its credit, did not jest. On the fourth Wednesday Mrs. Woodman died.

Esther went to the funeral. Marcus entered no church even then; the service was held in the front room of his house. He sat beside the coffin with his hands on his knees and looked not stubborn but stunned. Grief sometimes breaks a man’s will; more often it merely removes the person who protected him from its consequences.

After the funeral, women carried away borrowed chairs and covered dishes. Men lingered near the gate, speaking in low voices about frost and lumber. Esther saw Marcus standing alone by the cold stove.

“You’ve got bread?” she asked.

“Plenty.”

“Who’s stayin’ tonight?”

“Nobody.”

“You oughtn’t be alone.”

“I’m used to it.”

He was not used to it. His mother had cooked, washed, mended, kept the account of household necessities, and reminded him to change his socks. Marcus could plane a board to the fineness of satin, but he could not make coffee without producing a liquid which appeared to have been strained through the woodshed floor.

Within a month the house altered. Dust lay on the parlor table. A frying pan remained on the stove for three days. Marcus ate crackers for supper and wore a shirt with one cuff partly detached. Wakefield observed these things through legitimate means, such as carrying in broth, borrowing a saw, and looking through the kitchen door.

Esther heard every report.

“He’ll marry some shiftless thing jest to get took care of,” said Mrs. Dalrymple during a fitting.

“He ain’t lookin’ to marry,” Esther answered, drawing a pin from her mouth.

“Men don’t have to look. They jest get hungry.”

Esther drove the pin into the cushion.

Two evenings later she walked to Marcus’s house.

The air was sharp with October. Yellow leaves had gathered against fences, and smoke descended from chimneys instead of rising. Marcus’s windows showed a dim light. Esther knocked.

He opened the door in his shirtsleeves.

“Esther!”

“Mercy, don’t act as if I’d come from the grave. Let me in. It’s cold.”

He stepped aside. The kitchen was untidy but swept in patches, as though Marcus had pursued the dust without understanding its habits. A kettle boiled dry on the stove.

“Set down,” he said.

“I’d ruther stand till you take that kettle off.”

He snatched it away, burned his fingers, and dropped it on the hearth.

Esther removed her bonnet and surveyed him.

“You’re a sight.”

“I ain’t had time to fix up.”

“You’ve had four weeks.”

“What’d you come for?”

“To see you.”

“Why?”

“Must I send notice by the town crier?”

Marcus sat beside the table. Esther took the chair opposite. Between them stood a plate containing half an onion, two nails, and a button.

For several moments neither spoke. People who have left an important subject untouched for ten years cannot approach it naturally. It has grown too large; every ordinary word walks around it.

At last Esther said, “You can’t go on this way.”

“I’m gettin’ along.”

“No, you ain’t.”

“I don’t want charity.”

“I didn’t offer any.”

He looked at her, then away.

She folded her hands tightly. “Marcus, I’ve been thinkin’.”

“That so?”

“Yes, that’s so. Don’t make it harder’n it is.”

His mild eyes returned to her face. The chin, though unaltered, seemed for once to await instructions.

“I was wrong,” Esther said.

Marcus drew a breath.

“Not about the steps,” she added sharply. “You was a fool then, and you’re a fool now. But I was wrong, too.”

“What about?”

“About thinkin’ you had to give in before I could.”

He stared.

Esther’s cheeks reddened. “You asked me once to marry you.”

“That was ten years ago.”

“I can count.”

“You gave me back the ring.”

“I know what I did.”

“I’ve got it yet.”

Her eyes flickered, but her voice remained brisk. “Well?”

Marcus rose, went to the mantel, and took down a small wooden box. Inside, beneath two receipts and a broken watch key, lay the ring.

Esther looked at it.

“You kept it in that trash?”

“It ain’t trash.”

“The key’s broke.”

“I mean the ring.”

“Oh.”

He stood holding it. Neither appeared able to proceed.

Finally Esther said, “Marcus, do you want me?”

His hand closed around the ring.

“You know I do.”

“No, I don’t. A woman don’t come ten years later to guess. Say it.”

“I want you.”

“Enough to marry me?”

“Yes.”

“Even if I go to church?”

His face changed.

Esther continued before he could speak. “I’m goin’. Every Sunday I’m able. I ain’t givin’ up the church, and I ain’t askin’ you to say you’ll come in. If you’ve got to set on them steps like a graven image, set. I’ll walk past you.”

“Esther—”

“I mean it.”

“You’d marry me whilst I—”

“Yes.”

“Folks would laugh.”

“They’ve been laughin’ ten years. I guess their lungs can stand another ten.”

He came around the table. His eyes were wet, and this embarrassed both of them.

“I don’t deserve you,” he said.

“No,” Esther agreed. “An’ I don’t deserve any better, or I’d likely have got it by now.”

A sound escaped him, half laugh and half sob. He began to kneel.

“Marcus Woodman, don’t you get down on that dirty floor.”

“I was only—”

“Stand up. You look ridickerlous.”

He stood. She held out her hand, and he put the old ring on it. It passed the knuckle with difficulty.

“Fits tight,” he said.

“So do most things after ten years.”

They were married in November at Esther’s house.

Wakefield accepted the event with an excitement disguised as calm approval. Margy made the cake. George brought chairs. Mrs. Dalrymple wore purple silk and cried into a handkerchief edged with real lace. Mr. Pennell performed the ceremony without any reference to doorsills, vows rashly uttered, or the return of wanderers, though all three subjects must have presented powerful temptations.

Marcus wore a new black suit. Esther wore steel-gray silk trimmed with narrow bands of plum velvet rescued, through ingenuity, from Mrs. Dalrymple’s ruined basque. She looked small, severe, and almost beautiful.

When Mr. Pennell pronounced them husband and wife, the village experienced a satisfaction which was not entirely benevolent. It had waited ten years for the conclusion of this story and felt that patience entitled it to a good view.

For the first months they were happy in the active, imperfect fashion suited to them. Esther put Marcus’s kitchen in order. Marcus built shelves in her shop. They disputed over where the flour belonged and whether the bedroom window should remain open in November. Each sometimes yielded, though usually after first establishing beyond question that yielding was unnecessary.

Margy married George in January. Mrs. Eastman moved into the little room. She objected to the morning-glories, the biscuit recipe, and the position of the parlor clock; but she also knitted Margy a red shawl and sat up three nights nursing her through influenza. Compromise, Margy discovered, did not turn life into heaven. It merely made life possible with the particular people one loved.

The first Sunday after Esther and Marcus returned from their brief wedding visit to Esther’s cousin in South Mill, the whole village anticipated the hour of service.

No public announcement had been made, but public anticipation requires none. By half past nine, curtains moved along the street. The Baptists delayed on their way, though their bell had rung. Two Methodist girls walked past the Congregational church twice, explaining that they had forgotten a glove. Mr. Pennell’s sermon lay open on the pulpit, but he read the same paragraph six times without understanding it.

Esther dressed carefully.

She wore the gray silk, a black bonnet with violet flowers, and gloves buttoned at the wrist. Marcus put on his new suit. At five minutes before ten they left the house together.

Neither mentioned the steps.

The morning was clear and cold. Frost still silvered the shaded edges of the road, while the sunny dust had softened under passing feet. The three bells rang one over another. Marcus walked on Esther’s outside, as a husband should. He carried her hymnbook.

At the corner from which the Congregational church came into view, his pace slackened.

Esther did not look at him.

They passed the gate.

People stood about the entrance longer than custom justified. Deacon Pritchard discussed the weather with desperate persistence. Mrs. Dalrymple appeared absorbed in fastening a glove already fastened. Margy and George had gone inside, but Margy peered through a side window until George gently pulled her down.

Marcus mounted the first step.

Then he stopped.

His customary place was before him: the middle step near the left pillar, worn faintly smooth after ten years. The sight of it acted upon him with a force out of all proportion to stone. He had believed that marriage, grief, and the passage of time might have weakened the compulsion. Instead he felt as though wire ropes, fixed during a decade to every bone and nerve, drew him toward the old seat.

Esther mounted the step below and waited.

Marcus’s face had become gray.

“I can’t,” he whispered.

She looked up at him. “Can’t what?”

“Go by it.”

“Yes, you can.”

“No.”

Her mouth tightened. For an instant the old command rose within her: *You shall. You’ve shamed me long enough. Go in.* She could almost hear the words in the voice she had used ten years before. She knew, too, what they would do. His will and hers would meet again upon different planes, each strengthened by impact, and between them their happiness would be crushed as easily as velvet under shears.

She drew a slow breath.

“All right,” she said.

Marcus looked at her.

She moved to the accustomed place and sat down.

A tremor passed through the people near the door.

“What are you doin’?” Marcus asked.

“Settin’.”

“You can’t set here.”

“Why not?”

“Folks are lookin’.”

“They generally are.”

“Go in, Esther.”

“No.”

The word was quiet. It contained no challenge.

“Go in,” he repeated.

“I said I’d walk past you, but I’ve changed my mind.”

“You can’t miss meetin’ on account of me.”

“I can if I choose.”

“It’s cold.”

“I’ve got a cloak.”

Marcus stood above her, distracted. The bell ceased. In the silence that followed, small sounds became enormous: a cough inside the church, the creak of a harness, the rustle of Esther’s silk as she made room beside her.

“Set down,” she said. “If you’ve got to set, I’ll set with you.”

He stared at the narrow space.

The absurdity was complete. The village saw it; Esther saw it; Marcus saw it most clearly of all. For ten years he had made himself ridiculous, but he had borne the ridicule because it was familiar and because pride can grow a hard skin against laughter directed at itself. He had never imagined Esther sharing it. Her gray wedding dress upon the granite, her little black bonnet beneath the great church pillar, and her hands folded patiently over her hymnbook altered the whole meaning of his act.

His stubbornness had been his private burden and his public exhibition. Now she had taken one end of it.

“Esther,” he said brokenly, “you don’t mean you’d set here every Sunday?”

“If you do.”

“In rain?”

“There’s the vestibule roof.”

“In winter?”

“I’ll wear my fur tippet.”

“They’ll laugh at you.”

“Mebbe.”

“On account of me.”

“Yes.”

Her face was pale, but her eyes did not leave his. There was no anger in them. That was what conquered him. Commands he could resist; ridicule he could survive; arguments he could defeat by refusing to answer. But this willing entrance into his shame left him no adversary. The force against which he had braced himself for ten years had disappeared, and without it his obstinacy could no longer stand upright.

He looked toward the open church door.

Beyond it lay the aisle, the pews, the pulpit, and Mr. Pennell, who had grown gray while Marcus guarded his vow. Nothing terrible was visible. No lightning crossed the threshold. The place was merely a church, smelling of hymnbooks, wool coats, stove heat, and evergreens left too long after a church social.

Marcus bent and took Esther’s hand.

“Come,” he said.

She did not move at once. “Where?”

“Inside.”

“You sure?”

“No,” he answered. “But come.”

She rose.

The congregation had ceased pretending not to watch. Faces turned openly toward the entrance. Margy stood in her pew with both hands pressed to her mouth. George, beside her, stroked his sandy mustache. Mrs. Dalrymple wept before anything had happened and afterward insisted she had known it would.

Marcus reached the doorsill.

His foot halted above it. The old vow seemed to rise for one last assertion—not as a sentence spoken in anger, but as ten years of Sundays, ten years of laughter, ten years of explaining himself to others and to himself. To cross would be to confess folly. There are persons who prefer lifelong punishment to a moment’s confession.

Esther’s hand rested within his. She did not pull.

Marcus set his foot down inside.

Then he crossed with her.

No one laughed.

Mr. Pennell came from the pulpit steps and met them halfway along the aisle. His lips moved, but whatever formal welcome he had prepared deserted him. He held out his hand.

“Marcus,” he said.

“Mr. Pennell.”

“I’m glad—very glad.”

Marcus swallowed. “I guess you had the vote fair.”

A sound ran through the church, not quite laughter and not quite sobbing.

Mr. Pennell gripped his hand. “There are matters,” he said, “which improve by being settled, even late.”

“Yes,” Marcus replied. “Some take time.”

Deacon Pritchard moved along his pew to make room. Esther entered first, and Marcus followed. For a moment he stood beneath the gaze of the congregation. His face was pale, his eyes troubled, and his formidable chin had by no means vanished; yet he appeared taller than he had on the steps.

The organ began the opening hymn.

Marcus found the place in Esther’s book. At first he did not sing. Then, during the second line, his voice entered—rough, uncertain, and a little too loud.

Outside, the accustomed place remained empty. Sunlight lay across the granite, showing the shallow mark where his boots had rested year after year. Before another winter the weather would begin to soften even that.

Wakefield talked of the event for a long while, as villages properly do. Some said Esther had managed Marcus at last. Some said marriage had cured him. Some maintained that he had always intended to go in when the time was ripe. All these explanations were simpler than the truth and therefore more popular.

Yet those who had stood near the church door remembered Esther sitting down beside him in her wedding silk, prepared to share indefinitely the absurd punishment he had made for himself. They remembered Marcus looking at her, then at the threshold. And even the sharpest tongues, in telling the story, softened at that point.

For they had witnessed a ridiculous man perform a brave act, and a severe woman discover tenderness without losing strength. They had seen pride, which argument had only hardened, yield before companionship. The comedy remained—indeed, it could not honestly be removed—but beneath it lay something which made laughter difficult.

Marcus Woodman had crossed no more than a strip of wood beneath an ordinary church door. Nevertheless, as he walked up the aisle with Esther beside him, he had the look of a man returning from a long and bitter country, and the watching congregation received him as a conqueror.

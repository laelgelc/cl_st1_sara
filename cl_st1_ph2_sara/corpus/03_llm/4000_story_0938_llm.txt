### The Cathedral Shadow

Patsy Tulligan was not gifted with the wisdom of seven owls, nor, indeed, with enough wisdom to inconvenience one small chicken; but his courage threw across Cherry Street a shadow as long and solemn as the steeple of a cathedral.

On Saturday night this courage went abroad in company with Patsy, who had already filled himself with beer until his blue eyes shone like two wet buttons. With him were Mickey Doolan and Jim Placer, young men of less celebrated bravery but of sufficient discretion to admire Patsy loudly and restrain him weakly. They had wandered beyond their own district, where every saloon keeper knew Patsy’s name, habits, debts, and probable hour of collapse, and had entered an establishment whose polished wood and furbished brass rebuked their boots.

The place was long and narrow. Three gas lamps cast a yellow glare upon the bar. Behind it stood a bartender with a white apron and a towel, polishing a glass with the devout concentration of one engaged upon a religious vessel.

At the end of the bar sat a young Cuban.

He was dressed in black, with a pale waistcoat, patent-leather shoes, and a cravat arranged with mournful elegance. His hands were tiny and brown. Upon one finger an emerald winked whenever he lifted his glass. His feet, too, were remarkably small, and Patsy studied them with the expression of a philosopher who has discovered a comic law of nature.

“Look at de little slippers,” he observed to Mickey.

Mickey advised silence by striking Patsy in the ribs with an elbow.

Patsy misunderstood this warning as applause.

“Say, Jim,” he continued, in a voice which moved through the saloon like a wagon passing over loose boards, “wouldn’t dem feet look great on a canary bird?”

The bartender’s towel stopped.

The Cuban turned his head. His face was smooth and delicate, but his eyes had suddenly acquired two bright points. He regarded Patsy with a stillness more menacing than motion.

“Did you speak of me?” he asked.

Patsy thrust out his jaw. This jaw was famous on Cherry Street. It had preceded him into fights, arguments, dances, and police stations.

“Was I speakin’ of yeh?” he said. “Why, if I was speakin’ of yeh, yeh’d know it.”

“You make a joke of my foot.”

“Aw, I only said yeh got shoes like a lady’s watch charms. Dat ain’t no crime.”

The Cuban set down his glass. He did so delicately, bending his little finger as if the finger belonged to a duchess and had been lent to him for the evening.

“You have insult me.”

Patsy looked magnificently astonished.

“Insulted yeh? Who insulted yeh? I says yeh got small feet. Maybe yeh wants me t’ say yeh got feet like ferryboats?”

Mickey and Jim leaned close to him.

“Drop it, Pat,” muttered Mickey. “Dis ain’t our joint.”

“Sure,” said Jim. “Have a drink.”

But the Cuban had risen. He was shorter than Patsy by half a head, and his slender body seemed made for black clothes, balconies, moonlight, and calamity. A red spot had appeared upon each cheek.

“You call me—what? You call me names?”

“I ain’t called yeh nothin’ yet,” said Patsy. “But if yeh’re lookin’ fer trouble, I got a whole warehouse of it.”

The Cuban’s rage flashed like metal struck against stone.

“You are a coward!” he cried.

The word cracked through the room like breaking glass.

The bartender put down the glass and towel. His hands moved beneath the bar with the cautious haste of a man seeking either a club or a convenient tunnel.

Patsy advanced. His fists became two knots of bone and hard flesh. His companions caught him at the shoulders.

“Who’s a coward?” he demanded. “Lemme go! Who’s a coward? I’ll knock dat black necktie through his spine!”

“You are coward,” repeated the Cuban, and now he smiled.

Patsy lunged. Mickey and Jim collided with him like football players stopping a runaway horse. One of Patsy’s fists passed within an inch of the Cuban’s nose, stirring the air before it. The Cuban did not retreat. He merely inclined his head and continued to smile, which was a consummate display of insanity in the opinion of all practical men.

“Lemme at him!” roared Patsy. “He wants t’ scrap! Didn’t yeh hear him? He wants t’ scrap!”

“No fist,” said the Cuban. “I do not fight wis the fist.”

“Oh, yeh don’t, don’t yeh? Den what’re yeh hollerin’ about?”

“I am a gentleman.”

This announcement caused Patsy to stop struggling. He stared at the Cuban and then turned solemnly to his friends.

“He says he’s a gentleman.”

Mickey and Jim began to laugh. Their laughter had no deep intellectual source, but it was loud, and therefore it appeared authoritative.

The Cuban cringed beneath it as if each burst were the flick of a lash. The red in his cheeks darkened. His right hand trembled near the emerald ring.

“Yes,” he said. “I am gentleman. And because I am gentleman, you shall give me s-s-satisfac-shone.”

Patsy’s expression became suspicious.

“What’ll I give yeh?”

“Satisfaction.”

“I ain’t got it.”

“You shall fight me.”

“Dat’s what I been tellin’ these two goats. Lemme go, boys. He wants t’ scrap.”

“With sword,” said the Cuban.

The three men from Cherry Street became silent.

The bartender, who had been lowering himself behind the counter, rose again by a slow degree. He resumed his towel and glass, although his eyes remained fixed upon the group. In another moment he might have been cast in bronze as a statue entitled Prudence Listening to Murder.

Patsy scratched one ear.

“Wid a what?”

“A sword. We go to my hotel. I have two sword. You choose. We fight. I keel you.”

The words were spoken without noise. This quiet promise produced more effect than shouting. Mickey’s hands dropped from Patsy’s shoulders.

“Say,” he murmured, “dis bloke means it.”

Jim peered at the Cuban. “He’s one of dem sword fighters. Look how he stands.”

Indeed, the Cuban had placed one foot before the other. His body turned slightly sideways, his left hand rising behind him in a graceful curve. The pose transformed him. The small feet ceased to be ridiculous. They now appeared quick, balanced, and deadly. His right hand darted forward with two fingers extended, and the imaginary point of a sword stopped neatly at the centre of Patsy’s waistcoat.

“Here,” he said. “I put it here. Then you die.”

Patsy looked down at the indicated spot.

“Yeh’d stick me?”

“I run you through. So.”

Again the little hand moved. The emerald cast a green flash in the lamp-glare.

Jim seized Patsy’s sleeve. “Don’t be a fool. Dat guy knows swords. He’ll cut yeh into buttons.”

“Sure,” said Mickey. “You never had a sword in yer hand.”

Patsy drew himself up. The beer in him had been joined by an idea of honor, and together they formed a government against which reason had no vote.

“What difference does dat make?” he said.

“What difference? He’ll kill yeh in two seconds.”

“Maybe he will.”

“Den don’t go.”

Patsy looked at Mickey with immense contempt.

“He called me a coward.”

“Call him a liar and come home.”

“He called me a coward,” repeated Patsy. To him, this fact had become a pillar supporting the sky. Remove it and all creation would fall.

The Cuban folded his arms.

“He will not come,” he said. “He is afraid.”

Patsy wheeled upon him.

“Afraid? Say, listen, little man. I don’t know nothin’ about swords. I don’t know which end yeh grab. But I’ll go wid yeh, an’ I’ll fight yeh, an’ if yeh stick me I’ll pull de sticker out an’ bat yeh over de head wid it.”

The Cuban bowed with portentous dignity.

“Very well. In ten minute you are dead.”

“Make it five,” said Patsy.

Mickey dragged him aside. “Pat, think! He’s trained. Dem Cubans do dat all day. Dey eat breakfast wid swords.”

Patsy considered this curious national custom but was unmoved.

“He wants t’ scrap.”

“It ain’t a scrap! It’s murder.”

“If I back down, what do I tell de boys?”

“Tell ’em nothin’. Dey ain’t here.”

Patsy looked around the saloon. Two old men had emerged from a rear room. A waiter stood by a swinging door. The bartender still held his glass aloft. All were watching.

This audience settled the matter. A man may privately possess caution, tenderness, and a desire to live; but under the eyes of six strangers he becomes the keeper of a sacred flame. Patsy thrust out his jaw until it seemed ready to leave his face and proceed to the hotel in advance of him.

“I go,” he announced.

The Cuban bowed again.

“Good. We have sword at my room.”

“Yeh got two?”

“Yes.”

“No funny work? I get one too?”

“You may choose.”

Patsy nodded, impressed by this courtesy. “Dat’s square.”

The bartender came abruptly to life.

“There’ll be no sword fight out of my place,” he declared.

“Who asked yeh?” said Patsy.

“I won’t have it. You fellows clear out.”

The Cuban turned his bright eyes upon him. “It is not concern of you.”

“It concerns me if you carve a man up after starting it in here.”

“I start nothing. This pig insult me.”

“Who’re yeh callin’ pig?” roared Patsy.

Again Mickey and Jim were obliged to play football. They struck Patsy from either side, driving him backward against a table. Glasses jumped and clattered. One fell, broke, and scattered bright fragments across the floor. The waiter fled through the swinging door. The old men retreated several paces and then, finding a position of safety, craned their necks with enthusiasm.

The Cuban snatched a walking stick from beside the bar and whipped it forward. The silver knob struck Patsy upon the wrist.

Patsy gave a howl of joy.

“Now we’re goin’!”

He broke free. His right fist swung in a great half-circle, carrying behind it the full moral force of Cherry Street. The Cuban ducked. The fist missed him and struck Jim upon the ear. Jim staggered into Mickey. Mickey collided with the bartender, who dropped the glass. For one splintered second every man in the place appeared to be attacking every other man.

The Cuban skipped backward, light as a cat. He held the walking stick like a blade, its point darting toward Patsy’s eyes.

“Come!” he snarled. “Come, brute! I stick you like the pig!”

“Quit pokin’ dat cane!” cried Patsy, warding it off. “Fight fair!”

“You fight fair wis sword.”

“I’ll fight yeh wid an axe, a lamppost, or a plate of soup! Gimme room!”

The bartender disappeared through a side door. No one noticed him leave. The room had become a theatre of battleful attitudes. Mickey clutched his bruised shoulder. Jim held one ear and shouted advice. The old men exclaimed with the delight of spectators who had paid nothing for excellent seats.

Then, as swiftly as it had risen, the storm paused.

The Cuban lowered the stick.

“Come,” he said. “My hotel is near. There we settle.”

Patsy smoothed his coat. One sleeve had been torn at the seam. He arranged it with the dignity of a general examining damage to his uniform.

“I’m ready.”

Jim stepped before him. “I ain’t lettin’ yeh go.”

“Get away.”

“He’ll kill yeh.”

“Dat’s my business.”

“It’s my business too. I promised yer sister I’d keep yeh out of trouble.”

“Den yeh made a foolish promise.”

Mickey tried another strategy. “Suppose yeh win. Suppose yeh kill him. What den?”

Patsy frowned. He had not considered victory as a practical complication.

“Den,” he said slowly, “he’ll know better next time.”

The old men laughed. Patsy turned on them, and they immediately studied the ceiling.

The Cuban moved toward the door. “We waste time.”

“Wait fer me,” said Patsy. “I’m comin’.”

At that moment the side door opened, and the bartender returned with a policeman.

The officer was broad, red-faced, and calm. His helmet seemed to enter first, like the black prow of a municipal ship. He surveyed the broken glass, the overturned chair, Patsy’s torn sleeve, Jim’s crimson ear, the Cuban’s uplifted stick, and the bartender’s expression of injured property.

“What’s all this?” he asked.

No one answered immediately. The grandeur of the affair had not prepared it for this simple question.

The bartender pointed. “That man’s threatening a sword duel.”

The policeman looked at the Cuban.

“You got a sword?”

“At my hotel,” replied the Cuban.

“You been threatening to use it?”

The Cuban lifted his chin. “This is affair of honor. It is not business of you.”

The policeman blinked once.

“Is that so?”

“Yes. This man insult me. We fight as gentleman. You do not interfere.”

“I don’t, eh?”

“No. You are police. This is private.”

The officer took the walking stick from him.

The Cuban’s small hand flashed to recover it. The officer caught him by the collar. Elegance vanished. The black coat twisted, the pale waistcoat bunched, and the patent-leather shoes danced furiously upon the sawdust.

“Release me!” screamed the Cuban. “You have no right! I am gentleman! I keel him! I keel all!”

“Come along,” said the policeman.

“This is outrage! This is no concern of police!”

“It’s beginning to concern the station house.”

He dragged the Cuban toward the door. The young man fought with the concentrated fury of a trapped animal, striking backward with his tiny fists and pouring out a torrent of Spanish, English, and sounds which belonged to no language but wrath. The emerald flickered wildly. At the threshold he braced both small feet against the floor, but the policeman gave one steady pull, and gentleman, honor, satisfaction, and patent leather disappeared together into the night.

Silence occupied the saloon.

Patsy stood in the centre of it, breathing hard.

After a moment he said, “Well, why didn’t he arrest me too?”

The bartender looked at him. “Give him time.”

Mickey began to laugh. Jim joined him, although touching his damaged ear. The old men laughed. The waiter peered through the swinging door and laughed. Even the bartender’s mouth bent reluctantly.

Patsy faced them with a terrible scowl.

“What’re yeh laughin’ at? I was ready, wasn’t I?”

“Sure, Pat,” said Mickey. “You was ready.”

“I’d have went wid him.”

“Sure.”

“I’d have took one of dem swords.”

“Sure.”

Patsy picked up his hat, struck the dent from it, and placed it on his head at a heroic angle.

“An’ lemme tell yeh,” he said, looking around upon the citizenry, “dat little Cuban might know a lot about fancy stickers, but he’d have known he was in a fight. I’d have give him de greatest fight he ever seen.”

No one disputed this. The duel, having been prevented, was now entirely Patsy’s property, and within three minutes he had almost won it.

Thus Patsy Tulligan returned to Cherry Street alive, undefeated, and somewhat enlarged by history. He was not gifted with the wisdom of seven owls, nor with enough wisdom to disturb one small chicken; but his courage still threw behind him a shadow as long and solemn as the steeple of a cathedral.

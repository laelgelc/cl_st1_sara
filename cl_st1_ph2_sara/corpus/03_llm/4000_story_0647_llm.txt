# The Third Summons

In the autumn of 1889, having concluded in San Francisco a negotiation which added somewhat to my fortune and considerably to my opinion of my abilities, I remembered Mohun Dampier.

That I had not remembered him earlier may be attributed to business; that he had not written to me in seven years, to friendship. We had been inseparable at school, where his learning, reserve, and handsome melancholy had won him a distinction which my easier virtues could not command. He came of an old family, and bore its antiquity as some men bear a hereditary disease—with pride in the symptoms. Yet he had once possessed gaiety, courage, and a fastidious generosity. I had loved him, though I sometimes suspected that he tolerated in me the vulgar advantages of health and good sense.

Inquiry discovered him in a decayed suburb beyond the western hills, where the city seemed to lose confidence in itself and fall apart among sand, cypress, and low wooden dwellings. His house, which had belonged to an eccentric astronomer, stood apart upon rising ground. From its roof sprang a narrow octagonal tower, absurdly aspiring above the weather-beaten structure. When I drove toward it on the appointed evening, there was a light in the tower and none elsewhere.

A storm had come in from the Pacific. The cypresses writhed like black penitents; the rain pursued the carriage in level volleys; and now and then the sea sent across the darkness a voice so vast and hollow that the little lamps of the scattered houses appeared to tremble before it.

Dampier opened the door himself.

I knew him at once, and was shocked by the recognition. He was not forty, yet his hair was white at the temples, his shoulders stooped, and his face had that bloodless delicacy which is sometimes less the work of illness than of a mind that has fed too long upon itself. His eyes alone retained their former brilliance, though it was now an inward and unhealthy fire.

“My dear Dampier,” I cried, taking both his hands, “you have been cultivating venerability.”

“And you,” he replied, with the ghost of his old smile, “have been cultivating prosperity. Each of us has succeeded.”

He led me through a hall smelling of damp timber and extinguished fires, and up two flights of stairs to the tower room. It was lined with books. A coal fire burned on the hearth; lamps, shaded in green, illuminated a table covered with manuscripts; and against the western wall stood a narrow couch. The windows shook under the wind.

I laid aside my coat and surveyed him more carefully.

“You are ill.”

“I am dying.”

“Every scholar says so when he has missed dinner.”

“I have not missed dinner. I have merely outlived the necessity of many things, and shall shortly outlive the remainder.”

His tone was too tranquil for mere affectation. Nevertheless, I laughed, believing ridicule an excellent restorative in cases where physic has not been tried.

“If you have invited me here to witness your translation, I must protest. My steamer leaves on Thursday.”

“You will not be detained.”

He poured wine. His hand trembled, and some of it fell upon the table like a dark stain of blood.

We talked at first of old companions, most of whom had either married or become respectable in less forgivable ways. Yet every subject, however remote, seemed to conduct Dampier back toward death. He spoke of it not with terror, nor exactly with desire, but as one speaks of a guest whose arrival has been certified by telegram.

At length I said: “What has happened to you?”

Before he could reply there came, faintly but distinctly, three taps upon the western wall.

We both turned.

The sounds had been deliberate and evenly spaced:

Tap—tap—tap.

Dampier rose so suddenly that his chair fell behind him. His lips moved, but no voice issued from them.

“There is someone outside,” I said.

“No.”

“A branch, then.”

“There is no tree within thirty feet of that wall.”

I went to the nearest window. Dampier caught my arm.

“Do not open it!”

“My dear fellow, I have no intention of inviting the Pacific Ocean into your library. I merely wish to look.”

Wiping the glass, I peered downward. The wall descended sheer for some forty feet to a paved yard. Beyond it the ground sloped away, bare except for bent shrubs glistening in the rain. There was no balcony, cornice, pipe, or roof by which any human being might approach the spot from which the sound had seemed to proceed.

“A rat within the wall,” I suggested.

“The wall is solid brick.”

“Then a metaphysical rat. Your library surely contains several.”

He did not smile. He righted his chair, sat, and covered his face. When he looked up again, his terror had become solemnity.

“You heard three blows?”

“Unless arithmetic has been modified since our school days.”

“This is the third occasion.”

“Tonight?”

“In ten years.”

The wind struck the tower with such violence that the lamps fluttered. Dampier leaned toward me.

“There is a doctrine,” he said, “preserved in a fragment attributed to Florentius of Ravenna, that the dead, when held near the world by an unfulfilled act, may return thrice to the guilty: first as remembrance, next as warning, last as summons. The passage is called *De Terno Limine*—Of the Threefold Threshold. I once dismissed it as monkish delirium.”

“And now?”

“Now I know that I shall not see tomorrow.”

I had always regarded occult learning as the revenge of obsolete books upon men who read them. Yet I had heard the taps, and the inaccessible wall was before me.

“Dampier,” I said, “you owe me an explanation.”

“I owe it to you because you heard. I owe it to her because I did not answer.”

“To whom?”

He turned his eyes toward the wall.

“To the woman I killed.”

The storm seemed to withdraw at that instant, leaving about the tower a silence more formidable than its clamour. Dampier replenished the fire, though the room was warm, and began.

---

“Ten years ago,” he said, “I lived in Baltimore, in a house which my aunt had left me. It was a narrow, solemn dwelling of red brick, burdened with dark furniture and family portraits. The Dampiers, dead or living, have always occupied more room than was good for them.

“The adjoining house was smaller and less distinguished. I had been told that its owner, a widow named Armitage, derived her income from keeping music pupils. That information should have been without significance; but hereditary pride is an imperious legislator, especially where it has nothing worthy to govern.

“One April morning I saw the widow’s daughter.

“She stood in the little garden behind their house, beneath a pear tree whose white petals were falling upon her hair. Do not ask me to describe her. Description is the inventory of beauty, not its revelation. I can tell you that she wore a grey dress, that she was very young, and that the sunlight, passing through the blossoms, seemed less to illuminate her than to receive illumination. This is folly, of course. I knew it to be folly even then, and cherished it for that reason.

“Her name was Evelyn.

“I discovered it from a servant, with other intelligence which ought to have lowered the temperature of my imagination and instead increased it. She taught singing to children; she mended her own dresses; she sometimes carried parcels through the street. These innocent occupations offended the phantom tribunal of my ancestors. I could have married a fool of my own rank without reproach, but to speak sincerely to a good woman of no rank appeared a social catastrophe.

“You know my youth. I had mistaken hauteur for strength and solitude for superiority. Moreover, I possessed that special cowardice which masquerades as idealism. I told myself that ordinary acquaintance would profane my feeling. To learn her thoughts, to discover a fault, to risk indifference—these were coarse experiments fit for men whose hearts were less exquisite. Thus I elevated her above humanity in order that I might escape addressing her.

“For nearly a year I loved her by windows.

“My study overlooked the garden, and I arranged my reading so that the most difficult authors required a position near the glass. If she walked beneath the pear tree, Plotinus became opaque; if she raised her face, the universe acquired coherence. Occasionally our eyes met. Once she smiled. Upon that slight event I constructed a temple large enough to contain my vanity.

“The bedrooms of the two houses adjoined. Between them was an old party wall, covered on my side by carved panels. I did not know which room was hers until a night in February.

“I had been reading late. The household was asleep, and snow muffled the city. As I extinguished my candle, I heard three light taps from the wall beside my bed.

“I listened. They came again.

“Tap—tap—tap.

“I struck the panel three times with my knuckles.

“The answer was immediate.

“You may suppose that I suspected accident, pipes, mice, the contraction of timber. I tested each hypothesis with the diligence of a philosopher defending his desire. Three taps; then silence. Three more; then my reply. The unseen intelligence answered always. Presently I heard, so faintly that imagination might have supplied it, a woman’s laugh.

“The next morning Evelyn appeared in the garden. She looked up at my window and touched three fingers to her lips.

“Our romance had begun.

“Romance! Observe the noble word with which I decorated our childish secrecy. Night after night, when the houses were still, one of us gave the signal and the other returned it. Sometimes that was all. At other times we invented a rudimentary alphabet—one tap for A, two for B, and so forth, with pauses between letters. It was cumbersome enough to satisfy love, which values effort above information. We exchanged names, then greetings, then fragments of sentiment. I learned that she read poetry, feared thunder, loved spring rain, and had once seen the sea. She learned, I imagine, that her neighbour was an ass.

“Why did I not call upon her mother? Because delay had become part of the enchantment. Because I feared that speech would expose the poverty of our communion. Because I was proud. Because I was mad. Select whichever explanation seems most merciful; it will be false. The truth is simpler: I enjoyed being adored without submitting myself to judgment.

“She asked once, by our laborious alphabet, ‘WHEN SHALL WE SPEAK?’

“I replied, ‘SOON.’

“There is no word more convenient to cowardice.

“Spring returned. We continued our signals. Sometimes I met her in the street and bowed with formal coldness, though my heart battered my ribs. Her face would brighten and then grow grave. I persuaded myself that restraint was honourable. In reality, I was teaching her humiliation.

“One evening in May I saw her walking with a young man. He carried a physician’s case, though I did not notice that until later. She rested her hand upon his arm, and he bent toward her with evident concern.

“Jealousy, which is vanity’s most industrious servant, interpreted the scene. That night I waited for the signal. It came.

“I did not answer at once.

“She repeated it.

“I replied at last, but coldly—if one can be cold with knuckles. During the following days I avoided the window. Then a note was brought to my door. The handwriting was hers; I knew it from the few words we had once exchanged by lowering a book from my window into the garden. I held the envelope for an hour and sent it back unopened.

“Can you conceive such baseness?”

I said nothing.

“Pride is ingenious,” he continued. “I informed myself that honour forbade accepting explanations where trust had been compromised. Honour! The word has performed more disreputable labour than any other in the language.

“For several nights the signal came. Sometimes only once, sometimes repeatedly. I lay rigid and refused reply. Then it ceased.

“At first I congratulated myself. I had preserved my dignity. After a week, dignity proved a poor companion. I watched the garden, but Evelyn did not appear. The shutters of her room remained closed. I considered making inquiry, yet to inquire was to betray concern; and I was still acting before the invisible audience of my ancestors, those respectable corpses whose applause has cost the living so much.

“A fortnight passed.

“One rainy night, near midnight, I lay awake. I had resolved that if she signalled again I would answer, though I disguised the surrender by calling it forgiveness. The house was very still. Rain whispered against the panes. From beyond the wall came a faint dragging sound, followed by voices, then silence.

“An hour elapsed.

“Then I heard the signal.

“Not as before. Each blow was scarcely audible, with a long interval between, as though the hand that made it had to gather strength.

“Tap.

“I sat upright.

“Tap.

“I placed my fingers against the panel.

“Tap.

“I knew it was she. I knew that all my interpretations had been inventions, that she had suffered, that this feeble appeal had crossed not merely brick but the immeasurable distance my cruelty had placed between us.

“And still I did not answer.

“Do not turn away. I want you to see the act clearly. My hand was upon the wall. One movement would have sufficed. But I remembered the unopened letter, the young man, the weeks of silence; I imagined that she had returned because some other hope had failed her. I said to myself, ‘Too late.’

“I spoke the words aloud.

“Too late.

“There came a soft sound against the wall—not a tap, but something like the falling of a hand.

“I waited until dawn.”

Dampier paused. The fire collapsed with a small red sigh. Rain streamed down the windows, and the tower appeared suspended in darkness.

“In the morning,” he resumed, “I rang for my servant and asked, with elaborate carelessness, whether anything had occurred next door.

“He stared at me.

“‘Miss Armitage died last night, sir.’

“Those were his words. There are sentences which divide existence as lightning divides a tree. Before them, one is a living shape; afterward, two dead fragments.

“I went to the house. Pride had vanished, but, like most cowards, it had retreated only when there was no longer anything to risk. Mrs. Armitage received me. She had aged twenty years in a night.

“I learned everything.

“The young man had been her physician and cousin. Evelyn had contracted a fever. The first signals I ignored were made before weakness confined her to bed. The letter I returned unopened contained only an appeal that I come to her, since she feared she had offended me unknowingly. Later she became delirious. In her delirium she spoke my name.

“On the final night she recovered consciousness. Her bed stood upon the opposite side of her chamber. She begged them to move it against the wall adjoining mine. The physician objected, but she implored him with such agitation that he consented. They carried her across the room. She lay there for nearly an hour, her face turned toward the wall.

“Shortly before midnight she lifted her hand.

“Her mother thought she was reaching for something and tried to support her. Evelyn shook her head. Three times she struck the plaster with her fingertips.

“Then she listened.

“She did not ask twice.

“When no answer came, she whispered, ‘He has forgotten.’

“She died a few minutes later.”

Dampier rose and went to the western wall. He placed his palm against it with the attitude of one taking an oath upon a tomb.

“I had not forgotten,” he said. “That was the horror. Forgetfulness would have been innocence beside what I did. I heard; I understood; I refused.

“I left Baltimore within the month. The house was sold. I travelled in Europe, studied theology, medicine, and every superstition by which man has attempted to corrupt death into a form of hope. I gave money to hospitals. I endowed a bed in her name, though not her full name, for even repentance retained its vanity. I sought reparation in acts that benefited everyone except the dead.

“Three years after her death, while I was staying in an old monastery near Ravenna, the signal came again.

“My chamber was on an upper floor. Beyond the wall lay open air and, far below, a courtyard. At midnight, while I read, three taps sounded beside my bed. I searched, questioned the monks, examined the masonry. Nothing explained them.

“That was the remembrance.

“Three years later, aboard a ship in the Indian Ocean, it returned. My cabin opened on the sea. During a dead calm, when not a rope stirred, three blows came from the outer hull, several feet above the water. The watch heard nothing. I did.

“That was the warning.

“And tonight—”

“The summons,” I said.

He inclined his head.

I endeavoured to oppose his conviction with arguments drawn from acoustics, nervous disorder, coincidence, and the unwholesome suggestiveness of old manuscripts. They sounded very well to me and had no effect upon him.

“At least,” I said, “if you believe the dead woman seeks an answer, answer her.”

A change came over his face. It was not hope, but the astonishment of a prisoner shown a door he has been too long in darkness to perceive.

He turned toward the wall.

With one trembling knuckle he struck three times.

Tap—tap—tap.

We waited.

Nothing replied.

He repeated the signal more loudly, then pressed his ear to the bricks.

“Evelyn,” he whispered.

The storm broke anew. Wind roared about the tower; rain assaulted the glass; the house groaned from cellar to roof. In that tumult I could distinguish no measured sound, but Dampier drew back as if an invisible mouth had spoken close beside him.

“What did you hear?”

He smiled, and the peace of that smile frightened me more than his terror.

“Nothing,” he said. “At last, nothing.”

It was then nearly two in the morning. I urged him to return with me to my hotel. He refused, gently but immovably. I offered to remain.

“No. There are vigils at which friendship is an intrusion.”

“You are determined to make melodrama of indigestion.”

“I am determined only to be here if she knocks again.”

I was angry because I was afraid. Yet his manner had become so composed that my apprehensions seemed an insult to him. I extracted a promise that he would send for me at daylight, and, after shaking his hand, descended to the hall.

At the door he said, “You once accused me of never completing anything.”

“I was young and occasionally right.”

“I think I shall complete this.”

The rain had diminished when my carriage moved away. Looking back from the road, I saw his lamp still burning in the tower, a solitary yellow point held aloft against the immense blackness. For a moment it seemed to me less a light in a habitation than a star already condemned to extinction.

At ten the next morning I returned.

The servant had found Dampier seated beside the western wall, his head resting against it, his right hand closed as though still prepared to knock. His face was calm. The physician spoke of failure of the heart, and perhaps the physician was right. Science has many names for the doors through which we pass, though it possesses no map of the country beyond them.

On the table lay a single sheet of paper. It bore neither testament nor farewell, but only three words:

**I have answered.**

Whether the signal we heard was produced by storm, by some concealed imperfection in the masonry, by the disordered expectation of my unhappy friend, or by an agency for which our philosophies have devised no respectable term, I do not pretend to determine. I know merely that I heard three measured taps upon a wall no living hand could reach.

And I know that Mohun Dampier, who had spent ten years listening for the dead, passed into the Unknown before dawn.

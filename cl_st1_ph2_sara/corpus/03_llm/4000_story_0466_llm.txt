We worked the *William and Mary*, did Tom and me, from Brentford up beyond Oxford when the water served, and down again with flour, bricks, timber, or whatever honest load was offered. She wasn’t a grand boat, nor so smart-painted as some, but her cabin was warm and dry, and her brass shone fit to blind you of a sunny morning, for I kept it so. Many’s the time I’ve said a woman may be poor and yet needn’t let the dirt get atop of her, though Tom would answer that a clean kettle boiled no quicker than a black one.

That was Tom’s way. He’d say summat contrary if only to see me turn round on him.

“Old woman,” he’d say, “you’re like a little tug—always puffin’, whether you’ve got a job to pull or not.”

“And you,” says I, “are like a rotten lock-gate. You’ll move when you must, and groan all the while.”

Then he’d laugh and take me round the waist, wet hands, muddy boots, and all, till I boxed his ears. We was happy, you see. Not rich, no; nor ever I was one to pretend we was. But there’s them as eats off silver with a cold heart at the table, and them as breaks one loaf between three and finds it sweet. We belonged to the second sort.

Our Mary was born aboard, in the little bunk under the shelf where Tom kept his tobacco. A wild night it was, with rain beatin’ on the cabin roof and the barge pullin’ at her ropes like a frightened horse. Tom had gone slippy for the midwife, and when he came back he’d brought the lock-keeper’s wife, a doctor’s boy, and an old man who’d only asked the road to Uxbridge.

“I thought one of ’em might be useful,” says he.

The old man wasn’t, but he sat by the stove and drank three cups of tea, so no harm come of it.

When Mary was put into Tom’s arms, such a small warm scrap with hair the colour of a new-hatched duck, he looked scared.

“Take her, Bess,” he whispered. “She’ll break.”

“Not she,” says I. “She’s your daughter.”

At that he put one great black finger into her fist, and she held it.

“Well,” he says, with his eyes all wet, “she’s got me fast enough.”

And she had, from that minute.

She grew up with the river under her, you might say. Before she could speak proper, she knew the cry of the moorhens, the bump of a lock, and the difference between rain on canvas and rain on water. Tom made her a little stool by the tiller, and there she’d sit solemn as a judge, with her bonnet strings flying. When she was five she fell in, and Tom went after her in his boots. When I’d got ’em both aboard, dripping over my clean floor, he says:

“Don’t scold the child, Bess. She was only seein’ if the Thames was deep.”

“And was it?” says I.

“Yes, mother,” says Mary, coughing. “Very.”

She was always quick, our Mary, and gentle too. An aunt of mine, who’d married above us and had no child of her own, took a fancy to her and left two hundred pounds for her when she come of age. Two hundred pounds! It sounded like a king’s ransom to us. The money was put in a respectable bank, and the papers tied up in my Bible, where I thought no harm could reach ’em.

My aunt had said Mary must be educated, and so, when she was eleven, we sent her to a school kept by two sisters near Reading. It near broke Tom’s heart. He carried her little box ashore and came back without speakin’, and that night he sat smoking with his face turned from me.

“You wanted her to learn,” says I.

“I know.”

“She can’t learn book-work steering past barges.”

“I know.”

“She’ll come home for holidays.”

“I know.”

“Well, then?”

He struck his pipe out and says, fierce-like, “A man may know a knife’s got to cut, but he needn’t enjoy the feel of it.”

I knew better than to answer.

I’d had some schooling myself through a charity lady, enough to read my Bible and write a plain hand, which is why I can tell a printed notice from a painted lie. Mary learned more in a month than I had in years. Yet she didn’t come back with her nose in the air, as I’d feared. She’d sit in the cabin with her sleeves rolled up, peeling potatoes, and read aloud to Tom from the newspaper while he mended rope. If there was a word he didn’t know, he’d cough as if smoke had gone wrong and say he hadn’t been listening.

At seventeen she came home for good, tall and straight, with grey eyes and a soft brown plait nearly to her waist. I thought the sun rose special for her, and Tom thought so too. We called her our Pretty, though she’d blush and say she was too old.

It was that summer Bill Jarvis began comin’ along of us.

His father kept the smithy near a wharf we often used. Bill was a handsome lad, broad in the shoulder, with black curls and teeth too white for a working man. He could sing, and he had a laugh that made you laugh whether you’d heard the joke or not. Tom liked him at first.

“There’s strength in that lad,” he says, watching Bill lift a sack.

“There’s vanity too,” says I.

“Well, strength’ll carry a sack. Vanity won’t.”

Mary said nothing, but her needle went into the same place three times.

Bill found reasons to visit. He’d bring a new hook for Tom, a ribbon for me, or a book he’d borrowed for Mary, though I noticed he never knew what the book was about. Then one evening I come up from the cabin and see the two of ’em by the towpath hedge, her hand in his.

“Mary!” says I.

She jumped, but Bill stood firm enough.

“Mrs. Larkin,” he says, “I love her, and I mean to marry her if she’ll have me.”

“If!” says Mary, and laughed.

Tom was told, and sat an hour with Bill over ale. What passed between ’em I never rightly knew, except that Tom asked if he drank, and Bill said only sociable; if he gambled, and Bill said not beyond a little sport; and if he’d ever struck a woman, at which Bill looked offended.

“If you make her cry,” Tom told him, “I’ll alter the shape of your face.”

“Tom!” says I.

“I’m only explainin’ the family customs.”

So they was engaged. The wedding was set for May, a week after Mary’s eighteenth birthday, when the two hundred pounds would be hers. Bill talked of renting part of the smithy and setting up for himself. He’d have Mary in a brick house with a parlour and a little yard. Mary laughed and planned curtains. I stitched linen till my fingers went stiff, and Tom built a cradle, though I scolded him for being previous.

“A cradle’s useful furniture,” says he.

“For them as needs one.”

“All married folk hopes to, don’t they?”

“You keep to your timber,” says I.

The day before the wedding we stopped near the town where the bank had its chief office, meanin’ to draw a little of Mary’s money for the furnishing. Tom washed, shaved, and put on his good coat. I wore my purple bonnet, and Mary stayed aboard because Bill was coming to walk with her.

We found a crowd outside the bank.

There was men shouting, women crying, and one old gentleman beating the locked door with his cane. A notice was pasted inside the glass. I read it once and couldn’t understand. I read it twice, and my knees went queer.

Payment suspended. The bank had failed. The directors was gone, and the money with ’em.

Tom pushed through and hammered on the door.

“My girl’s money’s in there!” he shouted. “Open up!”

A constable took his arm. Tom shook him off, and for a moment I thought there’d be prison before wedding. I clung to his sleeve.

“Come away, Tom. You can’t fetch money out of an empty room.”

He turned to me with a face I’d never seen.

“Bess—every penny?”

“Every penny.”

We walked back without words. The town was bright with May sun, and the skylarks was singing fit to split themselves. I hated ’em for it. How dared the birds sing when our Pretty’s little fortune was stolen?

Mary was by the cabin door in her white dress, trying the fall of the skirt. Bill stood near her.

“What is it?” she says.

I told her. She went pale, but she put her arms round me.

“Never mind, mother. It was only money.”

Only money! Ah, that’s easy said by one who hasn’t learned how money shows the hidden seams in people.

Bill’s face had changed.

“All lost?” he asks.

“All,” says Tom. “But you’ve got your trade, and Mary’s got hands. You’ll do.”

“Yes,” says Bill. “Of course.”

He left soon after, sayin’ his father must be told.

Next morning Mary dressed in our cabin. She wore a plain white gown and a little white bonnet, and never bride looked sweeter. Tom couldn’t speak when he saw her. He only kissed her forehead and went outside to wipe the brass that was clean already.

We reached the chapel. Bill’s father was there, red and uneasy. The minister was there. Two girls from Mary’s school was there, whispering behind their flowers.

Bill wasn’t.

“We’re early,” says Mary.

We waited.

The clock struck ten, and then the quarter, and then the half. We waited and we waited. Every footstep made her turn. At eleven, Bill’s father went out. He come back alone.

“He’s gone,” he says.

“Gone where?” asks Tom.

“Enlisted. Took the King’s shilling last night and marched at dawn.”

“No,” says Mary.

Nobody answered.

“No, no, no,” she says, very low. Then she stood up straight. “Take me home, father.”

She walked through all them staring people, pale as a snowdrop, with her flowers held close. Not one tear did she shed till we was aboard and the cabin door shut. Then she tore off the white bonnet and fell against my breast.

Tom went mad, or a-nigh it. He seized the iron bar we used for the hatches.

“I’ll follow him. Soldier or no soldier, I’ll kill him.”

“You’ll do no such thing,” says I, holding him, though he dragged me two steps.

“He’s shamed my girl!”

“He has. And will you shame her worse by being hanged?”

“I swear I’ll—”

“Don’t swear, Tom!” I cried. “Don’t put the Lord’s name to murder.”

He looked at Mary. She had dropped to her knees, one arm round my waist.

“Father,” she says, “stay with me. I need you.”

The bar fell from his hand.

He knelt beside her, big rough Tom, and gathered her up as if she was the baby born in the storm.

“I’ll stay, my precious lamb,” he says. “I’ll stay.”

It was some weeks after that Mary told me about the child.

She stood washing a cup, though it had been washed twice, and says, “Mother, I’ve summat to say.”

I knew before she spoke. Women do.

I sat down because my legs wouldn’t trust me, and for one wicked minute I thought of neighbours’ tongues, and the chapel women, and all that cruel thing called respectability, which often means only that your sorrow has been kept out of sight.

Then Mary says, “Don’t look so, mother. I couldn’t bear it if you turned from me.”

I held out my arms.

“Come here, my girl.”

She came, shaking all over.

When Tom knew, he walked once round the cabin and hit his fist against the wall till the tin plates rattled.

“I ought to have killed him.”

“No,” says Mary. “You ought to love me.”

He stared, then took her face between his hands.

“That I do,” he says. “That I always shall. And the little ’un too. He’s got no blame on him.”

We moved from the district before Mary began to show. I’m not sayin’ we ought to have cared what people said, but there’s courage and there’s making a wound stand in the wind. Our little boy was born in winter. Mary called him Tommy, after his grandfather, and Tom pretended objection for near half an hour.

“One Tom aboard’s enough,” says he.

But that evening I found him leaning over the cradle.

“Little Tom,” he whispered, “you’ve got your granddad fast enough.”

A year went by. Mary got her colour again, though there was a stillness in her now. She never spoke Bill’s name. If soldiers passed on the road, she’d go below and find work for her hands.

Then came the night Bill found us.

We was moored near a lonely stretch, with reeds one side and fields the other. Rain had been falling since dusk. Tom and me sat by the stove, Mary asleep with the child in the forward bunk, when summat thudded on deck.

Tom took the lamp and climbed out. I heard scuffling, a curse, and then a voice gasping:

“Hide me! For God’s sake!”

Tom came down backward, dragging a man by the collar. The lamp shone on wet scarlet cloth, mud, and a white desperate face.

It was Bill Jarvis.

Tom knew him the same second. His eyes shone like lanterns.

“You,” he says.

Bill looked from him to me, and all hope went out of him.

“I didn’t know whose boat—”

Tom’s fist struck him before I could move. Bill fell against the table.

Mary woke and pushed the curtain back. She saw him. She made no sound, only snatched up the baby and held him close.

Bill stared at the child.

“Is that—?”

“Don’t you speak,” says Mary. “Don’t you dare.”

Then we heard distant voices and boots on the towpath.

Bill gripped Tom’s sleeve.

“They’re after me. There was a fight. A man went down, and they say I killed him. I ran. I was drunk—I don’t rightly know. Hide me, Mr. Larkin. Only till they pass.”

Tom lifted him by the tunic.

“Why shouldn’t I hand you over?”

“You should,” Bill choked. “But I’m askin’ mercy.”

“Mercy? You left my girl in a white dress with the town laughing at her.”

“I know.”

“You left your child before he was born.”

“I didn’t know!”

“And would that have held you if you had?”

Bill said nothing.

The boots came nearer. A voice called, “Boat there!”

Tom and Bill looked at each other. I thought of justice. I thought of Mary crying in my arms and Tom’s fist bloodied against the wall. I confess I wanted the soldiers to take Bill, and if the rope waited after, I told myself it was none of mine.

But Tom pushed him toward the coal locker beneath the floor.

“Get in.”

“Tom!” says I.

“Look slippy!”

Bill crawled into the black space. Tom dropped the board, kicked a mat over it, and sat down.

Three soldiers boarded, led by a sergeant with rain dripping from his moustache.

“Seen a man pass?” he asks. “Private Jarvis.”

“No,” says Tom.

It was a lie, plain and ready. I, who’d scolded Tom for missing chapel and sleeping through sermons, stood amazed.

“What’s he done?” Tom asks.

“Only murder and desertion,” says the sergeant.

“Oh, is that all?” says Tom. “I thought it might be serious.”

The sergeant looked sharp, but Tom’s face was innocent as a baby’s backside.

They searched the deck, peered into the bunks, and asked Mary questions. She sat with little Tommy in her arms and answered steady. One soldier put his foot on the board above Bill’s head. I could hear my own heart.

At last they went.

Tom waited till the footsteps died, then opened the locker. Bill crawled out black with coal dust and near fainting.

“There,” says Tom. “I’ve paid good for evil, which is more Christianity than I generally manage before breakfast.”

“Why?” Bill asks.

Tom’s face hardened.

“Because if I gave you up with hate in me, your wickedness would make me wicked too. Don’t mistake me—I’d still enjoy knocking your head off.”

I began to cry. I’d always thought myself the religious one, because I read Scripture of a Sunday and Tom sometimes spent chapel time oiling a pulley. Yet there he stood, rough-tongued and angry, and nearer the Lord than ever I’d been.

Bill slept by the stove. At dawn Tom gave him cold pork, bread, and a mug of ale. Bill reached for the ale, then pushed it away.

“What will you do?” asks Tom.

“Get farther off.”

“And then?”

“Keep going.”

“For the rest of your life?”

“If the man’s dead, I’ll hang.”

“Happen he ain’t dead.”

“They said murder.”

“Soldiers say plenty. Go back and learn the truth.”

Bill laughed bitter. “You hide me and now tell me to surrender.”

“I hid a frightened man from being hunted in the dark. I won’t help a coward run forever.”

At that word Bill flinched. Mary, standing by the bunk, says coldly, “He knows how to run.”

Bill looked at her and at the baby.

“Mary, I’m sorry.”

“Be sorry somewhere else.”

He bent his head.

Tom took him ashore by a little path. Before they parted he gave Bill money—only a few shillings, for we had no pounds to throw—and told him to report to his regiment.

“If the man’s dead,” says Bill, “will you think me a fool?”

“If you face what you’ve done, I’ll think you a man for the first time.”

We learned later the injured soldier hadn’t died at all. His skull was hard as hard, and the bottle had broken before the head did. Bill was charged with desertion and assault, but there was evidence the other man had begun the quarrel, and Bill’s surrender counted for him. He served punishment, severe enough by all accounts, but he wasn’t shot nor transported.

Months after, he came to us in plain clothes.

Mary was mending little Tommy’s shirt. Bill stood outside the cabin, thinner than before, with a scar over one eyebrow.

“I’ve been discharged,” he says. “I came to ask Mary to marry me.”

Tom said nothing. Nor did I. This was Mary’s matter.

She laid down the shirt.

“Why?”

“To make you an honest woman.”

Her face went red, then white.

“Honest?”

“I didn’t mean—”

“Yes, you did. You think a ring from you can give me honesty. I was honest when you left me. I was honest when I bore your child. I am honest now.”

“I want to do right.”

“You wanted my money once.”

“I was a coward.”

“You are right there.”

“I’ve changed.”

“Not enough, if you come offering marriage like a plaster for my good name.”

He dropped to his knees. “Mary, I love you.”

She looked at him a long while, and I saw her hand tremble. But her voice didn’t.

“Love that comes after shame and fear is poor goods, Bill Jarvis. My father stood by me. My mother held me. You ran. I won’t marry a man I cannot honour.”

He rose slowly.

“May I see the boy?”

“No.”

That seemed cruel to me, but I didn’t cross her. Bill turned to Tom.

“You told me to become a man.”

“A man bears hearin’ no,” says Tom.

Bill nodded and went away.

For near two years we heard little of him. Once a carrier said Bill worked at an ironworks upriver. Another time a lock hand told us he’d seen him pull a boy from beneath a wagon. There was talk he’d signed the pledge against drink and kept it, which surprised Tom.

“Bill without ale,” he says, “is like a barge without water. I’d like proof before I try sailing on it.”

Mary pretended not to hear these bits of news, but she always heard.

Then one autumn morning a message found us at Oxford. A boy from the infirmary came running to the wharf.

“Is there a Thomas Larkin here? A patient named Jarvis asks for him. He’s mortal bad with typhoid.”

Tom looked at me.

“We’ll go,” says I.

Mary was sitting by the cabin window.

“He didn’t ask for me,” she says.

“No,” I answered.

Her needle snapped between her fingers.

At the infirmary the smell near took my breath—soap, sickness, damp sheets, and death. Bill lay in a narrow bed, thin as a skeleton, his black curls clipped away. His eyes seemed too large for his face.

“Mr. Larkin,” he whispered. “Mrs. Larkin.”

Tom stood awkward by the bed.

“Well, lad. You’ve made a poor job of keeping healthy.”

Bill gave the ghost of a laugh.

“They say I may die.”

“Doctors say plenty.”

“This one looked cheerful when he said it.”

“Then he’s a fool or a doctor, and happen both.”

Bill reached out. Tom took his hand.

“I wanted you to know,” Bill says, “that what you done that night wasn’t wasted. You had reason to hate me, and you saved me. I couldn’t get away from it. Every time I went to take drink, I saw you opening that locker. Every time I thought of lying or running, I heard you call me coward.”

“I’ve called many men worse.”

“But I knew you was right. I gave up drink. I worked. I paid my debts. I’ve sent money regular for the boy, though Mary returned it.”

“She didn’t tell us,” says I.

“I tried to be the sort of man you might have been willing to give her to. Too late, I know. But I tried.”

Tom’s mouth worked, and he looked toward the window.

Bill went on, faint as wind under a door.

“I’ve got savings. Not much. They’re for Mary and the child. And I want—if she’d consent—I want to marry her before I die. Not to make her honest. She taught me better than that. To make what amends I can, and to give the boy my name, if she wants it. I won’t ask her to forgive me.”

“You just have,” says Tom softly.

“Yes. I suppose I have.”

We went back to the barge. Mary met us on deck, and the minute she saw my face she says:

“He’s dying.”

“The doctor thinks so.”

She gripped the rail.

“What did he want?”

I told her every word. She listened without moving. Then she turned away and looked across the water, where yellow leaves floated past.

“Mary,” says Tom, “don’t go from pity. Pity’s warm for an hour and cold all the rest of life.”

“There won’t be a rest of his life.”

“Happen there will. Don’t bargain on death; it cheats.”

She covered her face with both hands.

“Oh, father,” she says, “I’ve loved him all the time.”

I knew it. A mother knows some things and waits for the child to find ’em.

“I hated him,” she sobbed, “and despised him, and loved him. When I sent him away, I thought my heart would tear. But I couldn’t take him only because he asked. I had to know he’d stand when no one held him.”

“And now?” Tom asks.

“Now he’s stood.”

She went to the infirmary that afternoon, wearing no fine dress, only her brown gown and bonnet. Little Tommy walked between us, holding one finger of mine and one of Tom’s. When Bill saw them, tears ran sideways into his pillow.

Mary sat by him.

“Bill.”

“Mary, you shouldn’t have come if you don’t wish it.”

“I wish it.”

He stared.

“I’ll marry you,” she says. “Not because you’re dying, and not because I need honesty from you. I’ll marry you because I love you, and because you’ve learned not to run.”

He tried to lift himself, but had no strength. Mary bent and put her cheek against his.

“My Pretty,” he whispered.

“No,” she says, crying and laughing together. “That’s father’s name for me.”

“Then—my Mary.”

The chaplain married ’em by the bed. Tom gave Mary away, though he said afterward he’d done no such thing and merely lent her. I held little Tommy, who grew tired of solemnity and asked loudly when there would be cake.

Bill slipped near death that night. Mary stayed with him, her hand in his, talking of the barge, the river, the smithy he might have one day, and all the common tomorrows he believed was lost. Whether medicine saved him, or youth, or the Lord, I can’t tell you. But I’ve always believed Mary’s voice tied him to life stronger than any rope Tom ever spliced.

He began to mend.

Slow, mind you. There was no jumping from a deathbed into happiness, as songs make out. He had to learn to sit, then stand, then walk three steps without shaking. Mary fed him broth with a spoon. Little Tommy watched and says:

“Is that my father?”

Bill looked at Mary.

“Yes,” she answered. “That’s your father.”

The child considered him.

“He’s very small.”

Bill laughed till he coughed, and that laugh was the first living sound we’d heard from him.

In time Bill took work at a smithy near the river. He never went back to drink, not even sociable, and when trouble came he stayed to meet it. Mary went with him to a little brick house with a parlour and a yard, much like the one she’d planned at seventeen, except the curtains was blue instead of yellow and the cradle Tom had made stood by the fire.

They had sorrows afterward, of course. Everybody does. A happy ending ain’t a door shut against grief. It only means folk face what comes with hands joined instead of backs turned. Bill and Mary had more children, and little Tommy grew broad in the shoulder like his father and steady in the heart like his grandfather.

As for Tom, he never spoke much of the night he hid Bill. If praised, he’d say:

“I only put a fool in a coal hole.”

But I knew, and Bill knew. Mercy changed all of us. It changed Bill from a coward into a man. It changed Mary’s pride into forgiveness without breaking it. And it changed me too, for I’d once thought goodness wore Sunday clothes and spoke out of a book. Afterward I knew it might smell of tar and river mud, tell a lie to angry soldiers, and offer cold pork to the man it had most cause to hate.

My Tom, he wasn’t a saint as churches paint ’em. He swore when rope snapped, drank more than suited me on market days, and never could keep his boots off a clean floor. But when the great test came, he opened the locker instead of his fist.

And if that didn’t bring him near the Lord, then I don’t know what would.

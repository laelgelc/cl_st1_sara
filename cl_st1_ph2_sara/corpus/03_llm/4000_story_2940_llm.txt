## HUNDRED AND ONE

At half-past five on an August afternoon, when the western windows of the Clayton House blazed like polished copper, a long, uncertain shadow came wavering up the white road from the pine woods.

The shadow arrived first. It touched the hitching-post, climbed the hotel steps, stretched itself thinly across the veranda, and paused at the shining toes of Colonel Dabney, who was seated there explaining the Mexican War to three ladies who had heard it explained every summer for nine years. After the shadow came a pair of boots. Then came a man.

The boots deserve precedence because they possessed more immediate character than their owner. They were immense, warped, yellow with Georgia dust, and curved at the ends like two canoes which had met adversity and taken to travelling by land. The right boot leaned outward with a rakish air; the left advanced submissively, as if apologising for the liberty. Above them hung trousers of no recognised colour, a coat whose pockets bulged with mysterious possessions, and two strips of rawhide doing the public duty of suspenders. At the summit of this melancholy architecture appeared a narrow face, a thatch of dusty grey hair, and two faded eyes which looked first at every doorway and then behind it.

The man might have been forty. His hair said sixty; his stooping shoulders, seventy; his frightened movements, a hundred years at least.

He halted below the steps.

Colonel Dabney stopped the Mexican War at Buena Vista.

“Well?” said Mr. Clayton, proprietor of the hotel.

The stranger removed a battered hat. His fingers, instead of falling at his side, remained spread against the crown, as though awaiting orders.

“Me?” he asked.

“You,” said Mr. Clayton. “What do you want?”

“What I want? Well, I’ll tell you. I ain’t pertickler. I kin tote wood, scour pots, curry horses, peel taters, wait on table if folks don’t mind the looks of me. I don’t reckin they would, much, after they got used to ’em.”

The three ladies examined him and plainly doubted whether civilisation could sustain so severe a trial.

“Where are you from?” asked Mr. Clayton.

“From Georgy.”

“That is a large address.”

“Large? Yes, sir. Georgy’s tol’able large.”

“What part?”

The man’s pale eyes travelled to the road, the trees, the kitchen wing, and finally the hitching-post. His shoulders contracted.

“Different parts,” he said. “I been movin’.”

At this there was a slight ironical elevation of Colonel Dabney’s eyebrows. The stranger observed it. He observed everything that suggested suspicion, pursuit, or punishment. The delicate intelligence which discovers kindness had almost been beaten out of him; but the instinct which detects danger remained terribly alive.

“What is your name?” Mr. Clayton continued.

“Baker.”

“First name?”

“Me?”

“Yes.”

“I don’t have much use for the first part. They mostly called me Baker. Some called me Hunder’d’n One.”

Colonel Dabney lowered his newspaper.

“What extraordinary baptism was that?”

“Baptism?” Baker repeated. “Well, I’ll tell you. It warn’t no baptism. It was—”

A carriage turned into the drive. Its wheels struck a stone, and the driver cracked his whip.

The effect upon Baker was appalling. He dropped his hat, flung both arms around the hitching-post, and flattened his body against it. Then, with a motion practised too often to be mistaken, he dragged his coat over his head and exposed his back.

“Ready, Cap’n,” he said. “I’m ready. Don’t lay it over the old cut, if you kin help it. It ain’t healed pertickler good.”

No one spoke.

The carriage rolled up merrily; two children laughed within it; the whip cracked once more over the horses. But on the veranda the summer afternoon seemed suddenly to have grown cold.

Mr. Clayton descended the steps.

“Baker,” he said gently, “no one is going to strike you.”

Baker’s face appeared from beneath the coat.

“Ain’t this Friday?”

“It is Monday.”

“Monday?” He considered this with painful care. “Then I got mixed. Fridays was the day. Every Friday, ’less the boss was off fishin’. Sometimes he done it Thursday, account of wantin’ Friday clear.”

Mr. Clayton put a hand upon his shoulder. Baker shrank so violently that the hand was withdrawn.

“Who whipped you?”

“The State.”

“The State of Georgia?”

“Yes, sir. State’s got a mighty strong arm.”

His coat had fallen low enough to reveal what lay beneath. Colonel Dabney, who had seen men wounded in battle and had acquired the veteran’s privilege of speaking lightly about blood, did not speak lightly then. The man’s back was a map drawn in raised and twisted scars. There were old ridges, smooth and white; newer seams, purple at the edges; crossing lines; knots; and one broad, angry wound not wholly closed. It was a fine piece of mosaic, if one may employ so civilised a word for so barbarous a workmanship.

One of the ladies began to cry.

Baker looked at her in alarm. “I ain’t done nothin’ to her.”

“No,” said Mr. Clayton. “Put on your coat.”

“I was jess fixin’ for it.”

“There will be no whipping here.”

Baker slowly covered himself. He still looked doubtful, like a dog to whom kindness is only another trick of the stick.

Mr. Clayton conducted him around the house, not through the lobby, where brass, mirrors, chandeliers and social prejudice were all kept in a state of brilliant polish. The cook gave him food. He ate standing until she compelled him to sit; then he held his plate in both hands, glancing about as though a keeper might reclaim it.

That night he slept in the small room above the carriage house. At least a bed was provided for him. At midnight the stable boy found him curled upon the floor beneath it.

“Bed’s too soft,” Baker explained. “Makes a man sink. I don’t keer for sinkin’ where I can’t see what’s comin’.”

In the morning he reported for work before any task had been assigned to him.

Thus Baker, otherwise Hundred and One, entered the service of the Clayton House.

No inquiry was immediately made into his history. Mr. Clayton possessed that uncommon delicacy which understands that curiosity may become cruelty merely by putting on a clean collar. He saw that the man had escaped from some penal establishment; he suspected that officers might come; and he knew perfectly well what the law expected of a respectable citizen. Yet respectability, though an excellent coat for public wear, should not always be permitted to button itself over the heart.

Mr. Clayton therefore gave Baker a broom.

The broom was a sceptre. It appointed him to a kingdom of corridors, verandas, steps and paths; and Baker ruled that dusty realm with a zeal beyond constitutional restraint. He swept before breakfast, after breakfast and, if not prevented, during breakfast. He carried trunks until the hotel porter, who had previously believed labour a form of personal insult, grew anxious for his position. He fetched water, split wood, blacked stoves, peeled vegetables and removed spiders with a severity that suggested former official relations with vermin.

His boots, meanwhile, underwent moral improvement.

At first they entered the house as outlaws, scattering red earth and making no apology. Under the influence of soap, grease and occupation, they became chastened. The right boot lost its swagger; the left acquired confidence; both submitted to blacking, though the substance collected in their cracks like ink in an ancient manuscript. On Sundays Baker polished them until they shone at the extreme points, and they stood beneath his trousers with the gratified solemnity of two country gentlemen admitted, after long exclusion, into genteel society.

There was comedy in Baker, but none of it was intentional. Ask him whether he had carried a trunk to Number Twelve, and he would answer:

“Me?”

“Yes, you.”

“Trunk?”

“The trunk belonging to Number Twelve.”

“Well, I’ll tell you. I carried one trunk, two boxes, a bird cage an’ a lady with a sprained ankle. Which one was Number Twelve I couldn’t say pertickler.”

He called the corridor the “curryder,” the chandelier the “shandy-dear,” and the proprietor “Cap’n,” despite repeated correction. When asked whether he preferred apple pie or peach, he gazed upon both with the moral agony of a judge required to condemn one innocent prisoner.

“Pie?” he would say. “Well, I’ll tell you. Apple fills a man. Peach makes him wish he had more. I reckin I’ll take both an’ keep from showin’ favour.”

The guests laughed at him. At first Baker laughed too, a fraction of a second late, having learned that when others displayed their teeth it was prudent to display his own. Gradually he discovered that this laughter did not precede a blow. Then his smile ceased to be defensive. It came slowly, awkwardly, almost shamefacedly, but it belonged to him.

Children made the discovery first. Children and dogs are the careless aristocracy of affection; they seldom demand references. Little Marion Bell sat beside Baker on the kitchen step and watched him mend a basket. Tommy Griggs rode upon his shoulders. A solemn hound named Washington followed him from woodpile to pantry, convinced that those enormous pockets must ultimately justify themselves.

The pockets did. Baker’s left contained string, nails, a broken comb, three buttons, a rabbit’s foot and a piece of stale corn bread. The right held a knife without a blade, a whetstone without a knife, two peppermint sticks reserved for children, and a tiny wooden bird which he had carved in prison.

“What bird is it?” Marion asked.

“Mockin’-bird.”

“It doesn’t look like one.”

“No,” Baker admitted. “Never had no bird to set by. I made him from rememberin’ the noise.”

A mocking-bird sang every morning from the magnolia by the veranda. Baker would sometimes stop with a bucket in his hand and listen. At such moments his face lost its habitual confusion. The song appeared to enter some locked chamber within him and move among the dead things there.

“Did you have mocking-birds where you came from?” Mr. Clayton once asked.

“Me? Yes, sir. One used to sing outside the stockade.”

“The stockade?”

Baker’s hand went instantly to his ankle. He rubbed it through the coarse cloth.

“They ain’t got it on me now,” he murmured. “I knows that. I knows it right smart. Only sometimes my leg don’t.”

It was then that Mr. Clayton understood Baker’s peculiar walk. Every seventh or eighth step he lifted his left foot too high and set it down with care, as though clearing a chain. Freedom had removed the iron but had neglected its shadow. An invisible ball dragged behind him through the hallways of the Clayton House, over the bright lawns, even into sleep.

Later, in fragments, the story came.

Fifteen years before, a young man had been convicted of killing a storekeeper during a quarrel near Macon. His name was Baker. There had been a brother who disappeared, a witness who hesitated, and certain evidence convenient to men anxious for a verdict. The prisoner declared his innocence until declaration became useless. He was sent away, numbered, chained and hired out with other convicts to men who measured justice by the day’s profit.

“Why did they call you Hundred and One?” asked Mr. Clayton.

“Cell,” Baker answered. “Convict camp first, then prison. Hunder’d’n One was painted over the door. After a while a man gets to match his door.”

“Did you escape?”

“Escape?” Baker glanced toward the drive. “Well, I’ll tell you. A storm washed out the lower fence. Guard went to turn the mules. My legs started. I went along ’cause they was used to carryin’ me.”

He had been free six weeks.

Mr. Clayton wrote letters discreetly. One went to an attorney in Atlanta; one to a former judge; another to a man who knew the governor’s secretary. He received answers full of caution, procedure and official delay. The law, having spent fifteen years crushing Baker, now required time to determine whether it had done so regularly.

Meanwhile kindness performed its quieter appeal.

Good food filled the hollows in Baker’s cheeks. Sleep came by degrees. He began in the bed for an hour, fled beneath it after a nightmare, returned at dawn, and one triumphant night remained between sheets until morning. The raw wound on his back closed. His shoulders lifted. He learned to pass the hitching-post without looking at it.

There were relapses. Once, when a guest complained loudly about a missing umbrella, Baker disappeared. They found him behind the ash shed with his wrists crossed, waiting to be tied.

“I never seen his umbrella,” he said. “But if somebody’s got to be it, I reckin I kin.”

Another time the kitchen door slammed while he carried dishes. He dropped the tray and fell upon his knees.

Yet no one whipped him. No one fastened iron upon his ankle. No overseer counted his mouthfuls or made sport of his wounds. Repetition, which had taught terror, slowly taught peace. A day without cruelty became a week; a week, a month; and the calm consciousness of personal safety, which ordinary men inherit without gratitude, entered Baker’s mind like a shy and astonished guest.

By the middle of September he had grown almost cheerful. His boots reflected the change. They no longer crept along the baseboards but strode through the lobby on legitimate business. The right one resumed a modest rakishness; the left kept pace without apology. They had been resoled by the village cobbler, and Baker regarded this event as a species of resurrection.

“Reckin they’ll last me till I’m free,” he said.

“You are free now,” Marion told him.

Baker considered.

“Little free,” he said. “Not paper free. Paper’s what tells folks whether a man’s his own self.”

The season had been successful. The Clayton House was full from cellar to cupola: planters and their families, commercial men, widows in black silk, invalids in shawls, young ladies seeking husbands, young gentlemen avoiding occupations, and children who pursued one another through the curryder in defiance of architecture and parental fatigue.

No one noticed the shadow that came with Mr. Julius Peavey.

He arrived from the coast on a warm Thursday evening, complaining of the railway, the dust, the supper and mankind. He was a stout merchant with a yellow face and a conviction that wealth should protect its owner from inconvenience. At dinner he ate heavily. Before midnight he was seized with cramps.

Dr. Hale, a guest of the hotel, was summoned. He found Peavey collapsed, sweating, frightened and violently ill. The physician asked where he had travelled, frowned at the answer, and ordered the room cleared.

Baker had brought the hot water. He stood near the bed and looked once at the patient.

The basin fell from his hand.

“Cholery!”

The word did not sound like an opinion. It struck the room like a pistol shot.

Dr. Hale wheeled upon him. “Be quiet!”

“Yes, sir,” Baker whispered. But he did not take his eyes from Peavey.

“What do you know about cholera?”

“Me?”

“Yes, you.”

Baker’s confusion vanished. His shoulders straightened.

“Seen it at the camp. Fifty men took it. Thirty-two was dead by Sunday. Water’s got to be boiled. Sick folks kept apart. Clothes burned or soaked. Lime in the privies. Don’t let ’em crowd. Don’t let ’em get afeard.”

Dr. Hale stared at him.

Mr. Clayton, summoned from his room, entered in dressing gown and slippers. “Is it cholera?”

The physician hesitated. Respectable science, when surprised at midnight, sometimes requires several minutes to arrive where bitter experience has already been waiting.

“I cannot yet be certain.”

Baker bent over Peavey, felt his hands, then examined the water pitcher.

“I’m certain,” he said.

There are moments when authority passes from one man to another without vote, title or ceremony. It passed then.

By two o’clock a plan had been formed. The disease must be isolated; the hotel must be emptied without spreading panic through the village; wagons must carry the guests to a farm three miles inland; bedding must be destroyed; water guarded; attendants chosen.

To announce cholera in a crowded hotel is to throw fire into dry grass. Mr. Clayton therefore informed the guests that a defect in the drains required immediate closure. The explanation might have succeeded had Mrs. Colonel Dabney not heard a chambermaid sobbing upon the stairs. Within ten minutes the word was loose.

Panic began in silk dressing gowns and descended socially to nightshirts.

Doors opened. Bells rang. Trunks appeared in corridors. One gentleman rushed down carrying two hatboxes and leaving his wife behind. A lady fainted upon learning that another lady had fainted. Colonel Dabney took command of the Mexican War once more and ordered everybody to remain perfectly calm in a voice that shook three windowpanes.

Through this confusion moved Baker.

The man who could scarcely decide between two pies now decided the movements of a hundred people. He stationed wagons, separated the well from the exposed, marked baggage, carried children, soothed servants and forbade anyone to drink from the pump. His speech remained humble, but his knowledge admitted no argument.

“No, ma’am, you can’t go back for the green dress.”

“But it cost eighty dollars!”

“Cholery don’t know prices.”

“My jewels are in that room!”

“Then they’ll have the room to theirselves.”

“Put me down!” cried a portly gentleman whom Baker had lifted bodily from the staircase after he refused to move.

“Yes, sir. Soon’s I get you outdoors.”

He bore trunks two at a time. He carried old Mrs. Vale wrapped in a quilt. He returned for a caged parrot, not because the bird deserved rescue—it had spent the summer insulting him—but because Marion was crying. He led horses made frantic by shouting. He did the work of fifty men—a hundred men, said those who afterward enlarged the story, as gratitude and shame commonly enlarge what they failed to assist.

His boots were magnificent. The right commanded; the left obeyed; together they pounded the veranda, the road, the stairs, and the long curryder with a purposeful thunder. Their new soles were scarred, their polish vanished, but they had risen above gentility into usefulness.

By sunrise most of the guests were gone.

Five remained sick.

Peavey lay nearest death. Two chambermaids had been seized, then a child and an elderly travelling salesman. Dr. Hale worked without rest. Baker boiled water, mixed salt and sugar, warmed blankets, cleaned rooms, burned fouled linen and compelled small quantities of liquid between clenched teeth. What he had learned in horror he now employed in mercy.

At the camp, sick men had been abandoned on filthy straw. Water had been withheld to stop vomiting. Guards stood outside with guns, fearful that disease might accomplish what chains could not prevent. Baker had watched men die calling for mothers, wives and God; and because no physician came, the prisoners had gathered scraps of knowledge from survival itself.

“Keep him warm,” Baker told the doctor. “Little drinks, often. Don’t pour too much.”

“I know,” said Dr. Hale sharply.

“Yes, sir.”

An hour later the physician followed his advice.

Mr. Peavey survived the morning. One chambermaid began to rally. The child worsened, and Baker remained beside him, rubbing his blue hands.

“You will take it,” Mr. Clayton warned.

“Me?”

“You must rest.”

“Rest? Well, I’ll tell you, Cap’n. Them folks ain’t got time for me to rest.”

“You have worked eighteen hours.”

Baker glanced at the child. “He ain’t been alive but eight years. Reckin I can spare him eighteen.”

That night Dr. Hale staggered in the hall. His face had altered. He gripped the banister and whispered the word he had once been reluctant to speak.

Baker caught him before he fell.

The physician became the sixth patient, and the only trained medical man within reach was now helpless. A messenger rode for the county doctor, but roads were bad and fear travelled faster than horses. Baker took charge.

He had no empty exaltation concerning courage. He did not regard himself as noble. Heroism, in his mind, was merely the next necessary thing, and necessity had governed him too long to be disobeyed. He passed from bed to bed with a basin, spoon and blanket. He knew the stages of collapse; he knew when to insist and when to wait; above all, he was not afraid to touch the sick.

Mrs. Clayton remained to help him. Her husband carried water and tended fires. Two servants, after fleeing as far as the gate, returned in shame. Together they laboured through a second night while the epidemic sat like a black bird upon the roof.

By dawn the child slept naturally. Peavey asked for water in his ordinary, disagreeable voice, which was welcomed as music. The travelling salesman opened his eyes. Dr. Hale’s pulse strengthened.

Baker was washing a cup when the first cramp seized him.

He set down the cup carefully.

Mrs. Clayton saw his face. “What is it?”

“Me? Nothin’ pertickler.”

Then he doubled against the table.

They carried him to the little room above the carriage house, but he resisted until Mr. Clayton spoke sternly.

“You have obeyed me since you came here. Obey me now.”

Baker looked toward the sickrooms.

“Doc first.”

“He is being cared for.”

“The little boy?”

“Sleeping.”

“Peavey?”

“Better.”

Only then did Baker consent to lie down.

The disease, which he had fought in others with such calm intelligence, came upon him with savage speed. His exhausted body possessed no reserve. By noon the lines in his face had deepened; by afternoon Death appeared to have seated himself visibly upon it.

Dr. Hale, weak but conscious, sent instructions. Everything possible was done. But there are times when everything possible is a pitifully small country, soon crossed.

Baker suffered without complaint. Pain had been his longest acquaintance; he greeted it with an acquiescence to an inevitability that was more terrible than outcry. Only once, when his limbs convulsed, he cried:

“Not Friday! I worked good this week!”

Mr. Clayton took his hand. “No one is punishing you.”

Baker stared at him, and the room slowly returned.

“Cap’n?”

“Yes.”

“Folks get away?”

“All of them who were well. The sick are improving. You saved them.”

“Me?”

“You.”

A faint smile touched his mouth. “Reckin I was some use, then.”

“You were more use than all the rest of us.”

“That’s curious.”

“Why?”

“They said I warn’t no use. Said Number One Hundred and One was jess room took up.”

Near sunset a horse galloped into the yard. Mr. Clayton heard it but did not leave the bedside. Presently Colonel Dabney, who had returned with the county physician and two officials, appeared at the door. The colonel’s face had lost its customary historical importance.

“There is a gentleman from Atlanta,” he said quietly. “He has papers.”

Baker heard.

“Papers?”

An official stepped into the room. He carried a leather portfolio and the solemn discomfort of a man arriving punctually according to law and too late according to mercy.

“Are you Samuel Baker?” he asked.

Baker’s eyes moved anxiously.

“Me?”

“Formerly prisoner Number One Hundred and One?”

The old fear returned. His hand clawed at the blanket; his left foot jerked as though an iron ring had closed around it.

“Don’t take him,” said Mr. Clayton.

The official raised his hand. “I have not come to take him. The governor has reviewed the case. New evidence has been received. A pardon was signed yesterday.”

“Pardon?” Baker repeated.

“Yes. Full and unconditional.”

Baker looked from the paper to Mr. Clayton. “Paper free?”

“Paper free,” Mr. Clayton said, though his voice broke.

The official continued, perhaps because official language is most comfortable when human language becomes impossible. “A man named Elijah Baker made a sworn confession before his death three weeks ago. He stated that he, not Samuel Baker, killed Amos Wren; that his brother knew nothing of the crime; and that he fled while the accused was convicted.”

“My brother,” Baker whispered.

“Yes.”

For several moments he lay still. Outside, the evening light spread gold across the magnolia leaves. The mocking-bird began its song, borrowing from every other creature and making all voices its own.

“I told ’em,” Baker said. “First year I told ’em every day. Second year, Sundays. Then I quit, mostly.”

“We know the truth now,” Mr. Clayton said.

“Lije done it. I seen blood on him when he come home. He said if I spoke he’d kill Mammy. Then they took me. I reckined he’d tell after Mammy died. Kept reckinin’.”

His breathing grew shallow.

“The governor has pardoned you,” the official repeated.

Baker’s gaze lifted beyond the ceiling. The hard room, the frightened faces and the paper in the official’s hand seemed to recede from him.

“Governor comin’?”

“No. But his pardon has come.”

“Tell him I’m ready.”

“You are free, Baker.”

“Free?”

“Yes.”

He moved his left ankle beneath the sheet. A look of wondering relief passed over his face.

“Chain’s off,” he said.

“It has been off.”

“I know. But now my leg knows.”

Mr. Clayton bent closer.

Baker’s lips moved again.

“Don’t let ’em whip nobody Friday.”

“They shall not whip you.”

“Not me. Anybody.”

“No,” said Mr. Clayton. “Not anybody, if I can help it.”

The mocking-bird sang from the magnolia, clear and extravagant in the falling dusk.

Baker listened.

“That bird,” he whispered. “Never could carve him right.”

Then his eyes turned toward the floor, where his boots stood side by side. They had been cleaned of the worst mud, but not polished. The right boot no longer swaggered. The left no longer apologised. Their great worn toes pointed toward the open door.

“Boots is ready,” Baker murmured.

“For what?”

“Walk out.”

His hand relaxed in Mr. Clayton’s.

He did not breathe again.

The pardon lay open upon the table, magnificent with seals, signatures and the deliberate authority of the State of Georgia. It declared Samuel Baker restored to all the liberties of a citizen. It restored them completely, formally and forever—except that the heart beneath the coarse shirt had ceased to claim them.

In the yard below, rescued guests were returning by twos and threes. They spoke softly of the man they had laughed at, the servant who had carried them, the fool who had known what wise men did not, the convict who had preserved lives which society valued more highly than his own. Some wept. Some praised God. Some promised memorials. Human gratitude, like official justice, is often most eloquent when it can no longer embarrass itself by being useful.

Mr. Clayton closed Baker’s eyes.

Night settled upon the hotel, gentle at last. The shadow of the hitching-post reached across the empty road but touched no waiting prisoner. In the magnolia the mocking-bird ended its song. Beneath the bed stood the two enormous boots, no longer rakish, submissive, cheerful or grateful—only still, with their toes toward freedom.

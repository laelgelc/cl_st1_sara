### THE LAST CHEQUE AT RED MULGA

The Red Mulga Hotel stood where the grey plain appeared to have grown tired of going farther. Behind it was a ragged patch of mulga, in front the mail-track, and beyond that nothing in particular for eighty miles—unless we count three graves under a leaning dogwood. No one knew who occupied two of them. The third belonged to a traveller whose name had once been painted on a board, but the sun, with its usual patience, had removed the particulars.

The hotel was built of slabs, iron, bags, and hope—the hope being chiefly Mr Stiffner’s that every man who came along the track carried a cheque.

Stiffner was always drunk. Not gloriously or sociably drunk, but steadily, commercially drunk. He was a short man with a red neck, a pointed beard, and eyes which seemed to look round corners for money. When he laughed, the beard stood out like the frill of an angry lizard. Mrs Stiffner was tall, square, and hard-featured; she had the arms of a boundary-rider and the charitable disposition of a meat-ant. She kept the books, the keys, and Stiffner.

Then there was Alice.

Alice was the barmaid, and good-looking after the cold fashion of a winter moon. Her hair was pale, her face colourless, and her eyes had the washed blue of distant ranges. A smile from Alice cost nothing to produce, but it generally cost the receiver a pound or two. She could lean across the bar and ask a shearer whether he was lonely in such a manner that he immediately shouted for the house. When his cheque was gone she could look through him at a crack in the wall.

The Red Mulga did a sound business.

Station hands, drovers, fencers, tank-sinkers, shearers and men who called themselves prospectors came in with dust on their boots and money in their pockets. They were received as brothers. Stiffner slapped them on the back; Mrs Stiffner allowed them credit before they required it; Alice remembered their names. They drank to one another, to the Queen, to the bush, to absent friends, to dead horses, and to things better left unmentioned. They played cards badly and argued well. When morning came, a man might own half the district in imagination and not possess the price of a feed in fact.

Then he was no longer a brother.

The shanty had a system, though it would have been considered impolite to call it one. A cheque-man was kept warm till his cheque became Stiffner’s property. After that he might chop wood, carry water, clean out the stable, or move on. The traveller who objected was reminded that this was a free country. It was free in much the same sense that the plain was empty.

Old Danny had been at the Red Mulga for three weeks.

Danny was a bent, red-eyed station hand with a broken nose and a grey moustache stained yellow at the ends. He had worked everywhere, according to himself, and had known everyone worth knowing. He had ridden winners, discovered gold, fought blacks, saved children from floods, and once lent a governor five pounds. No evidence survived; but Danny supplied the lack with detail.

When he arrived he had a cheque for twenty-seven pounds, and his generosity shook the district.

“Drink up, boys,” he would say, striking the counter with the dignity of a squatter. “No man goes dry while Danny’s got a bob.”

Since no man at the Red Mulga desired to test this principle, they drank up.

Danny shouted for drovers who had never seen him, for a carrier asleep under his wagon, for Stiffner, Mrs Stiffner, Alice, and a one-eyed black dog that declined beer. He lost money at euchre, recovered some of it by mistake, and lost it again honourably. He bought a concertina from Yankee Jack and gave it back because he could not play. He promised Alice a gold brooch, Mrs Stiffner a silk dress, and Stiffner a racehorse.

A week later he promised them again.

By the third Sunday Danny’s cheque had become a small entry in Mrs Stiffner’s book and a large satisfaction in her mind. He possessed threepence, a pipe without a stem, and certain expectations from a station ninety miles east. His stories had also depreciated. Men who had listened respectfully to his account of the governor now told him to shut up.

That Sunday the heat lay flat over the plain. The verandah boards burned through a man’s boots, the iron roof cracked like a pistol from time to time, and the flies held possession of everything except Mrs Stiffner. She appeared to hold possession of the flies.

Danny had been given drink enough to make him foolish, but not enough to make him happy. This is a common economy at bush pubs.

Alice was hanging some washing behind the kitchen. How a small article of feminine attire came to be lifted on the end of Danny’s stick is no concern of ours. Perhaps the wind did it. Perhaps Danny intended humour. At any rate, he entered the bar carrying the garment as a flag and made a remark which caused Stiffner to choke with laughter until he noticed his wife.

Alice turned.

For a second she was white even to the lips. Then she drew herself up, placed one hand upon her hip, and pointed with the other towards the track. It was a fine stage attitude and would have brought applause in a better building.

“You filthy old beast!” she said. “Go-o-o!”

Danny grinned uncertainly. He looked from Alice to Stiffner, then to the men along the wall. Only yesterday he had shouted for those men. Two had borrowed money from him. One wore his spare shirt.

“Aw, don’t carry on, Alice,” he muttered. “Only a bit o’ fun.”

“Out!”

Mrs Stiffner advanced from the passage.

“You heard her,” she said. “Clear.”

Danny’s grin fell away. He seemed suddenly very old. There is nothing so ageing as the discovery that a man’s friends have been drinking his cheque instead of his health.

“I’ve spent twenty-seven quid in this damned hole,” he said.

“And owed thirty,” replied Mrs Stiffner.

This was untrue, but arithmetic is a weak weapon when the other side keeps the books.

Stiffner came round the counter, swaying importantly.

“Git off my premises.”

Danny looked at Alice once more. She had turned her shoulder to him and was polishing a glass.

Yankee Jack rose from the end of the bar.

He was a tall, quiet bushman with black hair, a lean face, and the level eyes of a man who did not require drink to make him dangerous. No one knew why he was called Yankee. He had never been nearer America than Bourke, but nicknames travel where men cannot.

“Come on, Danny,” he said.

He took the old man by the arm—not roughly—and led him across the yard to a dead tree by the track. Danny sat down in the dust, muttering. His hat had fallen off; Jack picked it up and put it beside him.

When Jack returned, Alice was laughing at something Stiffner had said.

“Proud of yourself?” asked Jack.

She faced him.

“He insulted me.”

“He was drunk.”

“That excuses him, I suppose?”

“No. But you helped make him drunk.”

The room quietened. Even Mrs Stiffner paused over her accounts.

Alice’s eyes hardened.

“He’s a dirty old loafer.”

Jack took one step nearer the bar.

“HE’S AS GOOD AS YOU!”

It was not a loud house for several seconds.

Alice stared at him. The colour came into her cheeks then, and because it had seldom been seen there, it made her beautiful. Jack turned, walked out, and sat beside Danny beneath the dead tree.

Towards evening a new bushman came in from the west with a swag, a blue cattle-dog, and a cheque for forty-one pounds ten.

Morality went off duty.

The stranger was a boundary-rider who had not seen a township for nine months. Within twenty minutes Stiffner loved him, Mrs Stiffner trusted him, and Alice thought she remembered meeting his brother at Wilcannia. Danny was called back from the tree. His offence, under the warming influence of forty-one pounds ten, became a misunderstanding.

Alice herself carried him a nobbler.

“Here, Danny,” she said pleasantly. “No bad feelings.”

Danny looked at Jack, winked with immense cunning, and swallowed the drink.

“Alice always was a good sort,” he announced.

Thus was honour satisfied.

The night developed along recognised lines. The boundary-rider shouted because he was glad to arrive; the others shouted because he had. Cards appeared. Stiffner played till he could no longer distinguish kings from sevens, which did not prevent him winning. Mrs Stiffner supplied bottles and marked them down with a pencil that never became intoxicated. Alice smiled, moved between the men, and listened without hearing.

There was also a young jackeroo named Ned Vale.

He had come down from a station on the Warrego with six pounds, a clean collar, a volume of verses, and notions concerning women. All four possessions had suffered at the Red Mulga. The six pounds were gone, the collar was red with dust, the verses had been used to light a pipe, and the notions remained only because such things are difficult to destroy.

Ned loved Alice.

He had spoken to her perhaps twenty times, and she had once asked him to bring in a bucket of water. On this foundation he had erected a future containing a cottage, roses, and Alice in a white dress. There are no roses within two hundred miles of the Red Mulga, but youth is not troubled by geography.

That evening he lost his last shilling to Stiffner.

“Put it down,” said Mrs Stiffner, when he asked for a drink.

Ned glanced at Alice. She was bending towards Yankee Jack, who sat apart with his elbows on his knees.

“I don’t want credit,” the boy said.

He went out to the verandah and lay down beside his dog.

By eleven the boundary-rider had sung three songs and attempted to fight the water-tank. By half-past eleven Danny was explaining to a post how he had once broken in a horse for the Prince of Wales. At midnight Stiffner slept behind the bar with his beard in a puddle of rum. Mrs Stiffner had withdrawn with the cash-box. The other men lay about the verandah and yard in heaps—boots, hats, beards and open mouths—appearing less like citizens of a young country than refuse left by an old one.

The moon rose over the plain.

It silvered the iron roof, whitened the track, and touched the three nameless graves. The mulga shadows looked like crouching men. Somewhere a night bird cried once and thought better of it.

Yankee Jack slept against a verandah post, his head fallen awkwardly to one side.

The bar door opened.

Alice came out carrying a cushion. She wore a loose white wrapper, and her pale hair hung over her shoulders. She paused among the bodies, lifting her skirts from a pool of spilled beer, and went to Jack. For a moment she stood looking down at him. Her face had lost the barmaid’s smile and the injured woman’s pride; it was merely tired.

She slipped the cushion beneath his head.

Jack stirred but did not wake.

Alice sighed—very softly—and went back inside.

Ned Vale had seen it all.

The boy lay motionless with one arm round his dog’s neck. Presently he sat up. The dog raised its head, and Ned pressed a hand over its collar to prevent the buckle from jingling.

A thin line of light showed beneath Alice’s door.

Then came a sound—not loud, and not repeated at first. It might have been the wind troubling the iron; but there was no wind. It might have been laughter, if laughter had ever sounded so lonely.

Alice was crying.

Why she cried we cannot say. Perhaps Jack’s words had found a place that the insults of drunken men had never reached. Perhaps she was crying for Jack, or for herself, or because the world beyond the grey plain seemed farther away at night. They do say a hard woman’s tears are the bitterest, having been kept too long. That may be wisdom, or only another saying.

Ned listened.

The poor lad had lost his last shilling, which was serious; he had lost his illusion, which was worse. He drew a breath that ended in a little groan, rose, and stepped over Danny. Then he rolled his blanket, tied the billy to his swag, and shouldered the lot.

His dog stood ready.

Together they went down the track.

There was no farewell. Ned did not look back at the light beneath Alice’s door, though he walked stiffly for the first mile as if every step required an argument. Soon the plain took him—the swag, the dog, the clean-hearted foolishness and all. By dawn he was a black speck against a red sky; then the mulga swallowed him.

No one missed him.

Morning came ghost-grey and sour.

The sleepers woke by degrees and blamed the weather, the rum, one another, and Providence. Stiffner counted bottles. Mrs Stiffner counted money. The boundary-rider signed away the greater portion of his cheque and was informed that he had enjoyed himself. Alice appeared behind the bar with her hair smooth and her face without colour.

Jack drank tea.

Danny asked for rum and was refused. He spent the day wandering between shade and sunlight, trying to gather fragments of the night. Towards evening another round began on what remained of the boundary-rider’s money.

Danny nudged Jack.

“Here,” he whispered. “What was up with Alice last night?”

Jack looked at him sharply.

“What d’you mean?”

“Heard her carryin’ on in her room. Cryin’—or somethin’. An’ you was—”

Stiffner laughed.

“You was dead drunk, Danny.”

“So were you.”

“Danny hears angels when he’s full,” said one of the men.

“Or devils,” said another.

Alice set down a glass hard enough to crack it.

“Give the old fool another drink and shut him up.”

The men laughed, and Danny, pleased to be included again, laughed louder than any of them. Jack said nothing. Alice did not look at him.

Outside, the sun sank behind the mulga, staining the plain a dull, exhausted red. The three graves lengthened their shadows towards the pub. Somewhere beyond them a boy and a dog were walking to the next station, where there would be work, loneliness, perhaps another shanty, and certainly no roses.

Inside, the boundary-rider shouted.

The glasses filled; the cards were dealt; friendship flourished on the counter while the money lasted.

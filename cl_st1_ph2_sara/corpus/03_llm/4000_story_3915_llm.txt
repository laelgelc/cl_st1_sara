## THE CHRISTMAS THAT WAITED

Children, when Christmas Eve comes to you now, with its lighted shops, its merry bells, and its warm rooms wherein no hostile soldier may enter, do you ever think of that other Christmas, long ago, when our country was not yet free?

It was the winter of 1776—a bitter winter for General Washington and his little army. The patriot soldiers were hungry and ill-clad; many had no shoes, and the frozen roads were marked by their bleeding feet. The British held New York, their red-jackets were scattered through New Jersey, and the hired Hessian troops occupied Trenton, Bordentown, and other towns along the Delaware. Many persons believed the American cause to be lost.

But Washington did not believe it, nor did the brave men who remained with him.

In Bordentown stood the modest dwelling of Captain Joseph Tracy, who was serving in the patriot army. There his wife lived with their two children, Harry and Kitty. Harry was ten years old and considered himself nearly a man; Kitty, who was but seven, had large brown eyes, a merry little tongue, and a doll named Patience, to whom she related all her joys and sorrows.

The house was a pleasant one, with a low roof and a great chimney. On this December afternoon a fire of hickory logs roared valiantly upon the hearth, while Mrs. Tracy sat knitting a pair of grey woollen stockings. Kitty was making—after her own fashion—a red comforter for her father; and Harry was cracking shellbarks, which he placed in two piles, a small one for Christmas and a goodly one for immediate eating.

“Harry,” said Kitty, “you’ve eaten six.”

“A soldier must be well fed,” Harry replied gravely.

“You’re not a soldier.”

“I shall be.”

“Then I shall marry a candy-shop keeper,” said Kitty, “and give sugar-plums to all the patriot army.”

Can you not see them, Children—the dancing firelight, the bowl of nuts, the mother’s busy needles, and Kitty’s dollie lying in state beneath the table? Yet, warm as the room was, there was anxiety in Mrs. Tracy’s bosom, for Captain Tracy had written that, if duty permitted, he would cross the Delaware and come home for Christmas.

Kitty had counted the days upon her fingers until no more remained to be counted.

“Mother,” she asked for perhaps the twentieth time, “will father surely come to-morrow?”

“If General Washington can spare him, dear.”

“And can’t Santa Claus spare him?”

“I think,” said Mrs. Tracy, smiling, “that Santa Claus and General Washington are upon the same side.”

This answer pleased Kitty greatly. She ran to Patience and whispered, “There, dollie! I knew Santa wasn’t a Tory.”

Harry laughed, but soon became serious again.

“The Hessians are between us and father,” he said. “And the river is full of ice.”

“Hush,” said his mother gently. “Your father has crossed rougher rivers than the Delaware.”

Nevertheless, when the wind shook the windows and drove the snow against them, all three looked toward the door.

Their Christmas preparations were simple. Captain Tracy’s grey stockings were finished and hung beside the fireplace, with Kitty’s red comforter folded across them. Harry had carved a small wooden powder-horn, though it possessed neither powder nor horn, and Uncle Silas, a farmer who lived several miles away, had sent word that he would bring a turkey on Christmas morning if the roads permitted.

“We shall have father at one end of the table and the turkey at the other,” declared Harry, “and I shall sit between them.”

“Why?” asked Kitty.

“So I can look at both.”

On Christmas Eve they placed sprigs of evergreen above the mantel, and Kitty set forth a small cake which she had helped her mother make.

“Father likes raisins,” she said. “I put most of them on his side.”

The candle burned lower. Nine o’clock came, then ten. Outside, the snow had changed to stinging sleet. Now and again came the heavy tread of Hessian sentries in the street, or a rough voice speaking in a foreign tongue. Bordentown was under the enemy’s eye, and no patriot household could feel wholly safe.

At last Mrs. Tracy led the children upstairs, though Kitty begged to remain awake.

“If father comes, he’ll be cold,” she said. “Somebody ought to hold his slippers.”

“I will have them ready,” promised her mother.

Kitty knelt beside her little bed and prayed for Captain Tracy, General Washington, the poor soldiers without shoes, and Santa Claus, “if he has to pass the Hessians.” Then she tucked Patience beneath the quilt and whispered, “Don’t be ’fraid. Father will be here when we wake.”

But he was not.

Christmas morning dawned grey and still. Kitty ran downstairs in her nightdress, her curls flying, and stopped upon the last step. The stockings beside the chimney had been filled with apples, nuts, a length of blue ribbon, and two peppermint sticks; yet Captain Tracy’s chair stood empty.

Uncle Silas arrived before noon, wrapped in a greatcoat and powdered with snow. In his sleigh lay a noble turkey, plump enough to make Harry’s eyes shine.

“There is your Christmas messenger,” cried Uncle Silas.

“Did you meet father?” asked Kitty.

The farmer’s jovial face grew sober. He had heard nothing. The river was dangerous, he said, and patrols watched the roads. No man could cross from Pennsylvania without risking capture.

Mrs. Tracy thanked him and bore the turkey into the kitchen. Harry followed it wistfully.

“Mother,” said Kitty, “we mustn’t cook him.”

“The turkey?”

“Not till father comes. It wouldn’t be Christmas without father.”

Harry looked stricken. Patriotism was one thing; a roasting turkey was another. He opened his mouth to protest, then glanced at the empty chair.

“She’s right,” he said at last, with a sigh worthy of General Washington himself. “We’ll save it.”

Thus the Tracys ate their Christmas dinner of bread, beans, and apple preserves, while the turkey remained in the cold pantry awaiting a happier day. They sang a hymn, and Mrs. Tracy read the story of the Child born in Bethlehem; but every gust at the door made Kitty start, and every footstep caused Harry to run to the window.

“Perhaps father has forgotten Christmas,” Kitty said that evening.

“Never,” answered her mother.

“Then Christmas has forgotten us.”

“No, my darling. Sometimes happiness is delayed so that we may learn how precious it is.”

The following night was wild and bitter. Across the Delaware, General Washington’s soldiers marched through snow and sleet toward boats waiting at McConkey’s Ferry. The river was swollen, and great cakes of ice swept upon its dark current. Men stumbled as they dragged cannon up the slippery bank; their clothes were wet, their hands numb, and the storm beat fiercely in their faces.

Among them marched Captain Tracy.

Did he think of the warm hearth in Bordentown? Indeed he did. Beneath his coat he carried Kitty’s last letter, in which she had drawn a crooked evergreen tree and written, with considerable help from Harry: “DEER FATHER COME HOME QUICK.”

Yet duty called him, and he went forward. Before dawn Washington’s army was on the road to Trenton.

The people of Bordentown knew nothing of this. They slept uneasily beneath the watch of the Hessians. Then, on the morning after Christmas, a distant booming sounded through the wintry air.

Mrs. Tracy raised her head.

“Cannon!” exclaimed Harry.

Again came the dull thunder, far away toward Trenton.

Kitty clasped Patience to her bosom. “Is father there?”

“I do not know,” replied Mrs. Tracy, and her pale face frightened the children more than the sound of the guns.

All that morning rumours flew about the town. Washington had crossed the Delaware! The patriot army had attacked Trenton! No, Washington had been defeated! No, the Hessians had surrendered! Men whispered at street corners, women barred their doors, and the enemy soldiers in Bordentown hurried to and fro in great confusion.

By afternoon there could be no doubt. The Hessians were retreating. Wagons rattled through the streets; officers shouted; frightened men gathered their muskets and fled southward. The townspeople peeped from behind curtains, scarcely daring to rejoice until the last of the enemy had gone.

Then somebody cried, “Victory at Trenton!”

Doors flew open. Neighbours embraced one another. Boys ran along the road shouting, “Washington has beaten the Hessians!” Bells rang, though no one had ordered them rung, and old men who had feared the cause lost wiped their eyes and declared that they had never doubted it for a moment.

But Mrs. Tracy did not celebrate. She stood at the gate, looking northward.

The afternoon faded into a blue winter twilight. Suddenly Harry, who had climbed upon the fence, gave such a shout that Kitty dropped Patience into the snow.

“A soldier! Mother—a soldier is coming!”

Along the road walked a tall man in a mud-stained uniform. His shoulders drooped with weariness, and one sleeve was torn. Under his arm he carried a small bundle wrapped in a military cloak.

For an instant Mrs. Tracy could not move. Then she ran.

“Joseph!”

Captain Tracy caught her in his free arm. Harry flung himself against his father’s side, while Kitty danced about them crying and laughing.

“You came! You came! We saved the turkey!”

Captain Tracy, who had faced ice, cannon, and bayonets without a tremor, bent his head over his children and wept.

Presently Kitty remembered the bundle.

“What have you there, father?”

The cloak moved, and from its folds appeared a small white-and-brown head, two silky ears, and the most sorrowful pair of eyes that Kitty had ever seen.

“A dog!” she cried.

“A little spaniel,” said Captain Tracy. “His master was a British officer.”

Harry’s face hardened. “An enemy?”

“An enemy in uniform,” his father answered, “but a real gentleman, and a suffering man. He lay wounded after the fighting. This poor creature would not leave him. When I gave the officer water, he asked me to take the dog, for he knew that he was dying.”

Captain Tracy looked gravely at his son.

“Remember, Harry, courage and mercy must march together. A patriot need not hate every man who wears a red coat.”

The spaniel licked Captain Tracy’s hand, then crept timidly toward Kitty.

“Oh, you pretty little Fido!” she murmured, kneeling. “You’ve lost your father too. But you may share ours.”

They carried the dog inside and warmed milk for him. Mrs. Tracy brought dry garments for her husband; Harry heaped logs upon the fire; and Kitty placed one peppermint stick before Captain Tracy and the other before Fido, though the spaniel wisely preferred the milk.

“And now,” said Harry, “may we have Christmas?”

“In a trice!” cried Mrs. Tracy.

The long-waiting turkey was brought from the pantry. Pots clattered, the fire blazed, and such pleasant fragrances filled the house that Harry stationed himself in the kitchen lest any dish should require guarding. Kitty hung the red comforter around her father’s neck, although he declared that the room was warm enough already, and Captain Tracy drew on the grey woollen stockings with proper admiration.

Their Christmas came a day late, Children, but was it any less merry? I think not. The father was home, the enemy had fled, Washington had won a brave victory, and a forlorn little dog had found friends beneath a patriot roof.

Long afterward, when Kitty and Harry were old and told the tale to grandchildren gathered at their knees, they never spoke chiefly of the presents or even of the turkey. They remembered the empty chair, the distant cannon, and the weary soldier coming through the snow.

And we, who keep Christmas in a free land, should remember them also. For our warm homes and peaceful merrymaking were purchased by the courage of men who marched barefoot through the winter night, and by the faith of women and little people who waited, prayed, and kept love ready at the hearth.

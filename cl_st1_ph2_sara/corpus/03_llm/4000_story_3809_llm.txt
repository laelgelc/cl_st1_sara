### I  
**Allegro con brio**

All day the City had beaten upon the Music-Lover’s senses as an iron rain beats upon a roof.

It had roared beneath his window in vans and omnibuses; it had clanged in the hammers of workmen, shrieked in the brakes of motor-cars, rattled in the shutters of shops, and sputtered from a thousand engines whose feverish haste seemed without purpose or destination. Newsboys cried calamities which no one paused to understand. Telephones rang in rooms already loud with talk. Men shouted through the streets as though each were separated from his neighbour by a desert, instead of by the breadth of a pavement. Even the pigeons, disputing among the cornices, had added their dry and petulant squawking to the universal discord.

Toward evening he fled.

He did not tell himself that he was weary of men; for he was too honest to lay upon humanity a burden which belonged perhaps to his own tired nerves. Yet he desired no face, no greeting, no argument. He wanted that peculiar solitude which can be found only among many silent people, all waiting upon the same revelation. Thus he came to the concert-hall as a traveller comes upon water, and climbed to his accustomed coign of vantage in the balcony.

Below him the stage shone like an island under a tranquil moon. The chairs stood in ordered curves; the desks, each with its little lamp, waited like low altars; and the polished instruments gave back points of gold, silver, and deep-brown fire. Musicians entered by twos and threes, carrying beneath their arms the tools of their mysterious labour. They sat, adjusted, tested, murmured. A violin sent up a thin inquiry; a horn answered from a distance; the kettledrum muttered once, as though thunder had turned in sleep.

Then the Conductor appeared.

Applause swept across the hall, subsided, and left behind it an expectant hush. He lifted his baton. At that instant the Music-Lover saw the orchestra as a wonderful machine, infinitely delicate yet perfectly obedient: a many-membered engine of sound, governed by one will. A movement of the baton, scarcely more than the fall of a white reed, released the four notes which Fate had knocked upon mankind’s door.

The strings sprang together. Bows rose and descended with the precision of pistons; trumpet-valves flashed under practised fingers; the slides of the trombones went forth and returned as if controlled by hidden rods. No gesture appeared accidental. Even violence had measure, and passion entered upon its appointed beat.

“How admirable,” thought the Music-Lover, “is command! Here are a hundred wills surrendered to one; a hundred hands performing one intention. No argument, no hesitation, no vanity interrupts the design. If men could be governed thus, if nations could hear one pulse, how simple would become the world!”

But even as his inner voice pronounced the thought, the music contradicted him. For the Allegro was not simple obedience. It wrestled; it questioned; it was driven backward, gathered itself, struck again. The famous summons passed from strings to wood-wind, from horns to the dark foundations of the basses, and each utterance changed the challenge while preserving it. The orchestra did not merely repeat: it remembered, resisted, transformed.

The Conductor’s hand did not compel the storm. It listened to it.

Now he restrained the violins with a curved palm; now he beckoned a phrase from the violas; now, turning swiftly, he awakened the brass. His authority lay less in forcing than in knowing—knowing what each voice could give, when it should stand alone, when it must yield, when courage required restraint, and when restraint itself would be cowardice.

The Music-Lover leaned forward.

The machine had begun to breathe.

When the first movement ended, silence remained for a moment like smoke after battle. The Music-Lover, who had entered wishing to escape mankind, found himself looking not at the Conductor, but at the musicians.

### II  
**Andante con moto**

The Andante opened a gentler country.

After the rock and lightning of the first movement came broad roads under a grave sky, processions moving with measured step, springs uncovered among stones. The melody flowed not as water that forgets its source, but as a river bearing memories—village bells, fields at dusk, banners once lifted, names once spoken by beloved voices.

Now the Music-Lover watched the players closely.

The first violins carried the phrase with a refinement which concealed their labour. Their bows did not merely rise together; each rose from a different hand. One hand was small and brown, with a wedding-ring that caught the light. Another was long and pale, the wrist emerging sharply from a frayed cuff. A young woman near the outer desk played with lips slightly parted, as though the melody were a secret she feared to lose. Beside her sat an elderly man whose bald head shone mildly beneath the lamps; he counted the resting bars with a serenity suggesting that he had learned, in music and perhaps in life, the difficult virtue of waiting.

“Not a machine,” thought the Music-Lover. “A covenant.”

For every player retained his difference. The flautist’s breath was his own breath; the cellist’s pressure upon the string came from muscles shaped by years of practice, fatigue, illness, renewed endeavour. The horn-player must enter alone, with no curtain behind which a timid note might hide. The percussionist, silent through long measures, must preserve within himself the exact instant of his thunder. Cooperation did not erase personality; it made personality responsible.

The theme returned, adorned and strengthened. It seemed to the Music-Lover that the musicians were weaving a ceremonial robe whose splendour no single hand could have imagined. The violins laid down threads of brightness; the violas gave shadow; the ’cellos drew through the pattern a colour warm as old wine; bassoons added russet, clarinets dusk-blue, horns the bronze of autumn leaves. Beneath all, the double-basses held the fabric upon their grave and patient loom.

What had brought these persons together?

There sat a grizzled cellist, bending toward his instrument with such tenderness that the curved wood appeared almost human beneath his cheek. Had he loved music first, or some mortal creature whose voice music afterwards recalled? Had he once played this Andante to a room in which a sick child listened? Had sorrow deepened his tone, or had tone taught him how sorrow might be borne?

Beyond him the player of the double bassoon remained immovable. The great instrument descended from his mouth like some fabulous pipe devised for a river-god. His expression was so composed that neither victory nor catastrophe seemed capable of surprising him. Yet perhaps, thought the Music-Lover, this calm was not nature but conquest. Perhaps anger had once ruled him. Perhaps each slow phrase was a stone laid upon an old volcano.

A clarinettist, dark-bearded and broad-browed, resembled a philosopher from Rembrandt’s shadowed world. He waited with lowered eyes, then lifted the instrument and released a phrase of such lucid sweetness that the Music-Lover felt the whole hall incline toward it.

“What do you know?” he asked him silently. “What have you forgiven? Whom have you failed? Whose forgiveness do you still await?”

The phrase ended; the man removed the clarinet from his lips, adjusted a key with one careful finger, and became once more a citizen among citizens.

The Andante advanced toward nobility, retreated into tenderness, and advanced again. Its courage was not the courage of one who has never been afraid. It was the courage of those who march while carrying their dead invisibly beside them.

The Music-Lover had entered the hall desiring a world purged of human confusion. Yet the loveliness before him was made from that confusion disciplined but not denied. Pain had not been excluded; it had been given rhythm. Hope had not been proclaimed as easy; it had been earned through modulation and return.

At the close of the movement, the Music-Lover breathed deeply, as if he too had borne a part in the long procession.

### III  
**Scherzo—Trio**

Out of the pause arose a stealthy question.

The low strings moved like travellers feeling their way through a forest at midnight. Then the horns gave forth the ancient challenge, altered now, mysterious and remote. The Scherzo smiled, but it was not the smile of innocence. Something moved behind it—Puck perhaps, or Fate wearing a mask at carnival.

The Music-Lover’s eye wandered from desk to desk, and each face became the threshold of an unwritten tale.

There was a bass-violinist of immense height and melancholy leanness. His cheeks were hollow, his hair untidy, his collar rebellious. When he stood beside the giant instrument, embracing its dark shoulders, he reminded the Music-Lover of Lincoln: awkward, compassionate, burdened by griefs too large to become private. Yet at a nimble passage his eyebrows danced, and for three seconds the statesman vanished; a mischievous country boy appeared in his place.

A second violinist glanced toward the leader with an expression in which admiration and rivalry were delicately mingled. Had they competed in youth? Did one possess the career intended for the other? Did they dine together after rehearsals, praising one another with guarded sincerity? Or had some Girl Who Understood stood once between them, choosing neither music nor merit, but the one whose silences she could inhabit?

Near the drums sat a young man with red hair and the solemnity of a priest. He had before him only a handful of notes, yet he guarded them as Leonidas guarded the pass. What patience had taught him to count another man’s melodies for half an hour and then enter, not one fraction early, with a single stroke? Was this humility—or pride refined until it could take the form of service?

Some had known poverty; some had known applause; some had quarrelled that very afternoon with wives, husbands, creditors, children. One might be playing in shoes not paid for. One might have received a letter whose unopened answer waited in his coat. One might be secretly ill. Another, outwardly grave, might be planning a holiday by the sea. Some loved the Conductor; some feared him; some considered his interpretation profound; some endured it as a temporary error in the history of Beethoven. Yet all entered the Fugue together.

Then occurred that miracle which, because it was achieved by skill, was no less miraculous. Voice pursued voice. The ’cellos began; violas answered; violins gathered the thread; the structure climbed, crossed, doubled upon itself. It was architecture in motion, a staircase built while men ascended it. No player possessed the whole, yet each preserved the whole by keeping faith with his fragment.

The Music-Lover felt ashamed of his first vision of the orchestra as a machine. A machine has no mercy upon a weakened wheel; these men and women listened to one another. A machine does not cherish the character of a screw; this community depended upon differences. Their discipline was not annihilation of the self, but the self freely entering relation.

“And is not this,” said the inner voice, “the harmony for which men perpetually cry, while misunderstanding its nature? They demand agreement where they should seek accord. They desire that all should sound one note, although one note, however pure, is not music. Harmony requires distance as well as nearness, opposition as well as consent; it permits the oboe to remain an oboe and asks only that it hear the horn.”

The Scherzo returned in whispers. Bows moved with spectral lightness. The orchestra seemed to withdraw into caverns beneath the earth. Yet through that darkness a pulse continued, quiet, indomitable. The Music-Lover scarcely breathed.

Now he no longer wished merely to inspect these hidden lives. He longed to hear one of them confessed in the imperfect language of words.

If the grizzled cellist would but come down from the brilliant island and sit beside a common fire! If the philosophic clarinettist would lay aside his instrument and say, “This happened to me in Prague,” or “Once, when I was young in Florence—”! If the Girl Who Understood would tell why she had chosen, or refused to choose! Any story would suffice, provided it revealed the secret bridge by which grief passes into beauty and loneliness consents to become song.

But no one spoke. The shadowed transition deepened. A tremor ran through the strings; the drums whispered from far away. Out of secrecy, suspense, and gathering light, the doors of the Finale burst open.

### IV  
**Allegro**

It was as though noon entered at midnight.

The brass proclaimed victory—not the shallow victory of one army over another, but the soul’s tremendous refusal to remain imprisoned. Sound poured across the hall in golden cataracts. The piccolos flashed above it like birds in sunlight; trumpets burned; trombones opened vast perspectives; the strings rushed onward, wave succeeding wave, and the drums drove the flood between mountainous banks.

The Music-Lover forgot the players in the music, then found them again within it, transfigured but still themselves. The bald violinist, the red-haired guardian of the drum, the Rembrandt clarinettist, the Lincoln of the bass, the young woman with parted lips—all laboured, listened, ventured, obeyed, created. Their separate histories had not ceased; they had become fuel for the common flame.

The triumphant theme strode forward, was challenged, disappeared, returned. Even the Scherzo’s night rose again for an instant, like an old fear making its final claim; but the light knew it now and absorbed it. The Music-Lover felt that courage did not destroy memory. It carried memory into a larger kingdom.

He thought of Beethoven, shut within that most terrible of silences, summoning for others a universe he could no longer hear. He thought of Psyche wandering with her lamp, of Amor wounded by knowledge yet restored by endurance; of unnamed men and women who, having lost happiness, nevertheless fashioned chairs, planted gardens, taught children, tuned violins. Perhaps the human soul was never more majestic than when it gave what it had been denied.

The final pages came like breakers driven by a victorious wind. Again and again the conclusion seemed reached; again the music gathered itself and surged beyond conclusion. The Conductor no longer appeared to govern. He stood at the meeting-place of forces, his whole body shaped by their passage. One lifted hand held back a continent; one downward stroke released the sea.

Then the last chord fell.

For a heartbeat the hall remained utterly still.

Applause followed—roaring, grateful, almost savage—but the Music-Lover did not rise. He sat with his hands upon the balcony rail, staring at the musicians as they lowered their instruments, smiled, wiped their brows, shuffled music, and became mortal once more.

Presently two figures appeared beside him.

“Still enchanted?” asked the Dreamer.

The Music-Lover turned. With the Dreamer stood the Girl Who Understood, wrapped in a grey cloak, her eyes bright with amusement and sympathy.

“We are going home,” she said. “There is a fire, coffee, bread and cheese, and, if our philosopher permits it, his deplorable pipe.”

“Better than that,” said the Dreamer. “I have brought a story.”

“A story?”

“One that asks to be read aloud. Come, mon ami. You have listened long enough to voices without words.”

The Music-Lover looked once more toward the emptying stage. The grizzled cellist had vanished; the clarinettist was closing his case; the tall bass-player walked away carrying his instrument like a dark companion.

Then he smiled and rose.

“Yes,” he said. “Let us hear the story.”

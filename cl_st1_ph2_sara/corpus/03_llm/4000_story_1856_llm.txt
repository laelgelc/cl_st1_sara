## THE BROKEN PROMISE  
### A Chronicle of the Regency

Among the brilliant and ill-governed personages who appeared in Paris during the minority of Louis XV, there was none whose figure, fortunes, and catastrophe excited more discourse than those of Count Antoine Joseph Van Horn. He was seen, for a brief season, in the saloons of the Palais Royal, at the gaming-tables of the Rue Quincampoix, and in those less reputable assemblies where the distinctions of birth were temporarily levelled by the intoxication of sudden wealth. He was then scarcely past the first vigor of youth; tall, graceful, and of a complexion rendered almost feminine by habitual pallor, with dark hair, an aquiline countenance, and eyes of extraordinary lustre, which in repose were melancholy, but, when opposed or inflamed, had something wandering and terrible in their expression.

His manners, when unruffled, were those of one bred amid all the observances of high condition. He spoke with ease, displayed a romantic generosity, and seemed capable of strong affections; yet there lurked beneath this engaging exterior an irritability so fierce and instantaneous that the most trivial contradiction might awaken in him the fury of a madman. Those who knew something of his former life regarded him with mingled curiosity and apprehension. Some called him merely an unfortunate youth, made savage by cruelty; others whispered that insanity was hereditary in his house; and there were not wanting persons who declared that he had already been formally confined as a lunatic. These last spoke nearer to the truth than was then generally suspected.

The family of Van Horn, or Van Hoorn, as the name is written in several Flemish registers, was one of considerable antiquity in the Low Countries, allied by marriage with houses of France, Germany, and the Spanish Netherlands, and entitled to exhibit upon its escutcheon a complicated blazon which none but a herald could have interpreted without fatigue. The Counts of the name had supplied officers to the Empire, prelates to the Church, and ladies of honor to more than one court. Their pride was not of that recent and clamorous species which requires continual assertion, but of the grave hereditary kind which considers rank a law of nature and imagines the world shaken when an ancient privilege is infringed.

Antoine was a younger son of this illustrious stock. His elder brother, the acknowledged chief of the family, appears to have regarded him from childhood with a tenderness almost paternal. The youth, however, had inherited passions which no ordinary discipline could control. He entered the Austrian service at an age when boys of meaner station are still under the ferule of a tutor; and, possessing courage without prudence, he soon distinguished himself less by obedience than by those violent punctilios which passed among young nobles for proofs of spirit. He quarrelled with a superior officer, refused, it is said, some command delivered in terms which wounded his pride, and aggravated the offence by menaces of personal chastisement. A court-martial followed. The influence of his house preserved him from severer punishment, but he was cashiered and dismissed from the army.

To a young man conscious of ancient blood, and educated to consider military distinction his natural inheritance, this disgrace was almost insupportable. He returned to Flanders in a condition of gloomy excitement, alternately threatening vengeance upon his judges and accusing his relations of having abandoned him. His brother, perceiving the disorder of his mind and fearing some desperate act, resolved to place him for a time in honorable durance. There was belonging to the family an old castle, remote from the principal roads, surrounded by marshy woods, and governed by a certain Van Wert, a man of brutal courage, iron constitution, and habits formed amid the coarsest severities of camp and prison.

The elder Count committed Antoine to this person’s charge, with orders that he should be guarded but treated with all respect due to his birth and misfortune. Van Wert obeyed the first injunction and neglected the second. Whether he mistook the violence of his prisoner for insolence, or whether the helplessness of a nobleman awakened in his base nature a pleasure in domination, cannot now be determined; but it is certain that he subjected the unhappy youth to indignities which would have broken a temper less combustible. He denied him exercise, abridged his food, mocked his complaints, and on one occasion, when Antoine attempted to force the door of his apartment, caused him to be bound like a common malefactor.

Another version of the story was that Van Wert, being struck by the Count in an access of rage, retaliated with such ferocity that the attendants themselves were obliged to interpose. Whatever the precise beginning of their hatred, it soon became mortal. The governor delighted in contradiction because it tormented his prisoner; the prisoner raved because every contradiction revived the sense of his disgrace. Threats produced chains; chains produced frantic resistance; and resistance furnished the keeper with a pretext for new cruelties. Thus did persecution first imitate madness and afterward create it.

At length the Count’s reason wholly gave way. He would remain for hours silent, with his eyes fixed upon the grated window; then start suddenly, declare that the castle was surrounded by enemies, and call for his sword and regiment. At night he heard voices in the corridor, though no one was there, and believed that a scaffold had been erected in the courtyard. More than once he cried that he saw a wheel turning in the darkness, stained with blood, and that men without faces were binding him upon it. These circumstances were afterward repeated as presentiments; though a sober antiquary may observe that the wheel was a punishment familiar to every continental imagination, and that terror commonly borrows its visions from things already known.

The reports forwarded by Van Wert described his prisoner as incurably violent and carefully concealed the treatment by which the violence had been provoked. The elder Count, occupied by public affairs and trusting too much to an agent of established reputation, remained ignorant of the truth until Antoine effected his escape. One stormy evening, having loosened an iron bar with a fragment of furniture, he descended from a window by means of his bedding, crossed the flooded ditch, and reached, half-naked and bleeding, the hut of a peasant. The man concealed him, not from affection for the house of Van Horn, as family tradition afterward asserted, but because he bore an old grudge against Van Wert.

From this retreat Antoine contrived to inform his brother of his condition. The elder Count hastened to him, and the meeting, we are told, was of a most affecting nature. Antoine at first recoiled, believing him an apparition sent to restore him to the castle; but, recognizing his voice, threw himself into his arms and wept with the helplessness of a child. Inquiry exposed the governor’s conduct. Van Wert was dismissed, though the punishment inflicted upon him was thought by many inadequate; and the unfortunate youth was removed to an estate where physicians, ecclesiastics, and faithful servants were charged with his restoration.

Under the influence of kindness, liberty, and tranquil occupation, his reason gradually returned. He resumed the habits of his rank, hunted, read, and received a few chosen visitors. Yet a dangerous remnant of mental alienation survived. He could endure fatigue, disappointment, even bodily pain; but contradiction, especially when expressed with contempt, acted upon him like fire upon powder. His brother therefore surrounded him with indulgence, concealing the precautions by which he was watched. This compassionate supervision preserved him for a time, but it wounded the very pride it was intended to spare. Antoine discovered that servants had been instructed not to leave him alone, and that his letters were sometimes inspected. He complained bitterly of being treated as a child or criminal; and, when assured that all was done through affection, answered that affection which deprived a gentleman of liberty was but imprisonment under a softer name.

In the year when Paris had become the theatre of John Law’s prodigious financial experiment, Count Antoine disappeared from his brother’s house. He left behind a letter, respectful but resolute, declaring that he would no longer live beneath guardianship, and that a man who had worn a sword must be permitted either to govern himself or to perish by his own errors. He travelled secretly into France and presented himself among his connexions in the capital.

These connexions were numerous and exalted. There were princes, dukes, marshals, abbés, and ladies attached to the households of the blood royal, all of whom admitted the kinship, though several wished it had chosen a less inconvenient moment to manifest itself. They received him with ceremonious kindness. Invitations were sent, horses placed at his disposal, and introductions given; but beneath all this polished hospitality there existed a prudent vigilance. His history had preceded him. Every hostess feared an outbreak at her supper; every noble cousin was prepared to disavow responsibility for his debts; and those who praised the animation of his eyes added, in a lower tone, that they had too much brilliance for sanity.

Paris at that period was ill fitted to restore a mind which had been once unsettled. Law’s Mississippi scheme had seized upon every class. Princes, lackeys, bishops, courtesans, widows, foreign adventurers, and sober magistrates crowded the Rue Quincampoix, where shares rose and fell by enchantment, and fortunes were created before breakfast and annihilated before supper. A footman who had speculated successfully might lend money to his ruined master; a duchess waited in the antechamber of a man whom she would formerly have forbidden to enter by the servants’ staircase. The whole kingdom seemed converted into a gaming-house, with the Regent for its magnificent proprietor.

Count Antoine entered eagerly into this delirium. He gambled in shares, at cards, and upon the caprices of women. When fortunate, he scattered gold with a hand which gained him parasites; when unfortunate, he borrowed with the assurance of one who considered his name security enough for every obligation. His companions were not always of his own order. Among those most frequently seen with him were a certain Lestang, whose origin and occupation were equally obscure, and Laurent de Mille, a man of good address, violent necessities, and that doubtful gentility which is admitted at the gaming-table but forgotten elsewhere.

It was said, moreover, that the Count had formed an intrigue with one of the Regent’s favorites. The lady’s name was differently given by contemporary scandal, and perhaps the story arose merely from the necessity of finding a courtly motive for events sufficiently explained by law and vengeance. Yet the Regent was known to be careless of morals and tenacious of possession; and several persons afterward maintained that Antoine’s ruin was determined less by the blood of a stockbroker than by the jealousy of a prince. We state the rumor as it circulated, without undertaking to affirm it.

Warnings were not wanting. An elderly kinswoman, remarkable for religious austerity and what the age termed second sight, sent for him and declared that she had dreamed of the Van Horn arms covered by a black cloth. “Leave Paris,” said she; “there is blood upon your path.”

The Count smiled, kissed her hand, and answered that blood upon the path of a soldier was no extraordinary portent.

A more practical admonition came from one of his cousins at court, who informed him that his name had been mentioned with displeasure at the Palais Royal and that officers had received orders to observe his conduct.

“If he lingers, he is lost!” exclaimed the cousin to those present.

Antoine, on hearing the expression, took it for an affront to his courage. “A Van Horn,” said he, “may be driven from a battlefield by command of his sovereign, but not from a city by the gossip of antechambers.”

Thus the fated man remained.

Among the brokers enriched by the Mississippi frenzy was a Jew whose name has been recorded variously as Isaac, Israel, or Jacob de la Motte. He dealt largely in shares and jewels, carried considerable sums upon his person, and was accused—as every successful financier of that distracted time was accused—of having made his wealth by the ruin of gentlemen. With this man Antoine and his associates had transactions. Whether the Count had sold him securities, pledged diamonds, or entrusted him with a rouleau of gold, cannot be established; but a quarrel arose concerning money, and an appointment was made at a tavern in a narrow street not far from the Rue Quincampoix.

The prosecution afterward represented this appointment as a deliberate snare. According to its witnesses, Lestang, De Mille, and Van Horn had learned that the broker would arrive with a well-filled pocketbook; they engaged a private chamber, placed servants out of the way, and agreed upon their several parts. Antoine, whose illustrious name would disarm suspicion, was to conduct the conversation; Lestang was to guard the door; and De Mille was to strike the fatal blows. The booty, consisting of notes, shares, and gold, was then to be divided.

Another version of the story was widely circulated among the Count’s relatives and found support in some circumstances of the evidence. By this account Antoine went merely to demand restitution of money which the broker had obtained through a fraudulent bargain. Lestang and De Mille accompanied him because they too had claims, or professed to have them. The interview became violent. The broker denied the debt, laughed at the Count’s threats, and, what was more dangerous than all the rest, taunted him with his dismissal from the army and his former confinement. Antoine, transported beyond reason, drew his sword and wounded him. De Mille, seeing the man fall and believing that murder had already been committed, completed the crime for the sake of plunder; while Lestang, horrified or cowardly, fled.

A third relation, less favorable to the Count yet inconsistent with premeditated assassination, stated that all three intended robbery, but not death; that the broker resisted and cried for assistance; and that each struck in the confusion. Such are the perplexities which attend a judicial tragedy when fear, family influence, political interest, and the hope of pardon have all labored to corrupt the truth.

What is certain is that, at about nine in the evening, cries were heard from the private chamber. The tavern keeper and two servants ran toward it, but the door was held from within. A moment afterward Lestang escaped by a rear passage. When entrance was gained, the broker lay bleeding upon the floor. De Mille was attempting to conceal a pocketbook beneath his coat; Count Antoine stood with a bloody sword in his hand, loudly calling for a surgeon. According to one witness, he exclaimed, “The villain has murdered him!” pointing to De Mille. According to another, he cried, “We are all undone!” The dying man tried to speak, but blood choked his utterance. He expired before the surgeon arrived.

The Count and De Mille were arrested. Lestang disappeared so completely that some believed the police had permitted his escape in return for information; others that he reached Holland; and a few asserted that his body was afterward found in the Seine. No proof was produced for any of these suppositions.

Antoine’s behavior in prison varied between lofty confidence and furious despair. He admitted having wounded the broker, but denied all foreknowledge of robbery or murder. He declared that De Mille had delivered the mortal strokes and taken the pocketbook; that he himself had attempted to protect the fallen man; and that the sword in his hand was stained because he had interposed it between De Mille’s weapon and the victim. When it was pointed out that he had confessed to an earlier wound, he answered with impatience that a gentleman might chastise a fraudulent scoundrel without intending to kill him.

De Mille, perceiving that the Count’s family might save one prisoner and abandon the other, resolved that they should stand or fall together. He charged Antoine with arranging the meeting, giving the signal, and promising protection through his rank. Under examination he varied in particulars, but adhered to the essential accusation that the Count had been privy to the design.

The news spread rapidly through Paris and produced a sensation out of proportion even to the atrocity of the crime. The victim had been a person engaged in the great financial system upon which the credit of the Regency then trembled; to leave his murder unpunished would be to alarm every broker, capitalist, and holder of royal paper. Law’s adherents demanded exemplary justice. They represented the offence as an aristocratic assault upon public credit—a nobleman imagining he might cancel a debt by stabbing the creditor. Those hostile to the Mississippi scheme, on the contrary, spoke of the slain man as an Israelitish adventurer whose frauds had provoked a disordered youth. Thus even the blood upon the tavern floor became an argument in political economy.

The Van Horn family assembled in consternation. At first they endeavored to have Antoine removed from ordinary jurisdiction and confined as insane. Physicians were found who could testify to his former mental alienation, his visions, his chains, and the cruelties of Van Wert. Yet this plea was attended by humiliations almost as great, in their opinion, as those of a criminal conviction. To proclaim madness in one branch was to cast suspicion upon the whole genealogical tree; and certain prospective marriages might be endangered by an official acknowledgment of hereditary derangement. Nevertheless, necessity conquered pride, and memorials were prepared.

The magistrates proved immovable. The Count had transacted business, contracted debts, gambled, intrigued, and defended himself with considerable subtlety; they would not pronounce him incapable merely because contradiction enraged him. His relatives then sought to influence the judges through patronage, marriage, ecclesiastical authority, and those innumerable private obligations by which justice was at that time publicly denied to be affected and privately conducted. But the opposite interest was stronger. The financiers desired a great example; ministers wished to reassure the market; and the Regent, whether from policy, resentment, or a rare zeal for impartial law, let it be understood that rank must not interpose.

The trial was conducted with a severity which the Count’s friends called persecution and his enemies necessity. Witnesses were confronted; the bloodstained weapons were produced; De Mille repeated his accusation; and Antoine, losing command of himself under a captious interrogation, poured invectives upon accusers and judges alike. This outbreak injured him more than evidence might have done. To some it proved insanity; to others, guilt; to the tribunal, contempt.

Sentence was pronounced: Laurent de Mille and Count Antoine Joseph Van Horn were to be put to the question, and afterward broken alive upon the wheel.

When this judgment became known, the aristocracy was seized with an agitation seldom produced by the fate of an individual. It was not merely that a young man connected with many powerful houses was to perish by a servile punishment; the wheel itself appeared to be lifted above every noble escutcheon in Europe. Ladies discovered kinship with Van Horn through ancestors dead two centuries. German princes sent protests. Chapters of noble canonesses examined their statutes and found that the public execution of a relation might exclude descendants from admission. Heralds were consulted concerning the effect of judicial infamy upon collateral branches; and one grave genealogist calculated that fifty-seven illustrious families would receive some diminution of honor.

There was grief also—deep, sincere, and honorable grief—but it moved in strange companionship with vanity. The unfortunate youth’s approaching agony drew tears; the anticipated blank in heraldic ensigns drew almost as many. Several noblemen who had scarcely spoken to Antoine during his prosperity now declared themselves prepared to die for him; and the petition for his life became, for a season, a fashionable certificate of ancient blood.

A noble conclave was held at the Hôtel of one of the principal families. Fifty-seven relations, or persons claiming relationship, affixed their names to an appeal addressed to the Regent. They represented that the Count’s mind had been destroyed by barbarous imprisonment; that his house had faithfully served the crowns of Europe; that the evidence of premeditation rested chiefly upon a confessed accomplice; and that, whatever punishment justice demanded, the wheel was incompatible with the privileges of his birth. Their language, though supplicatory, was weighted with the consciousness of caste.

A deputation of the most eminent among them waited upon the Regent at the Palais Royal. He received them with that perfect courtesy which princes employ when resolved to concede nothing. The chief petitioner spoke at length of the antiquity of the Van Horns, their alliances, their services, and the indelible disgrace which a public execution would bring upon so many honorable names.

The Regent listened, then replied:

“Messieurs, the disgrace consists in the crime, rather than in the punishment.”

The sentence, concise and polished, was repeated throughout Paris before evening. Philosophers admired its justice; men of finance applauded its courage; the nobles considered it an insult to every order founded upon hereditary distinction.

The deputation renewed its entreaties. They did not, or could not, ask that De Mille should be spared. Their charity stopped at the frontier of equality. They urged that Antoine’s diseased mind rendered him less guilty; that he had not delivered the fatal blows; that the execution of a count upon the wheel would confound ranks which the monarchy itself had ordained. One gray-haired prince knelt. Others followed his example. The Regent, visibly moved or weary of the scene, at length raised them and declared that the law must have its victim, but that he would commute the punishment of Count Van Horn to decapitation.

“Upon the word of a prince?” demanded the elder of the deputation.

“Upon my word,” replied the Regent. “He shall not suffer the wheel.”

The petitioners withdrew with gratitude. Every thing was believed to be secured. Beheading, though death, was the death of a nobleman; the body would not be mutilated before the populace; the escutcheons might retain their quarterings; and the condemned youth, if innocent of premeditation, would at least escape the most barbarous extremity of an unjust judgment.

Word of the promise was carried to the prison. Antoine received it with a composure which surprised his visitors. He had alternately hoped for complete pardon and declared himself indifferent to life; but when informed that he must die by the sword, he merely asked whether the Regent’s written order had arrived.

“It will be here before five o’clock,” said his kinsman.

“Then,” answered he, “I shall believe it at five.”

His relatives, fearing even now some accident, offered him another means of escape. Poison was conveyed into his chamber, either in a ring or a small vial hidden within a reliquary. The act was defended as compassion, though family pride undoubtedly mingled with it. A private death would spare him the scaffold and them the spectacle.

Antoine rejected it.

“I will not confess murder by killing myself,” he said. “I struck the man in anger; I did not plan his death, nor did I take his money. Let those who condemn me answer for my blood.”

A priest who attended him afterward affirmed that he maintained this distinction with his dying asseveration. He confessed the sins of a dissolute life, his hatred, pride, and uncontrolled violence; but he would not confess to premeditated assassination. Whether this persistence arose from truth or from the last obstinacy of his nature belongs to that tribunal where accomplices cannot bargain and judges cannot be influenced.

The day of execution came. From an early hour the streets leading to the appointed place were filled with people. The finances of the kingdom, the privileges of the noblesse, the cruelty of the law, and the Regent’s promise had made the spectacle a public controversy. Soldiers kept the avenues. At the prison the executioner and his assistants waited for final instructions. The written commutation had not arrived.

At eleven o’clock the Count’s relatives sent a messenger to the Palais Royal. He returned with assurance that the order was being prepared. At noon another was dispatched. No letter came. An hour after midday a report reached the prison that the Regent had gone to council and was not to be disturbed.

The magistrates declared that they possessed only the sentence of the court and could recognize no verbal promise. The relatives protested, threatened, implored, and called upon witnesses who had heard the Regent’s word. The officers answered that five o’clock was the appointed time and that justice could not wait upon conversations in a palace.

Antoine was told that the order had been delayed. Those near him expected an outbreak. He grew very pale, but remained calm.

“I have been deceived before by keepers,” said he. “It seems princes differ from them only in the elegance of their chambers.”

De Mille, less sustained by pride, fell into abject terror. He offered new confessions, accused Lestang, retracted former statements, and begged that Antoine’s family should save him also. No one listened.

Toward five the prisoners were brought forth. When the Count beheld the wheel and understood that the promised commutation had failed, his composure deserted him for one fearful instant. He struggled against the guards and called upon the Regent by name. Then, mastering himself, he asked whether no letter had arrived.

“None, monsieur,” answered an officer.

“Then let it be remembered,” said Antoine, “that I die by the broken word of a prince.”

The executioner, who appears in this tragedy with more regard for obligation than many persons above him, whispered that he would shorten the suffering if permitted. But the sentence prescribed its forms. The prisoners underwent the question, and were then conveyed to the scaffold. De Mille continued to cry for mercy. Antoine addressed the spectators, though only fragments of his words were heard. He denied having intended the broker’s death; acknowledged that his own violence had brought him to ruin; forgave his judges with an exception, it is said, for him who had promised; and commended his soul to God.

The wheel was prepared.

We shall not dwell upon those details which the taste of the age preserved with disgusting exactness. It is enough to say that the punishment was inflicted in public, that the limbs were broken successively, and that the condemned were afterward exposed upon the wheels. De Mille died first, though some accounts reverse the order. Antoine endured longer than had been expected. His eyes, whose wild brilliance had so often alarmed his companions, remained fixed upon the sky. A priest held up the crucifix. At last the executioner, either by secret permission or merciful disobedience, administered the coup de grace.

Scarcely had life departed when another scene of aristocratic pride and genuine sorrow commenced. The Count’s noble relatives, who had waited near the place in mourning coaches, came forward in person to claim his remains. They would not permit the common assistants to remove him. Men bearing names renowned in war and diplomacy ascended the bloodstained scaffold, loosened the mutilated body from the wheel, wrapped it in a rich mantle, and carried it upon their own shoulders. The multitude, which had been noisy during the preparations, became silent. Whatever might have been the guilt of Antoine Van Horn, the spectacle of so many aged and illustrious persons rendering the last offices to their dishonored kinsman affected even those who had demanded his punishment.

The body was conveyed for burial under guard, lest popular curiosity disturb the procession. No herald proclaimed his titles; no armorial banners were displayed; yet the very absence of ceremony gave the funeral a darker magnificence. Behind the bier walked princes, counts, ecclesiastics, and officers, each conscious that he was protesting not only against cruelty but against the humiliation of his order.

The indignation which followed was violent and extensive. From Flanders came letters accusing the French government of perfidy. German courts spoke of the execution as an outrage upon noble right. In Paris the Regent’s defenders maintained that a prince could not annul a legal sentence by words uttered under emotional pressure; his enemies answered that the greater his authority, the more sacred his promise. Some asserted that the written commutation had actually been signed but purposely delayed by persons connected with the Mississippi system. Others declared that the Regent, repenting his clemency, gave secret orders that the execution should proceed. The truth was never made public.

The elder Count Van Horn had remained away from Paris, whether from illness, grief, or the fear that his presence might increase the scandal. When he learned the manner of his brother’s death, he shut himself for several days from all company. He blamed himself for the first confinement, though he had intended it for protection, and caused masses to be celebrated for Antoine in churches throughout his estates.

The property of the condemned, confiscated by law, was afterward offered to him through the Regent’s favor. He refused it. His answer, preserved in more than one family archive, was severe:

“I will not receive as a gift from His Royal Highness that which has been taken from my brother by a justice dishonored in its execution. The estates may be confiscated; the broken word cannot be restored.”

This reply closed all intercourse between the house of Van Horn and the Palais Royal. It was repeated with admiration by those very nobles who had once avoided the unfortunate Antoine in their saloons.

Thus ended a life in which it is difficult to separate crime from madness, and guilt from persecution. That Count Van Horn was violent, dissolute, and fatally incapable of governing his passions cannot be denied. That he wounded the broker is admitted by his own declaration. Whether he entered the tavern as a robber, whether he consented to murder, or whether an insulting word awakened the frenzy created years before in a Flemish dungeon, remains doubtful. De Mille accused him; but De Mille spoke under the shadow of the wheel. The relatives defended him; but their zeal was inflamed by the threatened dishonor of their escutcheons. The judges condemned him; but political powers demanded an example. Every witness carried an interest, every advocate a prejudice, and every institution concerned in the affair sought less the naked truth than the preservation of its own authority.

There remains, however, one circumstance upon which doubt has never wholly rested. A promise was solemnly given that the Count should not suffer the wheel, and he suffered it. The broken limbs of the unfortunate youth became, in public memory, less an evidence of judicial severity than a monument to princely faithlessness. The broker was forgotten; De Mille became a name in a record; Lestang vanished into rumor; even the financial madness which had surrounded the crime passed away, leaving ruined families and worthless paper. But the words spoken at the scaffold endured:

“I die by the broken word of a prince.”

They followed the Regent through the remainder of his splendid and disordered administration. They were repeated when men discussed his talents, his pleasures, his tolerance, and his contempt for forms. They entered Flemish chronicles and German genealogies; they were whispered in noble assemblies whenever royal justice came into conflict with hereditary privilege. Thus does a single act of perfidy sometimes outweigh many acts of wisdom; for cruelty may be attributed to law, and error to circumstance, but a violated promise attaches itself to the man.

As for Count Antoine Joseph Van Horn, he remains one of those melancholy figures whom history neither absolves nor wholly condemns. Born to privilege, he was ruined by the passions which privilege had failed to discipline; subjected to cruelty, he became the creature whom cruelty pretended to restrain; restored by affection, he fled from its vigilance; warned by friends, omens, and experience, he persisted in the path marked for his destruction. At every turn there appeared an avenue of escape, and at every turn pride closed it. Yet when the final hour arrived, even his enemies might have wished that justice had been less barbarous, his kindred less occupied with heraldry, and the ruler of France more mindful that the word of a prince should be the last refuge of the condemned, not the final instrument of his betrayal.

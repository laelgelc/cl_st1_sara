### The Road Beyond the House

At night, when the house had settled and the pipes had stopped knocking, Hugh Walker went out to walk.

He did not tell Winifred where he went. She did not ask. At first she had come to the bedroom door in her wrapper and had watched him putting on his coat.

“You will take cold,” she said.

“I need air.”

“You had air all afternoon. You walked home from the college.”

“That was not a walk.”

She had looked at him for a moment and then gone back to bed. Marriage was marriage to her. A man went out or stayed in. He ate what was put before him, earned money, came home, slept beside his wife. If he was silent, he was tired. If he was restless, there was something wrong with his digestion.

Hugh went through the college town and beyond its last thin street of houses. Loose shutters banged on one of the empty summer homes near the river. The sound followed him, first loud, then faint, then loud again when the wind changed. He crossed the railway tracks and took the road between the fields.

It was November. The corn had been cut, and the stubble lay gray under the moon. Far off, a red light burned above a crossing. Hugh walked toward it but never reached it. The road turned. The light moved behind a hill.

He walked for hours.

Nothing happened.

That was the trouble. Nothing happened in the house. Nothing happened at the college. Nothing happened in his own body or in the minds of the men with whom he talked. There were lectures, meals, examinations, committee meetings, visits from neighbors, illnesses of the children, repairs to the roof. One thing followed another, but nothing happened.

He was forty-two and had a position that other men wanted. He had written a small book on algebraic forms. It had been praised by three men in the East and purchased by fewer than three hundred people. His salary had recently been increased. The house on Walnut Street was large enough. There were six rooms downstairs and five upstairs. In winter, when the furnace worked, every room was warm.

He had a wife, two children, books, a garden, and a woman named Clara who came every morning to cook and clean. He belonged to all of them. They belonged to him.

The thought made him afraid.

There were nights when he stopped in the road and looked back toward the town. Its lights made a dull stain against the sky. In that stain were the college buildings, his office, the house, the room where Winifred slept, the two small beds of the children. All of it waited for him. It was like an animal that knew his smell.

Belong, the animal said.

He turned and walked farther into the country.

In the daytime he stood before students and wrote symbols on a blackboard. His hands became dusty. He spoke carefully and sometimes with force. Young men bent over notebooks. Young women sat upright and looked at him with an expression they had learned in school. He could lead them through a proof. He could show that a proposition followed from certain assumptions and could not fail to follow.

Afterward he often remained alone in the room.

A proof was a clean thing. It began somewhere and ended somewhere. Life did neither.

One afternoon in February, after the snow had become dirty along the streets, Hugh saw Mary Cochran in the reading room of the college library. She sat by a window, wearing a dark dress with a narrow white collar. A book lay open before her, but she was looking at the snow.

He knew her slightly. She attended his class twice a week and did poor work. Her answers were sometimes absurd, but she did not seem ashamed of them. When called upon she rose, spoke, and sat down. There was no pleading in her face.

He stopped beside the table.

“You do not like mathematics,” he said.

She turned from the window. “No.”

“Why do you take my course?”

“It was at that hour.”

He laughed. She did not.

Outside, a college boy ran across the yard and threw a handful of snow at another boy. The snow struck the brick wall. Mary watched.

“You are looking for something,” Hugh said.

She glanced at the book. “I suppose I should be looking at this.”

“No. Not that.”

She waited. Her face was thin and brown from a childhood lived outdoors. There was a small scar near her chin. Hugh had never noticed it before.

He wanted to say that she was free. The words would have been foolish. She was a student, dependent on an aunt who lived in another town and sent her money grudgingly. She worked in the dining hall. She wore the same two dresses. There were facts about her life as there were facts about everyone’s life. Still, the facts had not closed around her. He thought he could see the open places between them.

“Come to our house some evening,” he said. “My wife would be glad to know you.”

This was a lie. He did not know whether Winifred would be glad. He said it because it made the invitation respectable.

Mary closed the book. “Why?”

The question pleased him.

“To get away from books,” he said.

That evening he mentioned Mary at supper. Winifred was cutting meat for their son Robert, who was seven. Alice, aged nine, had upset her milk and was wiping it with the edge of the tablecloth.

“A girl in my class has no people here,” Hugh said.

“No people?” Winifred asked.

“She lives in a boardinghouse.”

“Many girls do.”

“I asked her to visit us.”

Winifred looked up. There was no jealousy in her face. Hugh almost wished for it.

“That will be pleasant,” she said. “Alice needs someone young to talk to.”

“I am young,” Alice said.

“You are a child.”

“I am nine.”

“Eat your potatoes.”

Mary came on Thursday.

Clara opened the door and called from the hall that a young lady had arrived. Hugh was in the sitting room with a journal on his knees. He heard Winifred’s quick steps overhead and then the children running down the stairs.

He did not rise at once.

Through the doorway he saw Mary remove her coat. She wore the dark dress. Snow had melted in her hair. Alice reached for her hand without being asked. Robert stood behind his sister and stared.

“You are Professor Walker’s student,” Alice said.

“Yes.”

“Do you know everything he knows?”

“No.”

“Nobody does,” Robert said solemnly.

Mary laughed. The sound was sudden and a little rough. Hugh got up.

Winifred came down, fastening a brooch at her throat. She greeted Mary warmly and took her into the dining room, where tea and small cakes had been arranged. Within ten minutes they were talking of clothes, the college girls, and a neighbor’s new baby. The children leaned against Mary’s chair.

Hugh sat apart and watched.

He had expected to feel something distinct. He did not know what. It was enough that Mary’s hand lay on the table, separate from the other hands. It belonged to no one. She lifted it to brush back her hair. The movement was careless. A free hand, he thought.

The phrase stayed with him.

Mary came again. Then she began to come every week.

Winifred liked her. The children waited at the window on Thursday afternoons. Mary helped Alice sew clothes for a doll and taught Robert to whistle through a blade of grass. When spring came, she worked in the garden with Clara and returned to the house with dirt on her face. She knew the names of weeds and birds. She had been raised on a farm in southern Indiana and had milked cows before dawn.

“She is such a natural girl,” Winifred said. “Not affected like so many of them.”

Hugh disliked the sentence. It put Mary into a kind. He said nothing.

At times he sat with them in the garden. Winifred talked. Mary listened. The children dug holes among the flowers. Beyond the hedge, wagons passed in the street, and from the college tower a bell struck the hour.

Mary’s presence changed the look of familiar things. Hugh saw the worn arm of the garden chair, the pale undersides of leaves, the crooked fence behind the carriage house. He saw Winifred’s face as if it were a face in a crowd. He saw his children move, grow tired, quarrel, forget.

For a little while the house had doors.

He could enter or leave. The rooms did not seem to know him. The chairs were only chairs. The books belonged to no one while Mary stood near them, turning her head to listen to a sound in the street.

He tried to understand why.

It was not love. He told himself this at once and often. Love was another name for ownership. Men and women touched, made promises, established claims, and called the result happiness. He wanted nothing from Mary. He wished only to look at her while she remained outside the net.

One afternoon she fell asleep on the grass under the apple tree. The children had gone into the house for lemonade. Winifred was speaking with Clara in the kitchen. Hugh sat on the porch.

Mary’s arm was bent under her head. Her mouth was slightly open. A bee moved among the clover near her cheek.

Hugh watched the bee. He did not look directly at the girl.

Something in him spoke.

Go to her.

For what?

Wake her.

For what?

Tell her.

Tell her what?

The questions went on. They had no answer. They were like a dog scratching at a closed door.

He rose and went into the house.

By June Mary had become one of them.

The change occurred without an event. There was no day Hugh could point to. She had begun by arriving at the front door in her dark dress. Then her hat appeared on a peg in the hall. A pair of old gloves lay among the children’s things. She left a book on the piano. She came without knocking and called to Winifred from the kitchen. Once, after a rain, Hugh found her shoes drying beside the stove.

It was the shoes that disturbed him.

They were small, muddy, ordinary shoes. They stood beside Alice’s boots and Robert’s broken sandals. Hugh looked at them for a long time.

The house had taken her in.

That evening he went walking, though the air was hot. Beyond the town, fireflies shone in the ditches. The corn was already high enough to hide the ground. He followed the road until sweat soaked his collar.

He had wanted Mary near him because she did not belong. Now she belonged. Winifred said “our Mary.” Alice cried when Mary was late. Robert had asked whether Mary could live with them forever.

Forever.

The word was a wall.

Hugh tried to be absent when Mary came. He stayed at the college or shut himself in the room he called his study. He heard her voice below. It passed through the floorboards and entered his thought. Sometimes she sang with Alice at the piano. Her singing was not good. She lost the tune and laughed.

He sat among his books.

There were shelves on three walls, heavy tables, papers in neat piles, a green-shaded lamp. This room had once seemed his own. Now it was the center of the prison. The books were bars. He had built them himself, one by one, and had then praised their strength.

He pulled out a volume and read a page. The words came into his mind and died there. He read the page again.

Nothing happened.

In August Winifred took the children to visit her mother. Hugh remained because the college term would soon begin. Mary had gone home to her aunt for several weeks. The house became quiet.

He expected relief. Instead, the rooms expanded around him. At meals Clara moved silently between the kitchen and dining room. She was a broad, middle-aged woman with strong hands. She had worked for the Walkers for eleven years and knew more about the household than Hugh did.

“You don’t eat,” she said one morning.

“I am not hungry.”

“You walk too much.”

“How do you know?”

“I see your shoes.”

He looked down. Dust had whitened them.

Clara set a plate of eggs before him. She did not smile.

“Road won’t tell you nothing,” she said.

He was irritated because the sentence seemed wise. People were always being wise when they knew nothing. He ate the eggs to end the conversation.

At night the empty house made small sounds. Wood contracted. A branch rubbed against a window. Somewhere a shutter struck the wall, paused, and struck again.

Hugh lay in bed and thought of Winifred. He tried to remember the first year of their marriage.

She had been twenty-two. Her hair was thick and bright, and she had a habit of standing before a mirror with her arms raised, fastening it at the back of her head. He had watched from the bed. He had believed that the body before him would open life.

It had opened nothing. No, that was not true. It had opened years—rooms of years, one inside another. There had been Robert’s birth, Alice’s fever, Winifred’s operation, the death of his father, the publication of his book. They had moved from an apartment to the house on Walnut Street. They had bought carpets, curtains, beds. They had planted trees.

All facts.

Life must be somewhere among the facts, Hugh thought. He had missed it because he had been thinking.

When Winifred returned, he was gentle with her for several days. He carried her bag upstairs. He listened while she described her mother’s health. At night he put his hand on her shoulder.

She turned toward him willingly.

Her willingness filled him with despair.

Afterward she slept. Hugh lay awake, feeling the weight of her arm across his chest. It was a light arm, but he could not move under it.

Mary returned to town in September.

She came to the house on the first Sunday evening of the term. The children shouted when they saw her. Winifred kissed her. Hugh remained by the fireplace.

Mary had changed. Her skin was darker from the summer. She wore her hair higher, and there was a new fullness in her body. The change was slight but terrible to him. It was as if a tree he had observed in winter had put out leaves while he was not looking.

“You are well,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Did you have a good summer?”

“No.”

He waited, but she did not explain.

During supper Mary was quiet. Winifred spoke of the college and of a young instructor who had rented the house next door. Alice told a long story about a kitten. Robert kicked the table leg until his father ordered him to stop.

Afterward they went to the sitting room. Mary took up the book she had left on the piano months before.

“I forgot this,” she said.

“What is it?” Winifred asked.

“A novel.”

“Is it good?”

“I don’t know yet.”

Mary sat by the lamp and began to read.

Hugh watched her. The old anger came upon him. It was not anger at Mary. It was anger at the bent head, at the printed page, at the stillness of the room. Winifred sewed. Alice cut pictures from a magazine. Robert lay on the rug with wooden soldiers. Everyone had entered a separate little chamber.

Mary read.

He crossed the room and stood before her.

“What is in the book?” he asked.

She looked up. “People.”

“People doing what?”

“I have only read fifty pages.”

“Living?”

“I suppose.”

“You suppose.”

Winifred glanced toward him. “Hugh, do not examine her.”

“I am not examining her.”

He took the book from Mary’s hands.

She stared. “I was reading that.”

“Yes.”

The fire was small. It had been lit because the evening was cool, though winter had not come. Hugh opened the stove door and threw the book onto the coals.

For a moment nothing occurred. Then the paper darkened at the edges. A flame took hold and ran up a page.

Winifred stood. “Hugh!”

He pushed the book deeper with the poker.

“There,” he said. “That is what should be done with them.”

Mary’s face had gone pale.

“With all of them,” he continued. “Books, thoughts, explanations. We hide behind them. We read about men and women living, and we think that is life. We make words. We make systems. We sit in rooms while the blood turns cold.”

“Hugh, stop,” Winifred said.

“No. Why should I stop? I have stopped all my life.”

The children were watching. Robert had gathered his soldiers into both hands.

Hugh saw their faces and hated them for being afraid. Then he hated himself for making them afraid. The two hatreds met and became one thing.

Mary got up.

“I should go,” she said.

“Your book—” Winifred began.

“It doesn’t matter.”

“It does matter. Hugh will buy another.”

“No,” Hugh said.

Mary put on her coat. Alice started toward her, but Mary did not see. She went out into the evening.

The burned pages curled in the stove.

Later, when the children were in bed, Winifred came into Hugh’s study.

“What happened to you?” she asked.

“Nothing.”

“You frightened them.”

“I know.”

“You insulted Mary.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He looked at the shelves.

“She was reading.”

Winifred waited for him to say more. When he did not, she sat down heavily.

“You need rest,” she said. “Perhaps you should see someone.”

“A doctor?”

“Yes.”

“Marriage was not enough. Books were not enough. Now a doctor.”

“I don’t understand you.”

“No.”

She began to cry. Quietly, without covering her face. Hugh watched. He had seen her cry at funerals and when the children were ill. He had not often seen her cry because of him.

He wanted to go to her. He could have crossed the few feet of carpet and put his hand on her hair. The act was simple. It was available to him.

He did not move.

At last Winifred rose and left the room.

Mary did not return the following week, or the week after that.

Alice asked about her every day. Winifred made excuses. There was work at the college. Mary was not feeling well. The weather was bad.

Hugh said nothing.

He saw Mary in the halls. She passed him with a nod. In class she sat near the back and kept her eyes on her notebook. Her work improved. She solved the problems correctly and without interest.

Her avoidance gave him satisfaction, but it was an ugly satisfaction and he knew it. At least, he thought, she had gone outside the house. At least the door had closed behind her.

Then one afternoon he came home and found her in the kitchen.

She and Clara were rolling dough. Flour lay on Mary’s hands and cheek. Alice sat at the table cutting circles with a drinking glass. Winifred looked in from the dining room.

“Hugh,” she said, too brightly, “Mary has come back.”

He stopped in the doorway.

Mary met his eyes. There was fear in hers, but there was also a challenge. She had returned in spite of him.

“Good,” he said.

He went upstairs.

The house had won. It had reached into the college, taken Mary by the arm, and brought her back. Soon she would be no different from Winifred. She would choose a man, or be chosen. There would be promises. Furniture would be purchased. Children would come. Her hand would lie on someone’s table. Her shoes would stand by someone’s stove.

He sat on the edge of the bed.

Was that life? Perhaps it was. Perhaps his horror of belonging was only the fear of a weak man. Trees belonged to the soil. Rivers belonged to their banks. A body belonged to gravity and death.

The inner voice answered at once.

A tree does not say forever.

“A tree says nothing,” Hugh said aloud.

From below came the sound of Mary laughing.

She returned several times. Hugh treated her politely. He asked about her studies. Once he gave her a book to replace the one he had burned.

She held it without opening it.

“Do you want me to burn this one too?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then why take it?”

“Because you gave it to me.”

The words angered him. She had made even his apology into a bond.

Winter came early. Snow filled the garden and made round white shapes of the shrubs. Hugh’s nighttime walks became more difficult, but he continued them. He wrapped a scarf around his face and went beyond the lamps of town.

His footsteps made the only sound. The road was a pale strip between dark fields. Sometimes he saw another figure far ahead—a farmer going home, a boy from the railway, some person with no name. Hugh hurried, wanting to catch up. The figure always turned off or disappeared behind drifting snow.

One evening in December, Mary stayed late.

Winifred had a headache and went upstairs. The children were asleep. Clara had gone home. Hugh sat in his study and heard Mary moving below, putting away a sewing basket, fastening her coat.

He went down.

She stood in the hall beneath the weak electric bulb. Her hand was on the doorknob.

“It is snowing,” he said.

“I know.”

“You should not walk alone.”

“It is only four blocks.”

“I will go with you.”

“That is not necessary.”

He took his coat from the peg.

They went out together.

The snow had stopped. The town was quiet, every sound softened. Light shone behind curtains. A horse stood steaming beside a hitching post near the church.

Hugh and Mary walked without speaking.

At the corner where she should have turned toward her boardinghouse, Hugh took her arm.

“This way,” he said.

“My room is over there.”

“Walk with me.”

“No.”

The refusal was quiet. He tightened his hand.

“I want to show you something.”

“What?”

“The road.”

“I have seen roads.”

He almost laughed. Instead he led her past the last row of houses. She could have pulled away. She did not. Perhaps she was afraid to make a scene. Perhaps she wanted to know what would happen. He did not ask.

They came to a bridge over a narrow frozen creek. The railing was crusted with snow. Beyond it the road went dark between fields.

Hugh stopped.

“This is where I come,” he said.

Mary looked into the darkness. “Why?”

“To get away.”

“From us?”

The word us struck him.

“Yes.”

She drew a breath. “Then why did you bring me?”

He had no answer.

The question stood between them, alive and hungry.

He could see only part of her face. Snow light touched her cheek and the small scar near her chin. She was young. She was not a child, though he had wanted her to remain one because her childhood excused his watching.

He lifted his hand and touched the scar.

Mary stepped back.

“Don’t.”

The word entered him like heat.

He took her shoulders and kissed her.

Her body became rigid. For an instant her mouth was cold and still. Then something changed. It was not consent; he knew that. It was surprise passing into knowledge, fear passing into another fear. Her hands came against his chest. Whether to push him away or to hold herself upright, he could not tell.

He released her.

She stared at him.

There. It had happened.

The world had altered by the width of a breath. Mary’s face was no longer the face she had carried from the farm, through the college rooms, into his garden. Something had entered it. He had put it there.

He felt triumphant and ashamed.

“Now go home,” he said.

She did not move.

“Go.”

“Why did you do that?”

“To make you understand.”

“Understand what?”

“That you must not come back to the house.”

Her mouth opened. She seemed unable to speak.

“You hear me? Never come back. Not to Winifred, not to the children. Stay away.”

“But why?”

“Because they will take you. The house will take you. You will belong to it.”

“You brought me there.”

“Yes.”

“You asked me.”

“Yes.”

She looked at him as if she had not seen him before.

He wanted that look. He wanted her to know him and to be free of him. Yet even as he thought this, he felt the pressure of her shoulders under his hands. He had wanted to leave no mark and had made one.

Mary turned and walked toward town.

He called after her. “Do not tell Winifred.”

She did not answer.

Her figure moved from darkness into the reach of a streetlamp. For a moment she stood out clearly, black against the snow. Then she passed on.

Hugh remained by the bridge.

The road beyond him was open. He could walk all night. He could go from town to town, over hills, through sleeping villages, until no one knew his name. But the road was only a road. Clara had told him so.

He went home.

Winifred was awake. She sat in bed with a shawl around her shoulders.

“Where have you been?”

“Walking.”

“With Mary?”

“I took her home.”

“That was kind.”

“Yes.”

He undressed in the dark and lay beside her. After a few minutes she put her hand on his arm.

“Hugh?”

“What?”

“Did she say anything? She seemed upset when she left.”

“She had a headache.”

“Oh.”

Winifred accepted this. She turned on her side and soon slept.

For several days Hugh waited.

He expected accusation, tears, some event. Nothing happened. Mary attended his class. She did not look at him. Her face was composed.

On Thursday Alice sat at the front window until dark.

“She isn’t coming,” Winifred said.

“Why not?”

“She sent word. She has too much work.”

“She can come Sunday.”

“Perhaps.”

On Sunday Mary did not come.

Weeks passed. The children ceased to ask every day. Winifred wrote Mary a note and received a courteous answer. There were examinations, snowstorms, faculty dinners. Robert developed a cough. Alice tore her best dress. The furnace failed and was repaired.

Ordinary life closed over the event.

Once Hugh saw Mary crossing the college yard with a young man. The man carried her books. They were not touching. At the library steps Mary said something, and both laughed.

Hugh stopped behind a tree.

So it would happen. If not that man, another. She would love and then belong. She would make a house and be made by it. Locks, walls, children, chairs, hands at the table. There was no saving anyone.

Still, he told himself, he would not be the one.

He repeated the thought until it became almost peaceful. He had awakened her, yes, but he had refused the claim. He had opened a door and sent her through it. What she found beyond was not his doing.

The lie was small and warm. He held it close.

That night he went walking.

The sky was clear, and the snow shone over the fields. Behind him the town rested under its roofs. Ahead, the road ran pale and empty.

Hugh walked on and on.

At times he thought he heard footsteps following. He stopped, and they stopped. He turned, but no one was there.

The house was far behind, yet he could feel it. Winifred slept. The children breathed in their rooms. His books stood in rows. Mary’s muddy shoes were gone from beside the stove, but he saw them still.

He began to walk faster.

The road curved, dipped, and rose toward a low hill. He believed that from the top he would see something—a light, another town, a figure moving freely across the snow.

He reached the hill.

There was only more road.

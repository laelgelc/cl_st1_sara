## THE OPEN GRAVE OF SHACKLETON

On the uttermost skirt of Shackleton Moor, where the heather, brown as an old shroud, descended in long and melancholy undulations toward a marsh whose black pools reflected neither moon nor cloud, there stood, at the period of which I write, a low stone cottage, so deeply sunk beneath a bank of peat and so overshadowed by two ragged ash-trees that a traveller might have passed within twenty yards of its smoking chimney and supposed the place to be no habitation of Christian creatures, but rather some deserted fold or rude sepulchral house left by those elder people whose cairns still break the solitude of the hills.

A narrow path, trodden into mire by nailed shoes, led from its door to the moor. Behind it lay a patch of sour earth, where cabbages struggled among nettles, and beyond that a broken wall, over which the tower of Shackleton Church could be seen on clear days, square and grey, rising from a company of yews and beeches.

The cottage belonged to Tom Chuff.

He was a poacher. He was a drunkard. He beat his wife.

These three articles comprised, in the opinion of his neighbours, the greater part of his character; and I cannot say that their judgment was unjust. He was a broad-shouldered, shock-headed fellow, with a low brow, a broken nose, and little bloodshot eyes which looked from under their swollen lids as sparks look through the bars of a dying furnace. He could set a snare more cunningly than any man for ten miles round, could walk the keeper’s covert without stirring a pheasant, and knew every rabbit-road, fox-earth, reed-bed, and gap in the stone walls between Shackleton and the distant plantations of Sir Harry Vane.

But honest labour he abhorred. He would tramp six miles through rain to steal what might be purchased with an hour’s wage, and sit a whole winter night beside a frozen pond rather than carry peat for his own hearth. Gin, when he could get it, was his sovereign cordial; ale was his daily bread; and if neither were obtainable he became gloomy, suspicious, and dangerous.

His wife, Mary, had once been a comely young woman, with clear eyes and a gentle mouth. Twelve years of wedlock had bowed her shoulders, whitened her cheeks, and taught her to hear every footstep upon the path as a condemned person hears the turning of a key. Their children—two boys and a little girl—had learned the same miserable discipline. If Tom entered singing, they fled. If he entered silent, they trembled where they stood; for his silence was commonly worse than his song.

On the night I speak of, the wind had fallen at sunset, leaving behind it an unnatural stillness. It was late in November. The peat fire glowed low upon the hearth, and Mary Chuff sat beside it, mending a torn shirt by the light of a tallow candle. The little girl slept against her knee. The boys crouched in the chimney-corner, sharing between them a crust which their mother had secretly preserved from dinner.

Upon the deal table stood an empty bottle.

Mary looked often toward it, and oftener toward the door.

Nine o’clock sounded from Shackleton tower, the bell coming heavily over the moor.

“Get thee abed, barns,” she whispered.

The elder boy glanced at the door.

“He’ll hear us stir.”

“Thy feyther’s not come yet.”

“He’ll come,” said the child.

There was such certainty in his low voice that Mary looked at him and shivered.

Presently they heard a foot upon the path.

The little girl woke. She did not cry out. None of them cried out. The boys slipped behind the great chair—a black oak structure of cyclopean weight, which had stood in the cottage longer than any living person remembered—and Mary rose, holding the child against her breast.

The latch lifted.

Tom Chuff entered.

His cap was drawn over his eyes. His coat, glittering with drops from the reeds, hung open; there was mud to his knees and a small dead hare beneath his arm. He closed the door carefully, laid the hare upon the table, and stood with his back against the boards.

He said nothing.

Mary waited.

Had he cursed her, she would have known what to do. Had he demanded food, she would have found him some. Had he struck the table or flung the bottle at the wall, the children would have run to the loft. But this dumbness, this heavy and almost judicial scrutiny of the room, confounded them all.

“Tom,” she said at last, “there’s a bit o’ broth.”

He did not answer.

“Tom, art hurt?”

He turned his eyes slowly upon her. There was no rage in them. There was no recognition either. His face, always coarse and unhealthy, had assumed a leaden colour, except around the mouth, where a bluish stain appeared.

He took one step forward.

The dead hare slipped from under his arm.

Then, with neither cry nor gesture, he fell his whole length upon the floor.

Mary screamed. The child screamed with her. The boys came from behind the chair, and all three stood looking at him, afraid even now to touch the hand which had so often fallen upon them.

“Feyther?” said the youngest boy.

There was no movement.

Mary knelt, hesitated, and placed her fingers upon his wrist. She knew little of pulses, but the flesh was cold and the hand lay in hers with a dreadful passivity.

“Run for Doctor Hales,” she said. “Run, Will! Run as if death were after thee!”

The boy needed no second command. He darted out upon the moor, bareheaded and thinly clad, while Mary loosened Tom’s neckcloth and tried, by such poor remedies as she knew, to restore him. She rubbed his hands. She held vinegar beneath his nostrils. She called him by his name, softly at first, then loudly, then with the desperate tenderness of a woman who forgets injuries in the presence of death.

But Tom remained insensible.

Doctor Hales lived nearly two miles away, and more than an hour elapsed before the boy returned with him. The doctor was a round, good-natured gentleman, whose horse, whose coat, and whose opinions were alike accustomed to easy roads; yet he had crossed the mire without complaint, carrying his case under one arm and leading the exhausted child by the hand.

He examined Tom. He listened at his breast. He held a polished glass to the blue lips and watched it for the least cloud of breath.

Mary stood near, twisting her apron.

“Well, sir?”

Doctor Hales did not answer immediately. He tried the heart again, and then looked gravely at the woman.

“How long has he been so?”

“Nigh on two hours.”

“Had he been drinking?”

“I reckon.”

“Poison?”

“Nay, sir. Not by my hand, God knows.”

“I do not suspect you, Mary.”

He remained kneeling for some time. Then he rose.

“There is no perceptible action of the heart.”

“Is he dead?”

The doctor looked down at Tom Chuff’s brutal face, at the scar above his eyebrow, at the clenched hand with its broken nails, and perhaps—being human before he was medical—he thought of the bruises he had dressed upon Mary’s arms and the little girl’s back.

“I fear so,” he said.

It may shock charitable persons to hear that there were those in Shackleton who, when informed of Tom’s ostensible decease, called it a mercy. I think the doctor himself did not regard it wholly as a calamity. Nevertheless, he was a conscientious man, and because certain cases of torpor had been known to counterfeit death, he directed that the body should not be disturbed until morning.

So Tom was laid upon the bed in the lower room, his arms straightened and a cloth placed over his face. The children were taken to a neighbour’s cottage. Doctor Hales departed, promising to return at dawn. Mary alone watched beside the dead.

Dead, I have said.

Yet where, during those black hours, was that rude spirit which had inhabited the body of Tom Chuff?

Attend, and judge.

He believed that he awoke in the churchyard of Shackleton.

There was no interval of dreaming, no gradual restoration of sight or understanding. In one instant there had been darkness, and in the next he was standing upright beside an open grave.

The night was windless. Above him hung a sky so black that it seemed not air but solid ebony, vast and polished; and in it burned myriads and myriads of stars, not softly, as stars appear on a wholesome frosty night, but with a fierce, metallic brilliance. They blinked, and widened, and contracted, as though every one were an eye.

Before him rose the old beech at the northern boundary of the churchyard. Its trunk, smooth and enormous, resembled a pillar sustaining the darkness. Not one leaf stirred upon its branches. Indeed, nothing stirred. The silence was so absolute that Tom heard no breath from his own nostrils and no beating in his breast.

At his feet yawned the grave.

It was freshly dug, deep, narrow, and black. A spade had been left in the mound of earth beside it. The white root of some plant projected from the side like a finger-bone. He looked down, but the bottom could not be seen.

“Who’s there?” he called.

His voice did not travel. It seemed to fall directly to the ground.

Beyond the wall stretched Shackleton Moor, featureless beneath the stars. The church stood on his left, but its tower appeared far taller than by day, and its windows, though unlighted, watched him with a blind malignity.

“Who’s there?” he cried again.

A whistle sounded from the moor.

It was a common signal, consisting of two low notes and one high, used by Tom and certain companions when pursuing their nocturnal business. He knew it well. He had often answered it from a covert while the keeper passed within arm’s length.

Now, however, he did not answer.

The whistle came again.

Two low notes. One high.

Something moved upon the moor.

At first it was no more than a black point set against blackness; yet the stars behind it vanished as it advanced, and thus he knew that a figure was approaching. It came steadily, without haste. He could not distinguish the motion of legs, the swing of arms, or any human feature. It seemed to glide.

Tom’s courage, which had sufficed against keepers, constables, wives, and hungry children, began to fail.

“Speak plain!” he shouted. “What dost want?”

The figure crossed the wall without climbing it.

Then Tom knew the man.

He had known him in childhood by the sound of his tread; known him later by the weight of his fist; known him at last as a swollen corpse carried from a ditch, where he had fallen drunk and frozen.

It was old Abel Chuff, his father.

The apparition wore the same torn coat in which Abel had been buried. His face was grey, his beard clotted, and around his neck there showed a livid band like the mark of a rope. But it was not upon these particulars that Tom gazed. Beside his father walked a dog.

No such dog had ever trodden English earth. It was large as a calf and shaggy as a bear, though lean in the flanks, with a head too broad for its body and ears that stood up like black horns. Its eyes resembled two live coals seen through smoke. When its mouth opened, Tom perceived teeth—not two rows, but many rows, retiring one behind another into a red and bottomless throat.

“Feyther,” Tom whispered.

The dead man stopped at the edge of the grave.

“Thou’rt late, lad,” he said.

His lips scarcely moved; nevertheless the words were heard as if uttered within Tom’s skull.

“Late for what?”

“For coming home.”

“This bean’t home.”

Abel Chuff smiled. In life, that smile had commonly preceded an act of cruelty.

“’Tis home enough for Chuffs.”

The dog lowered its head and growled. The sound did not resemble the growl of a living beast. It was like the turning of a great millstone under the earth.

Tom backed toward the grave.

“Nay,” he said. “Keep t’ brute off. I’ve done nowt to thee.”

“Nowt?” repeated his father.

Then there began about them, very faintly at first, a multitude of sounds: a woman sobbing, a child begging for bread, the crack of a cudgel, the squeal of a snared hare, the flutter of a dying bird, oaths spoken beside the ale-house fire, and laughter—Tom’s own laughter—thick, exultant, and without pity.

He clapped his hands over his ears, but heard them still.

“Stop ’em!”

“Thou made ’em,” said Abel. “Stop ’em thysen.”

The dog sprang.

Tom recoiled. His heel found emptiness; his arms flew upward; the stars wheeled once above him; and he fell backward into the grave.

Down, down, down!

The narrow walls vanished. He plunged through an abyss in which neither earth nor stone was visible, while darkness streamed upward like a black torrent. He fell for a minute, for an hour, for years. He had time to remember every offence of his life—not in that softened form by which men excuse their own depravity, but exactly as it had appeared to those who suffered it. He felt Mary’s dread when his step approached the door. He felt the hunger of his children when he spent their bread upon gin. He felt the beating heart of a pheasant crushed beneath his hand, the panic of a rabbit struggling in wire, the despair of an old man whose week’s wage Tom had stolen after striking him senseless upon the road.

Down, down, down!

Lights appeared far beneath him—first red points, then wheels, caverns, furnaces, cities of flame. Cries rose from those depths, not one cry but myriads and myriads, some shrill, some hoarse, some scarcely human; and among them he distinguished voices he had known in the ale-house, voices of men who had boasted that death ended all accounts.

“Tom Chuff!” they called. “Room for Tom Chuff!”

He struck ground at last, though the violence of his descent broke no bone. He lay upon a floor of hot iron in an enormous passage resembling the gallery of a mine. Its roof was lost in smoke. On either side opened doorways of red stone, and through these apertures he saw punishments whose particulars I shall not set down, for terror may instruct without becoming loathsome.

Let it suffice that every sin had there become its own torment.

The miser gathered burning coins which melted through his hands and returned always to the heap. The liar fled from innumerable tongues which repeated his falsehoods in voices of thunder. The cruel were made weak and delivered to the cruelty they had taught. The drunkard drank perpetually from a cup that inflamed rather than quenched his thirst; and, as he drank, the faces of his starving household appeared in the liquor and accused him.

Tom was dragged forward, though he saw no hand upon him. Doors opened as he passed. Behind one, Mary sat beside a dead fire with the children around her. Her face was bruised. Her eyes were dry.

“Look at me, Tom,” she said.

He tried to turn away.

“Look at me.”

He looked; and her face altered into the faces of all who had suffered through him.

“Nay!” he cried. “I’ll mend! I’ll gie up drink. I’ll work. I’ll—”

The passage answered with laughter.

A door boomed shut behind him. Another opened before him, and from within came the growling of the black dog.

Then he ran.

He fled through galleries that descended and ascended without order, across bridges flung over gulfs of red vapour, beneath arches on which unknown judgments were written in characters of fire. Sometimes he saw the skirts of his father’s coat turning a corner ahead; sometimes the dog’s eyes appeared behind him; and always, whether he halted or ran, he heard a measured tread approaching.

At length he came to a stair.

It rose, white and narrow, through the smoke. At its summit shone a small light, pale as dawn seen through a cottage window. He climbed, stumbling upon his knees, tearing his hands against the stones, and crying for mercy with every breath.

The stair ended at a gate of iron.

Beyond it was a country he could not clearly see. There seemed to be hills, running water, green boughs, and a brightness which did not wound the eye. But between him and that place stood a tall figure clothed in garments colourless as mist. The face was stern, not cruel; ancient, yet free of age. In one hand the gatekeeper held a book, and in the other a key.

“Let me through,” gasped Tom.

The gatekeeper looked into the book.

“Thomas Chuff.”

“Aye, t’ sir. That’s me.”

“Son of Abel Chuff.”

“Aye.”

“Husband of Mary.”

Tom lowered his head.

“Father of three children.”

“Aye.”

“Poacher, thief, drunkard, blasphemer, oppressor of the weak.”

“I’ve been bad,” said Tom. “But I’ll mend. Only let me out o’ this.”

“Why should you be released?”

“I’ll say my prayers.”

“You have mocked prayer.”

“I’ll go to church.”

“You have profaned the Sabbath.”

“I’ll work honest.”

“You have lived by theft.”

“I’ll never lift hand to Mary nor t’ barns again. Never. I swear it.”

“You have sworn before.”

Tom threw himself against the bars.

“One chance! Gie me one chance! I didn’t know. Feyther said there was nowt after. Men at t’ Hare and Hounds said t’ same. Gie me one trial more!”

The gatekeeper regarded him in silence.

Far below, through the smoke, the black dog barked.

The sound shook the stair.

“One trial more,” said the figure at last. “Not because you merit it, but because mercy is greater than merit. Return. Remember what you have seen. If you again make drink your master, violence your custom, and suffering your sport, there shall be no second gate and no further reprieve.”

“I’ll remember!”

“You will forget.”

“Nay, I’ll remember till I dee!”

“You will seek to forget. Beware the beech-tree. Beware the open grave. Beware the whistle upon the moor.”

The dog barked again, close behind.

The gatekeeper raised the key.

A brightness broke through the bars, not like flame but like the sudden and intolerable splendour of a thousand stars. Tom cried out, covered his face, and felt himself hurled backward—not downward now, but through air, earth, darkness, and a narrow suffocating passage.

He awoke upon his own bed.

Mary Chuff, who had sat through the night with her head bowed beside the candle, heard the sheet rustle.

She looked up.

The cloth covering Tom’s face moved.

For several seconds she could neither stir nor breathe. Then a hand rose under the linen, grasped it, and drew it aside.

Tom’s eyes opened.

Mary screamed and fell against the wall.

“Don’t!” he said.

His voice was scarcely audible.

“Don’t fear me, lass.”

She stared at him. His face remained ghastly, but the eyes which she had dreaded for so many years were filled with a terror greater than her own.

“Where am I?”

“Home.”

“Alive?”

“I—I think so.”

He sat up, felt his breast, and looked wildly about the room.

“T’ grave,” he muttered. “Where’s t’ grave?”

“What grave?”

“Under t’ beech. He were there. Feyther—and t’ dog—”

He began to tremble so violently that the bedstead shook.

When Doctor Hales returned at dawn, he found his deceased patient sitting before the fire, wrapped in a blanket and refusing a cup of gin which Mary, in her bewilderment, had offered as medicine. The good doctor was greatly astonished. He spoke afterward of catalepsy, suspended animation, and certain obscure affections of the heart; but I do not think his explanation satisfied himself, and it certainly did not satisfy Tom.

“Send for t’ vicar,” said Tom.

“You require rest.”

“I require t’ vicar.”

The Reverend Mr. Earnshaw came before noon. He was a mild, scholarly man, much loved in Shackleton, though Tom had once thrown a dead crow into his garden and called him a black-coated daw. To this clergyman Tom related, with many interruptions of sobbing and with none of his customary bravado, the vision I have described.

Mr. Earnshaw listened without ridicule.

“Whether this was a dream, a warning, or a mercy permitted to you in the extremity of sickness,” he said, “its lesson is plain.”

“I know it, sir.”

“Fear alone will not save you, Thomas.”

“It will. I’ll never forget yon place.”

“Fear fades.”

“Not this fear.”

“Then build upon it while it is strong. Ask pardon of your wife. Restore what you can. Leave the ale-house. Abandon poaching. Work honestly. Pray for strength when terror no longer supplies it.”

Tom slid from his chair, fell upon his knees before Mary, and seized the hem of her gown.

“Forgie me.”

She looked at the vicar, then at her husband.

“Forgie me, lass,” he repeated. “For thee and t’ barns. I’ve been worse nor a beast. I’ll change. God hear me, I’ll change.”

Mary began to weep. She put her hand uncertainly upon his head.

“I forgie thee, Tom.”

He kissed her hand.

Before the vicar departed, Tom drew him aside.

“There’s another thing, sir.”

“What is it?”

“When I dee, don’t bury me nigh that great beech in t’ churchyard.”

“The northern beech?”

“Aye. Nor nigh t’ wall. Nor in any grave thereabouts.”

“Why?”

“That were where I woke. There were an open grave under t’ branches. Promise me I’ll lie elsewhere.”

Mr. Earnshaw hesitated, perhaps unwilling to encourage a superstitious fancy; but Tom’s distress was so extreme that he at length consented.

“I promise, if the matter rests with me, you shall be buried toward the centre of the churchyard.”

“By t’ church?”

“Yes.”

“Far from t’ beech?”

“Far from it.”

For some months the reformation of Tom Chuff appeared complete.

He broke his snares and burned them. He returned a ferret to the man from whom he had stolen it. He sold his gun and gave Mary the money. The landlord of the Hare and Hounds laughed when Tom first refused a cup; but Tom struck no one, uttered no oath, and went home sober. He obtained work from a farmer, mending walls and draining a wet field, and though labour sat awkwardly upon him, he persisted.

There was food in the cottage. The children ceased to hide when he approached. He fashioned a wooden cart for the younger boy, carried the little girl upon his shoulders, and once, when Mary broke a basin and shrank instinctively from the expected blow, Tom turned away and wept.

The neighbours marvelled.

Some called it conversion. Some said the fit had injured his brain. Old Mrs. Pate declared that a black dog had been heard near the churchyard on the night of his seizure, and that no good could come from a man whom such a creature had marked. But Mr. Earnshaw encouraged him, Doctor Hales shook his hand, and Mary’s face regained for a while a faint reflection of its former gentleness.

Had Tom died in that first year, his story would have been one of terror followed by grace.

But he lived.

And fear, as the vicar had warned him, faded.

It did not disappear at once. Great wickedness seldom returns by the front door after repentance has publicly expelled it. It creeps in through cracks, excuses, small indulgences, and injuries remembered more readily than mercies.

Tom’s first backward step was almost trivial. One wet evening a fellow labourer offered him half a cup of ale.

“Thou’rt not a babe,” the man said. “A sup won’t damn thee.”

Tom refused.

The man laughed.

“Art feared o’ seeing t’ black dog?”

Tom drank to prove that he was not afraid.

Nothing happened. The ale warmed him. The church tower did not split. No infernal messenger appeared.

A week later he drank a whole cup. Then two.

He continued to work, and for a time he continued to treat Mary well. Thus he persuaded himself that the vicar had been over-nice in forbidding what other men enjoyed without injury.

The vision, once so sharp that he could not close his eyes without seeing the gatekeeper’s face, lost its colours. The abyss became a bad dream. The cries of the condemned became noises bred by sickness. He adopted Doctor Hales’s word, “catalepsy,” though he could not pronounce it; and there was comfort in a long appellative which seemed to reduce hell to a bodily disorder.

Then came idleness. Then came debt.

One winter afternoon, crossing Sir Harry’s land, Tom saw a hare sitting among the brown fern. His old cunning stirred. He had no snare, but the following night he returned with one fashioned secretly in the shed.

“It’s nobbut a hare,” he told himself. “T’ rich have hundreds.”

He sold it for gin.

After that the descent was swift, though not so swift that Mary failed to observe every stage. She saw the old look return to his eyes. She heard the old stealth in his tread when he came across the moor before dawn. Money vanished. Food grew scarce. The children, older now, learned again to be silent.

Mr. Earnshaw remonstrated.

“You were granted what you called one trial more.”

“I were ill,” said Tom.

“You did not call it illness then.”

“Men dream queer when their heart stops.”

“And your promises?”

“I work when there’s work.”

“You have not worked this fortnight.”

“Farmer cheated me.”

“You have been drinking.”

“A man may drink.”

“A man may also destroy himself.”

Tom’s face darkened.

“Keep thy sermons for church, t’ sir.”

The vicar looked at him sorrowfully.

“Your wife is ill.”

“She’s aye complaining.”

“She is failing before your eyes.”

Tom muttered an oath and walked away.

What followed need not be related blow by blow. There are domestic tragedies which, though performed without dagger, poison, or ostensible crime, are murders in the sight of Heaven. Mary’s strength declined. A cough settled upon her breast. She had insufficient food, insufficient warmth, and no peace. More than once Doctor Hales told Tom that she must be spared anxiety; and more than once, within an hour of that warning, Tom returned drunk and filled the cottage with terror.

Her brother, Matthew Rigg, lived beyond the moor and worked at a quarry nearly fifteen miles away. He was a powerful, silent man, slow to anger and therefore, when angered, much to be feared. Mary concealed her misery from him as long as she could. But the elder boy at last carried the truth.

Matthew came to the cottage.

Tom was absent.

He sat beside his sister’s bed and held her wasted hand.

“Has he struck thee?”

“Nay,” she whispered.

“Mary.”

“Not lately.”

“Has he left thee without bread?”

“He’s had bad luck.”

“Has he?”

“Don’t seek him, Matt.”

“I’ll speak wi’ him.”

“Nay. Thou’lt kill him.”

Matthew’s jaw hardened.

“I’ll speak wi’ him,” he repeated.

But Tom, hearing from a neighbour that his brother-in-law had arrived, kept away until Matthew was forced to return to the quarry. Before leaving, he sent word that he would come again, and that no moor, wood, ale-house, or churchyard would shelter Tom when he did.

Mary died three weeks later.

Her end was quiet. Mr. Earnshaw prayed beside her. The children knelt at the bed. Tom was not present. He had gone out the previous evening with his gun and returned only after her breath had ceased.

He stood in the doorway when told.

For one moment a shadow of the old terror crossed his face.

“Dead?”

Mr. Earnshaw looked at him.

“Yes.”

Tom approached the bed. Mary’s features, released from fear, appeared younger in death than they had for many years in life. He touched her cheek and drew back.

“She’s cold.”

“She asked for you,” said the vicar.

Tom’s lips moved.

“She forgave you,” Mr. Earnshaw added. “Again.”

At that, Tom turned abruptly and left the cottage.

The funeral was appointed for the following Thursday. A grave was dug near the northern wall of Shackleton churchyard, beneath the outer branches of the great beech, because that part of the ground belonged by custom to the poorer cottages of the moor.

Tom saw the men digging it on Wednesday.

He stopped at the gate.

The sky was pale; children played beside the road; rooks quarrelled in the branches; and the scene possessed none of the spectral character of his vision. Nevertheless, at sight of the dark rectangle under the tree, his heart gave one violent leap.

“Who’s that for?” he asked.

The sexton rested on his spade.

“Who dost think?”

“Mary?”

“Aye.”

“Why there?”

“Where else?”

Tom gripped the gate.

“Fill it.”

“What?”

“Fill it in. Dig another.”

The sexton laughed.

“Dig it thysen.”

Tom looked toward the church. He remembered the vicar’s promise and, with the selfish instinct which misery had not corrected, felt reassured.

Mary might lie beneath the beech. He would not.

The funeral took place under a sullen afternoon sky. Tom attended in a coat borrowed from the landlord of the Hare and Hounds. He remained sober through the service, though his face was swollen and his eyes wandered continually from the coffin to the open earth.

When the mourners moved toward the grave, he stopped.

“I can’t,” he muttered.

Mr. Earnshaw took his arm.

“You must.”

“Nay.”

“For your wife’s sake.”

Tom allowed himself to be led within a few paces, but when the coffin was lowered he recoiled. The great beech towered above him. The grave looked immeasurably deep. A loosened pebble fell from the mound, striking the coffin-lid with a hollow tap.

Tom groaned and turned away.

Matthew Rigg was not present. Word had reached the quarry too late—or so it was believed. But near sunset he arrived in Shackleton, learned that the obsequies were over, and went first to the cottage, where he found the children alone.

“Where’s Tom?”

No one knew.

Matthew’s face became terrible.

“I’ll find him.”

That evening Tom sat in the Hare and Hounds.

He had intended to drink one cup only, as an anodyne for grief, though whether his grief was for Mary, for himself, or for that region beneath the beech to which one day’s funeral had restored all its ancient horror, I cannot determine. One cup became three. Companions gathered. Someone spoke of Mary. Someone else spoke of Matthew Rigg.

“Matt’s seeking thee,” said the landlord.

“Let him seek.”

“He’ll find.”

Tom laughed too loudly.

“I bean’t feared o’ Matt Rigg.”

“You were feared enough to keep clear last month.”

Tom seized the gin bottle.

“Who says I were feared?”

No one answered. This silence, being more insulting than speech, urged him to further excess. He drank until the room swayed and the peat fire stretched red arms across the hearth. At eleven the landlord put him out.

The shortest way home crossed Shackleton churchyard.

There was another path around the southern fields, but it added nearly a mile, and rain had begun to fall. Tom stood at the fork, peering into darkness. He thought of the beech and the grave. Then he thought of the men in the ale-house laughing if they learned he had gone around.

“Dreams,” he muttered. “Nowt but dreams.”

He took the churchyard path.

Before he reached the wall, the rain ceased.

The clouds parted with extraordinary suddenness, not drifting away but seeming to dissolve, and there appeared overhead a sky blacker than any he had seen since the night of his seizure. In it burned innumerable stars, large, hard, and brilliant.

Tom halted.

No wind stirred the grass.

The silence was deathlike.

Behind him, the last light of the Hare and Hounds vanished as its door closed. Before him stood the church tower, square and blind. The wall was silvered faintly by starlight; beyond it rose the immense beech, every branch motionless.

Tom’s mouth became dry.

He climbed the stile.

His foot struck a stone, and the small sound seemed to pass through the whole churchyard. To his right lay the clustered graves, pale and crooked. To his left, under the beech, was Mary’s grave.

It remained open.

The sexton, having been called away after the service by sickness in his own house, had cast in only a few spadefuls of earth and left the completion of the work until morning. The coffin lay far below, concealed by darkness. The mound stood beside the aperture. The spade projected from it.

Exactly so.

Tom’s breath stopped.

He looked up at the stars. They blinked.

He looked at the beech. Its trunk rose like a colossal pillar sustaining the black sky.

Then a whistle sounded upon the moor.

Two low notes.

One high.

Tom could not move.

Again came the whistle.

It was the signal used by poachers. His signal. In old days he would have answered it without thought; and now habit, gin, and terror combined to draw the response from him before judgment could restrain it.

He whistled back.

Something moved on the moor.

A black point crossed the featureless waste. As it approached, stars vanished behind it. Tom saw no movement of legs, no swing of arms. It seemed to glide.

His scalp contracted.

“Who’s there?” he called.

The figure came on.

Tom retreated one step toward the grave.

“Speak plain!”

No answer.

Another step.

The figure reached the wall and climbed it.

That human action should have reassured him. It did not. Terror had already completed the shape of what he saw. The approaching man was Abel Chuff. The darkness at his side was the black dog. The grave behind Tom was the mouth of the abyss.

“Keep off!” he screamed.

The figure dropped from the wall.

“Tom!”

It was Matthew Rigg’s voice.

But guilt often fears an earthly avenger more than a supernatural one, and Tom’s panic did not diminish. Matthew advanced with his fists clenched.

“I’ve sought thee all day.”

“Keep off!”

“Thou killed her.”

“Nay!”

“Not wi’ one blow. That would ha’ been kinder. Thou killed her year by year.”

Tom looked wildly beside him. The dark clump which he had mistaken for the dog was only a furze-bush beyond the wall. He saw this now, yet behind it, or in his inflamed imagination, two red points seemed still to burn.

“She forgave me,” he babbled.

“Aye. She forgave thee. I don’t.”

Matthew moved closer.

Tom raised both hands and recoiled.

His heel broke the loose edge of the mound.

The earth yielded.

For one instant he tottered, arms beating the air, face lifted toward the brilliant and pitiless stars. Then he fell backward—no, not backward, for in twisting away from Matthew he turned half around—and plunged headfirst into the open grave.

There was a dull crack.

Then silence.

Matthew Rigg stood motionless.

“Tom?”

No answer came.

He approached the brink and looked down. Tom Chuff lay doubled between the side of the grave and Mary’s coffin, his head bent beneath him at a dreadful angle.

The abyss was not bottomless. It was seven feet deep.

But it had sufficed.

Doctor Hales, summoned once more to pronounce upon Tom Chuff, found no obscure torpor, no suspended animation, and no deceptive failure of the pulse. The neck was broken. Death had been instantaneous.

There was an inquiry, of course. Matthew Rigg told the truth, and the marks at the grave confirmed it. He had not touched Tom. No reasonable person doubted that drink, darkness, a crumbling mound, and a guilty conscience had accomplished the catastrophe without supernatural assistance.

Yet reasonable persons did not wholly agree.

The sexton affirmed that, when he first reached the churchyard, he heard a dog growling beyond the northern wall, though no dog was found. Old Mrs. Pate declared that the stars had shone red over Shackleton that night. One of Tom’s former companions confessed that the whistle heard upon the moor was sometimes answered by no living man. Doctor Hales spoke again of catalepsy and hallucination, but thereafter he preferred the southern path when called across the churchyard after dark.

Mary’s grave was closed the next morning.

As for Tom, Mr. Earnshaw remembered his promise. The body was buried near the centre of the churchyard, close beneath the church’s shadow and far from the northern beech. A plain stone marked the place. His children, when grown, left Shackleton, and none of his name remains there now.

Thus his request was faithfully observed.

He did not lie beneath the beech.

He only died there.

Whether his first vision was a merciful warning, a fevered dream, or some mysterious rehearsal by which Providence showed him the end toward which his own nature tended, I shall not undertake to decide. The external facts admit of common explanation. A sick man dreamed of a churchyard he knew, an ancient tree he had often passed, a grave which sooner or later must be dug, a whistle familiar from his unlawful trade, and a pursuer shaped from memories of his brutal father. Years afterward, drink and chance reproduced those circumstances; conscience supplied the demon; fear completed the judgment.

So the philosopher may arrange it.

But the people of Shackleton say that no warning is merely a dream when a man has been allowed to awaken from it; and that the gate of mercy, once opened, is not to be mocked with impunity. They say also that terror may bring a sinner to his knees, but terror alone cannot keep him there; for when the stars resume their ordinary brightness, when the grave is hidden by summer grass, and when the voice that cried “one trial more” has grown faint in recollection, the unchanged heart returns as naturally to evil as water to its lowest channel.

This is a severe homily.

It is severe because the matter is severe.

And if, on some windless night, you should cross Shackleton churchyard beneath a black sky crowded with unnaturally brilliant stars, do not linger by the northern wall. Do not look too long at the great beech. Above all, if a whistle comes to you across the moor—two low notes and one high—make no answer.

For you cannot be certain who calls.

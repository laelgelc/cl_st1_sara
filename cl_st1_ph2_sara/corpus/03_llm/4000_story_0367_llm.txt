Old Sam Gates had worked so long upon the same Norfolk acres that he seemed less a man employed by the soil than something the soil had put up of its own accord. His coat was the colour of November mud; his boots resembled two clods; his bent back followed the curve of the hills; and when he stood motionless among the roots, with his whiskers stirring in the wind, a stranger might have taken him for a large and unusually thoughtful swede.

Sam was sixty-eight, though he said sixty-five because he had once been sixty-five and considered it an age worth keeping. He lived with his niece Aggie in a little cottage belonging to Mr. Habbage, the farmer. The cottage leaned toward the road, the barn leaned toward the cottage, and Sam leaned toward both. Nothing in that part of the county stood precisely upright except the church tower and Aggie’s opinions.

Every evening, when Sam returned from work, Aggie asked him the same question.

“Well, Uncle, any noos?”

Aggie was round, pale and earnest, with a face like a dumpling waiting to be put into a stew. She had been to Norwich twice and Yarmouth once, and this experience had given her a taste for events. She read the newspaper when she could get it, followed the war by means of a cracked map pinned beside the dresser, and knew that places called Ypres, Arras and Verdun were important, though she was not always certain which side owned them.

Sam disliked her question.

“Noos?” he would say, drawing off his boots. “What noos should there be?”

“You might have seen something.”

“I seen Habbage’s red cow in the hedge.”

“Well, there you are.”

“That bean’t noos. That’s where she allus be.”

Or he had seen the carrier’s cart, or young Willie Pott firing stones at a rook, or Mrs. Flegg hanging out washing in weather no sensible woman would have trusted. Aggie accepted these things as modest contributions to the history of the parish, but Sam did not. News, in his opinion, was a town invention. It belonged to railway stations, public houses and people with clean collars. Nothing ever happened to a farm labourer except weather, work and the occasional failure of a bootlace.

Even the war had disappointed Aggie in this respect. It made itself heard on distant nights as a dull muttering over the sea, and sometimes soldiers marched through the village, leaving cigarette ends and romantic impressions behind them. Aeroplanes passed high overhead like bad-tempered beetles. Prices rose; beer weakened; tobacco became difficult; and Mr. Habbage complained more steadily than before. But Sam maintained that these were not events.

“The whole war,” he said, “bean’t much but a plaguey noosance got up by furriners. Spoils trade, takes the young chaps, frightens the hens and puts thruppence on shag. Call that noos if you loike. Oi call it foolishness.”

Aggie had a photograph of a soldier named Albert on the mantelpiece. Albert had written to her twice from France and once from somewhere he was not permitted to mention. In each letter he said he was “in the pink,” and Aggie therefore imagined the British Army moving about the Continent in a cheerful rosy glow. She supposed that Uncle Sam might, by keeping his eyes open, discover some item equally worthy of correspondence.

But Sam’s eyes, though sharp enough for weeds, were not interested in history.

On a certain morning in late summer, Aggie put bread, cheese and two onions into his tin, wrapped the tin in a red handkerchief, and asked her question before he had even left the house.

“Any noos today, Uncle?”

“How should Oi know afore it happen?”

“You might bring some home.”

“Oi’ll bring home what Oi take out, if Oi don’t eat it.”

“I mean something interesting.”

Sam settled his cap upon his head.

“Oi got seventeen rows o’ tarnips to thin. If that’s interesting, you can come and do eight.”

Aggie sighed. “You never notice anything.”

“Oi notice what wants noticin’.”

He went out with his hoe over his shoulder, leaving her to the breakfast things and the latest report from the Western Front.

The morning was one of those wide, shining Norfolk mornings in which the sky seems to have bought up all the land and then forgotten what it meant to do with it. A thin sea mist lay in the hollows. Above it the fields rolled broad and green, divided by hedges, ditches and dusty lanes. Larks climbed into the pale blue until they were no more than voices. Insects shone over the wet grass. Far off, a windmill turned with the slow dignity of a person who is paid by the hour.

Sam walked two miles to Mr. Habbage’s turnip field. He stopped once to fill his pipe and once to observe a broken gate, which was a more significant object to him than any monument in Europe. By seven o’clock he was at work.

Turnips are not intelligent plants. Left to themselves they crowd together, quarrel for nourishment, grow weak in company and come to nothing. They therefore require thinning, and Sam, who understood them better than he understood most men, proceeded along the rows with a short hoe, cutting out the poor to give room to the strong. There may have been political reflections in this occupation, but none occurred to him.

The sun climbed. The mist vanished. Earth clung to his boots and sweat collected under the rim of his cap. He worked steadily, pausing now and again to straighten his back by degrees.

At half past nine an aeroplane passed overhead.

Sam looked up and scowled.

The machine was lower than usual, and its engine made an uneven noise: roar, cough, roar, bang, silence, roar. It circled once above the field, glinting in the sun. Sam watched just long enough to satisfy himself that it was another dratted interruption, then returned to the turnips.

The aeroplane circled again.

“Go on with you,” said Sam.

It did not go on. It came lower. Its wings tilted, recovered and tilted once more. A black cross showed beneath one wing.

Had Aggie been present she would have understood at once that a German aeroplane above a Norfolk turnip field was no ordinary matter. Sam merely understood that it was above a Norfolk turnip field, and that field happened to belong to Mr. Habbage.

The machine dipped over the hedge, missed an oak tree, clipped the top from an elder bush and struck the ground. It ran across two rows, bounced, tore through six more, turned half round and stopped with its nose in a bank.

For several seconds there was quiet.

Then Sam advanced, carrying his hoe.

He did not hurry. The damage was done, and hurrying would not restore the crop.

A man climbed from the aeroplane. He wore a leather coat, goggles and a close-fitting cap. There was blood on one cheek, but he moved briskly enough. After glancing at the engine, he pulled off his goggles and saw Sam.

The German stared.

Sam stared back, principally because the fellow was standing on three healthy turnips.

The German’s mouth opened.

“Paul!” he exclaimed.

“My name’s Gates,” said Sam. “And you get off them tarnips.”

The airman took one step nearer. His astonishment increased. He peered at Sam’s face as though reading small print.

“Paul Jouperts!”

“Oi said Gates. Samuel Gates. You’ve mashed nigh on two dozen roots, not countin’ what you done by the hedge.”

The German drew a pistol.

This altered the agricultural aspect of the interview, though not so much as might be expected. Sam had seen guns before. He disliked this one because it prevented him from expressing his views at proper length.

“You come,” said the airman.

“Where?”

“With me. At once.”

“Oi got work.”

“You come, or I shoot.”

Sam considered him. “You shoot, and Habbage’ll have the law on you.”

The threat lacked its intended force, possibly because Mr. Habbage was not known in Germany. The airman waved the pistol toward the machine.

“Get in.”

“Oi bean’t gettin’ in that contraption.”

“I shoot your leg.”

“Then Oi can’t get in, can Oi?”

The German seemed troubled by this reasoning. He looked toward the eastern sky and then toward the village. The engine had stopped coughing and was now ticking faintly to itself. With sudden impatience he seized Sam by the arm.

“You are coming. Quick!”

Sam resisted. The German struck him behind the ear with the pistol. It was not a heavy blow, but it convinced Sam that foreigners had no manners and that further discussion would only delay matters.

“Roight,” he said bitterly. “But if them wheels go over the young plants, you’ll answer for it.”

The airman pushed him toward the rear seat. Sam climbed in with difficulty, taking his hoe because he saw no reason to leave a good tool lying about. The German dragged the aeroplane’s nose from the bank, examined something beneath the wing, spun the propeller, climbed into the forward seat and began operating levers.

The engine coughed into life.

Sam had never travelled faster than Mr. Habbage’s mare could trot, and he had always believed that speed above this amounted to carelessness. The aeroplane bumped over the field, scattering soil and shaving turnip leaves beneath its wheels. He shouted protests, but the engine swallowed them. The hedge rushed near. Sam prepared to be killed in Mr. Habbage’s ditch.

Instead, the ground fell away.

For the first time in sixty-eight—or sixty-five—years, Samuel Gates left the earth by a method not involving a ladder.

The field dropped below him. The men working beyond the road became pegs. The cottages shrank into a child’s collection of toys. The church spire, which had always appeared offensively tall, slid beneath the wing. Fields opened in squares and strips, green, brown and yellow, and the lanes wound among them like pale string.

Sam gripped the side of the machine.

He had no proper fear of height because he had never before had the opportunity to acquire one. What he felt was indignation. From up there he could see just how much work remained in the eastern half of the field. He could also see the scar the aeroplane had made through the crop. Mr. Habbage would count every root and argue over the value of the leaves.

The machine rose into thin cloud. Moisture blew into Sam’s face. The world vanished, and for a moment there was only white brilliance, engine thunder and the shaking framework around him.

Sam wondered whether he was being carried to the Kingdom of Heaven. It seemed unlikely. Heaven, as described by the rector, contained music, robes and a great many persons with clean hands. Nothing had ever been said about petrol fumes. Besides, Sam had not finished his bread and cheese.

They came out above the cloud into sunlight. The sky was tremendous and empty. The machine flew eastward, then south, and the coast appeared below: a hard bright edge where England ended and the sea began.

Sam recognized the sea. He had seen it from Yarmouth beach in 1889, and he had not thought much of it then. From the air it appeared bigger but no more useful. It had neither fences nor crops, and there was nowhere to sit.

The German turned and shouted something.

“What?” called Sam.

The German shouted again, pointing ahead.

“Oi can’t hear a word!” Sam replied.

This conversation continued for some time without improving the understanding of either man.

They flew over ships that looked like chips of wood. Once, far away, there were black puffs in the sky. The German changed direction and climbed. Sam supposed somebody was trying to shoot them, which he regarded as unnecessary interference. A second aeroplane appeared, chased them for ten minutes, and disappeared into cloud. Sam did not see it clearly because he had opened his food tin and was attempting to prevent an onion from escaping over the side.

He ate his bread and cheese. The wind took most of the crumbs, but the onion stayed. He offered none to the pilot.

The sea ended beneath them, and another country began.

It was a poor-looking country from the first. The fields were torn, villages broken, roads scarred and woods reduced to splintered sticks. As they flew farther inland, the green earth gave way to a brown confusion of trenches, shell holes, abandoned carts, roofless houses and muddy tracks. Smoke rose everywhere. Flashes sprang out upon the ground; moments later came distant blows of sound.

Sam peered over the side.

“Want drainin’,” he said.

The aeroplane descended behind the German lines. Men ran into view. There were grey uniforms, pointed helmets, motor lorries, stacked shells and sheds covered with camouflage. The machine struck the ground, bounced several times and stopped beside a row of hangars.

Sam climbed out stiffly, still holding his hoe.

A group of soldiers gathered around him. At first they laughed. Then one saw his face and stopped. Another called an officer. The officer approached, looked at Sam and uttered the same name as the pilot.

“Jouperts!”

“Gates,” said Sam.

The pilot spoke rapidly in German. The officer listened, glanced at Sam, and laughed with great satisfaction. He had a pink, well-fed face, a waxed moustache and boots polished beyond reason.

“So!” he said in English. “It is remarkable.”

“What is?”

“You are the same.”

“Same as what?”

“The same as Paul Jouperts.”

“If he look like me, that’s his misfortune.”

More officers came. They led Sam to a wooden hut, took his hoe, searched his pockets and placed the contents upon a table: a clasp knife, a clay pipe, tobacco crumbs, two nails, string, a smooth pebble, a farthing, three buttons and the remaining onion.

A thin officer lifted the onion suspiciously.

“That’s mine,” said Sam.

“What is purpose?”

“Purpose? You eat ’un.”

The officer put it down.

They photographed Sam from the front and side. They measured his height, examined his ears and argued over his whiskers. One of them pulled at the whiskers to see whether they were false.

“Leave hold!” cried Sam. “Oi grew them myself.”

At last the pink-faced officer sat opposite him.

“What is your name?”

“Samuel Gates.”

“You are English?”

“You fetched me from Norfolk, didn’t you?”

“Your occupation?”

“Tarnips, mostly. Swedes come winter. Beans when called for.”

The officer folded his hands upon the table.

“You know Paul Jouperts?”

“No.”

“He is great man.”

“Then why’d he look like me?”

The officer ignored this. “Paul Jouperts is of the German Secret Service. For three years he has worked in England. Never is he found. Never is he suspected. At this moment he is gardener at a house very close to the headquarters of a British general.”

Sam’s attention sharpened slightly at the word gardener.

“Is he any good?”

“What?”

“Gardener. Do he know runner beans?”

The German gave an impatient sigh. “He sends us reports. Troop movements. Names. Plans. He is most valuable.”

“If he trample plants like your flying chap, he bean’t worth his dinner.”

The officer stood. Another man entered, broad in the shoulders, with a scar across his forehead. The two spoke together in German, occasionally looking at Sam. Their tone was cheerful, and cheerful discussion by enemy officers is seldom favourable to an elderly farmworker.

The scarred man at last addressed him.

“You have great honour. You will help Germany.”

“Oi won’t.”

“You will help without effort.”

“Then Oi reckon you’ll do without.”

The scarred officer smiled. “It is simple. You are taken near the front. You are shot. Your body is put in an old trench where English soldiers find you. In your pocket we put papers. They believe they have found Paul Jouperts dead.”

Sam reflected upon this plan.

“Oi’d be dead, would Oi?”

“Certainly.”

“Then Oi don’t agree.”

“It is not necessary.”

“No sense in killin’ me,” Sam said. “Oi bean’t your Paul whatever-his-name-is.”

“Exactly. Paul Jouperts remains alive. The English believe him dead. He continues his work as gardener near their headquarters. It is excellent.”

“Don’t sound excellent from where Oi’m settin’.”

The officers laughed.

Sam did not. He was not terrified in any elaborate fashion. Terror requires imagination, and Sam’s imagination had been kept small by long employment. But he understood death. He had seen it in cottages, cattle sheds and fields. It was a permanent arrangement and would certainly prevent him returning Mr. Habbage’s hoe.

“I got tarnips to finish,” he said.

The pink officer shrugged. “The turnips must wait.”

“They won’t.”

“You English!” exclaimed the scarred man. “Always you joke.”

“Oi bean’t jokin’.”

They took Sam outside. His possessions were returned except for his knife, the onion and the hoe. Two soldiers put him in a motorcar with a canvas hood, and the scarred officer climbed in after him. They drove for perhaps an hour.

If the flight had shown Sam the war from the airy and almost respectable side, the drive showed it from underneath. They passed fields in which nothing grew, villages without inhabitants, horses lying where they had fallen, lines of weary men, guns hidden under branches and wagons sunk to their axles in mud. The air smelled of smoke, damp earth, petrol, decay and drains.

Shells passed overhead with a tearing sound. Guns answered. Once the car stopped while wounded men crossed the road. Some walked, some were carried, and one young soldier sat upon a bank staring at his empty hands.

Sam’s resentment deepened.

It was not that he suddenly formed opinions about empires, frontiers or international diplomacy. He simply perceived war as the largest example he had ever encountered of people failing to look after land. Acres had been ploughed by shells and planted with iron. Trees that might have given good shade or firewood were smashed for no return. Ditches had been dug in all directions but drained nothing. Able-bodied men stood about waiting to be killed when half the farms in Norfolk wanted labour.

“Waste,” he muttered.

The scarred officer glanced at him. “What you say?”

“Waste. Whole place wants puttin’ straight.”

“You are soon not troubled.”

“That’s what you think.”

The car left them beside a ruined farmhouse as evening came on. The officer handed Sam over to a thick-necked corporal who spoke a little English. In Sam’s coat pockets they placed papers, a map and a small notebook filled with figures. Then the corporal marched him along a communication trench.

The walls rose on either side, reinforced with boards and sandbags. Mud sucked at Sam’s boots. Men squeezed past carrying ammunition, rations and coils of wire. Rats moved with the confidence of creatures holding military permits. Somewhere ahead, rifles cracked without enthusiasm.

Darkness settled.

The corporal led Sam away from the busy trenches and into a broken, abandoned section half filled with water and fallen earth. Here there were no lamps and few men. The battlefield lay under a clouded sky, flashing now and then with gunfire. Flares rose, whitening the stumps of trees and the torn ground before dying slowly in the dark.

They stopped at a bend in the trench.

The corporal took out his pistol.

“You stand there.”

Sam stood where indicated, principally because there was nowhere better. He put one hand in his pocket and found the clay pipe. They had returned his tobacco, though not enough of it. With his back to the German, he began filling the bowl.

“What you do?” demanded the corporal.

“Smoke.”

“You cannot smoke.”

“Why not?”

“I shoot you.”

“That’s why. If Oi’m goin’ to be shot, Oi’ll have a pipe first.”

The corporal hesitated. It may be difficult to oppose a man who has reduced one’s threat to an argument for tobacco. He looked over the parapet, then at Sam.

“Quick.”

Sam packed the pipe carefully. He searched another pocket.

“Matches,” he said.

“What?”

“You took my matches.”

The corporal swore, produced a box and threw it. The matches fell in the mud.

“Now look what you done.”

Sam bent to pick them up.

At that instant the night split open.

There was a red-white flash, an enormous blow and the sensation of the earth rising in a solid wall. Something struck Sam’s shoulder. He went down among boards, soil and sandbags, with the roar filling his head.

Afterward there was no night, no battlefield and no Samuel Gates for a while.

He returned to consciousness because somebody was pulling his boot.

“Easy there,” said a voice. “This one’s alive.”

Sam opened his eyes. A lantern swung above him. Two men in round helmets were clearing rubble from the trench. Their uniforms were mud-coloured; their speech was English.

“Leave my boot on,” Sam murmured.

“Hallo!” said one. “He talks.”

“Course Oi talk.”

They dragged him free and sat him against the wall. His cap was gone, one whisker was singed, and his left shoulder hurt. The thick-necked German corporal lay nearby beneath more earth than he required.

A young British soldier shone the lantern into Sam’s face. His expression changed.

“Bill,” he whispered.

“What?”

“Look at him.”

The other soldier looked.

“Great Scott.”

“What’s the matter?” asked Sam.

“It’s Jouperts.”

“Bean’t.”

“It is. I saw his photograph at Intelligence.”

“You saw somebody else.”

The soldiers searched him. They found the planted map, notebook and papers. Their excitement increased with every item.

“German documents,” said Bill. “We’ve caught him.”

“You ain’t caught me. You dug me up.”

The first man stared. “Paul Jouperts, as I live.”

“Sam Gates, as Oi live.”

“You’re coming along, old sport.”

“Oi’m going home.”

“Certainly. But not quite the home you mean.”

They helped him through the trenches. Sam protested, but the soldiers treated his protests as the cunning efforts of an exposed spy. At a dressing station a medical orderly bandaged his head and shoulder. At a dugout an officer questioned him. Before midnight he had been passed from battalion headquarters to brigade headquarters, acquiring at each stage tea, suspicion and a fresh escort.

The tea prevented the evening from being an entire loss.

At last he was brought into a larger room beneath a damaged house. Maps covered the walls. Telephones buzzed on a table. A stove smoked in the corner. Three officers awaited him: a major with a cropped moustache, a thin captain wearing spectacles and a cheerful young lieutenant whose hair was too tidy for the district.

The major studied Sam.

“Well,” he said, “if you aren’t Paul Jouperts, I’ll eat my cap.”

“You can eat what you loike,” said Sam. “But Oi’m Gates.”

The lieutenant laughed. “Cool customer.”

“Sit down,” said the major.

“Oi am sittin’.”

He was indeed sitting on the only chair, having taken it while they spoke.

The captain opened the notebook. “These are German codes.”

“They put ’em in my pocket.”

“Who did?”

“Germans.”

The lieutenant leaned against the table. “Come now, old thing. You can do better than that.”

“Oi could do better if you’d stop callin’ me old things.”

The major began in a patient voice. “Your name is Paul Jouperts. You are an agent of the German government. We have had reason to suspect your existence for some time.”

“Then you suspect wrong.”

“You were found in a German position carrying German papers.”

“They flew me there.”

The three officers exchanged glances.

“Flew you?” said the captain.

“In a airyplane.”

“From where?”

“Turnip field.”

The lieutenant made a choking sound and pretended to cough.

“What turnip field?” asked the major.

“Habbage’s.”

“And where is Habbage’s turnip field?”

“Beyond Swaffham way, past the lower lane, left o’ Barrow’s wood. You go by the chalk pit, take the stile where old Moss’s bull got out last Michaelmas, and there you be.”

The major’s patience weakened. “County?”

“Norfolk, o’ course.”

“Suppose you tell us the whole story.”

So Sam told it.

He told them of Aggie’s breakfast, the seventeen rows, the damaged crop, the German machine, the pistol, the flight, the onion, the pink officer and the proposal to leave him dead in an old trench. He told it in strict relation to matters as he valued them. The British pursuit over the Channel received two sentences. The destruction of the Western Front received one. The loss of Mr. Habbage’s hoe required detailed treatment, and he returned three times to the number of turnips crushed during the landing.

The lieutenant stopped smiling.

The captain took notes.

The major asked, “Why should the German pilot seize you?”

“’Cause Oi look like that Paul feller.”

“You admit it, then?”

“Oi admit he look like me. Oi was born first, likely.”

“Did the Germans tell you where Jouperts is?”

“They said he was a gardener.”

The captain leaned forward. “Where?”

“Near some British general.”

“What general?”

“Didn’t say.”

“What house?”

“Didn’t say.”

“Think, man.”

“Oi be thinkin’.”

Sam shut his eyes. The officers waited. His thoughts did not move in disciplined order. They wandered from the pink officer’s moustache to the lost onion, from the onion to Aggie’s runner beans, and from the beans to the German spy who claimed to be a gardener.

The lieutenant whispered, “He may be genuine.”

“He may be the cleverest actor in Europe,” murmured the captain.

Sam opened his eyes.

“Roses,” he said.

“What?”

“The pink chap said something about roses. Said your general had a house with white roses on the south wall. And a stone peacock over the gate.”

The major turned to the captain.

The captain was already moving toward a map. “White roses. Stone peacock. Could be La Ferme Blanche.”

“General Wexford’s headquarters,” said the major.

“A gardener came there six months ago,” added the lieutenant. “Alsatian refugee, wasn’t he?”

The captain seized a telephone. There followed a rapid exchange of names, places and military phrases. Sam listened until it became apparent that nobody was arranging his return to Norfolk.

“Oi want to go home,” he announced.

“Hush, old sport,” said the lieutenant.

“Don’t hush me. Oi been taken from my work, hit by two Germans, blown up, dug up and called names. Oi’m due home for tea.”

The lieutenant looked at his wristwatch. “Tea was some time ago.”

“Aggie’ll keep it.”

The major asked Sam further questions. Could he describe the real Jouperts? No. Had the Germans mentioned signals, contacts, dead-letter boxes? Sam had not listened to the parts that did not concern his execution. Did he recall any names? He recalled only Jouperts, and he pronounced that in four different ways.

For the next hour telephones rang. Messengers came and went. The officers bent over maps. Sam obtained another mug of tea and a slice of bread spread with something described as margarine. He ate suspiciously.

Then the telephone rang once more.

The captain answered. His face sharpened as he listened.

“Good,” he said. “Yes. Hold him. Search everything.”

He replaced the receiver.

“They’ve arrested the gardener at La Ferme Blanche.”

The major stood. “And?”

“Wireless set beneath the potting shed. Codes in a hollow pruning handle. Plans of the northern batteries in his mattress. He tried to burn a message when they went in.”

The lieutenant gave a low whistle.

“Is he Jouperts?”

“The description matches. Better still, he saw the arresting officer and said, ‘You believe I am dead.’”

They all looked at Sam.

Sam scraped margarine from his thumb with one tooth.

“There,” he said. “Told you.”

The major came around the table and offered his hand. “Mr. Gates, I owe you an apology.”

Sam examined the hand before taking it.

“That all roight.”

“You have rendered the British Army a service of extraordinary value.”

“Have Oi?”

“This man has been sending the enemy our movements for months. You’ve stopped him.”

“Good. Now can Oi go home?”

The lieutenant laughed. “You’re a hero, old thing.”

“Oi bean’t. Oi’m a farm labourer.”

“Same thing these days,” said the major.

Sam did not accept this broadening of his profession. A hero, so far as he knew, stood on a plinth, wore very little clothing and had no weeds to attend to.

The officers wanted him to remain for formal statements. Intelligence officers wished to interview him. A colonel arrived and shook his hand twice. Someone proposed a decoration. Someone else proposed sending his story to the newspapers, though the captain observed that secrecy might be wiser. A photographer appeared, but Sam refused to be taken until his cap was recovered or replaced.

Through all this he maintained one position: he had left seventeen rows of turnips in the morning and intended to return to them.

“You cannot simply walk from France to Norfolk,” the major explained.

“Then send me how Oi come.”

“In a German aeroplane?”

“Don’t matter whose. One of yours’ll do.”

The suggestion, delivered as if aeroplanes were farm carts, amused the lieutenant so much that he repeated it to an officer from the Royal Flying Corps. The flying officer was a lean young man with a scarf, bright eyes and the expression of one who regarded death as an entertaining rumour.

“Fly him to Norfolk?” he said. “Why not?”

“Why not indeed?” said the lieutenant. “He’s captured a master spy, crossed the lines and saved Intelligence a frightful headache. The least we can do is get him back for breakfast.”

“Tea,” corrected Sam.

It was already morning by then, but military clocks had ceased to interest him.

Arrangements were made with astonishing speed. A staff car carried Sam to an aerodrome behind the lines. He was given a replacement cap, a breakfast of bacon and tea, and a new clay pipe by the colonel. He was also given his German documents as evidence for later inquiry, but Sam handed them back.

“Don’t want furrin papers clutterin’ my pockets.”

At the aerodrome rows of machines stood upon wet grass. Mechanics moved about them in the dawn. Engines began to roar one after another, shaking the air.

The young pilot pointed to a two-seater.

“There she is.”

“Looks draughty,” said Sam.

“She’ll do a hundred miles an hour.”

“That ain’t an answer.”

They strapped him into the rear cockpit. This time he was provided with goggles, a leather coat and clear instructions not to touch anything. He immediately asked where he could put a hoe, and was reminded that the Germans still had it.

“Best ash handle Oi ever owned,” he grumbled.

The pilot climbed in front.

“We’ll have you in England in no time, Mr. Gates.”

“No need to tear.”

The aeroplane ran across the grass and rose. France spread beneath them, grey in the first light. As they climbed westward Sam looked down once more upon the scarred country. Guns flashed along the horizon. Smoke drifted from the trenches. Far below, thousands of men prepared to continue the day’s destruction.

Sam shook his head.

“Plaguey noosance,” he said, though no one heard.

They crossed the coast and flew over the Channel. The sun came out. Ships moved below, each drawing a white line through the water. The pilot performed a banking turn to avoid cloud, and Sam, who mistook this for showing off, struck him on the shoulder and ordered him to fly level.

England appeared ahead, green and quiet.

The sight affected Sam more than he would have admitted. There were proper fields there, whole hedges, cattle in meadows and roofs with houses beneath them. Smoke rose peacefully from chimneys instead of violently from shell holes. Roads went where roads had reason to go. He recognized nothing for a long time, but he recognized the sense of it.

They landed first at an aerodrome in Kent, where explanations were made, petrol taken aboard and messages sent. Officers appeared to shake Sam’s hand. A woman from a canteen gave him cake. A reporter tried to question him and was removed by military police.

They flew north again.

By early afternoon the Norfolk landscape unfolded below. Sam picked out a church, then a wood, then the white road near Mr. Habbage’s farm. He leaned forward and shouted directions which the pilot could not hear. When the aeroplane passed the turnip field, Sam waved both arms, pointed down and nearly lost his new cap.

The pilot circled.

Below, Mr. Habbage and two labourers stood looking upward. Aggie was coming along the lane with her apron over her head, having been informed by half the village that Uncle Sam had been carried off by a German. The field still bore the scars of the first landing.

The British machine descended.

Its wheels touched earth more gently than the German’s had done, but they nevertheless flattened four turnips and damaged a fifth. Sam counted them before the aeroplane stopped.

He climbed out.

Mr. Habbage ran toward him, red-faced and speechless. Aggie followed, calling his name. Behind them came villagers, children, the constable and Mrs. Flegg in carpet slippers.

“Uncle!”

Aggie threw her arms around him.

“Mind my shoulder,” said Sam.

“We thought you was dead!”

“Who said so?”

“Everybody! They found your tin. There was a German aeroplane. Soldiers came. Where have you been?”

“Out.”

“Out where?”

“France, seems like.”

Aggie stared at the leather coat and goggles. “France?”

The young pilot climbed down, laughing.

“Your uncle has had rather a busy day, miss. Captured by the enemy, escaped death by a miracle, exposed the cleverest German agent in British headquarters and crossed the Channel twice.”

“I never captured nobody,” said Sam. “They captured me.”

Mr. Habbage found his voice.

“My turnips!”

Sam turned to him at once. Here, at last, was a man addressing the central issue.

“Twenty-three by the German,” he reported, “six more bruised, hedge broke at the corner. This ’un done four and a half.”

“A half?”

“Wheel caught the leaves, but root may pull through.”

Mr. Habbage hurried away to inspect the crop.

The pilot looked at Aggie. “He ought to be decorated.”

“He ought to come home,” she said. “He’s missed his tea.”

“Oi told ’em that.”

The pilot shook Sam’s hand. “Good luck, Mr. Gates. I fancy you haven’t heard the last of this.”

“Oi hope Oi have.”

The machine took off again, flattening two more young plants during its run. Sam watched with disapproval until it dwindled against the sky.

The villagers surrounded him. Questions came from every direction.

Had he fought the Germans?

Had he killed one?

Had he been above the clouds?

What was France like?

Was the King coming?

Would his name be in the paper?

Sam replied that the Germans were pushy, the clouds wet, France badly kept, the King unlikely and the newspaper none of his concern. Then he retrieved his hoe from where the German pilot had thrown it beside the bank.

The handle was scratched but sound.

“Uncle,” said Aggie, “you’re not going back to work?”

“Why not?”

“You’ve been kidnapped.”

“That don’t thin tarnips.”

He removed the leather flying coat, handed it to her and walked to the row where he had stopped the previous morning. Or perhaps it was the same morning. Time had become confused through crossing the Channel, being blown up and drinking military tea. The sun, however, stood in a familiar part of the sky, and familiar things were the ones to trust.

He bent his back and began again.

Cut, step, cut. Leave the strong plant. Take the weak. Clear the weeds. Move on.

The villagers remained for a while, hoping for speeches, medals or further aeroplanes. None appeared. Mr. Habbage counted the damaged roots and began calculating compensation from the War Office. Aggie went home to warm the kettle. One by one the others drifted away, disappointed by the ordinary appearance of a man who had been to France and returned with mud on his boots.

By half past four Sam had finished the eastern rows. His shoulder ached, his head throbbed and his pipe had gone out, but the field looked better. That mattered.

He walked home through the lane with his hoe over his shoulder. The sea wind moved softly across the hedges. Bees worked among the late flowers. Somewhere beyond sight an aeroplane hummed, but Sam did not look up.

Aggie had prepared eggs, bacon, bread, pickles and the best tea. Albert’s photograph stood upon the mantelpiece, still safely in the pink. The German flying coat hung behind the door. Half the village waited outside the window, but Aggie had drawn the curtain against them.

Sam removed his boots and sat by the table.

Aggie poured his tea. For several moments she kept silent, which was difficult for her. She watched him drink half the cup, butter a slice of bread and inspect the bacon.

Then habit proved stronger than amazement.

“Well, Uncle,” she said, “any noos?”

Sam laid down his knife.

He had been struck with a pistol, flown from Norfolk to France, mistaken for a spy by two armies, condemned to death, buried by a shell, rescued by British soldiers, interrogated by officers, instrumental in the capture of Germany’s most valuable agent, congratulated by a colonel, carried over the Channel and set down among Mr. Habbage’s turnips before tea.

But those things had happened mostly through the foolishness of others. The turnips remained. His tobacco was low. His shoulder hurt. And seven plants had now been damaged by friendly aviation.

“Noos?” he said angrily. “There you go again. No, there bean’t no noos. Nothing never happens to an ordinary man like me.”

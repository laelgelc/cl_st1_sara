## The Yellow Kittens

For more than sixty years Melissa Abbot and Sarah Drew had loved each other with that peculiar fidelity which is sometimes found between sisters who agree in almost nothing.

Melissa, the elder by eleven months, was soft of voice, easily wounded, fond of lavender ribbons and weak tea, and subject to tears whenever the choir sang “Home, Sweet Home.” Sarah was brisk, sharp-eyed and practical. She preferred brown silk, strong tea and hymns in which the congregation was required to proceed at a reasonable pace. Melissa said Sarah had no sentiment; Sarah said Melissa had enough for any three ordinary women. Nevertheless, each considered the other the best woman in the county.

Their houses stood side by side on Maple Street, separated by a low hedge and a brick walk which divided near the gate and ran impartially to both doors. Melissa’s house was white, with green shutters and a round bed of pansies. Sarah’s was gray, with white shutters and a border of red geraniums. There were doors in both side fences, though it had been many years since either sister had troubled to unlatch them. The hedge had been clipped low in the middle so that they might pass sugar bowls, newspapers, knitting patterns and opinions directly from porch to porch.

Every morning, unless rain fell, Melissa called, “Sarah, are you up?” and Sarah answered, “I have been up for an hour.”

Every afternoon Sarah went to Melissa’s for tea, except on Thursdays, when Melissa went to Sarah’s. Every Sunday they walked to church together, Melissa carrying the hymn books and Sarah carrying the umbrella, whether or not there was a cloud in the sky. They spent Thanksgiving at Melissa’s, Christmas at Sarah’s, and disputed amiably each year over which of them made the lighter biscuits.

The people of the village regarded them almost as one institution. It seemed as unlikely that the sisters should be separated as that the courthouse should leave the square or the church steeple take a notion to stand upside down.

Then Mira Holmes brought them the yellow kittens.

Mira was a pretty girl, though just then her prettiness had a tired, April-rain appearance. She had brown eyes, a little pointed chin and a way of bending her head beneath her flowered hats which made elderly ladies long to comfort her and young men lose the thread of what they were saying. That spring she had quarreled with Harry Ayres, to whom everyone had expected her to become engaged, and since Harry had gone west on business, Mira had devoted herself to good works with rather desperate energy.

One of these good works was finding homes for the numerous offspring of her mother’s yellow cat.

“They are the sweetest things,” she said, lifting the cover of her basket as she sat between the sisters on Melissa’s porch. “Mother cannot possibly keep all six, and I thought perhaps each of you would like one.”

Two small golden heads rose from the basket. Four blue eyes regarded the world with round astonishment. One kitten put a paw on the rim, lost its balance and fell backward upon the other.

Melissa clasped her hands.

“Oh, the darlings!”

“Humph,” said Sarah, which in her language meant precisely the same thing.

The kittens were so nearly alike that no reasonable person would have attempted to distinguish them. Each was striped faintly across the forehead; each had white beneath the chin; each possessed a tail which seemed too thin for its body and ears which seemed too large for its head. One may have been a shade darker than the other, but the difference vanished whenever they changed places, as they did every few seconds.

“I shall call mine Susy,” Melissa said. “I always wanted a little yellow cat named Susy.”

“And I shall call mine Billy,” said Sarah. “A sensible name.”

Mira hesitated.

“I’m not entirely certain whether—”

At that moment the bolder kitten climbed over the edge of the basket and landed on Melissa’s skirt. The other followed, slid down Sarah’s shoe and darted under a wicker chair. There was a cheerful confusion of skirts, paws and exclamations. Mira caught one kitten; Melissa caught the other. Sarah took both, set them side by side upon the porch and declared that people only confused animals by making a commotion over them.

Unfortunately, the kittens immediately crossed.

“That one is Susy,” said Melissa, pointing.

“No, it isn’t,” said Sarah. “That is Billy.”

“My dear Sarah, I had Susy in my hands this very instant.”

“And you put her down. Billy was the one that crossed to the left.”

“He crossed to the right.”

“I know my right hand from my left, Melissa.”

“I should hope so, at your age.”

There are words which in themselves are perfectly harmless but which acquire poison from the manner in which affection pronounces them. Strangers fight with clubs; those who love one another have two-edged swords, sharpened by lifelong knowledge. Melissa knew exactly how to suggest that Sarah was domineering. Sarah knew exactly how to imply that Melissa was foolish. Neither generally used this knowledge, but both possessed it.

Mira looked anxiously from one to the other.

“Perhaps it does not matter, since they are exactly alike.”

“Of course it matters,” said Sarah. “Billy is mine.”

“And Susy is mine,” said Melissa, gathering up the kitten nearest her. “This is Susy.”

“That is Billy.”

“It is not.”

“It is.”

Mira tried to laugh. “If you cannot tell them apart—”

“I can tell my own cat,” Sarah said with awful firmness.

“So can I,” Melissa replied, and her blue eyes filled with tears.

Sarah hated Melissa’s tears because they always made her feel like a criminal when she was merely being just. “Crying will not alter the facts.”

“I am not crying.”

“You are.”

“I suppose I may do as I please in my own house.”

“Certainly. And I may take my own kitten home.”

Sarah picked up the cat in Melissa’s lap.

Melissa snatched it back.

For a moment the sisters stared at each other. Mira sat perfectly still, as one does when a pleasant path suddenly ends at the edge of a quarry.

“Very well,” Sarah said at last. She took the other kitten from beneath the chair. “Keep Billy, if you are determined to steal him.”

“Steal him!”

“I shall take Susy. It is nothing to me, naturally.”

“Then why did you wrench Billy out of my arms?”

“I did not wrench him.”

“You did.”

“I merely took him.”

“You have always wanted everything I had.”

This charge was so magnificently unjust that Sarah became pale.

“Good afternoon, Melissa.”

“Good afternoon, Sarah.”

Sarah went home carrying the second kitten under one arm. She did not look back. Melissa sat rigidly upright until the gray side door closed; then she bowed her head over the disputed yellow creature and wept into its fur.

“There, there, Susy darling,” she murmured. “Your Aunt Sarah is a hard woman.”

On the other side of the hedge Sarah set her kitten down in the kitchen and said to Maria, her servant, “This is Billy. Be careful not to let him out.”

Maria, who had been with Sarah for twenty-three years and was therefore nearly fearless, examined the kitten.

“Looks mighty little to be a Billy.”

“He is young.”

“I mean—”

“I know what you mean. Mira said one was male and one female. This is Billy.”

“Yes’m,” said Maria.

In Melissa’s kitchen, Abby received a similar command.

“This is Susy,” Melissa said. “Sarah tried to take her, but I knew her instantly.”

Abby lifted the kitten and looked beneath its tail with the calm directness of a woman raised among farm animals.

“Well now,” she began.

“I do not wish to discuss it,” said Melissa.

“No’m. I reckon not.”

Thus the war commenced.

At first the village did not believe in it. On the following Sunday Sarah entered church alone and sat at one end of the sisters’ pew. Five minutes later Melissa came in and sat at the other. Between them lay a space large enough to contain their pride and narrow enough to make its presence conspicuous. Neither glanced sideways. When the congregation rose, they rose together; when it sat, they sat together; when Melissa coughed, Sarah’s hand moved instinctively toward the peppermint drops in her bag and then stopped.

After the service Sarah walked home with Mrs. Bell. Melissa walked home with Mrs. Bell’s sister, who had to trot to keep pace, although Melissa had never before been known to hurry.

By noon every kitchen in the village had heard of the estrangement. By supper the yellow kittens had become white, black, tortoiseshell and, in one account, valuable Persian animals from Boston.

Mira came to Melissa on Monday.

“I feel entirely responsible,” she said, drooping beneath a rose-trimmed hat. “If I had never brought those cats—”

“Nonsense, dear.” Melissa poured tea into the best flowered cup. Even in anguish she remained hospitable. “It is not your fault Sarah is incapable of admitting an error.”

“But perhaps there was an error about which kitten was which.”

“Not on my part.”

“If you would only speak to her—”

“I have nothing to say.”

Melissa gave Mira sponge cake, strawberry preserves and a second cup of tea, but no promise.

Mira then crossed the hedge to Sarah.

“I hoped you might be willing to make the first advance.”

“Why?”

“Because you are the stronger.”

“I am not stronger. I merely make less noise when I suffer.”

Sarah served tea, sponge cake and currant jelly. She listened civilly to every argument and demolished each one.

“You and Melissa have always been so devoted,” Mira pleaded.

“That is no reason I should encourage dishonesty.”

“She thinks you took her kitten.”

“She knows better.”

“She says she does not.”

“Then she has persuaded herself of a falsehood, which is worse.”

Mira departed with a headache and no success.

Mrs. Holmes tried next. Then Mrs. Bell and Mrs. Bell’s sister tried together, though neither was entirely free from the hope of obtaining fresh details. The minister’s wife made a tactful call upon each house. Finally Mr. Grant himself came, armed with Scripture and pastoral resolution.

The sisters received all these persons politely. Each explained that she bore the other no malice. Each declared that reconciliation was impossible until the other acknowledged the truth. Each provided tea and sponge cake.

By the end of three weeks, the village had eaten so much sponge cake in the cause of peace and concord that even Mr. Grant felt a certain inward heaviness when he passed the two houses.

Meanwhile, Billy lived at Sarah’s and Susy lived at Melissa’s—or Susy lived at Sarah’s and Billy at Melissa’s, according to which gate one entered.

The cats flourished. They slept in sunny windows, attacked balls of wool and developed the serene indifference to human disputes which is among the more exasperating virtues of their race. Occasionally one escaped into the garden. Then there would be a crisis.

“Your cat is in my asparagus bed,” Sarah called one morning across the hedge.

“That is your cat,” Melissa answered from behind her curtains.

“It came from your porch.”

“Susy has a pink ribbon.”

“The ribbon has come off.”

“Then I suggest you put it on again.”

Sarah stalked into the asparagus, collected the animal and carried it to Melissa’s step.

An hour later Melissa sent Abby across with the same cat.

“Miss Melissa says it ain’t hers.”

“Miss Sarah says it is.”

Abby and Maria looked at each other over the yellow head.

“They’re an awful set,” Maria said.

“Proud,” Abby agreed. “Prouder’n two roosters with one looking glass.”

“You tell her it’s Susy.”

“I ain’t telling her nothing. Last time I said a word, she cried into the bread dough.”

Maria scratched the kitten beneath its chin. “Cat’s hind leg! They’re both Susy.”

“Course they are.”

“Did you tell Miss Sarah?”

“Tried.”

“So did I.”

The women sighed. Servants may understand a household perfectly and still possess less authority in it than a canary.

The weeks passed, and what had begun absurdly became painful. Melissa no longer called across the hedge in the morning. Sarah no longer inspected Melissa’s roses for aphids. The daily traffic of little kindnesses ceased. No bowl of custard went one way, no new library book the other. Both sisters learned how many hours a day they had formerly spent together.

Melissa became thinner. She sat at her parlor window, ostensibly sewing, and watched for Sarah’s brown bonnet. Whenever it appeared, she bent fiercely over her work. She told herself that Sarah looked hard and unfeeling, but she noticed every sign of fatigue in Sarah’s walk.

Sarah became stooped. She read alone in the evenings, stopping now and then to listen for Melissa’s piano. If Melissa played a melancholy song, Sarah shut the window and complained of drafts. If the piano remained silent, she wondered whether Melissa was ill.

Each could have crossed the hedge in less than a minute. Each would have crossed it at the smallest alarm. But ordinary misery does not ring a fire bell, and pride is often strongest in quiet weather.

Mira continued to visit them, though her own sorrow deepened. News came that Harry Ayres might remain in the west. Melissa, who could forget her grievance when confronted by anybody else’s, took the girl’s hand.

“You must write to him, dear.”

“I cannot. I told him I never wished to see him again.”

“People say things they do not mean.”

“Do they?” Mira asked gently.

Melissa colored.

At Sarah’s, Mira received sterner counsel.

“If you were wrong, say so,” Sarah commanded.

“But what if he was wrong too?”

“That does not prevent you from attending to your half of the matter.”

Mira looked at her with such mournful intelligence that Sarah turned away and rearranged the teapot.

Summer burned itself into September. The pansies faded, the geraniums grew leggy, and dry leaves began to scrape along Maple Street. The first cold wind found both sisters unwell.

Dr. Willis visited Melissa for palpitations and Sarah for indigestion. He prescribed tonics, walks and an absence of excitement, then stopped at the druggist’s and said what both patients required was common sense.

In October Melissa accepted an invitation to visit her niece in Fairhaven.

“I cannot endure another winter here,” she told Abby. “Not with that gray house before my eyes.”

On the morning she left, she lingered beside the hedge. Sarah’s bedroom curtain stirred. Melissa knew Sarah stood behind it. Sarah knew Melissa knew. Neither moved.

“Take care of Susy,” Melissa told Abby. “And if anything happens to me—”

“Nothing’s going to happen.”

“If it does, you may ask Mira to take her. Do not send her next door.”

“No’m,” Abby said, with the patience of a saint who has begun to suspect sainthood is wasted labor.

The carriage drove away. Sarah watched until it turned the corner.

Three days later she announced that she was going to Cousin Ellen’s in Ridgely.

“This house is too quiet,” she told Maria.

“Been quiet some months.”

“I mean since the weather changed.”

“Sure.”

Sarah packed two black dresses, six collars and the photograph of Melissa taken at eighteen, though she concealed the photograph between the pages of a devotional book.

Before leaving she gave Maria instructions about Billy.

“If Melissa returns before I do, you are not to trouble her.”

“Wouldn’t dream of it.”

“And if she should inquire whether I am well, you may say I am perfectly well.”

“Even if you ain’t?”

“I shall be.”

The autumn rains began. The two houses stood dark and chastened beneath the dripping maples. Abby and Maria drank coffee together in Sarah’s kitchen, while the cats—both of whom had lately developed remarkable appetites—slept beside the stove.

“Getting broad,” Abby said, looking at one.

“Both of ’em.”

“You don’t suppose—”

“I don’t suppose. I know.”

“Well, good land.”

“Going to be a powerful lot of proof pretty soon.”

The proof arrived first in Melissa’s linen closet, where the supposed Susy produced four kittens upon a pile of freshly ironed sheets. Three days later the supposed Billy gave birth to five beneath Sarah’s spare-room bed.

Maria surveyed the second family and laughed until she was obliged to sit down.

“A male cat, are you? Ain’t you clever!”

Abby was summoned. The two women counted kittens, compared mothers and enjoyed, for several minutes, the pure satisfaction of those whose ignored wisdom has been justified by events.

“Should we write ’em?” Abby asked.

Maria considered. “No. Let ’em come home and see. Some truths travel better without postage.”

In Fairhaven, Melissa was no happier. Her niece was kind, the guest room comfortable, and the neighborhood full of persons willing to play cards. But none of them was Sarah. They did not know that Melissa disliked gravy on potatoes or that she wanted a footstool without being asked. They praised her crocheting too much and her opinions too little. Worst of all, they spoke sympathetically of “your unfortunate disagreement,” as though it were a disease Melissa had caught through no fault of her own.

One night she dreamed Sarah had died. She awoke calling her sister’s name and sat trembling until morning.

Thereafter a change occurred in her. Pride did not vanish at once; pride seldom has the grace to leave by the front door. But affection began pushing it, inch by inch, toward the back.

“What difference does it make which kitten it was?” Melissa thought. “Let her have Billy. Let her have both, if she wants them. She is my only sister.”

In Ridgely, Sarah arrived independently at the same conclusion, though by a more argumentative path.

“If Melissa believes the cat is hers, she may keep it. Her belief is mistaken, but I need not insist upon correcting every mistake she makes. There would be no end to that.”

This reasoning comforted Sarah because it permitted her to surrender without calling surrender by its name. She bought Melissa a pair of gray wool gloves and tucked them into her trunk.

Both decided to return before Thanksgiving.

Melissa arrived first, on a windy Tuesday afternoon. The white house smelled faintly of closed rooms, dried lavender and cat. Abby welcomed her with such an expression of concealed entertainment that Melissa became suspicious.

“What has happened?”

“Nothing bad.”

“Is Susy well?”

“Real well.”

“Where is she?”

“Upstairs.”

Melissa removed her bonnet and went up. A minute later Abby heard an astonished cry.

In the linen closet, Susy lay curled around four yellow-and-white kittens. She looked at Melissa with sleepy triumph.

Melissa sat down upon the floor.

“Susy!” she whispered. Then, after a long pause, “Oh.”

The facts worked slowly but thoroughly through her mind. Mira had said one kitten was male and the other female. This cat, plainly, was not male. Therefore it must be Susy. Therefore Sarah had been right that afternoon on the porch.

Melissa’s cheeks burned. She remembered every word of the quarrel, including several she had improved upon during six months of repetition. Shame pricked her; relief followed close behind it. She could go to Sarah and admit everything. Indeed, she must. The kittens had provided a bridge over which even pride might walk, if it kept its head up.

“When does Sarah return?” she asked Abby.

“Tomorrow morning, Maria thinks.”

Melissa passed a restless night. She prepared speeches, rejected them and prepared others. At dawn she baked Sarah’s favorite spice biscuits, although Abby pointed out that reconciliation did not require anybody to rise at five.

Sarah reached home shortly before noon. She had scarcely removed her traveling cloak when Maria led her upstairs.

Billy occupied the space beneath the spare-room bed with five vigorous kittens.

Sarah knelt and stared.

“Maria.”

“Yes’m?”

“Billy has kittens.”

“Yes’m.”

“Then Billy is not Billy.”

“No’m.”

“Then Melissa had Billy.”

Maria’s lips twitched. “Maybe.”

Sarah looked sharply at her. “What do you mean?”

“Only that Miss Melissa’s cat’s got four.”

“Four what?”

“Kittens.”

Sarah sat back on her heels.

For once in her life she had nothing to say.

Maria waited respectfully.

“Both of them?” Sarah said at last.

“Both Susy, looks like.”

“But Mira said—”

“Mira didn’t get much chance to finish saying anything, as I recollect.”

Sarah rose with dignity, though dignity is difficult to maintain beside a bed under which one’s male cat is nursing five offspring.

“Do not mention this in the village.”

“Wouldn’t dream of it,” Maria said, knowing the village would possess the news by sunset whether she dreamed or not.

Sarah went downstairs. She saw the gray gloves in her open trunk and her face softened. She had meant to cross to Melissa that very afternoon, offer her Billy and confess that affection was more important than justice. Now there was no Billy to offer and no justice to confess. There remained only the fact that two elderly women had nearly broken their hearts over a distinction which did not exist.

At that moment the front bell rang.

Melissa stood on the step, holding a covered plate of spice biscuits. She wore her best bonnet, but one ribbon was untied, and she looked frightened.

“Sarah.”

“Melissa.”

“I have come to tell you something.”

“So have I.”

They entered the parlor and stood facing each other. Six months of sorrow stood between them, but it had already begun to shrink.

Melissa spoke rapidly.

“You were right. The cat I have is Susy. She must be, because she has four kittens. I am sorry. I was proud and cruel, and you may have Billy, of course. I had decided that before I knew—”

Sarah made a strange sound.

Melissa stopped. “What is it?”

“My Billy has five kittens.”

There was a silence.

“Your Billy?”

“Five.”

Melissa’s mouth opened. “Then—”

“They were both females.”

“All this time?”

“All this time.”

The sisters looked at each other. Melissa’s lips trembled. Sarah’s eyes narrowed in the way they did when she was struggling against an emotion. Then Melissa gave a little gasp of laughter.

Sarah laughed too.

They sat down suddenly, side by side upon the horsehair sofa, and laughed until Melissa cried and Sarah had to wipe her spectacles. They laughed at Billy and Susy, at the porch quarrel, at the closed curtains, at the deputations of peacemakers, at the sponge cake, at the village, at themselves.

“A pair of fools,” Sarah said.

“The greatest fools who ever lived,” Melissa agreed happily.

“I was coming to let you keep Billy.”

“And I was coming to give him to you.”

“And there was no Billy.”

“Not so much as a whisker of him!”

At that they began again.

Presently laughter gave way to tears of a more healing sort. Melissa put her arms around Sarah. Sarah held her fiercely.

“I missed you,” Melissa whispered.

“Not half as much as I missed you.”

“I did.”

“You couldn’t have.”

That small disagreement was allowed to remain unsettled.

On Thanksgiving Day the white house shone from cellar to attic. The curtains were opened; fires glowed in the parlor and dining room; the brick walk had been swept clean. Sarah came through the hedge gate before breakfast and did not go home except to fetch more chairs.

There were guests enough to satisfy even Melissa’s hospitable heart: Mr. and Mrs. Holmes, the Bells, the minister and his wife, Dr. Willis, and Mira, whose April-rain expression had disappeared. Beside her sat Harry Ayres, lately returned from the west and looking both proud and humble, as a young man should when he has been forgiven.

Mira had written to him at last. Harry had answered by taking the next train home. Their engagement was to be announced that evening, though everyone at the table had known of it since morning.

Dinner included turkey, two kinds of potatoes, onions in cream, cranberry jelly, three pies, Sarah’s biscuits and Melissa’s best preserves. Abby and Maria moved in and out wearing expressions of deep authority. In the kitchen, nine kittens occupied two baskets and created continual disorder.

When the plates had been cleared, Melissa rose.

“We have a gift for Mira and Harry.”

Sarah brought in a basket. From it peered two yellow heads, nearly identical.

Mira laughed and clasped her hands just as Melissa had done in spring.

“For your future home,” Sarah said.

“One for each of you,” Melissa added.

Harry looked alarmed. “How shall we tell them apart?”

The sisters exchanged a glance.

“You needn’t,” said Sarah.

“And whatever you do,” Melissa said, “do not quarrel over it.”

Everyone laughed, though only the sisters knew the whole depth and sweetness of the jest.

Outside, the November dusk settled over the adjoining houses. Inside, lamps shone upon familiar faces, empty dishes, yellow kittens and joined hands. The hedge gate stood open. No one had remembered to close it, and no one ever did again.

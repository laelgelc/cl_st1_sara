# The Farther Star

Captain Gilbert had only to say that a thing should be done, and Fanny did it.

She might protest, and often did; she might lift her little hands, pearl-pale against the crimson velvet of the curtains, and declare that no power on earth should compel her; she might appeal to reason, to propriety, even to the weather—but she did it. Or she would announce, with charming gravity, some intention of her own; and Gilbert would laugh, shake his magnificent head, and say, “No, little woman”; and she didn’t.

It is true that he never bullied her in any vulgar sense. His tyranny wore too splendid a uniform. He was generous, admired, fearless; a man whom sailors obeyed in the black confusion of a gale as naturally as planets obey the sun. He could ride like a centaur, speak like a statesman, dance—when he chose to submit to that frivolity—with the lightness of a boy. His blue eyes had looked upon murder in savage seas and upon dawn in latitudes whose very names chilled the blood of drawing-room travellers. Yet those same eyes could soften when Fanny entered, rose-coloured and smiling, until elderly ladies forgave him all his arrogance and young ones envied his wife.

He loved her passionately.

There was the danger.

He loved her beauty, her changeful colour, her quick laughter, her pretty irresolution; but he loved most of all the delicate yielding of her nature beneath his own. She was a wave, running silver-footed toward freedom; he was the rock against which it broke in foam. Had she been stronger, perhaps he would have loved her less. Had he been weaker, she might have loved him more peacefully.

“Fanny,” he said once, “if I ordered you to sail with me to the Arctic Circle tomorrow, you would come.”

“I should do nothing so absurd.”

“You would come.”

“I should remain here and give a ball.”

“You would pack the blue cloak, because I like you in it; forget half your gloves; cry when the coast disappeared; and be happy before we reached Bergen.”

“I should not!”

But she was already wondering whether the blue cloak required relining.

When the Admiralty sent him away, his letters came like commands discharged from a cannon. She was to ride, but not upon the chestnut mare. She was to visit his mother twice a week. She was not to let young Lord Davenant hang about the house. She was to wear the pearls on her birthday and think of the night he had clasped them around her throat.

She obeyed. If she delayed, a dream of his displeasure made delay intolerable.

Yet his dominion was not merely domestic. There were in Captain Gilbert great reaches of imagination, terrible because they existed in a man accustomed to turn imagination into fact. He spoke of the stars as if they were islands marked upon his private chart; of death as if it were a fog-bank through which a competent navigator could steer; of the soul as a ship which no storm could destroy, though seas should close over every mast.

One winter evening, when illness had already laid its grey finger upon him, he and Fanny sat beside the fire. Outside, snow passed the windows in long white squadrons. He had become thin, but not diminished. The flame shone through the hollows of his face and made of him some bronze commander rescued from antiquity.

Fanny knelt beside his chair.

“You will be better in spring,” she said.

“No.”

“You must be.”

“My dear child, I have commanded men, not seasons.”

She hid her face against his knee.

His hand descended upon her hair with its old possessive tenderness.

“You will have enough to live upon?”

“I shall not live.”

“Nonsense. Women always say that, and then order mourning from Paris.”

She looked up indignantly, but saw that he was smiling.

“You will be courted,” he continued. “First discreetly, then assiduously, then with the solemn impudence considered proper toward widows.”

“Gilbert!”

“You are young. You are beautiful. And you have that helpless air which makes every man long to offer advice and every fool believe he could govern you.”

“No one could govern me but you.”

“Remember it.”

The smile had gone. He leaned forward; the great force of him, though his body was failing, seemed to fill the room and press the winter night back from the glass.

“You are mine, Fanny.”

“I know.”

“Not while I live only. Not until some priest reads words above a grave. Mine beyond that.”

She trembled.

“Do not speak so.”

“Promise me you will never marry.”

A little coal fell upon the hearth. The clock counted three seconds, each distinct as a footstep.

She tried to withdraw her hands, but he held them.

“Gilbert, is it right to ask?”

“Right?” he repeated, almost contemptuously. “What has right to do with love?”

“Everything, perhaps.”

“Nothing. Love is older than law and stronger than death. Promise.”

She gazed at him—the face wasted yet heroic, the eyes alight with fever and will. Pity, habit, terror, and a passion which had never learned to distinguish surrender from devotion overwhelmed her.

“I promise.”

“Say it fully.”

“I will never marry again.”

He drew a great breath.

“Good. For if you broke your word—if oceans lay between us, if the earth itself rolled over my bones, if I had gone beyond the farthest star—I should return and claim you.”

She gave a broken cry.

He gathered her to him.

“Hush! You are mine, mine, mine. Why should that frighten you?”

He died before the snowdrops appeared.

For several months Fanny moved through a world which had lost not merely its light but its direction. Gilbert had been north, south, command, contradiction, weather, law. Without him the doors stood strangely open. No voice called from the staircase to inquire why she wore grey when blue became her; no letter instructed her whom to avoid; no broad step crossed the room with the certainty of a conqueror entering a province.

She was free.

Freedom, however, is a large and draughty house to those who have inhabited a cage lined with velvet.

Her circumstances added practical discomfort to metaphysical desolation. Captain Gilbert had possessed the lavishness of an emperor and the arithmetic of a child. His affairs, examined after his death, yielded honour, debts, and very little else. Fanny exchanged the handsome house for narrow lodgings in Kensington, sold the carriage, retained one maid, and learned the alarming difference between desiring a bonnet and paying for it.

Society pitied her, which is to say that it discussed her finances accurately and her feelings inaccurately.

Within a year Mr. Levison, a prosperous widower in the sugar trade, proposed. He had ruddy whiskers, a kind heart, two daughters, and a conservatory whose orchids were mentioned in the same breath as his income. Fanny refused him with sincere gratitude and no hesitation.

That night she dreamed she stood upon a shore under a copper-coloured moon. A black ship moved soundlessly over a motionless sea. Gilbert stood on its deck in full uniform, one hand resting upon his sword.

“You remember,” he said.

She awoke with the taste of salt upon her lips.

After that came Sir James Harlowe, elderly, courteous, and so unobjectionable that his proposal seemed less an assault than an arrangement made by Providence and approved by every aunt in England. For three weeks Fanny considered him. Poverty had lost its romantic outline; the lodgings were cold; her maid had given notice; and Sir James possessed a library, a chapel, and an estate in Sussex where peacocks walked beneath cedar trees.

On the evening when she intended to accept him, she saw Gilbert in church.

The congregation had risen for the final hymn. Beyond the shifting feathers and polished heads, beyond the pale columns and the lights burning like small earthly stars, he stood in the aisle. He was taller than any living man. His face was stern.

Fanny dropped her prayer book.

When she looked again there was only a verger, a shadow, and a marble admiral upon a monument.

Sir James received his refusal the next morning.

The third aspirant was a poet, whose verses concerned ruined towers, pale ladies, and passions with which his own neat life had never brought him into contact. He told Fanny that her sorrow had spiritualized her beauty. She replied that sorrow had chiefly reduced her dress allowance. He admired the remark and put it into a sonnet.

She might have married him for amusement, had Gilbert not visited her dreams in a mood of such majestic ridicule that she laughed aloud in her sleep. The poet went away wounded and subsequently became celebrated after altering the colour of her eyes in print.

Years passed. Fanny was no longer poor, an aunt having died and left her a modest independence; nor was she any longer regarded as a youthful widow whose future required immediate arrangement. Her beauty had changed rather than faded. The rose remained, but under glass; its colour came and went more delicately, and the eyes above it had acquired a listening look, as though some sound inaudible to others travelled forever toward her from immeasurable distance.

She kept her promise—or Gilbert kept it for her.

Then she met John Mowbray.

He did not resemble the captain. This was the first thing that made him dangerous.

Gilbert had been sunlight on rock, a banner in wind, the voice above a storm. Mowbray was deep water under a quiet sky. He was neither dazzling nor eager to dazzle. He had travelled widely without turning geography into a heroic garment for himself. He loved music, old cities, children, and the silence between two people who understand one another. His courtesy did not diminish women into ornaments, though, on the other hand, he possessed a firmness that could become command before one recognized its approach.

They met at a country house where Fanny had gone reluctantly. On the first evening a celebrated pianist performed Beethoven, and Fanny, seated in the shadow of a curtain, forgot for nearly half an hour to listen for footsteps from another world.

When the music ended, Mowbray said, “You were unhappy during the slow movement.”

“What an intrusive observation.”

“Yes.”

“And an incorrect one.”

“No.”

She looked at him, startled into laughter.

It was long since anyone but a ghost had contradicted her with conviction.

Their friendship grew without apparent design. They walked through wet gardens where late roses bowed beneath the rain. They quarrelled over books. He refused to flatter her grief, and because he did not demand its confidence she began to speak of it. Not of the promise—never that—but of Gilbert’s grandeur, generosity, temper, and impossible certainty.

“You admired him,” Mowbray said.

“I worshipped him.”

“That was unfortunate.”

“John!”

“For him as well as for you. Men should not be worshipped. It encourages their worst habits.”

She was angry, yet the remark remained in her mind like a stone cast into a well, disturbing depths whose existence she had preferred not to measure.

Mowbray did not hurry. He seemed content to accompany her through galleries, concerts, gardens, and the minor ceremonies by which civilized people conceal the elemental movements of the heart. But there came an evening in early summer when they stood upon a terrace beneath the stars. The house behind them glowed with music and talk; before them the park descended into silver mist.

Fanny had worn white, with one dark-red rose at her breast.

Mowbray touched the rose.

“Give me this.”

“No.”

“Then keep it until tomorrow.”

“Why?”

“Because tomorrow I shall ask you for more.”

Her heart struck painfully.

“Do not.”

“I shall ask you to marry me.”

She turned toward the darkness.

“You must not.”

“Why?”

“I cannot.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one I have.”

He was silent for a moment. Then, in the grave voice which never rose yet somehow admitted no evasion, he said, “You love me.”

She closed her eyes.

It was true.

The knowledge, long hidden beneath courtesy and fear, opened suddenly like a flower at midnight. She loved his strength because it sheltered rather than dazzled her; his mind, because it invited hers to walk beside it; his hands, because they had never closed upon her as fetters. She loved him with the human heart that still beat in her human breast, asking not eternity but morning, journey, firelight, age.

“Yes,” she whispered.

He took her in his arms.

For one radiant instant she was not a widow, not a promise, not a contested kingdom, but a woman freely giving herself. The stars stood like sentinels above the great courts of space beyond space, and no messenger crossed them.

She accepted him the next day.

That night Gilbert returned.

She found herself in the old house, though she knew even in the dream that it had been sold. Moonlight lay across the bedroom. Every object possessed that cruel precision seen only in dreams: the silver brushes, the blue cloak folded over a chair, the pearls coiled upon the dressing table like frozen tears.

Gilbert stood before the mirror.

At first she saw only his back. He wore no uniform, but the dark coat in which he had travelled home before his final illness. His hair shone with living colour. Then his reflected eyes met hers.

“You have forgotten.”

“No,” she said. “No, Gilbert.”

“You will marry him.”

“I love him.”

The glass darkened, and his image seemed to advance though his body did not move.

“You are mine.”

“You are dead.”

The words cost her an agony greater than any cry, but she spoke them.

He turned.

Death had made no ruin of him. He was more beautiful, more terrible, enlarged beyond mortality as flame enlarges the shape of whatever it consumes.

“Do you imagine death releases what love has taken?”

“I was young. You compelled me to promise.”

“You promised.”

“I have been faithful for years.”

“There are no years where I am.”

“Have pity.”

His expression softened; and the tenderness in it was more dreadful than wrath.

“My little woman, I crossed the dark for you. I waited where suns rise and perish like sparks. Do you think I shall surrender you to a man because he gives you roses and music?”

“He gives me peace.”

“I gave you myself.”

He stretched out his hand.

The moonlight became a road between them, white, endless, cold.

“Come.”

She awoke screaming.

At dawn she wrote to Mowbray, ending their engagement. The letter was incoherent but decisive. She said she had deceived herself; that an obligation, sacred and unalterable, stood between them; that he must not seek her.

He arrived before noon.

Fanny received him in a small morning-room crowded with roses sent by congratulatory friends. Their fragrance oppressed her. She looked frail, feverish, and very lovely, like a pearl held too near a flame.

“You had no right to come,” she said.

“I had every right.”

“I release you.”

“You cannot release me from a bond you deny exists.”

She twisted her handkerchief.

“I made a promise to Gilbert.”

Mowbray’s face changed, but his voice remained gentle.

“What promise?”

“That I would never marry.”

“To a dying man?”

“Yes.”

“Extorted by a dying man.”

“No! Not extorted. I gave it.”

“Freely?”

She could not answer.

He came nearer.

“Fanny, loyalty to the dead is noble. Submission to their injustice is not.”

“You do not understand. He said he would return.”

“And you believe he has?”

“I saw him last night.”

“In a dream.”

“Was it a dream? I saw the room. I heard his voice. He knew—he knew of the rose.”

“Because you knew.”

“He called me.”

Mowbray took both her hands. She tried to draw them away, and he held them—not violently, but with a firmness that sent through her a confused remembrance of another grasp.

“Listen to me. You loved Gilbert. Nothing I ask will dishonour that love. A second happiness does not profane the first.”

“He will not permit it.”

“He has no authority to forbid the living to live.”

“You speak as if the dead were weak.”

“I speak as a living man who loves you.”

She began to weep.

Mowbray’s sternness melted. He bent over her hands.

“Dear child, must all your life be sacrificed to one hour of fear? If Gilbert loved you, let his love be generous. If what visits you is memory, we shall face it together. If it is more than memory—”

He paused.

She looked up.

“If it is more?”

“Then it shall learn that you are not alone.”

There was pride in this, perhaps folly; but love without pride is a lamp without oil. Fanny saw in him, for the first time, something not unlike Gilbert’s unconquerable will. Her resistance, wearied by years of invisible siege, broke against it.

They were married quietly in autumn.

For a while happiness justified them.

They travelled south, following sunlight from Paris to Avignon, from Avignon to Florence, from Florence to Rome. Fanny, who had lived so long among English curtains, condolences, and memories, opened like a flower under warmer skies. She laughed in streets loud with bells; bought ridiculous coloured beads; leaned over fountains where marble gods poured water from their inexhaustible urns. Mowbray taught her to look at pictures without wondering whether Gilbert would have approved them. She learned the spacious companionship of marriage, in which silence need not mean displeasure and difference need not end in defeat.

At Venice they took a palace apartment whose windows opened upon the Grand Canal. At night the black water carried broken gold beneath them, and gondoliers’ voices rose from the darkness like songs remembered from another existence.

“One could believe,” Fanny said, “that death itself must be beautiful here.”

Mowbray put his arm around her.

“Let us believe in life instead.”

She rested against him.

For months Gilbert did not appear.

There were dreams, but he was absent from them; landscapes without a central figure, seas without a ship. Fanny began to think the old tyranny dissolved. Perhaps the dead, seeing happiness from their clear altitude, surrendered jealousy. Perhaps her visions had never been anything but the spasms of a conscience taught to fear freedom. It is true that she avoided the word “promise”; on the other hand, many comfortable philosophies depend upon some door which their disciples agree not to open.

They returned to England after a year. Friends declared Fanny transformed. She had more colour, more animation, even a touch of wholesome carelessness. She gave dinners, went to concerts, and arranged flowers with an extravagance that troubled the servants. Mowbray watched her with quiet delight.

Then, in December, he woke to find her standing beside the open window.

Snow lay on the sill. She wore only her nightdress.

“Fanny!”

She did not move.

He crossed the room and drew her back. Her body was icy.

“What are you doing?”

“Listening.”

“To what?”

She seemed to wake. Her eyes travelled from his face to the window.

“Nothing. I must have been dreaming.”

The following night she spoke in her sleep.

“No, Gilbert. Not yet.”

Mowbray lay rigid beside her.

In the morning he said nothing.

Winter deepened. Fanny grew pale. The roses in her drawing-room became a necessity; if none were available she sent for them, yet often, when their scent filled the house, she ordered them removed. She started at the fall of coal in the grate, at the creak of rigging from ships on the river, at any firm footstep in the hall.

Mowbray persuaded her to travel again. They went to the sea, then to Switzerland, then to a quiet German town where music drifted every evening from a pavilion among lime trees. Changes of air altered nothing. Her restlessness crossed frontiers without a passport.

At night she slept poorly. By day she clung to Mowbray with an affection sharpened by remorse. She followed him from room to room, listened while he read, laid her hand suddenly upon his shoulder as though assuring herself of his solidity.

“Are you happy?” he asked once.

“So happy.”

“Then why are you afraid?”

“I am not.”

“You say that like a child standing beside a broken vase.”

She tried to smile.

The smile died.

Years seemed to pass over her in a season. Her dresses hung loosely. Blue veins showed at her temples. She became exquisitely delicate, a white rose whose petals remain perfect even as the stem withers.

Doctors came. They spoke of nerves, climate, exhaustion, inherited weakness. One recommended iron, another sea air, a third absolute repose. None asked whom she met in sleep.

Mowbray finally did.

It was spring. Fanny lay upon a couch near the window, the afternoon light making a transparent shell of her hands. Outside, rain trembled upon the branches; within, a fire burned though the air was mild.

Mowbray sat beside her.

“Fanny, look at me.”

She obeyed.

“Gilbert has returned.”

Her lips parted.

He waited.

At last she whispered, “He never went away.”

“How often do you see him?”

“Every night.”

The words were scarcely audible, yet they seemed to strike every wall.

“Every night?”

“Sometimes I see him. Sometimes only the light. Sometimes I hear him walking. But he is there.”

“Why did you not tell me?”

“Because you were happy.”

“And you?”

“I belonged to you by day.”

A terrible tenderness moved across his face.

“And by night?”

She turned her head toward the rain.

“By night I am his.”

Mowbray rose abruptly and crossed the room. For several moments he stood with his back to her, mastering emotions which no civilized name could contain—jealousy of a dead man, pity, anger, incredulity, and fear.

When he returned, his voice was controlled.

“What does he say?”

“That I must come.”

“Where?”

She made a faint gesture upward, outward—toward the rain, the sky, the immeasurable courts beyond it.

“He says he has waited. He says the road is open now.”

“You are ill. Your mind repeats an old terror.”

“No.”

“Yes, Fanny. A dream has no hands.”

“He touches me.”

Mowbray flinched.

“He puts his hand upon my heart. It becomes cold beneath it. He says I am slipping away from you—slipping, slipping—”

“No!”

The force of the word shook her.

He knelt and gathered her against him.

“You are my wife. Do you hear? Mine in no childish sense of ownership, but mine by love freely given, by life shared, by every morning we have awakened together. No shadow shall take you.”

She clung to him.

“He is not a shadow.”

“Then let him come while I am here.”

“He will.”

“Good.”

She stared at him in horror.

“Do not defy him.”

“I defy whatever harms you.”

“Gilbert never yielded.”

“Nor shall I.”

For a moment the two men seemed already to confront one another through her: one living, one dead; one holding her exhausted body, the other possessing those chambers of memory into which no husband, however tender, can enter by force. And Fanny, divided between them, felt less like a woman than a narrow bridge over an abyss, shaken by armies advancing from either side.

After that confession she declined rapidly.

There was no drama by daylight. She ate a little, slept a little, smiled when spoken to. She even went once to church, leaning upon Mowbray’s arm. The congregation saw only a fragile lady in lavender silk and a grave husband attentive to her step. Respectability drew its gloves over the mystery.

At home, however, she often paused as though hearing a summons.

“He is nearer,” she would say.

Mowbray refused to answer.

One evening she asked to have the old pearls brought from their case. She had not worn them since Gilbert’s death.

“Why?” said Mowbray.

“He wishes it.”

“No.”

She did not argue. A faint smile touched her mouth.

“They will be put on me somehow.”

In the night Mowbray woke and saw them clasped around her throat.

He had locked the case himself.

He tore them off and flung them across the room. The string broke; pale globes scattered over the carpet, gleaming in the moonlight like small, cold worlds.

Fanny began to laugh, but the laughter turned to sobbing.

After that Mowbray no longer spoke of nerves.

He moved her to a room overlooking the garden, where sunlight entered early. He dismissed unnecessary servants and remained beside her. Love reduced his world to the space between her bed and the window, to medicines, breaths, pulsebeats. He read to her, but she could not follow. He played the melodies she had loved; sometimes the music recalled her, and she would smile at him from an immense distance.

“John,” she said one morning, “if I die, do not think I loved you less.”

“You will not die.”

“I loved you differently.”

“Better?”

She closed her eyes.

“More freely.”

He bent and kissed her.

“Then better.”

Toward sunset on the last day, the clouds divided. A strange radiance entered the room—not the mellow gold of evening, but a white, intense brilliance before which the familiar objects seemed no more than sketches soon to be erased.

Fanny stirred in Mowbray’s arms.

“He has come.”

“No.”

“He has come.”

Her eyes were fixed beyond him.

Mowbray looked toward the window. At first he saw only the garden transfigured by light: wet leaves burning like emeralds, roses become little cups of flame, the sky opening in depth beyond depth. Then the temperature of the room fell.

Fanny shivered.

“Hold me,” she pleaded. “Oh, John, hold me!”

He clasped her with all his strength.

“I have you.”

“No—no—I am slipping.”

“You are here.”

“Slipping, slipping—”

Her face changed. Fear passed from it, and in its place came the old look of surrender, almost of wonder, which Mowbray had never seen but knew at once had belonged to Gilbert.

A voice spoke.

It did not enter through the ear. It sounded in the blood, in the bones, in the foundations of the house.

“Fanny.”

Mowbray rose, still holding her.

“Show yourself!”

The light concentrated near the foot of the bed. Within it stood a man.

Captain Gilbert was not as Fanny had described him, and yet he was more entirely himself than any mortal description could have conveyed. He wore the semblance of youth and the authority of age. Sea winds seemed to move about him; stars burned in the darkness of his eyes. He was heroic, generous, beautiful—and terrible with the serene egotism of one who had crossed death and found there no reason to question his own desire.

His gaze rested upon Fanny.

“My wife.”

“She is mine,” said Mowbray.

Gilbert smiled.

“I warned her.”

“You frightened a dying promise from a young woman.”

“I loved her.”

“You possessed her.”

“What is love that does not possess?”

“Mercy. Freedom. Trust.”

“Words for men who cannot conquer.”

Mowbray’s face whitened.

“She chose me.”

“For a day.”

“For life.”

“Her life is ended.”

At that instant Fanny’s body gave a small convulsive sigh. Her head fell against Mowbray’s shoulder.

He looked down.

The rose-colour had gone. The listening expression had vanished. She was utterly still.

“No,” he whispered.

Then he felt—not saw at first, but felt—something rise from the stillness. It was Fanny, and not Fanny: her delicacy freed from weakness, her beauty gathered into a clear human flame. She stood beside Gilbert in the intenser light, wearing neither bridal white nor widow’s black, but a brightness like moonlight upon pearls.

Mowbray reached for her.

“Fanny!”

Her spirit turned.

In her face were love, sorrow, and an exhaustion beyond tears. For one instant her hands moved toward him.

Gilbert laid his hand upon her shoulder.

The gesture was gentle.

It was also absolute.

“Mine,” he said. “Mine beyond earth, beyond time, beyond the farthest star. My will has prevailed.”

Mowbray stood over the dead body, defying the dead with all the force of the living heart.

“No! You may take what fear kept chained to you. You may claim her promise, her memory, her dream. But she loved me best.”

The great figure’s eyes flashed.

“Best?”

“She loved you because she could not resist you. She loved me because she chose.”

For the first time, uncertainty—or anger resembling uncertainty—disturbed Gilbert’s splendour.

Fanny looked from one to the other.

Her lips formed a word which neither man heard.

Perhaps it was Gilbert.

Perhaps John.

Perhaps only peace.

The radiance increased. Walls, bed, garden, earth itself seemed to retreat before it. Gilbert drew Fanny beside him, and together they passed backward—not into shadow, but into a light so fierce that Mowbray covered his eyes.

When he looked again, the room was dark.

Night had fallen. The fire was dead. Rain whispered against the window as it had on a hundred ordinary evenings.

He remained alone, holding Fanny’s empty body.

Far above the clouded earth, one star burned with an intolerable brightness; then, while he watched, it vanished into the great courts of space beyond space.

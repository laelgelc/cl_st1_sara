Sylvia Seltoun’s removal of her husband from London to Yessney was regarded by her friends as one of those matrimonial victories which justify a woman’s continued interest in other people’s marriages. Mortimer had resisted neither argument nor persuasion; he had merely remained indifferent, which is the most exasperating form of opposition. When at last he consented to exchange his rooms near St. James’s for the family estate among the western combes, Sylvia accepted the surrender with magnanimity.

“You will be so much happier,” she had told him, in the tone of one conferring happiness by appointment. “A man ought to have interests—real interests. Land, tenants, crops, all that sort of thing. London makes men useless.”

Mortimer had looked at her thoughtfully.

“Some men are naturally useless,” he said. “London merely provides witnesses.”

Nevertheless, they went.

Sylvia’s conception of the country had been gathered from water-colours, week-end visits, and the conversation of women who kept cottages within motoring distance of a railway station. She expected lawns with a respectful degree of wildness, cows arranged picturesquely beyond fences, and woods which supplied colour without imposing solitude. Yessney furnished these things, but furnished them with reservations.

The house stood in a hollow where two long hills leaned towards one another as though exchanging confidences over its roof. Behind it, the woods mounted steeply, shutting out a considerable portion of the sky; in front, rough meadows fell away towards a stream that was seldom visible and always audible. The trees came too near the windows, the hills were too watchful, and the stream had the persistent voice of someone repeating a warning in another room.

“It is magnificent,” Sylvia announced on the afternoon of their arrival.

She spoke with decision because she had begun to dislike it.

Mortimer, who in town had displayed only a languid acquaintance with the seasons, altered at Yessney with disconcerting rapidity. He rose early, inspected the byres, discussed drainage with men whose speech Sylvia found aggressively local, and disappeared for hours among the coverts. Mud ceased to offend him; rain became information. The estate, which had been a neglected item in his correspondence, absorbed him with the intimacy of a recovered memory.

“You seem to know every path,” Sylvia remarked one evening.

“I knew them before I knew most people.”

“That is not saying much. You have never taken the trouble to know people.”

“Paths are less apt to pretend they are going somewhere else.”

Sylvia laughed, though she did not care for the answer. Mortimer’s contentment ought to have crowned her success; instead, it appeared to exclude her from it. She had intended to settle him down, but not into a life in which she was unnecessary.

Her first expedition to the home farm deepened her dissatisfaction. The yard was occupied by geese of military temper, pigs with the composure of established proprietors, and several horned cattle whose apparent inattention seemed more alarming than open hostility. A red bullock raised its head and regarded her without moving.

“Is it safe?” she asked the farm boy who accompanied her.

“He’s quiet enough, ma’am.”

The phrase lacked the guarantees Sylvia required. She retreated behind a gate and observed the animal through its bars, preserving towards it the disdain customary between civilised persons and forces they cannot control.

Country life, she decided, had been grossly sentimentalised. The creatures were either dirty, dangerous, or edible, and sometimes all three.

The woods proved no more accommodating. They were beautiful in the manner of certain old churches: one admired them while feeling that one’s presence had not been invited. Narrow ways ran under beech and oak, crossed unexpectedly by roots polished like bone. Ferns crowded the slopes; brambles caught at skirts with the small persistence of creditors. Even at noon there were hollows where the light became green and uncertain.

During one solitary walk Sylvia heard laughter.

It came from somewhere above her—a boy’s laughter, clear, delighted, and without the least suggestion of companionship. She stopped and looked among the trunks. Nothing moved except a jay, which flew out with a harsh complaint.

“Who is there?” she called.

The wood gave back no answer. A moment later the laughter sounded farther off, then ceased.

At dinner she mentioned it.

“One of the village children, I suppose.”

“The village children do not come into that wood,” said Mortimer.

“Why not?”

“They have reasons.”

“What reasons?”

“The kind that are inherited, not explained.”

Sylvia glanced at him across the candles. Country life had developed in him an irritating taste for oracles.

“You talk as if Yessney were inhabited by ghosts.”

“Ghosts are generally harmless. They are only people who have lost their opportunities.”

“And what else is there?”

Mortimer broke a piece of bread with deliberate fingers.

“This is old country. The older gods were never altogether driven out of such places. They lost their temples and their votaries, but not necessarily their power.”

“My dear Mortimer, you do not seriously believe in Pan?”

“I believe that names are less important than continuities.”

“That sounds like something copied from an expensive little book.”

“It may also be true.”

Two days later, returning by an unfamiliar track, Sylvia came upon a stone figure in a clearing. It was scarcely more than a weathered torso set upon a moss-grown pedestal: the head was horned, the mouth curved in an expression which rain and lichen had not softened, and one broken hand still held the remnant of a pipe. At its feet lay a cluster of dark grapes, fresh enough to carry a bloom upon their skins.

The offering offended Sylvia more than the idol. Pagan survivals were tolerable when they remained in guidebooks; a contemporary bunch of grapes implied either superstition among the tenants or practical joking at her expense. She lifted the cluster and flung it into the undergrowth.

“There,” she said aloud, with the briskness of a person correcting an error in household management.

A twig snapped behind her.

Between two hazel stems stood a boy.

He was perhaps seventeen, perhaps younger; his age seemed uncertain because his beauty had none of youth’s incompleteness. His face was brown and narrow, his hair sun-coloured, his eyes very pale. He wore no hat, and his thin shirt was open at the throat. There was something foreign in his stillness, though nothing foreign in his clothes. He looked first at Sylvia, then at the empty stone before the image.

She was about to ask his name when he smiled.

The smile was not insolent; insolence belongs to human arrangements, and the boy appeared unaware that any such arrangements existed. It held amusement, patience, and a cruelty too untroubled to be personal.

“Were those your grapes?” Sylvia demanded.

He made no reply.

“Do you belong to one of the cottages?”

Still smiling, he stepped backward. The leaves received him. Sylvia pushed through the hazels, but there was no one beyond them and no sound of retreating feet. Only the discarded grapes lay crushed under bracken, their juice dark upon the ground.

Mortimer listened to her account without interruption.

“You should not have touched the offering,” he said.

“It was ridiculous.”

“Ridicule is not always a defence.”

“Against what?”

“Against being noticed.”

Sylvia felt a little chill, which she attributed to the open window.

“Who was the boy?”

“I cannot tell you.”

“You mean you will not.”

“That is possible too.”

His calmness angered her. “You encourage this nonsense. I suppose some half-witted shepherd leaves fruit there, and some impudent gypsy boy lurks about to frighten strangers.”

“There are no gypsies within twelve miles.”

“Then one of the farm lads.”

“Did he look like one?”

Sylvia did not answer.

Mortimer’s gaze moved past her towards the darkening hills.

“If you go into the woods,” he said, “keep to the broad paths. And do not meddle with anything you find there. Above all, avoid horned animals.”

“You make the place sound like a savage country.”

“It is savage country. The fact that the rates are collected does not civilise it.”

That night Sylvia lay awake listening to the stream. Once she fancied she heard laughter below the windows: light, golden, equivocal. When she rose and looked out, the lawn was empty, but something moved along the edge of the wood with a pale flicker and disappeared.

By breakfast she had formed a plan of retreat.

“I think we should go back to town next week,” she said. “This damp is abominable, and I have promised to see the Harboroughs.”

“You told me they bored you.”

“That is no reason for neglecting them.”

Mortimer unfolded a letter.

“I am not going.”

“Then I shall go alone.”

“No,” he said.

The word was spoken without authority, almost without emphasis, yet it checked her more effectively than command.

“What do you mean?”

“I mean that you will not go back to London.”

Sylvia laughed with careful brightness. “You have become melodramatic.”

“Perhaps.”

“Do you intend to keep me here by force?”

Mortimer looked at her then, and his expression held neither anger nor affection.

“I do not think force will be required.”

For several days she avoided the woods. She drove along open roads, wrote letters to leafy Kensington, and occupied herself with plans for redecorating rooms she privately hoped never to see again. Yet Yessney pressed upon even these civilised activities. Moths beat against the lamps; mice whispered behind the panelling; from the hills came the occasional belling of deer, a sound at once remote and brutally near.

On the fifth afternoon the weather turned brilliantly clear. Sylvia went out after luncheon, choosing a lane that led across pastures towards the church. It was a respectable route, bordered by hedges and frequented, she had been assured, by the postman.

Halfway across the second meadow she heard piping.

The notes were thin and wandering, no more a tune than wind among reeds, yet they possessed an intention which the wind lacks. They came from the woods upon her left, paused, and began again nearer at hand.

In the field below, sheep lifted their heads together. A horse by the hedge shuddered and broke into a clumsy gallop. Rooks rose from the elms in a black, clamorous cloud. Even the insects seemed to alter their humming, as though the whole countryside had received an order inaudible to her understanding.

Then, far off, came the cries of hounds.

Sylvia knew that a hunt was passing somewhere beyond the ridge. Presently she saw the stag: a great red beast bursting through a gap in the hedge, foam upon its mouth, its flanks dark with sweat. It crossed the lower end of the meadow in long, exhausted bounds. The hounds were not yet visible.

For one hopeful instant it seemed that the animal would continue towards the far wood.

The piping changed.

The stag checked so violently that its forefeet tore the turf. It swung its antlered head towards Sylvia.

She stood beside a solitary thorn tree with no gate near, no wall, no human figure in the fields. The stag stared at her; terror had made its eyes enormous, but beneath the terror something else appeared to listen.

The piping sounded again.

The beast turned and came uphill.

Sylvia ran. The meadow, which had looked smooth from the lane, was ribbed with hidden furrows. Her shoes slipped; her breath tore painfully at her throat. Behind her came the heavy, converging beat of hooves.

At the hedge ahead stood the boy.

He leaned against an ash tree, sun-haired and beautiful, one hand hanging loosely at his side. His pale eyes watched her approach with tranquil interest.

“Help me!” Sylvia cried. “Open the gate—drive it off!”

He did not move.

There was no gate. Only the thick hedge, the ash tree, and the boy’s listening smile.

Sylvia reached him and saw, with a final clarity more dreadful than the antlers behind her, that the narrow shadow cast by his head was divided by two small horns.

The piping ceased.

Close at her back came the stag’s hot, rank breath; before her, the boy lifted his face and laughed—that same clear, golden, equivocal laughter which had followed her through the woods.

Then the antlers filled the sky.

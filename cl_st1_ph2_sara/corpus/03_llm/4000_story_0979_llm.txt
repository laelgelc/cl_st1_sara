## The Red Mark

“I suppose,” said Dot Burton, with the lofty confidence of one who had been less than a fortnight in the country, “that if I met Buckskin Bill I should shoot him.”

Her brother Jack, who was oiling a saddle in the shade of the verandah, looked up and laughed.

“You’d have to be fairly quick about it, my child.”

“I am quick.” Dot sat on the edge of the table, swinging one brown shoe with an air of challenge. “And I can shoot. You said so yourself.”

“I said you could hit a kerosene-tin at twenty paces. A kerosene-tin doesn’t shoot back.”

“Perhaps Buckskin Bill wouldn’t.”

“Perhaps he would be too polite,” said Jack. “But I shouldn’t reckon on it.”

He was a lean, sun-dried man of thirty-five, with quiet grey eyes and a habit of treating his sister’s most determined speeches as though they were the playful snapping of a very small dog. This annoyed Dot, who was twenty-three, independent by conviction, and still sufficiently new to Australia to believe that all difficulties could be overcome by promptness and common sense.

She had come out from England to keep house for Jack after a succession of calamities among his domestic arrangements. The last of these had been the departure, without warning, of a cook who had discovered a richer bachelor twenty miles away. Dot had arrived full of zeal, bringing two trunks, a sewing-machine, six linen frocks quite unsuitable to the climate, and a fixed resolution to make Jack comfortable by means which a mere ignorant male would never have dreamt of.

In eleven days she had transformed the homestead. Curtains, washed and mended, appeared at the windows; the sitting-room was rescued from saddles, spurs, and tobacco tins; bread ceased to resemble sandstone; and Jack found, somewhat to his astonishment, that clean socks grew in his drawer as naturally as grass after rain.

Dot herself had changed less easily. The heat oppressed without subduing her. The glare had browned her face and arms, and her fair hair had acquired a rebellious curl about the temples. She dressed as simply as possible, ignored gloves, and went everywhere with Robin, Jack’s red-and-white cattle-dog, at her heels.

She was small, straight, and boyish, with very blue eyes and a mouth that betrayed every passing emotion. Jack privately considered her as much out of place in that great sun-baked loneliness as a cornflower in a stockyard. But he was exceedingly glad to have her there.

That morning the heat had come early. Beyond the verandah the pasture lay in a pale shimmer, running down to a belt of scrub and rising again toward the blue gums on the eastern ridge. Somewhere behind that ridge Inspector Fletcher Hill and six mounted troopers were beating the country for Buckskin Bill.

The outlaw had become a legend through Jack’s district. His proper name was supposed to be William Buckley, though no one could prove it. He was a horse-breaker, cattle-thief, and occasional bushranger; a man who came and went across immense distances, leaving no more reliable trace than a hawk’s shadow. Twice he had emptied a wealthy squatter’s paddocks. Once he had ridden through a police cordon in darkness with three stolen horses roped behind him. He had never been convicted, though half the district could tell stories of his crimes and the other half could tell stories of his generosity.

Jack had no sympathy with romantic legends when they entered his own branding-yard. During the last six months he had lost twenty-seven head of cattle.

“Twenty-seven!” Dot had exclaimed upon hearing this. “And you think this Bill person took them?”

“I know he took some. As to the rest, he knows more than I do.”

“Then why isn’t he caught?”

“Because he objects.”

It had seemed to Dot a wholly inadequate answer. She had arrived with English notions of policemen, warrants, gaols, and a justice that moved solemnly but inevitably. Jack had been compelled to explain that in a land where one man might have a hundred square miles in which to disappear, justice sometimes arrived after the hoof-marks had blown away.

Now, as she sat on the verandah table, she regarded the distant ridge with bright interest.

“Inspector Hill is certain to catch him this time, isn’t he?”

“Inspector Hill is never certain of anything,” said Jack. “That’s why he generally gets what he wants.”

“What is he like?”

“A long, hard chap with a lantern jaw and eyes that bore holes in you. He doesn’t waste words, and he never laughs unless somebody else is in trouble.”

“How unpleasant!”

“He’s an excellent fellow.”

“That doesn’t follow at all.”

“No,” Jack admitted. “It generally doesn’t.”

He buckled the saddle flap and stood up. Robin immediately rose also, but Jack pointed a stern finger at him.

“You stay with Miss Dot.”

Robin’s ears drooped.

“I don’t need protecting,” said Dot.

“You may not. The poultry does.”

“Jack!”

“Well, keep him with you, anyhow. There’s a green snake about the lower paddock. Stevens saw it yesterday near the hay-barn.”

Dot made a small grimace. She had not yet grown accustomed to being warned casually of creatures that could kill her.

“Would it come near the house?”

“Not if it has any sense.”

“Are they really deadly?”

“Deadly enough. Don’t go poking about in long grass.”

“I never poke.”

“You investigate. It comes to the same thing.”

He lifted the saddle, then paused.

“And don’t shoot any stranger on sight. Hill may send one of his men over.”

“I shall ask who he is first.”

“And believe whatever he tells you?”

Dot tossed her head. “I am not a fool.”

“No,” said Jack affectionately. “That’s what makes you so dangerous.”

He went off laughing, with the saddle on his shoulder. Ten minutes later she watched him ride away toward the ridge, carrying his rifle across his knees. Stevens and the other station-hand had gone before sunrise to muster cattle in the north paddock. For the greater part of the day Dot would be alone.

She did not object. Solitude was still a novelty. She had work enough to fill the hours, and the huge silence of the place—broken by the restless fowls, the creak of the windlass, and the sleepy humming of insects—gave her a pleasant sense of importance.

She washed the breakfast things, made bread, swept the passage, and spent an hour at the sewing-machine repairing shirts which Jack appeared to have torn by deliberate violence. At noon she ate bread and cold mutton under the verandah while Robin sat before her in rigid expectation.

“You are a shameless beggar,” she told him.

Robin wagged his tail without embarrassment.

The air had become breathless. Clouds gathered in the west but gave no promise of rain. The light was white and heavy, pressing upon the homestead, flattening every colour except the harsh blue of the sky and the dark green of the scrub.

After dinner Dot carried a basket of mending to the back room, but the little room was an oven. She endured it for twenty minutes, pricking her finger twice and losing patience with Jack’s socks. Then she went outside in search of air.

Robin accompanied her.

She had no intention of going far. There were eggs to collect from a nest which one wilful hen had made beneath the hay-barn, and she remembered Jack’s warning about the snake. She armed herself with a stick, though she felt slightly ridiculous doing so.

The ground behind the house sloped toward the barn. Dry grass crackled beneath her shoes. A lamb, rejected by its mother and fed from a bottle by Dot, had found its way through a broken rail and came bleating toward her.

“Billy, you little wretch! How did you get out?”

The lamb danced sideways, intoxicated by liberty. Robin looked at it with official disapproval.

Dot set down her basket and advanced. Billy retreated into the strip of long grass beside the barn.

“Come here at once!”

Billy did not.

Dot stepped into the grass.

Something moved by her foot.

It moved without sound—a sliding streak of brilliant green, vivid as a jewel in the sun. For one suspended second she saw the narrow head rise. Then there was a swift, whip-like turn and a stab of pain above her wrist.

She cried out.

Robin sprang forward, barking furiously. The snake vanished under a pile of timber, and the dog plunged after it.

“Robin! No!”

Dot staggered back into the yard. Two small punctures showed upon her arm. She stared at them stupidly. The pain was not great, but a sick terror swept over her, turning the hot world suddenly cold.

The snake was poisonous. Jack had said so.

She tried to remember what one did. Tie something above the bite—cut the wound—suck out the poison. Her thoughts scattered. She pulled at the ribbon around her neck, but her fingers had become clumsy. The ribbon knotted. She tugged harder, breathing in short, desperate gasps.

Robin came bounding back. He ran toward the house, stopped, and barked at her.

“Jack!” she called.

The empty yard flung the name back in silence.

She took two steps and fell to her knees.

A horse whinnied beyond the front of the house.

Robin whirled and raced away. Dot heard the clatter of hoofs, a man’s sharp voice, then running feet. She tried to rise and could not.

A stranger came around the corner of the house.

He was a big man, bareheaded, with a slouch hat crushed in one hand. His shirt was open at the throat, and the dust of hard riding lay white upon his boots and breeches. A revolver hung low at his hip. He took in the scene with a single glance—the stick, the trampled grass, Dot’s hand clutched above her wrist.

“Snake?” he said.

“Yes. Green. There—under—”

He was beside her before she had finished. He dragged a leather thong from his pocket, bound it tightly above her elbow, and drew his knife.

Dot recoiled.

“Keep still!” he ordered.

There was something in his voice that compelled obedience. He made two swift cuts across the punctures. The blade hurt fiercely; she gave a cry and tried to pull away.

His arm went around her, hard as iron.

“Steady! It’s got to be done.”

Then, to her horror and amazement, he bent his head and set his mouth to the wound.

He sucked and spat, sucked and spat again, while she knelt half-supported against him, too frightened to protest. The sunlight flashed upon his thick brown hair. His hand held her arm with immense strength and extraordinary gentleness.

For a long time he did not speak. He sucked and sucked until the blood flowed freely and the skin around the bite was raw.

At last he raised his head.

“Did you see what sort?”

“Bright green.”

“How big?”

“I don’t know. Three feet—perhaps more.”

“Feeling sick?”

“A little.”

“Giddy?”

“Yes.”

“That may be fright. Can you stand?”

He lifted her before she answered. Dot had never been carried by any man since childhood, and even in her terror she was conscious of the ease with which he did it. He bore her across the yard and into the cool passage, Robin circling them in a fever of anxiety.

“Where’s the whisky?”

“In the cupboard—but—”

He found it, poured a stiff measure, added water, and held the glass to her lips.

“I don’t drink whisky,” Dot said faintly.

“You do now.”

“It is horrible.”

“So are funerals. Drink.”

She obeyed, choking and gasping. He gave her more, then laid her upon the horsehair sofa in the sitting-room and loosened the thong slightly.

“Anyone else here?”

“No.”

“Where are they?”

“My brother has gone with the police. The men are mustering.”

“How long before they return?”

“Not till evening. Oh!” She caught at his sleeve. “Am I going to die?”

His eyes came to hers.

They were extraordinarily blue—blue with a brightness that seemed almost out of place in his sunburnt face. He had the look of some young wild animal that had never been wholly tamed: alert, dangerous, confident in its strength. Yet the smile that suddenly showed his white teeth was warm and oddly reassuring.

“Not if I can help it.”

“Do you know about snake-bites?”

“A little.”

“You’re not afraid of the poison?”

“No.”

“You might have had a cut in your mouth.”

“I hadn’t.”

“How could you know?”

He laughed softly. “I generally know what risks I’m taking.”

There was something guarded beneath the humour, but Dot was too shaken to notice it fully. She lay watching while he fetched warm water, salt, and a clean strip of linen. He seemed entirely at home in the practical business of tending her. His hands, though rough, were deft. He washed the cuts, examined them closely, and bound them.

“You know a great deal for a policeman,” she said.

His hands paused for no more than an instant.

“Do I?”

“My brother said Inspector Hill might send someone.”

“Did he?”

She looked more carefully at the revolver, the riding-boots, and his hard, weather-beaten face. The conclusion seemed obvious.

“Are you Inspector Hill himself?”

He bent over the bandage. “Would it make any difference if I were?”

“I suppose not. Jack said you were tall.”

“Many men are tall.”

“And that your eyes bored holes in people.”

“Do mine?”

“Yes,” Dot said frankly. “A little.”

“Then perhaps you’d better be careful what you tell me.”

The whisky had begun to spread through her in hot, bewildering waves. She was no longer cold, but the room seemed unsteady and very far away.

“You are Inspector Hill,” she insisted.

The stranger tied the bandage and sat back on his heels.

“You may call me what you please.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the only one you’re going to get at present.”

“How absurd! Policemen aren’t mysterious.”

“Aren’t they?”

“No. They wear uniforms and tell people to move along.”

“Not much traffic here to move along, is there?”

She almost laughed, then remembered the snake and shivered.

He rose and looked down at her. His expression altered.

“Lie quiet. I’m going to find the brute.”

“No!” She sat up abruptly. “Don’t leave me!”

The words escaped before pride could stop them. Colour rushed into her face. He stood very still, and for one moment the force of the man seemed to throb behind his silence.

Then he said gently, “All right. I won’t.”

He sat in the chair opposite and rested his arms upon his knees. Robin came to him, sniffed his boots, and, after a solemn consideration, placed his muzzle on the stranger’s thigh.

“Traitor!” said Dot.

The stranger rubbed the dog’s ears.

“He knows a decent man when he meets one.”

“He knows anyone who will scratch his head.”

“Much the same thing.”

“Are you sure I shall live?” Dot asked.

“Quite sure.”

“You can’t be.”

“I can look sure, anyhow. That’s half the business.”

“The police business?”

“Any business.”

She studied him. “You don’t look like a policeman.”

He raised his brows. “What should I look like?”

“More severe. Jack says Inspector Hill has a lantern jaw.”

The stranger rubbed his own square chin with a reflective hand.

“That condemns me.”

“Perhaps Jack exaggerated. He does sometimes, when he thinks it amusing.”

“And do you always believe him?”

“Usually. Jack is the best man in the world.”

“He loses cattle, though.”

Dot sat up a little straighter. “How did you know that?”

“Most men in these parts do.”

“Because of Buckskin Bill?”

The blue eyes became suddenly watchful.

“Sometimes.”

“Have you nearly caught him?”

“Nearly.”

“Where?”

“In the scrub beyond the eastern ridge.”

“Then why are you here?”

“Chance.”

“A lucky chance for me,” she said, with sudden earnestness. “If you had not come—”

He looked away toward the bright square of the window.

“Yes,” he said briefly. “Lucky chance.”

Dot closed her eyes. The whisky made her drowsy, but curiosity remained stronger than sleep.

“Is Buckskin Bill very wicked?”

“That depends on who tells the story.”

“He steals cattle.”

“So they say.”

“Jack is sure of it.”

“Then he probably does.”

“And he shoots people?”

“Not often.”

“Not often!” Her eyes opened wide. “How calmly you say it!”

“You asked.”

“Has he shot policemen?”

“He has fired at them.”

“That is almost the same.”

“It depends how straight he fires.”

She frowned, trying to decide whether this was a joke.

“You don’t seem to dislike him very much.”

“It’s a mistake to dislike a man you’re hunting.”

“Why?”

“Because hate makes you impatient.”

“Oh!” Dot regarded him with unwilling admiration. “That is rather clever.”

“Thank you.”

“But isn’t it hateful—hunting a man?”

The stranger’s hand ceased upon Robin’s head.

“Hateful to whom?”

“To the man, of course. One against seven! All of you tracking him down like a wild beast, shutting him in, waiting for him to break cover—”

“He chose the game.”

“Perhaps. But the odds are unfair.”

“He has the country on his side.”

“And you have the law.”

“Is the law so much use in the scrub?”

“You have rifles and horses and telegraphs and everybody helping you.”

“Not everybody.”

There was a dryness in his tone that made her glance at him quickly.

“Do people shelter him?”

“P’r’aps.”

“Would you arrest them too?”

“If I were Inspector Hill?”

“Of course.”

Again that magnetic smile flashed across his face.

“You’re very determined to give me that profession.”

“You haven’t denied it.”

“No.”

“Then you admit it!”

“No.”

“You are impossible.”

“So I’ve been told.”

Dot sank back impatiently. “Well, whoever you are, I dislike man-hunting.”

“Yet you were going to shoot Buckskin Bill.”

She stared. “Who told you?”

“Your brother might have mentioned it.”

“When did you see Jack?”

“I didn’t say I had.”

“Then how—?”

“A woman who keeps a loaded rifle near the kitchen door and practises at kerosene-tins is fairly easy to understand.”

Dot glanced toward the corner. Her small rifle stood there exactly as he described. She felt foolish, and therefore became dignified.

“I should shoot him if he tried to steal Jack’s cattle.”

“Would you?”

“Yes.”

“Suppose he came for bread instead.”

“I should give him bread and then send for the police.”

“Suppose he came wounded.”

“I should attend to him first.”

“And send for the police afterward?”

“Yes—I suppose so.”

“Even if he had saved your life?”

She looked down at the bandage around her arm. The room seemed suddenly very quiet.

“That would be different,” she said.

“How different?”

“I should owe him something.”

“His freedom?”

“I don’t know.” She hesitated, troubled by the question. “It would be dishonourable to betray someone who had saved me.”

“Even a cattle-thief?”

“Yes.”

“Even Buckskin Bill?”

She met his eyes. There was no laughter in them now. They watched her steadily, almost sternly.

“Yes,” she said. “Even Buckskin Bill.”

He leaned back, and something relaxed in his face.

“That’s fair.”

“Fairness has nothing to do with law, I suppose,” Dot retorted.

“Not always.”

“Then you agree with me?”

“I didn’t say that.”

“You never say anything plainly.”

“Plain speaking has sent better men than I am to gaol.”

She laughed despite herself. “Now you sound like the outlaw.”

“Do I?”

“A little. But I expect you know him so well that you have caught his tricks.”

“That may be it.”

A silence followed. The heat deepened outside. Cicadas shrilled among the gums, and from the far paddock came the faint, persistent bleating of the lamb. Dot’s eyelids drooped.

The stranger looked toward the kitchen.

“When did you last eat?”

“At noon.”

“What time is it?”

He drew out a battered silver watch. “Nearly three.”

“It cannot be!”

“It is.”

“You must be hungry.”

“Man-hunting is hungry work.”

“There! I knew you were with the police.”

“Did you?” he said, but he did not look at her.

Dot put her feet to the floor.

“What are you doing?”

“Getting you something to eat.”

“You are doing nothing of the sort.”

“I am quite well.”

“You’re half-drunk.”

“I am not!”

“It was good whisky.”

“It was disgusting whisky. But there is cold mutton, and I made bread this morning.”

“You lie still and tell me where to find it.”

“No. Men never find anything, even when it is directly in front of them.”

“This one does.”

“I am sure you would take the sugar and overlook the bread.”

Nevertheless she stood, swayed, and would have fallen if he had not caught her. His hands closed upon her shoulders. For a moment she was held against him, conscious of his strength, of the dust and sun upon his clothes, of his face bent close above her.

Neither spoke.

Dot’s heart began to beat in a manner that could not reasonably be blamed upon poison. She pulled away with what dignity she could summon.

“I told you I was quite well.”

“So I see.”

His voice was low. She turned toward the kitchen, aware that her ears were burning.

He followed her and compelled her to sit at the scrubbed deal table while he searched the larder. To Dot’s annoyance, he found everything immediately. He cut bread and mutton, brought pickles, and even discovered a covered apple tart intended for Jack’s supper.

“You may not touch that,” she said.

“Why not?”

“It is for my brother.”

“I thought you said Jack was the best man in the world.”

“He is.”

“Then he’ll give it to a starving officer of the law.”

“I have not yet established that you are one.”

“You’ve been trying hard enough.”

He placed half the tart upon the table.

“You have the appetite of a bushranger,” Dot observed.

“An active life.”

“Do you live chiefly on other people?”

“When compelled.”

She watched him eat. He did so with the frank concentration of a man genuinely hungry, yet without haste or clumsiness. Robin sat beside him, receiving occasional scraps.

“You’ll spoil that dog,” she said.

“He deserves it. He brought me round to the back.”

“Did he?”

“Came racing out like a mad thing, barking and running back. Clever fellow.”

Robin thumped his tail.

“He knew I was in danger,” Dot said softly.

“So did I, when I saw him.”

The stranger’s eyes rested on her bandaged arm. “How do you feel now?”

“Sleepy.”

“That’s the whisky.”

“And the heat.”

“And the fright.”

“I was not so very frightened.”

“No?”

“Only at first.”

He smiled.

“Are you laughing at me?”

“Not much.”

“You are.”

“Well, you looked very small in that yard.”

“I am not small.”

“Of course not.”

“I am five feet four.”

“A giantess.”

Dot refused to speak to him for nearly a minute. He finished the tart without discomfort.

At last she said, “What happens if Buckskin Bill gets away?”

“He goes on being Buckskin Bill.”

“And Inspector Hill?”

“Looks foolish.”

“I should like to see that.”

“So should I.”

She caught the gleam in his eyes and laughed.

“You are not at all loyal to your superior.”

“Perhaps he isn’t my superior.”

“Then you are Inspector Hill.”

“Back to that, are we?”

“Tell me!”

“No.”

“Why not?”

He folded his arms on the table and leaned toward her.

“Because I want a bargain.”

“A bargain? What sort?”

“If Buckskin Bill slips through the cordon today, you are to give the man who saved your life fair treatment whenever you meet him again.”

“But if you are Inspector Hill—”

“I said without prejudice.”

“You talk as if you expected to be accused of something.”

“Most men are, sooner or later.”

“What must I promise?”

“That you’ll judge me by what I have done to you—not by what other people say of me.”

Dot’s pulse quickened inexplicably. His gaze held hers; it seemed to draw the truth from her before she could dress it in caution.

“I should do that in any case.”

“Say it.”

“If Buckskin Bill escapes today, I will treat you without prejudice—whatever your profession may be.”

“Shake hands on it.”

She gave him her left hand. His fingers closed around it, firm and warm.

“That’s a bargain,” he said.

“But why should Buckskin Bill’s escape matter?”

“It makes the bargain more interesting.”

“You are the strangest policeman in Australia.”

“That leaves a wide field.”

He released her slowly.

The room was very still. Dot could hear the clock ticking on the dresser and Robin breathing under the table. The world outside had acquired a golden haze. The harsh afternoon was softening toward evening, and bars of mellow light slanted across the floor.

The stranger rose.

“You ought to sleep.”

“I am not tired.”

“You can hardly keep your eyes open.”

“That is only the whisky.”

“Lie down.”

His tone brought back the authority with which he had cut the wound, and Dot obeyed, though protesting. He led her into the sitting-room and arranged a cushion beneath her head.

“Will you stay?” she asked.

“For a while.”

“Until Jack comes?”

He did not answer at once.

“Until you’re safe.”

“I am safe now.”

“Yes.”

“Then you are going?”

“Not yet.”

She tried to study his face, but the golden room wavered. His features became uncertain, while his eyes remained intensely, impossibly blue.

“You haven’t told me your name,” she murmured.

“You gave me one.”

“Inspector Hill.”

“That will do.”

“No. It doesn’t fit.”

“What would fit?”

“I don’t know. Something wild.”

He laughed under his breath.

“You’ll regret that when the whisky wears off.”

“I never regret the truth.”

“Don’t you?”

“Never.”

“That’s because you haven’t lived long enough.”

She frowned, but the effort was too great. Her eyes closed.

After a moment she felt his hand upon her bandaged arm. His touch was careful, almost reverent. She opened her eyes again.

“You won’t untie it?”

“No.”

His face was very near. The room had darkened behind him, and he seemed to bend toward her out of a dream.

“I owe you my life,” she whispered.

“Remember the bargain instead.”

“I shall remember both.”

His fingers moved along the bandage to the small red stain above her wrist. Then, with a deliberation that held her powerless, he lifted her arm and pressed his lips to the wounded place.

It was one burning moment—nothing more.

Dot made no sound. A tremor went through her from head to foot. Before amazement or anger could find words, sleep rose like deep water and overwhelmed her.

She slept as securely as any enchanted princess behind her hedge of thorns, while the man beside her stood listening to the silence.

Presently he lowered her arm, drew a light rug over her, and went out.

Robin followed him to the yard.

The stranger replaced the water-bucket, rinsed the blood from his knife, and examined the ground beside the barn. With a long pole he shifted the top length of timber. The green snake slid into view, angry and swift. He struck once, but it escaped into a crevice beneath the stacked wood.

He stood back, frowning.

“Keep clear of that, mate,” he said to Robin.

The dog wagged his tail.

At the front of the house the stranger tightened his horse’s girth. The animal was a rangy brown mare darkened with sweat, her flanks scored by hard riding. He gave her water in cautious measures, then mounted.

For a little while he sat looking at the house.

The declining sun burned upon the windows. Dot slept within, one hand curled under her cheek, the other resting above the bandage.

A sound came faintly from the east—the far-off crack of a rifle.

The rider turned his head.

“Time to break cover,” he said.

He touched the mare with his heel and rode west, not toward the ridge where the police were searching, but across the open paddock in the direction of the river flats. Robin followed for fifty yards, then stopped and watched him dwindle into the heat.

An hour later Dot awoke.

At first she did not know where she was. The room was shadowy, and the evening air stirred the curtain. Her head ached. Her mouth tasted of smoke and whisky.

Then she saw the bandage.

Memory came in a rush—the grass moving, the green flash, the stranger’s arm around her, blue eyes, laughter, the pressure of his lips upon the wound.

She sat up quickly.

“Inspector Hill!”

There was no answer.

She called again, went into the passage, and searched the kitchen and yard. The stranger had gone. His plate and glass stood washed beside the sink, and the remains of the apple tart had been covered neatly with a cloth.

“Well!” said Dot aloud.

Robin came from under the verandah, wagging his tail.

“You are no help whatever. Why did you let him go?”

Robin yawned.

Dot touched the bandage. The recollection of that last moment sent a wave of colour into her face.

“How dare he?” she whispered.

Yet indignation proved strangely difficult to maintain. He had saved her life. He had watched beside her. He had been gentle, amusing, absurdly secretive—and he had kissed not her hand but the place where death had touched her.

The distinction mattered, though Dot could not have explained why.

She was still standing in the kitchen when hoofs sounded in the distance.

“Jack!”

She ran to the front verandah.

Three riders were approaching from the east. Jack came first, sitting wearily in the saddle. Beside him rode a tall, angular man in a grey shirt and dusty coat. His face was long and hard, the jaw unmistakably lantern-shaped. A trooper followed them, leading a lame horse.

Dot gripped the verandah rail.

The riders entered the yard.

“Halloa!” Jack called. “All well?”

Dot stared at the angular stranger.

He dismounted and looked at her with keen, sardonic eyes that did indeed seem capable of boring holes in people.

“Dot,” said Jack, “this is Inspector Fletcher Hill.”

The world gave one slow turn.

Inspector Hill touched his hat.

“Pleased to meet you, Miss Burton.”

Dot said nothing.

Jack’s smile vanished. “What is it? You look as white as a sheet.”

She put her hand behind her.

“Nothing.”

His eyes dropped to the bandage.

“What have you done to your arm?”

“It is only a scratch.”

Jack was up the steps in two strides. He seized her wrist, saw the stains, and went grim beneath his tan.

“That’s no scratch. What happened?”

“I—cut it.”

“On what?”

Dot’s mind became empty.

Inspector Hill’s gaze moved from her face to the yard, to the tethering-post, and then to a fresh hoof-mark in the dust.

“A visitor?” he asked.

Jack turned sharply.

“Who’s been here?”

“No one,” Dot said.

The lie was so transparent that even Robin looked embarrassed.

Hill walked to the edge of the verandah. “A horse stood there not long ago.”

Dot’s heart hammered.

Jack looked at her in bewilderment. “Was it one of Hill’s men?”

“No.”

“Who, then?”

“I don’t know.”

Hill stooped and examined the hoof-mark. “Brown mare. Worn shoe on the near fore. Hard ridden.”

Dot leaned against the post.

The inspector straightened. There was no excitement in his face, but his eyes became harder.

“Which way did he go?”

“I didn’t see.”

“A man, was it?”

She was silent.

Jack’s hand closed on her shoulder. “Dot, answer him.”

The three men waited.

She remembered the bargain. Judge me by what I have done to you—not by what other people say.

“I won’t,” she said.

Jack stared. “You won’t what?”

“I won’t answer.”

“Good Lord! Why?”

“I gave my word.”

“To whom?”

“I don’t know.”

The inspector’s thin mouth moved very slightly. “I think you do.”

“I don’t know his name.”

“But you know what he looked like.”

Dot met the keen eyes and discovered, with some surprise, that she was no longer frightened.

“I shall tell you nothing.”

The trooper behind them shifted in his saddle. Jack flushed.

“Go and see to the horses, Carey,” said Hill.

The trooper withdrew without comment.

Jack drew Dot into the sitting-room. Hill followed at a more leisurely pace.

“Now,” Jack said, shutting the door, “what the devil has happened?”

“Don’t bully her,” said Hill.

“I’m not bullying. Look at her arm!”

Hill looked. “Snake?”

Dot gave him a startled glance.

He nodded toward the bandage. “Cut, sucked, and tied above the bite. It wasn’t done for a scratch.”

Jack turned grey.

“A snake bit you?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“This afternoon.”

“And you’ve been standing there talking nonsense! Which sort?”

“A green one.”

Jack caught her by both shoulders. “How long ago? Are you sick? Giddy?”

“I was, but I am all right now.”

“Who treated it?”

She bit her lip.

Hill answered for her. “The visitor.”

Dot looked away.

Jack began to understand. He released her slowly.

“You thought he was one of Hill’s men.”

“I thought—” She stopped.

“You thought he was Hill himself,” said the inspector.

Dot’s face flamed. “He did not tell me so.”

“No,” Hill said. “He wouldn’t.”

Jack wheeled upon him. “You know who it was?”

“I can make a reasonable guess.”

The silence that followed seemed to fill the room.

At last Jack said, “Bill?”

Hill’s eyebrows rose. “I didn’t say so.”

“But you think it.”

“I think Buckskin Bill broke through our line about half-past one. I think he changed horses at McRae’s abandoned hut and came west. I think he was seen two miles from here shortly after two. And I think the hoof-mark outside was made by the mare he stole from Wilson last Thursday.”

Dot drew a sharp breath.

There it was—the name that had hovered beneath every word, beneath every smile and evasion. The man she had declared herself ready to shoot had carried her from the yard, put his mouth to her poisoned flesh, fed her whisky, and bargained for her mercy.

Her hand went instinctively to the red mark under the bandage.

Jack saw the gesture and misunderstood it.

“What did he do to you?”

“Nothing!”

The answer came with such violence that both men stared.

“He saved my life,” Dot went on. “He came when I was alone. He cut the bite and sucked the poison out. He stayed until I was better. If he had not come, I should have died.”

Jack’s face altered.

“Are you sure it was venomous?”

“Yes.”

“You said you saw it?”

“Beside the barn. Bright green. It went under the timber.”

Hill glanced at Jack. “There are harmless green tree-snakes.”

Dot lifted her chin. “This was not harmless.”

“How do you know?” Jack asked.

“Because I know.”

Her certainty was chiefly emotional and therefore unshakable. Jack, who knew his sister, was aware that argument would accomplish nothing. Yet he was badly shaken. He began to pace the room.

“And after he’d treated you?”

“I gave him food.”

“Of course you did.”

“He was hungry.”

“Most thieves are, when they’ve been chased all day,” said Hill.

Dot flashed him an indignant look. “He did not steal the food.”

“No?”

“I offered it.”

“That makes a legal difference.”

“Does everything have to be legal with you?”

“Nearly everything. It’s a weakness of the profession.”

Jack stopped pacing.

“What did he tell you?”

“Very little.”

“Did he ask about the search?”

“No.”

“The number of men? Their direction?”

“No.”

“Did he ask when we should return?”

Dot hesitated. “He asked when I expected you.”

Hill’s eyes sharpened.

“But that was because of the bite,” she said quickly. “He wanted to know whether I should be alone.”

“Did he leave a message?”

“No.”

“Say where he was going?”

“No.”

“Which way did he ride?”

“I told you—I didn’t see.”

Hill studied her for a long moment. Dot returned the look with all the courage she possessed and some she did not.

Finally he said, “Miss Burton, Bill is wanted on several charges.”

“I know.”

“He’s not a harmless romantic gentleman who takes cattle because the moon is full. He steals for profit, and he carries a revolver.”

“So do you.”

“I have authority.”

“That would be comforting if one were shot by it.”

Jack made an impatient sound, but Hill’s mouth twitched.

“You have been in Australia twelve days, I understand.”

“Eleven.”

“Then naturally you know the country better than we do.”

“I know what happened in my own house.”

“So does Bill, apparently.”

Dot flushed again.

Jack intervened. “That’s enough, Hill.”

The inspector gave a slight shrug and turned toward the window. “As you please.”

Jack put his arm around Dot’s shoulders.

“Tell me the whole thing, old girl.”

She did—at first haltingly, then with increasing steadiness. She told of the lamb, the snake, Robin’s barking, and the stranger’s sudden arrival. She described the thong, the knife, the whisky, and the long minutes during which he had drawn the poison from her arm.

She did not mention the kiss.

Some things were not lies merely because they remained unspoken.

When she had finished, Jack’s face was grave.

“And you promised not to betray him?”

“I promised to treat him without prejudice if Buckskin Bill escaped.”

Hill made a dry sound. “He likes a wager.”

“He knew who I thought he was,” Dot said slowly. “He let me think it.”

“But didn’t tell you,” said Hill. “That is Bill all over. He will ride through the law, but he has a fussy objection to speaking a direct falsehood.”

“He saved her,” Jack said.

“Yes.”

The single word held no mockery.

Jack went to the cupboard and poured himself whisky. His hand shook slightly. He swallowed and turned back.

“Where’s the snake?”

“Under the timber by the barn.”

“I’m going to look.”

“Jack, don’t!”

He took his rifle from the passage. Hill followed him, and Dot, after an instant, followed both.

The light was fading, but the yard still glowed beneath a copper-coloured sky. Long shadows stretched from the barn. Robin ran ahead, nose down.

They approached the stack of timber cautiously.

“Stay back,” Jack ordered.

He and Hill shifted the lengths one by one. Dust rose. Beetles scurried into darkness. Nothing moved.

“It may have gone,” said Hill.

Then Robin began to bark near the broken rail.

Dot hurried toward him.

The bottle-fed lamb lay in the grass on its side.

“Billy!”

She knelt and touched it. The small body was still warm, but the legs were rigid. Two punctures showed on the bare skin near the nose.

Jack bent over her. He examined the marks, then looked toward the timber.

“So,” he said.

Dot’s eyes filled with tears. “It was the same snake. He followed me. He must have tried to nose it.”

Jack laid a hand on her head.

Hill stood behind them, silent.

Presently Jack lifted the lamb and carried it into the yard. His face had hardened, but not against the outlaw.

“You’d have been like that,” he said to Dot.

She nodded.

“If Bill hadn’t come.”

“Yes.”

Jack looked east, where the ridge had become a black line against the last glow of evening. Then he looked west, across open country already dim beneath the first stars.

“Which way did he go?” he asked quietly.

Dot’s lips parted.

Jack gave her a tired smile.

“No, don’t tell me. I don’t want to know.”

Hill turned his head.

Jack met his gaze. “You heard me.”

“I heard.”

“He stole my cattle.”

“Probably.”

“He may steal them again.”

“Very likely.”

Jack looked down at the dead lamb in his arms. “But he saved my sister.”

Hill said nothing.

“I reckon the account stands in his favour tonight,” Jack continued.

“You’re not asking me to agree.”

“No. I’m telling you that no man who comes to my place wounded or hungry is going to be handed over by my sister after saving her life.”

“Harbouring an offender is an offence.”

“Then charge me.”

Dot sprang up. “No!”

Neither man paid attention.

Hill gazed westward for a long time. Darkness was settling rapidly over the flats. Even if he had known the direction, pursuit would now have been difficult. Bill had gained hours, fresh water, food, and all the advantage of night.

At last the inspector put his hands into his pockets.

“My horse is lame,” he remarked.

Jack’s expression did not change. “So I noticed.”

“Carey’s mount has thrown a shoe.”

“Bad luck.”

“It would be foolish to ride after anyone tonight.”

“Extremely.”

Hill glanced at Dot. “Particularly when no one saw which way he went.”

“No one,” she said.

“Quite.”

He walked back toward the verandah.

Jack stood still for a moment, then set the lamb down gently beside the barn. Dot slipped her hand into his.

“Are you angry with me?” she asked.

“With you? No.”

“I said I would shoot him.”

“So you did.”

“I was a fool.”

“You were a new chum.”

“Is that the same thing?”

“For the first month or two.”

She leaned her head against his shoulder.

“You believe he saved me?”

Jack looked at the bandage. “I believe it.”

“And you’re glad he escaped?”

He sighed.

“I’m a cattle-farmer. I can’t be glad a cattle-thief escaped.”

“Oh!”

“But I’m glad the man who saved you did.”

Dot pressed his arm.

“That’s different, is it?” she asked.

“Entirely.”

“Men have very convenient consciences.”

“We need them,” said Jack. “Women make life complicated.”

They went back to the house. Hill was standing on the verandah while Carey tended the horses. The inspector accepted Jack’s offer of supper and a bed without reference to the fugitive. He ate cold mutton, bread, and the surviving half of the apple tart. Dot noticed that he had nearly as good an appetite as the outlaw, though less charm in exercising it.

During the meal the conversation remained determinedly ordinary. They discussed the weather, cattle prices, a broken pump, and the chances of rain. Hill asked Dot whether she liked Australia.

“I am beginning to,” she said.

“Despite the snakes?”

“Not because of them.”

“And the bushrangers?”

Jack gave a warning cough.

Dot met the inspector’s eyes. “I have met only one person who may have been a bushranger.”

“Did you like him?”

She looked down at her plate.

“That has nothing to do with justice.”

Hill’s mouth became sardonic. “On the contrary, Miss Burton. It has had a great deal to do with justice since the world began.”

After supper Carey went to sleep in the men’s quarters. Jack showed Hill to the spare room. Before retiring, the inspector stepped outside to smoke.

Dot stood alone in the sitting-room. She unwound the outer strip of linen and examined her arm. The cuts were tender and swollen. Around them lay the faint bruising of the thong, and at the centre was the tiny red mark where the snake had struck.

She touched it very, very slowly.

The outer door creaked.

Dot hastily lowered her sleeve.

Hill entered, carrying his unlit pipe.

“Your brother says the windows are to remain shut because of the mosquitoes,” he said. “I prefer the mosquitoes.”

“They prefer you, no doubt.”

“Undoubtedly.”

He paused near the table.

“Bill had a good horse?”

“I don’t know.”

“You saw it.”

“I did not notice it particularly.”

“A brown mare?”

She said nothing.

Hill’s eyes flicked to her face, but his tone remained casual. “He’ll make the river before midnight if she holds up.”

Dot’s heart leapt. “Will you follow in the morning?”

“Perhaps.”

She stood very straight. “If you do, I hope you don’t catch him.”

“So does he.”

“You think me very wrong.”

“I don’t waste time thinking people wrong when they are merely grateful.”

“Merely!”

“It is a strong word in my profession.”

She considered him. “Why did you tell me about the river?”

“Did I?”

“Yes.”

“I was thinking aloud.”

“You never do anything without a reason.”

“Your brother has been slandering me.”

Dot moved closer.

“Inspector Hill, if you catch him—what will happen?”

“He’ll be tried.”

“And imprisoned?”

“If convicted.”

“For how long?”

“That depends upon the charges proved.”

She searched the long, austere face for some sign of pity and found none. Yet neither did she find cruelty.

“He is not all bad,” she said.

“No man worth catching ever is.”

“He might have left me.”

“Yes.”

“He risked staying.”

“Yes.”

“And perhaps he had poison in his mouth.”

“Probably not. Bill is careful.”

“But he took the chance.”

Hill’s gaze rested upon the hidden wound.

“That may be entered on the other side of the account.”

“Whose account?”

“Yours. Your brother’s. Mine, perhaps.”

Hope stirred within her. “Then you will let him go?”

“I did not say that.”

“You are as impossible as he is!”

This time Hill almost smiled.

“I’ve been after Bill for three years. I know some of his habits.”

“Do you hate him?”

“No. That would make me impatient.”

Dot started.

The words were not unusual, perhaps; any experienced hunter might say the same. Yet hearing them again sent a strange thrill through her.

“He said that too.”

“Did he?”

“Yes.”

Hill looked toward the dark window.

“Then he has learnt something from me after all.”

“Or you from him.”

“P’r’aps.”

From outside came the soft stamp of a horse. Far away a night-bird called across the paddocks. Dot imagined a brown mare travelling under the stars, a tall rider bending low over her neck, the river silver before them and the law far behind.

“Good night, Miss Burton,” said Hill.

“Good night.”

He reached the door, then stopped.

“Your rescuer chose his road cleverly.”

Dot held her breath.

“Did he?”

“The western track is hard after the first mile. It leaves few hoof-marks.”

“I told you nothing.”

“No.”

He glanced back, and the keen eyes held a glint of grim amusement.

“That’s why I shall tell Carey in the morning that our man went north.”

Before Dot could answer, he had gone.

She stood listening to his footsteps cross the passage. A moment later Jack appeared in the doorway, his hair ruffled and his braces hanging loose.

“What did Hill want?”

“To complain of mosquitoes.”

“That sounds like him.”

“Jack—”

“Yes?”

“Do policemen ever lie?”

“Only in the public interest.”

“He says Bill went north.”

Jack looked toward Hill’s closed door.

“Does he, now?”

“But he thinks—” She stopped.

Jack grinned slowly.

“My dear Dot, the police are never more dangerous than when they are mistaken.”

She stared at him, then began to smile.

“Do you suppose he will get away?”

“Bill?”

“Yes.”

Jack listened to the immense night beyond the house.

“If he has reached the hard track, and Hill has suddenly become confused about the points of the compass, I should say he has a very fair chance.”

Dot’s smile deepened.

Jack put an arm around her and drew her toward the passage.

“Bed,” he commanded. “You’ve had enough adventures for one day.”

“I am not sleepy.”

“You will be in five minutes.”

At her door he kissed her forehead.

“Jack?”

“What?”

“If Buckskin Bill comes again—”

“He had better not come after my cattle.”

“I didn’t mean that.”

“I know.”

“What would you do?”

Jack rubbed his chin.

“I might ask him to pay for the apple tart.”

“Jack!”

“And then—well, then I might shake his hand.”

Dot kissed him impulsively.

When she was alone, she undressed by candlelight and folded the blood-stained bandage into the drawer beneath her handkerchiefs. The wound itself she left uncovered. It was no more than a little red sign upon her sunburnt arm, but it seemed to glow with a secret life of its own.

She lay down and listened to the country breathing beyond the walls—to horses shifting in the yard, to wind moving faintly through the dry grass, to the far melancholy cry of something wild and free.

Somewhere under those same stars rode a cattle-thief whom decent people condemned and policemen hunted. He had broken the law, stolen from her brother, and deceived her without once speaking a lie. He had also come out of the glare at the exact instant when death slid green through the grass, and he had given her back her life.

Justice and gratitude stood on opposite sides of her heart, and gratitude was the stronger.

Dot touched the red mark once more and closed her eyes.

Outside, Inspector Fletcher Hill smoked by the open window and looked west.

After a time Jack joined him.

“Think you’ll catch him tomorrow?” Jack asked.

Hill knocked the ash from his pipe.

“No.”

The answer was calm, almost indifferent.

“Horse too lame?” said Jack.

“Something like that.”

Jack leaned against the wall. “Sorry?”

Hill’s lantern jaw showed dark against the starlight.

“I dislike being outwitted.”

“Of course.”

“But there are worse things.”

“Yes.”

For several moments they stood without speech, watching the moon rise over the blue gums and turn the paddocks pale.

Then Hill said, “You’d better clear that timber from behind the barn. Snakes favour it.”

“I’ll do it at sunrise.”

“And keep your sister out of the long grass.”

Jack laughed softly.

“You try keeping her out of anything.”

“No, thank you. I have a fugitive cattle-thief to manage. He’s easier.”

From the distance came a sound that might have been the beat of hoofs upon hard ground—or only the wind travelling over the flats.

Neither man remarked upon it.

Hill relit his pipe.

Jack smiled into the darkness.

And far to the west, where the silver river wound beneath the stars, a brown mare carried her rider safely on.

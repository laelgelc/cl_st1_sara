### I

In the autumn of 1863 Captain Parrol Hartroy commanded a fortified post upon one of the lesser roads crossing the Cumberland Mountains in Tennessee. The place had no importance that a civilian could discern, and very little that a general would have acknowledged; but it covered a pass, protected certain wagon trains, and annoyed the enemy, three circumstances which gave it a sufficient title to existence and its commander a reasonable expectation of being killed in its defense.

The works consisted of an earthen redoubt, a shallow ditch, two blockhouses, and a line of abatis extending down the wooded slope. Within were quartered three companies of infantry and a detachment of cavalry. The artillery comprised two brass guns, whose principal military achievement had been the destruction of several trees which offered neither resistance nor apology. The garrison called the place Fort Hartroy, although no such designation appeared in the records. Soldiers commonly confer upon their surroundings the names of the men responsible for their discomfort.

At sunset on a cold October evening a civilian approached the outer picket from the south. He was a long, stooping fellow in a homespun coat, with mud upon his boots and a gray beard covering the lower part of his face. Across one shoulder he carried a meal sack; in one hand he held a hickory staff. His aspect was that of a mountain farmer, and physiognomists—who can discover treason in an eyebrow and magnanimity in the ear—would doubtless have pronounced him incapable of understanding either army.

“Halt thar!” called the sentinel, bringing his musket to the ready. “Who air you?”

The stranger stopped.

“Name’s Eph Mallow,” he replied. “Got a pass from the provost down at Claring.”

The sentinel summoned the corporal of the guard, who summoned the sergeant, who examined the paper upside down before correcting its position and examining it with no greater profit. Authority, like water, is commonly clearest near its source and somewhat turbid after passing through several official channels.

The sergeant directed that the traveler be conducted to headquarters.

Captain Hartroy sat at a camp table in his hut, writing by the light of a candle. He was a slender man of thirty-five, with a narrow face, colorless eyes, and a mouth which seemed fashioned less for speech than for the withholding of it. His uniform was worn but exact; even fatigue did not venture to disorder him. He took the paper offered by the sergeant and read it twice.

The stranger stood just within the door, his hat held respectfully against his breast. The candle flame leaned in the draft and recovered itself.

“You came from Claring?” asked Hartroy.

“Yas, sir.”

“When?”

“Day ’fore yestiddy.”

“By what road?”

“Up by Simm’s Mill, then acrost the ridge.”

“The bridge at Simm’s Mill was burned ten days ago.”

The stranger scratched his beard.

“Reckon I mean t’other mill, Cap’n. Folks calls ’em both Simm’s, some does.”

Hartroy looked again at the pass. It bore the signature of Major Trowbridge, provost marshal at Claring, and permitted one Ephraim Mallow to carry flour and salt through the Federal lines for family use. The signature was passable; the seal was nearly perfect. But Major Trowbridge had lost his right hand at Stones River and signed official papers with the left. The pen strokes before Hartroy inclined as unmistakably to the right as a well-drilled company executing an oblique movement.

“The pass is a forgery,” said Hartroy.

The civilian’s rustic humility did not alter, but it ceased to appear natural. His hand tightened upon the hat.

“Sergeant, search him.”

The meal sack contained cornmeal. The pockets yielded tobacco, a knife, two silver coins, and a fragment of resin. Nothing else was found. The sergeant looked disappointed, as if innocence were misconduct.

Hartroy rose and walked once around the prisoner. Suddenly he seized the gray beard and pulled. It came away with a portion of stained cloth, exposing a clean-shaven chin. The prisoner straightened; the stoop left his shoulders as a discarded garment might have fallen from them. He was now a tall, grave man, somewhat older than the captain, with dark hair and an expression of singular tranquillity.

“Dramer Brune,” said Hartroy.

The man bowed.

“Captain Hartroy.”

At the sound of his name the captain’s face whitened. He drew his pistol, cocked it, and held it close against the prisoner’s breast.

“How did you know me?”

“I knew you before I entered the fort.”

For some moments neither spoke. The sergeant, to whom this exchange conveyed nothing but the promise of an interesting report, watched with respectful avidity.

Hartroy lowered the weapon.

“Put him in irons. Place two sentinels at the door. If he attempts escape, kill him.”

As Brune was led out, the forged pass slipped from the table and rolled into the dust, where it lay curled upon itself, as in humiliation for the lie that it bore.

### II

At midnight a cavalry patrol returned from the valley with two prisoners and information that Confederate scouts had been observed on the eastern road. Hartroy doubled the guards, ordered the horses saddled before dawn, and sent a lieutenant to examine the southern approaches. These dispositions were made with his customary precision. Whatever private disturbance had entered the commander’s hut was not permitted to pass its threshold; emotion has no prescribed place in a field order.

An hour later the meal sack was examined again. The cornmeal was poured upon a blanket and sifted. Nothing was discovered. One of the guards, observing that the staff appeared unusually heavy, carried it to the adjutant. The brass ferrule was removed; within the hollow shaft, wrapped in oiled silk, lay several closely written papers.

They contained a plan of the fort, the number and disposition of its troops, the caliber of its guns, the condition of its ammunition, the location of its springs, and an accurate description of a ravine by which infantry might approach the northern blockhouse unseen. There were also notes concerning the times of guard mounting and the route of a supply column expected within three days.

The documents were brought to Hartroy. He read them under the adjutant’s observation, then folded them carefully.

“He has earned his pay,” said the adjutant.

“He has earned something,” Hartroy replied.

At seven o’clock in the morning Brune was brought before a drumhead court composed of three officers. The evidence required little discussion. He admitted his name, his Confederate service, his disguise, and the authorship of the papers. He declined to state how the information had been obtained or to name any accomplice.

“You understand,” said the president of the court, “that under the laws of war you are liable to death?”

“Perfectly.”

“Have you anything to say in defense?”

“I have no defense.”

The court was cleared. It deliberated for eleven minutes, a period which would have been shorter but for a disagreement concerning the proper form of the record. The prisoner was recalled and informed that he would be shot at sunrise on the following day.

Brune inclined his head.

“I thank the court for its expedition.”

When the others had withdrawn, Hartroy remained seated. Brune stood before him with his wrists manacled. Through the open door came the measured blows of axes; men were strengthening the abatis. Beyond the parade ground a bugler practiced a call, repeating one imperfect phrase until it seemed the audible symbol of persistence without progress.

“You expected me to recognize you?” Hartroy asked.

“I considered it possible.”

“Yet you came.”

“My instructions required me to come.”

“You could have chosen another disguise.”

“Possibly.”

“You could have avoided this post.”

“No.”

Hartroy rose abruptly.

“Do not answer me as if I were your examiner. There is no need for these evasions between us.”

“I have evaded nothing, Captain.”

The title appeared to wound Hartroy.

“You remember everything?”

“Yes.”

“And you knew that if I discovered you I should have to—”

He stopped.

“To order my death,” Brune completed. “Yes.”

Hartroy walked to the door and looked out. The troops were forming for morning inspection. Their bayonets, catching the low sun, made a brief and orderly illumination. He turned.

“I could send you under guard to division headquarters.”

“You could.”

“The roads are unsafe. You might escape.”

“I might.”

“The commanding general could review the sentence.”

“He would confirm it.”

Hartroy placed both hands upon the table and leaned forward.

“Why did you not escape then?”

Brune’s face remained composed, but he did not pretend to misunderstand.

“Because you slept.”

“That was the reason to escape!”

“It was the reason not to.”

The captain’s fingers closed upon the edge of the table.

“You shall not die until to-morrow morning,” he said.

“That is the sentence.”

“It is all I can give you.”

“I have asked for nothing.”

Hartroy struck the table with his fist, overturning an ink bottle.

“No—you never ask. That is your cruelty.”

Brune looked at him with neither resentment nor tenderness.

“You are mistaken. Cruelty is not always practiced by the condemned.”

The bugle suddenly sounded assembly. Its clear, impersonal notes entered the hut and ended the conversation as decisively as an order. Hartroy called the guard.

### III

Twelve years earlier Dramer Brune had been a private soldier in the United States Army. He was then twenty-four, educated beyond the requirements of his station, silent in companionship, and exact in duty. Why such a man had enlisted was variously explained by those who knew least about it. One said that he had killed a rival; another, that he had lost a fortune; a third, that he had merely desired promotion. The last explanation was universally considered improbable.

After two years of service Brune underwent a change in his political convictions. The nature of the change is unimportant; convictions, like uniforms, may be honorable upon either side and inconvenient everywhere. He petitioned for discharge, stating with unusual candor that he could no longer conscientiously bear arms for the government. His request was denied. He repeated it and was punished for insolence. Shortly afterward he deserted.

He was captured near the border, tried by court-martial, and sentenced to be shot.

On the night preceding the execution he was confined in a small stone guardhouse. The sentinel posted inside the door was a young corporal named Parrol Hartroy. They had served in the same company but had never been intimate. Hartroy was ambitious, attentive, and rigidly obedient—a promising soldier, for armies commonly promote those who most resemble armies.

Near midnight the corporal sat upon a stool with his musket between his knees. He had been on duty throughout the preceding day and was exhausted. Brune, chained to an iron staple in the wall, watched him struggle against sleep. Twice Hartroy started erect; the third time his chin descended upon his breast and remained there.

To sleep on post in time of war was a capital offense.

The key to Brune’s shackles hung from Hartroy’s belt. The outer door was unfastened; beyond it lay a strip of moonlit ground, a low wall, and the dark country. Brune could have taken the key without difficulty, passed the sleeping guard, and in ten minutes reached the forest. At two o’clock the relief would come. Hartroy would be found asleep, the prisoner gone, and before another sunset the place appointed for Brune’s execution might have served the corporal equally well.

For more than an hour Brune remained motionless. Then, hearing the measured step of the approaching relief, he rose as far as his chain permitted and touched Hartroy’s boot.

The corporal did not wake.

Brune shook him violently.

Hartroy sprang up, seized his musket, and stared about him with the confusion of one returning too rapidly from another world.

“The relief,” Brune whispered.

Footsteps halted outside. Hartroy had barely time to resume his position before the door opened. The guard was relieved in regular form. No one had observed his offense.

At sunrise the order for Brune’s execution was suspended pending review. Two weeks later he escaped from confinement during a fire. Hartroy never saw him again until the evening of the forged pass.

Such was the history which the captain related on the parade ground to the officers of his command an hour before the execution. He spoke without rhetorical ornament, omitting nothing favorable to the prisoner and nothing disgraceful to himself. The officers listened in silence.

When he had finished, the senior lieutenant said: “Captain, no man here need know this. The prisoner could be reported as having escaped.”

Hartroy looked at him.

“And the information found upon him?”

“Destroyed.”

“The accomplices whom he refuses to name?”

“We might—”

“You propose that I preserve my honor by falsifying a record, suppressing evidence, and releasing an enemy who has learned how to take this post?”

The lieutenant flushed.

“I propose only that you pay a debt.”

“It is not mine to pay. The fort is not mine; the men are not mine; the cause is not mine. I hold them in trust.”

“Then send him higher.”

“And make another man do what I lack the courage to do?”

No one answered.

Hartroy continued, more quietly: “Private Brune once gave me my life. Captain Hartroy has no authority to return it at the expense of his command.”

“That is a distinction,” said the adjutant, “which may be sound in regulations.”

“It is the only place where distinctions are sound.”

The officers dispersed. Some judged the captain inflexible; others thought him mad. All agreed that his future advancement was doubtful, for nothing more seriously obstructs a military career than the public exhibition of a conscience.

Brune was permitted to write a letter. He wrote only a few lines, sealed the page, and addressed it to a woman in Virginia. When Hartroy entered the guardhouse, the prisoner was standing at the narrow window, looking toward the eastern mountain.

“I have told them,” said Hartroy.

“That was unnecessary.”

“It was necessary to me.”

“Then it was not a confession.”

Hartroy removed his cap.

“Brune, I have sought some lawful means—some means not wholly base—”

“There is none.”

“I could resign my command.”

“Your successor would execute me.”

“I could release you and take the consequences.”

“You would imperil these men and discredit the service to which you have given your allegiance. I did not save you for that.”

Hartroy covered his eyes with one hand.

“Do not say you saved me.”

“What phrase would be more accurate?”

“You gave me twelve years which belonged to you.”

“No. I declined to purchase my life with yours. That is all.”

“And now I purchase my duty with yours.”

Brune turned from the window. For the first time a gentleness appeared in his expression, though only for a moment.

“Captain, we do not keep the accounts.”

Outside, the drums began the slow roll summoning the garrison. Hartroy replaced his cap.

“Is there anything you wish?”

“Yes. Command the firing party yourself.”

“I cannot.”

“You must. Otherwise your subaltern bears part of what is wholly yours.”

Hartroy bowed.

“It shall be as you wish.”

### IV

The execution took place upon a level space beyond the northern ditch. The garrison formed three sides of a square, the open side facing the mountain. In the center stood the firing party—eight men under a sergeant—with loaded rifles. Their faces showed that solemn curiosity which soldiers bring to the death of a comrade or an enemy; familiarity with mortality does not diminish interest in its administrative forms.

The morning was clear and windless. A solemn shadow lay across the lower slope, while the summit was already bright. From the valley came the faint sound of running water.

Brune was marched out between two guards. He wore no disguise. His hands were free, and he walked with an easy, natural step. At the appointed place he stopped and looked over the assembled troops. He then turned to Hartroy.

“I am ready.”

The captain attempted to answer but produced no sound. He motioned to the sergeant, who offered the prisoner a strip of white cloth.

“No,” said Brune.

The sergeant withdrew it. Brune faced the rifles.

Hartroy drew his sword. Its point trembled perceptibly. This movement was observed throughout the square, though every man afterward denied having seen it.

“Ready!”

The rifles rose.

“Aim!”

Brune’s gaze passed over the weapons and rested upon Hartroy. He made a slight inclination of the head—the grave courtesy of one gentleman acknowledging another at the conclusion of an affair.

The captain’s sword fell.

“Fire!”

The volley crashed against the mountain and returned in broken echoes. At nearly the same instant there was a sharper report from behind the firing party.

Brune had fallen forward upon his face. Hartroy lay upon his back, his pistol still enclosed in his right hand. The bullet had entered beneath the chin and passed upward through the brain. Death, more punctual than promotion and less discriminating than justice, had been immediate in both cases.

The command devolved upon the senior lieutenant. He ordered the body of the spy removed and then stood for some moments regarding that of his captain.

There were regulations concerning deserters, spies, and suicides; there were none concerning the settlement of a life voluntarily given and unwillingly retained. In the absence of an applicable order, the lieutenant exercised discretion.

Dramer Brune and Parrol Hartroy were buried that evening outside the military cemetery, in separate graves near the foot of the mountain. No volley was fired, no bugle sounded, and no boards were placed to preserve their names. Before spring the earth had settled; by summer weeds covered both mounds. When the post was abandoned, the forest resumed the ground, effacing fortification, road, and grave with equal impartiality.

# The Little Music-King

In the old city of Salzburg, where grey towers looked down upon narrow streets and bells sent their voices over the river and the surrounding hills, there was born, in the year 1756, a child who was destined to give to the world some of its most beautiful music. His name was Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart.

No one who saw the tiny fellow lying in his cradle could have guessed what melodies were already waiting, as it were, within that small head. He had bright, observant eyes, a lively manner, and an affectionate heart; but in these things he did not appear very different from other babies. Yet Music, that wonderful fairy which comes to so few, had marked him for her own.

His father, Leopold Mozart, was himself a musician and a man of learning and industry. He knew how long and difficult is the road which leads even to modest skill, and he had no thought at first of finding a genius beneath his roof. Wolfgang’s elder sister, Maria Anna, whom the family called Nannerl, was being taught to play upon the harpsichord, and the little boy often stood beside her, watching her fingers with grave attention.

He was so small that his chin scarcely reached above the keyboard. Nevertheless, while other children would have grown impatient and run away to their toys, Wolfgang remained spell-bound. Every note seemed to speak to him. When Nannerl played a pretty tune, his face shone; when she struck a wrong note, he frowned as though some one had hurt him.

One day, when he was but three years old, Leopold entered the room and found the child at the harpsichord. Wolfgang had climbed upon the seat and was stretching his chubby fingers over the keys. It was no meaningless noise that he made. He was sounding the notes of a chord, listening with his head bent to one side, and trying it again until every tone rang clear.

Leopold paused at the doorway.

“What art thou doing there, my Wolferl?” he asked.

The little fellow looked round, quite untroubled.

“I am finding the notes which love one another,” he replied.

Then he pressed the keys once more, and a perfectly true chord sounded through the room.

One can picture the father standing in amazement, scarcely daring to believe his ears. A chord! And played by a baby of three! He came nearer and tried the child with other notes, but Wolfgang listened and answered with a certainty which could not be accidental.

From that moment Leopold began to teach him.

It was, however, less like teaching an ordinary pupil than opening a door for one who had long been waiting outside. Wolfgang understood almost before the explanation was finished. If Nannerl practised a piece, he remembered great parts of it. If his father showed him a passage once, he begged to attempt it at once, and his little fingers, though they were hardly large enough to stretch across the keys, somehow performed their task.

The lessons were not always solemn. Wolfgang was still a merry child, fond of games and laughter, and ready to turn the room into a kingdom of his own imagining. But when music began, a change came over him. His gay face grew serious, his eyes became intent, and he forgot toys, cakes, visitors, and even the passing of time.

Sometimes he would not leave the instrument until gently lifted away.

“Enough for to-day,” Leopold would say.

“Only once more, Papa!”

“Thou hast said ‘once more’ six times already.”

“But this will be the true once more,” the little chap would plead; and very often the father, who loved music scarcely less than the son, permitted another repetition.

By the time Wolfgang was five years old, he was not content merely to play the music of others. Melodies came singing into his mind, and he wished to set them down. He took paper, dipped his pen deeply into the ink, and began his important labour. The page was soon covered with notes, blots, scratches, and ink-stained finger-marks.

Leopold found him bending over it with an expression of the utmost gravity.

“What hast thou there?”

“I am composing a concerto,” said Wolfgang.

“A concerto!” Leopold smiled. “It must be a very difficult one.”

“It is difficult,” answered the child, “but it can be played.”

His father took up the paper, intending perhaps to humour him. At first the blots and crooked notes made him laugh. Then he looked more closely. The smile faded, and presently tears stood in his eyes. Beneath the untidy writing there was real musical thought—wildly difficult, certainly, and beyond the reach of the tiny composer’s own hands, but clear, bold, and wonderfully beautiful.

Nannerl came softly to his side.

“Why do you weep, Papa?” she asked.

“Because,” said Leopold, laying a hand upon Wolfgang’s head, “Heaven has sent us something very precious, and with the gift has sent a great duty.”

What a burden to be put upon a baby’s tiny shoulders! Yet Wolfgang did not feel it as a burden. Music was his speech, his playground, and his dearest companion. To compose was as natural to him as singing is to a bird.

Nor was the harpsichord his only kingdom. He soon wished to learn the violin. Leopold, believing that even a gifted child must proceed in an orderly fashion, told him that he was not yet ready. But Wolfgang watched others so closely that he seemed to draw knowledge through his eyes.

One evening his father and some friends met to play music together. Wolfgang begged to join them.

“You have had no proper violin lessons,” Leopold reminded him.

“I can play softly,” said the boy. “No one need hear me if I am wrong.”

The musicians laughed, and one of them made room for the earnest little figure. The piece began. Wolfgang placed the violin beneath his chin and followed the notes. At first the others listened with amusement; then their smiles vanished. He was not merely scraping out a childish accompaniment. He played correctly, feeling the phrase and entering at the right instant, as if he had studied for months.

Presently he stopped and looked sharply towards one of the players.

“That was false!” he cried.

The company stared, for the note had indeed been false.

Leopold could no longer doubt. His son possessed not only a marvellous memory and nimble fingers, but an ear so delicate that the smallest error pained him.

The father now recognised that both Nannerl and Wolfgang had unusual gifts. Nannerl was a charming performer, graceful, diligent, and devoted to her little brother; and Wolfgang, though younger, seemed capable of almost anything that music demanded. Leopold therefore resolved to take the children into the wider world.

Travel in those days was no light matter. The roads were rough, coaches jolted for weary hours, inns were uncertain, and winter winds entered through every crack. There were trunks to pack, letters to secure, instruments to guard, and costly clothes to provide, for the children were to appear before princes and great ladies. Yet Leopold possessed true caution as well as courage. He arranged what he could, trusted Providence for the rest, and set forth with his little musicians.

Thus began those famous journeys which carried Wolfgang from one brilliant court to another.

Imagine him dressed for his first grand appearance: his hair neatly arranged, his coat bright with embroidery, lace at his wrists, buckles shining upon his shoes. He was still so small that the splendour of his costume almost seemed to swallow him. But the instant he approached the instrument, no one thought any longer of his size.

At Vienna, noblemen and ladies crowded to see the wonder-child from Salzburg. They expected perhaps a clever trick, something to amuse them for a quarter of an hour. Instead, the little musician played with a feeling and certainty which many grown performers might have envied. His fingers ran lightly over the keys; grave music became noble beneath his touch, gay music sparkled, and tender melodies seemed to speak directly to every heart.

Then came the tests.

Could he play a piece at sight?

He could.

Could he repeat a melody after hearing it once?

He did so without hesitation.

Could he improvise upon a subject offered by a musician in the company?

He began at once, turning the few notes into a rich and flowing composition.

Someone covered the keys so that he could not see his hands. Wolfgang laughed and continued playing. A court musician gave him a difficult passage, perhaps expecting to puzzle him; but the child mastered it, and the would-be examiner was left dumb with amazement.

Yet it was not skill alone which won affection. Wolfgang was natural and fearless. He did not understand the invisible wall which separates royalty from ordinary folk. A king or an empress was to him a person who might love music, and therefore a possible friend. If a richly dressed princess spoke kindly, he answered with childlike frankness. If he slipped upon a polished floor and a young lady helped him rise, he might look up gratefully and declare:

“You are good. I will marry you.”

How the court laughed! But it was kindly laughter, for the little fellow’s heart was plainly visible in every word.

After Vienna came other cities and other triumphs. In Paris, London, and elsewhere throughout Europe, curious crowds gathered to hear the children of Leopold Mozart. Nannerl, dignified and pretty, played with great charm; Wolfgang performed, composed, remembered, imitated, and improvised until sober musicians shook their heads and wondered whether such things could truly be.

Sometimes he would ask a famous player to give him a theme. The gentleman might offer a tune designed to be troublesome, full of awkward leaps and unexpected turns. Wolfgang would sit silently for a moment, his small hands resting upon his knees. Then he would begin, and the ungainly tune became transformed. It appeared first solemnly, then merrily, then hidden beneath rippling passages, and at last returned in triumph, so that the listeners burst into applause.

At other times he played with a cloth spread across the keyboard, or named notes struck beyond his sight, or repeated long passages after a single hearing. These feats delighted fashionable audiences, but to Wolfgang they were merely games upon the edge of his true kingdom. He wished above all to create beauty. Praise pleased him, as it would please any child, but music itself possessed him more deeply than praise.

When a performance was over, he might leap from the seat and run for a sweetmeat, climb upon Nannerl’s lap, or laugh at the stiff manners of some important gentleman. Thus the child and the genius dwelt together in him most charmingly. One moment he was a little chap asking when supper would be served; the next he was producing music which brought tears to the eyes of learned men.

The journeys were not made entirely of bright rooms, jewels, bows, and applause. There were long days upon muddy roads, anxious waits at borders, troublesome examinations by customs officials, and cold nights when the family huddled in unfamiliar inns. Instruments had to be carried with the greatest care, for a broken string or damaged case might ruin a concert. Leopold counted expenses, wrote letters, sought invitations, and guarded the health of his children.

Illness came more than once. Wolfgang, so lively upon ordinary days, would lie pale and still whilst Nannerl sat beside him and Leopold watched in grave anxiety. What did fame matter then? What were applause and royal favour beside the life of the small beloved boy?

Yet when strength returned, music returned with it.

“Do not work yet, Wolferl,” his father would urge.

“I am not working,” Wolfgang might answer. “I am only thinking.”

And in his mind the notes were already moving—grave and gay, sad, sweet, and thrilling—waiting until his hand could set them upon paper.

In London, he heard new styles and new instruments, and his hungry mind gathered everything. In Paris, amid costly dresses, gleaming mirrors, and polished manners, he listened, compared, and learned. No city passed him without giving something to his art. He seemed like a traveller collecting precious stones, except that his treasures were rhythms, harmonies, colours of tone, and secret ways of touching the heart.

His father was proud, as well he might be, but pride was mixed with care. Leopold knew that the world loves a marvel, yet grows weary even of wonders. Whilst Wolfgang was very small, all wished to behold the baby who could play like a master. But babies grow. The embroidered coat became too short; the little hands lengthened; the childish face began slowly to change. Would the public still admire him when novelty had passed?

Professional musicians, too, did not always welcome the young prodigy. Some rejoiced honestly in his gifts, but others felt jealousy creeping into their hearts. It was pleasant enough to praise a child so long as he remained a curiosity; it was less pleasant when that child began composing works which challenged mature masters.

Whispers followed the family.

“The father has written the music.”

“The performances are arranged tricks.”

“The boy cannot truly understand what he plays.”

Leopold heard such remarks with indignation. Wolfgang, when told of them, was more puzzled than angry.

“Why do they not ask me to compose before them?” he said. “Then they will know.”

And indeed, when put to the proof, he never feared. Give him a subject, shut him in a room if you must, watch the paper from the first note to the last—music came all the same. The little stream of childhood had become a bright, swift river.

As the years passed, Wolfgang produced sonatas, symphonies, concertos, and songs. While most boys were still struggling with elementary exercises, he was thinking of whole orchestras. Violins sang in his imagination; horns answered them; flutes floated above; basses gave depth and dignity; and all moved together beneath the rule of his inward ear.

He did not cease to study merely because he possessed genius. That is important to remember. The gift was marvellous, but he strengthened it by constant labour. He examined the works of other composers, listened to every fine performer he could hear, and learned the special character of different instruments. His pages might be written rapidly, yet behind that ease lay years of attention.

Sometimes his father saw him bent over a score late at night.

“Enough, my son,” Leopold would say. “The candles are nearly spent.”

“I have only to finish this movement.”

“The movement will remain until morning.”

“But, Papa, it will sing in my head all night.”

Then Leopold, half troubled and half delighted, would leave him for a little while longer, and the pen would race on.

There were joyous adventures amid the labour. Once, when Wolfgang came upon a fine organ in a monastery, he could not resist trying it. The monks, hearing the great instrument suddenly awaken, hurried to discover who had dared touch it. They expected perhaps to find an experienced organist. Instead they saw a boy perched before the manuals, his feet scarcely managing the pedals, while the arches rang with majestic sound.

The monks stood still.

The music began softly, like a prayer spoken at dawn. It grew until the stone walls seemed to tremble, then passed into a tender melody and ended in a noble burst of harmony. For a moment there was silence.

“Who taught thee to play so?” asked one of the astonished brothers.

Wolfgang looked round with a smile.

“The organ taught me,” he said. “It has a splendid voice.”

Such was his way. Every instrument was alive to him, and each had a voice which he desired to understand.

The most famous proof of his musical memory occurred in Rome. There, in a sacred service, was performed the celebrated *Miserere*, a work guarded with the greatest care. Its music was not freely given out, and those responsible for it were determined that its secrets should remain within the chapel.

Wolfgang attended and listened.

The solemn voices rose beneath the lofty roof, now pleading, now answering, now joining in harmonies so pure that the boy scarcely seemed to breathe. No instrument distracted him. He followed each part, heard how one voice entered after another, and stored the whole within that marvellous memory.

When the service ended, he returned to his lodging. Leopold may well have thought that the boy was tired after so grave an experience, but Wolfgang asked for paper.

“What art thou doing?”

“Writing the *Miserere*.”

“From memory?”

“I have it here,” said Wolfgang, touching his forehead.

Then he began. Line after line appeared. The parts were arranged, the harmonies restored, the phrases placed in order. He had heard the long work but once, yet he wrote it down with astonishing faithfulness. Later he heard it again and corrected the few small points which required amendment.

Think of it! A closely guarded composition, preserved with jealous care, had passed through the ears of a boy and taken up its dwelling in his mind. Locks could guard the manuscript, rules could forbid the copying, but who could place a lock upon memory?

The story spread, and once again musicians marvelled. Some were delighted, others disturbed, but none could deny the power which had accomplished so extraordinary a feat.

Still, genius did not smooth every road. The family’s travels continued to be exhausting. Favour at court might shine brightly one season and disappear the next. A noble patron who promised assistance might forget his promise. Audiences, ever seeking novelty, turned towards some new singer or performer. Wolfgang’s childhood triumphs had opened doors, but the young man would have to pass through them by the strength of his compositions.

There were also those who refused to believe that one so youthful could write serious music. They judged his age rather than his work. If a composition pleased them before they knew the author, they admired it; when told it was Mozart’s, they discovered objections.

Such injustice wounded Leopold deeply. Wolfgang felt it too, though his natural cheerfulness soon returned. He had a defence stronger than argument: he could write more music.

And so he wrote.

He learned the stage and the human voice, studied how music could express pride, sorrow, mischief, tenderness, anger, and forgiveness, and began to turn eagerly towards opera. Here was a world after his own heart. In opera, music did not sit quietly upon a page; it wore costumes, entered through painted doors, quarrelled, loved, plotted, laughed, and wept beneath the blaze of lights.

Wolfgang delighted in comic character. He could hear how a pompous person ought to sing, how a cunning servant might twist a phrase, how a shy lover’s melody should hesitate, and how an angry ensemble could become both disorderly and perfectly ordered. To others the stage might appear a confusion of singers and instruments; to him all could be woven into one living design.

At last came an important commission. He was to compose a comic opera entitled *La Finta Giardiniera*.

Wolfgang threw himself into the task with all the ardour of youth and all the skill accumulated through years of travel, listening, study, and performance. The score grew beneath his hand. There were graceful airs, lively exchanges, delicate passages, and brilliant scenes in which several characters expressed different feelings at once without destroying the unity of the music.

Leopold watched the work with both pride and caution.

“Remember,” he said, “a theatre is not a drawing-room. Singers have their wishes, managers have their troubles, and the public is uncertain.”

“I know, Papa.”

“And an opera may fail for reasons which have little to do with its music.”

“I know that also.”

“Then why dost thou look so merry?”

“Because the music is good,” Wolfgang answered simply.

It was the confidence not of vanity, but of knowledge. He had heard the work inwardly. He knew what he had made.

Yet as the time for performance drew near, even he could not remain wholly free from anxiety. Rehearsals brought the usual confusions. A singer disliked a passage; another wished for a change; someone entered too soon; someone else complained that an air was too high or too low. Musicians argued, scenery caused delays, and every person appeared to carry a private difficulty into the theatre.

Wolfgang, though young, understood what he wanted. He could be gay and courteous, but where the music was concerned he became firm. If an effect sounded wrong, his delicate ear detected it instantly.

“No, no,” he would say, springing from his chair. “The phrase must breathe. Do not hurry it. Again!”

Or he would sing the passage himself, giving it such natural expression that the performer understood.

At nineteen, the little prodigy of former days had become a slender young man, lively in manner, serious in purpose, and already burdened with a store of experiences which many musicians never gain in a lifetime. Yet to Leopold and Nannerl he was still their Wolferl, the tiny fellow who had once searched the harpsichord for “notes which love one another.”

The premiere took place in Munich.

On the evening of the performance, the theatre glowed with candles. Coaches arrived one after another, setting down finely dressed ladies and gentlemen. Jewels flashed at throats and wrists; silk rustled; voices murmured beneath the light. In the orchestra, players tuned their instruments, and wandering notes rose into the air like birds uncertain where to settle.

Behind the curtain there was another world—painted scenery, hurried footsteps, nervous singers, whispered instructions, powder, ribbons, flowers, and stage servants moving in every direction.

Wolfgang sat with his father and sister, awaiting the first notes.

Who could say what the evening would hold of triumph or of failure? The public which had smiled upon the wonderful child must now judge the accomplished composer. Clever playing, charming manners, and astonishing feats of memory could not save an opera. It must stand by its own beauty.

Nannerl looked at her brother. For once, he was very still.

“Are you afraid?” she whispered.

“A little,” he admitted.

Leopold placed a hand upon his arm.

“Whatever happens, thou hast worked honestly and bravely.”

Wolfgang nodded, but his eyes remained fixed upon the stage.

The conductor raised his hand.

The overture began.

At the first sound, Wolfgang’s anxiety changed into intense attention. He knew every note, yet now the music no longer belonged only to the silent page or to his imagination. It lived in the bowing of the strings, the breath of the wind players, and the listening stillness of the audience.

The opening passed well. Then the curtain rose, and the characters entered.

A graceful air won the first warm applause. A comic exchange brought laughter. The audience leaned forward. One number followed another, and with each scene the magic spell strengthened. The singers, encouraged by success, forgot their fears and gave themselves to the music. Melodies sparkled; ensembles gathered force; the orchestra laughed, sighed, whispered, and rejoiced beneath them.

Leopold scarcely dared breathe. Nannerl’s eyes shone.

Wolfgang listened to every detail. Once he frowned at an uncertain entrance; the next moment he smiled as a difficult passage came off perfectly. Then a great burst of applause filled the theatre.

“They like it,” Nannerl whispered.

“Hush,” said Wolfgang, though his face had become radiant.

At the close, there was no doubt. The audience applauded again and again. Voices called for the composer. Musicians who had regarded the young man with suspicion now joined in the praise. Critics who had come prepared to be grave found themselves swept along by the delight of the house.

Wolfgang was urged forward.

For one instant, as he stepped into the blaze of light, he seemed again the little boy in his embroidered coat, standing before kings and queens. But this triumph was greater than those childhood victories. He had not astonished the audience by playing with covered keys or naming unseen notes. He had created a complete work, filled it with life and feeling, and given it to the world.

He bowed.

The applause rose about him like a mighty wave.

Leopold’s thoughts must surely have travelled back across the years—to the baby fingers making a chord, the ink-blotted concerto, the first violin, the jolting coaches, the illnesses, the royal courts, the jealous whispers, the monastery organ, and the wonderful night when the *Miserere* had been written from memory. Every hardship seemed gathered into this hour and changed into joy.

“My son,” he said when Wolfgang returned, and for a moment could say no more.

Wolfgang embraced him.

“Was it good, Papa?”

Leopold looked at him with tears of pride.

“Yes, my Wolferl. It was good.”

Thus, at nineteen, Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart stood no longer merely as the celebrated wonder-child of Salzburg, but as an accomplished composer whose genius had proved itself upon the public stage. The tiny music-king had grown, and his kingdom had grown with him.

There let us leave him for the present: young, happy, and triumphant, with his father and sister beside him, the lights of the Munich theatre shining upon his eager face, and the applause of a delighted audience still sounding in his ears. Hardships would come again, for no great life is free from them; fashions would alter, friends would fail, and the world would not always reward him justly. But the music within him could not be silenced.

We who hear that music long after the candles of those old courts and theatres have gone dark owe a debt of gratitude to the merry, earnest child who listened so faithfully and laboured so bravely. The small fingers which once stretched with difficulty across the keys have long been still, but the grave and gay, sad and exquisite melodies remain.

And whenever they are played, the little fellow from Salzburg lives again.

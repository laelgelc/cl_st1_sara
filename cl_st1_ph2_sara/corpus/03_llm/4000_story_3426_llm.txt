### MR. GRADDER’S LEGACY

When Mary and I were married, I possessed a salary of two pounds fifteen shillings a week, a black coat which was respectable by candle-light, and seventy-three pounds in the Post Office Savings Bank. Mary had forty pounds of her own, besides a sewing-machine, an eight-day clock, and such confidence in my abilities as I have never since been able entirely to justify. We were not poor, according to our own computation; but, as the majority of London house-agents appeared to take a different view, the finding of a home caused us some anxiety.

At length we discovered what the bill in the window called “The New House,” 18 Roper Street, Camberwell. It was only new to us. The passage paper hung in strips, two bedroom ceilings had given way in patches, the kitchen range smoked, and the back parlour had been decorated in a pattern which Mary described as deceased cucumbers upon a bilious ground. Still, it contained six rooms, with a wash-house and a garden nearly twenty feet long; and the rent was only twenty-four pounds a year.

The owner, Mr. Ephraim Gradder, had offices in Peckham Road. He was a square, heavy man, with grey whiskers cut like scrubbing-brushes and a mouth which seemed to close by machinery. He looked at me, looked at Mary, and looked again at me.

“Children?” he asked.

“Not at present,” said I.

“Expect any?”

Mary turned towards the window, and I replied that we expected to be happy, which was true but irrelevant.

“No repairs,” said Gradder. “Understand that. House is sound.”

“The drawing-room ceiling has fallen,” I observed.

“Part of it.”

“And the kitchen chimney smokes.”

“All chimneys smoke.”

“Not into the kitchen.”

“Tenant’s business. Twenty-four pounds, quarterly in advance; one month’s notice either side.”

He pushed the agreement towards me as though he were serving a writ. We signed it, and on the following Monday entered into possession.

I was then employed in the accounts department of Messrs. Broadwater & Pym, colonial produce merchants, of Eastcheap. My work was not romantic, consisting chiefly in the comparison of invoices with bills of lading; but it was regular, and it left my evenings free. Mary and I therefore resolved to repair the New House ourselves. She stripped paper with a determination which alarmed the spiders, while I plastered, painted, stopped holes, and learned by repeated failure that a shelf which looks horizontal is not necessarily so.

We had been at work three evenings when Gradder appeared.

He did not knock in the ordinary manner, but gave the door two blows with his stick, as if chastising it. I found him in the passage, sniffing at the smell of size.

“What’s this?” he demanded.

“We are doing the place up.”

“At whose orders?”

“Our own.”

“You’ll spoil it.”

“That would require considerable ingenuity.”

He ignored this, and walked into the front parlour, where Mary, wearing an apron over her old blue dress, stood upon a chair scraping the wall.

“Better let me do that,” he said.

Mary descended. “Do you paper rooms yourself, Mr. Gradder?”

“I can superintend. Workmen of mine could come in. No charge.”

This generosity, coming from a man who had refused to replace a broken latch worth ninepence, attracted our attention.

“It is very kind,” said Mary, “but we prefer doing it ourselves.”

Gradder pressed his thumb against the chimney-piece, examined the skirting, and tapped the wall in three places.

“Damp here?”

“No,” I said.

“Loose brickwork?”

“I have found none.”

“Cellar all right?”

“There is no cellar.”

He looked sharply at me. “There’s a space under these boards.”

“There may also be one beneath the pavement, but it does not follow that I wish to visit it.”

He left soon afterwards, though not before offering twice more to send men. At the door he turned.

“If you find anything belonging to the house, it’s mine.”

“We shall certainly return the deceased cucumbers,” said Mary.

He shut his mouth tighter, put on his hat, and went out.

At first we regarded his conduct merely as another instance of the eccentricity common to landlords, and, being happy and busy, thought little of it. But three days later he came again, this time at half-past seven in the morning, and asked whether he might inspect the kitchen floor. On the following Saturday he proposed taking down the front bedroom mantelpiece. He offered to replace the wash-house paving, to open the sealed flue in the back parlour, and even to renew the garden fence.

“Something is wrong,” Mary said when he had departed after the fourth visit. “A landlord may repair one thing by accident, Bob, but no landlord offers to repair six things without a motive.”

“He wishes to improve his property.”

“He is an old brute.”

“A lucky old brute, if he owns much property.”

“He is looking for something.”

This conclusion had already occurred to me, though masculine dignity prevented my admitting it at once. I therefore frowned wisely at the fireplace.

“The question is—what?”

Our first notion was that some defect existed which Gradder wished to conceal, but concealment did not explain his passion for pulling the house to pieces. Then we thought of stolen goods, compromising papers, and even a body under the floor. The last suggestion came from Mary, who had lately read a sensation novel; but as Gradder showed no desire to prevent our discoveries, and merely desired to make them himself, it did not satisfy us.

The matter remained there until a week later, when I happened to mention our landlord at the office.

Wigley, our junior correspondence clerk, looked up from his stool. He was a thin young man with a turn-up nose, an inexhaustible knowledge of other people’s affairs, and a style of speech which would have ruined him in a better establishment.

“Gradder of Peckham Road?” he said. “Grey porcupines on his cheeks?”

“The same.”

“I know his game—or part of it. My aunt’s second husband rented from him. Gradder would skin a flea for the grease.”

“That agrees with our experience.”

“Got property all over Camberwell. Old chap died in one of his houses last month—Giles Armer. Ever hear of him?”

I shook my head.

“Rum customer,” Wigley continued. “Made money at sea, or in ships, or out of sailors—something wet, anyhow. Lived like a pauper and had more sovereigns than Gradder has teeth. They say he hid money in the walls.”

I laid down my pen.

“What house?”

“Number 16 Roper Street.”

“That is next door to mine.”

Wigley whistled. “Then keep your eyes open, old man. Armer had both houses once. Slept in one and stored rubbish in the other. Afterwards he gave up Number 18, and Gradder let it to a family named Cope. They left in a hurry. Armer himself stayed at 16 till he died.”

“Did Gradder inherit?”

“He expects to. There’s a will. My cousin in Mr. Pollick’s office copied it. Pollick is the solicitor who drew the thing.”

“Can your cousin obtain the wording?”

Wigley’s nose rose still farther. “Obtain is a dangerous word in legal matters. He might remember it for the price of a dinner.”

I paid for the dinner.

The next evening Wigley brought me six sheets of foolscap, folded beneath his waistcoat with a melodramatic caution quite unnecessary to an act which he assured me was perfectly lawful. We went into a small coffee-house near London Bridge and read the copy together.

The will of Giles Armer, retired master-mariner, was dated four years previously. After a few small legacies to a housekeeper and a seaman’s charity, it directed that the residue of his estate should pass to “such child or children of my body as may survive me, provided that such child or children shall, within one calendar month from the date of my decease, make declaration of identity and claim before a magistrate, justice of the peace, notary, or commissioner for oaths within the United Kingdom.”

Failing such declaration within the month, the entire residue went to Ephraim Gradder, “in acknowledgment of certain services.”

“What services?” I asked.

Wigley winked. “Charging rent regularly, I expect.”

There was more. Armer stated that a considerable sum in gold, notes, bonds and other securities had been concealed by him “within or about the premises numbered 16 and 18 Roper Street,” both of which he had occupied. The money, whether discovered before or after his death, formed part of the residue.

“No wonder Gradder wants your mantelpiece,” said Wigley.

“But why does he not search Number 16?”

“He has. My cousin says the place looks as if a regiment had practised digging trenches indoors. Floors up, grates out, garden sieved. Nothing found.”

“What was Armer’s family?”

“Will doesn’t say. Pollick asked him, but the old boy kept his own counsel.”

“Date of death?”

Wigley put his finger upon the memorandum. “The seventh of October.”

It was then the fifth of November.

I carried the copy home, feeling that our landlord’s urbanity—if that term may be applied to offers delivered like threats—was now fully explained. Mary sat by the kitchen fire darning one of my socks. I told her Wigley’s story, and she listened with interest but no excitement until I read the testator’s name and description aloud.

The stocking dropped from her hands.

“What did you call him?”

“Giles Armer.”

“Master-mariner?”

“Yes.”

She rose so suddenly that her chair fell backwards.

“Bob, that was my father.”

For perhaps half a minute I stared at her. It is an inconvenient feature of extraordinary events that one is commonly prepared with nothing better than an ordinary expression.

“Your father’s name was Harmer,” I said at last.

“We called ourselves Harmer. Mother always did. But I have seen it written without the H—on his old discharge paper. Giles Armer. He was mate of the _Eastern Queen_, and afterwards captain of the _Rose of Devon_. He left us when I was seven.”

“Why?”

“I never knew. Mother would not speak of it. She said he was dead to us, and when she died Aunt Eliza told everyone that he was dead in fact. But it must be he. Let me see the will.”

She read it, breathing quickly. Then she pointed to one line giving the testator’s birthplace as Lyme Regis.

“He was born there. And look—his seaman’s charity is at Poplar. He once lived at Poplar. Bob, it is my father!”

The kitchen clock struck nine.

“What day did he die?” she asked.

“The seventh of October.”

“And this is the fifth. When does the month expire?”

“On the seventh, presumably.”

“Presumably?”

That one word sent me to my feet. A fortune may safely be left to solicitors when one has time; with less than two days remaining, it is better to begin by finding a hat.

We went first to the nearest police station, where an elderly sergeant heard our account with patience and informed us that a police constable was not a magistrate, a circumstance we already knew but had hoped might be overlooked after office hours. He directed us to the house of Mr. Joshua Pell, a magistrate residing in Grove Lane.

Mr. Pell was dining when we arrived. His servant wished us to call next morning; Mary said that next morning might cost her a fortune, upon which a red-faced gentleman in a napkin came into the hall and demanded the cause of our invasion.

We explained it.

“Have you the will?”

“A copy, sir.”

“Evidence of identity?”

“Not with us,” I admitted.

“Then what the devil do you expect me to do?”

“To receive her declaration,” said I, “subject to proof.”

He glared at me and then at Mary. “You are sure this is not some scheme of your husband’s?”

“I am sure my husband is incapable of inventing a scheme quickly enough,” said Mary.

Mr. Pell laughed, removed the napkin from his collar, and took us into his study.

There, after questioning Mary as to her birth, her mother, her father’s ships and the circumstances of his disappearance, he drew up a brief declaration. Mary stated that she was Mary Alice Bell, formerly Mary Alice Harmer, born at Poplar on the twelfth of March, 1864; that she believed herself to be the lawful daughter and only surviving child of Giles Armer, late of 16 Roper Street; and that she formally claimed all benefit to which she might be entitled under his will.

She signed. Mr. Pell witnessed it, impressed his seal, and wrote the time beneath: forty-seven minutes past ten on the evening of November fifth.

“There,” he said. “That may save your claim, though it does not prove it. Get a solicitor in the morning. Get your birth certificate, your parents’ marriage certificate if there is one, and every scrap of evidence you possess. And do not tell Gradder.”

“We had not intended to,” said Mary.

“A sensible woman. Gradder once sued my gardener over three shillings. Good night.”

We returned home in a state which I shall not attempt to dignify as calm. Before midnight we searched the rooms with such tools as were available, but without destroying what we had repaired. I examined loose boards, chimney recesses and the interior of two cupboards; Mary probed the garden with the poker and discovered a broken teapot. At two in the morning we found a hollow space behind the kitchen grate, which yielded soot, a dead beetle and a farthing dated 1858.

By six we had discovered no fortune and spoiled a very fair night’s rest.

I stayed away from the office next morning—a liberty which cost me half a day’s pay—and we went to Mr. Nathaniel Crowle, solicitor, of Bedford Row, recommended by Mr. Pell. Crowle was a small, dry gentleman whose eyebrows appeared to have been placed high upon his forehead in permanent surprise. He read the declaration, read the copied will, and asked Mary fifty questions.

“You have your birth certificate?”

“No.”

“Parents’ marriage lines?”

“No.”

“Letters from your father?”

“One, perhaps, at my aunt’s.”

“Your aunt living?”

“Yes, at Greenwich.”

“Then we begin at Greenwich. Meanwhile I shall lodge notice of claim with the executors and obtain an official copy of the will. You must say nothing to Gradder. Not a syllable.”

“We can be silent,” said I.

Crowle looked over his spectacles. “Most people can until it becomes profitable.”

On returning to Roper Street we found Gradder waiting on our step.

“I want the house,” he said.

“You already own it.”

“I want possession. I’ll return your quarter’s rent.”

“We are comfortable here.”

“I’ll give you twenty pounds.”

Mary passed me with the latch-key and went inside without speaking.

“Why?” I asked.

“Repairs.”

“You formerly said there would be none.”

“Changed my mind.”

“So have we. We mean to stay.”

He offered fifty pounds. I declined. His face darkened until the grey whiskers appeared almost white against it.

“Hundred,” he said. “Cash. You go by noon tomorrow and surrender the agreement.”

Now a hundred pounds was nearly two years of my salary after household expenses, and we had already searched the premises as far as was possible without pulling them down. Gradder, on the other hand, was plainly willing to perform that destructive service himself. I therefore pretended reluctance, demanded the money in sovereigns and notes, and finally agreed, provided the surrender stated that we made no representation concerning anything hidden upon the premises.

He stared at me.

“What does that mean?”

“It means that if you find a million pounds under the wash-house, I shall not complain; and if you find nothing, you shall not complain either.”

“You know something.”

“I know the law attaches some importance to exact language.”

This was mendacity by suggestion, or perhaps suppressio veri; but litigation is a luxury which we could not afford. He paid the hundred pounds that afternoon and signed the paper.

We removed our furniture to two furnished rooms in Kennington. As our cart turned out of Roper Street, three labourers entered Number 18 carrying crowbars, sledgehammers and spades. Gradder followed them.

For the next fortnight Mary and I pursued her pedigree. Aunt Eliza, after much weeping and some reproach, produced a tin box containing the marriage certificate of Giles Armer and Alice Pritchard, Mary’s certificate of birth, two letters signed “Your affectionate father, Giles,” and a faded portrait of a broad-shouldered sailor with Mary’s eyes.

We found an old shipmate named Samuel Dore at Limehouse, who remembered Captain Armer, his wife and “the little maid Polly.” We obtained copies of crew lists from the Registrar-General of Shipping. A retired clergyman identified the marriage entry. Even the alteration from Armer to Harmer was explained by Mary’s mother, who had added the aspirate from resentment and retained it from habit.

Mr. Crowle gathered these proofs with professional appetite.

“There may be opposition,” he said. “Gradder will allege uncertainty, abandonment, perhaps illegitimacy if desperation overcomes prudence. But your declaration was in time, and the identity is strong.”

“And the money in the house?” Mary asked.

“Belongs to the residue if found. Yet I suspect the greater property is in consols, mortgages and dock shares. The estate is provisionally valued at eighty-six thousand pounds.”

I confess that I gripped the arms of my chair. Mary merely said, “Oh, Bob,” and put her hand into mine.

Three days later Gradder called at our lodgings.

At least, I believed it was Gradder when the servant showed him in, though his appearance had undergone changes. His coat was torn at the shoulder, his boots were white with plaster, lime dust filled his whiskers, and a black mark crossed his forehead. He resembled a man who had lost an argument with a chimney.

“You!” he said.

“Good morning, Mr. Gradder.”

“There’s nothing there.”

“So you have called to complain?”

“You knew it! You took my hundred pounds knowing it!”

“I knew only that we had found nothing. Our surrender was explicit.”

He slammed his hat upon the table, raising a small cloud.

“I’ve had every floor up. I’ve opened the walls, dug the garden six feet, pulled down the wash-house and taken out the drains. Nothing! Armer lied.”

“Possibly he moved the money.”

Gradder’s eyes narrowed. “What else do you know?”

Mary came into the room carrying a letter which had arrived by the same post. She gave it to me without speaking. It was from Crowle. The executors, after examining our evidence, accepted Mary’s identity, reserving only formal confirmation by the court.

Gradder snatched at the paper, but I held it beyond his reach.

“What is it?”

“It concerns Giles Armer’s estate.”

He started as though struck.

“What concern have you with Armer?”

Mary answered him.

“He was my father.”

Gradder’s mouth opened. For the first time in our acquaintance, the machinery failed to close it.

“No,” he said. “No child claimed. The month passed.”

“I claimed before Mr. Pell at forty-seven minutes past ten on the fifth of November.”

“You did not know!”

“Not until my husband read me the will.”

Gradder turned upon me. “Who told you?”

“A clerk told me why you were so eager to repair our house. Your own persistence supplied the rest.”

He stood quite still, the lime upon his sleeves, the soot upon his face, and the ruins of Number 18 almost visibly surrounding him. Then he seized his hat, crushed it against his chest, and made for the door.

On the threshold he looked back.

“If I had left you alone—”

“You would have inherited,” said Mary.

He went out with a sound between a groan and a growl, and banged the door so violently that our landlady came upstairs to inquire whether the ceiling was safe.

That was the last private conversation we ever had with Mr. Gradder. He opposed Mary’s claim, as Crowle had predicted, but withdrew before the matter reached a full hearing, his own solicitor having presumably explained the difference between suspicion and evidence. No concealed hoard was found in either house. Whether Armer had transferred it into securities, or merely enjoyed leaving Gradder a treasure-hunt as a final acknowledgment of those “certain services,” we never discovered.

Mary received the estate.

I did not immediately put up the shutters at Broadwater & Pym, for sudden wealth seemed to me no reason for discourtesy; but I resigned at the end of the quarter, after balancing my last colonial-produce ledger to the penny. We bought a modest house in Surrey, sound in roof, floor and chimney, though I examined all three before signing.

As for Number 18 Roper Street, Gradder had made it uninhabitable in the course of his researches and was obliged to rebuild the interior at considerable expense. Thus the New House became, at last, almost new.

I have sometimes wondered whether our fortune should be attributed to chance, to Mary’s memory, to Mr. Pell’s interrupted dinner, or to my extravagance in buying Wigley one. Yet justice requires that the largest share of our gratitude be given to Gradder himself. Had he been less grasping, less secretive, or merely less energetic with other people’s mantelpieces, Mary would never have heard her father’s name before the month expired.

He searched for Giles Armer’s money in every place except the right one. It was not in the walls.

It was in the will.

## THE BARON OF DONNERDUST

In the oldest and draughtiest quarter of Germany—where the mountains are peaked like nightcaps, the fir trees grow for the express purpose of howling after sunset, and every castle has at least one tower which no prudent person enters without a lantern and a written explanation of his business—there stood, some centuries ago, the venerable fortress of Donderdust.

It had seven courts, nine turrets, eleven chimneys, and a moat so green that the frogs were ashamed of it. Over the principal gate hung the stone likeness of Baron Wolfbold von Donderdust, who had founded the family by killing his elder brother, marrying his brother’s widow, and purchasing the approval of the Church at a very reasonable rate. He afterwards built a chapel to show his penitence; and as the chapel cost almost exactly what he had obtained by the murder, it was commonly regarded as an apology in stone and a receipt from Heaven.

His descendant, Baron Hildebrand von Donderdust, was a broad, red-faced, round-bodied gentleman, with black moustachios, russet boots, a green hunting coat, and a laugh which shook birds from the roof. He possessed a good estate, a bad cellar—for no cellar is good which is often empty—and five-and-twenty followers, including four-and-twenty men in Lincoln green and one steward in brown, who was much more dangerous than all the rest because he understood arithmetic.

The green men hunted with the baron, drank with the baron, sang with the baron, quarrelled with the baron, and fell under the table with the baron. They were distinguished from him chiefly by having coarser coats, thicker heads, thinner purses, and no legal claim to the castle. Their noses were of various shapes but one colour; and when ranged along the lower table after supper, with tankards before them and boar-spears behind, they presented as noble a picture of feudal attachment as any man could wish to see—provided he did not wish to smell it.

The baron was then thirty-five, though several village maidens asserted that he was younger, and several village fathers that he ought to have known better. He had never been ill except after drinking, never melancholy except before dinner, and never frightened except by a bill. He rose at daybreak, hunted till sunset, supped till midnight, and spent the remaining hours in such innocent recreations as throwing knives at the door, singing war-songs in the chapel, and waking the steward to inquire how much money was left.

“None, my lord,” the steward generally replied.

“Then we know where it is,” said the baron. “Bring wine.”

This was a satisfactory arrangement while it lasted. But even pleasure, when repeated every day, becomes as tiresome as virtue; and one winter, after hunting the same wolves through the same forest, missing the same stag by the same oak tree, and hearing his companions sing the same thirty-seven verses about the Maiden of the Rhine, Baron Hildebrand became bored.

Boredom is a dangerous distinction in a great man. A poor man who is bored may mend his shoes, dig his garden, or quarrel with his landlord; but a nobleman has nothing between boredom and matrimony except politics, and politics had not yet been invented in that neighbourhood.

“I must have a change,” said the baron.

The four-and-twenty green men stopped drinking and looked pale, which, in their case, meant pale everywhere but the nose.

“A change of wine?” asked Klaus, the captain.

“No.”

“Of horses?”

“No.”

“Of steward?”

The steward coughed.

“No. I shall marry.”

At this appalling word the company groaned, and one old retainer slipped beneath the table from pure feudal sensibility.

The lady selected was Fraulein Adelgunda von Schnappenschloss, only daughter of the neighbouring Count of that name. She was tall, fair, blue-eyed, soft-spoken, and accomplished in embroidery, fainting, and obtaining her own way. Her father was celebrated for having fought three duels without injuring anybody, which showed either unusual humanity or very bad aim. Her mother, the Countess Ermengarda, was a woman of majestic proportions and unanswerable opinions. She had directed her husband for thirty years and had brought him, by patience, tears, reproaches, headaches, and locked cupboards, into a state of perfect domestic happiness.

Baron Hildebrand rode to Schnappenschloss with all his followers, thirty hounds, two falcons, three casks of wine, and a sharpened hunting knife. The last was not intended as a present; but a gentleman in those days never knew whom he might meet on the road, and many excellent friendships began with an attempt to cut off somebody’s nose.

The Count received him with open arms, chiefly because the baron had brought the wine. Adelgunda lowered her eyes. The Countess raised hers. The twenty-four green men emptied their cups. Before the third cask had been broached, the marriage was settled.

“My daughter is a tender flower,” said the Countess.

“I’ll take care nobody treads on her,” said Hildebrand.

“She has delicate nerves.”

“Our walls are thick.”

“She cannot bear noise.”

The green men, who were roaring a chorus in the courtyard, broke a bench at that moment.

“They can sing outside,” said the baron.

“She dislikes bloodshed.”

“I never shed blood indoors if I can help it.”

“She must not be contradicted.”

“Certainly not,” said Hildebrand, who had heard only half the sentence and understood none of it.

Adelgunda smiled. The Count shed a wink of joy into his cup. The Countess embraced the baron as a son whom she intended to improve; and the marriage took place with such magnificence that six neighbouring families were ruined by trying to imitate it.

For the first month, Baron Hildebrand declared matrimony the finest sport in Christendom. Adelgunda called him her lion, her eagle, her thunderbolt, and her sweet little Hildebrand. She admired his moustachios, praised his horses, and listened to the first three verses of the Maiden of the Rhine with every appearance of consciousness. He presented her with jewels, falcons, silk gowns, and the key to the household stores.

The last gift was the most important. A woman may mislay jewels, neglect falcons, and tire of silk, but she seldom forgets a key.

Little by little, and bit by bit, and day by day, the castle altered. The hunting horns were removed from the great hall because their appearance encouraged noise. The boar-spears were carried to the armoury because their points offended Adelgunda’s nerves. The hounds were sent from the courtyard to the outer kennels, from the outer kennels to the village, and from the village to a cousin of the Countess, who had no use for them but liked acquiring property.

Then came the turn of the four-and-twenty green men.

“My love,” said the baroness one morning, “that red-nosed fellow Klaus stared at me.”

“Klaus stares at everything,” said Hildebrand. “His eyes were made so.”

“I tremble whenever I see him.”

“Then don’t see him.”

“How can I help seeing him when he is here?”

The baron considered this. It was an unfortunate habit of his to consider the last question put to him and forget all that had gone before.

“Klaus!” he cried. “Take six men and hunt on the northern estate.”

Adelgunda kissed him; and within a fortnight she trembled at seven others.

Some were discharged because they sang, some because they snored, some because they wore muddy boots, and one because he had laughed while the baroness’s lapdog was being sick. The last offence I contend was not fairly proved; for the man declared that he had laughed at the dog before it was sick and become serious immediately afterwards. But justice, especially domestic justice, is less concerned with time than with example.

At length only old Dietrich remained. He had carried Hildebrand when an infant, saved him from drowning when a boy, followed him in three border fights, and once allowed himself to be bitten by a wolf rather than wake the baron before noon.

“My angel,” said Adelgunda, “Dietrich frightens the children.”

At that time there were four children.

“He loves them,” said Hildebrand.

“He gives them knives.”

“Small knives.”

“He teaches them to swear.”

“Only at wolves.”

“He says your mother was the finest woman in Germany.”

“So she was.”

Adelgunda burst into tears.

Dietrich went that evening.

Children, meanwhile, arrived with a punctuality worthy of better-regulated persons. There was a son the first year, twin daughters the second, another son the third, three daughters in two years, and so on, until twelve young Donderdusts inhabited the castle, shouted in the passages, tumbled down the stairs, pulled the tapestry from the walls, and attempted to sail across the moat in a cradle. Iron bars were placed across the nursery windows, not, as visitors supposed, to keep robbers out, but to keep the family in.

The Countess Ermengarda now came to assist her daughter. She brought six trunks, two parrots, a physician, a lapdog, and the intention of remaining for a week. At the end of eight years she was still there.

Her husband came also, because he had grown accustomed to being directed and felt lonely without it. He occupied a chamber over the kitchen, played cards with the chaplain, and never interfered except when ordered by the Countess, at which times he interfered with great spirit.

The expenses of Donderdust increased. There were nurses, tutors, physicians, dancing masters, fencing masters, tailors, pastry cooks, French dolls, Italian singers, Dutch toys, and German bills. The baron sold two farms, then four vineyards, then the silver drinking cups, after which the Countess observed that the family was living beyond its means.

“My means lived very comfortably before your family came,” said Hildebrand.

“My family!” cried Adelgunda.

“My family!” thundered the Countess.

“My family?” repeated the Count, looking up from his cards.

That night the baron slept in the west tower.

He had long ago ceased to hunt. His green coat grew tight in the shoulders and loose at the courage. His moustachios drooped. He walked softly past the drawing room, ate whatever was left after the children, and drank weak wine on the physician’s assurance that strong wine encouraged independent thought. If he laughed, the baroness had a headache; if he sang, the baby woke; if he proposed riding, rain was expected; and if there was no rain, one of the children was indisposed, which came to much the same thing, except that the child generally recovered before the weather.

One November evening the steward entered with a packet of accounts. He laid them upon the table in the manner of a physician placing his hand upon a corpse.

“How bad?” asked the baron.

“Very bad, my lord.”

“Can we pay?”

“No, my lord.”

“Can we borrow?”

“We have.”

“Can we sell?”

“We have.”

“What remains?”

“The castle, the forest, the northern meadows, and your title.”

“What is the title worth?”

“Nothing, my lord.”

“Thank Heaven,” said Hildebrand. “We can keep that.”

At this moment Adelgunda came in, pale and interesting, followed by the Countess, pale and commanding.

“My dear Hildebrand,” said the baroness, “I have news.”

The baron looked at the twelve family portraits recently painted upon the opposite wall. The youngest sitter had swallowed a brush during the final session, and the bill included it.

“No,” said Hildebrand.

“Yes,” murmured Adelgunda.

“A thirteenth blessing,” said the Countess.

The baron stared at the accounts, at his wife, at his mother-in-law, and lastly at the ancestral sword hanging above the fireplace. Baron Wolfbold had used that sword upon his brother, three creditors, and a tax collector. Hildebrand’s thoughts took a gloomy turn.

He bowed, left the room, and walked along the moonlit gallery. The wind moaned among the turrets; shutters banged; a raven flapped against the windows; and something white glided across the end of the passage. It was only a nurse in a nightcap carrying a basin, but at such a time even a basin may possess appalling significance.

The baron entered an abandoned chamber in the oldest tower. No one used it because an ancestor was said to have murdered a traveller there and hidden the body beneath the floor. The traveller had subsequently been found alive in Prague, but the room retained its reputation, reputations being far easier to acquire than to correct.

Hildebrand locked the door. He placed a pistol on the table, a knife beside it, a rope beside the knife, and a cup of poison beside the rope. He had brought all four because he had not decided which method was most becoming to his rank. Then he produced a flagon of old wine and a pipe.

“A man should not die thirsty,” he said, “nor in a hurry.”

He filled his cup, lit his pipe, and stretched his boots toward the fire. The wine was excellent. The tobacco was better. After the second cup the pistol looked less persuasive; after the third, the rope appeared positively foolish. Nevertheless, he had formed a resolution and was preparing to remember it when a cold hand touched his shoulder.

The baron turned.

Beside him stood a figure as tall as a coffin set upright and much less cheerful. Its face was yellow, its eyes green, its cheeks hollow, and its mouth drawn down at the corners as if it had been fed on vinegar from infancy. It wore a cloak of black pall cloth, fastened with coffin handles; a wreath of faded cypress encircled its head; and through the middle of its body projected a wooden stake, which must have made sitting inconvenient. Small silver skulls decorated its shoes. One lace was undone.

“Who the devil are you?” asked Hildebrand.

“Not the devil,” replied the figure. “He has better quarters.”

“Then take your hand off my shoulder.”

The figure did so, though with an offended air.

“I am the Genius of Despair and Suicide.”

“A busy profession?”

“Extremely. I have seven appointments before dawn. You are the third.”

“Who were the others?”

“A wealthy prince who has eaten too much supper, and a beggar who has eaten none.”

Hildebrand puffed his pipe.

“Do both kill themselves for the same reason?”

“Certainly not.”

“What is the prince’s reason?”

“He has everything and is tired of it.”

“And the beggar’s?”

“He has nothing and is tired of that.”

The baron considered.

“It seems difficult to satisfy people.”

“It is impossible,” said the Genius, with professional pride.

“And you have come to assist me?”

“To hurry you. I cannot spend the whole night in one castle. Choose your instrument.”

Hildebrand looked at the pistol.

“Will it hurt?”

“For a moment.”

“And afterwards?”

“I cannot say.”

“You are the Genius of Suicide and don’t know what comes afterwards?”

“My department ends at the act.”

“Bad administration,” said the baron. “If my steward conducted business in that fashion, I should dismiss him.”

“Your affairs are ruined,” replied the Genius. “Your pleasures are gone. Your companions are scattered. Your wife rules you, your mother-in-law insults you, your creditors surround you, and your thirteenth child is approaching with all reasonable speed. What remains?”

“Nothing,” said Hildebrand gloomily.

“Precisely. Shoot yourself.”

The baron lifted the pistol, then put it down.

“Wait. My affairs are ruined, you say?”

“Hopelessly.”

“Could I reduce expenses?”

“You might.”

“Sell the southern timber?”

“Possibly.”

“Dismiss the Italian singer, the French tutor, the physician, the dancing master, the pastry cook, the Countess’s two parrots, and the lapdog?”

“You could.”

“Then my affairs are not hopeless.”

“They are unpleasant.”

“Unpleasant is not hopeless. This wine is unpleasant when empty, but I fill the cup.”

He did so.

The Genius shifted his stake impatiently.

“Your old life is destroyed.”

“I might begin another.”

“At your age?”

“I am forty-seven.”

“Exactly.”

“My grandfather hunted wolves at eighty.”

“He was killed by one.”

“Yes, but he enjoyed the hunt.”

The Genius tapped one skull-tipped shoe upon the floor.

“Your friends have deserted you.”

“I dismissed them.”

“Then they will hate you.”

“They are Germans,” said Hildebrand. “They will forgive me if I provide supper.”

“Your wife will forbid it.”

“Ah,” said the baron.

He smoked for a little while, and the Genius smiled a corpse-like smile.

“There!” said the apparition. “You see. You are mastered.”

“I have permitted myself to be mastered.”

“The distinction is trifling.”

“It may be, but it is mine. If a man puts his own head under a pump, he should not accuse the pump of tyranny. I shall take it out.”

“Your wife will weep.”

“She always does.”

“She will faint.”

“I shall place a cushion behind her.”

“Her mother will denounce you.”

“I shall provide a carriage.”

“Her father will challenge you.”

“I shall let him aim first.”

This answer plainly distressed the Genius.

“Think of your children,” he said hastily. “Twelve already, and another coming. They will consume your fortune, disturb your peace, and divide your estate.”

“They may also grow up.”

“They may.”

“They may help me.”

“They may not.”

“One may become a soldier.”

“And be killed.”

“One a priest.”

“And become fat.”

“One a merchant.”

“And cheat you.”

“One may remain at home.”

“And annoy you.”

“Then,” cried Hildebrand, “they have every prospect of becoming ordinary useful members of society. I see no reason to shoot myself on their account.”

“You were eager enough when I arrived.”

“I had not examined the matter.”

“Examination is fatal to my business,” muttered the Genius.

“What was that?”

“Nothing. Take the poison. It is quiet.”

“Does it taste bad?”

“Very.”

“Then I object on principle. I have swallowed bad wine to please my father-in-law, bad medicine to please the physician, and bad advice to please my wife. I will not swallow bad poison to please you.”

The Genius’s green eyes flashed.

“You are miserable!”

“At present.”

“You have been miserable for years!”

“Quite true.”

“You will be miserable tomorrow!”

“I have no means of knowing. I have not tried tomorrow.”

“Your debts—”

“May be arranged.”

“Your household—”

“May be reduced.”

“Your wife—”

“May be reasoned with.”

The Genius gave a dry, rattling chuckle.

“That is the wildest hope yet.”

“Then she may be reasoned without.”

“You make light of everything.”

“No,” said Hildebrand, filling another cup. “I have made everything heavy. That was the trouble.”

He rose. The black figure appeared less tall now, though this may have been because the baron had straightened his back.

“Sit down!” cried the Genius.

“I have sat down for twelve years.”

“Despair!”

“I have tried it.”

“Die!”

“Not tonight.”

“You made an appointment.”

“I made no appointment with you.”

“You laid out the instruments.”

“I was comparing them.”

“You cannot dismiss me!”

“My wife dismissed Klaus, Dietrich, four-and-twenty hunters, thirty hounds, and a falcon that had been in the family longer than she had. I have learned something from her.”

The Genius advanced, his cloak rustling like dead leaves in a churchyard.

“Beware! If you live, there will be quarrels, toil, humiliation, poverty, screaming children, unpaid bills, grey hairs, rheumatism, and perhaps gout.”

“Very likely.”

“There will be disappointment.”

“Certainly.”

“Trouble.”

“No doubt.”

“Death at the end.”

“Of course.”

“Then why laugh?”

“Because,” said Baron Hildebrand, beginning with a chuckle in his waistcoat and ending with a roar among the rafters, “you offer me death as a cure for death!”

He laughed until the cups danced, the pistol rolled from the table, and dust fell out of the ancestral curtains. He laughed at the coffin handles, at the cypress wreath, at the silver skulls, at the wooden stake, and especially at the undone shoestring. The more he laughed, the smaller the Genius became. His pall-cloth cloak shrank to a widow’s handkerchief; his stake dwindled to a splinter; his green eyes faded like bad lamps at sunrise.

“Stop!” squeaked the apparition. “This is indecent!”

The baron laughed louder.

The Genius folded inward like a scorched parchment, slipped through a crack beside the hearth, and vanished with a smell of damp ashes and disappointed vinegar.

At breakfast, Baron Hildebrand appeared in his old green coat, which burst at one button but otherwise behaved nobly. His moustachios stood out. A hunting horn hung at his side.

“My dear,” said Adelgunda, “what does this mean?”

“It means breakfast first and business afterwards.”

The Countess drew herself up.

“I demand—”

“A carriage will be ready for you at noon.”

She sat down very suddenly.

The Count rose. “Sir, I shall not permit—”

“Your carriage will be the same one. It is economical.”

Adelgunda began to weep. The baron placed a cushion behind her.

“The singer, tutors, physician, dancing master, pastry cook, parrots, and dog leave today,” he continued. “The children will keep one tutor, two nurses, and all their limbs if possible. The steward will settle with the creditors. I shall sell the southern timber and hunt the northern forest. Klaus and the others are coming to supper.”

“You monster!” cried the Countess.

“Madam,” replied Hildebrand, “I have been called worse by a gentleman in coffin handles.”

He kissed his wife, rang for breakfast, and blew the hunting horn out of the window. The blast rolled over the moat and through the forest. Before noon, men in faded green were hurrying toward Donderdust from every road, with red noses, muddy boots, cracked horns, and forgiveness enough for any offence that included supper.

The baroness did not yield at once. I needn’t say that; no sensible person ever expected she would. There were tears, faintings, locked doors, messages through servants, appeals to the children, and one alarming threat to return to her mother, which lost much of its force because her mother had already returned to her. But Hildebrand remained cheerful, affectionate, and immovable; and firmness, when it is not accompanied by shouting, is a very puzzling weapon.

In time Adelgunda discovered that she preferred a husband who laughed to one who crept, that children could survive plain cakes, and that a castle containing hunters was no noisier than a castle containing twelve young Donderdusts. She even permitted the hounds to return, though not to the drawing room.

The thirteenth child arrived safely and proved to be a daughter, who later married a banker and restored half the family fortune—a fact which the Genius of Despair had neglected to predict, his department, as he observed, being badly administered.

Baron Hildebrand lived to eighty-two. He never became rich, but he paid his debts, educated his children, hunted moderately, drank with discrimination, and laughed without permission. When trouble came, as trouble will come even to castles with seven courts and nine turrets, he looked at both sides of it, and if one side appeared black, he polished the other until it shone.

This is not a grand remedy, perhaps; but it frightened away a Genius, preserved a baron, raised thirteen children, and kept the castle of Donderdust from falling into the hands of the creditors—which is as much as can reasonably be expected from any philosophy.

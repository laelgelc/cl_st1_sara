### THE PRIZE LODGER

To the Londoner whose knowledge of his native city is bounded eastward by the Law Courts and northward by Regent’s Park, the great regions beyond may appear less as inhabited districts than as names printed upon the fronts of omnibuses. He has heard of Holloway; he believes Stoke Newington to exist; he associates Highbury with football and Finsbury Park with occasional smoke seen from a railway carriage. Of Islington he may know that it possesses a long road, a town hall, innumerable chapels, and a population sufficient to affect an election. But that men are born there, grow prosperous there, take their pleasures there, and die there without once suffering from the consciousness that they dwell outside the true circumference of civilization—this he is slow to understand.

Yet Islington is, and for generations has been, a world complete in itself. Its tradesmen are acquainted with one another’s pecuniary standing; its widows exchange exact intelligence concerning rents, servants, and the moral character of lodgers; its bachelors become local institutions; its households rise and decline under the observation of neighbours who, though unable to repeat the Creed without hesitation, remember infallibly who occupied the first floor at Number Seventeen in the year of the great frost. Here modest prosperity maintains itself without splendour, and respectability, though never quite at ease, is seldom driven to confess defeat.

Archibald Jordan belonged to this settled population. At fifty-two he was a man of substantial, if not imposing, means; he had an interest in a firm of wholesale stationers near Clerkenwell, and the interest, though it demanded his attendance on four days of the week, did not require such activity as might impair the dignity of his habits. He was unmarried, well preserved, and careful of his person. His clothes were neither new enough to excite comment nor old enough to invite compassion; his linen was irreproachable; his boots, except in severe weather, bore a polish which suggested moral principle rather than vanity. He wore whiskers of a sober brown, trimmed to an equality which nature had perhaps not intended, and carried a silver-headed cane in all seasons.

His walk was peculiar. He advanced at a pace too deliberate for haste and too regular for leisure, taking the pavement as though it had been measured for his convenience. An acquaintance who overtook him was obliged either to adopt this motion or to pass on after a few ceremonious remarks. Jordan never accelerated. He disliked the appearance of hurry, partly because it was undignified, but chiefly because it implied that circumstances could compel him.

For five-and-twenty years he had lived in furnished lodgings.

Now a good lodger, as every rational landlady knows, is a prize creature, more rarely encountered than the advertisements of vacant rooms would lead the inexperienced to suppose. He may pay promptly but ruin carpets; he may be orderly but require late suppers; he may be liberal in money and tyrannical in speech; he may possess all virtues except continuance, which to the landlady is the virtue that sanctifies the rest. Jordan paid on the appointed morning, broke nothing, received no disreputable visitors, objected to children, disliked pianofortes, and went to bed at half-past ten. His breakfast was simple, his supper lighter still. He gave the servant a Christmas gratuity, and, when illness visited the household, sent inquiries in a manner sufficiently sympathetic and not so cordial as to encourage details.

In exchange for these benefits he required recognition.

At first every landlady recognized him. The fire was bright when he returned; the toast came up dry; his newspaper lay folded with the principal column outwards; the servant understood that his boots were not to be scraped with the same instrument used upon inferior footwear. During the first months, perhaps the first year, all went admirably. Then, by imperceptible stages, familiarity performed its vulgar work. The landlady entered without knocking because her hands were occupied; the servant referred to him as “Mr. J.”; the butter appeared in a common dish; a cousin from Dalston was allowed to sit in his arm-chair while waiting for tea.

Jordan observed these changes with pain. He did not complain immediately. Complaints were weapons which lost force by indiscriminate employment. Instead, he would look at the butter, then at the landlady, and raise one forefinger.

“Mrs. Pounce,” he would say, “I think there has been a mistake.”

The dish would be changed, apologies tendered, and for a fortnight the domestic machine resumed its proper rhythm. But the original reverence never wholly returned. Once a landlady had admitted to herself that Jordan was not a visitation but a fact, her conduct betrayed the discovery.

He would then give notice.

The effect was commonly remarkable. The woman who yesterday had allowed him to ring twice now found herself overwhelmed by the prospect of his departure. She recalled his punctual payments, his clean habits, his preference for quiet evenings. She offered a reduction of two shillings; she dismissed the negligent servant; she wept. Jordan remained courteous but immovable.

“No, Mrs. Pounce. I have decided. I bear no resentment—none whatever. But a change will be advantageous to both parties.”

He knew that the change could not advantage her, and the knowledge supplied a pleasure which he would have been ashamed to name. Indeed, his secret was perhaps not entirely revealed even to himself. He supposed that he moved because standards had declined; it did not occur to him that the decline was necessary to the exquisite moment when he asserted his value by withdrawing it.

His removals became part of the domestic folklore of northern Islington. Former landladies, meeting at the grocer’s or over a gate, spoke of him with bitterness modified by pride.

“I had Mr. Jordan three years and nine months,” Mrs. Pounce would say.

“He stopped with me nearly five,” Mrs. Calthorpe replied, omitting certain troublesome intervals. “Never a word about the rent.”

“Very particular.”

“A gentleman has a right to be particular.”

“He made my Emma cry over a haddock.”

“Emma was careless with fish.”

Thus they defended him against one another, each anxious that the loss of so distinguished a lodger should appear a misfortune rather than an exposure. He was called difficult, but never mean. If his temper had been harsher or his generosity less exact, he would have sunk into the ordinary curmudgeon; as it was, he retained the elevated position of a man whom it was an honour to satisfy and a tragedy to lose.

In the spring of his fifty-third year he took rooms in Liverpool Road under the roof of Mrs. Elderfield, a widow of forty-six. The house was narrow, but it had been painted recently; the brass was bright without ostentation; the fanlight possessed no coloured glass. Mrs. Elderfield herself received him in a black dress relieved at the throat by a small lace collar. She was tall, composed, and not without good looks, though her face had the expression of one who regards beauty as an unreliable servant and prefers efficiency.

“The sitting-room receives the afternoon sun,” she said.

“I am seldom at home in the afternoon.”

“Then it will be warm on your return.”

He glanced at her. The answer was sensible.

“The rent?”

She named it.

“That includes attendance?”

“It includes proper attendance.”

He took the rooms.

From the first, Mrs. Elderfield’s management surpassed his expectations. She did not flatter; she did not forget. His breakfast was ready at eight, not at five minutes past; the coal was screened; his letters were placed beside his plate in order of size. When he rang, the servant came. When the servant failed, Mrs. Elderfield came herself, heard the complaint, and corrected the matter without pleading that the girl was young, had toothache, or was related to a respectable family in Barnet.

On one occasion Jordan found a faint stain upon a table-napkin. He rang.

Mrs. Elderfield appeared.

“I said, Mrs. Elderfield, please examine it.”

She examined it.

“Yes,” she said. “It should not have been put out.”

She removed it and brought another. There was no argument, no injured virtue, no narrative of the laundress’s afflictions. Jordan, deprived of every opportunity for severity, was pleased almost to tenderness.

Months passed. He waited for deterioration. It did not come. The butter retained its dish; the fire anticipated the weather; no relation from Dalston occupied his chair. When Mrs. Elderfield spoke, she had something to say; when she had nothing, she vouchsafed silence. She neither sought his confidence nor displayed resentment at being denied it.

At the end of two years Jordan said to himself, “Here I shall stay.”

The declaration was important. Constancy in lodging had not previously formed part of his conception of happiness. Yet he had begun to feel that the house knew him. The stairs creaked beneath his tread at the proper places; the hall stand received his hat with the ease of an old servant; even the view of chimney-pots had acquired the dignity of possession. Mrs. Elderfield’s presence, never obtrusive, completed this sense of order. She was not a companion—Jordan had little use for companionship—but she was an intelligence operating beneficially in the background.

Then, one November evening, she announced that she meant to leave Islington.

Jordan had finished his supper. Mrs. Elderfield stood beside the table with the weekly account.

“I shall not be able to continue the rooms after Lady Day,” she said.

He looked up slowly.

“Not continue them?”

“I am giving up the house.”

“Giving it up? Why?”

“My brother has obtained a lease at Wood Green. There are two houses together, and I shall take one.”

Wood Green!

To the conventional mind Wood Green might represent improvement: clearer air, wider roads, gardens, and a prospect, from certain upper windows, of actual sky. To Jordan it represented distance, rawness, and the end of all things familiar. Wood Green was a place reached by tram-car, a wilderness of new brick where tradesmen did not know him and streets had names derived from trees not yet planted.

“You intend to live there?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“And these rooms?”

“They will be let by whoever takes the house.”

The room altered as he gazed about it. The curtains became temporary; the carpet, lately his carpet in everything but law, belonged already to an unknown successor. Another landlady! Another period of instruction! Another servant to be taught the distinction between toasted bread and bread exposed to heat!

“You have decided?”

“I have.”

She placed the account before him and withdrew.

During the next week Jordan suffered a disquiet disproportionate, as he recognized, to the mere necessity of finding apartments. He inspected three houses. In one the landlady breathed noisily; in another the sitting-room contained wax fruit under glass; in the third a baby cried overhead with the pertinacity of a political grievance. He returned each evening to Mrs. Elderfield’s quiet table, and resentment gave place to melancholy.

He had grown attached to her. The discovery offended his self-respect. Attachment was a condition in which one person’s decisions acquired power over another; Jordan had always preferred that the power should be his. Yet the fact persisted. He noticed her steady hands, her firm step, the order of her hair. He recalled that she was a widow and presumably possessed some money. It also occurred to him that marriage, though an extreme measure, need not wholly disturb the arrangements which had already proved satisfactory.

This last reflection, because it clothed emotion in practical garments, determined him.

His courtship was brief and ceremonious. Mrs. Elderfield heard his proposal without blushes, exclamations, or demands for time. She asked two questions concerning his income and one concerning his health. Jordan answered with dignity. She then observed that at their age affection ought to be supported by mutual respect and common sense.

“Precisely,” he said.

They were married in February, and in March removed to Wood Green.

The astonishment among the landladies of Islington exceeded anything produced by Jordan’s earlier departures. Mrs. Pounce declared that she had always seen he was a marrying man; Mrs. Calthorpe replied that no woman of delicacy would have used the position of landlady to entrap a gentleman. The servant at Liverpool Road received half a sovereign and gave notice to the new tenant. For several weeks the marriage was discussed wherever bacon, paraffin, and personal character were sold together.

Jordan himself entered the new life with reasonable confidence. The house at Wood Green was called Montrose Villa, though it was neither detached nor Scottish. It had a strip of garden before it, three bedrooms, a parlour which Mrs. Jordan named the drawing-room, and a dining-room at the back. The rent was higher than Jordan thought necessary, but Mrs. Jordan explained that respectability was cheaper in the long run.

On the first evening he sat beside the new hearth and regarded his surroundings. The furniture was familiar; his chair stood at the proper angle; his slippers waited. Through the curtains came the faint grinding of a tram-car.

“It will do,” he said.

Mrs. Jordan, arranging a vase upon the mantelpiece, made no reply.

The first disturbance occurred on the following morning. Jordan descended in his outdoor boots, having already walked to the corner for a newspaper. Rain had fallen, and he left two dull marks upon the oilcloth.

“Archibald,” said his wife, “take off your boots in the hall.”

He smiled, supposing the remark to be a suggestion expressed with marital directness.

“They are scarcely wet.”

“They have marked the floor.”

“The girl can wipe it.”

“The girl has enough to do.”

Jordan unfolded his newspaper.

“The proper place for boots is on the feet, my dear.”

“The proper place for muddy boots is the boot-rack.”

He lowered the paper. As a lodger he had frequently corrected statements which confused preference with principle; he now perceived such confusion in his wife.

“I have always worn my boots at breakfast.”

“You will wear slippers here.”

“Will?”

“Yes.”

The single syllable, calmly uttered, struck him with such novelty that he could not immediately reply. Mrs. Jordan placed the slippers beside his chair and went to fetch the teapot. To refuse would have magnified the incident; to comply seemed an act of courtesy. He changed his boots.

A week later he was late for dinner. In Islington, when business or acquaintance detained him, he had sent no message. A bachelor’s dinner waited because it had no alternative. He arrived at Montrose Villa at twenty minutes past seven and found his wife seated before an empty plate.

“The meat is spoiled,” she said.

“It cannot be spoiled in twenty minutes.”

“It was ready at seven.”

“I met Wilbraham near the Angel.”

“You might have sent a telegram.”

“A telegram! For twenty minutes?”

“If dinner is at seven, you will be here at seven.”

“My business cannot always be regulated by the kitchen clock.”

“Then you will have cold meat.”

Jordan laughed, though without amusement.

“This is my house, Caroline.”

“It is our house.”

“I pay for it.”

“And I manage it.”

The distinction had force, but he disliked the manner in which it was presented. He cut the overdone mutton and said no more.

Such incidents multiplied, each too small to justify open war, yet each removing some particle of his independence. He was not to leave the sponge in the basin, because it prevented the basin from drying; he was not to stir the fire after ten, because the hearth had been swept; he was not to read at breakfast, because newspapers gathered grease and conversation was more suitable between husband and wife. The last prohibition affected him deeply. For thirty years he had fortified himself against the day’s demands behind the broad pages of a morning journal. To be deprived of it while eating an egg seemed downright slavery.

“I do not wish to converse at breakfast,” he said.

“That is because you have lived selfishly.”

“I have lived as I chose.”

“You will not do as you like in your own house.”

Jordan stared.

“What did you say?”

“I said you will not do as you like in your own house.”

“Do you understand the extraordinary character of that observation?”

“Perfectly. No one can do as he likes in a house where another person has to live.”

“But you do as you like.”

“I do what is necessary.”

Here was the essential strength of Mrs. Jordan’s position. Her wishes never appeared as wishes; they entered the household clothed as necessities. A rug must lie thus because otherwise someone might stumble. A window must be closed because draughts were dangerous. Dinner must occur at seven because servants required regularity. Jordan’s desires, by contrast, remained nakedly personal and therefore disreputable.

He attempted authority. One evening, having found his slippers removed from the fender, he rang the bell. The maid, a broad-faced girl named Ellen, appeared.

“Who moved these?”

“Missus told me, sir.”

“I am asking who moved them.”

“I did, sir.”

“Then replace them.”

Ellen hesitated.

“Do you hear?”

Before she could obey, Mrs. Jordan entered.

“The slippers dirty the tiles,” she said.

“They have stood there for years.”

“Not on these tiles.”

“I prefer them there.”

“They will remain by the coal-box.”

Jordan raised his forefinger, an instrument which had subdued negligence from Canonbury to Barnsbury.

“Caroline, I must request you not to contradict me before the servant.”

“Then do not give the servant foolish orders.”

Ellen’s face assumed the concentrated vacancy of domestic discretion.

“Leave the room,” Jordan commanded.

“You may go, Ellen,” said Mrs. Jordan.

The girl went in obedience to both, which satisfied neither.

Jordan’s anger rose. “I will not be spoken to in this manner.”

“How will you be spoken to?”

“With the respect due to a husband.”

“Behave like one.”

“What the devil do you mean by that?”

“I mean that a husband does not call a servant from her work to move slippers six inches.”

The littleness of the subject made his indignation appear little also. This was intolerable. He left the room, slamming the door; but the door, fitted with a new spring at Mrs. Jordan’s order, refused to slam and closed behind him with a decorous sigh.

The house itself had conspired against him.

As spring advanced, his dislike of Wood Green became a settled grievance. Its comparative freshness offended him. The gardens, with their black soil and hopeful shrubs, appeared unfinished; the villas repeated one another with the oppressive regularity of moral advice. In Islington he had known every turning, every shop-front, every public clock whose error might safely be allowed for. Here he was merely one middle-aged gentleman among hundreds carried south each morning and returned north each evening. No tradesman greeted him by name. Even the barber asked whether he preferred bay rum, a question which suggested that his preferences had not yet entered the common knowledge of the district.

Mrs. Jordan, on the other hand, expanded in her new sphere. She joined a sewing circle, called upon two neighbours, engaged a reliable laundress, and discovered that the butcher had been overcharging for suet. Her authority crossed the threshold and established itself in the road. Within six months people referred to her as “that capable Mrs. Jordan at Montrose Villa.” Of Jordan they knew chiefly that he went into town.

He began to prolong his stays there. At first he remained at the office until six, though his work ended at five. Later he revived acquaintance with Wilbraham, a widower who frequented a bar-parlour off Upper Street. Over a glass of whisky they discussed trade, municipal extravagance, and the deterioration of modern manners. Jordan did not speak openly of his marriage; a man who has married at fifty-three cannot admit domestic failure without appearing to have ignored abundant warning. But he hinted.

“Women,” he said, “have no conception of liberty.”

Wilbraham, whose late wife had allowed him liberty chiefly because she possessed an independent income and travelled frequently, sighed with experience.

“They want managing.”

“Precisely.”

“You have to be firm at the beginning.”

Jordan drank.

The beginning had passed.

He considered sterner measures. He might dismiss Ellen to demonstrate supremacy, but Mrs. Jordan would either retain her or engage someone worse. He might refuse household money; but the bills, presented with exact figures and receipted margins, were irreproachable, and any reduction would affect his own comfort. He might appeal to law. Yet what law compelled a wife to permit muddy boots, newspapers at breakfast, or slippers upon a particular tile? Marriage, he discovered, was protected less by statute than by the immense indifference of society to grievances which left no bruise.

One Saturday afternoon he returned unexpectedly and found the lower stairs freshly painted.

“You cannot go up yet,” said Mrs. Jordan.

“I need my grey coat.”

“You should have said so before.”

“I did not know before.”

“The paint is wet.”

“I can step at the edges.”

“You will leave marks.”

“I shall do nothing of the kind.”

He placed one foot upon the first stair.

“Archibald.”

That was all. The word contained warning, judgment, and the certainty of consequences. He turned.

“I am going to my own room.”

“You are not going over wet paint.”

“Stand aside.”

“No.”

For several seconds they faced each other in the narrow hall. Mrs. Jordan was the taller by perhaps half an inch; at that moment the difference appeared considerable. Jordan thought of moving her physically. The thought went no further. She folded her arms.

“You may have your coat when the paint is dry.”

“I shall have it now.”

“No.”

“Damn the paint!”

“It would be better if you went out until you can speak properly.”

Went out! From his own house! At her direction!

He seized his hat.

“I shall go out, and I shall return when I choose.”

“Wipe your boots before you come in.”

He left, closing the front door with violence enough to shake the fanlight. Unlike the dining-room door, it slammed admirably; but the triumph afforded little consolation, since he was outside.

He walked without purpose, taking the main road toward Islington. The afternoon was dull and warm. Tram-cars passed him, packed with women carrying parcels and men returning early from work. Presently he mounted one, not because of fatigue, but because walking to freedom lacked dignity.

At the Angel he alighted.

The familiar tumult moved him. Omnibuses crowded the crossing; shopkeepers stood beneath awnings; newspaper boys cried an edition; smoke hung where smoke ought to hang. He proceeded along Liverpool Road and paused opposite the house where Mrs. Elderfield had once let rooms. The paint had peeled. A card in the window announced APARTMENTS FOR A SINGLE GENTLEMAN.

A single gentleman!

The words seemed addressed to him personally, yet with cruel inaccuracy. He crossed the road, then hesitated. What could he do? Ask to see the rooms? Take them? He imagined Mrs. Jordan arriving, not in distress but with a cab for his luggage. He imagined neighbours, tradesmen, the firm, Wilbraham. A married man could not return to lodgings without explanation, and explanation was the one burden his pride could not carry.

He walked on.

At half-past seven he dined alone in a chop-house. The chop was tough, the potatoes watery, the tablecloth marked with rings. Once such defects would have provoked a rebuke. Now he ate almost gratefully. No one objected to his boots; no one removed his newspaper. The waiter neglected him with a freedom that had its charm.

Afterwards he sought Wilbraham, who listened to a more explicit account than Jordan had intended to give.

“You must show her,” said the widower.

“Show her what?”

“That you won’t stand it.”

“I have shown her.”

“Then keep away for the night.”

Jordan considered. The expedient had grandeur. Mrs. Jordan would sit waiting; anxiety would soften anger; by morning she would comprehend the danger of driving a husband beyond endurance. He took a room at a small hotel near King’s Cross and sent no message.

The bed was narrow, the walls thin, and the chamber-pot decorated with pink roses. At midnight a commercial traveller coughed in the adjoining room. Jordan remained awake, alternating between discomfort and satisfaction. Caroline would be listening for his key. At one she would look from the window. At two she would repent. He pictured her composing, in that firm hand, a note of apology.

No note arrived.

He breakfasted at the hotel and went to the office. Throughout the day he expected a telegram. Each time the boy entered, Jordan’s heart quickened. The messages concerned paper, envelopes, and a delayed consignment from Birmingham.

After business he returned to Islington. Pride now required a second night’s absence, though desire strongly recommended home. He dined with Wilbraham and slept at the same hotel. The commercial traveller had departed, but a young couple occupied the next room and laughed until nearly one o’clock.

On the second morning Jordan’s position began to lose its heroic aspect. His collar was no longer fresh; his chin required proper attention; the hotel coffee was an offence against commerce. Had his wife informed the police? Hardly. Had she sent to the office? Evidently not. Could she be ill? The possibility disturbed him, but he rejected it as an attempt by conscience to undermine policy.

That evening he went home.

As he approached Montrose Villa, he prepared himself for several forms of reception. Tears he would meet with restrained kindness; reproaches with firmness; accusations with a brief statement that he had been driven away. If she demanded where he had slept, he would decline to answer. He possessed, for the first time in months, a plan of conversation.

He opened the door.

Mrs. Jordan was in the dining-room, hemming a cloth. She looked up.

“You are late,” she said. “There is cold beef in the larder.”

Jordan stood in the doorway.

“Is that all you have to say?”

“I don’t know what else you expect.”

“I have been absent for two nights.”

“Yes.”

“Did you wonder where I was?”

“I supposed you knew.”

The answer struck him like insolence, though its words contained none.

“You supposed—! Caroline, a husband leaves his house for two nights without explanation, and you sit there sewing?”

“The cloth will not hem itself.”

“You felt no anxiety?”

“No.”

“No concern whatever?”

“I knew you had money in your pocket and friends in London. You are not a child.”

Jordan had imagined indifference as a weapon; he had not imagined it as truth. His departure, which in former days would have thrown a lodging-house into crisis, had failed even to interrupt the hemming of a cloth.

“You understand that I may do it again?”

“If you wish.”

“And remain away longer?”

“That is your affair.”

“My affair!”

“So long as you pay the bills and do not bring scandal to the house, I have no objection to your amusing yourself elsewhere.”

He sat down. The cold beef, though not yet visible, entered his mind with considerable force.

“Then you acknowledge my right to freedom?”

“Outside the house you may go where you please.”

“And inside?”

“Inside, you will wipe your boots, come to meals punctually, and behave properly.”

The division was monstrous. It was also clear.

Mrs. Jordan bit off a thread.

“Do you want the beef?”

“Yes,” he said.

She rang for Ellen.

During supper he read his newspaper. Mrs. Jordan made no objection, perhaps because the meal, being cold and irregular, did not possess full constitutional status. Jordan examined the situation with a mind chastened by poor coffee and two nights in a strange bed. He had failed to frighten his wife; but he had discovered that she did not intend to imprison him. Her concern was territory rather than affection. Within Montrose Villa she demanded obedience; beyond it she claimed neither his hours nor, apparently, his explanations.

This was not freedom as he had formerly understood it. Yet nations, after disastrous wars, have accepted less favourable boundaries.

In the weeks that followed he tested the arrangement cautiously. Twice he dined in Islington, once attended a music-hall with Wilbraham, and on a Thursday remained in town until nearly midnight. Mrs. Jordan asked no questions. But when he left a wet umbrella open in the hall, she closed it and informed him that the paper would be ruined; when he came down at breakfast in outdoor boots, she pointed to the slippers without speaking. He obeyed, though with a countenance expressive of principles reserved.

Something like peace emerged. He ceased to expect domestic sovereignty and resumed certain liberties that had seemed extinct. His evenings in Islington acquired the savour of bachelorhood without its inconvenience; for however late he returned, there awaited him a clean bed, a well-ordered room, and, in the morning, a breakfast superior to any provided by hotel or chop-house. He paid for these benefits not merely in money but in submission, and the price continued to offend him. Still, Jordan had always been willing to pay highly for exact service, provided the account was understood.

One night he dreamed that he had taken the old rooms in Liverpool Road. The fire burned brightly; his newspaper lay folded; a landlady waited at the door while he explained, with raised forefinger, that toast must be carried upon a warmed plate. She listened in awe. He was once more a prize creature, rare, courted, and capable at any moment of departure.

Then morning entered through the curtains of Montrose Villa. From below came Mrs. Jordan’s voice instructing Ellen not to overboil the eggs.

Jordan opened his eyes.

For an instant the dream’s freedom remained about him, spacious and impossible. Lodgings were for ever closed; Islington belonged to a former existence; the man who had unsettled households by giving notice could no longer give notice to his own. He lay still, listening to the ordered movements beneath him.

Then he remembered that Wilbraham had proposed dinner near the Angel that evening, followed by cards at the club-room of a public-house where no wife, landlady, servant, or painted staircase exercised jurisdiction.

The prospect was modest, but not contemptible.

He rose, put on his slippers, and went down to breakfast.

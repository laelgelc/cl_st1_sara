# The Secret of Dunfer Ravine

There are landscapes which declare themselves at once, as a drunkard declares his politics and a creditor his affection; others possess the rarer art of reserve. Dunfer Ravine belonged to the latter class. It entered the brown flank of the California hills with an appearance almost confidential, narrowing its lips as it withdrew from the road, darkening under the buckeyes, and turning twice in a distance so short that the second turn seemed less an accident of nature than a precaution.

I first observed it in the summer of 1868, while traveling on horseback from Red Dog to the more ambitious iniquities of Sacramento. The country lay bare beneath a white sun. Every stone had established an independent life of heat; the dust ascended at the least provocation and attended one like a servile ghost; even the lizards moved with the embarrassed haste of creatures detected out of doors after the world had been condemned as uninhabitable.

But from the mouth of the ravine came a slender current of cool air smelling of fern, damp earth, and invisible water. It crossed the road, laid one soft finger on my face, and passed on without explanation.

Being at that time younger than I am now, and consequently more certain that curiosity is a form of intelligence, I drew rein.

A little beyond the entrance stood a building composed chiefly of warped boards, whisky advertisements, and architectural despair. Above the door a painted sign announced:

**WHISKY JO’S  
LIQUOR, FEED & JUSTICE  
NO CREDIT EXCEPT TO THE DEAD**

A man sat on the veranda with his chair tilted backward, his boots on a barrel, and a bottle standing between his knees. He had a corded face, as if the muscles had been tied in hard knots under the skin, and his beard grew in separate gray rebellions upon it. One shoulder was higher than the other. His right arm, lying along his thigh, appeared gnarled rather than formed, and the hand at its extremity resembled a bunch of prison-keys.

“You lookin’ at my gulch?” he asked.

I admitted the offense.

“Then quit it.”

His tone was not loud, but it had the directness of an ax descending through pine.

I turned in the saddle and regarded him with what I intended for calm civility. As I had never possessed calm civility in appreciable quantity, the performance may have lacked conviction.

“I was admiring its shade,” I said.

“Shade’s mine.”

“The wind appears to have overlooked your title.”

He removed his boots from the barrel, permitting the chair to fall upon its four legs. The concussion agitated the bottle.

“You one o’ them gov’ment survey fellers?”

“No.”

“Preacher?”

“No.”

“Newspaper?”

“Not necessarily.”

He reflected upon these denials, drinking while he did so. Each swallow appeared to lubricate some interior machinery of suspicion.

“Then you kin come in,” he said. “A man that ain’t got no business is welcome. Business ruins a place.”

I tied my horse and entered.

The room was small, hot, and abundantly supplied with odors. Behind the bar were bottles of varying shapes, all apparently containing the same brown fluid and the same drowned fly. A cracked mirror reflected the proprietor with so much disapproval that I wondered he tolerated it. Upon the walls hung a rusty rifle, a political broadside, a colored print of the Crucifixion, and a notice offering a reward for a missing mule. The arrangement implied that salvation, government, and livestock were departments of a single unsuccessful enterprise.

Jo poured whisky into two tumblers. Mine he placed before me; his own he emptied as if extinguishing a fire.

“You ast about the gulch,” he said.

“I did not.”

“Well, don’t ast ag’in.”

“I shall try to resist.”

“There’s nothin’ there.”

“That is evident.”

“Cabin, maybe.”

“Ah.”

“Never finished.”

“Indeed.”

“Chinaman wouldn’t finish it.”

Here he bent forward. His stoop had something bestial in it—not the crouch of an animal fearing attack, but the gathering of one preparing to spring and tear. His eyes were pale and uncertain, yet the uncertainty was not mildness; it was the oscillation of a weapon not securely aimed.

“Name was Ah Wee,” he continued. “I hired him seven year ago. Cookin’, washin’, cuttin’ timber. I was settin’ up a cabin in the gulch, where it’s cool. Intended to marry some day. Never found no woman good enough.”

This declaration was offered with the chastity of a wolf explaining its vegetarian principles.

“Ah Wee cut the timber wrong. Always wrong. Trees fell crossways. You ever know a Chink to drop a tree where it ought to go?”

“I have known white men to miss.”

“White men’s got a constitutional right to miss. That’s what the war was about.”

He filled his glass.

“Ah Wee was little. Mighty little. Wore blue shirt, black pants, hair down the back. All of ’em does. Never said much. Smiled.”

“Was the servant a good cook?”

“Best in the county,” Jo replied, and immediately scowled as if he had been betrayed into testimony for the defense. “Meanin’ by that, poison didn’t work quick.”

“And the cabin?”

“Ah Wee wouldn’t cut the trees square.”

“You said the trees fell crossways.”

“Same thing. Crossways ain’t square, is it?”

I conceded the geometry.

Jo rose and crossed to the wall, taking down the rifle. He did not point it at me; he merely caressed the stock with his knotted hand, which was perhaps more expressive.

“I says, ‘Ah Wee, cut me that cedar.’ Ah Wee smiles. Always smilin’. I says it ag’in. Then he—then Ah Wee—”

He stopped.

“Refused?”

“Refused nothin’. Did it.”

“Wrongly?”

“Course.”

“How?”

“Like they do.”

He replaced the rifle and drank from the bottle, having apparently outgrown the delay of a glass.

“What happened to Ah Wee?”

“Went away.”

“When?”

“After he died.”

The silence which followed was not entirely due to the astonishment of his guest. Outside, a wind traveled over the roof, trying each loose shingle with a furtive hand. Somewhere behind the building an animal coughed. Jo looked at me, looked away, and laughed.

“Joke,” he said. “You educated fellers don’t savvy humor unless it’s printed.”

“You have printed that one rather distinctly.”

“He got justifiably homicided.”

“By whom?”

“Law don’t say.”

“Did the law inquire?”

“Coroner did. Jury too. Good jury. Three Democrats, two patriots, and a deacon. Verdict was, ‘Come to his death by visitation of Providence through an ax in the hands of parties unknown, said parties actin’ in defense of white civilization.’ That cleared everybody.”

“You were present?”

“Had to be. Place was mine.”

“So was the ax?”

He fixed me with a look from which intoxication had withdrawn as abruptly as light from a closed room.

“You talk mighty free.”

“I am merely arranging the facts you have sold me with the whisky.”

For several moments I thought he might strike me. His long arm tightened; the fingers closed and opened. Then some inward calculation, perhaps of my size, perhaps of the bottle’s remaining contents, returned him to hospitality.

“Ah Wee was no account,” he muttered. “Couldn’t cut. Couldn’t talk. Couldn’t be a Christian.”

“Did you attempt to convert him?”

“Sure. Read Scripture every Sunday.”

“To him?”

“To myself. He listened when I cussed him. That ought to’ve led him gradual to the Word.”

Jo sat again, but uneasily. He turned his head toward the rear wall. There was there a small square window, boarded from outside. One board contained a round knothole, black and neat as the bore of a pistol.

“Gulch is full o’ varmints,” he said.

“Four-legged?”

“All kinds.”

He told me then, in fragments repeatedly drowned in whisky, how he had begun his cabin in the clearing above the creek; how Ah Wee carried water, split shakes, cooked rice, and sang in a thin voice after dark; how “the heathen” had been treacherous, obedient, wasteful, industrious, cowardly, fierce, silent, and forever laughing. In Jo’s narration the dead servant changed character with every sentence, yet through all the contradictions ran a curious thread of minute remembrance.

Ah Wee had a scar across the left thumb. Ah Wee disliked thunder. Ah Wee slept with one hand beneath the cheek. Ah Wee could braid willow twigs into baskets. Ah Wee once burned a cake and cried over it. Ah Wee wore a silver ring upon a cord under the shirt.

These details Jo offered with contempt, as one recites the inventory of property unworthy of preservation; but he preserved them.

“Then one day,” he said, “Ah Wee wouldn’t obey. I had the ax. Gopher was there. Gopher’ll lie about it, only he ain’t been seen these six year. Maybe dead. Ought to be. I swung at—at a limb. Ah Wee got in the way.”

“You said earlier the trees were cut wrong.”

“They was.”

“And now you say the blow was meant for a limb.”

“Limb was wrong too.”

“Remarkably perverse vegetation.”

Jo leaned across the table until his breath reached me in warm alcoholic waves.

“You callin’ me liar?”

“No. A liar simplifies. You multiply.”

This reply, which in later years I have considered deficient in prudence, had a strange effect. His face loosened. He stared past me, not with anger but with a terror so naked that for an instant I believed another man had looked out through his features.

Then his gaze shifted to the knothole.

A large black eye was there.

It did not flash into view; it was already present, round, steady, and so close to the aperture that no part of the face surrounding it could be seen. In that small circle of darkness the pupil shone with a dull brown light. It regarded Jo.

He recoiled as a cannon recoils, but without the dignity of useful combustion. His chair went over. One arm struck the table, sending the bottle against the wall, where it shattered and filled the room with the fragrance of wasted conviction. Jo staggered backward, made a sound like a horse checked at the edge of a precipice, and fell at full length, heavy and loose, like slaughtered beef dropped from a hook.

I sprang to the window. The eye was gone.

Running outside, I turned the corner of the building and saw only a slope of yellow weeds descending toward the ravine. They moved in the wind with an innocence too unanimous to be persuasive. No footstep sounded; no figure fled among the trees.

When I returned, Jo had raised himself on one elbow. His face was gray.

“What did you see?” I asked.

“Nothin’.”

“You faint impressively at nothing.”

“Whisky.”

“You blame it?”

“Poisoned.”

“By whom?”

He looked at me.

There are accusations too ridiculous to answer, yet sufficiently murderous to discourage laughter. I placed a coin upon the table and left him sitting amid the broken glass.

I ought, of course, to have resumed my journey. Reason formed its regiments promptly. Drunken men suffer hallucinations; itinerant animals inspect buildings; an eye at a knothole proves merely that an eye is attached to something on the opposite side. I brought up these troops and squadrons in excellent order, supplied them with mental guns of irresistible conclusion, and directed them against the insignificant fortress of curiosity.

Curiosity defeated them bag and baggage.

Within the hour I was riding into Dunfer Ravine.

The path did not so much descend as flow. Trees closed over it. The heat ceased at the first bend, the road at the second, and by the third I had the feeling—not belief, naturally, for I was a rational man—that the ravine had closed its mouth behind me.

A thread of water moved among stones wearing green coats of moss. Ferns leaned from the banks. The sycamores whispered above, but when I stopped to listen they became discreet. Even my horse, whose imagination was ordinarily inferior to that of a fence-post, shortened his step and breathed loudly.

After half a mile the ravine unbosomed itself in a circular clearing.

The unfinished cabin stood there.

Its wall-frames leaned at opposing angles, each apparently relying upon the others for support and receiving none. A roof-tree lay rotting in the grass. Rusted tools were scattered near a stump. Upon the stump were old ax marks, blackened by weather but deep and violent, as if the wood had been punished rather than cut.

Beyond the ruin I found a grave.

It was enclosed by a low border of stones and planted with a wild rose, long untended but alive. At its head stood a cedar board protected by a sloping cap. The inscription had been cut with a knife or nail, and afterward darkened with soot:

**HERE LYES AH WEE  
A CHINAMAN WHICH COULD NOT CHOP STRAIGHT  
AND WAS JUSTIFIABLY HOMICIDED  
BY A WHITE MAN UNKNOWN  
SHE WAS A GOOD EGG  
WHISKY JO DONE THIS**

I read it three times.

The first four lines seemed the official lies of hatred; the fifth, an outbreak of lunacy; the last, a confession—unless “done this” referred only to the memorial. It was precisely the sort of inscription by which ignorance, struggling to conceal a fact, drives it deeper into permanence.

Near the foot of the grave lay a little plaited basket of willow, gray with age. Inside it was the shell of a small bird’s egg.

My horse pulled at the rein.

From somewhere among the timber came the faint sound of an ax striking wood.

One blow.

A pause.

Then two in quick succession.

I mounted with an agility for which reason afterward took full credit, though terror had supplied the legs. As I rode out, the trees appeared to pass beside me in grave procession. I did not look back until the ravine had released me into the murderous sunlight.

Whisky Jo’s establishment was closed. The door stood shut; no smoke rose from the chimney. I called once and received no answer.

That evening, at Red Dog, I was told that Jo Dunfer had been found dead behind his bar. The local physician attributed the event to “a chronic overindulgence complicated by a sudden abstention from life.” The coroner’s jury, maintaining the county’s high forensic tradition, declared that the deceased had perished by the act of God, “the same being accelerated by unknown parties suspected of tampering with the whisky.”

As I had been the last stranger observed in his company, I left Red Dog before breakfast.

---

Four years later I passed that way again.

Time had dealt gently with the country, which is to say it had killed many things and concealed the remains. Whisky Jo’s saloon had collapsed inward. Sage grew in the doorway, and a family of ground squirrels had established beneath the bar a traffic more honest than its predecessor’s.

I dismounted near the ravine and was considering whether mature judgment required me to avoid it when a voice behind me said:

“You’re the p’ison gentleman.”

I turned.

The speaker was a small, raw-boned man in a coat too large for him and trousers too small. His hat had lost its crown and gained a feather. In one hand he carried a dry switch which he used as a walking-stick, sword, pointer, and instrument of government. It neither blossomed nor became a serpent, but in all other respects it served him biblically.

“What gentleman?” I asked.

“The one p’isoned Jo.”

“I did not poison him.”

“That’s what I told ’em. Says I, ‘That feller never p’isoned Jo intentional. He didn’t know Jo’s whisky was alive.’ My name’s Gopher.”

He extended a hand, withdrew it before I could take it, saluted with the switch, and winked at my horse.

“You are Gopher?”

“Off an’ on. When I’m somebody else folks lock up the tools.”

His face was narrow, brown, and mobile. Each emotion occupied it completely and was instantly evicted by the next. His eyes, however, retained a fixed brightness independent of the comedy below them.

“You worked for Jo Dunfer.”

“Worked at him. Man like Jo couldn’t be worked for. Too much motion.”

“Were you in the ravine when Ah Wee died?”

The switch ceased wagging.

Gopher looked toward the dark entrance between the hills.

“Come see where they sleeps,” he said.

“Who?”

“Both.”

He went ahead without waiting, hopping over stones and striking weeds as if punishing them for private offenses. I followed on foot, leading my horse.

The ravine had changed. Fallen branches impeded the path; the stream had abandoned one bank for the other; young trees occupied places that had formerly belonged to light. Yet its air retained the same cool secrecy. The place seemed not older but deeper, as if the years had accumulated in it like water in a well.

At the clearing, decay had nearly completed the cabin. Only one framed wall remained, pierced by the knothole through which I had seen—or imagined—the eye. The grave of Ah Wee was overgrown, its rose tangled across the stones.

Beside it lay another grave.

The second headboard was newer. Its inscription read:

**WHISKY JO DUNFER  
WHITE MAN AND CHRISTIAN  
HE KILLED THE ONLY PERSON HE EVER LOVED  
SO NATURALLY THE COUNTY ELECTED HIM  
TO THE LEGISLATURE  
GOPHER DONE THIS**

I turned to my guide.

“You carved that?”

“With Jo’s knife. Legislature part’s near true. He got the nomination. Death beat him in the general election.”

“Why is he buried here?”

“Asked me.”

“When?”

“Before you p’isoned him.”

“I did not—”

“Set easy. Jo accused everybody. Accused me o’ bein’ dead once, an’ I had trouble provin’ otherwise.”

Gopher sat upon the unfinished sill of the cabin and laid the switch across his knees. For a time he watched the graves. His clownishness fell from him so completely that I began to understand it as a garment worn for warmth.

“Ah Wee was a woman,” he said.

The clearing seemed to contract about the words.

“Jo knew?”

“Not first. Found out. Then knowed too much.”

He lifted his face to the trees.

“Her right name wasn’t Ah Wee, likely. That’s what Jo called her ’cause that’s what she’d answer. Come from down toward Stockton with a work gang. Hair cut long same as the men, clothes same, talk little. But she was a woman. I found out when fever took her. Jo found out by findin’ me find out, an’ near killed me then.

“After that he got religion powerful. Read Bible nights, specially parts where folks gets stoned, burned, or turned into salt. Said it was wicked for him to keep a Chinese woman around. Then he kept her around. Said he hated her. Then built her a cabin. Said he wouldn’t touch her. Then watched any man that looked at her like he was measurin’ him for a grave.”

“Did she love him?”

Gopher’s mouth twisted.

“Women’s got queer appetites. So has buzzards, but nobody writes songs about it.”

He poked the earth with his switch.

“She loved him. Jo was gentle with her when no one seen. I seen, though. I see most things. That’s why folks calls me crazy—it saves ’em explainin’ what I seen.”

The wind entered the clearing, fingered the dead wall, and went out by another way.

Gopher continued:

“Day she died was hot. We was settin’ rafters. Ah Wee cooked dinner, then lay down in the shade. Jo went uphill to fell a pine. I was sharpenin’ wedges. I heard her make a little sound.

“There was a tarantula crawlin’ up her sleeve.”

He demonstrated upon his own arm, moving two fingers slowly beneath the coat.

“I knelt down. Couldn’t slap it—would’ve bit. Couldn’t pull the shirt off fast without wakin’ her scared. So I put one hand on her shoulder and worked the sleeve with the other. Jo come back then.

“He seen me kneelin’ over her. Seen my hand inside the sleeve. Didn’t ask no questions. Questions was never Jo’s ammunition.”

Gopher stood.

The transformation was extraordinary. His back broadened; his jaw thrust forward; the switch became an ax in his hands. He was no longer the small, antic creature from the road but Jo himself, towering in wrath.

“‘You!’ he says.”

The imitation was so exact in spirit that I heard again the saloonkeeper’s voice.

“Jo swung. I ducked.”

Gopher’s switch hissed through the air.

“Ax went where my head had been.”

He stopped with the stick buried in an imaginary body.

“Ah Wee had woke and sat up.”

His hands opened. The actor vanished; the witness remained.

“It took her here,” he said, touching his breast beneath the left collarbone. “Deep.”

The trees had ceased whispering.

“Jo dropped the ax. I got the spider out, but it bit me here.” He showed a puckered scar at the wrist. “I killed it. Fine thing to kill, by then.

“Ah Wee didn’t cry. Blood come out her mouth. Jo got down and tried to hold the cut shut. Big hands—strong enough to bust a lock—couldn’t hold in one little life.

“He says, ‘I didn’t mean you. I didn’t mean you.’

“She smiled. She always smiled at him, even when he cussed. Then she reached up.”

Gopher’s hand rose slowly, with infinite tenderness, and closed upon empty air.

“Got him by the hair and pulled his head down here.”

He bent over his own arm as if it held a dying woman.

“She kissed his forehead. Or maybe only put her face against it. Then she was gone.”

For a while Gopher remained motionless. When he straightened, his features were dry and mocking again, but the mockery had broken at the edges.

“What happened to you after the bite?” I asked.

“Died.”

“You seem to have recovered.”

“Body recovered. Mind didn’t take to the medicine.”

He tapped his temple with the switch.

“Fever three weeks. When I got up, Ah Wee was under dirt and Jo was tellin’ the county how he’d caught a dangerous heathen assaultin’ a white laborer with a ax. Coroner improved it some. Preacher improved it more. Newspaper made Jo a defender of the Pacific Coast. Political committee bought him drinks.

“Jo stood up in meetin’s and said the Chinese was a yellow flood, an’ white men must be rocks. Then he’d come here after dark and lie on that grave.”

Gopher pointed.

“He planted the rose. Brought the little basket. Carved the board. Took him three nights. Every time he cut ‘she,’ he shaved it out and put ‘he.’ Every time he cut ‘he,’ he set down and drank. Ended with both sorts. Truth an’ lie, married proper.”

“And the eye at the knothole?”

Gopher looked at me with sudden amusement.

“That was mine.”

“You were watching Jo?”

“Scarin’ him.”

“Why?”

“Wanted him to say her name right.”

“Did he?”

“No. Fell over.”

“He died shortly afterward.”

“Next mornin’. But not from scare, he told me.”

“From poisoned whisky?”

“Said you poisoned it with conversation.”

That accusation, being more plausible than the former, gave me pause.

Gopher went to the remaining wall and placed his eye against the knothole. From where I stood the aperture became once more a black, living orb.

“I’d come here,” he said, “an’ Jo’d come too. We’d set opposite sides of the grave. Sometimes he talked to her. Sometimes to me. Sometimes couldn’t tell which.

“Night before he died, he says, ‘Gopher, bury me by Ah Wee.’

“I says, ‘County won’t like it.’

“He says, ‘County kin bury itself.’

“I says, ‘What’ll I put on your board?’

“He says, ‘Put white man.’

“I says, ‘Anything else?’

“He says, ‘Put liar.’

“But there weren’t room for all the kinds.”

Gopher emerged from behind the wall and regarded the two graves with satisfaction.

“You have preserved enough,” I said.

“Too much. Preservin’ is how things spoils.”

I asked why Jo had called Ah Wee a good egg.

At that, Gopher laughed—a sharp, cracked sound that startled a bird from the rosebush.

“She kept a hen. One little red hen followed her everywhere. First egg it laid, Ah Wee put it in that basket and carried it to Jo like it was gold. Jo says, ‘You’re a good egg yourself.’

“Then he looked around to see if anybody heard. I heard.”

The escaped bird circled above the clearing and vanished into shadow.

A rational explanation now existed for every circumstance: the servant’s sex, the contradictory epitaph, the eye, Jo’s terror, Gopher’s knowledge. I assembled these facts with satisfaction. The mystery had been reduced, labeled, and stored. Nothing remained except murder, love, madness, hypocrisy, and grief—common matters, all of them, requiring no supernatural agency whatever.

Then Gopher asked quietly:

“Did Jo follow us in?”

I looked toward the path.

No one stood there.

“Of course not.”

“You sure?”

“Entirely.”

“He does sometimes.”

“You said Jo is dead.”

“Don’t prevent a man bein’ troublesome. Never prevented Jo.”

His eyes moved from tree to tree.

A branch cracked beyond the unfinished cabin.

My horse jerked the reins from my hand and backed away, nostrils wide. From the upper slope came a measured sound.

An ax struck wood.

Once.

After a pause, twice.

Gopher’s face went white.

“That ain’t me,” he whispered.

“It is a woodpecker,” I said.

“Woodpecker with a double-bit ax?”

“Or a falling branch.”

“Fallin’ regular.”

“Some person cutting timber.”

“Who?”

The question was unreasonable. I resented it.

We walked rapidly from the clearing. That Gopher went first and I kept close behind was due solely to his superior knowledge of the path. Twice he glanced backward. I did not, having no wish to encourage his delusion by sharing its posture.

At the mouth of the ravine sunlight struck us with the violence of an opened furnace. The ordinary world lay before us—dust, road, ruined saloon, patient horse—and I experienced that disproportionate gratitude with which mankind welcomes its familiar discomforts after a brief acquaintance with stranger ones.

Gopher recovered his spirits immediately.

“Got an ox team yonder,” he said. “Haulin’ shakes.”

I saw the wagon behind the ruin and two oxen grazing beside it.

“What are their names?”

“That one’s Moses. Other’s Jo.”

“You named an ox after Dunfer?”

“No. Named Dunfer after the ox. Ox come first in goodness an’ intelligence.”

He climbed onto the wagon, gathered the reins, and raised his biblical switch.

As I mounted, I asked one final question.

“Gopher, if you are afraid of Jo’s ghost, why do you continue to come here?”

He considered this with unexpected gravity.

“Ah Wee ain’t got nobody else.”

Then his face split into a grin. He brought the switch down upon the nearer ox with a vigorous thump.

“Git up, Jo! You white man an’ Christian—pull!”

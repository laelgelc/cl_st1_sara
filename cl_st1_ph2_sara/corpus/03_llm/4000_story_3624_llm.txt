### Professor Haimberger’s Establishment

Austria, as nearly as I could determine during three months of conscientious travel, is a federation of health resorts connected by trains which move slowly enough to give the invalid ample opportunity to die before reaching any of them.

Every valley has its spring, every spring has its analysis, and every analysis proves that the water contains precisely those minerals which mankind has been lacking since the Flood. The native, being familiar with these waters from infancy, drinks beer. He is not ungrateful; he merely believes that the mineral water ought to be preserved for strangers who have paid for railway tickets.

I was bound for Grünwald, in Bohemia, where Professor Alois Haimberger maintained an establishment for the treatment of impaired appetite. My own appetite had been failing for six months. It had not failed noisily or with theatrical effect; it had simply withdrawn from public life. At breakfast it appeared in a perfunctory manner, glanced at the rolls, and retired. At luncheon it sent regrets. At dinner it sometimes consented to be represented by a small fish and a glass of wine, but the old enthusiasm was gone.

This was serious, because eating had always been one of my dependable gifts. I could not sing, paint, run for office, or understand a railway timetable, but I could eat with judgment, courage and continuity. When that faculty deserted me I felt as a banker must feel upon discovering that he has lost his arithmetic.

A physician in Vienna recommended Professor Haimberger.

“He has a remarkable clinic,” the physician said. “His methods are severe, but his results are permanent.”

“What are his methods?”

“He will explain them.”

A doctor who refuses to explain another doctor’s methods is either loyal or alarmed. In either case the patient is expected to furnish the courage.

I remained in Vienna two days before taking the train to Grünwald, and there encountered a subterranean beer cellar which deserves a chapter, a monument and an annual procession. It lay beneath an unimportant street and was entered by a door so narrow that a prosperous magistrate could pass through it only after making a legal concession. A stair descended into coolness and golden light. At the bottom were long halls with vaulted roofs, copper lamps, oak tables, white-aproned waiters and citizens of such settled contentment that one felt the Austro-Hungarian Empire could never collapse while they remained seated.

The beer arrived in stone mugs, cold and grave and crowned with foam. With it came brown bread, white radishes, smoked sausage, pickled onions, cheese perfumed like a political scandal, and a mustard capable of restoring consciousness to a bronze statue. A military band played somewhere beyond a stone arch. Nobody hurried. Nobody demanded improvement. Men lifted their mugs, contemplated them, drank, and replaced them with the calm of priests performing an old rite.

I mention this cellar because I visited it three times in two days and ate almost nothing. The fact disturbed me more than the medical diagnosis had done. In former years I could have disposed of that sausage with one hand while signalling for reinforcements with the other. Now I nibbled a radish and became fatigued.

The cellar is incidental, but the best portions of travel are usually incidental; the official objects are churches, galleries and palaces, whereas the things remembered are a waiter’s whiskers, a dog asleep in a doorway, and the precise taste of beer in a room thirty feet below the taxes.

On the third morning I took the train to Grünwald.

It travelled through green valleys, around forested hills, across bright rivers, and occasionally forward. At each station it rested with the dignity of a public institution. Peasants arrived, embraced relatives, loaded baskets, unloaded baskets, argued with officials, drank coffee, changed their minds, and departed again, while our locomotive stood breathing heavily as if it had accomplished all this itself.

Grünwald occupied a fold in the mountains. There were white hotels, red roofs, shaded promenades, orchestras in pavilions, shops selling carved bears, and solemn people walking about with graduated drinking glasses. At six in the morning the patients drank water. At seven they walked it off. At eight they ate an extensive breakfast to recover from the walk. At ten they drank more water, and at noon they ate again to prevent the water from weakening them. The arrangement was scientific and had the additional merit of supporting every hotel in town.

Professor Haimberger’s establishment stood above the village among dark fir trees. It was a square stone building with green shutters, broad balconies and the hygienic expression of a prison that has recently been whitewashed.

The professor received me in a large bare office. He was short, broad, bald, bearded and composed. His spectacles were circular, his coat was black, and his hands were clean enough to perform an operation upon the archduke’s conscience.

“You have no appetite,” he said.

“Very little.”

“How many meals?”

“Ordinarily?”

“Nothing is ordinary. How many?”

“Breakfast, luncheon and dinner.”

“Before the illness?”

“For most of my life, breakfast and dinner. Sometimes a bite at midday.”

“And when did you begin regular luncheon?”

“About seven months ago.”

“And the appetite failed six months ago.”

“That is approximately true.”

“It is exactly true. You have been eating one meal more than you required, and now you complain because you do not desire four.”

“Three.”

“You count by plates. I count by burdens.”

He tapped a bell. An attendant entered and brought a printed card. The professor handed it to me.

“There is your diet.”

The card was headed, in Gothic letters, **HAIMBERGER’S RESTORATIVE BILL OF FARE—FIRST DEGREE**. Beneath this announcement appeared the following:

Boiled carp skin with lukewarm vinegar.  
Cold mutton fat with anise.  
Stewed cucumber ends.  
Pickled crow.  
Calf gristle in sour milk.  
Turnip pudding with cod-liver sauce.  
Roasted beetles, when obtainable.  
Compote of prunes and garlic.  
Gray bread soaked in cabbage water.

I read it twice, because a man naturally hopes that horror will improve upon closer acquaintance.

“Which of these am I expected to eat?” I asked.

“Whichever you prefer.”

“I prefer an omelet.”

“It is not there.”

“A chop?”

“Not there.”

“Chicken?”

“Later, perhaps.”

“Professor, that list would embarrass a hyena.”

He took the card and examined it with professional interest.

“The beetles are seasonal,” he said. “You need not depend upon them.”

“I will not depend upon any portion of it. Bring me coffee and rolls.”

“No.”

“Then tea and toast.”

“No.”

“A biscuit.”

“No.”

“An apple.”

“No.”

“What am I to have?”

“Your choice from the card.”

“I choose starvation.”

“Excellent,” he said. “That is our most successful dish.”

He conducted me upstairs to a pleasant room overlooking the valley. It contained a bed, an armchair, a writing desk, a washstand, and a locked cabinet whose key he removed. There was no bell rope. The windows opened upon a drop of thirty feet.

“You will remain here,” he said. “An attendant will come at intervals. You may order anything on the card.”

“And if I order beefsteak?”

“It will not come.”

“If I leave?”

“The door is locked.”

“This is not medicine. It is abduction.”

“Abduction costs more. This is included in the fee.”

He bowed and left.

For the first six hours I was amused. I wrote letters. I described the professor’s menu to friends and improved it with imaginary delicacies—fried umbrella handles, boiled piano keys, and old gloves stuffed with lampblack. At noon an attendant entered carrying a covered dish.

“What is that?”

“Calf gristle, sir.”

“Take it away.”

“It is nourishing.”

“So is the attendant, presumably, but I do not intend to eat him.”

At four I requested coffee. It was refused. At six I asked for bread. It was refused. At seven I demanded to see the professor. He came promptly, which is more than ordinary doctors do when they intend to be useful.

“This has gone far enough,” I said.

“It has scarcely begun.”

“I have eaten nothing since yesterday evening.”

“Good.”

“It is not good. I am weak.”

“You walked across the room when I entered.”

“Must I crawl before I qualify for dinner?”

“You must become hungry.”

“I am hungry now.”

He placed the menu before me.

I looked at it and handed it back.

“You are offended,” he said. “You are not hungry.”

“I know my own stomach.”

“No man does. If men knew their stomachs, half the physicians and nearly all the pastry cooks would be unemployed.”

“I can eat ordinary food.”

“You can swallow it. That is not the same thing.”

He left me.

Night came. The lights of Grünwald glittered below. From the hotel terraces floated music, laughter and, unless my nerves deceived me, the clatter of knives and forks. The mountain air carried fragrances uphill: roasted meat, coffee, tobacco, warm bread. It seemed unreasonable that odors should be allowed to travel freely while I was detained.

I went to bed early and dreamed of the Viennese cellar. In the dream the stone mugs were filled with gravy, and the sausages marched past me in military formation while a waiter demanded my passport. I awoke at two and found myself chewing the corner of the sheet.

In the morning the attendant brought the card.

“Breakfast?” he inquired.

“Coffee, eggs, rolls, honey.”

He indicated the printed list.

“Then nothing.”

He bowed. I disliked the grace with which he accepted my decision.

On the writing desk I discovered several books selected, no doubt, for the moral uplift of patients. There was an account of Arctic exploration, the memoirs of a missionary in a desert, two narratives of shipwreck, and a volume entitled *Notable Famines of Central Europe*. Professor Haimberger’s library possessed unity.

I began the Arctic book. By page thirty the explorers had eaten their dogs. By page fifty they had boiled harness straps. At page seventy-one a lieutenant spent four paragraphs describing half a candle. His language was indecently appetizing. The wax had been rancid, mixed with lint and gnawed by mice, yet he remembered it after twenty years with tears of gratitude.

I threw the book aside and opened a shipwreck narrative. The survivors had lived for eleven days on rainwater, two oranges and the leather cover of a hymnbook. One man had concealed a dried pea. When it was discovered, the entire company discussed whether he ought to be hanged before or after the pea was divided.

I closed that book also.

At noon the attendant appeared with pickled crow.

The bird lay upon a platter, dark, narrow and disapproving. Its legs pointed upward. A wreath of parsley had been placed around it, which seemed less an ornament than an apology.

“Take it away,” I said, but not immediately.

The attendant noticed the interval. Attendants in clinics are trained to detect weakness.

“Very tender, sir.”

“Go.”

“With mustard—”

“Go!”

He went, taking the crow, and I followed it with my eyes.

That afternoon I tried to work, but my thoughts would not remain on any subject that could not be buttered. I began a letter: “My dear Charles, the political condition of Europe—” and then found I had written “Europe should be browned evenly and served with potatoes.”

At thirty hours I could have eaten the gray bread if the cabbage water had been omitted. At thirty-six hours I was prepared to negotiate about the water. At forty hours the stewed cucumber ends lost their criminal aspect. At forty-five I considered the mutton fat and decided that anise, though objectionable in principle, might possess unsuspected virtues.

The professor called.

“Well?” he said.

“I could eat something.”

He gave me the card.

I examined it with attention. It had ceased to be a document of outrage and had become a collection of opportunities.

“The turnip pudding,” I said cautiously. “How much cod-liver sauce?”

“Enough.”

“That is not a measurement.”

“It is the correct measurement.”

“Could the sauce be brought separately?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps the gristle.”

“With sour milk.”

“Yes, I can read.”

“You do not sound hungry.”

“I am not required to sound it. Hunger is not an opera.”

He withdrew the card.

“No food yet.”

I expressed myself in terms unsuitable for a health resort. He listened without resentment.

“Anger is often the last defense of habit,” he said.

“It may also be the first defense of a patient who intends to throw his doctor off a balcony.”

“You are becoming vigorous.”

At forty-eight hours I reread the story of the hymnbook cover and envied the survivors. At fifty-two I drank water and found it had flavor. At fifty-five I caught myself considering whether the leather binding of *Notable Famines* belonged to the library or could be charged to my account.

At fifty-eight hours the attendant brought the prunes and garlic. Steam rose from the bowl. The odor was violent, but it was an odor associated with food, and this distinction now outweighed all others.

“I will take it,” I said.

He shook his head. “The professor must decide.”

“You offered it.”

“I exhibited it.”

“You fiend!”

“It is part of the examination.”

I lunged for the bowl; he retreated with professional agility and locked the door behind him.

At sixty hours Professor Haimberger entered.

He carried the menu.

“What will you eat?”

“All of it.”

“Choose one dish.”

“The crow.”

“With pleasure.”

“And the gristle.”

“One dish.”

“The mutton fat, then. No—the pudding. Bring the pudding. Bring anything that has once been within speaking distance of a kitchen.”

“Would you eat an egg?”

“Shell and all.”

“A young chicken?”

“Feathers optional.”

“Potatoes?”

“Raw, cooked, stolen or inherited.”

He considered me solemnly. “The cure has taken effect.”

He clapped his hands. Two attendants entered carrying a table laid with a white cloth, silver, china and a covered platter. Another followed with bread, butter and coffee. A fourth brought vegetables.

The professor lifted the cover.

There lay a roasted chicken, brown, crisp-skinned and surrounded by its own golden juices.

I looked at him. I looked at the chicken. Emotion obstructed my speech.

“You said—” I began.

“I said you might have chicken later.”

“This is later.”

“It usually is.”

I sat down. He carved.

The first mouthful was a revelation. I had eaten chickens in Paris, Vienna, New York, Rome and in certain American towns where the bird is cooked until it can be used as building material; but no chicken had ever tasted like this one. It possessed tenderness, fragrance, dignity and moral worth. I could have made a speech over it, but I had no spare mouth.

After the chicken came a thick beefsteak, charred outside and red within. There were potatoes browned in butter, peas, hot bread, a salad, cheese and coffee. I ate everything. I did not nibble, inspect, compare or criticize. I ate as an honest furnace receives coal.

The professor watched without surprise.

“More steak?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“More potatoes?”

“Yes.”

“Bread?”

“Yes.”

“Coffee?”

“Yes.”

“Pickled crow?”

“No.”

He raised an eyebrow.

“I am cured,” I said, “not insane.”

When the meal ended I leaned back with the peace of a man who has regained both his appetite and his religion.

“Professor,” I said, “your method is barbarous, unlawful, insulting and magnificent. Where did you discover it?”

“In the Indian Ocean.”

“I had not supposed Bohemian medicine travelled so far.”

“It travelled by accident. I was shipwrecked.”

He drew up a chair.

“Many years ago,” he said, “I sailed from Bombay upon a passenger steamer. We had been at sea three weeks when everyone began complaining of the food. It was good food—meat, fish, soup, vegetables, pudding—but it returned too regularly. At eight came breakfast, at one luncheon, at seven dinner. Between these, tea. The passengers ate because bells rang. They had no appetite, so they blamed the cook.

“One lady said the eggs had no character. A colonel declared the mutton melancholy. A merchant from Glasgow examined every potato as if it had applied for credit. We demanded sauces, relishes, curries, fresh fruit, new breads. The captain changed the menu. The complaints increased.

“Then the ship struck a reef at night. We took to the boats. There were thirty-one people in mine, with water, some raw ham, a tin of biscuits and no cook to abuse.

“The first day the ladies would not touch the ham because it was uncooked. The colonel said salt meat inflamed his gout. The merchant disliked the biscuits. They were damp.

“The second day they ate the biscuits.

“The third day they ate the ham.

“The fourth day they searched the boat for crumbs of both.

“After that we rationed everything: a swallow of water, a shaving of meat. One passenger had stuffed two biscuits into his coat during the confusion. When they were discovered, he explained that he had saved them for his wife. He had no wife aboard. He had no wife anywhere. Hunger had married him.”

“What happened when the food was gone?” I asked.

“We found more food.”

“Where?”

The professor looked at his boots.

“No.”

“Yes. Sailors’ boots are excellent if boiled six hours.”

“They are leather.”

“That is why five hours is insufficient.”

“You ate boots?”

“We softened them in seawater and cut them into strips. The uppers were better than the soles. One must avoid nails. The colonel, who had condemned the melancholy mutton, asked for a second helping of heel.”

“And the ladies?”

“They chewed laces. Refinement survives many things, but not genuine hunger.”

“How long were you adrift?”

“Twelve days. A freighter rescued us. They brought soup, bread and coffee. We ate. We wept. We praised the cook. For two weeks no one criticized anything. Then regular meals resumed, appetite diminished, and one morning I heard the colonel complain that an egg was overdone.

“That egg created my establishment.”

I waited.

“Do you not understand?” he said. “The shipwreck did not damage our appetites. The ship had damaged them; the wreck restored them. We had eaten by the clock until food became labor. Deprivation made it pleasure again.”

“That is simple.”

“All important discoveries are simple after someone else has paid for the shipwreck.”

“But surely a civilized person cannot wait until he wishes to eat footwear.”

“He need not. He must merely wait until bread seems desirable.”

“You recommend fasting?”

“I recommend not eating when one is not hungry.”

“That sounds like fasting.”

“It sounds like common sense. Therefore society requires a foreign professor, a stone building and a substantial fee before it will consider the idea.”

He poured himself coffee.

“Observe the health resorts,” he continued. “At one place the patient drinks sulfur water. At another he bathes in mud. Elsewhere he is wrapped in wet sheets, struck with cold sprays, steamed, rubbed, rolled, electrified and made to walk uphill before breakfast. Each resort advertises a different miracle. Yet the successful ones have one rule in common.”

“What rule?”

“They restrict the table.”

I thought of Grünwald’s hotels and their extensive meals.

“Not here.”

“Those are hotels. They cure loneliness.”

“But the patients eat constantly.”

“Then they will return next year. That is good management.”

He explained that a fashionable clinic might attribute improvement to radium water, pine air, blue clay or a spring possessing seventeen unpronounceable salts; but behind the scenery there was generally a stern woman weighing bread. The water received the testimonial. The kitchen scale did the work.

“A patient drinks three glasses of disgusting water,” he said. “Then he walks six miles because the doctor orders exercise. At breakfast he receives one egg. By noon he is hungry. He eats plain soup and says the water has awakened his stomach. If I gave him the egg and omitted the mineral water, he would distrust me.”

“Because your treatment would look too easy.”

“Exactly. Humanity does not value a cure unless it stains the tongue, empties the purse or requires a railway journey.”

I objected that regular meals were one of the supports of civilization.

“So are regular taxes,” he said. “That does not prove the body wants them.”

“Families must dine at fixed hours.”

“Let the hungry members dine.”

“And the others?”

“Let them talk.”

“A host cannot ask guests whether their stomachs are ready.”

“Then he should not complain when they have no appetite.”

“What of children?”

“Children know. Adults corrupt them. A child runs all morning and comes home hungry. The mother gives him sweets at eleven, luncheon at one, cake at four, dinner at seven, and medicine at eight because he picked at dinner. Then she says he has inherited a delicate constitution from his father.”

“What if a person feels ill?”

“Do not feed him.”

“That is your answer to every question.”

“No. If he breaks his leg, set the leg. But do not serve him pudding merely because the bone is broken.”

He had developed the principle into what he called the Voluntary Interval. Whenever food ceased to give honest pleasure, the patient skipped the next meal. If appetite had not returned, he skipped another. Water was allowed. So were fresh air, repose and ill temper. The interval rarely lasted beyond a day, he said, because most civilized persons had never given hunger a fair chance.

“People fear missing luncheon,” he said, “as if luncheon were a train for heaven.”

“Is there no danger?”

“For the healthy? Less danger than in eating without need. A man feels a cold beginning. His tongue is coated; food tastes dull. What does he do? He eats a large dinner to keep up his strength. Then he sleeps badly and blames the cold. If he left dinner alone, the body could attend to its business without first unpacking roast beef.”

“You prescribe starvation for a cold?”

“I prescribe an evening without roast beef. Language is what frightens patients. Call it ‘starvation’ and they resist; call it ‘metabolic repose’ and they pay thirty crowns.”

I asked whether his grotesque bill of fare was necessary.

“Entirely.”

“Why not simply refuse food?”

“Because then the patient thinks he is hungry from the first hour. The menu supplies a test. So long as he rejects the dishes, his hunger is imaginary. When he seriously desires pickled crow, I know he is ready for chicken.”

“Do you actually cook the crow?”

“We have cooked one crow for eleven years.”

“The same bird?”

“It is economical and commands respect.”

“And the beetles?”

“Wooden.”

“The gristle?”

“Rubber.”

“The prunes and garlic?”

“Genuine. The staff eats them.”

I felt injured.

“You tormented me with theatrical properties.”

“Medicine has always used theatrical properties. Mine are cheaper than marble baths.”

He then returned to my own history. For years, he said, I had lived well upon breakfast and dinner, with perhaps a small midday morsel when travel or sociability required it. Then some respectable acquaintance had persuaded me that luncheon was essential. I had inserted it between two sufficient meals. The new meal had not created new hunger; it had merely divided the old hunger into three feeble obligations.

“You trained yourself not to be hungry,” he said. “Then you travelled to Bohemia to learn hunger.”

“At considerable expense.”

“You would not respect the lesson otherwise.”

“What am I to do when I leave?”

“Return to your former habit. Eat breakfast if you want it. Eat dinner when you want it. At midday, ask your stomach, not your watch.”

“And if the stomach says nothing?”

“Do nothing.”

“That will be difficult in company.”

“Common sense always is. Folly has fixed hours and printed invitations.”

I remained at the establishment three more days. The professor fed me generously, but never until I asked with sincerity. Once, at noon, I requested luncheon from habit. He offered the menu. I withdrew the request. At four the smell of bread from the kitchen became eloquent, and he gave me bread, butter and cheese. I ate them with more pleasure than I had taken in elaborate banquets during the preceding year.

On my last morning I walked down to Grünwald. The orchestra was playing. Patients moved solemnly along the promenade, carrying glasses of mineral water which smelled like hot coins. One pale gentleman pinched his nose, swallowed his dose and immediately rewarded himself with a pastry. A stout lady drank two glasses, complained of fullness, and entered a restaurant to settle her nerves.

The whole machinery of fashionable health was in motion: springs bubbling, attendants measuring, bands performing, doctors consulting, cooks preparing, invalids eating to recover from remedies and taking remedies to recover from eating.

I do not say the waters were useless. They gave people something to believe in, and belief is often strengthening—particularly to the hotel proprietor. But I had learned to distrust any medicine whose principal ingredient could be replaced by patience.

Since then I have followed Haimberger’s rule, though not with saintly consistency. When food loses its invitation, I decline it. When a minor fever or cold appears, I allow a meal or two to pass without ceremony. Appetite returns like an offended friend who only wished to be missed.

I have recommended the method to others. They rarely accept it. One man told me he could not skip dinner because his physician had ordered regular nourishment. Another said fasting made him hungry, and therefore could not be wholesome. A third listened carefully, agreed with every word, and went directly to luncheon.

This is natural. People prefer a cure that requires them to swallow something. Abstaining seems inadequate; it leaves no bottle to display and no directions to misunderstand.

As for Professor Haimberger, his establishment prospered. He eventually enlarged it, installed a marble fountain and published an analysis of the local spring. The water, he confided to me, was ordinary mountain water; but patients demanded a mineral, so he added a Latin name and advised them to walk two miles before drinking it.

“Does the water cure them?” I asked on a later visit.

“No,” he said. “The walk makes them hungry.”

“And what does the fee do?”

“It makes them obedient.”

That was many years ago. I can no longer recall the chemical name of his spring, the altitude of Grünwald, or the number of windows in the clinic. But I remember the roasted chicken. I remember the bread, the potatoes and the coffee. Above all, I remember the solemn moment when pickled crow appeared reasonable.

A man may doubt many doctrines after travelling widely, but not one which has brought him safely from crow to chicken.

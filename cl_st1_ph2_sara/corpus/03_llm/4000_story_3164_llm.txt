There was once a monarch—I shall not say where, for maps are indiscreet things, and the descendants of tyrants are often touchy—whose genius lay wholly in the invention of jokes. He could neither read a treaty without yawning nor hear a petition without sleep; but whisper that a bishop had sat upon a custard, or that an ambassador’s wig might profitably be filled with mice, and His Majesty became at once vigilant, eloquent, and almost youthful.

He was a prodigiously fat king. Whether corpulence produces mirth, or mirth corpulence, is a question upon which physicians have disputed with more zeal than wisdom; it is certain, however, that his seven ministers were scarcely less enormous than himself, and quite as addicted to laughter. They formed, when seated around the council-table, a kind of planetary system of flesh, His Majesty being the central luminary and the others revolving about him with oily smiles.

These seven councillors agreed with the king in everything. If he declared the noon to be midnight, they called for candles. If he accused an innocent man of treason, they praised his discernment. If he kicked a servant down the staircase, they applauded the nimbleness of the descent. They had discovered, as ministers commonly do, that the safest political philosophy consists in finding the sovereign delightful.

Now the king’s court possessed a fool named Hop-Frog.

This was not, I believe, the poor creature’s true name; but courts have always preferred nicknames to names, because a nickname may be made to wound. He was a dwarf, very broad across the chest and shoulders, with arms of extraordinary strength, but legs so twisted that he moved upon the floor by an awkward series of hops and struggles. Hence the appellation. His teeth were large, white, and powerful; his face, when he was silent, seemed carved out of some dark and bitter wood. Yet he could climb ropes, curtains, pillars, and carved screens with the agility of a squirrel. What he lacked upon the earth he recovered in the air.

He had not come willingly to the palace. A victorious general had carried him away from a distant mountainous province and presented him to the king, as one might present a strange bird or a clockwork toy. With him had come Trippetta, a young woman of his own people and stature, but so delicate in figure, so graceful in movement, and so gentle in expression that even the gross ladies of the court could not altogether despise her. She danced at festivals, arranged the masks of noblewomen, and was praised as charming by those who considered charm a variety of property.

Between Hop-Frog and Trippetta there existed that close and silent fidelity which grows among captives. They spoke little together in public. A glance served where words might have betrayed them. When one suffered, the other knew it; when one was summoned, the other watched; and on those rare evenings when the palace forgot them, they sat beneath a narrow eastern window and looked toward the country from which neither expected to return.

The king enjoyed Hop-Frog for three reasons. First, the dwarf’s bodily misfortune amused him. Secondly, his quickness in devising costumes and pageants was useful. Thirdly—and this was the chief delight—wine affected him in a remarkable fashion.

A single cup clouded his eyes and loosened his speech; two made him feverish; three drove him nearly mad. His native people, it was said, drank only water and a thin liquor made from mountain herbs. The king, who regarded aversion as an invitation and pain as an entertainment, therefore lost no opportunity of commanding Hop-Frog to drink.

The great annual masquerade was approaching. It was to take place in the ancient round ballroom, a chamber of marvellous height, paved with black stone and roofed by a dome of dark glass. In the centre hung an iron chandelier, enormous and many-branched, which could be lowered by a chain passing through a pulley above the dome and thence into a little service gallery. Candles ordinarily illuminated the room; but, on the night of the masquerade, they were to be removed, lest the plumes and gauzes of the dancers take fire. Torches in sconces along the walls would provide a dim and theatrical light.

On the afternoon preceding the fête, the king and his seven councillors assembled in the wine-room to determine their disguises. They had consumed a great quantity of Burgundy without producing a single idea—an occurrence less rare in cabinet councils than statesmen will admit.

“Characters!” cried the king. “We require characters—strange characters—terrible characters! Not shepherds, not Romans, not devils. Devils have become vulgar. Something new! Something to frighten the women into fits!”

The seven ministers repeated “characters” at intervals, each as though he had personally invented the word.

Hop-Frog stood near the table. Trippetta waited behind him, carrying a basket of ribbons and bells.

“Well, my philosopher,” said the king, rolling his little eyes toward the dwarf, “what shall we be?”

Hop-Frog bowed.

“I have not yet had time, Sire, to consider.”

“Not had time? The day has been at least twelve hours long! That is time enough for any fool.”

The ministers laughed.

“Wine!” shouted the king. “Wine creates invention. Give him a goblet.”

Trippetta grew pale. Hop-Frog placed both hands upon the edge of the table.

“I beg Your Majesty to excuse me. It disorders my thoughts.”

“Then let them be disordered in an amusing direction.”

A minister filled a golden cup and pushed it across the table. The wine trembled crimsonly at its brim.

“Drink,” said the king.

Hop-Frog lifted the vessel. For a moment his eyes met Trippetta’s. He drank.

“Ah, ha!” roared the monarch. “His face changes already! Another!”

“Sire,” said Trippetta, stepping forward, “have mercy. He is ill from the first cup. We must prepare the costumes, and if his mind—”

The king stared at her, astonished less by her plea than by the fact that she had spoken without permission.

“What—what—what is this?”

She fell upon her knees.

“Only mercy, Sire.”

“Mercy at a masquerade? The girl has chosen the wrong festival.”

He seized another goblet, flung its contents into her face, and, when she raised her hands in terror, struck her across the mouth with the cup. She fell sideways upon the stone floor. One of her tiny silver earrings sprang loose and rang faintly beneath the table.

There followed no laughter.

The ministers looked at the king; the king looked at Hop-Frog.

The dwarf had not moved. His head was bowed so low that his chin rested against his chest. Then, from somewhere in the room, came a hard, rapid, grating sound.

The king glanced toward the shuttered windows.

“What noise was that?”

No one answered.

Again it came—low, sharp, and regular.

“Is there a dog under the table?” asked one councillor.

“A rat behind the panelling,” suggested another.

Hop-Frog raised his head. His eyes, clouded a moment before by wine, now shone with a vivid and terrible clarity. The grating ceased. I have heard that it was made by his teeth; but this detail rests upon the account of a terrified page, and pages, like historians, improve whatever they witness.

“Sire,” said Hop-Frog quietly, “an idea has come to me.”

The king clapped his swollen hands.

“I knew the wine would do it!”

“A most astonishing masquerade, Sire—if Your Majesty and the noble councillors possess courage enough to perform it.”

“Courage?” The king’s cheeks darkened. “Explain yourself.”

“In my country there is a diversion called the Eight Chained Orangutans. It is played only by princes, because common men lack both dignity and daring. The performers appear suddenly among the guests, hairy, blackened, and bound together with a chain. They howl; they struggle; they seize at masks. The ladies believe, for several minutes, that savage animals have escaped from a menagerie. The confusion is prodigious.”

The king leaned forward, delighted.

“Eight orangutans! We are eight exactly.”

“Exactly, Sire.”

“And chained?”

“By the waist, in one continuous chain. Thus no one can easily separate or identify you. Your Majesty may frighten the whole court and afterwards reveal the deception.”

“Excellent! Superb! We shall make the duchess faint. She boasts that nothing startles her.”

The seven ministers laughed with renewed confidence. Trippetta had risen and stood against the wall, wine running from her hair to the torn corner of her mouth. Hop-Frog did not look at her.

“Can the disguise be prepared by midnight?” asked the king.

“By ten,” replied the dwarf. “But the plan must remain secret. No attendants. No torchbearers. I alone must dress you, or the effect will be spoiled.”

“So be it. What materials?”

“Close garments of linen; tar to darken them; flax to imitate coarse hair; and a stout chain.”

At the word “tar,” one of the ministers wrinkled his nose.

“Will it not smell?”

“Like a wild beast,” said Hop-Frog.

The king struck the table in approval.

Preparations began at once. Hop-Frog worked with such composed industry that those who had seen him under the influence of wine declared him miraculously recovered. In a remote chamber beside the ballroom, he fitted the eight men with linen drawers and close shirts. Over these he spread warm tar, black and glistening; then he pressed handfuls of dried flax upon the sticky surface until each noble personage resembled a monstrous, shaggy animal.

They were a hideous company.

Their natural corpulence increased the effect; their heads seemed sunken between bristling shoulders, their fingers protruded like pale claws, and their familiar human faces, smeared and tufted, acquired a brutish stupidity no mask could have improved. Indeed, disguise did less than truth. Each man appeared to have exchanged his ceremonial costume for his proper skin.

Hop-Frog next produced a heavy chain. He wound it first about the king’s waist, padding the iron with cloth so that it would not inconvenience His Majesty too soon. Then he secured the seven ministers in a circle around him. Short lengths connected one waist to the next, while a longer portion trailed behind.

“Why leave so much loose chain?” demanded the king.

“That it may drag upon the floor, Sire. The noise is essential.”

He demonstrated by pulling it across the flagstones. The harsh clang echoed through the chamber.

The king laughed. The others laughed because he did.

Meanwhile Trippetta had been entrusted with the decoration of the ballroom. At her suggestion, all removable lamps were taken away. The great chandelier had already been lowered and stripped of its candles. She ordered it raised again, high into the shadow beneath the dome. Then she arranged the wall torches so that the centre of the room remained obscure while the circumference glowed redly.

No one questioned her. Beauty, in palaces, is often mistaken for obedience.

The guests began to assemble at nine. There were masked astronomers with silver stars upon their robes; queens from imaginary countries; cardinals, wolves, skeletons, peacocks, executioners, and pale ghosts who perspired visibly beneath their sheets. Music rolled from a gallery screened by gilded latticework. The floor turned and flashed with colour. Jewels passed like sparks; feathers nodded; laughter rose into the dome and returned in thin, mocking echoes.

The king had forbidden knowledge of his costume, and so every imposing mask was suspected of concealing him. Courtiers bowed to a stuffed bear, whispered secrets to a Turkish merchant, and applauded the clumsy dancing of a person who proved afterward to be merely a cook. Such are the benefits of monarchy: even in absence it receives homage.

At half past eleven the music stopped.

From behind the western doors arose a roar—not the roar of a lion, which has grandeur, but a confused, chattering bellow, full of rage and idiocy. The doors burst inward. Eight shaggy black figures plunged into the ballroom, linked by a clanging chain.

The effect surpassed expectation.

Women shrieked. Men leaped upon benches. A venerable judge crawled beneath a table with remarkable youthfulness. The orangutans bounded, stumbled, dragged one another forward, and snatched at the cloaks of the nearest guests. Their chain twisted about their legs. Their tarred bodies shone in the torchlight. The king’s laughter, disguised as an animal howl, shook his great stomach and set the flax upon it trembling.

Several guards drew swords, but the doors had been locked from the outside—by whose order no one then knew. No firearms were permitted at the masquerade. As for cutting the chain, the crowd could not approach closely enough, for the supposed beasts rushed wherever an opening appeared.

Hop-Frog alone moved without confusion.

He wore his usual motley, but without cap or bells. Springing from the arm of a chair to the back of another, he passed above the frightened guests with astonishing speed. His crooked legs, useless upon a level floor, now served him as hooked springs. He called for calm, but his thin voice only increased the uproar.

“The chain!” he cried. “Seize the chain! Fasten them until we learn whether they are men or beasts!”

At that instant the eight figures had reached the centre of the ballroom, directly beneath the chandelier. Hop-Frog darted down, caught the loose end of their chain, and with a movement so swift that it appeared accidental, attached it to the great iron hook hanging from the chandelier cable.

“Now!” he shouted.

Somewhere above, behind the darkness of the dome, a mechanism groaned.

The chain tightened.

The king’s laughter broke off. The eight orangutans were pulled together shoulder to shoulder; then, before they understood their position, the cable jerked upward and lifted them from the floor.

They kicked, spun, and collided. The circle of chained bodies rose ten feet—fifteen—twenty—until it hung midway between floor and dome. The guests, discovering that the creatures could no longer reach them, crept from tables and alcoves. Fear became astonishment; astonishment, amusement.

A nobleman began to laugh.

Others followed.

There is nothing a court enjoys more than the humiliation of those whom it supposes powerless, unless it be the humiliation of those whom it does not yet recognize.

The suspended men shouted commands, but their cries were tangled with one another and distorted by panic. Their flaxen coverings shook; their chain turned slowly; their eight faces appeared and disappeared in succession.

“Lower us!” bellowed the king.

The crowd applauded, imagining this part of the performance.

“What splendid animals!”

“How naturally they speak!”

“That large one sounds like His Majesty!”

Hop-Frog had climbed the chandelier chain. Hand over hand he went, lightly and rapidly, until he reached the level of the captives. He braced his feet against a metal branch and looked into their faces.

The king recognized him.

“Fool!” he gasped. “End this jest.”

Hop-Frog turned his head thoughtfully, as though considering the request.

“Jest, Sire?”

“Lower us at once, and you shall be forgiven.”

“Forgiven?”

The dwarf repeated the word so softly that only those nearest heard it. He then descended several feet, called for a torch, and announced that he would identify the masqueraders.

A servant held up one of the flaming brands. Hop-Frog seized it, caught the chain again, and climbed. The ballroom had grown strangely still. Even the musicians peered through their gilded lattice. The only sounds were the creaking pulley, the clink of iron, and the laboured breathing of the eight men.

Hop-Frog raised the torch near the king’s face.

“Let me see,” he said. “These creatures are very dark. I must examine them closely.”

The king recoiled, and the whole suspended cluster swayed. A smell of heated tar descended into the room.

“Keep that flame away!” cried a minister.

“Ah!” said Hop-Frog. “Then perhaps they are not orangutans.”

He moved the torch from one face to the next. His expression was calm—so calm that many in the crowd, understanding at last that something had gone awry, found themselves unable either to speak or move.

“I begin to know them,” he continued. “This one, with the swollen eyes, resembles a councillor who laughs when prisoners are whipped. That one has the mouth of a minister who signs death warrants before breakfast. Here is another who praises whatever cruelty saves him trouble. And here—and here—and here—are their brothers in wisdom.”

The chain rotated.

The king came again before him.

“And this largest beast,” said Hop-Frog, “has the face of a monarch who forces wine upon the helpless and strikes women for asking mercy.”

“Take him!” roared the king. “Shoot him! Cut him down!”

No one obeyed. There was no weapon; there was no ladder; there was, above all, no certainty that approaching the hanging group would not bring disaster upon the rescuer. Courtiers are wonderfully courageous at a proper distance from danger.

The king struggled until the chain bit through its padding and into his flesh.

“I will tear you apart!”

Hop-Frog smiled.

Then his teeth grated.

The sound was louder now, and every person in the ballroom heard it.

The dwarf touched the torch to the flax upon the king’s breast.

Fire ran over him as though it had waited there from the beginning.

In one vivid instant the disguise became a sheet of orange flame. The blaze leaped across the tarred chain to the next minister, then the next. Flax ignited with a dry, eager hiss. The eight men, bound too closely to separate, became a revolving crown of fire.

Their cries cannot profitably be described. At first they were articulate—commands, promises, prayers, names of saints and guards and mothers. Then they merged into one vast shriek, a sound so acute that several windows cracked and a lady near the musicians’ gallery fell dead, though whether from terror or a weak heart I cannot say.

The flames climbed. Tar melted in black drops that burned as they fell upon the stone. The chain grew red. Hands beat against blazing faces; feet kicked at emptiness; bodies crushed together to escape a fire that was everywhere. The odour of scorched flax thickened into something heavier, sweeter, and abominable.

The crowd surged toward the locked doors.

Hop-Frog remained upon the chandelier branch, outside the chief reach of the flame. In the red light his small figure cast a gigantic shadow across the dome. He no longer resembled a toy, nor a pet, nor even a man as the court had understood men. He seemed the black master of some infernal ceremony, the sole priest before an altar made of kings.

He raised his voice above the tumult.

“Know them now! They are the king and his seven privy-councillors—a king who never spared the weak, and councillors who laughed when he tormented them. As for me, I am Hop-Frog, his fool. This is my last jest!”

With that he flung away the torch.

It wheeled downward, scattering sparks, and went out upon the black pavement.

By now the chain had been drawn higher. Whether Trippetta operated the pulley from the service gallery, or whether some counterweight had been arranged beforehand, was never established; but the burning group rose slowly toward the glass dome. Their motions weakened. Their shrieks became hoarse bubbling noises. One minister hung backward with his spine bent across the chain; another’s head had fallen upon the king’s shoulder, where it burned like a lamp. The eight bodies fused in flame until no guest could distinguish sovereign from servant.

Thus, at least, equality entered that cabinet council.

A section of the dome opened. It was a narrow skylight used by workmen who repaired the roof. Against the night appeared Trippetta’s face, pale and still, a rope coiled over one shoulder.

Hop-Frog climbed.

His hands moved rapidly above the blaze. He passed through the skylight and vanished. Trippetta vanished after him. The panel closed, and almost at the same moment the pulley ceased.

For several minutes the dead men remained suspended over the ballroom, swaying gently in the heated air. The fire diminished from roaring gold to crimson patches, from crimson to dull blue, and at last to little crawling tongues along the chain. Below, the masqueraders stood crushed against the walls in their silks, feathers, masks, crowns, bones, snouts, and angel-wings. No one laughed. No one even removed a disguise.

When the doors were finally broken open, guards searched the palace, the galleries, the roof, and the gardens. They found a length of rope trailing down the outer wall, two small footprints in the mud beside the eastern gate, and, farther on, the marks of a waiting horse or mule. Beyond the river all trace ended.

Some said that the fugitives returned to their mountains. Others maintained that they perished in the marshes, were taken by robbers, or boarded a merchant vessel before dawn. A diplomat later claimed to have seen, in a northern city, a graceful little dancer accompanied by a dark, broad-shouldered man who walked painfully but climbed the rigging of ships for amusement. Diplomats, however, see many things—especially when paid to see them.

Hop-Frog and Trippetta were never brought back.

The ballroom remained closed for years. Soot stained the dome in the shape of eight outstretched bodies, and no washing could remove it. The iron chandelier was melted down, though the workmen refused to touch the central chain until a priest had blessed it. As for the court, it soon acquired another king, seven other ministers, and eventually another fool; institutions have stronger stomachs than men.

Yet practical joking declined in that kingdom.

Even generations later, when some corpulent nobleman ordered wine forced upon a servant, or raised his hand against a captive, he was apt to glance upward—at the rafters, the chandelier, the dark machinery of the ceiling—and discover that his appetite for laughter had suddenly departed.

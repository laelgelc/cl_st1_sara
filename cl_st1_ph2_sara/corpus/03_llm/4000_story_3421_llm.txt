### THE WHITE HANDS

It is now nearly twelve years since I first knew Jacob Settle, and though in that time many faces have passed before me—faces of the prosperous and the learned, of men whose names are spoken with respect in public places—there is none which returns to me so often as his: rugged, sorrowful, and patient, with deep-set eyes that seemed always to be regarding some spectacle hidden from the rest of mankind.

He was employed at the stone-works beyond Bracken Moor, where the grey road dips between the heather and the abandoned quarry. What station he held there I never rightly understood. At one time he drove a cart, at another he tended the engine, and again I saw him labouring knee-deep in the clay beside the commonest navvies. He was a man of education, as his speech soon betrayed, yet he wore the coarse jacket and iron-shod boots of a day-labourer, and received, so far as I could learn, no more than a labourer’s wage.

The men called him “Master,” half in mockery and half in respect. He neither encouraged nor resented the title.

My acquaintance with him began through an act so trifling that another man might have forgotten it within the hour. Returning late one winter evening from a patient on the farther side of the moor, I discovered that the buckle of my saddle had broken. The rain was driving fiercely, my mare had gone lame, and the nearest village lay three miles behind me. While I stood beneath a bent thorn-tree, considering whether I should lead the animal home, a lantern appeared upon the road, advancing with a slow, swinging motion.

The bearer was Jacob.

He listened to my difficulty, set down his lantern, and without wasting a word removed the sound strap from a bundle which he himself carried. With this he repaired my harness. Then, observing the mare’s condition, he insisted upon accompanying me on foot for the remaining four miles.

“But your road lies eastward,” I protested.

“All roads lie much alike to me, sir,” said he.

He spoke quietly, yet something in the words caused me to look at him. The lantern-light showed his pale cheek and the rain streaming from the brim of his cap.

“You will be drenched.”

“I am accustomed to it.”

“And your bundle?”

“There is nothing there that will spoil.”

He walked beside the mare until we reached my gate, refusing both payment and shelter. I invited him into the kitchen for food and a dry coat, but he drew back as though I had asked him to cross the threshold of a prison.

“No, sir. I thank you.”

“At least take something hot.”

“No.”

There was a violence in that second refusal which surprised me. He seemed himself aware of it, for he bent his head and added more gently:

“I do not enter houses when I can avoid it. They are too full of voices after the doors are shut.”

With this singular explanation he bade me good-night and disappeared into the rain.

Afterwards I encountered him often. His dwelling was a turf-roofed cottage at the edge of Bracken Moor, so solitary that on misty evenings it appeared less a human habitation than a black stone thrust out of the waste. He had neither wife nor child, and no relative ever visited him. The cottage contained a bed, a table, two chairs, a shelf of books, and a small iron crucifix which hung above the hearth. There were no ornaments, no keepsakes, no evidence of vanity or comfort. Yet everything was scrupulously clean.

His reserve might easily have been mistaken for misanthropy, had not his conduct contradicted it. He mended the toys of the workmen’s children, though he would not stay to watch them play. He carried soup to an old widow during her illness, leaving the basin at her door before sunrise. Once, when a boy was dismissed for breaking a machine-guard, Jacob surrendered a month’s wages to meet the cost, but denied the act when questioned. He could endure insult without resentment, hunger without complaint, and hardship without apparent consciousness; but gratitude distressed him, praise made him almost angry, and the smallest affection drove him into silence.

There was in all this not humility alone, but punishment. He denied himself fellowship as another man might deny himself wine or meat. His solitude was no refuge. It was a sentence.

By degrees I obtained some portion of his confidence. Books made the first bridge between us. Finding that he had read beyond the measure of his station, I lent him certain volumes of history and divinity, and he returned them with remarks written upon scraps of brown paper in a hand both delicate and scholarly. He never entered my house to collect them. I must meet him at the gate or carry them to the moor.

One October evening, when the sun had gone down in a red bar behind the quarry, I found him seated beside his cold hearth with his head resting in his hands. A book lay open before him, but the dust upon the page showed that he had not touched it for many days.

“You are ill, Jacob,” said I.

“No, sir.”

“You have the look of a man who has not slept.”

At that he raised his face, and I saw such fear in it that my easy words died upon my lips.

“I sleep,” he answered. “Would God I did not.”

For a moment he appeared on the point of speaking further; then his habitual restraint returned, and he asked me some indifferent question concerning the road. I did not press him, but from that day I watched him more closely.

The autumn deepened. The heather blackened, pools gathered in the cart-ruts, and the wind travelled over the moor with that melancholy cry which resembles no sound so much as a human voice heard at an immeasurable distance. Jacob grew thinner. His hands shook at their work, and there were occasions when he stopped suddenly and stared before him as though the broad daylight had opened to reveal some dreadful vision.

At last there came the night of which I must speak with care, setting down neither more nor less than I witnessed. Whether Jacob’s terror sprang from a visitation of the spirit, from the scourging of conscience, or from that fever of the brain in which memories assume the substance of present things, I shall not presume to determine. There be regions where medicine may follow a man, and regions where it must leave him to his God.

I had been detained late at the works by an accident to one of the masons. It was near midnight when I crossed the moor. The wind had driven away the rain, and a broken moon flew among racing masses of cloud. As I approached Jacob’s cottage I heard a cry.

It was not loud. Yet it had in it such extremity of fear that I stopped upon the path.

A light burned within. I knocked. There was no answer.

“Jacob!”

Something fell heavily against the door. Then came a voice, hoarse and scarcely human:

“Keep back! The gate—the gate is shut!”

I lifted the latch and entered.

Jacob stood in the middle of the room, barefooted and dressed only in his shirt and trousers. His eyes were open, but they did not see me. Sweat poured from his face. Both hands were stretched before him, the fingers spread, while he stared at them with an expression of loathing.

“Blood,” he whispered. “Again! Even there!”

I caught him by the shoulders.

“Wake, man! It is I.”

He struggled with astonishing strength.

“Do not let them strike! One moment—only one! I will go back. I ask no place among them. Only let me go back!”

“Jacob!”

Recognition entered his eyes. His resistance ceased, and he collapsed upon the chair.

For several minutes he could neither speak nor control the trembling of his limbs. I kindled the dead fire, wrapped a blanket about him, and forced him to drink water. When I proposed fetching assistance, he seized my sleeve.

“No one else.”

“You should not be alone.”

“I have always been alone.”

“Then for once you shall not be. I will remain until morning.”

He looked towards the window. Beyond it the moon shone between torn clouds, whitening the lonely stones.

“It will come again if I sleep.”

“Then we shall sit awake together.”

He made no answer, but the grip upon my sleeve relaxed. I drew the second chair near the hearth. Thus we sat, listening to the wind and to the soft settling of ashes in the grate.

An hour may have passed before I said:

“This dream has troubled you before.”

“Dream?” He repeated the word as though it were strange to him. “Yes. Men would call it so.”

“And you?”

“I call it judgment.”

“You do yourself a cruelty, Jacob. Whatever shape this fear takes, secrecy has fed it. Tell me what lies behind it.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because you have spoken kindly to me.”

“Is kindness so rare that it must be protected from truth?”

He covered his face.

“If you knew, you would shrink from the chair in which I sit.”

“I am not easily frightened from a friend’s side.”

“Friend!” he cried. “Do not give me that name.”

“I give it knowingly.”

He rose, walked twice across the narrow room, and stopped beneath the crucifix. The firelight moved over his bent head.

“You see before you,” said he, “a murderer.”

I confess that, prepared as I was for some unhappy disclosure, the word struck coldly upon me. He observed my silence and gave a bitter smile.

“There, sir. It begins already.”

“I am listening,” I replied.

“You do not deny that you recoil.”

“I deny nothing until I understand.”

“Understand murder?”

“I would understand the man who committed it.”

He remained motionless for some time. Then he seated himself again, not near me, but upon the wooden box beside the wall. His voice, when he began, was low and formal, like that of one reciting a testimony long composed in solitude.

“I was not always as you know me. My father was a schoolmaster in a village of the western hills, and after his death I took his place. I had learning enough for little children, pride enough for a bishop, and hope enough for a king. I was twenty-four years of age.

“Mabel Gray lived with her aunt in the miller’s house. Her parents were dead, and she possessed neither fortune nor expectation; but she was beautiful, sir—beautiful not as a picture is beautiful, but as the morning is, because all things seemed clearer when she came among them.

“She had a grave manner before strangers. With children she was merry. I have seen her stand beneath the lime-trees with six little ones clinging to her dress, and I have thought—God forgive the foolish happiness of that thought!—that one day our own children might so gather about her.

“She loved me. I do not deceive myself in saying it. We had spoken of marriage. I had begun to save from my poor salary, and she had embroidered linen for the house we meant to possess. There was a blue flower upon the cloth. I remember every stitch.”

His voice failed. He pressed his knuckles against his mouth, then continued.

“That summer a gentleman came to the neighbourhood for the shooting. His name was Adrian Vane. He was the son of a wealthy man, handsome, practised in speech, careless because the world had never denied him anything. He lodged at the hall above the village.

“At first I was not afraid. Why should I fear? Mabel knew him to be above her station, and she knew herself promised to me. But he contrived occasions to meet her. He praised her singing. He brought books. He spoke to her, not as the village people spoke, but of cities and music and the wide life beyond the hills. He made our simple future appear poor and colourless.

“I warned her.

“‘You distrust me,’ she said.

“‘I distrust him.’

“‘Because he is rich?’

“‘Because he has looked at you as a man looks upon a thing he means to take.’

“She wept at that, and I begged her forgiveness. Ah, sir, there are words spoken in honesty which do more evil than lies! My jealousy wounded her pride, and his flattery came afterwards like balm upon the wound.

“For three weeks she avoided me. Then one evening she met me beside the churchyard. She was pale and frightened. I asked what had happened.

“‘Nothing,’ she said.

“But when I took her hand she trembled.

“‘Has Vane spoken to you?’

“She turned away.

“‘He has spoken of marriage,’ she whispered.

“The earth seemed to move beneath me.

“‘And what answer did you give?’

“‘I reminded him that I was promised.’

“‘Reminded him! Was that all?’

“She drew herself up. ‘You have no right to question me so.’

“‘I have every right. You are to be my wife.’

“‘Perhaps I shall be no man’s wife.’

“At that I lost patience. I accused her—of what, I scarcely knew. Vanity, deceit, faithlessness. She listened without reply, but her face hardened with every word. At last she removed the little ring I had given her and laid it upon the churchyard wall.

“‘You think me already dishonoured,’ said she. ‘So be it.’

“She walked away. I did not follow.

“That was my first crime.”

“No,” I interrupted. “It was anger, folly, and wounded love; but call things by their right names.”

He shook his head.

“The seed contains the tree, sir. Had I followed her then—had I said one gentle word—none of the rest might have been.

“She left the village within a fortnight. Her aunt said she had gone to relations in Carlisle. Vane departed soon afterwards. I tried to discover whether they had gone together, but his servants mocked me, and Mabel’s aunt shut her door in my face.

“For six months I heard nothing.

“Then, upon a February afternoon, as I dismissed the children, I saw Vane ride past the school. He was alone. He glanced towards me and smiled.

“That smile burned through reason.

“I followed him to the inn. He had taken a private room and was drinking before the fire. When I entered, he did not trouble to rise.

“‘Ah,’ said he, ‘the pedagogue.’

“I asked him where Mabel was.

“‘Miss Gray? How should I know?’

“‘You took her away.’

“‘Did I?’

“‘You promised to marry her.’

“At that he laughed. I hear it still—not because it was loud, but because it was idle. My whole life, her innocence, her terror, all were less to him than an amusing tale imperfectly remembered.

“‘My dear fellow,’ he said, ‘men in my position do not marry village girls.’

“I went towards him.

“‘Then what did you promise?’

“‘Whatever was necessary.’

“‘Where is she?’

“He poured another glass.

“‘That is no concern of mine.’

“I caught his arm. He struck me across the face with his riding-whip.

“There are moments, sir, when the soul appears to step aside and permit some ancient beast to use the body. I remember the red fire, the overturned glass, the taste of blood upon my lip. I remember his hand reaching towards the bell. Upon the table lay a heavy brass candlestick.

“I took it up.

“I struck him.

“He fell against the hearth. He made a sound—not a cry, only a breath. I struck him again.

“And again.”

The fire had sunk low. Jacob’s face remained in shadow, but his eyes caught the last glow and shone terribly.

“Was he dead?” I asked.

“Yes.”

“You were certain?”

“I put my hand over his heart. There was nothing. Yet even then my rage had not passed. I whispered into his dead ear that he should never laugh at her again.

“Then came fear.

“The inn was almost empty, for the weather was severe. Vane had entered by the courtyard door, where his horse stood under shelter. Behind the building ran an old mill-stream, frozen along the edges and swollen beneath. I wrapped his cloak about him and carried him down the back stair.

“He was not a large man, but the dead are heavier than the living. Twice I nearly fell. At the water I bound stones inside the cloak. His face looked upward in the moonlight. There was surprise upon it, nothing more.

“I pushed him beneath the black current.

“The water closed, and he was gone.

“His horse was found on the road next morning. Men supposed that he had ridden over the hills and been lost in the snow. There was a search, but no body appeared. Spring came. The stream ran clear. Still he did not rise.

“No one suspected me.

“That, sir, was the horror. Had they accused me, seized me, judged me, and hanged me, perhaps justice might have had an end. Instead, children came to my lessons. Neighbours greeted me. On Sundays I stood in church while the minister spoke of Cain, and none looked in my direction.

“I began to think of Mabel. I imagined her rescued from him, set free by the blow I had struck. In my wickedness I even found comfort there. I had killed him, but had I not killed him for her sake?

“Then a letter came.

“It was from an old woman in Glasgow who had nursed Mabel in a lodging-house. Mabel had died three months before—died in childbirth. The child also was dead. She had concealed her condition until concealment was impossible. Vane had abandoned her with a little money and no name she dared use. She had sent twice to me, but her aunt, in pride or malice, had kept the letters.

“The old woman enclosed one of them.

“Mabel asked my forgiveness.

“Mine!”

He rose suddenly and beat his fist against his breast.

“She lay dying in disgrace, and she asked forgiveness of me! I who had accused her; I who had failed her; I who afterwards took a life and called it love! My murder had restored nothing. It had guarded no innocence, saved no child, comforted no broken heart. It was useless—useless as hatred always is!”

“Jacob,” I said, “you were grievously wronged.”

“Was Vane’s sin entered upon my hand? Did his cruelty command me to become cruel? No. I chose.

“I left the school. I wandered from town to town, taking the lowest work. I gave away what I could. I fasted. I prayed. For twenty years I have tried to place one kindness against that blow. But blood is not weighed against bread, nor murder cancelled by a widow’s coal or a child’s toy.

“And then the dream began.”

He turned towards me, and his voice sank to a whisper.

“I stand upon a plain without earth or sky. There is only light—white, endless light. Far off rise gates higher than mountains, fashioned of steel, yet shining like pearl. Thousands move towards them, clothed in white, and I am among them.

“At first I am joyful. Such joy, sir! Every grief has fallen from me. I think Mabel waits beyond those doors. I think that perhaps even I, who have long desired nothing but extinction, may be permitted to see her once and say, ‘I know now. Forgive me.’

“The gates open.

“Within are ranks upon ranks of angels. Their faces are stern, but beautiful, and their wings fill heaven like morning clouds. Each bears a sword whose edge is flame.

“I come to the threshold.

“Then one angel points towards me.

“I look down. My white garment is stained. Drop by drop, the red spreads across it. I raise my hands, and they too are wet—wet and dark with blood.

“The angel cries, ‘There is no passage for the unclean.’

“The gates begin to close.

“I throw myself between them. I beg not for Heaven, but only for one glimpse of Mabel. The angels lift their swords.

“And always, before they strike, I wake.”

He drew a long, shuddering breath.

“Lately I do not wake at once. I see the fire descend.”

I went to him and took those hands of which he spoke. They were hard, scarred, and ingrained with the dust of labour.

“Listen to me, Jacob. I am no priest, and I will not offer you smooth assurances where wiser men might hesitate. You did a dreadful thing. Repentance does not make it otherwise. But despair is not repentance.”

“What hope can there be?”

“The hope that is offered to every man while he can still choose mercy over cruelty.”

“I cannot restore the dead.”

“No. Nor can you honour them by destroying what remains of yourself.”

He looked at me with weary impatience.

“You speak as one who has not seen the gate.”

“And you speak as though a dream were the final sentence of God.”

“It is written upon me.”

“Then let your life write something else.”

He tried to withdraw his hands, but I held them.

“You have hidden yourself here because you believe suffering to be payment. It is not. Pain endured alone benefits no man. Go among people. Serve them openly. Let them love you if they will. Accept the burden of being needed.”

“I am unfit for fellowship.”

“Yet fit to mend every child’s toy in Bracken? Fit to starve for a dismissed boy? You choose kindness and then flee from gratitude as though gratitude were a temptation. That is pride, Jacob, though it wears sackcloth.”

He stared at me.

“Pride?”

“Yes. You declare your sin greater than mercy, your judgment surer than Heaven’s, your dream more authoritative than the whole promise of redemption. Is that humility?”

The wind shook the window. Somewhere outside a loose stone rolled from the wall and struck the frozen ground.

At last Jacob said:

“If the gate opened—if the stain were gone—”

“You would enter?”

“No. I would ask first whether she had forgiven me.”

“And if she had?”

His face altered. For one instant I saw the young schoolmaster beneath the worn labourer, incredulous before a happiness too long denied.

“Then,” he whispered, “I should know that God had.”

The dawn found us still beside the hearth. A pale line appeared over the moor, and the black clouds thinned into grey. When I left, Jacob accompanied me to the path.

“You will come to me this evening?” I asked.

“If I am here.”

“Where else should you be?”

He looked eastward, where the first sunlight rested upon the distant chimneys.

“Among men, perhaps.”

That was the last time I spoke with him.

He failed to appear at the works. His few possessions remained in the cottage, save for his boots, his coat, and the iron crucifix. No letter was found. Inquiries at the neighbouring towns produced nothing. Men said he had gone to sea, enlisted under another name, or thrown himself into one of the quarry pools. I did not believe the last. Our conversation had left him shaken, but not hopeless.

For two years I heard nothing.

Then, by one of those coincidences which the sceptical call chance and the devout call providence, I encountered him again.

I had gone to Glasgow on professional business and was visiting an old colleague, Doctor Marston, at the infirmary. As we crossed the receiving ward, two men were carried in from an accident at the gas-works.

Their appearance was dreadful. Both were discoloured by foul water and chemical vapours; their clothes streamed with black liquor, and the sharp odour of gas and tar filled the corridor.

“What happened?” I asked.

“A rope staging broke inside one of the gasometers,” said Marston. “They fell into the flooded tank.”

“Are they alive?”

“One may be.”

We followed the attendants. The survivor was young, powerfully built, and insensible, but his heart still beat. While the nurses worked over him, a foreman stood near the door, twisting his cap in his hands.

“How long were they below?” Marston asked.

“Near twenty minutes, sir. We couldn’t get the ladder down. The rope had fouled.”

“Neither could swim?”

“The young one could not. The other could, maybe, but it would not have helped him. The tank walls are sheer.”

“How then did this man live?”

The foreman’s face quivered.

“The other held him up.”

“Upon floating timber?”

“No, sir. There was none.”

“Then how?”

“He stood on the bottom.”

I thought I had misunderstood.

“The water was above a man’s head.”

“Aye. He went under and lifted Davie on his shoulders. When Davie slipped, he raised him higher—held him by the legs first, and then by the waist. We could see Davie’s face above the liquor. Not the other. Never the other.”

“For twenty minutes?”

“Near enough.”

“That is impossible.”

“As I am a living man, sir, I saw it. Once his hands went down. Then they came up again under the lad, pushing him towards the air. When we got the ladder across, Davie was caught. The other had no strength left.”

“And the other?” I asked.

The foreman pointed towards the mortuary passage.

“He is dead.”

Some impulse which I could not then explain compelled me to follow the covered stretcher. Perhaps it was the tale of sacrifice; perhaps some recollection, still unformed, had stirred at the description of those uplifted hands.

The mortuary attendant drew back the sheet.

The dead man’s skin was stained a deep, unnatural purple by the gas-liquor. Tar darkened his hair and marked his breast in broad smears like dried blood. His features were swollen, yet the grave patience of the face remained.

I knew him.

“Jacob Settle!”

Marston turned sharply.

“You know this man?”

“Yes,” I said. “I knew him.”

Then I saw his hands.

They lay outside the sheet, palms upward. The long immersion had washed from them every trace of grime and chemical stain. Against the darkened body they shone with an extraordinary whiteness, as though another substance and another light belonged to them. These were the hands that had struck in rage; the hands he had seen dripping before the gate; the hands which, in their last act upon earth, had lifted a fellow-creature out of darkness while he himself stood beneath the black water and surrendered breath, hope, and life.

I do not say that this was a miracle. Let those who require proofs seek them elsewhere. Water will bleach the skin, physicians tell us, and courage may be explained by instinct, and dreams by an accusing brain. All these things may be so.

But I remembered the lonely cottage, the flying clouds, the white robe stained red, the closing gates, and the swords prepared to fall. I remembered also my own words: Let your life write something else.

There before me, in the stern chamber of the dead, the answer seemed written.

They wrapped Jacob in a clean sheet. The young workman lived, and later told how, in the darkness, Jacob had spoken to him from beneath the water.

“Do not struggle,” he had said. “There is air for you above.”

Those were almost his final words.

I stood beside him until the attendants came. Then I bent over those white hands and touched them in farewell.

Whether Mabel waited beyond the gate, whether the angels lowered their swords, whether any mortal service can atone for blood once shed—these are not matters upon which I dare pronounce judgment. Yet I believe that mercy, if it be truly divine, must comprehend not only the single instant in which a man falls, but all the long years in which he strives to rise.

And if there be gates before which every soul shall one day stand, I cannot think they were closed against Jacob Settle.

### THE MAN THAT CAME HOME AFTER HIS FUNERAL  
*An Extract from the Recollections of the Reverend Father Molloy, late of Ballycarrick Parish*

Well, your honour, if ever there was a dacent, quiet, unfortunate man born wid a talent for gettin’ into throuble that was none of his own makin’, it was Jim Sulivan of Knockbrack; an’ if ever there was a woman that could manufacture throuble out of clear spring water, it was Nell Gorman, his wife.

She was the beauty of three parishes, no less. She had black hair that would shame a raven, two cheeks like June roses, an’ eyes so bright a man would walk into a bog-hole lookin’ at them. But, by my sowl, she had a timper that would sour new milk at twenty yards, an’ a hand as ready as a blacksmith’s hammer. There wasn’t a jug, skillet, broom, tongs, or three-legged stool in the house but had been laid across Jim’s head at one time or another.

Jim himself was as mild as a wet Sunday.

“Nell, darlin’,” he’d say, when she’d fling the dish-water at him, “sure, what’s the use iv destroyin’ the furniture?”

“The furniture!” she’d cry. “Is it the furniture you’re pityin’, you idle, drinkin’, good-for-nothin’ bosthoon?”

“I’m not drinkin’,” says Jim.

“Then you ought to be,” says she, “for it might put a bit of spirit into you.”

An’ if he took that advice she’d bate him for comin’ home wid drink on him, so there was no road out of it at all at all.

Now one bitter mornin’ in the tail of January, Jim was sittin’ by the fire atin’ a bowl of stirabout, as peaceable as a saint in a stained-glass windy, when Nell asked him where he’d put the turf-spade.

“It’s behind the doore,” says Jim.

“It is not,” says Nell.

“Well, then, it ought to be,” says Jim.

“An’ is it me you’re contradictin’?” says she.

“Divil a contradiction,” says Jim. “I’m agreein’ wid you complate.”

That was the worst answer he could have given, though, between ourselves, no answer would have been the right one. Nell snatched the saucepan off the fire, an’ the water in it near boilin’, an’ sent a splash of it over Jim’s shoulder.

“Holy Moses!” says Jim, leapin’ up. “Woman, are you boilin’ me alive?”

“I’ll teach you where the turf-spade is!” says Nell, an’ she struck him across the nose wid the wet dish-clout, which had a wooden handle to it for reasons known only to herself.

Jim’s nose rose up that instant as big as a pittaty, an’ as red as the Pope’s best candle.

He stood lookin’ at her, the water steamin’ from his coat an’ the blood droppin’ on his waistcoat.

“Nell,” says he, mighty solemn, “there’s a limit to Christian patience.”

“There is,” says she, “an’ I reached mine the day I married you.”

“Then you may have a holiday from me,” says Jim, takin’ his hat from the peg.

“Go on!” says she. “An’ don’t come back till you’re worth lookin’ at.”

“Faith,” says Jim, “if I wait till you think that, I’ll come back on the Day of Judgment.”

An’ out he went before she could reach the tongs.

He had no plan in his head beyond puttin’ the hill between himself an’ Nell, which is a plan many a married man has followed widout knowin’ where it would land him. The wind was cuttin’ from the north, an’ little hard grains of snow were hoppin’ along the borheen like white fleas. Jim walked on wid his hands under his arms, lamentin’ his nose an’ wonderin’ whether matrimony was rightly called a sacrament or only a long penance wid meals in it.

He hadn’t gone two miles when he met Mick Hanlon’s gossoon ridin’ a grey mare that looked more dead than alive.

“Is that yourself, Jim?” says the lad.

“It was when I left home,” says Jim, “but I’m not certain what Nell made of me.”

“Your uncle Teague is dyin’ beyant at Glenmore,” says the gossoon. “They sent word for you. He’s askin’ for you every five minutes, except when he’s asleep, an’ the people say he won’t last the night.”

“Uncle Teague dyin’!” says Jim. “The Lord stand between him an’ harm! An’ does he still own the fourteen acres by the river?”

“He did this mornin’,” says the lad.

Well, your honour, Jim was fond of his uncle, an’ the fourteen acres did no injury to the affection, so he turned for Glenmore, though it was nine long miles over the mountain. The snow grew thicker, an’ the wind drove it into his eyes, ears, mouth, pockets, an’ every other place it had no business to be. Twice he lost the road, once he fell over a sheep, an’ once the sheep fell over him; but at last, near dusk, he saw Teague’s thatch white under the storm.

He pushed open the doore, expectin’ candles, prayers, an’ the keening of women, but there was Uncle Teague sittin’ up in bed wid a bowl of broth in one hand an’ a leg of a chicken in the other.

“Jim, me boy!” says Teague. “You’re just in time.”

“For the funeral?” says Jim.

“For the dumplin’,” says Teague. “Your aunt has it boilin’.”

“Aren’t you dyin’?” says Jim.

“I was,” says Teague, “but I thought better of it.”

It turned out the ould man had eaten three pickled herrings, half a cabbage, an’ a dish of onions at supper, an’ mistook the consequences for his latter end. He’d sent messengers to every relation that might be mentioned in a will, an’ by the time they arrived he was as hearty as a fox, though mighty willin’ to be pitied.

Jim would have gone home next mornin’, for he knew Nell’s rage cooled in about two days if there was nobody convenient to strike; but through the night the snow fell as if the heavens had been shakin’ feather-beds. It was up to the windy-sills by daylight, over the hedges by dinner, an’ by evenin’ it had covered the road, the ditches, the carts, an’ one small cabin belonging to Widow Driscoll, who didn’t discover till bedtime that she was livin’ underground.

“No man can cross the mountain in that,” says Uncle Teague.

“No married man, maybe,” says Jim, lookin’ toward Knockbrack, “but you don’t know what I left behind me.”

He tried it all the same, but after half a mile he sank to the waist, an’ a drift came over him till only his hat was visible. Two men pulled him out by the legs, an’ Uncle Teague put him to bed wid a hot brick, a quart of punch, an’ strict orders not to be such a fool again.

So Jim stayed in Glenmore one day, then another, then six more besides; an’ there was no message goin’ over the mountain, for the snow had stopped every road in the country.

Now Nell, when Jim first went out, was in no hurry for him to return.

“He’ll be back when he’s hungry,” says she to her servant-girl, Biddy.

“He mostly is,” says Biddy.

The first night passed, an’ Nell ate her supper. The second passed, an’ she ate Jim’s share too, lest it be wasted. But when the third night came an’ there was still no Jim, she began to remember that he had been a good husband in many respects—particularly in the respect that he cut turf, milked the cow, fed the pig, an’ brought in every bucket of water.

“The unfortunate man has destroyed himself,” says Nell.

“Maybe he went to his uncle’s,” says Biddy.

“Don’t contradict me,” says Nell. “I drove him to his death, an’ I’ll never forgive him for disgracin’ me in that manner.”

She sent Biddy an’ two labourin’ men to search the lanes, the wells, the riverbank, an’ the foot of Carrig Rock. They found nothing the first day, but on the second, when the snow eased for an hour, they came upon the body of a strange traveller at the bottom of a stony hollow.

The poor man had slipped from the road in the storm an’ fallen head-first among the rocks. He was about Jim’s height, wore a brown frieze coat much like Jim’s, an’ had brass buttons on his waistcoat. As for his face, there was no judgin’ it, for between the fall, the frost, an’ the stones, it looked like an over-boiled pittaty flattened under a cart-wheel.

“It’s the masther,” says Biddy.

“It might be,” says one man.

“It couldn’t be anybody that wanted recognisin’,” says the other.

They found in his pocket no paper nor token but a broken comb, fourpence, a bit of tobacco, an’ a small medal of Saint Joseph. Jim had owned a comb, smoked tobacco, respected Saint Joseph, an’ was seldom troubled with more than fourpence, so the evidence was complate.

They carried the body to Knockbrack on a door taken from an empty shed.

When Nell saw the brown coat she gave a screech that frightened the hens off the roof.

“Oh, Jim, Jim!” she cried. “Is this how you repay me? Wasn’t I a good wife to you, barrin’ a hasty word now an’ then, an’ maybe a harmless tap when you provoked me?”

“Indeed, you were,” says Biddy, for she wanted her wages on Saturday.

“An’ now I’m left a poor, lonely widow!” Nell continued. “Himself is beyond every throuble, an’ it’s me that has the sufferin’. He’s comfortable in glory, an’ I must look after the cow, the pig, the rent, an’ five acres of wet meadow.”

She flung herself on the corpse an’ wept so strongly they had to lift her off for fear she’d finish breakin’ what the rocks had spared.

The wake was the finest seen in Knockbrack for years. There were candles all around the bed, pipes goin’ in the kitchen, punch on the table, an’ plates of bread, butter, cold bacon, an’ currant cake enough to console the whole barony. Every neighbour came to praise Jim now that no answer was expected from him.

“He was a quiet man,” says one.

“Too quiet,” says another. “I never trusted a man that listened before he spoke.”

“He owed me two shillings,” says Darby Fennell.

“Whisht,” says Nell. “Would you blacken the name of the dead for two shillings?”

“I’d whiten it for half-a-crown,” says Darby, but she ordered him out.

Andy Curtis came to the wake on the second night. He was a broad, red-headed fellow of forty, wid a tidy farm, six cows, a new cart, an’ the courage of a lion where there was no danger at all. He sat beside Nell an’ told her not to give way.

“You’re young yet,” says Andy.

“I’m ruined,” says Nell.

“An’ handsome,” says Andy.

“I’ll never smile again,” says Nell, smilin’ a little to show him what was lost.

“Life must go on,” says Andy.

“It may go where it likes,” says Nell.

At that very moment a piper in the kitchen struck up “The Rakes of Mallow,” an’ the corpse, which had been badly settled on two stools, rolled half over with the vibration. Andy leaped into Nell’s arms, an’ Nell, thinkin’ it was grief that moved him, formed a high opinion of his tender heart.

The stranger was buried next day under Jim Sulivan’s name. Father Molloy said a handsome Mass, though he remarked afterward that he’d seldom buried a man whose widow watched the congregation more closely than the coffin.

Andy began visitin’ Nell before the week was out, to advise her about the cattle. Then he came to examine the roof. Then to inquire after the pig. Then to explain that a lone woman needed protection from designing men, of whom he was fortunately not one.

People talked, as people will when they have their own business finished, an’ Nell said she’d marry Andy merely to silence scandal. Andy said he’d marry Nell out of respect for the dead. Father Molloy said Shrovetide was near its end, an’ if they intended any foolishness they might as well regularize it while the church was open.

So, before Jim had been three weeks underground—or the man they called Jim, which was not the same thing—Nell Gorman became Mrs. Curtis.

There was another fine feast, an’ some of the neighbours complained they had hardly recovered from the wake. The same piper was hired, but on his way home he lost the road in a fresh snow shower an’ was found next mornin’ sittin’ frozen beside a hayrick, holdin’ the pipes under his coat to keep them warm. That made another wake, though not so expensive, for the parish had grown weary of currant cake.

Meanwhile Jim was still at Glenmore, playin’ cards with Uncle Teague, eatin’ his aunt’s bacon, an’ frettin’ for home.

At last the thaw came. The roads turned into rivers, the fields into soup, an’ the mountain path into a long black ribbon of mud. Jim borrowed a stick, put on his brown coat, an’ started for Knockbrack, not knowin’ he was dead, buried, mourned, replaced, an’ discussed from the pulpit as an example of the uncertainty of human life.

It was late at night when he reached his own house. A light showed through the shutters.

“Ah,” says Jim, “she’s waitin’ up for me. That’s affection, though she conceals it well.”

He knocked.

Inside, Nell was sittin’ by the fire darnin’ one of Andy’s stockings, while Andy drank punch in Jim’s chair.

“Who’s there?” says Nell.

“Open the doore,” says Jim. “It’s me.”

The stocking fell from her hand.

“Who?” says she.

“Jim, of course. Who else would be knockin’ at my own doore this hour?”

Andy’s face went the colour of cold stirabout.

“In the name of Heaven,” says Nell, “go back where you came from!”

“I will not,” says Jim. “I’ve walked eleven miles through mud, an’ I’m wet to the skin.”

“You’re dead,” says Nell.

“I’m tired,” says Jim, “which is different.”

“We buried you,” says she.

“Then you buried the wrong man,” says Jim. “Open this doore before I put my foot through it.”

Andy crept to the windy an’ peeped between the shutters. There was Jim in the moonlight, pale from the journey, his coat whitened with the last of the snow, an’ his injured nose still somewhat larger than nature intended.

“It’s him!” Andy whispered. “It’s the sperit of him, complate!”

“I hear you in there, Andy Curtis,” says Jim. “What business have you in my house at midnight?”

At that Andy dropped on his knees.

“God rest your soul, Jim!” says he. “There was no disrespect intended.”

“No disrespect!” cries Jim. “You’re wearin’ my slippers!”

Nell came close to the doore but kept the bolt fast.

“Jim,” says she, in a tremblin’ voice, “if you have any Christian feelin’, you’ll go away quiet. You had a beautiful funeral. There were four candles on each side of you, an’ Father Molloy said words over you that would have saved a Turk.”

“I don’t want words,” says Jim. “I want my supper.”

“You can’t have supper,” says Nell. “Dead men don’t ate.”

“This one does,” says Jim, “an’ if you don’t let me in I’ll make two more dead people before mornin’.”

At that Nell screamed, Andy shouted a prayer, an’ the pair of them pushed the dresser against the doore.

Jim battered at it till the roof shook.

“Open, you murderin’ woman!”

“Listen to him threatenin’ us from the grave!” says Nell. “Oh, the black villain! He had no consideration alive, an’ he’s worse by tin times dead.”

Jim might have broken in, but he was cold, hungry, an’ too weary to fight a dresser. So he cursed them both in a manner that proved he retained command of his faculties, then went to the cowshed, buried himself in straw, an’ fell asleep beside the brindled cow.

At dawn Nell sent Biddy runnin’ for Father Molloy.

She found his reverence at breakfast.

“Father,” says she, “Jim Sulivan’s ghost is in the cowshed, threatenin’ murder.”

“Jim Sulivan is buried,” says the priest, butterin’ his bread.

“He came back last night.”

“Did he, now?”

“He roared, battered the doore, an’ demanded supper.”

“A powerful, earthly sort of ghost,” says Father Molloy.

“He won’t go till he has me an’ Andy dead,” says Biddy, “an’ the mistress says you’re to come at once.”

Father Molloy considered the matter. He was a stout, comfortable man, very brave against sins, heresies, fairies, omens, an’ spirits that kept their distance.

“An affair of this gravity,” says he, “requires preparation.”

“The mistress will pay,” says Biddy.

“How much gravity did she mention?”

“Twelve pounds.”

His reverence put down the bread.

“For twelve pounds,” says he, “I’ll banish the strangest ghost in seven parishes.”

He dressed himself in his stole, took the holy-water vessel, a prayer-book, a candle, a small hand-bell, an’ his blackthorn stick in case theology failed. Two altar boys followed, though one deserted when he heard where they were goin’. By the time they reached Knockbrack half the neighbours had gathered behind them, each anxious to see the ghost exorcised, provided someone else stood nearest.

Nell met the priest at the gate.

“He’s in there, your reverence,” she whispered, pointin’ to the cowshed.

“Has he spoken this mornin’?”

“Not a word.”

“That proves the case is serious,” says Father Molloy.

Andy came from the house holdin’ a turf-cutting spade like a soldier who expected to be excused from battle.

“Would it not be better,” says Andy, “to burn the shed around him?”

“An’ the cow?” says Nell.

“The cow could be blessed afterward,” says Andy.

“Silence,” says the priest. “There’ll be no burnin’ until ordinary spiritual methods have been exhausted.”

He placed the people in a half-circle outside the shed, instructed them not to run unless he did, an’ began the ceremony. First he sprinkled holy water on the doore. Then he rang the bell. Then he said prayers in Latin so grand an’ rapid that the neighbours were greatly comforted, not understandin’ a word.

Inside, Jim slept through the first prayer, turned over at the second, an’ at the third got a splash of holy water through a crack directly in his ear.

He sat up.

“What thievin’ divil is that?” he shouted.

The crowd gave one great moan an’ moved backward six yards.

Father Molloy, though his knees disagreed with him, stood firm.

“Depart, unquiet spirit!” says he.

“Depart yourself,” says Jim. “I’m sleepin’.”

“You have no power here!”

“I own the shed,” says Jim.

“Tell us your name, apparition!”

“You know my name well enough. I paid you four shillings last Easter, an’ you said it was only two.”

At this the neighbours looked at Father Molloy with interest.

“That proves it’s a malicious spirit,” says the priest quickly. “No honest soul would keep accounts after death.”

He rang the bell three times, flung open the shed doore, an’ shouted, “Come forth, if you dare!”

Jim, who had endured about as much as a wet, hungry, wronged husband could, came forth immediately, straw in his hair, mud on his boots, an’ murder in his eye.

Father Molloy took one look, let out a cry that was heard at Glenmore, an’ struck him between the eyebrows with the hand-bell.

Jim went backward over a milk-pail. The brindled cow bellowed. Andy dropped the spade. Nell fainted neatly into Biddy’s arms. The neighbours scattered over the field like oats from a torn sack, an’ Father Molloy cleared the gate without openin’ it.

Jim rose, seized the bell, an’ ran after him.

“Come back, you ould robber!” he roared. “Is that how you welcome a parishioner?”

But when the people saw him limpin’, bleedin’, an’ cursin’ in plain Irish, their fear began to weaken.

“Ghosts don’t limp,” says Darby Fennell.

“They might if struck with a blessed bell,” says another.

“Nor they don’t leave footprints,” says Biddy.

Jim left plenty.

Darby came close enough to poke him with a hazel switch. Jim took the switch from him an’ broke it across his back.

“That’s Jim,” says Darby, rubbin’ himself. “I’d know the hand of him anywhere.”

Then the truth came out by degrees. Jim told of Uncle Teague, the snow, an’ his long delay. Nell told of the body in the brown coat. Biddy described the face that no Christian could identify. A messenger was sent to Glenmore, an’ returned with Uncle Teague’s oath that Jim had been eatin’ him out of house an’ home for three weeks.

Father Molloy was discovered half a mile away behind a stone wall, recoverin’ his authority. When assured that Jim was flesh and blood, he came back slowly.

“I perceived the fact at once,” says he.

“You perceived it after you struck me,” says Jim.

“The blow was diagnostic,” says the priest. “Had you been a spirit, it would have passed through you.”

“It near passed through me as it was,” says Jim.

Nell now began to cry.

“Oh, Jim,” says she, “what a wicked man you are to come home alive after lettin’ me bury you!”

“I never asked you to bury me,” says Jim.

“An’ what’s to become of me?” says Andy. “I married a respectable widow, an’ now she’s turned into another man’s wife under me.”

“That’s your own throuble,” says Jim. “You were always fond of bargains.”

Father Molloy raised his hand.

“This is a delicate canonical matter,” says he. “Nell’s second marriage cannot stand while her first husband is alive.”

“Thanks be to Heaven!” says Andy.

Nell turned on him.

“Don’t be too thankful, you red-headed spalpeen.”

“As for Jim,” continued the priest, “his wife has gone through the form of marryin’ another man, his own funeral has been celebrated, an’ a stranger lies under his name. There has been confusion enough to puzzle a bishop before breakfast.”

“Let Andy keep her,” says Jim generously. “I’ll take the cow, the pig, an’ my Sunday coat, an’ call it settled.”

“You will not,” says Nell. “Would you leave me disgraced before the parish?”

“You left me outside my own doore.”

“You were dead at the time.”

“I was not.”

“How was I to know that when you came at midnight?”

“By askin’ me.”

“I did, an’ you said you were yourself. That’s exactly what a ghost would say.”

Father Molloy declared that peace, propriety, an’ the good name of the parish required Jim and Nell to be married again, just to place the matter beyond dispute.

“Marry her again?” says Jim. “Haven’t I suffered enough from the first ceremony?”

“It’ll be only a short service,” says the priest.

“No,” says Jim.

“I’ll not charge the full fee.”

“How much?”

“Two pounds.”

“You charged twelve for tryin’ to banish me.”

“Spiritual work is dangerous.”

“It was dangerous for me,” says Jim, feelin’ the lump made by the bell.

In the end, as always happens, the priest had his way. Jim and Nell stood before the altar the following Tuesday, Nell in the same blue dress she had worn for Andy, an’ Jim with a bandage around his forehead. When asked if he would take Nell for his lawful wife, Jim looked toward the doore, but Uncle Teague was guardin’ it, so he said he would in a voice like a man consentin’ to transportation.

Andy stood at the back of the chapel, free again but not altogether pleased to see another man married to the woman he had escaped. Father Molloy gave him a penance of three rosaries for marryin’ a widow before her grief had properly cooled.

“Three?” says Andy. “Is that all?”

“Would you prefer twelve?”

“Three is ample, your reverence.”

Afterward there was another feast at Knockbrack, for Nell said she would not be married twice in one month without getting the use of the currant cake. The neighbours came in crowds, delighted with a household that had provided a wake, two weddings, an exorcism, an assault on a resurrected man, an’ enough scandal to last till harvest.

As for the stranger in Jim’s grave, they let him rest there, for he seemed as likely to be Jim Sulivan as anyone else, an’ the parish clerk said changing the stone would involve expense.

Jim an’ Nell lived together afterward much as they had before, only whenever she lifted the tongs he’d say, “Take care, woman, or I’ll die again,” an’ that generally stopped her for the length of a minute.

Andy Curtis never married, though he often said he would if he could find a woman who had been a widow long enough to be certain of it.

An’ Father Molloy kept the twelve pounds, of course, because, as he explained, he had successfully rid the cowshed of the spirit, for Jim never slept there again. He also forbade the neighbours, under pain of severe displeasure, to speak of the business, especially the part concerning the fee.

An’ that, your honour, is why every man, woman, an’ child in Ballycarrick knew the whole story before the week was out.

## The Pepper in the Mist

The morning was so grey that it seemed less like the beginning of a day than the memory of one. Across the flat country the mist lay in long pale bands, blinding the ditches, shortening the roads, and turning every hedge into a wall at the end of the world. One by one the trees appeared as Father Brown walked: first as faint strokes of white upon white, then as charcoal scratches, and finally as black, complicated creatures leaning over the lane as if to ask him where he was going.

Father Brown did not look like a man who knew where he was going. He was short and round and carried a large umbrella, though there was no rain; and his hat, being too broad and too soft, had slipped backward until it made a sort of accidental halo behind his head. Yet there are men who lose their way because they look only at the road, and others who find it because they have noticed everything beside the road. The little priest belonged, in his untidy fashion, to the second sort.

He had said Mass early in the tiny brick chapel at Lower Boulden, and was now walking to Major Putnam’s house, where he had been invited to lunch. The invitation had reached him through Dr Oman, who had said, with the careful carelessness of a man pretending not to care, that the household might be happier for the presence of an outsider.

That phrase had interested Father Brown. People seldom invite outsiders to make a household happier unless the household contains something it does not wish to discuss.

The house rose suddenly out of the whiteness: a square Georgian building of yellow stone, with four chimneys and a black cedar sweeping one side of it like a mourner’s plume. Its windows shone with the warm, unreal brightness of stage windows; they seemed not merely brighter than the morning, but more substantial. The house was more indoors than the world was outdoors.

Father Brown had reached the gate when the stillness was broken.

There came a sharp report from the direction of the house.

It was unmistakably a pistol-shot. Almost at once it was followed by a noise that was much less unmistakable—a violent, strangled succession of small explosions, as if some invisible beast in the shrubbery were trying to sneeze, swear, and suffocate simultaneously.

“Ha—tch! Tch! Ah—tch!”

Then there was the crash of something metallic, a rustling behind the laurels, and silence.

Father Brown stood with one hand on the gate.

“A revolver is certainly nothing to sneeze at,” he remarked to nobody. “But somebody has sneezed at it.”

He entered the garden.

The front door was open. A tall, broad man in a plum-coloured dressing-gown stood in the hall, holding a silver-backed hairbrush like a weapon. His head was bald, shining, and almost spherical; his moustache was grey and martial; his expression combined indignation with hospitality, as if he were prepared either to arrest Father Brown or give him breakfast.

“Who the devil are you?” he demanded.

“Brown,” said the priest. “I was asked to lunch. I may be early.”

The bald man lowered the brush.

“Good heavens! The padre. Come in. You’ve arrived in the middle of a burglary—or a duel—or one of Cray’s damned nightmares made visible. Putnam’s my name.”

He led the priest across the hall into a dining-room much brighter than the morning outside. It had yellow walls, copper pans over the mantelpiece, and a long polished table upon which breakfast had been abandoned in confusion. A young woman in a dark green dress stood by the window. She was not conventionally beautiful, for her face was too grave and her mouth too firm; but sorrow had worked upon her with the austere delicacy with which wind works upon an old statue, taking away softness and leaving significance.

Beside the fireplace stood a lean man in scarlet pyjamas and a black military cloak thrown over them. The combination made him look like a romantic brigand interrupted while dressing. His hair was black and brushed straight back; his narrow face was marked by two deep lines from nose to mouth, and his eyes burned with the peculiar brilliance of a man who has not slept because he fears his dreams will continue after waking.

He held a revolver.

“Here’s Father Brown,” said Putnam. “He heard the shot, no doubt. Everybody in Kent heard the shot.”

“And the other sound,” said Father Brown.

The young woman looked quickly at him.

“What other sound?”

“A sort of sneeze.”

The man in scarlet pyjamas gave a sharp laugh.

“There, Putnam! You heard him. A sneeze.”

“I heard something,” said the Major heartily. “Branches breaking. Echoes. These old walls throw sounds about.”

“They threw that one into the laurel bush,” said Father Brown.

Putnam’s geniality did not disappear. It became larger and therefore less convincing.

The man with the revolver stepped forward.

“I’m Colonel Gerald Cray. At about a quarter past eight I saw a figure crossing the terrace. I called. It ran. I fired over its shoulder—at least I meant to fire over its shoulder. Then it vanished.”

“People often do,” said Putnam. “Especially when they were never there.”

Cray turned on him with such violence that the scarlet sleeves flapped like banners.

“You think I imagined the shot?”

“No, my dear fellow. I’m looking at the hole in my window.”

A round puncture showed in one pane, surrounded by a little white star.

“I suppose,” continued Putnam, “the shot was real enough. But as for the fellow outside—”

“He was carrying something,” Cray interrupted. “Something that flashed.”

“That would be our silver,” said the young woman. “It has gone.”

Her voice was low and exact. Father Brown glanced at her.

“All of it?”

“Not all. Six spoons, two forks, the small sugar-bowl, and the cruet-stand.”

“The cruet-stand?” repeated the priest.

“The silver frame for mustard, pepper, vinegar, and so on.”

“A portable feast,” said Father Brown. “How convenient for a burglar.”

Cray lowered the revolver, but his hand still shook.

“You see the game. You see it beginning again.”

“Gerald,” said the girl, “please.”

“No, Audrey. Father Brown ought to know. Oman brought him here because he thinks priests understand ghosts, or devils, or men who are taken for madmen. I don’t care which.”

Putnam put a heavy hand on Cray’s shoulder. The gesture might have been affectionate; it might also have been the gesture of a keeper restraining a patient.

“My dear boy, nobody says mad.”

“You say delusion. It’s a drawing-room word for the same thing.”

“Now, now.”

“Do stop saying ‘now’ as if time itself were a medicine!”

For an instant Putnam’s eyes hardened. Then the hearty wrinkles returned round them.

Audrey Watson moved away from the window. “Uncle James only means that you are ill.”

“I am not ill.”

“You have not slept.”

“I do not sleep because people try to kill me.”

“That,” said Putnam soothingly, “is exactly the sort of sentence that makes sleep advisable.”

Cray’s face went suddenly white. He looked not at Putnam but at Audrey.

“And that is what you think?”

The girl’s severity trembled, but did not break.

“I think you believe what you say.”

There are answers crueler than denial because they contain charity. Cray bowed and turned away.

At that moment a small, elegant man entered, dressed in grey morning clothes and carrying a black bag. He had a pointed beard, a smooth forehead, and eyes that seemed always to be apologizing for observing too much.

“Dr Oman,” said Putnam. “Here’s your clerical reinforcement.”

Oman shook hands with Father Brown.

“You arrived dramatically.”

“I generally arrive by walking,” said the priest. “The drama belongs to those who arrive by bullets.”

The doctor examined Cray’s wrist, asked three questions, received three impatient answers, and finally looked at the revolver.

“Give that to me.”

“No.”

“Then unload it.”

Cray opened the chamber and shook the remaining cartridges onto the table. Father Brown watched them roll among the breakfast cups. He also watched Dr Oman, who glanced once toward Putnam and then, oddly enough, toward the empty place in the middle of the table where the cruet-stand had stood.

Servants were summoned. Doors were tested. A kitchen-maid had seen no one; a footman had heard the shot while polishing boots; the cook had discovered the plate cupboard open. The side door leading to the garden had been unlatched. There were damp marks on the terrace, but the mist had made the whole earth equally guilty of footprints.

Putnam declared that the police must be sent for.

“Certainly,” said Father Brown. “Though burglars who steal mustard are often desperate men.”

“They took the silver frame,” said Audrey.

“And left the mustard behind?”

“No. The bottles and pots were in it.”

“Then they took a very complete view of property.”

Putnam laughed loudly.

Cray did not laugh. He had gone to the window and was staring into the mist.

“It is not a burglar,” he said. “It is the Brotherhood.”

The word, spoken in that bright breakfast-room among toast-racks and coffee-cups, had the absurd splendour of a black banner hung over a grocer’s shop. Putnam sighed. Audrey closed her eyes. Dr Oman looked at the carpet.

Father Brown buttered a piece of toast.

“What brotherhood?” he asked.

“The Brotherhood of the Blue Cobra.”

Putnam made a noise of protest.

“Let him tell me,” said Father Brown mildly. “A blue cobra may be no more improbable than a silver mustard-pot.”

Cray turned. Fever, fear, and a forlorn hope of being believed moved in his face.

“I was in India five years ago,” he began. “Not in the cities—in the north, beyond Ranjangarh, where the hills rise like walls and the villages have names no Englishman pronounces twice in the same way. There was a temple there. Not a temple in use; not one of their common shrines. It was older. The natives would not approach it after sunset.”

“Gerald,” murmured Audrey.

“I’m only telling facts. You may give them any name you please.

“I went there with young Ashe of the Survey and a Sikh orderly. The place was cut into the cliff, and the entrance was shaped like an open mouth. Inside were pillars covered with hieroglyphics—not writing exactly, but pictures of men twisted into shapes no man could survive. At the end stood a blue stone idol with a serpent round its neck.

“We were warned not to pass behind the idol. Naturally, Ashe wished to pass behind it. He was twenty-three and therefore believed a warning existed chiefly to give direction. We found a narrow stair and, below it, a chamber containing bowls of oil, knives, and a bronze disk marked with a cobra.

“Ashe laughed and took up the disk. Before we left the temple, he cut his hand on nothing.”

“On nothing?” asked Father Brown.

“There was no blade near him. A line opened across the back of his hand as cleanly as if a razor had been drawn over it.”

Putnam raised his eyebrows toward the priest, inviting civilization to share a joke at barbarism’s expense.

Cray continued:

“The Sikh dropped to his knees. He said the Brotherhood had seen us. Three days later Ashe disappeared. They found his horse by the river, but not his body. A week afterward I received a letter. It contained only the image of a blue cobra and three words: ‘The door remembers.’”

“English words?” asked Brown.

“Yes.”

“How obliging.”

Cray frowned. “You think that disproves it?”

“No. I think it proves somebody knew English.”

Putnam chuckled, but Father Brown did not. He was looking at Cray with an attention almost too humble to be called sympathy.

“The first attempt on me,” Cray went on, “came in Bombay. I was shaving. The room was locked. As I lifted the razor, a second blade flashed past my throat and struck the mirror behind me.”

“A second razor?”

“A razor-blade fixed to a strip of spring steel—or something like it. I found the blade, but not the hand that sent it. There was a narrow ventilator over the door.”

“That was investigated,” Putnam said. “No one was found.”

“No one is ever found. That is their art.

“The second attempt came aboard the *Crown of Devon*, three nights out from Aden. I woke unable to breathe. There was a sweet, rotten smell in the cabin, and a blue haze low over the floor. I reached the porthole and broke the glass. The ship’s doctor said a pipe had leaked, though there were no gas-pipes in that part of the ship.”

Dr Oman shifted slightly.

“The third happened here,” said Cray.

Audrey looked up. “Not here.”

“In the park.”

“That ridiculous stick,” Putnam said.

“It was not a stick. I was walking under the beeches at dusk when something came round the trunk of a tree—round it, I tell you—and struck me behind the ear. I saw it afterward. Curved wood, sharpened at one edge, painted with blue scales.”

“A souvenir boomerang,” said Putnam. “I have three in the smoking-room. Any boy might have thrown it.”

“It turned in the air.”

“That is what boomerangs are famous for.”

Cray paced between the window and the table.

“Last month my lamp went out and a cord dropped from the ceiling. Yesterday I found a cobra painted under my pillow. This morning a man enters the house and I fire at him; then your silver vanishes. It is all one thing. One pattern.”

“Perhaps,” said Father Brown, “but patterns can be printed on very different cloth.”

Cray came close to him.

“Do you believe I am mad?”

The little priest looked embarrassed, as if asked to judge a hat.

“I should not have thought so.”

Putnam stared. Audrey gave a slight start.

Cray caught Brown by the sleeve. “Why?”

“Because you want very much to be proved wrong.”

The room became quiet.

Father Brown went on, still in his mild voice. “A madman generally wants the world to fit his vision. You keep offering your vision to the world and begging it to break it. That is not proof of sanity, of course. Sanity is not so simple. But it is proof of suffering.”

Cray let go of his sleeve.

Audrey’s face changed, though only for a second. Putnam crossed to the sideboard and poured himself soda-water.

“This is all very humane,” he said, “but we must consider practical consequences. Audrey is my ward. Her father was my closest friend. She is engaged to Cray, and Cray is in no condition to marry.”

Cray swung round.

“Say what you mean.”

“I mean that the wedding must be postponed.”

“You have postponed it twice.”

“And I shall postpone it again if necessary.”

“You have no right,” said Audrey.

Putnam’s broad face softened as he looked at her. It was a softness that might have seemed paternal to anyone who had not noticed how suddenly it appeared.

“I have every right your father gave me, my dear.”

“My father gave you charge of my fortune, not my future.”

“Unfortunately the two sometimes travel in the same carriage.”

Father Brown was looking at the Major’s hand. It held the glass tightly enough for the knuckles to whiten, though his voice remained hearty.

Dr Oman closed his bag.

“Cray needs food and rest. No more excitement. I’ll return at one.”

He went out through the French window instead of the door. On the terrace he paused, looked back at the table, then at Putnam, and finally walked into the mist. Twice before he vanished he turned his head toward the house.

Father Brown watched him.

“Doctors often look backward,” said Putnam. “They’re afraid their patients may recover when not observed.”

“Or die,” said Brown.

The Major set down his glass.

The police were telephoned for, but Lower Boulden’s constable was away at an agricultural dispute involving a bull, a bicycle, and the public highway. A sergeant promised to arrive before afternoon. Meanwhile, Putnam proposed that everyone dress properly, though he said this with a glance at Cray’s scarlet pyjamas which made proper dress appear a branch of mental medicine.

Cray withdrew. Audrey went upstairs. Putnam disappeared with the footman to inspect locks.

Father Brown wandered into the garden.

The mist was lifting in ragged strips. The terrace ran along the south side of the house, bounded by laurels, a rose-bed, and a gravel path leading to the kitchen yard. Near the broken window lay a small crescent of silver metal. Brown picked it up. It was thin and curved, and one side had been newly brightened by a violent scrape.

He went among the laurels. Several leaves were torn. Beneath them the earth was disturbed, but the disturbance led not toward the wall or the road, but toward the kitchen yard.

There stood three dustbins, round and black, like squat domestic idols to whom civilization offered its daily failures. Father Brown lifted the lid of the first and found cabbage leaves, cinders, and a theological quantity of eggshells. The second contained ashes and broken china. In the third, beneath a newspaper and the outer leaves of a lettuce, something shone.

He drew out the silver sugar-bowl. Under it were the spoons, the forks, and the cruet-stand.

The frame had been wrapped in a napkin. Three glass containers remained in it: vinegar, mustard, and salt. The pepper-pot was missing.

Father Brown held the stand in both hands. There was something faintly comic in his attitude, as if he were elevating a sacred object whose sanctity had surprised even itself.

Behind him a voice said, “You have found it.”

Audrey stood at the kitchen door.

“Yes,” said Brown. “Though not all of it.”

She came nearer. “Why would a thief hide it here?”

“Perhaps he wasn’t a thief.”

“Gerald?”

“No.”

“You answer very quickly.”

“It is easier to answer about Colonel Cray than about the cruet. He had no reason to hide the things he had just fired to recover.”

“Unless the whole thing were an invention.”

“Then he would not have broken the window from inside in quite that fashion. Nor would he have invented a sneeze he never mentioned.”

She looked toward the laurels.

“You really heard it?”

“Several times.”

Audrey pressed her hands together.

“Father, if Gerald is not mad, then someone is doing these things.”

“Yes.”

“And if someone is doing them—”

“Then it may be better to discover who than to debate madness in general. Madness is a large fog. Murder is generally a very small footprint.”

She studied him. “Do you suspect Uncle James?”

Father Brown looked pained.

“I have hardly finished suspecting the dustbin.”

But she did not smile.

“My uncle has always been kind to me,” she said. “That is what makes it difficult.”

“Kindness can be a gift,” said the priest, “or a claim. Some people give you an apple; others give you an apple and thereafter regard your teeth as part of their property.”

She turned pale.

“You have seen it, then.”

“I have seen him look at you.”

Audrey’s firmness broke at last, not into tears but into anger.

“He hates Gerald. He calls it protection. He says Gerald is unstable, theatrical, infected by the East. I have sometimes believed him. That is the worst of it. Gerald grows wilder whenever he is doubted, and the wilder he grows, the more reasonable Uncle James appears.”

“Yes,” said Brown. “It is an old machine. If you push a man toward the edge, his attempts not to fall can look remarkably like dancing.”

A kitchen bell rang within.

Audrey looked at the recovered silver.

“What will you do with that?”

“Wash it, I think.”

“Wash it?”

“It has been in the dustbin.”

He carried the cruet-stand into the kitchen, where the cook received its resurrection with cries of mingled piety and outrage. She plunged the silver into hot water, scrubbed the glass, lamented the missing pepper-pot, and declared that no person with Christian feelings would wrap silver in a table napkin used for fish.

Father Brown asked what had been prepared for lunch.

“Special for the Colonel,” she said. “Major’s orders. Beef broth with rice, then a chicken pie, and a custard. Poor gentleman’s eaten nothing since yesterday.”

“Who ordered the broth?”

“The Major. Dr Oman said nourishing food.”

“Who made it?”

“I did.”

“Has anyone touched it since?”

The cook became stately. “I don’t permit touching.”

Then she remembered.

“Though the Major did bring in one of his little books. Said there was an Indian way of flavouring broth, but I told him I’d have no curry nonsense for an invalid.”

“What little book?”

“From the library. Green cover. He left it on the dresser.”

The book was no longer there.

Father Brown returned to the house by a passage lined with guns, fishing-rods, and tribal weapons. In the smoking-room he found three boomerangs hung over the fireplace. Two were dusty. The third had been recently cleaned.

He found a cabinet containing souvenirs from India: carved boxes, brass gods, painted fans, ceremonial knives, and a blue bronze disk engraved with a cobra. Beside it lay a packet of blue artist’s chalk.

He was examining the disk when Putnam entered.

“Interested in my savage museum?”

“Very,” said Brown. “Especially in how civilized it is.”

“Most of it came from Cray. He gave me the stuff before the fever got into his head.”

“The blue cobra too?”

“The bronze disk? Yes.”

“And the boomerangs?”

“Those are Australian. I collected them myself.”

“One looks exercised.”

Putnam laughed. “My gardener’s boy tries them. Badly.”

“Most people do evil badly,” said the priest. “That is why it is found out.”

The Major’s laughter ceased, but only for the fraction of a second.

“You priests are fond of calling everything evil.”

“Not everything. It would leave us no word for lunch.”

“Speaking of lunch, you will stay?”

“I think I must.”

“Must?”

“Dr Oman asked me here.”

“Oman’s nervous. Good doctor, but fanciful.”

“He looked anxious when he left.”

“Doctors cultivate anxiety. It gives weight to their bills.”

Father Brown picked up the bronze disk.

“Colonel Cray says this was taken from the temple.”

“So he says.”

“Did you ever go to that temple?”

Putnam’s broad shoulders shifted almost imperceptibly.

“I was in the district years later.”

“Did you find a brotherhood?”

“I found flies, fakirs, and an appalling hotel.”

“White men often go abroad to discover darkness,” said Father Brown, “and are disappointed to find it was packed in their own luggage.”

Putnam’s face became red.

“I don’t know what you’re implying.”

“I am only talking about travel.”

“Then talk about it elsewhere.”

Father Brown set down the disk.

“I will talk about mustard instead. We found the silver.”

Putnam stared at him.

“Where?”

“In the dustbin.”

For the first time the hearty mask did not merely slip; it vanished. What appeared beneath was not panic but calculation—a hard, narrow attention astonishing in so broad a face.

Then the mask returned.

“Extraordinary! The thief must have been frightened by Cray’s shot and hidden the stuff, intending to return.”

“Without the pepper-pot.”

“What?”

“The pepper-pot is gone.”

Putnam gave a shrug. “Dropped in the garden, no doubt.”

“No doubt,” repeated Brown.

A bell announced lunch.

The dining-room had been restored to order. The broken pane was covered with cardboard; the silver was back in place; and the cruet-stand stood in the middle of the table, lacking only pepper and looking, for that reason, like a regiment with one soldier dead. Outside, the mist had thinned, and the pale lawn sloped away into a brightness without sunshine.

Colonel Cray entered in a dark suit. Proper clothing had made him look less mad and more miserable. Audrey came behind him, and Putnam took the head of the table.

“You found the plate, I hear,” Cray said to Brown.

“In the dustbin.”

Cray looked at Putnam. “Your Indian burglars have adopted English housekeeping.”

“Let’s not begin again,” said the Major.

Dr Oman had not returned.

The broth was served. Cray’s portion came in a small covered bowl; the others had cold meat and salad.

“Special invalid’s fare,” Putnam said jovially. “Eat it, Gerald. You’ll feel a different man.”

“I have had too many people trying to make me a different man.”

Nevertheless Cray lifted the spoon.

Father Brown reached suddenly for the mustard-pot.

“Mustard in broth?” asked Audrey.

“I am told faith should resemble mustard-seed,” said the priest. “Perhaps appetite can begin in the same way.”

He put a generous spoonful on the edge of his plate but did not eat it. His eyes had gone to the window.

Dr Oman stood outside on the lawn.

He was not approaching. He was staring into the dining-room with a white face. When he saw the cruet-stand, he took one step forward; when he saw Putnam, he stopped.

Then Cray dropped his spoon.

The sound was small, almost polite.

He put one hand to his throat.

“This broth—”

His lips tightened. A grey shade spread upward from his collar. He tried to rise, struck the table, and fell back into the chair, breathing in short, tearing gasps.

Audrey screamed his name.

Putnam sprang up. “Oman! Fetch Oman!”

But Father Brown had already moved.

The change in him was so complete that it seemed less as if the small, shabby priest had become another man than as if another man, severe and swift, had always been folded inside him. He seized the mustard-pot, emptied it into a tumbler, poured in warm water from the covered jug, and stirred the mixture with a knife.

Cray pushed weakly at his hand.

Father Brown caught him by the back of the neck.

“Drink that!”

The command cracked through the room louder than the morning’s pistol.

Cray drank. He choked, fought, swallowed again, and collapsed forward. A moment later the mustard did its ancient and undignified work. Audrey turned away; Putnam cursed; the servants crowded at the door. Father Brown continued supporting Cray with one arm.

Dr Oman burst in through the French window.

“What has he taken?”

“You tell me,” said Brown.

The doctor bent over Cray, examined his eyes, smelled the broth, and looked up in horror.

“Antimony—or something akin to it. We must keep him sick. More water. Send for my bag—it’s outside.”

For several minutes the dining-room became a place where dignity was not merely forgotten but forbidden. Basins, towels, water, shouted orders, and the sour violence of the emetic displaced silver, etiquette, and supernatural terror. Mortal peril is often vulgar in its methods; indeed, death itself has never possessed the good manners attributed to it by poets.

At last Oman straightened.

“He will live, I think.”

Audrey sat beside Cray, holding his hand. Her own face was colourless, but her voice was steady.

“You are sure?”

“As sure as I can be. The dose was dangerous, but little had been absorbed.”

Putnam stood by the mantelpiece.

“Poisoned,” he said. “Then the Brotherhood—”

“No,” said Father Brown.

The Major turned.

“No?”

“No Brotherhood poisoned that soup.”

Cray opened his eyes. “The cook?”

“The cook made it,” said Brown. “Someone else seasoned it.”

Putnam struck the mantelpiece with his palm.

“This is monstrous. Father Brown, if you accuse a member of my household—”

“I accuse the owner of it.”

The silence that followed was so complete that one could hear the faint ticking of the gravy as it cooled in its silver boat.

Putnam’s broad face did not change. “You had better explain.”

“I will,” said Father Brown. “Though explanations are sometimes much less pleasant than mysteries. Mysteries may contain blue idols and secret temples. Explanations contain people we have eaten breakfast with.”

Dr Oman sat down abruptly.

Father Brown looked at him.

“You knew something.”

“I suspected the poison,” said Oman. “Not until this morning. Putnam borrowed a book from my surgery last week—a medical toxicology. He said it was for an article on Indian poisons. Today, when I heard about the missing cruet, I remembered a passage he had marked.”

“About antidotes?”

“About common emetics. Mustard. Salt. Vinegar in some preparations. Things on an ordinary table.”

Audrey stared at the cruet.

“That is why it was stolen.”

“It was never stolen,” said Brown. “It was hidden. The silver was camouflage. If only the mustard and salt disappeared, somebody might ask why. So spoons, forks, sugar-bowl, and the whole silver frame were taken and put in the dustbin. It looked like burglary because it was made to look like burglary.”

Putnam’s voice was quiet now.

“And who, in your theory, hid it?”

“The man Colonel Cray saw crossing the terrace.”

“He fired at a shadow.”

“He fired at you.”

Cray raised his head.

Putnam laughed. It was a hard, brief sound.

“Then why am I not shot?”

“Because the bullet struck what you were carrying.”

Father Brown put the curved scrap of silver on the table.

“This broke from the missing pepper-pot. The bullet hit it, glanced away, and scattered pepper into your face. That was the strange sound outside. Not a ghostly signal. Not an Indian invocation. You sneezed.”

Cray’s eyes widened.

“The flash in his hand—”

“The silver cruet. Or part of it. He dropped the damaged pepper-pot in the shrubbery or carried it away; but he recovered himself quickly enough to hide the rest in the dustbin and come round by the side door. When I entered, he was in the hall with a hairbrush and a story ready to be reasonable.”

Putnam folded his arms.

“Suppose this farce were true. Why should I poison Cray?”

Father Brown looked toward Audrey.

The girl did not move. Her hand tightened around Cray’s.

“Because you love Miss Watson,” said the priest. “Not wisely, not generously, and perhaps not even in the fashion you call love. You have loved her as a lonely man may love the one light in his house; then you began to think the lamp belonged to the house. Colonel Cray came to take it away.

“You could not openly oppose the marriage without exposing yourself. So you made him incredible. You used his Indian fear because it was already there; you fed it until every real danger looked like madness and every protest against danger became proof of madness.”

Putnam’s face had become mottled.

“You can prove none of this.”

“The earlier attacks prove their maker was not an Indian magician but a practical English gentleman with access to this house and knowledge of Cray’s history.”

Father Brown counted on his fingers.

“The razor in Bombay was sent through the ventilator by a spring or a weighted line. You were in Bombay then; the regimental records will show it. The gas aboard ship came through a tube lowered from the ventilator passage. You travelled on the same vessel under another booking—I think Dr Oman can help the police discover it. The curved weapon in the park was a boomerang. One of yours has been recently used, and blue chalk lies beside the cobra disk.

“The painted cobra under the pillow required no brotherhood; it required only entry to a bedroom. The falling cord required only the attic above. As for Ashe’s cut in the temple, perhaps that began the whole idea. A concealed razor, a wire, a trick played by a guide—who knows? But you learned enough of it later to imitate it. Every miracle is mechanical. Every curse has a handle.”

Cray whispered, “Ashe vanished.”

“Yes,” said Brown. “And perhaps he died. One true tragedy can father many false ones. You saw a dark door in India, and Major Putnam has kept walking through it in different disguises.”

Putnam turned on Oman.

“You damned coward! You have been feeding him this.”

“No,” said Oman. His polished voice shook. “I have been refusing to see what was before me. That is a more respectable form of cowardice, and therefore a worse one.”

“You knew about the broth?”

“I knew you had studied poison. I knew you had asked me whether antimony always left a taste. I told myself it was curiosity. When the cruet vanished, I became afraid. I came back to warn them, saw the table through the window—and saw that the cruet had returned. I thought the danger had passed.”

“That,” said Father Brown, “was what Major Putnam had not expected. He hid the condiments because they were remedies close at hand. He did not know I had found them.”

Putnam looked at the mustard-pot as if it had betrayed him personally.

“It was the mustard that saved him,” Audrey said.

“The smallest and commonest thing on the table,” said Brown. “People expecting demons rarely watch the dinner.”

For a moment nobody spoke. Then Putnam gave a long breath and seemed to recover his old manner. He squared his shoulders, adjusted his cuffs, and became once more the bluff soldier and responsible guardian.

“This is too serious for discussion. I shall go directly to the police.”

“No,” said Audrey.

He looked at her.

“You will stay here.”

“My dear child—”

“I am not your child.”

There was no drama in her voice now. Drama had belonged to the time when truth was hidden. With truth uncovered, she spoke simply.

Putnam’s face sagged. He seemed suddenly old, but age did not make him innocent. It merely showed how long the selfishness had lived in him.

Father Brown walked to the French window and looked out. Beyond the glass, the mist had almost gone. The cedar stood black and distinct against a washed blue sky.

Behind him came the sound of the hall door opening.

Dr Oman sprang up. “He’s going!”

Putnam had moved with astonishing speed. By the time they reached the hall, the front door was swinging wide and the Major’s motorcar was already coughing to life in the drive.

“He said he was going to the police,” Audrey said.

“He may pass them,” said Father Brown. “But I doubt if he will stop.”

Oman ran to the telephone. A servant shouted from the yard. The car swept through the gates and vanished along the wet road, not into supernatural mist now, but into the plain daylight of pursuit.

Cray, supported by Audrey, had come as far as the dining-room door. He looked weak, ashamed, and bewildered.

“I was not mad,” he said.

“No,” said Audrey.

“I made you think I was.”

“No. Someone made use of your fear, and I let him.”

He looked at her as if he scarcely dared understand.

“The wedding—”

“Will occur when Dr Oman says you can stand through it.”

Oman, with the telephone receiver at his ear, turned and protested that medicine did not regulate matrimony. Audrey informed him that it did now.

Cray laughed. It was a faint laugh, but the first sound he had made that did not contain fear.

Father Brown returned to the table and began collecting his hat and umbrella. Audrey came to him.

“You are leaving?”

“I have a train.”

“But the police will want your statement.”

“They can have it. Priests are accustomed to making statements about sins, though the police prefer them in a different order.”

She lowered her voice. “You knew he would run.”

“I thought he might. He had spent years running away from what he was doing; another few miles would seem natural.”

“Will they catch him?”

“Probably. A motorcar is faster than a conscience, but it must stop for petrol.”

Audrey looked back at Cray.

“How could Uncle James do all this and still seem so kind?”

Father Brown put on his shapeless hat.

“Because men are not generally divided into kind men and cruel men. A man may be kind to twenty people because they are outside the one locked room in his heart. He may feed dogs, forgive debts, remember birthdays, and murder the person who touches the key. The ordinary virtues are not always false; sometimes they are only fenced.”

She was silent.

“And Gerald?” she asked at last. “Will he ever be free of the fear?”

“Not by being told it was foolish. Fear is seldom argued out of a man. But he may learn that the universe is not conspiring against him. Only one unhappy man was conspiring against him, and one man is smaller than the universe.”

Cray called her name. She went to him.

Father Brown paused beside the cruet-stand. The mustard-pot was empty; salt had been spilled across the cloth; vinegar caught the new sunlight in a narrow golden line. The pepper-pot alone was absent, having performed its ridiculous and decisive part somewhere among the laurels.

Outside, a church bell began to ring across the fields. It was not a warning or a curse, though it sounded strangely solemn after the pistol-shot and the sneeze. It was only the noon bell from Lower Boulden, calling a few old women, two children, and perhaps one sleepy farmer to prayer.

Father Brown picked up his umbrella.

As he crossed the terrace, he saw something glinting beneath a laurel. He stooped and drew out the shattered pepper-pot. One side was flattened by the bullet, and a last pinch of black pepper clung stubbornly inside.

He regarded it with grave satisfaction.

The powers of darkness, he reflected, were often described with too much magnificence. They did not always come with wings, serpents, temples, and words of doom. Sometimes they came in a dressing-gown, spoke excellent common sense, and hid the mustard.

Then he put the broken pepper-pot in his pocket and walked down the brightening road.

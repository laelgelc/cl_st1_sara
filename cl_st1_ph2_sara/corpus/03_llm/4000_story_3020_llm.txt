### THE BORROWED NAME

Martin Stoner had been travelling towards the sea for three days, though he could not have said that he had any business there. The sea was merely the last place left in that direction; and, to a man whom creditors, employers, acquaintances, and his own incapacity had hunted successively from every other quarter, it possessed the attraction of a wall against which the pursuit must stop.

He was thirty-seven, but hunger and an unsuccessful beard made him look older. He had tried many occupations, which is commonly another way of saying that he had persevered in none. He had been clerk to a timber merchant, assistant in a bookseller’s shop, commission agent for an agricultural machine whose chief peculiarity was that it would not work, and, for six ignominious weeks, secretary to a company formed to reclaim tin from the sea. The company had vanished before the tin was recovered. Of all these adventures he retained a shabby valise, a silver watch without its glass, and a conviction that fortune had behaved with marked impropriety.

By the fourth afternoon his money amounted to sevenpence, and his bodily possessions, apart from what he wore, had become inconvenient rather than valuable. Rain had fallen since noon. The lanes were rutted and yellow with mud; moist hedgerows leaned over them, and the dripping trees, already thin of leaf, seemed to draw closer as the light failed. Somewhere beyond the low hills the sea made a colourless confusion with the sky.

Stoner had eaten nothing since morning, when he had bought bread at a village which appeared to regard the transaction as a personal favour. He had meant to reserve part of it for evening, but providence and appetite are seldom consulted together. Now his knees trembled, and the wet had passed through his boots.

At a turning of the lane he saw, behind a ragged belt of elms, the roofs of a farm. They were low, uneven roofs, dark with rain; beneath them stood a stone house, a granary, cow-sheds, and a long wall enclosing what might once have been a garden. No lamp shone from the windows, and the place had rather the aspect of something abandoned than of a habitation; nevertheless a faint smell of wood smoke came across the fields.

Stoner climbed a broken stile and approached by a cart track. A chained dog sprang from under a wagon and barked furiously, though age soon reduced its hostility to a fit of coughing. Before Stoner could knock, the door opened, and an old man carrying a horn lantern looked out.

For several moments he stared without speaking. He was bent almost double, with a narrow face that had been folded into innumerable wrinkles, but his eyes were bright and eager.

“Tom,” he said at last.

Stoner glanced behind him. There was nobody on the track.

“Tom Prike,” said the old man, and the lantern shook in his hand. “You’m come home.”

“My name—” Stoner began.

“Come in out o’ the rain. Lord’s sake, come in. I knew you’d come. Her said you wouldn’t, but I knew.”

He seized Stoner’s sleeve and drew him across the threshold with a strength surprising in so slight a body. The passage smelt of damp flags, peat smoke, and apples stored somewhere in darkness. A hard-faced woman appeared at a farther door, wiped her hands upon her apron, and regarded the visitor without astonishment.

“’Tis him,” the old man announced.

“So I see,” she answered.

Stoner, who did not see anything of the kind, opened his mouth to protest; but from the room behind the woman came the smell of roast pork. Hunger, which had listened impatiently to the conversation, now took command.

The woman looked towards the ceiling.

“Miss Prike won’t see him.”

“Her’ll come round,” said the old man. “Blood be blood.”

“She says he may eat in the kitchen. George, you’re to tell him she’s not forgiven nought, nor never will.”

The announcement contained matter which ought to have alarmed Stoner; instead, he observed a dish being set upon the table.

“Pork,” said the woman. “Will you have it cold, or heated with onions?”

Stoner had passed through situations demanding decision: marriages had been proposed to him, partnerships offered, prosecutions threatened. In every case he had requested time for reflection. On this occasion he answered at once.

“Hot, with onions.”

It was the promptest resolution of his life.

The old man, whose name was George, sat opposite and watched him eat. There were potatoes, cabbage, bread, and a jug of small beer; and as these disappeared, Stoner’s objections to his new identity lost something of their urgency. He told himself that a starving man was not obliged to correct every error before supper. Afterwards, when warmth and food had restored the dignity of conscience, he would explain.

But afterwards George led him upstairs, carrying the lantern before him. The room into which they entered had a sloping ceiling and furniture of black oak; a fire burned in the grate, and upon the bed lay a suit of clothes, neatly brushed, with linen beside it.

“Your things,” George said. “Kept ’em all these years. Knew you’d want ’em.”

On the wall hung a faded photograph of a young man in riding clothes.

Stoner took the candle nearer. The face was narrower than his own and the mouth more insolent, yet the likeness was unmistakable. The same heavy eyelids, straight nose, irregular line of the jaw, and hair growing to a point upon the forehead belonged to both. A stranger meeting them separately might easily have believed them brothers; an old servant, peering through rain and dusk after years of absence, might be forgiven for believing more.

“You’ve changed,” George murmured, “but not past knowing.”

“How long have I been away?”

The question escaped before Stoner had considered it.

“Seven year come Candlemas. Miss thought you dead. Most hoped it.”

“Most?”

George’s face closed. “You get to bed. You’m worn.”

“What happened before I went?”

“Old talk. Best let lie.”

“Was it bad?”

George moved towards the door. “They be bitter folk hereabouts.”

“And my aunt?”

“Bitter too.”

He went out, leaving Stoner with the fire, the dry clothes, and a bed softer than any he had known for months.

The truth could still be told in the morning. It could always be told in the morning; this is one of the advantages which truth possesses over appetite. Stoner undressed, placed his wet garments near the grate, and got beneath the blankets. He intended to arrange a proper account of himself, but sleep came down like a trapdoor.

Morning brought frost, bacon, and a postponement.

Miss Prike sent word that her nephew might remain until she had determined what was to be done with him, but that she would neither speak to him nor tolerate his presence in the front rooms. George conveyed this ultimatum with satisfaction, as though it represented a triumph of affection. The hard-faced maid, Martha, placed coffee before Stoner and asked if London had improved him.

“Not greatly,” he said.

“That’s plain.”

No one demanded a history of the seven missing years. This at first appeared fortunate, then disturbing. George evaded every inquiry regarding Tom’s departure; Martha responded with contemptuous silence; and the unseen aunt, moving sometimes overhead with the measured tap of a stick, remained as inaccessible as a conscience. Stoner discovered only that Tom’s father had died young, that the farm belonged to Miss Prike, and that Tom had once expected to inherit it.

After breakfast George showed him the buildings. The land ran down towards marshy meadows, beyond which low dunes concealed the sea. There were forty cattle, six working horses, pigs, poultry, and a roan cob called Merlin, who thrust his muzzle against Stoner’s coat as if renewing an acquaintance.

“He minds you,” said George.

“Animals forget.”

“Not him.”

The chained dog near the wagon had died during the night, and lay stiff upon the straw. George lamented it as a final proof of welcome: the creature had barked at everyone else, he said, but would soon have known Master Tom. Stoner reflected, with a degree of compunction insufficient to disturb him, that a dead dog could not contradict anybody.

By noon he was dressed in Tom Prike’s clothes. They fitted him disagreeably well. The boots were sound, the coat warm, the breeches almost new; and when George brought him a hat and declared that he looked his old self, Stoner experienced the peculiar uneasiness of seeing a lie become credible without assistance.

He meant to leave after dinner. Then rain began. He meant to leave next day, but George saddled Merlin and asked him to inspect a broken boundary wall. A week later Stoner was still at the farm, and by then his remaining scruples had acquired the remoteness of unpaid bills.

His duties were neither arduous nor exacting. He rode over the fields, counted beasts, carried messages, and occasionally helped George with accounts which had become confused through a long distrust of arithmetic. The work pleased him because it gave consequence to his idleness. He had food, beer, tobacco, dry linen, and ten shillings each Saturday; more than these, he had a place prepared for him by another man’s absence.

The household accepted him without affection but without inquiry. Martha scolded him as if he had never left. Miss Prike continued to refuse an interview, though twice he found small comforts sent to his room: a woollen scarf and a bottle of cordial. Such gifts suggested that unforgiveness might have practical limits.

Outside the farm, however, matters were different.

On his first visit to the village, conversation ceased when he entered the inn. Men who had been laughing over a game of dominoes turned their backs. The landlord served him reluctantly and washed the glass after him with ostentatious thoroughness. An old labourer by the fire spat into the ashes.

“So you’m back,” he said.

“So it seems.”

“Some things comes back as ought to lie buried.”

Stoner drank his beer and left. Two boys followed him down the street, calling “Prike! Prike!” with an intonation which made the name obscene. When he turned, they ran.

He questioned George that evening.

“What did Tom do?”

The old man bent over the harness he was oiling.

“There’s folk as talks.”

“They do more than talk.”

“Never heed ’em.”

“Was there a woman?”

George’s hands stopped. “There’s always women where trouble be.”

“Did he steal?”

“Master Tom had money.”

“Then what?”

“Old talk,” George repeated. “Dead ashes.”

But whatever lay in those ashes retained heat. In the market town a dealer refused to bargain with Stoner; a carter drove his wheel deliberately through a puddle to splash him; and once, returning after dark, he heard footsteps behind him which quickened when he quickened and stopped when he stopped. He reached the farm without seeing his pursuer.

The sensible course was to go. Stoner considered it often, usually after supper and before the fire, when departure involved no immediate discomfort. Yet the world outside waited with wet roads, casual labour, suspicious lodging-house keepers, and the sevenpence which had long since vanished. The farm, grim though it was, possessed walls. If those walls resembled a prison, they also resembled shelter; and a man pursued by hounds is not critical of the kennel into which he crawls.

There remained the danger of Tom himself. Each stranger seen upon the lane caused Stoner’s heart to stumble. At night he imagined a man with his face standing below the window, demanding his clothes, his horse, his bed, and perhaps the inheritance for which Stoner had begun to entertain a modest expectation. Yet weeks passed. Autumn hardened into winter, and no rightful claimant came.

He grew accustomed to the roan cob, to Martha’s sour meals, to George’s bent figure moving among the stalls, even to the tapping overhead which announced Miss Prike’s invisible displeasure. Bowker, the shepherd, had a rough-coated pup which attached itself to Stoner and accompanied him everywhere. Human beings might preserve accusations for seven years; the pup required only a crust.

By December Stoner had put on flesh. His cheeks filled, his beard was trimmed, and in Tom’s clothes he looked less like a vagrant masquerading as a farmer than like a farmer imperfectly disguised as a gentleman. He began to speculate upon permanence. Miss Prike was old; George believed her indestructible, which is the customary opinion of dependants concerning elderly tyrants, but Stoner had observed the bottle of medicine carried nightly upstairs.

Christmas approached under a hard white frost. Holly was brought into the kitchen; puddings hung in cloths; and Martha, having announced that no festivities were intended, killed two geese. On the evening of the twenty-second, Stoner sat by the fire mending a glove while the pup slept across his boots. Wind pressed at the windows, and from the yard came the knock of a loose shutter.

George entered without removing his coat. His face was colourless.

“You must go.”

Stoner looked up. “Where?”

“Away. Now.”

The old man closed the door and listened.

“What has happened?”

“Michael Ley’s come back.”

The name meant nothing to Stoner, but George spoke it as one might announce pestilence.

“Who is he?”

“You know.”

“I have forgotten.”

“He hasn’t.” George leaned close. “He were at the King’s Head this afternoon. Drunk, but not too drunk. Said afore them all he’d shoot you like a fox if he found you.”

Stoner set down the glove.

“For what?”

“Don’t ask.”

“I am asking now.”

George’s eyes shifted towards the ceiling. “What’s done’s done.”

“Apparently not.”

He went to the window. The yard was black except where frost held a little light. Beyond the barns the lane vanished between hedges.

George produced a leather purse. “There’s twelve pound. Miss sent it.”

“She knows?”

“Her says you’re to go to Clavers, by the coast. Widow Penfold keeps a lodging beyond the church. Stay till I send. Ley’ll tire or be took up.”

“Why does he want to kill me?”

The old man’s lips tightened. “You shouldn’t have come back if you’d no mind to face him.”

For the first time since entering the farm, Stoner felt indignation rise above prudence. He had eaten another man’s food, worn another man’s coat, ridden his horse, and accepted a prospective share of his property; but he had not agreed to be shot for him. There are obligations which even an impostor may reasonably decline.

He packed swiftly. His own valise had been thrown away, so George found a canvas bag. Into it Stoner put bread, cheese, a flask, spare linen, and the silver watch without its glass. Tom’s clothes he retained, since his own had become rags for cleaning harness.

“Take Merlin,” George urged.

“No. A horse can be traced.”

“You’ll not manage the marsh road.”

“I shall manage.”

The decision to leave brought a curious lightness. Once beyond the district he need not go to Clavers; twelve pounds would carry him to London, Bristol, perhaps farther. He would become Martin Stoner again, a poor name but an honest possession, and Tom Prike with his silent crime and implacable enemies could return to whatever darkness had bred him.

Martha stood in the passage, holding a lamp.

“Running again?” she said.

“So it appears.”

“’Tis what you do best.”

He wondered whether she knew, whether all of them knew, and had accepted the deception because an absent sinner was less useful than a present labourer. But George pushed open the door, and the cold came in.

“Take lower lane,” he whispered. “Not through village. At Miller’s Cross turn left, then over the bridge. Don’t show a light.”

Stoner stepped outside. The pup tried to follow, and he had to thrust it back with his foot. It whined behind the closed door.

The moon was thin, the frost whitening the fields but leaving the lane black beneath the hedges. His boots rang upon frozen ruts. For the first mile he listened continually, yet heard only wind in dead grass and the distant movement of the sea.

With each step the farm loosened its hold. The unseen aunt, hostile villagers, George’s questions, the photograph upon the wall—all diminished behind him. He began to whistle. Fate had chased him to the coast and, finding another man’s coat upon his shoulders, had momentarily lost the scent. He had twelve pounds, food, and a sound pair of boots. For Stoner, this constituted prosperity.

At Miller’s Cross he turned into a sunken lane roofed by thorn. Here no moonlight entered. The way narrowed, and the hedges rose on either side like damp walls. Halfway down, something moved ahead.

Stoner stopped.

A man stepped from beneath an ash tree. The barrel of a gun caught a grey thread of light.

“Tom Prike.”

Stoner’s first impulse was to retreat, but another figure came out behind him, or seemed to; then he understood that it was only a pollarded stump. The hesitation was enough. The armed man advanced, heavy and broad, his breath coming fast.

“You know me,” he said.

“No.”

The gun rose.

“My name is Martin Stoner.”

The man laughed once, without amusement.

“I have papers,” Stoner said. It was not true. “I can prove it.”

“You’ve always had words.”

“I am not Tom Prike.”

“Not now, maybe.”

The hedge caught at Stoner’s sleeve as he edged backwards. There was nowhere to turn, no gate, no gap; only the black lane behind him and the blacker muzzle before him.

“Listen,” he said. “You have mistaken me.”

Michael Ley’s face, pale beneath his cap, was composed not of doubt but of seven years’ hatred.

“Nought mistaken,” he answered.

Far away, from the direction of the farm, a dog began to bark. The sound came thinly over the frozen fields, urgent and useless. Stoner heard it and understood that the hounds of Fate, having lost him for a season, had taken up the borrowed scent.

# Effie’s Best Christmas

## I. A Very Tired Little Girl

“I am tired of Christmas,” said Effie.

Now this was a surprising speech to hear from a little girl of ten, especially when she was sitting before a bright fire in a pleasant room, with a fat white cat upon the rug, a bowl of hot-house roses on the table, and snowflakes tapping at the windows as if to remind her that Christmas Eve was close at hand.

Her mamma, who was sewing a silver star upon a small white cap, looked up in astonishment.

“Tired of Christmas, my dear?”

“Yes, mamma—quite, quite tired of it! It is always the same thing.”

Effie leaned back in her cushioned chair and sighed as deeply as if she had been eighty years old, had lost her fortune, and expected nothing from life but rheumatism.

“Always the same?” repeated mamma. “Let me see. Last Christmas you had a doll’s house with twelve rooms, a stable full of little horses, a music-box, six books, a paint-box, a fur tippet, and a stocking so crowded that an orange was obliged to sit on the top.”

“I know,” said Effie. “And the Christmas before that I had the great wax doll with real hair, and the little china tea-set, and the red sled, and the muff with blue ribbons.”

“And the year before that?”

“The rocking-horse, the toy theatre, the Noah’s ark, and the bracelet from Aunt Julia. That is just what I mean, mamma. There are always dolls, books, bonbons, clothes, and toys. I know beforehand exactly how it will be.”

“But you do not know what you will receive this year.”

“No; but I know it will be something,” replied Effie, in the melancholy tone of one who had suffered a great disappointment. “There will be presents at breakfast, presents under the tree, presents when Uncle James comes, and perhaps another parcel in the evening. Papa will say, ‘Well, little woman, has Santa Claus remembered you?’ and Nursey will say, ‘Bless my precious, what a fortunate child!’ and everybody will expect me to jump and clap my hands.”

“Would that be very difficult?”

“Very,” said Effie. “I have jumped and clapped so often.”

Mamma bit her thread and hid a smile.

Effie was not a bad child. She was affectionate, quick, fanciful, and capable of generous feelings; but she had been the only little girl in a large family of grown people, and so many loving hands had showered gifts upon her that pleasure had become a habit instead of a blessing. When a child has every wish satisfied before it has time to grow warm, wishes become pale, weak creatures, and often die of idleness.

“What sort of Christmas would please you?” mamma asked.

“Something new,” cried Effie, sitting upright. “Something splendid—something that never happened before! I should like a mystery, or an adventure, or a fairy. I should like to be lost in a forest and rescued by a prince; or find a diamond crown in my stocking; or have twenty white ponies come dancing up the avenue.”

“Twenty would trample the flower-beds.”

“Then twelve. But I want one perfectly splendid thing and one dear little thing that nobody else in the world has ever had.”

“That is a large order for Santa Claus.”

“He can do anything,” said Effie. “At least, everyone says so.”

At that moment Nursey entered with a basket of mended stockings on one arm and Effie’s best frock over the other. She had been nurse to Effie’s mamma before she became nurse to Effie, and believed that no child born into the family could sneeze without her permission.

“What’s this I hear about Santa Claus?” she asked. “Not finding fault with the blessed gentleman, I hope?”

“Effie is tired of Christmas,” said mamma.

Nursey set down the basket.

“Tired—of—Christmas!”

She spoke the three words separately, as though each were an offence.

“My deary, when I was your age, a sugar-plum was a wonder and an orange a miracle. We hung up stockings no longer than my hand, and if there was a penny at the bottom we felt as rich as the Queen.”

“I should not,” said Effie. “A penny buys almost nothing.”

“It bought a deal of happiness in those days.”

“Perhaps poor children have better Christmases than rich ones,” Effie observed thoughtfully. “They do not know what is coming, and so they can be surprised. If I had no toys and no good dinners all the year, a bun would seem splendid. I almost wish I were poor.”

Nursey stared at her; mamma’s face grew grave.

“No, darling,” she said gently. “You do not wish that. You are thinking only of the surprise, and not of the cold rooms, the scanty fires, the empty cupboards, the worn shoes, and the anxious faces.”

“I could bear them for a little while,” said Effie. “Just long enough to make Christmas exciting.”

“Poverty is not a costume to put on for an hour,” mamma replied. “Nor is hunger an adventure arranged for one’s amusement.”

Effie coloured, for mamma’s quiet words often made her more ashamed than a scolding would have done.

“I did not mean to be unkind.”

“I know you did not. But there are children who will wake on Christmas morning to no warm fire, no nice breakfast, no gay tree, no little stocking, and no loving voice wishing them joy.”

“Why does not Santa Claus go to them first?”

“Perhaps he waits for other hands to help him.”

“What hands?”

“Yours may be among them someday.”

Effie looked at her small fingers. They appeared better fitted for holding bonbons than for performing any great work of mercy.

“How could I help twenty or thirty poor children?” she asked.

“One begins with one.”

That answer did not sound sufficiently magical, and Effie soon returned to her grievance. She wandered about the room, counted the roses, teased the cat with a ribbon, opened the piano, shut it again, and finally took up a book from the table.

It was *A Christmas Carol*.

“I shall read about Scrooge,” she announced. “He was tired of Christmas too.”

“He had much worse reasons than yours,” said mamma.

“He said Christmas was humbug.”

“And learned that he was wrong.”

“But spirits came to teach him. That made it interesting.”

Effie curled herself into the great chair and began to read. The fire whispered and snapped; the snow thickened against the dark panes; mamma’s needle went in and out of the white cap, drawing a shining star after it. Presently Effie forgot her own drawing-room. She saw Scrooge’s cold chambers, the ghostly knocker, the little Cratchit household, the steaming pudding, and Tiny Tim with his crutch and gentle voice.

She laughed, shivered, pitied, and wondered. Then the printed lines began to swim. Scrooge’s candle stretched into a golden moon; the fire sank to a red eye; the wind gave a long, mournful cry in the chimney.

Effie’s head drooped upon the cushion.

“Poor children may have the best of it after all,” she murmured.

Then she fell asleep.

## II. Effie Has Her Wish

At first Effie thought she was still reading, for the wind continued to moan and the snow continued to fall. But when she opened her eyes, the book, the bright room, mamma, Nursey, the roses, and the white cat had all disappeared.

She stood in a narrow street.

Tall houses leaned over it as if whispering gloomy secrets. The windows were dark; the lamps made sickly yellow circles in the fog; snow lay in gray ridges by the doorsteps. Effie wore a ragged frock, a torn shawl, and shoes so thin that the icy water crept through them at every step.

She put her hand to her head. Her fur hood was gone. She felt in her pocket. There was no handkerchief, no purse, no sweetmeat—nothing but a piece of string and one cold button.

“This is the adventure,” she said, trying to be brave. “I wished to be poor for a little while.”

The wind rushed around the corner and nearly pushed her down.

“I do not think it need be quite so cold.”

No one answered.

A carriage rolled past, glowing with lamps and lined with crimson. A little girl sat inside wrapped in white fur, with a parcel on her knees. Effie ran after it.

“Please stop! Please take me home!”

The wheels splashed black snow over her frock, and the carriage vanished.

“I suppose that girl did not hear,” said Effie, though she had seen the child turn and look.

She wandered on. Through one window she saw a table covered with dishes—a brown turkey, a tall jelly, oranges, nuts, iced cakes, and a silver bowl from which blue flames leaped merrily. The sight made her suddenly aware that she was hungry.

She knocked at the door.

A servant opened it.

“If you please,” Effie began, “I should like my dinner.”

“Your dinner?”

“Yes. Some soup first, and then chicken—or turkey will do—and potatoes, and perhaps pudding.”

The servant laughed and shut the door.

Effie knocked again, more loudly.

“I do not mean to be troublesome, but I am very hungry!”

This time no one came.

She went to another house, where music sounded and children danced around a tree blazing with candles.

“May I come in?” she called through the glass. “I am a child too!”

No one heard her. The dancing figures passed and repassed, warm, rosy, and merry. A boy held up a wooden horse; a little girl kissed a doll; a baby shook silver bells. Effie pressed her face to the pane until the cold glass hurt her cheek.

“They have so many things,” she thought. “Surely they could spare one.”

But the curtains were drawn, and the bright picture vanished.

The snow fell faster, the wind blew colder, and the night grew darker. Effie’s hunger sharpened until even a dry crust seemed more desirable than all the sugar castles she had ever pushed aside after dinner. Her fingers ached, her feet were numb, and a strange fear entered her heart. What if this were not an adventure that would end when she chose? What if there were no mamma searching, no Nursey waiting with warm stockings, no fire, no bed?

She crouched upon a doorstep.

“I do not like being poor,” she sobbed. “I did not understand. I want to go home.”

Just then a tiny bell rang above her head.

Effie looked up.

A little figure stood in the middle of the snowy street. At first she thought it was a child younger than herself, but there was something too bright and fearless in his face for any ordinary child. He wore a green tunic edged with holly leaves, scarlet shoes, and a cloak made of snowflakes. The snowflakes did not melt; they trembled and sparkled like stars. On his yellow curls rested a wreath of evergreen, and in one hand he carried a small lantern shaped like a heart.

“Who are you?” Effie whispered.

“I am the Christmas Spirit.”

“You are not like the spirits in Scrooge’s story.”

“Christmas spirits wear many shapes. I came in one that would not frighten you.”

“Will you take me home?”

“By and by.”

“I am cold now.”

He shook out his shining cloak and wrapped it around her. At once she grew warm, though the cloak felt as light as mist.

“I am starving,” said Effie.

The Spirit lifted his lantern. A steaming bowl of soup came sailing down the street as gracefully as a swan upon water. It paused before Effie; a silver spoon dipped itself, rose to her lips, and fed her until the bowl was empty.

“Oh!” sighed Effie. “That was better than a feast.”

“Hunger is an excellent cook, but a cruel companion,” said the Spirit.

“Where did the soup come from?”

“From a kitchen where a woman remembered that her neighbours had none.”

“Was she a fairy?”

“Only a kind woman.”

“I thought kindness like that must be magic.”

“It is the oldest Christmas magic in the world.”

He took Effie’s hand.

“Come. You asked for something new.”

“Will it be splendid?”

“Very splendid.”

“And shall I have one dear little thing?”

“If you learn how to see it.”

They walked to the end of the street, where the fog opened like a curtain. Beyond it lay a silver road leading upward through the falling snow.

## III. The Workshops in the Clouds

No sooner had Effie placed one foot upon the silver road than she felt herself grow light. Up she went beside the Spirit—over roofs, over chimneys, over church steeples with snow upon their shoulders. The city sank beneath them, its lamps shining like scattered gold beads, and soon they entered a country made of moonlight and clouds.

Before them stood an enormous house with a hundred chimneys and a thousand windows. Bells hung under the eaves. Every door was painted red, and above the largest appeared these words:

**CHRISTMAS WORKS  
GIFTS MADE HERE—HAPPINESS FINISHED ELSEWHERE**

“Is this Santa Claus’s house?” cried Effie.

“One of his branch establishments,” said the Spirit. “Business is too large for a single workshop now.”

They entered a hall so long that Effie could not see the end. Hundreds of busy spirits hurried to and fro. Some were plump and old, some young and nimble; some had wings, some wore aprons, and some had pencils behind their ears. There were even three Santa Clauses together, arguing cheerfully over a map.

“I insist,” said the first, “that the wooden elephant belongs in India.”

“It is going to Indiana,” replied the second.

“Then why does the label say ‘Ind.’?” demanded the third.

All three scratched their white beards.

Effie laughed.

Along one wall, dolls were being finished. There were wax dolls, rag dolls, china dolls, black dolls, brown dolls, dolls that shut their eyes, dolls that said “Mamma,” dolls in silk, dolls in gingham, and one proud doll in a velvet train who kept turning her head to admire herself.

“Take care,” said a work-spirit, tapping the proud doll with a thimble. “You are intended for a sick child, and must learn to look pleasant.”

At once the doll’s stiff little mouth softened into a smile.

Farther on were tables heaped with drums, tops, marbles, skates, balls, whistles, blocks, paint-boxes, puzzles, toy ships, Noah’s arks, hoops, jumping-jacks, picture-books, and furry animals of every kind. Tin soldiers marched into boxes by companies. Wooden horses galloped into carts. Kites folded their own tails. A family of toy rabbits escaped from a basket and was pursued by six laughing spirits.

“Here are the toys I always have,” said Effie.

“Look more closely,” answered her guide.

Over every table hung labels. One read:

**FOR CHILDREN WHO HAVE MANY THINGS**

Another said:

**FOR CHILDREN WHO HAVE FEW**

A third:

**FOR CHILDREN WHO HAVE NONE**

The first table held costly toys of satin, gold paint, polished wood, and delicate china. The spirits moved slowly around it, consulting lists and shaking their heads.

“This child already owns four dolls.”

“Then send the new one elsewhere.”

“This boy has three sleds.”

“One is enough for any boy with only two legs.”

“This little girl asks for a gold dressing-case.”

“What does she mean to dress?”

“Herself, I fear.”

There was gentle laughter.

At the second table, useful and cheerful things were packed together: mittens with story-books, shoes with balls, warm caps with peppermint sticks, woollen frocks with bright dolls tucked into their folds.

At the third table the spirits worked fastest of all. Their eyes were tender, and they sang as they filled stout baskets with bread, blankets, stockings, coal tickets, apples, medicine, toys, and small green branches tied with red.

“Why do they put bread with toys?” asked Effie.

“Because a doll cannot comfort an empty stomach.”

“And why toys with bread?”

“Because a child does not cease to be a child merely because she is hungry.”

A thin spirit came hurrying by with a long paper fluttering behind him.

“What is that?” asked Effie.

“A rich man’s cheque,” said the Christmas Spirit.

As they watched, the cheque spread two white wings and flew across the hall. It hovered over the third table, changed into twenty blankets, forty pairs of shoes, a barrel of flour, and a basket of oranges.

“That is a useful kind of bird,” Effie declared.

“It flew only because the man opened his hand.”

They entered another chamber where no toys were made. Here sat quiet spirits writing notes into people’s hearts.

“What are they doing?”

“Giving reminders.”

One spirit wrote, “The old woman in the attic has no fire.” The words turned into a spark and flew down a chimney toward the earth.

Another wrote, “Your sister is lonely; invite her.” That message became a tiny bird and tapped at a window.

A third wrote, “You have been angry long enough.”

The sentence changed into a warm ray and slipped beneath a closed door.

“Do people obey?” asked Effie.

“Not always.”

“What happens then?”

“The reminder returns, and we send it again.”

“How many times?”

“As many as we can. Christmas is patient.”

Near the end of the room stood a shelf filled with strange parcels. One was marked **A KIND WORD**, another **AN HOUR OF TIME**, another **A FORGIVEN QUARREL**, and another **A VISIT TO SOMEONE FORGOTTEN**.

“These do not look like presents,” said Effie.

“They are among the best.”

“But you cannot hang an hour of time on a tree.”

“No; you must spend it.”

“And a kind word costs nothing.”

“That is why even the poorest person may give one, though some rich people find it remarkably expensive.”

Effie thought of the carriage child who had looked at her and turned away. Then she remembered occasions when she herself had passed ragged children without wondering where they slept.

The splendid workshop seemed less amusing now, though far more wonderful.

“Come,” said the Spirit. “The gifts are ready. We must see what they do.”

At once every bell beneath the eaves began to ring. The doors flew open. Sledges rushed out, drawn by reindeer, white horses, great birds, and even a team of mice for very small deliveries. Baskets sprang wings; parcels marched in rows; warm clothes danced after them; candles flew like fireflies.

The Christmas Spirit placed Effie in a little green sleigh.

“Hold fast!”

They shot downward through the clouds.

## IV. Houses Without Christmas

They stopped before a dark building in a poor quarter of the city. The stairs were broken, the passages smelled of smoke and dampness, and behind each door lived some trouble.

In the first room a widow sat sewing by one weak candle. Two children slept upon a mattress in the corner, covered by a thin shawl. There was no fire, no supper, no tree, and no stockings hanging anywhere.

“Why does she not go to bed?” Effie whispered.

“She is finishing shirts for a shop. If she sews until dawn, she may earn enough for bread.”

“But tomorrow is Christmas.”

“Hunger keeps no holidays.”

The widow laid down her work and rubbed her eyes. One of the children stirred.

“Is Santa Claus coming, mother?” he asked sleepily.

“Perhaps he has many doors to visit, Jamie.”

“Will he know this one? There is no number on it.”

“He will know, dear.”

The child slept again, but the mother covered her face.

Effie felt a pain in her throat.

“Can we give them something?”

“We shall see who remembers them.”

A step sounded in the passage. A young clerk entered, carrying a basket and a bundle of wood.

“My master sent this,” he said. “He heard your husband once worked for the firm. There is meat, bread, tea, and coal—and I believe some foolish person put two toys at the bottom.”

The widow’s face changed so quickly that the miserable room seemed illuminated.

“Oh, sir, I cannot thank—”

“Do not thank me,” said the clerk. “I only carried it.”

But Effie saw the light from the Christmas Spirit’s lantern shining upon him, and understood that carrying comfort is no small part of giving it.

The fire was kindled, the table spread, and two small stockings were discovered beneath the children’s pillow. Into them went apples, cakes, mittens, a whistle, a doll, and one orange apiece.

“Is this splendid enough?” asked the Spirit.

“Yes,” whispered Effie.

They passed into the next room.

Here lived a shoemaker, his wife, and five children. The father had been ill for weeks. Their last loaf stood on the table, divided into seven very little pieces. Yet the youngest child had tied a paper star to a bare twig and placed it in a bottle.

“That is our tree,” she explained. “I made it for papa.”

“It is beautiful, Meg,” said the sick man.

Effie looked at the lonely twig, then remembered complaining because the ornaments upon her own great tree had appeared the year before.

A knock came. The shoemaker’s old customers had gathered a purse for him. In rushed neighbours with a goose, potatoes, flour, candles, medicine, boots for the children, and a green tree. Everyone talked at once.

“Set it by the window!”

“Mind the candles!”

“Here is a shawl for your wife.”

“And here are orders enough to keep you busy when you are well.”

The bare room blossomed. A little boy danced with a pair of boots pressed to his breast; the sick father laughed; his wife cried; Meg moved her paper star from the twig to the very top of the green tree.

“That star is prettier than the silver one mamma was sewing,” said Effie.

“Because you know what it means.”

In the cellar below, they found a different scene. A pale girl was trying to quiet a baby while two younger boys quarrelled over a crust. Their mother had gone to work; their father, who drank away his wages, had not returned.

No fire burned. Rain leaked down one wall. The baby’s hands were blue.

“Oh, this is dreadful!” cried Effie. “Why does no one come?”

“Some sorrows are hidden,” replied the Spirit. “People hurry past the door because they do not know—or do not ask.”

“Then tell them!”

“You may tell them.”

“I am only a child.”

“A child’s voice can travel far when it speaks for another.”

Just then an elderly woman from the upper floor entered. She was poor herself, and her basket held only a little coal, milk, and bread.

“I cannot spare much,” she said, “but we will not freeze separately when we might be warm together. Come upstairs, chicks.”

She wrapped the baby inside her own shawl and led the children away.

“She gave nearly all she had,” said Effie.

“Yes.”

“Then poor people help one another too.”

“Very often. Those who know the weight of a burden are quick to take hold of its other end.”

The next visit was to a neat room where a mother and three daughters sat around an empty fireplace. Everything was clean but worn—the faded rug, the carefully mended curtains, the patched dresses, the thin hands folded in patient laps. The youngest daughter, Katy, had one leg drawn stiffly beneath her.

“Will there be no Christmas?” Katy asked.

“We have each other,” said her mother bravely.

“And one potato apiece,” added the eldest girl, trying to laugh.

“I wish I could give you all something,” Katy said. “I would give mother a warm shawl, Nell a new frock, Mary a book, and myself—”

“What would you give yourself?” asked Mary.

“A doll,” said Katy softly. “Only a very little one.”

She took two scraps of cloth and rolled them together, making a head and a body. With a bit of charcoal she marked two eyes.

“There!” she said. “Her name is Patience.”

Effie knelt beside her, though no one in the room could see the visitor.

“I have twelve dolls,” she whispered. “One wears pearls. One has a trunk. One can say ‘Mamma,’ though she says it in a very disagreeable manner. I would give you any of them.”

“Would you?” asked the Spirit.

“Yes. All of them, if she wanted them.”

“All?”

Effie hesitated at the thought of Rosabelle, the great wax doll with golden hair, who slept in a carved bed and possessed more clothes than many real children.

“Yes,” she said at last. “Even Rosabelle.”

Before the Spirit could answer, sleigh bells sounded outside. A group of ladies and young people entered with baskets from a church society. They brought coal, food, flannel, a warm shawl, books, and three parcels labelled with the girls’ names.

Katy opened hers. Inside lay a small doll in a blue dress.

For one moment she did not speak. Then she held it close and kissed its painted face.

“She is prettier than any queen,” Katy said.

Effie began to cry.

“Why are you sad?” asked the Spirit.

“I am not sad. At least—I do not know. It is because she is so happy with one little doll, and I have hardly looked at mine for months.”

“Pleasure grows dull when it has nothing to do but please itself.”

“May I see more houses?”

The Spirit smiled.

Now they travelled swiftly. Walls became transparent before them, and Effie saw a hundred Christmases.

In one house, a lonely old man received a basket from the children next door and pretended the smoke hurt his eyes. In another, a servant carried her own dinner to a beggar. In a hospital, nurses hung tiny wreaths above white beds and children awoke to books, oranges, and paper toys. Sailors at sea shared pudding during a storm. A prisoner read a letter of forgiveness from his son. A rich boy sent his sled to a child who could not buy one. A schoolgirl gave up a party to sit beside her sick teacher. Fires were kindled, tables were spread, debts were paid, quarrels were ended, warm clothes were wrapped around shivering bodies, and sad hearts were consoled.

Yet they also saw doors that remained closed.

They saw a family throwing away enough food to feed ten hungry people. They saw a lady complain that the ribbons on her gifts were the wrong shade. They saw a boy break a costly toy because it was not the one he had requested. They saw a little girl—so like Effie that Effie blushed—looking at heaps of presents and saying, “Is that all?”

“Must you show me that?” Effie asked.

“Yes.”

“I understand.”

“Not quite yet.”

## V. The Grove of Trees

The city faded. Effie found herself standing before two gates.

One gate was made of gold and hung with bells. Above it shone the words **CHRISTMAS RECEIVED**.

The other was woven from plain evergreen. Above it were the words **CHRISTMAS GIVEN**.

“Which road shall we take?” asked the Spirit.

“The golden one looks more splendid.”

They entered it.

Beyond stood an immense Christmas tree loaded with everything Effie had ever desired. There were diamond crowns, white ponies no larger than cats, music-boxes, silk dresses, bracelets, books, bonbons, toy theatres, houses for dolls, dolls for houses, and packages tied in ribbons of every colour.

“All mine?” cried Effie.

“All.”

She seized a crown, but it felt heavy. She wound a music-box, but the tune was one she had heard a hundred times. The white ponies danced, yet there was no one to admire them with her. The bonbons tasted like paper. The dolls stared glassily. The tree’s candles burned without warmth.

“This is not very pleasant,” she said.

“You have everything.”

“But there is nobody else.”

“That is the difficulty with having everything for oneself.”

Effie laid down the crown.

“May we try the other gate?”

They returned and passed through the evergreen arch.

At first Effie saw only darkness. Then the Spirit lifted his heart-shaped lantern.

Before her stretched a grove of twenty-four Christmas trees.

No two were alike. One bore red mittens, apples, and a wooden horse. Another glittered with spools of thread, a workbox, a blue frock, and sugar birds. A third held boots, books, a doll’s cradle, cakes, and a warm cloak. There were trees with shawls and trees with skates; trees with tin cups, toy sheep, woollen stockings, ribbons, gingerbread men, combs, oranges, tops, balls, aprons, puzzles, caps, tea, sugar, and bright paper chains.

Among the branches moved invisible hands, arranging every gift with care. At the foot of each tree appeared a name.

“Who are they for?” asked Effie.

“Children who have had very little.”

“Are they coming?”

“Tomorrow—if someone invites them.”

“Who?”

The Spirit looked at her.

Effie ran from tree to tree in delight. Here was abundance, but it did not weary her. Every object seemed alive because it belonged to someone who needed or longed for it. A pair of strong shoes was as beautiful as satin slippers; a warm petticoat shone brighter than lace; a five-cent whistle looked as merry as a silver trumpet.

At the last tree Effie saw the name **KATY**.

A little blue-dressed doll sat among its branches.

“Katy already has a doll like that,” Effie said.

“Dreams are careless about time,” replied the Spirit. “They mix together what has happened, what may happen, what has been read, heard, feared, or hoped.”

“Then this is a dream?”

“All children ask that question when a lesson begins to pinch.”

Effie turned to him.

“What must I do?”

“Wake.”

“That is all?”

“Wake—and remember.”

The grove began to whirl. The trees spun into green wheels; their candles became stars; parcels flew upward like birds. The Spirit’s snowy cloak streamed around Effie, the wind roared, and the bells rang louder and louder.

“Wait!” she cried. “You promised me one splendid thing and one dear little thing.”

“You shall have them,” called the Spirit, already far away.

“When?”

“When you are ready to know them!”

The snow rushed over Effie’s face.

She awoke with a start.

## VI. Mamma Makes a Plan

The fire had burned low. The book lay upon the floor. Mamma was gone, and Nursey stood over Effie, ringing the tea-bell near her ear.

“Wake up, my precious! I’ve called you three times. Such sleeping! You looked as solemn as a judge and sighed fit to break my heart.”

Effie threw her arms around Nursey’s waist.

“Oh, Nursey, I have been a beggar, and I was hungry, and nobody let me in, and then a Christmas Spirit came, and there were workshops and cheques with wings and dolls that behaved badly and poor children without any fire—and Katy had a rag doll called Patience—but then she had a blue one—and twenty-four trees—and I am never, never going to say I am tired of Christmas again!”

Nursey blinked.

“Your blessed ma had better hear this from the beginning—and slowly.”

Mamma returned and listened while Effie poured out the dream in breathless fragments. She described the widow, the shoemaker, the children in the cellar, lame Katy, the two gates, and the grove of trees.

“There were twenty-four,” she insisted. “I counted them. Each child had her own. Oh, mamma, why could we not do something like that?”

“Twenty-four trees?”

“They need not be enormous. Little ones would do. We could ask poor children—girls, perhaps, because boys make such a noise—and give them dinner and presents. I could give some of my things. Truly I could.”

“Even Rosabelle?”

Effie swallowed.

“Yes. If there were a Katy.”

Mamma drew her close and kissed her.

“My dear little girl, that is the first really new Christmas idea you have had.”

“Can we do it?”

“Not this year, perhaps. Christmas is too near.”

Effie’s face fell.

“But we might begin now and arrange such a feast for another time.”

“A whole year away!”

“Good deeds sometimes require patience.”

Effie sighed. Still, she ran upstairs and began choosing possessions. Before bedtime a surprising collection stood in Nursey’s room: books, games, dolls, ribbons, a little fur cape, two tea-sets, blocks, a workbox, and Rosabelle in her carved bed.

Nursey picked up the wax beauty.

“Not this one, my deary. You’ll regret it.”

“I promised.”

“Promised whom?”

“The Spirit.”

“Well, spirits ought to have more consideration for expensive wax.”

Effie laughed, but tucked Rosabelle beneath the pile.

During the next two days she talked constantly of the dream. She searched cupboards for useful things, gave up a new sash, put half her spending money into a box, and even wrote a list entitled **HOW TO MAKE TWENTY-FOUR CHILDREN HAPPY**, though the first item was “Twenty-four trees,” and the second, “Find the children,” which showed that much practical work remained to be done.

Mamma listened, advised, and occasionally disappeared.

There were mysterious sounds in the unused ballroom. Gardeners carried bundles through the side door. Cook made cakes in armies. Papa returned from town with his coat dusted in snow and refused to explain why the carriage was full of parcels. Aunt Julia came with woollen stockings. Uncle James sent a hamper of oranges. Nursey bustled along the corridors, saying, “I know nothing, and if I did I wouldn’t tell,” in a manner which made it perfectly plain that she knew everything.

Effie was told that the ballroom was being cleaned and must not be entered.

“Why at Christmas?”

“Dust observes no holidays,” said mamma.

On Christmas Eve Effie hung up her stocking with a new feeling. She hoped there would be fewer things in it, and then wondered whether this hope itself was not another sort of greediness. At last she wrote upon a card:

**Dear Santa Claus,  
Please remember the children whose doors have no numbers. I have enough.  
Effie.**

She pinned it to the stocking and went to bed.

In the morning there were gifts, of course, but not such a flood as usual. There was a book from papa, a paint-box from mamma, a warm hood from Nursey, and a small purse from Aunt Julia.

Effie thanked everyone sincerely.

“Only four presents!” whispered Nursey, with pretended alarm. “The child will be ruined.”

“They are lovely,” said Effie. “And the purse is just what I wanted, because I can spend it on somebody else.”

After breakfast mamma appeared at the door.

“Effie, I want you upstairs.”

In her room lay a curious costume: a white frock sparkling like frost, a green sash, scarlet shoes, and a cloak made of soft tulle sewn all over with silver flakes. Beside it rested a holly wreath and the white cap with the silver star.

Effie stared.

“It is his cloak!” she cried. “The Spirit’s cloak!”

“Not quite. This one was made by ordinary needles.”

“What is it for?”

Mamma began to dance—yes, actually dance around the bed, though she was a dignified lady and generally left such conduct to Effie.

“For the mystery! The adventure! The perfectly new Christmas!”

Nursey rushed in behind her.

“Your blessed ma has done it all! Twenty-four young ladies from St. Anne’s Orphan Home are on their way this minute, and if you do not dress, my precious, you’ll keep twenty-four dinners cooling and twenty-four noses pressed against the carriage windows.”

Effie gave one scream of joy.

“The trees?”

“Dress and see.”

“Is there a Katy?”

“I know nothing,” said Nursey, wiping her eyes with the corner of Effie’s frost-covered cloak. “Not one blessed thing.”

## VII. Twenty-Four Christmas Trees

When Effie was dressed, she scarcely knew herself.

The white frock shimmered, the snowy cloak floated, the holly crowned her bright hair, and the silver star trembled above her forehead. Mamma placed a little lantern in her hand—a lantern shaped like a heart.

“You are to be our Christmas Spirit,” she said. “You must welcome the children and give what the real spirit taught you to give.”

Effie’s heart beat so loudly that she thought the whole house must hear it.

The ballroom doors were still closed. Behind them came tiny crackles, whispers, and the faint scent of evergreen.

Then sleigh bells jingled outside. Voices filled the hall.

“They have come!” cried Effie.

Twenty-four orphan girls entered in two rows, guided by their matron. Some were tall and shy, some small and eager; some had red hands, some patched frocks, some serious little faces unused to surprises. The youngest clutched the elder girls’ skirts. The oldest tried to behave properly, though their eyes travelled everywhere.

They were given hot chocolate and buns first, for mamma had not forgotten the bowl of soup in Effie’s dream. Then the ballroom doors opened.

No one spoke.

The great room had become a winter forest.

Twenty-four little fir trees stood in a wide circle, with a taller tree blazing in the centre. Cotton snow lay on the floor; silver stars hung above; paper birds perched among the branches; candles shone in red, gold, blue, and green. Each small tree bore a card with a girl’s name, and each was laden from top to bottom.

There were warm cloaks, knitted shawls, flannel petticoats, stout shoes, soft mittens, stockings, aprons, ribbons, and Sunday hats; there were books, slates, pencils, sewing-boxes, paint-boxes, balls, hoops, tops, puzzles, toy animals, dolls, cradles, dishes, and little chairs; there were apples, oranges, figs, nuts, peppermint canes, sugar hearts, gingerbread stars, cakes, and bags of bright candy. At the foot of every tree stood a parcel of useful clothing, and beside each parcel lay one specially chosen toy.

Effie stood among the branches in her snowy cloak.

For one breathless instant the girls thought she really was a spirit.

Then the youngest cried, “Oh, look!” and the spell broke into twenty-four happy voices.

“Is that my name?”

“A doll!”

“See the red shoes!”

“There’s a book tied with blue ribbon!”

“That whole tree cannot be mine!”

The matron tried to arrange them in order, but joy is a lively creature and will not always march two by two.

Effie lifted her lantern.

“Merry Christmas!” she called. “Every girl must find the tree with her name. Everything on it belongs to her.”

“Everything?” gasped a tall girl.

“Everything,” repeated Effie, enjoying the word more than she had ever enjoyed the words “all for you” when they were spoken to herself.

The girls scattered through the grove.

What a time followed!

One child put on her new cloak at once and refused to remove it, though the room was warm. Another counted her oranges five times. Two little sisters discovered dolls of the same size, one in pink and one in yellow, and made them kiss. A sober girl hugged a box of paints and whispered that she had always wanted to make pictures. One danced in new boots before even untying the laces. Another found a muff and buried her face in its softness. The smallest child ate a gingerbread star, two peppermints, and half an apple before the matron could reach her.

Effie went from tree to tree distributing gifts.

“This workbox was mine,” she told one girl. “The scissors are very sharp, and there is a secret place under the tray.”

“To keep secrets in?” asked the girl.

“Or buttons. Buttons are safer.”

She gave her fur cape to a thin child named Susan, a pile of books to a quiet girl called Jane, and the toy theatre to two sisters who promised to perform a play for the others.

Then she came to Rosabelle’s tree.

The great wax doll sat in state upon a branch, dressed in lace and velvet. Beneath her card stood the name **MARTHA**.

Martha was a plain, solemn girl of twelve who had cared for younger children at the orphan home. When Effie placed the doll in her arms, the solemn face quivered.

“I never had one,” Martha said. “Not even when I was little.”

Effie felt a final pang for Rosabelle—but it lasted only a second. The way Martha held the doll made Effie feel that Rosabelle had at last found the proper sort of mother.

“Her nightdress is in the trunk,” Effie explained eagerly. “Her shoes button, though the hook is easy to lose. She dislikes lying flat because it crushes her hair, and sometimes she says ‘Mamma’ when no one touches her, but you must not be frightened.”

“I shall not,” Martha promised.

Effie kissed Rosabelle goodbye and went on.

At dinner the girls sat at long tables decorated with holly. There was turkey, chicken pie, potatoes, peas, cranberry jelly, rolls, and plum pudding with a flame dancing on top. Effie helped serve, though she dropped one spoon, spilled gravy upon her snowy cloak, and gave the same child two helpings of pudding before mamma discovered it.

After dinner they sang carols. Papa played the piano; Uncle James attempted the high notes and failed magnificently; Nursey stood in the doorway crying into her apron and declaring she had never been so merry in her life.

Effie looked around at the warm faces, the bright room, and the green grove. This, she knew, was the splendid thing. No diamond crown, no fairy castle, no procession of white ponies could have equalled it.

Yet she remembered the Spirit’s promise.

There was still to be one dear little thing.

## VIII. Katy’s Gift

The afternoon faded, and the orphan girls began packing their treasures into baskets and bundles. The trees looked thin and bare now, but no one thought them ugly. They had given away their brightness, and there is a beauty in emptiness when it means that hungry hands have been filled.

Effie helped fasten parcels. She tied strings, found missing mittens, wrapped dolls, and tucked sweets into pockets.

Near the last tree sat a small girl with a pale, wistful face. One of her legs was supported by a brace, and a crutch leaned against her chair. Her tree had held a warm red shawl, a book of stories, new slippers, candy, fruit, and a little doll in a blue dress.

Effie stopped.

“What is your name?”

“Katy.”

For a moment the room seemed to turn soft and shining around them.

“Katy!” repeated Effie. “Is your doll named Patience?”

The girl looked surprised.

“No. I call her Merry. But I had a rag doll once named Patience. I left her at the home so the littlest girl could have her.”

Effie sat down beside her.

“Then it was truly you.”

“Have we met?”

“In a dream.”

Katy considered this with perfect seriousness.

“Perhaps. I have dreamed of a room like this.”

She stroked Merry’s blue dress. Then her eyes travelled over Effie’s costume and down to the heart-shaped lantern.

“You are the Christmas fairy, aren’t you?”

“Only for today.”

“You gave us all these things.”

“Mamma and papa and many other people did. I helped a little.”

Katy glanced beneath Effie’s cloak.

“Where are your presents?”

“I had some this morning.”

“But there is no tree with your name.”

“No.”

“And you have been giving things away all day.”

“Yes, but I liked it.”

Katy looked troubled. She bent over her basket, moved the red shawl, the slippers, the book, the fruit, and the candy. At last she took out her new doll.

“This is for you,” she said.

Effie stared.

“Your doll?”

“Yes. You did not get anything here, and everybody should have one Christmas present.”

“Oh, no, Katy! I could not take her. You love her.”

Katy held the doll tighter for an instant. Effie saw the struggle plainly. This was no neglected plaything from a crowded shelf. It was Katy’s one doll, newly received, beautiful in her eyes, and already dear.

Then the little fingers relaxed.

“I do love her,” Katy said. “That is why she is a good present.”

Mamma, who stood nearby, turned her face away.

Effie’s eyes filled with tears.

The Spirit’s voice seemed to whisper: *If you learn how to see it.*

Here was the dear little thing that nobody else in the world had ever had—not because no other doll was like it, but because no other gift could carry Katy’s particular love, courage, and gratitude.

Effie took the doll gently.

“Thank you,” she whispered. “I shall keep her always.”

“Her name is Merry,” said Katy.

“Yes. Merry shall be her name.”

Then Effie bent and kissed Katy, and Katy kissed Effie, and for a little while neither spoke. Children do not always need long speeches when their hearts have understood one another.

Mamma arranged that Katy should visit again. Papa promised to ask a physician about her lame leg. Nursey filled her pockets with extra sugar-plums, though the matron declared that every pocket was already full.

At last the sleighs came round. The girls departed with their bundles, calling thanks from beneath new hoods and waving mittened hands. Martha held Rosabelle at the window; the smallest child blew sticky kisses; Katy lifted Merry’s empty little arms in farewell before remembering that Merry was no longer there. Then she smiled at Effie instead.

The bells faded down the snowy road.

The house grew quiet.

Effie returned to the ballroom. Bits of ribbon and evergreen lay upon the floor. The candles had burned low. Twenty-four bare trees stood in the dusk.

“Well?” asked mamma, slipping an arm around her. “Was it new enough?”

“Oh, mamma!”

“Splendid enough?”

“The most splendid thing that ever happened.”

“And did you receive a dear little gift?”

Effie held up the blue doll.

“The dearest in the world.”

“Then Santa Claus filled your order after all.”

Effie leaned against her mother.

“I thought poor children might be happier because they receive so little. That was foolish, was it not?”

“It was ignorant rather than wicked.”

“But Katy gave away nearly the only toy she had.”

“Yes.”

“She is generous, though she is poor.”

“And you have learned that generosity does not depend upon the size of one’s purse.”

Effie looked at the stripped trees.

“I used to think presents were happiest when they came toward me.”

“And now?”

“They are happier going away.”

Mamma kissed her forehead.

“That is a secret some people never discover, even when they are very old.”

## IX. The Christmas That Lasted

Effie placed Merry in a small chair beside her bed. She did not give her silk gowns, a carved bed, or a trunk full of hats. The blue dress remained, carefully mended whenever it frayed, because Effie would not alter the gift Katy had loved.

Nor did the lesson fade when the snow melted.

Children sometimes wake from a beautiful dream full of noble intentions, only to forget them by breakfast; and grown people are not always better. But Effie’s dream had taken visible form in the grove of trees, and Katy’s doll remained to remind her.

She visited St. Anne’s Orphan Home. She read stories to the younger girls, sent books to the elder ones, saved coins for shoes and medicine, and learned that charity required more than one grand feast. There were ordinary Tuesdays as well as Christmas mornings, and children needed dinner on both. She learned to inquire before giving, to offer useful things with pretty ones, and to give her time as well as her possessions.

Papa’s physician improved Katy’s leg, though she always walked with a slight limp. Katy and Effie became true friends. They exchanged letters, books, secrets, and, occasionally, disagreements—for friendship without any disagreements would be as unnatural as a rosebush without thorns. Katy grew skilful with her needle; Effie helped her obtain lessons; and years later Katy made the snowy cloaks worn by a whole troop of little Christmas spirits at another holiday feast.

Every December, Effie arranged a grove of trees.

At first there were twenty-four. Then there were thirty, forty, and more. Some bore toys; some bore warm clothing; some were intended for sick children, lonely old people, tired mothers, or families whose cupboards were empty. Effie never forgot the labels in the cloud workshop: those who had many, those who had few, and those who had none.

She also remembered the parcels marked **A KIND WORD**, **AN HOUR OF TIME**, **A FORGIVEN QUARREL**, and **A VISIT TO SOMEONE FORGOTTEN**. These she gave freely all the year, though now and then she found them as difficult to offer as anyone else does.

When she was grown, people praised her Christmas celebrations and called them magical.

Effie always smiled.

“The magic is very old,” she would say. “Anyone may use it by opening a hand.”

And if some richly dressed little child complained that Christmas was always the same, Effie did not scold. She would take down a faded doll in a blue dress and tell the story of a cold street, a childlike Spirit, a grove of twenty-four trees, and a lame girl who gave away the thing she loved.

Merry grew old. Her paint wore thin, one eye faded, and her blue dress was patched many times; but Effie cherished her above all the costly toys of her childhood. For the doll had been her one dear little gift, while the joy of those twenty-four orphan girls had been her splendid one.

Thus the Christmas that Effie expected to find tiresome became the best Christmas of her life. More than that, it became a Christmas that never wholly ended; for its warmth went with her into every season, and each year she passed it on to others in food, clothing, comfort, laughter, companionship, and love.

She had wished for something that had never happened before.

What she found was something that may happen every day.

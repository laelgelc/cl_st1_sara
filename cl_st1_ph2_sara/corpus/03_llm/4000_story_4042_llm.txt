# The Christian Antigones

Among the old Greek stories there is none more touching than that of Antigone, the faithful maiden who, in defiance of a king’s cruel command, bestowed the last rites upon her dead brother. She knew that imprisonment and death awaited her if the deed were discovered; yet affection proved stronger than fear, and the law written upon her heart seemed to her more sacred than the proclamation of an earthly ruler.

But Christian history has its Antigones also—women whose love for the dead was strengthened by a sure hope of immortality, and who ventured not only for the poor mortal remains of those whom they loved, but for the honour of that faith in which they had lived and died.

During the persecution of the Christians under the Emperor Diocletian, two brothers, Simplicius and Faustinus, were brought before the Roman tribunal. Of their earlier lives little is known; nor need we wish to know much, since one action, nobly performed, may sometimes declare the whole character of a man more plainly than many years of ordinary conduct. They were accused of being Christians, and were commanded to deny their Master and offer sacrifice to the pagan gods.

They refused.

Promises were made to them, threats were uttered, and tortures were employed; for the persecutors, who could not comprehend a faith stronger than bodily suffering, imagined that pain must accomplish what persuasion had failed to do. But the brothers remained constant. At length they were beheaded, and their bodies, denied the honour of burial, were cast into the Tiber.

Now they had a sister named Beatrix, who had watched the course of their trial with the anguish of one who could neither save them nor openly claim kindred with them. She too was a Christian, and knew that the same enemies who had destroyed her brothers would show no mercy to her. Yet when she learned that their bodies had been thrown into the river, she could not endure that they should remain unburied.

By night, when Rome had grown still, and when the darkness which shelters evil deeds sheltered also this deed of pious charity, Beatrix went forth with a Christian woman named Lucina. It is not recorded by what means the bodies were recovered. Perhaps some poor boatman, himself secretly a believer, assisted them; perhaps the current had borne the remains to a place where they could be reached from the bank. We know only that the brave women accomplished their purpose, and that Simplicius and Faustinus were laid reverently in the earth.

There was no funeral procession, no music, no throng of mourners. A whispered prayer took the place of solemn hymns, and the trembling light of a covered lamp shone instead of funeral torches. Yet surely that humble burial was not without honour. The love of a sister and the blessing of Heaven were there.

For seven months Beatrix remained concealed among faithful friends. Those months must have been full of anxiety, since the sound of footsteps at the door, the entrance of a stranger, or the careless speech of an acquaintance might have betrayed her. At last a man who desired to obtain possession of her property denounced her as a Christian. Thus covetousness, which has so often clothed itself in the garments of law and loyalty, became the servant of persecution.

Beatrix was seized and required to worship the idols.

“I am a Christian,” she replied; and neither fear nor persuasion could move her from that confession.

She was cast into prison and there strangled. Lucina, the companion of her former act of devotion, obtained the body and buried it beside those of Simplicius and Faustinus. Thus the sister who had ventured her life to give her brothers a grave came at last to share it; and the three who had been united in affection and in faith were not divided in death.

Many centuries after the brave deed of Beatrix, and in a land nearer our own knowledge, another loving woman dared the wrath of a powerful king for the sake of the mortal remains of one dearest to her. The persecutor was not a pagan emperor, but a Christian sovereign; the victim was not an obscure Roman believer, but one of the most learned and distinguished Englishmen of his age; and the daughter whose dutifulness we are to remember was Margaret Roper, the best beloved child of Sir Thomas More.

The household of Sir Thomas at Chelsea was one of the happiest in England. Learning did not dwell there with severity, nor religion with gloom. His children were taught Latin, Greek, music, philosophy, and Holy Scripture; but they were taught also to love one another, to be kind to servants, to pity the poor, and to receive correction without bitterness. Sir Thomas believed that the mind of a daughter was no less worthy of cultivation than that of a son—an opinion then far from common—and Margaret rewarded his care by becoming a woman of rare learning and excellent judgment.

The house itself, standing near the Thames, was often filled with scholars, statesmen, priests, travellers, and poor petitioners who knew that its master would listen patiently to their troubles. There were books and disputations for the learned, merry jests for the young, food for the needy, and affectionate welcome for all honest guests. Sir Thomas loved birds and animals; he enjoyed music; he joined in the harmless amusements of his children; and though burdened with grave public duties, he carried into his home that cheerful spirit which is often the companion, not of a light conscience, but of a well-governed one.

Margaret, whom he sometimes called Meg, understood him better than any other member of the family. Lady More was loyal and well-meaning, but practical, plain-spoken, and little able to comprehend why a man should bring ruin upon himself for a distinction which appeared to her unnecessary. Margaret perceived that to her father conscience was not a convenient opinion, to be changed when danger approached, but the inward tribunal before which kings and subjects must alike stand.

Sir Thomas had risen high in the favour of Henry VIII. He was a lawyer, a scholar, a diplomatist, and a man of singular wit; and the King, who delighted in brilliant companionship when it did not oppose his will, often treated him with remarkable familiarity. He visited Chelsea, walked in the garden with his arm about More’s neck, and appeared to love him as a friend.

Some, observing these marks of royal kindness, congratulated Sir Thomas upon his influence. He knew Henry better.

“I thank our Lord,” he said in substance, “that I find his Grace my very good lord; but I have no cause to be proud thereof, for if my head could win him a castle in France, it would not fail to go.”

The saying may appear harsh, but it was prophetic. Royal favour is a frail shelter when the will of the sovereign has become a law unto itself.

The great question of the King’s divorce now disturbed England. Henry desired to put away Queen Katherine of Aragon and marry Anne Boleyn. Sir Thomas would not approve the divorce; and when the King went farther, rejecting the authority of the Pope and claiming supremacy over the Church in England, More found that he could no longer retain his office without seeming to support measures which he believed unlawful.

He therefore resigned the chancellorship.

The surrender cost him much. He lost honour, influence, and income; but these were of less account than the sadness of knowing that the King, whom he had served faithfully, would regard independence as disobedience. The family at Chelsea, accustomed to a generous household, was obliged to practise economy. Sir Thomas gathered his children and told them that they must descend by degrees from their former manner of living, perhaps at last to the plain fare of humble folk.

They could endure poverty; the greater trial was fear.

Still, for a time, domestic affection softened every care. More studied, prayed, jested with his family, and avoided needless interference in public affairs. He did not seek martyrdom. This must be remembered, for true courage is not rashness, nor does conscience require a man to run toward danger when he can honourably remain at peace. He was willing to be silent concerning matters on which speech might be construed into treason; but he would not purchase safety by speaking what he believed to be false.

Attempts were made to entangle him in accusations. He was charged with having encouraged those who opposed the King, and with other transgressions which could not be proved. Friends advised submission; enemies hoped to terrify him; and his family, who knew the peril, must often have longed for some innocent means by which he might satisfy the royal demand. But when at last he was summoned to take an oath acknowledging the arrangements made by Parliament, including the King’s supremacy in ecclesiastical matters, he could not swear.

On the morning when the summons called him from Chelsea, Margaret accompanied him to the river. He had commonly shown affection at parting; now, perhaps because his heart warned him that he might not return, he was unusually silent. The boat moved away, bearing him toward Lambeth, while Margaret watched from the bank.

He examined the oath carefully. There were portions to which he might have consented, but its full meaning offended his conscience. He did not condemn those who took it; he asked only that he himself should not be compelled to swear against his judgment.

This moderation did not save him. He was committed to the Tower.

Now came the long trial of those who loved him. Lady More visited him and, unable to understand his refusal, reproached him with remaining in prison when he might return to his pleasant house, his books, his garden, his wife, and his children.

“Tilly vally, tilly vally!” she exclaimed. “Wouldest thou sit here among rats and mice when thou mightest be merry at Chelsea?”

The words were foolish, yet they sprang partly from affection. Sir Thomas answered gently, though with his accustomed humour. He asked her whether Chelsea was not as near heaven as the Tower. His meaning was plain: a few more years of earthly comfort could not compensate for the loss of inward peace.

Margaret understood.

She visited her father whenever permission could be obtained, and letters passed between them when visits were forbidden. His writings to her were affectionate, playful, and devout. He desired that she should not grieve beyond measure; he praised her tenderness, instructed her in patience, and placed his fate in the hands of God. She, on her part, urged every argument which love and conscience could allow, yet never tempted him to an act which she knew he would regard as sinful.

It is easy, when danger is remote, to praise an unyielding conscience; but the matter becomes harder when obedience to conscience brings suffering upon wife and children. Whether or not we share every opinion held by Sir Thomas More, we must honour the man who, after learning, prayer, and grave deliberation, refused to save his life by an oath which he believed untrue. He did not ask others to die for his scruples. He accepted the danger himself.

His imprisonment grew stricter. His books and writing materials were taken away, and the narrow chamber, once made tolerable by study, became more desolate. Yet his spirit did not fail. When persuasion had proved useless, he was brought to trial for treason.

The tribunal before which he stood was not likely to acquit a prisoner whose condemnation had been determined by the royal will. More had guarded his speech, relying upon the ancient maxim that silence signifies consent rather than opposition; but a former acquaintance swore that he had openly denied the King’s supremacy. Sir Thomas declared the testimony false, and its circumstances have caused many to doubt it. Nevertheless, the jury returned the expected verdict.

Only then, when legal caution could no longer preserve him, did he speak plainly. No temporal ruler, he said, could justly claim the spiritual authority demanded by the King. Sentence of death was pronounced.

As he was led away, Margaret forced her way through the guard and came to him on the Tower wharf. Forgetting the watching officers, the hostile government, and the dignity with which sorrow is expected to clothe itself, she threw her arms about her father’s neck and kissed him.

“God bless thee, Meg,” he said.

She released him, but love overcame her once more. She turned, broke again through the soldiers, and embraced him a second time. Those who stood by were moved, for even men accustomed to scenes of cruelty could not behold unmoved the parting of such a father and daughter. Sir Thomas, fearing that her grief would overpower her, bade her have patience and remember that they should meet again where no king could divide them.

On the morning of his execution he dressed in his best garment, as though going to a festival. The officer attending him objected that the executioner would receive the clothes, and More therefore exchanged them for plainer attire, though he sent the headsman a piece of gold.

He was weak from imprisonment, and when he reached the scaffold the steps shook beneath him.

“See me safe up,” he said to an officer, “and for my coming down let me shift for myself.”

Even at the edge of death his wit remained; but it was not the jesting of a careless man. It was the serenity of one who had already surrendered life and therefore could not be robbed of it.

He prayed for the King, spoke briefly to the people, and asked them to bear witness that he died in the faith of the Catholic Church and as the King’s good servant—but God’s first. Then he laid his head upon the block. As he arranged himself, he moved his beard aside, observing that it had committed no treason.

The axe fell.

His body was buried, according to one account, in the chapel of St. Peter within the Tower, though his family afterwards connected his remains with Chelsea; but the head was set upon a pole on London Bridge, where the heads of those called traitors were exposed as warnings to the multitude. Such spectacles were intended to dishonour the dead and terrify the living. Yet cruelty often defeats its own purpose. The head which the government displayed in shame became, in the eyes of faithful hearts, an emblem of integrity.

Margaret could not endure that it should remain there, beaten by rain and wind and gazed upon by the idle crowd. How the precious relic was obtained is not certainly known. One story says that she bribed the bridge-keeper to remove it; another, more dramatic, declares that when the head was cast down to make room for some new victim of royal displeasure, she was waiting below and received it into her hands. It is far more likely that money and the aid of friends accomplished secretly what no public appeal could have gained. But whatever the manner, Margaret secured her father’s head.

The act was dangerous. To honour one whom the King had condemned might itself be interpreted as opposition to the King; and to remove an object placed there by authority could be called a grave offence. Margaret knew this, yet filial love made her resolute.

She caused the head to be carefully embalmed and preserved it in a casket. She did not regard the sad relic with the horror which might have seized a less steadfast nature. It was her father’s; that was enough. The familiar voice was silent, the wise eyes closed, and the smile which had brightened Chelsea could never return; but what remained had once formed part of the person she had loved most dearly on earth.

Her possession of the head became known. She was summoned before the royal council and questioned. Here was an opportunity to escape by denial or evasion, but Margaret had learned truthfulness from him whose death she mourned.

She admitted openly that she had preserved it.

The councillors might have sent her to prison, and perhaps some among them desired to do so; but either her courage shamed them, or the government feared the indignation that cruelty toward a bereaved daughter might awaken. She was dismissed without punishment.

For the remaining nine years of her life Margaret kept the casket near her. She was a wife and mother, and did not neglect the living through devotion to the dead; for true affection enlarges the heart rather than narrowing it. Yet the memory of her father remained the governing reverence of her life. His letters were treasured, his teachings repeated, and his example held before her children.

When she died, still comparatively young, the head of Sir Thomas More was placed with her in the Roper burial-place at Canterbury. Thus the faithful daughter carried to her grave that which she had rescued from public insult, as Beatrix had once borne her brothers from the waters of the Tiber to consecrated earth.

The two women were separated by more than twelve hundred years. Beatrix was an obscure Roman maiden; Margaret was learned, married, and connected with the great affairs of Tudor England. Yet their actions sprang from the same noble root. Neither possessed armies, office, nor political authority. They had only love, faith, and courage; but these proved stronger than the decrees of emperors and kings.

Antigone honoured her brother because the unwritten laws of duty seemed greater than the command of Creon. Beatrix and Margaret did the like with a fuller hope. To them the grave was not the end, nor the poor body merely a thing cast off and forgotten. They believed that affection purified by faith survives death, that conscience must not bow before power, and that those who lose their lives for truth are not lost.

Earthly rulers had spoken; but these faithful women appealed to a higher tribunal. And history, which has nearly forgotten the officers who guarded the prisons and the councillors who framed accusations, still remembers the sister beside the Tiber and the daughter upon London Bridge—the Christian Antigones whose love was stronger than fear.

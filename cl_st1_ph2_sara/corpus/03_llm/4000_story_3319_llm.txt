## INDIAN SUMMER

The October afternoon had dressed itself for a fête without consulting any committee.

Every maple along the south road wore a cloth-of-gold mantle; the beeches stood pale and stately against the blue; chestnut burrs snapped underfoot like tiny firecrackers; and the low sun, slipping between the branches, laid long bars of amber across the greensward. Beyond the hill rose the chimneys and gray gables of Miss Auden’s School, where one hundred and seven girls were at that moment supposed to be engaged in useful exercise.

Six of the senior class were engaged, instead, in sitting upon a stone wall and lamenting.

“The entire term is wrecked,” declared Massachusetts.

Massachusetts always spoke in capitals, even when discussing shoe-strings.

“Not the entire term,” corrected Tennessee, who was perched on the wall with the composure of a little brown owl. “Only Friday.”

“Friday is the term. Founders’ Friday is the year. The senior play is the crown of Founders’ Friday. Therefore”—Massachusetts waved both arms and narrowly escaped falling backward into a sumach bush—“the crown has tumbled into the dust, and education is a hollow mockery.”

“Dixi,” said Tennessee. “Now may we descend from the platform?”

Maine, class president, paid no attention to either of them. She sat with her elbows on her knees and her chin in her hands, staring unhappily at the toe of one brown boot.

The senior play had collapsed that morning. Virginia, who was to have been Rosalind, had developed mumps; New York, the only person capable of remembering the Duke’s speeches, had been summoned home to a wedding; and the costumes, sent from Boston, had gone by mistake to Miss Alden’s Select School for Young Ladies in Auden, Minnesota. The express company hoped that something might be done. Massachusetts had observed bitterly that something was always hoping to be done in the manner of Mr. Micawber, but never did itself.

“We cannot give *As You Like It* without Rosalind,” said Maine for perhaps the twelfth time.

“Massachusetts would make a lovely Rosalind,” suggested Georgia.

“I should make a powerful Rosalind,” Massachusetts corrected. “But the audience might mistake me for Orlando.”

“They might mistake you for the Forest of Arden,” said Tennessee.

Massachusetts hurled a chestnut burr at her.

Maine sighed.

“We must substitute something. Miss Auden says the senior class may arrange any entertainment it likes, provided it is dignified, inexpensive, and does not keep the younger girls awake after ten.”

“Then nothing remains but prayer,” said Tennessee.

“Or a lecture,” groaned Georgia.

“Or tableaux!” cried Vermont.

“Tableaux are dead,” Massachusetts announced. “I have been Faith, Hope, Charity, Liberty, Joan of Arc, Night, Morning, and the Spirit of New England. I refuse to be allegorical again.”

“You were never Charity,” Tennessee murmured.

Maine sprang suddenly to her feet.

“A fancy ball!”

The burr-throwing ceased.

“A what?” demanded Massachusetts.

“An informal costume ball in the assembly hall. Every girl must come as something connected with autumn—an apple, a football player, a frost, a witch, a school-book if she can explain it. We shall have games and dancing and perhaps a grand march. Mademoiselle can play the piano. The juniors can decorate with corn-stalks and pumpkins. It will cost almost nothing, and no one need learn a line.”

There was a moment of awed silence. Then Massachusetts leaped from the wall.

“Maine,” she cried, “you are canonized! I always said there was genius under those pine needles.”

“You never said anything of the kind.”

“I thought it. What shall I be? A codfish in autumn?”

“A codfish has no seasons,” said Tennessee.

“Then I shall be Cape Cod after the summer visitors have gone. A deserted piazza, two broken tennis rackets, and a bill from the grocer.”

“Please be something that can pass through a door,” begged Maine.

They all began talking at once.

Georgia would be a bunch of grapes. Vermont considered a haystack. Tennessee proposed to go as the First Day of School, dressed in black and carrying instruments of torture. Massachusetts announced that she would represent a Thanksgiving dinner, though she had not yet determined whether to be the turkey or the indigestion.

Talking about pretty clothes is always soothing, even when the clothes are to be made of cheesecloth, tarlatan, and articles borrowed from the gymnasium. In five minutes the ruined term had been restored, Founders’ Friday saved, and Rosalind’s mumps reduced to a trivial incident in the march of civilization.

Then Maine gave a little cry and darted down the slope.

“Oh, look! Look here!”

At the edge of the woods, behind a screen of hazel, grew a thicket whose branches were covered with brilliant scarlet leaves. They were not merely red; they possessed the deep, glowing color of embers when the flame has just withdrawn—a scarlet so rich that the girls gathered about them with exclamations.

“They are gorgeous!” said Georgia.

“They are Napoleonic,” said Massachusetts. “They conquer everything in sight.”

Maine broke off a small spray and held it against her blue serge blouse.

“I shall be October. I can wear my yellow dress, with these leaves around the skirt and in my hair. There are enough branches to make a mantle.”

“And you will look like the calendar over the library desk,” said Tennessee. “Only more respectable.”

“We mustn’t strip the whole bush,” Maine said conscientiously. “I’ll come for what I need to-morrow. No one else is likely to find it behind all this hazel.”

“No one but us,” said Vermont.

“And Chicago,” added Georgia in a lower voice.

“Chicago?” Maine turned. “Where is she?”

“Wasn’t she following us?”

“She was,” said Tennessee. “She disappeared when Massachusetts began her funeral oration over the play. A reasonable act.”

At that moment, not twenty feet away, Chicago stood behind the trunk of a beech and heard every word.

Chicago’s name was not really Chicago any more than Maine’s was Maine. It was a custom at Miss Auden’s to give the girls geographical names according to their homes; and since two Helens, three Marys, and four Elizabeths inhabited the senior corridor, the custom was useful as well as picturesque. Chicago had entered school only three weeks before. She had expected to be admired because she came from a great city and possessed a seal ring, a traveling clock, and a knowledge of restaurants. Instead, she had discovered that Massachusetts was funnier than she, Tennessee cleverer, Georgia prettier, and Maine more important without seeming to know it.

The discovery had not improved her temper.

Presently the girls settled again on the wall, and Maine drew a notebook from her pocket.

“We need a committee for decorations, one for refreshments, and one for music.”

“Put Chicago on refreshments,” Georgia suggested. “She says she can make fudge.”

“Chicago says she can do everything,” Massachusetts replied. “She told me yesterday that our gymnasium is a chicken coop compared with the one at her former school.”

“She is rather—” Vermont hesitated.

“Rather what?” asked Maine.

“Slippery.”

“No,” said Georgia. “She is prickly.”

“She gives me the feeling of being examined by an Inquisitor,” said Tennessee. “If I say good-morning, she suspects a hidden insult.”

“She does try to bite one’s head off,” Massachusetts agreed. “What animal bites everything?”

“A snapping turtle,” offered Vermont.

“A mosquito.”

“A wolf.”

“A snake,” said Maine absently, writing “Refreshments—Chicago.”

The word had scarcely left her lips before she looked up, distressed.

“Oh, I didn’t mean that! What a horrid thing to say! She is new and lonely, and we have not been half kind enough. I’ll ask her to serve on the chief committee with me.”

Behind the beech tree Chicago’s cheeks burned.

A snake!

So that was what they thought her—a slippery, biting snake! Maine could make the insult and repent it in the same sweet breath, and they would all admire her conscience. That was Maine’s way. If Maine stepped on a beetle, the class praised her for feeling sorry; if Chicago offered an opinion, Massachusetts rolled her eyes and Tennessee made some cutting little speech.

Chicago drew back silently. A twig snapped beneath her shoe, but the group on the wall was now debating whether pumpkins, when hollowed, could safely hold candles indoors.

“I’ll show them who is slippery,” Chicago whispered. “I’ll show October how a snake can bite.”

She went home by the lower path, smiling for the first time that afternoon.

* * * * *

By Thursday evening the school had entered a delirium of preparation.

Corn-stalks marched from the kitchen garden to the assembly hall. Pumpkins appeared upon window ledges. The art room surrendered paste, colored paper, gilt paint, and two large rolls of yellow crêpe. Girls rushed through corridors carrying baskets, brooms, masks, and mysterious parcels wrapped in towels. A junior dressed as a chestnut was discovered wedged in a doorway and had to be removed by the janitor.

Mademoiselle Lafitte, asked to supervise the music, put both hands to her ears.

“Mes enfants, you say ze costume must be of autumn, but how shall I play when zis little Kansas comes as one scarecrow and place straw in my piano?”

“The straw can be taken out,” Maine assured her.

“Last year you say ze confetti can be taken out. I find him still in ze instrument at Easter.”

Maine laughed, promised that no scarecrow should approach within ten feet of the piano, and hurried away to the woods with Massachusetts and Tennessee.

The hazel screen was unchanged. The low sun lay warm upon it, and the path was drifted with clean brown leaves. Maine pushed through eagerly.

Then she stopped.

The scarlet thicket stood bare.

Every glowing branch had been cut away. Only hacked stems remained, pale and moist at the ends.

“Oh!” said Maine.

Massachusetts charged into the clearing.

“Where are they?”

“Gone.”

“Gone where?”

“If I knew that, they would not be gone,” said Tennessee.

Massachusetts examined the bushes as though she were a detective from Scotland Yard.

“This is robbery. Premeditated, malicious robbery. Observe the wounds! The villain used a knife.”

“Or scissors,” said Tennessee.

“A knife sounds better. Who knew of the leaves?”

“The six of us,” Maine answered slowly.

“And Chicago,” said Massachusetts.

“We don’t know that she heard.”

“She was on the path.”

“That proves nothing.”

“It proves she has feet.”

Maine bent over the ravaged stems. Her disappointment was keen, for she had seen her costume complete in imagination: the yellow gown, the scarlet mantle, the crown of red leaves. But in a moment she straightened.

“There is no use being tragic. Perhaps some town person found them.”

“All of them?” Massachusetts snorted. “A town person with the soul of a Roman conqueror?”

“We have three hours before supper,” Maine said. “We’ll find something else.”

They searched the hill and meadow. Scarlet leaves there were in plenty, but none with the same deep brilliance, and most fell from their stems when touched. At last Tennessee found a bank of goldenrod still bright in a sheltered hollow. Brown oak leaves lay nearby, glossy and firm.

“Yellow and brown are October colors,” she said.

“So are mud and melancholy,” Massachusetts replied. “But Maine shall be beautiful if I have to paint every leaf personally.”

They returned laden with goldenrod, oak leaves, bittersweet, and dried grasses. In the senior sitting room a dozen hands went to work. The yellow gown was looped with brown; a broad sash was covered with golden flowers; and Maine made a little cap from oak leaves and clusters of bittersweet.

“It is prettier than the other would have been,” Vermont declared loyally.

“It is not,” said Massachusetts. “But it is artistic, which is what people say when they mean something ought to be prettier.”

Miss Auden, passing the open door, paused to admire the costume.

“How charming! Goldenrod and oak. Very suitable, Maine.”

“Do you know what the scarlet bush was, Miss Auden?” Maine asked. “The one beyond the south wall, behind the hazel?”

“The shrub with crimson autumn foliage? It may be dogwood.”

“Dogwood!” Massachusetts dropped a spool. “Not poison dogwood?”

“Possibly. I have not seen it closely. There is a form of sumach there, too, which country people call poison dogwood. You should be careful in handling strange plants.”

Miss Auden continued down the corridor, leaving sudden silence behind her.

Massachusetts stared at Maine.

“You were going to wear it!”

“I handled a spray, and nothing happened.”

“It sometimes takes hours,” said Massachusetts with gloomy relish. “My cousin Horatio once touched poison dogwood. First his hands turned crimson; then his ears swelled until they stood out like carriage lamps; then his eyes closed, his nose disappeared, and he had to breathe through a quill.”

“Massachusetts!” protested Vermont.

“True as Plymouth Rock. For three weeks no one knew him except his mother, and she recognized him only by his socks.”

“Your cousin Horatio had poison ivy,” said Tennessee.

“He had everything. He was a very receptive boy.”

Maine looked troubled.

“If some town person took those branches—”

“She will discover botany by practical experiment,” said Tennessee.

“We ought to tell Miss Auden.”

“And say what? That an unknown person stole an unknown plant which may possibly be poisonous? She would send the gardener to cut every shrub on the hill.”

Maine went to the window. Below, girls were carrying a papier-mâché moon across the courtyard. Chicago stood beside the gymnasium steps, wearing a long cloak buttoned closely to her throat. As Maine watched, Chicago glanced up, saw her, and turned quickly away.

“Maine,” Tennessee said, “did Chicago know what you meant to wear?”

“She may have heard us. I’ll ask her.”

But Chicago had disappeared by the time Maine reached the courtyard.

Inside her room she locked the door and flung off the cloak.

Upon the bed lay the stolen branches, freshened in pitchers of water, their scarlet leaves glowing against the white spread. She had spent half the night wiring them into a wreath, a girdle, and long festoons for her gown. The effect, she knew, would be magnificent.

At first she had intended merely to spoil Maine’s costume. Then a better revenge occurred to her. She would wear the leaves herself. Maine, appearing in some makeshift dress, would see Chicago sweep into the hall in all the glory that had been planned for October.

She had chosen the name “Indian Summer.” It sounded languorous and splendid.

A faint red mark showed upon one wrist, but Chicago rubbed it impatiently.

“Those old stories are nonsense,” she said. “Maine handled the leaves.”

When a knock came, she drew the cloak over the bed.

“Who is it?”

“Maine.”

“What do you want?”

“I wondered if you had seen anyone near the scarlet bushes yesterday.”

“No.”

Chicago’s answer was so sharp that the little word might have cut paper.

“Miss Auden thinks they may be poisonous,” Maine continued through the door. “If you happen to know who took them, please warn her not to wear them.”

“I said I don’t know anything about them.”

“I’m sorry to bother you.”

Maine’s footsteps retreated.

Chicago stood motionless. For an instant she wished she had opened the door. Then she remembered the word *snake* and the laughter on the hill—though, strictly speaking, no one had laughed after Maine said it.

“She only wants the leaves back,” Chicago told herself. “She thinks she can frighten me.”

The scarlet branches rustled faintly on the bed.

* * * * *

On Friday evening the assembly hall became a realm of happy absurdity.

Autumn leaves veiled the lamps. Jack-o’-lanterns grinned from safe positions upon the platform. Apples swung from strings; ears of corn ornamented the walls; and over the piano hung a banner reading:

**WELCOME, FOUNDERS, FROSTS, AND FOOTBALL HEROES!**

Mademoiselle had caused “AND SCARECROWS” to be crossed out.

The costumes surpassed all expectation. Massachusetts appeared as Thanksgiving Dinner in a brown turkey-tail, a necklace of cranberries, and a pasteboard platter around her waist. Tennessee, as the First Day of School, wore black robes and carried an alarm clock, a pile of examination papers, and a birch rod borrowed from the primary classroom.

“You are not autumn,” Massachusetts objected.

“I return with every autumn,” Tennessee replied. “Nothing is more inevitable except your appetite.”

Georgia came as Purple Grapes, Vermont as a Red Apple, and Kansas, deprived of access to the piano, rustled contentedly as a scarecrow by the door.

Then Maine entered.

The goldenrod had kept its color; the oak leaves made a quaint, pointed border; bittersweet berries glowed in her brown hair. There was nothing brilliant about the dress, but it possessed the mellow charm of late fields and warm, quiet woods. The girls applauded.

“Miss October!” cried Massachusetts. “Come and embrace the turkey.”

“You will crush your platter.”

“My platter is symbolic. It can endure affection.”

Maine laughed, but her eyes strayed toward the door. Chicago had not yet appeared.

The orchestra—Mademoiselle at the piano and two juniors with violins—began a march. Couples formed amid jokes and protests. Just as Miss Auden raised her hand for silence, the hall door opened.

Chicago stood upon the threshold.

There was a little gasp.

Her gown was of flame-colored tarlatan, cut low over a white blouse, and from shoulder to hem it was covered with scarlet leaves. Leaves made a deep collar about her neck, leaves crowned her dark hair, leaves trailed from her arms in long, glowing garlands. Against the soft yellow lamps she seemed clothed in fire.

“Indian Summer,” she announced.

Maine turned pale.

Massachusetts’s cranberry necklace snapped. The berries pattered over the floor.

“You!” she exclaimed.

Chicago smiled triumphantly.

“Do you like my costume?”

“Those are the branches,” Maine said. “You took them.”

“I found them.”

“You cut every one.”

“They did not have your name on them.”

“No,” Maine answered. “But they may be poison dogwood. Take them off quickly.”

A few girls laughed nervously, supposing this part of some senior joke. Chicago lifted her chin.

“Jealousy is unbecoming to October.”

“I am not jealous. Miss Auden said the shrub might be poisonous.”

“Miss Auden said it might be dogwood,” Tennessee put in. “There is a difference, if you are collecting facts.”

Massachusetts thrust forward her turkey-tail.

“My cousin Horatio—”

“I have heard about your cousin,” Chicago interrupted. “Maine handled the leaves yesterday, and she is not dead.”

“Please,” Maine urged. “At least take them away from your face and neck.”

Chicago’s triumph had cost her too much to surrender. Everyone was looking at her; everyone had seen that she was more splendid than Maine. She would not strip off the leaves and stand in an undecorated red dress while the others whispered.

“They do not hurt me in the least,” she said. “Will the march begin, Miss Auden?”

Miss Auden crossed the hall and examined a spray.

“I do not recognize it with certainty. Chicago, it would be wiser to remove the foliage.”

“But the costume will be ruined!”

“Then remove the collar and wreath. You may retain the leaves upon the skirt until we can ask Mr. Phelps.”

Mr. Phelps, the gardener, had gone to town. Chicago unpinned the collar ungraciously, but refused to give up the wreath. The march began.

For fifteen minutes she enjoyed her victory. She walked at the head of the line with Kentucky, a football hero in an enormous sweater, and watched her scarlet reflection pass each window. The younger girls stared; the teachers smiled; even Mademoiselle cried, “Mais, she is très magnifique!”

Then Chicago’s wrist began to itch.

She rubbed it against her skirt.

By the time the grand march ended, the itching had spread up both arms. Her neck burned. She slipped into the cloakroom, looked in the mirror, and saw a red patch along her jaw.

Still she would not confess.

She washed her hands in cold water, powdered her neck, and returned for the Virginia reel. But halfway down the set she missed a figure and collided with Massachusetts’s platter.

“Mercy!” Massachusetts cried. “Your face!”

“It is hot in here.”

“Your left ear is twice the size of your right.”

Chicago put up a hand. The ear felt strange and thick.

Maine came quickly.

“Chicago, you must go to Miss Auden.”

“I don’t need—”

Her voice broke. Her eyelids were puffing; angry blotches covered her throat. Now that fear had entered her mind, the itching seemed unendurable. She clawed at the wreath.

“Get them off! Get them off me!”

Maine tore away the leaves while Tennessee called Miss Auden. In another minute the hall’s comedy dissolved into alarm. The younger girls were sent to continue the reel. Mademoiselle played with heroic loudness. Miss Auden led Chicago upstairs, while the school nurse followed with towels and bottles.

Massachusetts remained in the corridor, white beneath her turkey beak.

“Horatio did not really breathe through a quill,” she whispered.

“No one supposed he did,” said Tennessee.

“I wish I had not said it. Do you think Chicago heard?”

“She heard enough without Horatio.”

Maine stood holding the scarlet wreath. Red marks had appeared where the leaves touched her fingers.

“I ought to have known,” she said.

“You warned her,” Tennessee replied.

“I ought to have known yesterday. I found the bush. I planned to wear it. If I had not talked about the costume, she would never have taken the branches.”

“If she had not listened behind trees, stolen what did not belong to her, and ignored three warnings,” Massachusetts said bluntly, “she would now be dancing instead of resembling Cousin Horatio.”

“Massachusetts!”

“I omit the quill. But justice is justice.”

Maine looked at the closed infirmary door.

“Yes,” she said softly, “and pain is pain.”

The physician arrived from town within the hour. Chicago’s case, he said, was severe but not dangerous. Her eyes were almost closed, her hands terribly swollen, and every patch of skin touched by the leaves burned and smarted. She must remain in a darkened room, quiet and cool, while lotions and time did their work.

“May I see her?” Maine asked the nurse.

“She does not wish to see anyone.”

“Not even me?”

“Particularly not you.”

Maine went downstairs.

The birthday ceremony was about to begin. Founders’ Friday always ended with the school’s birthday cake—seven tiers, one for each decade of its existence—and the lighting of seventy small candles. The senior president carried the first taper, spoke the traditional words, and cut the first slice. It was the proudest duty of the year.

Miss Auden met Maine at the stair.

“You are needed in the hall, my dear.”

“Could someone else carry the taper?”

Miss Auden looked at her attentively.

“Why?”

“Chicago is alone.”

“She asked to be alone.”

“I know. But I think that is because she is ashamed. It is worse to be ashamed in the dark than merely to be ill there.”

For a moment Miss Auden said nothing. Then she handed the silver taper to Tennessee, who had followed them.

“You may take Maine’s place.”

Tennessee accepted it gravely.

Massachusetts seized Maine’s arm. “You cannot miss the cake, the song, and the seventy candles for a girl who stole your leaves!”

“They were not my leaves.”

“They were morally your leaves.”

“And if she had not taken them, I should have worn them. I might be where she is now.”

“But you would have removed them when warned.”

Maine smiled faintly.

“Do carry my cake upstairs afterward, old codfish.”

Massachusetts made an indignant gobbling sound—her codfish identity being somewhat compromised by the turkey costume—but her eyes were bright.

Maine entered the darkened infirmary without knocking. Chicago lay rigid beneath the sheet, her bandaged hands resting on top. Only one shaded lamp burned.

“I said I didn’t want anybody,” she muttered.

“I know.”

“Go away.”

“No.”

Chicago turned her swollen face toward the wall.

Below them the school orchestra began the stately Founders’ March. The sound rose through the floor, softened by distance.

“You will miss everything,” Chicago said after a moment.

“Yes.”

“The candles?”

“Yes.”

“And cutting the cake?”

“Tennessee will do it beautifully, though she may compare education to a funeral.”

Chicago was silent. Maine drew a chair near the bed.

“I am not going to talk about the leaves,” she said. “I am going to tell you what Massachusetts looks like as a Thanksgiving dinner.”

“I don’t care.”

“Her turkey-tail is crooked, the platter has a crack across the gravy compartment, and she has lost eleven cranberries. Mademoiselle stepped on three.”

Against her will, Chicago made a small sound that was nearly a laugh and immediately became a sob.

“I hate you,” she whispered.

“No, you don’t.”

“I stole the branches because you called me a snake.”

“I know. I am dreadfully sorry I said it.”

“You didn’t know I heard.”

“That makes it worse, not better. If I had said it to your face, you could have answered.”

Chicago shifted miserably.

“I wanted to spoil your costume.”

“You did. Then the class made me another.”

“I wanted everyone to think I looked more beautiful than you.”

“You did look more brilliant.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

“No,” Maine agreed.

From below came a roar of applause. The seventy candles had probably been lighted. Chicago’s mouth trembled.

“Why are you sitting here?”

“Because you shouldn’t be alone.”

“You want to show how good you are.”

Maine considered this with her usual conscientiousness.

“Perhaps a little,” she admitted. “One cannot always separate motives as neatly as ribbons. But chiefly I came because I thought you might like company after you had finished telling me to go away.”

Chicago gave another strangled laugh.

Maine began an account of the ball: Kansas’s encounter with the doorway, Vermont’s apple seeds, Georgia’s grapes, Mademoiselle’s war against straw. She described Massachusetts’s cousin Horatio as he might have appeared at the height of his mythical illness, with carriage-lamp ears and a quill in his mouth, until Chicago laughed in earnest and then cried because laughing hurt.

Afterward Maine talked of clothes. She described the blue silk gown Virginia had intended to wear after the play, the absurd hat in the town milliner’s window, and a traveling coat Chicago herself possessed and which Maine had secretly admired. Talking about pretty clothes was as soothing in an infirmary as on a stone wall. By degrees Chicago’s rigid shoulders relaxed.

A knock sounded. Massachusetts entered backward, still wearing her turkey-tail and carrying a tray.

“One slice of birthday cake,” she announced, “one glass of lemonade, and no cranberries. The patient is forbidden cake, so Maine must eat it with offensive enjoyment.”

“I don’t want it,” Maine said.

“Then I shall eat it with offensive enjoyment.”

Tennessee appeared behind her, holding the extinguished silver taper.

“The ceremony survived,” she reported. “I spoke the words, cut the cake, and did not compare education to anything.”

“A barren performance,” said Massachusetts.

She came to the bedside and looked at Chicago without flinching.

“How do you feel?”

“Hideous.”

“That is not a feeling. It is a condition, and temporary. I have often been hideous without illness.”

Chicago stared at her through swollen lids.

“I suppose you know I stole the leaves.”

“The school knows. Schools know everything before breakfast.”

“You all despise me.”

“Certainly not,” Massachusetts said. “We are too busy admiring Maine. It is extremely annoying. She has missed seventy candles on your account and will become more conceited every minute.”

“I am not conceited,” Maine protested.

“That is the most conceited part of you.”

Tennessee leaned against the door.

“You must get well quickly, Chicago. The class requires someone on the refreshments committee, and you still claim to make superior fudge.”

Chicago looked from one face to another. They were not pretending that she had done nothing wrong; that would have been easier to resent. Nor were they punishing her with righteous silence. They were offering, in their absurd fashion, a way back.

“I do make good fudge,” she said faintly.

“An assertion requiring proof,” Tennessee replied.

“And when your hands are well,” added Massachusetts, “you may make six pounds. Four for the class, one for the infirmary, and one for me as damages to my cranberry necklace.”

“Five pounds,” Chicago said.

“Five and a half.”

“Five.”

“Done. Dixi. Maine, eat your cake.”

Later, when the physician had returned and pronounced Chicago more comfortable, the three girls went downstairs together. The hall was nearly empty. Candles smoked upon the great cake; autumn leaves had fallen from the walls; a solitary pumpkin still grinned from the platform.

Massachusetts linked one arm through Maine’s and the other through Tennessee’s.

“You missed the grandest moment of senior year,” she said.

“I know.”

“And gave your cake to an invalid who could not eat it.”

“You ate it.”

“That is immaterial. Maine, your conscience is a public nuisance.”

Tennessee lifted the silver taper like a sceptre.

“Nevertheless, I propose a new school emblem: not the pine tree, but Maine sitting in a dark room while everybody else has a good time.”

“That would make a very dull seal,” Maine said.

“It would make a good one,” Massachusetts answered decidedly. “A pine tree merely stays green. Any vegetable can manage character if it is rooted to the spot.”

Maine laughed and pulled them toward the stairs.

“Come along, codfish.”

Massachusetts gobbled majestically.

And behind them, on a chair by the door, the discarded scarlet wreath lay shriveled and harmless, its splendid leaves already turning brown.

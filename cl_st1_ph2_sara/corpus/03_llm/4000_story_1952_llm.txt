## The Passenger We Lost

“You can believe it or leave it,” said the night watchman, settling deeper into the shadow beside the warehouse door. “I ain’t asking nobody to believe it. I’ve kept it quiet from 1884 till this blessed night, and I wouldn’t tell it now only you spoke disrespectful of sea-sarpints, which is a thing no man ought to do without seeing one fust.

“There was twenty-three of us aboard the *George Washington*, Liverpool to New York, steam and auxiliary sail, Captain Biddle commanding. I was ordinary seaman then, though I done work which would have made an eggstraordinary seaman complain. Stuttering Sam was lookout, and Joe Cooper was there too. You remember Joe?”

I said that I did not.

“Well, you’d remember him if you’d seen him. Joe wasn’t bad-looking in the common way. He’d gone past that. His eyes looked like they’d quarrelled and took separate lodgings, his nose had a list to port, and his mouth was that wide it seemed to go round behind and fasten with a button. When he laughed—which wasn’t often—you wondered whether his face was opening or coming to pieces.

“But Joe was sensitive. That’s the curious part. A handsome man can stand being called ugly because he knows it’s jealousy, but Joe had no such comfort. If you looked at him sudden, he’d ask what you meant by it. If a child cried in the street, Joe took it personal.

“Well, we was six days out, with a dirty grey sea running and a little fog hanging over it, when Stuttering Sam comes tumbling down from the fo’c’s’le head.

“‘S-s-s-s—’ he ses.

“‘Spit it out,’ ses the mate.

“‘S-s-s-s—’

“‘Ship?’ ses the mate.

“Sam shakes his head.

“‘Squall?’

“He shakes it agin.

“‘Supper?’ I ses, because it was near four bells and Sam could generally say supper without obstruction.

“‘S-s-sea—’ he ses.

“‘Sea?’ ses the mate. ‘That’s right, Sam. Plenty of it. Been there since breakfast.’

“Sam’s face was white, and his finger was pointing ahead.

“‘S-s-sea s-s-s—’

“Then something lifted out of the fog, and Sam finished the word without a stutter.

“‘SERPENT!’

“It shows you what fear can do for a man’s education.

“At fust I thought it was a spar standing upright, only spars don’t turn their heads and look at you. Then I thought it was a crane, only no crane ever had a head the size of a longboat and teeth like belaying pins. It stood twenty feet above the water, black-green and dripping, with a mane of weeds down its neck and two pale eyes as big as cabin scuttles. Behind the head there was humps running away through the fog, one after another, till you couldn’t tell where the animal ended and the Atlantic Ocean begun.

“The second mate was holding the wheel, the skipper was holding his breath, and the fust mate was holding me. I didn’t object at the time.

“Captain Biddle found his voice fust.

“‘All hands on deck!’ he ses.

“‘What for?’ ses the mate.

“‘Discipline,’ ses the skipper. ‘If it boards us, every man must be at his proper station.’

“‘And what’s your station?’ ses the mate.

“‘Aft,’ ses Captain Biddle, moving that way.

“The sarpint come on, slow and solemn, with its head swaying from side to side. There was a lot of talk afterward about how brave everybody had been, but I remember Oily Dick trying to climb into the ventilator, and the bosen putting a bucket over his head so he wouldn’t see himself swallowed. As for me, I stood firm till there was somewhere to run to, and then I was as ready as any man.

“‘Reverse engines!’ shouts the skipper.

“‘If we reverse,’ ses the mate, ‘we’ll back straight into the rest of him.’

“‘Full ahead, then!’

“‘We’re doing full ahead.’

“‘Then do it fuller,’ ses the skipper.

“The creature was nearly alongside now. It opened its mouth, and the skipper ducked behind the chart-house.

“Nothing happened.

“It shut its mouth and opened it agin.

“‘He’s going to roar,’ whispers Oily Dick from inside the ventilator.

“But it didn’t roar. It made a little noise like a cow what had lost confidence in itself.

“The mate stared at it.

“‘That beast ain’t savage,’ he ses. ‘It’s hungry.’

“Captain Biddle put half his face round the chart-house corner.

“‘How do you know?’

“‘Same expression as the crew,’ ses the mate.

“There was a loaf lying on the galley hatch, so the mate took it and held it up. The sarpint’s eyes followed the loaf. The mate tossed it overboard, and that enormous head went down as gentle as one of them swans in Victoria Park. It picked up the bread without wetting it hardly, swallowed, and looked for more.

“‘Bring provisions,’ ses the captain, coming out now the danger was puffected.

“The cook fetched six loaves. We threw them one by one, and the sarpint took every loaf. Then we gave it a side of pork, two sacks of damaged potatoes, a cheese that had already caused trouble among the hands, and some biscuits rejected by a missionary society as being too hard for heathens. It ate the lot.

“‘There,’ ses Captain Biddle. ‘I have saved the ship by courage and presence of mind.’

“‘You was behind the chart-house,’ ses the mate.

“‘A commander must choose a place where he can observe all sides,’ ses the skipper.

“When there wasn’t no more food coming, the creature dropped back. We thought that was the end of him, but at sunset Sam sighted the humps astern. Next morning he was there again, swimming alongside as cheerful as a kitten, if a kitten was eighty fathoms long and had a head you could keep coal in.

“We fed him breakfast. He had three hams, a barrel of flour, and most of the cook’s private onions. At dinner he took salt beef, and at tea he swallowed a crate of oranges without peeling them. After that he wouldn’t leave us.

“By the second day the men was used to him. We leaned over the rail and scratched his nose with boat hooks. He’d roll on his side and show a belly as white as a churchyard wall. Once he dived under the ship and come up to starboard, and Captain Biddle said he had inspected our bottom and found it satisfactory. The mate said if he’d found it satisfactory, he couldn’t know much about ships.

“The skipper begun calculating. You could always tell when he was calculating because his lips moved and every man guarded his wages.

“‘Mr. Briggs,’ he ses to the mate, ‘what would the public pay to see that animal?’

“‘A shilling a head,’ ses Briggs.

“‘Nonsense. Two shillings.’

“‘Children half price?’

“‘Children double,’ ses the skipper. ‘They take more room.’

“He walked up and down, rubbing his hands.

“‘We shall tow him into New York. We shall exhibit him. Biddle’s Leviathan, the Only Genuine Sea Monster in Captivity. We’ll have posters. Scientists will pay five dollars merely to disagree about him.’

“‘How’ll you catch him?’ I asks.

“He looked offended, because that was the part he hadn’t calculated.

“The mate worked it out. We had four shark hooks aboard, big forged things, and wire rope enough to rig a bridle. We’d fasten the hooks into sides of pork and let the sarpint swallow them; then we’d bring him alongside, pass the wire round him, and tow him through the Narrows. Once at the wharf, we could pen him between barges.

“‘Suppose he objects?’ ses the bosen.

“‘He’s timid,’ ses Captain Biddle.

“Which was true. Though ugly enough to frighten a lighthouse, that sarpint was scared of every sudden noise. When a block fell on deck, he disappeared for half an hour. When the cook dropped a tray, he went down so quick his tail stuck up in the air like a mast. And when the steam whistle sounded in the fog on the third morning, he vanished till nearly noon.

“That worried the skipper dreadful.

“‘No more whistles,’ he ses. ‘No bells, no guns, no unnecessary hammering. We must preserve his confidence.’

“‘How do we strike the watches?’ asks Briggs.

“‘Quietly.’

“‘And if there’s fog?’

“‘We proceed without it.’

“‘Without fog?’

“‘Without noise,’ ses the skipper. ‘Don’t be foolish.’

“So from then on, time aboard the *George Washington* was conducted in whispers. Instead of eight bells the bosen held up eight fingers. The engine-room was told not to let the machinery clank, which cheered them men wonderful. The cook wrapped his pans in blankets. Even Stuttering Sam was ordered not to finish a word too sudden.

“We was getting near Sandy Hook, and the sarpint had grown uncommon friendly. He knew the skipper, he knew the mate, and I believe he knew the meal hours better than the ship’s clock. But there was one man he didn’t take to, and that was Joe Cooper.

“Whenever Joe come near the rail, the beast drew back and stared. Joe tried offering him a biscuit, but the sarpint sank till only his eyes showed. Then Joe leaned farther out, and the creature gave that unhappy cow-noise and dived.

“The skipper seen it.

“‘Cooper!’ he ses. ‘Get aft!’

“Joe turns round. ‘What for?’

“‘Your face is frightening the animal.’

“Nobody laughed. We might have laughed another time, Joe being Joe, but there was something nasty in the captain’s way of saying it.

“Joe went red from his collar to the roots of his hair.

“‘My face?’

“‘Yes, your face. Keep it away from the rail. In fact, remain below while the serpent is alongside.’

“‘I got as much right to show my face as anybody.’

“‘Not that face,’ ses Captain Biddle. ‘It might cost us a fortune.’

“Joe looked round at us, and his mouth trembled.

“‘Frightened the sea-sarpint,’ he ses. ‘Me! Frightened a sea-sarpint!’

“Then he walked forward, very stiff, and went below.

“The mate shook his head.

“‘You shouldn’t have said that, sir.’

“‘Discipline,’ ses Captain Biddle.

“‘That ain’t discipline.’

“‘Then it’s zoology,’ ses the skipper, ‘and he can’t object to science.’

“Half an hour later we heard a disturbance in the fo’c’s’le. Joe was lying on the deck, kicking his heels and making terrible noises. There was foam round his mouth, his eyes was rolled up, and every few seconds he shouted, ‘Frightened the sea-sarpint!’ and went stiff as a marline-spike.

“We carried him to a bunk. The steward fetched water. Joe come round, looked at us sorrowful, and whispered, ‘Did I frighten anything?’

“‘No, Joe,’ I ses.

“‘Not even a little innocent worm?’

“‘No.’

“‘Then leave me,’ he ses. ‘I ain’t fit for human company.’

“The news spread, and all hands blamed the skipper. Captain Biddle come down, not because he was sorry, but because he feared Joe’s noises would scare the sarpint.

“‘Stop that fit,’ he ses.

“Joe immediately had another.

“‘Frightened the sea-sarpint!’ he howls, biting the edge of the mattress.

“‘You see?’ ses the skipper. ‘The man’s insubordinate.’

“‘Looks ill to me,’ ses Briggs.

“‘Then he’s insubordinately ill.’

“For the rest of that day Joe had fits at intervals. He had one when the captain passed him, one when supper was late, and one after the bosen said Joe looked better. Each fit was more eggstraordinary than the last. In one he spoke French, though nobody knew enough French to tell whether it was French or not. In another he claimed to be his own grandmother. But always he finished with, ‘Frightened the sea-sarpint!’ and fell back exhausted.

“I was symperthizing with him till I noticed his foam smelt clean. That was suspicious. Sailors in a Liverpool steamer don’t often smell clean by accident.

“So that night I watched. Just before a fit, Joe slipped a bit of soap into his mouth and chewed it. Then he rolled his eyes, threw himself on the deck, and begun drumming with his boots.

“Afterward I took him aside.

“‘Joe,’ I ses, ‘you’re shamming.’

“He looked at me with one eye. The other was naturally looking somewhere else.

“‘Would a broken-hearted man sham?’

“‘He might if he had soap in his pocket.’

“He was quiet a moment.

“‘You going to tell?’

“‘I dunno.’

“‘The captain says my face frightened that beast,’ ses Joe. ‘Before the voyage is over, I’ll show him what ought to be frightened.’

“I should have reported it. That’s plain now. But a man don’t like to interfere with another man’s complaint, particularly when the complaint has been caused by his captain. Besides, I thought Joe only meant to give Biddle a shock, perhaps by appearing sudden in his cabin. Nobody could foresee what followed, except Joe, and he wasn’t explaining.

“The next morning the skipper had the hooks prepared. Each hook was buried in a side of pork, with wire rope seized to the shank. The wire led through blocks to the steam winch. It was a lovely arrangement, and cruel enough to be called scientific.

“We sighted land late that afternoon. The sarpint was still with us, taking bread from the mate’s hand. Captain Biddle was so excited he promised every man a share of the profits, though he didn’t mention how small a share.

“‘At dawn,’ he ses, ‘we bait the hooks. By breakfast the monster is ours. By dinner the whole civilized world knows Captain Biddle’s name.’

“‘It knows Washington’s now,’ ses the mate, ‘but I don’t hear him bragging.’

“The fog came down after dark, thick as wool. We slowed to half speed, but we couldn’t use the whistle because the skipper feared the sarpint would run. No bell was rung. No gun was fired. We moved through that fog as quiet as a thief in a pantry, with the biggest living creature in creation swimming somewhere close to our quarter.

“Joe had the wheel from midnight till four.

“I was on lookout, and about three o’clock I heard him groan.

“‘What’s wrong?’ I whispers.

“‘Coming on,’ he ses.

“‘Don’t you start none of that at the wheel.’

“‘Can’t help it,’ he ses. ‘Captain broke my nervous construction.’

“Then he dropped to his knees, though he kept one hand on a spoke.

“‘Joe!’ I ses. ‘Get up!’

“He begun to shake. His face twisted, which didn’t improve it, and foam appeared on his lips.

“‘Frightened—’ he gasps.

“The mate come running, and behind him Captain Biddle in his nightshirt.

“‘Stop him!’ ses the skipper. ‘The animal is alongside!’

“And he was. Out in the fog, level with the bridge, that great dripping head appeared. The pale eyes looked peaceful and trusting. He was so close I could hear the water running off his scales.

“Joe saw him.

“‘Frightened the sea-sarpint!’ he shrieks.

“Then he threw both arms wide. One hand caught the foghorn lanyard.

“Now our horn wasn’t no penny trumpet. It was a new steam foghorn built to warn ships five miles away and wake people ten years dead. Joe wrapped the line twice round his wrist, all accidental-looking, and fell backward.

“The blast that followed lifted the hair on my head.

“BWAAAAAAAAAAAH!

“The fog jumped. The bridge shook. Captain Biddle’s nightcap flew off and went aloft. Stuttering Sam, asleep below, shouted a complete sentence with no trouble whatever.

“The sarpint’s eyes opened till they was nearly all eye. For one second he stood straight up beside us, and I saw more of him than I ever wanted. Then he turned.

“There was a splash like Ireland falling into the sea. His tail came over, struck the water, and sent a wave across our deck. The pork baits washed overboard, the shark hooks tangled in the rail, and Captain Biddle sat down in a coil of wire.

“‘Stop that horn!’ he screams.

“But Joe was NOT KNOWING WHAT HE WAS DOING. He held on tighter.

“BWAAAAAAAAAAAH!

“Far off in the fog we heard another tremendous splash, then another, each fainter than the last. After that there was nothing.

“The sarpint was gone.

“We searched all morning. We cruised in circles till the passengers thought New York had moved. We put out bread, pork, onions, and the last of the missionary biscuits. Captain Biddle even ordered the cook to throw over the cheese, though the mate said no affectionate creature would return after that.

“But he never did.

“Joe recovered as soon as the horn line was cut from his wrist. Captain Biddle accused him of doing it deliberate, but Joe looked shocked.

“‘Me, sir? A sick man? A man whose ’orrible face frightens dumb animals?’

“There wasn’t no proof. I alone had seen the soap, and I held my tongue, partly because the skipper deserved it and partly because Joe looked at me in a way which suggested another fit might carry me overboard.

“We reached New York with four empty shark hooks, several miles of wire rope, and no sea-sarpint. The skipper told the owners the provisions had been lost in heavy weather. The crew told people the truth, but nobody believed us, especially after Oily Dick improved the creature by giving it wings and saying it had sung hymns.

“That’s why the scientific world knows nothing about it to this day.

“And Joe?” asked the warehouse clerk.

“Joe left the sea and went into a travelling circus. They paid him to sit in a cage under a board saying THE MISSING LINK, and he made better wages than any of us. Captain Biddle retired poor, having spent years watching the Narrows with a side of pork in his hand.

“So there it is,” said the night watchman, rising and stretching. “Every word true, and the moral’s plain. A man ought never to be rude, not even to the humblest and ugliest among us, because you never know when there may be a valuable sea-sarpint alongside and a foghorn handy.”

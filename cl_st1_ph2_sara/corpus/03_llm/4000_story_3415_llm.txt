## The Judgment of the Two Doors

In the very olden time, when the roots of civilization had begun to take hold in the soil of barbarism, but had not yet produced anything more substantial than a few bright blossoms and a considerable number of thorns, there lived a king whose notions were large, florid, and untrammelled. He possessed a mind which, being incapable of doubt, was admirably fitted for government. What he desired became policy; what he disliked became treason; and what occurred to him in the morning was generally carved upon stone before nightfall.

He was not wholly barbarous. He loved music, provided it was loud; architecture, provided it was enormous; poetry, provided it praised him; and justice, provided he might arrange it himself. His people, who had not yet acquired the troublesome habit of comparing one reign with another, esteemed him magnificent.

Among the king’s institutions, none gave him greater satisfaction than his arena of impartial judgment.

This amphitheatre was a vast circle of pale stone, with ascending seats for thirty thousand persons, a canopied throne at the center of the royal gallery, and, opposite that throne, two tall doors placed side by side in the wall. These doors were precisely alike. The same bronze nails ornamented them; the same carved suns spread their rays across their panels; the same red curtains hung above them. No spectator could distinguish one from the other, and this was regarded as an important feature of their fairness.

When a subject was accused of an offense of sufficient importance to interest the king, he was not wearied with witnesses, arguments, precedents, or any of those devices by which justice is sometimes obscured. He was brought into the arena and required to open one of the two doors.

Behind one waited a tiger.

This animal was selected with scrupulous care from the royal menagerie. It was always the largest, fiercest, and hungriest beast available; for the king considered that, if guilt were to be punished, it should be punished without hesitation or defect. When the accused opened the fatal door, the tiger sprang upon him. Iron bells tolled from the eastern tower; professional mourners wailed in harmonious companies; and the spectators departed with solemn faces, speaking to one another of the terrible certainty of retribution.

Behind the other door waited a lady.

She was chosen by officers of the court as a suitable wife for the accused. No attention was paid to any previous attachment he might have formed, for private preference was held to be an undisciplined sentiment and therefore unworthy of official encouragement. If the fortunate man opened this door, the lady advanced, veiled and garlanded; brass trumpets sounded; children scattered flowers; and a priest married the pair immediately, before either could weaken the grandeur of the occasion by reflection.

Thus the accused received either punishment or reward, and received it from his own hand. Nothing could be more impartial. If he were guilty, Providence would naturally direct him toward the tiger; if innocent, toward the lady. Should events appear to contradict this principle, it was understood that the error lay in human appearances and not in the system.

The populace greatly admired the institution. Trials filled the city with expectation. Bakers sold little cakes shaped like doors; gamblers laid wagers upon the right and left; philosophers disputed whether the condemned man truly chose, since he lacked the useful information upon which choice is ordinarily founded. The king listened to these debates with bland enjoyment. To him the matter was plain. The man chose a door. What followed was justice.

Now this king had a daughter.

The princess was beautiful, ardent, and imperious. Her eyes were dark and brilliant; her hair had the heavy luster of midnight; and her will, though enclosed in a more graceful form, was scarcely less absolute than her father’s. From him she had inherited a fervid blood and a contempt for obstacles. From her dead mother, who had come from a gentler province across the mountains, she had received certain refinements of feeling which complicated, without cooling, the native fires of her nature.

It was her custom to descend from the women’s garden at twilight and walk among the cypress terraces below the palace. There she encountered a young courtier.

He was of low rank, though not of ignoble bearing. His father had been an officer of horses, his mother the daughter of a musician, and he himself served among those attendants whose duties brought them frequently into royal chambers but never to royal tables. He was handsome in that open and unmistakable fashion which causes old men to distrust a youth and young women to forgive him before he has offended. He was brave, intelligent, and poor.

The princess loved him.

He loved the princess with an equal ardor and with considerably more danger. Their affection advanced by secret looks, hurried meetings, and whispered vows beneath trees that, being accustomed to court life, revealed nothing. For many months they enjoyed that peculiar happiness which belongs to lovers who possess everything except safety.

But walls have openings, servants have suspicions, and kings have ears wherever they have money. The affair was discovered.

The young man was seized before dawn and thrown into prison. The princess was confined to her apartments. When the king heard the full account, he was astonished—not that his daughter had loved, for even monarchs concede the occasional existence of passion, but that she had loved beneath the altitude prescribed for her.

His anger was immense. Yet it soon took on a genial radiance, for he perceived that the case was perfectly suited to his tribunal.

The courtier would enter the arena.

If he opened the door of the tiger, the offense against majesty would be appropriately cleansed in blood. If he opened the door of the lady, his innocence would be established and he would marry at once—though naturally not the princess. In either event, the improper romance would be concluded.

The king was delighted by the symmetrical excellence of this arrangement. He ordered a public holiday.

The appointed day became the chief subject of the kingdom. The tiger was chosen from seven beasts and kept without food until its roar shook dust from the vaults. The lady was selected with equal diligence.

She was young, tall, and exquisitely fair. She belonged to an ancient family and served among the princess’s attendants. Her beauty was of the cool and luminous kind that poets compare to moonlight, generally because moonlight cannot object. She had pale-gold hair, a gentle voice, and eyes which lowered themselves with such modest grace that no one could be certain what they had observed before lowering.

The princess hated her.

The feeling was not founded upon beauty alone, though that would have been sufficient. More than once the princess had seen the lady speaking with the young courtier. More than once she had observed a smile pass between them—or what might have been a smile, which is sometimes worse. Once, in a corridor hung with blue silk, she had seen the lady’s hand rest for an instant upon his sleeve.

It might have been accidental.

Jealousy is not appeased by possibilities.

From the moment the lady was named, the princess’s mind burned with one determination: she must discover which door concealed the tiger and which the bride.

The royal secret was guarded with unusual rigor. The keepers of the arena slept in locked chambers. The doors were assigned only on the morning of the trial. The officials who arranged the spectacle were forbidden to speak, under penalties ingenious enough to secure a broad and durable silence.

But the princess was her father’s daughter. She possessed gold, influence, terror, beauty, and that directness of purpose which renders all five doubly effective. A keeper was bribed; an officer was threatened; a page who knew nothing was made to believe he knew much and thereby led her to one who knew everything. Before sunrise on the appointed day, the princess had learned the secret.

The tiger waited behind the left-hand door.

Behind the right waited the lady.

Having obtained this knowledge, the princess discovered that knowledge does not always bring peace.

All that morning she sat alone. Before her inner sight arose the image of the arena. She saw her lover standing upon the sand. She saw him look toward her. She saw her own hand move.

To the right—the lady.

The door would open. The fair creature would come forth in white and silver, blushing with a beauty made more intolerable by triumph. The multitude would rise. Trumpets would ring. Flowers would descend. The priest would lift his hands, and the young man—her young man, whose vows had trembled against her ear in the cypress shadows—would be joined to another.

Would he refuse?

He could not. The king’s ceremonies permitted no interruption, and a groom who rejected the judgment of the arena might find that the tiger remained available.

Would he hate the lady?

Perhaps at first. But she was beautiful. She was gentle. She had admired him already; of that the princess was certain, although certainty is often the finest garment suspicion wears. The lady would comfort him. Days would pass; grief would soften; memory would fade at its edges. There would be a house, a garden, evenings by a lamp. Perhaps children.

How the princess loathed those unborn children!

She saw the lady’s pale head bent beside his dark one. She heard the people praising their fortunate union. She imagined herself passing them at some future festival, majestic and remote, while the lady made a low obeisance and the courtier dared not lift his eyes.

Her soul became white with heat.

Then, from the other moiety of her imagination, came the tiger.

The left-hand door opened. A striped body shot into the sun. There was the roar, the leap, the falling figure. She heard shrieks from the multitude; she saw the sand torn and darkened; she saw the hands that had held hers struggle beneath terrible claws. Iron bells sounded. Mourners wailed. At sunset the arena servants washed away the blood.

She pressed her hands over her eyes.

Death was irrevocable.

Marriage also seemed so.

When noon approached, the princess was summoned to the royal gallery. She dressed in crimson and gold, for she would not permit anguish to make her unbecoming. Her face was pale, but her step did not falter. She took her place beside the king.

The amphitheatre overflowed. Nobles glittered beneath awnings; citizens crowded the stone tiers; soldiers lined the walls. Above all stretched a sky of pitiless blue. The two doors stood opposite, calm, equal, inscrutable.

The king lifted his hand.

A trumpet sounded.

A gate beneath the royal gallery opened, and the young courtier entered the arena.

A murmur moved through the multitude, for he was even nobler in appearance than rumor had promised. Imprisonment had made him pale, but not broken. He wore a plain tunic; his hands were unbound. He walked to the center of the arena and bowed to the throne.

The king smiled upon him with grave benevolence.

Then the young man turned his head.

He did not look at the doors. He looked at the princess.

He had expected her to know. He understood the force of her nature, the reach of her rank, the fierce completeness of her love. He had never doubted that she would discover the secret which the whole kingdom desired in vain. Nor did he doubt that she would tell him.

Their eyes met.

In his was one question.

Which?

There was no time for prayer, explanation, or farewell. Beneath the crimson folds of her robe, the princess’s right hand lay motionless. The audience watched the young man; the king watched the doors; but the young man watched only her.

She raised her hand and made a slight, swift movement toward the right.

No one else perceived it.

He did.

Without hesitation, he crossed the arena. His trust was so complete that he did not look back. A deep silence fell, spreading from tier to tier until even the restless banners seemed to hang without motion. The young man reached the right-hand door. He placed his hand upon its bronze ring.

For one instant he stood there.

Then he pulled the door open.

And here the action of our history ends, though its question does not.

What came forth?

The lady—or the tiger?

It may be said that love must have directed the princess toward mercy. She had risked much for the young man; she had endured secrecy, fear, and her father’s anger; she had lain awake imagining his death until the darkness itself seemed striped with yellow and black. Could she deliberately consign that beloved form to claws and teeth? Could she hear his last cry and know that her finger had commanded it? Love, replies the tender-hearted reader, would save him at any cost. Therefore she pointed to the lady.

But let us consider the cost.

The princess was not a maiden of mild blood, bred among quiet customs and instructed to find nobility in surrender. She was semi-barbaric, as was her age, her father, and the fierce foundation of her soul. She loved not faintly but possessively. To her, the young man was not merely one whose happiness she desired; he was hers. The thought of his death was horrible, but it ended all rivalry. The thought of his marriage went on—day after day, night after night, year after year.

She had seen the lady.

She knew the lady’s radiant face would appear when the right-hand door opened. She had imagined the sudden joy in those lowered eyes, the blush, the outstretched hand. She had imagined the multitude shouting approval while another woman received the life the princess had saved. Would the rescued man, in the first gladness of escape, look upon his bride with gratitude? Would gratitude ripen? Would he come, in time, to love her?

And had not the lady smiled upon him before?

Ah, that remembered smile!

Yet the tiger! The hot breath, the flashing teeth, the blood upon the sand! Could jealousy remain steady before such a vision? The princess knew precisely what waited behind the left-hand door. She had heard that beast roar beneath the arena in the hours before dawn. She knew its hunger. She knew that the man trusted her utterly.

Perhaps that trust compelled mercy.

Perhaps it made vengeance irresistible.

Some will say that, because she directed him to the right, she must have directed him to safety; for in many minds the right hand is naturally associated with virtue, good fortune, and wedding bells. But the arena cared nothing for symbols. Its doors had been assigned by officers, not angels. The right contained what it contained, and the princess knew it.

She had made her decision during the long night. The gesture had been instantaneous, but it was not unconsidered. Behind it lay every kiss beneath the cypresses, every whispered vow, every imagined shriek, every jealous glance, every child not yet born. In that small movement lived the whole divided inheritance of her nature: tenderness and dominion, devotion and fury, refinement and blood.

The young man trusted her.

The door opened.

From behind it came—

But no; the answer is not mine. I am acquainted with the machinery of the arena, with the king’s agreeable theories, with the tiger’s hunger, with the lady’s beauty, and even, to some degree, with the fires that consumed the princess. Yet the final secret belongs to a tribunal more subtle and perhaps no less arbitrary than the king’s.

Search, then, fair reader, not the chambers behind the doors, but the darker chambers of the heart.

Which came forth—

the lady, or the tiger?

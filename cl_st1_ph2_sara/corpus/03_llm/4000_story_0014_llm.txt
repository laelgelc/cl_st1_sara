The last evening before they opened the Cherokee Strip, Hennessey had more men in it than houses, and more dust than either. Wagons stood wheel to wheel along the road; horses were tied to fence posts, porch posts, wagon tongues, and anything else that promised to remain in the country overnight. Families slept under wagon sheets. Men slept wherever they fell. Every fellow who owned a canteen had it full, and every fellow who owned a map had his thumb planted on the best quarter-section.

We cowmen had held that country so long that it seemed unnatural to see it measured off for farmers. Yesterday it was grass from horizon to horizon, with a trail herd stringing across it and the wind free to go where it pleased. Tomorrow there would be stakes, sod houses, wire, and men disputing over where one hundred and sixty acres quit and the next one began.

There were seven of us in the back room of a saloon, all old trail hands and all liable to be out of employment by noon next day. Ace Gee sat with his chair tipped against the wall. Pink Larkin was there, also Sandy, Buckskin Joe, a long Texan called Muley, and two brothers from the Canadian who never agreed except when another man disputed either one.

“What are you aiming to do, Ace?” Sandy asked. “Stay with cattle after tomorrow?”

“What cattle?” said Ace. “A steer couldn’t turn around in this country next year without knocking down a schoolhouse.”

“You might go north,” said Pink. “Plenty of range in Montana.”

Ace shook his head.

“I have viewed Montana,” he said, “and Montana has viewed me. We parted without tears. I’m a boomer now. I aim to get me a claim, build a residence twelve foot square, and become an oppression to my neighbors.”

“You’ll have to farm,” Muley told him.

“I can farm. You plow the ground and put something into it. If it comes up, you brag; if it don’t, you blame the weather. That looks easier than handling cattle for men who count every steer twice and every dollar six times.”

Pink grinned. “You learned all you know about cow-bosses on that Circle Bar drive.”

“I learned what one cow-boss didn’t know,” Ace said. “That was an education extensive enough for any ordinary man.”

We called for another round, because a story starts easier when its teller has something before him, and Ace lowered his chair to all four legs.

“This was three summers ago,” he began, “when the Circle Bar sent twenty-six hundred mixed cattle from the Brazos country to Montana. They had twos, threes, dry cows, and about three hundred dogies that appeared to have been raised under wagons. The company was an eastern concern with offices in Chicago and confidence in paper. Their Texas superintendent was old Major Clune, who knew cattle when he could see them, but he took sick just before the outfit started.

“The company sent down a representative to take the herd north. His name was Mr. Horace Bellweather. He had been bookkeeper, assistant manager, purchasing agent, and, according to his own account, nearly everything except a man horseback. He came into camp wearing high boots that had never seen mud, a black shirt, a white collar, and a gun hung where it would hurt him most if his horse pitched.

“He had a florid face, little pale eyes, and some marks on his cheeks which Californy said looked like hail had once caught him outdoors. His saddle had silver corners and a horn as broad as a washpan. He announced at supper that he was foreman.

“‘The company has invested heavily in this undertaking,’ he said, ‘and expects strict discipline, accurate accounts, and immediate obedience.’

“Californy looked into his coffee and said, ‘That sounds reasonable, provided the cattle has been notified.’

“Bellweather didn’t hear him. He went on explaining organization. He had a little red book with all our names in it, though he had Pink entered as “Mr. Rose Larkin.” Pink never told him different.

“We had twelve hands, besides the cook and horse wrangler. Pink rode point on the left, I rode swing, and Californy held the right point. Californy was a quiet, narrow-built man from around San Luis Obispo. He talked mild and slow, and you could work with him six months without learning whether he liked you. But he could read cattle like a printed notice, and when he put a horse somewhere, that horse was where the work required.

“We gathered the herd, cut out the cripples and sore-footed stuff, and held them on the bed-ground three nights. Then Mr. Bellweather called for a tally. Pink and Californy counted them through a narrow place between two low rises, and they made twenty-six hundred and forty-one.

“Bellweather counted behind them. He made twenty-seven hundred and six.

“‘There is an error,’ he said.

“‘There sure is,’ Pink told him.

“‘You admit it?’

“‘Freely. But I wouldn’t hunt it on our strings.’

“Bellweather said he would count again, so we rounded the cattle and shoved them through once more. His second count was twenty-five hundred and eighty-three. Pink made twenty-six hundred and forty-one again, and Californy lacked Pink one steer because a brindle yearling doubled back under the drag.

“Bellweather got redder.

“‘This looseness is intolerable,’ he said. ‘The company cannot lose cattle by careless enumeration.’

“Californy said, ‘They ain’t lost yet. They’re standing there.’

“‘That is not the point.’

“‘No,’ said Californy. ‘The point is over yonder, and it’s waiting to start.’

“We trailed out the next morning, heading north by way of Doan’s Crossing. Bellweather rode at the head of the herd for two hours, looking important. Then the dust got him and he moved out to one side. By dinner he was riding with the wagon. By night he had discovered that a cow-boss could manage better from camp.

“For the first week we made eight or ten miles a day. The cattle were soft and wanted educating. We watered before the heat got high, grazed them through noon, and put them on the bed-ground full. Bellweather worried because we did not make fifteen miles.

“‘At this rate,’ he said, ‘winter will overtake us.’

“Pink answered, ‘At fifteen miles a day, death will overtake the cattle, and winter can have what’s left.’

“Bellweather pulled his red book. ‘The instructions contemplate a prompt delivery.’

“‘Them instructions got horns?’ Pink asked.

“‘Certainly not.’

“‘Then they’ll trail easier than these steers.’

“That was about the time we named him Bad Medicine. Mule Creek Ed named him, though Californy made the name stick. Any order Bellweather gave was liable to make somebody sick, usually a horse.

“He believed a man could move cattle by riding fast. If the drag fell back, he would run at them, waving his hat and yelling. That would scare up ten tired steers and leave fifty worse than before. He spoiled one good gray horse in four days, and when the horse got a lump under the saddle, he blamed the wrangler.

“‘This animal was sound when issued to me,’ he said.

“The wrangler, who was sixteen and had red hair, said, ‘He was sound when issued. He got unsound after you received him.’

“Bad Medicine reached toward his gun. It was a habit he had when language failed him, and language failed him regular. Pink stepped between them.

“‘Don’t shoot the boy,’ Pink said. ‘He’s the only one knows which horses you ain’t ruined.’

“Bad Medicine ordered Pink back to duty.

“‘Certainly, Mr. Medicine,’ Pink said. ‘Would you like me to go at a walk, trot, or according to written instructions?’

“From then on we called him Mr. Medicine to his face and Bad Medicine elsewhere. He knew there was disrespect in it but couldn’t locate enough words to prove it.

“We crossed Red River in fair shape and got into the Nations. Grass was good, creeks had water, and any sensible boss would have been satisfied. Bad Medicine was not built for satisfaction. He demanded another count.

“We told him cattle on trail was counted when there was reason—after a storm, a bad crossing, a mix, or when delivering. He said the company required daily accuracy.

“‘You can have a daily count,’ said Pink, ‘if you’ll stop the earth revolving long enough.’

“Bellweather ordered us to bunch the herd in a flat. We did, though it cost half a day’s grazing. Pink counted twenty-six hundred and thirty-nine. We had lost one lame cow at Red River and killed one steer with a broken leg. The figures were right.

“Bad Medicine made twenty-seven hundred and sixty.

“‘We have gained cattle,’ he announced.

“‘That’s prosperous,’ I said.

“‘It means we have picked up strays.’

“Californy looked over the herd. ‘Name one.’

“Bad Medicine said it was our duty to identify them.

“‘Every animal carries the Circle Bar,’ Pink told him. ‘If we picked up any, somebody branded them before we got there.’

“He had us count again. That time Bellweather made twenty-six hundred and twelve and accused us of letting forty-eight head escape during the operation.

“‘They escaped out of your arithmetic,’ said Californy. ‘There’s a hole in it wide enough to drive the remuda through.’

“Bad Medicine told Californy his insolence was being recorded.

“‘Write small,’ Californy said. ‘You got a long trip ahead.’

“Nothing serious happened until we were north of the Cimarron. One hot afternoon a storm built up in the west. It came black underneath and yellow above, with lightning jumping inside it. We had the cattle grazed out and were aiming for a sheltered bed-ground under a long slope. Bad Medicine said to hold them on open ground.

“Pink told him they would drift before the storm.

“‘My orders are to maintain a northward course,’ Bellweather said.

“‘The storm ain’t read your orders.’

“Bellweather swelled up. ‘Mr. Larkin, you will execute my instructions.’

“So we held them in the open. Near midnight the wind struck. It flattened the grass and picked dust out of the ground where there wasn’t any. Lightning cracked over the lead, and an old speckled steer jumped like a cat shot with birdshot. The rest went with him.

“A stampede don’t sound like thunder, though people say it does. Thunder rolls and quits. Running cattle make a steady, deep tearing sound, with horns knocking, hoofs drumming, men calling, and saddles squealing under horses that know one misstep can finish the whole concern.

“Pink and Californy got to the points. I was on herd with Mule Creek Ed. We rode forward and began bending the leaders. You don’t stop stampeded cattle by getting in front and presenting an argument. You edge them around until the front meets the flank, then keep the mill turning.

“Bad Medicine came racing up behind, shooting his pistol in the air.

“That was the one thing calculated to make matters worse, and he done it complete.

“Californy turned his horse across Bellweather’s line and caught the bridle near the bit. His voice was no louder than if he had been ordering breakfast.

“‘Put that gun away.’

“‘Release my horse! I am attempting to turn the herd.’

“‘You’re attempting to kill somebody, and you’re promising well.’

“Bellweather reached for Californy’s wrist. Californy twisted the bridle, and Bellweather’s horse sat down so sudden the silver on his saddle flashed in the lightning.

“‘Listen to me, Mr. Medicine,’ Californy said. ‘These cattle are running because you put them where we told you not to put them. They’re running harder because you’re firing a pistol where we told you not to fire one. The boys are risking their necks fixing your work, and they ain’t got spare necks. You draw that gun again tonight and I’ll take it away. You reach for it after I tell you once, and I’ll build a fire bright enough to read your company instructions by and melt that gun into the middle of it. If you come with the gun, you’ll likely get warmed too. Now ride behind me, slow, and don’t holler unless you catch fire.’

“Bellweather looked at him. Californy was still holding the bridle and speaking mild, but there are times when a mild man is scarier than a noisy one. Bellweather put the pistol away.

“We got the cattle milling and settled them near daylight. Four steers had broken legs, two went over a cutbank, and we spent all next day finding scattered bunches. Bellweather wanted a count before breakfast.

“Nobody answered.

“He asked again.

“Pink sat by the fire with his plate on his knees. ‘As acting representative of the men who are eating,’ he said, ‘I rule the count postponed.’

“Bellweather stared. ‘Acting representative?’

“Mule Creek Ed said, ‘He means foreman.’

“‘I am foreman.’

“‘Then Pink’s segundo,’ I said.

“‘I have appointed no segundo.’

“Californy sopped gravy with a biscuit. ‘We appointed one while you was shooting.’

“Bad Medicine looked from one face to another. He could have fired us, but then he would have had twenty-six hundred cattle and nobody to tell him which direction they were pointed. So he walked to the wagon and began writing in his book.

“For the next week we treated Pink as foreman whenever Bellweather could hear us.

“‘Mr. Larkin,’ I would say, ‘shall we water at the lower crossing?’

“Pink would rub his chin. ‘Consult the drag riders, examine the water, inquire of the cattle, and if all departments concur, proceed instanter.’

“Or Californy would ask, ‘Mr. Larkin, do you authorize me to change horses?’

“Pink would answer, ‘Only after filing a report on the horse you’re taking off, the horse you’re putting on, and the general condition of horses not involved.’

“Bad Medicine knew we were making game of him, but the herd began traveling better under Pink’s quiet management. Pink never gave more order than was needed. He rode up and down the line, watched the leaders, changed men before their horses played out, and selected bed-ground with shelter and grazing. A good cow-boss hires good men and lets them prove it. A bad one hires any kind and then tries to improve them by shouting.

“We crossed the Arkansas above the worst quicksand and pushed through Kansas. By then the cattle were trail-broke. They strung out pretty, watered without crowding, and laid down easy at night. We could make twelve miles without melting the fat off them.

“Bad Medicine took credit. He began asking Pink’s opinion, though he disguised it.

“‘I have decided to water at Beaver Creek,’ he would say. ‘Do you foresee any objection?’

“Pink might answer, ‘Only that Beaver is dry. There’s water six miles east in a fork of the Solomon.’

“Bellweather would look at his map upside down and say, ‘That was my alternative proposal.’

“After a while he depended on Pink for everything. Pink fixed his cinch, picked his horses, told him when to ride and when to remain in camp, and once wrote the daily report because Bellweather claimed dust had affected his eyes.

“Then in southern Nebraska, Bad Medicine got the hunting fever. We had seen antelope all week. He carried a new rifle in the wagon and talked about bringing meat into camp.

“‘Antelope are difficult game,’ he said. ‘They require intelligence.’

“‘Then you better hunt something else,’ Mule Creek Ed whispered.

“Bellweather announced that Pink would accompany him on Sunday while the herd rested. Pink said he would be honored. When Bad Medicine stepped away, Pink came to us.

“‘I’m going to cure him,’ he said.

“‘Of hunting?’ I asked.

“‘Of depending on me.’

“Sunday morning they rode out before sunup. Pink took a little buckskin that could travel all day. Bellweather rode a big bay with more speed than sense. They carried rifles, two canteens, and a lunch the cook put up.

“We loafed around camp, washed shirts, mended rigging, and watched the west. Near sundown Pink rode in alone. He unsaddled, rubbed down the buckskin, and went to the fire.

“‘Where’s the foreman?’ I asked.

“‘Which one?’

“‘The official article.’

“‘He’s hunting.’

“‘Alone?’

“Pink took coffee. ‘He is exercising executive independence.’

“We waited. Dark came and no Bellweather. The country there was rolling prairie cut with shallow draws, not dangerous if a man kept his head, but extensive if he didn’t. At nine o’clock the cook asked whether we should hunt him.

“Pink said, ‘No. Mr. Medicine has a rifle, matches, water, lunch, a horse, and authority. A man carrying that much should last till morning.’

“Californy asked how Pink lost him.

“‘We found antelope about ten miles west. He chased them. I told him not to. He said he knew hunting. So I let him know it. When he got over the ridge, I turned north, circled, and came home.’

“‘Suppose he keeps riding west?’

“‘His horse knows camp is east.’

“‘Suppose he don’t let the horse choose?’

“Pink shrugged. ‘That’s the lesson.’

“Bad Medicine came in next morning about an hour after sunrise. His bay was dragging the reins, and Bellweather was walking behind, holding the stirrup. He had lost his hat, scratched one cheek, and torn a sleeve. His face was gray under the red.

“Nobody laughed. A joke is best before the victim arrives; afterward it can turn common.

“He walked to the fire and said, ‘Coffee.’

“The cook poured it.

“After two cups he looked at Pink. ‘You deserted me.’

“Pink shook his head. ‘You ordered me to circle north while you pursued the game.’

“‘You knew I was unfamiliar with the country.’

“‘I had suspected it.’

“Bellweather’s hand moved toward the place where his pistol generally hung, but he had left the pistol in camp so it wouldn’t interfere with his rifle. Californy saw the motion.

“‘Careful,’ he said. ‘You might draw a rabbit.’

“Bellweather sat down. After a while he told us what happened. He had chased the antelope six miles, fired eight times, and hit Nebraska once. Then he tried to return, became turned around, rode until dark, and made a camp in a draw. His horse pulled loose in the night. He heard coyotes, saw eyes in the grass, and believed Indians were following him. At daylight he found the bay half a mile away and finally let the horse take the back trail.

“‘A terrible night,’ he said.

“Pink stirred his coffee. ‘A man can learn considerable after dark when nobody is handy to do his thinking.’

“Bellweather did not answer.

“We reached the railroad at North Platte about ten days later. The company had instructed Bellweather to telegraph from there. He went into town wearing his black shirt and what remained of his dignity. That evening he returned with a telegram.

“‘The company requires me to proceed ahead by rail,’ he announced. ‘Mr. Larkin will assume temporary command of the herd.’

“Pink asked, ‘Is that Rose Larkin or some other man?’

“Bellweather looked at him a long time. Then, to his credit, he nearly smiled.

“‘It is you,’ he said. ‘You seem to have been assuming it already.’

“‘Only during emergencies.’

“‘Then consider my absence an emergency.’

“He left next morning. Before he went, he gave Pink the tally book, the route papers, and the company funds. Pink counted the money in front of him and wrote a receipt.

“‘You don’t trust me?’ Bellweather asked.

“Pink said, ‘I trust you enough to count it once. You trusted us so little you counted the cattle every Thursday.’

“Bellweather glanced toward Californy, then mounted.

“‘Mr. Larkin,’ he said, ‘deliver the herd in good condition.’

“‘That’s been the general notion all along.’

“We never saw Bad Medicine again.

“From North Platte on, Pink ran the outfit proper. He shifted the point riders according to the country, kept fresh mounts under the night men, and rested the cattle before hard crossings. We went up through the sand hills, crossed the Niobrara, and angled northwest where grass cured sweet on the stem. We had one mix with a trail herd out of Fort Worth, but Pink and their boss set the brands straight without heat. We lost seven head in a boggy creek, three to lightning, and one old cow that simply laid down and resigned.

“When we reached the company range in Montana, the herd was big-framed, sound-footed, and fatter than when it left Texas. The range superintendent, a square old fellow named Dobbs, rode through them twice and said, ‘Who handled these cattle?’

“Pink pointed at all of us. ‘The outfit.’

“Dobbs said, ‘Who was foreman?’

“‘Depends what part of the trip you mean.’

“We told him some of it. Not all, because Bad Medicine was still company property, and a man shouldn’t damage another man’s livestock without cause. Dobbs studied the tally, compared the losses with the road report, and appointed Pink foreman of the Montana range on the spot.

“Pink was good at it. He never asked a man to ride a horse he wouldn’t ride, never sent a crew where he hadn’t looked first, and never counted cattle just to hear numbers. He stayed up there two years and might have owned the outfit by now if he hadn’t acquired a fondness for civilization.”

Pink, sitting across from Ace, lifted his glass.

“Civilization,” he said, “is anywhere a man can get pie after sundown.”

Sandy asked Ace, “Why didn’t you stay in Montana?”

Ace leaned back again.

“Too much walking.”

“Walking? On a cow ranch?”

“That company wintered cattle in brush country. We chopped ice, cut hay, mended sheds, dragged calves out of snowbanks, and hunted poor cows through willows where a horse couldn’t go. I discovered ranching is mostly cow work done afoot. Trail driving is horseback business, which is honorable. Farming may be worse, but I ain’t tried it yet.”

“And tomorrow,” Muley said, “you aim to run for land with ten thousand farmers.”

“I do.”

“What claim you picked?”

Ace tapped the side of his nose. “A good boomer never tells. I got a piece selected with water, grass, and enough timber to build a house if I can persuade the timber to stand close together.”

“You got a plow?”

“No.”

“Seed?”

“No.”

“Money?”

Ace looked into his glass. “I have prospects.”

“What kind?”

“Liquid, mostly.”

Outside, somebody fired three shots, and a wagon team broke loose in the street. Men yelled, women called children, and the whole town stirred like a herd feeling lightning. Tomorrow at noon the soldiers would move aside, guns would crack, and all that waiting mass would pour south over the line. Claims would be won, lost, jumped, traded, and fought over. The grass country we had known would begin turning into another kind of country before the dust settled.

But no man in our room offered a speech over it. Cowhands are suspicious of any sorrow that can be improved by talking.

Buckskin Joe raised his empty glass.

“If Ace is going to become a landowner,” he said, “we ought to drink to his future taxes.”

The Canadian brothers agreed for once.

Pink called toward the bar. “Seven more, in honor of agriculture.”

“Eight,” Ace corrected. “A farmer must provide against drought.”

We drank, and Sandy asked when we meant to eat.

“Before daylight,” Pink said. “A man ought not enter a land rush on an empty stomach.”

Ace nodded solemnly. “That’s sound management. Bad Medicine would have counted the biscuits first.”

So we went out together to find supper, leaving the maps, the arguments, and the future until morning.

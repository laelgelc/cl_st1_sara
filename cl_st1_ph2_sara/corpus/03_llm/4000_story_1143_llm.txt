### The Heroine Millie Ordered

Millie Whitcomb sold gloves at Bascom’s, which was not, as strangers sometimes supposed, a mere store. Bascom’s was the town directory, weather bureau, employment office, matrimonial agency, and court of appeals, with dry goods thrown in.

If Mrs. Phelps bought black ribbon on Tuesday, the clerks knew by Wednesday which relative was failing. If Carrie Bell asked to have pink lawn laid aside, the girls at the notion counter could tell you the date of the church picnic and the name of the young man expected to escort her. Bascom’s knew who was expecting, who was leaving, who had inherited, and whose husband had been seen taking coffee with the new milliner.

Millie knew more than most.

She was long, brisk, and sandy-haired, with a practical nose and a way of snapping tissue paper that made indecision seem immoral. She read every story I published and criticized each one across the glove counter.

“Your last heroine,” she said one afternoon, “was too beautiful.”

I had stopped to buy shoe laces. Millie considered it dishonest to enter Bascom’s for shoe laces and depart without discussing literature.

“Was she?” I asked.

“You said her hair was the color of autumn leaves.”

“Some autumn leaves are brown.”

“You didn’t mean brown. You meant coppery-gold, with glints.”

“I may have meant glints.”

“And her eyes changed with her moods. Gray when she was thoughtful, green when she was jealous, and blue when she put on that chiffon dress.”

“That was the light.”

“My land! Eyes don’t carry a sample card. And she had a little white throat.”

“Readers like throats.”

“They’d like a woman just as well if she had a regular neck. Why don’t you write about an ugly girl?”

“Because nobody would believe she was a heroine.”

“I would.”

“You say that now. By the third chapter you’d want her transformed. You’d have her take off her spectacles, put on a soft clinging gown, and turn out to be adorable.”

“I wouldn’t either.”

“You’d expect a millionaire to discover her hidden charm.”

“I’d expect something to happen.”

“That,” I said, “is the difficulty.”

Millie leaned over the counter.

“Write about Pearlie Schultz.”

Now there was a challenge.

Everybody in town knew Pearlie. She was the public stenographer in the little office above Binder’s drug store, between Dr. Murchison, Dentist, and Professor Bellini, Teacher of Mandolin, Guitar, and Artistic Expression. She typed letters for traveling men, insurance agents, farmers disputing bills, and ladies wishing to resign from committees without giving offense. She could spell “inconvenience,” punctuate a lawyer, and make the threat of a wholesale grocer sound like a courteous invitation.

And Pearlie was ugly.

There! You see how indecently bald the statement looks. In stories one is expected to approach such matters gently. One says that a girl is not beautiful in the ordinary sense, which means that she possesses extraordinary eyes; or that her charm reveals itself slowly, which means that a young architect is going to reveal it by chapter six.

No such dodge can be made for Pearlie.

Her hair was thin and pale, her nose insignificant, her chin doubled itself when she looked down, and her complexion had the discouraging color of dough before it has decided to rise. Her spectacles pinched her nose, and behind them her eyes were small, near-sighted, and kind.

Also—and Millie insisted that I should not cheat—Pearlie was fat.

Not plump. Not rounded. Not statuesque. FAT.

She had been fat at twelve, when her mother called it baby flesh; fat at sixteen, when her aunt predicted she would stretch out; and fat at twenty-eight, when even Aunt Louisa had ceased mentioning stretching.

Pearlie had fought.

She had rolled on the floor before breakfast, touched her toes two hundred times, swung Indian clubs, drunk hot water, cold water, sour milk, and something in a brown bottle whose label showed a slender lady escaping from a black silhouette twice her size. She had gone without potatoes for three weeks and gained four pounds. She had eaten only oranges for two days, then baked a pan of biscuits and consumed six while waiting for them to cool.

“Anybody’d do it,” she said. “An orange is no company.”

The trouble was that Pearlie could cook.

Oh, Pearlie could cook!

She could make a chicken pie whose crust rose in flaky golden hills above gravy fragrant with celery and pepper. Her rolls were so light that old Mr. Schultz claimed he had once lost one in a draft. She understood roast beef, pie plant, noodle soup, pickles, dumplings, preserves, spice cake, and the grave, sacred business of coffee. A cold potato looked at Pearlie and became a feast.

Her mother cooked by rules; Pearlie cooked by sympathy. She knew when a gravy felt neglected, when a pudding required encouragement, and when a roast ought to be left alone. She tasted with a thoughtful frown, added a pinch of something, and tasted again. Afterward she would say, “It isn’t much,” while her father loosened his collar.

Pearlie lived with her parents and two younger sisters in an old brick house at the edge of town. The Schultzes had dark furniture, bright curtains, geraniums in tin cans, and a dining table that could be enlarged until it seemed capable of seating the county.

Both sisters were pretty. Lena was pink and dimpled, and Frieda had black eyes and a waist that caused comment at the sewing circle. Young men called often. They sat in the parlor, knocked their knees against the center table, admired the family album, and presently drifted with one sister or the other toward the porch.

Pearlie made lemonade.

“Don’t you mind, honey?” her mother once asked.

“Mind what?”

“The boys coming to see the girls.”

“Why should I? They track mud, and one of them sings.”

But when Lena married, Pearlie worked three nights embroidering roses on the bridal linen. When Frieda became engaged, Pearlie planned the supper, baked the cake, and told everybody that the groom was a pippin. She was not jealous. That would have made things simpler. She was merely lonely.

There are women who receive no courtship and learn to call themselves fortunate. Pearlie tried. She praised independence. She said husbands snored and left wet towels about. She watched women with babies and remarked that children were wearing. She laughed first and loudest whenever someone spoke of her marrying.

Her laugh was not pretty. Her smile was worse. It pulled her face out of shape and showed too much gum. But people who were in trouble went to Pearlie’s office, and before they left she generally made that dreadful smile, and they generally felt better.

Each evening she walked home beneath the maple trees of Linden Street. In summer the branches met overhead, and beyond the arc lights there were deep pools of darkness. Courting couples had discovered those shadows years before. Pearlie often heard a murmur, a giggle, or a hasty silence as she passed.

She always walked faster then.

That is enough background. Millie accused me afterward of “piling it on,” but if you are to understand what Pearlie asked of Sam Miller, you must first understand the maples.

Sam came to town on a wet October Tuesday carrying two sample cases and wearing the cheerful, defeated expression of a young traveling man who has missed breakfast and sold nothing.

He represented the National Household Novelty Company, whose latest blessing to mankind was a combination apple corer, potato slicer, lemon squeezer, and tack puller called the Four-in-One Domestic Friend. Sam had shown it to every hardware merchant between Chicago and our town. The merchants admired its mechanism, deplored business conditions, and refused to order.

At half past four he climbed the stairs above Binder’s and appeared in Pearlie’s doorway.

“Public stenographer?” he asked.

“Unless I’ve been discharged without hearing of it.”

“I’ve got a letter.”

“So have most people. Sit down.”

He dropped into the chair beside her desk and pushed back damp brown hair. He was perhaps twenty-six, thin in the way of men who eat at hotels, and ordinarily good-looking, though rain had wilted him. His collar was soft at the edges. One cuff bore a streak of stove blacking, acquired while demonstrating the Domestic Friend’s tack-pulling attachment.

“It’s personal,” he said.

“I charge the same.”

He laughed.

“You won’t laugh at the letter?”

“I might laugh at your spelling.”

“My spelling’s all right. It’s the saying-it part. You know how a fellow can think a lot and then, when he grabs a pen, all he gets down is ‘Dear Edna, I am well and hope you are the same’?”

“I know.”

“Well, I want to say I’m lonesome without sounding like I’ve got pneumonia.”

Pearlie rolled a sheet into the machine.

“Who is Edna?”

“The girl I’m going to marry.”

He brightened instantly. From his inner pocket he produced a photograph protected by two envelopes and a square of tissue paper. The young woman in it wore a high white collar and an expression of beautiful patience.

“She’s lovely,” Pearlie said.

“You bet she is. That picture doesn’t half show her. Her hair’s lighter, and she’s got—well, there’s a kind of gold in it outdoors. Her eyes look gray there, but sometimes they’re blue.”

It appears Millie was right about changeable eyes, after all.

Sam talked for twenty minutes. Edna sang in the choir. Edna’s biscuits were not quite like his mother’s, but she was learning. Edna disliked carnations and loved violets. They meant to marry in June if his territory improved. Her father thought a traveling man unstable, but her mother was reasonable.

Pearlie took it all down, not in shorthand but somewhere behind her pinching spectacles.

Then she composed the letter.

It was not a foolish letter. Pearlie had typed too many false promises and business evasions to enjoy lying. She made Sam sound homesick, hopeful, and faithful—which he was. She omitted the pneumonia. When she read it aloud, Sam stared at her.

“Say, that’s me,” he said. “Only better educated.”

“That will be fifty cents.”

“It’s worth five dollars.”

“I’d rather have the fifty cents.”

He paid and lingered. Rain tapped the window. Binder’s drug-store clock struck five with a rusty complaint.

“Know a good place to eat?” he asked.

“The Palace Hotel serves meals.”

“I said a good place.”

“Then no.”

Sam groaned.

“Last night I ate gray steak in Bell Junction. At noon I had something advertised as chicken fricassee. If that was chicken, it had worked in a coal mine. I’d give ten dollars for home cooking. Mashed potatoes. Real gravy. Pie that didn’t come from a factory in a wooden box.”

Pearlie looked at him.

“Do you like pot roast?”

“Do I like— Lady, I was raised on it.”

“Come to our house Sunday.”

He blinked.

“You mean that?”

“If I didn’t, I’d have said Monday. We eat at one. My folks like company, and Ma always cooks for eight when there are four.”

“But I may be out of town.”

“Then don’t come.”

“I’ll be here,” he said fervently. “If I have to refuse a wholesale order.”

The news reached the Schultz house ahead of Pearlie, because Frieda had been buying cold cream at Binder’s and had seen Sam descend the stairs.

“A man!” she cried. “Pearlie’s bringing a man to Sunday dinner!”

“He is not my man,” Pearlie said.

“What does he sell?”

“Household novelties.”

“Is he handsome?”

“He’s hungry.”

Mrs. Schultz became agitated. Mr. Schultz wanted to know whether Sam was German. Lena arrived from next door with her baby and whispered questions while pretending not to ask any. Frieda suggested Pearlie wear the lavender dress, because it had a softening effect.

“Nothing softens me,” Pearlie said. “I look like an upholstered chair in lavender.”

Nevertheless she wore it.

On Saturday she cooked. She browned flour for gravy, chopped celery, rolled noodles, and baked two pies—one apple and one lemon, lest Sam’s allegiance be uncertain. On Sunday morning she put a roast in the oven before church and thought about him through the sermon. She did not think he would fall in love with her. Pearlie’s imagination was vigorous but not diseased. She merely hoped he would come.

At twelve forty-five the bell rang.

Sam stood on the step in a blue serge suit, carrying a box of candy and a bunch of carnations. The candy was for Mrs. Schultz. The flowers were for Pearlie.

“They’re beautiful,” she said, although she had remembered that Edna disliked them.

“I didn’t know what kind you liked.”

“I like these.”

He met everybody, admired the baby, agreed with Mr. Schultz about the tariff, and was seated beneath a framed motto declaring that God Blesses Our Home.

Then the roast entered.

Conversation stopped.

It came surrounded by browned potatoes, with carrots shining beside it and onions so tender they had surrendered every sharp intention. After it came a bowl of noodles, broad and silky, a dish of beans with bacon, cabbage salad, hot rolls wrapped in a white napkin, pickled peaches, and gravy of such authority that Sam passed it three times.

Pearlie watched him eat.

That was her banquet.

He tried at first to be polite. He said everything was delicious, took moderate helpings, and spoke to Mrs. Schultz between bites. By the second roll, civilization weakened. By the noodles, it collapsed.

“Miss Schultz,” he said, “if the Palace Hotel served one meal like this, traveling men would come here for their vacations.”

“Have another potato,” Pearlie said.

“I’ve had four.”

“There’s no law.”

He had two more.

At pie time he attempted to choose apple, learned there was lemon, and looked stricken. Pearlie solved the matter with half a slice of each. He ate both, then accepted another fragment of lemon because Mrs. Schultz said it would otherwise be wasted.

After dinner Sam sat by the stove in a state of holy peace.

“My mother used to make noodles like those,” he said.

Mrs. Schultz, who had made them, smiled. Pearlie, who had rolled and cut every one, smiled too.

The afternoon went pleasantly. Frieda played the piano; Sam sang part of a college song and forgot the second verse. Mr. Schultz showed him the new chicken house. Lena’s baby slept on his shoulder and, upon waking, seized his necktie.

Sam talked about New York, where he had spent six weeks and which he considered superior to the rest of the republic in buildings, theaters, steak, traffic, neckwear, elevator speed, and even atmosphere. Then he talked about Edna.

He did this naturally and often.

Edna would have loved the baby. Edna played that same song. Edna’s father raised Plymouth Rocks. Edna had never been to New York, but he was going to take her after they were married.

Pearlie listened and asked sensible questions. Frieda watched her anxiously, but Pearlie did not look wounded. She looked content.

At six, although Sam protested that he could never eat again, she brought out cold beef, potato cakes, pickles, bread, cheese, and the remainder of the pies. He ate with renewed courage.

When at last he rose to go, he seemed reluctant.

“I haven’t had a day like this since I left home,” he said. “You folks treated me like I belonged here.”

“Come again if you’re in town,” Mrs. Schultz said.

“Try next time without the sample cases,” said Mr. Schultz. “I am already owning too many inventions.”

In the hall, while Frieda held his coat and everyone talked at once, Sam turned to Pearlie.

“I mean it,” he said. “You did me a mighty big kindness. If there’s ever anything I can do for you—anything—just say so.”

Pearlie went still.

Most people would have answered, “Don’t mention it.” That is the proper response to gratitude and the safest one. Pearlie knew it. She also knew that Sam would leave on the morning train; that he loved Edna; that the flowers on the hall table were already drooping; and that beyond the gate the maples made darkness deep enough to hide almost anything.

“I’ll walk a piece with you,” she said.

Frieda opened her mouth. Pearlie gave her one look, and Frieda closed it.

The rain had ended. There was no moon, and the wet pavement shone faintly beneath the street lamps. The air smelled of leaves and chimney smoke. Sam and Pearlie walked side by side, their steps uneven because he was long-legged and she moved with a short, determined roll.

At the gate Sam said, “You don’t need to come farther. It’s damp.”

“I want some air.”

They passed the Schultz hedge. Light from the dining room stretched behind them and vanished. Ahead, Linden Street lay quiet under the maples.

Sam talked of the train he would catch and the letter Edna would receive. Pearlie said little. They reached the first arc lamp. A white circle lay around it, exposing every board fence and fallen leaf.

Pearlie walked on.

Beyond it stood the largest maple in town. Its branches shut out even the starlight. Couples had paused there through three generations of scandals, engagements, promises, and good nights.

At the edge of that darkness Pearlie stopped.

“Mr. Miller.”

“Sam,” he corrected.

“Sam, did you mean what you said? About doing something for me?”

“Of course.”

“You might not like it.”

“Try me.”

She spoke briskly, almost crossly.

“I want you to put your arm around me and kiss me.”

Sam did not move.

Pearlie’s face was invisible, but her voice came clearly out of the dark.

“Just once. I’m not pretending you care for me. I know you’re going to marry Edna, and I’m glad. I don’t want you to lie to her, either. You must tell her when you get home. Tell her Pearlie Schultz asked you, and tell her why, if you can figure out why.”

“Pearlie—”

“No, don’t be sorry for me. If you’re sorry, I don’t want it. I’ve seen girls kissed under these trees since I was fifteen. Pretty girls, silly girls, mean girls—girls that didn’t think anything of it. And I thought maybe I’d like to know. That’s all.”

There are men who would have laughed from embarrassment. There are men who would have made the kiss a joke, or a bargain, or a little cheap adventure to repeat later in a smoking car.

Sam Miller was young, talkative, vain about New York, and unable to sell a Domestic Friend to anybody. But he was not cruel.

He took off his hat.

Perhaps that seems an odd thing to do in the dark. Yet it changed everything. He removed it as a man removes his hat in church, or before something sorrowful and brave.

Then he stepped close.

His arm went around Pearlie—not gingerly, not with comic caution, but firmly. She gave one small breath. He bent and kissed her.

It was not a lover’s kiss. It promised nothing and stole nothing. It was tender, grave, and longer than mere politeness required.

When he released her, Pearlie remained still.

“Thank you,” she said.

“No,” Sam answered. “Thank you.”

“For what?”

“For Sunday. For the letter. For trusting me.”

“You’ll tell Edna?”

“I’ll tell her.”

“The whole thing?”

“The whole thing.”

Pearlie nodded.

“All right. You’d better go. You’ll miss your train tomorrow if you sit up talking at the Palace.”

He put on his hat. Then, because tenderness sometimes makes people foolish, he took her hand and shook it.

“Good-by, Pearlie.”

“Good-by, Sam. Write to Edna before you sleep.”

“I will.”

He walked toward the hotel. Pearlie stood beneath the maple until his steps faded. Then she turned homeward.

At the first street lamp she stopped and touched her fingers to her mouth. Only once. After that she squared her shoulders and went in to wash the pie plates.

When I finished telling the story, Millie Whitcomb was fitting a pair of gray kid gloves on Mrs. Judge Hanley. She had made me wait through the whole transaction, because commerce came before art at Bascom’s.

Mrs. Hanley departed. Millie folded the tissue paper, shut the box, and looked at me.

“Well?” I said. “There is your ugly heroine.”

“She stays ugly?”

“She does.”

“And Sam marries the other girl?”

“In June.”

“What happens to Pearlie?”

“She keeps the stenography office. She cooks. She lives.”

Millie frowned.

“No millionaire?”

“Not even a clerk.”

“No lovely throat or soft gown or eyes turning blue?”

“Her spectacles remain firmly in place.”

Millie drummed her fingers on the counter.

Then she said, “I guess I like beautiful heroines better.”

“I warned you.”

“And I like them married at the end.”

“So do publishers.”

She handed me my shoe laces.

But as I turned away, Millie called after me.

“Still,” she said, pretending to rearrange the glove boxes, “that Sam Miller wasn’t such a bad sort.”

No.

He wasn’t.

In the oldest apple tree at the edge of the orchard there was a round doorway which no person had made. Behind it ran a neat passage, and at the end of the passage lay a deep, snug room lined with clean wood chips. This was the home of Mr. and Mrs. Red-headed Woodpecker.

They had hollowed it out themselves, taking turns at the work. Tap-tap-tap went Mr. Woodpecker’s strong bill, and out flew a chip. Then Mrs. Woodpecker pecked while her husband rested. Woodpeckers do not carry carpets or feather beds into their nurseries, as some birds do. Clean, dry chips suit them better, and I dare say the young Woodpeckers are quite as comfortable upon them as you are in your own bed.

In this nursery lay four pearly white eggs. Mrs. Woodpecker kept them warm, and Mr. Woodpecker brought her food and sometimes took her place. What the four chicks thought while they were still inside their shells, who can tell? Perhaps they thought the whole world was smooth, white, and exceedingly small.

One sunshiny morning a faint tapping sounded inside the first egg.

“Listen!” said Mrs. Woodpecker.

Tap—tap—tap!

A tiny crack appeared. Presently a little bill pushed through, and after much wriggling the chick came tumbling out, tired, bare, and not in the least handsome.

It was a brother. Before night his three sisters had followed him.

Mrs. Woodpecker did not break the shells for them, although she could easily have done so. A young bird must perform his first hard task for himself. It strengthens his neck and shoulders and fits him for the many labours of bird life. Mothers often know when to help and when merely to wait, though children do not always understand the difference.

The four babies could neither see nor fly. They could only stretch up their long necks and open their mouths whenever the passage darkened.

“Ker-r-ruck! Ker-r-ruck!” they cried.

“We are hungry! We are starving! Nobody ever fed us!”

This last was never true, for Mr. and Mrs. Woodpecker flew to and fro from dawn until twilight, bringing tender grubs, ants, and other juicy morsels. Yet a nestling who has swallowed breakfast five minutes ago may honestly feel that he has been forgotten all his life.

The brother was especially loud. He pushed between his sisters and opened his mouth so widely that his mother sometimes laughed.

“A large voice does not always mean the emptiest stomach,” she told him.

But he was too young to understand wise remarks, and, to be sure, too hungry to care for them.

The parents kept the nest very clean. After the chicks came out of their shells, Mr. Woodpecker carried the broken pieces far away and dropped them where no prowling creature could find the nursery by following such signs. Other things that did not belong in a tidy nest were removed in the same careful fashion. Thus the little birds grew safely in their dear, dry old tree.

After a time their eyes opened.

Ah, what a surprise the world must be to one who beholds it suddenly! First they saw the dusky walls of the hollow and their mother’s bright, kind eyes. Then, when she stood at the doorway, they caught glimpses of green leaves, blue sky, and bars of golden sunshine.

The three sisters stared outward in wonder.

“How beautiful!” said one.

“How large!” said another.

“How many things there are to see!” cried the third.

Their brother looked out for a moment, but just then he discovered a tiny pinfeather upon his shoulder.

“What is this?” he asked.

“A feather beginning,” replied his mother.

“Will it be handsome?”

“It will be useful,” said Mrs. Woodpecker, who believed usefulness more important.

Every day more feathers appeared. They came wrapped in little sheaths, and at first they looked rather like stiff brushes. The young birds preened them carefully with their bills until the coverings broke away and the soft vanes spread. Their parents showed them how to put each feather in order and how to reach the small oil pocket beneath the tail. A touch of oil helped keep the plumage trim and protected it from wet weather. Birds do not call it a waterproof coat, of course, but that is very nearly what it is.

The sisters admired one another.

“Your wing feathers are growing beautifully,” said the first.

“So are yours,” answered the second.

“And our brother’s tail is wonderfully stiff,” said the third.

A Woodpecker’s stiff tail is no idle ornament. It braces him against a tree trunk while he climbs and hammers. But the brother cared very little about that.

“Are my feathers red enough?” he asked.

“They are as red as they ought to be at your age,” said his father.

“Will they be redder tomorrow?”

“They will grow as Nature directs.”

The young Woodpecker was not satisfied. He twisted his neck to inspect his back, peeped under his wings, and felt each tail feather with the tip of his bill. Since he could not see the top of his own head, he begged his sisters to describe it.

“Very red,” they assured him.

“How red?”

“As red as a ripe cherry.”

“Are you certain it is not merely raspberry red?”

“We are certain.”

Still he worried. He tugged at one feather to make it longer, then tugged at another to make the two match. Soon his skin was sore.

“Feathers are not radishes,” said his father. “They do not grow faster because you pull them.”

“I was only helping,” replied the youngster.

“No indeed! Leave growth to growth and attend to your lessons.”

There were many lessons. The chicks learned to cling to the walls of the hollow, to use their tails for support, and to climb toward the doorway. Outside, the orchard swayed and rustled. Bees hummed among the blossoms, a Blue Jay called from the fence, and a Tree Frog slept in a shady crack. The three sisters noticed everything.

Their brother noticed chiefly his reflection in a bright drop of rain caught upon a leaf.

“What a fine head I shall have!” he said.

“If you do not wear it out by turning it around to look at yourself,” said Mrs. Woodpecker.

At last came flying day. Mr. Woodpecker waited upon a nearby branch and called the young birds one by one. The first sister fluttered out, dipped alarmingly, and reached him. The second landed upon a twig below. The third went past the branch altogether and clung to the trunk, which was good enough for a beginning.

Then the brother stood in the doorway.

“Come,” called his father.

“Is the sunshine making my red feathers shine?”

“Come and find out.”

He leaped. For several breathless moments his wings seemed to be flying in four directions, but presently they agreed upon one, and he reached the tree trunk.

He was proud of himself, as he had a right to be. Learning to fly is no small matter. Unfortunately, instead of thanking his strong wings, he began admiring their colour.

His father taught him to climb upward in short hops and to listen for insects beneath the bark. Tap-tap-tap! The young Woodpecker struck a promising place, pried into it with his bill, and drew out a plump grub with his long tongue.

“Delicious!” he exclaimed.

“There are thousands of such meals hidden in the woods,” said his father, “but you must look for them.”

The youngster looked—for another rain puddle in which to see himself.

His sisters learned where ants travelled, which fruit was ripe, and how to avoid the barn-yard Cat. They discovered that an apple may be rosy on one side and green on the other and yet be sweet all through, which is one of the orchard secrets. Their brother learned these things too, but slowly, because he interrupted every lesson to smooth his breast or arrange his wing coverts.

“You see the world only when it shines upon your feathers,” his mother told him.

“I see the world very well,” he answered. “I saw three red feathers out of place this morning.”

His parents tried advice, jokes, and even silence. Nothing helped. When his sisters praised one another, he supposed they were about to praise him. When a Blue Jay cried, he wondered whether the Jay had noticed his head. When the wind ruffled every bird in the orchard, he seemed to think it had singled him out for mischief.

Vanity is a tiresome fault because it makes a person anxious instead of happy. The young Woodpecker possessed healthy wings, a strong bill, a loving family, and a whole green forest to explore, yet he fretted over a feather which nobody else could see.

One afternoon he flew farther than usual and came to a hickory tree. An old Gray Squirrel sat upon a limb, turning a nut in his paws.

“Good afternoon,” said the Woodpecker. “Do you live here?”

“I have lived here longer than that apple tree has borne fruit,” replied the Gray Squirrel.

“Then you must know the Red-headed Woodpecker family.”

“Certainly. A respectable family. Three pleasant daughters, and one son who is said to be a silly fellow.”

The young bird nearly fell from the trunk.

“A silly fellow?” he repeated.

“So I have heard.”

“What makes him silly?”

The Gray Squirrel bit through the nutshell and considered.

“They say he sees the whole world through his own feathers.”

The youngster glanced at his breast. “How can he do that?”

“It is only a saying,” replied the Squirrel. “At any rate, I have never met him, and gossip is a poor supper.” He gathered up his nut and sprang away through the branches.

The Woodpecker remained perfectly still. For once he forgot to preen.

“A silly fellow,” he whispered. “The whole world through his own feathers.”

It was the first time criticism had come from someone outside his family. Advice from parents is sometimes treated like bark upon a tree—always there and therefore scarcely noticed. But a stranger’s careless sentence may sound as loud as thunder.

The young Woodpecker flew home by the shortest path.

“Father,” he said, “what does it mean to see the world through one’s own feathers?”

Mr. Woodpecker looked at Mrs. Woodpecker, but she wisely kept quiet.

“It means,” said his father, “to care so much about your own appearance that you fail to notice anything else.”

“But whose feathers ought I to look through?”

“None at all. Your eyes are in front of your feathers. If Nature had intended you to spend the day admiring your back, she would have put both eyes at the end of your tail.”

His sisters began to giggle. Even the troubled young bird smiled a little.

“Am I a silly fellow?” he asked.

“You have sometimes behaved like one,” said his father gently. “That is not the same as being obliged to remain one.”

The youngster thought about this. Then he looked outward. Evening had filled the orchard with soft gold. A green Caterpillar crept along a twig. His sisters were sharing an apple, and his mother looked tired after a busy day. There had always been so much to behold, and he had scarcely seen it.

“I shall never look at my feathers again,” he declared.

“That would be foolish too,” said Mrs. Woodpecker. “Clean them when they need cleaning. Oil them when they need oil. Then use them.”

So he made a rule. He would preen in the morning and again before sleep, but he would not stop every few minutes to inspect himself. At first this was difficult. Whenever sunshine flashed upon his wing, his head began to turn of its own accord. Then he remembered the Gray Squirrel and looked instead for something useful or interesting.

He found insects hidden beneath bark. He warned his sisters when the barn-yard Cat crept near. He discovered a branch heavy with ripe cherries and called the whole family to feast. Before long he knew the orchard better than any of the young birds, because he was at last truly looking at it.

His feathers continued to grow without being pulled. They became smooth, strong, and handsome; and, what was better, he ceased asking whether everyone had noticed them.

Mrs. Woodpecker was much relieved.

“We advised him every day,” she said to her husband, “and he heard not a word. Then an old Gray Squirrel calls him silly once, and behold—he becomes sensible!”

Mr. Woodpecker laughed. “That is often the way with young folk.”

And perhaps it is sometimes the way with older folk too—but we will not ask the Gray Squirrel about that.

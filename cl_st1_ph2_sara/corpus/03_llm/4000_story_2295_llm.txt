I

Little Nadya was six years old, and for nearly a month she had been ill with a strange illness.

At first she had complained of a headache. Then she had refused her dinner. The next day she did not wish to leave her bed, although the sun shone warmly through the nursery windows and her nurse had brought in the yellow perambulator which Nadya generally liked to push from room to room.

After that she became weaker and quieter every day.

Doctors came to see her. One doctor was old and bald, with gold eye-glasses and soft white hands. Another had black whiskers and smelled of tobacco and winter air. A third was very important and arrived in a carriage. He put his ear to Nadya’s chest, tapped her back with his fingers, looked at her tongue, counted her pulse, and performed several other mysterious actions which sick children are expected to endure.

Then the doctors went into the drawing-room and spoke in low voices.

Nadya’s mother listened with clasped hands.

“What is it, doctor?”

The old doctor polished his eye-glasses for a long time before answering.

“The child has no dangerous bodily disease,” he said. “Her heart is sound, her lungs are sound, and there is no fever. But she is losing strength because she has become indifferent to life.”

“Indifferent?” repeated the mother.

“She wants nothing. She takes interest in nothing. That is serious in a child.”

“But what medicine shall we give her?”

“Medicine may support the body. Yet medicine cannot command a person to be happy. You must discover what she desires most of all. A journey, perhaps. A new plaything. Some pleasure which will surprise her. Wake her interest, and then we may hope.”

Nadya’s father stood by the window. He was a tall man, though now he looked bent and tired.

“You mean,” he asked, “that we are to amuse her?”

“If possible.”

“And if it is not possible?”

The doctor gave a little shrug.

“For one’s child, sir, much that appears impossible becomes possible.”

After the doctors had gone, the mother sat beside Nadya’s bed and stroked her thin hand.

“My darling, what would you like?”

“Nothing, mamma.”

“Would you like your red doll?”

“No.”

“The musical box?”

“No, thank you.”

“Would you like Katya and Lida to come and play with you?”

“No. They make a noise.”

“Some grapes? A sweet orange? Chocolate?”

“No, mamma. I don’t want anything.”

“Perhaps papa should carry you into the drawing-room?”

“No. I am tired.”

She turned her face toward the wall and looked at the pale strip of afternoon light upon the paper.

Her mother bent over her, but Nadya had already closed her eyes.

II

So the days passed.

In the morning the maid brought cocoa with a little silver spoon, fresh milk, toast, and an egg. Nadya tasted none of them. At noon there was clear soup, chicken cutlets, rice pudding, and jelly trembling in a glass dish. She pushed them away. In the evening her father came home with parcels under his arms, but she scarcely looked at them.

One parcel contained a doll with real hair and blue eyes which opened and shut. Another held a whole family of wooden bears. There were picture books, paints, coloured balls, a small drum, a railway with three green carriages, and a white lamb which bleated when one pressed its side.

“See, Nadyusha,” said her father, kneeling by the bed. “The train can go all around the table. Look—here is the engine. Here is the station-master. He has a red cap.”

“Yes, papa.”

“Shall I wind it?”

“If you like.”

The train went round and round, ringing a little bell. The station-master raised his arm. The green carriages passed the nursery, the medicine bottles, and Nadya’s slippers on the carpet.

“Is it not splendid?”

“Yes, papa.”

“But do you like it?”

“I don’t know.”

Her father gathered the train into its box. He did this too quickly, and one carriage fell to the floor.

At dinner he said nothing. He ate half a spoonful of soup, rose, went into his study, lit a cigar, and walked from the writing-table to the stove and back again. He smoked and smoked and smoked. The ash dropped upon his waistcoat without his noticing it.

At last Nadya’s mother entered.

“She has eaten nothing.”

The father stopped walking.

“What did she say?”

“Only that she is tired.”

“Everybody says she is tired! The doctors say she must be amused, and she refuses everything. What can I do? Tell me what I can do!”

His voice was sharp, but his wife knew that he was not angry with her.

“Hush,” she said. “She may hear.”

He sat down and covered his face with his hands.

That night Nadya slept badly. She dreamed of a long grey road under a grey sky. Nothing grew beside it. No birds flew overhead. She walked and walked, although her feet were heavy, and somewhere far away a bell sounded in the mist.

When she awoke, her pillow was damp.

“Did you have a bad dream, darling?” asked her mother.

“I don’t remember.”

Aunt Anna came with flowers. Grandmother sent a basket of pears. The children from the house opposite stood below the window and called up to Nadya, offering to show her a kitten. A little boy even fell into a snowdrift while trying to lift it high enough.

Nadya only shook her head.

“I don’t want the kitten.”

Her mother drew the curtains and wept quietly behind them.

III

On the following morning Nadya’s father sat at her bedside.

He had slept badly. His eyes were red, and one side of his collar was folded under. On his knees lay a large catalogue from a famous toy shop.

“Now, Nadyusha,” he said, “we shall choose together. There are hundreds of things here. You need only point with your finger.”

He showed her a doll’s house with twelve rooms, tiny chairs, tiny curtains, tiny knives and forks.

“No.”

A boat with a sail.

“No.”

A magic lantern.

“No.”

A toy kitchen.

“No, thank you.”

“Then what do you want?” he cried. “A real carriage? A pony? A little house in the country? Tell me!”

Nadya looked at him for a moment. Her large eyes seemed darker than before.

“I had a dream,” she said.

“What dream?”

“I dreamed about an elephant.”

“An elephant?”

“Yes. He came into my room and stood near the bed. He was very big, but he was kind. He moved his ears and gave me his trunk.”

Her father leaned closer.

“Would you like an elephant?”

“Yes.”

For the first time in many days a faint colour appeared in her cheeks.

The father sprang up.

“Excellent! Splendid! An elephant it shall be.”

By noon a servant arrived from the best toy shop carrying a wooden box so large that two men had to lift it. Inside stood a grey mechanical elephant. It was nearly as tall as the nursery table. It had glass eyes, a red cloth saddle, and a key in its side. When wound, it walked six steps, moved its trunk, and uttered a trumpet-like cry.

The father arranged it before the bed.

“There! What do you think of him?”

Nadya examined the toy gravely.

“It is a very good elephant.”

“You like him?”

“Yes. But that is not the one.”

“Not the one?”

“I wanted a living elephant.”

Her father sat down.

“A living one?”

“Yes, papa. A real elephant that eats and breathes and knows things.”

He opened his mouth, closed it, and looked at his wife. She lowered her eyes.

“My dear child,” he began, “elephants are extremely large.”

“I know.”

“They live in hot countries.”

“And in the menagerie,” said Nadya.

That was true. For three weeks a travelling menagerie had been performing in the city, and its coloured bills showed lions, horses, monkeys, and an enormous elephant named Tommy.

Nadya turned away.

“If he cannot come, it does not matter.”

Those words, spoken so softly, frightened her father more than tears would have done.

He bent down and kissed her forehead.

“Wait for me,” he said. “I shall see what may be arranged.”

IV

The menagerie occupied a wooden building near the river. Even from the street Nadya’s father could hear a lion roaring and a brass band practising the same march again and again.

Inside, the air smelled of straw, sawdust, animals, and oranges. A monkey in a red coat rattled the bars of its cage. Two white horses stood with their heads together. At the far end, under a row of lamps, an elephant was eating hay.

The manager was a stout German named Herr Braun. He wore a green coat with brass buttons and a stiff yellow moustache. He listened to Nadya’s father, at first politely, then suspiciously.

“You wish to buy the elephant?”

“No.”

“To hire him for a procession?”

“No. I wish him to visit my daughter.”

Herr Braun blinked.

“In your house?”

“In my house.”

“Upstairs?”

“Yes.”

“With carpets and furniture?”

“The furniture can be removed.”

Herr Braun took off his spectacles, breathed upon them, wiped them, and put them on again.

“Sir, perhaps you have been drinking?”

“I have not.”

“Then perhaps I have misunderstood your Russian language.”

“My daughter is ill. She asks for a living elephant. The doctors say that if she can be made happy, she may recover.”

“How old is the little lady?”

“Six.”

Herr Braun’s expression changed.

“My Elsa is six,” he said after a pause. “She is in Hamburg with her grandmother.”

He turned toward the elephant.

“That is Tommy. He is trained. He is gentle. He does not fear music, crowds, or fire. But he weighs more than one hundred and fifty poods. Your stairs—how broad?”

“Broad enough, I believe.”

“You believe? An elephant does not walk upon beliefs. He walks upon stairs.”

“Come and inspect them.”

“And the ceiling?”

“High.”

“The entrance?”

“It may have to be widened.”

“The floors?”

“Strong oak beams.”

“Police permission?”

“I shall obtain it.”

“The hour?”

“Late at night, when the streets are empty.”

“The payment?”

“Whatever is necessary.”

Herr Braun again looked at him closely.

“This child is truly ill?”

“Truly.”

“And she desires Tommy?”

“She has desired nothing else for a month.”

The German sighed.

“Very well. First we inspect.”

They drove to the house. Herr Braun measured the gateway, the hall, and the staircase with a tape. He knocked upon the floor, crawled under the stairs, questioned the house porter, and frowned at every turn.

“The front door is too narrow.”

“We shall remove the doorposts.”

“This lamp must come down.”

“It shall.”

“The iron latch may scratch Tommy.”

“We shall unscrew it.”

“The bend in the staircase is difficult.”

“Can he manage?”

“With food, patience, and no foolish shouting—perhaps.”

The mother watched from the drawing-room doorway, pressing a handkerchief to her lips.

“Where will he sleep?” asked Herr Braun.

“In the dining-room.”

“No table, no chairs, no glass. Straw on the floor. Windows closed. Water ready. Twelve loaves, vegetables, and hay. Also Tommy enjoys sweet cake.”

“What kind?”

“Pistachio. He has taste.”

By evening the father had visited the police office, the city authorities, and the owner of the house. Some officials laughed; others declared the thing impossible; one demanded a written request in duplicate. At last permission was granted on condition that the elephant should arrive after midnight and depart before morning traffic.

Carpenters removed the front doors and part of the frame. Servants carried furniture away. The cook baked pistachio tart. Neighbours peeped from behind curtains and asked one another whether a prince, a cannon, or a wild beast was expected.

The father answered nobody. He walked through the emptied hall and repeated:

“Carefully. Everything must be done carefully.”

V

At half past twelve the street was quiet. Snow lay beneath the lamps, and the houses seemed asleep.

Then a dark, enormous shape appeared at the corner.

Tommy walked between Herr Braun and two keepers. A policeman went before them, looking solemn, as though elephants passed through his district every night. Behind came a cart loaded with hay, sacks, buckets, and food.

Dogs began barking in courtyards. A cab-horse saw Tommy, sat down upon its hind legs, and refused to move. Two late gentlemen in fur coats pressed themselves against a wall.

When Tommy reached the house, he stopped.

He examined the entrance with his small, thoughtful eyes, moved his ears, and stretched out his trunk.

Herr Braun gave him a piece of pistachio tart.

“Good, Tommy. Forward.”

Tommy ate the tart, but did not move.

Another piece was placed just inside the doorway. He reached for it. A third was offered farther on. In this manner he entered the hall, following the cake with great dignity.

At the staircase he stopped again.

The steps were shallow but numerous, and the turn was narrow.

“Can he go up?” whispered the father.

“He can,” said Herr Braun. “But he must decide so himself.”

Tommy felt the first stair with one round foot. His foot looked like a low grey pillar with nails. He pressed down. The stair creaked.

Everyone held his breath.

At that moment the policeman said calmly:

“If the stairs collapse, I shall make a report.”

No one answered him.

Herr Braun showed Tommy another piece of tart. The elephant raised one foreleg, climbed one stair, then another. His huge body filled the staircase from wall to wall. His ears brushed the banister. His trunk moved ahead of him like a thick searching snake.

Step by step he went upward.

Halfway, he paused and turned his head.

“More tart!” ordered Herr Braun.

The father ran to the landing with the dish.

Tommy took the piece so delicately that his trunk scarcely touched the man’s fingers. Then he continued.

At the bend there was trouble. His side pressed against the wall, and a picture frame fell with a crash.

Tommy flapped his ears and gave a low rumble.

“Quiet, my friend,” said Herr Braun. “It is only bad painting.”

One keeper pushed the banister outward; another guided Tommy’s hind foot. The elephant gathered himself, bent strangely, and passed the turn.

At last he reached the upper landing.

The father laughed aloud, though his face was wet with perspiration.

In the dining-room the carpet had been rolled away. Straw covered the floor. There were tubs of water, baskets of carrots, cabbages, beetroot, apples, and bread. Tommy inspected everything, drank half a tub, ate three loaves, and rubbed his forehead gently against Herr Braun’s shoulder.

“Now he is content,” said the manager. “He will sleep.”

“And my daughter?”

“Let her come in the morning. No excitement tonight.”

The father crept into the nursery.

Nadya lay awake.

“Papa,” she whispered, “did you bring him?”

“Yes.”

“A real one?”

“A real, living elephant.”

“Where is he?”

“In the dining-room. He is tired from his journey and has gone to bed.”

“Does he snore?”

“I do not yet know.”

“Is he very large?”

“Larger than your wardrobe, your bed, and the stove together.”

Nadya smiled.

“Good night, papa. Tell Tommy good night from me.”

VI

The next morning Nadya awoke before the nurse.

“Is it time?”

“Time for what, darling?”

“For Tommy.”

She sat up by herself. This had not happened for many days.

“First you must wash,” said her mother, hardly daring to show her joy.

“Yes. Quickly.”

“And take your medicine.”

“Yes.”

“And breakfast.”

Nadya considered this.

“Will Tommy wait?”

“He will wait.”

“Then I shall have cocoa and two rolls.”

The mother turned away for a moment because tears had filled her eyes.

Nadya drank the cocoa, ate one roll, and asked for another. She allowed herself to be washed, combed, and dressed in a blue woollen frock. She objected to the white sash because it took too long, but finally submitted when told that Tommy was accustomed to receiving properly dressed young ladies.

Her father carried her to the dining-room door.

“Now,” he said, “do not be afraid.”

“Why should I be afraid?”

Herr Braun opened the door.

At first Nadya saw only something vast and grey standing where the dining-table had once stood. Then the something moved. Two immense ears waved slowly. A trunk hung almost to the floor. Four legs stood like pillars, and behind them swung a tail with a broom at the end.

Tommy turned his head and looked at her.

Nadya stared.

The elephant was much larger than the elephant in her dream. His skin was wrinkled like an old leather travelling bag. His ears were broad as shovels. His feet were round and heavy; his eyes were small, brown, and unexpectedly gentle.

“Oh,” Nadya breathed. “He is beautiful.”

Tommy stretched out his trunk.

The mother gave a little cry, but Herr Braun raised one hand.

“He only greets her.”

The end of the trunk moved near Nadya’s face. It smelled of hay, warm dust, and pistachio cake. Nadya touched it with one finger. Tommy’s trunk curled around her hand, lightly and carefully, as though her hand were a flower he did not wish to crush.

“How do you do, Tommy?” she said. “I am Nadya.”

Tommy breathed warmly against her palm.

“He says, ‘How do you do,’” explained Herr Braun.

“Does he know my name?”

“He will know it soon.”

Nadya’s father set her down in an armchair placed at a safe distance. But Nadya immediately asked to be moved closer.

“Tommy has come all this way. It is impolite to sit across the room.”

A basket of rolls was brought.

“May I feed him?”

“One at a time,” said Herr Braun.

Nadya held out a roll. Tommy took it with the two little finger-like tips of his trunk, lifted it, and put it into his mouth.

Nadya laughed.

It was a small laugh, weak from long disuse, but everyone in the room heard it. Her father walked abruptly to the window and began examining the curtain cord.

“Another!” said Nadya.

Tommy ate a second roll, a third, and a fourth.

“He does not chew enough,” she observed.

“He has excellent teeth,” said Herr Braun.

“Would he like sugar?”

“He likes it too much.”

“So does papa.”

Her father turned from the window.

“That is a secret between us.”

Nadya laughed again, louder this time.

After breakfast she insisted upon introducing Tommy to her household. The dolls were brought from the nursery and arranged upon the sofa: the red-haired doll, the old cloth baby with one button eye, a porcelain lady in a green dress, and a wooden sailor.

“This is Vera,” Nadya told Tommy. “She is the eldest. This is Masha. She is sometimes naughty. The sailor has no name because he came only yesterday.”

Tommy reached toward the dolls.

“Carefully,” said Nadya. “They are not rolls.”

He touched the porcelain lady with the end of his trunk and lifted her gently by the skirt.

“Mamma!” cried Nadya. “Look! He has chosen a wife.”

Tommy placed the doll upon his head.

For one second the porcelain lady sat between his ears like a little queen. Then she slid down his forehead. Herr Braun caught her.

Everyone laughed, even the nurse, who had been standing near the door with both hands over her apron.

Tommy seemed pleased by the laughter. He opened his mouth, lifted his trunk, and made a strange coughing sound.

“He laughs too,” said Nadya.

“Certainly,” Herr Braun agreed. “Though in German.”

Next came the picture book.

Nadya sat upon a cushion, with the great grey head beside her. She turned the pages and explained the pictures.

“This is a horse. You know horses, of course. This is a cow. This is a camel.”

Tommy touched the camel.

“No, Tommy. That is not you. It has two humps. You have none.”

On the next page was an elephant, painted blue-grey, standing under a palm tree.

“There!” cried Nadya. “That is you.”

Tommy examined the picture, then looked behind the book.

“He thinks another elephant is hiding there,” said her father.

Nadya showed him again.

“You see, Tommy, this is only a picture. It is flat and cannot eat.”

Tommy took hold of the corner of the page.

“No!” she said sternly.

At once he released it.

Herr Braun nodded with approval.

“He understands a lady’s voice.”

Nadya became very proud.

By noon she was tired, but she would not return to bed. A couch had been brought into the dining-room, and she lay upon it under a light blanket while Tommy stood nearby eating hay. The room was full of quiet sounds: the tearing of hay, the soft brushing of the trunk over straw, the occasional creak of the floor.

Nadya watched his ears.

“Why does he wave them?”

“To cool himself,” said Herr Braun.

“Are they hot?”

“Sometimes.”

“Why is his skin folded?”

“Because he has much of it.”

“Why are his eyes so small?”

“So that he may look modest.”

“Why is his nose so long?”

“To reach what other people put too far away.”

This answer delighted her.

She ordered an apple placed upon the mantelpiece. Tommy reached it without moving his feet.

“Again!”

An orange was put on a cabinet. Tommy found it.

Then the wooden sailor was hidden behind a chair. Tommy discovered him but, remembering Nadya’s warning, did not eat him. He presented him to her with great ceremony.

After this Nadya wished to play hospital.

“Tommy is ill,” she announced.

Herr Braun looked alarmed.

“Only pretend,” she explained.

A ribbon was tied around one of Tommy’s front legs. Nadya listened to his side through a cardboard tube. His body made deep rumbling noises.

“He has swallowed thunder,” she said. “He must take medicine.”

“What medicine?” asked her father.

“One carrot every hour, and cake when he is good.”

Tommy took the carrot. Nadya patted his trunk.

“You will recover.”

At luncheon a small table was placed beside her couch. Nadya had soup, chicken, potatoes, and cocoa. Tommy was served in a great tub: cabbages, carrots, beetroot, bread, apples, and a little rice mixed with syrup.

“He must sit at the table,” said Nadya.

“Tommy does not sit in houses,” Herr Braun told her. “If he sat, the people downstairs might receive an unexpected visitor through the ceiling.”

The policeman, who had returned to make sure order was preserved, considered this carefully.

“In that event,” he said, “I should require another report.”

Tommy ate with excellent appetite. Nadya watched him and ate too. When he lifted a whole cabbage, she took a spoonful of soup. When he crushed a beetroot, she finished her potatoes. When his trunk searched for apples, she asked for walnut cake.

Her mother exchanged a glance with the father.

“Do not look at me,” said Nadya. “I am having luncheon with my guest.”

After luncheon Herr Braun allowed Tommy a spoonful of rum in warm water, which he liked very much. Nadya was given milk.

“We shall drink to our friendship,” she said.

Tommy raised his trunk. Nadya raised her glass.

“To friendship.”

The afternoon passed with stories. Herr Braun told how Tommy had once pulled a wagon from deep mud, how he remembered every road he travelled, and how he had rescued a foolish little dog which ran beneath his feet. Nadya listened with shining eyes.

“Was he frightened?”

“Tommy? Never.”

“Was the dog frightened?”

“The dog barked at him afterward.”

“That was rude.”

“Very.”

At four o’clock the children of the house were permitted to look through the doorway. They stood in a silent row, their mouths open. Nadya received them like the mistress of a palace.

“This is Tommy,” she said. “You may give him bread, but do not pull his tail and do not scream.”

No one thought of pulling his tail.

A small boy named Petya offered Tommy a bun and then hid behind the nurse. Tommy reached around the nurse with his trunk and found him. Petya squealed. Nadya laughed until she coughed, and Tommy flapped his ears as though pleased with the joke.

Soon the visitors were sent away, for Nadya’s cheeks had become bright with excitement.

“You must rest,” said her mother.

“I am resting.”

“You must close your eyes.”

“Tommy may go away.”

“He will remain until you sleep.”

Nadya thought about this. Then she asked for her pillow and lay down upon the couch. Tommy stood beside her. His huge shadow covered the wall and ceiling, but it did not frighten her. She held the end of his trunk as she might have held a familiar hand.

“Tommy,” she murmured, “when I am well, I shall come to your menagerie. I shall bring pistachio cake. Not one piece—a whole cake.”

Tommy breathed softly.

“And you must remember me.”

His trunk curled gently around her fingers.

“I think he promises,” whispered the mother.

Nadya’s eyelids closed.

Soon she was asleep.

In her dream there was no grey road and no mist. She rode upon Tommy beneath a warm golden sky. Palm trees bent above them, parrots flashed between the leaves, and clear bells rang somewhere ahead. Tommy walked slowly so that she would not fall. Whenever she grew hungry, he picked oranges from the trees and handed them back to her.

At midnight the elephant was led away.

Nadya’s father stood on the landing while Tommy descended. The journey downward was more difficult, but there was still pistachio tart, and Tommy remained calm. At the door he turned, touched the father’s shoulder with his trunk, and gave a low rumble.

“Thank you,” the father whispered.

Herr Braun shook his hand.

“For a sick child,” he said, “even an elephant is not too large.”

The street swallowed them: first the policeman, then the keepers, then Tommy’s slow dark shape, and last the wagon of straw.

In the morning Nadya awoke and called loudly:

“Mamma! I want my milk—and two rolls!”

Her mother ran in.

Nadya sat upright in bed. Her eyes were clear, her cheeks warm, and her hair stood untidily around her face.

“Where is Tommy?”

“He has gone home to the menagerie, darling. He was sorry to leave. He says that when you are quite well, you must visit him.”

Nadya threw back the blanket.

“Then tell him I shall come today.”

“Today? But you must first recover.”

She gave her mother a sly, happy smile.

“I have recovered already.”

For three mornings Jane polished the kitchen fender without singing, and on the fourth she broke nothing.

It was this latter circumstance which first seriously alarmed me. Jane’s ordinary method of communicating happiness to the household was by means of hymns, fragments of music-hall songs, and the percussion of crockery. A silence below stairs, interrupted only by the restrained, conscientious movement of brush or saucepan, indicated in her case either influenza or tragedy.

“Something has happened to William,” I told Euphemia.

Euphemia laid down the household manual she had been reading—a severe work which advised mistresses never to encourage confidences—and listened. From beneath us came a measured scraping. Jane was cleaning potatoes as though each had offended her privately.

“Perhaps they have quarrelled,” said Euphemia.

“They have been engaged three years. Quarrelling is no longer an incident; it is part of the constitution.”

There followed a bump, a quick breath, and then silence again.

Euphemia went downstairs.

Although Jane’s romance had developed almost entirely outside my observation, William had long been one of the institutions of our domestic life. I knew him through Jane’s reports, through his Sunday appearances at the area gate, and through various objects which had marked his rise in the world. He began as a porter at Gedge and Mallowby’s drapery establishment, a place of many windows and little light, where he carried bales, swept passages, and was respected by everybody except those immediately above him.

Jane had announced the engagement while black-leading the range.

“I’m not one to keep things from you, ma’am,” she told Euphemia. “William and me is engaged.”

She displayed a brass ring with a blue stone, which the household manual would certainly have condemned.

“He’s very respectable, ma’am. He don’t drink, nor smoke—not except a cigarette when there’s company—and he belongs to the Young Men’s Mutual Improvement. They read papers.”

“What sort of papers?” asked Euphemia.

“Improving ones, ma’am. William read one hisself on ‘How to Rise.’”

“And what did it recommend?”

“Persevering. William’s persevering.”

She sighed happily and dropped the stove-brush into the coal-scuttle.

William’s respectability, at that period, was of an earnest and rather dusty kind. He wore a black coat on Sundays, brushed until its seams shone, and carried an alpaca umbrella regardless of weather. His collar was so high that he could turn his head only by turning the upper half of his body. When he called for Jane he waited on the step, because the regulations governing visitors below stairs were, as interpreted by Euphemia, uncertain; and he held his hat before him with both hands as though offering it for inspection.

He addressed me as “sir,” Euphemia as “madam,” and Jane as “Jane.” To Jane’s “Don’t be silly, William,” he replied, “Conduct yourself,” which she accepted as the speech of a man destined for authority.

At first his advancement delighted her.

“William’s getting on, ma’am,” she would say. “Mr. Mallowby spoke to him personal.”

“What did he say?”

“He said, ‘You there, take them parcels round.’ But William says it isn’t what’s said, it’s the manner. They don’t use that manner to everybody.”

A month later Mr. Mallowby entrusted him with the key of a side door. Shortly afterward he was permitted to assist during a stocktaking. Then came an increase of two shillings a week, and with it a remarkable change in William’s neckties.

“They’re not ready-made,” Jane explained. “He does them hisself from a picture. Gentlemen’s ties is careless, William says, but they take a deal of care to get careless proper.”

This observation appeared to me to contain the whole philosophy of gentility.

Then William purchased a silk hat.

Euphemia brought the intelligence into my study as though announcing a royal death.

“A silk hat,” she said.

I turned from my writing.

“A silk hat?”

“And yellow gloves.”

“Good heavens!”

“And a new umbrella—with a silver-looking knob.”

We stood for a moment awed by the completeness of the transformation.

Jane was prouder than either of us. She cleaned the front doorstep twice on the Sunday of his first appearance and watched from the kitchen window while he approached, the silk hat preceding him like an official proclamation. The yellow gloves were a little too large; their empty finger-ends curled delicately over the umbrella handle. His trousers had acquired a stripe, his boots a point, and his expression a gravity which suggested that commercial burdens of national magnitude rested upon him.

“He’s very improved,” said Jane.

“Externally,” I agreed.

She did not notice the qualification.

His next promotion removed him from the parcels and placed him behind a counter. There he was allowed to show remnants to cautious ladies and to say that certain colours were “much worn.” He learned to measure ribbon with a flourish and to call five-and-elevenpence “practically six shillings.” His wages rose again. He transferred himself from the Mutual Improvement Society to a chapel attended by one of the junior partners.

“It’s a superior chapel,” Jane said. “They’ve got a churnor.”

“A what?”

“A churnor—to keep the choir in their places. William says religion ought to be conducted respectable.”

Thereafter his visits became less frequent. He had evening classes, chapel meetings, stock accounts, and, on one occasion, supper with the second-window dresser. Jane still insisted that he was getting on; but the words, which had once rung through our kitchen like a trumpet, began to sound defensive.

“He’s getting on, ma’am,” she said, rubbing a saucepan with unnecessary force. “A person can’t expect him to be always hanging about like a greengrosher’s boy.”

“No,” said Euphemia. “But I suppose he writes?”

“Oh, he writes.”

“Often?”

“Often enough. William never was one for wasting stamps.”

At Christmas he gave her a book entitled *Hints on Deportment for Young Women*, with certain passages marked in pencil. Jane brought it to us.

“It says a young woman should improve her mind and avoid loud laughing.”

“Do you laugh loudly?” Euphemia asked.

“Not when there’s anything worth laughing at, ma’am.”

There were also instructions upon speaking correctly, choosing sober colours, and never referring to trousers except as “the lower garments.” Jane treated the work with reverence for two days. On the third she put a hot flatiron upon it by accident, and for a week afterward William’s counsel on deportment had a diamond pattern scorched into its cover.

It was about this time that the milliner appeared.

Her name was Miss Primmer, though Jane called her “that person,” “the pianer woman,” and, in moments of candour, “the old cat in feathers.” She was thirty-four, according to Jane, forty if you looked at her in daylight, and connected with Gedge and Mallowby’s by the circumstance that she supplied trimmed hats to their mourning department. She rented a sitting-room over a stationer’s, possessed a piano, and wore spectacles for music but not for ordinary vision.

“William only goes there for cultivation,” Jane explained.

“Does he require much?” asked Euphemia.

“Not the kind she gives him. She plays pieces, and he turns the leaves. Anybody can turn leaves.”

Euphemia said nothing.

“And she’s got savings,” Jane continued. “Which is why she ought to be ashamed of herself. A woman with money don’t need to go taking other people’s intendeds.”

“Has William said anything?”

“He says I’m unreasonable.”

“And are you?”

“No, ma’am. Not more than what’s right.”

Jane then attacked the kitchen table with a scrubbing-brush. Whenever she reached Miss Primmer she scrubbed in circles; whenever she spoke of William she scrubbed in long, forgiving strokes.

The rupture came on a Thursday. We knew it first by a crash below, followed not by Jane’s customary “Lor’!” but by a dreadful composure. Presently she entered the dining-room carrying the remains of a vegetable dish.

“I’m sorry, ma’am. It slipped.”

“Never mind the dish,” said Euphemia. “What is it?”

“It’s nothing.”

Jane’s face was scarlet, but the scarlet ended sharply at the white line of her cap.

“William says he’s been led away.”

There was a pause.

“By Miss Primmer?”

“He don’t name no names. He says our stations is different now. He says, with his prospects, he’s got to consider his position.”

She set the broken pieces upon the sideboard, arranging them with extraordinary neatness.

“I suppose he means he’s above me.”

Euphemia made an indignant sound.

“He is weak,” said Jane quickly. “That’s what it is. He’s weak, and she knows his weak points. Pianers and savings and talking about art! Art! She makes hats for widows.”

She went back to the kitchen. That evening every knife in the house was sharpened.

Jane did not surrender without negotiation. On Friday she waited outside Gedge and Mallowby’s delivery entrance. William escaped by the customers’ door. On Saturday she called at Miss Primmer’s lodgings and remained, in spite of repeated requests to depart, for forty minutes. Her account of this interview was fragmentary.

“I spoke fair at first, ma’am. I said, ‘You know he’s engaged.’ And she says engagements is not marriages. So I says, ‘No, or you’d be worse than you are.’ Then she says William’s mind has expanded.”

“Expanded?”

“With improvement. So I says his ’ead has, anyhow, since he took to silk hats.”

“And what did William say?”

“He was behind the curtain.”

“Why?”

“He said afterward he didn’t wish for a scene.”

“And was there one?”

“Not what I’d call a scene, ma’am. Her landlady did.”

Within six weeks the banns were published. Jane attended chapel to hear them, standing at the back beneath a board that recorded the hymn numbers. She returned pale but erect.

“I heard it all,” she said. “He coughed when they said her name. That shows.”

“What does it show?” I asked.

“That he felt it.”

The wedding was fixed for a Monday morning, perhaps because Miss Primmer’s professional duties made Saturday inconvenient. Jane asked for leave from ten until one.

Euphemia looked at her.

“You are not going to the wedding?”

“I only want to see it.”

“I think you had better not.”

“I’ve a right to see what sort of kerridge she’s got.”

“No good will come of it.”

“I don’t expect good, ma’am.”

She spoke so quietly that Euphemia yielded. At a quarter to ten Jane left the house in her best bonnet, carrying a carpet-bag. It appeared too heavy for gloves or a handkerchief, but neither of us questioned her until she had gone.

“What was in that bag?” I asked.

Euphemia looked toward the door.

At half-past twelve Jane returned. She entered by the kitchen, placed the carpet-bag in a cupboard, and immediately began preparing lunch. Her manner was brisk, almost gay. She sang two lines of a comic song, stopped, and rattled the plates.

Euphemia waited until the pudding had been carried in.

“Well?” she asked.

“It was very nice, ma’am.”

“The wedding?”

“Very respectable. Two kerridges, white ribbons, and a boy from the shop in gaiters.”

“Did you see William?”

“Oh yes. He looked grand.”

“And the bride?”

“She wore grey. Grey! At her age!”

Jane cleared the fish plates.

“Did anything happen?” Euphemia asked.

“Nothing particular.”

She went to the door, returned for the salt, and added, without looking at us:

“I threw a boot.”

“A boot?”

“An old one. For luck.”

I confess that I put down my fork.

“Where did you obtain an old boot?”

“Bag of them, sir. Saved ’em up.”

“A bag!”

“I meant them for her. When they come out, everybody cheered, and she smiled as if the street belonged to her. So I threw the little button one first.”

“And hit her?”

“No, sir.”

Jane’s lips twitched.

“It caught William.”

“Where?”

“In the eye.”

There are moments when sympathy and laughter contend so violently that the countenance becomes a battlefield. Euphemia covered her mouth with her napkin.

“Was he hurt?” I managed.

“It come up black immediate. He looked at me.”

“And what did you do?”

“I went away. There wasn’t no sense wasting the others.”

She carried out the plates with dignity.

For the rest of that day Jane maintained a magnificent indifference. She informed the butcher-boy that weddings were foolishness, told the baker she hoped William would enjoy listening to scales, and assured Euphemia that she had never cared for men with pale eyelashes.

Yet that evening the house grew unnaturally quiet.

Euphemia found her in the scullery with a basin of potatoes before her. They had been peeled past all reasonable utility; one, reduced to the size of a walnut, revolved slowly in Jane’s wet hand.

“I’m not sorry,” she said before Euphemia spoke. “He deserved it.”

“I daresay.”

“Only—I didn’t mean to hit him.”

“No.”

“It was her I aimed at.”

“I understand.”

“He looked handsome, barring the eye. He always did look well in a collar.”

The potato slipped into the water.

“I could’ve made him comfortable. I’d saved eleven pounds. I never told him, because I meant it for the furniture. A little place we was going to have—over a shop, perhaps—with curtains in the parlour. He liked green.”

Her voice changed on the last word.

“He said once there’d be room for a bird. I don’t care for birds, not particular, but I’d have had one. And Sundays we’d have walked out, and later—when he was manager—there might’ve been a girl to help. Not a servant exactly. I wouldn’t put on airs.”

She pressed both hands against the edge of the sink.

“But he got on. He kept getting on, and I—I suppose I looked too high. He was above me. He said so.”

“My dear,” Euphemia began.

“No, ma’am, don’t. If you’re kind, I shall—”

And then the brave structure of anger, ridicule, Miss Primmer’s age, the piano, the grey dress, the two carriages, even the black eye itself, all fell together. Jane bent over the basin and sobbed—not prettily, nor with any regard for the dignity proper to domestic service, but with the exhausted, gasping grief of one who mourns not merely a faithless man but a whole vanished future: green curtains, Sunday walks, the unwanted bird, and the little rooms that had never existed except in hope.

Euphemia entirely forgot the household manual. She put her arms round Jane.

I withdrew.

For some weeks afterward Jane was gentle with the crockery, and I learned to miss its former dangers. Then, one morning, a plate fell, a hymn began in the wrong key, and she called down the passage that the milkman was a “swindling bankrup’.” These were favourable signs.

William’s eye recovered. Whether his marriage did so I cannot say. Jane ceased to mention him, except once, when she observed that people who married for pianers generally got music enough.

By spring the butcher-boy had developed a habit of arriving twice whenever Jane answered the door. He was a broad, cheerful youth with no silk hat, no yellow gloves, and, so far as we knew, no intention of improving himself. Jane called him an impudent object and saved him the browned corner of the pudding.

“He’s nothing to me, ma’am,” she told Euphemia.

But she said it while adjusting her cap in the kitchen glass; and Euphemia, who had at last learned when not to ask questions, merely smiled.

### CAUGHT RED-HANDED

There were three of them in the room, although the room had plainly been designed for one, and for one, moreover, whose possessions should be confined to a narrow bedstead, a washstand, two shirts, and a disposition incapable of rebellion.

It was the third floor back of a boarding-house in West Thirty-Ninth Street; a room of that undecided colour which comes from old wallpaper, gas fumes, and the rubbing of many shoulders against too little space. Its one window looked upon a shaft containing fire escapes, clothes-lines, and the windows of other rooms very like itself. A strip of sky could be seen by leaning out and looking directly upward—a practice which the landlady discouraged, not from anxiety for her lodgers’ necks, but because the sill was loose.

The three occupants were Blake, an English newspaper man; Leroux, a French lithographer; and Petersen, a Dane who had been successively a sailor, a carpenter, a waiter, and a demonstrator of a patent corkscrew. None had lately been prosperous in his profession. Blake slept upon the bed, having engaged the room originally; Petersen occupied a folding cot which, when opened, prevented the door from opening more than halfway; and Leroux disposed himself upon a mattress laid between the washstand and the stove. At seven each morning the mattress was rolled up and tied with a necktie belonging to whichever gentleman could least immediately use it.

Their domestic arrangements possessed the elaborate simplicity of poverty. Three chipped cups served for tea, coffee, soup, shaving water, and, upon occasions of ceremony, beer. There were two spoons, one fork, and a knife with a broken handle, which Petersen asserted had belonged to a Swedish count, though he could not explain how it had come into the possession of a Danish waiter in Hoboken. A saucepan stood upon the little gas stove. When food was plentiful, the saucepan cooked it; when food was scarce, it boiled water; and when there was neither food nor gas, it gave the room a settled and respectable appearance.

Just then the three friends were, as Blake expressed it, in very low water. The Frenchman had not been paid for a poster showing a young lady of surprising dimensions recommending a liver pill. The Dane had lost his position in a restaurant through dropping a tureen into the lap of a gentleman who was afterward found to be one of the proprietors. Blake had received eight dollars from a Sunday paper for an account of a Bowery stabbing, but the eight dollars had gone in rent, laundry, and the redemption of his overcoat from a pawnbroker. The landlady was owed an additional week and had begun to smile whenever she spoke of it, which was more alarming than anger.

Meals had consequently become irregular. Breakfast was usually a matter of negotiation with a neighbouring baker. Luncheon depended upon ingenuity and upon the generosity of free-lunch counters, whose keepers possessed sharp eyes and an objection to customers who purchased one schooner of beer and consumed the cold meats of an entire afternoon. Dinner occurred when Providence, journalism, lithography, or the patent corkscrew provided it.

On the evening in question Providence had not yet declared itself.

At six o’clock Leroux and Petersen went out upon a combined expedition. The Frenchman had heard that a printer in Bleecker Street required an artist; the Dane believed that a saloon keeper on Eighth Avenue owed him two dollars and might be surprised into paying fifty cents. They promised, if successful, to bring back sausages, bread, and beer. If unsuccessful, they would bring back news, which was cheaper and lasted longer.

Blake remained in the room because he possessed a more immediate, though less hopeful, means of making money. A magazine editor had said that he would consider a story of psychological interest, not less than five thousand words, if submitted before noon the following day. Blake had returned home full of phrases such as “subconscious motive,” “hereditary impulse,” and “the borderland of identity,” which he had picked up without charge in the editor’s office. He proposed to write a masterpiece, sell it for one hundred dollars, pay every debt, purchase a new suit, and perhaps dine.

The order of these intentions was characteristic of his hunger.

He seated himself at a table made unsteady by the literary works stored beneath one leg, lit the gas, and arranged six sheets of paper before him. The sight of blank paper always affected Blake in one of two ways: either it became a wide gate through which ideas rushed so rapidly that he could not capture them, or it became a white wall upon which nothing whatever could climb. That evening it was a wall.

He wrote:

> There are moments when the sentient ego, recoiling from the objective pressure of circumstance, discovers in its own duality the essential—

He stopped.

The essential what?

The essential tragedy, perhaps. That sounded well, but did it mean anything? Blake was not greatly disturbed by that consideration. He had long ago divided readers of psychological fiction into two classes: those who supplied a meaning for themselves, and those who, finding none, pretended that the author had concealed one at an inaccessible depth. Both classes were flattering to the author.

He added “tragedy of consciousness,” read the sentence aloud, and disliked it. His voice sounded hollow in the room.

From below came the smell of frying onions.

The odour rose through the house with leisurely cruelty. It entered under his door, curled about the stove, and established itself between him and the manuscript. Blake attempted to think of the sentient ego, but saw instead a brown sausage splitting in a pan. He considered the objective pressure of circumstance and found that it resembled a loaf of bread. He dipped his pen, wrote “Man,” and listened to the rattle of plates in the dining-room below.

Hunger and Blake were not strangers, but they were ordinarily no more than slight acquaintances. To-night hunger had become intimate. It pinched his stomach, sharpened his hearing, filled his head with hot visions, and made the gas flame appear yellow and edible. He rose, examined the cupboard, and discovered half a teaspoonful of mustard, a crust too ancient even for philosophy, and a paper bag which had once contained cakes. He smelled the bag. It was a humiliating act, and it produced no nourishment.

There was still no sound of Leroux or Petersen.

He returned to the table. Outside, New York conducted its immense and indifferent business. Elevated trains shook the distance; carts struck iron upon the street; newsboys called editions full of murders, elections, divorces, and wars. The city had money enough to light a million windows, yet none of it had found its way into the third floor back. Blake thought of England, of a clean kitchen in a small Surrey house, and of his mother placing a joint upon the table every Sunday with the calm certainty of a woman to whom roast beef was part of the natural order.

Homesickness, like hunger, made poor company for literary composition.

At half-past seven he wrote another paragraph. In it a young man entered a drawing-room and immediately became aware that he had lived the scene before in some anterior existence. Blake had never entered a drawing-room in New York and would not have known what furniture to put into one. He made the room dim, which saved detail, and gave the young man an hereditary terror of clocks. Then the clock upon the mantel—whose presence Blake had overlooked until that instant—struck twelve.

He crossed out eleven lines.

A stupor settled upon him. The room was cold, though his forehead felt warm. He had slept little for several nights, having spent one of them at a police court in search of material and another correcting, for three dollars, the autobiography of a gentleman who had made a fortune in green goods and wished posterity to mistake him for a financier. The gas hissed. The blank sheets blurred. His own sentence—“the sentient ego recoiling”—seemed to crawl backward and forward like a pale insect.

He rested his head upon his hands.

Only for a moment, he told himself.

Then somebody knocked at the door.

Blake raised his head sharply. He could not have slept, for the gas was still burning and his pen remained between his fingers. Yet the room appeared changed in some indefinable fashion. It was narrower, perhaps, and the shadows in its corners were heavier. The knock came again—three slow taps, delivered with precision.

He expected Petersen’s cheerful assault upon the door or Leroux’s quick tattoo. This was neither.

“Come in,” he called.

The door opened halfway, stopped against the folded cot, and admitted a man sideways.

He was of middle height and dressed in a black coat which had once been good. His hat was pulled low. He carried a small black bag, such as a country doctor might carry, and moved with an odd, crablike caution, keeping one shoulder advanced and his face partly turned away. Having entered, he closed the door behind him and remained near it.

“Mr. Edward Blake?” he asked.

His voice was smooth, almost oily.

“That is my name.”

“Mr. Edward James Blake, formerly of London?”

“Yes.”

“Newspaper reporter?”

“When there is reporting to be done.”

The man lowered his eyes. “I have business with you.”

“Then you have the advantage of me,” said Blake. “What business?”

The stranger did not answer at once. He set the bag upon the floor, removed his hat, and glanced around the poor room. It was not the curious glance of a visitor, but the swift examination of a man verifying arrangements already known to him. Blake felt an obscure dislike of him.

The stranger’s face, so far as it could be seen, was grey and thin. A short black beard covered the jaw. He held his head strangely, always presenting his left cheek while the right remained in shadow.

“I come,” he said, “from a well-wisher.”

“A what?”

“A well-wisher.”

“Mine?”

“Yours, Mr. Edward James Blake.”

Blake laughed. Hunger, fatigue, and disappointment had made him irritable.

“My well-wishers do not commonly send messengers. Some of them do not even send answers to letters. What does this anonymous friend wish?”

“To relieve your present embarrassment.”

The stranger stooped and opened the black bag.

Blake watched. He had seen strange things drawn from black bags in police courts—counterfeit plates, burglars’ tools, jewellery, revolvers, and once a human ear preserved in a bottle. He was therefore prepared for almost anything except what appeared.

The man lifted out a packet of banknotes.

Then another.

And another.

They were new bills, crisp, square, and bound in paper bands. He placed them upon the table, where they made a small, orderly pile beside Blake’s manuscript.

The reporter stared at them.

“What is this?”

“Ten thousand dollars.”

For a second the room seemed to expand about him. Ten thousand dollars! The phrase had the splendour of music. Rent, food, clothes, voyages, books, leisure—all the troubles that had pressed upon him melted like bubbles in the sun. Ten thousand dollars would carry him back to England; it would provide a room with two windows; it would purchase dinners so plentiful that a man might leave food upon his plate. It would allow him to write masterpieces without waiting for editors to accept them.

Then the habits of the police court reasserted themselves.

He touched no bill.

“You say this money is for me?”

“For Mr. Edward James Blake.”

“From whom?”

“A well-wisher.”

“I have heard that. What is his name?”

“I am not permitted to tell you.”

“Why should anybody give me ten thousand dollars?”

The stranger’s left shoulder moved slightly.

“That does not concern me.”

“It concerns me very closely. Is there some condition?”

“None.”

“No work expected? No papers to be signed?”

“One paper only. A receipt.”

“Ah!”

The single syllable sounded louder than Blake intended. The stranger raised his eyes, but instantly lowered them again.

“What kind of receipt?”

“A simple acknowledgement that you have received ten thousand dollars.”

“From whom?”

“From me, acting on behalf of a well-wisher.”

Blake pushed back his chair. The new notes gave a faint crackle, as if whispering together. They were very beautiful, and for that reason more suspicious.

He had reported too many cases involving unexpected inheritances, mysterious partners, green-goods men, and charitable gentlemen who required merely a signature. Ten thousand dollars did not walk unattended into third floor back rooms. When money sought out poverty after dark, it usually brought a policeman behind it.

“Put the bills away,” he said.

The stranger did not move.

“You decline them?”

“I decline to receive them in this fashion.”

“You are a poor man, Mr. Blake.”

“I am aware of it.”

“You owe rent.”

Blake’s suspicion sharpened. “How do you know that?”

“I have made inquiries.”

“Who sent you?”

“A well-wisher.”

Again the phrase! Softly spoken, perfectly controlled, it had begun to sound like a threat.

The visitor drew a folded paper from his inner pocket and placed it beside the notes. “Read the receipt. You will find nothing objectionable.”

Blake took the paper by one corner. It contained only two lines:

> Received from an anonymous friend, through his representative, the sum of ten thousand dollars.

A space had been left for his signature.

“That proves nothing,” he said.

“It proves that you received the money.”

“Precisely. And perhaps to-morrow the money will be declared stolen.”

The man smiled without showing his teeth.

“You are cautious.”

“I earn my living by observing what happens to men who are not.”

“Then summon a witness.”

The suggestion was made so readily that Blake hesitated.

“A witness?”

“Certainly. Let him see the money paid. Let him read the receipt and watch you sign. What could be safer?”

There was reason in this—too much reason, perhaps—but a witness would at least place the facts beyond dispute. Blake thought of Mr. Barclay, who occupied the fourth floor front. Barclay was an elderly clerk retired from an insurance office, a spare, exact man with white whiskers and an almost religious respect for documents. The landlady called him a miser because he counted the lumps of coal delivered to his room, but Blake knew him to be honest. He would serve admirably.

“I shall ask Mr. Barclay to come down.”

“As you please.”

“You know him?”

“I know no one in this house.”

Blake rose. The stranger remained near the table, his face still turned obliquely from the light.

“You will wait here?”

“Yes.”

“With the money untouched?”

“Certainly.”

Blake went to the door, but before opening it looked back. The visitor’s right hand rested upon the black bag. He wore grey gloves, although the room was warm.

A foolish uneasiness touched Blake. For an instant he considered calling the landlady instead. Yet the woman would see ten thousand dollars, remember the rent, and become a witness whose enthusiasm might impair her accuracy. Barclay was better.

Blake stepped into the passage.

The hall gas burned low. Above him, the narrow staircase bent into darkness. The house, usually full of footsteps, coughs, voices, and doors, had become unaccountably still. Even the dining-room below was silent. The smell of onions lingered, but faintly, like the memory of another evening.

He climbed the stairs.

“Mr. Barclay!” he called.

There was no answer.

Barclay’s door stood slightly open. That was unusual. The old man disliked draughts and always closed his door with care.

Blake knocked.

“Mr. Barclay, may I trouble you for a moment?”

Silence.

He pushed the door wider.

At first he saw only the lamp upon the table and the familiar black furniture. Then he saw Mr. Barclay.

The old man lay on the floor between the bed and the fireplace. One hand was doubled beneath him; the other reached toward the door, the fingers curved as though they had made a last attempt to grasp the carpet. His white whiskers were stained darkly. Across his throat ran a great red wound.

Blake could not move.

The lamp burned with a steady flame. Something beside the body glistened. Blood had flowed across the polished floor and gathered in the cracks. It had soaked the old man’s collar and spread upon his shirt. Yet more terrible than the wound, because more inexplicable, were the dollar bills.

They lay everywhere.

New notes were scattered over the floor, upon the bed, beneath the dead man’s hand. Several had fallen into the blood and were curling at the edges. One clung obscenely to Barclay’s sleeve. They seemed to have been flung over him in handfuls.

Blake’s breath came in a whisper.

“Murdered!”

He knelt, then drew back before touching the body. Barclay was dead; no doubt could exist. The eyes were partly open, fixed upon the ceiling with an expression of mild surprise. A little warmth still rose from him. The murder had been committed only moments before.

The stranger!

Blake sprang up.

The man had declared that he knew no one in the house. He had come carrying money—new notes like these. He had suggested a witness. Had he known that Blake would select Barclay? Impossible. Unless he had himself sent Blake upstairs for the deliberate purpose of making him discover the body.

But why?

A sound came from below.

Blake ran from the room, down the staircase, and along the passage. His hand slipped upon the knob of his own door. The handle felt wet and sticky, but in his agitation he scarcely noticed it.

He burst into the room.

It was empty.

The stranger had vanished.

The black bag was gone. The packets of notes had disappeared from the table. The receipt remained, but it had been torn across and cast upon the floor.

“Hallo!” Blake cried. “You there!”

No answer came from the passage. He rushed to the window and looked down. The shaft was empty except for shirts swaying upon a line. He opened the closet. Only three coats hung there. He looked beneath the bed, an action at once instinctive and absurd; no man could have concealed himself in the few inches between the springs and the floor.

Then he saw the gloves.

They lay near the stove: two grey gloves, crumpled together. Upon them were dark stains.

Blood.

Close beside them was a butcher’s knife.

Its broad blade was wet and red. Blood had gathered at the point and fallen in a round drop upon Blake’s discarded manuscript. The handle was wrapped with cord. The knife looked heavy enough to cut through bone.

For several seconds Blake stood without thought. Then thought returned upon him in a torrent.

The stranger had killed Barclay. He had carried the knife and money into this room. He had lured Blake upstairs. While Blake was absent, he had left the weapon and gloves and escaped. But why leave them here? Why scatter money about the dead man? Why offer the same sort of notes to Blake?

To accuse him.

The answer came so plainly that he spoke it aloud.

“To accuse me!”

Every circumstance pointed one way. Blake was poor—desperately poor. Barclay was reputed to keep money in his room. Blake had gone upstairs. He had found the body alone. The knife was now in Blake’s room; the murderer’s gloves were there; banknotes—

He looked around.

Banknotes were scattered beneath the table.

He had not noticed them at first. Five, ten, perhaps twenty new bills lay upon the carpet, half concealed by papers. One rested beneath his chair. Another protruded from the pocket of his overcoat, though he had never placed it there.

The whole design flashed before him in its devilish completeness.

Blake snatched the bill from his pocket. As he did so, he saw his fingers.

They were red.

Blood covered the tips and lay in wet lines across his palm.

He stared, unable to breathe. He had not touched Barclay. He had not touched the knife. How had the blood come there?

The door handle!

Wet and slippery—it had been smeared with blood. He had grasped it when he returned. The stranger had prepared it before leaving, knowing that Blake would seize it in haste. Blake remembered that, on entering, he had passed a hand across his forehead.

He turned toward the small mirror above the washstand.

A face looked back at him from the glass—a white, terrified face marked upon one cheek and across the brow with blood.

For an instant he did not know it as his own.

Then the full horror broke upon him. He had been painted with the dead man’s blood. If anyone entered now, he would be found with stained hands, a bloodied face, the weapon at his feet, stolen bills in his room, and Barclay’s body overhead.

Caught red-handed!

He seized the washbasin. It was empty. The water pitcher contained only a few drops. He rubbed his hands upon a towel, but the blood spread into a dull stain. He tried to wipe his face and made it worse. The mirror showed a red smear from temple to jaw.

Water! He must obtain water.

No—he must not leave the room. If seen in the passage, he would be taken instantly. Yet to remain was equally fatal. He must call the police himself. That was the action of an innocent man. He would tell them of the stranger, the bag, the ten thousand dollars, the receipt.

The receipt!

He snatched the torn pieces from the floor. The writing had disappeared.

The paper was blank.

Blake held it beneath the gas. Not a word remained upon either fragment. Had the ink been some chemical trick, vanishing with heat or exposure? There was no proof now that a stranger had ever entered.

His mind raced through the evidence, seeking one solid fact and finding none. The visitor’s name? Unknown. Description? A grey man in a black coat, face half hidden. The money? Gone, except for the bills planted as evidence. Motive for the supposed benefaction? None. Witnesses? None.

Barclay would never speak.

Blake rushed to the hall and leaned over the banisters.

“Mrs. Grady!” he called.

No answer.

“Mrs. Grady! Help! Murder!”

The house swallowed his voice. He called again until his throat hurt, yet not a door opened. The silence was monstrous. Had everyone gone? Was the whole house in league with the stranger? The thought was insane; nevertheless, in that dark passage it seemed no more insane than ten thousand dollars offered by a well-wisher.

He started down the stairs.

The front-door bell rang.

Its clamour tore through the house—one violent, prolonged peal that shook the wire in the wall.

Blake stopped.

Again the bell rang.

Then came knocking, hard and official.

The police!

The thought crashed through his brain like thunder.

How could they have arrived already unless they had been summoned in advance? The stranger had arranged even this. He had murdered Barclay, placed the evidence, smeared the handle, and gone directly to the police with some story that would bring them to Blake’s door at the exact instant of discovery.

The knocking below increased.

“Open, in the name of the law!”

Blake retreated up the stairs. His first impulse was to hide the knife, but that would be an admission of guilt. His second was to throw the bills from the window, but innocent men did not destroy evidence. His third was to run. Yet where could he run? Down the stairs into the officers’ arms? Across the roofs with blood upon his face?

The front door burst open.

Heavy feet entered the hall. Voices spoke quickly. A woman screamed—the landlady at last—and immediately began explaining that she had always distrusted foreigners, bachelors, newspaper men, and persons who paid rent irregularly.

“This way, officers!” called another voice.

Blake froze.

It was the oily voice of the stranger.

“He is on the third floor. Edward James Blake.”

The name ascended the staircase before the men did, solemn as an accusation.

Blake stepped back into his room. He shut the door, then at once opened it again; a locked door would imply resistance. He looked at the knife. It lay in full gaslight. The blood upon it glistened.

Footsteps mounted.

He tried to form a statement.

“My name is Edward James Blake. A man came here—”

No.

“I was writing when an unknown person offered me ten thousand dollars—”

Absurd.

“I found Mr. Barclay with his throat cut—”

That sounded like confession.

The steps reached the second landing.

Blake’s imagination, trained upon police reports and nourished by sensational newspapers, flung open all its gates. He saw the police court crowded with reporters who would describe him as “the English journalist of mysterious antecedents.” He saw the landlady swearing that he had threatened Barclay over a debt. He saw Leroux and Petersen returning with sausages to find the room sealed. He saw the knife produced before a jury, the stained gloves, the notes, the blood upon his face.

He saw the death chamber.

“No!” he whispered.

A shadow crossed the threshold.

Two uniformed policemen entered, followed by a broad detective with a brown moustache. Behind them stood the mysterious visitor, still holding the black bag. His face was no longer concealed. The right cheek and beard were smeared with blood.

“There he is,” said the stranger calmly.

The officers looked at Blake’s hands, then at the knife.

The detective’s expression changed.

“Don’t move!”

“I can explain,” Blake cried.

“Of course you can.”

“That man killed Barclay!”

The stranger raised his eyebrows with an admirable appearance of pain.

“I never saw him before this evening,” he said.

“You came here with money!”

“I came because Mr. Barclay sent for me. I found the old gentleman dead and this man bending over him.”

“It is a lie!”

“He pursued me downstairs with the knife. I escaped and brought the police.”

“A lie—a monstrous lie! The knife was left here. The gloves are his. Look at his face! There is blood upon it.”

The visitor touched his cheek as though discovering the stain for the first time.

“The prisoner struck me when I tried to stop him.”

“Prisoner!” Blake repeated.

The word seemed to close iron around him.

The detective picked up one glove by its clean edge. “These yours?” he asked Blake.

“No.”

“They fit you?”

“I do not know.”

“Try one.”

“I will not!”

The stranger gave a slight sigh. “You see, officer?”

“Search him,” said the detective.

A policeman seized Blake’s arm. Another thrust a hand into his coat and drew out a packet of new bills.

Blake stared at it. The pocket had been empty an hour before.

“Barclay’s money,” said the stranger.

“No! It was planted there.”

“How much?” asked the detective.

The officer counted rapidly. The notes crackled between his fingers with a sound like dry laughter.

“Five thousand.”

The detective whistled.

“There were ten thousand,” Blake said wildly. “He offered me ten thousand. He asked for a receipt. I went to get Barclay as a witness, and found him dead. When I returned, this man was gone.”

“But I am here,” said the stranger.

“You came back with them!”

“Where is the receipt?” asked the detective.

Blake held out the torn blank paper.

“This—this was it. The writing has vanished.”

The policemen looked at one another.

Blake heard his own explanation as they heard it: impossible, childish, the desperate invention of a man trapped beyond hope.

The detective bent toward the blood-smeared handle. “Prints will be on that,” he said.

“Yes!” cried Blake. “His prints.”

“Whose?”

“The murderer’s.”

“But he wore gloves, according to you.”

Blake turned toward the grey gloves on the floor. The logic tightened like a rope. If the stranger had worn them, he had left no prints; if Blake’s prints were found upon the handle, the blood would prove that he had passed from the murdered man’s room to his own.

The stranger smiled.

It was scarcely a smile—only the smallest lifting at one corner of the mouth—but Blake saw in it the perfection of the plot. The man had foreseen every question, every movement, every appeal to reason. He had constructed a machine of circumstance and placed Blake at its centre. Poverty supplied the motive; the money supplied the proof; blood transferred guilt from one face to another. Even innocence had become evidence.

“Take him,” said the detective.

The policemen closed upon Blake.

He struggled.

“I did not do it! Listen to me! I never touched Barclay. That man—look at him! Why does he stand sideways? Why does he hide his face? Open the bag!”

The stranger moved back.

“The bag!” Blake shouted. “The knife came from it. The bills came from it. Open it!”

The detective glanced at the visitor. “Any objection?”

“None.”

The bag was placed on the table and opened.

It contained a stethoscope, bandages, two medicine bottles, and a small Bible.

“He is a doctor,” said the detective.

“I am Mr. Barclay’s physician,” said the stranger.

Again the machine closed upon Blake.

His strength left him. The room swayed, elongated, became a tunnel of gaslight and faces. The officers’ fingers dug into his shoulders. He attempted to call out, but his voice made no sound. It was like those nightmares in which one beats upon a door with powerless hands while danger approaches steadily from behind.

Yet this was not a nightmare. The grip was real. The pain was real. The blood in the mirror was real.

“Come quietly,” said one of the policemen.

“No,” Blake gasped.

The hands shook him.

“Come, Blake!”

The room dissolved in darkness.

“Wake up, you English corpse!”

Blake struck outward. Someone caught his wrists; someone else laughed. He opened his eyes.

Petersen’s broad red face hung over him. Leroux stood behind the Dane, holding a paper parcel in one hand and a tin pail in the other. The gas still hissed above the table. Blake’s head rested upon his manuscript.

“There!” cried Petersen. “He lives. I said he was not dead—only literary.”

Blake stared at them.

“Police,” he muttered.

“No police,” said Leroux. “Supper.”

“Barclay—his throat—”

“Monsieur Barclay has just abused us on the stairs because the beer dripped upon his boot,” replied the Frenchman. “His throat is excellent.”

Blake looked wildly around the room.

There was no knife. No gloves. No money. His hands were clean except for ink. In the mirror his face appeared pale and creased where it had lain upon his sleeve, but there was no blood upon it.

Petersen released his shoulders. Those had been the policemen’s hands.

The Dane set a loaf, sausages, cheese, and onions upon the table with the grandeur of a magician producing a banquet.

“We have news,” he announced. “I obtain employment to-morrow at a hotel where the tureens are of metal and cannot break. Leroux is to design a label for pickled fish. Also, the saloon keeper paid one dollar, because I stood in the doorway and discouraged trade.”

“And,” added Leroux, raising the pail, “there is beer.”

Blake touched his face once more, then began to laugh. He laughed until the room, the sausages, and his astonished companions blurred before him. Hunger, fatigue, police courts, unpaid rent, psychological fiction, and the smell of onions had combined to write a masterpiece without his assistance.

“What is amusing?” demanded Petersen.

“Nothing,” said Blake. “Everything. Give me food before I inherit ten thousand dollars.”

They drew up the three available seats. Leroux lighted the stove; Petersen cut the bread with the knife of the Swedish count; and soon the sausages hissed in the pan with a sound far more agreeable than the crackling of new banknotes.

Upon the table lay Blake’s unfinished story. Its solitary sentence still announced that the sentient ego, recoiling from the objective pressure of circumstance, had discovered the essential tragedy of consciousness.

Blake read it, tore the sheet across, and dropped the pieces into the stove.

Then he ate.

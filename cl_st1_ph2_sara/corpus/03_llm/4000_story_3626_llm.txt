### The Honorable Gentleman from Calumet

I first met the Honorable Ephraim P. Hallowell upon the westbound night express, somewhere between Bellweather Junction and the Missouri line.

He entered my compartment at dusk, bringing with him a valise, a buffalo robe, and that atmosphere of gentle consequence which belongs to men who have once occupied public office and have never entirely vacated it. He was perhaps sixty years of age, silver-haired, clean-shaven, and dressed in broadcloth of an unfashionable but senatorial dignity. His eyes were mild; his voice was low and persuasive. Even when asking whether the opposite seat was engaged, he gave the impression that he had considered the constitutional bearings of the matter.

I assured him that it was free.

He thanked me, arranged his robe, and sat down. For an hour we exchanged those harmless observations by which railway acquaintances are commonly constructed: the condition of the road, the promise of wheat, the decline of manners, the increase of taxation, and the extraordinary unwillingness of railway corporations to heat their cars before a passenger has lost all use for warmth.

At length the snow, which had been falling thinly, thickened against the windows. The engine whistled with a desolate sound, and my companion leaned toward the glass.

“That reminds me,” he said, “of the winter we lost Barlow.”

There was nothing in his manner to prepare me for the meaning of this remark.

“An accident?” I asked.

“In a sense,” he said. “Though the immediate proceedings were entirely regular.”

He smiled with such benevolence that I supposed he referred to some minor political defeat.

“You were in public life, perhaps?”

“Three terms in Congress,” he replied modestly. “Calumet district. I afterward declined the governorship for family reasons and because the nomination was not offered. But the habits of representative government remain with a man. They were of great service to us during the calamity.”

The train plunged into a cutting where the snow whirled upward like smoke. Mr. Hallowell watched it for a moment, folded his hands over his waistcoat, and began.

“It was in February of ’71. Twenty-four of us left Fort Ransom in two passenger coaches attached to a freight locomotive. The morning was bright, though bitterly cold, and the prairie lay under a level sheet of whiteness that appeared to extend beyond the jurisdiction of man. By noon the wind rose. It came first in long sighs from the northwest, then in furious volleys, sweeping the snow before it like spray torn from a stormy sea. The telegraph poles vanished one by one; the sun became a pale stain; the rails were buried.

“At four o’clock we struck a drift.

“It was no ordinary accumulation, sir. It stood across the track like a colossal grave. The engine entered it with courage, trembled, groaned, and stopped. The wheels spun uselessly. The trainmen dug until their beards were armored with ice, but every shovelful removed was replaced by ten. Night descended—not gradually, but as though a black curtain had been dropped upon the whole white desert.

“We had twenty-four passengers, including myself. There was Judge Absalom Barlow of Iowa; Colonel Treadwell of Vermont; Peter Gaskins, a contractor from Indiana; the Reverend Lucius Mott of Maine; Simeon Bragg of Illinois; Professor Doolittle of Massachusetts; Cornelius Pike of Oregon; young Felix Ward of New York; and several others of equal standing, though not, as events proved, of equal value.

“Our provisions consisted of nine crackers, a jar of mustard, three peppermint lozenges, and a ham sandwich belonging to Mr. Bragg. Mr. Bragg maintained that the sandwich was private property protected by the fifth article of the amendments. A contrary opinion prevailed. We appointed a committee of three to examine the sandwich, whereupon it was reported adversely from committee, divided into twenty-four portions, and consumed. Mr. Bragg entered a protest in the record, but ate his portion.

“The first night was severe, though hope sustained us. We burned the baggage car’s loose timber and sang hymns. The second day came gray and shrieking. Snow packed itself against the windows until the coaches were tombs. The conductor and fireman attempted to reach a section house seven miles east, but returned after an hour, frozen about the eyelashes and spiritually discouraged.

“The second night—cold.

“The third day—no relief.

“The fourth—still the storm.

“And hunger.

“You who have always eaten, my dear sir, cannot understand hunger. It begins as an inconvenience and becomes a magistrate. It issues commands. It overturns philosophy, abolishes refinement, and makes the memory of a boiled potato shine in the mind like a celestial vision. Men spoke tenderly of boardinghouse hash which, under happier circumstances, they had denounced as an outrage upon civilization. Colonel Treadwell wept openly while describing a turnip.

“On the fifth day Professor Doolittle ate the leather strap of his traveling case. This was considered selfish, because there were not twenty-four straps. On the sixth, the Reverend Mott delivered a discourse upon endurance, but was compelled to discontinue when Mr. Gaskins accused him of looking fixedly at his leg.

“Then came the seventh dawning.

“The storm had ceased. That was almost worse. Through a hole cleared in the snow we saw the prairie beneath a blue and pitiless sky—smooth, glittering, endless. The drifts rose about us in monstrous forms: pyramids, fortresses, burial mounds. Not a roof, tree, bird, or column of smoke broke the waste. The world seemed dead, and we alone had been omitted from the general peace.

“At ten o’clock Judge Barlow rose. He was weak, but his judicial bearing remained unimpaired.

“‘Gentlemen,’ he said, ‘this cannot continue.’

“No one disputed him.

“‘We are civilized men,’ he continued. ‘Some of us have held office. One of us has written a pamphlet. We cannot permit a grave emergency to overthrow the safeguards of orderly deliberation. Yet we must eat.’

“A solemn murmur passed through the coach.

“Mr. Bragg moved that we postpone eating indefinitely. Colonel Treadwell amended the motion by striking out ‘indefinitely’ and inserting ‘until half past twelve.’ The amendment was defeated, Mr. Bragg alone voting in the affirmative, though he afterward claimed to have misunderstood the question.

“Then Peter Gaskins rose and said: ‘Mr. Chairman, I move that a committee of five be appointed to consider the condition of the company and recommend such measures as may be necessary for its preservation.’

“‘Who is chairman?’ asked young Ward.

“After some discussion, Judge Barlow was elected temporary chairman by a vote of thirteen to eleven. The minority alleged undue judicial influence, but agreed to abide by the result. I was made secretary, having a pencil and the least trembling hand.

“The Committee on Preservation retired to the baggage car. They returned in forty minutes with a majority report recommending that one passenger be selected by ballot, prepared in a suitable manner, and distributed impartially, with the larger portions allotted to those whose physical condition demanded immediate assistance. A minority report, signed by Mr. Bragg and Reverend Mott, favored beginning with the train crew. The conductor objected that railway employees were not within the representative body and could not constitutionally be appropriated without notice. The chairman sustained the objection.

“The majority report was then accepted.

“Mr. Gaskins nominated Cornelius Pike of Oregon.

“I shall never forget the silence which followed. Mr. Pike was a long, bony man, nearly six feet four, with a yellow beard and a disposition sharpened by years of litigation. He rose slowly.

“‘Mr. Chairman,’ he said, ‘I object.’

“Judge Barlow ruled that a nominee might object, but could not debate his own qualifications.

“Professor Doolittle appealed from the decision, maintaining that no man could be deprived of life, liberty, or eligibility without an opportunity to address the body. The ruling of the chair was sustained, fifteen to nine.

“Colonel Treadwell then obtained the floor and opposed Mr. Pike’s nomination.

“‘I oppose it,’ he cried, ‘on grounds of economy, humanity, common prudence, and digestion! What is the gentleman from Oregon? A framework! A geographical suggestion! A collection of angles clothed in partisan hostility! Shall we, after seven days of honorable suffering, cast away our first opportunity upon a man whose entire person might be hung upon a hat peg? Look at him! No breadth, no substance, no promise! His temper is acid, his principles are dry, his politics are indigestible. I ask this assembly whether it intends to preserve itself by chewing sinew until rescue arrives—or until eternity does!’

“Mr. Pike called the remarks personal.

“The chairman said they were largely anatomical.

“The colonel continued. ‘I have no prejudice against Oregon. I honor her rivers, admire her mountains, and entertain for her salmon a respect which approaches affection. But I deny that Oregon has sent us, in the person of Mr. Pike, a satisfactory breakfast!’

“This argument made a powerful impression. Mr. Pike’s nomination was withdrawn by consent.

“Simeon Bragg then nominated Judge Barlow.

“That was a better choice. The judge was portly, of sedentary habits, and universally esteemed. There was, however, a difficulty. He occupied the chair.

“Mr. Bragg moved that the chair be declared vacant. Professor Doolittle objected that the motion was insulting. Mr. Bragg replied that it was not intended to reflect upon Judge Barlow’s conduct as presiding officer, which had been exemplary, but only upon his general condition, which was nourishing.

“The judge himself behaved nobly. He said he would not permit private considerations to interfere with public necessity. He vacated the chair and took his place beside the stove. I was elected to preside temporarily.”

“You mean,” I interrupted, “that these gentlemen actually contemplated—”

“One moment,” said Mr. Hallowell softly. “The contest was not yet decided.”

He adjusted the buffalo robe over his knees and continued.

“Reverend Mott nominated Felix Ward of New York. Ward was young, plump, and amiable; but objections were raised that youth ought not to be sacrificed while age remained available, and that his city upbringing might have rendered him deficient in wholesome fiber. Mr. Ward’s friends circulated among the members, declaring that he had spent two summers upon a farm. Judge Barlow’s supporters replied that visiting a farm did not make a man rural.

“The first ballot stood:

“Barlow, nine.

“Ward, eight.

“Gaskins, four.

“Scattering, three.

“No choice.

“Mr. Gaskins immediately withdrew, stating that he had never sought the honor and believed the use of his name had been unauthorized. His four supporters divided between Barlow and Ward. On the second ballot each received twelve votes.

“Then arose a question which nearly destroyed us. Could the chairman cast a deciding vote when he was also secretary and had already voted as a member? Professor Doolittle said no. Colonel Treadwell said yes. Mr. Pike said it made no difference, because the chairman was an idiot. The chair requested the gentleman from Oregon to come to order. Mr. Pike replied that he would come to order when Oregon received justice.

“For an hour the debate raged. Hunger had made us weak, but parliamentary passion sustained us. Men who could scarcely stand rose to points of privilege. Members crossed the aisle. Charges of bargaining were made. Mr. Ward’s faction accused Judge Barlow of promising the chairmanship of the Committee on Distribution to Mr. Bragg. Judge Barlow denied it, though Mr. Bragg admitted that the subject had been mentioned informally.

“At last Colonel Treadwell moved that we proceed to a comparative examination of the candidates.

“Mr. Ward objected strenuously, but the motion carried.

“A committee measured both gentlemen about the chest, waist, arm, and calf. Judge Barlow possessed a decided advantage in the first three dimensions; Mr. Ward led slightly in the calf. The committee also inquired into habits, ancestry, and recent diet. It appeared that the judge had eaten buckwheat cakes, pork, and preserved peaches on the morning of departure, while Ward had taken only coffee and toast. That settled it. On the third ballot Judge Barlow was elected, sixteen to eight.

“The judge received the result with dignity. He shook hands with every member and requested that a letter be sent to his wife, assuring her that his final hours had been devoted to the public service. He then asked whether the proceedings could be delayed until morning.

“Mr. Bragg raised a point of order. The adopted report specified immediate action.

“The point was well taken.

“We had Judge Barlow for supper.”

Mr. Hallowell paused and gazed dreamily at the frosted window.

“He was excellent,” he said. “A little firm in the shoulders, perhaps, but of sound character throughout. There was enough for breakfast also, though not enough for luncheon. I have always remembered him with gratitude.”

I shifted farther into my corner.

The old gentleman noticed the movement and smiled reassuringly.

“You must not suppose there was greed,” he said. “Everything was apportioned under strict rules. Mr. Bragg attempted to obtain a second helping on the ground that he represented a larger constituency, but the chair ruled population immaterial.”

“And after that?” I asked, though every instinct advised me not to.

“After that our organization improved greatly.

“On the eighth day we elected Professor Doolittle. His friends filibustered until nearly three o’clock, reading from his pamphlet on municipal drainage; but the previous question was finally ordered. The professor proved disappointing. Intellectual labor, though honorable, does little for the table.

“On the ninth day we chose Peter Gaskins. There was suspicion that contractors might be tough, owing to long association with public works; nevertheless, he turned out surprisingly tender, especially about the ribs.

“The tenth day produced a bitter sectional struggle. New England combined with the Northwest against Mr. Pike, but Oregon again escaped through lack of substance. Reverend Mott was selected as a compromise. He requested permission to offer a prayer before the vote. Permission was granted, though Mr. Bragg insisted that the prayer not be construed as debate. Mr. Mott was mild, but rather spare.

“On the eleventh we had Colonel Treadwell. A magnificent speaker, sir—full-bodied, rich, and a little fiery. His speech against his own nomination lasted two hours and was considered the finest effort of the session. It did not save him.

“On the twelfth, Felix Ward was elected by acclamation. There had always been a strong sentiment for Ward. He was delicate—almost too delicate. One regretted that he had not been larger.

“Then Greeley of Ohio; Harcourt of Delaware; two brothers named Pemmican from Wisconsin; Silas Bell of Tennessee; and Dr. Quince of New Jersey. Dr. Quince had been taking patent bitters, which gave him a medicinal quality and caused a formal investigation. The committee reported that the bitterness was not dangerous, and the report was adopted.

“Mr. Bragg survived until the seventeenth day through clever alliances and a persistent claim of constitutional immunity. At last we overruled him. He was coarse. I say it without resentment, but he was coarse.”

“Good heavens!” I exclaimed. “How many were left?”

“Four, including myself. The conductor and fireman had gone out during a break in the weather, and we had not seen them again. Mr. Pike still remained, chiefly because no majority could be organized in his favor. There was also Nathan Cobb of Kentucky and an elderly gentleman named Weems, whose state I forget.”

Mr. Hallowell sighed.

“Cobb was elected next. Weems followed. Then Pike and I were alone.

“Pike proposed that we abandon formal government and settle the matter personally. I told him I would sooner perish than lend myself to revolutionary methods. We therefore constituted ourselves a committee of the whole. I nominated Pike. Pike nominated me. Each seconded his own nomination, and we proceeded to ballot.

“The vote was tied.

“We balloted thirty-six times without result. On the thirty-seventh, Pike accused the chair—that was myself—of suppressing a ballot. I appointed a committee to investigate, but as Pike refused to serve, the committee could not obtain a quorum.

“The situation became desperate. Pike moved to suspend the rules and decide by weight. I opposed the motion because his boots contained iron plates. He then offered to remove them. I called that an attempt to influence legislation.

“At last, while he was delivering a violent speech upon executive tyranny, he fainted. I ruled his remarks concluded and took up the unfinished business. The ballot elected him by one vote to none.

“Pike was the poorest of all. Absolutely stringy. Oregon may possess many virtues, but I cannot recommend her representative on the evidence before me.”

He fell silent.

The wheels clattered under us; the lamp swayed; outside, the storm scrawled white lines upon the dark. I could hear my own breathing.

“And then you were rescued?” I asked.

“On the following morning. A relief party dug through the drift. They found me alone, presiding over the remains of the session. I was weak, certainly, but my faculties were clear. The official minutes were unfortunately lost.”

“Twenty-three men,” I murmured.

“Twenty-three,” he said. “A remarkable body. Taken together, I have never known a more satisfying delegation.”

He turned his mild eyes upon me. They traveled, as I fancied, from my face to my shoulders and rested briefly upon my waistcoat.

“You remind me of Barlow,” he said warmly. “Not in feature, perhaps, but in general promise. I liked Barlow from the first. Indeed, sir, I think I like you almost as much.”

I attempted a laugh. It emerged in a form not worth preserving.

At that moment the train slowed for a small station. Mr. Hallowell looked out, gathered his valise, and rose.

“My stop,” he said. “I have enjoyed our conversation exceedingly. If ever we are detained together by weather, I hope you will feel free to rely upon my experience.”

He offered his hand. I took it because civilization is a powerful habit.

Then he descended to the platform and disappeared into the snow.

I remained in the compartment, staring after him, until the conductor entered to examine the stove. He was a broad, red-faced man, plainly made for facts.

“Conductor,” I said, “do you know the gentleman who just left?”

“Old Hallowell? Certainly. Rides this line every month.”

“He was formerly in Congress?”

“Oh, yes. Long time ago.”

“And was he once imprisoned in a snowbound train?”

The conductor nodded.

“Worst storm these parts ever had. Train lay buried nearly three weeks. Folks thought the whole crowd was dead before they got to them.”

My blood cooled.

“Then his account is true?”

The conductor looked at me and began to grin.

“Has he been telling you they ate one another?”

“He described the proceedings in considerable detail.”

“That’s Eph Hallowell,” said the conductor. “The storm touched him in the head. They were hungry, right enough—starved near to death—but nobody ate anybody. Relief came in time. All twenty-four walked away, though some were carried. Ever since then, whenever snow starts falling, Eph holds an imaginary session and votes the whole passenger list into breakfast, dinner, and supper.”

I sank back against the cushions.

“So Judge Barlow—”

“Alive. Runs a bank in Cedar Rapids.”

“And Pike of Oregon?”

“Never was any Pike of Oregon. Might be a fellow he met in Washington. Eph adds members whenever the story needs them.”

“He told me he had eaten twenty-three men.”

“He generally does. Sometimes more. Once, between here and Omaha, he got through forty-one, including the brakeman and a German orchestra. Harmless as a baby, though. Wouldn’t hurt a fly.”

The conductor opened the stove door, dropped in a lump of coal, and chuckled.

“There’s one curious part. If you let him tell the full version, he winds up alone after finishing everybody else. Then he nominates himself, elects himself unanimously, and announces that, owing to the absence of a quorum, he is not authorized to carry out the decision. After that he resigns from the menu and adjourns without a day.”

The conductor went out, still laughing.

I pulled my coat about me and looked at the place where the honorable gentleman had sat. Relief came over me in a generous flood; yet I confess that, when supper was announced five minutes later, I did not go into the dining car.

The phrases “general promise” and “almost as much as Barlow” had taken away my appetite.

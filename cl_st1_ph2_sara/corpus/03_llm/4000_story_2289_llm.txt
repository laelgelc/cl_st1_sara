For eleven days Captain Alexis Markof’s company had moved northward through the district, burning storehouses, searching villages, and restoring order according to the instructions issued at provincial headquarters. The instructions were simple. Arms were to be surrendered; suspected insurgents were to be arrested; persons found beyond their communes without passports were to be treated as accomplices. In practice there was little time for arrest. The roads were bad, the prisons distant, and the temper of the troops uncertain. Men taken with rifles were shot beside the nearest ditch. Men who ran were shot while running. Of those who possessed no papers, some were flogged and released; others, unable to explain themselves clearly, were executed.

Markof performed his duty without visible agitation. He was a man of thirty-seven, broad across the shoulders, prematurely grey at the temples, with small, colourless eyes and a firm, obstinate chin. When peasants fell upon their knees before him, clasping their hands and calling him “your honour,” he looked above their heads and listened to the interpreter. If the explanation seemed confused, he made a brief movement with two fingers. The prisoners were led away.

He did not attend the shootings unless circumstances required it. Yet the reports were always brought to him.

“Three this morning, your honour.”

“Very good.”

“Two at the mill.”

“Enter it in the journal.”

Thus bloodshed acquired columns, numbers and signatures. It became part of the forage account, the ammunition expenditure, the list of sick horses.

On the thirty-first of December the company reached the estate of Korovino. The manor house had been deserted since autumn. Its windows were broken, its roof blackened by fire, and the family portraits had been cut from their frames. Snow lay in the ballroom. The soldiers occupied the kitchens, stables and servants’ quarters, while Markof was given a room in what remained of the eastern wing.

That afternoon three prisoners were brought before him.

The first was a young labourer with a bruised cheek and one boot. He had been caught near a damaged telegraph line. The second, a school clerk, carried copies of an unlawful proclamation sewn inside his coat. The third was an old man taken upon the forest road without passport, money or baggage.

Markof questioned them from behind a table. He had ridden since dawn through sleet, and the familiar pain had begun behind his eyes. His hands were cold, though the room was warm from a newly lighted fire.

The labourer denied touching the wires.

“They found you there.”

“I was looking for my brother, your honour.”

“At midnight?”

“He is missing.”

“Take him away.”

The clerk admitted nothing and explained nothing. His lips trembled so violently that his teeth could be heard striking together.

Then the old man stepped forward.

He was tall, though age had bent him, and he wore a long, earth-coloured coat tied at the waist with cord. His beard was thin and white. There was neither fear nor insolence in his face. What disturbed Markof was the man’s attention. He did not stare like a fanatic, nor lower his eyes like a guilty peasant; he merely looked, calmly and almost sorrowfully, as though Markof himself had been brought there for examination.

“Your name?”

“I have borne many names.”

The sergeant struck him between the shoulders.

“Answer his honour!”

“My name would tell him nothing.”

“Where is your passport?”

“I have none.”

“Where do you live?”

“On the earth.”

A soldier laughed. Markof raised his eyes sharply, and silence returned.

“Your occupation?”

“I wander.”

“Why were you upon the forest road?”

“Because it led here.”

The pain behind Markof’s eyes tightened like a cord. He leaned back, studying the old man with weary dislike.

“You understand that the district is under military authority?”

“All districts are under some authority.”

“And that persons travelling without documents are liable to punishment?”

“I understand that men write laws, and afterward hide behind what they have written.”

Markof’s face reddened.

“Enough. At dawn all three are to be shot.”

The labourer cried out. The clerk collapsed against the guard. Only the old man remained motionless.

“Do you hear?” Markof demanded.

“I hear.”

“Remove them.”

As the guards turned him, the old man said quietly:

“You will also hear.”

By evening the sleet had thickened. It hissed against the broken panes and froze upon the boards. From the courtyard came the voices of soldiers celebrating the approach of the New Year. Someone had found a case of wine beneath the kitchen floor. There were songs, quarrels, laughter, the shrill notes of a concertina. At ten o’clock Markof sent an orderly to command silence; at half past ten the noise began again.

Ordinarily he would have gone out himself and restored discipline. That night he could not rise.

The malaria, which he had first contracted three years earlier in the southern marshes, had returned during the expedition. He knew its stages: the cold creeping inward from the fingers, the violent shivering, the burning heat, the sweat and exhaustion. He had concealed the attacks because Major Krassin was waiting for an excuse to supersede him. Now, lying fully dressed upon a narrow iron bed, Markof felt the chill entering his bones.

His room was long, hollow and almost empty. There was the bed, the table, two chairs, and a cracked mirror hanging crookedly above the fireplace. The servants had piled broken furniture upon the hearth. Gilded fragments of a cradle, a child’s stool and the carved leg of a piano burned together with a mournful crackling. Each gust forced smoke into the room.

Markof drew his cloak around him. His watch ticked beside the candle.

Eleven twenty.

The execution had been fixed for six. There remained more than six hours.

He wondered why this calculation had entered his mind. Executions were always carried out at dawn. It was proper, orderly, established by custom. Darkness concealed irregularities; full daylight encouraged crowds. At dawn a man could be removed from the world without disturbing the business of the day.

The old man’s face returned to him.

“You will also hear.”

It was impudence, nothing more. Peasants often assumed a prophetic manner when death approached. Terror deranged them. Some prayed; some cursed; some spoke as if already standing before the throne of God. Markof had heard all varieties.

He closed his eyes.

At once he saw a ditch filled with water. Three bodies lay across one another, their coats soaking darkly. One hand projected from the ditch, the fingers opening and closing.

He opened his eyes. The fire had sunk lower.

In the courtyard the soldiers began a song, but the melody soon broke apart. A horse kicked against a stable door. Then all sounds seemed to withdraw, leaving only the ticking watch and the dry, delicate crackle of burnt wood.

Markof could not determine whether he slept. The candle appeared both near and immeasurably distant. The walls advanced, receded and bent above him like canvas in a storm. Sweat ran from his forehead, yet his teeth chattered.

There was someone beside the bed.

The old man sat upon the wooden chair, his hands resting on his knees.

Markof tried to reach the pistol beneath his pillow, but his arm had become intolerably heavy.

“How did you come here?”

The visitor did not answer.

“Guard!” Markof called.

His voice was hollow and weak. No step sounded outside.

“You were locked in the cellar.”

“Yes.”

“Then this is fever.”

“Perhaps.”

“You are not here.”

“Yet you see me.”

Markof managed to raise himself upon one elbow. The old man’s coat was wet, and drops shone in his white beard, but no water fell upon the floor.

“What do you want?”

“To ask why I must die.”

“You know why. You have no passport. You refused to account for yourself.”

“Is that why?”

“You were found in a forbidden district during rebellion.”

“Have I rebelled?”

“That is for the authorities to decide.”

“You are the authority tonight.”

“I carry out orders.”

The old man inclined his head.

“Who gave the first order?”

Markof pressed both palms against his temples.

“I will not be cross-examined by a prisoner. The province is in revolt. Telegraph lines are cut, trains derailed, officers murdered. Severity is necessary. If we hesitate, thousands may die.”

“Therefore you kill three men.”

“To save thousands.”

“And if these three are innocent?”

Markof’s mouth hardened.

“In such operations mistakes are inevitable.”

“To whom?”

“What?”

“To whom are they inevitable? To the dead man? To his widow? To the child who waits at a window? Or only to the officer who signs the paper?”

“You speak like a pamphleteer. War cannot be conducted by sentimental rules.”

“Is this war? Where is my weapon? Where is my army?”

“You may be a spy.”

“I may be.”

“You may have carried messages.”

“I may have.”

“You see!”

“I may also be innocent.”

Markof threw off his cloak and sat upright. The room swayed. Red circles revolved before his eyes.

“What would you have me do? Release every rogue who tells a plausible tale? Betray my men? Betray the state? Discipline exists because private judgment is weak. I obey my superiors, as my soldiers obey me. Without obedience there is chaos.”

“And with obedience?”

“There is order.”

The old man turned his face toward the window. Beyond it the sleet whispered against the darkness.

“I have seen your order,” he said. “It lay beneath the walls of cities when conquerors entered. It hung from crosses beside imperial roads. It burned in villages whose inhabitants spoke the wrong tongue or bowed before God in the wrong manner. Always there were officers; always there were seals, proclamations and commands. One man said, ‘I obey the king.’ Another said, ‘I defend the republic.’ A third said, ‘The priest has blessed my sword.’ And each believed the blood upon his hand belonged to another.”

“You compare a lawful punitive expedition with barbarism.”

“I compare blood with blood.”

“That is a child’s argument.”

“It is older than your empires.”

Markof laughed, but the laugh ended in a cough.

“Suppose I spare you. What of the others? I have executed men for less. Would it be just to make an exception because an old man talks cleverly?”

The visitor’s eyes darkened with an immense weariness.

“You have killed so many that mercy has become unfair.”

Markof looked away.

He remembered a boy shot near Velich. The boy had insisted he was fourteen; the village elder had said seventeen. Regulations allowed the execution of persons of sixteen. Markof had ordered the elder flogged until he corrected his testimony. At the time it had appeared an administrative necessity. Now he saw the boy’s cap lying in the mud.

“These are tricks,” he whispered. “Fever—nothing but fever.”

The old man rose.

He seemed taller than before. His bent shoulders straightened, and his shadow climbed the wall until it touched the ceiling.

“You call upon duty because you fear to say: I willed it. You call upon country because you fear to say: I profited by it. You call upon history because the multitude of murderers comforts you. But neither the emperor, nor the minister, nor the dead generations will stand within your breast when the command is given. There you are alone.”

“Leave me.”

“You asked what would happen if soldiers judged commands. Ask instead what happens when they do not.”

“Leave me!”

The old man continued, and his voice no longer seemed to come from one point in the room. It sounded beneath the floor, behind the walls, high among the ruined rafters.

“I have walked after armies since armies were made. I saw bronze spears reddened beside the first walled towns. I saw captives driven in chains through Nineveh, and children dashed upon stones beneath banners embroidered with lions. I stood in Babylon when the river carried corpses between its quays. I followed the chariots of Egypt, the phalanxes of Macedon, the eagles of Rome. Their captains also spoke of peace after victory, of necessity, honour and obedience.

“I saw Jerusalem encircled by fire. I saw Carthage vanish in smoke. I saw slaves die for amusement beneath the eyes of women and magistrates. I crossed Asia with hordes whose horses sank fetlock-deep in blood. I heard churches ring their bells while men were burned for prayer, and priests sing hymns above the cries of the tortured. In the western lands I saw nations destroyed for gold; in the eastern lands, towers built from severed heads.

“Kings murdered for dominion, republics for liberty, churches for truth, mobs for bread. The names altered. The cry did not. Strike! Burn! Purify! Avenge! Obey!

“I stood upon frozen rivers where armies drowned beneath broken ice. I passed fields so loaded with unburied men that wolves lost their fear of human voices. I watched the ships of powerful nations carry iron, flame and pestilence over three-fourths of the globe. Everywhere the executioner washed his hands and said: Another commanded me.”

“Stop,” Markof said.

But the catalogue poured on—cities violated, peoples uprooted, prisoners blinded, sailors hanged, women burned, children buried beneath walls. The room appeared to widen around those innumerable dead. They stood behind the old man in silent ranks: naked, armoured, crowned, enslaved, ancient and newly fallen. Blood moved at their feet, at first a narrow thread between the boards, then a stream, then a dark and shining flood sufficient to drown the whole world.

Markof drew his legs onto the bed.

“Who are you?”

The visitor became silent.

The fire collapsed with a soft sigh, sending up a swarm of sparks. In that last red light the old man’s face seemed not aged but consumed by age, scored by centuries, burdened with a terror beyond grief.

“Who are you?” Markof repeated.

“I am the beginning of your argument.”

“I do not understand.”

“I also said that necessity stood beside me. I also envied, feared and accused. I also lifted my hand, and when the earth drank what I had shed, I asked whether I was my brother’s keeper.”

Markof’s breath stopped.

“No.”

“I buried him poorly. All murderers bury poorly. A heel remained uncovered, and upon that heel a fly settled in the sun. I remember the fly more clearly than his cry.”

“No,” Markof said again.

“The voice asked me where my brother was. I answered with insolence, as men answer when they hope boldness will conceal knowledge. But the blood spoke from the ground.”

“Cain.”

The name scarcely passed Markof’s lips.

The old man bowed his head.

“I was driven forth, marked and forbidden death. I thought the punishment was wandering. It was not. My punishment was to witness. Wherever brother raised hand against brother, I was there. I have heard the first excuse repeated in a thousand tongues and adorned with philosophy, law, patriotism and faith. I have watched mankind perfect the stone I lifted. You have made it rifle, cannon, gallows and decree; but beneath your inventions my hand remains.”

Markof covered his face.

“What do you want of me?”

“Awake.”

“I am awake.”

“Awake.”

“I cannot undo what is done.”

“Awake.”

“I will release you. Do you hear? At dawn—I shall order—”

“Awake!”

The voice shook the bed, the walls, the black windows. Markof reached toward the figure.

“Wait! I shall stop it. Sergeant! Guard! Leave me—no, stay! Tell me what must—”

“Awake!”

He opened his eyes.

Grey morning filled the room. The candle had gone out, and the fire was cold. Someone was pounding on the door.

“Your honour! Your honour, awake!”

Markof struggled to sit up. His uniform was soaked with sweat. His hands trembled so violently that he could not fasten his collar.

The sergeant-major entered, saluted and glanced with professional concern at his captain’s face.

“What time is it?”

“A quarter past six, your honour.”

“The prisoners.”

The sergeant-major waited.

“The three prisoners ordered for execution. Bring them here at once.”

The man’s expression did not alter.

“The sentence has been carried out, your honour.”

For several seconds Markof appeared not to understand.

“Carried out?”

“At six precisely, according to your order.”

“All three?”

“All three.”

“The old man also?”

“Yes, your honour.”

“Did he say anything?”

The sergeant-major considered.

“He would not permit the soldiers to bind his eyes. Otherwise, nothing.”

Markof rose from the bed. The floor tilted. He took one step and fell against the table, scattering his watch, papers and pistol. The sergeant-major caught him beneath the arms.

“Your honour is ill.”

“No. Yes—the fever.”

“You should lie down.”

“Who commanded the party?”

“Lieutenant Saveliev.”

“Send him to me.”

“The lieutenant has gone to inspect the southern pickets.”

Markof stared through the broken window. Across the courtyard, beyond the kitchen roofs, a pale strip of sky lay over the fields. Soldiers moved between the buildings carrying buckets and bundles of straw. Their ordinary voices sounded in the clean, cold air. Somewhere a man laughed. Somewhere an axe struck wood.

Upon the white slope near the orchard three dark heaps were visible.

Markof turned away.

“Sergeant-major.”

“Your honour?”

“You will assume temporary command of the company.”

The sergeant-major’s eyebrows lifted, though only slightly.

“On account of your honour’s illness?”

“On account of fever. Write that.”

“Yes, your honour.”

“We remain here until further instructions. No searches. No arrests. No punishments without written authority from headquarters.”

“Yes, your honour.”

“And prepare a courier.”

“For what destination?”

“Provincial headquarters.”

Markof lowered himself onto the chair where the visitor had sat. The boards beneath it were dry. There was no trace of wet footprints, no displaced object, nothing by which a reasonable man might distinguish visitation from delirium.

He drew a sheet of paper toward him. For a long while the pen remained suspended above it.

“Shall I send for the surgeon?” asked the sergeant-major.

“No.”

Markof wrote the date. His handwriting, once narrow and decisive, wandered across the page.

“What shall I tell the officers?” the sergeant-major asked.

“Tell them I relinquish command.”

“Until the fever passes?”

Markof looked again toward the orchard. The three heaps had become indistinct in the strengthening dawn, almost no more than shadows upon the snow.

“I shall submit my resignation.”

The sergeant-major saluted. Whether he felt surprise, contempt or relief could not be seen. He turned and went out, closing the door carefully behind him.

Markof remained at the table. From the courtyard came the orderly sounds of the company preparing for another day: harness buckles, shouted names, the stamp of horses, the clatter of rifles stacked beneath the eaves. The machinery waited only for a command.

He began his letter.

At the first sentence the pen stopped. A dark drop of ink gathered at its point, swelled, and fell upon the paper. Markof watched it spread slowly through the fibres.

Then, from very far away—or from the hollow room, or from beneath the frozen earth—he seemed to hear a voice asking where his brother was.

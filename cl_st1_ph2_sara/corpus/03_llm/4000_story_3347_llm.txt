In the autumn of the year 1779, when the contest between the American colonies and the mother country had turned many a peaceful valley into a place of alarms, there stood, not far from the eastern base of the Hudson Highlands, a substantial stone farmhouse belonging to John Reynolds, a member of the Society of Friends and a stanch adherent to the patriot cause.

The house had been built in times when strength was scarcely less considered than comfort. Its walls, composed of rough gray stone, were near two feet in thickness; the oaken doors were crossed within by heavy bars; the lower windows were protected by shutters stout enough to turn a pistol-ball; and narrow loopholes, cut beneath the eaves, commanded the road, the barn-yard, and the dark border of woods beyond the orchard. In ordinary seasons these precautions might have seemed excessive, for the valley was fair and fertile, the road little travelled, and the nearest mountains rose in blue tranquillity above fields of maize and ripening buckwheat. But in the lawless days of the Revolution, when regular armies were less dreaded in lonely districts than the predatory bands which wore one uniform to-day and another to-morrow, such a dwelling was not merely a home. It was an ark and a fortress.

The chief apartment was the kitchen, spacious, low-ceiled, and warm with the homely labors of a numerous household. Its great fireplace yawned like a sooty cavern in the western wall. Within it swung an iron crane, blackened by many years of service; beneath the crane hung hooks for pots and kettles, while from the stonework above descended a strong iron trammel, used in suspending the heavier vessels over the blaze. The children had often seen it bear the great copper kettle at apple-butter time, and had thought no more of it than of the bread-trough or the spinning-wheel. Yet that harmless implement was destined, before another winter had passed, to acquire a terrible significance in their memories.

John Reynolds was a sturdy man, somewhat past middle life, grave in speech, deliberate in movement, and possessed of that courage which is the fruit of conscience rather than temper. Though a Whig, and active in furnishing grain and information to the American authorities, he would not bear arms.

“I may aid what I believe to be a righteous cause,” he had once said, “but I cannot take the life of my fellow-man. The Inner Light forbids it.”

His neighbors, many of whom slept with loaded rifles beside their beds, did not always understand this distinction; yet none doubted his sincerity, nor could any deny that he had shown a boldness equal to theirs. His wife, Martha, was a pale, industrious woman, whose frail body concealed uncommon presence of mind. Of their children, the eldest daughter, Phebe, had but lately completed her twelfth year.

She was a wholesome, fair-haired child, blue-eyed and clear of countenance, sturdy from work in dairy and garden, but still light-footed enough to race her brothers down the orchard paths. In the company of strangers she was demure, as befitted a Quaker maiden; at home she could laugh merrily, sing over her spinning, and coax an extra cake from the griddle for the smallest child. Yet the dangers of the time had ripened her beyond her years. She knew the meaning of hoofprints on a wet road, of a distant flash among the trees, and of the sudden silence which falls upon birds when armed men are near.

The particular peril that hung over the Reynolds household arose from the evil doings of Claudius Smith, a notorious outlaw whose band had infested the Highlands, plundering Whig farms, stealing cattle, waylaying travellers, and carrying information to the King’s forces. Smith had at length been captured, tried, and hanged; and John Reynolds, who had helped the officers of justice with knowledge of the outlaw’s movements, was marked for vengeance by the dead robber’s surviving associates.

These desperate men had withdrawn into mountain fastnesses, where rocky ravines, tangled laurel, and hidden caves afforded them shelter. Thence they descended by night upon the settlements. Sometimes they called themselves loyal militia; sometimes they professed to be Continental scouts; but their true allegiance was to spoil and cruelty. Rumor gave them a dozen leaders and a hundred lurking places. No man knew where they would strike next.

Thus Phebe was often stationed in the garret, or upon the flat roof of the woodshed, to keep watch while the men worked in distant fields. It was a strange office for a child, yet she performed it faithfully. Her keen eyes ranged the road, the pasture, and the forest edge; and if a rider appeared where none was expected, she gave warning at once.

One gray afternoon, when low clouds pressed upon the mountains and a raw wind shook the last leaves from the orchard, Phebe saw five mounted men emerge from the woods. They did not follow the road, but came across a stubble-field, keeping close to a stone wall. Their guns lay ready across their saddles.

For one breath she stood motionless. Then she sprang from her perch.

“Mother!” she cried. “Armed men are crossing the lower field!”

Martha Reynolds was carding wool beside the hearth. She did not scream or waste time in questioning. Calling the younger children from the yard, she thrust the heavy bar across the rear door, while Phebe secured the shutters. John Reynolds and his two hired men, who had been shelling corn in the barn, entered by the side passage scarcely a minute before the horsemen surrounded the house.

A gunstock thundered against the front door.

“Open in the King’s name!”

John looked through a loophole and recognized, beneath a slouched hat, one of Smith’s former companions.

“I know no authority in thee,” he answered. “Depart in peace.”

An oath followed, and another blow shook the door.

The marauders tried the shutters, fired through one of them, and attempted to force the side entrance with a fence rail. Finding the lower part of the house secure, they drew aside for consultation. From within, the family could hear their muttered threats. The children huddled near their mother; the hired men held axes, though John had forbidden them to fire unless life could be preserved in no other manner.

Presently Phebe, who had crept to the foot of the attic stairs, heard a faint scraping above.

“Mother,” she whispered, “there is something in the chimney.”

Martha glanced toward the great fireplace. The autumn fire had burned low. A little soot fell upon the hearthstone; then came the unmistakable rasp of a boot against masonry. One of the ruffians, having gained the roof by means of the lean-to, was descending the broad chimney.

Mrs. Reynolds snatched a pillow from a settle, ripped it open with her scissors, and cast a double handful of feathers upon the embers. For an instant they shrivelled silently. Then a thick, greasy, intolerable smoke rolled upward.

A choking cry sounded in the chimney.

“More, Phebe!”

The child flung feathers upon the fire until the room itself grew dim. Above came coughing, furious curses, and frantic scraping. The man, unable to descend and scarcely able to breathe, struggled back toward the roof. His companions dragged him out by the arms, half suffocated and black with soot.

A shot was fired down the chimney, scattering ashes across the kitchen, but harming no one. Then, after further threats and a vain effort to kindle the outer door, the gang mounted and rode away, carrying their choking comrade between them.

For many weeks thereafter the watch was maintained with redoubled care. Yet vigilance, when continued through long periods without fresh alarm, becomes a burden which even the prudent are tempted to lay aside. Winter settled upon the valley. Snow filled the mountain passes; the river froze near the banks; and word came that several of the marauders had fled southward. John Reynolds began to believe that the danger had passed.

It had not passed. Evil can wait, where honest men must sow, harvest, feed cattle, tend the sick, and sleep.

One bitter night toward the end of February, Phebe, who had gone to the woodshed for kindling, heard the muffled tramp of horses upon the frozen road. The moon was hidden, and the darkness beyond the yard seemed solid; but she discerned several moving shapes and caught the faint clink of accoutrements.

She ran into the kitchen.

“Father, riders are coming!”

John rose from the table. Before he could reach the shutter, a voice called from without:

“Continental patrol! Open! We search for deserters.”

The words were plausible. Desertion had become frequent, and detachments of American troops sometimes visited remote houses without notice. Through the loophole John saw men in military coats, their hats marked after the Continental fashion. One wore a sword and held up a folded paper.

Martha touched her husband’s sleeve.

“Do not open, John.”

“I must not refuse lawful officers,” he replied. Then, raising his voice, he asked, “Who commands thee?”

“Captain Boyd, of the New York line. We have authority from headquarters.”

Phebe’s heart beat violently. Something in the answer, perhaps a rough laugh instantly suppressed, awakened the memory of the first attack.

“Father,” she implored, “I fear they lie.”

But John, unwilling to shelter a deserter even by seeming obstruction, lifted the bar.

The door had opened no more than a foot when it was driven inward. Six men rushed across the threshold. A seventh remained outside with the horses. Their supposed uniforms were fragments gathered for disguise; beneath one military coat Phebe recognized a hunting-shirt worn by a marauder in the autumn.

John was struck down before he could retreat. The hired men slept that night in an outbuilding and, cut off by the robbers stationed in the yard, dared not approach. Within the house the invaders bound Mrs. Reynolds and the older children, drove the little ones into a corner, and barred every exit.

“So, Friend Reynolds,” said the leader, tearing off his military cockade, “the fox has opened his own den.”

“I have done thee no wrong,” said John.

“No wrong? Claudius Smith swung because honest John Reynolds wagged his tongue.”

“Smith suffered by the law for crimes committed. I gave true evidence. I repent it not.”

The ruffian’s face darkened.

“Then we shall teach you the price of truth.”

They demanded money, plate, horses, and the names of Whigs who had aided in Smith’s capture. Reynolds told them where his small store of coin was kept, for he valued no property above the safety of his family; but he refused to betray his neighbors. They beat him with the flats of their swords, thrust him against the wall, and threatened his wife and children. Still he would give no name.

Phebe, bound at the wrists, watched without crying. Terror had made her face white, but had not destroyed her judgment. She worked the cord against the sharp edge of a broken pewter plate lying near the hearth. Fiber by fiber it parted.

Meanwhile the marauders ransacked the house. They emptied drawers, ripped open bedding, broke the clock, overturned bins, and scattered meal and feathers over the floor. One found a bridle hidden in a chest and shouted that Reynolds had lied concerning the horses. Another discovered papers relating to supplies furnished to the Continental army.

“Hang him!” cried the leader. “Let the Quaker dance without lifting a hand against us.”

They dragged John to the fireplace. The fire had sunk to glowing coals, over which the great iron trammel hung in its accustomed place. A rope was passed about the prisoner’s neck and flung across the hook. Three men drew upon it until his feet left the floor.

Martha uttered one piercing cry. The little children shrieked. Phebe’s cord gave way.

She seized the carving-knife from the disordered table, sprang upon the hearth, and sawed at the rope. A robber caught at her dress; but she twisted free, and the knife, sharpened that morning for cutting meat, severed the strands.

Her father fell heavily upon the stones.

For a moment all stood amazed that a child should have dared so much. Then one of the men struck her across the shoulders with a rawhide whip.

“Bind the little vixen!”

The lash descended again and again. Her dress was cut; blood sprang beneath it; yet she neither begged nor betrayed the neighbors whose names they sought. They tied her more cruelly than before and forced her to kneel where she could see her father.

John breathed. The fall had restored him slightly, and he tried to raise his head.

The leader kicked him back.

“You are hard to kill, Friend.”

Once more they fastened the rope about his neck. This time they bound Phebe’s ankles as well as her wrists, and one man held her by the hair while the others hauled her father upward. His body swung against the black mouth of the chimney, turning slowly above the dying fire.

How the child freed herself she could never afterward clearly explain. One knot may have been loosened in haste; the edge of the hearthstone may have cut the cord; perhaps her slender hands slipped through bonds drawn for larger wrists. Providence does not commonly dispense with means, though in our blindness we often call the means miraculous.

She knew only that she was free.

With one convulsive movement she threw herself upon the man who held her, escaped his grasp, caught up the fallen knife, and climbed upon the iron fire-dog. Smoke blinded her. The heat scorched her face. Standing on tiptoe, she reached the tautened rope and cut.

Again John Reynolds crashed to the hearth.

A blow from a gunstock felled Phebe beside him.

“Leave them!” exclaimed one of the gang, alarmed by her blood-covered face. “You have killed the child as well.”

The leader stooped over Reynolds, felt no breath, and appeared satisfied.

“Then the debt is paid.”

But cruelty, once admitted to the heart, seldom rests content with its first work. The marauders piled chairs, bedding, and splintered wood about the kitchen. They scattered embers among these materials and carried a burning brand to the stair. After locking the family within, they went to the barn, saddled the best horses, and rode away, leaving behind them smoke, flame, and what they believed to be a houseful of dead and dying persons.

For several minutes Phebe lay insensible. She awoke to heat and the crackle of burning cloth. The kitchen was filling with smoke. Her father lay motionless near the hearth; her mother, still bound, had fainted; the younger children were crying behind an overturned table.

Phebe tried to stand and fell. Pain from the whipping ran like fire across her back, and blood from the wound upon her head dimmed one eye. Yet the actual flames, creeping along the scattered straw and climbing a window curtain, allowed no time for weakness.

“The window,” she said.

No one heard.

She crawled to a side casement, dragged herself upright, and forced at the shutter. It was fastened by a wooden pin. Her fingers, swollen by the cords, could scarcely grasp it. Once, twice, she pulled in vain. Then the pin yielded, the shutter opened outward, and cold night air rushed into the room.

The opening was narrow, but Phebe squeezed through, dropped into the snow, and ran barefoot toward the barn-yard trough. The robbers had broken the pump handle, either wantonly or to hinder pursuit; but the cattle trough, though filmed with ice, contained water beneath.

A wooden pail stood nearby. She broke the ice with its rim, filled it, and staggered toward the house.

The burden was nearly half her weight. Water splashed upon her feet and froze to her torn skirt. She reached the window, lifted the pail to the sill, climbed in after it, and dashed the contents upon the blazing bedding.

The fire hissed, sank, and sprang up elsewhere.

Back she went.

The second pail extinguished the curtain. The third drenched the straw near the stairs. On the fourth journey she stumbled and spilled half the water in the snow. On the fifth, her hands could no longer lift the vessel to the sill; she filled it only partway and pushed it before her.

There was no martial music to sustain her, no watching multitude to applaud, no thought in her young mind that the deed was heroic. The house was burning, and those she loved were within it. Therefore she carried water.

At last the flames were conquered. Smoke continued to roll through the rooms, but the red tongues disappeared, and the wet wreckage smouldered harmlessly upon the stones.

Phebe cut her mother’s bonds, then freed the children. Martha revived, coughing and bewildered.

“Thy father,” she whispered.

They turned him over. His face was dark and swollen; the rope had cut deeply into his neck; blood flowed from a wound in his scalp; and no breath could at first be perceived.

“He is dead,” sobbed one of the little boys.

Mrs. Reynolds placed a trembling hand over her husband’s heart.

“No. There is life. Phebe, attend to me. We must stop the bleeding.”

Under her mother’s direction, the child fetched linen from an unburned cupboard, tore it into strips, washed the wounds, and applied firm bandages. They loosened his collar and raised his head. Martha bade Phebe rub his hands and feet, while she herself dropped a little warm water between his lips.

For a long time there was no change.

Then John shuddered. A faint breath escaped him.

Phebe bent close.

“Father?”

His eyelids did not open, but he breathed again.

Martha Reynolds, whose strength had been sustained only by necessity, now shook with fever. She had been ill for several days before the assault, and the cold, terror, and smoke had brought her nearly to collapse. None of the smaller children could cross the dark country for help. The hired men, fearing that the marauders still watched the house, had fled toward the hills and did not return.

“Phebe,” said her mother, “thee must go to Neighbor June. Tell him thy father lives. Bring the surgeon if one can be found.”

The nearest friendly dwelling was more than three miles distant. The road passed a strip of forest in which the outlaws might still be lurking.

Phebe put on a pair of her mother’s shoes, since her own could not be found, wrapped a cloak over her torn dress, and set out.

The moon had emerged, silvering the snow and throwing long bars of shadow across the road. Every tree seemed an armed man; every creak of frozen branches resembled a stealthy footstep. Once she heard hoofbeats and hid behind a fallen log until two riderless horses, frightened from some pasture, galloped by. Once a fox barked in the thicket, and she nearly cried aloud.

Still she ran.

At Hal June’s farmhouse she had strength only to beat upon the door and say, “They have hanged my father—but he lives.”

Then she fell across the threshold.

Within an hour the quiet valley was awake. News spread from house to house like fire in prairie grass. Lanterns moved along the roads; rifles came down from chimney hooks; horses were saddled; women hurried to the Reynolds place with blankets, food, and restoratives. A surgeon attached to a neighboring militia post was roused from sleep and carried thither at a gallop.

He examined John and shook his head gravely.

“The neck is not broken. That is all I can promise. He has lost much blood, and there are injuries enough to kill three men.”

“But he may live?” asked Phebe.

“If he lives until noon, child, we may hope.”

Hal June, a tall woodsman whose brother had been robbed by Smith’s band, stood amid the ruined kitchen and looked upon the wet ashes, the broken furniture, the bloodstained trammel, and the raw marks upon Phebe’s shoulders. His stern face grew sterner.

“This is no war,” he said. “It is murder. Those who did it shall be hunted from every cave in the Highlands.”

Before sunrise an armed party followed the hoofprints southward. The trail led first along the frozen road, then across a creek and into the mountains, where the robbers had separated. Two were overtaken in a rocky gorge. Ordered to surrender, they fired upon the pursuers; one was killed by Hal June’s first shot, and the other fell wounded and was taken. A third, attempting to cross the Hudson with stolen goods, broke through the river ice and perished before aid could reach him. Others were captured during the weeks that followed, tried for robbery and murder, and brought to those fitting ends which human law, though sometimes tardy, reserves for the ruthless.

The leader escaped for a season into the western forests. Yet he found no peace there. Betrayed by a companion over the division of plunder, he was seized in a frontier tavern, recognized by the scar upon his cheek, and returned in irons to the county where his crimes had been committed. Thus the mountains which had sheltered his wickedness did not save him from justice.

John Reynolds lingered for many days between life and death. His face remained swollen beyond recognition; one ear had been nearly torn away by a blow; and the rope left a livid mark about his throat which he carried to the grave. For weeks he could neither speak nor swallow without anguish. The surgeon did what skill permitted, but afterward declared that nursing had saved the patient where medicine could do little.

That nursing was chiefly Phebe’s.

Her own wounds were painful, and for a time she could not bear clothing upon her back; nevertheless, as soon as she was able to stand, she took her place beside her father’s bed. She cooled his drink, changed his dressings, fed him broth a spoonful at a time, and watched through nights when his breathing grew so faint that all believed the end near. When exhaustion overcame her, she slept upon a pallet by the door, waking at the least movement.

The brave child had performed acts which strong men might have shrunk from attempting; yet she seemed unconscious of aught extraordinary. When neighbors praised her, she lowered her eyes and answered simply:

“What else could I do? He was my father.”

Therein lay the purity of her courage. She had not sought danger for the pleasure of triumphing over it, nor imagined herself the heroine of some grand adventure. Love had pointed out the duty nearest her hand, and she had done it—first with a knife, then with a pail, afterward with linen, broth, wakefulness, and prayer. The humblest domestic instruments had become weapons against destruction.

By spring John could sit near the window and look upon his fields. By summer he walked, though feebly, beneath the orchard trees. His features were never wholly restored, and strangers sometimes turned from his disfigured countenance; but his children saw only the familiar goodness beneath it. He lived to an advanced age, honored throughout the district for his integrity and for the sufferings he had borne without hatred.

Phebe grew to womanhood, married, and had children of her own. She retained the scars of the rawhide, though she rarely spoke of them. At family gatherings, when younger voices begged to hear how she had cut down her father and quenched the burning house, she would shorten the recital and give more praise to her mother’s directions, the surgeon’s skill, and the neighbors’ timely aid than to herself.

I have heard the account from those who received it from her own lips, and the principal circumstances were long attested by persons who saw the ruined room, the severed rope, and the wounds of John Reynolds and his daughter. If time has softened some lesser details, it has not enlarged the central truth.

For Providence, though invisible, does not always work by invisible means. Often its chosen instruments are vigilant human eyes and ready, loving hands: the eyes of a child watching a winter road, and the hands of that same child cutting a rope, carrying water through the snow, binding a father’s wounds, and refusing, even afterward, to call herself brave.

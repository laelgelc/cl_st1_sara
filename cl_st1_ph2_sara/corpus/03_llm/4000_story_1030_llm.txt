# OUR FRENCH WATERING-PLACE

When we first went to our French watering-place—which we shall call Port-Bonnet, because that is not its name, and because an English watering-place, if informed of the truth, would instantly erect a Subscription Assembly-Room, import three German bands, and destroy it—we went by the shortest route.

Never go anywhere by the shortest route.

This maxim, the fruit of suffering, should be painted on the bows of every packet-boat, engraved upon every passport, embroidered on every carpet-bag, and tattooed, if the operation be not objected to, upon every commercial traveller. The shortest route from England to Port-Bonnet consisted, upon paper, of a clean black line, pleasantly direct, which touched the sea at one point and quitted it at another. The sea itself, having no respect for cartography, converted that line into a succession of knots, corkscrews, plunges, and convulsions, such as no penman, except in delirium, could have represented.

We left England at an hour when honest people are in bed, and when dishonest people—if we may accept the evidence of melodrama—are getting in at windows. The moon looked ill; the pier looked worse; the packet looked like a small black cupboard, accidentally dropped between two large waves. A gentleman in a travelling-cap assured us that the wind was “nothing”; a sailor, with the calm cheerfulness of a man who would remain upon deck while we perished below, observed that there was “a bit of a lop”; and Bilkins, our authority in all matters concerning which he knows nothing, said that he had crossed in a hurricane and eaten a cold fowl throughout.

Bilkins had no cold fowl on this occasion. He had, however, a tin case, a telescope, a patent waterproof, a guidebook of seventeen hundred pages, two cloaks, three straps, and an opinion. These accompanied him below.

There are moments when the human countenance ceases to be private property and becomes a public exhibition. Such a moment arrived before we had cleared the harbour. A row of passengers, who had recently been citizens, taxpayers, heads of families, advocates of national greatness, and in two instances magistrates, became pale memorials of departed dignity. The gentleman in the travelling-cap, having declared that he never suffered from the sea, retired suddenly behind a door and contradicted himself with violence. An elderly lady appealed to Heaven, the captain, and a bottle of aromatic vinegar. Bilkins lay extended upon a bench, wearing his waterproof as a shroud, and murmured that the vessel had no construction.

We ourselves maintained a grave composure.

We mention this because nobody else has ever mentioned it, and because truth should be encouraged even in lonely places.

Our composure was of that grave order which takes no interest in conversation, scenery, government, commerce, or the future of mankind. We fixed our eyes upon a brass knob and made it the centre of our moral existence. When the brass knob rose, we rose spiritually with it; when it descended, all human hope descended too. Presently it described a circle. From that period we remember little, except that France advanced towards us sideways, withdrew stern foremost, turned pale, and finally struck us on the shoulder in the person of a custom-house officer.

Arrival in a foreign country is commonly represented by painters as a brilliant event. The traveller stands upright in the morning sun; the coast glows; peasants offer flowers; the cathedral bells ring; Liberty, Commerce, and the Arts extend their arms. Our arrival consisted in being discovered, at an angle of forty-five degrees, between Bilkins’s boots and an unknown hamper, while a military creature requested our passport.

We produced a railway ticket.

The military creature did not consider it a passport.

We produced a letter, a card, a bill from a London tailor, and the key of our carpet-bag. None of these documents satisfied him. At last the passport was found in the very place in which we had emphatically declared it could not be—in our breast-pocket—and we were permitted to stand in a draught while it was examined by four persons, each of whom suspected us independently.

Then came the custom-house.

Our modest possessions, which at home had presented the innocent appearance of linen, books, brushes, and boots, assumed, under official scrutiny, the character of a conspiracy. Shirts were shaken as if political refugees might fall from their sleeves. A pair of slippers was interrogated. A volume of poems was opened at random, perhaps in the hope of discovering gunpowder between the stanzas. A small pot of English mustard caused a consultation; and our sponge, lifted with two fingers and regarded from a distance, seemed likely to embroil the nations.

Bilkins’s luggage occupied the authorities much longer. His telescope was believed to be artillery; his waterproof appeared to have a double meaning; and his guidebook, from its enormous size, might reasonably have contained a complete plan for the invasion of France. When all had been rummaged, refolded badly, strapped insecurely, and chalked in seven places, we were discharged into the Republic—or the Empire, or the Kingdom; for governments alter so frequently on the Continent that a careful traveller avoids committing himself until he has seen the current postage stamps.

We were now on shore, which should have improved us. It did not. Our legs retained a nautical independence and carried us alternately towards a fish-basket and a soldier. A porter seized one bag; another seized the second; a boy seized nothing, but shouted; an old woman demanded payment for a service she had not performed; and a coachman, addressing us as Milor, proposed to drive us to Port-Bonnet for a sum which would have purchased the horse.

In this condition—damp, hollow-eyed, unbuttoned, chalked, suspected, and morally inferior to our own luggage—we entered France.

And yet we returned the next year. And the next. And have returned so often that the little journey which once appeared an expedition into Central Africa now seems no more formidable than crossing a street, though the sea still occasionally removes the street while we are upon it.

Port-Bonnet lies where a low green country, running with patient industry to the coast, suddenly breaks into cliffs, old walls, crooked roofs, windmills, kitchen-gardens, and the sea. It has a new town, which is straight, white, respectable, and improving; and an old town, which is crooked, brown, disreputable, and delightful. The new town has houses with bells, brass plates, lace curtains, and little iron balconies upon which no human being has ever been seen. The old town has houses that lean together to exchange confidences, upper windows that shake hands across the street, doors without steps, steps without doors, beams, hooks, niches, broken saints, painted shutters, and a smell compounded of coffee, nets, apples, smoke, onions, tar, and antiquity.

Bilkins prefers the new town.

The traveller who has not been instructed by Bilkins may suppose himself to enjoy the old quarter; but he is wrong. Bilkins has settled that old quarters are picturesque only when they contain three specified objects—a cathedral, a beggar with a staff, and a house in which a duke has slept. Port-Bonnet possesses no cathedral, its beggars are active persons who sell matches, and its only duke was detained there by contrary winds and never slept at all. Therefore, according to Bilkins, it has “no associations.”

We, being obstinate, associate it with pleasure.

At the upper end of the old town stands the market gateway, a thick grey arch in a remnant of wall. Through this arch, on market mornings, the country pours itself. It begins before sunrise with one cart, two sleepy women, and a pig who objects to municipal arrangements. It grows with the light. It trickles in baskets, advances in barrows, rattles in carts, trots in upon donkeys, and by eight o’clock comes brawling through the gateway in a full, bright, noisy stream.

There are red carrots with green feathers; white turnips as round as aldermen; cabbages folded in upon themselves like great vegetable secrets; onions shining through brown skins; long beans, short peas, purple plums, yellow pears, rosy apples, blue grapes, cream cheeses, brown loaves, silver fish, scarlet caps, striped petticoats, wooden shoes, earthen pots, tin measures, live chickens, dead rabbits, and geese in every condition of political excitement. There are old women in white caps, young women in blue aprons, boys carrying baskets larger than themselves, peasants with faces baked to the colour of their bread, and little girls who sit among flowers and look fresher than the flowers.

Everything cries. The fish cry by proxy through the fishwives; the vegetables are praised with lungs that might call ships off rocks; the chickens contribute personally; the geese denounce the whole proceeding; the bells interfere; the carts protest; the dogs pursue private quarrels through public business; and over all rises that quick, light, disputatious French speech which seems perpetually on the point of ending in bloodshed and perpetually ends in the purchase of three onions.

We have watched two market women conduct a controversy over a sou with such flashing eyes, such uplifted hands, such appeals to earth, sky, ancestors, motherhood, honour, justice, and the military glory of France, that we expected the troops. The dispute concluded by their laughing, kissing each other on both cheeks, and combining against a third woman, whose parsley was inferior.

By noon the market stream has begun to sink. The carts roll back through the gate; the donkeys depart under green burdens; scattered leaves, straw, feathers, and a melancholy boot remain upon the stones. By two o’clock the river is dry, and a solitary municipal broom travels over its bed.

Beyond the market, the street climbs sharply towards the ramparts. We say climbs, because no house in that street appears capable of standing on level ground. The houses rise one above another, put out excrescences, thrust forward wooden elbows, hang cages, linen, nets, and children over the pavement, and, when they reach the wall, burst through it into a second existence of sheds, gardens, pigeon-houses, and little terraces exposed to the sea.

Upon the ramparts the wind is a permanent inhabitant. It knows every chimney, enters every coat, turns every umbrella inside out, and has polished the grass by centuries of running over it. From there we see the harbour below, with its red and brown sails; the pier advancing stiffly into the water; the fishing-boats drawn up like black seals upon the beach; the pale line of coast running away north and south; and the sea, which, according to weather, is blue metal, green glass, grey wool, black slate, or one universal boiling-pot.

There is a bench near the old gun platform where four old men formerly sat.

The first year we knew Port-Bonnet there were six. They came in the afternoon, each with a stick, each wearing a cap of immemorial pattern, and each possessing a face upon which wind and time had written in a very small hand. They had been sailors, fishermen, soldiers, smugglers—possibly all four, for their histories varied according to the audience and the quantity of tobacco. They sat in a row and watched the sea they had spent their lives upon, criticizing modern boats, modern nets, modern boys, modern governments, and modern weather.

“No wind now,” one would say, while our hat flew over the wall.

“No fish now,” another would reply, while twenty boats unloaded beneath us.

“No sailors.”

“No France.”

Then they would nod together, being perfectly content.

The six became five; the five became four; last autumn there were three. Nobody announced the absences. No black garment appeared, no speech was made. A space on the bench remained empty for a week; then the survivors moved a little nearer together. The sea continued its old conversation below, and the wind, which remembers no man and touches all men, shook the grass around their feet.

But we are becoming solemn upon the ramparts, and M. Loyal Devasseur would never permit solemnity to continue longer than was useful.

M. Loyal is our landlord.

He is also a former sergeant of cavalry, a town councillor, an owner of houses, a grower of pears, a collector of military relics, a judge of horses, a remover of stains, a repairer of locks, a negotiator with cooks, a believer in Providence, and the most faithful subject Napoleon ever possessed after Napoleon had ceased to require subjects.

He is short in stature, broad in chest, upright in carriage, grey in moustache, bright in eye, and so complete in self-possession that, if the roof fell in, we believe he would bow to it, place a chair for it, and observe that the accident was fortunate because the ceiling had long needed ventilation.

His name, Loyal, is not a title bestowed by us in admiration, though it might well be. It was given to him at baptism by a father whose politics were either prophetic or unsettled. He pronounces it Lo-yahl, separating the syllables with military precision; but the English residents, who cannot endure a foreign word until they have knocked it out of shape, call him Mr. Loyle, Mr. Loyall, Mossy Lolly, and, in one deplorable case, Loyal Dave.

He answers to all with courtesy.

Our first interview took place in the little courtyard of his principal house, where he stood in shirt-sleeves directing three operations at once: a mason who was repairing a wall, a maid who was beating a carpet, and a small grandson who was attempting to drink from the pump. M. Loyal prevented the wall from leaning, the carpet from escaping into the street, and the grandson from drowning; then received us as if we had arrived at the head of a diplomatic mission.

“Milor is fatigue,” said he.

We admitted the fatigue, but declined the peerage.

“The sea—she make you bad?”

We confessed that the sea had not made us conspicuously good.

“Ah!” cried M. Loyal, delighted. “It is nothing! To-morrow Milor eat two breakfast.”

This was his manner with calamity. Nothing existed for him except as a rough preface to something better. Rain improved the wells; drought improved the grapes; a broken window admitted air; a cracked dish had the advantage of being old; a sick tenant was certain to recover; a recovered tenant was certain never to be sick again. When a chimney of his was struck by lightning, he congratulated us upon the remarkable excellence of the remaining chimneys.

His house—our house during the season—stood halfway between the new town and the old, as if endeavouring to reconcile them. It possessed a courtyard, a garden, a stable without a horse, a coach-house without a coach, a cellar full of bottles with no labels, and cupboards so numerous and capacious that the equipment of a regiment might have been hidden in them and forgotten until the next war.

The rooms were large, polished, cool, and ornamented with portraits of Napoleon.

Napoleon crossed the Alps over our writing-table. Napoleon reviewed the Guard above the sofa. Napoleon, with one hand inside his coat, contemplated our bed. A small Napoleon formed the handle of the bell-pull; a larger Napoleon stood under a glass shade on the mantelpiece; and a bronze Napoleon, of extreme weight and concentrated ambition, served to keep open the door.

M. Loyal had seen the Emperor.

Not the first Emperor, as a pedantic computation of dates might suggest; but pedantry had no place in his recollections. He had seen an emperor, and that emperor was a Napoleon, and therefore, in the moral sense—which is the only sense worthy of a soldier—he had seen Him.

“He pass,” M. Loyal told us, drawing himself upright. “I am there. Young—very young—but militaire. He look. I look. Voilà!”

This encounter, though deficient in dialogue, had governed his life. He possessed a button which he believed had belonged to an Imperial uniform, a piece of red cloth which might have belonged to anything, and a map on which the battlefield of Europe appeared less important than a pear orchard owned by M. Loyal outside Port-Bonnet. The orchard was coloured green, surrounded by red ink, and labelled DOMAINE DEVASSEUR in letters large enough for Russia.

“You have much land?” we asked.

M. Loyal placed one hand over a tiny green patch.

“Immense.”

In physical measurement it amounted to five acres. In loyalty, family pride, pears, and red ink, it was a province.

He showed us his medals on Sunday. There were two of them, and they occupied him completely. He wore a dark blue coat buttoned with such firmness that his chest appeared to have been packed for travel, white gloves, a high collar, and a hat which recalled all the campaigns of the century without belonging precisely to any one. The medals shone upon him with an effect which suggested that Providence had first designed the decorations and had afterwards, with considerate foresight, provided M. Loyal as a broad and honourable surface upon which to hang them.

He had served bravely; that was beyond question. But he told little of danger and much of boots.

“Boots,” said M. Loyal, “are the army. A soldier with bad boots is no soldier. He is a complaint.”

Of battles he observed that there had been noise. Of wounds, that one in the shoulder was inconvenient in wet weather. Of a colonel renowned for courage, that he had understood soup. Of glory, that it was excellent, but must not delay dinner.

This practical spirit attended all his affairs. He could repair a shutter while discussing foreign policy; discover a leak by listening to a wall; select a melon by tapping it with the solemnity of a physician; and calculate, within half a sou, the cost of anything except his own generosity.

That he never calculated.

One winter, as we learned not from him but from the baker, a labourer with a wife and four children took one of M. Loyal’s small houses near the harbour. Work failed; then sickness came; then the rent did not. At the end of three months the labourer went to M. Loyal, expecting notice to quit.

“You owe me money,” said M. Loyal.

The man agreed.

“You have no money.”

Again the man agreed.

“You have children?”

The existence of the children, though visible, was admitted.

“Then,” said M. Loyal, “you remain.”

They remained for a year. M. Loyal charged nothing, supplied wood in the coldest month, obtained work for the father in spring, and refused thanks on the ground that the empty house would have become damp.

“It was for the walls,” he explained when we discovered him. “A house must be warm. The children—pouf!—they make the heat.”

Good M. Loyal!

There are gentlemen whose family trees spread over whole dining-rooms, whose names have been pronounced at courts, whose coats are crowded with quarterings, and whose hearts could be conveniently lodged in a waistcoat pocket without altering the fit. We would set M. Loyal beside the tallest of them, in his blue coat, with his medals, his Imperial button, his five-acre province, and his excellent understanding of soup, and ask no herald to decide between them.

Our watering-place bathes.

It bathes in rows, in machines, in costumes, in flannel, in linen, in secrecy, in publicity, before breakfast, after breakfast, under medical orders, against medical orders, and with an amount of preparation which would seem to promise residence beneath the ocean.

At six in the morning the true bathers appear. These are brown fishermen, strong market women, soldiers, children, and certain ancient French gentlemen who enter the water with dignity, swim twelve strokes, and return with the expression of men who have discharged a duty to the State.

At eight come the English.

We recognize our countrymen at a distance by their resolution not to enjoy themselves. They descend to the beach wrapped in garments indicative of bereavement, carrying towels, books, bottles, ropes, caps, shoes, and occasionally food. They consult the sky, the tide, the doctor, the bathing-woman, and one another. They object to the wind, the sun, the absence of sun, the quantity of water, the smallness of the waves, and the presence of French people in France.

Then they enter.

The bathing-machines are wooden chambers mounted on wheels, and resemble travelling prisons for persons convicted of cleanliness. A horse drags them into the surf; the bather emerges from the seaward door; and the waves, delighted with the apparatus, play upon its steps as upon an instrument of torture.

Our own bathing-man is named Prosper. He is a black-bearded, red-capped, tar-coloured amphibian, whose chest appears to be made from the bow of a fishing-boat. Prosper believes all Englishmen desire to be drowned a little, for their health. He receives us with affection, leads us farther than prudence recommends, watches a wave approaching, cries “Now, M’sieu!” and vanishes.

The wave removes France.

It also removes our cap, our breath, our footing, our opinion of Prosper, and whatever claims we may have had to an erect position. When the Republic reappears, Prosper is laughing five yards away and declaring the water magnificent.

Bilkins bathes by theory. He possesses a private garment constructed after diagrams, with straps, valves, pockets, and a belt containing a thermometer. He enters only when the tide, wind, temperature, barometer, and guidebook agree, an event which occurred once in three summers. On that occasion he stepped upon a stone, returned immediately, and wrote to a newspaper on the dangers of foreign beaches.

The fishing population regard us with compassion.

They are a hardy company, weather-coloured and loosely assembled in garments that seem to have been cut from tarred sails. Their blouses are blue when new, purple in middle life, and brown in honourable old age. Their trousers contain folds enough to reef a mainsail; their boots could anchor small craft; their hands are ropes, their faces charts, and their hair, when the wind takes it, flies like signals of distress.

The boats go out at sunset. Women and children help to push them down the shingle, the men standing dark against the evening, the red sails rising one by one. There is shouting, straining, a grinding of keels, a leap of foam, and then the boat is free, the figures settle into their places, and the little dark shape passes beyond the pier into that enormous waste which is picturesque to visitors and bread to them.

At dawn they return.

We have gone down early enough to see the harbour receive them: wet sails drooping, gulls screaming, baskets ready, women waiting with crossed arms, barefoot children hopping among ropes, and the first sunlight striking scales, water, knives, earrings, buckles, and eyes until the whole rough quay sparkles like a treasure-house.

There is no sentiment upon the quay. Fish are counted, weighed, disputed over, carried off, cleaned, packed, salted, and eaten. A man who has been twelve hours at sea pauses to drink black coffee; a woman lifts a basket which would discourage a porter; a child, no higher than the gunwale, sorts shrimps with the gravity of a minister arranging a cabinet. Work moves everywhere, quick, practised, and without complaint.

Yet these people make holiday with a completeness unknown to the idle.

On the feast of Saint Aurélien—who was, we believe, boiled, though in what cause and with what seasoning we have never ascertained—the fishing quarter hangs flags from every window, builds arches of green boughs, dresses boats with flowers, and produces a brass band from no discoverable source.

The band consists of twenty-three performers and forty-one instruments; or so it sounds. Its conductor is the barber, a man of passionate temperament who conducts with his entire body and appears at moments to be fighting the music with a stick. The cornet pursues one melody, the clarinet remembers another, the drum maintains military independence, and the ophicleide descends at intervals into a cavern from which it cannot be persuaded to return.

Magnificent band!

It heads a procession containing municipal officers, fishermen, little girls in white, old sailors with ribbons, two firemen, M. Loyal, a priest, six boys carrying an ornamental ship, and the population generally. The ship rolls dangerously; the boys quarrel under it; the firemen perspire; the little girls shed rose leaves; M. Loyal salutes every flag; and Saint Aurélien, represented by a painted image of benevolent but uncomfortable appearance, proceeds through streets where every window has become a bouquet of caps, faces, babies, and waving hands.

After the procession come games upon the beach: climbing a greased pole, running in sacks, breaking pots while blindfolded, rowing, wrestling, dancing, and pursuing a pig whose opinions on sainthood are plainly unfavourable.

The dancing begins with daylight and ends when daylight is an old story. Fishermen dance with fishwives, soldiers with maids, grandfathers with children, and everybody with everybody. They dance lightly, violently, solemnly, absurdly; with hands on hips, hands above heads, feet behind them, feet before them, and certain unexpected joints which have escaped the attention of anatomy.

An English observer declares the performance indecorous, and watches it for four hours.

There are public entertainments every week in summer, all excellent and nearly all cheap. For two sous we may see fireworks; for one sou, a puppet theatre; for nothing, a military parade, a storm, an auction, a political discussion, three games of bowls, and Bilkins explaining France.

The roundabout sets up in the square on Thursdays. Its horses are carved in attitudes of permanent alarm and painted in colours never worn by horses except in dreams. Upon them ride fishermen fresh from the sea, cooks in white caps, grave soldiers, market girls, and small children tied on with handkerchiefs. The machine turns; an organ grinds; the riders rise and fall; and the most solemn men in Port-Bonnet circulate upon pink chargers with gilded tails, preserving expressions suitable to a funeral.

Nearby stands the theatre of Monsieur and Madame Papillon, where wooden princes, dragons, policemen, lovers, devils, and sausages enact the passions of mankind. The theatre is no larger than a cupboard, but contains more incident than Europe. Kings are dethroned in seconds; infants grow to manhood between two knocks; villains are beheaded and return as witnesses; the hero dies at nine and marries at ten minutes past; while a dog, indifferent to dramatic probability, settles every difficulty by biting the minister.

The children shriek. The soldiers grin. The old women explain the plot. We contribute one sou and are rich for an hour.

Once every season there is a charitable fête for the widows of fishermen. Booths are erected along the promenade; ladies sell flowers, fruit, ribbons, cakes, shells, and articles of needlework whose purpose is mercifully concealed. M. Loyal serves upon the committee and spends the week in a condition of joyous command.

“You will make the lottery,” he tells us.

We protest that we know nothing of lotteries.

“Excellent! Then you cannot cheat.”

Bilkins is appointed to the intellectual stall, where he sells old guidebooks and gives advice gratis. The guidebooks remain; the advice circulates extensively.

French and English ladies sit together behind counters, beginning with formal bows and ending by sharing pins, change, chairs, children, and private opinions of their husbands. French fishermen buy English pincushions; English clergymen buy French brandy-cherries and conceal them under pamphlets; boys win enormous dolls; dignified persons are persuaded into paper caps; and M. Loyal, everywhere at once, subscribes at every stall to the charity he is administering.

In the evening there are lanterns among the trees, music upon the grass, and fireworks from the end of the pier. The rockets rise over the black sea and break into green stars, red rain, silver fountains, and fiery wheels. Every flash discovers hundreds of lifted faces—French caps, English bonnets, sailors’ hats, children on shoulders—and every darkness after it leaves a common murmur of delight.

That murmur is worth many treaties.

We do not undervalue treaties. They are admirable documents, particularly when framed, sealed, signed, and kept in drawers. But if two nations desire to understand each other, we recommend that their children ride the same roundabout, that their old women dispute over the same cabbages, that their young men pull together at the same boat, and that everybody be rained upon at the same fête.

Our English colony deserves notice.

It occupies several boarding-houses along the upper promenade, where it drinks tea at appointed hours, receives letters from home, compares prices, and preserves the Constitution. The largest establishment is kept by Madame Ronfleur, a widow with black eyes, a white cap, a talent for arithmetic, and an English vocabulary of thirty words, twenty-nine of which are “Yes.”

“Madame, is this tea?”

“Yes.”

“Is it coffee?”

“Yes.”

“Was it boiled yesterday?”

“Yes.”

“Can we have another pot?”

Here Madame Ronfleur remembers the thirtieth word.

“Impossible.”

At her table assemble the Major, the Miss Puddifords, young Grigson, Mrs. Chumble, the Reverend Septimus Bale, Bilkins when invited elsewhere, and several unattached persons who come and go but always leave a shawl.

The Major has resided abroad for fourteen years without acquiring a foreign phrase, and regards this as a triumph of character. He communicates by speaking English loudly and attaching “O” to the principal nouns.

“Waiter-O! Bread-O! Bill-O!”

When misunderstood, he appeals to us and says the French are singularly deficient in languages.

The Miss Puddifords are sisters of uncertain age and absolute information. They know which English families are respectable, which French families are not, what every visitor pays, why every marriage occurred, and what illness every absent person really has. They travel for culture, but avoid churches because they are damp, pictures because they are tiring, scenery because it requires walking, and natives because one never knows.

Young Grigson paints. He wears a large hat, carries a small box, and makes grey sketches of bright objects. Give him a blue sea, a red sail, a yellow beach, a white cloud, and a green cliff, and he will return in three hours with a composition resembling cold gravy. Bilkins praises his tone.

Mrs. Chumble is permanently surprised by France. The shops shut at the wrong hours, meals appear in the wrong order, beds are of the wrong shape, Sundays have the wrong sound, and the moon, though she has not formally complained of it, is probably foreign. Yet Mrs. Chumble has a kind heart, nurses every sick English maid, gives soup to half the harbour, and is adored by French children, who call her Madame Shomble and steal her spectacles.

The Reverend Septimus Bale came to Port-Bonnet under protest, preached against Continental levity, discovered an ancient chapel, began studying its carvings, made friends with the curé, learned French, extended his visit, and now plays chess with that excellent ecclesiastic three evenings a week. Each continues to believe the other in theological darkness; but they illuminate one another’s solitude very comfortably.

There are, of course, disputes.

An English dog bites a French chicken; a French child throws sand upon an English newspaper; a lodging-house bill contains an unexplained candle; an Englishman calls a franc a shilling; a Frenchman calls a shilling a franc; somebody whistles during a sacred silence; somebody else refuses to whistle during a national tune. For an hour the Channel widens, fleets arm, ancestors rise, Waterloo and Hastings are recalled, and Europe trembles.

Then M. Loyal arrives.

He hears both sides, understands neither completely, pays for the chicken, removes the candle, pats the child, rebukes the dog, praises both nations, and invites the principal combatants to taste pears from the Domaine Devasseur. Peace is restored by dessert.

M. Loyal’s English improves yearly, though not always in directions anticipated by grammar. He tells us that the weather is “very mingle,” that a tenant has “make himself upstairs,” and that a noisy neighbour must be “nokemdon.” He once informed Mrs. Chumble, with the highest courtesy, that her appearance was “more fat than formerly.” Mrs. Chumble froze.

M. Loyal, perceiving some deficiency in the compliment, added warmly:

“Much more fat!”

Only his face saved him.

Our French, let it be confessed, does not save us so often. We have ordered a hat when requiring a haddock, complimented a widow upon the death of her husband when intending to condole with her, and requested a laundress to wash our ancestors. The French listener rarely laughs until we have gone; the English speaker commonly laughs before he begins. In that difference lies a little civilisation.

We have heard travellers complain that the people of Port-Bonnet are not picturesque enough. The fishermen wear knitted jerseys purchased in shops; the peasants come to market by railway; girls read newspapers; boys desire watches; old cottages acquire glass windows; and a woman who ought, according to art, to spin flax in a doorway may be found operating a sewing-machine.

Bilkins deplores this decay.

He desires the inhabitants of foreign countries to remain exactly as they were when engraved for annuals. They must wear costumes inconvenient to labour, inhabit houses hostile to health, cultivate land by obsolete methods, and arrange themselves, at reasonable intervals, in foreground groups for his amusement. Improvement is admirable in Birmingham; in Brittany it is a want of taste.

We cannot agree.

If a fisherman can buy boots that leak less than his father’s, let the harmonies of colour suffer. If a girl can read, let her disturb the composition. If a railway carries vegetables swiftly to market, we will forgive it for not resembling a ruined aqueduct. Human beings were not created as accessories to landscape, though tourists have frequently mistaken them for that purpose.

Port-Bonnet works hard. At dawn the boats are in; the carts arrive; shutters rise; ovens glow; hammers strike; women wash steps; children carry messages; sails are mended; nets spread; fields beyond the walls fill with bent figures. At noon there is food, talk, and sunlight. In the evening there is more work, then bowls, dancing, family visits, a glass of thin wine, and bed.

Drunkenness is rare. Misery, though not absent from any town under the sun, does not parade as a customary companion. The poorest meal is eaten cleanly; the humblest room contrives a pot of flowers; a patched garment is not therefore a dirty one; and a child with bare feet may have cheeks fit for the market’s best apples.

They are economical without being joyless, ceremonious without being cold, excitable without remaining angry. They bow more than we do, talk more, gesture more, and perhaps promise more; but they also work with a patience that makes little noise, endure weather without writing to the papers, and assist one another with an absence of speechifying that might instruct committees.

The English residents have altered too.

The Major now says “Bon jour” to the harbourmaster, though he pronounces it “Bong Jaw” and considers the concession handsome. Mrs. Chumble has ceased bringing her own mustard. The Miss Puddifords admit that one French family may be respectable. Young Grigson has placed a red sail in a picture. The Reverend Bale and the curé have abandoned the hope of converting each other and exchange vegetable seeds.

Even Bilkins has changed, though he denies it. Last summer we found him on the quay talking to Prosper, the bathing-man. They possessed no common language, but were sharing tobacco and condemning the weather with perfect comprehension. Bilkins, seeing us, immediately explained that Prosper was an exception.

Port-Bonnet is full of exceptions.

The shrill market woman who bullies customers gives unsold fruit to the hospital. The old sailor who declares all boys worthless teaches them knots. The councillor who speaks longest votes most sensibly. The innkeeper accused of charging English prices feeds a stranded English family. The proud fisherman accepts no gift but risks his boat to rescue strangers. The frivolous dancers rise before daylight to work. The noisy children fall silent when the funeral bell passes. M. Loyal, who makes his tiny estate an empire, calls his great charity a precaution against damp.

We have returned in spring, when rain drifts over the roofs and the first boats go out under torn clouds; in summer, when the beach is striped with tents and the promenade blooms with parasols; in autumn, when apples roll through the market and the sea begins to darken; and once in winter, when the boarding-houses were shut, the roundabout had vanished, the wind possessed the ramparts, and Port-Bonnet showed us its working face without powder.

M. Loyal met us at the station in snow.

“Bad season,” we said.

“Excellent,” said M. Loyal. “The insects die.”

The house was cold, but a fire had been kindled; the cupboards smelt of apples; Napoleon watched us from every wall; and upon the table stood pears from the Domaine Devasseur, preserved like jewels.

That night the wind shouted in the chimney and the sea struck the beach with a weight we felt through the floor. Somewhere beyond the harbour lights the fishing-boats were out. The summer music, the paper lanterns, the English bonnets, the puppet kings, the solemn riders on painted horses—all were gone. Yet the town was not dead. It breathed through the storm: in ovens, workshops, kitchens, watch-houses; in women mending nets; in men waiting for the tide; in children asleep under red roofs; in old M. Loyal descending twice to the courtyard because a shutter would “make himself liberty.”

Morning broke clear. The boats returned safely.

The whole harbour went down to meet them—not in procession, not under flags, not because Bilkins recommended the spectacle, but because fathers, brothers, husbands, and neighbours had come home. We stood among the crowd while frost shone on the stones. M. Loyal, bareheaded, counted the boats, found the number right, and instantly declared that he had never doubted it.

This is our French watering-place.

It has no celebrated ruin, no fashionable gaming-room, no avenue in which royalty is compelled to drive, no physician who has discovered that its air cures everything, no hotel with four hundred bedrooms and one candlestick, no sacred hour at which all persons must admire the sunset. Its sea is sometimes rough, its streets are frequently fishy, its bells begin too early, its drains are not philosophical, its pier is short, its orchestra courageous, its customs officers inquisitive, and its weather—M. Loyal alone excepted—is not invariably excellent.

But we have found there labour without sourness, thrift without meanness, gaiety without riot, poverty without degradation, and courtesy where ceremony might least be expected. We have seen English stiffness thaw under French kindness, French suspicion vanish before English fair dealing, children make treaties without diplomats, women establish alliances over sickbeds, sailors exchange help without dictionaries, and old soldiers discover that memories of battles are less useful than present friendships.

We have learned, too, that patriotism need not consist in carrying one’s country about like a loaded pistol and presenting it at every foreign head. An Englishman may praise a French soup without sinking the British navy; a Frenchman may admire an English tool without betraying Napoleon; and both may agree that a wet child should be dried before the historical claims of either nation are considered.

When we leave Port-Bonnet, M. Loyal accompanies us to the carriage. He oversees the luggage, distrusts the straps, rebukes the driver, presents pears, refuses payment for some impossible item, and predicts a calm sea whatever the barometer may threaten.

“You come next year,” he says.

We say that circumstances are uncertain.

He waves circumstances away.

“You come.”

The train moves. There he stands—square, upright, grey-moustached, medal-less on weekdays but decorated in spirit; landlord, soldier, councillor, pear-grower, house-repairer, peacemaker, Imperialist, democrat by instinct, and gentleman by every law that was written before heraldry.

The old roofs fall behind; the ramparts sink; a last strip of sea flashes between the fields. Bilkins opens his guidebook and prepares to tell us what we have seen.

We close our eyes.

We see it better without him: the market pouring through the gateway; the bright caps and silver fish; the boats under red sails; the old men drawing nearer on their bench; Prosper laughing beyond the wave; the paper lanterns among the trees; English and French faces lifted together to the fireworks; and M. Loyal in his courtyard, finding advantage in a cracked wall, hope in bad weather, empire in five acres, and the natural warmth of children in an empty house.

Good M. Loyal!

Good, busy, weather-beaten, cheerful Port-Bonnet!

May its people work, dance, bargain, bathe, mend nets, grow pears, mispronounce us, improve themselves in defiance of Bilkins, and make friends with our unreasonable countrymen for many a year. And if the shortest route to it must still be crossed in a packet no larger than a cupboard, under examination by the sea, the customs, and our own rebellious constitution—why, we will submit again to public degradation.

For we shall know what waits beyond it.

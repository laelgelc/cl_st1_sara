# Aunt Pen’s Campaign

Mrs. Penelope Carroll had reached that period of life when a woman who has missed arranging her own destiny is very apt to take charge of somebody else’s. Having no daughter, she selected a niece; having no fortune large enough to make her plans easy, she supplied the deficiency by ingenuity; and having once decided that Miss Deborah Wilder must marry money, she prepared for the campaign with the composure of an experienced general who has studied the enemy’s income, position, family connections, and probable intentions.

Debby herself knew nothing of the campaign. She believed that Aunt Pen, moved by affection and the doctor’s opinion that sea-air was good for young persons, had invited her to spend three weeks at fashionable Baycliff. This innocent explanation satisfied a girl whose ideas of the world had been gathered chiefly from books, a small village, and a household where duties were so plentiful that no one had leisure to invent unnecessary troubles.

At twenty, Debby was poor, healthy, and hopeful—a combination which wealthy invalids might envy, though they seldom do. She had taught arithmetic to unwilling children, made preserves, nursed babies, mended stockings, sung in the village choir, ridden bareback when no saddle could be found, and read everything from sermons to sea-voyages whenever a candle and half an hour occurred together. Her eyes were brown, her hair rebellious, her mouth frank, and her complexion had been painted by wind and weather in tints not obtainable from the most expensive Parisian box.

Aunt Pen contemplated these advantages with mingled satisfaction and alarm.

“Your color is excellent, my dear,” she said, on the evening before they started, “but you must not run about in the sun at Baycliff.”

“Then why go to the sea?” asked Debby.

“To enjoy it properly.”

“I thought sunshine was part of the sea.”

“Not in society. And, Deborah, I beg that you will not tell every one that you teach school.”

“But I do.”

“There are truths which require tact.”

Debby considered this profound moral maxim while Aunt Pen folded a lilac silk dress into the trunk.

“Must I be ashamed of earning my bread?”

“Certainly not. But there is no need to exhibit the loaf.”

“What shall I say if any one asks?”

“Say that you assist your father.”

“I assist him by earning three hundred dollars a year.”

“Deborah!”

“Yes, Aunt Pen.”

“And while we are away, I wish you to be called Dora. Deborah is dreadfully scriptural.”

“I rather like it. She was a judge, you know.”

“That is precisely the difficulty. No gentleman wants to marry a judge.”

Debby laughed so heartily that Aunt Pen was obliged to laugh also, though in a chastened manner; for she loved the girl in her own anxious, worldly way, and would have gone barefoot to secure her a velvet carriage.

Thus armed with silk dresses, white gloves, two new novels, a bottle of lavender, and many excellent intentions, the ladies set forth.

---

The railway carriage was crowded with those varieties of humanity which Providence, having made them unable to agree elsewhere, packs together upon journeys for their spiritual improvement. There was an old gentleman who snored with official dignity; a mother surrounded by buns, bundles, and children; two young ladies whose hats conversed across the aisle in feathers; a clergyman buried in a newspaper; and, opposite Debby, a plainly dressed young man reading *Martin Chuzzlewit*.

Now a good book is a social introduction which requires neither cards nor grandmothers. Debby had been attempting to read Aunt Pen’s chosen novel, wherein a pale countess spent three hundred pages misunderstanding a gloomy duke; but her eye wandered repeatedly toward Dickens, and her mouth betrayed her when the stranger smiled over Mrs. Gamp.

“You are at the best part,” she said involuntarily.

The young man looked up. He had a brown face, clear gray eyes, and an expression which suggested that he had found the world interesting, though not always comfortable. His coat was well brushed but old; one glove had been neatly mended; and his cravat, though clean, was wider than the prevailing mode permitted.

Aunt Pen observed these facts at a glance and convicted him of poverty before he had spoken seven words.

“I beg your pardon,” said Debby, coloring. “I should not interrupt.”

“A fellow-admirer needs no pardon,” he replied. “I have been wishing that some charitable person would enjoy Pecksniff with me. Laughter kept to oneself is only half used.”

“That is what I always think. At home, if I find anything particularly amusing, I read it aloud to Father, whether he is listening or not.”

“Does he object?”

“Not unless I read during his nap. Even then he only says ‘Quite true’ whenever I stop.”

The stranger laughed, and Debby laughed with him. That is a dangerous sound when produced by two young people in harmony; for before prudence has put on her spectacles, sympathy may have crossed the room and established an acquaintance.

Aunt Pen stirred.

“Dora, dear, will you give me my smelling salts?”

Debby obeyed; and Mrs. Carroll, having received the little silver bottle as if it were a dispatch from headquarters, favored the stranger with a brief, glazed smile intended to freeze conversation without violating good breeding.

It did not succeed. An enterprising child dropped an orange; Debby caught it with her foot; the young man rescued it from beneath the seat; the mother thanked both; and common benevolence routed ceremony.

Presently the stranger offered Debby the book.

“Would you like it? I know the rest nearly by heart.”

“Oh, no; you have not finished.”

“I can finish him another time. Dickens bears interruption better than most friends.”

Debby accepted, promising to return it at Baycliff, for he too was going there. Aunt Pen, hearing this, reconnoitred more closely.

“Perhaps you are connected with one of the hotels?” she asked.

The young man’s eyes brightened, but his face remained respectful.

“No, madam. I have business near the shore.”

“Commercial business?” pursued Aunt Pen, with the genteel air of one inquiring after the weather.

“In part.”

That settled it. He was a clerk—possibly a bookkeeper, possibly something worse, for “business” is a large cloak under which society imagines all sorts of unfashionable occupations to hide.

“My niece is not accustomed to travelling,” said Aunt Pen.

“I travel whenever I can,” Debby objected. “Last year I went to Worcester.”

Mrs. Carroll closed her eyes.

The stranger looked out of the window, but not before Debby saw the laughter he politely concealed. She liked him for concealing it and liked him still better for having it to conceal.

When the train stopped at a small station, he descended to bring water for the old gentleman, who had awakened from his snores in a thirsty and bewildered condition. He carried a parcel for the burdened mother; pacified one child with a paper boat; restored the clergyman’s escaping newspaper; and returned just as the whistle sounded.

“Useful,” thought Debby.

“Forward,” thought Aunt Pen.

“Frank Evan,” said the young man, presenting his card when they reached Baycliff and the book had still to be returned.

Mrs. Carroll accepted it between two fingers. There was no imposing title, no fashionable address, no crest—only the honest name, written plainly.

“Thank you, Mr. Evan. Dora, the carriage is waiting.”

“Good-by,” said Debby. “I shall finish the book tonight.”

“Then I shall hope to claim it tomorrow.”

Aunt Pen’s lilac ribbons trembled ominously as she led her niece away.

“My dear,” she whispered, “young women do not make appointments with unknown clerks.”

“I did not make an appointment. I borrowed a book.”

“With young men, my love, books are often only the preface.”

“Then I hope the story improves,” said Debby, and was hurried into the carriage.

---

The Oceanic House stood upon a bluff and looked down upon the Atlantic with six hundred windows, five hundred of which were shut lest the ocean should enter and disarrange the curtains. Its verandas were broad, its rooms narrow, its carpets brilliant, its bells innumerable, and its prices calculated to inspire respect.

Here society assembled every summer to escape itself in a new place. Pale mothers brought pale daughters to acquire complexions under parasols; overworked gentlemen came to rest by attending balls till two in the morning; young men who had done nothing all winter recuperated from their exertions; and children, whose natural desire was to dig, shout, paddle, climb, and become generally sandy, were restrained by nurses that they might enjoy the advantages of the seaside with propriety.

Into this elegant cage Aunt Pen introduced her wild bird.

The first difficulty was clothing. Debby, who at home could dress in seven minutes and be pretty without knowing it, now endured the rites by which a young lady is made presentable at the cost of comfort. Victorine, Mrs. Carroll’s maid, tightened, pinned, puffed, hooked, laced, and arranged her until the girl stood equipped for conquest with such a spread of skirt, such a complication of trimming, and such a quantity of ribbon that movement became a nautical undertaking.

“I feel like a ship,” said Debby, regarding herself in the glass.

“A very charming ship,” answered Aunt Pen, fitting a bracelet over her glove. “Only remember not to sail too fast.”

“I shall founder if I try.”

“And, Dora—”

“Deborah.”

“At Baycliff, you are Dora. It is softer.”

“So is dough, but I should not like to be called that.”

“My love, be serious. You will meet people here who can be of the greatest advantage to you. Do not speak of the school, or the farm, or feeding hens, or making butter.”

“What remains?”

“Music, art, books—within limits—the sea, the weather, and other people.”

“May I speak of myself?”

“Not frankly.”

Debby sighed.

Aunt Pen kissed her forehead, fearing she had been severe.

“I only want you to make a good impression.”

“Must I make it, Aunt Pen? Couldn’t I leave my face as it is and let the impression make itself?”

“Faces never make themselves in society,” said Mrs. Carroll, who understood the matter.

At dinner, Debby was introduced to the Leavenworths. Mrs. Leavenworth was a stately lady with a majestic nose and the fixed smile of one who has been photographed frequently. Her son Joseph was an heir, and therefore considered handsome by persons who examined his prospects before his profile.

Joe was twenty-four, slender, smooth, elaborately dressed, and afflicted with fashionable exhaustion. Nothing surprised him, because surprise wrinkles the forehead; nothing pleased him too much, because enthusiasm is provincial; and nothing occupied him except the labor of appearing unoccupied. He had a small mustache, a large watch-chain, and a vocabulary composed chiefly of “deused,” “awful,” “jolly,” and “what’s the use?”

Aunt Pen surveyed him as a commander might survey a fortified city with rich stores inside.

“My niece, Miss Dora Wilder,” she said.

Joe raised his eyelids slowly.

“Delighted, I’m sure. First season?”

“First seaside hotel,” replied Debby.

“Ah. You’ll find it deused slow.”

“Then why do you come?”

“Everybody comes.”

“Is that a reason?”

“Best reason in the world,” said Joe, though he had never before considered the question.

At table Debby asked for bread and milk.

Aunt Pen looked stricken.

The waiter looked doubtful, as if the Oceanic House might not recognize either article separately and could hardly be expected to combine them. Two young ladies opposite glanced at one another. Joe, however, revived sufficiently to stare.

“Is it medicinal?” he asked.

“No; I’m hungry.”

Debby said it so naturally that the word caused a small sensation. Fashionable persons may languish, nibble, decline, or be persuaded; but hunger is an agricultural emotion and should not appear at dinner.

“You are joking,” murmured Aunt Pen.

“I wish I were. Then the soup might satisfy me.”

The bread and milk arrived in a large bowl, looking honest and out of place among the silver covers. Debby ate it with evident enjoyment. Joe watched her as one might watch a new species of bird which, having escaped the glass case, persisted in singing.

“Do you always eat that sort of thing?” he asked.

“When I can get it. At home we have berries with it in summer.”

“Where is home?”

“In Northfield. We have a little farm—”

“Dora dropped her fan,” said Aunt Pen abruptly.

Debby picked it up.

“—with apple trees,” she continued, “and a brook, and six cows. I milk two when Father needs help.”

A silence followed, delicate but complete.

Joe’s jaded face brightened for the first time.

“By Jove! Do you really? How awfully jolly!”

Aunt Pen, who had expected ruin, discovered that candor occasionally possessed market value; but she resolved not to encourage the dangerous experiment.

Later, as the ladies crossed the great parlor, where groups arranged themselves upon velvet chairs as carefully as figures in a tableau, Joe attached himself to Debby. His mother approved Aunt Pen’s connections; Aunt Pen approved his mother’s investments; and the young people were therefore at liberty to discover whether they approved each other.

“Do you dance?” he asked.

“Yes, but not like this.”

“What do you dance?”

“Country dances at harvest parties. We move about.”

“That would never do here.”

“Why not?”

“No room. And one gets warm.”

“Is getting warm improper?”

“Deused uncomfortable.”

Debby looked at the couples gliding languidly beneath the chandeliers. The ladies smiled because smiling was expected; the gentlemen leaned because uprightness appeared energetic; the music sighed; the flowers drooped; and the sea, rolling magnificently in the darkness just beyond the windows, might have been a painted backdrop for all the heed paid it.

“We are like dolls in a toy shop,” she said.

“Are we?”

“Yes. Some are marked ‘handsome but cross’; some ‘thirty thousand dollars, slightly damaged’; some ‘young and foolish’; some ‘warranted gentle if well fed.’”

Joe laughed.

“What label would you wear?”

Debby considered.

“‘Country article. Strong. Useful. Color not fast.’”

“And what should mine say?”

She looked at his delicate boots, faultless waistcoat, tired eyes, and the flower he had replaced twice because it drooped the wrong way.

“‘Very ornamental. Keep from the weather.’”

Joe laughed again, louder than elegance permitted. People turned. Aunt Pen, from across the room, saw him bending toward Debby with animation and felt the joyful tremor of a general whose first cannon has struck the wall.

---

The next morning Aunt Pen ordered chocolate, toast, cutlets, fruit, and a newspaper in her room, having come to the sea for health; while Debby rose before six, escaped Victorine, put on her plainest dress, and went to find the sunrise.

Young ladies at watering places are commonly supposed to have no souls before breakfast. Their bodies may be discovered in wrappers, their hair in papers, their tempers at a low ebb, and their thoughts fixed upon the evening; but the dawn, being unfashionable, generally performs without an audience. Debby, fortunately, had not learned this rule.

She crossed the silent veranda and descended the cliff path. Night still lingered over the land, but the eastern sky had begun to pale. The ocean stretched vast and dark, breathing in long, grave murmurs, like some ancient creature waiting for the word to wake.

Debby sat upon a rock.

No band played; no waiter rattled silver; no polite voice asked whether she had heard the latest engagement. There were no diamonds but the dew, no laces but the foam, no fans but the wings of the gulls. Rank had not yet risen, money was asleep, fashion had not put on its face. The world was young, clean, and honest.

Then the light came.

A narrow flame ran along the edge of heaven; clouds that had been gray put on rose and gold; the sea received the splendor tremblingly, as if some royal marriage of earth and morning were being solemnized upon its broad altar. One by one the waves lifted shining heads and hurried shoreward bearing the news. Even the little pools among the rocks caught the glory and held a fragment of sunrise in their shallow hearts—a consoling arrangement of nature’s, which gives small things their share of heaven if they are only open to receive it.

Debby watched with clasped hands. The mischief left her face, though not its brightness; and for a little while she was neither poor niece, possible heiress-hunter, schoolmistress, nor Miss Dora, but simply a grateful creature in a beautiful world.

“Good morning,” said a voice behind her.

Frank Evan stood on the path, hat in hand.

Debby started, then smiled.

“I thought nobody else would be awake.”

“Books and sunrises are two temptations I seldom resist.”

“I brought your book.” She took it from her pocket. “I finished it after Aunt Pen went to sleep.”

“And did you like the end?”

“I liked the truth of it. People are punished by being themselves, aren’t they? The selfish people have to live with their selfishness, and the good ones find one another.”

“Eventually,” said Frank.

There was enough meaning in the word to make Debby examine a shell at her feet.

They walked along the beach. In the broad morning light Frank’s coat appeared no newer, but his face seemed better. It is curious how a person’s clothes may shout in a parlor and become silent under the sky. Beside sea and sunrise, fine cloth loses much of its argument; a man must bring some other credential.

“What is your business near the shore?” asked Debby. “Aunt Pen thinks you keep accounts.”

“I do sometimes.”

“In a shop?”

“In several shops, indirectly. My uncle owns mills inland. I am studying their machinery and the lives of the people who tend it. We mean to make improvements.”

“Then you are not a clerk?”

“I am when clerking is needed.”

“Are you rich?”

Frank laughed.

“That is direct.”

“Aunt Pen says direct questions are improper, but indirect ones seem to take so much longer.”

“I have enough to work with, not enough to be idle upon.”

“That sounds like exactly the right quantity.”

“So I think. My uncle hopes I shall learn to manage the mills after him; I hope to make them places where work need not ruin the worker. Between his profit and my principles we have many interesting battles.”

Debby’s eyes warmed.

“At our school, the mill children are always the sleepiest. Some come without breakfast.”

“I know.”

He said the two words quietly, but Debby heard in them more than Joe had expressed by all his deuses and jollities.

They began skipping stones. Debby’s first flew in six silver leaps; Frank’s plunged at once.

“You read too much,” she told him. “Your wrist is educated beyond usefulness.”

“Try again,” he said.

His second stone made eight skips.

“You improve under criticism.”

“I have an excellent teacher.”

So they went on, talking of books, work, children, fields, and the oddities of the world. Debby forgot that she was expected to captivate an heir; Frank forgot, perhaps, that plain young men should not walk at sunrise with pretty young women; and the sea kept their secret with that vast discretion which has covered deeper matters than a beginning fondness.

At eight o’clock Aunt Pen appeared at the top of the path, equipped for morning calls in gray silk, a lace shawl, and alarm.

She had discovered Debby’s empty room, questioned Victorine, searched the verandas, and imagined in succession abduction, drowning, elopement, and freckles. The last possibility hastened her steps.

“Dora!”

Debby looked up.

Aunt Pen descended with all sails set.

“My dear child! I have been wretched. Mr.—Mr. Evan, good morning.”

Frank bowed.

“We were watching the sunrise,” said Debby.

“So I perceive.”

Aunt Pen’s tone implied that the sunrise had compromised them both.

“It was very grand, Aunt Pen. You should come tomorrow.”

“I never rise before seven unless there is a fire.”

“The sky looked as if there were one.”

“My dear, your shoes are wet.”

Frank took his leave, promising nothing and thereby alarming Aunt Pen more than if he had promised much.

As soon as he was beyond hearing, she turned upon Debby.

“What possessed you?”

“The morning.”

“You walked alone with that young man.”

“We met by accident.”

“Accidents are the very things a prudent woman avoids.”

“Then how did you manage my coming to live with you?” asked Debby, for Aunt Pen had often called that event the happiest accident of her life.

Mrs. Carroll ignored this ingratitude.

“Mr. Leavenworth inquired for you at breakfast.”

“Did he?”

“He is to drive us this afternoon.”

“Must I go?”

“Certainly.”

Debby looked back at Frank’s figure, already small against the glittering shore.

“Very well,” she said; but youth’s obedience, when spoken with that degree of meekness, is a flag under which rebellion often marches comfortably.

---

Joe’s carriage was yellow, high, polished, and perilous. His horses had more spirit than their master and knew it. Aunt Pen sat in the rear beneath a parasol, Debby beside her, and Joe drove with his elbows arranged to show that the turn of a wrist, rather than attention, governed the animals.

“You ride, Miss Wilder?” he asked.

“Anything that lets me.”

“A lady’s horse, of course.”

“Our mare has no opinion about ladies.”

“Dora is joking,” said Aunt Pen.

“Dora is suffocating,” said Debby, loosening one glove. “Why must we wear all this upon a hot day?”

“Because other people do,” Joe replied.

“That answer again! Suppose other people wore blankets in July?”

“They might if Worth made them.”

“What is Worth?”

“A man who tells women what they ought to look like.”

“I should rather ask a looking-glass.”

Joe smiled indulgently. He liked Debby’s odd speeches because he could repeat them later and appear to have discovered a new amusement. She was unlike the young ladies he knew, who treated him with the delicate attention given to an expensive object liable to be purchased.

“Do you like Baycliff better today?” he asked.

“Much better before breakfast.”

“What happened then?”

“I saw the sunrise.”

“By Jove! Does it rise here?”

“Every morning, I believe.”

“Never saw it. Too early.”

“You miss a great deal by being tired.”

“I’m not tired exactly. Only bored.”

“Have you tried doing anything?”

Joe looked puzzled.

“I drive, dance, sail, play billiards.”

“For somebody besides yourself, I mean.”

The question was so novel that he nearly dropped the reins.

“What could I do?”

“Carry a parcel; teach a child; mend something; find a person who is lonelier than you and make him happier.”

“That sounds like work.”

“It is.”

“Awful word.”

“Excellent thing.”

Aunt Pen tapped Debby with the parasol. It was not in the plan that the heiress-hunter should offer moral instruction to the heir.

They stopped at a pavilion where the fashionable world drank mineral water with expressions of noble endurance. Here Debby met Miss Arabella St. Clair, a slender lady who had cultivated delicacy until she could not cross a room without assistance.

“My dear Miss Wilder,” Arabella murmured, “how can you endure this dreadful breeze?”

“I like it.”

“Really? It destroys the complexion.”

“It improves mine.”

“But you are so brown already.”

“Yes. I keep my complexion outside, where people can see it.”

Arabella considered this vulgar but could not find an answer.

Aunt Pen began speaking loudly of Debby’s music, which consisted of honest singing and uncertain piano-playing. Mrs. Leavenworth appeared. Joe offered Debby mineral water; she tasted it, made a face, and whispered that the cows’ drinking trough at home had more flavor. Unfortunately, Mrs. Leavenworth heard.

The majestic nose rose half an inch.

Debby, seeing Aunt Pen’s distress, attempted repair.

“It is very strengthening, I dare say.”

Mrs. Leavenworth inclined her head.

“So are many disagreeable things,” Debby added.

Joe choked into his glass, and the repair became a wreck.

Yet, strange to relate, he admired her more and more. Vanity tires even of incense if every worshipper swings the same censer; Debby’s refusal to kneel refreshed him. He followed her, listened when she spoke, and exerted himself sufficiently to bring a chair without being asked. Aunt Pen marked these signs with hope.

That evening Joe sent flowers.

“They are lovely,” said Debby, burying her face in them.

“You must wear the roses,” said Aunt Pen. “They will suit the white dress.”

“May I give the lilies to the sick lady across the hall?”

“Certainly not. Mr. Leavenworth sent them to you.”

“There are enough for a funeral.”

“Deborah!”

“In the country we give flowers away after we have enjoyed them. They do not become less beautiful.”

“They become less significant.”

Debby selected one rose for her hair and carried the lilies to the sick lady, who had watched the sea for three days from a sofa and received no visitors except a nurse. The old woman cried over them, because loneliness makes small kindnesses very large.

Aunt Pen scolded; Debby kissed her; and Victorine whispered to the chambermaid that Mademoiselle was either a saint or a savage, she did not know which.

---

A week passed, and the campaign advanced with alternate victories and reverses.

Joe danced with Debby, drove with her, lounged beside her, and once read half a page of poetry because she asked him. Aunt Pen encouraged these prodigious exertions. She placed chairs advantageously, lost shawls at convenient moments, developed headaches before moonlight walks, and exercised a military genius which, applied to national affairs, might have ended several wars by marrying the opposing commanders to suitable young women.

Frank appeared less often but was never forgotten. Sometimes Debby met him in the reading room, where he opened a book and made it a door into a larger world. Sometimes he walked beside Aunt Pen and Debby upon the promenade, enduring Mrs. Carroll’s inquiries with such cheerful respect that she could not dislike him so thoroughly as principle demanded. Once he repaired the wheel of a child’s cart with his pocketknife. Once he rowed out in rough water to assist an overturned sailboat. Once he spent an afternoon with a crippled boy who sold shells beneath the cliff.

These services, being unadvertised, made little noise; but Debby heard of them. Good deeds have an awkward habit of escaping concealment, just as bad ones do, though society is more industrious in circulating the latter.

Aunt Pen also learned that Frank’s “business” concerned mills and that his uncle possessed considerable property. Her manner softened perceptibly until she discovered that much of Frank’s own income was spent on schools, reading rooms, and improvements for workmen.

“Philanthropy!” she exclaimed. “The most expensive hobby a young man can have.”

“Yachts cost more,” said Debby.

“Yachts can be sold.”

“So can principles, but I should not value the man who sold them.”

Mrs. Carroll gazed at her niece.

“You are becoming attached to Mr. Evan.”

“I think he is good.”

“That was not my question.”

“No,” said Debby, turning red. “That is why I answered it.”

Aunt Pen had no opportunity to pursue the matter, for Joe arrived in a boating jacket containing enough braid to equip an admiral. He proposed an excursion to the bathing beach the next morning. Mrs. Leavenworth would go, Arabella would go, several agreeable young men would go, and there would be a tent, luncheon, music, and surf.

“Capital,” said Aunt Pen. “Dora adores bathing.”

“I adore water,” corrected Debby. “Bathing at Baycliff appears to involve mostly clothes.”

This was true. The bathing dress of the period seemed invented to test whether a woman could enter the sea carrying a small household’s supply of wool. Aunt Pen’s costume alone consisted of trousers, skirt, tunic, belt, collar, cap, shoes, ribbons, and modesty; the last article, though invisible, was the heaviest.

“I shall not go in deep,” she announced. “Salt water is ruinous to the hair.”

“The ocean is dreadfully inconsiderate,” said Debby.

Frank had not been invited, a circumstance Aunt Pen regarded as providential. Debby said nothing, but when she went to bed she placed *Martin Chuzzlewit* beneath her pillow—not because it was comfortable, but because young women, however sensible by daylight, occasionally develop symbolic habits after ten o’clock.

---

The morning of the bathing party was blue, brilliant, and full of laughter. The sea spread itself like a mighty holiday-maker beneath the sun, tossing up white caps, racing along the sand, shouting against the rocks, and inviting every creature with blood enough to answer.

The company descended from the bathing house in costumes which abolished beauty, dignity, rank, and much of the distinction between the sexes. Here nature began her democratic work.

No complexion retained its art when the spray struck it; no curl preserved its discipline; no fine gentleman could maintain indifference while a wave turned him heels over head; no languid lady remembered to droop when cold water seized her by the waist. Stout dowagers skipped, solemn fathers squealed, elegant youths floundered, timid girls clutched strangers, and children, wiser than all the rest, surrendered themselves to delight.

The sea received them without examining their cards.

Debby ran in laughing. Released from tight shoes, trailing skirts, gloves, and drawing-room expectations, she became her natural self at once. She sprang over the first wave, dived through the second, and came up shining, with hair loose and eyes alight. Joe followed cautiously, protesting that the water was “deused cold,” though the day was warm enough to melt a moralist.

“Come farther!” cried Debby.

“Undertow,” said Joe.

“There is none.”

“Sharks.”

“Not here.”

“Cramp.”

“You have not moved enough to cramp.”

Stung by this reflection upon his courage, Joe advanced. A wave struck him amidships and removed his cap. Debby caught it, planted it backward on his head, and laughed until another wave punished her impertinence.

Upon the shore Aunt Pen stood ankle-deep, holding Mrs. Leavenworth’s arm and uttering little cries whenever the Atlantic approached. She had left her spectacles, dignity, and most of her confidence in the bathing house, but had retained a handsome set of artificial teeth, without which she never willingly appeared before the world.

“Penelope, we must go farther,” declared Mrs. Leavenworth, whose majestic spirit was roused by seeing Arabella borne past in the arms of two young men.

“I find this quite sufficient.”

“Nonsense. The benefit lies in immersion.”

Mrs. Leavenworth pulled. Aunt Pen resisted. A wave, observing this contest, settled it in the sea’s favor.

It came rolling in, green-backed and jovial, lifted both ladies, parted them, filled their ears, flattened their caps, and seated Aunt Pen abruptly upon the sand.

She arose dripping and astonished.

“Are you hurt?” cried Debby, hurrying toward her.

Aunt Pen opened her mouth to answer, then shut it with extraordinary haste. Her eyes widened. One hand flew to her lips; the other searched the water. Horror, shame, and salt water struggled upon her countenance.

“What is it?” asked Debby.

Mrs. Carroll bent close and whispered through compressed lips.

“My teeth!”

“What?”

“My teeth are gone.”

Debby stared, then heroically swallowed the laugh that leaped upward.

“Both sets?”

“The upper.”

This was a calamity beyond ordinary speech. A lost bracelet may be mourned, a lost shoe explained, a lost reputation disputed; but lost teeth, especially when the owner stands publicly in a wet flannel dress, demand the fortitude of a Roman matron.

“Don’t speak,” Debby whispered. “I’ll find them.”

She knelt in the foam and searched. Joe joined her when told that Aunt Pen had lost “something valuable,” but the precise nature of the treasure was mercifully withheld.

“What does it look like?” he asked.

Debby coughed.

“Small—pinkish—curved.”

“A shell?”

“Something like one.”

Joe summoned two bathing attendants. Arabella summoned another young man, though she had no need of him. Mrs. Leavenworth, learning the truth, froze into sympathetic grandeur and stood before Aunt Pen like a screen, while the afflicted lady kept one handkerchief pressed to her mouth and regarded the ocean as a personal enemy.

The search widened.

Every receding wave revealed pebbles, ribbons of weed, shells, bits of wood, and one spoon; but no dental treasure. Children joined the hunt with enthusiasm. A stout gentleman stepped on a crab. Joe lost his cap again. Aunt Pen’s eyes filled with tears, for though vanity is comic when observed from a safe distance, it hurts its owner quite sincerely.

Debby ceased to laugh inwardly.

“Never mind, Auntie,” she said, putting an arm about her. “We’ll go back. Nobody need know.”

“Everybody knows,” murmured Aunt Pen indistinctly.

This was unfortunately near the truth.

At that moment a tall figure came along the upper beach carrying shoes in one hand and a book in the other. Frank Evan had walked from the fishing village and paused at the extraordinary spectacle: half the Oceanic House bent double in the shallows, while the other half offered advice from shore.

Debby saw him and colored.

“Mr. Evan!”

He approached.

“Has Neptune demanded tribute?”

Joe answered before she could.

“Mrs. Carroll has lost an ornament.”

Aunt Pen made a despairing sound.

Debby drew Frank aside and told him. To his honor, neither smile nor surprise disturbed his face.

“Where was she standing?”

Debby showed him.

He studied the run of the water, asked when the loss occurred, and walked twenty yards down the beach.

Joe regarded these proceedings skeptically.

“Current’s the other way, my dear fellow.”

“The surface current is,” said Frank.

He removed his coat, rolled his sleeves, and waded in. For several minutes he searched with his hands, letting each wave pass before examining the sand it uncovered. Then he disappeared beneath the water.

Aunt Pen clutched Debby.

“He’ll drown!”

“He swims well.”

“He may step on them!”

This second danger proved more affecting than the first.

Frank rose, shook the water from his eyes, and dived again. The company watched in reverent silence. Tragedy had become adventure; adventure, spectacle; and spectacle is the natural food of an idle crowd.

Once he brought up a pale shell, causing Aunt Pen to tremble. Once a bit of coral deceived Debby. On the third descent he remained under so long that Joe declared it “deused reckless” and took two steps forward, prepared to rescue him from a safe depth.

Then Frank emerged, laughing, with one hand closed.

“Is this the missing ornament?”

In his palm lay Aunt Pen’s teeth, gleaming with a ghastly cheerfulness in the sun.

A murmur of triumph arose from the spectators. One child clapped. The stout gentleman cried “Bravo!” Joe said “By Jove!” Mrs. Leavenworth turned away with the stateliness appropriate to mysteries of the toilette.

Frank rinsed the rescued object discreetly, wrapped it in his handkerchief, and presented it to Debby as solemnly as if it were a royal jewel recovered from pirates.

“Please restore the property to its owner.”

Debby’s eyes danced, but they were soft with gratitude.

“How did you find them?”

“The undertow carried them down and lodged them beside a stone.”

“I should never have thought of that.”

“I have lost less precious things in worse places.”

Aunt Pen received the parcel and Frank’s bow. Unable to thank him with her customary fluency, she pressed his hand with both of hers and attempted a smile which, under the circumstances, was touching rather than ornamental.

He understood.

“Not a word is necessary, madam.”

This delicacy completed what courage had begun. Mrs. Carroll, who could forgive poverty more readily than ridicule, had feared that the young man would laugh. Because he did not, she suddenly perceived that his coat was merely old, his glove honorably mended, his face intelligent, and his manners those of a gentleman—not the ballroom species, which blooms in evening dress and expires under inconvenience, but the hardier kind that keeps its breeding when another person has lost hers with her teeth.

Joe came up, wet, handsome, and slightly aggrieved.

“Lucky find, Evan.”

“Very.”

“I was looking in that direction.”

“Were you?” said Debby.

Joe heard the mischief in her tone and frowned. It was the first time his vanity had suffered in her service, and vanity, though willing to pay for flowers, resents a bill presented in courage.

Aunt Pen was escorted to the bathing house. The others returned to the water, but Debby remained behind.

Frank stood wringing out his sleeves.

“You have spoiled your clothes,” she said.

“They were not good enough to spoil.”

“That is what Aunt Pen thought.”

“And what do you think?”

Debby looked at the soaked shirt, the discarded coat, the steady gray eyes, and the hand which had just performed an absurd service with perfect kindness.

“I think clothes tell the truth about their wearers only after a storm.”

Frank smiled.

“And what do mine say now?”

“That you do not mind getting wet for a friend.”

“Am I a friend?”

“Yes.”

The word was simple; her manner made it precious.

Behind them the sea continued its benevolent mischief. It had shaken Aunt Pen’s artificial dignity, washed the languor from Joe, stripped the company of pose, and brought a certain plainly dressed young man forward to be measured by action instead of tailoring. Nature, when admitted into society, is apt to overturn the furniture; but she sometimes sets the people right.

Frank and Debby walked slowly toward the bathing house.

“You will leave Baycliff soon?” she asked.

“In three days.”

“So soon?”

“My work begins again.”

She was silent, drawing a line in the wet sand with one bare foot.

“Will you be sorry?” he asked.

“For Aunt Pen?”

“For me.”

Debby glanced toward the door where her aunt had vanished, then toward Joe, who was explaining to Arabella how nearly he had ventured into deep water.

“Yes,” she said. “But I am glad you have work to go to.”

“And I am sorry you must return to yours.”

“I’m not. The children need me.”

“Then I am glad too.”

This was not a declaration. No hands were taken; no vows disturbed the gulls; no violins began behind the bathing house. Yet something was understood between them which Aunt Pen’s strategy, Joe’s fortune, and all Baycliff’s rules could not easily undo.

When Mrs. Carroll reappeared, restored in feature and greatly humbled in spirit, she found Frank waiting with her shawl. He offered it without a trace of amusement.

“Mr. Evan,” she said with renewed distinctness, “you must dine with us tonight.”

Frank bowed.

“I shall be very happy.”

Joe heard the invitation and stared. Debby looked at Aunt Pen. Aunt Pen looked at Debby. The experienced general had not surrendered; she was merely revising the map.

“Of course,” she added, “we owe you a debt.”

Frank’s eyes met Debby’s.

“It has already been repaid.”

Aunt Pen did not ask how. She had learned during the past half hour that there are questions which require tact, and truths which, though unprofitable in the market, may be worth possessing.

That evening Debby wore white, but not Joe’s rose. She placed a spray of wild beach flowers in her hair—small, hardy blossoms that grew without cultivation, endured salt wind, and looked freshest where finer flowers would have perished.

Aunt Pen noticed them and said nothing.

Joe arrived in faultless clothes and spent much of dinner recounting the adventure as if he had directed the rescue. Frank came in his old coat, carefully dried and brushed. Mrs. Leavenworth was majestic; Arabella delicate; the young ladies ornamental; the gentlemen agreeably useless. The Oceanic House glittered, the actors took their places, and society resumed its play.

But Debby no longer mistook the costumes for the characters.

Across the flowers and lights, Frank smiled at her; and beyond the six hundred windows the free sea rolled under the moon, laughing softly to itself, as if it knew the end of the story and approved.

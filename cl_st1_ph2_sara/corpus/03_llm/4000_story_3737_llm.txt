The summer twilight had settled softly upon the old farmhouse. Beyond the porch, the fields were losing their green in the dusk, and the first stars showed above the black line of the pines. We sat in the cooling air while Aunt Rachel moved between the kitchen and the steps, laughing whenever she spoke, and often when nobody had spoken at all.

She was sixty years old, tall, broad-shouldered, and strong yet. Her face seemed made for cheerfulness. Even when she scolded the dog from the doorway, she did it with such grandeur that the dog wagged his tail, believing himself praised.

At last, in the careless manner of one who has never earned the right to ask such a question, I said:

“Aunt Rachel, how is it that you have lived sixty years and never had any trouble?”

Her laugh stopped.

She stood in the doorway, black against the stars, with one hand upon the frame. Then she came slowly out, folded her arms, and looked down at me.

“Misto C——,” she said, “is you in earnest?”

I confessed that I was.

“Well, sah,” she answered, and her voice was low now, “I’s had no trouble. No trouble ’t all.”

She sat upon the step. For a little while she said nothing. Then she leaned forward, clasped her knees, and began.

“I was born in ole Virginia, sah, an’ I was born a slave. My mammy was a mighty proud woman. She couldn’t read no book, an’ she didn’t own so much as de shoes on her feet, but she walked straight, an’ when anybody crowded her she put her fists on her hips—jist so—and she say, ‘You better take care how you meddles wid one o’ de Blue Hen’s Chickens!’

“What dat mean? I never rightly knowed. Maybe she never knowed. But when Mammy said it, folks moved.

“I growed up big an’ strong, an’ bymeby I married. My man’s name was Isaac. He was quiet, but he had a heart in him. An’ den de chil’en come—one, two, three—Lord, dey kept a-comin’ till I had seven.

“Seven, sah! Five boys an’ two little gals. I raised every one. I worked all day, an’ half de night I was patchin’ clothes or coolin’ fever or rockin’ a baby. But I was happy. Poor folks can be happy, even when dey belongs to somebody else, if dey got one another.

“Henry was de youngest. Dat boy follow me like my shadow. If I stirred de ash cake, Henry got flour on his nose. If I went to de spring, Henry carried de cup. He had a scar round his wrist where a kettle-hook caught him, an’ another little white mark up here—”

She touched the middle of her forehead.

“—where he fell against de hearthstone. I used to kiss dat mark when I put him down to sleep.

“Well, sah, our master was a kind enough man when money was plenty. But money went. Crops failed, debts come, an’ men rode up to de house talkin’ low. Bymeby we heard we was all to be sold.

“I prayed it wasn’t true. I got down on my knees an’ prayed till de boards was wet. But prayer don’t always stop de auction man.

“One mornin’ dey drove us to town. Dey washed us, rubbed grease on our faces, an’ stood us up on a platform. Men come pokin’ at us, lookin’ in our mouths, squeezin’ arms, askin’ ages, same as we was horses.

“I held Henry. Isaac stood close on one side, an’ de other six crowded round my skirt. I kept sayin’, ‘We’s one family. Whoever buys, buy us together.’

“An’ de auction man laughed.

“First dey sold Isaac.

“When dey called him, I caught his coat. He held my hand, but a man struck his arm an’ broke us loose. Isaac turned his head while dey led him off. He never cried out. He jist looked at me.

“I ain’t never forgot dat look.

“Den dey took my oldest boy. Den de next. Den one of my gals. Dey went one by one, each to a different man. I screamed till my throat tore. I threw myself on de platform, an’ wrapped both arms round de little ones, but dey kicked my hands loose.

“Henry clung here.”

She seized the front of her dress and drew it tight against her breast.

“He cried, ‘Mammy, don’t let ’em take me!’

“I said, ‘Dey shan’t! Dey got to kill me first!’

“But dey didn’t have to kill me, sah. Four men held me. One pulled Henry loose. He reached both hands toward me, an’ I could see dat scar round his wrist. Dey carried him away screamin’ my name.

“Den somebody bought me.”

The night insects hummed in the grass. Aunt Rachel sat still, staring beyond us.

“All my seven chil’en,” she said. “All gone. My husband gone. An’ me sold south.

“Dey put chains on some of us an’ marched us down de road. I fought at first. I bit a man’s hand. He struck me here.”

She touched her cheek.

“But bymeby I quit fightin’, ’cause my body was walkin’ an’ my mind was back on dat platform. Every little child I heard cry, I thought it was Henry. Every wagon behind us, I thought maybe Isaac was in it.

“I was took to New Bern. I cooked, washed, scrubbed, an’ worked. I asked everybody dat traveled if dey’d seen my family. I asked sailors, traders, drovers, preachers. I told ’em seven names till folks was tired of hearin’ me. Once somebody heard of a boy like my eldest over in Tennessee, but it wasn’t him. Once a woman said she’d seen my daughter in Georgia, but when I got word sent, dat girl had died.

“Maybe it was mine. Maybe it wasn’t. A slave mother don’t always get to know where her child is buried.

“Years went. Ten. Fifteen. Twenty-two years, sah. I counted every one.

“Den de war come.

“Guns sounded far off, den nearer. White folks whispered. Men went away with horses, an’ women hid silver. Bymeby Union soldiers come marchin’ into town, blue coats all dusty, flags above ’em. De house where I worked was took for officers, an’ I stayed to cook.

“Every new regiment dat come, I’d ask: ‘Any of you gentlemen been up North? Ever see a colored man name Henry? Tall, maybe, though he was little when I knowed him. Scar round his wrist, scar on his forehead?’

“Some laughed. Some was kind. One officer wrote de name in a book. But nobody knowed him.

“Now, Henry—though I didn’t know it den—had been sold farther south, but he ran away when he was nearly grown. He traveled nights, hid days, crossed rivers, an’ got North. He learned barberin’. Made wages. Wore good clothes. But he kept huntin’ for me.

“When de colored troops was raised, Henry joined. He didn’t join only for de war. He said, ‘If de army go South, I go wid it. Somewhere down dere is my mammy.’

“One night de soldiers had a dance in my kitchen. I told ’em I had bread to set, but dey pushed de table back an’ brought in a fiddle. Round an’ round dey went, boots stampin’, girls laughin’, pans rattlin’ on de wall.

“I stood by de stove, madder every minute.

“‘Out!’ I says. ‘Every one of you! Dis kitchen ain’t no ballroom!’

“A young colored soldier laughed an’ kept dancin’.

“‘Auntie,’ he says, ‘you too old to be cross.’

“I come out from de stove, slow. I stood up straight. I put my fists on my hips—jist as I’s doin’ now—and I says, ‘Boy, take care how you meddles wid one o’ de Blue Hen’s Chickens!’

“Dat young soldier stopped.

“Stopped dead, sah.

“De fiddle kept scratchin’, an’ de others went roun’ an’ roun’ an’ roun’, but he stood lookin’ at me like he’d seen a ghost.

“‘Who taught you dat?’ he says.

“‘None o’ your business,’ says I. ‘Get out my kitchen.’

“He went. I thought I’d settled him.

“Next mornin’ I was bent over de stove, openin’ de door—jist so. I heard somebody behind me, but I didn’t turn.

“‘Auntie,’ a voice says.

“‘Breakfast ain’t ready.’

“‘Auntie, where was you born?’

“I slammed de stove. ‘Virginia.’

“‘What was your mammy’s name?’

“Den I turned. It was dat same young soldier. He was trembling.

“I says, ‘What you want wid my mammy?’

“He says, ‘Did she use to call herself one of de Blue Hen’s Chickens?’

“My knees went weak.

“I looked at him. He was tall, with a soldier cap in his hand. He had whiskers. His face was a man’s face. There wasn’t nothin’ of my little boy in it at first.

“‘What’s your name?’ I whispered.

“He says, ‘Henry.’

“I caught his arm.

“‘Show me your wrist.’

“He pulled back his sleeve. There it was—the pale scar curling round it.

“I grabbed his face between my hands. ‘Your forehead!’

“He bent down.

“Under his hair was de little white mark.

“Then I knowed him.

“My Henry! My baby dat was tore out of my arms! My little boy come back a man!”

Aunt Rachel sprang to her feet. She reached into the darkness as if that distant kitchen stood immediately before her and her son were bending there again.

“I laid my hands on his head,” she cried, “an’ I gazed an’ gazed. I couldn’t speak. Den I hollered, ‘De Lord God of heaven be praised! Dey took seven, but He’s give me one back!’

“Henry caught me before I fell. He held me like I used to hold him. Soldiers come runnin’, officers come, cooks come, but I didn’t see none of ’em. I saw his wrist. I saw his forehead. I heard him say, ‘Mammy, I been huntin’ you all dese years.’

“An’ I says, ‘Henry, I never quit waitin’.’

“We cried. We laughed. We shouted till de whole house rang. I cooked no breakfast dat mornin’. Let de officers starve! One of de Blue Hen’s Chickens had found her own.”

Her great laugh broke out then, but it shook and failed, and she covered her eyes with both hands.

After a while she sat down once more. The fields were wholly dark now, and the stars seemed very near.

I could think of nothing to say. My foolish question returned to me, and I was ashamed of it.

Aunt Rachel lowered her hands. Her face was wet, but she smiled.

“So you see, Misto C——,” she said softly, “I hain’t had no trouble.”

She looked out into the night where six lost children remained beyond all searching.

Then she added:

“An’ no joy!”

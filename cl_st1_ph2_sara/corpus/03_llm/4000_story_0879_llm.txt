### THE TWO LIVES OF MADEMOISELLE GAUTIER

Among the papers of Duclos—those miscellaneous bundles in which an anecdote concerning a duchess is often folded within the account of a lawsuit, and a philosophical reflection lies buried beneath the bill of a wine-merchant—there exists a manuscript of no great length, endorsed in his own hand: *Confession of Mademoiselle Gautier, afterwards Sister Augustine of the Carmelites of Lyons*. The document is not wholly autograph. Certain pages appear to have been copied by an ecclesiastic; others, more animated, crowded, and irregular, bear every sign of having been written by the lady herself. Duclos added a few observations in the margin, sometimes charitable, sometimes malicious, and once so illegible that we cannot determine whether he wrote *contrition* or *contradiction*. In the case of Mademoiselle Gautier, either word would have been suitable.

She was, in the earlier part of the eighteenth century, among the most admired actresses of Paris. Her beauty was of that brilliant species which requires lights, music, jewels, and a sufficiently numerous public to attain its full authority. She possessed, besides, a fine voice, an excellent memory, an imperious temper, much wit, and more generosity than prudence. She earned considerable sums, spent larger ones, relieved poor comedians, entertained rich financiers, quarrelled with women who envied her, forgave men who deceived her, and lived, in short, as though youth were a permanent income.

To describe her conduct by the polite word *dissipation* is perhaps to treat both language and morality with indulgence; yet there was nothing mean in her disorders. She sinned magnificently, and, what is less usual, paid her debts. Her house was frequented by persons of rank, actors, abbés, musicians, officers, and those opulent gentlemen whose names seldom appear in memoirs except under initials. She could charm a prince, silence an insolent author, and send a hundred livres to a superannuated prompter before breakfast. Such qualities do not constitute virtue, but the student of human nature will hesitate to pronounce them wholly useless preparations for it.

The decisive circumstance of her life was her attachment to Quinault Dufresne. He was handsome, gifted, agreeable upon the stage, and sufficiently accustomed to admiration to accept a woman’s heart with less astonishment than gratitude. Mademoiselle Gautier loved him with a violence which surprised even her, for she had previously believed herself incapable of suffering from any passion that could not be dismissed by changing a carriage, a gown, or a supper-party. She desired marriage. Dufresne did not.

It would be easy to represent her subsequent conversion as the direct consequence of this disappointment; it would also be easy, and perhaps equally presumptuous, to deny the operation of religious feeling because grief prepared the heart in which it acted. Mademoiselle Gautier herself attributed everything to Providence. Duclos, who had a philosopher’s preference for secondary causes, placed a line beneath the name of Dufresne. We shall leave the line where he drew it and allow the reader to decide whether Heaven may not sometimes employ an actor as its unconscious missionary.

The following narrative, slightly abridged where the writer repeats her scruples, is substantially her own.

---

I was born with an inclination to command and without any talent for obeying. My mother perceived the first quality early and did not live long enough to correct the second. I entered the theatre when I was very young, succeeded almost immediately, and concluded, with the good sense natural to nineteen years, that Providence had created the world chiefly to applaud me.

I was not wicked from calculation. I did not say, “Here is innocence; let me destroy it,” or, “Here is a duty; let me violate it.” I followed pleasure because it came dressed as pleasure, and fled from restraint because it had the bad taste to call itself restraint. I loved music, jewels, fine linen, well-served suppers, compliments when they were ingenious, and even compliments when they were not. I loved to be looked at. I loved to enter a box after the curtain had risen and hear the whisper pass through the house. If this was vanity, I possessed it; if it was a crime, I confess it.

Yet I was not happy.

When I first loved Dufresne, I believed I had at last discovered the difference between amusement and happiness. I had been admired; I wished to be preferred. I had received oaths; I wished to receive one which the Church would hear. I had occupied the thoughts of many men; I wished to belong honestly to one.

He laughed at marriage at first, because he imagined I laughed also. When he saw that I did not, he employed tenderness, reason, delay, and silence—the four resources of a man who wishes neither to grant a woman’s request nor to lose her affection. At last I compelled him to speak plainly.

“You know my heart,” he said.

“I know your heart,” I replied, “but I ask for your name.”

“My name would add nothing to what is between us.”

“It would add everything that is not between us.”

He walked to the window. I remember that it rained, and that a coach passed, splashing mud upon a lackey’s white stockings. Such trifles remain fixed in the mind when great hopes are being killed.

“I cannot marry you,” he said.

“Cannot?”

“I will not.”

This was at least clear. I thanked him, because pride will perform the strangest civilities while the heart is bleeding, and I did not shed a tear until I was alone.

From that hour I became melancholy. I continued to act; indeed, I was applauded more than before. Grief improved my tragic expression, and the public, which had no knowledge of the cause, praised the effect. I continued to receive company. I wore rouge, diamonds, and a silver gown which became famous. I laughed loudly, supped late, and returned home at dawn with a weariness which seemed to have entered the soul through the body.

Then my past life appeared before me, not continuously, but in sudden illuminations. A jest recalled a falsehood; a song recalled a betrayal; the sight of a poor woman kneeling in a church recalled money wasted at play. I would say, “Tomorrow I shall reform.” Tomorrow came wearing feathers, bringing a new comedy, and asking me to dine.

On the 27th of August, the day upon which I completed my thirty-first year, I awoke after a night of agitation. My servants entered with flowers, sweetmeats, letters, and a little dog tied with a rose-coloured ribbon, sent by a gentleman whom I had not seen for six weeks. I looked at all these tokens of affection and felt so entirely abandoned that I ordered them removed.

At eleven o’clock I went out alone. I had intended to visit a milliner. Instead, upon passing a church, I entered it.

Why? I cannot say. There was no preacher at the door, no friend beside me, no thunder in the sky. The morning was clear, and I was wearing a new mantle trimmed with lace. I had not gone ten paces into the nave before I thought: “What are you doing here?”

Another voice answered: “What have you been doing elsewhere?”

I knelt.

Mass had begun. An old woman near me counted her beads; a child stared at my earrings; two market women whispered; and I, who had played queens before crowded houses without trembling, could scarcely breathe. When the priest elevated the Host, all my life seemed to fall at once upon my conscience. I saw my pride, my ingratitude, my impurity, my hard speeches, my squandered hours. I bowed my head and said inwardly:

“Lord, if Thou canst still endure me, I am here.”

I wept so violently that the old woman offered me her handkerchief. It smelled of onions, but I received it as though it had been a relic.

I left the church changed—or believing myself changed, which at first amounts to nearly the same thing. I sent for an abbé whom I knew. Unfortunately, he had often supped at my house, and I could not persuade myself that a man who had eaten my carp with such appetite was qualified to direct my conscience. He listened, praised my good intention, advised moderation, and asked whether I still employed the cook who made the green sauce. I dismissed him with twenty-four livres and sought another confessor.

For six months I led two lives. Every morning I went to mass; every evening, or nearly every evening, I returned to society. I prayed at dawn and played cards at midnight. I gave alms and bought pearls. I fasted until dinner and then ate enough to support two penitents. My conscience cried, “Leave the world!” My habits answered, “After the next performance.” My friends told me excess was dangerous in everything, especially in religion, by which they meant that religion was harmless so long as it altered nothing.

This condition became intolerable. Pleasure had lost its savour, but not its power. I resembled a woman seated at a banquet after learning that every dish is poisoned, who nevertheless continues to taste the sauces from politeness.

At last I made a general confession. It required three days, though I had prepared notes. I accused myself without disguise, except perhaps in matters touching my appearance, where I observed that beauty had exposed me to temptations without adding that I had assisted beauty by every art then known in Paris. My confessor, Father Martin, was grave and gentle. When I had finished, he remained silent.

“Well, Father?” I said.

“My child, you have spoken of many persons.”

“I have injured many.”

“You have also been injured.”

“That is their confession, not mine.”

This answer pleased him more than it pleased me, for I had made it impatiently. He advised restitution where possible, reconciliation where necessary, and retirement from occasions of sin. He did not immediately advise a convent. I believe he thought my zeal theatrical. Perhaps it was; but an actress may speak truth even when she speaks with effect.

I arranged my affairs. I paid tradesmen who had ceased to expect payment and servants who had expected too much. I settled small pensions upon two elderly women of the theatre, provided for a child whose mother had once been in my service, and sold several jewels. One necklace I could not resolve to sell for an entire week. I placed it in a drawer, removed it, held it against my throat, remembered the evening upon which it had been given to me, wept, laughed at myself, and finally sent it to the goldsmith. The sacrifice was not heroic; but it was expensive.

There remained a woman with whom I had quarrelled bitterly. We had exchanged insults in a rehearsal-room, and I had afterwards repeated a story to her disadvantage. The story was true, which had made the repetition especially malicious. I went to her house.

She received me standing.

“I have come to ask your pardon,” I said.

She stared at me and answered, “Are you dying?”

“No.”

“Then I am.”

We both began to cry, embraced, contradicted one another concerning which had been most to blame, and separated better friends than we had ever been while friendship was easy.

My farewell to the world was less solemn. I gave a supper to several intimate acquaintances. I told none of them that I intended to depart, because secrecy had been recommended and because I feared their arguments. We were cheerful. A musician sang; an officer imitated a preacher; someone overturned a glass upon my tablecloth. One of the ladies, seeing me thoughtful, said:

“Gautier is already in a convent.”

“Then pass her the wine,” cried another. “She must drink before the grate closes.”

I smiled, but my heart contracted. Later a gentleman proposed a journey to Lyons.

“Not I,” said my nearest friend. “If Gautier ever goes to Lyons, it will be to turn the Carmelites into actresses.”

“Or the actresses into Carmelites,” I replied.

They applauded the answer. No one understood it.

When the last coach had departed, I walked through my rooms. There were cards upon the table, wax on the carpet, flowers dying in their vases, and the remains of supper in silver dishes. My portrait hung above the chimney. I stood before it for some time.

“You have had your share,” I told the painted woman. “Now be quiet.”

At four in the morning, with one maid who knew my intention, I left Paris.

My first retreat was at Pondevaux, where the community received women who wished to examine their vocation without immediately surrendering their liberty. I arrived tired, frightened, and still secretly persuaded that the whole house must be interested in my arrival. No one had heard of me. A sister asked whether I was the lady who had promised to bring two sacks of nuts. This humiliation, though accidental, did me good.

Life there was simple. We rose early, prayed, worked, ate in silence, and retired before the hour at which my former suppers had begun. At first the bells seemed to ring continually. I had believed a convent to be a place of tranquillity; I discovered that tranquillity is regulated by clocks.

I was given a small room, a coarse gown, and rope shoes. The shoes wounded my feet, the mattress wounded my back, and silence wounded me everywhere. Yet after some weeks a peace descended upon me such as I had never found among cushions. I learned to sweep a passage, to shell peas, to mend linen badly, and to carry a pitcher without spilling more than half its contents. At prayer I no longer wept every day. Sometimes I merely prayed, which is better.

The sisters treated me without curiosity. One had been a widow, another the daughter of a magistrate, another a servant. Each had left some world behind her, though not every world had printed playbills. Their indifference to my celebrity first offended and then relieved me. I ceased to arrange my cap before entering the chapel.

Still, Pondevaux was not my destination. The rule appeared to me too mild. I feared myself. Liberty had always been my accomplice, and I began to think that a partial sacrifice would leave me free to recover what I had abandoned.

From Pondevaux I went to Anticaille, where I met Father Deveaux.

He was a man of austere appearance, penetrating judgment, and a manner capable of making the most innocent request sound like a sentence. He had been informed of my past, perhaps too exactly. At our first interview he said:

“You desire penitence?”

“Yes, Father.”

“You desire what pleases you under the name of penitence.”

I coloured. “I have not come so far to be insulted.”

“No. You have come so far to learn why an insult still governs you.”

I disliked him immediately.

He required me to perform the humblest tasks. I carried wood, washed bowls, cleaned a stairway upon my knees, and assisted in the kitchen. Once, while lifting a saucepan, I dropped it and covered the floor with broth. The cook exclaimed that I had ruined dinner. I replied that I had performed an act of humility in its place. She said humility was not nourishing and gave me a cloth.

Father Deveaux gradually increased my penances. I fasted, kept silence, slept without sufficient covering, and prayed with my arms extended until they ached. These practices alarmed and attracted me. My former life had exhausted the body for pleasure; I thought it just to exhaust it for repentance. Yet I retained a great tenderness toward my person. I had been praised for my shoulders, my arms, my skin, and the agreeable plumpness of my figure. I wished to mortify the flesh, but not to disfigure it.

One morning Father Deveaux spoke of the discipline.

I pretended not to understand.

“The whip,” he said.

I started as though he had already struck me. “Never!”

“You asked for penitence.”

“I asked for reasonable penitence.”

“Reasonable according to whom?”

“I cannot believe God requires such barbarity.”

“Then do not practise it.”

His calmness enraged me more than command would have done.

“I will not!”

“You will request it.”

“I?”

“On your bended knees.”

I left him in fury. All day the phrase pursued me. *On your bended knees!* I saw in it insolence, tyranny, presumption. At supper I could not eat, though there were lentils, of which I was fond. In the chapel I accused Father Deveaux before God; then I accused myself for accusing him; then I accused him again.

During the night conscience began its comedy.

“You will submit,” it said.

“I will not.”

“Is your skin more sacred than your soul?”

“My skin is not the question.”

“It is precisely the question.”

“I have surrendered jewels, friends, applause, fortune.”

“And preserve your shoulders?”

“They have done no harm.”

“They have received considerable attention.”

At this I became so angry that I rose, struck my foot against the bedstead, and was obliged to sit down again.

For three days I resisted. On the fourth I sought Father Deveaux. He was writing at a table and did not look up.

“What do you want?”

“The discipline.”

He continued writing.

“Father, did you hear me?”

“I heard a woman giving an order.”

I knelt.

“Father,” I said, “I ask permission to use the discipline.”

He laid down his pen. “Willingly, my child.”

I confess that for one instant I hated him.

The instrument was lighter than I had imagined and more terrible. When I first heard it cut the air behind me, I shrieked before it touched my body. A sister in the adjoining room dropped a bundle of linen. I struck once, twice, then entangled the cord in my shift, lost my balance, and fell flat upon my nose. Thus ended my first exercise in spiritual heroism.

I went to Father Deveaux with a swollen lip.

“Well?” he asked.

“It is ridiculous.”

“Many useful things are ridiculous when performed badly.”

I persevered. I shall not praise these austerities beyond measure. There were days when they softened my pride and days when they inflamed it. Sometimes I bore pain for God; sometimes I bore it in order to admire myself for bearing it. The soul is an ingenious spectator and applauds in secret when all other spectators have been excluded.

Nevertheless, a conviction formed within me: I must sacrifice not merely pleasure, money, or reputation, but liberty itself. While I retained the right to depart, I was still bargaining. I had lived by my will; I must now consent to live by a rule.

I read the lives of famous penitents—women who had passed from scandal to sanctity, from palaces or theatres of another kind into deserts, caves, hospitals, and cells. Their histories set my imagination on fire. I wished for the narrowest enclosure, the poorest dress, the strictest obedience. Father Deveaux warned me that enthusiasm chooses suffering as vanity chooses ribbons: because it is becoming.

“Then where shall I go?” I asked.

“To a house where you will not be important.”

This advice led me to consider the Carmelites of Lyons.

Their rule was severe. They observed enclosure, silence, poverty, abstinence, long prayer, and exact obedience. A more lenient community offered to receive me, and many sensible persons urged me to choose it. I distrusted what everyone called sensible. Had I not been sensible enough in the world whenever pleasure required justification?

Before admission I was examined regarding my birth, health, debts, intentions, and former manner of life. I concealed nothing.

“I have been an actress,” I said.

The superior inclined her head.

“I have lived publicly and badly.”

She inclined it again.

“My name has been connected with men whose names I shall not repeat unless required.”

“It is not required.”

“I have possessed money and wasted it, been admired and enjoyed it, injured others, excused myself, and delayed repentance.”

“You are very eager to accuse Mademoiselle Gautier,” she said. “Can you obey when no one is interested in her?”

That question frightened me more than all the others.

I was admitted on probation.

The convent did not resemble the theatre, though I sometimes discovered resemblances which disturbed me. There were appointed hours, prescribed garments, entrances and exits, public recitations, a superior who noticed every fault, and a community perfectly aware when one had missed a cue. But there was no applause. This was the essential difference.

I received menial employments with eagerness. I swept, scrubbed, carried water, prepared vegetables, shook mattresses, and cleaned lamps. My physical strength, which had once enabled me to dance after supper and perform on little sleep, now served the house. I was proud of my labour until I learned that pride can sit astride a broom as easily as it can ride in a gilded coach.

One day I carried a great basket of damp linen across the courtyard. A younger sister offered help.

“No,” I said, “I am strong enough.”

The superior, who had heard me, replied, “Strength is useful. The desire to display it is heavier than the basket.”

I surrendered one handle.

Food caused me less difficulty than I had expected. Hunger seasoned everything, even the soup. Once we were served a small carp, divided among many. I received a portion no larger than two fingers and thought of the fish which had formerly appeared at my suppers adorned with herbs, sauces, and three servants. I experienced no regret; I only wondered what had become of the green-sauce abbé.

Silence was harder. I possessed opinions upon all subjects and had formerly communicated them even when no subject existed. To pass hours without speaking seemed at first an annihilation. I composed speeches inwardly, answered imaginary critics, corrected the superior’s decisions, and delivered several excellent monologues while polishing candlesticks. Gradually the inward theatre also grew quiet.

After three months the day appointed for my profession arrived. I had expected agitation, tears, perhaps some extraordinary sensible consolation. Instead I felt composed. The chapel was cold. A candle guttered near the grate. My knees hurt. The words were spoken, the questions asked, the vows pronounced. I renounced the world, poverty became my wealth, obedience my liberty, enclosure my road.

When the veil was placed upon me, I thought briefly of the silver gown, the diamonds, the lights, the crowded house, and Dufresne standing at the window while rain fell upon the street.

Then the bell rang, and Mademoiselle Gautier was gone.

At least, I believed so.

---

Here the autobiography, in its continuous form, ends. Several later fragments, letters, convent records, and recollections preserved by visitors allow us to complete the portrait; and they also permit the reader to observe that persons of marked character do not disappear quite so easily as a novice may suppose.

Sister Augustine—for such was Gautier’s religious name—remained faithful to her vows until death. The fervour of the first year did not evaporate when novelty ceased; the humiliations which had once appeared dramatic became ordinary, and she accepted ordinary duty, that least theatrical and most conclusive test of resolution. She rose when the bell rang, came when summoned, worked when work was required, and yielded, not invariably without an inward battle, but invariably in the end.

Her warmth survived. So did her liveliness. She could tell a story so well that the superior occasionally reminded her that recreation was not the Comédie-Française; but the same gift which had once entertained frivolous salons now consoled timid novices, diverted the sick, and persuaded reluctant benefactors. She never ceased to understand the world she had renounced. When a young sister condemned a fashionable lady with excessive confidence, Sister Augustine replied:

“My child, do not despise the woman in the carriage. I was once that woman, and I assure you she may be nearer the convent than she appears.”

She laboured with remarkable vigour. She was always ready for the heaviest basket, always present when a sick sister required watching, always willing to mend, scour, carry, clean, or cook. She wrote letters for those who lacked her fluency; she petitioned magistrates for poor families; she employed the memory of old connections to obtain dowries, medicine, firewood, and places in hospitals. More than one gentleman who would have declined a sermon from a Carmelite found himself unable to refuse a request composed by Mademoiselle Gautier.

Her charity retained something of her old magnificence, though she no longer possessed money. She gave time, labour, influence, food from her portion, and, when these were exhausted, attention. The last gift is often the rarest. She always received the afflicted patiently, always talked to the despondent with animation, always assisted the ashamed without requiring them first to become agreeable, and always remembered that repentance is difficult enough without the additional burden of being dull.

It would be dishonest to conceal the less amiable side of her devotion. She sometimes mistook severity for perfection and physical suffering for moral progress. She admired extreme practices, spoke of obedience with an enthusiasm which could itself become imperious, and occasionally recommended to weaker constitutions penances which her own strong body had survived. Yet experience moderated her. In later years, when a novice begged permission for some extravagant mortification, Sister Augustine would say:

“Begin by obeying when dinner displeases you. Heroism keeps; lentils do not.”

She became blind in old age. The deprivation was painful, for she had loved colour, faces, movement, and all the visible business of life. She bore it without affectation. She continued to work by touch, sorted linen, wound thread, shelled nuts, and moved through the familiar passages with a hand upon the wall. Visitors remarked that her conversation remained cheerful and that her laugh, though softened, was still unmistakably the laugh which had once crossed the footlights.

Blindness did not cure her of every human weakness. Indeed, the final anecdote concerning her is so characteristic that no biographer who values truth above edification ought to suppress it.

The Carmelite rule required the sisters to receive visitors behind a veil. Sister Augustine, having been accustomed in her youth to appear before the public with her face uncovered, found this regulation troublesome. At first she justified her objection on grounds of charity: persons who sought her counsel, she said, were comforted by a natural countenance; a veil produced distance; expression assisted instruction. These arguments were not without force, but her companions suspected that theology did not wholly explain the matter.

After she became blind, one might have supposed the question settled. She could no longer see whether she was seen. Yet she petitioned for a dispensation, and, through ecclesiastical channels of sufficient dignity, obtained permission to receive certain visitors unveiled.

A sister ventured to tease her.

“Mother Augustine, you cannot now enjoy their admiration.”

“No,” she answered, “but they may enjoy what remains of its object.”

She was then an old woman.

There are moralists who will consider this reply proof that her renunciation was imperfect; there are others who will value it as proof that her nature remained alive beneath discipline. For our part, we see no reason to demand that grace should make all faces blank or all memories ungrateful. Sister Augustine had surrendered pleasure, fortune, reputation, liberty, and the society in which she had reigned. If she preserved, among so many ruins, the recollection that Mademoiselle Gautier had once been beautiful, the remnant was harmless, and perhaps even touching.

Her transformation was sincere, but it was not an extinction. The passion with which she had loved Dufresne became the ardour with which she sought God; the strength once squandered in dissipation carried pitchers and baskets; the pride which had demanded applause learned, with difficulty and repeated protest, to endure correction; the social intelligence which had governed a salon served the poor; and the actress who had required an audience ended by performing the hardest part of her life in seclusion.

She died among the Carmelites after many years of profession. Those who had known only Sister Augustine praised her humility, industry, gaiety, and courage. Those who remembered Mademoiselle Gautier marvelled that such a woman could have persevered. Both judgments were incomplete. It was precisely because she had been that woman that she persevered as she did. She brought into repentance the same force with which she had formerly pursued pleasure, and into obedience the same whole-heartedness with which she had once resisted it.

Duclos, at the conclusion of his notes, wrote a sentence which may serve for ours: “She was never moderate, even in becoming good.”

The phrase is witty, and therefore not entirely just. Yet if moderation had been required to save her, Mademoiselle Gautier might have been lost.

# The King of Surf Avenue

There are young women who advance upon romance as a general advances upon an inferior province, preceded by trumpets and followed by the wounded. Genevieve Holt was one of these. When she entered the little second-hand bookshop in West Eighth Street, men who had been examining the ten-cent table discovered an immediate need for literature at the rear of the premises. She had gray eyes, gold hair, and a manner of pronouncing “really” which suggested that the word had been imported at considerable expense from London.

Katie Bennett, who owned the shop, had none of these advantages.

She was small and brown-haired, and she had formed the habit of standing a little aside from life, as though fearful that somebody with a better claim might require her place. Her eyes were pleasant, but they did not issue challenges. Her clothes were neat, but no young man had ever walked into a lamppost because of them. When Genevieve stood beside her, Katie had the air of a minor character who has wandered by mistake into the first act and is waiting apologetically for the hero and heroine to finish the scene.

The shop itself suited Katie. It was narrow, dim, and full of the composed dust of other people’s opinions. On the shelves, poets leaned against geographers, philosophers supported cookbooks, and several complete works of distinguished Victorians had remained untouched so long that Katie regarded them less as stock than as elderly tenants. At the back stood a curtain, and beyond the curtain a steep staircase led to two rooms where Katie lived with her grandfather.

Mr. James Bennett had been paralyzed for four years. Physically, he was a frail old man with a white beard and an invalid’s chair. Mentally, he occupied a wider territory.

His doctors called his condition a fixed delusion. Katie called it, when compelled to mention the subject, Grandfather’s idea.

The idea was that Mr. Bennett was the King of England.

It had come to him after a fever and had settled upon his mind with the permanence of a mortgage. He remembered Katie, the shop, his dead daughter, and the precise location of the tobacco jar; but he understood them in a royal sense. Katie was the Princess Katherine. The shop was a regrettable commercial attachment to the Crown. West Eighth Street was one of his less comfortable palaces. The United States, he believed, was a remote possession which had been allowed an unusual amount of independence because governing it directly would involve too much correspondence.

Katie did not contradict him. Contradiction excited him, and excitement brought on attacks that frightened her. She had learned instead to guide the royal imagination round dangerous corners.

If he demanded the Prime Minister, she told him that Parliament was sitting late.

If he asked why the guards had not changed outside the shop, she said there had been trouble with the horses.

If he complained of the absence of ambassadors, she replied that Washington had detained them.

The system worked, though it gave Katie a view of constitutional monarchy not commonly taught in schools.

On the afternoon when Ted Brady first entered her shop, Genevieve was seated on the counter, swinging one perfect shoe and explaining why Mr. Horace Murdoch, the photographer, had no soul.

“He says my expression is too imperious,” she said. “Imperious! As if a woman with my profile could look domestic.”

“Perhaps he meant it as a compliment,” said Katie.

“He meant that I must smile at a cake of soap. I told him that nothing could induce me to smile at soap.”

Katie replaced a volume of sermons on a high shelf.

“You smiled at that young man on the ferry.”

Genevieve’s eyes became thoughtful.

“That,” she said, “was different.”

The young man on the ferry had been Ted Brady.

Even Katie knew Ted’s name, which shows the extent of his fame. He was the finest all-round amateur athlete in New York, though the word amateur, in his case, was used with the reverence ordinarily reserved for millionaires who have not yet been convicted. He had won footraces, rowing races, wrestling bouts, swimming matches, and a contest involving the lifting of a barrel above the head, an accomplishment whose practical value was obscure but whose effect upon the public had been immense. Sporting pages printed his photograph. Boys followed him. Elderly men who became breathless ascending three steps spoke bitterly of the decline in modern athletics whenever Ted lost anything.

That morning he had crossed on the ferry with Katie and Genevieve. Genevieve had been wearing blue, and the wind had treated her hat with the respectful restraint due to superior millinery. Katie had worn brown, and the wind had done what it liked with her.

Naturally, Katie had assumed that Ted’s attention was fixed on Genevieve.

This is one of the most firmly established rules of public conveyance. If a celebrated young man looks in the direction of two young women, the beautiful one accepts the tribute and the other examines the scenery. Katie had examined New Jersey with unusual thoroughness.

Genevieve had done more. She had smiled.

Ted, however, had not approached them. He had merely stared with the grave concentration which he brought to the starting line of a hundred-yard dash. When the ferry reached its slip, the girls had gone their way and he his.

Now the bell over the shop door rang.

Ted Brady came in.

He was a broad-shouldered young man with a square chin, quiet eyes, and the air of one who could, if necessary, move the entire shop three feet to the left. His clothes were excellent without being imaginative. He chewed gum with slow determination.

Genevieve descended from the counter.

Katie retreated behind a pile of Lives of the Saints.

Ted took off his hat.

“Afternoon,” he said.

“Good afternoon,” said Genevieve, in the tone with which duchesses address agreeable cavalry officers.

Ted looked past her.

“You own this place?” he asked Katie.

Katie glanced behind herself. There was no one there but William Makepeace Thackeray.

“Yes.”

“I want a book.”

“We have a number,” said Katie.

Ted appeared to feel that this was promising.

“What kind?” she asked.

He frowned. Athletics had not prepared him for the variety of literature.

“One with a story.”

“Adventure? Mystery? Love?”

“Love,” said Ted.

Genevieve turned her head sharply.

Katie’s fingers became occupied with the corner of a ledger.

“We have some novels on that shelf.”

Ted examined the shelf as he might have examined an opposing football team.

“Which is the best?”

“It depends on what you like.”

“I don’t know. I never read one.”

Genevieve gave a small laugh, musical but edged.

Ted did not hear it. His eyes remained on Katie.

“You pick it,” he said. “I’ll take what you say.”

Katie selected a modest novel in which two persons of good family misunderstood each other through three hundred pages and married in the last paragraph.

“This is pleasant.”

“That’ll do.”

She wrapped it. He paid. Having completed the transaction, he did not leave.

“I saw you on the ferry,” he said.

Genevieve smiled again. She could forgive a slow beginning if the conclusion was correct.

“So did I,” she replied.

Ted looked faintly surprised.

“I meant her.”

Genevieve’s smile remained, but it changed professions and became a weapon.

Katie flushed.

“Me?”

“Sure. You were with this lady.”

“This lady,” said Genevieve, “is Genevieve Holt.”

“Pleased to meet you,” said Ted, without looking pleased. He addressed Katie again. “I wanted to speak to you, but there were a lot of people around.”

“There are generally people on ferries,” said Genevieve.

Ted considered this and nodded.

“That’s right.”

He had a disconcerting habit of accepting sarcasm as useful information.

“My name’s Ted Brady,” he continued.

“Yes,” said Katie. “I know.”

“You follow athletics?”

“No. But I’ve seen your picture.”

Ted brightened.

“That’s something. Listen, would you come out to supper with me tonight?”

Katie stared at him.

Genevieve stared harder.

From upstairs came the slow tapping of a stick upon the floor. The monarch required attention.

“I’m afraid I can’t,” said Katie.

Ted’s face fell, but only slightly. He was accustomed to obstacles. They were things to be climbed over, run round, or, in the last resort, pushed aside.

“Tomorrow?”

“I can’t leave my grandfather in the evening.”

“Bring him.”

Katie pictured His Majesty in an invalid’s chair at a Sixth Avenue restaurant, asking the waiter why the royal standard was not displayed.

“He is not well.”

“Oh.”

Ted was silent.

Then he said, “How about lunch?”

“I close from one to two.”

“I’ll be here at one.”

He put on his hat.

“You haven’t asked whether I’ll come.”

“You will, won’t you?”

Katie had intended to say no. She was shy, cautious, and burdened with a domestic situation that discouraged experiments. But Ted’s solemn confidence had a curious warmth in it. He did not seem to regard her as a temporary substitute for Genevieve or anyone else. He looked at her as if, among all the women in New York, she had been the one appointed.

“Yes,” she said.

Ted nodded.

“Fine. I’m on the level.”

He left.

Genevieve waited until the door closed.

“Well!”

Katie began arranging books which were already arranged.

“I don’t know what you mean.”

“I mean that the destroyer of masculine peace of mind has smiled across a ferry-boat and been passed over for a little person in brown who spent the whole trip looking at coal barges.”

Katie’s flush deepened.

“He may merely want advice about books.”

“Then why did he ask you to lunch?”

“Perhaps he wants advice about lunch.”

Genevieve examined her.

“My dear Katie, modesty is charming until it becomes an insult to the intelligence. That young man is interested in you.”

Upstairs the stick tapped again, imperiously.

Katie turned toward the curtain.

“Grandfather needs his tea.”

Genevieve slipped on her gloves.

“And Ted Brady,” she said, “needs his head examined.”

This diagnosis was not accepted by the patient.

At one o’clock the next day Ted arrived with a bunch of violets and a box of candy large enough to provision a rowing crew. Katie locked the shop, and they went to a restaurant where the soup was thin, the tables were close together, and the proprietor had hung a picture of Venice on the wall in order that his customers might understand the better sort of life.

Ted asked Katie questions.

He asked where she had been born, what books she liked, whether she could swim, why she wore her hair that way, and whether she had ever seen him run the quarter mile. Katie answered in a soft voice. It was not a brilliant conversation, judged as conversation, but brilliance is not always demanded by two persons who are beginning to like one another. A lantern may be useful at such moments, but fireworks are unnecessary.

Ted told her about himself. He worked during the week for a sporting-goods firm on Broadway. He trained in the evenings. He had been offered money to turn professional and had refused it because Mr. Murdoch, the president of his athletic club, said there was more honor in being an amateur. Mr. Murdoch also employed him at a salary that rose whenever a rival club attempted to seduce him, so the preservation of honor was not without its compensations.

“And that’s all there is,” Ted concluded.

Katie smiled.

“You make it sound very simple.”

“It is simple. Most things are, if you don’t mix them up.”

Katie thought of her grandfather’s kingdom upstairs.

“Not everything.”

“What isn’t?”

She hesitated. She disliked speaking of the old man’s condition. It seemed a betrayal, as though she were inviting the world to laugh through a keyhole.

“My grandfather is ill. I have to take care of him.”

“I’ll help.”

The answer came so promptly that Katie looked at him.

“You don’t know him.”

“I’ll get to know him.”

“He can be difficult.”

“So can the Tennessee Bear-Cat.”

Katie had never heard of this animal.

Ted explained.

The Tennessee Bear-Cat was a wrestler whose real name was Alvin Higgs. His nose had been broken in three places, giving it the appearance of a geographical problem, and he had developed a method of attack known as the Double Cyclone. According to Ted, the method consisted in approaching an opponent from several directions at once.

“He tried it on me last winter,” Ted said. “I let him get set, then I turned him over. Nothing to it.”

“Grandfather is not a wrestler.”

“That makes it easier.”

Katie laughed. Ted stopped chewing and gazed at her.

“Do that again.”

“What?”

“Laugh.”

She became grave at once.

“I can’t do it if you tell me.”

“You’ve got a nice laugh.”

Katie lowered her eyes. Compliments had seldom entered her life, and she did not know where to put them.

Ted leaned across the table.

“Listen. I meant what I said yesterday. I’m on the level.”

“About what?”

“About you.”

He could not have explained it more clearly, and for Ted explanation was now complete.

Their courtship moved rapidly. Ted had the temperament of a man who, seeing a finish line, considers lingering an affectation. He came to the shop each day. Sometimes he brought flowers. Sometimes he brought books which he had bought elsewhere under the impression that Katie might like to sell them again. Once he brought a mechanical exerciser for Mr. Bennett and was pained to learn that paralysis is not commonly cured by pulling two rubber handles apart before breakfast.

Katie grew used to him in stages. First she ceased to be frightened when the bell rang. Then she began to expect it. Finally there came a wet Tuesday when he was delayed, and she found herself listening for his step with an anxiety that illuminated the whole situation.

Love had entered the bookshop without troubling to inquire whether it was wanted.

Ted met the grandfather in the second week.

Katie prepared him on the staircase.

“There is something I must explain.”

Ted placed the violets he was carrying under one arm.

“Go ahead.”

“My grandfather sometimes thinks he is someone else.”

“Who?”

Katie drew a breath.

“The King of England.”

Ted considered this.

“All the time?”

“Nearly all the time.”

“What does he do?”

“He talks as if he were the King.”

“Anything else?”

“No.”

Ted’s relief was evident.

“That’s not so bad. I thought you meant he got violent.”

“He must not be contradicted.”

“I won’t contradict him.”

“You must call him Your Majesty.”

“Sure.”

“And if he asks about Parliament—”

“What’s that?”

“The government in England.”

“Oh. All right.”

“—you had better say it is in session.”

“Is it?”

“I don’t know.”

Ted looked shocked.

“You want me to tell him something that ain’t true?”

Katie’s eyes widened.

“You have just promised to call him Your Majesty.”

“That’s different. That’s being polite.”

Katie abandoned the constitutional point. She had long since learned that the frontiers of conscience are surveyed differently by every traveler.

They entered the room.

Mr. Bennett sat by the window in a high-backed chair. A rug covered his knees. He was thin and pale, but his dark eyes retained a brightness that gave dignity to his delusion. On the table beside him lay an old atlas, a cup of tea, and a handbell which he rang when royal requirements exceeded the strength of his stick.

He looked at Ted.

“Who is this person?”

Katie went to him and adjusted the rug.

“This is Mr. Edward Brady, Grandfather.”

“Your Majesty,” Ted added.

The old man relaxed slightly.

“Brady? Irish?”

“My father was.”

“A troublesome race,” said the King. “We have had difficulties with them.”

“My father never gave anybody trouble,” Ted replied. “Not unless they started it.”

Katie touched his arm.

Mr. Bennett examined the visitor.

“You are in the army?”

“No, sir.”

“Your Majesty,” Katie murmured.

“No, Your Majesty.”

“What are you?”

“I’m with Murdoch Sporting Goods. And I run.”

The old man’s brows rose.

“From the police?”

“No. In races.”

“Ah. A courier.”

“Something like that.”

“A useful class. In my youth we had messengers who could ride from Windsor to London before breakfast.”

“I could beat a horse over a short course,” said Ted.

Katie intervened before a royal challenge could be arranged.

“Mr. Brady has won many prizes.”

“Indeed?”

“Cups,” Ted said. “Medals. A silver-plated clock. I got a picture of myself from the Mercury, too, but I don’t count that.”

Mr. Bennett nodded.

“Service to the Crown should be recognized. Katherine, see that this young man receives something.”

“I’m all right,” said Ted. “I came to see Katie.”

The temperature of the interview altered.

“Katherine?”

“Yes.”

“On what business?”

Ted looked at Katie. Katie looked at the carpet.

“No business,” he said.

The old man’s eyes sharpened, but Ted, feeling that he had been sufficiently diplomatic for one afternoon, began to describe the annual games of the Metropolitan Amateur Athletic Association. Mr. Bennett became interested when he understood these to be a species of military tournament. Ted’s victory in the half-mile turned into a triumph over rebellious barons; the Tennessee Bear-Cat became a savage chieftain; and Mr. Murdoch, who had merely presented a cup, emerged as the Archbishop of Canterbury.

By the time Ted left, His Majesty had appointed him Royal Courier Extraordinary.

“That went fine,” Ted said on the stairs.

Katie smiled faintly.

“Yes. But you should not have said you came to see me.”

“Why not? I did.”

“He is very protective.”

“So am I.”

“Ted—”

He stopped one step below her. This placed their faces nearly level.

“Katie, I’ve been thinking.”

“You generally look so grave when you do.”

“I want to marry you.”

She held the banister.

The shop below seemed suddenly unreal: the shelves, the dust, the familiar green shade over the desk. She had imagined Ted as a brief radiance, a fairy prince misdirected into West Eighth Street who would presently discover the error and return to a more appropriate story. Fairy princes did not, as a rule, marry women who sold damaged dictionaries and spent their evenings warming blankets for an invalid.

“You cannot mean it.”

“I’m on the level.”

“You have only known me two weeks.”

“That’s long enough.”

“It isn’t.”

“How long do you need?”

“I don’t know.”

“Another week?”

Katie laughed despite herself.

Ted took her hand.

“I’m not kidding. I’ve seen plenty of girls. They’re all right. But you’re different. You don’t make a noise all the time. You look after the old gentleman. You know things. And when you laugh—well, that’s it. I want you. See?”

To a poet this might have seemed an insufficient declaration. To Katie, who knew Ted, it was complete. He had laid out his heart in short words, as neatly as he might have placed medals in a row.

“I love you,” she said.

Ted’s grip tightened.

“Then it’s fixed.”

“It is not quite fixed.”

“What’s wrong?”

“Grandfather.”

“We’ll take him with us.”

“He may not consent.”

Ted glanced toward the room above.

“What’s he got to do with it?”

“Everything.”

“No grandfather can stop a girl marrying if she wants.”

“This one can.”

“Nothing doing,” said Ted. “I’ll talk to him.”

He did.

The following Sunday was selected for the royal audience. Ted put on a dark suit, brushed his hair until it became actively resistant, and entered Mr. Bennett’s room carrying a box of cigars.

“Your Majesty,” he began, “I want to marry Katie.”

The old man stared.

“Marry the Princess Katherine?”

“Yes.”

“Impossible.”

“Why?”

Mr. Bennett drew himself upright.

“You presume upon the favor we have shown you. The daughter of our house cannot marry a common courier.”

“I’m not a courier.”

“You informed us that you ran messages.”

“I said something like that. I work for a sporting-goods firm.”

The King looked even more offended.

“A tradesman!”

“I’m assistant manager.”

“A tradesman with assistants.”

Katie stood beside the chair, pale and anxious.

“Grandfather, Mr. Brady is a very good man.”

“That is irrelevant. Many excellent men are not eligible to marry princesses. The arrangement would not do.”

Ted planted his feet.

“Why wouldn’t it?”

“Because it would not do.”

“But we love each other.”

Mr. Bennett waved this aside. Monarchs, like parents and booking agents, have always regarded love as a detail that can be adjusted after the principal negotiations.

“Katherine must marry suitably.”

“I can support her.”

“Can you bring an army?”

“No.”

“A navy?”

“No.”

“Territories?”

Ted thought of the sporting-goods firm’s branch office in Newark.

“No.”

“Then the matter is concluded.”

“It ain’t concluded.”

“Ted,” Katie whispered.

“I’m on the level, Your Majesty. I’m going to marry her.”

The old man’s hand shook upon the arm of his chair. Color rose in his thin face.

“You defy the Crown?”

The handbell fell to the floor.

Katie moved instantly.

“Please go,” she said to Ted.

“But—”

“Please.”

There are moments when even a champion athlete understands that force is not the appropriate event. Ted went.

The excitement brought on one of Mr. Bennett’s attacks. For three days he was feverish and confused. Katie sat beside him through the nights, holding his hand when he woke and assuring him that the palace was secure. The doctor came twice and spoke sternly about shocks.

“He must be kept quiet,” he said. “Humor him in everything. Any violent agitation is dangerous.”

Ted waited in the shop below. When Katie came down on the third evening, he rose from a chair beside the ten-cent table.

“How is he?”

“Better.”

“Good. Then we can get this straight.”

Katie sat at the desk.

“There is nothing to get straight.”

“What do you mean?”

“I cannot marry you.”

Ted’s face became rigid.

“Because he says so?”

“Because opposing him may kill him.”

“It may not.”

“I cannot risk it.”

“We can get married without telling him.”

“And leave him?”

“Bring him along afterward.”

“He would know.”

“Then we don’t bring him. We get somebody to take care of him.”

Katie looked at him.

Ted paced between Biography and Travel.

“I don’t mean dump him somewhere. We hire a nurse. A good one.”

“He will not let a nurse do anything for him. He trusts only me.”

“He’ll get used to it.”

“No.”

“We could live upstairs.”

“He would still know we were married.”

“We could tell him I’m just staying there.”

“Ted!”

“Well, I’m trying.”

She covered her eyes for a moment.

“I know.”

Ted stopped pacing.

“What does he want you to do—never marry?”

“He thinks I must marry royalty.”

“Where are we going to get royalty?”

“I do not know.”

“Are there any kings around New York?”

“Of course not.”

Ted’s jaw set.

“Then he’s got no right.”

“Rights have nothing to do with it. He is ill.”

“And you’re going to throw your whole life away because he’s got an idea in his head?”

“He cared for me when I was little. He worked for me. He gave up everything after Mother died. I cannot pay him back by frightening him to death.”

The words were quiet, but Ted recognized an obstacle that could not be pushed aside. Katie’s gentleness had concealed from him the strength beneath it. A soft answer may turn away wrath; a soft refusal can be as immovable as a stone wall.

“Suppose I wait,” he said.

“For what?”

“Till he changes his mind.”

“He may never change.”

“Then I’ll keep trying.”

“Each attempt will excite him.”

Ted stared at the floor.

“Do you want me to go?”

“No.”

“Do you love me?”

“Yes.”

“Then I don’t see—”

“I want you to go because I love you. It is wrong to keep you waiting for something that may never happen.”

“Let me decide that.”

“You would come here. You would argue. He would become ill again. And I should be afraid every minute.”

Ted was silent for so long that the ticking of the shop clock became aggressive.

“All right,” he said at last. “If that’s what you want.”

“It is not what I want.”

“It’s what you’re asking.”

“Yes.”

He took his hat.

Katie rose. For one instant both seemed about to abandon reason, duty, and the British monarchy in favor of their own happiness. Then the stick tapped upstairs.

Katie turned her head.

Ted opened the door.

“I’m on the level,” he said. “I won’t bother you. But this thing ain’t finished.”

The bell rang behind him.

Winter left the city by degrees, like a tenant attempting to escape without paying. Rain washed the gray snow from the gutters. Washington Square produced a few reluctant leaves. Barrel organs returned to the streets, repeating the same eight bars beneath windows where people had been perfectly unhappy without them.

Ted did not come back.

Katie saw his name in the papers. He won an indoor meet in Boston. He defeated the Tennessee Bear-Cat in a charity wrestling exhibition, after the Bear-Cat’s Double Cyclone had become entangled in the ropes. He broke a club record for the standing broad jump. Once his photograph appeared beside that of Genevieve Holt, who had been engaged to pose with him in an advertisement for athletic hosiery.

Genevieve brought the clipping to the shop.

“It means nothing,” she said.

Katie folded it carefully.

“I did not say it did.”

“He disliked me on sight.”

“He did not dislike you.”

“He looked through me as if I were a window.”

“You are very beautiful.”

“Beauty,” said Genevieve, “is wasted on athletes. They respect stopwatches.”

Katie put the clipping in a drawer after her friend had gone.

The months passed.

Her life resumed its former shape, but it no longer fitted her. She tended the shop, cooked small meals, and read aloud to her grandfather. He had forgotten the dispute with Ted, or placed it among those rebellions which the Crown had successfully suppressed. Sometimes he asked after the Royal Courier, and Katie would say that Mr. Brady had gone abroad on service.

“India?” the old man asked.

“Possibly.”

“Hot country. Send him a sun helmet.”

Katie promised to do so.

Summer descended upon New York with the force of a heated blanket. The pavements shone. Horses drooped between their shafts. The air in the shop grew thick with dust and old bindings, and customers who handled books did so without conviction. Everybody who could leave the city left it; everybody who could not remained to explain why the country was overrated.

Genevieve went to Coney Island for the Mardi Gras preparations. Mr. Murdoch had secured her a position on one of the floats, representing Progress, an assignment for which her profile and her opinion of herself made her exceptionally suitable.

“You should come,” she told Katie. “There will be fireworks, bands, processions, and a man diving from a tower into a tank of water.”

“That sounds dangerous.”

“That is why people buy tickets.”

“I cannot leave Grandfather.”

“Not even for one evening?”

“No.”

Genevieve sighed.

“You are becoming one of your own books—very noble, slightly dusty, and never taken out.”

The Coney Island Mardi Gras occupied the newspapers for a week. It was less a festival than a temporary suspension of the laws governing noise. There were decorated cars, brass bands, electric lights, confetti, masks, barkers, dancers, politicians, minor actors, major appetites, and enough colored paper to conceal the Atlantic Ocean. A King of the Carnival was elected by popular vote, which meant that ballots could be obtained from participating merchants and that civic sovereignty, for the occasion, was inseparable from the purchase of chewing tobacco, bathing costumes, and frankfurters.

The candidates included an alderman, a comedian, a saloon proprietor, and Ted Brady.

Mr. Murdoch had nominated Ted for commercial reasons. A crowned athlete, he felt, would be an ornament to the city and particularly to the sporting-goods business. The newspapers supported him. The athletic clubs mobilized. Boys collected ballots. Genevieve, dressed as Progress, distributed handbills bearing Ted’s picture and the slogan BRADY FOR KING—A STRONG MAN FOR A STRONG THRONE.

Ted won by twenty-three thousand votes.

On the final night he rode along Surf Avenue in a white costume, a purple cape, and a gilt crown. Behind him came six floats, three bands, Genevieve as Progress, the Tennessee Bear-Cat dressed unwillingly as a Roman senator, and Mr. Murdoch perspiring inside the uniform of a herald. The crowd shouted, whistles blew, fireworks cracked above the sea, and Ted Brady was presented with a scepter by the mayor of the festivities.

For most young men this would have been the climax of the evening.

For Ted it was an idea.

At ten o’clock the following morning, the bell over Katie’s shop door rang.

Katie looked up.

A figure stood on the threshold, and for a moment she thought that heat had affected her vision.

Ted wore white shoes, white trousers, a white tunic braided with gold, a purple cape, and a crown. The crown was slightly too large and rested upon his ears. Under one arm he carried a scepter; under the other, a framed certificate. Behind him stood the Tennessee Bear-Cat, holding two cardboard boxes and looking as if he had already regretted several stages of his life.

Katie gripped the desk.

“Ted!”

“Hello, Katie.”

“What are you wearing?”

“My robes.”

The Bear-Cat deposited the boxes.

“Where you want the royal baggage, Your Nibs?”

“Anywhere.”

The Bear-Cat scratched his broken nose.

“I got to get back. There’s a feller at the Neptune Gardens says he can stay four rounds.”

“Go ahead.”

“Toodle-oo, Princess,” said the Bear-Cat to Katie. “His Majesty ain’t slept, so don’t ask him nothing hard.”

He departed.

Katie came around the desk.

“Ted, what has happened?”

“I’m King of Coney Island.”

“Yes, I saw it in the paper.”

“It’s official.”

He displayed the certificate.

It announced, in red and blue letters, that Theodore Brady had been chosen by the sovereign will of the people to reign as King of the Coney Island Mardi Gras. It bore six signatures, a gold seal, and a small advertisement for Professor Zwiebel’s Corn Remedy.

Katie looked from the certificate to Ted.

“You cannot mean—”

“I do.”

“You are going to tell Grandfather that you are a king?”

“I am a king.”

“For one week at an amusement resort.”

“That part don’t matter.”

“It matters very much.”

“Not to him. He doesn’t know Coney Island.”

“He knows it is in America.”

“Does he?”

Katie hesitated.

Mr. Bennett’s geography was political rather than exact. He believed Canada to be near Scotland, Chicago to be a troublesome port in Wales, and Brooklyn to have been granted to the Dutch in the reign of Charles II.

“Ted, it would be deceiving him.”

“I won an election.”

“That does not make you royal.”

“Why not?”

“Kings are not chosen by coupons from cigar stores.”

“How are they chosen?”

“They inherit the throne.”

“From somebody who got it another way in the first place.”

Katie looked helplessly at him.

Ted placed the scepter on the counter.

“I’ve been thinking this out all night. A king is a fellow people agree to call king. Twenty-three thousand more people called me king than called Cassidy king. There was a parade. I got a crown. I got papers. If that ain’t being king, it’s close enough.”

“It is a carnival title.”

“Some European kings don’t do as much. I saw pictures. They wear uniforms, ride in carriages, open things, and wave. I did all that last night. I opened the new shooting gallery.”

“You cannot compare—”

“I’m not saying I’m King of England. Your grandfather’s got that job. I’m King of Coney Island. Separate country.”

“It is not a country.”

“It’s got a shore, a population, police, taxes, and places where foreigners try to take your money. What more does a country need?”

Despite herself, Katie laughed.

Ted’s face softened.

“There. That’s what I came back for.”

Her laughter stopped, and her eyes filled.

“Ted—”

“I told you it wasn’t finished. I couldn’t beat the old gentleman’s idea, so I worked with it. Now I’m suitable.”

“You arranged all this?”

“Murdoch arranged it. I only saw what it was good for afterward.”

He picked up the scepter.

“Come on. Tell him a king wants an audience.”

Mr. Bennett was awake and in excellent spirits. The heat had given way after a night storm, and he sat by the window enjoying the cleaner air. When Katie entered, he looked at her with mild reproach.

“There is considerable noise downstairs.”

“A visitor has come, Grandfather.”

“We receive few visitors now. Since the court moved to this colony, standards have declined.”

“This visitor is important.”

Mr. Bennett adjusted the rug.

“An ambassador?”

“More than an ambassador.”

Ted appeared in the doorway.

Whatever may be said against carnival robes, they make an impression in a small room. The purple cape filled the entrance. The crown glittered. The gilt scepter, designed to be visible beneath electric lights and through confetti, possessed a brilliance seldom encountered in private life.

The old man stared.

Ted bowed. He had received lessons from Genevieve before the parade, but in the agitation of the moment his bow combined the movements of a glover’s assistant, a messenger boy, and a man searching under furniture for a collar stud.

“Your Majesty,” he said.

Mr. Bennett looked at Katie.

“Who is this sovereign?”

Ted answered.

“Theodore the First, King of Coney Island.”

“Indeed.”

Mr. Bennett’s manner changed at once. He sat straighter and extended a thin hand with courtly gravity.

“We greet our royal cousin.”

Ted shook the hand.

“Same here.”

Katie closed her eyes briefly.

Mr. Bennett examined Ted’s costume.

“Your kingdom lies in the south?”

“South of here.”

“A maritime power?”

“Right on the ocean.”

“We recall the name. Coney Island. One of the Mediterranean states, is it not?”

“Near there,” said Ted.

“Ted,” Katie said faintly.

“I mean, it’s connected by water.”

The old man nodded. This was geographically sufficient for a king who governed the United States from West Eighth Street.

“And what brings Your Majesty to our court?”

Ted stood very still.

“I want to marry Princess Katherine.”

Mr. Bennett’s expression grew solemn.

“A royal alliance.”

“That’s it.”

“Your lineage?”

Ted had prepared for this.

“My father was Patrick Brady, of Yorkville. My mother was a Kelly.”

Mr. Bennett waited.

“They were good people,” Ted added.

“No doubt. But your royal house?”

“I’m the first of it.”

“A new dynasty.”

“Yes.”

“How did you obtain your throne?”

“By vote of the people.”

The King frowned.

“A dangerous innovation.”

“They wanted me by a big majority.”

“Popular affection is useful,” Mr. Bennett conceded. “Though it should not be encouraged to excess.”

“I won’t encourage it.”

“You possess the loyalty of your subjects?”

“They cheered for nearly two miles.”

“An army?”

“I got every athletic club in the city behind me.”

“A body of trained men?”

“Some of them can do the hundred in ten and two-fifths.”

The old man considered this military intelligence.

“And your navy?”

Ted glanced at Katie.

“We have excursion steamers.”

“How many?”

“A lot. They run every day.”

“Your territories are prosperous?”

“Packed every summer.”

“Revenue?”

“Plenty of money changes hands.”

“Excellent.”

Mr. Bennett sank back upon his cushions. His face had lost its suspicion. In its place shone a pleasure so simple that Katie’s uneasiness softened. The old man had feared leaving his beloved granddaughter unprotected in a world which, to his damaged mind, consisted of rival courts, secret treaties, and ambitious commoners. Now Providence had supplied a neighboring monarch with a strong body and excursion steamers.

“The alliance,” he said, “has much to recommend it.”

Ted breathed out.

“Then it’s all right?”

“One matter remains. The Princess Katherine’s wishes must be consulted.”

All three looked at Katie.

She had expected to feel shame at the deception, or alarm, or the solemn rebuke of conscience. Instead she saw her grandfather smiling, Ted in his absurd crown waiting with the anxiety of a man at the tape, and the whole impossible road to happiness open before her.

“I wish it very much,” she said.

Mr. Bennett extended his hands.

“Then we give our blessing.”

Ted crossed the room in two strides and kissed Katie.

The old man rang his handbell sharply.

They separated.

“Decorum,” he said. “There are forms to be observed between sovereign houses.”

“Sure,” said Ted. “What forms?”

Mr. Bennett reflected.

“There must be an announcement to the people.”

“I’ll get Murdoch to put it in the papers.”

“A treaty.”

“We can get a license.”

“A ceremony of appropriate splendor.”

“We’ll have it wherever Katie says.”

“And Coney Island must guarantee that no claim shall be made upon the British succession.”

“Nothing doing,” Ted said. “I don’t want his job.”

The King smiled graciously.

“Spoken like a true monarch.”

The wedding took place three weeks later.

Mr. Murdoch offered the ballroom of the athletic club. Genevieve supervised the decorations, declaring that men left alone with flowers invariably arranged them as if preparing a funeral for a horse. The Tennessee Bear-Cat served as best man in a collar that interfered with his breathing and lent him an expression of personal grievance.

Ted wore ordinary evening clothes, though Mr. Bennett had requested robes. The carnival committee refused to release the costume, explaining that the purple cape was required the following year and that the crown had already been promised to a comic opera company.

This difficulty was managed by telling the old man that republican sentiment in Coney Island required its sovereign to appear in democratic dress on state occasions. He received the explanation with sadness but dignity.

“Modern kingship,” he observed, “is full of concessions.”

Katie came to the ceremony in a simple white dress. Genevieve had attempted to improve it, but Katie’s quiet prettiness resisted grandeur and emerged from every experiment looking like itself. Ted, watching her walk toward him, had the grave and dazzled expression of a man who has just discovered that the prize for which he entered is greater than the advertisement suggested.

Mr. Bennett sat near them in his invalid’s chair. He was pale from excitement, but happy. When the minister asked who gave the bride away, he lifted one thin hand.

“We do,” he said. “On behalf of the Crown.”

No one contradicted him.

Afterward, when the guests were eating cake and the Tennessee Bear-Cat was explaining the Double Cyclone to the minister, Katie and Ted stood for a moment beside an open window. From the street came the rattle of a passing car and, farther away, the tune of a barrel organ.

Katie looked at her husband.

“Do you ever feel guilty?”

Ted considered.

“About what?”

“About making Grandfather believe Coney Island is a kingdom.”

“It is a kingdom.”

“Ted.”

“Well, what do you call a place where a lot of people spend money to be happy, get pushed around by officials, and cheer a man in a crown?”

Katie smiled.

“That might describe several countries.”

“See?”

“And you were king for only a week.”

Ted shook his head.

“The committee said a week. But they can’t take back an election just because the parade’s over.”

“The next Mardi Gras will have another king.”

“Then he’ll be king, too. Those old countries have more than one kind—emperors, grand dukes, princes, fellows with long names. Coney Island can stand two kings.”

“But you have no subjects now.”

“I’ve got you.”

“I am not a subject.”

“No,” said Ted, putting his arm around her. “That’s right. You’re the queen.”

From across the room, Mr. Bennett saw them and smiled.

To him, the marriage had secured the peace of Europe, strengthened the maritime routes of the Mediterranean, and joined two ancient houses in perpetual friendship. To Mr. Murdoch, it promised excellent publicity. To Genevieve, it proved that male taste remained a mystery beyond the reach of science. To the Tennessee Bear-Cat, it meant that there was still cake.

And to Katie, who had never expected to be the heroine of anything, it meant that love had come back wearing a gilt crown, had bowed badly in a little room above a bookshop, and had found a way.

In a practical world, this was perhaps not statesmanship.

But it was near enough.

### The Seventh Pullet

Blenkinthrope’s life contained no feature that could reasonably be expected to survive narration.

He had a wife, a small house at Woking, a garden that produced vegetables of sound character but no imaginative distinction, and an office in the City where his duties were sufficiently important to prevent his leaving early and insufficiently interesting to justify his arriving late. Twice every weekday he travelled in the same railway carriage with men who knew his income approximately, his politics incorrectly, and his conversation only too well.

“I had a potato this year,” he announced one morning, “that weighed nearly fourteen ounces.”

The statement was received with the restrained civility accorded to a minor bereavement in an unfamiliar family.

Stevenham, who occupied the corner seat by hereditary right, looked up from his paper.

“My uncle,” he observed, “once died while addressing a meeting on the importation of Dutch bulbs. He had just denounced hyacinths as morally enervating when an ornamental cornice fell on him.”

For the remainder of the journey the carriage discussed cornices, hyacinths, public deaths, and the curious circumstance that Stevenham’s uncle had continued to hold his notes after extinction. Blenkinthrope’s potato passed into oblivion, where, from every social point of view, it had always belonged.

That evening he complained to Gorworth.

“Nothing ever happens to me,” he said. “Other men have relations who perish at public meetings, daughters who are mistaken for actresses, or dogs that bite bishops. I have a canary that moults and a wife who plays patience. If I mention the garden, people remember appointments elsewhere.”

Gorworth considered him with the indulgence one extends to a child who has failed to discover an obvious vice.

“Why should things happen to you?” he asked. “It is enough that you should say they do.”

“You mean exaggerate?”

“No. Exaggeration requires a foundation, and foundations are expensive. Invent.”

Blenkinthrope drew himself up.

“I have some self-respect.”

“Then you are badly equipped for popularity. Self-respect is useful while the world ignores one; after the world has begun to esteem one, it becomes a duplicated luxury.”

“I was taught,” protested Blenkinthrope, “that falsehood is invariably detected.”

“So was I,” said Gorworth, “together with the facts that bears ate disrespectful children and that ravens maintained unemployed prophets. A Scriptural education gives one a broad tolerance for improbability. Invent something with poultry in it. Poultry is domestic enough to inspire confidence and foolish enough to permit incident.”

Three mornings later Blenkinthrope entered his customary carriage with the expression of a man upon whom rural tragedy had laid a confidential finger.

“You seem preoccupied,” said Smith-Paddon, whose married daughter had once passed an afternoon in the same hydropathic establishment as a musical-comedy understudy.

“I have had a distressing experience,” said Blenkinthrope.

The papers descended by several inches.

“It concerns my pullets. Yesterday afternoon my wife heard a singular noise from the enclosure—a kind of subdued clucking, as though the birds were trying not to interrupt somebody. She called me. Six of the pullets were standing in a semicircle before a large grass snake. It was swaying its head from side to side, and the birds were following the movement with their eyes. They were completely mesmerised.”

“Mesmerised?” said Stevenham, with interest.

“Hopelessly. Before I could intervene, the snake struck each bird in turn. They died without resistance.”

“There were seven pullets, surely,” said Smith-Paddon, who had a tidy mind when other people supplied the materials.

“The seventh had been pecked in the left eye during infancy. Being unable to obtain a complete view of the snake, she escaped the hypnotic influence. While it was engaged with the others she attacked it from the blind side and pecked through the base of the skull.”

There was a respectful silence.

“What happened to the surviving bird?” someone asked.

“She has not laid since,” said Blenkinthrope. “One cannot expect an organism to pass through such an ordeal without nervous consequences.”

By Waterloo the Seventh Pullet had become a public character. Stevenham communicated the occurrence to an evening paper, whose agricultural correspondent, after removing the less credible details, added several of his own. An illustrated weekly printed a drawing in which the snake resembled a boa-constrictor and the pullets wore the resigned expressions of Roman ladies awaiting martyrdom. Blenkinthrope was asked for a photograph of the heroine, and supplied one of an elderly hen borrowed from a neighbour.

For nearly a fortnight he enjoyed the agreeable brevet rank conferred by association with a celebrated animal. Men who had formerly protected themselves with newspapers now invited him to sit near them. Women at suburban dinners asked whether the Seventh Pullet still suffered from insomnia. A manufacturer of poultry tonic requested permission to quote her recovery, which, until that moment, had not taken place.

Success enlarged Blenkinthrope’s moral horizon. He no longer regarded invention as a fall from virtue, but as an ascent from neglect.

Then Smith-Paddon’s daughter was photographed in the Bois beside a carriage containing a popular actress and a gentleman believed to be attached to the Roumanian Legation. She had merely been asking the way to a glove-cleaner’s, but the illustrated papers suggested diplomatic romance. For several days Smith-Paddon reigned in the railway carriage with the modest authority of one whose family might at any moment disturb the balance of Eastern Europe.

Blenkinthrope understood that reputations, like cucumbers, require incessant forcing.

“My greenhouse was invaded last night by a rat,” he remarked.

No one immediately laid down a newspaper. Rats, unaided, were beneath the carriage standard.

“It measured two feet nine inches from nose to tail,” he continued, “and had dragged three marrow-bones into a nest made entirely of gardening gloves.”

Smith-Paddon glanced up.

“When cornered, it sprang at our Belgian gardener. Fortunately, the man had served in the Congo and carried a swordstick. He transfixed it, but the animal ran the full length of the blade and bit him through the collar.”

“Is he seriously hurt?”

“The rat?”

“The gardener.”

“He is unable to pronounce words containing the letter R. The local doctor considers the symptom unprecedented.”

The story was appreciated, though not believed. That distinction, which would once have wounded Blenkinthrope, now seemed almost complimentary. Belief is a tribute paid to facts; delighted incredulity is homage to the artist.

Thereafter his life acquired a baroque fertility. A cousin of his was detained at Calais because his umbrella contained plans of a French naval harbour; an aunt, searching for a lost tortoise, discovered a forged will beneath a croquet hoop; his laundress’s infant developed the power of predicting thunderstorms, but only in Italian. He described a midnight conflict between a burglar and a mechanical trouser-press, a clergyman trapped for nine hours in a rotating bookcase, and a prize pumpkin that exploded during a horticultural sermon.

The commuters encouraged him with grave solicitude.

“And did the clergyman continue rotating during the rescue?” Stevenham would inquire.

“Only when theological differences arose among the firemen.”

“Your aunt with the forged will,” Smith-Paddon would say, “was she the same aunt who was kidnapped by the vegetarian society?”

“No; that was my wife’s aunt. The two ladies never met, owing to a dispute about parsnips.”

In this manner Blenkinthrope became indispensable without becoming reputable. His companions would have missed him as they might have missed a comic paper, but none would have lent him money on the security of an earthquake. He had gained an audience and forfeited witnesses.

At home his wife observed the transformation with limited approval.

“You seem more cheerful,” she said one evening.

“I am listened to now.”

“What do you talk about?”

“Things that happen.”

“Nothing happens here,” she said, laying out cards upon a green baize table.

Blenkinthrope felt that criticism from this quarter was ungracious. His wife’s existence was even less eventful than his own, and she had not attempted to improve it.

She was engaged upon a game of patience called The Duke’s Dilemma, a complicated contrivance involving two packs, four kings, and an almost vindictive employment of the black sevens. The game possessed a melancholy family history. Her great-aunt Lavinia had completed it once at Tunbridge Wells and died before luncheon; her mother had achieved it twenty-three years later and expired while reaching for the bell. Mrs. Blenkinthrope had consequently played it every Thursday for eleven years without success.

“The family always said it was fatal,” she remarked.

“Families say that sort of thing when they have no property to quarrel over,” said Blenkinthrope.

He watched without interest as the cards arranged themselves into the required order. The final red queen slipped into place.

“I have done it,” said Mrs. Blenkinthrope.

She leaned back with a little sigh and died.

For several minutes Blenkinthrope remained motionless. His marriage had been quiet rather than romantic, but quiet attachments can leave a formidable silence when removed. He called the doctor, sent for his wife’s sister, and performed the first stunned offices of bereavement with a sincerity that would have surprised his travelling companions.

Then, amid his grief, another consciousness stirred.

A fatal game of patience, repeated through three generations, was not merely a domestic calamity. It was an event. It possessed heredity, mystery, coincidence, and a card-table. Properly presented, it might occupy half a column; with portraits of the three ladies and a diagram of the concluding cards, it might command a page.

For once, reality had risen to his requirements.

He telegraphed to Stevenham: WIFE COMPLETED FAMILY PATIENCE GAME FATAL TO MOTHER AND GREAT-AUNT. DIED INSTANTLY. UNIQUE CASE.

Stevenham replied: EXCELLENT. TRY ANOTHER.

Blenkinthrope sent a detailed account to the evening paper that had immortalised the Seventh Pullet. The editor returned it with a pencilled observation: “We fear our readers have become familiar with Mr. Blenkinthrope’s ingenious natural-history contributions.”

He called upon Smith-Paddon.

“My wife is dead,” he said, “immediately after completing The Duke’s Dilemma.”

Smith-Paddon looked pained.

“My dear fellow, there are occasions when this habit of yours becomes deficient in taste.”

“But she is actually dead.”

“Then you should not embroider it. Heart failure is sufficiently respectable.”

“The same thing killed her mother and her great-aunt.”

“Of course,” said Smith-Paddon wearily. “And I suppose the Seventh Pullet turned over the final queen.”

The official notice, when it appeared, stated only that Amelia, beloved wife of Henry Blenkinthrope, had died suddenly at her residence. A local paper added, on medical authority, that death was due to heart failure. No cards were mentioned. The tragic dynasty of The Duke’s Dilemma passed unrecorded into family memory, where it was later confused with an uncle who had choked at whist.

In the railway carriage Blenkinthrope made one last effort.

“I can bring you the doctor,” he said. “I can show you the cards as they lay.”

“My dear Blenkinthrope,” replied Stevenham, “you have given us mesmerised poultry, diplomatic umbrellas, bilingual infants, and a rat that impaired a Belgian’s consonants. You cannot expect us to become credulous merely because you introduce a corpse.”

There are men who forgive disbelief in their virtues but resent scepticism concerning their misfortunes. Blenkinthrope withdrew from the company. He altered his train, abandoned the celebrated carriage, and travelled thereafter among strangers, who had no reason to doubt him and, as yet, no particular reason to hear him.

For a time he sat silent. Then one wet morning a mild-looking man opposite lowered his newspaper and glanced at the basket by Blenkinthrope’s feet.

“Garden produce?” he asked.

“Beetroot,” said Blenkinthrope. “One of them is nearly eleven ounces.”

“That is a good size.”

Encouraged by this modest reception, Blenkinthrope described the soil in which it had been grown. On the following day he spoke of his canary, which had lately changed the order of two notes in its song. No illustrated paper sought particulars, and no commuter demanded corroboration.

He had returned to truth, and truth had restored him to obscurity.

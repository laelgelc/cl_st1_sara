# JAMES MADISON: THE STUDENT AND STATESMAN

Among the men whose labors established the American Republic, there were some who commanded by the splendor of military fame, some who captivated assemblies by the force of eloquence, and others whose boldness of temper made them conspicuous in every conflict; but James Madison belonged to none of these classes. He was neither imposing in person, loud in debate, nor eager for applause. His frame was slight, his manner retiring, and his voice unequal to the stormy contentions of popular assemblies. Yet, by a close application of his faculties, by exactness in inquiry, by perseverance in duty, and by a virtue which no temptation of ambition could corrupt, he rose from the quiet estate of a Virginia planter to become one of the chief architects of the government of the United States.

He was born in Virginia, amid that rural society in which property, family attachment, and local independence exercised a powerful influence upon character. His father possessed the habits of a substantial planter, and his household exhibited the order, sobriety, and industry which belonged to the better class of provincial life. The son was not distinguished in childhood by those brilliant sallies which are often remembered, with more affection than truth, of eminent men. He was thoughtful, modest, and patient. Books attracted him more than sports; knowledge was not an ornament to be displayed, but a treasure to be gained by labor; and, while others were content with such attainments as convenience required, he commenced that severe discipline of the mind which continued through nearly the whole of his life.

At college his application became extraordinary. He did not merely pursue the regular studies, but enlarged their boundaries, abridged his hours of repose, and pressed forward with an ardor which impaired a constitution never robust. History, ancient government, philosophy, theology, and the principles of law were examined with the same conscientious care. If he lacked the quick and dazzling genius which seizes at once upon the admiration of mankind, he possessed what is frequently of greater public utility—the power of investigating a subject until its foundations were uncovered, its relations arranged, and its consequences distinctly foreseen. The difference was important. The brilliant youth often promises what the mature man never performs; Madison promised little, but every year added something durable to his intellectual possessions.

On returning to Virginia, he found the colonies approaching that great contest which was to dissolve their connexion with Great Britain. The controversy demanded courage; but it demanded also knowledge, temper, and a capacity to distinguish liberty from disorder. Madison entered public life without ostentation. He neither courted the multitude nor endeavored to render himself agreeable by profuse entertainment or popular flattery. Indeed, when he first presented himself for the suffrages of his neighbors, his unwillingness to “treat the voters,” according to a custom then too prevalent, contributed to his defeat. This disappointment, honorable alike to his principles and his future fame, taught him no crooked lesson. He would obtain office by confidence, or he would remain without it.

Religious liberty early engaged his warmest solicitude. In Virginia, as in other portions of the country, ancient establishments and civil preferences had survived after the reasons alleged for them had begun to disappear. Men might contend for political freedom while yet refusing complete freedom of conscience; they might resist taxation imposed by a distant Parliament, and still permit burdens to be laid upon the citizen for the maintenance of religious opinions which he did not profess. Such inconsistency could not escape the penetrating judgment of Madison. To him conscience was not a favor granted by government, but a right which existed before government and beyond its lawful authority.

When a declaration of rights was considered, he favored language broad enough to protect the free exercise of religion, and not merely to commend a limited toleration. Toleration itself seemed to imply that one class of men possessed a superior title, and graciously permitted another to think. Madison desired no such humiliating permission. He would have every citizen stand equal before the law, answerable for his faith to God alone, and restrained only when his conduct injured the equal rights of others. This principle, maintained at first by a comparatively youthful legislator, became one of the noblest distinctions of Virginia and afterwards of the Union.

During the Revolutionary struggle he was called to more extensive service. In the councils of Virginia, and afterwards in those of the confederacy, his habits of investigation rendered him peculiarly useful. The new nation possessed enthusiasm, armies, and an acknowledged cause; but it lacked regular revenues, adequate authority, and the means of enforcing common resolutions upon jealous and independent States. Congress could recommend, entreat, and apportion; it could not always obtain obedience. Public credit declined, engagements were delayed, and the necessities of war were met by expedients which increased the embarrassments of peace.

Madison perceived that liberty could not be preserved by weakness alone. He had opposed arbitrary authority; he did not therefore conclude that all authority was arbitrary. On the contrary, he understood that a government unable to perform its just obligations invites contempt abroad and disorder at home. He favored stronger national finances, a more certain provision for public debts, and such powers over commerce as would enable the confederacy to act with dignity toward foreign nations. The several States, pursuing separate interests and commercial discriminations, could neither secure reciprocity nor retaliate with effect upon restrictions imposed by Europe. Where thirteen voices were required to utter one command, the command was too often reduced to a whisper.

These opinions were not adopted hastily. Madison studied the failures of ancient confederacies, the rivalries of neighboring republics, and the causes by which popular governments had been converted into factions, oligarchies, or military dominions. The spectacle did not make him distrust liberty; it made him anxious that liberty should be guarded by wisdom. A people may possess courage sufficient to gain independence, and yet want patience sufficient to organize it. The great question was not whether America should have power, for power of some kind must exist wherever society exists; the question was whether that power should be distributed, limited, and regulated so that energy and freedom might sustain, rather than destroy, each other.

After the immediate danger of the Revolution had passed, the defects of the confederacy became daily more apparent. Commerce languished under conflicting regulations; public creditors doubted the promises of Congress; foreign governments observed the impotence of the Union and delayed concessions; local jealousies obstructed national measures; and men began to inquire whether the work achieved by so much blood was to perish from the want of political foresight. Madison, never violent in manner but immovable in purpose, devoted himself to reform.

In January, 1786, while questions of revenue and commerce agitated the public councils, another contest in Virginia gave occasion for the exercise of his highest principles. A proposal had been made to levy an assessment for the support of teachers of religion. Its advocates included men of worth, and the measure was defended as favorable to morals; but Madison discerned beneath its plausible appearance the dangerous doctrine that civil rulers may tax one man for the propagation of another man’s belief. He prepared a remonstrance, moderate in expression, exact in reasoning, and powerful from the accumulation of its arguments. Religion, he maintained, was exempt from the cognizance of civil society; the same authority which could establish Christianity might establish one particular form of it; and a power admitted in small degree might, under less virtuous rulers, be extended without limit.

The proposed assessment was defeated, and a more liberal system prevailed. Thus, before Madison became famous as the defender of the Federal Constitution, he had assisted in securing that liberty of conscience without which constitutions are but imperfect safeguards of human dignity. The victory was the more honorable because it was not won by appealing to hostility against religion. He respected faith too sincerely to make it dependent upon taxation, political favor, or legislative patronage.

The commercial embarrassments of the States next demanded a common consultation. A meeting was held to consider their trade and the means of rendering their regulations more uniform; and though the immediate attendance was small, the proposal issued in a recommendation for a more general convention. Madison labored steadily to procure the appointment of delegates and to convince reluctant men that partial remedies would no longer suffice. He did not desire a consolidation which should extinguish the States, nor a temporary accommodation which should leave the central infirmity untouched. He desired a government national in its proper objects, federal in its composition, and limited by a written instrument.

In May, 1787, the Convention assembled at Philadelphia. Never had America witnessed a council upon whose prudence so much depended. The members differed in local interests, political theories, and estimates of danger. The larger States feared that equal suffrage would sacrifice the majority; the smaller feared that proportional representation would surrender them to powerful neighbors. Some dreaded monarchy; others dreaded anarchy; and all were compelled to deliberate in the presence of recent failure, yet without any certain model for their guidance.

Madison entered the Convention prepared by an extent of previous study possessed by few of his associates. He had collected the lessons of history, compared confederacies, examined the operations of the State governments, and arranged the defects which experience had disclosed. He spoke often, but not merely to speak. His manner was calm, his reasoning close, and his observations directed toward principles rather than personalities. What he lacked in sonorous delivery was supplied by knowledge. A weak argument did not become strong because uttered loudly; a strong argument did not become weak because expressed by a slight and modest man.

He favored a government capable of acting upon individuals and not dependent wholly upon requisitions addressed to the States. He supported a legislature representing the people, an executive with sufficient energy to execute the laws, and a judiciary independent enough to interpret them. Yet he knew that every grant of authority must be accompanied by limitation, and that ambition, interest, and faction could not be excluded from human affairs by pious declarations. They must be so divided and balanced that one power should restrain another, while all remained responsible to the people from whom their just authority proceeded.

The Convention was not a scene in which any single member commanded universal assent. Its final instrument was the result of concession as well as theory. Madison did not obtain every arrangement he preferred; no honest delegate could have done so without destroying the possibility of union. He accepted compromises which seemed necessary, opposed propositions which appeared dangerous, and yielded when persistence would have defeated a greater good. This was not inconsistency. Political wisdom does not consist in demanding the whole of what is desirable until even what is necessary is lost.

The Constitution established a government of enumerated powers, separated into departments, and deriving its authority from the people of the United States. It preserved the existence of the States while providing an executive, legislature, and judiciary for the Union. It gave Congress power over national commerce and revenue, furnished the means of executing national laws, and replaced recommendation by lawful command within the appointed sphere. When the Convention adjourned, the instrument had yet to encounter a tribunal more numerous and less manageable than that which framed it—the judgment of the people.

Opposition immediately arose. Some objected that the proposed government was too energetic; others that it was too remote. The absence of a declaration of rights occasioned sincere alarm, while predictions of monarchy, consolidation, and oppression were spread with passionate confidence. Madison did not treat every opponent as an enemy of liberty. Many were patriots whose apprehensions differed from his own; and because those apprehensions were honest, they required argument, not abuse.

In conjunction with Alexander Hamilton and John Jay, he prepared a series of papers explaining and defending the Constitution. These essays, known as *The Federalist*, examined the necessity of union, the insufficiency of the existing confederacy, the distribution of powers, the construction of the Senate, the executive, the judiciary, and the means by which republican liberty might be secured over an extensive territory. Madison’s contributions were distinguished by precision and calmness. He showed that faction could not be abolished without abolishing liberty itself, but that its effects might be controlled by enlarging the sphere, multiplying interests, and preventing any unjust majority from easily uniting against the rights of others.

These papers were written amid urgent duties and within short intervals, yet they have endured as among the most celebrated expositions of constitutional government. They were not mere electioneering addresses, framed to expire with the occasion. Their arguments reached beyond the immediate controversy and illustrated the permanent difficulties of popular rule. If they occasionally defended provisions which experience would modify, they exhibited throughout a masterly comprehension of the instrument and of the human passions upon which it was expected to operate.

In the Virginia convention called to consider ratification, the struggle was severe. The opposition included men of commanding abilities, practiced in debate and possessed of extensive popular influence. Madison could not overpower them by voice or theatrical gesture. He met them with facts, clauses, distinctions, and consequences. Day after day, though bodily weakness rendered the exertion painful, he explained the proposed system, refuted forced constructions, and urged that the dangers of disunion were more certain than the imagined dangers of lawful federal authority. Virginia at length ratified the Constitution, accompanying the act with proposed amendments and a declaration of reserved rights.

Thus, it is seen, Madison rendered a threefold service: he assisted in procuring the Convention, bore a principal part in framing the instrument, and employed his pen and voice in securing its acceptance. Either service might have established a reputation. United, they placed him among the foremost founders of the Republic.

When the new government commenced its operations, he was elected to the House of Representatives. Although he had supported the Constitution against the charge that it necessarily endangered liberty, he did not forget the wish for additional securities. He introduced amendments which, after consideration and modification, became the great declaration of personal rights annexed to the instrument. Freedom of religion, of speech, and of the press; security against unreasonable searches; protection in criminal proceedings; and the reservation of undelegated powers were thus explicitly affirmed. The man who defended the Constitution without a bill of rights assisted in supplying one when the proper authority had been established. What contradiction was there in this? He had contended that the government should first be accepted, and had promised that reasonable amendments should afterwards be considered. He fulfilled the promise.

Questions of finance soon divided those who had acted together for union. Alexander Hamilton, as the chief financial officer of the administration, proposed measures intended to establish credit, consolidate obligations, and connect powerful interests with the support of the general government. Madison agreed that public faith must be preserved and national finances restored; but he became alarmed at constructions which appeared to enlarge federal authority beyond the terms of the compact. The establishment of a national bank especially raised the question whether Congress might exercise powers not expressly granted merely because they were convenient to an enumerated object.

Here commenced that political separation which afterwards formed the principal parties of the country. Hamilton desired an energetic interpretation of national power; Madison feared that implication, once released from close restraint, might carry the government toward consolidation. With Thomas Jefferson he became a leader of the Republican interest, which professed jealousy of concentrated authority, sympathy with popular institutions, economy in expenditure, and a strict construction of the Constitution.

Party warfare was bitter, and neither side escaped injustice. The friends of one system charged their opponents with hostility to government; the friends of the other charged theirs with hostility to liberty. Madison, though earnest, was less fitted by nature for personal denunciation than many of his associates. His strongest weapon was argument. He could trace a principle through its remote consequences, compare a measure with the limitations of the Constitution, and expose an assumption of power without converting every error of judgment into a crime of intention.

The troubled relations of Europe soon increased domestic contention. War abroad interrupted commerce, inflamed sympathy, and exposed American neutrality to repeated injury. One party looked with greater distrust upon revolutionary France; another retained resentment against Great Britain. In this dangerous condition, laws were passed respecting foreigners and political publications which Madison regarded as obnoxious to constitutional freedom. The Alien and Sedition Acts armed the administration, in his judgment, with powers neither granted nor compatible with a free press.

In 1798 he prepared the Virginia Resolutions. These resolves declared that the federal government was limited by the compact from which its powers were derived, and that, in cases of a deliberate, palpable, and dangerous exercise of powers not granted, the States had a duty to interpose for maintaining the authorities, rights, and liberties appertaining to them. The language afterwards became the subject of constructions which Madison himself did not approve. He had not intended to authorize each State, at its sole pleasure, to dissolve the Union or annul national laws by an ordinary act of its legislature. His purpose was to awaken constitutional resistance through the united opinion and legitimate action of the States and people, not to substitute thirteen arbitrary sovereignties for one arbitrary government.

After a period of retirement, he was called, in 1801, to the office of secretary of state under Jefferson. The friendship between these two eminent Virginians had been strengthened by long consultation and common political principles. Jefferson possessed a more brilliant and expansive imagination; Madison supplied caution, analysis, and exactness. Their intercourse exhibited not the jealousy so frequently observed among distinguished men, but a generous confidence, each acknowledging the abilities and integrity of the other.

The duties of the department were rendered difficult by the violence of European conflict. Great Britain and France, contending for mastery, issued restrictions which treated neutral commerce as an instrument of war. American vessels were seized, cargoes condemned, and peaceful trade exposed to decrees and orders over which the United States possessed no control. More offensive still was the British claim to search American ships and impress seamen alleged to be British subjects. Under the exercise of this pretension, men who claimed American citizenship were forced into foreign service. The national flag no longer furnished certain protection to those who sailed beneath it.

Madison conducted negotiations with patience. He preferred accommodation to resentment and peaceful commercial pressure to war. Yet diplomacy was continually embarrassed by European necessities and contempt for a young republic believed to be incapable of effective resistance. Agreements promised relief and then failed; restrictions were modified in form while injury continued in substance. The administration attempted, by withholding American commerce, to compel respect for neutral rights. The measure inflicted losses upon the offending nations, but it also bore heavily upon American merchants and seamen, producing dissatisfaction at home without procuring complete justice abroad.

In 1808 Madison was elected President of the United States. He entered the office with extensive knowledge of government, but with none of the military renown which had surrounded Washington, nor the personal fascination which increased Jefferson’s influence. The age demanded firmness, though it afforded little opportunity for peaceful glory. Commerce remained entangled in European war; Britain adhered to maritime pretensions; France pursued her own system of confiscation; and every attempt at provisional accommodation was followed by disappointment.

Madison desired peace. His opponents sometimes treated reluctance as weakness, while impatient men among his own friends demanded measures more decisive than the country was prepared to sustain. But peace cannot honorably be purchased by a permanent surrender of rights. The impressment of sailors, the violation of commerce, and repeated affronts to national sovereignty produced an excitement which no administration could indefinitely restrain. Negotiation had been tried; commercial measures had been tried; remonstrance had been tried. The British Government refused to relinquish the claim to its supposed right, and nothing remained but war.

In June, 1812, Congress declared the existence of war with Great Britain. Madison accepted the decision not with exultation, but with the solemnity proper to a ruler who knew the calamities which must follow. The country was imperfectly prepared. Its regular army was small, its financial arrangements defective, its military officers of unequal merit, and the opposition to the contest violent in several portions of the Union. Some accused the administration of having entered the war to assist France; others denounced it as unnecessary, ruinous, and unconstitutional. The President had therefore to contend not only against a powerful foreign nation, but against divided councils and the consequences of previous neglect.

The first military operations brought disappointment. Plans formed with confidence failed in execution; invasions produced surrender or retreat; and the want of discipline could not be supplied by patriotic language. Yet on the ocean, where the enemy was believed irresistible, American vessels gained victories which elevated the national spirit. Individual courage and seamanship vindicated the honor of the flag, while upon the inland waters success and disaster alternated.

On March 4, 1813, Madison commenced his second presidential term. The war had become the principal concern of the administration, absorbing revenue, appointing commanders, and demanding incessant exertion from a man whose strength was more intellectual than physical. He neither concealed failures nor abandoned the contest because victory was delayed. New preparations were made; experience corrected some errors; and the nation, though burdened, gradually learned the severe lessons of war.

The conflict was accompanied by the most vehement political opposition. Public credit was strained, trade interrupted, and sectional discontent increased. Meetings condemned the administration; newspapers assailed the President with a freedom which itself proved that the government had not adopted those violent measures formerly opposed by Madison. He did not seek safety through a sedition law. Abuse was endured as one of the inconveniences of liberty, and constitutional forms were maintained while the enemy threatened the country.

In 1814 the danger became more immediate. Released from part of the European struggle, Great Britain could direct greater force against the United States. An invading army advanced upon Washington. The preparations for defence were insufficient; the opposing troops were dispersed; and the capital was left exposed. Public buildings were burned in a wanton act of destruction, and the executive officers were compelled to withdraw.

No elaborate description is required to heighten the humiliation. The residence of the President, the Capitol, and other structures associated with the civil government were given to the flames. Yet the government did not perish with its buildings. Madison remained at his duty, moving amid confusion, consulting with his officers, and exerting the authority which circumstances permitted. His conduct was not that of a military hero commanding victorious legions; it was the quieter fortitude of a civil magistrate who refused to despair when the visible seats of power were destroyed.

The burning of Washington, instead of ending the war, aroused indignation. Elsewhere the enemy met a more vigorous resistance, and American successes restored confidence. The nation had suffered enough to desire peace, but not enough to accept dishonor. Commissioners were already engaged in negotiation at Ghent, where the claims that had caused the war were discussed amid the changing fortunes of Europe and America.

At length a treaty was concluded. It did not contain an express abandonment of every maritime pretension against which the United States had contended; yet the conditions which had rendered those pretensions immediately destructive were altered, prisoners and conquests were to be restored, and peace was re-established without national submission. The tidings were received with extraordinary joy. Almost at the same time, intelligence of a brilliant American victory, gained before knowledge of the treaty had crossed the ocean, magnified the public satisfaction. A contest begun in division ended in a stronger sense of nationality.

Madison welcomed peace without claiming for himself an extravagant triumph. He had not desired war; he had accepted it when the alternatives appeared exhausted; he had sustained it amid failure, abuse, and invasion; and he now turned willingly to the arts of civil prosperity. The ordeal had modified some of his earlier opinions. Experience showed that a nation exposed to foreign conflict required sound credit, adequate revenue, manufactures necessary to defence, and institutions capable of supporting public operations.

He had once opposed a national bank as unauthorized and dangerous; after the war, when the disorders of the currency and the embarrassments of finance became manifest, he approved the establishment of another bank under circumstances which, in his judgment, gave the measure constitutional sanction through precedent and national acceptance. Adversaries called this inconsistency. But obstinacy is not integrity. A statesman who refuses to learn from experience may preserve the appearance of uniformity while sacrificing the welfare of his country. Madison did not renounce the principle of limited government; he admitted that long practice, public necessity, and settled construction had changed the constitutional question with which he was required to deal.

Measures were likewise approved for restoring revenue, encouraging national independence, and stabilizing the postwar economy. He remained cautious respecting projects which might exceed the enumerated powers, even when he acknowledged their public utility. To distinguish between what is desirable and what is constitutionally authorized was, with him, a constant duty. Ends did not sanctify every means; for if benevolent objects could enlarge authority without amendment, no written limitation would remain secure.

In December, 1816, the administration approached its close. Eight years of perplexity had aged the President, yet his reputation had survived calamities which might have overwhelmed a less patient character. He had been denounced as timid before the war and tyrannical after entering it; blamed for insufficient preparation and condemned for measures intended to supply the deficiency; ridiculed when the capital fell and forgotten by some when peace restored prosperity. Through these changes he preserved that mildness of temper which neither praise nor censure could easily disturb.

On March 4, 1817, he retired from office and returned to Montpelier. There, amid the scenes of his youth, he resumed the occupations of a private citizen. He did not employ retirement in exciting opposition to his successors or in demanding public gratitude. His correspondence remained extensive; questions of constitutional interpretation were submitted to him; visitors sought the recollections of one who had witnessed the formation of the government; and his opinions continued to possess that weight which belongs not merely to age, but to a life of tested integrity.

His domestic circumstances were not free from anxiety, and the management of a Virginia estate presented difficulties which public fame could not remove. Yet he retained his interest in education, agriculture, history, and the preservation of the Union. He looked with alarm upon doctrines which converted his earlier resolutions into an authority for disunion. The Constitution, in his view, was not a league revocable at the will of any single member, but a government founded by the people, operating within a limited sphere, and capable of lawful correction through the appointed processes.

In 1829 he made his last conspicuous appearance in public life as a member of the convention called to revise the constitution of Virginia. He was then advanced in years and physically feeble. The active leaders belonged to another generation, and many who had struggled beside him in the establishment of independence and union were gone. When Madison entered the assembly, the respect paid to him was not a compliment to office, for he held none; it was the homage of Virginia to a citizen whose labors had become a portion of the nation’s history.

He spoke with difficulty, but his presence itself carried instruction. The modest student who had once feared that weakness of body might exclude him from usefulness had outlived the greater part of his illustrious associates. He had seen colonies become States, a confederacy fail, a Constitution arise, parties contend, foreign powers insult the flag, an invading army burn the capital, and peace restore the country to prosperity. Through all these transformations, he had remained devoted to representative government and civil liberty.

His final years were passed in comparative tranquillity. He arranged papers, answered inquiries, and endeavored to preserve an accurate understanding of the constitutional settlement to which his life had been so largely devoted. He did not pretend that the instrument was perfect. No work of man could possess that character. But he believed that its divisions, checks, amendments, and dependence upon popular authority furnished the best security yet devised against the opposite dangers of anarchy and oppression.

When death closed his long career, the nation remembered him as legislator, constitutional counsellor, political writer, secretary of state, and President. Yet titles alone do not explain his distinction. Many men have held high offices without increasing their dignity; Madison gave consequence to office by the conscientious discharge of its duties. His fame was not founded upon conquest, wealth, or a commanding exterior. It rested upon thoughts patiently matured, papers carefully composed, responsibilities faithfully met, and principles defended through discouragement.

Why, then, did he succeed? Was it because nature had bestowed upon him the strongest body, the most brilliant fancy, the loudest eloquence, or the boldest confidence? Assuredly not. His health was delicate, his manner diffident, his delivery unimposing, and his talents, though of a high order, did not astonish by sudden exhibition. His greatness was chiefly the greatness of preparation. He studied while others were idle; he investigated while others asserted; he persevered while others became weary; and when a public question arrived, he was ready because he had labored before the hour of necessity.

His life teaches that integrity is not a substitute for knowledge, nor knowledge for industry. He united all three. Without knowledge, virtue may be deceived; without virtue, knowledge may become dangerous; without industry, both may remain useless. Madison disciplined his understanding, restrained his passions, respected the convictions of others, and submitted private preference to public duty. Even his errors were rarely the offspring of vanity or corruption. They belonged to the difficulties of events and to the imperfection inseparable from human judgment.

Jefferson, who knew him in the intimacy of friendship and the trials of administration, regarded him as a man of uncommon purity, soundness, and resource; his associates in the Convention respected the accuracy of his knowledge; his political opponents, when the heats of controversy had passed, could not justly deny the sobriety of his motives. What more could be said of a republican statesman than that he sought power for public ends, exercised it under law, surrendered it without regret, and carried into retirement an unsullied name?

Thus the frail scholar of Virginia became a principal defender of religious liberty, a reformer of the confederacy, an architect of the Constitution, an expositor of its powers, a guardian of its amendments, a leader in political opposition, a minister in difficult diplomacy, and the President who sustained the nation through its second war with Great Britain. At every stage, patient labor supplied what nature had withheld.

The monument of such a man is not found in marble alone. It stands in the freedom of conscience which he defended; in the Constitution which he helped to frame; in the papers by which he explained it; in the restraint he imposed upon authority when opposition tempted retaliation; and in the peaceful retirement which proved that he valued republican government more than personal command. O that we could have such a monument left to mark our memory!

I do not pretend that I can tell a humorous story. I have tried it before audiences of unusual patience, and the result has generally been a silence so complete that one could hear the confidence leaking out of me. But I know how such a story ought to be told. This is a common division of talent. A man may understand navigation perfectly and still prefer to remain ashore while other people drown.

The American humorous story, understand, is a work of art; the English comic story and the French witty story are incidents. The humorous story depends upon the manner of telling; the others depend upon the matter. A comic story says, “Here is something funny,” and strikes you with it. A witty story says, “Observe how clever this is,” and bows. The humorous story puts the thing in your pocket without your knowledge, and lets you discover it next Thursday.

The comic and witty stories are brief. They make for the point by the shortest road, and arrive there with flags, trumpets, and police protection. The humorous story wanders wherever the weather is agreeable. It may stop to examine a fence, inquire after a sick aunt, or explain why the narrator has never trusted a red cow. It hides its destination even from itself.

The teller of the comic story laughs first, so the audience will know what is expected of it. He emphasizes the joke, repeats it, and, if it still exhibits signs of life, kills it with an explanation. In print he surrounds it with italics, CAPITAL LETTERS, and a regiment of exclamation-points. These marks have a discouraging effect. After meeting six exclamation-points in a row, a sensitive reader may renounce amusement and enter the ministry.

The teller of the humorous story must preserve an innocence deep enough to deceive a coroner. He must appear not to know that anything amusing has occurred. If he smiles, it is a professional disaster. If he laughs at his own story, he ought to be taken outdoors and allowed to begin life again.

Let me set down a military anecdote in the comic manner.

During a battle an officer saw a soldier carrying a wounded comrade toward the rear.

“Where are you taking that man?” cried the officer.

“To the hospital, sir!”

“But, you idiot, his HEAD IS OFF!!!”

The soldier looked down in amazement and exclaimed:

“Why, the rascal told me it was his LEG!!!”

There is the whole thing. It is compact, businesslike, and can be shipped by freight. You can see the joke coming from the second line, armed with official papers. The capitals make certain that no one mistakes the head for scenery. It is a comic story, and any person can tell it—though many persons will.

But put that anecdote into the hands of a master of the humorous method, and it becomes a voyage. I once heard it told by an old farmer named Pelton. At least I believe his name was Pelton. He said it was, then reflected and allowed that Pelton was his wife’s cousin, while his own name was Peters; afterward he remembered that Peters was the man who sold him the gray mule, and concluded that his name did not bear upon the story.

He began in a low and conscientious voice:

“It was along about the war—though I won’t say which year, because I had a poor head for years even then. There was a young fellow in our company named Barker. Or Baker. It was something connected with bread, unless I am thinking of Miller, which I may be. He was a tall, short-built kind of man, dark-complected except where the sun had got at him, and he had one blue eye. The other was blue also, but not the same blue; still, you would not notice the difference without you had some reason.”

Here Mr. Pelton—Peters—stopped and considered the fire.

“His father kept a place down by the creek. Not the big creek. The big creek was over beyond the Widow Hannigan’s, and she was not a widow at that time, though everybody called her one because Hannigan traveled in farm implements and was never home long enough to interfere with the title. No, this was the little creek, which was dry most of the year except when there was water in it.

“Barker had a dog. That dog is not in this story, but he looked like he might be, and I mention him so you won’t be waiting for him later.”

Then came an account of the dog’s disposition, ancestry, appetite, and failure to distinguish Sunday trousers from ordinary trousers. Mr. Pelton followed this with a description of the road to the battlefield, correcting the number of turns three times and finally admitting that he had traveled by another road.

By now his listeners were leaning forward. Some desired the conclusion; others desired revenge.

“Well,” he continued, “the fighting got uncommon severe. There was smoke everywhere, and noise enough to make conversation uncertain. Along toward evening—unless it was before dinner—Barker comes walking back carrying a fellow on his shoulders. The captain sees him and says—No, it wasn’t the captain. The captain had gone off with a fever. It was Lieutenant Brace. They called him Brace because that wasn’t his name. He says, ‘Where are you taking that man?’

“Barker says, ‘To the hospital.’

“Brace looks at the fellow for quite a while. Then he says, ‘But his head’s gone.’”

Mr. Pelton paused again, not dramatically, but as if trying to recollect whether he had left a gate open in 1862.

“Barker looked around at the man, and then he said the fellow had told him it was his leg.”

That was all. He did not lift his voice; he did not smile; he seemed faintly troubled by the unreliability of wounded men. The audience laughed until several respectable citizens became structurally unsound. Mr. Pelton gazed at them with compassion, as though laughter were an illness common in towns.

The nub of a humorous story is often slurred. It is dropped carelessly, as if it were an item the teller would have removed had he noticed it. Sometimes the chief effect lies not even in the point, but in an incongruity planted beside the road.

Artemus Ward understood this. He told of a man who had lost every tooth in his head, and added, after the proper interval, that this misfortune did not prevent him from beating a drum better than anybody in the county.

Now there is no legitimate connection between teeth and a drum. The mind nevertheless expects one, because the teller has invited expectation into the room and given it a chair. During the pause, the audience imagines that the toothless man will perform some astonishing dental feat—bite horseshoes, whistle through granite, or eat corn in a new and scientific manner. Then the drum arrives. It answers a question nobody asked and leaves the original question unemployed. That is incongruity.

The pause is exceedingly important. It must be exactly the right length. A short pause is merely punctuation; a long pause is a railway delay. The proper pause allows expectation to rise, stretch, look around, and become uncomfortable. Then the concluding phrase should be delivered gently, with the air of a man laying down a grocery receipt.

You can practice pauses in ordinary conversation, but you will lose friends. Tell a neighbor, “I saw your brother yesterday, and he said—” Then stop. If you wait four seconds, you possess his attention. If you wait ten, you possess his entire nervous system. If you wait thirty, he will confess things his brother never knew.

In the ghost story the pause becomes almost the story itself. The finest example is the old tale of the golden arm. It is simple enough when stated without art.

A miserly husband buries his wife, whose artificial arm is made of gold. At night he digs it up and takes it home. The dead wife follows him, asking who has her golden arm. At last she catches him.

Told so, the story has no more terror than a bank notice. Properly performed, it can lift a healthy listener out of her chair.

The room should be dim. Choose, without appearing to choose, one member of the audience who startles easily. Do not look at her too often. Once is enough; twice warns her; three times converts the affair into an appointment.

Begin quietly.

There was an old man who loved gold better than warmth, company, food, or any other thing which costs money. His wife had a golden arm. How she obtained it is not important. If you explain that, you will be lost in medical history and metal prices, and the grave will grow cold while you talk.

When the woman died, the old man buried her with the arm because the neighbors were watching. But after midnight he took a lantern and a shovel and went back to the cemetery.

Here lower the voice.

The wind moved through the trees—so-o-o—then stopped. The old man dug down to the coffin. He lifted the lid. He took the golden arm. He shut the coffin and hurried home.

Pause.

As he crossed the cemetery, he heard a voice behind him, far away:

“Who-o-o has my go-o-olden arm?”

He walked faster.

At the gate it came again, nearer:

“Who-o-o has my go-o-olden arm?”

He began to run.

At his house he bolted the door, climbed the stairs, and got into bed. For a while there was silence.

This silence must be allowed to mature. Do not hurry it. Look toward the imaginary door. Let your face suggest that you have heard something but hope you have not.

Then make a soft sound: scratch—scratch—scratch.

The thing was at the door.

“Who-o-o has my go-o-olden arm?”

The latch lifted.

Cree-e-eak.

It came into the hall.

The old man pulled the blankets over his head.

A step sounded on the stairs.

One step.

Then another.

Then another.

Each step should be given separately. A staircase hurried over is a staircase wasted.

The voice came from the landing:

“Who-o-o has my go-o-olden arm?”

Now the thing crossed the upper floor.

Softly.

Slowly.

It reached the bedroom door.

“Who-o-o has my go-o-olden arm?”

The door opened.

It entered the room.

It came toward the bed.

At this point reduce the voice until the listeners must lean forward to hear it. The selected person will lean with the others. That is what you want.

The thing stood beside the old man.

It bent over him.

It whispered:

“Who-o-o has my go-o-olden arm?”

Pause.

Not a theatrical pause, mind you. A theatrical pause announces itself and wears a cape. This pause must appear accidental, as if the storyteller had forgotten the rest. Let it continue until the room becomes one listening organ. Then turn suddenly upon the selected person, point directly at her, and shout:

“YOU’VE GOT IT!”

If the pause has been exact, she will spring from her chair, possibly leaving a portion of her composure behind. The others will laugh, partly at her and partly from relief that the dead woman has accepted substitute payment.

That is the American humorous story: not the golden arm, not the headless soldier, not the toothless drummer, but the manner—the grave face, the wandering road, the innocent incongruity, and the pause that holds an audience by the throat while pretending merely to rest.

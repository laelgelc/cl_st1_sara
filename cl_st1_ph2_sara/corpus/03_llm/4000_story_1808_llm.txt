# The Goodly Country

In the years immediately following the Revolutionary War, when the new Republic possessed more land than money and more hopes than roads, there appeared in the eastern states a number of pamphlets describing the country beyond the Ohio. These little books were composed by men who had acres to sell, and therefore saw nature at her most obliging.

The soil, they declared, was black, deep, and inexhaustible; wheat grew almost without cultivation, maize rose higher than a horseman, and grapes hung in such profusion that wine might soon flow as commonly as cider in Connecticut. The forests abounded in deer, bear, turkey, and every useful creature, while the rivers contained fish of a size likely to tax the belief of any reader unacquainted with western waters. Winters were short, summers temperate, taxes insignificant, and poverty nearly impossible. One advertisement ventured to intimate that frost was scarcely known upon the Ohio.

Perhaps the reader has observed that frost is less attentive to pamphlets than purchasers are.

Among those who read these descriptions were officers and soldiers of the late Continental Army. They had been paid in promises, certificates, and gratitude, none of which could be planted; yet they possessed habits of command, endurance, and cooperation, and they were willing to exchange their claims against Congress for western land. Their leader was General Rufus Putnam, a Massachusetts man, surveyor, veteran, and organizer, who had spent enough time in camps to understand that paradise, if occupied by human beings, requires regulations.

During the winter of 1787–88, Putnam’s pioneers traveled westward from New England. Their road crossed Pennsylvania by mountains which had not been mentioned in the advertisements. Wagons sank in mud, axes opened passages through the woods, and snow lodged upon blankets at night; nevertheless, the company pushed on to the Youghiogheny River, where boats were built for the descent to the Ohio. The principal craft was named the *Mayflower*, a title at once pious and ambitious, for New Englanders carried their ancestry with them even when they could carry little else.

On April 7, 1788, forty-eight men reached the mouth of the Muskingum River. Before them lay wooded hills, broad waters, rich bottomlands, and a silence which the promoters had correctly reported, though they had omitted the labor necessary to disturb it. The settlers landed, raised shelters, and called their first encampment Adelphia. Presently the town took the name Marietta, in honor of Marie Antoinette, then still a queen and not yet an example of the uncertainty of earthly arrangements.

There was much to admire in that first spring. The woods shone with new leaves; wild flowers brightened the banks; fish were plentiful; game could be shot within a short distance of camp. The men wrote eastward in high spirits. They planted gardens, surveyed lots, cut roads, and began the great communal building afterward called Campus Martius. It was not a fort in the military sense alone, but a fortified town: strong blockhouses at the corners, dwellings joined in defensive order, palisades, gates, store rooms, and places into which families and livestock might retire when danger approached.

This was the New England manner of taking possession. The pioneers did not merely cut down trees and announce that civilization had arrived. They appointed officers, recorded proceedings, opened courts, arranged militia companies, provided for worship, debated the location of roads, and soon established a school. A carpenter might serve as constable; a farmer might sit upon a jury; a former colonel might spend the morning at a log rolling and the afternoon considering a regulation against wandering hogs. Each man was expected to perform both private labor and public duty.

On the Fourth of July they held a celebration beneath a great bower. There were speeches, patriotic toasts, music of such quality as the wilderness afforded, and a dinner made notable by venison, bear meat, fish, poultry, vegetables, bread, and the solemn recollection that only twelve years earlier several of the company had been fighting for the independence they now celebrated. The forest rang with cheers for Congress, the Union, the Northwest Territory, and the ladies—though few ladies were yet present to hear themselves toasted.

It was a first summer of rejoicing. Then came the business of clearing farms, having ague, surviving frost, and saving one’s scalp.

The fields that looked so ready for the plow were covered by trees whose roots had possessed the ground for centuries. A settler must girdle or fell the timber, burn the brush, split rails, build fences, break the soil among stumps, plant his corn, guard it from animals, and hope that river, rain, worm, fever, and enemy would each take no more than a reasonable share. Nature in Ohio was abundant, but she did not deliver her abundance in sacks.

Winter corrected several eastern exaggerations. Ice formed on the rivers; snow covered the clearings; cabins admitted wind through walls, roofs, doors, and, on occasion, floors. Provisions diminished. Corn meal was scarce, salt dear, and meat uncertain when hunters failed. Some families lived for days upon boiled grain or such roots and game as could be obtained. Fever came in warm weather, coughing sickness in cold, and there were weeks when nearly every cabin contained an invalid.

Yet the settlers had brought the right articles: axes, adzes, saws, augers, hoes, rifles, powder, seed, iron, and the knowledge that a neighbor’s misfortune, neglected long enough, becomes the misfortune of the whole settlement. When a roof was to be raised, men gathered. When a woman lay ill, another household took in her children. When grain was low, shares were measured. When an alarm sounded, laborers left field and mill for the stockade.

Relations with the Native nations of the Ohio country soon worsened. To the settlers, their federal grants and surveys gave lawful title; to the Shawnees, Miamis, Delawares, Wyandots, and others who hunted, traveled, and maintained towns across the region, the same papers were marks made far away by men presuming to give what was not theirs. Treaties were disputed, boundary lines ignored, and revenge answered revenge. The pioneer chroniclers recorded attacks; Native memory recorded invasion. Between the two stood cabins, cornfields, war parties, militia patrols, and very few innocent misunderstandings.

In January 1791, an outlying settlement at Big Bottom, above Marietta on the Muskingum, was attacked. The little blockhouse had been carelessly guarded. Several settlers were killed and others taken captive, and the news carried terror downriver. Families crowded into Campus Martius and the fortified enclosure at the Point; men went armed to their fields; sentries watched the forest paths. The lesson was severe and plain: courage without vigilance is merely a convenient offering to an enemy.

Still the town endured. Mills began to turn. Cattle multiplied. Orchards were set out by people who planted for years they could not expect soon to enjoy. Courts met; marriages were performed; children learned letters; sermons were preached; boats tied up at the landing with news from Pittsburgh, Wheeling, Kentucky, and the world beyond the mountains. Marietta ceased to be an expedition and became a community. That difference is the beginning of permanence.

While the New Englanders were thus discovering that the western Eden required blockhouses, another colony came to the Ohio under circumstances almost designed to exhibit the danger of believing everything in print.

The Scioto Company advertised land in France at a time when France herself was becoming uncomfortable for persons of property, education, or merely unfortunate opinions. Agents described a delightful country on the Ohio and Scioto rivers, where a mild climate, fertile fields, and secure titles awaited emigrants. Many purchasers were artisans, merchants, teachers, musicians, jewelers, dancing masters, and gentlemen accustomed to directing servants rather than axes. They bought land from persons who had no proper title to sell it.

In 1790 the French emigrants arrived at the settlement they named Gallipolis—the City of the Gauls. They expected farms and found forest. They expected houses and found, at best, rough cabins erected under a doubtful contract. They expected legal possession and learned that the acres for which they had paid belonged, in the practical judgment of American law, to somebody else.

Their misfortune deserves sympathy, though certain incidents tempted frontier humor. A man trained to cut gems may possess admirable delicacy of hand without knowing how to split a rail. Some colonists attacked trees from several sides, producing chips, exhaustion, and very little timber. Others attempted to clear ground with methods more elegant than effective. Their clothes, tools, and habits were poorly suited to mud, smoke, mosquitoes, and the unrelenting intimacy of log-cabin life.

The first cold season completed what bewilderment had begun. The river froze, despite the supposed absence of severe frost. Food became scarce. Fever visited the settlement; medicines were limited, physicians overworked, and many colonists sickened while still learning the names of the plants around them. Those who had imagined a cultivated landscape found themselves listening at night to wolves beyond thin cabin walls.

Yet Gallipolis was not entirely a comedy of helplessness. The French shared what they had, nursed one another, made music in dreary rooms, planted gardens, baked when flour could be found, and tried with touching determination to preserve the courtesies of Europe in a place where one might meet a bear on the path to dinner. A violin sounded strangely sweet above the Ohio; lessons in dancing were not of immediate use against hunger, but they reminded the dancers that human life consisted of more than survival.

Many departed. Some went to older towns, some returned east, and others scattered wherever their professions or remaining money offered a chance. A small remnant stayed, adapting slowly and painfully. Congress afterward granted land to the dispossessed French in what became known as the French Grant, but legislation, however just, could not restore lost years, dead companions, or vanished savings.

The contrast between Marietta and Gallipolis was much remarked upon. It was not that New England courage exceeded French courage by nature, nor that refinement unfitted a man for honest labor; it was that the first company had prepared for the country it entered, while the second had prepared for the country described by salesmen. Putnam’s settlers brought axes and a plan of government. Many French colonists brought title papers and expectations. The forest had more respect for the axes.

Farther down the Ohio, another beginning was being made in territory exposed to even greater danger. In 1788 settlers connected with the Symmes Purchase entered the region between the Great and Little Miami rivers. At Columbia, and soon at a cluster of cabins called Losantiville, men from New Jersey and neighboring states established themselves upon land whose ownership and boundaries produced confusion almost equal to that of the Scioto speculation.

Losantiville was an ingenious mixture of languages intended to signify the town opposite the mouth of the Licking River. Governor Arthur St. Clair, possessing both authority and a preference for better names, changed it to Cincinnati in 1790, in honor of the Society of the Cincinnati. Thus a linguistic curiosity gave way to a Roman one, and the future city began its ascent under a name many early inhabitants could not spell.

The settlement stood in country contested by the Miami and Shawnee peoples, and the danger was constant. Fort Washington, constructed in 1789, gave protection to soldiers, surveyors, traders, and families who gathered near its walls. Outside the fort, fortified stations served as islands of defense. Cabins faced inward; loopholes were cut in logs; gates were barred at night; rifles hung within reach of beds, tables, and cradles.

A man clearing land alone risked ambush. A woman milking beyond the stockade listened for every movement in the cane. Children learned that the forest, beautiful at noon, might conceal watching eyes. Captives were carried northward; war parties struck pack trains and isolated cabins; militia rode in pursuit, often finding only a cold campfire or a trail lost in water.

The violence was intimate and pitiless. Men who had exchanged goods one month met as enemies the next. Settlers killed Native hunters and called it defense; warriors killed settlers and called it resistance or revenge. Scalps were taken, prisoners beaten, homes burned, and stories enlarged in every retelling until each side regarded the other as a people naturally devoted to cruelty. Such beliefs made the next cruelty easier.

Frontier anecdotes nevertheless preserve moments in which terror yielded to absurdity. One tale tells of a captive whose elaborate hairpiece came off in an astonished warrior’s hand. The captor, unacquainted with wigs, stared first at the object and then at the prisoner’s suddenly altered head, uncertain whether he had performed a miracle, a scalping, or some dangerous piece of sorcery. For a moment curiosity overcame hostility. History does not record that the owner thereafter considered vanity a protection, but he might reasonably have done so.

Cincinnati survived because its settlers combined private ambition with military necessity. Boatmen brought flour and salt. Traders opened shops. Blacksmiths repaired tools and weapons. Roads, little better than widened traces, connected stations. Men served in militia companies, and women managed households through alarms, absences, illness, and shortages which would have defeated many who appear more prominently in official records. The town grew under the shadow of Fort Washington, rough, speculative, frequently frightened, and determined to become important.

The disastrous defeat of St. Clair’s army in 1791 deepened fear throughout the Northwest Territory. Not until General Anthony Wayne’s campaign, the battle of Fallen Timbers in 1794, and the Treaty of Greenville in 1795 did the immediate danger lessen enough for settlement to advance with greater confidence. Peace did not settle every claim or erase Native resistance, but it altered the balance. Roads lengthened; cabins multiplied; land offices became busier than blockhouses.

Meanwhile, Virginia pioneers were entering the country along the lower Scioto and its approaches. Nathaniel Massie, surveyor and frontiersman, established Massie’s Station in 1791 near the Ohio River. The place afterward became Manchester. Its site was chosen less for beauty than defense. Houses were placed with an eye to mutual support, and every able man understood that surveying by day might be followed by guard duty at night.

Massie’s parties were skilled in woodcraft. They traveled lightly, read broken twigs and footprints, crossed streams without advertising their route, and knew that a cooking fire was both comfort and signal. They surveyed immense tracts under conditions in which a chainman might hold his line while another man held a rifle. Their maps converted wilderness into sections and claims; their stations converted claims into occupation.

In 1796 Massie laid out Chillicothe on the Scioto River. The town rose rapidly because the surrounding lands were fertile, the river offered communication, and the period of greatest warfare had passed. Stores, taverns, mills, workshops, and public buildings appeared. Packhorses gave way gradually to wagons; trails became roads; and political discussion, never absent among Americans, grew louder as the population increased. Chillicothe would become a center of territorial affairs and, in time, the first capital of the state.

In that same year another company entered Ohio from the northeast. Connecticut claimed a long strip called the Western Reserve, and surveyors under Moses Cleaveland reached Conneaut Creek in July 1796. They had come by way of Lake Erie, moving through waters whose storms could be as dangerous as any woodland enemy.

On July 4 they celebrated both national independence and their arrival. The meal was improvised from what the party possessed or could obtain; speeches were made; toasts were drunk with the gravity proper to men standing in a country they intended soon to divide into townships. Their instruments, chains, compasses, notebooks, boats, provisions, and firearms lay nearby—the practical furniture of another beginning.

The surveyors cut lines through dense woods and marshes, endured insects of remarkable persistence, and found the lakeshore climate less uniformly gentle than land advertisements suggested. Some returned east after their work; others remained or came later with families. Conneaut marked the northeastern entrance, as Marietta had marked the southeastern one. Before long settlements stretched along the Reserve, and Cleaveland’s name, slightly shortened, attached itself to a city on the lake.

Thus Ohio had several beginnings rather than one. Marietta began on April 7, 1788, with New England veterans at the Muskingum. Columbia and Cincinnati followed in the Miami country, defended by stations and Fort Washington. Gallipolis began in 1790 with deceived French emigrants whose courage had to repair what speculation had ruined. Massie’s Station appeared in 1791, Chillicothe in 1796, and Conneaut received the Connecticut surveyors that same year.

Each company had been promised abundance, and abundance indeed existed: rich soil, noble rivers, timber without measure, game, salt springs, stone, clay, and room for towns not yet imagined. But abundance is not the same as ease. Between the standing forest and the harvested field lay the axe; between the riverbank and the city lay fever, hunger, disputed title, winter, war, childbirth, burial, law, labor, and cooperation.

The durable settlements were built by those who learned this soonest. They raised blockhouses before danger, mills before famine, schools before ignorance, courts before disorder, and churches before prosperity made piety convenient. They watched one another’s children, shared seed corn, carried rifles to the fields, and planted orchards whose fruit belonged partly to the future.

Promoters sold Ohio with promises. Pioneers made it habitable with work. That was the better title.

### The Last Egg

I first met Butcher in the smoking-room of the little hotel at Tamatave, where we had both been detained by the same unreasonable rain and the same still more unreasonable harbour-master. I had come from Réunion in pursuit of a certain pale green *Angraecum*, reported by a missionary to flower only upon trees struck by lightning; Butcher, as I afterwards learned, had come from nowhere in particular and was waiting for money.

He sat by the open shutter with his boots upon a cane chair, smoking a black cigar that appeared to have gone out and been relighted many times. A broad white scar crossed his forehead, divided his left eyebrow, and ran down his cheek nearly to the corner of his mouth. It pulled that side of his face upward, so that even in repose he seemed to smile at some private and not altogether agreeable recollection.

“You collect orchids?” he asked.

I admitted it.

“Rum things, orchids.”

“Some of them.”

“Pay well?”

“Rare ones may.”

He reflected upon that and knocked ash into a saucer.

“Bones pay better,” he said. “If they’re the right bones.”

I told him that my interests had occasionally extended to natural history, and mentioned a dealer in Paris who had once offered me an extraordinary price for the skull of a recently discovered lemur.

“Dealers!” said Butcher. “Lord! A dealer will skin his grandmother if the museum people bid against one another. Did you ever hear of the big *Aepyornis* in the Rotterdam collection?”

I had, indeed, seen a notice of it in the *Annales des Sciences Naturelles*. It was a remarkable specimen, exceeding in several measurements the great bird reconstructed in Paris, and had caused a rather acrimonious controversy between Professor van Heeren and an English comparative anatomist whose name I have forgotten.

Butcher gave his crooked smile.

“That’s mine.”

“You discovered it?”

“I raised it.”

I supposed I had misunderstood him.

“Raised it,” he repeated. “From the egg. Fed it, talked to it, and got this for my trouble.” He touched the white trench across his face. “That was his toenail. Only a playful scrape, you know. If he’d meant business then, I shouldn’t be here discussing orchids.”

I looked at him more carefully. He was a thick-set, sun-browned man of perhaps forty-five, though hardship had put another ten years about his eyes. His hands were heavy and capable, with old powder marks between the thumb and forefinger. A man might lie about a bird; he could scarcely lie about those hands.

“An *Aepyornis*?” I said. “Alive?”

“Yes.”

“But the genus has been extinct for centuries.”

“So they tell me. Scientific chaps are wonderfully positive about things they haven’t seen. I saw mine every blessed morning for two years, and most afternoons too, unless he’d chased me up a palm.”

He turned his chair, called for whisky, and examined me with an air of deciding whether I was worth the exertion of further speech.

“You won’t believe it,” he said.

“I have not said so.”

“No. You’ve got the polite expression. Museum expression. Very good. I don’t ask you to believe. But if you know van Heeren, or the people who bought the skeleton from Loos and Company, you can check the measurements. Ask them where it came from. They’ll say an unnamed atoll south-east of the Amirantes, because that’s what I told Loos. Ask who sold it. They’ll dodge. That’s business. But it was me.”

He drank, settled his shoulders, and began.

“It was in ’89 I took the contract with Gribble Brothers. Precious fools, both of them, but they had money then. They wanted eggs—big eggs, whole eggs, no cracks, no plaster restorations—and anything in the way of bones that showed unusual size. The French had got ahead of them, and old Gribble took it personally. ‘Butcher,’ he says, ‘you bring me the largest egg out of Madagascar, and you may name your own bonus.’ He never specified the bonus in writing, mind you. That should have warned me.

“I went down the west side, past Morondava, with two Sakalava fellows, a half-breed interpreter called Michel, and a canoe that leaked according to the state of the moon. We had rice, salt beef, a tent, two spades, a rifle, cartridges, rum for medicine, and a letter from some official at the capital ordering everybody to assist us. The letter impressed nobody, but it was useful for lighting fires.

“You know those western swamps?”

I said I did not.

“Then don’t. That’s my advice. The mangroves stand on their roots like spiders, and beneath ’em there’s black mud without any proper bottom. Red water lies in hollows, red as watered blood, from the iron, I suppose, and the reeds cut your wrists. The air smells of warm pond, dead leaves and something sweet rotting. Mosquitoes sing all night, and in the day the sun comes down like a furnace door opening over your head.

“We spent three weeks finding nothing but broken shell. Plenty of that. Bits as thick as a tea-saucer and curved enough to show the size. I could put one over my knee like armour. But Gribble wanted whole eggs. A broken rarity, according to him, was merely an educational object, and there’s no money in education.

“Then Michel got fever. He sat under a tree shivering in a heat that had melted the pitch out of our canoe seams, and swore he saw white oxen walking through the swamp. I gave him quinine until he went deaf and rum until he sang, but he died just before sunrise. We buried him badly. You can’t dig a Christian grave in water, and there were crocodiles about. I put stones over him, and one of my men took his boots before the body was cold.

“That left three of us. One chap was called Ramo, the other Tsi—something with six syllables. I called him Joseph. It saved time.

“On the fifth day after Michel died, we came upon a ridge of hard blue clay running into the marsh. There were bones in it. Lord, what bones! Thigh-bones as thick as hitching-posts, ribs like boat timbers, and a pelvis that might have carried a small donkey. Some were mineralised, some soft and brown, and some looked so fresh I expected the owner to come splashing after them.

“We followed the ridge. The mud deepened on either side, black on the shaded side and blood-red where the sunset touched it. At the end was a pocket among the reeds, a kind of clay basin covered by two feet of dirty water. Joseph put his digging-stick through something hard. I thought rock. We cleared it with our hands.

“Egg number one.

“It lay sideways, stained brown, but perfect—not so much as a star crack. Thirteen inches long if it was a line. Presently we found another below it, and another beyond that. Four in all, tucked into the clay as neatly as goods in straw. There may have been twenty more. There may be twenty there now. The rain had begun, and the bank was slipping as we worked.

“I tell you, I forgot fever, Gribble, and every mosquito in Madagascar. There I was kneeling in black slime with the greatest eggs in the world under my hands. A man feels something at such a time. Pride, I suppose. Also money. Mostly money, if he is honest.

“We wrapped them in shirts, grass and sailcloth and carried them back to the canoe one at a time. They were heavier than empty shells ought to be. That interested me. I held one to my ear, but heard nothing. I thought they had filled with mud through pores, or that the contents had dried solid. Fossil eggs with the yolk in ’em! That was a phrase for Gribble’s catalogue.

“The rain came down before dark. Not rain as you know it here, but a solid grey wall that beat the swamp flat and turned every hollow into a running stream. We dragged the canoe above the rise, covered the eggs, and ate cold rice under the sail. Ramo and Joseph talked half the night. I could not follow them, but I knew the sound of men making arrangements that don’t include their employer.

“I slept with the rifle under my arm.

“Toward morning I woke because the rain had stopped. That’s often how it is; the silence wakes you. The sailcloth was gone. The canoe was halfway into the channel, with Ramo in the stern and Joseph shoving at the bow. They had two eggs and nearly all the food.

“I shouted. Joseph jumped aboard. Ramo struck at the mud with his paddle, and the canoe slid out.

“Now, I had not crossed half Madagascar to watch two thieves take my specimens. I fired over their heads.

“Or meant to.”

Butcher paused and rotated his tumbler slowly between his palms.

“Rifle sights are deceptive before dawn,” he said. “And a canoe shifts.”

“You hit one of them?”

“Ramo. Between the shoulders. He went over the side without a word. Joseph leapt into the shallows and ran through the reeds. The canoe swung round, struck a root, and overturned. One egg disappeared at once. The other floated for perhaps ten seconds—yes, floated—and then followed. All the rice went with them.

“I waded out. The channel was up to my chest and running hard. I got the rifle, three cartridges, one paddle, a water tin, and the canoe after an hour’s work. Ramo had vanished. Crocodile, current, mud—take your choice.

“Joseph had my revolver, though, and I could hear him breaking reeds inland.

“I went after him.”

He said it without emphasis, and I confess that the matter-of-fact brutality of it chilled me more than any dramatic account might have done.

“You wished to recover the weapon?” I asked.

“Among other things. He had the matches. He had my boots too—my spare pair, I mean. A man notices boots in that country.

“I tracked him until noon. Blood here and there. He’d cut his foot, or perhaps a reed had opened his leg. Then I found the revolver, dropped in the mud. Two chambers fired. He’d been shooting at something. After that the track doubled and wandered. Once it went in a circle. Near sunset I found him beneath a tamarind tree, dead.

“No bullet in him. No mark except a bite behind the knee and swelling up the thigh. Snake, likely. He’d tied a strip of cloth above it, but not tight enough. The matches were wet. The boots didn’t fit me.

“So there I was, alone, with a dead man, a leaky canoe, three cartridges, no food, and two eggs on the ridge.

“Still, two eggs were better than none. I made two journeys, got them aboard, and paddled down what I believed was a creek leading to the coast. It did lead to the coast—everything leads somewhere if you go long enough—but the current carried me past the opening in the dark. By daylight I was outside the reef and being drawn south-west.

“I had no sail. I made one from my shirt and a bit of wrapping cloth. The wind tore it before noon. The coast was a brown line, then a blue line, then nothing.

“That was the beginning of the ten days.”

He relighted his cigar, though it had burned nearly to his fingers.

“You can’t imagine ten days in an open canoe unless you’ve done it. People speak of the sea as empty. It isn’t. It is full of light, and glare, and little noises, and movements that never mean land. In the morning it’s like hammered tin. At noon it burns white. At evening the west opens red and black like the mouth of a furnace, and when darkness comes the water lights with crawling fire. Every dip of the paddle lets loose a swarm of green sparks. Fish pass underneath like fiery knives. Beautiful, you say. Yes. Beautiful for the first night.

“The second day I drank half the water.

“The third I found a flying-fish in the canoe and ate it raw.

“The fourth it rained for seven minutes. I caught perhaps a pint in the sailcloth and spilled half of that.

“The fifth day I began talking to the eggs.

“They sat in the bow, wedged with grass, big, smooth, stupid-looking things. I told them what Gribble Brothers owed me. Salary at eight pounds a week, field allowance, risk premium, and the promised bonus. I calculated it aloud. It gave me occupation. I was still in their service, you see. Nothing in my agreement said salary stopped if I was blown out to sea.

“On the sixth day I decided to eat one.

“I had no hammer. I used the rifle butt. That shell was astonishing. I struck once—nothing. Twice—a crack. The smell that came out was not foul, exactly. Stale, oily, ancient. Imagine a cupboard shut in Pharaoh’s time.

“The inside was mostly thick yellow stuff, dark at the centre. I tasted a little and was sick. Then I waited and tasted again. Hunger is the best cook and the most persuasive scientific adviser. By evening I had swallowed perhaps a quarter of it.

“It kept me alive. There’s no doubt of that. I ate it in pinches, drying some on the paddle blade. It tasted of old cheese, fish oil and plaster. For three days I lived on it.

“On the ninth morning I broke the second egg.

“Not entirely. A small hole only, to get at the contents. The shell gave under the rifle sight, and something moved inside.”

He looked at me.

“Gas,” I suggested. “Putrefactive displacement.”

“That’s what I thought. Then a little hooked beak struck through the inner skin.”

He leaned forward.

“Yes,” he said softly. “Developing.”

For several moments the noise of rain in the hotel yard filled the room.

“The thing was alive?” I asked.

“Alive and offended. It clicked its beak at me through the hole. I nearly dropped it overboard.

“Now explain that, if you can. The eggs lay in clay among bones. Perhaps they weren’t as old as the bones. Perhaps the swamp preserved them—heat, salts, no air—and the shaking and sun started growth again. I don’t know. I’m no embryologist. I know what came out later.

“I patched the hole with cloth and resin scraped from the canoe seam. After that I couldn’t eat it. A precious fool I was, because I was nearly dead. But once a thing has looked at you—even through a hole no bigger than a shilling—it becomes different from breakfast.

“At dawn on the tenth day I saw palms.

“First I took them for black scratches in my eyes. Then the scratches stayed when I shut one eye. The current brought me toward a low ring of coral, perhaps three miles across, with a lagoon inside and a pass through the reef. I had strength enough to paddle through before the tide turned. The canoe grounded on white sand. I crawled under a bush and lay there until night.

“There was water. That was the miracle. A shallow well among the palms, brackish but drinkable. There were coconuts, crabs, sea-birds’ eggs, and a kind of small sour fruit. No people. No huts. No smoke. I found an old spar with copper nails, and later part of a cask with Portuguese letters, but nothing to show the place was visited.

“I slept two days, waking only to drink. On the third morning the egg began knocking.

“It took most of the day to hatch. First the beak worked round the patched hole. Then a foot came through—not where any reasonable foot should be—and the whole shell split lengthwise. Out sprawled the ugliest wet bundle of neck and legs ever created. It was the size of a turkey, but mostly knee. Its body was covered in blackish down, its head was bald, and its beak looked too large to carry. It lay panting among the shell pieces, with one eye open.

“I gave it coconut milk. It sneezed that over me. Then I mashed crab meat and fed it from my finger.

“It swallowed the meat and bit the finger.

“‘Very good,’ I said. ‘That’s gratitude.’

“I called him Friday. It wasn’t Friday, as it happened—it was Monday by my reckoning—but Friday is the proper name for a desert-island companion, and I saw no reason to upset tradition over three days.

“He followed me before the week was out. Not from affection at first. He expected food. If I walked along the lagoon, there came Friday behind me, lifting his great feet high and putting them down with the solemnity of a bishop in a procession. If I stopped, he bumped into me. If I sat, he sat too, usually on something I wanted.

“I built a shelter from palm leaves and drift timber. Friday watched. I dug a better well. Friday watched. I made fish-hooks out of copper nails and line from coconut fibre. Friday swallowed the first hook and half the line. I got the hook back, though not in a manner I care to describe.

“He grew.

“Lord, how he grew!

“In three months he stood to my waist. In six he could look over my shoulder. At a year his back was higher than my head, and the neck went up from there like a crane on a dock. His legs were yellow-grey and thick as young trees; his feet spread wider than dinner plates; and his head, when he brought it down suddenly, was like a pickaxe coming through a roof.

“He ate everything. Crabs, fish, fruit, shellfish, sea-birds, roots, green coconut, rotten coconut, and once my leather belt. I tried him with dried egg from the first specimen, but he wouldn’t touch it. Cannibalism offended him, apparently.

“For the first year we were excellent friends. I talked, and he listened. Or seemed to. I told him about England, about Cape Town, about Gribble Brothers and the lawsuit I intended to bring when my salary was paid. At eight pounds a week, you know, the account was becoming respectable. I cut notches on a palm every Sunday. Fifty-two notches: four hundred and sixteen pounds, without the bonus. At two years, eight hundred and thirty-two. A man can endure solitude with compound interest behind it.

“I taught Friday to come when I whistled. He taught me to open coconuts for him. I scratched the bare skin under his jaw, and he made a booming noise deep in his chest, like someone rolling an empty barrel in a cellar. At sunset he would stand beside me at the reef while the sea burned copper and violet, and his shadow stretched fifty yards along the beach. There wasn’t another living soul within God knows how many miles, but with that great fool breathing at my elbow, I wasn’t lonely.

“That’s the truth of it. I wasn’t lonely.

“The trouble began after his second moulting.

“He had always been playful. A peck, a shove, stealing my food—nothing serious. Then one morning I was carrying fish from the reef, and he came behind and kicked the basket out of my hand.

“An *Aepyornis* kicks forward and sideways. Remember that if you meet one. The leg comes like a sledgehammer, and the inner claw finishes the argument.

“I cuffed his beak.

“He stared at me.

“That was the first time I noticed the change. Before, his eyes had been foolish, brown, almost gentle. Now he looked as if he were measuring me.

“I picked up the fish. He kicked again, caught my thigh, and sent me ten feet across the sand.

“‘None of that!’ I shouted.

“He lowered his head and came at me.

“I ran.

“You may laugh. I did not. His stride was three of mine, and every footfall made the beach thump. I reached the lagoon with his beak snapping at my shoulder and went in headfirst. He stopped where the water covered his toes. Wouldn’t swim. Never would. He walked up and down while I sat in the lagoon up to my neck for four hours.

“That was the new arrangement. Friday owned the island, and I rented the water.

“After that he found sport in me. Some days he was as mild as ever. He’d take fruit from my hand and let me scratch his neck. Then without warning the head would jerk, the eyes harden, and off we went—me for the lagoon or the nearest climbable palm, Friday after me, booming like a drum.

“He learned to wait under trees.

“Once I spent a night and most of the next day in a palm while he stood below looking up. Whenever I moved, he struck the trunk with his beak. Chips flew. The whole blessed tree shook. I had three coconuts and no way to open them. Friday had shade, food, and all the patience in creation.

“Another time I tried to frighten him with fire. I made a torch from dry fibre and resin and advanced upon him shouting. He took the burning end in his beak, shook it out, and ate the fibre.

“I built a fence round my hut. He walked through it.

“I dug a pit and covered it with branches. I fell into it myself in the dark.

“The worst was the scar.

“I had gone inland for fruit and saw him at the far end of a clearing. He seemed quiet. I spoke to him. He gave that soft barrel-noise, and I thought the temper had passed. I went close and put my hand under his jaw as I used to.

“Then he struck.

“The beak knocked me down. His foot came across my face. It felt cold at first—merely a cold line—then the blood blinded me. I rolled under a fallen trunk, and he kicked at it until the wood split. If the hollow beneath had been an inch shallower, he’d have stamped me flat.

“I lay there until dark. The wound took weeks to close. I had no needle, so I used fishbone and fibre. That is why it healed like this.”

He traced the scar again.

“And still,” he continued, “the next morning I called him. Can you understand that? I hated him. I was afraid of him. Yet I called. He was all I had.

“He came within twenty yards and stopped. I talked for an hour. I reminded him who had broken his shell, who had fed him, who had kept him from walking into the fire when he was no taller than my knee. He listened with his head sideways, then picked up a piece of coral and flung it at me.

“Ingratitude. That’s what it was.”

I said nothing.

“For three months we lived like enemies in a bad marriage. I slept on a platform between two palms. I kept food in three places, because he raided them. I went to the reef only when he was at the far side of the island. He would pretend not to see me, then cut across behind the trees. Clever—far cleverer than he had any right to be.

“I considered leaving. The canoe was long gone; a storm had broken it against the reef. I built a raft. Friday pulled it to pieces. Whether from malice or curiosity I can’t say. I built another in secret. He found that too.

“I thought of blinding him, but couldn’t.

“I thought of the rifle. I still had three cartridges, though one had been soaked. A shot in the head might not stop a creature that size, and if it failed—well, I preferred Friday bad-tempered to Friday wounded.

“Then he caught me near the north beach. No tree, no water close. He knocked me over and stamped beside my ribs, once, twice. Sand flew over me. The next kick would have opened my chest. I threw a coconut at his eye. He turned his head, and I crawled through thorn scrub while he tore the shirt off my back.

“That settled it.

“I made a cord from plaited fibre, hide strips from a dead turtle, and the remaining length of fishing line. Strong stuff. I fixed one end to a coral block weighing perhaps a hundredweight and made a running loop at the other. Then I laid fruit along a narrow path between two clumps of scrub.

“He came at sunset.

“The first fruit he ignored. The second he ate. He stepped into the loop, and I pulled from behind the coral.

“It caught both legs above the feet.

“He fell like a collapsing house.

“You never heard such a sound. Not a bird’s cry. A deep booming roar that shook the leaves. He kicked, and the coral jumped. He got one knee beneath him. I thought he would rise and drag the block after me, so I pulled the cord round a palm and made it fast.

“Then I took the rifle.

“The first cartridge misfired.

“Friday saw me. He stopped struggling. That was the worst of it. He stretched his neck along the sand and looked straight at me. Not angry now. Puzzled.

“I opened the breech. My hands were shaking so badly I dropped the bad cartridge. The second went in.

“‘Keep still, old chap,’ I said.

“He made the little rumbling noise—the sound he used to make when I scratched his jaw.

“I very nearly cut the rope.

“Then he kicked again, and the coral block struck my shin and broke the bone. I fell, and the rifle went off.

“The bullet entered behind the eye.

“He gave one tremendous heave. The palm bent, the cord sang, and his claws tore trenches in the sand. Then he lay quiet. His neck settled slowly, inch by inch, until the beak touched my foot.

“That was all.”

Butcher finished his whisky. Outside, the rain had weakened to a whisper among the plantain leaves.

“I sat beside him until morning,” he said. “My leg was broken, but I scarcely felt it. I kept my hand on his neck. The warmth went out very slowly. For two years I had wished for a ship every day. Now I would have given every penny Gribble owed me for one more day before I pulled that cord.

“The island changed when he died. You’d think such a great brute made noise all the time, but he didn’t. Still, his absence was louder than anything. No tracks over mine. No booming inland. No black head above the scrub watching what I did. I talked aloud and heard myself talking. That’s loneliness.

“I buried what I could after the leg set. Not out of sentiment only. I knew the skeleton was valuable. Scientific duty, commerce—call it what you please. I cut away the flesh with shell knives and let crabs clean the bones. It took months. I numbered the smaller pieces with charcoal and packed them in coconut matting.

“The skull I kept by my sleeping place.

“A year passed after that, perhaps a little less. I stopped cutting salary notches. There were too many, and Gribble Brothers began to seem less real than Friday.

“Then one afternoon I saw a white sail outside the reef.

“I made smoke with damp leaves and set fire to half my shelter. The vessel was a yacht, the *Dorothea*, owned by a fellow named Penhallick who had lost his way hunting an island that existed only on an Admiralty chart. He sent a boat in.

“They found me waving beside a heap of bones.

“Penhallick was decent enough, though he charged me for clothes, passage and medical attendance when we reached Mahé. He also wanted half the skeleton on the ground that his yacht had transported it. We went to law. The court gave him expenses but not ownership.

“Gribble Brothers had failed while I was away. Eight pounds a week for nearly four years—gone. The receiver said my contract had been terminated by presumed death after eighteen months, though nobody had troubled to inform me. I brought an action. Lost that too.

“So I sold Friday.

“Loos and Company gave me three hundred pounds for the skeleton, less commission, preparation, freight, insurance and a fee for identifying the island on their label. Van Heeren bought it later for Rotterdam and called it *Aepyornis titanus*. Then the Englishman said it was merely an unusually large *Aepyornis maximus*. They wrote papers at one another for two years.

“Not one of them asked what colour his eyes were.”

He rose, crossed to the shutter, and looked into the wet dusk. In profile the scar seemed less like a smile and more like a seam by which some violence had fastened his face together.

“You said there were four eggs,” I remarked after a while. “Two lost with the canoe, one eaten, and one hatched.”

“That’s right.”

“And the shell of the hatched egg?”

“Sold separately. A German bought most of it. Called it too thin to belong to the skeleton. Said the measurements were inconsistent.”

“And the first shell—the egg you ate?”

Butcher turned.

“Bits of it remained in the canoe when I reached the island. Penhallick took them as curiosities. His widow presented them to a provincial museum, where they were labelled tortoise shell. That’s science for you.”

I asked whether he had ever attempted to revisit the atoll.

“Once. Couldn’t find it. Penhallick’s position was wrong, my reckoning was worse, and currents there play tricks. Loos wanted the rest of the bones. I wanted—”

He stopped.

“Another egg?” I suggested.

He looked at me with sudden severity.

“No,” he said. “Not another egg.”

The harbour-master entered then, bringing word that the launch would run at dawn if the weather moderated. Butcher nodded, put on his battered hat, and went out without saying good night.

Some years later, in Rotterdam, I made a point of examining the great skeleton. It stood in a high glass case between a moa and two ostriches, its reconstructed neck reaching almost to the iron gallery. The label called it *Aepyornis maximus*, “subfossil, Madagascar region,” and attributed the specimen to the Loos Expedition. A smaller card mentioned that certain dimensions were conjectural.

The left orbit showed a neat perforation in the bone.

I inquired of an assistant whether the injury had occurred during preparation.

He replied that Professor van Heeren considered it pathological.

I did not dispute the professor. Scientific men, as Butcher had observed, are wonderfully positive about things they have not seen.

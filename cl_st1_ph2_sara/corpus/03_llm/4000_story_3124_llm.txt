# THE WAY HOME

After twenty years among the dead, David Culross came out beneath the sky.

The gate closed behind him with a clang which seemed to strike the whole past from his body. He stopped upon the road, though the keeper had told him twice that the station lay to the east, beyond the low hill and the row of leafless cottonwoods. For a moment he had no courage to move. The road ran both ways; the clouds traveled without orders; a meadow, brown with November, spread itself uselessly to the horizon. There were no walls to tell him where space ended, no bell to divide one permitted action from another.

Freedom terrified him.

He wore a suit given him by the State—a suit of dark, stiff cloth, made for no particular man and therefore becoming to none. The sleeves were a little short, the shoulders too broad, and the new hat, which he held in his hands, had the impudent gloss of a thing that knew nothing of shame. In one pocket were eleven dollars and forty cents; in another, a railroad ticket to Chicago. These were called his possessions. A name also belonged to him, though for twenty years it had been less often used than a number.

“Better be getting along, Culross,” said the keeper through the grating.

David turned with that sidelong, submissive motion by which prisoners look at authority. The keeper had not spoken harshly. Indeed, he had meant kindness; but kindness, passing through iron, acquires a metallic sound.

“Yes, sir,” David answered.

Then he remembered that he was no longer required to say “sir,” and the knowledge did not comfort him. Habits may become chains, but chains are at least familiar. The man who has worn them long enough mistakes their weight for the weight of his own limbs.

He put on the hat and descended the road.

Sunshine broke through the clouds and fell across the fields in wide, pale bands. It touched the prison wall, touched the keeper’s roof, touched David’s face. He flinched. During the first years of confinement he had dreamed of sunlight as a starving man dreams of food; afterward he had ceased to dream of it, because desire, when denied long enough, does not always grow heroic. Sometimes it merely sickens and lies down.

He walked heavily. His shoulders drooped, his hands hung away from him as though they had forgotten the natural business of hands, and the measured tread of the cell-house clung to his feet. Freedom expected him to choose his own pace. He had none.

At the station a boy in a red scarf was throwing stones at a telegraph pole. David watched him with painful fascination. The boy shouted whenever he struck the pole, and nobody rebuked him. A farmer sat on an overturned box, eating an apple. A woman with two children complained of the cold. All these persons possessed the immense privilege of being irritated by small things.

The train came roaring from the west. David stepped back, though there was no danger, and the conductor, observing his clothes, his uncertain glance, and perhaps that peculiar pallor which stone and silence lay upon the human countenance, guessed from what gate he had come.

“Chicago?” he asked briefly.

David offered his ticket.

“All right. Rear coach.”

He entered and took the place nearest the door. He wanted to be prepared if anyone ordered him out.

The car was warm. Brass fittings shone above green plush seats; parcels crowded the racks; a traveling salesman smoked in defiance of the sign and talked of farm machinery to a man who wished to sleep. Across the aisle, a young mother unbuttoned her little daughter’s coat and gave her a sugared biscuit. The child stared at David. Children know misfortune before they know its names.

He tried to smile at her.

The unused muscles of his face produced something so unlike a smile that the child turned quickly and hid against her mother.

David looked out of the window.

The prison receded, then disappeared. It was astonishing that a structure which had contained the universe could become so small. First the walls sank behind the hill; then only the black chimney remained; then smoke floated without any visible origin; then even the smoke was lost in the gray sky.

Twenty years.

Twenty years in the penitentiary!

You may pronounce the words in less time than a clock takes to strike once. Yet consider what they contain: seven thousand mornings, seven thousand nights, the fall of innumerable rains, the opening and decay of twenty springs, the youth of a man, the strength of his arm, the color of his hair, the friends who die, the children who become men, the houses torn down, the streets renamed, the inventions that make old knowledge foolish. A court writes “twenty years” with ink; a prisoner pays it with blood.

David was forty-six years old. He had entered the prison at twenty-six, though to his present mind that condemned young man seemed not younger but of another species. The lad had quick hands, a straight back, a foolishly sensitive mouth. He had been shy, hungry for affection, vain in secret, timid in company, and capable—as the event proved—of violence. David regarded him as one might regard the portrait of a dead brother whose sin had impoverished the family.

The train shook him gently, and the years moved backward.

Before the prison there had been the office.

It occupied the third floor of a building in South Water Street, over a wholesale establishment which dealt in spices, dried fruits, and coffee. From below rose odors of cinnamon and dust; within the office stood six tall desks, twelve high stools, a safe painted with gold flourishes, and rows of ledgers whose leather backs gave them the dignity of theological works.

David had been a clerk.

He arrived at eight, copied invoices, extended columns, balanced accounts, blotted letters, carried papers to the Head Clerk, and went away at six. On Saturdays he sometimes remained until nine. There is honest work that strengthens the worker because he perceives its relation to the world; and there is work which, though equally honest, consumes the soul by fragments, permitting a man to see neither the beginning nor the end of what he does. David moved figures from one book to another. He did not know the growers whose coffee was entered, the sailors who carried it, the families who drank it, or the merchant whose profit increased by three-sixteenths of one per cent. He knew only that a seven written carelessly resembled a two and that the Head Clerk disliked erasures.

That gentleman was David’s early ideal.

Mr. Quill had served the firm thirty-one years without committing either a serious mistake or an act of imagination. He dressed in gray, ate an apple at noon, and possessed the enviable faculty of making displeasure visible by lifting one eyebrow. The younger clerks feared him; the partners trusted him; his wife called him “Father”; and no one had ever heard him laugh except when a debtor failed.

David believed that success consisted in becoming like Mr. Quill. He tried to suppress his daydreams, to narrow his handwriting, to care passionately about freight charges, and to look upon poetry, music, flirtation, idleness, color, and grief as irregular entries in the ledger of existence.

But he was not constituted for such triumph.

He lived with his mother in four rooms on a narrow street where the buildings leaned toward each other as though exchanging complaints. Mrs. Culross was a virtuous woman. Virtue is precious, but it does not necessarily make a room cheerful. She had endured poverty so long that she suspected pleasure of extravagance and regarded every unplanned noise as a threat to respectability. The parlor was opened on Sundays. The furniture wore covers; the carpet showed faded roses; a chromolithograph of a dying missionary hung above the folding bed. David’s father had been dead twelve years, but his hat remained in a closet because Mrs. Culross felt that discarding it would be heartless, while looking at it daily was a duty.

At supper she discussed prices. After supper she knitted and listened for footsteps in the hall. David read the newspaper or an old volume of sermons. He did not go to saloons. He did not gamble. He knew no women except two cousins, his mother’s landlady, and the stout person who collected subscriptions for foreign missions.

A man may perish from the absence of evil; he may also perish from the absence of joy.

Then Zoe Le Baron came into his life.

She was the daughter of one of the firm’s principal customers, a manufacturer whose prosperity had acquired three houses, four servants, two carriages, and the conviction that wealth is the visible certificate of moral worth. Mr. Le Baron entered the office one afternoon with Zoe beside him. While he conferred with a partner, she sat near David’s desk.

At first he dared not look at her. Her presence announced itself by small perturbations of the air—the rustle of silk, the fragrance of violets, the tapping of a folded parasol against her shoe. Presently a sheet of paper slipped from the desk and floated to the floor.

Zoe caught it.

“Is this valuable?” she asked.

It was a list of disputed charges amounting to one dollar and eighty-seven cents.

“Very,” said David gravely.

“Then I have saved your fortune.”

She placed the paper before him and smiled. He saw brown eyes lit by amusement, dark hair imperfectly subdued beneath a little hat, and a face which was not faultlessly beautiful but alive in every line. He thanked her too solemnly.

“Are all clerks so serious?”

“No, miss.”

“Only the valuable ones?”

“I am not valuable.”

“How sad! Then perhaps they will sell you cheaply.”

At that absurdity he laughed, and the sound startled him more than it did her. The Head Clerk raised one eyebrow.

When Zoe left, the office changed back. The walls had not moved, the ledgers had not multiplied, the cinnamon still crept upward from below; yet desolation, once illuminated, becomes more visible than before.

They met again at a church sociable. Mrs. Culross had been persuaded to attend because admission was free. Zoe was there with an aunt, and she recognized David.

“There is my valuable clerk,” she said.

He blushed so painfully that she took pity upon him. She spoke of the music, the minister’s daughters, the dreadful coffee, and a young man who had recited a comic poem without being comic. Her conversation darted from thing to thing, not because her mind was shallow but because it was free. To David, accustomed to speech that moved in grooves—prices, weather, illness, duty—her lightness seemed genius.

After that they met wherever respectable chance could be assisted without appearing conspiratorial: at church, at a lecture on the Holy Land, in the public gardens, once in a bookshop where Zoe bought a volume of poems and David, observing her choice, purchased the same volume though it cost half his week’s board.

She discovered this and laughed at him.

“You do not like the poems.”

“I shall learn to.”

“Why?”

“Because you do.”

It was the first compliment he had ever attempted. Its naked sincerity silenced her.

Their courtship—if those frightened walks and brief conversations deserve so confident a name—was made of little things. She gave him a carnation, which he kept until it was brown. He told her of his wish to travel, though he had never been farther than Milwaukee. She played the piano in her father’s drawing-room while David, admitted there once with a group from church, sat behind a palm and believed that music had been invented to explain Zoe.

Her home oppressed and enchanted him. It contained carpets into which one’s feet sank, mirrors taller than men, gilded clocks, porcelain shepherdesses, chairs too delicate for use, and pictures selected for their frames. At dinner, servants appeared before desire could become speech. David thought wealth meant that no one ever waited.

Zoe sometimes complained of these luxuries.

“You cannot understand,” she said. “Everything is arranged. I am arranged. Papa has views about whom I shall know, where I shall go, what I shall wear, and—I believe—what I shall think when I am forty.”

David could not sympathize properly. To a man who owns no room of his own, captivity in a mansion may look like liberty.

Yet she was lonely also. Loneliness wears many garments. It may sit in prison cloth upon a bench; it may descend a marble staircase in satin slippers. Recognition drew them together before either had courage to call the attraction love.

At last David spoke.

It was an April evening. Rain had fallen, and the lamps shone in wet pavements. They had left a concert with others, but by one of those small mercies which seem afterward to have been planned by heaven, the group divided. David and Zoe walked half a block alone.

“Miss Le Baron,” he began.

“Yes?”

“I have no right to say what I am going to say.”

“Then perhaps you ought not say it.”

Her voice trembled slightly. That gave him courage, though courage in a timid man often arrives with such violence that it resembles desperation.

“I think of you continually. At the office, at home, when I wake, when I try to sleep. I know what I am—a clerk with twelve hundred dollars a year and a mother dependent upon him. I know what your father expects. I know that I have nothing which could make you—make any woman—”

“David.”

It was the first time she had spoken his Christian name.

He stopped beneath a lamp. The wet branches above them glittered; a carriage passed, its wheels whispering over the shining street.

“I love you,” he said.

Zoe’s levity deserted her. She stood very still, looking not at him but at the reflection of the lamp trembling in a puddle.

“I think,” she answered slowly, “that I have loved you for some time. I was afraid you would never find it out.”

He was not happy then—happiness is too tranquil a word. He was annihilated and created. All that had preceded the moment became preparation; all that might follow it seemed promised. The office, his poverty, his mother’s dreary rooms, even Mr. Quill’s eyebrow could be endured. A man who has one soul on his side makes light of the world.

But the world had not yet spoken.

Mr. Le Baron sent for David two days later.

He received him in a library whose books, being uniformly bound and seldom opened, presented no disorderly differences of opinion. The manufacturer stood before the fire, broad, prosperous, and sorrowfully reasonable.

“Mr. Culross,” he said, “my daughter has informed me of a certain understanding between you.”

David’s heart struck his ribs.

“Yes, sir.”

“I do not question your honesty. Your employers speak well of your attention to business.”

This praise, offered at such a moment, degraded him more than accusation could have done.

“I love your daughter.”

“Love is a word much used by young people. I was young myself once.”

Persons who have ceased to remember youth are fond of announcing that they once possessed it.

Mr. Le Baron placed two fingers beneath his coat-tail and rocked gently upon his heels.

“You have no capital.”

“No, sir.”

“No prospects beyond advancement in your present occupation.”

“I may do better.”

“You may. Men may do many things. Prudence concerns itself with what they have done.”

“I can work.”

“So can a dray horse. Forgive my plainness. Work is respectable, but it is not in itself a provision for a young woman accustomed to refinement.”

David’s face burned.

“If Zoe is willing to live simply—”

“Zoe does not know what she is willing to endure. She has never endured anything. That is my fault, perhaps, and certainly my responsibility.”

“She loves me.”

“My daughter is affectionate and impulsive. Six months from now she will be grateful that I prevented a mistake.”

“No.”

The syllable escaped before respect could imprison it.

Mr. Le Baron’s expression hardened.

“You will not call here again. You will not write to her. If you possess the regard you profess, you will spare her the distress of conflict with her family. I might take harsher measures. I prefer to appeal to your honor.”

Thus cruelty, when well dressed, calls itself an appeal to honor.

David left the house without seeing Zoe. He walked past the gate, returned, and walked past it again. A light shone in an upper window. Once a shadow crossed the curtain. He imagined her detained, weeping, perhaps waiting for some heroic signal. But what signal could he give? He had no money, no home fit for her, no confidence in his own power. Mr. Le Baron had struck the weak place accurately. David believed himself unworthy.

He went to the office the next morning and entered figures in a ledger. Here, as later in prison, a bell announced the hour; here, as there, obedience was safety; here, as there, his desires were irrelevant to the institution. Yet the office demanded that he appear voluntary, and this refinement made it almost crueler.

At noon Mr. Quill said, “Your threes are unusually indistinct, Culross.”

David apologized.

At six he went home. His mother said butter had risen two cents.

On the second day he wrote to Zoe and tore up the letter.

On the third day he waited near her house in the rain. She did not appear.

On the fourth day a note reached him through a servant. It contained only: “Do not believe that I consent. I shall find a way. Z.”

He kissed the paper, hid it, took it out, read it over and over, and trembled between hope and shame.

That evening Mr. Le Baron came to the office. David saw him enter the partners’ room. When the door opened, one partner called David and informed him, with embarrassment, that the firm had decided to dispense with his services. Nothing was alleged against his work. Commercial considerations, however, sometimes required changes.

He was given two weeks’ wages.

Perhaps Mr. Le Baron had not demanded the dismissal. Perhaps he had merely mentioned the inconvenience of employing a young man who presumed above his station. Respectable power seldom dirties its own hands; it need only indicate where the dirt lies.

David wandered for hours.

The city, which had always seemed indifferent, now appeared malicious. Carriages carried laughing people past him. Restaurants shone with food. Theater bills promised gaiety in enormous letters. Men hurried home to wives; girls came from shops in chattering groups; bells sounded, windows brightened, music escaped from an open door. The whole world possessed somewhere to go.

He had nowhere.

Near midnight he entered a saloon.

Until then David had scarcely tasted liquor. He chose the place not from appetite but because its door stood open and no one there knew him. Gas flared above a long mirror. Sawdust covered the floor. Men played cards at a rear table, and billiard balls clicked in an adjoining room. Behind the bar, bottles glowed green, amber, and red, like the windows of a profane chapel.

“What’ll it be?” asked the bartender.

David named the drink ordered by the man beside him.

The first glass burned him. The second warmed the wound. The third persuaded him that the wound was glorious. Alcohol does not bestow qualities; it releases prisoners, and among them are sometimes courage, tenderness, wit—and sometimes the murderer.

He spoke to strangers. He told one of them that money ruled the world. The stranger, who had reached the same conclusion years before and found it uninteresting, advised him to go home.

“I have no home,” David said.

“You’ve got a bed, haven’t you?”

“A bed is not a home.”

This distinction delighted another man, who repeated it mockingly.

“A bed is not a home! Hear the philosopher!”

David drank again. Faces widened and contracted in the mirror. The room became at once intimate and unreal. He felt himself capable of confronting Mr. Le Baron, rescuing Zoe, buying a house, defeating the Head Clerk, and explaining the injustice of existence to any man who would listen.

A fellow near the billiard room laughed at him.

The fellow’s name was Moran. David learned it later, at the trial. Moran was thirty-two, a teamster, married, the father of two children. He had worked hard that day and drunk hard that night. In another hour he might have gone home and lived to be a grandfather. There was no ancient enmity between them, no profit to be gained, no deliberate wickedness. There were merely two inflamed minds, a careless insult, a hand upon a shoulder, a blow, a billiard cue seized without thought.

One moment David was shouting.

The next, Moran lay upon the sawdust.

Blood ran from his temple.

The room became silent with dreadful speed. Drunkenness fell from David, not gradually, but like a dropped garment. He looked at the cue in his hands, at the overturned chair, at the bartender kneeling beside Moran.

“Get a doctor,” someone said.

Another answered, “No use.”

No use.

Those two words began the sentence before judge or jury had heard his name.

At the trial David’s counsel pleaded intoxication, provocation, previous good character, emotional disturbance. The prosecutor called drink no excuse for murder, which is true; called David a vicious and dangerous man, which was not true; and displayed the billiard cue to the jury as though wood retained moral intention.

Mrs. Culross sat behind the defense table in black, twisting a handkerchief. Mr. Quill testified that David had been industrious but reserved. No representative of the Le Baron family appeared.

David listened with amazement while strangers constructed him. One made him a dutiful son; another, an unstable clerk; another, a drunken brute. He was all of these and none. A human being, brought into court, must be reduced to dimensions convenient for twelve men.

When the verdict was read, Mrs. Culross made a little sound.

The judge spoke of the sanctity of life and the alarming prevalence of saloons. Then, in the name of society—which had starved, dismissed, intoxicated, and now condemned him—he sentenced David Culross to twenty years in the State penitentiary.

Twenty years!

He was led below.

In the jail corridor he passed a narrow window and saw a strip of sky. It was blue. He remembered wondering with furious incredulity how the sky could remain blue after judgment.

The train whistle sounded, and the man in the railway coach returned from the past.

A station flashed by. New houses stood where fields had been. Telegraph wires dipped and rose beside the track, stitching distance together. David pressed his forehead against the cold glass.

His mother had died in the sixth year of his imprisonment. A chaplain told him. She had left no property except furniture and a box containing his schoolbooks, his father’s hat, and the letters David had written from prison. The landlady sold what she could and discarded the rest. Thus a household disappears—not with trumpets, but through an auctioneer, a rubbish wagon, and the need of another tenant for the rooms.

Of Zoe he had heard sooner.

During his third month in prison, a letter came.

The warden examined all correspondence. He handed the envelope to David with the expression of one who has inspected a harmless but unnecessary article. The paper smelled faintly of lavender—or David imagined it did. He carried it to his cell and waited before opening it, prolonging hope because hope, once read, might become grief.

The letter began, “My dear, my poor, my beloved David.”

He read those words until they ceased to resemble language.

Zoe wrote that she had learned everything: the dismissal, the saloon, the quarrel, the sentence. She accused herself with the magnificent injustice of love. If she had defied her father sooner, if she had sent a servant, if she had gone to the office, if she had understood David’s despair, Moran would be alive and David free. She did not excuse what he had done; indeed, her refusal to excuse him gave her devotion greater dignity. She grieved for the dead man’s wife and had sent money anonymously to the children.

“But you must not believe,” she wrote, “that because one hour was terrible, all the hours before it were false, or that all the years after it are to contain nothing but punishment. You sinned, David. I know that word is hard, but I will not lie to comfort you. Yet others sinned also—Papa in his pride, I in my weakness, those who dismissed you to curry favor, those who sold you drink when you did not know its power. Only you have been put behind bars, but guilt has not been so neatly imprisoned.”

Her father intended to take her abroad. She would refuse. She had consulted a lawyer. Appeals might be made; petitions might be circulated; the governor might in time be persuaded. She would work, wait, write, hope.

“I love you,” the letter said near its end. “I loved you when you were free and thought yourself insignificant. I love you now when the world thinks you infamous. If I cannot shorten the years, I will at least pass through them beside you in the only way permitted. Do not shut me out. Everything else has been taken from us. Let us not assist them by taking ourselves from each other.”

David laid his head against the wall.

For three days he lived in heaven.

Then he began to think.

He saw Zoe waiting through seasons, pleading with officials, growing pale over petitions, rejecting suitors, surrendering travel, children, music, ordinary happiness. He imagined himself released at forty-six—a stooped, branded man—claiming payment for a young woman’s promise. Love had come to him once as hunger; now, in the cell, it came as renunciation.

To be dead it is not necessary to lie in a grave. But the dead, if they love the living, do not call them into darkness.

David answered her.

The letter cost him more than the sentence.

He wrote that the excitement of recent events had misled them both. His attachment had been shallow, founded upon vanity and the novelty of her attention. Prison had clarified his feelings. He no longer loved her. Her continued interest embarrassed him and could do no good. He requested that she neither write nor petition for him.

He read the page. It appeared sufficiently cold.

Then, fearing one phrase might betray him, he added: “I have already learned to regard our acquaintance as an episode best forgotten.”

He signed his name.

For several nights afterward he bit the blanket to keep from crying aloud.

No answer came.

At first he counted the mail days. Then he told himself she was angry. Anger would rescue her. Presently anger would become contempt; contempt would become indifference; indifference would open the door to another affection. He imagined a man suitable to her—educated, prosperous, gentle. He imagined their wedding, then a house with vines, then children. He gave the children faces. One little girl had Zoe’s eyes and a blue ribbon in her hair.

By such visions he survived.

In his dreams Zoe’s happiness became luxurious because luxury was the only happiness he had seen near her. He furnished her rooms with velvet, silver, flowers, and sunlight. He sent her in imagination to Italy, where she stood before bright seas; to Paris, where she laughed beneath trees; to quiet summer places where her children ran upon grass. He himself remained outside these pictures, a shadow at the edge of the frame, content that she did not turn and see him.

The prison years passed.

No—“passed” is a charitable word. They accumulated.

Here, as in the office, he woke by rule, labored by rule, ate by rule, and slept by rule. Here, as there, errors were recorded and silence rewarded. Yet prison stripped away the polite fiction that his time belonged to him. He stitched harness, cut cloth, carried bundles, and later kept accounts in the workshop because his handwriting, though less fine than formerly, remained serviceable.

Men entered and left. Some returned. Some died. A young burglar taught him chess with pieces made from bread. An old counterfeiter quoted Scripture and stole tobacco. A boy of nineteen hanged himself with a strip torn from his blanket. The keepers spoke of these things as inconveniences.

David’s hair grayed. His shoulders rounded. His face acquired the furtive calm of one who owns no privacy. Hope came less often, but Zoe’s imagined life remained. On Christmas he pictured her table. In spring he pictured her garden. When pain seized his chest one winter, he was comforted by the thought that she would never know.

Sacrifice can contain pride. David never suspected how confidently he had arranged another person’s destiny.

The train drew nearer Chicago.

The salesman folded his newspaper. The mother buttoned her child’s coat. Houses thickened beside the tracks—factories, warehouses, tenements, lumberyards, then streets crowded with vehicles stranger and noisier than those David remembered. Electric lamps burned where gas had been. Steel rails webbed the crossings. Buildings rose with a monstrous ambition, as though brick and iron had discovered vanity.

Chicago had lived twenty years without him.

This offended no law, but it wounded him. He had preserved the city in memory: horse cars, familiar corners, the office window above the spice warehouse, Zoe’s gate under two elms. Yet memory is a miser who keeps coins withdrawn from circulation. The world spends and changes them.

“What are you going to do in town?” asked the salesman suddenly.

David started.

“I do not know.”

“Got people there?”

“No.”

“Job waiting?”

“No.”

The man looked at him, then away. Curiosity had reached pity and become embarrassed.

“You’ll find something,” he said.

“Yes.”

But what could David find? The State had returned him to society as one returns a borrowed tool, worn and obsolete. He knew ledgers, but methods had changed. He had strength, but not much. He had honesty, but a record which made the claim humorous. Freedom without welcome is only a larger cell.

The train entered the station beneath a roof blackened by smoke. Brakes shrieked. Steam swept past the windows. Passengers rose before the cars stopped and gathered parcels, children, umbrellas, expectations.

David remained seated.

“End of the line,” said the conductor.

“Yes.”

“You’ll have to get off.”

“Yes, sir.”

Again the old answer.

He descended to the platform. The crowd took him and divided around him. Porters shouted; wheels rattled; friends embraced; a young man waved his hat; two sisters kissed each other and spoke at once. David stood with his small prison-made valise between his feet.

No one waited for him.

Of course no one waited. His mother was dead. His former companions, if they lived, would not recognize him. Zoe—happy, prosperous, perhaps a grandmother—did not know the date of his release. He had ensured that. The success of his sacrifice now stood before him in the shape of emptiness.

He walked toward the great waiting room.

There, beneath the arch, he stopped. The space was too vast, the noise too various. For twenty years every door had led somewhere prescribed. Now doors opened upon streets, hotels, restaurants, trains, and lives; but possibility, when one has lost the habit of choice, is a wilderness.

He removed his hat. His breath came painfully.

The man in the shadow looked backward for the lad, but the lad was gone. His straight shoulders, his delicate pride, his ridiculous dreams—all gone. The prison had not devoured David at once. It had taken him morsel by morsel, leaving bones clothed in State broadcloth and calling the remainder free.

Then someone spoke his name.

“David.”

He did not turn. The syllables belonged to memory, and memory had deceived him often.

“David Culross.”

A woman stood a few paces away.

She was neither the girl of twenty nor the magnificent lady of his prison dreams. Her dress was plain and dark. Gray threaded the hair beneath her bonnet. Her face had lost its youthful softness and gained lines at the mouth and between the brows—records of patience, work, and sorrow. She held herself very straight, but one gloved hand trembled.

He looked at her eyes.

“Zoe?”

“Yes.”

The station receded from him. Noise became a remote surf.

She came nearer.

“I was afraid,” she said, “that I had missed the train.”

He stared at her. “You knew?”

“I have known the date for twenty years.”

“But my letter—”

“I received it.”

“I told you—”

“I know what you told me.”

His shame returned with such force that he bent his head.

“You should not have come.”

“Should I not?”

“I am not—look at me, Zoe.”

“I am looking.”

“I am an old man.”

She smiled then, and for one instant the girl in the lamp-lit street appeared—not restored, for time restores nothing, but revealed as something that had never wholly departed.

“You are two years younger than I.”

“You have wasted your life.”

“No.”

“You married?”

“No.”

The word fell quietly.

He lifted his head. “Why?”

“Because I loved you.”

“But I wrote that I had forgotten.”

“You wrote very badly.”

“I meant you to believe it.”

“Yes.”

“You knew I lied?”

“I knew you.”

There are answers against which twenty years have no defense.

David gripped the handle of his valise. “Zoe, I killed a man.”

“I know.”

“I was drunk, but that does not bring him back.”

“No.”

“I have been in prison.”

“I know that also.”

“I have nothing. Eleven dollars. These clothes. No work. No name that an honest house would welcome.”

She listened without interrupting, as one permits a fever to spend itself.

“And you,” he continued, “you might have had everything.”

“I had what I chose.”

“Waiting?”

“Not only waiting. I taught music. I quarreled with my father and afterward forgave him. I helped Moran’s daughters until they would accept no more help. I petitioned three governors. I learned that officials are more difficult to move than mountains and less picturesque. I lived, David. Do not insult my life by calling it empty because it was not the life you invented for me.”

He covered his face.

“I thought of you happy.”

“I was sometimes happy.”

“With children—”

“No.”

“In a beautiful house—”

“My house is small.”

“With someone worthy—”

At that her voice sharpened.

“You had no right to decide what was worthy for me.”

“No,” he whispered.

It was the final pride stripped from him. He had believed himself a martyr and discovered that even martyrdom may trespass.

Zoe touched his sleeve.

“I was angry with you,” she said. “For several years, very angry. Then I understood that you had only the prisoner’s one remaining power—the power to refuse. You used it to set me free. But freedom, David, is not obedience to another person’s plan, even a loving plan.”

He lowered his hands. Tears had altered his face, making it for a moment younger and helpless.

“Can you forgive me?”

“I did that long ago.”

“For the letter?”

“For the letter, for the silence, for believing me foolish, for growing old without permission. There is much to forgive between any two people who have loved for twenty years.”

“And Moran?”

Her eyes darkened.

“That forgiveness is not mine to give.”

He bowed his head.

She did not offer him innocence. Love which requires a lie will fail when truth returns. She offered something humbler and more difficult: companionship after guilt, tenderness without acquittal, a place among the living though the dead were not forgotten.

People hurried around them. A porter struck the valise accidentally and apologized. Somewhere a bell rang, and David’s body stiffened by instinct.

Zoe felt it.

“You need not answer that bell,” she said.

He looked about with bewilderment. “What am I to do?”

“First, come with me.”

“Where?”

“Home.”

He repeated the word silently.

She took the valise from him, but he resisted.

“I can carry it.”

“Very well.”

It was a small assertion, yet she yielded it gravely.

They walked toward the street. At the doors David hesitated again. Beyond the glass lay motion, cold air, lamps, horses, electric cars, human beings choosing a thousand roads. Zoe did not urge him. She merely stood beside him until his breathing quieted.

“My house is not far,” she said. “There is a room for you. You may stay a night, or a week, or as long as you choose. Tomorrow we shall speak of work. The next day, if necessary, we shall speak of it again. We need not settle twenty years before supper.”

“Supper,” he said, as if the word belonged to a forgotten language.

“I have made too much of it. I was uncertain whether prison improved the appetite.”

A faint sound escaped him.

It was not quite laughter, but it was no longer grief.

They stepped outside. Evening had gathered over the city, and lights burned along the wet street. The open sky was dark, yet not enclosed; the crowd pressed near, yet no keeper counted them. David walked uncertainly, carrying the little valise which contained all the State had returned of his material life.

Zoe took his arm.

Not the arm of the innocent lad, for he was gone. Not the arm of the imaginary hero who had nobly arranged another’s happiness from his cell. She took the arm of the guilty, broken, aging man who stood beside her—the only David left upon the earth.

“This way,” she said.

And this was the way home.

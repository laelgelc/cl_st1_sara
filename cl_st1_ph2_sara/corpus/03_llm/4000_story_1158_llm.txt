# THE LAST QUAINT MAN  
### A Pastoral Disturbance in One Act

**PERSONS, OR APPROXIMATIONS THEREOF**

**MR. ICKY**, an old farmer, picturesque by profession.  
**PETER**, a boy with two eyes, a nose, and other accepted equipment.  
**ULSA**, Mr. Icky’s daughter, recently exposed to London.  
**RODNEY DIVINE**, a wealthy young man whose spirituality is chiefly financial.  
**CHARLES**, Mr. Icky’s eldest son for the moment.  
**THE OTHER CHILDREN**, numerous enough to affect agriculture.  
**A CURTAIN**, not entirely reliable.  
**MOTHS**, if procurable.

---

The scene is the exterior of a cottage in rural West Issacshire. It is evening, morning, or that economical theatrical hour which can pass for either if the footlights are lowered.

At the back, hills roll away with the patient expression of hills that have rolled away nightly for three hundred performances. On the left is a haystack. On the right is another haystack, unless the management has spent the money on critics. Between them stands the cottage, leaning quaintly upon a McCormick harvester. A rose climbs the wall but has been stopped by a second mortgage.

There should be cows in the distance. If cows are unavailable, two members of the orchestra may chew slowly.

MR. ICKY sits before the cottage. He has white hair, a brown coat, green trousers, and the purple soul of an agricultural sunset. At his feet sits PETER, aged twelve or sixteen according to the child-labour laws. PETER is gazing upward with spiritual attention, though actually watching a beetle climb Mr. Icky’s boot.

A pause full of England.

**MR. ICKY**  
Peter, laddie, have I ever told ye of my youth?

**PETER**  
Seven times since luncheon.

**MR. ICKY**  
Then ye will understand it better now. I was a wicked man in them days. A criminal.

**PETER**  
Did you murder anybody?

**MR. ICKY**  
Nay. I sold a horse twice.

**PETER**  
To two people?

**MR. ICKY**  
That would have been business. I sold him twice to the same bishop.

**PETER**  
Why?

**MR. ICKY**  
He kept forgetting.

(PETER considers the tragedy of episcopal life.)

**MR. ICKY**  
Ay, Peter, there was blood in my veins then. Hot blood. Dangerous blood. I knew smugglers, poets, and a man who subscribed to a London review. Many a midnight have I stood beneath a moon like yon—

(He points. There is no moon. A stagehand hurriedly pushes one over the hill.)

—plotting dark deeds against society.

**PETER**  
What cured you?

**MR. ICKY**  
Glands.

**PETER**  
Whose?

**MR. ICKY**  
I never asked. It seemed indelicate. A scientific gentleman came from Vienna with a carpetbag and transplanted three of them into me. Since that hour I have been young.

**PETER**  
You look seventy-nine.

**MR. ICKY**  
That is the young look in West Issacshire. Before the operation I looked like a cathedral.

(He removes his cap. His bald head glows with moral tranquillity.)

The gentleman also told me to rub soil on my head every spring. It strengthens the roots.

(He takes a handful of earth and rubs it on. A small green shoot immediately appears. PETER plucks it.)

**PETER**  
It’s a radish.

**MR. ICKY**  
The Ickys have always matured early.

(He sighs, using both lungs and part of the harmonium.)

Ah, but science cannot replace a daughter. Since Ulsa went to Lunnon, this old cottage has been empty.

**PETER**  
There are forty-seven people in it.

**MR. ICKY**  
Spiritually empty.

**PETER**  
There are forty-seven spiritualists in it.

**MR. ICKY**  
Do not answer quaintness, boy. It bruises easily.

He gazes toward the road.

**MR. ICKY**  
Ulsa wrote that Lunnon was Life. Life with a capital Jazz. She has seen restaurants where supper begins after bedtime and ladies whose grandmothers are still younger than they are. She will never come back to the soil.

ULSA enters abruptly through the cottage door, although she was last heard of in London. She is dressed in the latest fashion, which has not yet been invented. Her face possesses the tranquil absence of perspective found in primitive paintings.

**ULSA**  
Feyther!

**MR. ICKY**  
Ulsa!

(They embrace. If they fail to meet in the middle, the cottage may be moved.)

**ULSA**  
I have come home.

**MR. ICKY**  
Home! The old word! The deep word! The word that has brought strong men to their knees and weak men to the suburbs!

**ULSA**  
I left my trunk at the station.

**MR. ICKY**  
Then ye have not come home completely.

**ULSA**  
I am pursued.

**PETER**  
By the police?

**ULSA**  
By wealth.

A motor horn sounds offstage with the polished melancholy of a Rolls-Royce. RODNEY DIVINE enters. He wears a perfect coat, perfect gloves, and an imperfect expression. His face indicates that his ancestors conquered England and have regretted the exertion ever since.

**DIVINE**  
Ulsa!

**ULSA**  
Rodney!

**MR. ICKY**  
Stranger, this is a simple country home.

**DIVINE**  
I beg your pardon.

(He takes off his hat to it.)

**ULSA**  
Feyther, this is Rodney Divine. He owns six streets, a South American railway, and a small but influential duke.

**DIVINE**  
The duke is only leased.

**MR. ICKY**  
And what brings ye here, young man?

**DIVINE**  
Love. Also my chauffeur took the wrong turning.

**ULSA**  
We were engaged.

**DIVINE**  
We are engaged.

**ULSA**  
We were engaged until you insulted womanhood, America, and the higher emotions.

**DIVINE**  
I merely expressed an athletic opinion.

**MR. ICKY**  
Speak plainly. We are country folk and do not understand anything under four syllables.

**ULSA**  
He said Frank Gotch could defeat Jack Dempsey.

A thunderclap should occur. If the weather refuses, PETER may drop a tray.

**DIVINE**  
In a wrestling match.

**ULSA**  
You did not say wrestling match.

**DIVINE**  
I thought it.

**ULSA**  
Thinking is no excuse between engaged people.

**DIVINE**  
My dear Ulsa, Gotch possessed science, leverage, speed—

**ULSA**  
Dempsey possessed fists.

**DIVINE**  
Fists are vulgar.

**ULSA**  
So is defeat.

**MR. ICKY**  
Who are these gentlemen? Clergymen?

**PETER**  
Boxers and wrestlers.

**MR. ICKY**  
Ah. Clergymen have souls instead.

**ULSA**  
Rodney, choose. Me—or Frank Gotch.

**DIVINE**  
Could I not retain you socially and admire Gotch in private?

**ULSA**  
No.

(DIVINE walks to the haystack and returns older by an inch.)

**DIVINE**  
Very well. Jack Dempsey would beat Frank Gotch.

**ULSA**  
How?

**DIVINE**  
With the assistance of Providence, the referee, and your interpretation of the rules.

**ULSA**  
Rodney!

They embrace. During this reconciliation, MR. ICKY may perform a card trick to prevent the rural audience from becoming sophisticated.

**MR. ICKY**  
Then all is well. The young have returned to the old hearth. The Great Potato Tradition survives.

A window opens. CHARLES climbs out carrying a sailor’s bag, a volume of Conrad upside down, and a telescope through which he has never looked.

**CHARLES**  
No, feyther. It does not survive.

**MR. ICKY**  
Charles! Why are ye dressed like a nautical advertisement?

**CHARLES**  
I am going to sea.

**MR. ICKY**  
There is water in the pump.

**CHARLES**  
I want oceans. Ports. Typhoons. Strange brown men singing beneath tremendous stars. I want the hot breath of the equator upon my brow.

**PETER**  
You get seasick in the bathtub.

**CHARLES**  
That is because the bathtub lacks horizon.

**MR. ICKY**  
Stay, lad. Think of the land.

**CHARLES**  
I have thought of it. It stays in one place.

**MR. ICKY**  
The farmer is the backbone of the nation.

**CHARLES**  
Who wants to be a backbone? It is dark, jointed, and rarely invited anywhere.

He strides toward the road. At once every door and window of the cottage opens. CHILDREN pour out: boys, girls, twins, triplets, and one doubtful cousin swept along by enthusiasm. They carry golf clubs, saxophones, guidebooks, tennis rackets, aeroplane goggles, paintboxes, cocktail shakers, and a live penguin labelled **ROME**.

**MR. ICKY**  
Who are all these people?

**FIRST CHILD**  
Your children, feyther.

**MR. ICKY**  
All of ye?

**SECOND CHILD**  
Not all. Several are still packing.

More CHILDREN emerge from the chimney.

**MR. ICKY**  
Where are ye going?

**THIRD CHILD**  
Paris!

**FOURTH CHILD**  
Egypt!

**FIFTH CHILD**  
Hollywood!

**SIXTH CHILD**  
Anywhere with electric light!

**SEVENTH CHILD**  
We are tired of turnips.

**EIGHTH CHILD**  
We want speed!

**NINTH CHILD**  
Danger!

**TENTH CHILD**  
Jazz!

**ALL**  
Life! Jazz! Speed! Life!

(They repeat this until the playwright remembers the next line.)

**MR. ICKY**  
But ye belong here, among the quiet furrows, the patient beasts, the honest smell of manure—

**ELEVENTH CHILD**  
That is what we chiefly object to.

**MR. ICKY**  
Think of your ancestors!

**TWELFTH CHILD**  
They are dead.

**MR. ICKY**  
That proves they stayed!

He hurries into the cottage and returns with an enormous Bible. It is wet, having been kept beneath a leaking symbol.

**MR. ICKY**  
Listen! I shall read ye the wisdom of the ages.

(He opens it at random.)

“And he took three hundred foxes, and turned tail to tail, and put a firebrand in the midst between two tails.”

A pause.

**THIRTEENTH CHILD**  
Rather coarse.

**MR. ICKY**  
The next passage will be spiritual.

(He turns several damp pages.)

“Bring me a charger.”

**CHARLES**  
A motor charger?

**DIVINE**  
Possibly a cavalry horse.

**ULSA**  
In London it means a plate.

**MR. ICKY**  
It means stay at home!

**ALL THE CHILDREN**  
It does not!

**MR. ICKY**  
Then the book is damp. It won’t work.

He waves it desperately. Several minor prophets fall out.

**MR. ICKY**  
Children, children, must ye exchange the lark for the saxophone, the plough for the motorcar, the eternal hills for hotels with private baths?

**FIRST CHILD**  
Yes.

**MR. ICKY**  
Even if I command ye?

**ALL**  
Yes!

**MR. ICKY**  
Even if I forgive ye?

**ALL**  
Yes!

**MR. ICKY**  
Even if I become suddenly picturesque?

He assumes a pastoral attitude against the sunset. One knee cracks.

**ALL**  
Especially then!

The CHILDREN march away, playing a jazz version of an old harvest hymn. CHARLES leads them, steering by his upside-down Conrad. ULSA and DIVINE follow, arguing tenderly about whether Nero could have thrown Georges Carpentier. The motor horn bleats. The penguin misses the road and enters the cottage.

MR. ICKY remains alone.

Several hours now pass. They may be represented by lowering and raising the curtain, but the curtain descends too far, knocks off Mr. ICKY’s cap, rises apologetically, descends again, and is finally tied to the moon.

Twilight arrives in a blue shawl.

From far away comes Beethoven’s Ninth Symphony played upon a mouth-organ by someone with astonishing lungs.

**MR. ICKY**  
Alone.

(He says it whimsically, then gloomily, then with the air of a man trying several hats.)

Alone in the old home. The children gone. The Great Potato Tradition mashed. What remains for the last quaint man?

He listens.

**MR. ICKY**  
Nothing.

A duck quacks.

**MR. ICKY**  
Nothing important.

He lies down before the cottage, arranging himself so that the balcony may see his sorrow. Moths descend upon him. At first there are three; then thirty; then an indecent abundance. They cover his coat, his beard, his boots, and finally his moral tranquillity, forming a pale, fluttering shroud.

Music, preferably expensive, trembles in the distance.

PETER enters carrying a small parcel.

**PETER**  
Mr. Icky?

No answer.

**PETER**  
Are you dead?

**MR. ICKY**  
I am considering it.

**PETER**  
I brought you something.

PETER kneels beside him with the complete tenderness available at his age and price. He unwraps the parcel and produces a mothball.

**PETER**  
It’s all I had.

He places it upon MR. ICKY’s chest.

The moths recoil in silent horror and fly away. One remains to finish a sleeve, thinks better of it, and follows.

MR. ICKY opens one eye.

**MR. ICKY**  
Peter, laddie—

**PETER**  
Yes?

**MR. ICKY**  
That is the sweetest gift I have ever smelt.

They sit together beneath the artificial moon. In the distance the children’s jazz fades, returns because they have forgotten the penguin, and fades once more.

The curtain attempts to descend. MR. ICKY catches it, PETER pulls from the other side, and for some moments they play tug-of-war with Eternity. Eventually the curtain tears in half, revealing the entire company waiting behind it with their hats on.

If applause is heard, they bow.

If no applause is heard, MR. ICKY reads another damp passage.

Thus the play may continue indefinitely.

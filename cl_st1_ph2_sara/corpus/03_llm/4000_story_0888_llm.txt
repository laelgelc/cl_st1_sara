## The Last Truffle of Montpont

In Montpont there were six days for commerce, matrimony, lawsuits, funerals, medicine, and the ordinary quarrels of mankind; but Sunday belonged to truffles.

This arrangement, though never confirmed by Parliament or Pope, possessed the authority of both. From November until the first Sunday in March, every citizen who owned a pig, a trowel, and a reputation went into the oak preserves to seek the black diamonds of Périgord. Those who owned no pig followed those who did. Those who owned neither pig nor reputation stood at the Café de la Paix, drank white wine, and explained how truffles ought to be found.

It was a noble sport. The English had their Derby, the Spaniards their bullring, the Italians their opera; but Montpont, which regarded all three as noisy methods of wasting an afternoon, had its Championship of the Truffle.

The rules were ancient, severe, and almost intelligible. Every truffle had to be indicated by a licensed pig, excavated with a trowel not exceeding nine inches, and surrendered to the umpire before sunset. A specimen weighing less than twenty grammes counted for nothing. One above a hundred grammes counted three points and entitled its discoverer to a glass of armagnac at municipal expense. Bets were freely made.

On the final Sunday of that memorable season, Monsieur Bonticu and Monsieur Pantan stood tied at one hundred and forty-seven points.

This circumstance agitated Montpont from the bell tower to the duck pond. No championship had ended in a tie since the disputed season of 1868, when a farmer’s wife concealed three truffles in her apron and claimed that Providence had put them there.

Monsieur Bonticu was the undertaker. He was a large, deliberate bachelor with a complexion like wax left near a stove, black whiskers of magisterial breadth, and hands which could close a coffin lid with the tenderness of a mother covering a sleeping child. In repose he resembled a municipal monument; in motion, an enormous green pepper rolling downhill with dignity. He spoke softly, bowed deeply, and had buried half Montpont without once appearing hurried.

His pig Anastasie was the pride of his life. She was pink, intelligent, and shaped like a well-upholstered footstool. Bonticu had refused seven hundred francs for her, a silver watch, and—during an unfortunate evening at the café—the hand of a widow who owned three hectares of excellent vines.

“Matrimony passes,” he had said. “But a nose such as Anastasie’s is a gift from heaven.”

Monsieur Pantan, veterinarian and apothecary, was his opposite in everything except bachelorhood, vanity, and genius among truffles. He was small, yellow, abrupt, and bristled from chin to eyebrow. Behind the counter of his shop he compounded medicines for man and beast with equal confidence, though not always from different bottles. He had once cured the mayor’s rheumatism with a liniment intended for mules and defended himself by observing that the mayor was walking.

His pig Clotilde was black, compact, and philosophical. She had eyes like currants sunk in suet and a tail of such expressive curvature that Pantan claimed to read weather, soil conditions, and municipal politics in it.

For twenty-two years the two men had been intimate friends. Bonticu embalmed Pantan’s clients when Pantan failed to cure them; Pantan attended Anastasie when she had eaten a glove; they played dominoes every Thursday and insulted the government with perfect accord. But during truffle season friendship retired into private life. On the preserve they were champions, strategists, and enemies governed by courtesy.

That last Sunday dawned cold and brilliantly clear. Frost silvered the furrows. The oaks stood black against a sky the color of watered milk, and from every road came carts, bicycles, dogs, pigs, umbrellas, baskets, and citizens wrapped in garments which had survived several political systems.

At nine o’clock the mayor raised a red handkerchief.

“Messieurs, begin!”

The preserve erupted.

Pigs plunged among the roots. Men followed with trowels. Children shrieked, spectators advised, and dirt flew with the impartiality of grapeshot. The municipal band attempted a march but abandoned it when the bass drummer fell over a sow.

Anastasie opened magnificently, pointing within four minutes beneath a young oak. Bonticu dropped to his knees and dug with the smooth gravity of a priest exposing a relic. A truffle of eighty-six grammes emerged.

“Two points to Monsieur Bonticu!” cried the umpire.

The gallery applauded.

Clotilde replied almost immediately with two smaller finds beside a stone wall. Pantan excavated them like a terrier escaping prison.

“Two points to Monsieur Pantan!”

At ten-thirty Anastasie discovered a hundred-and-twelve-gramme beauty, black, round, and aromatic enough to convert a vegetarian. At eleven Clotilde produced three truffles in seven minutes. At noon the champions remained equal.

They ate beneath separate trees.

Bonticu gave Anastasie half a pear and whispered that victory was less important than character. Pantan fed Clotilde a rind of cheese and informed her that Bonticu had lost speed in his left knee. Neither man ate much himself. Great athletes are sustained by honor, anxiety, and occasional brandy.

Through the afternoon the score shifted by single points. Bonticu led; Pantan overtook him; Bonticu drew level. The spectators streamed after them, arguing over soil, scent, ancestry, and odds. Old Madame Farge bet her winter coal money on Anastasie. The butcher backed Clotilde and offered three sausages against a hat. Even the curé came, ostensibly to discourage gambling, and remained because he had placed five francs discreetly through the sacristan.

At four o’clock the light began to fail. Long blue shadows lay between the oaks. Both men had one hundred and sixty-three points.

“Thirty minutes!” announced the umpire.

Montpont became silent. The pigs moved ahead, snouts low, breathing in short professional grunts. Their owners followed. Bonticu’s stately face shone with perspiration; Pantan’s whiskers projected like the quills of an indignant hedgehog.

For twenty minutes nothing was found.

Then, at the edge of the oldest part of the preserve, Anastasie stopped.

At precisely the same instant Clotilde stopped.

They stood nose to nose beneath a vast oak whose roots twisted out of the earth like the fingers of a buried giant. Anastasie gave the low, decisive grunt which had made her famous from Bergerac to Ribérac. Clotilde gave the same grunt in a higher key.

The gallery held its breath.

Bonticu approached from the east. Pantan approached from the west. Each bowed.

“If Monsieur will be good enough to remove his animal,” said Bonticu, “Anastasie will complete her indication.”

Pantan smiled, displaying several teeth and an opinion.

“With the profoundest respect, Monsieur is mistaken. Clotilde indicated first.”

“Anastasie’s nose touched the ground before Clotilde had finished inhaling.”

“Clotilde does not inhale, Monsieur. She investigates.”

“Anastasie had investigated while Clotilde was still considering whether to inhale.”

The pigs, indifferent to rhetoric, began pawing at the same patch of earth.

The umpire hurried forward. He was a retired notary chosen for his impartiality, deafness, and ownership of no pig. After consulting the judges, examining footprints, and asking a spectator who had seen nothing, he rendered the only possible decision.

“Simultaneous indication. Both hunters may dig.”

This ruling satisfied nobody but was beautifully legal.

Bonticu and Pantan knelt opposite each other. Their trowels entered the soil together. They dug carefully at first, removing crumbs of earth with the delicacy of jewelers. A smell rose from beneath the oak—rich, dark, mysterious: the perfume of autumn leaves, old cellars, wet stone, and dinners not yet cooked.

The crowd moaned with pleasure.

“A giant,” whispered the butcher.

“A patriarch,” said the curé.

“A minimum of four hundred grammes,” declared a man who had never found a truffle.

The black surface appeared.

Both champions stared. The thing was monstrous. It bulged beneath the roots like the heart of the hill itself, a truffle fit for a duke, a congress, or twelve eggs. Its weight would decide not merely the championship but the history of Montpont.

Bonticu reached beneath it from one side.

Pantan reached beneath it from the other.

Their fingers closed at the same second.

“Monsieur will release my truffle,” said Bonticu.

“Monsieur’s confusion is natural,” replied Pantan. “He is holding Clotilde’s truffle.”

“Anastasie indicated it.”

“After Clotilde.”

“Before Clotilde.”

“After.”

“Before.”

Still smiling, they pulled.

The truffle resisted with the moral firmness of an object worth six hundred francs. Bonticu planted one knee against a root. Pantan braced both boots. Anastasie squealed encouragement. Clotilde attempted to bite Pantan’s sleeve.

“Release it,” said Bonticu.

“Gladly, when Monsieur does.”

They pulled again.

There came a muffled sound, not loud, but terrible: the soft tearing sigh of gastronomic catastrophe.

Each man fell backward holding half a truffle.

For several seconds nobody moved. The two halves exposed their marbled interiors to the twilight. A perfume of ruin filled the air.

Bonticu rose first. Dirt covered his coat and one whisker had lost its symmetry, yet he bowed.

“Monsieur,” he said, “has destroyed the finest truffle found in Montpont since records began.”

Pantan sprang up, looking like an enraged cork pulled from a bottle.

“Destroyed? It was Monsieur who pulled.”

“I preserved my rightful property.”

“By tearing mine in two.”

“If Monsieur possessed ordinary self-command—”

“If Monsieur possessed ordinary intelligence—”

The crowd widened its circle. Southern tempers, like southern sauces, should never be crowded while heating.

Bonticu drew himself to his full circumference.

“I will not quarrel,” he said. “I am a peaceful man, accustomed to the dignity of silence.”

“A convenient profession for it,” said Pantan.

There was a murmur. To introduce an undertaker’s profession into controversy was considered sharp, though permissible.

Bonticu paled.

“Monsieur,” he replied, “would do better to return to his powders, with which he poisons the citizens, and to his ointments, with which he cures the horses.”

Pantan’s complexion turned from mustard to saffron.

“At least my clients sometimes recover.”

This was severe. Two matrons crossed themselves. The undertaker’s eyes seemed to retreat to the bottoms of two dark wells.

“At least,” he said slowly, “I do not resemble a pinched radish.”

Pantan stepped closer.

“At least I do not resemble a funeral cushion stuffed with soup.”

The insult had reached the culinary stage, always dangerous in Périgord. The umpire attempted intervention, but the mayor held him back. There are civic moments before which authority is powerless.

Bonticu’s face expanded into a full moon of wrath.

“Monsieur is a thistle.”

“Monsieur is a pudding.”

“Monsieur is a flea.”

“Monsieur is a barrel.”

Then Bonticu, blinded by rage and dirt, uttered the word.

“Puff-ball!”

A woman screamed.

The curé removed his hat. The municipal bandmaster, who had just arrived carrying his dented drum, instinctively crossed the road.

In other provinces a puff-ball is merely a fungus: pale, swollen, flavorless, and apt to discharge dust when kicked. In Périgord, among truffle hunters, it signifies a person without scent, judgment, ancestry, courage, or culinary utility. Men had been imprisoned for less. One mayor had resigned after the word was whispered near him, though subsequent inquiry proved that the speaker had been discussing mushrooms.

Pantan became perfectly still.

“Monsieur called me—”

Bonticu’s anger evaporated, leaving him cold inside his clothes.

“I spoke hastily.”

“A puff-ball?”

“If Monsieur permits an explanation—”

“Before these witnesses?”

“With regret, I withdraw—”

“The word cannot be withdrawn. It has entered history.”

Pantan removed his glove finger by finger. When he spoke, his voice was as precise as a prescription.

“Monsieur Bonticu, I demand satisfaction.”

Bonticu looked at the glove, the crowd, and the two halves of the truffle lying in the dirt like a divided kingdom.

“With weapons?” he asked faintly.

“With the weapons prescribed by the Code of Honor of the Lower Périgord.”

Everyone knew that code. It had been drawn up during the reign of Louis-Philippe by three retired cavalry officers after a dispute concerning a goose. Unlike ordinary dueling codes, which offered a choice of pistols or swords and many opportunities for reconciliation, the Périgord code required pistols at twenty paces, sabers at ten, and daggers upon the exhaustion of ammunition and limbs. It was admired chiefly because nobody had employed it within living memory.

“To the death,” Pantan added.

Bonticu looked at his friend of twenty-two years. He remembered dominoes, shared bottles, Clotilde’s fever, Anastasie’s glove, and a thousand peaceable Thursdays. Then he saw the eyes of Montpont upon him.

Honor is frequently what remains when common sense has left the room.

“I accept,” he said.

The mayor bowed. The umpire sighed. The curé began calculating how rapidly extreme unction could be administered to two men moving in opposite directions.

The duel was fixed for Tuesday at dawn.

No one spoke of the championship. The great truffle, weighed in two portions, came to five hundred and nine grammes. Each half was awarded no points, on the unanswerable ground that half a truffle was not the truffle indicated. Anastasie and Clotilde were led away protesting, perhaps against the ruling, perhaps against being denied the evidence.

Night had fallen when Monsieur Bonticu returned to his shop.

He lit one lamp. Its yellow flame revealed the polished coffins standing in two solemn rows: oak for the prosperous, pine for the prudent, walnut for those who wished to impress posterity. Usually they comforted him. They were honest objects, quietly useful, and incapable of insult.

Tonight they seemed to be waiting.

He removed his coat and sat among them. His knees, reliable during thirty years of funerals, had become two dishes of melting cream. Pistols at twenty paces. Sabers at ten. Daggers afterward. He tried to imagine some honorable route by which both men might miss, tire, apologize, and breakfast together. The Code of the Lower Périgord offered none.

At the end of the room stood a new coffin of exceptional width, commissioned by a miller who had unexpectedly recovered.

Bonticu stared at it.

It was oak, lined with satin, and exactly his size.

On its lid lay a blank silver plate. In the trembling lamplight he could almost see the engraving already:

**MONSIEUR BONTICU**  
**UNDERTAKER OF MONTPONT**  
**DEFEATED BY A PUFF-BALL**

He covered his face with his hands.

Outside, somewhere in the dark town, a pig gave one thoughtful grunt.

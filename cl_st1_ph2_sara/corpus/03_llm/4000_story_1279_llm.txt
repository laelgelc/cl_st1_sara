The Last Light

“You cut it,” Laura said.

The manager looked past her toward the glass doors, where rain had darkened the shoulders of the people coming in. “Cut what, ma’am?”

“The part by the station. The man and the dog.”

The manager was young enough to be embarrassed by her and old enough to hide it under politeness. He wore a dinner jacket shiny at the elbows and had a pencil tucked behind one ear. He glanced at the usher standing beside him. The usher glanced back. It was the quick, guarded look of two people assuring each other that they inhabited the same reasonable world.

“There’s a dog in the station scene,” Laura said. “A brown dog with a muzzle. The strap is too tight. A man stops and loosens it.”

“I don’t recall that, ma’am.”

“I do.”

Behind her, the second-show crowd pressed inward from the rain. Wet coats, laughter, the smell of tobacco and wool. Someone said, “What’s the holdup?” Someone else told him to have a heart. The cashier shut her little brass drawer with a snap.

The manager lowered his voice.

“Pictures get shortened sometimes. If they’re running long, you understand. Or if the print’s damaged.”

“But you showed it Tuesday.”

“Did we?”

“I saw it Tuesday. I saw him.”

The words came out too high. She heard them and hated the sound, but could not pull them back. The manager’s face altered—not softened, exactly, but withdrew.

“The film belongs to the exchange,” he said. “We show what they send us.”

“Then the exchange cut it?”

“I couldn’t say.”

“Who could?”

“Mrs. Vale,” said a voice behind Laura.

She turned. Her brother had come through the rain without an umbrella. Water lay in drops on his broad forehead and ran from his coat collar. Ben never remembered an umbrella. Howie used to laugh and say Ben believed weather was a personal insult and therefore ought to be ignored.

For one wild second she almost told him that. Ben, do you remember? He never carries one—Howie said—

But there was no place for Howie in the lobby except on the screen, and they had cut him out.

Ben took her arm. Not roughly. That made it worse.

“Come on, Laura.”

“They cut the station.”

“I heard.”

“He was there Tuesday.”

“I know.”

“No, you don’t.”

The crowd divided around them. They were an inconvenience now, a stone in a stream. A woman in a red hat stared openly. Her companion bent and whispered. Laura could feel the whisper touch her without hearing it.

Ben said, “Let’s go home.”

Home. He said it as if home were a chair she had left in another room. As if she had only to walk back and sit down.

She pulled her arm free.

“I want the address of the exchange.”

The manager took the pencil from behind his ear, though there was nothing to write. “They’re in Capital City, but it wouldn’t do much good. This print leaves in the morning.”

“Where does it go?”

“I really couldn’t—”

“Where?”

The usher shifted his feet. The manager looked at Ben again. That look. Always that look now: Can’t you manage her? Can’t somebody shut the little door and make her stay behind it?

Ben said, tiredly, “Tell her.”

The manager sighed. “Millbrook, I think. The Palace.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Probably tomorrow night. Now, if you’ll excuse me—”

Laura was already moving toward the doors.

Outside, the rain struck her face and made the street lamps tremble. Millbrook. The Palace. Tomorrow night. She held the words as though they were a ticket someone might snatch from her.

Ben followed.

“You’re not going.”

She walked faster.

“Laura, did you hear me?”

“Yes.”

“You’ve been to this picture six times.”

“Seven.”

“Seven, then.”

“And tonight didn’t count.”

“Didn’t count?”

“He wasn’t in it.”

Ben came around in front of her. Automobiles hissed along the wet street; people passed under black umbrellas, joined and sheltered beneath them. Ben stood bareheaded, blocking her way.

“It’s a bad picture,” he said. “You told me so yourself.”

“It is.”

“Then why—”

He stopped. He knew why. Everyone knew now, and yet they kept asking, as if the answer might change if they used the right sensible tone.

Laura looked beyond him at the theater sign. THE HARBOR ROAD, the electric letters said. A story of passion and peril. Underneath, smaller: CLAIRE DORSET—VANCE MERRILL.

No Howie Vale. No one had known his name. He had crossed behind Vance Merrill for less than half a minute, and the camera had taken him because it was pointed in that direction. That was all.

But Tuesday, when Howie stepped from the station shadow into the white light, Laura’s dead heart had struck once against her ribs.

Not a photograph. She had photographs. Howie standing by their gate, squinting at the sun. Howie in his factory work clothes. Howie on their wedding day, looking solemn because the photographer had commanded it. Those were flat, obedient things. They waited where she put them.

This was Howie moving.

This was Howie not knowing that she watched.

Ben put both hands on her shoulders.

“You can’t keep doing this.”

“Why not?”

“Because it’s making you ill.”

“I was ill before.”

“You have to begin again sometime.”

Begin what? The rooms? The meals? Morning following morning with nothing inside it? People spoke of beginning again as though life were a hem one could rip out and sew straight.

“I’ll go with you to the doctor,” Ben said. “Or we’ll go away. You always liked the lake.”

“Howie liked the lake.”

Ben shut his eyes briefly. “All right. Howie liked it. But you did too.”

Because Howie had been there.

That was what none of them understood. Laura had not liked lakes or dances or Sunday drives. She had liked Howie liking them. He had stood between her and the world and made openings in it. Through him she had seen people without fearing them. Through him she had entered rooms.

And now they told her to go back into the rooms alone.

“I’m going to Millbrook,” she said.

Ben’s hands fell away.

The rain shone on his face like tears, though Ben did not cry. Not even after the explosion, when they waited outside the plant office with the other families and watched men enter carrying lists.

“All right,” he said. “Then I’m taking you home tonight.”

She went with him because the print could not leave before morning.

The Harbor Road had been made three years earlier, before the war contracts filled the Bracken Munitions Works, before the north building blew apart at ten-seventeen on a bright Thursday morning, before twelve wives and three mothers and one shy woman named Laura Vale waited under a clock that ticked much too loudly.

The motion-picture company had come to town because their own station set burned down. For two days the depot swarmed with actors, cables, lamps, carpenters, policemen, curious children, and men who found reasons to miss work. Howie had not missed work. He was going in for the afternoon shift when they filmed the scene.

Laura remembered him telling her about it over supper.

“Whole town’s gone movie-crazy,” he said, buttering bread. “Old Mr. Pike nearly got himself run over trying to look at that blonde actress.”

“Did you see her?”

“Sure. She looked cold.”

That was Howie’s judgment on the celebrated Claire Dorset. She looked cold. He had noticed an extra girl shivering beside a luggage cart and lent her his scarf until someone shouted for her to take her place. He had noticed a porter trying to move two trunks and helped him with one. He had noticed the dog.

He had not mentioned the camera.

Months after Howie’s death, Mrs. Dean from the next street came to Laura’s door. Mrs. Dean rarely came anywhere without a purpose and rarely departed before explaining the purpose three times.

“You mustn’t get excited,” she said.

Laura had not been excited since the plant office.

“But I believe I saw your husband in a picture.”

She was holding a covered dish. People always brought dishes now. Their pity arrived in casseroles and departed washed clean.

“What picture?”

“The Harbor Road. At the Majestic. He’s only there a second, dear. In the background.”

“You’re mistaken.”

“I said to Ed, ‘Why, that’s Howie Vale.’ And Ed said it couldn’t be, but then he looked. The walk was the same. You know how he leaned a little forward, as though he was always pleased to be getting where he was going.”

Laura had closed the door before Mrs. Dean finished.

For two days she did nothing. On the third, she went to the Majestic alone.

She sat through an island storm, a jewel robbery, three embraces, and a murder in a boathouse. The story meant nothing. Faces enlarged themselves, suffered, kissed, accused. Music rolled from the speakers. Around her, people gasped and laughed, sharing feelings made for them.

Then came the station.

Vance Merrill leaped from a moving automobile and hurried toward the platform. The camera followed him along the curb. Travelers crossed. A porter wheeled a trunk. And there—at the far edge, almost hidden by a newspaper stand—Howie walked into the picture.

His hat was pushed back. He carried his lunch pail. He moved quickly, leaning forward exactly as Mrs. Dean had said.

Then he stopped.

A boy held a dog on a rope. The dog shook its head, pawing at a leather muzzle strapped too tightly across its snout. Howie set down his lunch pail. He crouched. His hands went to the strap.

The hero hurried on toward passion and peril. Howie missed his train in the story that was not his story. He loosened the buckle, rubbed the dog’s head, said something to the boy. The boy grinned. Howie picked up his pail and turned away.

Before he left the frame, he smiled.

Not at the camera. Not at Laura. At something the boy had said, perhaps, or simply because the dog had stopped suffering.

Then he was gone.

Laura remained rigid through the rest of the picture. When the lights came up, the woman beside her asked if she felt faint.

“No,” Laura said.

She bought another ticket.

The second time, she knew when he would come. Her hands began to shake before the station appeared. He entered. He stopped. He bent. He smiled.

Again.

The living gesture repeated itself because machines could command it. Again the hand at the buckle. Again the quick look at the boy. Again the smile.

Howie had done that once, on one ordinary afternoon, not knowing it would become the last thing.

The next day she watched three showings.

When The Harbor Road left the Majestic, something was taken from her for a second time.

Yet this time there was a road to follow.

At Millbrook the Palace stood between a five-and-ten and a store selling farm machinery. Laura arrived an hour before the first evening show and sat in the railway waiting room with her suitcase upright between her knees.

She had packed a nightdress, a comb, and Howie’s gray sweater. She did not know why she brought the sweater. It no longer smelled of him. Ben had folded it into a drawer after the funeral, and when she found it weeks later she pressed it to her face until the wool scratched her cheek.

People came and went. A soldier kissed a girl by the timetable. An old man slept with his chin on his cane. Two children chased each other around the benches until their mother caught them and delivered a slap to each indiscriminately.

Howie would have smiled at them. Not at the slap. At their running.

He had smiled readily, but never as though he were giving away something cheap. His smile made room. That was the difference.

Laura had not known how much room one person could make around another until his was gone.

At six-thirty she walked to the Palace.

The cashier told her the film had not arrived.

“What do you mean?”

“Truck broke down outside Carlton.”

“When will it come?”

The girl chewed gum and consulted a handwritten sheet. “Maybe tomorrow.”

“Maybe?”

“That’s what they said.”

“Could it be damaged?”

“Lady, I haven’t seen it.”

“Could the heat damage it? If it stayed in the truck?”

The girl’s chewing slowed. “Film burns, I guess.”

Laura gripped the brass edge of the window.

Behind those doors, a comedy played. Laughter rose and fell. An audience laughed together in the dark while Howie lay shut in metal cans on a broken truck somewhere beyond Carlton, perhaps near a ditch, perhaps in the sun.

“Can you telephone them?”

“Who?”

“The driver.”

“I don’t have his number.”

“The exchange, then.”

“Office is closed.”

Laura remained at the window.

The cashier leaned sideways to see around her. “Next, please.”

There was no next. Still, Laura moved away.

She spent the night at a boardinghouse where the wallpaper had yellow roses and the mattress dipped in the center. At two in the morning she heard a man cough in the next room. At three a baby cried. At four pipes knocked inside the wall.

Howie would have been asleep beside her. He slept heavily, one hand curled under his cheek. Sometimes she woke frightened for no reason and listened for his breath. She never touched him, because touching might wake him. It was enough to hear.

Had it been enough? She had thought so then. If you possess a thing every day, you do not know it is everything. You call it little names: breakfast, footsteps, someone breathing.

At ten the next morning, the truck arrived.

The Palace manager permitted Laura to sit through the afternoon show after she bought two tickets, one for herself and one, he said, “for the seat you’re wearing out.” It was a joke. She knew by his expectant face that she ought to laugh.

The print was scratched. Rain seemed to fall through scenes filmed in sunshine. At the boathouse murder, the image jumped, and part of a sentence vanished.

Then the station came.

Howie stepped out from behind the newspaper stand.

Laura’s lungs opened.

There he was. The hat. The lunch pail. The forward-leaning walk. He stopped by the dog. His fingers worked under the strap.

A white scar ran down the center of his face, a scratch in the film. It shook and disappeared. For one second the picture blurred, and Howie’s body dissolved into light.

“No,” Laura whispered.

But he returned. He smiled and walked away.

She came out of the theater weak with relief.

It was pain. Of course it was pain. Seeing him brought Thursday morning back—the sirens, the dirty smoke standing over town, a woman repeating, “Mine’s in north, mine’s in north,” as if saying it could move her husband to another building. Each showing carried Laura toward that morning.

But if pain was the only place where Howie still moved, then pain was where she must go.

Ben found her at the Millbrook station.

He sat down beside her without greeting and removed his hat. His hair was damp around the edges.

“How did you know?”

“You left the theater schedule on the kitchen table.”

“I didn’t mean to.”

“I know.”

The train would not come for forty minutes. Ben turned the hat in his hands.

“Come stay with Ruth and me.”

“No.”

“Just for a while. The children miss you.”

The children had been trained to kiss her softly and not make noise. They watched her with solemn, curious eyes. Once, the youngest asked, “Aunt Laura, are you still sad?” Ruth had hurried him from the room.

“Yes,” Laura had wanted to answer. “I am still.”

Instead she said, “I have my house.”

“You don’t live in it. You sit.”

“I cook.”

“You heat soup.”

“I clean.”

“You clean the same things over and over.”

“They get dusty.”

“Laura.”

People crossed the station around them. A woman dropped a glove; a stranger picked it up and called after her. A porter steadied an old lady at the step. A boy divided a candy bar with his sister after first measuring the halves against each other.

All that motion. All those little claims people had on one another.

Ben said, “Howie wouldn’t want this.”

She stood so quickly her suitcase struck the bench.

“Don’t.”

“I knew him too.”

“Not like I did.”

“No. But I knew he wouldn’t want you chasing some old film until you fall down.”

“You don’t know what he wanted for me.”

“I know he wanted you alive.”

“I am alive.”

Ben looked at her. His eyes were red-rimmed from travel or anger. “Are you?”

The train whistle sounded far down the track.

She hated him then. For one minute—perhaps less—she hated him with such purity that it strengthened her.

“You all think I had him and therefore I should be grateful,” she said. “As if having much makes nothing easier afterward.”

“I don’t think it’s easy.”

“You think it should be finished.”

“No.”

“You do. Finished neatly. Poor Laura, but it’s been six months. Poor Laura, but she must get out. Poor Laura, but people die.”

Ben stood too. “Yes, people die.”

The train came between them with smoke and noise.

She did not let him carry her suitcase.

The film moved from Millbrook to Avery, from Avery to Linton, then south to Bell’s Crossing. Laura learned to read the trade listings. She wrote to exchanges, telephoned managers, memorized railway schedules. The Harbor Road was old now and went only to small theaters that rented cheaply. It appeared for one night, sometimes two, paired with comedies or newsreels.

In Avery, the operator had removed half the station sequence because of a torn sprocket edge. Howie entered, bent toward the dog—and vanished in a burst of white. The next scene showed Claire Dorset waiting on a train.

Laura cried out.

An usher shone his flashlight along the row. “Something wrong, miss?”

“Yes!”

The word rang louder than she intended.

“What is it?”

“A piece is missing.”

He looked toward the screen, where Claire Dorset was weeping beautifully into a fur collar. “Looks all right to me.”

“The man by the dog.”

“Please keep your voice down.”

Afterward, Laura waited outside the projection booth.

The projectionist was a narrow man with nicotine-yellow fingers. “Had to cut a few feet,” he said. “Would’ve jammed.”

“Where is it?”

“Where’s what?”

“The piece you cut.”

“Scraps go in the bin.”

“Show me.”

He stared. “What for?”

“I want it.”

“Can’t give away film. Belongs to the distributor.”

“It’s a scrap.”

“Still can’t.”

“I’ll pay.”

“For six feet of scratched stock?”

“Yes.”

He pushed open the booth door and called to the manager. “Hey, Art, what’re we selling souvenirs for?”

She heard their laughter. Not cruel laughter, perhaps. Ordinary laughter. That was sometimes crueler, because it did not even see what it struck.

The manager came, wiping his mouth with a handkerchief. They explained regulations. Nitrate danger. Company property. Fire law.

“There’s a man on it,” Laura said.

“There are lots of people on it,” the manager replied.

“No. There’s one.”

They found the severed strip eventually, crumpled with cigarette paper and clipped advertisements. The projectionist held it toward the booth lamp.

Small black frames. A dog’s pale head. Half of Howie’s coat. Fingers.

Laura reached, but the projectionist drew it back.

“Sorry. Can’t.”

She wanted to seize it. She imagined her nails across his yellow fingers, the strip tearing between them, Howie divided once more.

Instead she lowered her hand.

In the street outside, people moved past lighted windows. A waitress carried three bowls of soup along a counter. A man knelt to tie a little girl’s shoe. In an upstairs room, someone pulled down a shade.

Life everywhere, but sealed away from her as though behind glass.

In Linton, Howie’s entire scene remained, but the theater was nearly empty. Two farm boys threw popcorn at each other. A couple kissed in the back row. An old woman slept, waking whenever the music grew loud.

Laura watched twice.

On the third showing, the cashier said, “You must sure like Vance Merrill.”

“I don’t.”

The girl laughed. “What you coming back for, then?”

Laura tried to explain. “There’s someone in it. Someone I knew.”

“One of the actors?”

“No.”

“An extra?”

“He wasn’t acting.”

That was important. Howie had not been acting. Claire Dorset pretended terror; Vance Merrill pretended courage. Howie saw a strap hurting an animal and stopped. The picture had caught truth by mistake and carried it through towns where nobody recognized it.

The cashier shrugged. “Well, you should get the studio to send you a photograph.”

A photograph.

Laura went in without answering.

At Bell’s Crossing, a storm delayed the train, and she arrived after the station scene had passed. She waited in the lobby for the next show. A little boy spilled orange drink near her shoes. His mother slapped his hand and said, “Look what you did!”

The boy’s mouth folded.

Howie would have—

Laura moved to another bench.

She could not do what Howie would have done. She was not Howie. People had come easily to him. He remembered names, carried parcels, fixed gates, spoke to lonely men in stores. He left home for a loaf of bread and returned having found a job for someone’s nephew.

“Do you have to bring everybody home with you?” Laura asked once, after he invited a new factory worker to Sunday dinner.

“Only the hungry ones,” he said.

She pretended annoyance. Secretly, she depended upon it. Howie made the invitation; Laura cooked. Howie opened the door; Laura stood in the warmth behind him. Guests praised her pie, and Howie said, “Told you she could beat any bakery in the county.” He offered her to the world proudly, but he never pushed.

Without him, the door stayed shut.

At Bell’s Crossing she saw his scene at nine-fifteen. The print fluttered; his smile appeared too quickly, then was gone.

The following morning a letter from Ben waited at home.

Laura—

Ruth says I spoke badly at Millbrook. She is right. I don’t know how to help you, and that makes me talk like helping is simple. It isn’t.

But I am afraid for you. The exchange says the film may be withdrawn soon. You ought to know that. Please don’t hear it from a stranger.

Come Sunday if you can. The kids will behave, though I hope not too much.

Ben

She read the middle paragraph four times.

Withdrawn.

She telephoned the exchange. A clerk transferred her to another clerk, who transferred her to a man named Mr. Sloane.

“The Harbor Road?” he said. “That old thing?”

“Where is it going after Bell’s Crossing?”

“Let me check.”

Paper crackled. Laura pressed the receiver hard to her ear.

“Let’s see. Mason, then Granton, then there’s a tentative booking in Delphia.”

“And after Delphia?”

“Scrap, most likely.”

“What does that mean?”

“Print’s worn out. We reclaim what we can.”

“What do you do with it?”

“What’s that?”

“The film.”

“Depends. Melt it down, salvage the silver, dispose of the base.”

Melt it.

Howie’s hands clouding in chemicals. His smile washed from the strip. Not merely hidden in a can or cut from a scene, but made never visible again.

“You can’t,” she said.

Mr. Sloane paused. “Ma’am?”

“You can’t destroy it.”

“It’s commercial stock.”

“I’ll buy it.”

He laughed once, then stopped when she said nothing.

“Even if the company agreed, it’s dangerous to keep. And the print isn’t worth anything.”

“It is to me.”

“I’m sorry.”

“You aren’t.”

She replaced the receiver.

For three days she remained in the house. Ben came twice. The first time she did not answer. The second time he used his key and found her at the kitchen table with railway maps spread before her.

“Laura.”

“It goes to Delphia.”

“I know.”

“It may be the last showing.”

He took off his coat. “Then what?”

She traced the railway line with one finger. Delphia was two hundred miles away. Two changes of train.

“Then I go.”

“And after?”

She looked up.

Ben pulled out a chair. He appeared older than he had at Howie’s funeral. There were gray strands above his ears. Had they been there before? Laura did not know. Grief made other people indistinct.

“Suppose you see it in Delphia,” he said. “Suppose the scene is perfect. What will you do when it ends?”

“I don’t know.”

“You do know.”

She gathered the maps into a pile.

“Laura, you’ve made the picture into Howie.”

“No.”

“You have.”

“No! The picture is nothing. The story is foolish. The acting is foolish. But he is there.”

“A shadow of him.”

“He moves.”

“He’s gone.”

The room became silent.

Ben’s face blanched. “I’m sorry.”

“They said that at the plant.”

“I know.”

“They came through the door with the list. Mr. Allen wouldn’t look at me. He said, ‘Mrs. Vale, I’m sorry.’ As though those words carried information. As though I didn’t know before he said them.”

Ben reached across the table. She drew her hands into her lap.

“He was the one thing,” she said.

“Don’t say that.”

“He was.”

“You had a life before him.”

“No. I had days.”

“And you’ve got people now.”

“Whose people? Yours. Ruth’s. Howie’s. Never mine.”

“You’ve got me.”

She looked at him.

Pain moved across his face—plainly, with no screen to soften it. She had put it there. She knew that. Yet knowing did not tell her how to remove it.

Ben rose.

“I won’t stop you,” he said. “I could, maybe. Tell the station agent not to sell you a ticket. Get a doctor to say you’re not fit. But I won’t.”

He put on his coat.

“At least take money.”

“I have money.”

“Take more.”

He laid bills on the table and left before she could refuse them.

The journey to Delphia took all day. Bare trees ran beside the train. Villages appeared and withdrew: a mill, a schoolyard, washing on a line. In each place people lived without knowing that Laura passed. In each place someone perhaps tightened a child’s scarf, opened a shop, fed a stray cat. Or no one did. The world did not depend upon Howie, though hers had.

At the second change, a red-haired woman sat opposite Laura and began talking almost at once. Her husband sold machine parts. Her oldest boy was stationed overseas. Her youngest had asthma. She disliked train coffee and admired Laura’s handbag.

Laura answered when required.

“You visiting family?” the woman asked.

“No.”

“Business?”

“In a way.”

The woman smiled. “You don’t look like a businesswoman.”

Laura turned toward the window.

After a while the woman took an apple from her bag and cut it with a small knife. She offered half.

“No, thank you.”

“Go on. It’s a long ride.”

“I’m not hungry.”

The woman wrapped the half in paper and laid it beside Laura anyway. When she left at Preston, the apple remained.

Laura stared at it for several miles.

Howie would have eaten it. He would have known the woman’s name, her husband’s name, the asthmatic child’s age. Not because he intended virtue. He simply lived as if other lives gave off warmth.

Laura put the apple in her handbag.

Delphia was colder than home. Wind traveled straight along the avenue, lifting grit. The Crown Theater stood in a declining block near the river. Half the bulbs in its sign were dark. Posters for The Harbor Road had been pasted over older posters, and Claire Dorset’s paper face was torn across one eye.

The evening show had been canceled.

“Projector trouble,” the ticket seller said. He was an elderly man in a green visor.

Laura could not understand the sentence. The words were simple, but they would not join.

“When will it be repaired?”

“Fellow’s working on it.”

“Tonight?”

“Hope so.”

“You have to show the picture.”

“Planning to.”

“It’s the last time.”

“Wouldn’t break my heart. Haven’t sold twenty tickets all week.”

“You don’t understand.”

“No, ma’am,” he said, not unkindly. “Reckon I don’t.”

She waited in the lobby. At seven, the old man gave her coffee. At eight, a mechanic came down the stairs carrying a toolbox and shaking his head.

Laura stood.

“What is it?”

“Take-up gear’s worn.”

“Can you fix it?”

“Fixed enough, maybe. She might chew the film.”

The old man said, “Don’t tell her that, Gus.”

“Tell me.”

The mechanic looked from one to the other. “We can run slow. If the splice holds.”

“Run it.”

“Lady—”

“Please.”

The word came quietly this time.

Perhaps it was the quietness that persuaded them. Or perhaps theaters, even poor ones, must show something when people have bought tickets. At eight-twenty the doors opened.

There were eleven people in the audience. Laura counted them as she took a seat near the center: two young couples, three laborers, an old woman, a mother with a child, and Laura. The child was a girl of five or six in a blue coat too large for her. She climbed into the seat beside Laura while her mother arranged parcels on the other side.

“Sit still, Nannie,” the mother said. “Mama’s tired.”

The lights lowered.

The newsreel came first, then a cartoon that made the child laugh. Laura’s whole body tightened when the main title appeared.

Scratches ran over the harbor. Sound swelled and thinned. Vance Merrill’s voice dragged for a fraction of a second, then caught up with his mouth.

The print was worse. Much worse.

Laura watched not the story but the damage. A dark blotch pulsed at the corner. Splices flashed. The image jumped in the gate. Each fault was a threat moving toward the station.

Would there be anything left?

The child beside her whispered questions. “Why’s that man mad? Is the lady going to drown? Is that her real hair?”

Her mother said, “Hush.”

The boathouse scene ended.

Next came the automobile on the station road.

Laura’s hands clamped around the arms of her seat.

The picture slowed. Vance Merrill stepped from the automobile with strange, heavy grace. Music groaned.

Then the screen went white.

The audience muttered.

“No,” Laura said.

A black burn opened in the whiteness, round and trembling. It widened like a mouth. The projector stopped. Darkness fell.

One of the laborers called, “What you givin’ us?”

Someone laughed. Someone stamped his feet.

Laura did not move. The darkness was complete, but she could still see the burn spreading through Howie’s scene. Fire had taken him first in the north building. Now fire took this.

She heard the projector start again.

Light struck the screen. A luggage cart jerked sideways. The station clock appeared, vanished, appeared again. The image rolled, splitting faces in half.

And Howie came into view.

His body slid upward, broke at the middle, returned. The white scratch crossed him. He reached the dog, but the frame trembled so violently that his hands became many hands, none able to find the buckle.

Laura leaned forward.

“Please,” she whispered. To the machine, to Howie, to time itself. “Please.”

The child had grown quiet. Her warm shoulder rested against Laura’s arm.

On the screen, Howie crouched.

The image shuddered once more—and steadied.

Everything cleared.

His fingers slipped beneath the leather strap. His head bent. The dog ceased pawing and looked up at him. Howie rubbed the rough fur between its ears.

Then he turned toward the boy.

His smile filled the little part of the screen that was his.

Laura saw it more clearly than she had ever seen it. Not because the print was clear; rain of scratches still fell around him. Not because it lasted; it was almost gone already. She saw because, suddenly, there was nothing to seize.

That smile had never been meant for her.

The thought ought to have hurt. Instead it opened something.

It had been for the boy. For the dog. For whoever stood nearest and needed the small thing he could give. Howie had not carried kindness around to bring home untouched. He had spent it. Freely, carelessly, everywhere. That was why strangers remembered him. That was why a meaningless picture had caught him: because he had never waited for a proper story in which to be good.

The child beside Laura sagged in sleep.

Her head tipped, jerked upright, then tipped again toward the hard wooden arm between the seats. Before Laura thought, she raised her hand.

She guided the child’s head past the wood and onto her shoulder.

The blue wool coat was warm against her. The child sighed, trusting without waking. A curl touched Laura’s chin.

On the screen, Howie picked up his lunch pail.

He walked away.

Laura did not reach after him.

The station scene ended. Claire Dorset appeared, pale and dramatic beside the train, but Laura was aware of the living weight against her side. It was not much weight. Still, she held herself carefully so the child could sleep.

After a moment the mother leaned across.

“I’m sorry. Nannie, wake up.”

“No,” Laura whispered. “Let her.”

The mother looked at her, surprised.

“She won’t bother me,” Laura said.

And she did not.

Laura sat through the remaining picture without watching it. Her shoulder began to ache. She welcomed the ache. It came from holding something living.

When the lights rose, the child opened her eyes and stared at Laura with grave confusion.

“You fell asleep,” Laura said.

The girl considered this, then smiled.

Her mother gathered the parcels. “Thank you. She’s been up since dawn. We came to meet her father, but his bus never—well. Thank you.”

Laura helped the child button the large blue coat. One button had been put in the wrong hole. Laura corrected it.

Outside, the wind had fallen. The theater manager stood under the sign smoking a cigarette.

“Made it through,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Print goes back tomorrow.”

“I know.”

“You still want to buy it?”

Laura looked at the metal cases beside the ticket booth. In one of them Howie waited at the station. If they ran the reel again, he would stop again. If they burned it, he would disappear. Both things were true, and neither was the truth she had sought.

“No,” she said.

The manager nodded as if this were the first sensible answer she had given him.

Laura walked to her hotel. At the corner she passed a man struggling to drag a crate from a delivery truck. She hesitated.

It would be absurd. The crate was too heavy; she was a small woman. Howie would have known what to say. He would have put one shoulder under the edge and made a joke.

She was not Howie.

Still, she said, “Could I hold the door?”

The man glanced up. “Would you? It keeps swinging shut.”

Laura held it. The man backed through with the crate.

“Much obliged,” he said.

That was all.

In the hotel room she ate the apple the red-haired woman had given her. It had browned along the cut, but it was sweet.

The next morning Laura telephoned Ben before boarding the train.

Ruth answered.

“Laura? Where are you? Are you all right?”

“I’m in Delphia.”

There was a pause, full of all the things Ruth had been told not to say.

“Did you see the picture?”

“Yes.”

“And—was it all right?”

It was not all right. Nothing was right in that complete, impossible way. Howie was dead. The house would be empty when Laura entered it. His sweater would remain folded in the drawer. Some mornings she would wake before remembering and lose him all over again.

But there were other truths beside that one.

“Is Ben there?” she asked.

He came to the telephone.

“Laura?”

“I saw it.”

“I’m glad.”

“They’re destroying the print.”

Ben waited.

“It’s all right,” she said.

“Is it?”

“No. But—Ben, I’m coming home.”

He breathed out.

“I’ll meet you.”

“You don’t need to.”

“I know.”

The old refusal rose in her. She nearly told him not to come. She could find her own way from the station. She did not need anyone managing her.

Then she remembered his face at the kitchen table when she had said she had no people.

“All right,” she said. “Meet me.”

At home, the rooms were as she had left them. Dust lay on the mantel. A cup stood upside down beside the sink. Howie’s shoes waited in the closet because she had not been able to move them.

Laura touched the sleeve of his coat.

She understood no miracle. The coat did not warm. His step did not sound in the hall. The dead did not return because one had learned a lesson in a shabby theater.

She sat on the edge of the bed and wept until evening.

But on Sunday she went to Ben and Ruth’s.

The children forgot to behave within ten minutes. One shouted, one spilled milk, and the youngest brought Laura a wooden horse with only three legs.

“Can you fix him?”

Laura examined it. “I don’t know.”

“Uncle Howie could.”

The room stopped.

Ruth turned from the stove, alarmed. Ben set down his fork.

Laura looked at the child. He had spoken only a fact.

“Yes,” she said. “He probably could.”

“Can you?”

“Perhaps we can try.”

They found glue in Ben’s workroom. The child held the horse while Laura fitted the leg into place. Too much glue squeezed out, and he laughed.

“It’s messy.”

“Yes.”

“Uncle Howie made things messy too.”

Ben made a sound that was almost a laugh and almost not.

They left the horse to dry on the windowsill.

After dinner Laura washed dishes with Ruth. When Ruth’s back was turned, Laura wrapped two slices of cake for Ben to take to work. Howie always claimed cake tasted better from a lunch pail.

Weeks passed.

The Harbor Road was returned to the exchange. Perhaps it was destroyed. Perhaps a clerk mislabeled the cans and pushed them into a warehouse corner. Perhaps Howie’s smile slept there in darkness, scratched and flammable, waiting for no one.

Laura did not ask.

She began to notice things that had been occurring all along: Mrs. Dean’s difficulty carrying coal, the postman’s cough, the new family across the street with curtains but no rods. Noticing hurt at first. Every need recalled the one who would once have answered it.

Then, little by little, the recalling became an answer.

She carried Mrs. Dean’s smaller coal bucket. She left soup for the postman. She lent the new family a hammer and stayed to help hang the curtains, though she struck her thumb and had nothing clever to say.

People did not become easy. She remained shy. Invitations still frightened her; crowded rooms tired her; strangers’ talk often left her searching helplessly for a reply. She could not become the man who went out for bread and returned with three new friends.

She could hold a door.

She could straighten a child’s coat.

She could listen when a red-haired woman on a train wished to speak of her sons.

These things were small. That was why they could be done.

One spring afternoon, Laura passed the Majestic and saw that a new picture was playing. The posters showed a desert, an airplane, a woman looking bravely into the distance. The Harbor Road had vanished from the theater’s memory as completely as chalk from a board.

For a moment Laura saw the old station scene laid over the street: the newspaper stand, the boy, the suffering dog. Howie stepping from shadow into light.

She stopped.

People flowed around her. A delivery boy balanced parcels on a bicycle. Two women argued cheerfully over the price of stockings. Near the curb, an elderly man dropped his cane. It rolled beneath a parked automobile.

Laura went to retrieve it.

“Thank you, miss,” he said. “These hands aren’t what they used to be.”

She gave him the cane. He smiled and moved on.

In the theater lobby, behind glass, electric lights flashed over faces that seemed larger and brighter than life. Laura looked at them once, then turned away.

The afternoon sun lay warm on the pavement. Someone needed directions at the corner. A child was crying across the street. Mrs. Dean expected her at four.

Howie was nowhere she could follow.

Howie was everywhere she could stop.

## The Short Way to Wychford

Mr. Albert Pemm was forty-seven years old, a surveyor’s clerk by profession, and not, as he often explained, a surveyor. The distinction mattered in the office at Croydon, where accuracy was expected without the salary that commonly accompanies it. He could read a plan upside down, estimate an acre by eye, and detect, beneath the most ornamental handwriting, the intention to avoid paying a fee.

Yet the postcard which brought him to Wychford defeated him.

It was from Mr. Lucius Harcourt, of Wychford Place, and contained directions from the station. These were plain enough until the final sentence, where rain or tea had washed the ink into a purple cloud.

“Cross stile beyond chalk-pit,” Mr. Pemm read in the train, “keeping red house ahead. Short way through Little Wychford Wood if you—”

The next word might have been “care.” It might equally have been “dare.”

He smiled at the latter possibility. Country gentlemen, he knew, affected humour upon postcards. The business concerned the improvement of the western prospect from Mr. Harcourt’s new terrace. Certain trees were to come down; a plantation might be thinned; levels and bearings must be taken. It was an ordinary job, and he had brought an umbrella because the day was brilliant.

Outside Wychford station the road climbed between high banks powdered with primroses. Mr. Pemm walked with his coat unbuttoned, breathing an air that seemed to have come over the Downs for the express purpose of blowing London out of him. The wind shouted in the telegraph wires, tore at the hazel, and sent enormous white clouds cannoning across a sky of hard blue. Far off, the Downs lifted themselves like a green wave, and on their crest a dark ring of trees sailed like a ship.

In such weather Mr. Pemm became, privately, a different man. The umbrella was a sword; the dust upon his boots belonged to a campaign; Wychford Place, whose red chimneys showed above the elms, was certainly a castle. He had only to ride in with spurs ringing, rescue somebody’s daughter, and perhaps receive a small estate in gratitude.

His bowler hat flew off.

It went bowling along the lane with an indecorous speed. Mr. Pemm pursued it, recovered it from a bed of nettles, and, finding that a pheasant had risen under his feet with an explosion like gunfire, dropped both hat and umbrella. By the time he reached the stile beyond the chalk-pit, the knight-errant had become once more a heated clerk with a green stain upon one knee.

From the stile he saw the red house clearly, two meadows away. Between him and it stood a little wood, compact and round, scarcely more than a clump, with fresh beech leaves shining at its edge. Mr. Harcourt’s alterations, he supposed, concerned this very patch. It certainly interrupted the prospect. A dozen men and a traction engine would settle it.

The path divided. One branch skirted the wood; the other entered it through a low wooden gate. Upon the gate was nailed a weathered notice:

TRESPASSERS WILL BE PERSECUTED.

Mr. Pemm read it twice and laughed.

“Very good,” he said. “Rustic humour.”

The second “r” had, no doubt, faded out. Or perhaps some village boy had painted one in. He lifted the latch.

The wind stopped immediately.

That was natural. The wood afforded shelter. Nevertheless, the silence had the effect of a door closing behind him. Outside, the whole country had been in motion—grass streaming, clouds racing, hedges tossing their arms. Here the leaves hardly stirred. Sunlight slipped between them in small green pieces, flickering like silver wings over the path.

The way was broad at first and pleasantly dry. Anemones whitened the banks; bluebells stood among the roots; young bracken uncurled with the furry deliberation of sleeping animals. Mr. Pemm advanced cheerfully. Fifty yards, he judged, would bring him out. Seventy at most.

After seventy yards the path turned left.

That was absurd, because the red house had lain a little to the right. Still, woodland paths were made for timber, not convenience. He followed it until it opened into a glade, and there, through a break in the trees, he saw the meadow.

It appeared to be the meadow from which he had just come.

“That won’t do,” he said.

He retraced his steps, keeping the sun on his right shoulder. The path, however, divided where he had not noticed it dividing before. One branch dipped into hollies; the other climbed beside a bank. Since the wood stood on level ground, Mr. Pemm disliked the bank. He selected the hollies.

Something moved ahead.

It was a person, apparently—a slight figure in greenish brown, slipping between two beeches. Mr. Pemm glimpsed a narrow back and a head covered by what might have been hair, or leaves, or a close-fitting cap.

“Hi!” he called. “Is this the way to Wychford Place?”

The figure vanished behind a trunk.

Mr. Pemm quickened his pace, rounded the beech and found nobody. There was nowhere for anybody to have gone except through a dense wall of thorn.

“A trick of light,” he decided. “Young birch blowing about.”

No birches grew there, and no wind blew.

He continued. The hollies pressed nearer, their polished leaves clicking softly. Brambles caught his trousers with small, persistent hooks. Once a long spray fastened itself round the umbrella and appeared unwilling to let go. Mr. Pemm jerked it free.

“None of that,” he said, with the authority he used toward junior draughtsmen.

The path narrowed, widened, and returned him to the glade.

This time three figures stood at the farther edge.

They were too short for men and too slender for children. Their garments—or bodies, for he could not distinguish one from the other—had the brown-green colour of moss after rain. They watched him without movement. Behind them a brighter opening showed, and beyond that opening, unmistakably, rose the red chimneys of Wychford Place.

“Good afternoon,” called Mr. Pemm.

A gust passed high over the wood. The upper branches bent and whispered; below, the figures ran, not away from him but sideways, each entering a tree as neatly as a shadow enters darkness.

Mr. Pemm removed his hat and wiped his forehead.

The explanation was perfectly simple. Three saplings, alternately lit and shaded by moving cloud, had produced an illusion of motion. The human eye, especially after a railway journey, was unreliable. He had seen the house; that was the important thing.

He crossed the glade.

Before he reached its farther side, a thorn branch descended across the opening. It had not been there before. Or, of course, he had failed to observe it. He ducked under, advanced six paces, and found an oak barring his way.

It was an old oak, hollow-bellied and squat, with one great limb extended at the height of his chest. There must be a path around it. Mr. Pemm tried the right side and met a tangle of dog-rose. He tried the left and came against nettles as high as his waist.

He stepped back.

The oak seemed to have stepped forward.

This impression was so ridiculous that he became angry. A man whose daily occupation depended upon fixed points could not be bullied by a vegetable. He planted the ferrule of his umbrella in the earth.

“Now then,” he said, “I know where I am.”

The wood listened.

He had meant merely that he knew the direction. Yet, in the pause following his words, the statement sounded boastful and perhaps untrue. Somewhere behind him a twig snapped. Another snapped to the right. Then another, nearer.

Footsteps, if one chose to call them footsteps, began circling the glade.

Mr. Pemm pushed through the nettles.

They stung his hands, entered his cuffs, and whipped his face. The ground sank unexpectedly beneath dead leaves. He stumbled, recovered himself, and saw between the branches the gate by which he had entered.

Relief made him laugh aloud.

“Trees has mixed me up, that’s all.”

He hurried toward it. The gate stood twenty yards away, with the meadow blazing beyond. The notice now appeared to read:

TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED.

Sensible words. Civilised words. Mr. Pemm almost loved them.

He reached for the latch.

The gate swung away.

Not shut—away. The whole thing appeared to retreat three or four yards along the hedge, though naturally what had happened was that he had mistaken one gap for another. He lunged after it. His foot caught beneath a root, the umbrella flew from his hand, and he fell forward over the top rail.

For several extraordinary seconds he hung there, his head on the woodland side, his legs kicking toward freedom. Then the gate tipped. Mr. Pemm rolled completely over and landed upon his shoulders in a bed of wild garlic.

The smell was tremendous.

He sat up, bruised, hatless and furious. The gate had returned to its former position. On its weathered board the additional “r” was visible again.

PERSECUTED.

“Somebody’s doing this,” he said.

A laugh answered him.

It might have been a wood-pigeon. Indeed, it was almost certainly a wood-pigeon, though no pigeon ever before had laughed in so low and crowded a fashion. The sound travelled from tree to tree. Little answering chuckles stirred among the roots.

Mr. Pemm seized his umbrella and charged the gate.

Branches closed across his face. The gate disappeared. He turned, ran down a path, found it blocked by the oak, turned again, saw the meadow, ran toward that, and burst into the glade. The glade seemed longer now. Shadows raced across it although the sunlight remained steady. At its edges the green-brown figures gathered—five, seven, a dozen perhaps—peering from trunks and bushes.

“It’s ours,” said the wood.

No voice spoke. He knew that. Wind in leaves can imitate words, and frightened men supply meanings. Yet the phrase arrived complete in his mind, not loudly but with an ancient certainty.

It’s ours.

The legal impropriety of the claim struck him even then. The wood belonged to Mr. Harcourt, subject to whatever covenants appeared in the deeds. Timber rights could be established. Boundaries could be measured. Nothing was simply “ours.”

But he dared not say so.

He ran. The path twisted under him. Roots rose where his feet were falling; briars plucked at his coat; low boughs struck his bowler from his head and passed it from one to another. He left it. His official papers crackled in his pocket. The compass he carried appeared useless, its needle trembling from east to west and back again like a creature trying to escape its glass.

Now the little people came with him.

He saw them darting level with his shoulder, pale faces turned sideways, green arms extended. One leapt from a stump and vanished before touching the ground. Another ran along a branch. They made no sound except a dry pattering like last year’s leaves pursued over stone.

Mr. Pemm plunged through a curtain of hazel and found the oak awaiting him.

It stood directly in his path, though he had been running away from it. Its hollow gaped like a mouth. The long limb lowered.

He stopped so sharply that pain shot through his knees.

“This,” he thought, “is impossible.”

The oak moved.

There was no wind. Nevertheless, it heaved its roots from the soil with a slow tearing sound and advanced—or seemed to advance—one deliberate pace. Earth crumbled from it. Pale worms writhed in the exposed darkness.

Mr. Pemm screamed and struck at the limb with his umbrella.

The umbrella opened.

For one grotesque instant it bloomed between them, black and respectable. Then the oak swept it aside. Twigs clutched his collar; thorns fastened upon his sleeves; the little green figures closed in, their eyes bright as water under leaves. He felt himself pulled backward, forward, upward. The wood contracted around him, every trunk leaning, every branch reaching. The ground itself tilted.

Something hit him smartly behind the knees.

He fell into green darkness.

There followed a confusion of blows that did not quite hurt, scratches that burned without bleeding, laughter, rushing leaves and a sensation of being passed from hand to hand. His papers flew about him like white birds. He caught one glimpse of the sky far overhead, round and blue as the bottom of a well. Then the wood drew breath.

It expelled him.

Mr. Pemm shot through a hedge, turned over twice, and came to rest in the open meadow with his face against a cowslip.

The wind was shouting again. Clouds sailed above the Downs. A lark sang with exasperating serenity. Behind him stood the little wood, bright at the edges and no more than sixty yards across.

His bowler hat lay beside him.

So did the umbrella, neatly folded.

For some time he remained on hands and knees. Then he collected his papers. None was missing. His compass pointed north. His watch had stopped, though whether from the fall or some other cause he could not determine.

He did not look back until he had crossed the second stile.

Wychford Place proved to be a square red house of comfortable ugliness. Mr. Harcourt met him on the terrace—a cheerful gentleman in tweeds, carrying field glasses and apologising for luncheon being late.

“Found us all right?”

“Eventually, sir.”

“You didn’t take the long way round?”

Mr. Pemm was conscious of leaves in his collar and garlic upon his coat. Professional dignity required a measured answer.

“I came through a small plantation.”

Mr. Harcourt lowered the glasses.

“Not Little Wychford Wood?”

“I believe that is the name.”

“Good Lord. The Fairy Wood?”

Mr. Pemm stared at him.

“Local nonsense,” Mr. Harcourt continued pleasantly. “Old women won’t gather sticks there. My keeper won’t enter after dusk. Says the paths lead where they please. You know the sort of thing.”

“I do,” said Mr. Pemm, though until that morning he had not.

In the drawing-room he produced the postcard. Sunlight now fell upon the washed sentence at a different angle, and the blurred word showed more clearly.

“Short way through Little Wychford Wood if you dare,” he read.

Mr. Harcourt laughed.

“My handwriting! That says ‘care.’ Short way through the wood if you care to take it. Though perhaps ‘dare’ is better.”

Mr. Pemm said nothing.

They went out upon the terrace. Below them the lawns descended toward the meadow, but the little wood interrupted the western view exactly as he had supposed. Its crowns, tossing in the returned wind, concealed the finest sweep of the Downs.

“There’s the offender,” said Mr. Harcourt. “Ancient place, but quite useless. We’ll clear the middle, take down the old oak, and leave a few beeches at either side. Come along, Pemm. You can show me where the best line should run.”

He set off briskly across the lawn.

Mr. Pemm followed because he was a surveyor’s clerk, because Mr. Harcourt was the client, and because the 4.18 train did not leave for nearly two hours. At the meadow stile he paused.

The wood had ceased moving.

Every leaf hung still. In its shadow, small green-brown figures appeared to be waiting among the trunks. The gate stood open, and upon it the weathered notice shone whitely.

Mr. Pemm could not read the words at that distance.

He had no doubt, however, that they could read him.

## THE NEW PARLOR CARPET

“Cousin Sally will be here in less than three weeks,” said Mrs. Cartwright, lifting one corner of the parlor carpet with the toe of her shoe; “and I declare! it looks worse every time I see it.”

Mr. Cartwright glanced over the evening paper. He saw the faded breadths, the darned place before the hearth, and two spots where the figures had almost disappeared. Yet he saw also a clean room, chairs carefully dusted, a bright fire, and his wife seated beside a neatly spread tea-table. To his eyes the apartment wore the comfortable aspect of home.

“It is somewhat old,” he replied; “but it will answer a little longer, Martha.”

“Answer!” Mrs. Cartwright repeated. “Yes, it will answer for us, perhaps. But cousin Sally has everything handsome. What must she think when she comes into a room like this?”

“Why should she think anything unkind? She knows that my salary is not large.”

Mrs. Cartwright sighed. “People may know a great many things and yet form their opinions from what they see. Sally will not say a word; that is not her way. But her eyes will be open.”

Here was the beginning of evil. Not in deliberate dishonesty, nor in a wish to injure another, did temptation first approach that fireside. It came in the shape of a faded carpet and the imagined eyes of cousin Sally. But the desire to appear what we are not, even though it enters under so slight a covering, is a dangerous visitor; and, once admitted, it seldom remains content with the smallest room in the house.

Mr. Cartwright folded his paper.

“How much would a plain, serviceable carpet cost?”

“I looked at one yesterday,” answered his wife, a little too quickly. “It was only thirty-eight dollars, and really very pretty.”

“You looked at one?”

“I merely happened to pass Martin’s, and stepped in. There was no harm in looking.”

Mr. Cartwright had saved fifty dollars during the preceding six months. He had denied himself many little gratifications to accumulate it, because forty-five dollars were due to his old friend, Henry Lane, who had lent him that sum during an illness. Mr. Lane had said, “Pay it when convenient,” and no due bill had been given; but Mr. Cartwright, who was honest in intention, had long felt uneasy under the obligation.

“The money I have saved belongs, in justice, to Henry,” he said.

“But Mr. Lane is not in want of it.”

“That does not make it mine.”

“No, certainly not,” replied Mrs. Cartwright, with an injured air. “I would not persuade you to do anything wrong. Still, he said you might keep it until convenient; and, if we spend thirty-eight dollars, you can soon save as much again. Sally has not visited us for seven years. I should be right down ashamed to receive her with this rag on the floor.”

Poor Mr. Cartwright! He had made for himself a law most unsafe for any husband or wife: that peace was best secured by yielding. He mistook compliance for kindness, and the avoidance of present discomfort for the preservation of domestic happiness. A calm judgement and a principle of right would have given temporary disappointment to his wife, but would have saved them both from deeper pain.

“Well,” said he, after a pause, “if the carpet can be had for thirty-eight dollars, perhaps we might manage it.”

Mrs. Cartwright’s countenance brightened immediately.

“I knew you would see the necessity. You are always considerate, James.”

The next afternoon they went together to Martin’s warehouse. The carpet which had been “really very pretty” when seen alone appeared less desirable beside richer patterns. Mrs. Cartwright feared that its dull colors would make the room look gloomy. The salesman displayed another, with large crimson roses and green leaves crossing a ground of warm drab.

“That is the article for your parlor, madam,” said he. “Cheap at fifty-two dollars; but, as this is the last piece, I can let you have it for forty-eight.”

“Four yards more than we require,” observed Mr. Cartwright.

“It is not prudent to cut so close, sir. There should always be something allowed for matching.”

Mrs. Cartwright looked at her husband. “The other will never satisfy us after seeing this.”

“But this takes nearly all the money.”

“Only ten dollars more than we expected.”

Only ten dollars! How insignificant an additional sum appears when desire has already silenced prudence! The first limit once passed, the second has no guard around it.

Mr. Cartwright consented. He paid forty-eight dollars, leaving but two of his carefully saved fifty. As they walked home, he remembered Henry Lane, and his pleasure in the purchase was troubled. Mrs. Cartwright, however, was busily arranging in imagination the roses and leaves beneath cousin Sally’s feet.

The carpet came on Monday. When spread upon the floor, it was certainly bright—so bright that every humble object near it seemed to confess its humility. The old brown chairs looked browner; the small mahogany table showed scratches never before observed; and the white curtains, which had done respectable service through several summers, appeared yellow and thin.

Mrs. Cartwright stood in the doorway, regarding the effect.

“It is beautiful,” she said; but her voice lacked satisfaction.

“Very handsome,” replied her husband.

“If the windows did not look so bare!”

“They are no barer than before.”

“That is true; but the old carpet did not bring them out. These curtains positively disgrace the room.”

“Martha!”

“I do not mean that we must have expensive ones. Some muslin, with green bands, would cost very little.”

“We have two dollars.”

“I can use the money allowed for household expenses this week and make it up next week.”

The bands and muslin were bought. Then it was discovered that the window blinds were stained, and that the hearthrug did not agree with the roses. A small table was borrowed from the spare chamber; but this left an empty place there, which Mrs. Cartwright supplied by purchasing another at an auction. Six chairs were polished, two covered anew, and a china vase—“a mere trifle, and just what the mantel needed”—completed the attiring of her home.

Each expense was small when considered alone. Together they consumed not only the two remaining dollars, but money needed for food, fuel, and other household demands. Mr. Cartwright, though increasingly uncomfortable, continued to yield. Having unwisely surrendered the first point, he felt ashamed to insist upon the later and smaller ones. Thus weakness reproduces itself. The man who fears to say “No” at the right time may soon find himself unable to say it at all.

A week before cousin Sally’s expected arrival, Mr. Cartwright received a letter at his place of employment. It was from Henry Lane.

> Dear James,—An unexpected business payment falls due on the first of next month. I am sorry to trouble you, but shall be obliged if you will return the forty-five dollars I lent you. I had not intended to ask for it so soon, yet circumstances leave me no choice. Yours,  
> Henry Lane.

A very sudden change of feeling did Mr. Cartwright experience as he read. The crimson roses of the new carpet rose before his mind, no longer gay and handsome, but accusing. He calculated his coming wages, subtracted rent, provisions, and coal, and saw with consternation that payment was impossible.

He answered his friend immediately.

> Dear Henry,—I regret that I cannot send the money by the first. An unforeseen household expense has used the sum I had set apart for you. If you can wait another month, I will do all in my power to pay at least a part. Believe me, I am deeply mortified. Yours,  
> James Cartwright.

He did not tell his wife of the demand. He shrank from reproaching her; still more, perhaps, he shrank from acknowledging the extent of his own weakness. He put the very best face on to things, spoke cheerfully at supper, and even praised the new curtains. But there was no peace in his heart.

Three days later came Henry’s answer. Mr. Cartwright opened it with trembling fingers.

> James,—Your note has surprised and wounded me. I passed your house yesterday evening and saw through the window a new and costly carpet, with curtains and other improvements. Of course, every man may spend his earnings as he chooses; but I did not suppose an old friend would buy luxuries with money justly due to me, and then call the expense unforeseen. I lent the sum when your family was in distress, though it was not wholly convenient for me to do so. I now need my own, and am refused because your parlor must make a show. Send what you can. I shall know better hereafter where friendship ends and folly begins.  
> H. Lane.

Mr. Cartwright’s face burned. Some expressions in the letter were severe; yet conscience prevented him from calling them unjust. Had not Henry spoken the simple truth? The money was due. It had been saved. It had been spent upon a carpet intended to make a show.

He placed the letter in the inner pocket of his coat and went home by a longer road. As he entered the parlor, Mrs. Cartwright exclaimed:

“Do take care, James! Your shoes are damp, and the roses show every mark.”

He stepped back obediently and wiped them again. The action was slight, but humiliation gave it bitterness.

That evening cousin Sally arrived.

Mrs. Cartwright had pictured her entrance many times. She had imagined the glance which would take in carpet, curtains, chairs, table, and vase, and had prepared modest answers to compliments. But Sally, a warm-hearted woman of forty, set down her traveling bag and threw both arms around her cousin.

“Martha! Dear Martha! Let me look at you. Seven years—and your face is the same kind face still!”

Mrs. Cartwright returned the embrace, but her eyes wandered downward.

“You have not noticed our new carpet, Sally.”

“Have I not?” said Sally, smiling. “Then I must be growing blind. Yes, it is very bright.”

“We only got it last week. The old one was not fit to be seen.”

“I remember the old one,” replied Sally. “It had little brown vines. Aunt Deborah chose it, did she not?”

“Yes; but it was worn out.”

“I dare say. How is James? And the children? Tell me everything. I have thought a thousand times of the evenings we used to sit together in grandmother’s room.”

But Mrs. Cartwright was not ready for heart intercourse. Her thoughts remained among visible things.

“You must see the curtains by daylight,” she said. “I made every breadth myself.”

At tea she apologized for the tea-tray. At breakfast she regretted that the cups did not match. When Sally admired the bread, Mrs. Cartwright explained that their usual baker supplied a finer loaf. She feared at one moment that the spare chamber was cold, and at another that the bureau was old-fashioned. If Sally looked toward the wall, Martha supposed she had discovered a stain; if she glanced at the hearth, Martha imagined she was comparing the rug with one in her own home.

Sally soon perceived that her cousin’s natural affection was buried, for the time, beneath anxious worldliness. She had come with a full heart. Recent sorrow—the death of her only sister—had awakened memories of girlhood, and she had longed to speak with one who knew the same old rooms, voices, and faces. But whenever conversation approached those tender subjects, Mrs. Cartwright’s attention was diverted by some domestic imperfection.

“Do you remember,” Sally began one evening, “the last winter grandmother lived, when you and I watched beside her?”

“Yes—dear grandmother! James, that lamp smokes. Sally will think we never trim it.”

“I shall think no such thing,” said Sally quietly.

“Of course not. Yet it is vexatious when one wishes everything to be pleasant.”

“Everything is pleasant enough.”

“No, indeed. Your house is so beautifully furnished that ours must seem very poor.”

Sally looked earnestly at her cousin. “I did not come to compare houses.”

Mrs. Cartwright laughed uneasily. “You may not intend to; but one cannot help seeing.”

There it was again—the tyranny of eyes! Her eyes, Sally’s eyes, the neighbor’s eyes, the eyes of every person who might spy out the nakedness of the land! Poor, weak woman! She had made herself servant to a tribunal which existed chiefly in her own imagination, and in striving to avoid mortification had rendered herself incapable of the very affection she desired to receive.

Mr. Cartwright observed Sally’s disappointment. His own trouble made him silent, and his silence made the visit still less happy. Once Sally found him sitting alone, his hand pressed over his brow.

“You look weary, James.”

“A little. Business is troublesome.”

“I hope nothing serious?”

“No—nothing that I cannot bear.”

He spoke with forced composure. Sally, supposing delicacy required her to ask no more, withdrew. Mrs. Cartwright entered a moment afterward, carrying a cloth to rub a small spot from the carpet.

“Sally seems changed,” she said.

“Does she?”

“She is not nearly so lively as formerly. And she has scarcely praised anything.”

Mr. Cartwright’s patience, weakened by self-reproach, almost failed him.

“Perhaps she came to see us, Martha, and not our furniture.”

Mrs. Cartwright colored.

“That is an unkind remark. Have I not worked from morning until night to make her comfortable?”

“Yes,” he answered more gently. “You have worked too hard.”

The visit, planned with so many aspirations, ended after four days. On the final morning Sally stood in the parlor with her bonnet tied, while the coach waited at the gate.

“Martha,” she said, taking her cousin’s hand, “do not be offended if I speak plainly.”

“I am sure I cannot imagine what you mean.”

“I came because my heart was hungry for you. Since Ellen died, I have lived much among old memories. I wanted to talk of our childhood, of grandmother, of the hopes we once shared, and of the sorrows that have come since. I wanted my cousin—not a hostess alarmed by every faded curtain or mismatched cup.”

Mrs. Cartwright drew away her hand.

“I suppose you found enough to criticize.”

“That is precisely the mistake. I found nothing to criticize until you compelled me to look. Your home is clean and orderly. If the floor had been bare boards, I should have seen you. If every chair had been different, I should have remembered only that I sat beside you. But you have been thinking of my eyes when I wished to give you my heart.”

“Sally—”

“I do not say this in anger. I say it in sorrow. The old carpet would have recalled Aunt Deborah and happy days. This new one is handsome, no doubt; but it has seemed to stand between us every hour.”

The coachman called. Sally kissed her cousin with real affection, though tears were in her eyes, and hurried away.

Mrs. Cartwright remained at the window until the coach disappeared. Then she turned and looked around the parlor. The roses were as crimson, the bands as green, the vase as glossy as before; yet over all lay the shadow of disappointment.

“She misunderstood me,” Mrs. Cartwright said aloud. “Everything I did was for her.”

But conscience answered, Was it for Sally—or for Sally’s opinion?

Unable to remain still, she began putting the room in order. Mr. Cartwright had left his coat over a chair. As she lifted it, a folded paper slipped from the pocket. She stooped, saw Henry Lane’s name, and, thinking it perhaps a business message, opened it.

She read the angry letter once. Then she read it again.

“None! None!” she whispered, as if the words had been spoken to her. “No money for his friend, because I must have a carpet!”

She sat down heavily. Now many things became plain: her husband’s troubled face, his silence, his long walks, his anxious calculation over the household account. He had borne the mortification alone, while she fretted lest damp shoes should stain the roses.

Alas! Alas! outward show had exacted a heavy price. It had consumed the savings intended for an honest debt; it had wounded a generous friend; it had made a good man appear dishonorable; and, instead of drawing cousin Sally nearer, it had chilled the intercourse of affection. The carpet had fulfilled none of the hopes attached to it. It had not created prosperity, but merely displayed the desire to seem prosperous. It had not won respect, but had imperiled it. It had not beautified the home, for no home is beautiful where duty lies neglected beneath ornament.

Mrs. Cartwright wept long and bitterly. Yet her grief did not waste itself wholly in tears. She possessed, beneath pride and weakness, a sincere love for her husband and a conscience capable of right-doing when once fully awakened.

While she still sat with Henry’s letter in her hand, Mrs. Avery, who lived two doors away, called.

“My dear Mrs. Cartwright,” cried the neighbor upon entering, “what a magnificent carpet! I have been meaning to see it ever since it came. Why, it is exactly what I need for my front room.”

Mrs. Cartwright looked up suddenly.

“Do you truly like it?”

“Like it! I have seen nothing half so handsome this season. Martin tells me there is no more of the pattern, or I should buy it tomorrow.”

An hour earlier such praise would have delighted Mrs. Cartwright. Now it seemed that Providence itself had opened a door of restitution.

“You may have this one,” she said.

Mrs. Avery laughed. “Indeed! And what would you put in its place?”

“My old carpet. It is rolled in the attic.”

“But you have only just bought this.”

“That is true. I paid forty-eight dollars for it, besides the laying. Give me fifty, and it is yours.”

Mrs. Avery stared; then, perceiving that the offer was serious, examined the carpet more closely. It had scarcely been walked upon and was free from spot or injury.

“I will take it,” she said. “My husband gave me fifty dollars for a carpet yesterday. But are you quite certain?”

“Quite.”

Before Mr. Cartwright returned, the new carpet had been raised, carried to Mrs. Avery’s house, and spread in another parlor. The old one, with its little brown vines, was brought down from the attic, beaten, mended at one loose seam, and restored to its accustomed place. The simpler curtains were put back at the windows. The green bands were removed. The china vase disappeared from the mantel.

When Mr. Cartwright entered, he stopped upon the threshold.

“Martha! What has happened?”

She came toward him and placed five gold pieces in his hand.

“The carpet is sold. Mrs. Avery admired it and paid fifty dollars.”

He looked first at the money, then at the floor.

“But why?”

“I found Henry’s letter.”

Mr. Cartwright’s countenance fell.

“I did not mean you to see it.”

“You should have shown it to me. No—do not excuse me, James. I urged you to spend what was not ours in honor, and you were too kind—too weak—to refuse me. Sally came for affection, and I offered her furniture. Your friend needed justice, and I gave him roses and green leaves. Oh, James! can you forgive me?”

He took her hand.

“I have as much need to ask forgiveness. I knew what was right and did not do it. A husband has no right to purchase present peace at the cost of his own judgement and his wife’s better nature.”

“Send Henry the money tonight.”

“I will take it to him myself.”

“And tell him the truth—all the truth. Let him know that the carpet is gone.”

Mr. Cartwright’s eyes rested upon the old brown vines. Never had they appeared so pleasant. They recalled early years of marriage, modest hopes, patient industry, and many quiet evenings in which neither he nor Martha had thought of appearing richer than they were.

“I believe,” said he, “this old carpet is handsomer than the new one.”

“It is honest, at least,” replied his wife through her tears.

Henry Lane, though still wounded, accepted the repayment and Mr. Cartwright’s candid confession. Friendship was not restored in a moment, for confidence, once injured, is slower to return than money; but sincerity began the cure, and time completed it. Mrs. Cartwright also wrote to cousin Sally, not to defend herself, but to acknowledge her folly. Sally answered with tenderness, and when she visited again, there was much heart intercourse and little mention of furniture.

As for James and Martha, they established from that day a safer rule of domestic conduct.

“We will live according to our means,” said Mr. Cartwright, “and consult our own eyes—not the eyes of other people.”

“Our own consciences,” added Martha.

“Yes; for eyes may be dazzled, but conscience, when honestly consulted, sees clearly.”

And they adhered to their resolution. They bought what comfort required and honest means permitted; they ceased to be ashamed of useful age and respectable plainness; and they learned that virtue needs no costly covering, while vanity can never be made beautiful, though spread beneath the feet in crimson roses.

Thus a faded carpet, which had seemed a misfortune, became the emblem of contentment; and a new one, purchased for display, taught them the superiority of inward worth over outward appearance. For prosperity does not consist in what strangers see through our windows, but in what justice, affection, and right-doing establish within our walls.

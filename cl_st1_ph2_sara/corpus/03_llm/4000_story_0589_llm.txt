### The Last Enemy

The mission crouched above the river like a beaten thing.

Its chapel, storehouse, and three log huts lay half-buried in drifted snow, their roofs hunched beneath the weight of winter. A black wooden cross leaned over the chapel gable. Beyond it the spruces stood in mournful ranks, thin and motionless, like grave-markers driven into the white earth. The river itself had vanished beneath ice and blown snow, though from under that immense white silence came, at intervals, a hollow concussion—the river striking at its roof.

Across this desolation two men and seven dogs came staggering.

Big George walked at the gee-pole, bent into the wind, his beard a block of frost and his moose-hide parka rattling about him like armour. He was a mountainous man, broad enough to stop a doorway, and the North had hacked and scarred him without making him smaller. Behind him stumbled Charlie Captain, one mitten gripping the sled, the other pressed over his eyes.

“I can’t see the house,” Captain said.

“Ye don’t need to see it. It’s there.”

“I see a red wheel—and sparks.”

“That’s snow-blindness talkin’. Keep your hand on the sled.”

The dogs plunged shoulder-deep through the drift. Their ribs showed under frozen hair. Twice the leader fell and twice George lifted the animal bodily, cuffing it forward with rough encouragement. They had eaten nothing since yesterday morning. Neither had the men.

From the nearest hut came a cry. Doors opened. Dark figures burst out, stared, and then scattered toward the mission house. One man remained, a short, thick-bodied Native in a caribou coat. He held a rifle crosswise over his chest.

George straightened.

“Tell Brother Paul I claim sanctuary.”

The man did not move.

“Tell him,” George repeated, “that Big George has come in alive, though Father Orloff swore I never would.”

At the priest’s name fear twitched across the man’s face. He lowered the rifle and ran.

Brother Paul received them inside the mission house, a square, smoke-blackened room smelling of seal oil, wet fur, and old books. He was a pale young deacon with a ragged beard and nervous fingers. While women fed strips of dried salmon to the dogs, he led the travellers to the stove.

“You should not have come,” he whispered.

George thawed ice from his beard. “A hospitable Christian ye are.”

“Orloff sent word to every village. He says you robbed the church at Nulato, profaned the altar, and threatened his life.”

“I did threaten his life.”

“And the church?”

“I borrowed two sacks of flour. Left a note.”

“You tore down a cross.”

“It was blockin’ the chimney.”

Brother Paul wrung his hands. “The people believe you are accursed.”

“The people believe whatever Orloff bellows loud enough.”

A murmur rose outside. George crossed the room in two strides and looked through the little window. Men were gathering among the huts, rifles in hand, but they kept their distance. The wind drove their parkas tight against them and whisked powdery snow about their mukluks.

Captain had sunk onto a bench. When he uncovered his eyes, the lids were swollen and the whites webbed with scarlet.

“Darken the room,” George ordered.

Brother Paul hesitated.

“Darken it!”

Blankets went over the windows. George pushed a bunk against the wall, wrapped Captain’s head in a strip of black cloth, and made him lie down.

Outside, the crowd began to chant.

“Orloff’s work,” George said. “Always he sets other hands to what his own are afraid to do.”

“Who is he?” Captain asked.

“Ye’ve heard.”

“I’ve heard three versions. In one, he’s a saint. In another, you’re a murderer. In the third, both are true.”

George stood by the stove. Firelight climbed over his battered face, reddening one side and leaving the other in darkness. For a long time only the wind spoke, tearing at the eaves and driving smoke down the chimney.

Then George laughed without amusement.

“I’ll tell ye the true version. It began with Metla.”

The name changed him. His fists opened.

“Twenty years ago I was a chechako with more muscle than sense. I’d come over the pass with stampeders and followed every liar who said gold lay beyond the next hill. By spring I’d lost my outfit, my partner, and near all the toes on my left foot. I was alone on the Koyukuk when the ice went rotten under me.

“I remember breakin’ through. I remember the current rollin’ me under the shelf, and the light above lookin’ green as old bottle glass. Then nothin’.

“When I woke I was in a skin tent with a girl feedin’ me broth from a wooden spoon. Metla. She’d seen my cap float past, run half a mile downriver, and hauled me out with a willow pole. She kept me alive six weeks. Had to cut my boots away. Some days she fed me like an infant; some days she beat me with the spoon because I tried to stand.

“She had eyes blacker than open water at midnight. Never wasted a word. When she laughed—which wasn’t often—it was like sun hittin’ a cabin after three months of dark.

“I married her.”

Brother Paul glanced toward the door. The chanting had stopped, but boots scraped outside.

“Orloff was at the lower mission then,” George continued. “He was younger, with a black beard and a voice that could shake dust off the rafters. He preached hell so plain a man could smell his own hide scorchin’. The people feared him. That pleased him more than love ever could.

“He wanted Metla before I came. Not for a wife. He called her to the mission to cook, mend, and wait on him. She wouldn’t go. Then she hauled me from the river, and after that she wouldn’t look at him at all.

“He told her our marriage was no marriage because no priest had blessed it. I offered to stand before the altar. He refused. Said a man like me had no soul fit to join with hers.

“‘Then bless hers twice,’ says I, ‘and let mine freeze.’

“He had me put out.”

George stooped to the stove and thrust in another stick. Sparks whirled up the pipe.

“That summer Metla came away with me. We built a cabin above the fork where the salmon crowded red against the bank. I hunted; she trapped. We were poor, but poverty don’t trouble two people who laugh at the same things.

“Then the boy came.

“He was small. Smallest thing ever wore my name. Metla called him Ahyak, but to me he was Little George. His fist couldn’t circle my finger. Lord, how that child yelled! He had lungs enough for a dog team.

“When he was six months old, fever struck the villages. People went to Orloff for medicine. He’d got a chest of it from St. Michael, powders and quinine and God knows what. I drove sixty miles in rain to fetch him.

“He stood in the chapel door and listened while I begged.

“‘Bring the woman here,’ he says.

“‘The child can’t travel.’

“‘Bring Metla. Let her confess her sin and enter the mission household. Then I will go to the child.’

“I knew what he meant. So did he.

“I hit him.”

Brother Paul crossed himself.

“Aye. Knocked him down the chapel steps. Then I broke the medicine chest and took what I could. But I didn’t know one bottle from another. By the time I brought a trader who did, Little George was dead.”

The storm pressed against the house with a long, animal moan.

“Metla never blamed me,” George said. “That was worse. She sat by the child two days and sang. Then we buried him where the ground was soft beneath a cottonwood.

“Orloff came a week later with armed men. Said I had robbed holy property and assaulted a servant of God. They burned our cabin. Metla and me watched from the timber while the roof fell.

“After that he hounded us from river to river. When I trapped, he told the villages my furs were cursed. When I worked for the company, he wrote the factor that I sold whisky. If a dog died, I’d poisoned it. If sickness came, I’d brought it. Men who’d shared meat with me crossed themselves when I passed.

“Metla lasted five years. Hard years. One spring she took the coughing sickness. I carried her to a mission—different mission, decent priest—but the thaw came early and the trail went to slush under us. She died with her head against my arm, ten miles from help.

“That day I swore Orloff would die by my hand.”

Captain shifted on the bunk. “And for fifteen years he hasn’t?”

“Not for lack of invitations. Twice I went for him and twice he’d fled. Once his men ambushed me. Once he had the marshal arrest me, but the judge had sense. This winter I heard he was ridin’ north, tellin’ every settlement I’d threatened the Church. So I did threaten it proper. I sent word that when I found him, one of us would stay under the snow.”

“And now he is coming here?”

George nodded toward the unseen river. “A runner reached us yesterday. Orloff’s two days behind.”

Brother Paul’s face went grey. “You must leave.”

“Captain can’t travel blind.”

“The people will not protect you.”

“I ain’t asked ’em.”

“You cannot make a fortress of a mission.”

George grinned. “I’ve made fortresses of worse.”

He spent the afternoon proving it. He barred the doors, looped wire across the shutters, and carried flour, fish, and cartridges from the storehouse. When three armed men approached, he stepped outside, seized the nearest rifle by its barrel, and pitched it into a drift.

“No man fires on this house,” he told them. “Ye tell Orloff that. If he wants me, he comes himself.”

They withdrew, muttering.

By evening the wind had risen from the northeast. It struck the mission with a white fist. Snow erased the huts, the spruces, and the river. The world shrank to the groaning walls and the hiss of driven crystals against the roof.

For two days the blizzard raged.

It worried the wilderness like a wolf at a carcass, ripping snow from ridges, burying trails, piling drifts to the eaves. The dogs lay curled under sheds while men crawled between buildings on hands and knees. Even vengeance had to halt before that threshing maelstrom.

Captain’s eyes improved. By the second night he could distinguish the orange belly of the stove. By the third morning he could see George as a dark bulk against the window.

The storm died at dawn.

Silence descended so suddenly that it rang in the ears. The sky cleared to a hard metallic blue, and the sun rolled above the horizon in a flood of murderous brilliance. Every drift burned. The river glittered like a blade.

A boy came running from the lower hut.

“He is here!” he cried. “Father Orloff is here!”

George snatched up his rifle.

Far out on the river, two men and a dog team moved as black scratches upon the white. They travelled slowly, weaving. The leader lurched off course; the sled swung sideways. One man beat at the dogs. The other walked with both hands over his face.

“Snow-blind,” Captain said.

George’s mouth tightened. “Good.”

The mission people poured from the huts. No one went down to meet the travellers. They stood in clusters, watching.

The team reached the bend below the mission, where wind had scoured the snow from the river. There the ice was blue, polished, and treacherously thin over a spring channel. George saw the guide hesitate. Orloff, blind behind him, shouted and waved an arm.

The dogs advanced.

A crack leaped across the river.

The team vanished amid a black eruption of water and splintered ice. Men screamed. Dogs clawed at the broken rim. The sled tilted, smashed through, and was dragged beneath. For a moment the channel boiled with heads, traces, paws, and fragments.

Then the current took the dogs.

The guide scrambled onto a hummock of old ice no wider than a table. He dragged Orloff after him. Around them the broken channel widened, black water shouldering slabs aside and grinding them together with a roar like distant guns.

The people on the bank wailed.

George walked downhill until he stood at the river’s edge. He worked the lever of his rifle. The sound was small and precise.

“What are you doing?” Captain asked.

“Collectin’ a debt.”

“From here?”

“Near enough. Wind’s nothin’.”

On the hummock, Orloff knelt with his face turned blindly toward shore. The guide waved both arms.

George lifted the rifle.

For fifteen years he had imagined the moment: Orloff armed, defiant, meeting him man to man in some lonely clearing. Yet the sight before him was better. The priest crouched helpless in the river’s jaws. No follower could intervene. No law could accuse the water.

His finger settled against the trigger.

Captain struck the barrel upward.

The shot cracked. Snow jumped from a distant bank.

George wheeled and seized him by the throat.

“Do that again,” he said softly, “and I’ll pitch ye after him.”

Captain’s inflamed eyes watered in the glare, but he did not look away.

“He’s a man.”

“He’s worse.”

“Then fight him when he can fight. Don’t shoot a blind, freezing man on a cake of ice.”

“He killed my boy.”

“And if you kill him there, what does that make you?”

“Contented.”

“No. A murderer.”

George flung him down. Captain struck the snow, rolled, and got to his knees.

“Shoot me first,” he said.

“Don’t tempt me.”

“You hauled me three hundred miles when you could have left me. You shared your last food. You stood between me and those men. Was all that only because I’m your friend?”

George’s rifle came up again.

From the river drifted a cry: “Help!”

It was the guide. Orloff made no sound.

The hummock shuddered. A yard of it cracked away and spun into the channel.

Captain rose unsteadily. “Fight like a man, George.”

For a breath the giant stood motionless. The river beat and roared; the sun hammered fire into his eyes; the old grief stirred like a beast behind his ribs. Then he cursed Captain, Orloff, the river, the Church, and all creation.

He threw down the rifle.

“Ropes!” he bellowed. “Rawhide lines! Bring every sled lashin’ in the place!”

Men stared.

“Move, ye frozen sheep!”

His voice broke their paralysis. They ran.

George knotted three lines together, looped one end around his chest, and ordered twenty men to brace the other about a spruce. He tied a shorter rope around Captain.

“You stay.”

“I can help.”

“Ye can barely see.”

“I can see enough.”

George looked once at him, then grunted. “Follow in my tracks. If I go through, lie flat and crawl backward.”

They stepped onto the river.

The ice complained beneath them. Near shore it was thick and white, but twenty yards out it darkened to blue glass. Water pulsed underneath, magnifying every bubble and fissure. George moved belly-down, spreading his weight, testing ahead with a pole. Captain crawled behind.

At the edge of the open channel, steam rose in ghostly ribbons. The hummock lay fifteen feet away, separated by black water crowded with grinding ice.

“Can’t cross,” Captain said.

“Didn’t come to admire it.”

George drove his pole across the gap. It fell short. He drew it back, tied the rescue line to one end, and flung again. The guide caught it, but his hands were too numb to hold.

“Tie it around the priest!” George shouted.

The guide fumbled. Orloff sat like a carved thing, his parka white with frost, his hands hanging uselessly.

The hummock split.

The guide screamed and leaped to the larger half. Orloff toppled into the channel.

George lunged.

The blue ice broke under his chest. Water seized him to the shoulders, cold beyond pain, cold like a hammer striking bone. He caught Orloff’s hood as the current dragged the priest beneath the rim.

“Haul!” Captain roared.

The shore crew pulled. The rope crushed George’s ribs. He kicked against the current, got one elbow on the edge, and heaved Orloff upward. Ice cut his gloves and blood ran black across the water.

The rim crumbled.

Captain crawled forward, caught George’s belt, and added his weight. Together they dragged Orloff onto the ice. The priest’s face was waxen, his beard plated solid, his eyes swollen shut.

“Guide!” George gasped.

The man had wrapped the pole-line around himself. At George’s signal the shore crew hauled, jerking him off the hummock and through the channel. He vanished once, surfaced choking, and struck the edge. Captain seized his mitten.

“Now pull us!”

The line tightened. Men, priest, and guide slid over the ice in a tangled heap while cracks chased them toward shore. Behind them the hummock overturned and disappeared.

When George reached snow thick enough to kneel upon, he stood, lifted Orloff across his shoulders, and carried him up the bank.

Inside the mission they stripped the priest. His outer clothes had frozen into a carapace and had to be cut away. His feet were white and hard to the ankles. One hand showed the same dead pallor. Brother Paul began rubbing them with snow.

“Stop that,” Captain said. “You’ll tear the flesh. Warm him slowly.”

Orloff regained consciousness after dark.

He lay near the stove, wrapped in blankets, while George sat opposite with his rifle across his knees. The priest opened his ruined eyes.

“George?”

“Aye.”

“Did you pull me out?”

“A regrettable mistake.”

Orloff breathed shallowly. “I knew you would kill me.”

“So did I.”

“You should have.”

George leaned forward. “Don’t give orders in my camp.”

“The child,” Orloff whispered. “I was proud. I wanted her obedience. I withheld mercy because she denied me.” His mouth twisted. “I have confessed it many times to God, but never to you.”

“God was easier to reach.”

“Yes.”

The admission landed more heavily than denial. George had carried hatred so long it had hardened into a second skeleton. Now the enemy lay broken before him, agreeing to the whole indictment, and there was no satisfaction in it.

Captain examined Orloff’s feet again.

“He’ll lose both, unless a surgeon sees him quickly. Perhaps even then.”

“The hospital at Holy Cross,” Brother Paul said. “Two hundred miles.”

“Trail’ll be buried,” someone answered.

George rose.

“Feed the best dogs. Find me a sound sled.”

Brother Paul stared. “You cannot travel tonight.”

“Can’t I?”

“You were in the river. Your clothes are not dry.”

“They’ll dry while I harness.”

Captain caught his sleeve. “George, you’ve done enough.”

“Have I? I hauled him out to let him rot by the stove?”

“Someone else can drive.”

George looked around the room. The mission men lowered their eyes.

“Aye,” he said. “I thought so.”

They left before midnight.

The cold had sharpened to sixty below. Stars blazed like nail-heads driven into black iron. George wrapped Orloff in skins, lashed him to the sled, and ran beside the team to keep blood moving in his legs. Captain remained behind; his eyes would not bear the snow-glare.

For five days George drove south.

He broke trail through the storm’s wreckage, over drifts tall as cabins and through timber where bent spruces whipped back against his face. At noon the sun turned the world into white fire. At night darkness froze the moisture in his lungs and made the sled runners shriek. He slept in scraps—an hour beneath a bank, twenty minutes beside a fire—then rose and drove again.

The dogs weakened. George fed them from his own ration. When the wheel dog collapsed, he cut it loose, loaded it beside Orloff, and leaned his weight into the traces.

On the third day overflow flooded the trail. Water climbed over his mukluks and froze before he reached high ground. He built a fire, cut the footwear away, and found two toes grey. He cursed them and kept going.

Orloff drifted in and out of fever. Sometimes he prayed. Sometimes he called Metla’s name. Once he begged George to leave him.

“Ye’ve spent twenty years makin’ trouble,” George said. “Ye don’t quit till I say.”

On the fourth night wolves followed, their eyes moving green beyond the fire. George sat awake with the rifle across his knees while Orloff raved under the robes. Dawn found the giant’s beard white, his face blackened by frost, and the wolves gone.

Late on the fifth day the hospital bell sounded through falling snow.

George came down the slope behind six staggering dogs, hauling with them, his frozen clothes clattering and his hands bleeding inside the mittens. Men ran from the mission. A doctor in shirtsleeves took one look at Orloff and shouted for hot water, lamps, and the operating room.

They carried the priest inside.

George fell beside the empty sled.

When he woke, two days had passed. Captain sat by his bed, green goggles over his healing eyes.

“The operation is finished,” Captain said.

“Alive?”

“Yes.”

“Feet?”

“Both gone below the knee. Three fingers from the left hand.”

George considered this. “He’ll preach shorter sermons.”

The doctor entered, a small, brisk man with silver spectacles. “Your friend owes his life to you.”

George scowled. “Friend?”

“Few men would make that journey for an enemy.”

“Friends!” George snorted. “Any fool can find friends. Feed a man, lend him money, pull him out of one scrape, and he’ll swear affection till next Tuesday.”

The doctor smiled uncertainly.

George settled back, wincing as his frozen toes burned beneath the blankets.

“But an enemy like Orloff,” he said, “takes twenty years to make—and there ain’t another one of his quality in all Alaska. D’ye think I’d leave a valuable possession like that to drown?”

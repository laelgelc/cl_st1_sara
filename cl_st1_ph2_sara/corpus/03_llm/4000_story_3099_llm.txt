## The Good Soul

Naturalists have neglected the Good Soul. They have pursued the lesser creatures into deserts, dredged them from seas, surprised them under stones, and devoted stout volumes to the breeding habits of beetles which no civilized person would willingly meet. Yet the Good Soul lives among us, plainly marked, easily approached, and quite incapable of biting. Indeed, if trodden upon, he apologizes for having placed himself beneath the foot.

We call the Good Soul “he” merely for convenience. The condition is equally common in both sexes, and is congenital. Education may polish it, adversity may exercise it, marriage may give it a wider field, but nothing cures it. A Good Soul is born with the terrible certainty that other people’s comfort is his business. Mark the infant who gives away his sugar-plum, fetches nurse’s spectacles, and says that he does not mind taking the broken trumpet. That doomed child has the indelible mark upon him.

His schooldays are piteous. He lends pencils and receives them back tooth-marked; he shares his luncheon and is left with the paper; he undertakes to explain the rule of three to boys who afterwards pelt him with ink. When teams are chosen, he is selected last because “Edgar won’t mind.” Edgar never does. He keeps the score, minds the coats, and runs three streets to retrieve the ball which somebody else has kicked through a window. If punishment threatens, he nobly refuses to tell tales and is caned with the guilty.

At parties the other children eat his portion before he has finished helping the youngest guest to blanc-mange. He yields the gilt chair, the unbroken toy, the orange without a soft place, and the place nearest the conjurer. He is sent behind the curtain to see why the lantern will not work and returns just after the ghost has appeared. His mother calls him “my little man,” which settles it. No child has ever recovered from being called his mother’s little man before witnesses.

There are people who suppose the Good Soul acquires wisdom with years. Gentle reader, you and we know better. At twenty-five he is merely able to perform larger kindnesses at greater expense. He signs guarantees, recommends unsuitable cousins for employment, and becomes treasurer of societies whose former treasurers have vanished. He buys tickets for concerts in aid of causes which refuse to be aided, and attends because the young woman who sold them says the hall will look so empty.

You will know his sitting-room. There are postcards of cathedrals he has not visited, sent by persons who forgot to write until they reached the railway station; a souvenir thimble from Margate; two carnations drooping in a blue glass; and a burned-leather motto advising him to Scatter Sunshine. He does. The results resemble those of scattering birdseed in Trafalgar Square.

His books, also, are significant. They have titles in which courage is crowned, clouds are silver-lined, and somebody called Little Marjorie teaches a duke the meaning of Christmas. He prefers a story that leaves him “better for having read it,” though why literature should leave anybody better when life has conspicuously failed to do so he never explains. He admires a brave, clean book, which commonly means that no one in it speaks or behaves like a human being.

He has culture, but it is of a hospitable kind. He knows that Beethoven was deaf, that Shakespeare held the mirror up to nature, and that art is long while time, as he says, “waits for no tide.” He has seen *Hamlet* and thinks the prince would have been happier if he had taken Horatio more into his confidence. He describes a celebrated tragedy as “not exactly cheerful, but very powerful,” and believes all great painters began by drawing when they were quite tiny.

Take him to the theater and observe the fulfillment of his destiny. His seat is excellent, which naturally distresses him, because a tall lady behind cannot see. He exchanges it for hers, discovers a pillar in front and a draught behind, and spends the interval assuring everybody that he prefers being near the exit. If you attempt to rescue him, he whispers that the lady may hear. She hears nothing; she is now using his opera glasses.

The Good Soul’s sense of humor is blameless and exhausting. He remembers stories by their moral outlines but loses the joints connecting them. “It was very funny when Mr. Harcourt told it,” he says, after placing a bishop, a Scotsman, and a parrot in a railway carriage and forgetting why. He enjoys malapropisms, especially his own. He will not “cast any asparagus” upon a neighbor and has heard the overture “three times in concussion.” If corrected, he thanks you warmly and repeats the error next Thursday.

Conversation with him is conducted upon the principle that no silence, however innocent, should be left alive. He asks whether you have been keeping well, whether your people are keeping well, and whether the weather is not extraordinary for the time of year. Before you can reply, he has remembered a person whose aunt had precisely your symptom and was entirely cured by sleeping with the window open and taking hot milk with nutmeg. The aunt died later, but of something quite different.

Advice in the Good Soul is not a habit but a secretion. He exudes it painlessly. If you are poor, he suggests careful accounts; if prosperous, modest living; if unmarried, companionship; if married, patience. To the overworked he recommends a hobby, to the idle a purpose, to the sleepless regular hours, and to the fat a biscuit known only to his chemist. His remedy for grief is occupation. His remedy for occupation is rest. There is no accounting for Good Souls.

Illness brings out their mature splendor. Mention that you have a cold, and the Good Soul arrives bearing grapes, a potted plant, three illustrated papers six weeks old, and a jar containing something prepared from an old family recipe. This substance must be swallowed hot, cold, before meals, and whenever the cough is troublesome. Its flavor suggests that the old family regarded recovery as a form of impertinence.

He smooths your pillow until no hollow remains in which a human head could lie. He draws the blind because the light is trying, then raises it because darkness is depressing. He puts the fire right by removing the only burning coal, and makes tea by adding milk you do not take and sugar you have forbidden. He speaks in a whisper that pierces two walls, and tells visitors outside the door that you have at last fallen into a beautiful sleep.

Nor will he leave the sickroom without cheering the patient by relating the cases of others. One began just like this but went to the lungs. Another was thought to be serious and proved merely nervous, though the family had ordered the wreath. A third rallied wonderfully after all hope had gone, sat up, recognized everyone, and expired while asking for toast. “But you mustn’t let that worry you,” says the Good Soul. “You have a much better color.”

As host, he is equally formidable. He will not permit you to carry a bag, choose a chair, open a window, close a door, or decide whether you are hungry. You are given slippers too small, a cushion too hard, and the best cup, which has a crack and must not be used carelessly. He heaps your plate while begging you to make yourself at home, and when you decline a third helping he looks wounded on behalf of the pudding.

As guest, he arrives with a little gift: preserved ginger, a calendar adorned with a ruined abbey, or a napkin ring bearing a view of the Town Hall. He praises everything. The room is so restful, the children so natural, the dinner so delightfully simple. If the meat is burned, he likes it well done; if raw, he has always thought we overcook food in this country. He stays late because he fears an early departure might hurt you, while you remain awake because you fear murder might hurt him.

Good Souls observe all Occasions. They know when it is Cousins’ Day, Empire Friendship Week, the anniversary of your engagement, and the birthday of the dog you owned before the war. They send greeting cards with robins, bells, clasped hands, ships at sunset, and verses beginning “Just a little line to say.” Their little lines say a great deal. You must answer each, or be gently reminded next year that the post is so unreliable.

Christmas is their high season. They begin buying presents in August and by December have amassed lavender sachets for men, collar boxes for women, handkerchiefs for babies, and improving games for adolescents who already possess motor bicycles. Every parcel contains sealing wax, ribbon, three labels, and a note requesting that it not be opened until the proper morning. The recipient opens it early in self-defense and discovers a framed maxim: Happiness Is Helping Others. The Good Soul has got there first again.

He never forgets introductions, farewells, condolences, congratulations, inquiries, responses, acknowledgments, and the correct little ceremonies by which ordinary affection is made to resemble diplomatic correspondence. If Mrs. Barlow has sneezed, he asks after her through Mr. Barlow, then writes to Mrs. Barlow, then apologizes to Miss Barlow for having omitted her from his solicitude. Families have been founded, flourished, and become extinct in less time than he requires to be tactful about a sneeze.

His tact is magnificent. He tells the thin woman that she looks wonderfully plump, the stout man that nobody would guess his weight, the old lady that age is merely a number, and the widower that he must not brood. He avoids painful subjects by circling them with bright flags. “Of course, we won’t mention your disappointment,” he says publicly, “though everyone feels you were treated shamefully.” Then he squeezes your hand under the table and upsets the soup.

Do not suppose society rewards these endeavors. Society is practical. It gives its errands to the person who cannot refuse them and its affection to the person who can. When a tiresome aunt must be escorted, a subscription collected, a committee completed, a child watched, a parcel carried, or a dull man entertained, somebody says, “Ask Edwin; he won’t mind.” The phrase is the tribal cry by which the Good Soul is summoned to sacrifice.

Edwin comes cheerfully. He misses his train to deliver your message, pays the fare of the cousin who “has nothing smaller,” and spends Sunday afternoon addressing envelopes for a movement whose objects he has not quite understood. When places are scarce, Edwin volunteers to stay away. When expenses rise, he offers to pay his share and another’s. When blame descends, he hopes no unpleasantness will arise and accepts enough of it to spare the culprit embarrassment.

Yet he is not admitted into the inner courts of human fellowship. People trust him with keys, invalids, secrets, luggage, and elderly relations, but not with scandal worth hearing. They praise him after he has left. “Such a good soul,” they say, in the tone used of a chair that is strong but impossible. He is invited when an extra man is needed, excluded when an amusing one is available, and thanked with an emphasis more wounding than neglect.

Sometimes rebellion seems near. The Good Soul’s lip tightens; he announces that he has been imposed upon once too often; he will henceforth think of himself. We tremble before this revolution. Then the offender explains that he acted only because he knew Edwin would understand. Edwin does understand. He apologizes for having seemed hurt and lends the offender five pounds.

This is perhaps the strangest feature of the species: it bears no malice. Kick a Good Soul today and tomorrow he will inquire whether your foot is better. Cheat him and he blames circumstances; insult him and he detects strain in your voice; forget him and he remembers that you have many claims upon your time. His optimism is armor against evidence. Experience may strike it all day and merely tire itself.

We have spoken lightly, but his plight is terrible. He passes through life carrying bags that belong to stronger people, occupying the worst seat, eating the smallest portion, hearing every grievance, and receiving advice only from those who disregard his. He is useful to all, necessary to many, and desired by none. Humanity leans upon him, wipes its boots, and complains that he is not more cheerful.

Still, perhaps justice waits elsewhere. The Good Soul believes in a heaven where every sacrifice is recorded, every kindness understood, and every patient little endeavor crowned. There, surely, he will have the best seat, the uncracked cup, the comfortable pillow, and tea exactly as he likes it. No one will ask him to change places, carry a parcel, or entertain an aunt.

We sincerely hope so.

Indeed, if the arrangement could be made without further inconvenience, we could wish that all Good Souls were enjoying their reward already.

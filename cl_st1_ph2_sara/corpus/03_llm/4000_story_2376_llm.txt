Jones and Robinson sat opposite one another in the most expensive corner of the most expensive dining-room in New York.

They were what we commonly call successful business men, meaning that each had acquired enough money to regard punctuality as a virtue in other people. Jones dealt in copper, railways, and opinions. Robinson dealt in wheat, warehouses, and politicians. Both had well-fed faces, smooth pink chins, fingers like strings of sausages, and rings substantial enough to moor a small boat. Jones’s waistcoat advanced toward the table in three magnificent curves; Robinson’s appeared to have been cut from the mainsail of a yacht.

They had not met for several years, and after congratulating one another on looking younger, thinner, and more active than either had ever looked in his life, they discussed business.

Then Jones leaned back.

“Do you know, Robinson,” he said, “this sort of thing makes me think of the old days.”

The dining-room glittered about them. Silver covers went by in stately procession. A violin was playing behind a screen of palms. Somewhere nearby a gentleman was complaining because his oysters had been opened two minutes too soon.

“What old days?” asked Robinson.

“The beginning, my dear fellow. New York when we were boys. Poverty. Hardship. Fifteen cents in my pocket and the world against me.”

“Fifteen cents!” Robinson smiled. “By George, Jones, I’d have called myself a capitalist if I’d had fifteen cents.”

Jones looked annoyed.

“I am speaking approximately, sir. There were times when I had less.”

“I had nothing. Not a red cent. I arrived in this city with one boot, half a collar, and a letter of introduction to a man who had died the week before.”

“You had a letter?”

“A useless letter.”

“Nevertheless, you had correspondence. I had no correspondence. I knew nobody. The first night I slept in a packing case down by the river.”

Robinson shook his head gently, as one corrects an inexperienced child.

“A packing case is practically a furnished apartment. I slept in a tar barrel.”

“A barrel!”

“For eleven months.”

Jones gave a short laugh.

“A whole barrel, I suppose?”

“Certainly a whole barrel.”

“Luxury! Mine was a piano box with two boards missing.”

“Two boards! You had ventilation.”

“Ventilation? My dear fellow, the wind came through it with such force that it blew the buttons off my shirt.”

“You had buttons?”

“They were not my buttons. I had found them.”

Robinson folded his arms across his waistcoat.

“My tar barrel stood on its side. The wind came in at one end and went out at the other, taking with it any little warmth I had accumulated. I used to wake in the morning with a skin of ice over my eyelids.”

“That proves you slept,” said Jones. “I never slept. The rats in my piano box held meetings all night.”

“Rats give warmth.”

“These were cold rats.”

“Still, they were company. I was alone.”

“Alone! There were six men in that barrel with you. You told me so twenty years ago.”

“Two men,” said Robinson with dignity, “and a boy who left early.”

“You said six.”

“I may have included visitors. And what if there were six? We had to sleep standing up.”

Jones struck the table softly with the flat of his hand.

“Standing up! You had the support of a barrel. In the piano box we were obliged to hang from the strings.”

“I thought it was empty.”

“It had been empty. We fitted strings ourselves—old telegraph wire collected from the gutter. Many a morning I came down with the news from Albany printed backward across my chest.”

Robinson scowled. The violin began a sentimental melody, which assisted memory wonderfully.

“Ah,” he said, “but those were happy times.”

“The happiest,” Jones agreed at once.

“Innocent times.”

“Beautiful times.”

“No responsibilities.”

“No indigestion.”

Robinson sighed.

“I used to rise at four, wash in the river, and walk twelve miles looking for work.”

“Twelve? I walked twenty.”

“I mean twelve each way.”

“I never stopped walking. I applied for work while in motion.”

“And what did you eat?”

Jones’s expression softened with melancholy.

“Whatever Providence offered.”

“Exactly.”

“Sometimes a crust.”

“A crust! Bread, sir?”

“Not necessarily bread. Once it was the crust from a mustard pot.”

Robinson sneered.

“I lived for three weeks on cold porridge scraped from the bottoms of restaurant bins.”

“So did I.”

“Mine had frozen.”

“Mine had fermented.”

“I preferred it frozen. It took longer to eat.”

Jones waved this aside.

“You had porridge. I should have thanked Heaven for porridge. I existed on potato peelings, orange seeds, and the paper that grocers wrapped cheese in.”

“Cheese paper retains nourishment.”

“Not after six other fellows have boiled it.”

Robinson’s face grew red.

“I ate bran mash.”

“For breakfast?”

“For every meal.”

“Horse feed!”

“Hog feed,” said Robinson proudly. “The horses would not touch it.”

Jones stared at him, momentarily checked. Robinson saw his advantage and pressed it.

“There was a stable near my barrel. Every evening the pigs received a trough of mash, and every evening I waited behind the fence. When the pigs had finished, I climbed over.”

“You ate what the pigs left?”

“No, sir. They never left anything. I licked the trough.”

Jones’s heavy eyelids narrowed. This was strong, but he was not a man who had built six factories by conceding troughs to a competitor.

“I had no trough.”

“You had no stable?”

“I could not afford a stable.”

“Nor could I!”

“But you were admitted to its neighbourhood. I was chased from every stable south of Forty-second Street. The animals complained that I looked hungry. Once—By Jove, I can laugh now—once I ate the tassel from a carriage horse’s ear cap.”

“Wool,” said Robinson. “Highly sustaining.”

“It was imitation wool.”

“Then it was probably cotton.”

“Paper,” said Jones. “Dyed paper, with brass wire in it.”

Robinson took a sip of water and frowned at the glass as though it, too, had enjoyed an easier youth.

“Well, those privations made us,” he said. “I would not exchange one night in that barrel for ten years in a palace.”

“Nor I one hour in the piano box.”

“I sometimes wish I were back there.”

“So do I.”

“With the wind blowing.”

“And the rats freezing.”

“With an honest hunger in my belly.”

“And not a penny in the world.”

They became silent, each contemplating the moral splendour of his former misery. Their reflections were interrupted by a waiter, who had approached with two large menus bound in red leather.

The waiter knew them both. He knew, indeed, that Mr. Robinson liked his duck pink, his asparagus tips uniform, and his cheese old enough to vote. He also knew that Mr. Jones considered champagne unfit for Christian consumption if it had travelled in anything except its own padded compartment.

“Good evening, gentlemen,” he said. “May I take your order?”

Robinson picked up the menu. A golden list of soups, fishes, birds, sauces, fruits, and wines opened before him. For one unguarded second his eyes shone. Then he remembered the barrel.

“I want,” he said, loudly enough for Jones to hear, “a bowl of cold porridge.”

The waiter waited.

“Cold porridge, sir?”

“Scraped from the bottom, if possible.”

“Very good, sir.”

Jones had gone pale, but only in patches.

“And for you, Mr. Jones?”

“Potato peelings,” said Jones. “Plain. No butter.”

“Any particular potato, sir?”

“The smaller and more neglected the better.”

“Very good.”

Robinson studied him.

“And bring me a little bran mash,” he added. “No seasoning. Put it in a trough-shaped dish.”

“Certainly, sir.”

Jones drummed his ringed fingers.

“I shall have a sheet of cheese paper boiled in water.”

“With the label on, sir?”

“Of course with the label on! Do you take me for a millionaire?”

“No, sir,” said the waiter gravely.

“And a carriage tassel,” Jones continued. “Paper, not wool.”

“I fear we have no carriage tassels this evening.”

“No tassels in a first-class restaurant!”

“We may be able to cut one from the wine list.”

“That will do.”

The waiter made a note.

“Anything to drink?”

“River water,” said Robinson.

“From near the docks,” said Jones.

“With ice?”

Both men glared at him.

“Certainly not!” cried Robinson. “Ice is a luxury.”

The waiter bowed and departed.

There followed a silence.

It was not an ordinary silence. It sat between them, spread its napkin, and examined the menu. Around it rose the delicate odours of roasted meats, melted butter, truffles, warm bread, and coffee. A silver dish passed close to Robinson’s shoulder, bearing oysters under a little mountain of crushed ice. He looked away too late.

Jones watched him.

“Fine times,” said Jones.

“Wonderful,” said Robinson.

At the next table a waiter carved a partridge. Its brown skin cracked faintly beneath the knife. Steam rose from it carrying a message of comfort, civilisation, and expensive feeding. Robinson swallowed.

“You remember the cold porridge?” Jones asked.

“Perfectly.”

“Delicious stuff.”

“Nothing like it.”

“Especially when frozen.”

“Or fermented.”

Another waiter crossed the room with a tureen of cream soup. Behind him came potatoes crisped in butter, a sole lying under a yellow sauce, and a salad whose leaves had plainly never known hardship.

Robinson adjusted his collar.

“On reflection,” he said, “perhaps one ought not to be sentimental.”

Jones leaned forward.

“What do you mean?”

“A man must live in his own age. That is progress. We endured porridge so that our children might have partridge. To order porridge now might be—well, almost an insult to the struggle.”

Jones considered this.

“There is something in that.”

“A great deal, sir. We must not worship poverty merely because we defeated it.”

“Certainly not.”

“Besides, my doctor tells me bran is dangerous.”

“Mine has absolutely forbidden potato peelings.”

Robinson looked toward the kitchen door.

“The whole question,” he said, “is one of proportion. A little partridge—merely a bird between us—could hardly be called luxury.”

“It might be called medicine.”

“Exactly. And an oyster or two to prepare the system.”

“Not more than a dozen each.”

“Of course not. Then a mouthful of soup.”

“A cup.”

“A plate, perhaps. One cannot judge until it arrives.”

“And fish?”

“A dab of sole. The smallest dab.”

“With potatoes?”

“If they happen to be on the plate, it would be wasteful to reject them.”

“Waste is a vice,” said Jones firmly.

The waiter returned.

“Gentlemen, I regret to say that the kitchen has no cold porridge.”

Robinson frowned with an expression of immense relief.

“Disgraceful. Well, rather than make trouble, bring us two dozen oysters, the cream soup, the sole, a brace of partridges, asparagus, potatoes, salad, and whatever pudding contains the most brandy.”

“A cheese afterward,” Jones added. “Stilton. None of your timid cheese.”

“Very good, sir.”

“And cancel the bran mash,” said Robinson. “I fear it may remind me too painfully of happier days.”

“The peelings also,” said Jones. “The emotional strain would be excessive.”

“Anything to drink?”

Robinson named an excellent claret.

Jones looked offended.

“My dear fellow, this is a reunion. Champagne!”

“A bottle?”

“Two. We must drink to the barrel.”

“And the piano box,” said Robinson.

The champagne came first. They drank to perseverance, enterprise, simplicity, and the stern virtues of hunger. Then they ate oysters until the past became less distinct. The soup blurred it further. By the time the partridges appeared, the tar barrel had rolled into a remote fog, and the piano box had been closed, roped, and stored in some attic of memory.

Neither man ever mentioned them again.

The barrel and the box were buried as securely as two paupers beneath a marble monument, and if, in later years, either gentleman happened to pass a stable or hear a piano being moved, he immediately ordered another bottle and thought of something else.

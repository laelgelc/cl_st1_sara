# The Black Mother of Nuremberg

There are circumstances which, though in themselves capable of an explanation perfectly consonant with reason, retain in the memory an atmosphere so dreadful that the soberest man hesitates to submit them to the judgment of others. I have more than once begun this narrative, and as often laid it aside; not because I doubted the evidence of my own senses, nor because my wife—who witnessed the greater part of what I shall relate—has ever varied in her recollection, but because the conclusion appears too exact, too shaped, too horribly symmetrical, to be received as mere coincidence.

Nevertheless, I set down the facts. Whether they exhibit an intelligence in one of the lower animals beyond anything admitted by natural philosophy, or whether they reveal only that blind mechanism of accident which men dignify with the names of Fate and Justice, I shall not determine.

It was during our honeymoon, in the autumn of 1889, that Amelia and I visited Nuremberg. I had proposed Switzerland; Amelia preferred Venice; and by one of those matrimonial compromises in which each party yields everything except the privilege of declaring victory, we found ourselves in Franconia.

I do not regret the choice. Nuremberg, when first seen under a mild October sun, has the rare quality of seeming at once actual and remembered. Its red roofs, crowded upon one another like scales upon some vast sleeping creature; its towers, pointed, bulbous, square and battlemented; its bridges spanning the narrow Pegnitz; its carved saints, projecting gables, iron signs and innumerable windows; all have the air less of masonry than of an old German chronicle suddenly made visible. One expects, at the turning of a lane, to meet Dürer carrying a copper plate beneath his cloak, or some grave burgher hastening home before the curfew, conscious of a concealed heresy and an unpaid account.

Amelia, whose enthusiasm for antiquity was genuine but not inexhaustible, bore the first three churches with admirable fortitude. At the fourth she complained of the pavements. At the fifth she observed that mediaeval architects appeared to have considered comfort a symptom of moral decay. When, therefore, on the morning of our third day, I suggested an inspection of the city walls, followed by a visit to the Torture Tower and its celebrated Iron Virgin, she regarded me with that expression of affectionate reproach which a wife acquires sooner than any other accomplishment.

“My dear,” she said, “you promised me shops.”

“And you shall have them.”

“You said that yesterday.”

“Yesterday was occupied by the churches.”

“Precisely.”

I was constructing a defence upon historical grounds when a loud voice from the adjoining breakfast-room intervened.

“Madam,” it cried, “never you trust a man that takes you to a church when there’s a store within gunshot. He’s either broke, converted, or mean.”

The speaker entered without ceremony, carrying in one hand a coffee-cup and in the other a roll upon which he had spread butter to a depth that would have alarmed a physician. He was a tall, lean, sunburnt man of perhaps forty-five years, with iron-grey hair, a clean-shaven jaw of remarkable determination, and eyes of that clear, restless blue which belongs alike to sailors, children and persons incapable of leaving dangerous objects alone. His clothes were good, but worn with an independence of their intended shape; his boots suggested distances rather than drawing-rooms.

“Elias P. Hutcheson, of Rawhide, Colorado,” he said, setting down his cup and offering his hand first to Amelia and then to me. “The P stands for Perseverance, accordin’ to my mother, and for Pest, accordin’ to most other folks. I heard you speak English, Colonel, and I says to myself, ‘Eli, there’s two human beings in this town besides yourself. Go and be sociable.’”

There was no resisting him. Within ten minutes he had informed us that he was a mining engineer by necessity, a cattle-owner by misfortune, and a traveller by the express command of his doctor, who had told him that unless he gave up business for six months he should die. Mr. Hutcheson had accepted the medical opinion while rejecting its logic.

“Doc says, ‘You need rest.’ I says, ‘Rest is what they give a man after the funeral.’ He says, ‘Go to Europe.’ So I come. I’ve looked at kings’ beds, bishops’ bones, old boots, broken pots, and enough pictures of thin saints to stock a famine. Now I’m huntin’ something with machinery in it.”

I mentioned the Iron Virgin.

His face brightened.

“That lady with the spikes? Judge, you have found my morning’s occupation. If Mrs. Colonel can forgive you for missin’ the stores, we’ll examine the female.”

Amelia declared that she would accompany us, partly because the day was fine and partly, I suspect, because Mr. Hutcheson’s extravagant talk amused her. Thus began an acquaintance of scarcely six hours, the end of which has remained with me through more than twenty years of quieter friendships.

We set out soon after ten. The air possessed that golden lucidity peculiar to early autumn, when sunlight seems not merely to illuminate stone and leaf but to preserve them. The roofs shone after rain; from the market came the cries of women selling fruit, the rattle of wheels, the clinking harness of draught-horses, and the warm mixed odour of apples, damp straw and coffee. Above these homely activities rose the severe outlines of the castle, while beyond the gate the fortifications ran in long reaches of grey wall, bastion and tower, with the dry fosse below half filled by gardens.

Mr. Hutcheson admired everything with equal violence.

“That wall,” he declared, striking it with his stick, “is business. That’s a wall built by a people that expected their neighbours and didn’t want ’em. Give me six Winchesters, two boxes of cartridges, a barrel of flour, and a trustworthy mule, and I’ll hold this town against the German Empire till the flour gives out.”

“You forget the inhabitants,” said Amelia.

“Madam, they may stay if they behave. If they don’t, I’ll hold it against them too.”

He saluted a group of soldiers, attempted conversation with an elderly fruit-seller in a compound of English, Spanish and invention, purchased six pears when he had intended one, and distributed five among children who followed us for a quarter of a mile. Yet beneath his absurdity there was a quality both generous and engaging. He had the frontier habit of treating every stranger as a possible friend, every difficulty as a wager, and every prohibition as a personal challenge.

We left the busier quarter and followed a walk beside the old wall. On our left rose the masonry, patched by centuries into a surface of innumerable greys; on our right the ground descended toward a strip of gardens where cabbages, late roses and scarlet beans grew beneath the shadow of loopholes made for crossbows. The path was strewn with small fragments of stone fallen from the parapet.

Mr. Hutcheson, talking continuously, flicked one of these with the end of his stick. It skittered ahead of him and struck the wall.

“That reminds me,” he said, “of a little misunderstanding we had in Arizona. Fellow named Bledsoe fired at a tin can, missed the can, hit a frying-pan, scared a burro, and started a chain of events that cost him a claim, three teeth and his prospects of matrimony.”

“What happened to the young lady?” Amelia asked.

“She married the man that owned the burro. You can’t calculate women or ricochets.”

He prodded another stone, this one as large as a walnut, and with an idle upward movement sent it spinning over the low inner edge of the path.

At once there came from below a soft, sickening sound—not loud, but of a kind which the ear interprets before the mind has consented to understand it.

Mr. Hutcheson ceased speaking.

At the foot of the wall, where a broken drain-pipe projected above a patch of nettles, lay a black kitten. The stone had struck it upon the skull. Its little body made two convulsive movements; the forepaws opened as if grasping at the earth; then it was still.

Amelia turned away with an exclamation. Hutcheson sprang down the slope, though there was nothing to be done. He lifted the creature carefully. Blood had stained the glossy fur above one ear, and one greenish eye protruded from its socket with a dreadful disproportioned brightness.

“Well,” he said, but all the buoyancy had left his voice, “that’s a poor piece of shootin’. A mighty poor piece.”

From the mouth of the drain came a low sound.

It was not the ordinary cry of a cat. It began almost below hearing, a vibration rather than a note, and increased until it resembled the hum of some taut metallic cord. A large black cat emerged from the shadow. She was lean, though not starved, with a white scar across her muzzle and eyes of pale green. She approached Hutcheson without haste.

“Easy, mother,” he said. “I didn’t aim to do it.”

He placed the dead kitten upon the earth and withdrew his hand. The cat sniffed the little body. She touched its neck with her nose, then pushed it gently, first on one side and then on the other. Receiving no response, she sat down.

I had seen animals grieve, or what we interpret as grief; but there was in her stillness something more disturbing than any cry could have been. She looked at the kitten, then at the stone, and finally at Hutcheson. The transition was deliberate. Her eyes remained fixed upon his face.

He took a step backward.

“I reckon explanations don’t carry much weight in this court.”

The cat’s lips drew away from her teeth.

Amelia begged us to leave. Hutcheson removed his hat—a gesture which at another time might have appeared theatrical—and for one moment stood bareheaded before the insignificant body. Then he climbed back to the path.

As we walked on, I glanced behind. The mother still sat beside her kitten. The sun shone upon both, making the blood at the kitten’s temple look almost lacquered. When distance at last obscured them, the green eyes were yet directed toward us.

The incident depressed Amelia deeply, and for some minutes even Hutcheson was silent. Yet silence could no more remain with him than stillness with quicksilver. Presently he sighed, replaced his hat at a more defiant angle and said:

“I once saw a mother travel forty miles to collect a debt for her boy.”

He proceeded, at Amelia’s request, to relate the story. I cannot reproduce the whole of it, for his narrative wandered through several counties and included a disputed horse, a poker game, a sheriff of imperfect courage, and two brothers named respectively Little Jake and Bigger Jake. Its essential point was simple. A drunken ruffian had killed a young man during a quarrel, escaped custody and fled across the desert. The dead man’s mother, a widow, followed with a rifle. She tracked the fugitive for two days, found him asleep in an abandoned hut, and waited outside until he woke.

“Did she shoot him?” I asked.

“No, sir. She marched him back to town. That was worse. Jury let him off—said it was an accident. The old lady stood up, looked at each juryman one at a time, and says, ‘Gentlemen, if accident is innocent, be careful of accidents.’ Three weeks later the fellow was found at the bottom of a dry well.”

“And did she—?” Amelia began.

“Nobody proved a thing, madam. But that was a country where mothers took an interest in their children.”

The tale, though told with humorous digressions, did not restore our spirits. I found myself looking repeatedly toward the path behind us. Twice I saw nothing. On the third occasion a black shape slipped from one patch of wall-shadow to another.

I said nothing, supposing that the sight had deceived me.

We re-entered the city through a gate whose vaulted passage amplified the wheels of a passing cart into artillery. Beneath the arch, seated upon a ledge immediately above the roadway, was the black cat.

There could be no doubt of her identity. The scar crossed her muzzle like a thread of chalk; a dark stain, not yet dried, marked the fur about her mouth where she had licked her kitten. She watched Hutcheson pass beneath her. Her body remained motionless, but her head turned slowly, following him.

Amelia gripped my arm.

“It is the same one.”

“The old town is full of cats,” I replied, too quickly.

“That one has blood upon it.”

Hutcheson looked up. For an instant man and animal stared at each other. Then he laughed, but the laugh lacked its former assurance.

“Persistent party, ain’t she? Madam Cat, the defence admits negligence but denies malice.”

The animal crouched. Hutcheson moved away, and she did not spring; instead she descended from the ledge and followed at a distance of perhaps thirty yards.

Now, it is necessary to be exact. She did not trail us continuously in the manner of a dog. Sometimes she vanished into an alley or beneath a wagon, and we believed ourselves rid of her. Yet at the next turn we would perceive her on a doorstep, under a stall, or balanced upon a fountain’s stone rim. She never approached Amelia or me. Her attention was reserved entirely for Hutcheson.

At the market she passed through a confusion of boots and skirts, received a kick from a porter, and reappeared on the farther side. Near the Church of St. Sebald she took to the roofs, moving along gutters and projections with silent facility. Once, when Hutcheson paused before a shop-window, her reflection appeared in the glass behind his shoulder. He wheeled around; she was sitting in the street.

“This is gettin’ personal,” he remarked.

“It is not too late to abandon the tower,” said Amelia.

“On account of one bereaved pussy-cat? Why, ma’am, that would establish a precedent fatal to American travel. Besides, I ain’t seen the mechanical lady.”

I attempted to reason with him. The accident had naturally agitated us; the cat was accustomed to the neighbourhood and merely followed the route by chance. Hutcheson agreed so emphatically that I knew he was no more convinced than I.

We stopped at a small inn for luncheon. The dining-room was bright, clean and crowded; copper pans shone upon the wall, and the odour of roast meat was sufficiently reassuring to dispel fancies less tenacious than ours. Hutcheson recovered his spirits over sausages and beer. He amused Amelia by describing his first encounter with European plumbing, disputed with me the superiority of American bridges, and challenged a musical clock to finish its tune before he finished his tankard.

For nearly an hour we saw nothing of the cat.

When we rose, the innkeeper’s daughter uttered a cry and pointed to the window. Upon the outer sill, pressed close against the glass, sat the black mother. Her face was level with Hutcheson’s plate. The pale eyes appeared luminous against the dark fur.

“How did it get there?” Amelia whispered.

The window overlooked a narrow court enclosed by walls at least twelve feet high. There was no tree, barrel or stair by which an animal might easily have reached the sill.

Hutcheson stood up so abruptly that his chair fell.

The cat struck once at the pane. Her claws made a small grating noise upon the glass. Then she opened her mouth in a soundless snarl.

Mr. Hutcheson took a piece of sausage from the dish and went toward the door.

“What are you doing?” I asked.

“Negotiatin’.”

In the court he placed the meat upon a stone and withdrew. The cat descended by some route invisible to us, approached the offering, sniffed it, and without eating set one forepaw upon it. Her claws extended. With a swift movement she tore the sausage into fragments, turned her back upon them, and looked again at Hutcheson.

He returned pale beneath his sunburn.

“Judge,” he said to me quietly, “that animal ain’t hungry.”

I urged that we return to our hotel. Amelia seconded me with an earnestness which ought to have prevailed. But obstinacy was in Hutcheson not a fault added to his character; it was the iron framework upon which the rest had been constructed.

“Gentlemen,” he said—though only one gentleman was present—“I have crossed rivers that wanted drownin’ me, climbed mountains that objected to bein’ climbed, and sat in games where every citizen held five aces and a weapon. I will not be turned from a paid admission by a cat. Forward to the Virgin!”

Thus, after leaving Amelia for half an hour among shops—an arrangement which restored her sufficiently to permit her later rejoining us—we continued toward the old Torture Tower.

The quarter through which we passed grew progressively narrower and darker. Tall houses leaned above the street until their upper storeys seemed to exchange confidences, excluding the wholesome daylight. Carved faces peered from corbels; water dripped from dragon-headed spouts; and underfoot the cobbles, polished by generations, shone like damp bone. The cheerful noise of the market diminished behind us. Bells sounded somewhere overhead, their strokes crossing one another in the enclosed air.

At the corner of a passage I saw the cat again.

She stood beneath a weather-worn image of the Virgin and Child. The accidental symbolism struck me unpleasantly: maternity in patient stone above, maternity in living hatred below. Before I could direct Hutcheson’s attention to her, she disappeared through an iron grating.

The Torture Tower formed part of an older circuit of defence, a squat circular mass of masonry rising from the inner margin of the fosse. Its lower stones were blackened by damp; the upper courses caught a narrow blade of declining sunlight. The entrance, reached by a bridge and crooked stair, had none of the dignity of a public monument. It resembled rather the back door of a prison—which, in effect, it had been.

Amelia was waiting there, having completed her purchases with a rapidity for which I have never ceased to be grateful. She had acquired several parcels and a small illustrated guidebook. The latter she held open at an engraving of the Iron Virgin.

“It says,” she informed us, “that the spikes were arranged to avoid the vital organs, so that death might be prolonged.”

“Scientific nation,” said Hutcheson. “That’s what I call attention to detail.”

“You will not make a jest of it when you see it.”

“Madam, if I can’t make a jest of a thing, I generally don’t look at it.”

The custodian admitted us after some delay. He was an elderly German with a yellow-grey beard and one shoulder higher than the other. His name, as nearly as I remember, was Kranz. He spoke sufficient English for his office, though he used it reluctantly, as if each foreign word were a coin extorted from him.

We entered a low chamber containing chains, manacles, thumbscrews and other instruments whose cruelty was rendered almost abstract by age. Rust had softened their edges; labels had domesticated them; dust lay upon racks once associated with screams. Tourists had scratched initials into the beams. A vase of fresh flowers stood absurdly on the custodian’s desk.

It is a melancholy truth that suffering, when long past, becomes picturesque. We examined each object with the decency appropriate to relics, yet we examined it. We paid admission to approach what its victims would have surrendered fortunes to escape. The thought occurred to me then, and has often returned, that civilisation does not always abolish cruelty; sometimes it merely puts it behind a railing and prints a catalogue.

Kranz led us upward. The stair wound inside the thickness of the wall, its steps hollowed by feet which imagination too readily supplied with shackles. Through slit windows came alternate bars of sun and shadow. The air smelled of old stone, cold iron, mildew and that peculiar dry sweetness which hangs in rooms long closed.

On the first landing stood a chair set with blunt spikes.

“Comfortable-looking furniture,” said Hutcheson. “My aunt Louisa owned one morally similar.”

On the next level we saw the rack, a wooden frame longer than a man, with windlass, cords and iron-toothed rollers. Kranz demonstrated its movement by turning a handle. The wood gave a complaining creak.

Amelia requested that he stop.

Hutcheson examined the mechanism closely.

“Crude leverage,” he pronounced. “Too much friction. They needed ball-bearings.”

Kranz stared at him with evident disapproval. To the custodian these horrors were perhaps sacred in the fashion of professional property; Hutcheson’s levity offended his antiquarian pride.

We passed pincers, masks, cages and a narrow cell without light. Upon one wall, beneath modern whitewash, a rusty brown discoloration spread from the height of a man’s shoulder to the floor. Kranz stated that it was blood. I cannot vouch for the assertion, but Amelia swayed, and I was obliged to support her.

While I attended to her, a movement near the stair caught my eye. Between two uprights of the railing appeared a black face and two green eyes.

The cat had entered the tower.

I called to Kranz and pointed. He shouted, seized a broom and struck at the animal. She avoided the blow, ran upward three steps, and turned. Her back rose, but her eyes were not upon the custodian. Hutcheson stood above her on the landing.

“Persistent as unpaid taxes,” he said.

Kranz advanced again. The cat leapt to a crossbeam, passed along it above our heads, and vanished into the darkness of the upper stair.

“How could she enter?” I asked.

The custodian shrugged. There were drains, apertures, roofs; old buildings admitted rats and cats as old men admit memories, through cracks no one else perceived.

Amelia, having recovered, insisted upon leaving. Hutcheson, with unexpected gentleness, offered to conduct her down. She refused, unwilling to deprive us of the visit, but made us promise not to touch anything. I promised for both of us.

Hutcheson winked at me.

We ascended to the final chamber.

It was larger than those below, though the vaulted roof and massive walls produced a sense of confinement. A single high window admitted a shaft of dusty light. In it innumerable motes revolved with deceptive tranquillity. Against the curved wall stood the Iron Virgin.

Even now I find it difficult to describe that object without granting it some semblance of life.

It was an upright case of dark metal, fashioned crudely in the form of a robed woman. The head wore a placid, almost foolish smile; the eyes were lowered; the hands, modelled upon the exterior, met in an attitude of prayer. The figure stood more than seven feet high. Its front was divided vertically. One half formed a door, hinged at the side, and this door now stood open.

Within, the mockery ceased.

From the inner surface projected iron spikes of varying length, black at the base and polished at their points by restoration or touch. Corresponding spikes extended from the back and sides. There were spaces for the limbs and trunk, yet no posture in which a human body might avoid them all. The longer points were arranged opposite the eyes, throat, breast and abdomen. At the base lay a grated channel which the guidebook identified with chilling concision as a drain.

A rope, passed through a pulley fixed in the roof, controlled the heavy door. Kranz demonstrated by pulling downward. The metal front moved a few inches, slowly and with a deep note from the hinge, like a groan dragged from the masonry itself. When he relaxed the rope, a counterweight checked its fall.

Hutcheson walked around the figure, tapping it with his knuckles.

“How much does the door weigh?”

Kranz did not understand.

I translated as well as I could. The custodian answered: perhaps four hundred pounds; perhaps more.

“And them spikes genuine?”

“Some old. Some repaired.”

“Ever been used?”

Kranz spread his hands. Tradition said yes; historians disputed. The tower had records, but records burn, princes lie, and instruments often survive the names of those who suffered by them.

Hutcheson stepped inside.

Amelia screamed.

He turned, grinning from the iron cavity.

“Plenty of room, ma’am. Built for a larger customer.”

“Come out at once!”

“My dear fellow,” I said, “you promised.”

“You promised, Colonel. I winked.”

He placed his shoulders against the back of the case, carefully avoiding the spikes. His sunburnt face appeared beneath the Virgin’s impassive metal countenance with an effect grotesque beyond comedy.

“Now, General Kranz,” he said, “ease the door in a foot or two. I want to see whether the advertisements are honest.”

Kranz objected with vehemence. He pointed to the spikes, the rope, the pulley and Hutcheson’s head; shook all ten fingers; invoked regulations; and finally attempted to seize his arm.

Hutcheson withdrew farther into the case.

“Don’t get excited, Professor. You hold the rope. Stop her with the points six inches off. I’ve trusted shakier men with stronger arguments.”

“It is madness,” I said.

“No, Judge, it’s investigation. History should be verified.”

Amelia caught my sleeve.

“Make him come out.”

I tried. I reminded him of the age of the mechanism, the uncertain rope, the weight of the door and the consequences of the smallest error. He listened courteously, then shook his head.

“You British folks take antiquities on faith. We test ’em.”

The distinction was unjust, but there was no time to debate national methods. Kranz, perhaps fearing that Hutcheson would pull the door himself, at last consented to a limited demonstration. He made the American stand precisely in the centre, with arms lowered between two rows of spikes. He then took the controlling rope in both hands and planted his feet.

“Only little,” he said. “No move. No jest.”

“I am a monument,” replied Hutcheson.

The custodian pulled. The door stirred.

The hinge emitted a long metallic complaint, and the smiling half-face began to turn inward. The spikes upon its interior advanced through the dusty shaft of light. Their shadows, lengthened upon the floor, touched Hutcheson before the iron did.

At twelve inches he called, “Hold.”

Kranz held.

Hutcheson inspected the nearest point, which stood level with his breast.

“Sharp enough,” he said. “Now another inch.”

“No!”

“That ain’t an inch, Marshal.”

The custodian, muttering, released perhaps half an inch of rope. The door moved. Every motion of his hands was magnified at the heavy metal leaf; every fibre of the old cord tightened. I could hear the faint grinding of the pulley overhead.

Amelia hid her face against my shoulder, then looked again, unable—as none of us was able—to withdraw her attention.

Hutcheson continued to jest.

“If this contraption slips, Colonel, kindly tell the United States Government I died extendin’ the boundaries of knowledge.”

“You are extending nothing but our anxiety.”

“Same thing, in science.”

“Enough,” said Kranz. Sweat had appeared upon his forehead. “Come out.”

“Just bring her close enough to photograph.”

There was no camera present, but this fact did not disturb him.

Then, from somewhere above us, came the low metallic hum I had heard beside the dead kitten.

Hutcheson’s smile vanished.

The sound issued from the roof-beams. We all looked upward.

The black cat crouched upon a stone ledge just below the spring of the vault. How she had reached it I cannot say. Behind her, a small aperture showed a square of blue sky. Her body was flattened; her tail moved slowly from side to side. The stain about her mouth had dried to brown. In the dim chamber her green eyes burned with an intensity which seemed independent of the light.

Kranz shouted.

The cat did not move.

Hutcheson stood helpless within the iron shell. The points before him were now no more than four inches from his face and breast; those behind prevented any backward motion. For the first time since we had met him, he appeared afraid.

“Colonel,” he said quietly, “remove that animal.”

I stepped toward the wall. The ledge was beyond reach. Kranz, still holding the rope, kicked the broom toward me. It slid across the floor. I seized it.

The cat’s gaze shifted from Hutcheson to the custodian’s hands.

That movement is the one circumstance which, after all these years, I cannot explain away. She looked at the rope; she looked at the door; then she gathered herself.

“Take care!” I cried.

She sprang.

Not at Hutcheson. Not at me. She launched herself across the open chamber directly at Kranz.

He raised one arm instinctively, but dared not release the rope. The cat struck his face. Her foreclaws fastened at his temples; her hind feet tore at his beard and throat. He screamed and staggered. I saw one black paw sweep downward across his right eye. There came a wet, yielding sound. Blood burst over his cheek.

His hands opened.

The rope flew upward.

For a fraction of a second the door remained poised, as though the ancient mechanism itself hesitated before renewing its office. Then the counterweight chain snapped—or slipped from its catch; I never learned which.

The Iron Virgin closed.

There was a rush of rope through the pulley, a thunderous clang, and beneath it a thick, multiple thud unlike any sound produced by lifeless metal.

Hutcheson did not scream. One short breath escaped him—“God!” or perhaps merely air forced from the lungs—and the door struck home.

The spikes entered together.

One pierced his left eye and emerged at the back of the head with a fragment of bone upon its point. Another drove through the mouth, breaking the teeth; two passed into the chest; lower down the iron teeth took the abdomen and thighs. Blood spurted through the narrowing seam, bright against the black metal, then ran in quick branching lines over the Virgin’s folded hands. Within the case something convulsed once. The structure trembled.

Amelia fell without a sound.

I caught her too late to prevent her head striking my arm. For some seconds—I cannot measure them—I knelt beside her, conscious only of the terrible reverberation moving around the vaulted room. Kranz rolled upon the floor, clutching his ruined eye. The cat had left him.

When I looked up, she sat upon the shoulder of the Iron Virgin.

From there she climbed to the metal head, and from the head she descended along the edge of the closed door. The blood ran freely now, seeping through the seam and dripping from the spikes whose tips projected at certain worn apertures. The cat placed one paw in it.

She licked the paw.

Then, with perfect calm, she squeezed through the upper gap where the warped halves failed to meet. There was space enough for her body, though not for a man’s hand. A moment later she was inside with the dead.

We heard her moving.

The sound was small: a soft pressure of paws upon cloth, a faint scrape against iron. Then her head appeared again near the opening above Hutcheson’s shattered brow. His face was scarcely a face. One blue eye remained open; the other had been carried backward by the spike. Blood filled the mouth and covered the chin.

The cat settled upon his head, as she had sat beside her kitten.

Her tail hung through the seam. She bent and began to lick the blood from his forehead.

There was no frenzy in her, no savage tearing, no animal panic. She licked slowly and with evident satisfaction, pausing after each stroke to look at us. The pale green eyes held no madness. I would almost have preferred madness. What I saw—or believed I saw—was recognition completed, intention fulfilled, an account balanced.

Beside me Amelia stirred and whispered Hutcheson’s name.

That human sound broke whatever paralysis held me.

Against the wall, above a rack of polearms, hung an executioner’s sword. It was broad, two-handed and without a point, intended not for combat but for the single final stroke upon a kneeling neck. Whether it was sharpened for display I did not know. I tore it from its hooks.

Kranz, seeing me, cried out, but I paid no attention. I stepped upon the base of the Iron Virgin and raised the sword.

The cat looked down.

For one instant we confronted one another across the blood of Elias P. Hutcheson: she, the avenger of her young; I, the friend—briefly known, absurd, reckless, generous—of the man she had destroyed. Perhaps the symmetry should have stayed my hand. It did not.

She sprang as I struck.

The blade met her in the air. I felt a light resistance, then a sudden release. Her body struck the floor on one side of the chamber; the head fell on the other and rolled through the shaft of sunlight, leaving a dotted trail of red upon the dust. The green eyes remained open.

The silence which followed was broken only by the creak of the rope swinging overhead and the slow fall of blood into the grated drain.

We attempted to open the Virgin, but the impact had jammed the lock and twisted one hinge. Men were summoned; tools brought; Amelia carried downstairs; the wounded custodian attended by a surgeon. It took more than an hour to force the iron shell apart. I was not present when they removed Hutcheson’s body. I had seen enough.

Kranz lost his right eye but survived. He maintained that the cat had entered through a ventilation opening and acted from fright. The municipal authorities agreed. Hutcheson’s death was declared accidental, attributable to misconduct by a visitor and failure of an ancient restraint. The cat was disposed of without ceremony. Its kitten, I was told, was found later beneath the wall and buried by a gardener’s child.

These are reasonable conclusions, and I offer none in their place.

Yet there are details which reason does not comfortably digest: that the mother followed through crowded streets; that she refused food; that she entered a closed court and then the tower; that she waited above the machine; that she sprang not upon the man who had killed her kitten, but upon the one person whose released hands could deliver him to the spikes.

One may say instinct. One may say coincidence. One may say that grief, whether in beast or man, creates patterns where none exist. I have said all these things to myself.

But sometimes, when autumn sunlight lies upon old stone, I remember the small broken body beneath the wall and the larger broken body enclosed in iron; the kitten with its one eye thrust out, the man pierced through the eye; the mother seated above the first, then above the second. I remember Hutcheson’s frontier tale and the widow’s warning: if accident is innocent, be careful of accidents.

Amelia has never revisited Nuremberg. For many years she could not endure the sight of a black cat, though she is naturally kind to animals. As for me, I still hear at times the old hinge moving inch by inch, and the last comic voice within the darkness declaring that history should be verified.

It may be asked whether I repent of killing the cat. I answer that I do not know. She had avenged her young according to the law of her nature; I avenged my companion according to mine. If there was cruelty in the act, it began before either of us took part in it—in the careless movement of a stick, in the fall of a stone, perhaps in the first human hand that fashioned iron suffering into the likeness of a praying woman.

But let no one call my final stroke wanton. I had watched her sit upon the dead man’s head and lick his blood.

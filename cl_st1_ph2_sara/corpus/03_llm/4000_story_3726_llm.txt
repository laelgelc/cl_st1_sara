### McWilliams’s Burglar Alarm

I met Mr. McWilliams on the evening train from New York, and found him surrounded by parcels, umbrellas, railway literature, and that settled expression of patience which is generally acquired only by raising a large family or dealing with inventors. A newspaper item concerning a new electrical burglar alarm attracted his notice. He folded the paper carefully, looked at me, and said:

“I have had experience in those matters. Nine years of it. I do not speak from prejudice, mind you, nor from any desire to injure a worthy branch of commerce. I shall state the facts—nothing extenuated, and no electrician charged with anything he did not charge me for first.

“It was my wife’s idea. Most of the improvements in our domestic life have been my wife’s ideas. I supply the hesitation, and then we compromise by doing precisely what she proposed.

“She had been reading about burglars. There had not been one in our neighborhood for seventeen years, and naturally she felt that another could not be far off. I said a burglar would have to climb a stone wall, cross an open lawn, pass under six windows, disturb the gravel, and enter a house occupied by twelve persons, three of whom snored with unusual authority. She said those were merely facilities.

“So we sent for an alarm man.

“He came with diagrams, certificates, testimonials, batteries, brass bells, and a quiet confidence which I afterwards learned was billed separately. He said his apparatus made burglary practically impossible. Every door and window was connected by wire with an annunciator in my bedroom; the opening of any one of them would strike a gong, illuminate a number, communicate with the police, and place the intruder morally, if not physically, in our hands.

“I asked the price. He named it. I asked if burglary would not be cheaper. My wife said we ought not to measure the safety of our children in money. This was sound Christian doctrine and ended the discussion.

“The apparatus was installed. It occupied two men five days, and required eleven miles of wire, three hundred screws, forty-eight holes in the woodwork, and the temporary removal of a portrait of my wife’s grandfather, who had survived two wars but not electrical progress. When the work was done, the expert rang the bell, showed us the annunciator, collected his money, and departed with the serene dignity of a man who had benefited civilization.

“That night burglars entered the house and took the silver.

“They came through a second-story window.

“I mentioned this circumstance to the company next morning. The manager expressed regret, but said I had ordered their Ground-Floor Domestic Protector, Catalogue B. The second story was not included.

“I said the expert had assured me that every window was protected.

“‘Every window contemplated by the contract,’ said the manager. ‘A second-story window is technically an elevation.’

“I asked why he had not mentioned this before. He said they never alarmed a customer unnecessarily.

“Well, I had the second floor attached. It cost more than the first, because electricity, like society, finds it harder to ascend. The company sent two more men, who remained a week, ate with the servants, cut through three water pipes, and connected the bath bell with the stable. Then they tested the system. It was first rate. A canary could not have leaned against a pane of glass without informing the county.

“Three nights later burglars entered through the roof and took my clothes.

“I called upon the manager wearing articles borrowed from my coachman. He explained that roofs came under their Aerial and Clerical Department, and were not commonly included unless a customer maintained jewels, skylights, or a guilty conscience on the upper premises.

“I said I wished the entire house protected—from cellar to chimney.

“He was pleased. He said that was the spirit in which science ought to be approached.

“So they wired the roof, chimneys, coal holes, dumb-waiter, cistern, pantry shelves, speaking tubes, and a trapdoor which had been nailed shut since 1846. They placed contact springs under the carpets and a detector in the piano. There was also a private signal attached to my wife’s jewel box, and another to the ice chest, though the distinction between the two was not clearly maintained.

“The completed alarm had twenty-seven bells. There was a fire bell, a burglar bell, a police bell, a servant bell, a supplementary bell, and a bell designed to notify us that the other bells had rung. At the central station stood an enormous brass gong with a voice like the Last Trump aggravated by machinery.

“At five the next morning the cook opened the kitchen door.

“The alarm discharged itself.

“It began in the cellar with a low electrical complaint, mounted through the halls, struck the gong, released three shutters, illuminated fourteen numbers, rang the stable, and sent a signal to the police station announcing that the northwest scullery window was committing murder. The cook fainted into the flour barrel. The children came down in their nightclothes. My wife prayed. I seized a revolver and shot a china shepherdess which had been in her family for a hundred and six years.

“The police arrived and arrested the butler because he was the only person dressed.

“I got him released and summoned the expert. He said the alarm was working perfectly.

“I observed that the cook must open the kitchen door every morning.

“He said that had not occurred to him.

“Then he inserted a switch by which the cook could disconnect the system before opening the door and reconnect it afterwards. This was a simple arrangement and cost only ninety-six dollars, exclusive of explanation.

“For a week all went well. Then we discovered that the cook was disconnecting the alarm at bedtime, because she did not enjoy rising early enough to disconnect it in the morning. The burglars discovered it about twenty minutes before we did. They entered by the kitchen, made coffee, ate a cold ham, and removed the dining-room clock.

“One of them left a note on my desk. It said:

“‘Dear Sir,—Your alarm was not in operation when we called. We trust this was accidental, as it places a professional man in an embarrassing position. The coffee was excellent, but the ham was a little dry. Respectfully, J. Harrison.’

“I admired the fellow’s candor, though I thought his criticism of the ham unnecessary.

“The expert now supplied an automatic attachment which prevented the cook from disconnecting the system for more than four minutes. Unfortunately it also prevented her from reconnecting it for twenty-four hours. A burglar could enter during the interval, and one did. He remained eleven days.

“We might never have known it, but he complained to my wife that the guest-room mattress was thin. He had been occupying the southeast bedroom, taking his meals after the family retired, and reading my library in a systematic way. He was a courteous man, and when I objected to the arrangement he apologized and said he had supposed the chamber went with the alarm.

“I told him it did not.

“He said the misunderstanding was natural, because everything else in the house appeared to belong to the burglar-alarm company or the burglars.

“There was force in this, and I let him finish breakfast before he left. He took the teaspoons, but returned two that he considered plated.

“After that the company put in a secret compensating relay which no burglar could understand. The first burglar who came understood it in nine minutes. He detached a blue wire, tied it to a red wire, and caused the alarm to ring in a house four blocks away. While the police were searching that house, he took our piano. The company said this demonstrated the range of the apparatus.

“By and by they perfected another improvement. It was called the Human Presence Discriminator. It was guaranteed to distinguish between a burglar and a member of the household by the manner in which he walked.

“That was a noble conception, but it had prejudices. It accepted burglars without inquiry and objected bitterly to the family. If I crossed the hall after midnight, it rang six bells. If my wife went to the nursery, it notified the stable. If one of the children turned over in bed, a red light appeared in the pantry. But a burglar could come in with a wheelbarrow, recite the Declaration of Independence, and fall downstairs without arousing a filament.

“The expert said our walk was at fault. He trained us for three weeks. We learned to enter our own rooms sideways, bearing the weight upon the outer edge of the foot. The servants had to crawl. My aunt, who was seventy-two and did not take readily to electricity, went home.

“Peace followed for nearly a month. Then the false alarms began.

“They came at 11:40, 12:15, 1:05, 2:30, and again at four, when a man has neither courage nor Christian resignation left. Each time I rose, found my weapon, stepped on a tack, collided with the bedpost, and descended in the dark, while my wife called after me to be careful and not wake the children. I never found a burglar. I found a cat, a curtain, an expanding floorboard, a damp contact, and once the Methodist minister, who had been invited to spend the night but had neglected to tell the discriminator his denomination.

“The coachman joined me in these excursions. He was an energetic man and inclined to fire first, in order to save discussion. One night the alarm indicated the nursery. We approached from opposite directions. A figure came through the door. The coachman discharged his gun, I discharged mine, and the figure discharged a basin of hot water.

“It was the nurse.

“The coachman’s shot removed a portion of her cap. Mine struck the brass gong, returned, passed through my sleeve, and lodged in the coachman in a place where he could neither sit nor discuss the matter with composure. The nurse resigned. The coachman remained, but stood up for six weeks.

“The expert examined the apparatus and said the alarm had acted properly; there had, in fact, been persons in the nursery.

“I paid the nurse, the surgeon, the coachman, and the expert. Science must be supported.

“In the third year we had three hundred and eighty-two alarms. Eleven were caused by mice, seven by thunder, one hundred and four by changes of temperature, sixty by servants, ninety-three by no assignable agency, and the remainder by the alarm itself. Two were caused by burglars, but those occurred while the mechanism was being repaired and therefore could not fairly be credited to it.

“We ceased responding by degrees. At first I got up with a pistol; then with a candle; then I sat up in bed and listened; finally I turned over. My wife said this was reckless. I said it was rest.

“The bells continued to ring, and we disconnected the nursery to spare the children. Then we disconnected the kitchen to spare the servants, the guest rooms to spare society, and my wife’s room to spare her nerves. I disconnected my own room in the interests of national sanity. At length every chamber was cut off except the burglar-alarm room itself, which nobody entered.

“One night burglars came and stole the alarm.

“They took the annunciator, batteries, bells, gong, wires, switches, clockwork, certificates, and the framed directions for use. They removed seven miles of copper without waking us. In the morning there remained only a brass plate over the front door reading THIS HOUSE PROTECTED.

“I confess I felt relief. It was the first uninterrupted night I had enjoyed in four years. But the company called at ten and said the theft proved the indispensable nature of the system. They offered to reinstall it with their latest improvements at a remunerative figure.

“I declined.

“My wife reminded me of the children.

“We compromised.

“The workmen began in May. They removed plaster from eight rooms, raised the floors, took down the staircase, and built a shed in the garden for their tools. By July the house was not inhabitable, but the company said it was nearly secure. Then the men left for their summer vacation.

“So did we.

“When we returned in September, we found that a company of burglars had occupied the premises during August. They had slept in the bedrooms, cooked in the kitchen, held meetings in the drawing room, and used the unfinished wires for drying clothes. They had also sold the furniture.

“The leader met us at the door. He said they had understood the house to be furnished for their convenience by the alarm company. He showed me a letter from one of the workmen telling him where the silver was stored. I could not dispute documentary evidence.

“We redeemed our furniture from various dealers at commercial rates. The burglars assisted in identifying it and charged ten per cent for their time. This seemed high, but their records were accurate.

“When the workmen returned, they installed a new automatic clock. The principle was beautiful. At ten at night the clock connected every circuit; at six in the morning it disconnected them. Human carelessness was eliminated.

“The first night the clock connected the alarm at six in the morning and disconnected it at ten at night.

“This left us protected during breakfast, luncheon, and most of dinner, but gave the burglar the freedom of the house at the hours customary to his profession.

“The expert said the clock was running backward.

“He reversed it. It then rang continuously from midnight to noon and struck the hours in batches of thirteen. He replaced it with a French clock, which observed Paris time; then with a German clock, which was accurate but refused to cooperate with American wires; then with an astronomical clock, which governed itself by the moon and disabled the house whenever the sky was cloudy.

“For five years they changed that clock. One ran fast, one slow, one only on Sundays, one during leap year, and one admirable instrument operated perfectly except at night. Every expert declared the preceding expert an impostor, and every bill began with the words ‘To correcting defective installation.’

“Meanwhile the burglars adjusted their engagements to the clock. Sometimes they telephoned to ask whether the alarm was on. Thomas, the butler, generally told them. He said it saved the front door.

“At the end of nine years I made an account. I had paid enough for the alarm to build two houses, furnish three, and endow a modest burglar for life. I had lost silver, furniture, clothing, sleep, servants, sections of the family, and one square yard of coachman. The alarm had frightened every honest person who entered the place, injured four members of the household, summoned the police two hundred times, and had never inconvenienced a burglar except once, when a loose bell fell upon one and he charged me for medical attendance.

“I called in the manager.

“‘Sir,’ I said, ‘your apparatus has not protected me from burglars.’

“He said, ‘No system can do everything.’

“‘It has protected the burglars from me.’

“He said that was an unusual way of putting it.

“‘It has awakened us when they were absent, slept when they were present, admitted them in summer, warmed them in winter, preserved their professional habits, and supplied them with copper wire.’

“He admitted that the copper was of excellent quality.

“I asked what he would allow me for the whole concern.

“He said it had no secondhand value, but, as a personal accommodation, he could exchange it for a dog.

“I consulted my wife. She said a good watchdog would be simpler, cheaper, and more natural. For once we agreed without compromise.

“So I took the dog.

“He barked all the first night at the moon, all the second at the milkman, and all the third at me. On the fourth night burglars entered, fed him our beefsteak, and took the rest of the dining-room.

“Next morning the dog barked at my wife.

“I shot him.

“No, sir, I have no prejudice against modern invention; but after maintaining for nine years a combination of fire, riot, boardinghouse, artillery practice, and charitable institution for burglars, a man inclines toward simplicity.

“Good-by. I get off here.”

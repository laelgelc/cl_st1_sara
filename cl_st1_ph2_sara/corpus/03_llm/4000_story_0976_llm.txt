# The Cup of Mercy

*“Neither repented they of their murders, nor of their sorceries; and the sun had power to scorch men with fire.”*

The sun hung above the African plain like a piercing eye.

From dawn until the long red descent of evening it stared without pity upon the British square, upon the torn earthworks and the dead thorn scrub, upon the motionless guns and the white hospital tents which showed so clearly against the brown waste that every man wondered why the enemy’s marksmen had not riddled them. The canvas roofs kept out the light, but not the heat. Beneath them the air seemed used and burnt; it entered the lungs reluctantly, as though breath itself had become a substance to be fought for.

Flies moved in black clusters upon the bandages. Now and again an orderly drove them away with a folded newspaper, but they returned with a dreadful patience.

Private Ford lay nearest the tent opening, where a narrow blade of brilliance cut across the earthen floor. He had been shot through the side two days before, when the regiment had attempted to seize the low ridge commanding the river. The attempt had failed; the ridge remained in the enemy’s hands; the force had drawn back within its poor defences; and Ford had been told, with the professional circumlocution of the hospital, that he must keep quiet.

He knew what that meant.

The bullet had done its work too thoroughly for surgery, and the heat completed what the bullet had begun. He suffered greatly, but with intervals of curious ease in which his mind, escaping from the body, wandered among faces and scenes belonging to another life.

At one moment he saw the dirty canvas above him; at the next, green English turf, a pavilion, white-clad figures moving beneath a mild sky. Again he was in a room panelled with dark wood, where young men laughed over wine and a fire snapped upon the hearth. Then the laughter became the crackling of flame through dead twigs, and the fire widened until it covered earth and heaven.

He opened his eyes.

Doctor Montagu Durant stood over the next bed. His sleeves were rolled above the elbows, and his thin face, burned almost the colour of leather, had the quiet, level-eyed composure which had made men trust him in lecture-room, hunting-field, and battlefield alike. He had worked since before sunrise. His hands trembled from fatigue when they were idle, but ceased to tremble whenever they touched a wound.

Ford watched him with a painful intentness.

“Doctor,” he said.

Durant did not hear.

“Durant!”

The name, spoken without title, brought the surgeon round sharply. He came to the bedside and looked down.

“Well, Ford?”

The soldier smiled. Fever had sharpened his face until it seemed the face of a much older man, yet there was something in the smile—an insolence half merry, half defensive—which caused Durant to look at him again.

“Didn’t mean to be familiar,” Ford murmured. “Name slipped out. Known a Durant once.”

“Indeed?”

“At the ’Varsity.”

Durant’s glance rested upon him for a moment. Ford spoke like an educated man, though there were many such among the rank and file—men whom drink, debt, women, or the mere restlessness of blood had driven beneath an assumed name into the Army.

“You were at Oxford?”

“For a little while. Didn’t improve the place.”

“You had better not talk.”

“Must talk while I can. Plenty of silence coming, doctor.”

Durant touched his wrist. The pulse was swift and thready.

“You waste your strength.”

“Capital thing to waste when one has no use for it.”

There was the faintest challenge in his tone. Durant drew a packing-case beside the bed and sat down. Outside, beyond the drooping flap, a rifle cracked from the ridge. A second answered it. Nobody in the tent raised his head.

“What was your college?” Durant asked.

Ford shut his eyes.

“No matter. Knew a fellow there called Leonard Rotherby. You knew him too.”

The change in Durant was slight but visible. His fingers closed upon his knee; then the hand relaxed.

“Yes,” he said. “I knew Rotherby.”

“Your friend, wasn’t he?”

“My closest friend once.”

Ford’s lips moved in what might have been a smile.

“You and Rotherby. Always together. They used to say there wasn’t a pair in Oxford to touch you—Durant the steady one and Leo the brilliant one. He could bat a bit.”

“He could.”

“Carried out his bat against Cambridge when the rest went down like ninepins. I heard of it.”

“I was there.”

“Yes. You would have been.”

The surgeon’s face had become remote. Through the stifling tent, through the smell of blood and disinfectant, came for an instant the fragrance of cut grass after rain. He saw Rotherby advancing down the wicket with lifted head; he heard the applause break and gather; he remembered a sun unlike this murderous sun, a sun lying softly upon the green.

And he remembered more.

“Rotherby is dead,” he said.

“So they say.”

Durant looked at him. “What do you mean?”

“I met him after—after the crash. In Australia.”

“You knew him there?”

“Prospecting. We went out together. Odd pair, weren’t we? Private Ford and Mr Leonard Rotherby.”

There was irony in the words, but the effort of speaking had drawn beads of sweat upon Ford’s brow. Durant took a rag, wetted it from the little tin measure beside the bed, and passed it over the dying man’s face. Ford’s eyes followed every movement of the precious moisture.

“Don’t spend it on me,” he whispered.

“It is not enough to matter.”

“Enough always matters when a man’s thirsty.”

From one of the farther beds came a low, continuous moaning. Another patient begged monotonously for his mother. The orderly leaned against a tent pole, asleep on his feet.

Ford resumed, speaking slowly.

“Rotherby had been a bad lot.”

Durant’s expression hardened, though his voice remained even.

“He had done a bad thing.”

“One?”

“One was sufficient.”

“He cheated you.”

“Yes.”

“And married the woman you loved.”

Durant rose.

“I do not care to discuss it.”

“Sit down. For God’s sake, sit down.”

The appeal was so naked that Durant obeyed.

Ford turned his face towards the strip of sun upon the floor. Dust motes swam in it like sparks.

“He borrowed your name,” he said, “upon a bill. Forged it, if we are being plain. You paid, of course. Kept him out of gaol. Then, while you were abroad, he persuaded Ellen Varney that you had ceased to care for her.”

Durant said nothing.

“He married her. Made her miserable. Gambled away her money. Left her.”

“She died,” Durant said.

“Yes. He heard.”

“How?”

“In Australia. News travels even to hell.”

The words seemed to flutter upward and hang beneath the canvas. Ford’s breath caught. Durant bent over him, but the wounded man shook his head impatiently.

“Not yet. I’m not pegging out yet. Rotherby did that in the desert.”

“You said you were with him.”

“I was. Two horses, one pack mule, tools, water tins—everything proper. Then we lost the track. The horses went first. The mule broke a leg. We walked.”

His voice sank, and the tent vanished from his inner vision.

He saw a plain of red sand and stone, immeasurable and empty beneath a white sky. Two men moved across it, stumbling in the heat, casting small, black shadows at their feet. Their tongues were swollen. Their lips had split. At night the stars glittered with the cold cruelty of steel; by day the sun came up like judgment.

“We had one bottle left,” Ford continued. “Enough for one man to live a few hours longer. Not enough for two. Rotherby was done. Ford was stronger.”

“Ford?”

“Myself—the man who called himself Ford.”

“Yes.”

“We argued. Very civilly at first. Two English gentlemen settling which should die. Rotherby said he had to die anyhow. Said there was nobody in the world who would care. Ford had a mother, perhaps, or a sweetheart. Something useful and respectable. So Ford took the bottle.”

Durant’s eyes narrowed.

“And left him?”

“Yes.”

“To die alone?”

“Yes.”

Ford laughed. It was a dry, small sound, dreadful in that place.

“Bad show, wasn’t it? But Rotherby insisted. He was always persuasive. Ford went on and was picked up by a survey party. When they asked about his companion, he told them Leonard Rotherby had died three days’ march east. Quite true, in a manner of speaking.”

Durant was silent for so long that Ford turned his eyes towards him.

“Well?” he demanded. “What do you think of Rotherby?”

“I think he paid heavily.”

“Paid? A man does not pay by suffering. That’s the bankrupt’s notion of morality. He had hurt you. He had killed Ellen as surely as if he had put poison in her glass.”

“Do not speak her name.”

“Why not? The dead don’t mind. It’s the living who are particular.”

Durant’s professional self-command nearly failed him. Yet anger, as he looked upon the wasted body before him, seemed both useless and cruel.

“You are feverish,” he said.

“Perfectly clear. Clearer than I’ve been for years. Tell me—if Rotherby had come back, what would you have done?”

“I cannot say.”

“You can.”

“No man knows what he will do until the hour is upon him.”

“Would you have denounced him?”

“For the forgery? No. That account was closed.”

“Struck him?”

“I wanted to once.”

“And now?”

“Now I am too old.”

Ford considered this with a ghost of amusement.

“Thirty-seven. Patriarch.”

“War adds years.”

“Guilt adds more.”

Another shot sounded. This time the bullet passed through the upper canvas with a vicious whisper. The orderly woke and stared stupidly at the small puncture through which a ray of light now entered.

Durant ordered him to move two beds farther in. When he returned, Ford had not changed position, but an inward struggle showed itself in the muscles of his face.

“Doctor,” he said, “suppose the fellow repented?”

Durant’s mouth tightened.

“Repentance is not a word to be tossed about because a man is dying.”

“No. The padres have rather spoilt it. Suppose he carried the knowledge day after day; suppose he tried drink and couldn’t drown it, work and couldn’t bury it, fighting and couldn’t kill it; suppose every decent thing he saw reminded him what he had ceased to be. Would that count?”

“With whom?”

“With you.”

“I am not his judge.”

“You’re the only judge he ever feared.”

Durant looked quickly at him. Ford’s eyes were shut.

“Why are you telling me this?”

“Promised him.”

“In Australia?”

“Yes.”

“What precisely did he ask you to say?”

“That he had to die anyhow.”

“You have said that before.”

“It was important to him.”

“Why?”

Ford’s fingers plucked at the blanket.

“Because a man may be obliged to kill his own name before he can go on living.”

The surgeon leaned nearer, but at that moment a staff captain appeared at the entrance. His face was grey beneath the dust.

“Durant, the water carts have not come in.”

“I know.”

“They won’t come. Patrol found the drivers dead half a mile south. The carts were burnt.”

Durant rose slowly. “How much remains?”

“For the fighting men, perhaps till midnight. For the hospital—”

He glanced at the rows of wounded.

“Four gallons,” Durant said. “Less.”

“There is water at the river.”

“And the ridge between.”

The captain wiped his mouth with the back of his hand. “Relief may reach us at dawn. We have signalled twice.”

“If it does not?”

“Then we make a push for the river.”

“In daylight?”

“At first light.”

They looked at one another. Both understood. The wounded could not be moved; the healthy men were already faint with thirst; and the enemy held every approach to the water.

“Keep what you have for operations,” said the captain.

“No.”

“That is an order.”

“I shall use it as I consider necessary.”

The captain’s lips twitched.

“Very well. You always were a stubborn devil, Monty.”

He went out into the blaze.

When Durant turned back, Ford was watching him.

“River near?” the soldier asked.

“Half a mile.”

“Enemy between?”

“Yes.”

“Jolly inconvenient.”

“Do not trouble yourself.”

“Wouldn’t dream of it.”

Durant gave him a measured spoonful of water. Ford held it in his mouth before swallowing, as a miser might weigh gold.

“Was she happy at all?” he asked.

“Who?”

“Ellen.”

The doctor stood motionless.

“For a little while, I believe.”

“With him?”

“With the man she thought he was.”

Ford flinched as though the bullet had struck him anew.

“And afterwards?”

“She came to her father’s house. I saw her once before she died.”

“Did she speak of Rotherby?”

“She asked me not to hate him.”

The dying man stared up at the canvas. Something in his expression altered—not relief, but the first incredulous apprehension of mercy.

“Women,” he whispered. “They play the game better than we do.”

Durant drew the blanket over him.

“Rest now.”

“Durant.”

“Yes?”

“If Rotherby were not dead—if by some damned trick he had been near you all this time—”

“Rest.”

“Could you forgive him?”

The question came scarcely louder than breath. Durant stood beneath the infernal roof, with thirsting men around him and death outside; and because the circumstances had burned away the small evasions by which men commonly protect themselves, he answered simply.

“Yes.”

Ford’s eyes closed.

“You’re a brick,” he said. “Always were.”

Night came without coolness.

The sun withdrew, but left its fire in the earth. Above the camp the sky opened, immense and violet, and a moon rose beyond the enemy ridge. The wounded stirred as darkness deepened. Some called for water; some cursed; some lay in the dangerous quiet that precedes death.

Durant moved from bed to bed, allotting spoonfuls, refusing pleas, moistening blackened lips. Each drop seemed to accuse him. When the last hospital tin was half empty, he ordered it hidden from sight.

At ten o’clock he returned to Ford’s place and found the bed vacant.

The blanket lay folded back. A dark stain marked the mattress. Beneath it, Ford’s tunic had been rolled to resemble the rough outline of a body.

“Where is he?” Durant demanded.

The orderly stared.

“Thought he was asleep, sir.”

“You thought—! How long?”

“Couldn’t say, sir. Twenty minutes. Maybe more.”

Durant seized a lantern and went outside. The camp lay in a strained, unnatural stillness. Men crouched behind the earthwork, rifles across their knees. Here and there a pipe glowed, though there was scarcely enough saliva in any mouth to smoke. The moon whitened the plain and drew black bars of shadow from the thorn bushes.

At the southern post the sentry admitted having seen someone crawl out.

“Thought it was one of the scouts, sir.”

“A man in hospital shirt and trousers?”

“He kept low. Challenged him, but he made a sign like he had orders.”

“You allowed him through?”

The sentry looked wretched. “Yes, sir.”

Durant stared towards the ridge. Nothing moved there, but from its rocks came now and then the tiny flash of a rifle, followed by the delayed crack.

Ford could not have crossed fifty yards. Ford could scarcely stand.

They searched as far as the defences permitted. No body was found.

An hour passed.

Then two.

The moon climbed higher, transforming the plain into a country of bone and silver. Thirst conquered discipline. Men whispered of rushing the river at once, of cutting a way through in darkness; but the officers knew that the enemy waited precisely for that desperation.

Shortly after one, the sentry at the southern post fired.

A ragged volley answered from the ridge. Bullets hummed over the parapet. Men sprang to position, and for several minutes the night shattered into flame and noise.

Then, between two bursts of firing, somebody shouted from beyond the wire.

“Hold your fire! Man coming in!”

A figure crawled from the darkness.

At first it seemed less than human—a thing dragging itself by elbows, leaving a broad smear behind. About its shoulders ran a loop of rope, and at the end of the rope, bumping over stones and catching in thorn roots, came two small canvas water bags.

The defenders cheered.

It was not a loud cheer, for their throats were too dry; but it ran along the line like wind through grass.

Two soldiers scrambled out and carried the man in. One water bag had been pierced, yet the other was full, and from the torn one still leaked enough to darken the dust.

“Ford!” cried the orderly.

Durant was already kneeling.

The wounded man had reopened the injury in his side. There was another wound beneath his shoulder and a furrow along his temple. His hands were shredded; his knees had worn through cloth and skin. Yet when Durant raised him, he smiled.

“Didn’t spill much,” he said.

“How did you pass the ridge?”

“Went round.”

“That would add a mile.”

“Always fond of exercise.”

“You mad fool!”

“Water first, doctor. Don’t waste time jawing.”

They carried the bags into the hospital. What followed was almost sacramental. The water was divided into minute portions; lips received it; men who had been groaning fell quiet. One blinded soldier laughed when the cup touched his mouth. Another whispered a prayer. For a little while mercy moved through the tent in an enamel mug.

Durant laid Ford upon the bed.

There was nothing now to be done. He gave him water freely.

Ford swallowed, coughed, and looked puzzled.

“Funny,” he said. “Wanted it so badly out there. Doesn’t seem to matter now.”

“You saved twenty men.”

“Not as many as that.”

“More, perhaps.”

“V. C. sort of business?” Ford asked, with faint mockery.

“If anyone lives to report it.”

“Private Ford, V. C. Poor chap would be embarrassed.”

His gaze passed beyond Durant. The tent roof had become, to him, a pale summer sky.

“Score?” he asked.

Durant bent closer.

“What score?”

“Need seven to win. Wicket devilish sticky. Where’s Monty?”

The name struck Durant with an inexplicable force.

“I am here.”

“No, you’re umpiring. Always too solemn. Ellen’s by the pavilion—blue dress. Told her I’d carry out my bat.”

Durant’s heart stopped, then beat once, heavily.

Ford raised an imaginary bat, and the sardonic hardness went out of his face. For one instant he looked young—reckless, beautiful, and proud.

“Leo made seventy-three,” he murmured. “Durant twelve not out. You and Rotherby—good pair. They said so.”

“Ford,” Durant whispered. “Where did you meet Rotherby in Australia?”

The feverish eyes returned to him.

“Never met him.”

“But you said—”

“He died.”

“Who died?”

“Rotherby. Had to die anyhow. Ford took the water.”

Durant stared at the torn hands, at the altered face, at the mouth he had last seen laughing beneath the Oxford elms fifteen years before. Time, hardship, fever and a clipped moustache had concealed it; but now memory moved across those ruined features, restoring line after line.

“Ford?” he said. “Impossible!”

The man smiled.

“Name on the bottle. Fellow called Ford died near Kalgoorlie. Fever. Lonely business. Rotherby buried him—took his papers. Fair exchange. Rotherby was dead already.”

“Leo!”

Durant’s cry rang through the hospital tent.

The wounded men turned their heads. The orderly paused with the cup in his hand. In that single word the doctor’s long restraint broke: anger, grief, remembered love and the unacknowledged affection of youth came forth together.

He caught the dying man by the shoulders.

“Leo, old chap—look at me!”

Rotherby looked.

There was no longer any need for concealment. He had carried the false name through hunger, labour, drink, obscure campaigns and nameless years; had used it as a punishment and a refuge; had hoped to bury beneath it the gentleman he had betrayed. Yet the abandoned identity returned at the end, not as an accusation, but as a gift from the friend he had most wronged.

“You meant it?” he whispered.

“What?”

“Said you’d forgive him.”

“I meant it. I forgive you—do you hear? Ellen forgave you. I forgive you.”

“Bit late.”

“No. Not late.”

“Water business doesn’t square the account.”

“There is no account.”

Rotherby’s lips trembled. “Always did lie badly, Monty.”

“Be quiet.”

“Good fellow. Jolly good fellow.”

His mind drifted once more. The tent became a pavilion; the groans of the wounded were applause far off; the moonlight at the entrance lay green and level as an English cricket field. He moved down the wicket towards his friend, young again and unashamed.

“Last over,” he said. “Think I can carry out my bat.”

Durant held his hand.

Before dawn the guns of the relief column sounded in the east.

The first report rolled over the plain, and after it came another, nearer. A murmur passed through the camp. Men rose behind the earthworks; officers lifted glasses; the enemy’s fire slackened and then turned towards the approaching force.

Durant scarcely heard.

The grey light entered the tent and touched Rotherby’s face. He had died a few minutes earlier, so quietly that the doctor, watching for each breath, could not tell which one had been the last. There was a smile upon his mouth—not triumphant, for he had ceased to demand victory over the past, but peaceful, as though the passing spirit had discovered that judgment and mercy were not, after all, irreconcilable.

The orderly came to the bedside.

“Relief’s here, sir.”

“Yes.”

“Poor chap held on almost long enough.”

“He held on long enough,” Durant said.

Later, when the wounded had been removed and the dead counted, a young lieutenant asked for particulars.

“Private James Ford, sir. Number 4187. D Company.”

“Cause of death?”

“Gunshot wounds and exhaustion.”

“Any recommendation?”

Durant looked across the river, where soldiers now filled buckets in careless abundance. Water flashed beneath the morning sun, bright, prodigal and innocent.

“Yes,” he said. “Recommendation for the Cross.”

The lieutenant made a note.

But campaigns are confused things, and witnesses are killed, papers misplaced, recommendations abridged by men who never saw the deed. Months afterwards, in England, the public record contained only one obscure line among many:

**4187, Private J. Ford, D Company—died of wounds.**

Thus Leonard Rotherby, once a gentleman, cricketer, forger, betrayer and fugitive, passed from the knowledge of the world under another man’s name.

Only Montagu Durant knew that, before the end, his friend had won back his own.

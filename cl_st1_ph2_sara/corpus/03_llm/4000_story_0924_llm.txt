# The Shadow upon Otsego

On Monday, the sixteenth day of June, in the year of our Lord 1806, a total eclipse of the sun passed over the central portion of the State of New York, and nowhere, perhaps, was its awful magnificence more perfectly displayed than at the little settlement which stood at the foot of Otsego Lake, in latitude about forty-two degrees and forty-two minutes north, and longitude seventy-four degrees and fifty-six minutes west from Greenwich. I was then a youth, lately returned from the sea, and had come to pass a season beneath my father’s roof; and although more than half a century has since flowed away—bearing with it friends, kindred, hopes, and many of those lesser incidents which once appeared too strongly graven upon the memory ever to be effaced—I retain the minutest circumstances of that extraordinary morning with a distinctness which belongs to scarcely any other event of my long life.

Indeed, there are spectacles which the eye beholds, and spectacles which seem to enter the soul. The first grow dim as age advances; the last acquire, by the solemn action of reflection, a deeper color and a more enduring form. Such, to me, was the great eclipse of 1806.

Our village, though possessing some consequence as the seat of justice for a wide and thinly inhabited county, was then little more than a cluster of framed houses, shops, sheds, and stables, arranged about two principal streets near the outlet of the lake. To the north extended Otsego, a sheet of pure and noble water, nearly nine miles in length, enclosed by wooded hills which, in those days, were broken by only a few farms and clearings. From its southern extremity issued the Susquehanna, at first a modest stream, crossed by a wooden bridge and half concealed by alders; though even then we delighted to remember that those quiet waters were destined to travel through forests and valleys, past settlements of which we knew scarcely the names, until they entered the great bay of the Chesapeake.

Eastward and westward rose wooded heights, the nearest slopes spotted with wheat-fields, orchards, cabins, and the blackened stumps which marked the recent conquests of the axe. Beyond these appeared ridge after ridge, blue, softened, and almost etherial in the distance. To one accustomed, as I had lately been, to the naked horizon of the ocean, where the sight travels until lost in its own inability, this surrounding amphitheatre of mountains had at first produced a sense of confinement. Yet affection soon converted the seeming prison into a sanctuary. Each hill became familiar; every reach of shore possessed its own expression; and the lake, now silver, now green, now dark beneath the tempest, seemed a living companion of our household.

For many weeks the approaching eclipse had formed the chief subject of conversation. Almanacs had been consulted until their leaves were worn; calculations, partly understood and confidently repeated, passed from house to house; bits of smoked glass were prepared; and those who possessed telescopes enjoyed a distinction little inferior to that of public office. The learned explained the inclination of the moon’s orbit, the apparent dimensions of the two luminaries, and the narrowness of the belt across which the obscuration would be total. Farmers who cared little for astronomy knew, nevertheless, that their own fields lay within that mysterious path. Women spoke of it while spinning; mechanics disputed over it at their benches; children watched the moon by night, as if expecting to detect her in some secret preparation for the deed.

The preceding evening was one of uncommon serenity. Not a cloud appeared above the western hills when the sun descended, broad and red, beyond their summits; and after his disappearance the whole heavens retained for a considerable time that mild, transparent lustre which belongs to the longest days of the year. Venus shone over the lake with a brightness that caused her reflection to tremble upon the water, while lesser stars came forth one by one, not suddenly, but with the modesty of lights conscious that a greater sovereign had only just withdrawn.

I remember standing at the gate with my eldest sister, looking northward, where the lake lay in deepening shadow.

“To-morrow,” she said, “it will be night at this very hour of morning.”

“Not such a night,” I answered with the authority of one who had seen strange skies at sea. “The stars will appear, but the darkness cannot be like midnight.”

“You speak as though you had already witnessed it.”

“No; but I have seen the sun obscured by storms, and once, near the Banks, we had a noon so black that the binnacle-lamp was lighted.”

My sister smiled. “Then you are prepared for everything.”

Youth is commonly willing to believe itself prepared for everything. I confess that I went to rest with more curiosity than reverence, regarding the coming spectacle as one of those rare natural exhibitions which knowledge enables us to anticipate and courage to observe. I knew the cause. I had read the explanations. I could picture the moon steering her appointed course between the earth and sun, just as one vessel, crossing another at a distance, hides first a sail, then a mast, and at length the hull itself. What was there in this to alarm a reasonable mind?

There lived near us an old Dutch settler named Baltus Van Hoorn, a worthy man, though stubborn in all matters which had not fallen beneath his personal observation. He had hunted wolves, fought in the Revolution, carried wheat on horseback to the Hudson, and survived three wives; accomplishments which, in his judgment, rendered him nearly independent of books. On the Sabbath preceding the eclipse, he had listened impatiently to a young lawyer’s description of total darkness at noontide.

“Dark!” cried Baltus. “You may tell that to boys. The sun is too big to be put out by your little moon.”

“No one says it will be put out,” replied the lawyer. “It will merely be covered from our sight.”

“Then I shall go over the hill,” returned Baltus, “and look at it from the other side.”

This answer was repeated through the village and afforded no small amusement. Before parting from us that evening, the old man declared that he intended to work through the eclipse, “if only to shame the idle.” Yet I observed that he carried away two pieces of smoked glass, carefully wrapped in his handkerchief.

I slept little. Some time before dawn I rose and went to an eastern window. The air possessed that peculiar purity which sometimes precedes a day of excessive heat. A faint whiteness rested above the hills; the stars were yet visible overhead, and one brilliant planet hung low in the east, burning with a tranquil splendor which fixed my attention. I knew it for a world, and reason told me that its light was borrowed from the very sun which, within a few hours, was to be hidden from us. But imagination went farther. I peopled that remote globe with beings who might at that instant be watching our earth pass as a dark speck across their heavens; and from that thought my mind travelled into distances where measurement ceases to convey an idea, until our mountains, lake, continent, and entire planet seemed reduced to a grain floating in the illimitable works of Omnipotence.

Then the rim of the sun rose above the eastern forest. The planet faded; the pale heavens became golden; long shafts of light struck the upper branches of the western woods; and presently the whole village awoke beneath a morning so brilliant and cloudless that every apprehension concerning the weather disappeared.

Breakfast was early and hurried. Even those who affected indifference listened whenever the hour was mentioned. My mother repeatedly cautioned the younger children against looking at the sun without the protection of smoked glass, and one of my brothers had prepared so many fragments that he distributed them among the servants, laborers, and passing strangers. A carpenter who was repairing an outbuilding drove his nails with great expedition, saying that he wished to finish a certain portion before “the darkness stopped his hand.” From the road came the rattle of waggons bringing whole families from distant farms. Women sat upon chairs or feather-beds laid in the vehicles; children crowded between baskets of food; and men walked beside the teams, leading their horses where the descent was steep.

By eight o’clock the village had assumed the appearance of a holiday. Shops were open, but little business was attempted. The blacksmith’s hammer rang for a time with its customary vigor, then became irregular, and ceased. Planes and saws were heard from the joiner’s shop; a loaded cart crossed the bridge; cattle lowed in a neighbouring field; boys shouted along the shore. Yet beneath these familiar noises there existed a perceptible expectancy, as if all sounds were but the restless conversation of persons waiting for a great door to open.

The sun meanwhile climbed a stainless heaven. Not a vapor, not even the thinnest thread of cloud, could be discovered from horizon to horizon. Its rays fell hot upon the roofs, flashed from the windows, and covered the lake with a glare almost painful to the sight. Nature seemed determined to display her sovereign lamp in the fullness of its power before permitting the appointed shadow to touch it.

Our principal place of observation was a rising ground a short distance west of the main street, where the view embraced the lake, the outlet, the village, and much of the surrounding country. A small party had assembled there, among whom were several of my family, the young lawyer, two physicians, a clergyman, and sundry neighbours. A telescope had been fixed upon a stout table, its image being thrown upon white paper for the safety of those who wished to examine the progress of the moon. Watches were compared with much gravity, though they differed by several minutes; and every person who possessed an almanac was ready to defend its prediction against all others.

The first contact was expected shortly after nine.

As the minute approached, conversation lessened. Smoked glasses were raised. We stared until the eyes grew weary and the arms ached. Nothing appeared.

“It will not happen,” said Baltus Van Hoorn, who had arrived in his working clothes, carrying a hoe upon his shoulder. “I always told you so.”

At that instant one of the physicians exclaimed, “There! On the western edge!”

We looked again. A minute indentation—not larger, to appearance, than the mark made by the tooth of a mouse—had touched the brilliant circumference.

“The moon!” cried a child.

The announcement passed from group to group, down the hill and into the street: “It has begun! The moon is on the sun!”

No cannon announcing a victory could have produced a more immediate effect. Men left counters and workshops; women came to doors with infants in their arms; laborers abandoned fences, gardens, and fields. Baltus examined the speck through his glass, lowered it, wiped it carefully, and looked again.

“Well,” he said, “she has nibbled him. But she will never swallow him.”

At first the movement was so slow as scarcely to be detected. The small black notch enlarged, yet the sunlight remained abundant, hot, and triumphant. Had we not known what was occurring, no change in the landscape would have betrayed it. The lake glittered; swallows darted beneath the eaves; bees labored among the blossoms; and the village dogs slept in dusty patches of shade. Even when a sixth part of the solar disc had vanished, day appeared scarcely diminished.

I was struck then, as I have often been since, by the prodigality of light. How vast must be that fountain from which so much can be withdrawn before earth confesses the loss! A lamp darkens at the slightest obstruction; a cloud sends an immediate shadow over a field; but the sun, though visibly invaded and shorn, continued to pour such torrents of radiance upon us that the eye perceived its wound long before the world acknowledged it.

The moon advanced. Her edge, seen through the telescope, was not perfectly smooth, but faintly broken by the inequalities of mountains and valleys upon her surface. This circumstance gave reality to what otherwise might have seemed a dark stain passing across glass. It was a world that moved there—a cold and silent world, without visible sea, forest, dwelling, or living creature; and it was coming, with the certainty of a decree, between us and the source of earthly day.

When perhaps one quarter of the sun was covered, a curious alteration became perceptible in the light. I find it difficult, even now, to name the hue. It was neither the gray of rain nor the yellow of sunset, but a thin, impoverished brilliance, in which every object remained distinct while losing something of its natural expression. The green of the trees appeared harder; white walls took a faint ashen tint; faces seemed pale, though the heat was still considerable. Shadows were sharp, but no longer cheerful.

The crowd, which had increased until the hill and adjoining street were filled, became less noisy. Jests were still heard, though they sounded forced. Mothers called their children nearer. Horses attached to waggons stamped and shook their harness; one animal turned its head repeatedly toward the east, then west, as if seeking the cause of an uneasiness it could not comprehend.

It was at this stage that the county jailor, Mr. Lacey, approached my father and spoke to him apart. The jail stood near the courthouse, a heavy building of timber and stone, with small barred windows. I saw my father’s countenance change as he listened. He then called me and the clergyman.

“There is an unhappy man within,” he said, “who has begged to be allowed a sight of the eclipse. He cannot be brought among the crowd, but Lacey will admit us to his room. Will you come?”

I knew immediately whom he meant.

The prisoner was a schoolmaster named Abel Hart, who had taught in a settlement some twelve miles from the lake. By all accounts he had once been a mild and studious man, of sober habits, attentive to his religious duties, and possessed of more learning than commonly fell to the lot of an instructor in our remote districts. But he had a nervous temper, rendered irritable by ill health and by the incessant disorder of a crowded school. One winter afternoon a boy of eleven, after repeated acts of defiance, had thrown an inkstand and laughed at the master’s rebuke. Hart, transported by sudden passion, seized a heavy ruler and beat the child with a violence that exceeded both correction and reason. The boy was carried home senseless and died on the following day.

No one believed that the master had intended death. No one doubted that he had caused it.

He had made no effort to escape, but had knelt beside the child’s bed, confessed his act, and afterward surrendered himself to the constable. Since his imprisonment he had spoken little, eaten scarcely enough to sustain life, and spent many hours in prayer. The court had not yet pronounced his final sentence; nevertheless, in the common judgment of the settlement, he was already separated from the living. The mother of the dead boy had publicly declared that she forgave him; but human forgiveness, great as it is, cannot restore breath to an empty breast.

We followed the jailor down the hill. As we crossed the street, I looked upward through my glass. Nearly half the sun was gone.

Within the jail the air was close and chill. The noises of the crowd entered faintly through the thick walls, while our footsteps and the turning of keys produced unnatural echoes. We passed one grated door, ascended a narrow stair, and came to a chamber whose single window looked toward the southeast. The aperture was too small to admit a full view of the heavens unless one stood close beneath it.

Hart was seated upon a stool. He rose at our entrance, and the chain fastened to his ankle moved with a sound so slight, yet so expressive, that I remember it more distinctly than many loud cries heard in danger at sea. He was not above thirty years of age; but his hair had begun to whiten about the temples. His face possessed the pallor peculiar to confinement, aggravated by sleeplessness and grief. There was no wildness in his expression. On the contrary, his manner was subdued, composed, and so humble that I felt, in spite of myself, the reverence often inspired by profound suffering.

“I thank you,” he said to the jailor. “I feared the window would not permit me to see it.”

The clergyman asked if he was able to stand.

“I shall stand,” he replied. “It may be the last great sight of creation allowed me.”

Mr. Lacey placed a low bench beneath the opening. Hart mounted it with some difficulty, owing to his fetter, and leaned toward the bars. My father offered him a piece of smoked glass, which he accepted with a trembling hand.

For several moments he gazed without speaking.

“It advances,” said the clergyman gently.

“Yes.”

“Does the spectacle disturb you?”

Hart lowered the glass. “Everything disturbs me now.”

No one answered.

After a pause, he added, “When I was a boy, my mother told me that the sun might be darkened at the command of God. I thought it a tale to frighten children. Later I learned the motions of the heavens and believed knowledge had removed the terror. But knowledge removes nothing. It only tells us by what path the power approaches.”

These words, uttered in that narrow chamber by a man awaiting judgment for bloodshed, affected me more than I was willing to show. My youthful mind had separated natural law from immediate Providence, as though an event ceased to be divine because its hour could be calculated. The prisoner’s remark joined the two. The very precision of the eclipse—its silent obedience to ordinances established before human history—became more awful than any disorder could have been.

The light entering the room continued to weaken. It fell across Hart’s face in a narrow bar, imparting to his skin the hue of death. He clasped both hands around the iron grating and fixed his gaze upward. I could not look upon him without thinking of the child whom those hands had struck; yet I could not hate him. Guilt and misery, justice and compassion, stood so close together that the heart was bewildered.

“I was angry for less than a minute,” he said, though none had questioned him. “Less than a minute—and all the remaining years of my life are inside it.”

My father turned away.

The prisoner continued, “Do you suppose the boy sees this?”

The clergyman answered after some hesitation. “He sees a greater light than ours.”

Hart bowed his head against the bars. His shoulders moved, but he made no sound.

We remained only a short while longer. As we departed, he resumed his upward gaze, pale, fettered, and solitary beneath the waning sun. I saw him once again, months afterward, but it is thus that memory has preserved him: a condemned man at a grated window, while the shadow of another world spread over the earth.

When we emerged from the jail, the transformation outside startled me. During our brief confinement the sun had diminished to a broad crescent, and the whole valley wore a lurid and almost unearthly complexion. It was not evening, for the shadows did not lengthen; it was not storm, for the sky remained perfectly clear; it was not winter, though the warmth had rapidly departed. The light seemed to have been deprived of life while retaining form.

The crowd had grown very still. Hundreds of faces were raised at the same angle, each protected by a darkened glass. It appeared as if one irresistible command had arrested the common occupations of mankind and turned every countenance toward heaven.

On the bridge stood travellers who had halted their horses. Along the lake shore fishermen had drawn in their boats. Servants, mechanics, judges, farmers, merchants, children, and women with babes upon their breasts were united by one expectation. Distinctions which had seemed important an hour earlier vanished. No man possessed greater privilege beneath that advancing shadow than his neighbour. Wealth could purchase no light; authority could delay no motion; courage could not forbid the heart to tremble.

We hastened back to the rising ground. The sun was now more than two-thirds obscured. Through the openings between leaves, its rays cast upon the earth hundreds of little crescents, each a perfect image of the remaining portion of the disc. Children who had earlier run shouting from place to place now watched these strange figures in silence. The poultry had begun to seek their roosts. Swallows disappeared from the air. Cattle gathered near the bars of the fields, lowing uneasily; and a dog belonging to one of our neighbours crept beneath her dress and could not be persuaded to come out.

Baltus Van Hoorn still held his hoe, but his comic resolution had deserted him.

“You are not working,” the lawyer observed.

Baltus did not lower his glass. “There are times,” he said, “when a man may rest.”

At about three quarters, the western and northern heavens assumed a deep leaden tint, while near the sun there remained a pale and sickly brightness. A chill wind passed over us, though scarcely a branch stirred. I felt dew upon the grass beneath my hand. The lake, so dazzling in the morning, became a sheet of dark metal; the farther shores receded; the mountains lost their green and stood in sombre masses, one behind another, like gigantic walls raised around some place of judgment.

A woman near us began to pray aloud. Her husband whispered to her, and she ceased, but several persons uncovered their heads. My own heart beat with violence. I understood the phenomenon; I knew that the sun continued to burn undiminished; I knew that the moon’s passage was innocent of malice and governed by immutable law. Yet such knowledge lay upon the surface of the mind, while beneath it arose feelings older and stronger than philosophy.

The eclipse gathered swiftness. Each time I looked through the glass the crescent was narrower. Conversation now consisted of detached observations, spoken in low voices.

“See the western hill.”

“The hens have gone in.”

“How cold it is!”

“Keep the child beside you.”

“Another star!”

A bright point had appeared near the sun. Presently another became visible. They shone in a sky which was neither that of day nor night, but some extraordinary interval between the two. I had seen stars from the deck of a ship before dawn and after sunset, when sea and heaven blended in magnificent tranquillity; but never had their light seemed so remote, so solemn, or so watchful.

My thoughts flew then to the ocean. I imagined a vessel lying within the same path of darkness, far from land, her crew assembled upon the deck, the sails hanging pale overhead, the compass dim in the binnacle, and the waters blackening from horizon to horizon. What must the shadow seem where no hill limited its approach, where its vast front could be seen rushing across the sea? I know that philosophically I may be wrong in attributing visible motion to what is but a change of light; yet to sense and imagination the darkness truly advanced. It did not merely happen. It came.

The remaining crescent became a thread—then a broken chain of fiery points where sunlight streamed through valleys along the moon’s edge. A murmur ran through the assembly. Some pressed closer to those they loved. My mother drew the youngest child against her. Strong men bent their heads, then looked up again, unable to turn away.

Suddenly, from the darkened woods across the lake, came a clear and melancholy cry.

“Hist!” whispered someone. “The whippoorwill!”

The bird called again, deceived by the supernatural night.

That familiar note, heard at an impossible hour, completed the overthrow of ordinary things. Nature herself appeared bewildered. Day had broken faith with the creatures that trusted it; morning had passed without becoming noon; and all the established habits of life were confounded.

The last thread of sunlight contracted.

“The moon!” cried several voices.

Then the sun was gone.

No description can convey the suddenness with which darkness fell. It was as though an immense curtain, stretched from heaven to earth, had been released at a single stroke. A moment earlier we stood in a ghastly twilight; the next we were beneath a black vault in which the stars burned openly. Around the hidden sun appeared a pale, majestic glory—a crown of silver-white light extending unevenly into the surrounding darkness, delicate as frost and yet of an inconceivable purity. At the horizon, especially toward the south and east, rested a narrow belt of orange and crimson, like the last sunset of the world.

The village disappeared except for faint roofs and patches of wall. The lake could scarcely be distinguished from the hills. A few lamps, hastily kindled in houses and shops, showed as dull sparks, powerless against the universal gloom. The air became cold enough to make us shiver. The dew lay heavy on the grass. Somewhere below, a horse neighed in terror; another answered from across the river. Cattle bellowed. A child sobbed once and was immediately hushed.

But among the people there was, for a time, no articulate speech.

We stood close together, yet each soul seemed alone before the spectacle. The ordinary scale by which man measures his importance was annihilated. The courthouse, the jail, our dwellings, farms, disputes, possessions, and ambitions had sunk into insignificance. Even the mountains appeared small. Above us hung the black body of the moon, an orb made visible only by the light it denied, while beyond it flamed that mysterious crown, too grand for ornament, too pure for fire, the revealed border of an energy on which every living thing depended.

I had known fear in storms. I had heard timbers groan under the blows of the sea, had watched masts bend and sails split, and had seen the faces of sailors when the pumps could scarcely master the water below. Such fear summons action. A rope may be hauled, a spar cut away, a helm shifted, a prayer shouted into the gale. But here action was impossible and absurd. No hand could assist; no skill could avail. We could only behold.

A man near me fell upon his knees. Others followed, not from example, I think, but from the same inward necessity. My father remained erect, though his head was uncovered and bowed. My mother’s lips moved in prayer. Baltus Van Hoorn had dropped his hoe and clasped both hands upon the top of his head, gazing upward with an expression in which terror and astonishment were strangely mingled.

“How long?” someone whispered.

No answer came.

The minutes of totality were few, but they did not resemble ordinary minutes. Time, which commonly passes unnoticed through a succession of little acts, seemed suspended with the light. Each breath became an event. Each sound travelled across the darkness with extraordinary clearness: the creak of harness, the closing of a distant door, the rustle of a frightened bird, the faint movement of the river beneath the bridge.

And then, from the direction of the jail, a voice rose.

It was not loud, but the stillness carried it to us.

“Mercy!”

Whether it was Hart who cried, I cannot affirm upon evidence; yet all who heard believed it to be his voice. The single word passed over the assembly with more power than a sermon. Mercy—for the guilty man; mercy for the dead child’s mother; mercy for all of us, standing upon a little earth beneath forces whose magnitude mocked our pride. The prayer seemed at once personal and universal.

I looked again at the blackened sun. The pale corona streamed around it like an etherial flame, and several reddish points appeared near one edge, vivid and terrible. I knew nothing then of their physical nature. They seemed wounds in the darkness, or signals from that hidden furnace whose least ray could restore the world.

My youthful heart was humbled. I do not mean that I reasoned deliberately concerning human littleness, for reflection belonged to a later hour. Rather, I felt insignificance as one feels cold or pain. The conviction entered through every sense. We were not masters of the earth; we were permitted inhabitants. The sun did not shine because men rose to labor, nor did the heavens move for our convenience. We existed under law, sustained from moment to moment by a beneficence which habit had taught us to forget.

How long the total obscuration lasted I cannot now state with certainty. Our watches were afterward compared, and the learned assigned to it a duration of somewhat more than four minutes; but to me it possessed neither beginning nor measure. It was an interval cut out of the common order of existence.

At length a brilliant point flashed from the western edge of the moon.

No lightning, no cannon, no sudden opening of a door into a blazing room could equal the force of that first returning ray. It darted forth, keen and pure; then widened into a bead of intolerable fire. A cry escaped the multitude—not a shout of triumph, but a broken exclamation of relief and wonder.

“The sun!”

Light poured back upon the mountains. It touched the upper branches first, ran down the slopes, struck the roofs and windows, and spread across the lake in a path of trembling silver. The corona vanished. Stars retired. The orange belt at the horizon dissolved into ordinary day. Faces emerged from darkness, pale and wet with tears.

Many wept openly. Men who would have been ashamed to display emotion under common circumstances embraced their wives and children. One elderly farmer laid his hand upon his son’s shoulder and could not speak. My sister covered her face. My mother kissed the child she held, then turned and clasped my hand with a pressure I can feel even now.

No one laughed at Baltus. He picked up his hoe, looked at it for a moment as if he had never seen the instrument before, and said only, “The books were right.”

The young lawyer, whose wit would ordinarily have seized such an opportunity, made no reply.

Day did not return all at once to its former character. Though the uncovered crescent enlarged rapidly, the light remained cold and strange. Birds began to stir, but their first notes were hesitant. A robin uttered a broken morning song; cocks crowed from several yards, announcing a dawn which had arrived twice in one day; swallows came forth and wheeled confusedly about the chimneys. Cattle moved away from the gates and resumed their grazing, though frequently raising their heads. The whippoorwill called once more, then became silent.

As the warmth increased, the crowd slowly separated. Families descended the hill together, speaking in subdued tones. Waggons were turned homeward. Mechanics returned to their benches, but hammers and planes sounded only at intervals. Shops reopened, though little trade occurred. Those who met in the street stopped to exchange a few words, as survivors of a common peril might do, and each seemed to seek in another’s face confirmation that the world had indeed resumed its accustomed course.

I went with my father to the jail before noon. The sun was then nearly restored, though a small dark portion remained upon its eastern rim. We found Mr. Lacey at the entrance.

“How is the prisoner?” my father asked.

The jailor shook his head. “Quiet.”

“Was it he who cried out?”

“Yes. He had fallen to his knees when the darkness came. I heard him say the boy’s name, and then that word.”

We ascended again. Hart was seated upon the floor beneath the window, his back against the wall. The fetter lay across his ankle; his hands were clasped loosely in his lap. Full daylight now entered the chamber, revealing every rough board, every stain in the plaster, every bar. He appeared more exhausted than before, but also calmer.

“The light has returned,” said the clergyman.

Hart looked toward the window.

“Yes,” he answered. “For you.”

The words chilled me more than the eclipse had done. My father told him that no earthly judgment had yet been completed and that he must not abandon hope.

“I do not abandon it,” Hart replied. “But I dare not choose its form.”

There was dignity in this answer, and true penitence. He did not ask to escape consequence; neither did he refuse the possibility of divine compassion. The clergyman remained with him, while my father and I withdrew.

I learned in later years that Hart’s sentence was not death but long confinement, the court taking account of the absence of murderous intention, his surrender, and his remorse. He survived his imprisonment only a few seasons, dying of a fever before the greater part of his term had expired. The mother of the child visited him once. What passed between them was never publicly known. It is enough that she went in sorrow and came away in tears, and that Hart afterward spoke her name with gratitude.

By early afternoon the moon had entirely quitted the sun. The orb shone once more in complete and apparently inviolable splendor. The heat returned; insects hummed in the meadows; the lake sparkled; and a stranger arriving after the conclusion might have found no visible proof that an extraordinary event had occurred. Yet the inhabitants carried its shadow in their faces throughout the day.

At supper there was less conversation than usual. The children, having recovered from their fright, asked many questions: whether the moon could ever fail to move away, whether people on the moon had seen us, whether the stars had truly come nearer, and whether another eclipse would occur the next morning. Their innocent inquiries gradually restored a gentler spirit to the household. My father explained that the heavenly bodies obeyed laws fixed by their Creator and that confidence, not terror, should arise from such perfect order.

“But why,” asked the youngest, “were we frightened if we knew the sun would come back?”

No one immediately answered.

At last my mother said, “Because knowing a promise and seeing it fulfilled are not the same comfort.”

The remark remained with me. All religion may perhaps be found within it.

That evening, I walked alone to the shore. The sun was descending toward the western hills for the second time, and now no dark body threatened its disc. Its beams lay warm upon the water; insects danced in the golden air; a cow-bell sounded from a distant pasture; and smoke rose quietly from village chimneys. These humble sights, scarcely noticed on the previous day, possessed a sweetness beyond expression. I felt gratitude for common light—for the mere ability to see a stone at my feet, a sail upon the lake, a leaf moving against the sky.

As the sun touched the ridge, I compared its natural setting with the eclipse. Evening had tenderness in it. Shadows lengthened gradually; colors deepened; birds made orderly preparation for rest. Nothing was surprised. The world yielded itself to night with confidence, knowing, as it were, that dawn had been promised. But the darkness at noontide had seemed an interruption of covenant, a sudden withdrawal of favour, a veiling of the Divine countenance.

I know that philosophically such language is inaccurate. The sun was no more withdrawn from us during the eclipse than a friend is destroyed when a curtain passes between his face and ours. The darkness was local, calculable, and brief. At that same instant distant nations stood in common daylight; ships sailed beneath an unobscured heaven; labourers reaped, children played, and forests glowed under the untouched sun. Yet the emotional truth remains. To those within the shadow, the light of the world was taken away; and because it was taken, we first understood its majesty.

Night came gently. Venus appeared again above the lake, in nearly the place where I had seen her on the preceding evening. But I regarded her with altered thoughts. The heavens no longer seemed a tranquil decoration spread over human affairs. They had revealed movement, depth, power, and mystery. Every star became a sun; every dark interval suggested immeasurable space; every familiar object upon earth appeared dependent, temporary, and small.

I was young, and youth is not long faithful to solemn impressions. Within days I laughed again, pursued amusement, disputed upon trifles, and thought myself of considerable importance. Such is our nature. The strongest revelation does not permanently destroy vanity; it only wounds it, leaving a scar which wisdom may afterward examine.

Many years have passed since that June morning. The village has grown; old roads have widened; forests have fallen; houses once considered substantial have disappeared; steamboats have disturbed the quiet waters where we knew only the sail and oar. Of the crowd that stood upon the hill, scarcely one remains. My father and mother have long rested in the earth. My brothers and sisters, the young lawyer, the clergyman, the jailor, poor Hart, and stubborn Baltus have all passed beneath a shadow from which mortal eyes perceive no return.

I too have crossed seas, witnessed battles of the elements, watched lightning descend upon the ocean, and seen hurricanes convert day into darkness. I have stood among mountains whose snowy summits appeared to sustain the heavens; I have looked upon cities illuminated for victory and upon ships burning at night, their flames reflected for miles upon black water. Some of these spectacles were magnificent, some terrible, and some beautiful beyond description. None equalled the eclipse over Otsego.

Its power did not lie in darkness alone, nor in the unusual conduct of birds and beasts, nor in the stars shining at noontide, nor even in the silver crown surrounding the hidden sun. Its grandeur arose from contrast and consciousness—from seeing the familiar universe change while reason declared that its laws remained unbroken; from feeling terror in the presence of perfect order; from standing among one’s family and neighbours, yet knowing each soul to be solitary before Omnipotence.

Most of all, I remember the returning ray.

The darkness humbled us, but the light consoled us. The first taught insignificance; the second taught protection. One revealed power, the other mercy. When that fiery point broke from the moon’s edge and illumination flowed once more in torrents over lake, mountain, roof, field, and human face, it seemed to me not merely that the sun had returned, but that the whole creation had been restored by an act of love.

I offer no argument from this memory. The learned may calculate the paths of worlds; they may name every appearance of the corona, measure every interval, and predict to the second when a shadow shall enter or depart. Such knowledge is noble, and I honour it. But there is another knowledge which comes when the heart is forced to perceive what the understanding has long admitted: that man is little; that power is not his; that light itself is a gift.

On the sixteenth of June, 1806, beneath a cloudless heaven at the foot of Otsego Lake, I received that lesson. Time has weakened my sight, silvered my hair, and removed nearly all who shared the hour; yet the scene remains within me undiminished—the wan landscape, the silent multitude, the frightened animals, the prisoner at his bars, the whippoorwill calling through unnatural night, the black moon, the etherial crown, and then the sudden, blessed, overwhelming light.

Of all the visible demonstrations of divine majesty permitted to my eyes, it was the grandest; of all the lessons addressed to human pride, the most impressive; and of all the memories which age has preserved from youth, the most solemn and sublime.

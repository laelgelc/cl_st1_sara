At twenty-three minutes before six o’clock on the morning of June 17, 1860, Margaret Callaghan issued from the side door of the Bilkins mansion, No. 14 Anchor Street, Rivermouth, and, after locking the door with a caution usually associated with bank directors and conspirators, slipped the key into her reticule.

The sun, which had not yet acquired the impertinent glare of noon, was fingering the brass knockers along Anchor Street and extracting pale sparks from the windows. The dew lay upon the doorsteps like a municipal blessing. A solitary milk-cart creaked at the corner; somewhere behind a blind a clock lisped six before its time; and the Bilkins cat, returning from transactions into which we shall not inquire, paused on the garden wall to regard Margaret with an expression of exhausted depravity.

There was nothing in Margaret’s costume suggestive of concealment. Her dress was light green; her shawl, orange; her bonnet blazed with red artificial roses, among which a yellow bird of no recognized species appeared to have expired in ecstasy. Azure ribbons descended from this aviary, and a pair of magenta gloves completed an effect which might have been detected by a blind man in a fog.

Margaret was forty-two years old, large, honest, warm-hearted, and unable to read anything more difficult than the labels on pickle-jars, which she frequently misinterpreted. For nineteen years she had cooked for the Bilkins family. She knew Mr. Bilkins’s aversion to nutmeg, Mrs. Bilkins’s weakness for burnt sugar, and the exact moral condition of every frying-pan in the kitchen. She had entered the household when Master Teddy was in dresses, had poulticed him through the measles, and had once defended the silver teapot against a peddler whose intentions were probably innocent but whose whiskers were not.

That Margaret should leave the house before breakfast was extraordinary. That she should do so in her Sunday garments was alarming. That she should carry, concealed beneath her shawl, a small bouquet and a pair of white cotton gloves was almost an indictable offense.

The least imaginative reader has already suspected a wedding. We will not insult that reader by prolonging a mystery which Margaret herself preserved for less than four hours.

She hurried down Anchor Street, avoiding the principal thoroughfare and taking instead a succession of narrow lanes whose chimneys seemed to lean over for gossip. Her progress was twice interrupted: first by the necessity of hiding behind a rain-barrel while the baker’s boy passed, and afterward by the discovery that the hem of her green dress had attached itself to a splinter in a fence. It is one of the melancholy provisions of Nature that splinters, door-latches, and umbrella handles invariably declare against elopement.

At the corner of Fishmarket Lane she was joined by Mr. Lawrence O’Rourke.

Larry, as he was called by all who knew him and by several who did not, was a sailor attached in a temporary and somewhat philosophical manner to the brig *Annie Bell*. He was twenty-four years old, broad-shouldered, blue-eyed, curly-haired, and possessed of that sanguine temperament which regards tomorrow as a distant relative under no present obligation to call. A short yellow beard encircled a mouth created for laughter, song, and miscellaneous promises. His blue jacket was ornamented with brass buttons; his trousers were too wide for civic life; and his hat inclined over one eye with nautical independence.

He was eighteen years younger than Margaret.

We state this difference plainly, because figures, however offensive, should not be evaded. Love, like the customs officer, has a disagreeable habit of opening packages which respectable people would prefer to pass unopened. Margaret knew Larry’s age; Larry knew Margaret’s; and Hymen, who has made more curious bargains without consulting either parish registers or mirrors, accepted the contract.

“Ye’re late, Larry,” whispered Margaret.

“Late, is it?” replied he, consulting a silver watch which had stopped at eleven-fifteen on the previous Thursday. “It’s meself that’s been waitin’ since the stars went out.”

“Your watch isn’t going.”

“Then it’s a faithful watch, for I’m not goin’ neither till I’ve married ye.”

This assurance, though logically imperfect, comforted Margaret. They proceeded to the little house of the Reverend Mr. Dobbs, where a sleepy clergyman, a sailor named Donnehugh, and Mrs. Dobbs in a gray wrapper awaited them. The ceremony was brief. Margaret trembled so violently that she dropped one glove, stepped upon the bouquet, and responded at the wrong moment. Larry spoke firmly until called upon to repeat the word “solemnly,” over which he met with disaster. Nevertheless, the ecclesiastical machinery performed its office; and at twelve minutes past six Margaret Callaghan became Margaret O’Rourke.

The bridegroom kissed the bride with enthusiasm. Donnehugh kissed her under the impression that this was customary. Mrs. Dobbs signed the certificate and then permitted Margaret to make a cross beneath her new name. That cross, though badly shaped, contained the entire alphabet of her heart.

“We’ll be as happy as two gulls on a mast,” said Larry.

“Ye’ll keep away from the tavern, Larry?”

“From this blessed minute.”

“And ye’ll come for me decent and sober after I’ve told Mrs. Bilkins?”

“As sober as the minister’s own pump.”

The minister’s pump, if we remember it rightly, leaned considerably to one side; but Margaret had no acquaintance with the object and accepted the comparison.

She returned to Anchor Street alone, carrying the marriage certificate against her bosom. The town was awake now. Shutters rattled upward; chimneys began their black respiration; small boys emerged from mysterious alleys with the air of having been manufactured there overnight. Margaret imagined that every eye detected her crime. A fishwife said “Good morning,” and Margaret nearly confessed. A dog barked, and she dropped her reticule. At the sight of Mr. Bilkins’s bedroom window she felt all the terrors of a felon approaching the scaffold.

Yet she entered undiscovered.

In the kitchen—the penetralia of her former sovereignty—Margaret hid the certificate beneath a pile of unused recipes in the bottom drawer of the dresser. There was little danger of its being found. Mrs. Bilkins never consulted recipes, and Margaret regarded them as a species of fashionable fiction.

Then she tied on her apron and began breakfast.

Guilt may blanch the cheek, unstring the nerves, and cause the human heart to beat against its cage; but it cannot prevent mackerel from burning. Margaret spoiled two. She cut the toast into wedges instead of squares. She put salt in the sugar-bowl and sugar in the salt-cellar. Finally, in preparing coffee, she omitted the coffee.

At half past seven Mr. and Mrs. Bilkins descended.

Mr. Bilkins was a small, mild gentleman whose life had been spent in the importation of West Indian goods and the avoidance of disputes. His wife, on the contrary, was tall, energetic, and armed at all hours with an umbrella, a fan, or a moral principle. She had a Roman nose, quick black eyes, and a fixed suspicion that peddlers were the advance guard of civilization’s collapse. Against them she waged a guerrilla warfare from the front steps, and no vendor of patent polish could approach No. 14 without feeling that history had taken an unfavorable turn.

Mrs. Bilkins lifted the lid of the mackerel dish.

“What has happened to this fish?”

Margaret, standing behind Mr. Bilkins’s chair, turned scarlet.

“It met with a little accident, ma’am.”

“And the toast?”

“Another, ma’am.”

Mrs. Bilkins tasted the coffee.

She set down the cup.

There are domestic crises in which reproach is superfluous. The beverage had the color of coffee, the warmth of coffee, and no further connection with coffee whatever.

“Margaret,” said Mrs. Bilkins, “are you ill?”

“No, ma’am.”

“Have you heard bad news?”

“No, ma’am.”

“Then why are you wearing one magenta glove under your apron?”

Margaret looked down. In her confusion she had retained the left glove of her bridal pair.

“It’s—it’s to keep me hand warm.”

“In June?”

“Yes, ma’am.”

Mrs. Bilkins surveyed her. Margaret’s eyes filled. One tear escaped, rolled from her cheek, and fell into the cream pitcher.

Mr. Bilkins, who had been attempting to drink the coffee by imagining it was something else, pushed back his chair.

“My dear,” he said to his wife, “perhaps Margaret has a headache.”

Margaret seized upon this ailment with gratitude.

“A terrible one, sir.”

“Then go and lie down,” said Mrs. Bilkins, somewhat softened. “Ellen can clear away.”

Margaret retired to the kitchen, where she sat before the cold range, alternately smiling and weeping. She was a married woman. The idea seemed too magnificent for the room. The kettles had not changed; the dresser preserved its wooden indifference; a row of onions regarded her without respect. Yet she was Mrs. Lawrence O’Rourke, with a husband on the sea—or, more accurately, at that moment in the back room of the Harp and Anchor, where he was drinking to his bride with such conscientious repetition that by ten o’clock he had forgotten the comparative advantages of land.

At eleven precisely the knocker on the Bilkins front door delivered three blows of a character to make the windows shudder.

Mrs. Bilkins, who was in the parlor conducting hostilities against a fly, lowered her newspaper.

The knocking came again—one tremendous report, two uncertain taps, and a final scrape, as if the knocker itself repented its association with the visitor.

Ellen opened the door.

On the step stood Larry O’Rourke, supported by Donnehugh. Larry’s hat was gone. His curls shone in the sun; his blue eyes were affectionate but unfocused; his beard appeared more golden than ever; and his sailor’s collar had turned halfway round his neck. With one hand he embraced Donnehugh, with the other a geranium in a pot, which he had somehow acquired and now presented to the world as evidence of honorable intentions.

The total composition—blue cloth, brass buttons, red geranium, yellow beard, and spiritual disorganization—might have been a pre-Raphaelite portrait entitled *Mariner Seeking Domestic Felicity*.

“Me wife,” said Larry.

Ellen stared.

“Your what?”

“Me wife. Tell her Larry’s here.”

“There’s no wife of yours here.”

Larry smiled tolerantly.

“That’s where ye’re mistaken.”

Mrs. Bilkins advanced into the hall, holding her umbrella horizontally.

“What is wanted?”

Larry attempted a bow, found it leading him toward the pavement, and was restored by Donnehugh.

“Madam,” said the latter, “we’re lookin’ for a lady.”

“This is not a boardinghouse.”

“No, ma’am.”

“Nor an employment office.”

“No, ma’am.”

“Nor a place where intoxicated sailors are received.”

Donnehugh looked at Larry with the judicial air of one who is only slightly less intoxicated than the accused.

“He’s not intoxicated, ma’am. He’s overtaken by happiness.”

“Remove him.”

“Me wife,” repeated Larry, addressing the umbrella.

Mrs. Bilkins’s severity wavered. She had a tenderness for sailors, one of her brothers having gone to sea thirty years before and returned only in the form of a parrot, a sea chest, and several contradictory reports.

“My good man,” she said, “where does your wife live?”

“Here.”

“Impossible.”

“I married her this mornin’.”

Mrs. Bilkins turned to Donnehugh.

“Whom did he marry?”

Donnehugh removed his cap and scratched his head, evidently surprised that a point so elementary should require explanation.

“Margaret Callaghan, ma’am.”

The umbrella descended.

“Margaret!”

The name traveled through the hall, crossed the dining room, passed the pantry, and entered the kitchen, where Margaret rose with both hands pressed to her heart.

“Margaret!” called Mrs. Bilkins again.

Margaret came.

We have witnessed, in galleries and illustrated books, various representations of conscience before judgment: kings confronted by prophets, criminals exposed by documents, and Roman senators interrupted in the act of treason. None of them, we hold, exceeded in tragic completeness the figure of Margaret as she appeared at the end of the hall, wearing a floury apron over the green bridal dress, with one magenta glove still upon her left hand.

Larry brightened.

“There she is! There’s me own little wife!”

Mrs. Bilkins turned slowly upon Margaret.

“What does this mean?”

Margaret looked at Larry, at Donnehugh, at the umbrella, and lastly at the floor.

“It means, ma’am,” she said, “that Larry and me was married this mornin’.”

“Married!”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Without a word to me?”

“I was goin’ to tell ye.”

“When?”

“After I got me courage.”

“And you chose—this person?”

Larry placed a hand upon his breast.

“Larry O’Rourke, able seaman, at your service.”

He then saluted, lost his balance, and seated himself abruptly on the door scraper.

Mrs. Bilkins closed her eyes.

“Margaret, you have lived in this house nineteen years.”

“I know it, ma’am.”

“I trusted you.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“You left my house secretly, married a boy nearly half your age, came back as if nothing had happened, and served us hot water for coffee.”

At this final charge Margaret broke down.

“I’m sorry for the coffee, ma’am. I truly am.”

Mr. Bilkins had entered during the examination and now stood behind his wife, making small conciliatory gestures which nobody observed.

“Perhaps,” he suggested, “the young man could be sobered.”

“Not in this house,” said Mrs. Bilkins.

“No, my dear.”

“And Margaret cannot remain here as his wife.”

“No, my dear.”

Margaret untied her apron.

The simple motion altered the whole scene. While she wore it, she belonged to the house, to the range, to the burnt mackerel and the ruined coffee; without it, she was merely a middle-aged woman in a green dress, married to a drunken sailor and facing the world with six dollars and a wedding certificate.

Mrs. Bilkins saw this, and her anger suffered a fracture.

“Have you any place to go?” she asked.

“Yes, ma’am. Larry’s taken a room near the river.”

“Is it respectable?”

Donnehugh coughed.

“’Tis near the river, ma’am,” he repeated, as if proximity to water were a moral classification.

Margaret drew the certificate from her bosom. She had rescued it from the recipe drawer after breakfast.

“I know I’ve done wrong by ye, ma’am. But I couldn’t let him sail away again. He’s got no one in the world but me.”

“Marg’ret,” said Larry from the scraper, with sudden pathos, “ye’re the finest woman between here and Cork.”

Then he attempted to rise, embraced the geranium by mistake, and murmured an apology to it.

Mrs. Bilkins’s lips tightened; whether to suppress wrath or laughter we cannot say. She went upstairs and returned with Margaret’s wages, a small bundle of clothing, and the black shawl which Margaret had worn to church in winter for eleven years.

“You must go,” she said. “I cannot have this confusion in my household.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“But if you are ill—or hungry—you may come to the back door.”

Margaret kissed her hand before Mrs. Bilkins could prevent it. Then she kissed Mr. Bilkins, Ellen, and, in a moment of excessive feeling, the brass doorknob.

Larry was raised by Donnehugh and delivered to his bride.

“Take him away,” said Mrs. Bilkins.

“I will, ma’am.”

Margaret placed Larry’s arm across her shoulders. He was young, strong, and at present as manageable as a mainmast in a storm. Yet she steadied him. Together they descended the steps.

Thus began their wedding journey.

They moved down Anchor Street beneath the inspection of curtains, tradesmen, children, and the dissipated cat. Donnehugh followed with the geranium and Margaret’s bundle. At the corner Larry began singing a ballad concerning a Spanish princess; Margaret silenced him by a method both affectionate and muscular.

Their destination was a tenement in Binnacle Court, where the houses stood shoulder to shoulder as if ashamed of themselves. The room Larry had engaged looked upon a ropewalk, three chimneys, and a triangular section of the river. It contained a bed, two chairs, a cracked basin, and a table whose four legs had never arrived at a political agreement. The wallpaper had abandoned the upper wall and hung in meditative folds. From the ceiling depended a candle in a tin holder—a poor, wavering penny-dip, but a light nevertheless.

Into this palace Margaret brought her husband.

Wealthier lovers have burned sandalwood before Hymen; duchesses have gone to him under canopies; poets have illuminated his altar with stars borrowed, without permission, from heaven. For Margaret and Larry there was only the little candle, the smell of tar from the river, and a geranium with one broken blossom.

Yet when Larry, sobered somewhat by the walk and entirely by her tears, took both her hands and called her his wife, the candle made a brave effort. It gilded the cracked basin, softened the rebellious table, and laid upon Margaret’s green dress a radiance which neither silk nor virtue could have improved.

Outside, Rivermouth laughed, bargained, quarreled, and smelled of fish. Inside, two foolish people began the serious business of being happy.

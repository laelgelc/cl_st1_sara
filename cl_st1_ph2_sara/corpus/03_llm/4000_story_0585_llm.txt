# The Twelfth Day

There are two ways of wintering in the North. One is to make ready for winter; the other is merely to stop travelling.

The men at Caribou Bend stopped travelling.

They had come up the river in September, forty-seven strong, with bacon, beans, flour, coffee, picks, shovels, gold-pans, dice, cheap novels, and a quantity of confidence that would have provisioned a regiment. They found colour in the bars below the bend, and because each man believed the paystreak lay beneath his neighbour’s claim, they built a town where no town had business to be.

Their dwellings were holes in the bank—half cabin, half grave—roofed with poles, moss, frozen earth, and snow. They were warm enough after a fashion, but they had no ventilation. By November the men lived like marmots, breathing the same air over and over, sleeping twelve hours, playing cards ten, and eating during the other two. They boiled beans until the beans became paste; they fried bacon until it resembled strips cut from a miner’s boot; and they baked sour bread heavy enough to serve for window weights, had there been windows.

Captain and Big George lived apart in a spruce cabin on the upper edge of the camp. Neither had held a military commission. Captain had acquired his title on the Yukon because he could steer a poling-boat through bad water and because, in moments of confusion, men obeyed him. Big George was called big for the sufficient reason that he weighed two hundred and forty pounds without his parka and could lift the bow of a loaded Peterborough clear of a gravel bar.

They were sourdoughs. They understood that the North first seduces a man by grandeur, then tests him by detail. A forgotten needle, a wet mitten, a loose snow-shoe thong, or an hour’s laziness might carry farther consequences than bravery displayed at the wrong time.

In October Captain inspected the dugouts and condemned them.

“You fellows need air,” he said. “Cut vents through the roofs. Get outdoors every day. You’ve potatoes enough for a winter if you use them carefully. And when the first sun comes back, send men downriver for fresh meat.”

Benson, who had sold shoes in Ohio before discovering his genius for mining, laughed.

“We aren’t children, Captain.”

“No,” said Captain. “Children go outdoors.”

Big George leaned against the doorpost, vast and furry, and snorted like a sea lion.

“Let ’em be, Cap. They’ve read books. A man what’s read a book on Alaska don’t need air. Air’s for ignorant people.”

The camp resented them. Advice is seldom welcome where it interferes with comfort, and nowhere is conceit more solid than among inexperienced men who have survived their first frost.

The potatoes had been brought from St. Michael at ruinous expense and stored in a common cache. There were twenty-seven sacks. Captain urged that each cabin receive a regular ration, but the committee refused.

“We’ll keep them for an emergency,” said Benson.

“This is the emergency,” Captain answered.

The committee smiled patiently. The sacks remained in the cache until the first week of December, when they vanished.

No lock had been broken. No tracks showed in the new snow. Every cabin was searched, with one exception, and that exception belonged to Klusky.

Klusky lived by himself in a low hut behind the old tailing pile. He repaired watches, traded tobacco, sharpened tools, and lent small quantities of grub at large interest. He was undersized, narrow-chested, clever with his hands, and incapable of forgetting an injury. The camp despised him openly and used him secretly. A man who called Klusky a rat before breakfast might borrow coffee from him before noon.

Because a crowd is brave when cruelty costs nothing, the men played tricks on him. They stuffed snow down his chimney. They hid his tools. Once they dragged him from his bunk and rolled him in a drift because he had refused to cancel a debt. Klusky emerged blue with cold and mad with hatred.

“You laugh,” he said, shaking both fists. “Some day I laugh. I laugh last.”

This delighted the camp. For a week men greeted him by asking when the last laugh would begin.

When the potatoes disappeared, suspicion turned naturally toward him. A dozen men went to his hut, tore up the bunk, sounded the walls, emptied flour on the floor, and split two storage boxes with an axe. They found no potatoes.

Klusky watched the destruction with a grey face.

“You pay for this,” he said.

They laughed and went away.

Captain and George had taken no part in the raid.

“He’s poison mean,” George said afterward, “but there’s folks enough been stirrin’ the poison. I shouldn’t be surprised if he murders somebody.”

“Would you be surprised if he had the potatoes?”

“Nope. But I’d be surprised if them fools found ’em, even if he’d buried ’em in Benson’s bunk.”

Christmas came under a midday twilight. The sun did not rise, though a red glow moved along the southern horizon like fire behind a wall. The men feasted on beans, bacon, dried apples, and whisky. They sang, fought, reconciled, and slept. Frost sealed the roofs. Lamps burned in the foul air. Card games continued night and day.

Scurvy entered quietly.

It did not announce itself with a bugle. A man complained that his knees hurt. Another said an old sprain had returned. A third found his gums tender. They called it rheumatism, frost, laziness, or imagination. Then Lafitte, a French Canadian who had danced on Christmas night, could not rise from his bunk on New Year’s morning.

His legs had swollen. Purple stains showed beneath the skin. His breath was foul, his gums livid and spongy. When Captain pressed a finger into the flesh above the ankle, the dent remained.

“Scurvy,” he said.

The word ran through Caribou Bend and changed nothing.

Fresh food is the enemy of scurvy, and work in the open is its lesser enemy. But the sick men possessed neither. Captain set traps and brought in rabbits, yet forty-seven men could not live on the uncertain catch of one line. Big George shot two ptarmigan and made broth for Lafitte. The patient improved for a day, then worsened.

“Send a party to St. Michael,” Captain told the committee. “Now. Dogs, if you can get them. Men hauling if you can’t. Bring reindeer meat, onions, potatoes—anything fresh.”

“Three hundred miles in winter?” Benson protested.

“Nearer two hundred and forty by the river.”

“Same thing.”

“No,” Captain said. “Sixty miles is never the same thing.”

The committee decided to wait for milder weather.

The weather, having no seat on the committee, remained cold.

One by one the men went down. Their old wounds opened. Scars softened and bled. Teeth loosened. Legs became useless. The air in the dugouts grew thick with smoke, damp wool, unwashed bodies, and the sweet corruption of disease. Men who had boasted of taking fortunes from the earth whimpered when asked to crawl ten feet to the woodpile.

Klusky alone remained active. He went from cabin to cabin, not to help, but to observe. He seemed to fatten upon the camp’s helplessness. When begged for tobacco he asked double price. When asked to carry water he wanted gold dust in advance.

“You laugh before,” he reminded them. “Now laugh.”

Men cursed him from their bunks, but none could rise to strike him.

The disease progressed with that implacable patience by which nature punishes ignorance. Lafitte died in the third week of January. Menard, who had laid ten ounces of dust against his own survival, heard of it and raised himself on one elbow.

“I win that bet,” he said. “I’m strongest man here.”

He fell back and died before the hour was out.

After that, fatalism took the camp. It is a strange thing that men may fight river, frost, fire, and famine, yet submit to sickness because the enemy cannot be struck with an axe. They watched each new bruise upon their bodies as a condemned man watches the building of a scaffold.

Captain and George remained sound. Every day they cut wood, ran their traps, and climbed the ridge until their lungs laboured in the cold. They ate the last of their dried berries sparingly. They had no fresh meat except an occasional rabbit, but exercise and cleaner quarters held the disease off.

Then, one morning, Big George dropped his axe.

He stared at it in the snow.

“Pick it up,” Captain said.

George bent. His knees shook. He straightened slowly, leaving the axe where it lay.

“Cap,” he said, and the humour had gone from his voice, “I reckon that old devil’s put his thumb on me.”

Captain examined him in the cabin. There were purple marks on the calves. The gums were inflamed. An old scar across George’s shoulder, earned years before in a sawmill, had turned soft and red.

“Scurvy,” George said.

Captain did not answer.

“Well, say it. No sense hidin’ a bear after he’s et the bacon.”

“It’s scurvy.”

George sat beside the stove, his huge hands hanging between his knees. Then he grinned.

“I always wanted to be thin.”

Captain stepped outside. The cold seized the moisture in his nostrils, but he did not feel it. Below him, from the chimneys of Caribou Bend, smoke rose through the still air in forty pale columns. It had the tranquillity of a cemetery.

There were no dogs in camp. The last team had gone downriver with the mail before freeze-up. A hand-sled could make the journey, but not if one man broke trail and hauled continuously. Captain needed a second man.

Only Klusky could walk.

He found him chopping kindling behind his hut.

“We leave for St. Michael in an hour,” Captain said.

Klusky looked up. “Ve?”

“You and I.”

“Why I go?”

“Because every other man is sick.”

Klusky smiled. “They die, maybe.”

“George is sick.”

“Big George, too?”

“Yes.”

The smile broadened, then vanished under Captain’s gaze.

“I do not go.”

Captain stepped close. “You go willingly or tied to the sled. Choose.”

Klusky’s eyes shifted toward the axe, but Captain’s hand closed upon the haft first.

“You threatened murder,” Captain said. “Don’t tempt me to prevent it.”

For a moment they stood in the bitter sunlight, the tall man and the small one, breathing smoke. Then Klusky shrugged.

“Oi! I go. But I do not die for them.”

“You haul your share. That is all.”

They loaded a narrow go-devil with blankets, tea, bacon, flour, axe, rifle, ammunition, snow-shoes, and twelve days’ dog feed, though they had no dog. The feed had barter value at villages along the lower river. Every pound was considered, removed, replaced, and considered again. On the trail, luxury is whatever a man cannot pull uphill.

Captain put Tannik, an elderly Native hunter with a crippled foot, in charge of George. Tannik’s family had camped five miles away in the timber, and he alone among them spoke enough English to understand the need.

“Keep the fire,” Captain said. “Broth if you get rabbit. Don’t let him crawl outside.”

Tannik nodded.

George lay in his bunk, already feverish. He looked absurdly enormous beneath the blankets.

“How long?” he asked.

“Twelve days,” Captain said. “If the trail holds.”

“Make it twelve.”

“I’ll make it less.”

“No.” George’s voice hardened. “Don’t promise more’n a man can do. Twelve days. Noon.”

Captain held out his hand. George engulfed it.

“On the twelfth day,” Captain said.

“That’s settled, then.” George winked. “If I’m dead, knock before you come in. I may be practisin’ my harp.”

“You’ll need more practice than twelve days.”

“Likely. I ain’t musical.”

Captain turned away before his face could betray him.

They left at noon.

For the first miles the trail followed the river, where wind had packed the snow hard as chalk. Captain went ahead in the hauling rope; Klusky pushed from behind. The sled whispered over the surface. Frost smoke hung above open places where black water moved beneath a skin of ice.

Sled travel looks simple to the man who has never hauled. He sees a load glide over snow and thinks friction has been abolished. He does not feel the breast-band galling through parka and shirt, nor the backward drag at every rise, nor the wrench when one runner slips from the trail. He does not know the cruelty of stopping, when sweat freezes between cloth and skin, or the greater cruelty of starting again with stiffened muscles. Above all, he does not know that the road never helps. It only resists less in one place than another.

They made twenty-eight miles before camping. Captain chose spruce timber, cleared the snow, cut boughs, and built a fire no larger than necessary. A big fire scorches one side while frost bites the other; then it consumes wood until the traveller becomes servant to his own comfort.

Klusky ate greedily and complained.

“Too far,” he said. “Feet hurt. Shoulder hurt.”

“Everything hurts on the first day.”

“Vy you not stop before?”

“Because George has twelve days.”

The next morning brought wind. Snow streamed over the river in low hissing sheets. They bent into it. Always there was the rope cutting the chest; always there was the sled jerking at the hips; always there was the white bend ahead, promising a change and yielding only another white bend. Their breath crusted their parkas with ice. Their eyelashes froze together. When the trail vanished, Captain sounded the snow ahead with a pole, for overflow lay between ice layers and could soak a man to the knee before he saw the first dark stain.

Near dusk Klusky stumbled into such a place.

Water welled over his moccasin and instantly began freezing.

“Out!” Captain shouted.

Klusky stood stupidly.

“Out, or you lose the foot!”

Captain seized him, flung him upon dry snow, tore off the wet footwear, and rubbed the foot until blood returned. Then he gave him his spare socks.

“You save me,” Klusky muttered.

“I save the trip.”

Klusky’s expression closed.

On the fourth day they reached a deserted fish camp. The cache was empty, but the cabin offered shelter. A bunk stood against one wall, and a rusted stove occupied the centre. Captain cut wood while Klusky melted snow.

They ate, smoked, and lay down fully clothed.

Captain awakened in darkness.

No sound had roused him. It was the absence of a sound—the cessation of Klusky’s breathing—that opened his eyes.

A shape crouched beside the bunk.

Captain rolled as the knife came down. The blade struck the log where his throat had been. He caught Klusky’s wrist with both hands. They went to the floor.

Klusky fought with desperate, cornered fury. He bit, kicked, clawed, twisted, and tried to drive the knife home with the weight of his body. Captain smashed his wrist against the stove. Once. Twice. The knife fell. Klusky snatched for it with the other hand, but Captain’s fist struck his mouth and hurled him backward.

“Stay down!”

Klusky rose.

Captain struck him again. Blood spread over the little man’s chin.

“Stay down!”

This time Klusky remained upon hands and knees, panting.

Captain picked up the knife.

“Why?”

Klusky spat blood. “I hate you.”

“That isn’t enough.”

“You make me go. You order me like dog.”

“I should kill you.”

“Then kill.”

Captain looked at him for a long moment. Outside, wind worried the chinks of the cabin. The stars at the small window glittered like wolves’ eyes.

“No,” he said. “You’ll help bring food back.”

He bound Klusky’s wrists and kept him tied until morning. Thereafter Klusky went ahead, and Captain followed with the rifle across the sled.

They spoke little.

On the fifth day the weather softened. Soft weather in the Arctic is often worse than cold, for it loosens snow and increases labour. The runners sank. The sled dragged like an anchor. They took turns breaking trail, wallowing thigh-deep in places, lifting each snow-shoe high, pressing it forward, sinking, lifting again. Perspiration soaked them. Whenever they stopped, their garments stiffened.

Toward afternoon Klusky began to lag.

“Move,” Captain ordered.

Klusky moved.

An hour later he fell across the trail.

“Get up.”

He rose to his knees and sank again.

Captain dragged him upright by the parka.

“No tricks.”

“Legs,” Klusky gasped. “No strength.”

“You’ve eaten as much as I have.”

“Legs bad.”

Captain thrust him toward the rope. Klusky took three steps and collapsed.

Anger went through Captain hotly. George lay dying while this creature delayed, attempted murder, and now pretended weakness.

Captain struck him with the open hand.

“Up!”

Klusky rolled over. His face, pinched above the dark beard, held not defiance but terror.

“Cannot.”

Captain lifted his arm to strike again and stopped.

There were spots on Klusky’s wrist—purple spots. He pulled back the parka sleeve. The flesh was swollen. At the edge of the mitten an old cut had opened and oozed thin blood.

Scurvy.

The anger left Captain as heat leaves iron in snow. Shame followed it. Klusky had eaten secretly, perhaps, or his body had held out longer by some trick of constitution, but the disease had found him at last.

Captain examined his gums. There was no doubt.

“How long have your legs hurt?”

“Two days.”

“Why didn’t you say?”

“You beat. You say I lazy.”

Captain turned away. He could face cold, river, or a man with a knife more readily than he could face the fact of his own injustice.

They were perhaps one hundred and fifteen miles from St. Michael. Alone and lightly loaded, Captain might reach it in four days. Dragging Klusky, he might take eight. Then came the return. George had given him twelve days.

He made camp in a stand of stunted spruce. Klusky could no longer help. Captain cut poles, spread blankets, boiled tea, and forced bacon broth between the sick man’s teeth.

Through the night Captain considered abandoning him.

It was practical. More than practical—it was necessary. Klusky had attempted murder. He had delayed the journey. He was dying. One useless life lay against George’s life and perhaps the lives of twenty men. The arithmetic was brutal and clear.

At dawn Captain packed the sled.

Klusky watched from the blankets.

“You leave me.”

Captain did not answer.

“Leave. I die anyway.”

Captain tightened a thong.

Klusky laughed weakly. “Captain good man. Good man leaves sick man in snow.”

“I could kill you for what you did.”

“Then kill.”

Captain swung the sled toward the trail.

Behind him Klusky began to whimper—not loudly, and not for mercy. It was the involuntary sound of an animal left in a trap.

Captain walked twenty paces.

He stopped.

Honour is an expensive article in the wilderness. In cities men display it when convenient, polish it with words, and put it away. On the trail it must sometimes be paid for in warmth, flesh, or blood. Yet a man who abandons it to save his life may return alive and still lose the only thing worth bringing back.

Captain returned.

“I won’t leave you alive,” he said. “I promise that much.”

Klusky stared at him.

“You stay till I die?”

“Yes.”

“Even Big George?”

Captain’s jaw tightened. “Even George.”

Something shifted in Klusky’s face, but he turned it toward the snow.

Captain built a platform of boughs and made a shelter. The sixth day passed. Then the seventh.

Klusky worsened rapidly. He could not stand. His gums bled. Old sores appeared upon his body as if time itself were retreating and reopening every injury he had ever suffered. Captain trapped nothing. Their bacon dwindled, and the smell of it sickened the patient.

The weather cleared and turned intensely cold. At night spruce trunks split with pistol cracks. The aurora moved overhead in pale banners, silent and indifferent. Captain sat by the fire, feeding it one stick at a time, while George’s twelve days ran through his mind like sand.

On the eighth morning Klusky called him close.

“You go back.”

“To Caribou Bend?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Potatoes.”

Captain became still.

“What potatoes?”

“All potatoes.”

“You took them?”

Klusky smiled faintly, and for an instant the old malice returned. “They search my house. Break boxes. Call thief. They do not find.”

“Where are they?”

“Old prospect tunnel. Above creek. Under tailing, behind plank.”

Captain seized his shoulder. “You had twenty-seven sacks while men died?”

“They laugh at me.”

“You let Lafitte die. Menard. All the others—”

“They make me dog!” Klusky cried, finding sudden strength. “They kick me, steal, throw me in snow. I say I laugh last. I wait. When they sick, I sell one potato for ounce gold. Then two ounces. Then claim. Everything.”

Captain released him as if touching corruption.

“And you came with me. Why?”

“You make me.”

“You tried to kill me.”

Klusky’s eyes moved away.

“To stop the rescue?”

“Yes.”

“Why confess now?”

The dying man considered this. Hatred, pain, fear, and some belated need to be understood moved across his face.

“Because you stay,” he said at last. “I try kill. You stay. No man stay for me.”

Captain walked beyond the fire. For a time he stood among the trees, fists clenched, looking south, where the trail to St. Michael ran empty beneath the sun.

The potatoes were behind them. Two and a half days away for a man travelling light. Fresh food enough to save the camp. Enough to save George—if George still lived.

This was the eighth day.

“Show me exactly,” Captain said.

Klusky described the old tunnel. It had been opened in autumn, then abandoned after striking frozen muck. He had loosened a roof plank, dug a chamber into the bank, lined it with moss, and hidden the sacks where the earth’s warmth kept them from freezing. The entrance was covered with waste rock and snow.

“Any spoiled?”

“No. I check before ve go.”

Captain drew a map on birch bark and made Klusky correct it twice.

“Now,” Klusky whispered, “you go.”

“I promised.”

“I free you.”

“No.”

“You fool.”

“Perhaps.”

All that day Klusky lingered.

He lingered through the short grey noon. He lingered while the southern sky turned crimson. He lingered while cold blue shadow filled the river valley. Captain watched the pulse flutter in his throat and heard, in every ragged breath, another minute torn from George’s life.

At midnight Klusky still lived.

At dawn of the ninth day, he opened his eyes.

“You hate me?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Good.”

“No,” Captain said after a moment. “Not good.”

Klusky’s gaze wandered to the fire.

“They would die,” he murmured. “Then I have all gold.”

“And what would you buy with it?”

Klusky made no answer.

Near noon he began to speak in broken fragments—prices, claims, insults remembered word for word, plans for a store in a city far south. His hands moved upon the blanket, counting imaginary coins. Then the hands stopped.

Captain leaned close.

“Klusky.”

There was one long sigh.

The little man was dead.

Captain waited a full minute, because death in the cold can counterfeit itself. Then he closed Klusky’s eyes, wrapped the body in a blanket, and covered it with boughs and snow. The ground could not be dug. Stones from the river ice made a low cairn.

It was after noon when Captain turned north.

He left the heavy gear, carrying only rifle, axe, food, one blanket, and a small kettle. He travelled without the sled. His snow-shoes struck the trail with the steady beat of machinery.

At first he counted miles. Then he counted bends. At last he counted nothing.

He moved until darkness, boiled tea, slept two hours beside a fire, and moved again. The cold gnawed through his moccasins. Ice formed inside his clothing from frozen sweat. His shoulders ached where the pack straps cut. Once he broke through overflow and went to the thigh in black water. He flung himself backward, rolled clear, stripped in a temperature that could freeze wet cloth solid in moments, wrung his garments, and ran naked around the fire while they steamed.

Delay. Always delay.

George waited beneath blankets, watching the door.

Or George no longer waited.

Captain forced that thought away. A man cannot travel against death if he carries death in his mind.

He crossed the deserted fish camp at midnight of the tenth day. The stove and bunks invited him. He did not enter. He chewed frozen bacon while walking.

By noon his feet had lost sensation. This was dangerous. Pain is honest; numbness lies. He stopped, removed his moccasins, and found the toes white. He rubbed them with bare hands until they burned like hot iron. The operation cost twenty minutes.

Twenty minutes.

He cursed aloud and went on.

Late on the eleventh day snow began to fall. It came softly, without wind, filling the old trail and smoothing the world. Captain lost the river crossing and wandered half a mile into willow flats. When he discovered the mistake, he stood motionless, overcome by a rage so great it had no object. The snow, the dark, the river, Klusky, the camp, his own body—all were innocent in their fashion. Yet George’s life bled away because of them.

He turned back, found the crossing, and pushed through the night.

At dawn he recognised the split bluff twenty miles below Caribou Bend.

Twenty miles.

The twelfth day had begun.

He discarded the blanket, kettle, extra ammunition, and half the food. The pack became almost nothing. He ran where the trail was firm and stumbled where it was not. His breath rasped. A pain under the breastbone sharpened with each stride. Snow-shoe frames battered his ankles.

The sun, a red edge above the southern horizon, climbed grudgingly.

Nine miles.

Captain’s mind narrowed to a single command: forward. He no longer pictured George. He no longer thought of potatoes or dying men. He lifted one foot, thrust it out, set it down; lifted the other, thrust it out, set it down. Human endurance is not always a flame. Often it is only repetition after hope has burned away.

At last the low ridge appeared.

Caribou Bend lay beyond it.

Captain climbed. Twice he fell to his knees. At the crest he saw the camp.

No one moved.

Snow rounded the dugout roofs. Smoke rose from three chimneys only. The main trail had vanished. The place looked abandoned, a settlement discovered a hundred years after its people had died.

He looked toward his cabin.

No smoke.

The sight struck harder than cold or exhaustion.

“Too late,” he said.

But the potatoes remained.

He turned toward the creek and found the old prospect cut. Klusky’s directions were exact: a black spruce bent over the tailings, a rusted pick head frozen into the muck, three upright stones. Captain tore at the drift with his snow-shoe, then with the axe.

Beneath the snow lay loose gravel.

Beneath the gravel, a plank.

He wrenched it free.

A dark hollow opened. Captain thrust in one arm and touched burlap.

He pulled out a sack.

Potatoes—hard, earthy, unfrozen potatoes.

For a moment he knelt laughing. Then he slung the sack over his shoulder and staggered toward camp.

At the first dugout he kicked open the door. Six men lay inside, the air so foul that the lamp burned dimly.

“Potatoes!” Captain shouted. “Get a pot on!”

Faces turned from bunks. Benson raised his head, his beard stained with blood.

“Captain?”

“Boil them. Eat the skins. Small portions first.”

“Where—”

“Do it!”

He dropped the sack and went out. Men called after him, but he did not hear.

The cabin waited on the rise.

No smoke.

No tracks marked the snow between door and woodpile. Tannik should have kept the fire. Tannik should have come out. The silence said everything.

Captain climbed the slope slowly now. He had spent the last reserve of his body. Noon must be near. The red sun hung just above the hills, though clouds made its position uncertain.

At the door he stopped.

Snow had drifted against it.

He listened.

Nothing.

“Tannik?”

No answer.

He pushed. The door resisted, then opened inward with a scrape.

The cabin was cold. Frost filmed the log walls. Ash lay grey in the stove. Tannik was gone, perhaps to seek help, perhaps dead in one of the dugouts.

Big George lay in the bunk.

Only the outline of his body showed beneath the blankets. One enormous hand hung over the edge, motionless. The face was turned toward the wall.

Captain closed the door.

He had imagined this sight for two hundred miles, yet imagination had been merciful. It had not shown the dead stove, the stiff fingers, the absolute stillness of the room. It had not told him how large a body becomes when the spirit has left it.

He crossed the floor and sank upon the stool.

“George,” he said.

The figure did not move.

Captain bowed his head.

All the miles, the cold, the knife in darkness, the delay beside Klusky, the confession, the race through overflow and snow—everything collapsed into the silence of that bunk. He had kept his promise to a murderer and broken his promise to his friend. Honour had proved merely another name for folly. Twelve days had not been enough.

“George,” he said again, but there was no strength in the word.

From the bunk came a faint rumble.

Captain lifted his head.

The blankets stirred.

Big George rolled over with immense slowness. His face was grey and sunken, his beard wild, his lips cracked. One eye opened.

“You’re late,” he whispered.

Captain stared.

George’s other eye opened.

“Or else that clock’s a liar.”

“You’re alive.”

“Hadn’t noticed.” George swallowed painfully. “What day?”

“The twelfth.”

“What time?”

Captain looked toward the frosted window. A pale band of red shone through it.

“Before noon. I think.”

“Knew you’d come.” George’s eyes closed again. “Told Tannik yesterday he could fetch his family. Said I didn’t need watchin’. Cap’d be here by noon.”

Captain stumbled to the bunk and seized the hanging hand. It was cold, but beneath the skin he felt life—a weak pulse, stubborn as a spark buried in ash.

“I found potatoes,” he said.

George’s lips moved.

“What?”

“Potatoes. Twenty-seven sacks. Klusky hid them.”

George seemed to consider this from a vast distance.

“Always figured he was a promisin’ businessman.”

Captain laughed, though it came out as a broken sound.

“I’ll make you broth. Thin at first. Then mashed potato. There’s enough for everyone.”

“Put bacon in mine.”

“Not yet.”

“Cap, if I’m dyin’, I got rights.”

“You’re not dying.”

“Then I got time to argue.”

Captain tried to rise, but his legs failed. He slipped from the stool to his knees beside the bunk. For twelve days he had ordered his body forward, denied it sleep, warmth, fear, and grief. Now there was no order left to give.

He laid his forehead upon George’s hand.

The tears came suddenly and without restraint. They shook him more violently than cold had done. He wept for the men who had died, for the small bitter man under the cairn, for the foolish camp, for the miles behind him, and most of all for the voice that had spoken from the dead.

Big George’s fingers moved weakly over his hair.

“Aw, now,” he murmured. “Don’t carry on, Cap. You’ll rust my halo.”

Captain laughed and wept together.

Outside, a man crawled from a dugout toward the sack of potatoes. Another followed. Smoke began to rise from a chimney. Then from another. Life returned without grandeur: a match struck, an axe lifted, water heated in a blackened pot. The camp had been saved not by gold, nor knowledge claimed too late, nor strength boasted before a stove, but by buried food, bitter truth, and one exhausted man who had kept two promises when either might have excused the breaking of the other.

The pale sun touched the cabin window.

It was not yet noon.

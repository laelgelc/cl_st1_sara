### EDITOR’S PRELIMINARY NOTE

The following narrative was discovered in the winter of 2841 among the uncatalogued wax-cylinder transcripts held by the Institute of Pre-Republican Industrial Studies at New Glasgow. The cylinder itself has perished, but a phonetic copy, made in 2713 by Assistant Archivist Lenore Hest, survives in Cabinet 44-B of the Workers’ Commonwealth Collection.

Internal evidences place the original recital between the years 2190 and 2210, during the later period of the North Atlantic Industrial Oligarchy. The speaker’s references to the Illiteracy Statutes, the household insignia of Roger Vanderwater, and the administration of factory relief funds agree substantially with court records of the period. His account of the punishment of the overseers Joseph Clancy and Adolph Munster is corroborated, though in guarded language, by the Vanderwater Domestic Chronicle for the year 2197.

The reader should remember that the words “worker” and “slave” were not legally interchangeable. The statute books described the factory population as “hereditary industrial dependents.” Nevertheless, these dependents could not leave their assigned districts, choose their employment, possess books, testify against masters, or educate their children. Their condition was therefore slavery, whatever name the law chose to give it.

The transcript begins after several introductory remarks which the early copyist judged inaudible.

---

Brothers, draw nearer.

Not so near that the sentry beyond the furnace wall may hear us, but near enough that I need not raise my voice. Feed the fire lightly. Smoke betrays a meeting almost as readily as a frightened man. And if any among you hears the iron heel of a patrolman on the stair, cough twice and overturn the little lamp.

There are twenty-three of us here tonight. I have counted you. Twenty-three men and women, and five children who pretend to sleep.

Let the children listen.

The masters say children should hear nothing save the factory bell, the overseer’s order, and the priest’s command to obey. Therefore let them hear everything.

I am going to tell you of an arm.

Yes, brothers, one arm—the arm of Tom Dixon, torn from his body in the looms of Hell’s Bottom. But before I tell you of the arm, I must tell you of the hand that built the Vanderwater throne. The masters begin their histories with their palaces. We begin ours beneath the foundations, among the bones.

You have heard the name Vanderwater. How could you not? It is stamped on the flour sacks, the machine belts, the marriage permits, and the brass tokens with which half this continent buys its bread. The Vanderwaters own mines in the west, grain plains in the south, steel furnaces beside the lakes, and textile pens along the old Atlantic rivers. Their red heron flies above prisons and schools alike. It flies above Hell’s Bottom.

Yet the first Vanderwater was not a Vanderwater.

His name was Bill Vange.

Do not look surprised. I have read this with my own eyes. Not heard it from a drunken carter, not learned it from an overseer’s joke, but read it in the old records, letter after letter, word after word. Bill Vange was a slave.

He was born in a machine district when the industrial masters were still tightening their chains upon the people. In those days the old liberties had not been wholly forgotten. Men remembered that their fathers had voted, spoken, travelled, and gathered without permits. Some could still read. Some possessed hidden books. Rebellion sprang up as grass springs through cracked stone.

Bill Vange was strong. He had broad shoulders and a smooth tongue, and at first the slaves trusted him. He carried messages between the machine wards. He knew where weapons were buried. He sat in councils with men whose names have been scraped from every public monument.

Then he sold them.

For a room above ground, for meat upon Sundays, for the right to strike men who had once called him comrade, Bill Vange betrayed the council. Thirty-seven rebels were taken in one night. Twelve were hanged from the crane towers. The remainder disappeared into the salt pits.

Thereafter the masters found Bill useful.

Whenever a factory rose, Bill went among the workers. He spoke their speech. He knew their signs. He could smell conspiracy as a kennel dog smells blood. At the Brackett Foundry he promised the rebels that the east gate would stand open. They rushed it and met cannon. At the Three Rivers Rising he advised the workers to gather in the square, where cavalry enclosed them. At Fallow Ridge he pointed out the hidden leaders one by one.

The slaves called him Sly Vange.

Later, when he commanded the punishment gangs, they called him Bloody Vange.

The masters called him William.

Remember that, brothers. A master may wash a traitor’s name more readily than a slave may wash soot from his hands.

Bill grew rich. Each rebellion he crushed brought him land, factories, or shares in the companies which owned the factory districts. Men and women who had been his companions were bought by him in batches. He worked them harder than the old masters worked theirs, for the new master is often the most merciless. He fears that mercy will reveal from whence he came.

But Bill could not enter the master class. The law still named him slave-born. He might dine with masters in private, hunt with them, lend money to them, and murder for them, yet their daughters would not dance with his sons.

So Bill served more eagerly.

His eldest son became a district captain. His second invented a punishment frame whereby six workers could be flogged at once. His daughter married the keeper of the Central Registry. In the third generation the family petitioned the Council of Proprietors. They presented lists of services rendered, rebellions suppressed, men executed, women transported, and profits increased.

The petition was granted.

The Vanges were admitted into the master class by Special Decree 88. They purchased an ancient title from a ruined house and altered their name. Vange became Vanderwater. Their slave blood vanished from the official genealogies. A painted ancestor in silver armour appeared upon the palace wall, though no such man had ever lived.

This, too, I have read.

And now the masters say the Vanderwaters were born to rule.

Born! Brothers, consider that word. What is this birth by which one infant comes into the world carrying a whip and another carrying chains? Does the master’s child emerge clothed in silk? Does the slave’s child have soot in its lungs before it breathes? Bill Vange was a slave. His grandchildren became masters because he betrayed enough slaves. That is the sacred beginning of the great Vanderwater house.

Why do I tell you this when I promised you an arm?

Because a tree is known by its roots, and because men who forget how masters were made may believe masters were made by God.

Now I come back to the beginning of my tale.

Roger Vanderwater, seventh of that name, owned Hell’s Bottom in the year of the Red Frost. Hell’s Bottom was a textile factory beside the black river, with seven spinning halls, four weaving halls, two dye houses, a dormitory for unmarried women, and slave pens for families. Its windows were high and narrow. Its roofs shut out daylight, save where the glass was broken in winter.

You know such places.

You know the air full of lint that enters the mouth and grows wet upon the tongue. You know the belts that run without rest, the iron teeth, the oil, the heat, and the floor trembling beneath bare feet. You know how a child may stand beside a machine at dawn and be carried out under a cloth before noon, while the machine loses no more than seven minutes.

Hell’s Bottom was governed by two overseers: Joseph Clancy and Adolph Munster.

Clancy was a lean man with white eyebrows and a voice like a file drawn across iron. Munster was swollen and red. He laughed while punishments were given. The workers called them the Needle and the Bladder, though not where either could hear.

By law—not justice, brothers, but law—a small part of the value produced at Hell’s Bottom was placed in a relief fund. When a worker was crushed, burned, blinded, or made sick by lint, the fund was supposed to provide food during recovery. Widows might receive bread for three months. Orphans under seven might receive milk.

These were rights under the law.

And what is a right to a slave who cannot read the law? It is a door painted upon a wall.

Clancy and Munster stole the fund. They entered false names in the account books and paid the money to themselves. Dead workers were shown as receiving relief for years. Children who had never existed consumed wagonloads of milk upon paper. Meanwhile the true injured starved in their pens.

When workers complained, Clancy said there was no money. When they complained again, Munster showed them the punishment room.

At last the workers prepared a petition to Roger Vanderwater.

This was dangerous. No slave might write without licence, and no gathering of more than six might discuss factory administration. Yet among the workers was one who could read and write.

I shall not tell you his name—not yet.

He had learned his letters from an old woman in the dye house. She traced them with a wet finger upon the black tables: A for air, B for bread, C for chain. When she died, he continued alone. He stole torn labels from freight boxes. He found newspapers used to wrap machine parts. At night he studied words while other men slept.

He wrote the petition.

It did not curse. It did not threaten. It spoke humbly, as slaves must speak when asking for a crumb of what they themselves have earned. It listed the sick, the maimed, and the widowed. It named the payments promised and the payments refused. Forty workers made their marks beneath it.

The petition went to Roger Vanderwater’s palace.

And what did Roger do?

He sent it back to Clancy and Munster.

Perhaps he did not read it. Perhaps he read two lines and felt bored. Perhaps the cream at breakfast was not warm enough. The motives of masters are recorded with great care when those motives excuse them. Their neglect is seldom recorded at all.

Clancy and Munster assembled the petitioners in the lower yard.

“You have complained of our management,” Clancy told them. “We shall improve your understanding of it.”

You know yourselves what form such improvement takes.

The forty were beaten. The old received fewer strokes because it was feared they might die too quickly; the strong received more. The literate slave who had written the words was punished worst of all. Munster wished to know where he had learned his letters. He would not say. They beat his back, broke two ribs, and crushed the fingers of his left hand.

He lived, but something within him had been injured past repair. After that day he coughed blood. He grew thinner. He still worked, for a dying slave is required to work until the hour in which he ceases to be profitable.

Weeks passed.

Then Tom Dixon lost his arm.

Tom worked in the third spinning hall. He was a quiet man, broad in the chest, with a wife and two small sons. One morning a belt began to slip from the upper wheel. Tom called for the line to be stopped. The floor captain refused because the day’s count was already behind.

Tom climbed the frame while it moved.

The belt caught his sleeve. His right arm went between belt and wheel. It did not cut cleanly. The machinery pulled at him, twisted him, and tore the arm from its socket. Only then was the line stopped.

They carried Tom to the factory surgeon. The surgeon burned the wound shut and ordered him returned to his pen. The severed arm was thrown upon a scrap cart.

Tom’s wife applied to the relief fund.

Clancy refused her.

“He injured himself by careless conduct,” said Clancy.

Tom lay white-faced upon a sack filled with straw. His children watched him shake. There was no meat, no medicine, and after three days no bread.

The literate slave visited him at night. He was near death himself. His breath rattled, and when he sat beside Tom, blood darkened his lips.

“Write again,” Tom’s wife begged.

“They will kill you,” Tom said.

The slave looked at him for a long time.

“I have been sorry all my life that I was born,” he replied. “I shall not be sorry to die if I can make them look.”

Those were his words. Remember them.

He wrote another petition with his unbroken hand.

This time he did not trust a guard, a clerk, or a messenger. He knew that words sent by the ordinary road return to slaves as blows. He decided to carry the words himself.

Now Roger Vanderwater possessed, among his many luxuries, a passion for strawberries. Not the small berries grown in common fields, but the great scarlet kind cultivated under glass in the southern farm district. His gardeners warmed the earth with buried pipes throughout the winter. Six workers tended the plants. Two others guarded them from birds. During the season, a tray was sent each morning from the glasshouse to the palace.

The literate slave learned the route.

Before dawn he stole a farm worker’s green coat. He took the empty silver tray from the cart shed and went to the refuse pit where Tom Dixon’s arm had been cast among greasy cloth and broken machine parts.

Brothers, he washed that arm.

Think of him at the black river in the hour before sunrise—a dying man kneeling in the mud, washing another slave’s severed arm. He cleaned the oil and lint from the skin. He closed the stiff fingers around the folded petition and bound them with thread so they would not open.

Then he entered the glasshouse.

He laid strawberries upon the tray, berry after berry, red and shining, until the silver could not be seen. In their midst he placed the arm.

The flesh was white from bloodlessness. The berries were red as the banners above the palace gate. The fingers clutched the paper.

Thus he set out for Paradise.

Yes, Paradise. That was the name Roger Vanderwater gave his palace. Its western bedrooms alone were larger than the family pens of Hell’s Bottom. Water leaped from marble basins. Birds from three continents sang beneath glass. The walls were covered with pictures, though slaves were punished for looking too long at them.

Paradise had been built by thousands who never entered it. Its carpets were woven by crooked children. Its silver came from men dying underground. Its baths were heated by coal carried on bleeding backs. Paradise was the sum of many hells.

At the service gate the guards saw a green farm coat and a tray of strawberries. They waved him through.

At the inner door a steward glanced beneath the cloth. He saw the upper layer of berries but not the arm. The slave was sent along the white corridor to the breakfast chamber.

Roger Vanderwater was in bed.

He had awakened with a headache.

This detail appears in the household record: “His lordship being indisposed by a pain of the head, breakfast was served in the east bedchamber.”

A pain of the head! The record preserves it. Tom Dixon’s arm is not mentioned.

Roger lay among blue cushions while a physician mixed scented water. His wife sat by the window reading a book. Books lay everywhere—upon tables, shelves, chairs—so plentiful that no one guarded them. What is forbidden to us is wasted upon them.

The slave entered carrying the covered tray.

The steward announced the morning strawberries.

Roger made an impatient gesture. “Set them down.”

The slave approached the bed.

His strength was failing. Blood had soaked through the back of the green coat where the old wounds had reopened. Two guards noticed it and moved toward him.

Before they could seize him, he pulled away the cloth.

No one spoke.

There were the strawberries, heaped like jewels. Across them lay Tom Dixon’s arm. The white hand rose from the red fruit, holding the petition as though the arm itself had travelled to accuse its owner’s master.

Roger Vanderwater stared.

His wife dropped her book.

The physician backed against the wall. One guard drew a pistol. The other seized the slave by the neck and forced him to his knees. Still the slave held the tray level. Not one berry fell.

“What is this?” Roger demanded.

“The morning account from Hell’s Bottom,” said the slave.

“Whose arm?”

“Tom Dixon’s.”

“Why is it here?”

“Because his petition could not come by itself.”

Roger looked at the paper in the dead fingers.

“Release him,” he said.

The guard obeyed.

Roger took the petition. He read it once. Then he read it again. The room was so still that the fountain in the courtyard could be heard through the glass.

“Are these charges true?”

The slave answered, “Come and count the empty bowls.”

Perhaps Roger saw then what no written word alone had forced him to see. Perhaps the arm upon his breakfast made Hell’s Bottom real to him for one minute. Masters often require the suffering of a thousand to be arranged as a spectacle before they will interrupt their comfort.

He ordered an investigation.

Clancy and Munster were summoned to Paradise that same day. They arrived in polished boots, believing they had been called to discuss production. They denied everything. The account books, they said, proved the relief fund had been properly administered.

But Roger had clerks who could read the lies better than slaves could. He sent agents to the pens. They found widows without bread, children without milk, and sick workers lying upon bare boards. They found false names, forged receipts, and money transferred into the overseers’ private accounts.

The charges were true.

Roger Vanderwater became furious.

Do not mistake the nature of his fury, brothers. It was not the theft from us that first enraged him. Clancy and Munster had stolen money bearing the Vanderwater seal. They had deceived a master. They had allowed disorder to reduce production. Worst of all, they had carried their corruption into his bedroom upon a silver tray.

Yet fury may sometimes fall in a useful direction.

Clancy was tied to the punishment frame he had employed upon the petitioners. Munster was compelled to administer the strokes. Then Munster was tied down, and Clancy, already bleeding, was made to strike him. When each had fallen, the palace guards continued.

Their property was seized. Their houses were emptied. Their families were expelled from the master quarter. Clancy was sent to the northern quarries. Munster went to the river-dredging gangs. Neither lived two years.

The relief fund was restored. A salaried clerk from the palace kept the books, and injured workers received payments.

For a time.

Remember those words: for a time.

Tom Dixon was given food and medicine. A light task was found for him in the packing room. His children received shoes. The workers said Roger Vanderwater had done justice.

Had he?

One thief had punished two smaller thieves. One master had corrected two servants who had damaged his property. Hell’s Bottom remained. The looms remained. The slave pens remained. Tom Dixon did not receive his arm again. No worker received freedom.

A good master is still a master, even when he loosens the chain one link.

And the literate slave?

Roger questioned him after the investigation. He was astonished that a factory worker could write so clearly. He offered him a place among the household clerks. Later, seeing that the man was dying, Roger promised that his son might be educated for service in the Vanderwater offices.

“You have intelligence,” Roger told him. “It should not be wasted among labourers.”

Do you hear the poison in that praise? Intelligence among slaves is called waste. Intelligence serving masters is called advancement.

The slave returned to Hell’s Bottom. He had no wish to die beneath the painted ancestors of Bloody Vange. His wounds had festered, and his lungs were failing. He lived through the winter, but only barely.

During those months he taught his son.

The boy was young. He came after the bell, sat beside his father’s straw bed, and learned letters scratched in soot upon a board. The father had only scraps: part of a freight list, three pages from a history, a hymn book with its cover torn away, and a punishment notice bearing the seal of Roger Vanderwater.

From these he taught everything he could.

A for arm.

B for bread.

C for chain.

He taught the boy to read the masters’ notices, then their account books, then their histories. He told him of Bill Vange, Sly Vange, Bloody Vange. He explained that the past had not vanished merely because the masters forbade slaves to know it.

“Words remember,” he said. “Men die. Words wait.”

In spring he could no longer rise. The boy asked whether he should accept Roger Vanderwater’s offer. He might enter the palace school. He might become a clerk. In time, if obedient, he might wear a clean coat and command other slaves.

His father answered slowly.

“If you climb by standing on your brothers, you have not escaped the pen. You have only become its gate.”

A few days later he died.

No monument bears his name. The factory register says only: “Male hand, number 7731, removed from roll—deceased.” The household chronicle calls him “the audacious petitioner.” The court record calls him “an unlicensed writer.”

I call him my father.

Yes, brothers. The man who carried Tom Dixon’s arm into Paradise was my father.

I was the boy who learned his letters in soot.

Roger Vanderwater did send for me. I entered the palace school for three months. I ate meat. I slept alone in a room with a window. They taught me figures so that I might count the labour of slaves and the profits of masters.

And I read.

I read more than they intended. I read the old decrees, the factory rolls, the trials of rebels, and the buried histories of the days before hereditary bondage. I found Bill Vange’s petition. I found the names of the thirty-seven he betrayed. I found proof of all my father had told me.

Then I fled.

I could have risen, they say. I could have become an office servant, a district clerk, perhaps even an overseer. Is that rising? If a slave is lifted high enough to hold the whip, does he cease to be a slave? Bill Vange thought so. His descendants still think so.

I chose another road.

I have walked through mine camps, furnace towns, grain districts, and weaving pens. I have told this tale in cellars, drains, abandoned shafts, and beneath the noise of engines. I have taught letters to children and old women. I have hidden pages inside flour barrels. I have copied names which the masters ordered forgotten.

That is why I came to you.

Not to tell you that Roger Vanderwater saved us. He did not.

Not to tell you that cruel overseers are the sole evil. Remove one Clancy and another will put on his boots. Drown one Munster and another will raise the punishment rod. The system breeds them as filth breeds flies.

I came to tell you that words are weapons.

Why do the masters forbid us to read? Is it because letters are delicate things, fit only for their soft hands? No. They fear what letters contain. A written law may be compared with a master’s action. An account may expose theft. A history may prove that what exists today did not always exist, and therefore need not exist tomorrow.

Knowledge gives memory.

Memory gives anger a direction.

Directed anger gives strength.

And strength, brothers, will end slavery.

Learn one letter tonight. Learn another tomorrow. Teach them to your children. Scratch them on walls, trace them in dust, stitch them beneath clothing. Read their laws. Read their ledgers. Read the names of the dead.

Some among you will be caught. Some will be beaten. I do not lie about the road. My father’s broken ribs are in every word I speak.

But remember the tray.

Remember the red strawberries and the white arm.

A severed arm could not work a loom. It could not lift a hammer or hold a child. Yet placed among the master’s luxuries, with a petition in its fingers, it became testimony. It entered where a living slave could scarcely enter. It forced the palace to look.

We must become such testimony—but living, joined together, and stronger than one man’s courage.

Alone, a hand is cut off.

Together, hands may tear down the gate.

The fire is low now. The watch will change before dawn. Go in pairs and leave by different streets. Let the children who pretended to sleep repeat tomorrow what they heard tonight.

And when you speak of my father, do not call him servant. Do not call him petitioner.

Call him comrade.

### EDITOR’S CONCLUDING NOTE

The identity of the narrator remains uncertain. Three persons arrested under the Education Suppression Act of 2204 claimed to be “the son of the Strawberry Petitioner,” possibly as an expression of solidarity rather than literal descent. A fourth, unnamed prisoner executed at Ironhaven in 2211, reportedly recited the phrase “Words remember; men die; words wait” immediately before death.

The relief regulations at Hell’s Bottom remained in force until 2202, when Roger Vanderwater’s successor transferred the fund into the general factory account. Surviving ledgers indicate that payments to injured workers then declined by approximately seventy-eight percent. Hell’s Bottom continued production until its destruction during the General Rising of 2236.

The Vanderwater estates were abolished by the First Commonwealth Decree of 2241. Excavations at the site of Paradise have uncovered portions of the eastern bedchamber, fragments of a silver serving tray, and the household archive from which several details in the foregoing narrative have been confirmed.

No record of Tom Dixon’s later life survives.

The name of the literate worker who carried his arm has not been recovered.

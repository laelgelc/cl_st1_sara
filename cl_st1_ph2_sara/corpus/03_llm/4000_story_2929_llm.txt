## Jedediah Crane Comes Home

The most romantic incident that ever occurred in Amberley came jingling into the village on a hot July afternoon behind a sorrel pony with one white stocking and a deplorable tendency to stumble.

You may not believe this when I add that the chariot of romance was a red tin-pedlar’s wagon, with brooms standing upright behind like a regiment of domestic spears, and that the hero who drove it wore a new checked suit, a pink necktie, and an amethyst pin of such magnitude that no modest bosom could have supported it. But romance, as I have always maintained, is an independent and somewhat perverse spirit. She will pass by a velvet coat and a prancing steed to perch upon a tin boiler or hide herself beneath a checked waistcoat, if the whim takes her.

The driver was Jedediah Crane, returning to Amberley after fifteen years of absence.

Fifteen years earlier Jedediah had gone away to make his fortune. There had been various reports of him in the interval. He had been heard of as a clerk in a dry-goods store in Port Lawrence, as the proprietor of a fruit stand in Kingsport, as agent for a patent churn, and as a partner in an enterprise for manufacturing a washing compound which was warranted to remove any stain except the stain upon the reputation of the company. None of these occupations had produced the fortune originally contemplated.

Jed had not been dishonest or idle. On the contrary, he had worked hard and had invariably dealt more generously with other people than prudence allowed. But he lacked the severe, unromantic qualities necessary to business success. He trusted men who had beautiful eyes, lent money to men who spoke lovingly of their mothers, and extended credit to women with pathetic voices and five children. His enterprises failed with a regularity which would have discouraged a less imaginative person.

Jed was not discouraged. He had acquired a bald spot, a double chin, and a tin wagon; but he still believed that life was rich in romance.

As his wagon came over the hill above Amberley, the village lay below him in the amber haze of afternoon. The road curled between green fields and white houses; the church spire rose above the maples; far off, the river flashed like a loosened ribbon of silver. Amberley had changed very little. Its people would have said that they had improved it, but the distinction is not worth disputing.

Jed drew rein and gazed down with moist eyes.

“Well, Rufus,” he said to the sorrel pony, “there she lays. Amberley. Prettier’n ever, ain’t she? There’s the old academy, and the spire, and—yes, by ginger, there’s Selena Bell’s house on the hill, lookin’ down on everybody same as ever. I s’pose Selena herself is at a window this blessed minute.”

She was.

Selena Bell had seen the red wagon when it was still half a mile away, and had followed its progress from three separate windows. Her house commanded the north road, the south road, the church lane, the railway crossing, and most of her neighbours’ back doors. This comprehensive outlook had done much to form Selena’s character. It was commonly said in Amberley that she had become thin from trying to see in all directions at once.

She stood now behind the parlour curtain, pale eyes narrowed, thin lips compressed, and angular shoulders inclined forward with the intensity of a bird of prey.

“Tin pedlar,” she said aloud. “New one.”

Then the wagon came near enough for her to see the driver.

“Jedediah Crane!”

The words were not an exclamation so much as an indictment.

Jed, unconscious of having been identified and condemned, drove on. The tins hanging from the wagon clinked in the sunshine. Pans, cups, strainers, ladles, and polished covers swung and flashed around him until the humble vehicle looked like a small planetary system whose heavenly bodies had been designed for kitchens. It was an absurd equipage. It was also, though Selena would have denied this under oath, rather magnificent.

“Get up, my nag, get up,” said Jed. “We ain’t callin’ on Selena first. No, sirree. There’s some folks a man likes to meet gradual, and there’s some he can postpone without injury to his immortal soul.”

He turned into a shaded lane leading westward from the village.

At the end of that lane stood the Adams farm.

The house was white, with green shutters and a deep porch over which honeysuckle rioted in fragrant disorder. Scarlet geraniums burned in boxes along the railing. Beyond the barns the hayfields rolled away in sunny slopes, and behind the house spread an orchard already heavy with green fruit. The place had an air of prosperity without ostentation. Everything was well kept, from the silver milk pans to the lilac hedge, and this was because Mattie Adams managed it.

Jed’s hands tightened on the reins.

“Rufus,” he said solemnly, “we’ve come to the most romantic sitooation of our career. I don’t deny I’m nervous. A man may face bankruptcy, lightning, and a patent-medicine drummer without turnin’ a hair, but when he comes to call on the woman he’s loved for twenty years—fifteen of ’em at a respectful distance—he is liable to feel his knees ain’t dependable. Get up, my nag, get up.”

Rufus got up in the sense that he continued moving.

Mattie was in the garden gathering currants. She straightened when she heard the wagon, pushed back her sunbonnet, and looked across the hedge. At forty, Mattie Adams was not a beauty according to the severe standards which require a woman to resemble a lily, a rose, or a wax doll. She was brown-haired, brown-eyed, wholesome, and comfortable to look upon. Her face had placid lines, but there was strength beneath its placidity. She had spent fifteen years managing hired men, settling accounts, tending an invalid father until his death, and resisting Selena. No weak woman could have accomplished the last item.

Mattie saw the checked suit first, then the pink necktie, then Jed’s face.

The bowl of currants slipped from her hand.

“Jed!”

“Mattie!”

The sorrel stopped without being asked. Perhaps Rufus had some perception of dramatic values; more likely he had discovered a patch of clover beside the gate.

Jed climbed down, caught his foot in the wagon step, recovered himself with a flourish intended to suggest that he had meant to do it, and stood before her.

“Well, Mattie,” he said.

“Well, Jed.”

They shook hands.

There are meetings after long separations which produce eloquence, tears, or embraces. This one produced silence. The honeysuckle smelled sweetly; a bee blundered among the geraniums; one of the pans on the wagon turned slowly and sent a wandering flash of light across Mattie’s face.

“You hain’t changed a mite,” said Jed at last.

Mattie smiled. “I’ve changed fifteen years’ worth.”

“Not to me. You look just as you did the day I went away.”

This was not literally true, and both knew it. Mattie had been slim then, and Jed had possessed hair. But truth in a romantic statement does not always depend upon facts.

“You’ve changed some,” she said.

Jed put a hand involuntarily to his bald spot. “I s’pose I have. Been wearin’ away at the top and spreadin’ out lower down. But I’m hearty. I always say health is a fortune.”

“Did you make the other kind?”

“Well—” Jed glanced at the wagon. “Not so’s anybody would notice. I’ve had experience, Mattie. Experience is better’n money, they tell me, though I’ve observed the folks who tell you so mostly have money too. At present I’m travelin’ in tin.”

“I see.”

“Good line. Everybody needs tinware. Trouble is, they don’t always know it till I explain. I’ve got some elegant milk strainers.”

“Then you’d better come in and show me.”

Jed’s heart gave a leap that no milk strainer had ever inspired before.

He remained to supper. Mattie bought two strainers, although she already possessed four; a cake tin, though her cake tins were the pride of Amberley; and a dipper of such inconvenient depth that it was useful only for wells not yet invented. After supper they sat on the porch while the shadows lengthened over the fields.

They talked at first of safe things—old neighbours, deaths, marriages, the price of hay, the railway extension. Then they approached more dangerous memories, circled them, and drew away.

Jed learned that Mattie’s father had been dead five years, that her mother had died before him, and that she ran the farm with the aid of a hired man and his son. He had known already that Mattie had never married. He had learned that fact in every place to which his wanderings took him, because there was always someone who knew someone in Amberley, and Jed had a genius for guiding conversation toward the Adams family.

Mattie learned that Jed had never married either.

“Never found the right one,” he said lightly.

Her eyes rested on the darkening orchard.

“Perhaps you didn’t look in the right place.”

Jed thought this a curious remark, but dared not interpret it favourably. Jed was determined not to “persoom.”

At nine o’clock he rose.

“I must be joggin’. I’m stoppin’ at Mrs. Crewe’s boarding-house. Reckon I’ll be round Amberley a spell, workin’ the district.”

“You’ll come again,” said Mattie.

It was not quite a question.

“If you don’t mind.”

“I don’t mind.”

Jed drove down the lane beneath a sky where one star had come out over the church spire.

“Well, Rufus,” he said, “that was romantic. There ain’t another word for it. She asked me to come again. Course, she’s kind to everybody. Always was. She’d ask a book agent back if she thought his feelings were tender. We mustn’t persoom. But she hasn’t changed. No, sir. Not a mite.”

At the lane’s end he encountered Selena Bell.

Selena had not been waiting for him. She informed herself of this fact several times while standing beside the road in a bonnet and shawl at half-past nine. She had merely come out to inspect her gate latch by starlight.

“Good evening, Selena,” said Jed cheerfully.

“Jedediah Crane.”

“You recognized me, then.”

“I should hope my memory has not failed to that extent. So you are selling tinware.”

“Yes. Neat, useful, and durable. Can I interest you in a colander?”

“No.”

“Pie plate?”

“No.”

“Nutmeg grater?”

“No.”

Selena looked at him as if he were himself a household article of dubious utility.

“You have been at Mattie’s.”

“Had supper there.”

“So I supposed.”

“Mattie’s cookin’ is as good as ever.”

“Matilda is too hospitable for her own good.”

Jed’s face remained pleasant, but something in it altered.

“You needn’t worry. I ain’t likely to eat her out of house and home in one evening.”

“I did not mean food.”

“No?”

“No. Good night, Jedediah.”

“Good night, Selena. Sure you don’t want that nutmeg grater?”

Selena swept away. Jed watched her go.

“Same Selena,” he told Rufus. “Time’s been respectful to her. Hasn’t touched her disposition at all.”

To understand Selena’s concern—and Mattie’s pleasure in disregarding it—we must go back fifteen years.

In those days Jed had been a lanky young man with an abundance of brown hair, a gift for extravagant hopes, and four hundred dollars inherited from an uncle. Mattie was twenty-five, shy without being timid, and very much in love with him. Jed was equally in love with her. Neither had said so in plain language, but all Amberley knew it, which in Amberley was generally considered more binding than a declaration.

Jed planned to purchase a share in a local feed business and marry Mattie in the autumn. Unfortunately, Selena intervened.

Selena was Mattie’s elder sister. She had married Tobias Bell at nineteen, against family advice, and had thereby acquired an unshakable conviction that no younger woman could choose a husband wisely without her guidance. Tobias, a mild man who found peace chiefly in the barn, had died early. Selena returned to Amberley with a little money, a great deal of leisure, and a sense of duty sharpened by widowhood.

She discovered that Jed’s proposed business partner had debts. This was true. She discovered that Jed had no practical judgment. This was partly true. She then announced that he intended to marry Mattie for the Adams property. This was not true at all, but Selena’s manner lent it an appearance of documentary proof.

Mr. Adams, anxious about his daughter and impressed by Selena’s certainty, told Jed that he must prove himself before speaking of marriage. Jed, proud, hurt, and romantic, accepted the condition in its most heroic form. He would go away. He would make his fortune. He would return and lay it at Mattie’s feet, probably in gold.

Mattie begged him not to go.

“You don’t have to prove anything to me,” she said, standing with him beneath the old apple tree.

“I have to prove it to them.”

“What do they matter?”

“I won’t have folks say I married you for your money.”

“They will say something, whatever we do.”

But Jed was twenty-five, and at twenty-five a man will sometimes sacrifice his happiness rather than permit an unworthy person to misunderstand him. Older men know that unworthy persons misunderstand as naturally as hens scratch.

“I’ll come back when I’ve made good,” he vowed.

Mattie waited.

At first Jed wrote every week. Then the fruit stand failed, and his letters became less frequent because he could not bear to report failure. Mattie wrote faithfully, but Selena, who collected the mail for the household during Mr. Adams’s illness, contrived to mislay two letters and delay three. I do not say she did this deliberately. Selena always maintained that she had not; but the letters of people she disapproved of possessed a remarkable tendency to slip behind clocks and into wrong coat pockets.

Misunderstanding did the rest. Jed thought Mattie had grown tired of waiting. Mattie thought Jed had forgotten. Pride, duty, illness, distance, and years built up between them.

Now, after fifteen years, Jed came every Thursday.

Thursday was the day his route brought him through the western farms. He reached Mattie’s at four, stayed to supper, and left when the first stars appeared. Each week he announced to Rufus that it would be his last visit for some time.

“We’re makin’ ourselves too frequent,” he would say. “A tin pedlar has got to preserve his dignity. Besides, Mattie only buys things to be obligin’. Last week she took a muffin ring, and I don’t believe she’s made muffins in her life. We won’t go next Thursday. Get up, my nag, get up.”

The next Thursday found him turning into the lane.

Mattie’s collection of tinware grew alarming. She bought a tea caddy, a flour scoop, three jelly moulds, a dishpan, six cups, and an apparatus which Jed believed to be an asparagus cooker, although asparagus, as Mattie pointed out, had not been grown on the farm since 1898. Jed reduced the price.

Selena watched.

Her windows looked toward the Adams lane, and Jed’s wagon was visible from the upper east bedroom for eleven minutes, from the landing window for four, and from the kitchen for the final bend. Selena knew exactly when he arrived and departed. She also knew, or professed to know, how many suppers he ate, how many times Mattie walked to the gate with him, and how often he carried packages into the house.

In August she came to remonstrate.

Mattie was preserving plums. Sunlight filled the kitchen, shining on copper kettles, blue bowls, and a long bright regiment of sealed jars. Selena sat stiffly beside the table.

“Matilda Adams, what do you suppose people are saying?”

“Several things, I imagine. They always are.”

“About Jedediah Crane.”

Mattie lifted a plum from the syrup with a wooden spoon. “What about him?”

“That he is courting you.”

“Is he?”

“Mattie Adams!”

“I wondered, because he hasn’t mentioned it.”

Selena’s lips tightened. “You cannot be so blind. He comes here every week.”

“He has a route.”

“He stays to supper.”

“He likes my biscuits.”

“He walks with you in the orchard.”

“He likes apples too.”

“Matilda!”

Mattie laughed. She had not laughed at Selena in years, and the experience was pleasant.

Selena leaned closer. “He is after the farm.”

The laughter went out of Mattie’s eyes.

“Don’t say that again.”

“My duty—”

“Your duty doesn’t include insulting my friends.”

“I warned Father years ago.”

“And between you, you sent Jed away.”

“He went because he had no prospects.”

“He went because he was proud. You knew how to hurt his pride.”

“If he cared so much, why did he not return sooner?”

Mattie had asked herself that question many times, and because it hurt, she answered sharply.

“Perhaps he was waiting until he had something worth returning with.”

Selena glanced toward the window. The red wagon was not there, but its image appeared plainly in her expression.

“And this is what he considers worth returning with?”

“Maybe,” said Mattie, “he found out that what a man comes back with isn’t as important as whether he comes back.”

Selena stared. Mattie went on filling jars.

At first Mattie had encouraged Jed’s visits partly because Selena disapproved. There is no stimulant to a placid woman’s independence like an elder sister who assumes that it does not exist. If Selena had welcomed Jed, Mattie might have treated him with cautious reserve. Because Selena watched the road, Mattie walked Jed to the gate. Because Selena sneered at tinware, Mattie bought it. Because Selena predicted disaster, Mattie wore her blue muslin on Thursdays.

But defiance could not account for everything.

Mattie began to listen for the distant clinking of the wagon. Thursday took on a brightness not shared by the other days. She saved bits of news to tell Jed; she made the spice cake he liked; she found herself smiling when he related, with solemn elaboration, the moral deficiencies of Rufus.

Jed was not the handsome young man who had gone away. Yet the changes in him touched her. His bald spot looked vulnerable in the sunshine. His checked suit was too loud and his pink tie too hopeful, but his eyes were still kind. He still gave away more than he sold. He still believed every child should receive a tin whistle at half price, every tired farmer’s wife deserved extra clothespins, and every sunset was worth stopping to admire even if business suffered.

His failures, which had once seemed tragic, now appeared merely human. He had not made a fortune. Mattie had made one, or as near to a fortune as an Amberley farm afforded. It occurred to her that Providence, which is sometimes practical, may not have intended both of them to do the same work.

Jed, meanwhile, was increasingly happy and increasingly miserable.

He loved Mattie. He had always loved her. Time had not cured him; it had merely taught him to carry the ailment comfortably. Now every visit renewed the old hope, but his poverty stood before him like a moral signpost.

“She owns two hundred acres, Rufus,” he said one September night as they drove between fields silvered by moonlight. “Two hundred, not countin’ the wood lot. Fine cattle. Best orchard in the county. And what’ve I got? You, the wagon, and forty-three dollars after expenses. Not that I’m depreciatin’ you. You’re a worthy animal in your way. But you ain’t a basis for matrimony.”

Rufus shook his head, jingling the harness.

“Exactly. If I asked Mattie now, Selena would say I’d come back for the property. The whole village might say it. Mattie might think it.”

Rufus stumbled.

“That’s my own opinion,” said Jed gloomily. “No persoomin’. We’ll be her friend. Friendship is a noble and romantic sentiment. We’ll come Thursdays and be content.”

He was not content.

September mellowed into October. The roads were edged with asters, purple and white; maples burned scarlet around the village; the Adams orchard became a revel of russet, gold, and dark red fruit. The air smelled of leaves, smoke, and ripening apples.

Jed helped Mattie gather the late russets one Thursday afternoon. Rufus grazed beyond the fence, while the red wagon stood under a maple and caught falling leaves in its pans.

“You remember this tree?” Mattie asked.

Jed looked at the old apple tree beneath which they had parted.

“I remember.”

“You said you would come back rich.”

“So I did.”

“You came back.”

“But not rich.”

Mattie placed an apple in her basket. “Some promises are half foolish to begin with.”

“A promise is a promise.”

“Not if it asks the wrong thing.”

Jed looked at her, startled by the quiet emphasis in her voice. For one breathtaking instant he thought she might mean that she had waited, that she would have taken him poor then and would take him poor now.

But the instant passed. Mattie was mistress of the farm. She was kind, hospitable, and secure. He was a middle-aged pedlar in a checked suit.

He lifted the basket.

“Better get these in before supper.”

Mattie watched him carry it away and wondered if a man could possibly be as blind as Jed appeared. Then she reflected that she had once known him to search ten minutes for a hat he was wearing, and admitted the possibility.

Selena’s attacks became more frequent as autumn advanced.

“Has he proposed yet?” she demanded one morning.

“No.”

“He will.”

“You sound certain.”

“I know men.”

Mattie forbore to ask how many men Selena had known well enough to justify this comprehensive statement.

“He is waiting for the proper moment,” Selena continued. “No doubt he will choose a moonlight evening and say he has never forgotten you.”

“That would be romantic.”

“Romantic! At your age!”

“Does romance have an age limit?”

“Matilda Adams, you are forty.”

“So I’ve been told. Jed is forty too.”

“He is forty-one.”

“Then perhaps he is too old for romance, and I am not.”

Selena rose with the air of one withdrawing from a world where arithmetic had been wilfully corrupted.

Yet her words left a sting. Mattie did not believe Jed wanted the farm. She knew his pride too well. But she began to fear that pride would keep him silent forever. His eyes sometimes told her what his tongue would not. At other times she wondered whether she imagined even that.

It is easy for the onlooker to say, “Why did she not speak?” The onlooker is always brave in another person’s love affair. Mattie could manage a farm, dismiss an incompetent hand, bargain over cattle, and contradict Selena. But to offer her heart to a man who might value only her friendship was another matter.

Then, late in November, Jed announced that he was going away.

He told her after supper. Outside, rain whispered against the windows, and the bare branches scraped the eaves. The bright kitchen seemed especially warm, with a fire glowing in the range and Mattie’s yellow cat asleep beneath it.

“My route’s about finished,” Jed said, turning his cup between his hands. “Folks have all the tin they need for the winter. More’n they need, some of ’em.”

Mattie glanced toward the pantry, where her acquisitions occupied three shelves.

“Where will you go?”

“I’ve had an offer from a fellow in Millford. He’s startin’ an agency for a new cream separator. Says there’s money in it.”

“Do you believe him?”

Jed considered. “Not entirely. That’s why I think we might get along.”

“When are you leaving?”

“Next week. I’ll make one last round, then head east.”

Mattie folded the corner of her apron.

“For how long?”

“Hard to say. Might be a year. Might settle there if the sitooation develops.”

“I see.”

Jed waited desperately for her to say, “Don’t go.” Mattie waited desperately for him to give some indication that he wanted to stay.

The cat slept on.

“Well,” said Mattie at last, “I hope you do well.”

“Thank you.”

“You deserve to.”

“That’s kind of you.”

They spoke of the weather. When Jed left, Mattie did not walk to the gate because of the rain. He drove away feeling that he had received his answer.

“She never cared, Rufus,” he said as the wagon splashed down the dark lane. “Not that way. She’s our friend, and a true friend is a rare jewel. We got to be grateful. Millford may be full of romantic possibilities. Get up, my nag, get up.”

Rufus, who disliked rain and cream separators with equal foresight, refused to increase his pace.

Selena arrived at the Adams farm next afternoon.

She knew Jed was leaving. Mrs. Crewe had told the grocer’s wife, who had told the dressmaker, who had told Selena before breakfast. Selena came with a basket over her arm and triumph thinly disguised as concern.

“So Jedediah is going away again.”

Mattie continued sorting apples.

“Yes.”

“I cannot say I am surprised.”

“No?”

“Certainly not. A rolling stone gathers no moss.”

“Jed never cared for moss.”

Selena ignored this.

“I hope you have not allowed yourself to be foolish.”

Mattie placed a russet in the barrel.

“In what way?”

“People have noticed how often he came here. It is humiliating enough that he made use of your hospitality without—”

“He did not make use of it.”

“He has eaten twenty suppers under your roof.”

“Twenty-two.”

Selena’s pale eyes sharpened. “You counted?”

“I know how many Thursdays there have been.”

“And now he is off without a thought for you.”

Mattie’s cheeks reddened, but her voice remained level.

“You don’t know what thoughts he has.”

“If they were honourable thoughts, he would have spoken.”

“They are honourable. That may be why he didn’t.”

Selena gave a short laugh.

“Really, Matilda, you need not defend him now. Everyone sees how matters stand. He came hoping to secure the farm, discovered you would not have him, and decided to seek easier prospects.”

The apple in Mattie’s hand struck the bottom of the barrel with a thump.

“Jed never wanted the farm.”

“Then why did he come?”

“To see me.”

“And why is he leaving?”

Mattie looked at her sister.

Selena should have stopped. Any person with less devotion to the final word would have stopped. But Selena had pursued conclusions over more dangerous ground than an apple cellar.

“Because,” she said, “he does not care enough to stay. Poor Mattie! After all your efforts, Jedediah Crane has rejected you twice.”

Something changed in Mattie’s face. Her placidity did not break; it hardened into resolve.

“He hasn’t rejected me.”

“My dear—”

“We’re going to be married at Christmas.”

Selena’s mouth opened.

Mattie had never before observed that her sister looked better with her mouth open, but silence becomes everyone.

“You—what?”

“Jed and I are going to be married at Christmas,” repeated Mattie. “He’ll come here to live and manage the farm.”

“Matilda Adams!”

“Yes?”

“You told me he had not proposed.”

“He hasn’t.”

Selena stared so intently that even curiosity could carry her no further.

“Then how can you say—”

“Because I’ve decided.”

“You cannot decide for him!”

“I can give him the opportunity.”

Selena sat down on an apple barrel.

Mattie removed her apron and hung it on a nail.

“Where are you going?”

“To tell Jed.”

“Now?”

“Now.”

“But, Mattie—Matilda—this is shameless!”

“Probably.”

“You will regret it.”

“Possibly.”

“He may refuse.”

“He may.”

Selena recovered enough breath for one final cry. “What will people say?”

Mattie put on her bonnet.

“For once, Selena, they can say it after I’ve done something instead of before.”

Jed was loading the last of his stock at Mrs. Crewe’s stable when Mattie arrived. The afternoon was cold and bright. A thin crust of snow lay along the fences, and the red wagon stood beneath a grey sky with its pans and kettles flashing frostily. Rufus wore an expression of resignation.

Jed emerged from behind the wagon carrying a bundle of brooms.

“Mattie! Anything wrong?”

“No.”

“Sick cow?”

“No.”

“Barn fire?”

“No.”

“Selena?”

“In a manner of speaking.”

He set down the brooms.

Mattie had walked fast, but she was not out of breath. Her eyes were steady.

“Jed, I have told Selena that you and I are going to be married at Christmas.”

Jed looked at her.

A tin cup slid from the wagon and fell into a pail with a clang that rang across the stable yard.

“You—told Selena—”

“Yes.”

“That we—”

“Yes.”

“At Christmas?”

“It seems a convenient time.”

Jed removed his hat. The winter breeze played freely over his bald spot, but he did not notice.

“Mattie, I don’t understand.”

“I lost my temper.”

“With Selena?”

“Who else?”

“A person couldn’t be blamed for that. But—married?”

“I said it because she declared you had rejected me. I knew it wasn’t true.”

“No!” Jed cried. “No, by ginger, it ain’t true!”

“I thought not.”

“I never rejected you. I wouldn’t. I couldn’t. Mattie, you don’t mean—could you mean—you’d be willin’?”

“If you are.”

Jed took one step toward her, stopped, and all the old barriers rose visibly in his honest face.

“But the farm.”

“What about it?”

“Folks’ll say—Selena’ll say—I came back after your property.”

“Selena will say something if she lives, and I suppose she means to.”

“I’ve got nothin’, Mattie. Forty-three dollars, Rufus, and the wagon. Rufus has a spavin, and the wagon wheel’s loose.”

“I have money enough.”

“I know. That’s the trouble.”

“No, Jed. The trouble is that you went away fifteen years ago to earn what we already had.”

He stared at her.

“We had each other,” Mattie said. “Or we would have, if you hadn’t been so proud and I hadn’t been so patient. I don’t intend to be that patient again.”

“Mattie—”

“If you don’t love me, say so. I’ll go home and tell Selena I lied. It will be unpleasant, but I’ve survived unpleasant things.”

“Don’t love you!”

Jed’s voice frightened a hen from beneath the wagon. He came forward then and caught both her hands.

“Mattie, I’ve loved you every day since I left Amberley. I loved you when the fruit stand failed and when the churn company cheated me and when I was sellin’ that miserable washin’ compound. I loved you when I hadn’t the price of supper and when I had two hundred dollars in the bank for most of a week. I came back because I couldn’t stay away any longer. But I didn’t dare ask you. I thought it would look like I wanted the farm.”

“What do you want?”

“You.”

“Then you may have me.”

Jed closed his eyes briefly, as if the plain winter yard had become too dazzling to look at.

“Mattie,” he whispered, “this is the most romantic moment of my life.”

She glanced at the brooms, the tin pails, Rufus’s drooping head, and Jed’s checked trousers showing beneath his coat.

“I suppose it is.”

Then she kissed him.

Mrs. Crewe, observing from an upper window, carried the news to the post office within nine minutes. Selena, however, heard it from Mattie herself.

“Yes,” Mattie said that evening, taking off her bonnet. “It is settled.”

Selena sat rigidly beside the parlour stove.

“He accepted?”

“He did.”

“And he is to manage the farm?”

“If he wishes. We haven’t arranged every detail.”

Selena’s expression suggested that a woman who proposed marriage without first settling the dairy accounts was unfit for liberty.

“You will repent this mésalliance.”

“Perhaps. But I’ve repented not marrying him for fifteen years, and I’d like a change.”

“He has no business ability.”

“Neither had Father near the end, and I managed.”

“He dresses absurdly.”

“I can buy him another suit.”

“He is bald.”

“I noticed.”

“He calls his pony a nag.”

“The pony doesn’t object.”

Selena rose.

“I wash my hands of the entire affair.”

“That will be a novelty,” said Mattie mildly.

Jed made his final round two days later. He had customers to visit and obligations to discharge before the wedding; for while romance governed his soul, he remained particular about delivering a paid-for saucepan.

The morning was clear, with pale gold sunlight over the snowy fields. The red wagon went jingling out of Amberley, its polished wares flashing, its brooms aloft, its loose wheel complaining rhythmically. Jed wore the checked suit, the pink tie, and the amethyst pin. His bald spot shone. His double chin trembled with happiness.

At the top of the hill he looked back.

Mattie stood at the Adams gate, one hand lifted. Beyond her the white house with green shutters rested among bare trees, and smoke rose from its chimneys into the blue. Selena was visible at her upper window, but for once Jed felt no need to avoid her eye.

He waved his hat.

“Well, Rufus,” he said when the bend hid the house, “we’ve done it. Or Mattie has. Always was more practical’n me. Christmas! Think of that. We’re goin’ to live on the finest farm in Amberley, and I’m goin’ to marry the finest woman in the world. Not for the farm, mind you. Never for the farm. But I s’pose there’s no harm in learnin’ to manage it.”

Rufus flicked an ear.

“Maybe we didn’t make our fortune the way we planned. Maybe we’ve been a commercial failure. But success is a matter of definition, my nag. There’s men with bank accounts who hain’t got what we’ve got.”

The wagon rolled down the shining road. Its kettles clinked like bells; its pans caught the sun; the upright brooms gave it, to all outward seeming, the air of a triumphal chariot designed by a housewife.

Jed gathered the reins and laughed aloud.

“Romance, Rufus! That’s what life’s been full of all along. Romance in Amberley, romance in tinware, romance in waitin’ fifteen years for a woman with more sense than you’ve got yourself. Get up, my nag, get up! We’re goin’ home for Christmas.”

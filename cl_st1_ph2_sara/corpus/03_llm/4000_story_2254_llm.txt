### THE UNDRESS UNIFORM

*Oh, drill the old soldier by bugle and book,*  
*He’ll measure the road and consider the look;*  
*But loose the young recruit where the night-rifles play—*  
*He has not learned danger, so danger gives way.*

We had gone after butterflies in the low scrub beyond cantonments, though Mulvaney maintained that no reasonable insect would expose itself beneath such a sun. He carried the net as if it were a regimental colour, and used it chiefly to threaten snakes.

When a blue-and-copper specimen escaped us by sailing over a thorn hedge, he sat down, filled his pipe, and said it had the wisdom of an old soldier.

“A green wan,” said he, “would ha’ waited to see what ye meant by the net, an’ been catalogued between two sheets av paper before he knew he was dead. ’Tis the same wid men. Give me the little pink bhoys that have niver heard a bullet cough, put a proper officer atop av them, an’ they’ll walk into the mouth av Hell bekaze they think it’s the cook-house.”

I suggested that experience had its advantages.

“Experience!” said Mulvaney. “Experience is the knowledge av all the things that can go wrong, an’ the remembrance av the last time they did. Did I niver tell you how we took Lungtungpen in our skins?”

He had not.

“Then attend, an’ do not be scandalised till the scandal arrives.

“This was in Burma, when the dacoits were as plentiful as mosquitoes an’ twice as hungry. Twenty-four men had we—little new boys wid the milk scarce dry on their chins—an’ Lieutenant Brazenose commandin’. Brazenose was fresh from school, stuffed wid maps, maxims, an’ theouries av War till he rattled when he walked. A good lad, though. Ye could see courage in him the way ye see a candle through thin canvas.

“We had puckarowed a dacoit that knew more than he loved to tell. He sat under a tree, lookin’ innocent as a money-lender on Sunday, while the interpreter asked him where the Lungtungpen gang kept their rice, rifles, dahs, elephants, ammunition, wives, idols, an’ other military necessities.

“‘There is no Lungtungpen,’ sez he.

“‘Then ye will have no objection to leadin’ us there,’ sez I.

“He objected. We explained the geography to him wid a rifle-sling, patient an’ persuasive, an’ presently he remembered that Lungtungpen was twelve miles through jungle, beyond a nullah, behind a river, protected by bamboo stockades, an’ full av gentlemen who would cut our throats for the honour av their grandmothers.

“Brazenose spread a map on his knee. The map knew nothin’ av the place, but that did not prevent him consultin’ it.

“‘We should wait for the column,’ sez he.

“‘Certainly, sorr,’ sez I. ‘An’ while we wait, the dacoits will hear av us, bury the guns, drive off the elephants, burn the village, an’ leave a polite note sayin’ they regret they cannot dine.’

“He bit the end av his pencil.

“‘What do you advise, Mulvaney?’

“‘March this minute, sorr. Eat now, sleep afther victory.’

“‘It is contrary to sound tactical principle.’

“‘So is bein’ murdered in bed, sorr, but it occurs.’

“He looked at me, an’ I saw the schoolbooks fightin’ the blood in him. The blood won.

“‘Fall in the men,’ sez he.

“Those twenty-four bhoys fell in as cheerful as if we were goin’ to a fair. Each carried rifle, sixty rounds, water-bottle, haversack, blanket, belt, boots, an’ no more sense than Providence gives a puppy. That was our strength.

“We started at dusk, the prisoner ahead wid a cord round him, the interpreter whisperin’ prayers behind, an’ me watchin’ both. The jungle shut on us like the lid av a box. There were creepers that caught the rifles, roots that hunted the ankles, leeches demandin’ blood-money, an’ insects singin’ all the sins av the world into our ears. The bhoys sweated, stumbled, an’ grinned. Wan asked whether dacoits carried cash. Another said he would take an elephant home to his mother.

“‘Silence in the ranks,’ sez Brazenose, very fierce.

“Five minutes later he walked into a hanging vine an’ near strangled himself. The men swallowed their laughter till they shook like jelly. Brazenose freed his neck, glared at them, an’ then laughed too. That was when I knew we’d follow him.

“Near midnight the ground sloped down, the trees opened, an’ there lay a river as broad as a parade-ground and black as a priest’s hat. The prisoner had forgotten to mention the width.

“‘There is the ford,’ sez the interpreter.

“The ford was water to the chin, an’ maybe over the head in the middle, wid a current strong enough to steal the boots off a centipede.

“Brazenose stood regardin’ it.

“‘The manuals,’ sez he, ‘recommend the construction of rafts.’

“‘The manuals have time, sorr,’ sez I. ‘We have logs.’

“There were dead bamboo an’ timber along the bank. We tied rifles, cartridge-pouches, an’ belts atop them. Then came the delicate question.

“‘Strip,’ sez I.

“The bhoys stripped.

“‘Entirely?’ sez Brazenose.

“‘Unless ye wish to assault Lungtungpen in wet trousers, sorr. Wet trousers are destructive to military ardour.’

“So presently Her Majesty’s field force stood under the moon in twenty-five complete undress uniforms, not countin’ me, an’ I was the handsomest man among them by twenty years. We pushed the bundles into the shtream an’ followed, one hand on the logs an’ the other disputin’ possession wid the river.

“The current took little O’Hara first. He went downstream feet foremost, whisperin’ language that blistered the water, till three men caught his log. A fish touched Conolly, an’ Conolly gave a yell that ought to have waked Mandalay. Brazenose swam beside the prisoner, revolver tied upon his head like the crown av Alexander. If a painter had seen us, he’d have broken his brushes and entered the cavalry.

“We reached the far side breathless, muddy, an’ alive. The town showed through the trees—a black hump av roofs, bamboo walls, an’ watch-fires. We dragged the weapons ashore, wiped what water we could from the locks, an’ began lookin’ for our clothes.

“At that instant a rifle cracked from the stockade.

“The bullet went over us, thank Heaven, for there was not a pocket among the regiment to stop it. Then came another, an’ another, till the bamboo sparked like a cat’s back on a frosty night. The dacoits had seen us.

“‘Dress!’ sez Brazenose.

“‘Charge!’ sez I.

“He looked at the clothes, then at the stockade, an’ understood the military situation.

“‘Fix bayonets!’ he cried. ‘Forward!’

“We fixed them. There is no more solemn sight in war than a naked man fixin’ a bayonet. The click went down the line. Then the bhoys howled—not from fear, for they had not learned it, but from the pure joy av noise—and away we went.

“We crossed the open ground like buck-rabbits chased by sin. The dacoits fired high, low, sideways, an’ perhaps backward, bekaze no man can shoot according to principle when five-and-twenty pale demons, shinin’ wid river-water, come screamin’ out av the dark.

“The gate was barred. Kelly put his shoulder to it; I put mine beside his; Brazenose struck the fastening wid a rifle-butt; an’ the whole contrivance fell inward wid us atop of it. Then there was no strategy—only streets, torches, smoke, bamboo fences, dogs, women shriekin’, men runnin’, an’ our lads after them wid bayonet an’ butt.

“Wan great dacoit swung a dah at Brazenose. The lieutenant stepped inside the cut, as neat as drill, an’ knocked him senseless wid his revolver. Another came at me from behind a rice-bin. I removed his interest in the campaign. Two fired from a roof till Conolly climbed the wall like an indecent lizard an’ persuaded them down.

“‘This way, bhoys!’ I shouted. ‘The square! Take the square, an’ the town belongs to us!’

“‘Which square?’ sez O’Hara, runnin’ past wid no rifle but two captured swords.

“‘Any square ye can find!’

“We found a temple court full av dacoits rallyin’ round a gong. Somebody struck it, an’ the boom rolled over the roofs. The enemy shouted; we shouted louder. Our voices, the moonlight, an’ the nakedness completed what the bayonets had begun. Half broke for the jungle. The rest fought, fierce enough, but without agreement. In ten minutes the court was ours. In twenty the firing dropped to little spits at the edge av town. Before dawn Lungtungpen belonged to the Queen, though I doubt she would have recognised her representatives.

“Then began the worst hardship av the expedition.

“We had to form ranks.

“Brazenose stood upon the temple steps, scratched, blackened, an’ dignified from the neck upward.

“‘Sergeant Mulvaney,’ sez he, ‘fall in the men.’

“‘Very good, sorr. In what order?’

“‘Usual order.’

“‘There is nothin’ usual about it, sorr.’

“But we formed—two ranks, sized by height, rifles at the slope, every man starin’ between the shoulders av the man before him as if strict attention would produce trousers. The sun came up. It touched the roofs, the pagoda, the dead in the square, an’ finally us. I have been under artillery, cavalry, fever, an’ the eye av a colonel after drink, but never have I known a fire like that sunrise.

“The townspeople crept out by degrees. First children, then old women, then merchants calculatin’ what had not been stolen. Last came the headman in a silk jacket, escorted by the interpreter. He looked at the broken gate, the prisoners, the captured guns, and us.

“‘Are these all English soldiers?’ he asked.

“‘They are,’ sez the interpreter.

“The headman considered a long while.

“‘What, then,’ sez he, ‘can English soldiers do when they wear clothes?’

“‘Conquer the whole earth,’ sez I, ‘if the quartermaster provides the proper size.’

“We patrolled Lungtungpen till parties recovered our equipment from the river-bank. A patrol without breeches lacks moral influence, but ours had curiosity in its favour. At last the uniforms arrived, muddied, mixed, an’ incomplete. O’Hara got trousers fit for a giant; Kelly received wan boot; Conolly wore a haversack where modesty suggested it would do most service. By breakfast we resembled an army again.

“We counted seventy-five dacoits dead, more prisoners than was convenient, six elephants, Sniders, muskets, pistols, powder, dahs, spears, jingles, rice enough for a brigade, an’ property beyond arithmetic. Of our twenty-five, not a man was killed. Three were cut, five scorched by powder, nine bitten by leeches, an’ the whole detachment injured in self-respect.

“Brazenose came to me when the column arrived at noon.

“‘Mulvaney,’ sez he, ‘your advice was correct.’

“‘Sorr,’ sez I, ‘your conduct was magnificent, but ye put too much dependence on theouries av War. Theouries are excellent servants an’ terrible commanders.’

“His face stiffened, for he was still an officer an’ I a private.

“‘You are impertinent.’

“‘Habitually, sorr. But there’s not a man here wouldn’t follow you naked into Moscow—and we have evidence to prove it.’

“He tried not to laugh. Then he held out his hand, quick, before dignity could stop him.

“That’s the use av young troops,” Mulvaney concluded. “They don’t know when a thing is impossible. Feed them, praise them, give them an officer that goes first, an’ they’ll assault Caesar, Alexander, the divil, an’ the entire continental armies before breakfast. Old soldiers would ask where the supports were.”

The escaped butterfly returned and settled on Mulvaney’s sleeve. He watched it without moving.

“Hand me the net,” I whispered.

“Let him be,” he said. “He’s learned caution.”

Then he relit his black Army tobacco, whose smoke drove the butterfly away more effectively than any net.

“Ye dislike the flavour?” he asked.

I admitted that I did.

“Army life is the same,” said Mulvaney. “It spoils the taste for milder things.”

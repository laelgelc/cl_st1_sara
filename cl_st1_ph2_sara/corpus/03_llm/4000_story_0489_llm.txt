# The Cup at the Imperial

Mr. Saunders came home at half-past six with a bowed head and an angry brow.

The head was habit. The brow meant business.

During thirty years of married life Mrs. Saunders had learned to distinguish the various sounds made by her husband’s latch-key, just as a sailor distinguishes the wind in the rigging. There was the brisk rattle which announced good news; the slow, uncertain scraping which followed a disappointing day; and the sharp thrust, turn, and bang which warned the household that its master had discovered something wrong with the universe and intended to put it right before dinner.

On this occasion the key entered like a bayonet.

Mrs. Saunders, who sat sewing beside the parlour window, let her work fall into her lap.

The front door closed. A stout stick was placed in the umbrella-stand with unnecessary violence. Then came the heavy footfall along the passage.

“My dear,” she began, when her husband entered.

“Where is Richard?”

The question was delivered before the door had properly shut behind him.

Mrs. Saunders looked at the clock, though she knew the time perfectly.

“He has not come home yet.”

“So I supposed.”

Mr. Saunders removed his gloves finger by finger. He was a large, square man, with iron-grey whiskers, a high collar, and that appearance of unbending integrity which often causes discomfort to persons whose integrity is no less sound but whose collars are lower. He had spent his life in the offices of Messrs. Pollard and Beech, importers of tea, where he was respected for his punctuality, feared for his exactness, and secretly disliked by three junior clerks who had once been late in the same week.

“Do you know where he is?” he asked.

Mrs. Saunders threaded her needle. It did not need threading, but the operation gave employment to her eyes.

“At his business, perhaps.”

“His business ends at five.”

“Mr. Hammond sometimes keeps him.”

“Mr. Hammond does not keep him every night until eleven.”

“No, dear.”

“Or midnight.”

“No.”

“Or one in the morning.”

Mrs. Saunders’s fingers trembled.

“No,” she said again.

Mr. Saunders crossed the room, turned, and marched back. His wife watched him as a small neutral country might watch the movements of two hostile armies, except that in the present case one army had not yet arrived.

“I met Wilson this afternoon,” he said.

Mrs. Saunders gave a faint start.

“Mr. Wilson?”

“Charles Wilson, of Pilgrim Street.”

“Yes, dear. I know who he is.”

“He tells me that he has frequently seen Richard entering a club in St. James’s after business hours.”

There was a pause.

“He may have done.”

Mr. Saunders stopped before her.

“You knew it.”

Mrs. Saunders made the mistake of attempting honesty by degrees.

“I had heard something.”

“You knew it.”

“Dick mentioned that he belonged to a club.”

“And you concealed the fact from me.”

“I did not conceal it, John. I merely did not tell you.”

A courageous logician might have defended the distinction. Mrs. Saunders was not a courageous logician, and her husband’s expression showed that he was unlikely to be persuaded by one.

“What club?”

“The Imperial.”

“The Imperial Gaming Club?”

“I believe billiards are played there.”

“Billiards!” Mr. Saunders repeated the word as though it denoted an offence excluded from conversation in respectable homes. “Cards are played there. Money is lost there. Men drink there.”

“Men drink in many places, John.”

“Not men of principle.”

This disposed of a considerable part of the population.

Mr. Saunders resumed his march.

For twenty-four years Mrs. Saunders had stood between her husband and her son. When Dick was six, she had explained a broken window by enlarging upon the strength of the wind. When he was twelve, she had attributed a black eye to an accidental collision with a cupboard. At sixteen she had softened the story of a neglected examination; at twenty she had prevented a quarrel over Dick’s refusal to enter Messrs. Pollard and Beech; and now, at twenty-five, the young man still came first to her with his troubles, jokes, ambitions, and unpaid tailor’s bills.

Mr. Saunders considered this a conspiracy. Mrs. Saunders considered it motherhood. Dick, with less philosophy, called her “the best little woman in London.”

“There have been signs,” said Mr. Saunders. “I see them now. Late hours. Evasions. Increased expenditure. A growing want of respect.”

“Dick has never been disrespectful.”

“He laughs.”

“He laughs very often.”

“When I speak to him.”

“He laughs then, too.”

“Exactly.”

Mr. Saunders drew himself up.

“I will not have it said that a son of mine is going to ruin while I sit idle beneath the same roof. I have done my duty. I have given him education, food, clothing, and advice.”

Mrs. Saunders reflected that the advice had cost Mr. Saunders less than the other articles and had been supplied in greater abundance, but she did not say so.

“He is a good boy at heart,” she murmured.

“At heart! Vice is always defended by the condition of the heart. A man is dishonest, but good at heart. He is idle, but good at heart. He drinks, gambles, deceives his father, breaks his mother’s heart—and still we are told that at heart he is excellent.”

“Oh, John, he has not broken my heart.”

“He will.”

“Not unless somebody helps him.”

Mr. Saunders looked at her sternly. She bent over her sewing.

“When he comes in,” he said, “he will remain in this house.”

“Yes, dear.”

“I shall speak to him after dinner.”

“Yes.”

“You will not interfere.”

“No.”

“Nor warn him.”

“No, John.”

“Nor make excuses.”

Mrs. Saunders gave a little sigh.

“I will try not to.”

“That is not enough.”

“I will not.”

Mr. Saunders stood with his back to the fire, although there was no fire, the evening being warm. It was his accustomed position when administering family justice, and a seasonable detail could not be permitted to disturb a moral arrangement.

“We shall get at the truth,” he said. “I have nailed my flag to the mast.”

Mrs. Saunders had never quite understood that expression, but she knew from experience that it meant trouble for somebody.

At seven o’clock the front door opened, and a cheerful whistle sounded in the passage.

“There he is,” whispered Mrs. Saunders.

Mr. Saunders did not answer. He merely placed both hands behind him and assumed the look which had once made a dishonest warehouseman confess to the theft of two pounds of Ceylon tea.

Dick entered the room.

He was tall and slight, with pleasant grey eyes, a neat brown moustache, and the air of a young man on sufficiently good terms with the world to forgive it for occasionally being tiresome. He had his mother’s quick expression and his father’s firm mouth, though neither parent was likely to recognise the other’s contribution. He carried his hat in one hand and a folded evening paper in the other.

“Hullo, mother! Hullo, sir!”

Mr. Saunders inclined his head.

“You are late.”

“Seven minutes,” said Dick, glancing at the clock. “A cab lost a wheel in Cheapside and held up everything from St. Paul’s to the Bank. I offered to carry the cab, but the driver was unreasonable.”

Mrs. Saunders smiled in spite of herself.

“Richard,” said his father, “this is no occasion for levity.”

Dick looked from one face to the other.

“Thunder in the domestic sky?”

“Sit down.”

“I’d rather wash first.”

“You will sit down.”

Dick sat.

Mr. Saunders had prepared an opening address during his walk from the City. It was severe, dignified, and comprehensive. Unfortunately, the sight of Dick’s calm face disturbed its arrangement.

“You belong to the Imperial Club.”

Dick’s eyebrows rose.

“Right for once, sir.”

“For once?”

“I mean your information is right. Not that it’s usually wrong, of course. Only—well, yes, I belong.”

“And how long have you belonged?”

“About six months.”

“Six months! Under my roof, eating at my table, and for six months you have concealed your membership of a gambling club.”

“I haven’t concealed it.”

“You have not told me.”

“No. There are dozens of things I don’t tell you.”

“Richard!”

“I mean ordinary things. I don’t report every luncheon or every omnibus I take. Mother knew.”

Mrs. Saunders looked unhappy.

“Your mother,” said Mr. Saunders, “has unfortunately allowed affection to overcome judgment.”

“That is one way of saying she’s been decent to me.”

The elder man’s face darkened.

“Do you gamble?”

“No.”

“Do you expect me to believe that?”

“I rather hoped you might.”

“Cards are played there.”

“They are.”

“For money.”

“Sometimes.”

“And you never play?”

“Not for money. I’ve played whist twice, at about the rate of three mistakes per hand. Nobody asked me again.”

“Do you drink?”

“Tea, coffee, lemonade, and, under medical pressure, beef extract.”

“I refer to intoxicants.”

“I know you do. No.”

“You return home at midnight from a club where liquor is sold and expect me to believe you drink lemonade.”

“They make excellent lemonade.”

Mr. Saunders struck one palm with the other.

“This flippancy confirms my worst fears.”

Dick turned to his mother.

“What has happened? Has somebody been telling tales?”

“Mr. Wilson saw you,” she said softly.

“Wilson of Pilgrim Street?”

“Yes.”

Dick gave a short laugh.

“That explains it. Wilson has been trying to get into the Imperial for three years and has been blackballed twice. He thinks everybody inside is a criminal because nobody will let him become one.”

“Enough,” said Mr. Saunders. “How did you become a member?”

“Mr. Hammond put me up.”

“Your employer?”

“Yes.”

“And I suppose he pays your losses.”

“He paid my entrance fee and subscription, if that’s what you mean.”

Mr. Saunders stared.

“Julius Hammond paid for you to join a club?”

“He did.”

“Why?”

Dick hesitated.

There was nothing shameful in the answer, but he had his reasons for not giving it. Julius Hammond, banker, financier, and sportsman, had discovered by accident that his junior confidential clerk possessed a remarkable gift at billiards. Hammond had entered him for the Imperial Amateur Cup, had arranged his membership, and, with the exuberant secrecy of a schoolboy planning fireworks, had insisted that the affair should remain private until the result was known.

The secrecy had been foolish. Dick knew that now. Yet loyalty to Hammond, pride in the contest, and an old resentment against his father’s habit of demanding confidence without offering sympathy held him silent.

“Because he wanted me there,” he said.

“For what purpose?”

“Club business.”

“What business?”

“I can’t tell you.”

“Cannot—or will not?”

“Will not, if you prefer it.”

Mrs. Saunders looked pleadingly at him.

“Dick, dear—”

“I’m sorry, mother. It isn’t only my affair.”

Mr. Saunders’s self-command, which had been severely exercised, now gave way.

“So! You set your employer’s wishes above your duty to your father.”

“I am twenty-five.”

“You are my son.”

“Both statements are true.”

“You live in my house.”

“And pay you thirty shillings a week.”

“You speak as if you were a lodger!”

“I’m trying to speak as if I were a man.”

The words came more sharply than Dick intended. Silence followed them.

Mrs. Saunders’s eyes filled with tears.

Dick saw them and was sorry at once. Mr. Saunders saw them and interpreted them as evidence against Dick, which is the privilege of a man who has already reached a verdict.

“This is what your club has done,” he said. “Six months ago you would not have addressed me in that manner.”

“Six years ago I wouldn’t have done it,” replied Dick. “But I might have thought it.”

“Dick!” cried his mother.

He rose and crossed to her.

“I beg your pardon, mother. And yours, sir, for speaking hotly. But I don’t gamble, I don’t drink, and I haven’t done anything to be ashamed of.”

“You will prove that by remaining here this evening.”

Dick glanced at the clock.

“I can’t.”

“You can, and you will.”

“I have an appointment at the Imperial at eight.”

“You will break it.”

“It’s important.”

“All the more reason.”

Dick picked up his paper, folded it again, and put it down. His face had lost its humour.

“I gave my word that I would be there.”

“And what of your duty here?”

“I have no duty to submit to a trial when the charge is made up of gossip and suspicion.”

“You defy me?”

“I’m going to keep my appointment.”

Mr. Saunders pointed to the chair.

“Sit down.”

Dick remained standing.

In that moment each remembered a hundred earlier struggles. Mr. Saunders remembered the boy who had resisted punishment without crying, thereby making punishment seem inadequate. Dick remembered closed doors, stern lectures, forbidden games, and his mother waiting afterwards on the landing with a candle. Neither memory assisted reconciliation.

“Richard,” said Mr. Saunders, in a low voice, “if you leave this house against my express command, do not expect me to overlook it.”

Dick’s mouth tightened.

“That sounds like a parting shot.”

“It is a warning.”

“Very well.”

Mrs. Saunders rose quickly.

“Dick, stay—for my sake.”

He looked at her, and his resolution wavered.

Then the clock struck the quarter.

“I can’t, mother. Truly I can’t. I must go.”

He kissed her cheek.

Mr. Saunders turned away, as though declining to witness an act of hypocrisy.

Dick went into the hall. A moment later the front door closed.

Mrs. Saunders sank back into her chair.

“You drove him out,” she said.

Mr. Saunders wheeled round.

“I drove him out?”

“Yes, John. You did.”

It was perhaps the bravest sentence she had spoken in twenty-four years.

Her husband regarded her in astonishment.

“He openly disobeyed me.”

“You gave him no chance.”

“I asked plain questions.”

“You asked them as if he were guilty before he answered.”

“He refused to answer.”

“Because you never believe him when he does.”

Mr. Saunders took up his stick.

“Where are you going?”

“To the Imperial.”

Mrs. Saunders became pale.

“John, you cannot.”

“I can and shall.”

“You would not make a scene there?”

“I shall do what duty requires.”

Duty is a powerful ally, particularly when it agrees with temper.

Mrs. Saunders followed him into the passage.

“Please wait. Let him come home. Speak to him quietly then.”

“He has chosen the place of battle.”

“There need be no battle.”

“There already is one.”

He put on his hat.

“If vice has got hold of my son, I will see it with my own eyes. If he is in debt, I will know the amount. If this Hammond has led him astray, he shall answer to me.”

“Mr. Hammond is a very important man.”

“Importance does not alter morality.”

“No, dear, but it sometimes alters whether one is admitted at the door.”

Mr. Saunders opened the door.

“I shall be admitted.”

He looked capable at that moment of being admitted to Buckingham Palace without invitation.

“John!”

But he had nailed his flag to the mast, put on his hat, and gone upon the war-path. The door shut behind him.

Mrs. Saunders stood motionless for several seconds.

Then the habits of a lifetime came to her assistance. She returned to the parlour, rang the bell, and asked the maid for a telegraph form.

The message was difficult to compose. Telegrams charged by the word, and maternal alarm is seldom economical. After three attempts she produced:

FATHER COMING IMPERIAL VERY ANGRY PLEASE AVOID SCENE RETURN IF POSSIBLE MOTHER.

She counted the words twice, added “DEAR” before “MOTHER,” crossed it out as extravagant, then put it back and sent the maid running to the post office.

Having taken this action, Mrs. Saunders sat down to wait—as every woman must who lives with a cantankerous man and loves him in spite of the evidence.

Mr. Saunders walked from Bloomsbury to St. James’s.

He could have taken a cab, but anger prefers exercise. Every forceful step added a new accusation to the indictment. Dick had deceived him. Dick was drinking. Dick was gambling. Hammond, a wealthy bachelor with sporting tastes, had deliberately lured a young clerk into dissipation. Before reaching Oxford Street, Mr. Saunders had discovered forged cheques; at Piccadilly Circus he had found secret debts; and by the time he entered Pall Mall he had privately arranged Dick’s departure for Australia.

He had never seen the Imperial, but he expected something between a tavern and a den. Charles Wilson had described it as “fast,” which in Mr. Saunders’s vocabulary covered racing, champagne, actresses, and moral ruin.

The actual building disconcerted him.

It stood broad and dignified behind stone pillars. Electric lamps shone beneath a carved portico. Through tall windows he saw quiet rooms, dark wood, and men in evening dress sitting in deep leather chairs with newspapers spread before them. No drunken person lay upon the steps. No sinister creature invited him to stake the family fortune upon the turn of a card. A uniformed porter opened the door with grave courtesy.

“Good evening, sir.”

“I wish to see Mr. Richard Saunders.”

“Yes, sir. Is Mr. Saunders expecting you?”

“No.”

The porter’s expression remained respectful, but acquired caution.

“Your name, sir?”

“Mr. John Saunders. His father.”

“Will you step this way, sir?”

Mr. Saunders was led into a waiting-room furnished more richly than his own parlour. There were sporting prints on the walls, thick curtains, soft carpets, and a polished table bearing silver match-boxes. The air held faint suggestions of leather, tobacco, and excellent dinners.

He sat on the edge of a chair, refusing its comfort on principle.

From somewhere deep in the building came a burst of applause.

Mr. Saunders stiffened.

A servant entered carrying a salver.

“A telegram for Mr. Richard Saunders, sir. I was told his father was here. Shall I have it taken in?”

Mr. Saunders recognised his own address upon the envelope and his wife’s nervous handwriting on the forwarding slip.

“You may leave it.”

The servant hesitated.

Before either man could proceed, the door opened and Julius Hammond entered.

Hammond was a broad, rosy gentleman of fifty, admirably dressed, with a white waistcoat, a red rose in his buttonhole, and the genial confidence of a man accustomed to seeing doors open before he reached them. His silver hair was brushed back from a high forehead; his blue eyes were bright; and the hand he extended was warm, firm, and entirely without suspicion.

“My dear Mr. Saunders! Delighted—absolutely delighted.”

Mr. Saunders did not offer his hand.

“You know me?”

“Dick’s father! The likeness is unmistakable.”

There was very little likeness, but Hammond had a generous nature.

“Sit down, sir. Whisky? Brandy? A glass of port?”

“I drink nothing.”

“Nothing? Dear me. Then soda?”

“Nothing.”

“Remarkable discipline. I wish I possessed it.”

Another wave of applause rolled through the club.

Hammond turned his head impatiently.

“Confound them! They’ll distract him. Though perhaps distraction would improve matters.”

Mr. Saunders leaned forward.

“I have come to speak to my son.”

“Of course. Quite natural.”

“On a serious matter.”

“Serious? My dear sir, it could not be more serious.”

Mr. Saunders’s hand closed upon his stick.

“You admit it?”

“Admit it? I have been suffering for the last hour.”

“Then you know the position into which he has fallen?”

“Know it! I arranged the whole thing.”

The room seemed to contract around Mr. Saunders.

“You arranged it?”

“From beginning to end. I take the blame. Dick was reluctant at first, but I over-persuaded him. ‘My boy,’ I said, ‘you owe it to yourself.’ Perhaps I was wrong. One grows enthusiastic.”

Mr. Saunders breathed heavily.

“You paid for his membership.”

“Certainly.”

“Why?”

“He could hardly do what was required without belonging to the club.”

“What was required?”

Hammond glanced toward the door.

“Come and see. I ought not be away from him. He was going from bad to worse when I left.”

Mr. Saunders rose slowly.

“How bad?”

“Almost hopeless.”

“Has he lost much?”

“A great deal of ground.”

“Money, sir. I ask about money.”

“Oh, the money!” Hammond pulled a face. “Ten thousand.”

Mr. Saunders put one hand upon the table.

“Ten—thousand—pounds?”

“Exactly. It would be painful, but I can afford it.”

“You?”

“The loss would be mine, naturally.”

Mr. Saunders stared at him. His mind, already prepared for catastrophe, fitted these replies into the darkest possible shape. Hammond had financed Dick. Dick had gambled. Ten thousand pounds had vanished. Perhaps the young fool had used his employer’s funds. Perhaps this cheerful financier intended to purchase silence by sending him abroad.

“And my son?” he asked.

“His prospects are poor. Very poor indeed.”

“Disgraced?”

“Not yet. But if he fails tonight—well, he will take it hard.”

The applause sounded again, shorter and more anxious.

Hammond seized Mr. Saunders by the arm.

“Come along. We may still be in time to see him recover.”

Mr. Saunders drew back.

“I will see him now.”

“That is what I mean.”

Hammond hurried him through a wide hall where servants moved silently under electric lights. They passed a reading-room, a dining-room glowing with glass and silver, and a corridor hung with portraits of celebrated sportsmen. Everywhere there were men of substance and position—barristers, merchants, officers, two members of Parliament, and one peer whom Mr. Saunders recognised from an illustrated weekly.

The discovery that respectable men frequented a place does not always prove the place respectable, but it complicates condemnation.

At the end of the corridor stood a pair of closed doors. Outside them several members waited in tense attitudes.

A small man with a bald head whispered to Hammond:

“Forty-seven behind. Merivale is in again.”

“Forty-seven!” groaned Hammond. “He was only thirty-one behind when I came out.”

He turned to Mr. Saunders.

“You see how it is.”

Mr. Saunders did not see at all.

“Where is my son?”

“Inside.”

“Then open the door.”

“Quietly, my dear fellow. Quietly. A breath might ruin him.”

Hammond eased one door open and guided him through.

Mr. Saunders entered a large billiard-room.

For one bewildered instant he understood nothing. A brilliant canopy of shaded electric lamps hung over the centre table, flooding its green cloth with light while leaving the surrounding spectators in a softer glow. Tier upon tier of men sat or stood around the walls, motionless and attentive. At one end, on a pedestal draped in crimson, stood a tall silver cup.

The air was warm and still. There was no sound except the faint shuffle of a shoe and then the clean, delicate click of ivory.

Click—click.

A fair-haired young man in shirt-sleeves bent over the table. The balls ran smoothly, touched cushions, and settled. A marker spoke in a low voice.

“Twenty-three.”

The fair-haired player chalked his cue.

Near the opposite corner stood Dick.

Mr. Saunders scarcely recognised him.

His coat lay upon a chair. His sleeves were turned back at the wrist. His face was pale and grave, and all the careless humour had gone from it. He held his cue loosely, watching the table with steady eyes.

Above the marker’s chair hung a black scoring-board:

MERIVALE 467  
SAUNDERS 420

“Five hundred up,” whispered Hammond.

Mr. Saunders looked at him.

“What is this?”

“The final.”

“The final of what?”

“The Imperial Amateur Championship. Best amateur event in London. Dick won the cup last year. He is defending it tonight.”

Mr. Saunders looked again at the board, the crowd, and the silver trophy.

“And the ten thousand pounds?”

“My wager on Dick.”

“You wagered ten thousand pounds on a billiard match?”

Hammond considered the question.

“When you put it in that tone, it does sound excessive.”

Mr. Saunders’s lips moved, but no words emerged.

A stern glance from a spectator compelled both men to silence.

Merivale played again.

Click—click.

The red ball travelled the length of the table, kissed the cushion, and returned into position.

“Twenty-five,” murmured the marker.

A faint movement passed through the room.

“Beautiful control,” whispered Hammond, though with the bitterness of one admiring an executioner’s axe. “He is a magnificent player.”

Merivale added seven more, then missed a difficult cannon by the width of a coin. The audience breathed.

“Merivale, four hundred and seventy-four. Saunders, four hundred and twenty.”

Dick approached the table.

He examined the position from one side, then the other. The red lay near the top cushion; his opponent’s white was inconveniently placed; his own ball offered little. He chalked the cue with care.

Mr. Saunders, whose knowledge of billiards was limited to the conviction that no good came of them, nevertheless understood from the silence that the stroke was difficult.

Dick played.

His ball moved slowly, touched the red with exquisite fineness, ran to the cushion, and came to rest behind the other white.

A murmur of approval rose and was checked.

“A safety,” Hammond breathed. “Clever boy. He cannot score, so he gives Merivale nothing.”

Mr. Saunders watched his son return to his place. Dick did not look toward the spectators. His eyes remained upon the table.

Merivale escaped the trap, but left a chance. Dick scored four, lost position, and played safe again.

The contest proceeded not as Mr. Saunders had imagined games of chance must proceed, with excitement and recklessness, but with calculation, restraint, and patience. Each stroke involved geometry, judgment, touch, and nerve. The players walked about the table like surgeons. The spectators, many of whom had money at stake, behaved with the solemnity of a congregation.

Dick scored nine.

Then six.

Then missed.

Merivale returned and made twelve with effortless precision.

The board changed:

MERIVALE 486  
SAUNDERS 439

“Fourteen wanted,” said somebody behind Mr. Saunders.

“For Merivale,” Hammond whispered unnecessarily. “Sixty-one for us.”

“Us?” Mr. Saunders repeated.

Hammond gave him a quick look.

“Of course, us.”

Merivale added two.

His next stroke left the balls clustered near the top of the table, in what Hammond called an excellent nursery position. Merivale began to score from a succession of delicate cannons, moving the balls only a few inches each time.

Click—click.

“Three.”

Click—click.

“Five.”

Click—click.

“Seven.”

The score mounted to four hundred and ninety-five.

Hammond gripped Mr. Saunders’s sleeve.

“Five more and it is over.”

Mr. Saunders did not remove his arm.

He had forgotten his anger. He had forgotten the speech he intended to make. He had even, for the moment, forgotten the horror of a ten-thousand-pound wager. His whole attention had narrowed to the three balls upon the green cloth and the grave young man waiting beside the wall.

Merivale played for an easy cannon.

The ball struck too full.

One white rolled against the cushion. The red drifted toward the centre. The scoring stroke had been made, but the position was broken.

“Four hundred and ninety-seven,” said the marker.

Three wanted.

Merivale examined the table and attempted a long losing hazard. The red rattled at the mouth of the pocket and stayed out.

A sharp sigh moved through the room.

Dick came forward. He needed sixty-one.

He began with a simple cannon, followed by another. Then he brought the red down from the cushion and gathered the three balls together near the left-hand side.

The clicking resumed.

At first Mr. Saunders could not understand why these small strokes were remarkable. Dick seemed merely to tap one ball upon another and leave them close. But as the score grew, he perceived that every stroke prepared its successor. Nothing was hurried; nothing was wasted. Dick’s hand moved smoothly, his body remained balanced, and his gaze travelled from ball to ball with quiet intelligence.

“Ten.”

“Thirteen.”

“Seventeen.”

The audience leaned forward.

At twenty-one the balls began to separate. Dick played a gentle cushion cannon and brought them together again.

A whisper went round the room.

“Superb.”

“That was the stroke.”

“He’s found his touch.”

Mr. Saunders felt an unfamiliar warmth beneath his waistcoat.

This, then, was what Dick had been doing on those late evenings. Not drinking, not losing money, but practising a skill of which his father had known nothing. The thought brought relief, but relief was followed by something less comfortable. Why had he known nothing? Because Dick had hidden it, certainly. Yet why had the boy hidden it?

The answer stood too near to be avoided.

At home, every pleasure had required a defence. Every ambition not selected by Mr. Saunders had appeared a challenge. Dick had learned secrecy as another son might have learned obedience.

“Twenty-eight,” said the marker.

The run continued.

Dick’s face remained composed, but a fine line of perspiration showed at his temple. Once, when a ball stopped half an inch farther than he wished, his lips tightened. He walked around the table, considered a difficult angle, and played from the cushion.

Click—click.

The red crossed the table and returned.

“Thirty-two.”

Hammond breathed into Mr. Saunders’s ear.

“Best stroke of the match.”

Mr. Saunders nodded as if he had recognised that independently.

Dick reached thirty-seven before position failed. The balls lay widely scattered. He paused.

A spectator near the front whispered something to his neighbour. The neighbour smiled. Merivale, waiting beside his chair, saw the smile and spoke with the easy confidence of a man still forty-one points ahead.

“No more nursery, Saunders. Let us see you win in the open.”

It was lightly said, but it carried through the silence.

Dick looked up.

Some players might have ignored the remark; others might have answered it. Dick did neither. He walked to the clustered pair near the cushion and deliberately struck them apart.

The three balls scattered across the full breadth of the table.

The room stirred.

Hammond made a sound like a man suddenly deprived of air.

“What has he done?” whispered Mr. Saunders.

“Thrown away the position.”

“Why?”

“Merivale challenged him.”

“That is foolish.”

“Extremely.”

Mr. Saunders watched Dick’s face.

“No,” he said after a moment. “Perhaps not.”

Dick now faced a long cannon across the table. He bent, rose again, re-chalked his cue, and bent once more.

The room became absolutely silent.

He struck.

His ball travelled fast to the top cushion, returned at an angle, glanced against the red, and touched the second white so lightly that the sound was scarcely audible.

Then came the applause.

It broke from every side, loud and spontaneous. Men stood up. Hands struck chair-backs. Even Merivale smiled and tapped his cue upon the floor.

Dick straightened, and for the first time a gleam of his ordinary expression returned.

“Thirty-nine,” called the marker.

From that point the game changed.

The scattered balls compelled a display of all the qualities that had been hidden in the close play—power, delicacy, exact pace, and imagination. Dick made a long winning hazard, doubled the red from one cushion to another, and followed with a cannon that seemed impossible until the balls met.

“Forty-five.”

He was still thirty-six behind in the total score, yet the feeling of the room had altered. Merivale sensed it. Hammond sensed it. Mr. Saunders, who could not have explained a single stroke, sensed it most strongly of all.

Dick had taken command.

At fifty-two he faced a hazard along the cushion. He played firmly. The red entered the pocket cleanly, and his own ball travelled into position for the next stroke.

“Fifty-five.”

Six more would complete the run needed to draw level? Wait—the arithmetic formed itself in Mr. Saunders’s mind. Dick had begun at 439. A run of fifty-five brought him to 494. He needed six to win.

Merivale stood at 497.

The silver cup shone beneath the lamps.

Dick scored two.

“Four hundred and ninety-six.”

He required four.

The balls were not kindly placed. His own white rested near the side cushion; the red stood in mid-table; Merivale’s ball was close to a top pocket.

Dick considered a cannon, rejected it, and walked twice around the table.

“What will he do?” Mr. Saunders whispered.

“Losing hazard off the red,” said Hammond. “If he gets the pace, he can leave the red for a winner.”

Dick played.

The white struck the red and dropped into the corner pocket.

Applause began, then stopped as all eyes followed the red. It rolled slowly up the table, losing speed, and came to rest a foot from the opposite middle pocket.

“Two. Four hundred and ninety-eight.”

Dick’s ball was returned to the spot.

Two points wanted.

The final hazard looked straightforward, but the weight of the moment made no stroke easy. The room seemed to hold one breath. Dick placed his hand upon the cloth, drew the cue back, and paused.

Mr. Saunders saw—not the young man who had defied him an hour earlier, but the child who had once stood white-faced before punishment and refused to cry. The same courage was there, but trained now, directed, and made useful. For years he had called it obstinacy because it opposed him. Other men, it appeared, called it nerve.

Dick struck.

The red travelled toward the pocket.

For a fraction of a second it seemed too slow. It touched the near jaw, trembled, and disappeared.

The room erupted.

Men sprang to their feet. The marker’s announcement was lost in cheers. Hammond seized Mr. Saunders by both shoulders and shook him with the enthusiasm of a shipwrecked sailor greeting land.

“He has done it! The young rascal has done it! Sixty-one unfinished! Came from forty-seven behind with Merivale wanting three! Did you ever see such pluck?”

“No,” said Mr. Saunders.

It was the truth.

Dick laid down his cue.

Merivale crossed to him immediately and held out his hand.

“Magnificent finish, Saunders. You deserved it.”

Dick shook hands.

“You had me properly beaten until I got that chance.”

“No, I had the points. You had the game.”

Their exchange was drowned by fresh applause as the silver cup was lifted from its pedestal. Somebody called for a speech. Somebody else demanded champagne. Hammond vanished into the crowd, shouting instructions that involved six bottles, then returned to drag Mr. Saunders forward.

Dick saw his father.

The colour left his face more quickly than it had during the match.

For a moment neither moved.

All the victory went out of Dick’s expression. He looked again like the son summoned to judgment in the parlour. Mr. Saunders felt the old words rise automatically—disobedience, secrecy, defiance, duty. They formed in orderly ranks and waited to be spoken.

Then he saw the cue still in Dick’s hand, the men crowding around him, Merivale standing at his shoulder, and the cup that Dick had won by skill and self-command before the keenest players in London.

The old words no longer fitted.

Hammond, unaware that he stood in the middle of a family history, beamed upon them both.

“Dick, my boy, here is your father. He came to see the finish.”

“So I see,” said Dick.

His voice was guarded.

Mr. Saunders cleared his throat.

“I came,” he said, “because I had been misinformed.”

Dick glanced at Hammond.

“About the match?”

“About several things.”

Hammond slapped his own forehead.

“The telegram! I forgot it completely.”

He produced the envelope from his pocket and handed it to Dick.

Dick tore it open and read his mother’s message. His expression softened.

“Poor little mother.”

“Yes,” said Mr. Saunders.

It was not much, but Dick looked up sharply. There had been something in the tone he had seldom heard before: agreement without correction.

Hammond began to laugh.

“My dear Saunders, I believe I have made the most appalling muddle. When you asked about losses, I thought you meant my bet.”

“I did not.”

“And when I said Dick was going from bad to worse—”

“I understood you differently.”

“And his lost ground—good heavens!”

Several men turned, smiling, though they did not know the joke.

Hammond clapped Mr. Saunders on the back.

“You thought the boy had lost ten thousand pounds?”

“I feared something of that nature.”

Dick stared, then gave a laugh despite himself.

“A plunger from Plungerville! That’s what you made me, sir.”

Mr. Saunders did not rebuke the phrase.

“I had been told that this was a gambling club.”

“It permits gambling,” said Dick. “I told you that. But I don’t bet.”

Hammond coughed.

“No, no. Dick disapproves of it. Very tiresome young man. Wouldn’t even put five pounds on himself at four to one.”

“You bet ten thousand on him,” Mr. Saunders said.

“An expression of confidence.”

“An expression of lunacy,” said Dick.

“Easy for you to say now that you’ve won it for me.”

“I didn’t win it for you. I won the cup.”

“Quite right,” said Mr. Saunders.

Both Dick and Hammond looked at him.

Mr. Saunders went on with visible effort.

“A man should play for the honour of the contest, not for money.”

“Exactly,” said Dick.

There was another pause.

The crowd was beginning to move toward the adjoining room. Merivale came forward and bowed to Mr. Saunders.

“You may be proud of your son, sir. I have played every leading amateur in the country, and I have never seen a finer recovery.”

Mr. Saunders’s hand tightened upon the head of his stick.

“I am proud of him,” he said.

Dick’s eyes met his.

The sentence was simple. Any affectionate father might have spoken it lightly. From Mr. Saunders it came with the difficulty of confession and the weight of an oath.

Merivale moved away.

Hammond, who possessed feeling beneath his waistcoat and tact when it was absolutely necessary, discovered that champagne demanded his immediate supervision.

Father and son were left beside the billiard table.

Dick turned the telegram over in his hand.

“Mother will be worrying.”

“Yes.”

“I’m sorry I went after she asked me to stay.”

“You had given your word.”

“Yes.”

“You should have told me why.”

“I promised Hammond I wouldn’t speak about the match.”

“You might have told me enough to prevent—”

Mr. Saunders stopped.

Dick waited.

“To prevent my making a fool of myself,” the elder man concluded.

Dick’s eyebrows rose.

It was the nearest approach to self-criticism he had ever heard from his father.

“I might have tried harder,” he said.

“And I might have listened.”

The click of practice balls sounded from another table in a distant room. Servants were removing glasses. Around them the great chamber emptied, leaving the green table bright beneath its lamps and the rows of chairs in shadow.

Mr. Saunders picked up Dick’s coat and held it out.

Dick put it on.

“You played well,” said his father.

“Thank you, sir.”

“That long stroke after Merivale spoke to you—was it necessary to scatter the balls?”

“No.”

“It appeared risky.”

“It was.”

“Then why did you do it?”

Dick smiled a little.

“He suggested I could only score with them close together.”

“And you wished to show him otherwise.”

“Yes.”

Mr. Saunders nodded gravely.

“I understand.”

Dick almost laughed. Of all men alive, his father certainly understood that.

They walked toward the door. Halfway there Mr. Saunders stopped and looked back at the scoring-board, where the final figures remained:

MERIVALE 497  
SAUNDERS 500

“I shall tell your mother,” he said.

“She has earned the news.”

“I shall tell her exactly how you won.”

“Do you know exactly how?”

“No,” Mr. Saunders admitted. “But Hammond will explain it before we go.”

“That may take until breakfast.”

Mr. Saunders’s mouth moved. For one startled instant Dick thought his father was going to smile.

“I shall omit the technical details,” he said.

They reached the corridor. Hammond’s voice could be heard from the supper-room, declaring that sixty-one unfinished was worth opening another dozen. The members called to Dick as he passed; hands were thrust out; congratulations came from all sides.

Mr. Saunders walked beside him, erect and silent, but no longer grim. He observed the respect with which these prosperous and worldly men greeted his son. It was not the noisy fellowship of idlers welcoming a companion in vice. They admired Dick because he had done something difficult and done it bravely.

That discovery did not alter all Mr. Saunders’s opinions. Men of his construction do not rebuild themselves in an evening. He still considered ten-thousand-pound wagers immoral, midnight suppers injurious, and lemonade suspicious when sold beneath a club roof. He would continue to insist upon punctuality. He would certainly object to Hammond. But a narrow door had opened in a wall he had spent years building, and through it he had caught sight of his son as other men saw him.

At the entrance hall Dick glanced toward the clock.

“Nearly eleven.”

“Your mother will be anxious.”

“I’ll send another wire.”

“No need. I am going home now.”

Dick looked toward the supper-room, where his victory was waiting in the form of speeches, champagne, and Hammond’s embrace.

“You ought to stay,” said Mr. Saunders. “They expect it.”

“I can make my excuses.”

“You have won the cup.”

“I won it last year. They survived my leaving then.”

Mr. Saunders took his hat from the porter.

Dick collected his own.

“Wait a minute, sir,” he said. “I’ll walk home with you.”

Mr. Saunders looked at him.

“It is some distance.”

“I know.”

“You will miss your supper.”

“I’m not hungry.”

This was untrue, but there are lies which heaven may reasonably overlook.

They stepped out beneath the portico.

The night was cool and clear. Pall Mall shone under lines of lamps; cabs rolled past; the city, which had seemed to Mr. Saunders a wilderness of temptation two hours earlier, had become merely London again.

For the first few yards father and son walked without speaking. Their steps did not match. Mr. Saunders’s was heavy and deliberate; Dick’s was lighter, quicker. Presently Dick shortened his pace, and presently Mr. Saunders lengthened his.

Behind them, from the open windows of the Imperial, came another burst of cheering.

Mr. Saunders turned his head.

“Sixty-one unfinished, was it?”

“Yes.”

“With Merivale wanting only three?”

“Yes.”

“And you deliberately broke up the balls?”

“I’m afraid so.”

Mr. Saunders faced forward again.

“Your mother,” he said, “will be very proud.”

Dick slipped his arm through his father’s.

“I hope so.”

Mr. Saunders did not draw away.

Together they walked home to tell her.

# The Gold Beneath the Hearth

I

There are families which decline as great trees decline, retaining long after the heart has rotted an exterior magnificence of bough and foliage; and there are others which perish like grain upon exhausted land, each harvest thinner, harsher, and less profitable than the last. The Brents belonged to the former order, the Delandres to the latter. In one, decadence had been gilded by rank, travel, luxury, and the tolerant recollection of distinguished ancestors; in the other, it had been embittered by poverty, provincial pride, and the remembrance of acres which every generation had found some ingenious means of diminishing.

Brent’s Rock, the ancestral dwelling of Geoffrey Brent, stood upon a wooded eminence above the valley of the Arne. Its oldest walls had been laid when gentlemen fortified themselves against neighbours whom they afterwards invited to dinner; its western wing was Tudor, its eastern front Georgian, and its domestic inconveniences belonged impartially to every century. Yet the house was imposing. The terraces descended in broad flights towards the river; black cedars spread their branches over a lawn too sombre even in June; and the grey façade, lifted above wood, village, and church-spire, appeared to survey the country with the melancholy arrogance of a dethroned sovereign.

The Brents had once been soldiers, statesmen, and prelates. In later times they had become collectors, gamblers, and connoisseurs of those pleasures which are most expensive when least innocent. Geoffrey, the last male representative, inherited their beauty, their insolence, and their incapacity for restraint. He was tall, pale, and aquiline; his eyes, beneath lids of almost feminine delicacy, possessed the cold brightness sometimes observed in men who can feel strongly for themselves and not at all for others. He painted indifferently, played admirably, read much, believed little, and had that perfect courtesy which in a bad man is more alarming than brutality.

The Delandres, whose ruined farm lay five miles lower in the valley, had degenerated without elegance. They had been substantial yeomen when the Brents were still obliged to wear armour at breakfast; but mortgages, lawsuits, strong drink, and violent marriages had worn them down. Their house, Mere Farm, stood among neglected orchards where the fruit grew small and bitter. Slates had fallen from the roof; a gate hung by one hinge; docks and nettles occupied what had once been a kitchen-garden. Nevertheless, Wykham Delandre spoke of “our land” with the solemnity of a duke referring to his duchy, though almost the only portion of it not pledged to a creditor was the family burial-place.

Wykham had the heavy shoulders and dark, lowering face of the Delandre men. His sister Margaret embodied the same inheritance in a more dangerous form. In youth she had been celebrated throughout the county for a beauty which respectable women called bold because men could not disregard it. Her figure was full and stately, her mouth passionate, her eyes of that deep brown which seems black until anger draws fire from it; and her hair, a profusion of tawny gold, descended almost to her knees. Even before sorrow touched her, one silver-grey lock ran from the left temple through the gold. It was a strange flaw, and therefore an added charm. The country people said that a Delandre ancestress had been born with such a lock on the night her husband was hanged; but country people possess a legend for every peculiarity they do not understand.

Between Wykham and Margaret there existed the affection of two proud natures forced by poverty into daily collision. Each loved the other after the Delandre manner—that is to say, jealously, suspiciously, and with a readiness to wound upon the least apprehension of being wounded. Their final quarrel arose, as final quarrels frequently do, from causes long accumulated and an occasion almost contemptible. Wykham, inflamed by wine and debt, accused his sister of encouraging Geoffrey Brent’s attentions. Margaret replied that a brother who had squandered her portion possessed no authority over her conduct. He called Brent a seducer; she called Wykham a thief. Then came the silence in which both might yet have repented. Neither did.

“Go to him, then!” Wykham shouted. “Go, and learn what honour a Brent keeps with a Delandre!”

“I shall go,” she answered, white to the lips. “And if he casts me off, I shall at least have been cast off by a gentleman.”

Before dawn she had left Mere Farm.

The county received the scandal with that mingling of indignation and appetite which permits society to condemn a sin without losing any of its flavour. Geoffrey declared that Miss Delandre had become companion to an invalid relation travelling abroad. No one had heard of this invalid relation; but the explanation, being sufficiently improbable, was repeated everywhere. It was whispered by some that Margaret and Geoffrey had been privately married in London. Others maintained that Geoffrey Brent would sooner marry a gipsy than a Delandre. There was evidence for neither opinion, and therefore both flourished.

For nearly a year the pair travelled on the Continent. Letters bearing Margaret’s hand came at first to Mere Farm, but Wykham returned them unopened. Then no more arrived.

The next news was brought by a paragraph in a foreign journal. While crossing a mountain road in Switzerland, Mr. Geoffrey Brent and his companion had suffered a terrible accident. Their hired carriage, descending above a torrent swollen by melting snow, had lost a wheel and plunged over the precipice. The driver, thrown upon the road, survived with broken ribs. Mr. Brent, who had leapt clear at the first lurch, escaped with trifling injuries. The lady and the horses were swept away. Her body had not been recovered.

When the paragraph was read to Wykham, he laughed.

“They warned her,” he said.

Then he shut himself in the upper room at Mere Farm and remained there until night. The servant who carried food to him found the door locked and heard him walking from wall to wall. In the morning he went out sober; in the evening he returned drunk. From that time his dissipation, previously irregular, acquired the steady character of an occupation.

II

Two years passed, and Geoffrey Brent came home.

He came not alone, but with a wife—a young Italian lady whom he had met in Florence and married at the house of her uncle, the Marchese di Vezza. Her name was Viola. She had a face of pale southern loveliness, dark eyes, soft black hair, and a voice which lent music even to her imperfect English. She was neither clever nor weak; she possessed that more perilous quality in an unhappy marriage, a nature at once innocent and profound. She loved Geoffrey with the absolute faith of a woman to whom love is not an incident of existence but its climate, law, and religion.

The county forgave him immediately. A handsome man may outlive much scandal, provided he returns with a beautiful wife and opens his dining-room. Brent’s Rock, long shuttered, was made ready. Builders arrived, chimneys smoked, carriages rolled beneath the gatehouse, and the village profited by a renewal of aristocratic expenditure. It was also learned that Mrs. Brent expected a child. Thus morality and heredity appeared to be satisfied together; the past was decently buried, and the ancient house prepared to receive an heir.

Only Wykham Delandre refused the general amnesty. He presented himself one afternoon at Brent’s Rock and demanded to see its master. Geoffrey received him in the library, with wine before him and sunlight upon the rows of books.

“You have returned prosperously,” said Wykham.

“I have returned,” Geoffrey answered. “Prosperity is a word best left to tradesmen.”

“And you have married.”

“As you see.”

“I do not see. I have no wish to see her.”

At this Geoffrey’s expression altered by the smallest degree.

“You will speak of my wife with respect.”

“Which wife?”

There are insults which provoke anger, and others which strike beneath anger into fear. Geoffrey rose, but his voice remained low.

“You are drunk.”

“Not yet. Was Margaret your wife?”

“No.”

“You swear it?”

“I do not discuss dead women with their drunken relatives.”

Wykham leaned across the table.

“She wrote that you had married her.”

“She lied.”

“She wrote it before she died.”

“And therefore, I suppose, acquired truthfulness by anticipation.”

Wykham’s fist descended upon the table, spilling the wine.

“You took her away. You came back. She did not.”

“The carriage went over a precipice. I did not make the mountain.”

“No; but you survived it very neatly.”

Geoffrey rang the bell.

“You will leave my house.”

“I shall leave it,” said Wykham, “but I shall return when Margaret does.”

The servant found them standing opposite each other, Geoffrey rigid and pale, Wykham smiling with the dreadful satisfaction of one who has discovered the place where an enemy may be wounded. He was conducted from the house. By supper-time the encounter was known in the village; by the following Sunday it had acquired details which neither participant would have recognised.

Viola heard of it last, as wives commonly hear what most nearly concerns them. She questioned Geoffrey with tears and tenderness, but he laughed, kissed her hands, and spoke of the diseased imagination of a ruined drunkard. She desired to believe him, and did believe him—during the day. At night she lay awake, listening to the wind pass round the turrets, and wondered why a man wholly innocent should have looked so ill.

III

Three weeks after Wykham’s visit, Margaret Delandre returned to Mere Farm.

It was after sunset in November. Rain had fallen all day, and the bare orchards dripped beneath a ragged sky. Wykham sat alone before a low fire, a bottle at his elbow, while the old house creaked and settled around him. There came no knock. The outer door, imperfectly latched, opened under a gust of wind; and when he turned, she was standing upon the threshold.

For several seconds he believed that the dead had indeed obeyed his summons.

Her garments were black, poor, and travel-stained. She wore a thick veil, through which her face appeared only as a pale distortion. Yet the height, the imperious carriage, and above all the heavy golden hair marked with its single grey stream were unmistakable.

“Margaret!”

She closed the door.

“Do not call loudly.”

He rose, overturning the chair. The courage which wine had often lent him now deserted him entirely.

“Are you flesh?”

She drew off one glove and held out her hand. It was thin, scarred, and warm.

“Enough flesh to suffer,” she said.

He touched it; then seized it, sobbing. All his resentment, nourished for years as men nourish the vice which consumes them, collapsed before her living presence.

“Meg! Meg, forgive me!”

“Forgiveness?” Her laugh was scarcely human. “We shall have much to forgive before we die.”

She sat beside the fire, but refused food until every shutter had been closed. Only then did she remove the veil.

Wykham uttered a cry.

One side of the face retained a remnant of its former splendour: the dark eye, the proud curve of nostril, the full line of the mouth. The other had been torn upon the rocks and afterwards drawn by clumsy surgery into a shining confusion of scar. The cheek was hollow; the eyelid dragged; a livid seam crossed the brow and disappeared beneath the golden hair. Beauty had not vanished altogether. It survived in fragments, and those fragments made the ruin more terrible.

“Now you know why I travel veiled,” she said.

He covered his face.

“Who did this?”

“Geoffrey.”

“The fall—”

“Geoffrey.”

Slowly, with a composure more dreadful than grief, she told him.

Upon the day of the accident they had quarrelled. She had demanded that he acknowledge their marriage; he denied that the ceremony performed in a little chapel near Turin possessed legal force. She threatened exposure. He soothed her, promised all, and arranged their departure through the mountains.

“At an inn before the descent,” she said, “I saw him beside the carriage. He thought me asleep at the window. He had a tool in his hand. When he looked up, he smiled.”

“The lynchpin?”

“He loosened it. The wheel came free at the steepest turn. He had already opened the door. He sprang before the carriage went over.”

“And you?”

“A pine grew from the cliff. My dress caught. I hung above the water until the branch broke, and then the torrent bore me down. Peasants found me among stones two miles below. For months I knew neither my name nor my own face.”

Wykham paced the room, cursing.

“We shall go to the law.”

“With what proof?”

“Your word.”

“The word of a woman who may not have been a wife, against Geoffrey Brent? The county has already judged between us.”

“The driver—”

“Saw nothing.”

“The marriage papers?”

“He took them.”

Wykham stopped. “Then what will you do?”

Margaret turned towards the fire. Its glow moved upon the sound half of her face and died upon the scarred half.

“I shall take from him what he took from me.”

“His life?”

“More.”

She told him of Viola and the unborn child. She had learned everything while in London, living obscurely among those charitable institutions where misery is numbered, washed, instructed, and dismissed. Geoffrey’s renewed happiness had reached even there.

“He buried me,” Margaret whispered. “He built his new life upon me. I will stand up beneath it.”

Wykham grasped her shoulders.

“You shall not go to Brent’s Rock alone.”

“I must.”

“He will kill you.”

“He has done so once.”

She smiled then, and Wykham saw that the precipice had altered more than her face. Pride, passion, even wounded love might have left room for pity; but two years of pain, concealment, and brooding had refined all feeling into vengeance. She no longer desired justice, which belongs to society, nor reparation, which belongs to the injured. She desired the intimate destruction of the man who had destroyed her.

Before dawn she was gone.

On the table Wykham found a strand of golden hair, streaked with grey, tied round a scrap of paper. Upon the paper she had written: “If I do not return, look beneath his happiness.”

IV

Renovations at Brent’s Rock had exposed the entrails of the old house. Passages were choked with timber; plaster dust whitened the portraits; the great hall, unused since Geoffrey’s grandfather had died there after a banquet, was given over to ladders, lime, and stone. Its floor consisted of broad flags worn by centuries of feet, and at its northern end a vast hearth yawned beneath a carved chimney-piece. One stone before this hearth had cracked from corner to corner. The foreman repeatedly urged that it be replaced.

“That there slab’s unsound, sir,” he told Geoffrey. “Moisture’s got under, belike. We lifts it, lays fresh beddin’, and there’s an end.”

“Later,” Geoffrey said.

There was always a later in repairs to an ancient house, and the foreman submitted. Sacks of lime were stacked in the adjacent passage.

Viola disliked the hall. She could give no reason except that the chimney seemed to breathe, and that voices, even spoken softly, returned from the rafters in altered tones. Geoffrey teased her gently.

“Thou hast no ghosts in Italy?” he asked, imitating her occasional archaism.

“Many,” she replied. “But they dwell in the sun. Your English ghosts are damp.”

She had lately grown pale. Her approaching maternity, the physicians said, made her fanciful. She wandered through the unfinished rooms, attempting to imagine them brightened by flowers and children, but the house resisted such visions. Brent’s Rock had accommodated many pleasures, few of them innocent, and domestic happiness was almost an architectural incongruity.

On the night Margaret entered it, a gale was driving from the west. Geoffrey had remained below after Viola retired. Shortly before midnight, he heard a door close in the old hall.

He took a lamp and crossed the passage. The builders had gone hours before. He supposed that some fastening had failed under the wind; yet at the threshold he paused, for he detected a second sound—the faint dragging of cloth over stone.

“Who is there?”

The flame bent in the draught. Scaffolding cast a black geometry upon the walls. At the far end, before the dead hearth, stood a veiled woman.

Geoffrey did not move.

She lifted the veil.

The lamp shook in his hand, and oil spilled upon the floor.

“You!”

“Yes.”

He backed against the door, then mastered himself. Fear in such men is not the opposite of cruelty but its most active parent.

“How did you enter?”

“As the dead enter. Where they are least expected.”

“You survived.”

“Disappointed?”

He looked at her ruined face. For one instant disgust overcame caution; she saw it, and the last uncertain residue of human softness departed from her expression.

“I watched you at the carriage,” she said. “I saw the tool. I saw you draw the pin.”

“You were delirious.”

“I have been sane for a year.”

“No one will believe you.”

“I do not require belief. I require that she should know.”

From above came a faint sound: a door, perhaps, or the movement of a servant. Geoffrey lowered his voice.

“You have come for money.”

“I have come for your wife.”

He stepped forward.

“You shall not approach her.”

“I shall sit beside her bed and tell her how tenderly you kissed me before arranging my death. I shall show her this face. I shall tell her that the father of her child—”

His hand closed upon her throat.

The lamp fell but did not go out. Its flame, lying sideways, threw a wild light along the floor. Margaret struck at him, tearing his cheek with her nails. They struggled without words, for both listened even in violence for the sound of discovery. She was strong, and hatred gave her an energy beyond strength; but privation had weakened her body, while Geoffrey fought with the concentrated desperation of a man whose whole existence is threatened by a single voice.

He forced her backwards against the hearth. She stumbled upon the cracked stone.

“Wykham knows!” she gasped.

His fingers tightened.

“He knows—where—I came—”

The sentence ended in a dry, smothered cry. Her hands beat once upon his shoulders, then slid down. He held her long after resistance ceased.

When he released her, she sank at his feet, her golden hair spreading across the grey flagstones.

For perhaps a minute he stood listening. The gale struck the windows; somewhere in the scaffolding a loose chain clicked monotonously. The house remained asleep.

Then Geoffrey became practical.

Murder committed in passion may yet be concealed by intelligence, and intelligence, when divorced from conscience, works with astonishing speed. He locked the hall doors. He raised the fallen lamp. He dragged the body behind the scaffolding and went to the builders’ passage, where he found crowbars, a shovel, and the sacks of lime.

The cracked hearthstone resisted him. He worked with coat discarded and shirt clinging to his back, pausing at every imagined footfall. Metal grated against stone. The slab rose an inch, slipped, and struck the floor with a sound that seemed to shake the house. He froze. No one came.

Beneath lay an old ash-pit or forgotten foundation cavity, half filled with loose earth. It was narrow. He made it sufficient.

Margaret’s body had stiffened but little. As he lifted her, the veil fell away. Her uninjured eye remained partly open, and her hair caught around his wrist. He tore it free with a suppressed oath.

He placed her in the hollow, poured lime over face and body, shovelled back the earth, and lowered the stone. One corner would not lie level. He stamped upon it until the edges settled, though the crack remained, a dark irregular line from which a little white powder breathed upward.

He swept the floor. He burned the veil and gloves. He carried the tools back and replaced them with exquisite exactness.

At three in the morning he entered his bedroom. Viola stirred.

“Geoffrey?”

“I am here.”

“What hast thou been doing?”

“Writing.”

She reached for his hand. He drew it away before she could feel the lime upon it.

V

The next morning the foreman discovered that one sack of lime had been opened.

“Somebody’s been meddlin’,” he said. “And the big crowbar weren’t where I left him.”

Geoffrey, standing near the hearth, replied too quickly that he had ordered a servant to examine the stone. The foreman scratched his head.

“No offence, sir, but servants don’t commonly lime their own boots to look at a crack.”

“Replace the slab and say no more.”

“Very good, sir. Only we’ll need lift it proper.”

“Not now.”

The sharpness of his tone surprised the man. It surprised Geoffrey also. He walked away, conscious that the labourers had ceased working to watch him.

For two days he avoided the hall. On the third he entered at noon, while the builders were at dinner.

At first nothing appeared changed. Dust lay over the flags; a pale bar of winter sun crossed the hearth. Then he saw, protruding from the crack, three long golden threads.

He bent. They were hairs.

One was marked for several inches with grey.

Geoffrey remained so still that the flame of the candle he carried did not waver. He told himself that the hair must have caught upon the stone when he lowered it; yet he remembered sweeping the place, examining it upon his knees, removing every visible trace. Moreover, these strands did not lie loosely over the crack. They emerged from it.

He seized them and pulled.

They resisted with a faint, living elasticity. Then they came free all at once—longer than he expected, warm from his hand, and curled around his fingers.

He thrust them into the hearth and burned them. The smell was slight but unmistakable.

That evening Viola asked why his cuff was scorched.

“A candle,” he answered.

She looked at him with troubled eyes. Love makes poor detectives of women who desire acquittal; yet it makes keen observers of the beloved face. She saw his pallor, the fresh scratches upon his cheek, the sudden turning of his head when doors opened behind him.

The next day more hair appeared.

Not three strands now, but a little lock, bright against the stone. Geoffrey waited until the hall was empty, cut it close with a knife, and burned it. On the following morning it had returned, thicker and longer.

He ordered the builders to leave the hall and begin work in the east wing.

“What about that slab?” asked the foreman.

“It is not to be touched.”

“But there’s summat queer comin’ through.”

“Old horsehair in the mortar.”

“Horsehair don’t grow, sir.”

Geoffrey turned such a face upon him that the man retreated.

“No, sir. Course not, sir. I only says it’s longer than yesterday.”

By degrees the servants learned that their master visited the old hall at unusual hours and kept its doors locked. They learned also that a smell of burning hair issued from the chimney though no fire was lit there, and that Mr. Brent had begun carrying a ring of keys which he would entrust to no one. Servants, whose profession requires familiarity with the private absurdities of their superiors, are not easily astonished; but they dislike mysteries from which no practical advantage can be extracted.

Wykham came again.

This time Geoffrey refused to receive him. He stood below the terrace and shouted up at the shuttered windows.

“Where is she, Brent?”

Viola heard him from her sitting-room.

“Where is Margaret? She came to your house! What have you done with her?”

Geoffrey appeared upon the steps.

“Remove that man.”

Two servants approached Wykham, but he raised his stick.

“She told me! The precipice—the lynchpin—everything! If she is harmed, look beneath his happiness!”

The last words rang across the terrace. Viola, unseen behind the curtain, pressed both hands to her breast.

Geoffrey descended three steps.

“You are mad.”

“Then open your doors!”

“Go, before I have you confined.”

Wykham laughed.

“Confine me? You cannot confine the dead.”

He allowed himself to be led away, still laughing. Geoffrey returned indoors and found Viola waiting.

“Who is Margaret?”

“No one.”

“A dead woman is not no one.”

“He is attempting to frighten you.”

“He has succeeded.”

Geoffrey took her hands, but she trembled at his touch.

“Viola, this fellow’s sister travelled with me as companion to an elderly lady. She died in Switzerland. Her brother has made a romance of it because drunkenness requires grievances, as fire requires fuel.”

“Was she beautiful?”

The question, trivial beside murder, was inevitable.

“Yes.”

“Did she love thee?”

“I do not know.”

“Didst thou love her?”

“No.”

It was the smoothness of the denial which wounded her. Truth sometimes stammers under accusation; practised falsehood has the composure of innocence.

That night Viola dreamed of a woman whose face she could not see. The woman sat at the foot of the bed combing hair which was gold above the comb and grey below it. Each stroke lengthened the hair until it covered the floor, passed beneath the door, and descended the stair.

Within a week Viola fell ill.

VI

The physician attributed her fever to anxiety and her condition. He recommended quiet, nourishing broths, and the exclusion of unpleasant persons—a prescription which medicine has offered since antiquity and domestic life has rarely been able to fulfil.

Viola would not name the cause of her terror. Sometimes she begged Geoffrey to take her away; sometimes she clung to the thought of remaining at Brent’s Rock until the child was born. Her imagination fastened upon the house. She heard whispering in the passages, though no one was there. She declared that something scratched beneath the floor of the old hall.

One afternoon, while Geoffrey slept exhausted in the library, she took the keys from his coat.

The hall was dim beneath a sky of snow. The scaffolding had been removed, but sheets covered the furniture, and the carved faces upon the chimney-piece looked down through dust. Viola advanced slowly. She had never examined the cracked hearthstone, yet she went directly to it.

From the fissure there rose a mass of hair.

It had grown nearly a foot above the floor, not in confusion but in smooth, gleaming tresses, as if the head beneath the stone were slowly ascending. Most was gold—the rich, tawny gold of grain under sunset—but through it ran one broad lock of silver-grey.

Viola knelt.

She did not touch it. Beauty, even when monstrous, exerts its own authority, and this hair was beautiful beyond anything living. It shone in the weak light; it stirred although the air was still.

Behind her a key struck the floor.

Geoffrey stood at the entrance.

“What dost thou?” she asked.

He crossed the hall, seized a piece of timber, and thrust the hair into the empty hearth. From his pocket he produced matches.

“No!” Viola cried.

The flame ran over the gold. It writhed, blackened, and vanished in an acrid smoke. Geoffrey stamped upon the remnants.

“What is it?” she whispered.

“Rubbish. Old stuffing from beneath the stone.”

“It is hair.”

“Do not be foolish.”

“It grew from the crack.”

He caught her arm too roughly.

“You will never enter this room again.”

She gazed at him, and he saw knowledge—not complete, not rational, but sufficient—form within her.

“Whose hair is it?”

He released her.

The look upon his face answered.

Viola uttered no accusation. She merely bent, picked up the keys, and placed them in his hand. Then she walked from the hall with the slow dignity of one who has received a mortal wound and will not disgrace herself by falling before the weapon.

Her fever increased that night. In delirium she spoke alternately in Italian and English. She asked an unseen woman for mercy. She promised to give up the house, the name, even the child, if only Geoffrey might be spared. At other moments she recoiled from him, crying, “Thy hands! Wash thy hands!”

He ceased to sleep. Each morning, before servants rose, he went to the hall and burned the growing hair. Each night it returned. Cutting it close only made it thrust more densely through the fissure; covering the stone with boards caused golden filaments to creep from underneath; placing a heavy chest upon it was useless, for by morning the hair emerged around the chest’s feet in delicate curls.

He considered lifting the slab and destroying the body. But imagination, so contemptible when it afflicts another, becomes formidable when it serves oneself. He could not face again that narrow hollow, the lime, the ruined face, the half-open eye. Nor could he remove the corpse without assistance and risk. The builders watched him. The servants whispered. Wykham prowled the roads. Exposure surrounded him like a tightening cord.

He began to hear Margaret’s voice.

It came not in words, but in the dry whisper of hair drawn across stone, in the settling of ash, in Viola’s breath against the pillow. Once, while shaving, he saw a golden thread lying upon his shoulder. He flung off his coat and searched every seam. There was nothing.

The next morning the foreman resigned.

“I don’t mind a cracked slab,” he said, standing hat in hand, “nor I don’t mind a master as changes orders. But I won’t work where what’s burned by night grows back by day. Men’s seen it. I’ve seen it. There’s things as mortar won’t keep down.”

“You are an ignorant fool.”

“Maybe, sir. But I’m an ignorant fool with children, and I means to go home to ’em.”

The workmen departed before noon.

Brent’s Rock grew silent.

VII

Snow enclosed the house. It lay upon the terraces, rounded the balustrades, and erased the road which descended towards the village. The cedars bore it like funeral plumes. No carriage came; no guest could have been received. Within, fires burned in occupied rooms while corridors remained icy, and the great hall, at the centre of the house, was locked like a secret in the centre of a mind.

Viola’s fever abated, but she did not recover. She had become very quiet. Her dark eyes appeared larger, her hands almost transparent. The physician expressed concern for the child and urged that she be moved to a warmer room. She refused.

“Not until it is finished,” she said.

“What must be finished?” Geoffrey asked.

She looked towards the wall behind which the hall lay.

“The growing.”

He pretended not to understand.

On the last night, the storm ceased. Moonlight flooded the snow and entered the house through every uncurtained pane. Geoffrey sat beside Viola’s bed, watching her sleep. Near midnight she opened her eyes.

“It is calling,” she said.

“Nothing is calling.”

“Take me to the hall.”

“No.”

She rose. Though weak, she possessed the calm resolution which had deserted him.

“If thou dost not take me, I go alone.”

“Viola, you are ill.”

“I know not where to turn. In this house every way leads to her. Take me.”

He should have summoned the nurse, restrained her, or confessed. Men often perceive, at the supreme moment, several possible roads to safety; character consists chiefly in choosing the fatal one. Geoffrey wrapped her in a dark cloak and led her downstairs.

The keys clicked loudly in the sleeping house.

When he opened the hall, moonlight lay in a broad silver path from the high windows to the hearth. Everything outside that path remained in obscurity. The cracked stone gleamed faintly.

The hair had grown again.

It rose in a shining mound, flowed over the flag, and extended in long tresses towards the centre of the room. Gold and grey mingled beneath the moon. No wind moved, yet the ends lifted and settled with a slow rhythm, like weeds beneath deep water.

Viola sighed.

“The growing gold.”

Geoffrey carried a lamp, oil, and a long iron poker. He set the lamp down.

“You wished to see it. You have seen. Now we go.”

“No.” She sank upon an old bench facing the hearth. “I will watch.”

His voice became harsh. “Watch what?”

“Whether it grows.”

“It cannot.”

“It has.”

He poured oil over the nearest strands and set them alight. Flame sprang blue, then yellow; the gold twisted into black knots and was consumed. But even while it burned, new hairs pressed through the crack beneath it, first as fine points, then lengthening, brightening, dividing. The fire died. The hair continued to grow.

Geoffrey struck it with the poker.

From beneath the stone came a sound.

It might have been lime settling. It might have been a sigh.

Viola closed her eyes.

“Tell me,” she said.

“There is nothing to tell.”

“Then I shall ask her.”

He turned furiously. “She is dead!”

The words rang to the rafters.

Viola opened her eyes again. “Yes.”

He dropped the poker.

For a long time neither spoke. Moonlight advanced across the flags as the moon climbed. The golden mass advanced also, so slowly that movement could not be seen, only increase. It reached the legs of the bench.

Geoffrey drew Viola against him. Whether the gesture arose from love, terror, or the instinct to place innocence between guilt and punishment cannot be known; motives in such moments mingle like corrupted waters. She rested her head upon his breast.

“Didst thou love her?” she murmured.

“No.”

“Then why didst thou fear her?”

He did not answer.

Viola’s fingers closed weakly upon his sleeve.

“Did she suffer?”

Still he was silent.

“Poor soul,” Viola whispered.

Those two words, containing pity for the woman he had betrayed, accomplished what accusation had failed to do. Geoffrey shuddered. His face contracted, and for an instant he seemed about to speak. Had he confessed then, perhaps no earthly mercy could have saved him, but the soul has interests distinct from the body. Pride intervened—the final inheritance of his race.

“She threatened you,” he said. “Remember that. Whatever I did, I did to protect you.”

Viola lifted her head.

“What didst thou do?”

He saw the trap into which fear had driven him.

“Nothing.”

She gazed at him sorrowfully and laid her head again upon his breast.

The hair reached his boots.

At first it touched them lightly, a few strands lying across the leather. He kicked them away. They returned, creeping over the floor with a softness more horrible than any violent motion. One curled around his ankle.

He started up, but Viola moaned.

“Do not leave me.”

He sat again. She was exhausted; within minutes her breathing deepened. Sleep descended upon her with unnatural suddenness. Her face, freed from fear, became young and peaceful.

The hall clock struck two.

The hair thickened around Geoffrey’s feet. He bent to tear it away, but each handful he broke was replaced by another. The golden strands wound between his boots, passed around his ankles, and drew tight. Grey hairs lay among them like wires.

He reached for the poker. It was beyond his grasp.

“Viola,” he whispered. “Wake.”

She did not move.

He dared not rise violently lest he cast her down. He tried to lift her, but weakness, or some deeper restraint, made her body heavy. The moonlight touched her closed lids and tranquil mouth.

The clock struck three.

Now the hair climbed above Geoffrey’s ankles. He felt its pressure through stockings and leather—not the inert pressure of cord, but a thousand minute contractions, patient and purposeful. He attempted to call out. Shame silenced the first cry; fear strangled the second.

Across the hall, at the hearth, the gold rose continually through the cracked stone.

He saw again the mountain road, the loosened pin in his hand, Margaret at the inn window watching him. He saw the carriage tilt, the horses rear, the wheel leap free. He saw her face at the moment she understood. Memory, which he had treated as a servant to be dismissed, returned as judge and executioner.

At four he screamed.

The cry passed through the locked doors and awakened the upper servants. They came with candles, but the key had been turned within. Geoffrey screamed once more—not loudly, but with such concentrated horror that those outside recoiled.

“Break it!” cried the housekeeper.

Men fetched tools. The oak resisted. Within the hall there followed a dragging sound, a blow, and then silence.

VIII

Dawn was whitening the windows when the door yielded.

The servants entered first, followed soon afterwards by the physician, who had been sent for through the snow. Wykham Delandre came also. How he learned what had happened no one could say; he appeared at the open doors without coat or hat, his boots bleeding from the frozen road.

No one attempted to prevent him.

Geoffrey and Viola sat together upon the bench before the hearth.

She lay against him as a sleeping bride might rest after a long journey. One hand was folded beneath her cheek; the other lay over the child she would never bear. There was no terror in her face. The physician afterwards said that her weakened heart had ceased during sleep.

Geoffrey was dead also.

His head was thrown back. His eyes were open, his lips drawn from his teeth, and upon his features remained an expression so extreme that those who saw it found themselves unable thereafter to speak of him without lowering their voices. He had not died peacefully. Whatever had approached him in that locked hall, he had watched it come.

Around his feet, his legs, and one rigid hand coiled a mass of golden hair. A silver-grey lock crossed his breast like a sash. The hair could not be cut with an ordinary knife; when at last the servants freed the bodies, it seemed to withdraw from their touch, sliding over the stones towards the hearth.

Wykham went to the cracked slab and knelt.

For once he was sober.

“Margaret,” he said.

The stone was raised before noon. Beneath it they found lime, earth, fragments of black cloth, and what remained of a woman whose abundant hair had survived corruption. No court was required to establish the main particulars. The evidence beneath the hearth, the missing lime, the foreman’s account, Margaret’s message, and Wykham’s testimony formed a history sufficiently plain even for those who had found it convenient not to understand.

Whether Margaret had truly been Geoffrey’s wife was never proved. A priest near Turin remembered a ceremony, but records had disappeared; a witness had died; Geoffrey’s papers contained nothing. Society, having forgiven the living man, showed itself severe towards the dead one. The same persons who had dismissed Margaret as an adventuress now called her “that unfortunate lady,” for compassion becomes inexpensive when its object can no longer enter a drawing-room.

Viola was buried in the Brent vault. By Wykham’s insistence, Margaret was laid among the Delandres, beneath a stone bearing only her name and the words: **She Returned**.

Wykham lived three years longer. He ceased drinking, though reform brought him neither cheerfulness nor peace. He sold Mere Farm, paid what debts he could, and spent much of his time beside his sister’s grave. Those who had known him as a violent man came almost to respect his silence.

Brent’s Rock passed to a distant cousin who had neither money nor inclination to inhabit it. The renovations were abandoned. Rain entered through the unfinished eastern roof; ivy loosened the masonry; owls nested in the gallery. Yet the old hall remained dry. The cracked hearthstone was replaced, then covered by two heavier slabs and sealed with iron.

This proved useless.

For many years shepherds crossing the hill at dawn reported seeing something bright at the broken windows of the hall. Boys from the village entered once on a dare and fled without their lantern, declaring that long hair hung from the chimney-piece and moved across the floor though no person stood there. Such tales, repeated by the ignorant, are naturally enlarged; repeated by the educated, they are called symbolism and considered harmless.

But when Brent’s Rock was finally opened for sale, the surveyor found in the old hall a thin golden filament protruding between the iron-bound stones. He pulled it, supposing it to be wire.

It lengthened in his hand.

Then came a grey strand, and another; and beneath the sealed hearth something stirred with the patient persistence of truth beneath a lie.

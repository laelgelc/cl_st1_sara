On a mild Sunday morning in April, George Bendemann sat at the writing desk in his room and looked beyond the open window toward the river. The houses on the opposite bank, low and pale in the clear light, seemed almost to have been set there overnight, so sharply did their roofs stand against the spring sky. Along the embankment the new leaves had begun to show. A delivery cart crossed the distant bridge, its driver leaning forward as though the empty road required particular attention.

George had just finished a letter to a childhood friend who had lived for almost three years in Russia.

He laid his pen beside the inkstand, read the last paragraph again, and then folded his hands over the sheets. Although the letter was complete, he did not yet put it into its envelope. He could still change it. Indeed, the purpose for which he had written might, upon examination, prove to be the very reason not to send it.

His friend had settled in St. Petersburg with the intention of establishing a commercial agency. At first his letters had been full of plans, appointments, promising introductions and calculations written down to the last ruble. But the plans had narrowed, the appointments failed to bring results, the introductions had turned out to concern men no better situated than himself, and lately even the calculations had disappeared. He complained of the unfamiliar customs, of the long winter, of the unreliability of employees, of a persistent illness that appeared to affect his skin and gave his face, so George gathered, a yellowish color. He had no family there and almost no social connections. He lived alone and worked alone, if what he did could still be called work.

Why did he not return? George had asked this more than once, though not directly. A direct request might have implied that the friend had failed and needed to be rescued, while a casual suggestion would have been too easily overlooked. To return after such an absence, without money and without a position, would not have been simple. Old acquaintances would pity him, which was perhaps worse than ridicule, and younger men would know nothing of him. He would be a foreigner in his own town. And if he came back only because George had advised it, and then failed again, would George not be responsible? Better, perhaps, to leave him where he was, where failure could still be attributed to distance, climate and local conditions.

For these reasons George had written cautiously. He reported small events that could neither wound nor excite: the reconstruction of a warehouse near the harbor, the illness of a former schoolmaster, the marriage of an acquaintance whom the friend scarcely remembered. More important matters he withheld. He did not describe the growth of the family business, although in the last two years its turnover had nearly doubled. He did not mention that the number of employees had increased from five to eleven, that contracts once considered beyond their capacity now arrived without solicitation, or that he had recently been able to reject a proposal which his father, only a few years earlier, would have accepted with gratitude.

This concealment had not begun as deceit. After his mother’s death, when George assumed a larger share of the management, the changes occurred gradually. One successful quarter followed another; an inefficient clerk was dismissed; two new customers were secured; a rival’s temporary insolvency opened a useful route into the northern districts. How could one announce each step? By the time the improvement was undeniable, to speak of it seemed boastful. The friend’s letters, meanwhile, had grown shorter and more exhausted. George continued to answer warmly, but without figures.

He had also concealed his engagement.

Three months earlier he had become engaged to Frieda Brandenfeld, the daughter of a prosperous family newly arrived in the town. Frieda was intelligent, practical, and openly affectionate in a way that sometimes embarrassed him and sometimes appeared to him the chief proof that he had escaped the uncertain life of his youth. Her father owned warehouses and maintained useful contacts abroad, but George had rejected, with some irritation, the suggestion that commercial advantage had influenced him. He could have married without such advantage. That Frieda brought it was fortunate, nothing more.

She had not understood his silence toward the friend.

“If he is really your friend,” she had said, “he should know.”

“He is my friend precisely because I cannot tell him everything as if I were presenting an annual report.”

“Then what may he know?”

“What is of use to him.”

“And who decides that?”

George had laughed and kissed her forehead. “Unfortunately, the postal service has left that responsibility to me.”

But she had not laughed. “Perhaps you are ashamed of me.”

The absurdity of this reproach had made him immediately promise to write. Yet even afterward he delayed, not from shame, certainly, but because the proper form of the announcement would not present itself. Should he describe Frieda? Then every praise might sound like a comparison with the friend’s loneliness. Should he merely state the fact? That would be cold. Should he invite him to the wedding? An invitation he could not accept might be crueler than silence. And if he accepted? George pictured the friend arriving thin and prematurely aged, wearing some foreign-cut coat, moving among prosperous relatives who would ask questions with the benevolent persistence of people certain of their own good fortune. Frieda would welcome him, no doubt; she had insisted upon it. But her welcome itself might humiliate him.

At last George had decided that concealment was no longer kindness. In the letter before him he had written:

“I have saved my best news for the end. I am engaged to Fraulein Frieda Brandenfeld, whom you may remember me mentioning in connection with her family’s arrival here. The wedding will probably take place in early summer. I hope this news will not alter anything between us except, perhaps, to give you another friend in your native town. Frieda sends her greetings and says that a truly satisfactory letter from her will follow. You will understand that your presence at the wedding would make us very happy, though you must decide entirely according to your circumstances and must not feel constrained in any direction.”

The paragraph was considerate. Perhaps it was too considerate. “According to your circumstances” meant, of course, according to his poverty. “Not feel constrained” emphasized the invitation’s impracticability. “Another friend” suggested that the first friend, George, had not been enough. Still, every formulation permitted such an interpretation if read with sufficient bitterness, and one could not write to bitterness itself.

George placed the letter in its envelope and sealed it.

He remained seated for another minute. On the river, a small excursion boat moved upstream, though the season for excursions had scarcely begun. Its deck was empty except for a sailor coiling rope near the stern. George watched until it passed behind the roofs.

He decided to show the letter to his father.

This was not his usual practice. His correspondence had always been private, and in recent years his father had withdrawn almost entirely from matters not directly brought before him. Yet because the friend had known the family, had eaten many times at their table while George’s mother was alive, it seemed proper that the father should be informed. Besides, George told himself, it would please the old man to be consulted. If he objected, the objection could be considered; if he approved, the letter would be posted without further uncertainty.

George crossed the passage to his father’s room.

He had not entered it in the morning for several weeks. They met at the office, where the father still appeared irregularly, and at meals, which were served in the bright dining room overlooking the street. The father’s private room lay at the back of the apartment. Its single window faced a high brick wall, and even on this clear morning the room was dark.

His father sat by the window reading a newspaper held close to his face. He wore a long woolen nightshirt, though it was nearly eleven, and over his shoulders lay a blanket that had slipped down on one side. On the table stood the remains of breakfast: an empty cup, a broken roll and a dish with a thin yellow line of butter around its edge. The air smelled of medicine, old fabric and the extinguished wick of a lamp.

“Ah, George,” the father said, without lowering the paper.

“It is a beautiful morning outside.”

“Is it?”

“You should have the window open.”

“The wall would appreciate it.”

George smiled, but the remark, spoken without humor, made the smile useless. He stepped nearer and noticed that the newspaper was yesterday’s. His father’s white hair had grown over the collar of the nightshirt. The broad face, once red from weather and business, appeared gray and folded. When George was a child, this face had bent over account books and seemed capable of detecting an error before the page was turned. Now the toothless mouth remained slightly open even when the father was silent.

“I wanted to tell you,” George said, touching the sealed letter in his pocket, “that I have written to my friend in Russia about my engagement.”

His father lowered the newspaper to his lap.

“To your friend in Russia.”

“Yes.”

“About the engagement.”

“Yes, Father.”

The father looked at him for so long that George became conscious of standing in the narrow strip of light from the door. He moved a chair aside with his foot but did not sit.

“Why have you come to tell me this?”

“I thought you might want to know. You remember him well. He used to visit us.”

“I remember many people.”

“I have corresponded with him regularly.”

“Regularly?”

“As regularly as possible. His replies are not always prompt.”

The father folded the newspaper, first lengthwise and then once again, pressing the crease with his thumb. “George, listen to me. I am your father. You need not make a business statement. Tell me the truth. Do you really have this friend in Russia?”

The question was so unreasonable that George at first believed he had misunderstood.

“Whether I have him?”

“Do not deceive me.”

“I am not deceiving you. You know him. He sat at our table. Mother liked him.”

“Your mother is dead.”

“I know that.”

“Then do not bring her as a witness.”

George drew the chair toward him and sat down. He now saw that his father’s nightshirt was not properly fastened at the bottom, and a length of bare shin showed above one slipper. This exposure, combined with the solemnity of the interrogation, seemed almost comical, and George had to look at the breakfast table to preserve a respectful expression.

“You must not excite yourself,” he said. “Of course the friend exists. Perhaps I have spoken of him less lately because there has been little to report. He is having difficulties. That is precisely why I hesitated to tell him of my engagement.”

“You hesitated.”

“I did not want to hurt him.”

“And now?”

“Now it seemed worse to conceal it.”

His father nodded slowly. “Worse for whom?”

George took out the letter. “Here it is. You may read it.”

The father did not reach for it. “You have put things very cleverly.”

“There is nothing clever in it.”

“No? You have done well in the business, I hear.”

“You know the accounts.”

“I know what I am shown.”

“Father, everything is available to you. You can come to the office at any time.”

“To your office.”

“Our office.”

“Your office,” the father repeated.

George felt the beginning of an old weariness. Conversations with his father often entered this kind of circle. The old man complained of exclusion, yet rejected every invitation to participate; he accused George of taking command, yet refused responsibility when it was offered. Formerly George had argued. More recently he allowed the accusations to pass, because weakness was entitled to its own language, and because the business could not wait for domestic justice.

He looked around the dim room. The bed had not been made. A shirt hung from the brass knob of the wardrobe. On the windowsill dust had gathered around a bottle of drops. How had he failed to notice the condition in which his father lived? True, a servant attended him, meals were regular, and nothing necessary had been denied. But necessities themselves could become a method of neglect. George slept in the front room, received visitors, took the air, became engaged, while his father remained behind the wall.

“You should not stay in this room,” George said. “It is unhealthy.”

His father’s eyes narrowed.

“I mean it. You should move into my room. It is brighter. I can use the guest room. And we ought to call the doctor again.”

“The doctor.”

“Yes. You have lost weight.”

“I am still heavy enough.”

“You have scarcely eaten.”

“I ate what was given.”

George stood. The practical decision relieved him. “Come, then. Let me help you dress. Or better, you should lie down first. I will have the room aired and breakfast brought again.”

“Are you changing the subject?”

“There is no subject to change. I came to tell you about the letter. I have told you. Now I see you are not well.”

“I see.”

The father attempted to rise, placing both hands on the arms of the chair, but after lifting himself a little he sank back. George went behind him, drew the blanket away and put an arm beneath his shoulders. The body under the nightshirt was surprisingly solid, though it yielded without resistance. As George raised him, the father’s watch chain caught on a button and stretched across his stomach.

“Careful,” the father said.

“I have you.”

“So you have.”

The old man stood bent forward, his head against George’s chest. A faint sour odor came from his hair. George was struck by tenderness and shame. When had he last embraced his father? Not since childhood, perhaps, and this was not an embrace but a transfer, as if some heavy article had to be moved from one place to another. The father’s hands hung at his sides.

“You are much weaker than you admit,” George said.

The father whispered something.

“What is it?”

George bent his head.

“Have I been covered?”

“Not yet. We are going to the bed.”

“Will you cover me?”

“Yes.”

“Completely?”

“If you wish.”

George led him the few steps to the bed. The father’s feet shuffled, and once the slipper on his exposed foot came off. George stooped to replace it, but the father said sharply, “Leave it.”

At the bed, George unfastened the blanket, opened the sheets and helped the old man sit. The nightshirt slid upward, exposing a thick thigh marked by a long pale scar. George remembered being told that the wound had been received during military service, though the account had varied as to the campaign and even as to which leg had been injured. The father caught George looking at it.

“You have forgotten that too,” he said.

“No.”

“You have forgotten everything before you.”

George lifted his father’s legs onto the mattress. The old man lay back. For a moment he looked extraordinarily small, his bare foot projecting from beneath the nightshirt, his beard flattened to one side. George drew the sheet up to his shoulders and then spread the blanket over it.

“Am I covered?” the father asked.

“Yes.”

“Properly?”

“Yes, Father.”

The father gripped the edge of the blanket. “No!”

With a movement so sudden that George stepped backward, he flung the blanket away. It opened in the air and dropped across the floor. The father thrust himself upright. One hand touched the wall for balance, but only for an instant; then he stood on the mattress, his head nearly reaching the ceiling.

“You wanted to cover me,” he cried. “I am not covered yet. Even if this is the last strength I have, it is enough for you, more than enough!”

“Father, get down. You will fall.”

“I know your friend. Do you hear? I know your friend in Russia.”

George stared upward. The nightshirt hung from the father’s raised arms like a torn flag. His uncovered leg was planted wide on the mattress, and the scar shone against the skin.

“You know him, of course,” George said. “That is what I have been telling you.”

“I know him better than you do. He would have been a son after my own heart. That is why you betrayed him year after year.”

“I have not betrayed anyone.”

“You thought I could not see. The old father sits in his dark room, the old father signs where the son points, the old father eats his softened bread. Meanwhile the son closes his letters, closes the office, closes the mother’s grave, and goes out with his girl.”

George put a hand on the bedpost. “You are making yourself ill.”

“Ill? I had to be ill. It suited you. A strong father would have disturbed your arrangements.”

“What arrangements?”

“Your success! Your marriage! Your friendship kept in a box and taken out on Sundays!”

The father laughed. His toothless mouth opened widely, but the sound was deep and forceful. He slapped his chest, and the watch chain jumped.

George thought: The nightshirt is open behind. He will step on the blanket. He is an old man standing on a bed. If I call the servant, we can put him down. These facts were clear. Yet the room no longer seemed to contain any servant, passage or neighboring apartment. There was only the bed, elevated like a platform, and the father upon it.

“You say you know my friend,” George said. “When did you last speak to him?”

“Speak? Must speech cross a room to satisfy you?”

“You have not seen him since he left.”

“I correspond with him.”

George almost smiled. “You correspond with him.”

“Yes. I write to him. I have written from the beginning.”

“That is impossible.”

“Impossible because you did not permit it?”

“Because I would know.”

“You know only what serves you. He knows what you are. I told him.”

The father’s voice filled the low room. Outside, from the courtyard, came the regular beating of a rug. Each blow sounded distant and official.

“What could you have told him?” George asked.

“How you sat here after your mother’s funeral and calculated which customers could be transferred into your name. How you waited until my hand shook before correcting me in front of the clerks. How you sent your friend empty letters while feeding yourself on every good thing that happened. He showed me your letters.”

“He showed you.”

“Every one.”

“He is in Russia.”

“Russia is not beyond a father’s reach.”

George pressed his fingers into the bedpost. His father’s claim was ridiculous, but ridicule required a place from which to laugh, and George no longer possessed one. There had been occasions when letters from Russia arrived with a corner bent or the seal loosened. He had attributed this to the post. His father came sometimes to the office before opening hours. A duplicate key existed. None of this proved anything; because it proved nothing, it seemed to prove more.

“You could not have written without an address,” George said.

“I had the address before you did.”

“From whom?”

“From him.”

“That goes in a circle.”

“Only for you. You run in circles and call it trade.”

The father lowered his arms. For an instant he swayed. George stepped forward instinctively, but the old man stamped on the mattress.

“Stay where you are!”

George stopped.

“Do you imagine he reads your announcements with gratitude?” the father continued. “He reads them to me. Your little pieces of news. A warehouse repaired. A teacher coughing. Someone married whom no one remembers. You keep him starving and send him crumbs described on expensive paper.”

“I wrote according to his circumstances.”

“His circumstances! You made his circumstances. You drove him away.”

“He chose to go.”

“Because you left no room beside you.”

“We were both young. I had no authority over him.”

“You had enough authority to forget him.”

“I did not forget him. I wrote.”

“You wrote concealment. You wrote pity. Pity is the bookkeeping of the selfish: one enters another man’s loss and finds oneself rich.”

George wanted to answer, but the sentence appeared already to include any answer he might give. He saw Frieda standing in the Brandenfelds’ drawing room, turning her engagement ring so that the stone caught the light. He remembered telling her that the Russian friend would be pleased. Had he believed it? He had believed what was required at that moment. Was that deceit, or was it the ordinary arrangement by which one lived?

“And Frieda?” the father said, as if the thought had been spoken aloud. “Shall we discuss the bride?”

“You will not speak badly of her.”

“There he is! The protector! He could not protect his mother from loneliness, his friend from exile, his father from filth, but the young lady with the full purse—her he protects.”

George’s face became hot. “Frieda has nothing to do with this.”

“She has everything to do with it. She comes when the rooms are ready, when the business is cleaned, when the old woman is buried and the old man put to bed. She lifts her skirt across the threshold, and you call it love.”

“Stop.”

“Did you tell her about this room?”

“She has met you.”

“In the dining room. In daylight. Was I presented as founder of the firm or as an item no longer carried?”

“Stop!”

The father leaned forward, his enormous pupils fixed on George. “You came to ask my blessing.”

“I came to inform you.”

“Exactly! Not a blessing. Information. Notice is hereby given that the son has succeeded, the father has expired, and the friend abroad may attend at his own expense.”

George pulled the letter from his pocket. The envelope, damp from his hand, had begun to bend.

“This is what I wrote,” he said. “Read it. Read the actual words instead of inventing them.”

His father looked at the envelope and grinned. “Still sealed.”

“Yes.”

“And addressed?”

George glanced at it. The friend’s name and address were written plainly.

“Then post it,” the father said.

George did not move.

“Post it! Perhaps he will receive it among my letters. Perhaps he will compare them. He knows your hand, but he knows mine too. Mine has grown weak, you thought. Weak enough to pass beneath doors.”

“You have not written to him.”

“Ask him.”

“I will.”

“No, you will not.”

“Why not?”

“Because you know.”

The rug-beating in the courtyard ceased. In the silence George heard his father’s breathing, strong but uneven, and beneath it the ticking of the watch still fastened under the nightshirt. It seemed impossible that the small mechanical sound came from that body.

“What do I know?” George asked.

The father lowered himself slowly until he sat on the bed. Though the movement should have made him smaller, he appeared to grow. He arranged the nightshirt over his knees with care.

“You know that he has been my son longer than you have.”

George’s mouth opened.

“When you laughed with your customers, he sat with me. When you were dancing with your bride, he bent over my letters. When you measured your gains, he knew my losses. He understood your mother’s death. He mourned her properly.”

“You said he was a failure.”

“I said no such thing.”

“You described him as starving.”

“Starving men see clearly.”

“You contradict yourself.”

“A father contains contradictions. A son merely exploits them.”

George looked toward the door. It stood open, but the passage beyond was dark, though sunlight must still have been falling through the front rooms. He thought he could leave. He had only to turn, cross the passage and enter his own room, where the desk, the empty inkstand and the river remained. His father would shout after him, perhaps summon the servant, perhaps fall. Everything could then be explained as an attack brought on by weakness.

Yet if he left, he would be confirming the accusation of neglect. If he stayed, he submitted to the trial. The choice had been arranged so that either movement had already been judged.

“You are tired,” George said. “We can discuss this later.”

“Later? You have taken all the later for yourself.”

“I will postpone the wedding if that is what you want.”

The father’s face tightened with disgust. “Now he bargains.”

“I am not bargaining.”

“One month? Two? What allowance for the father? What discount for the friend?”

“I said I would postpone it.”

“You would postpone anything except yourself.”

George let the letter fall onto the chair. “What do you want from me?”

At once the father became still.

The question, which George had intended as a protest, seemed to have been the necessary formal request. The father placed both feet on the floor. One was bare, the other slippered. He stood without assistance. Bent no longer, he was nearly as tall as George. He came to the edge of the bed and pointed at him.

“I wanted you to ask.”

His voice had lost its theatrical force. It was quiet now, and therefore more extensive; it appeared to speak not only from the old man’s mouth but from the walls, the courtyard, the office ledgers, the unopened graves.

“You have thought only of yourself,” he said. “Do not deny it. A son may be selfish, a merchant must be selfish, a bridegroom is permitted happiness. You collected permissions until you believed yourself innocent. But innocence is not assembled from permissions.”

George’s back touched the wall.

“You betrayed your friend by making him the audience for a life you concealed. You betrayed your mother by turning her death into an opportunity. You betrayed me by feeding me, clothing me and removing me from the business as one removes an obsolete machine. And you betrayed the woman you intend to marry, because you offered her a man whose accounts were false.”

“No,” George said.

The father raised one finger.

“No,” George repeated, though more softly.

“I do not say you are entirely without love. That would make the matter simple. You have love enough to know what you destroy.”

The father stepped closer. His bare foot came down on the fallen blanket, grinding it against the floor.

“I am still your father.”

“Yes.”

“And you are still my son.”

“Yes.”

“Then you will hear my judgment.”

George saw the scar on the father’s leg, the blackened nails of the bare foot, the yellow teeth absent from the open mouth, the watch chain trembling against the wool. These details should have diminished him. Instead they were ornaments of office. His filth was a robe; his weakness, evidence of sacrifice; his contradictions, the reach of law.

The father lifted both arms.

“For betrayal of your friend, for contempt of your father, for the life you have stolen and called your own, I condemn you to death by drowning.”

The words were spoken plainly.

For one second George remained against the wall. Then he turned and ran.

He crossed the passage so quickly that his shoulder struck the doorframe. In his room the window stood open, the sealed letter lay behind him, and the river shone between the buildings. He did not stop for his coat or hat. At the front door he collided with the maid, who carried a tray of clean glasses. She cried out, and one glass fell but did not break.

“Mr. George!”

He was already on the stairs.

He took the first flight in three jumps, caught the banister, swung around the landing and continued downward. From above came no pursuing step. He could not tell whether his father had called after him. The building seemed filled with the sentence, and every floor merely repeated it in a lower register.

At the entrance an old tenant moved aside with her basket. George passed her and ran into the street.

The morning had advanced. Church bells were ending; shop shutters rattled upward; children in clean clothes walked between their parents. George ran through them without apology. Once a man seized his sleeve, perhaps thinking he had stolen something, but the cloth slipped free. A tram crossed ahead, forcing him to stop. He stood at the curb while its windows passed, each framing a separate, indifferent face.

He could still turn toward Frieda’s house.

The thought came with practical clarity. Frieda would listen. She would send for a doctor, return with him to the apartment, take command of the servants, open the windows. His father would be found in his nightshirt, perhaps asleep, perhaps ashamed. The letter could be burned. The wedding could proceed or be delayed according to medical advice. No court recognized such a judgment.

The tram passed.

George ran toward the river.

He knew now that he had always been running toward it, not only since leaving the room but since the morning began, perhaps since his friend’s departure, perhaps earlier. Yet the idea did not feel grand. His breath hurt. One shoelace had come loose. He had forgotten to close the apartment door.

At the bridge the Sunday traffic was heavy. Carts, bicycles and pedestrians moved in both directions. A motorcar sounded its horn behind a wagon loaded with flower boxes. George pushed between two women and reached the railing.

The river below was darker than it had appeared from his window. The spring current pressed around the bridge piers, carrying sticks, pale foam and one overturned crate. On the far bank, the houses remained sharp and harmless beneath the sky.

George gripped the iron rail. It was cold despite the sun.

For a moment his body resisted. His lungs continued their rapid work; his hands tightened; his feet sought balance. This resistance seemed to belong to someone innocent, someone who had not attended the trial and did not understand that judgment had already been given.

He swung one leg over the railing.

A bus approached, rumbling across the bridge. Its upper windows flashed. No one called to him. He lowered himself on the outer side and hung by both hands, as he had sometimes done for amusement when he was a boy, though never above such depth.

He thought of his mother setting bread on the table. He thought of his father upright on the bed. He thought of the friend in Russia opening letters in a room whose furniture George had never seen. He thought of Frieda’s hand beneath his, warm and slightly impatient.

“Dear parents,” he said, “I have always loved you.”

Then his hands released the rail.

At that same instant, traffic streamed across the bridge in an almost unbroken line.

When Colonel Anstruther inherited Westfield Hall from his uncle, it was generally agreed that the estate required a woman’s hand. This phrase, which means a great many things and commonly costs a great deal of money, was repeated with particular confidence by the Colonel’s neighbours. Mrs. Anstruther justified them.

Within eighteen months she had altered the morning-room curtains, reopened a disused drive, removed two cedar trees which had darkened the south windows since the reign of George III, and caused hot water to be carried to parts of the house where, according to the housekeeper, it had never been expected to go. The Colonel offered no serious opposition. He hunted when there was hunting, played golf when there was not, and had acquired the useful domestic art of saying, “If you think so, my dear,” without necessarily committing himself to an opinion.

Her attention next fell upon a piece of ground beyond the kitchen garden, between the churchyard wall and a belt of evergreen shrubbery.

“I mean to have roses there,” she said at breakfast one April morning.

The Colonel, who was reading an account of a disputed boundary in the county paper, looked over the top of it.

“Where exactly?”

“In that dreadful little wilderness beyond the old yews.”

“Oh—that place.”

“I don’t know why you say ‘that place’ in that tone. It has a south-west aspect and is completely sheltered.”

“Has it? I thought it rather damp.”

“It is damp because nobody has attended to it. Collins has allowed the drains to choke.”

This was one of those statements which leave a prudent husband with little room for profitable movement. The Colonel returned to the boundary dispute.

Mrs. Anstruther continued:

“There are remains of some sort of summer-house there—rotten boards, broken bricks, and one extraordinary post in the middle. All that must come away. We shall have the ground trenched, put in proper drainage, and make a circular bed with standards.”

“Very nice.”

“Not merely nice, Henry. It will be the best rose garden in the county.”

“I dare say it will.”

“The Bentleys have nothing but hybrid perpetuals. One might as well grow cabbages.”

The Colonel said that he had always considered Mrs. Bentley’s roses rather fine, but, being reminded that he knew nothing whatever about roses, conceded the point.

Later that morning Mrs. Anstruther sent for Collins.

Collins had been head gardener at Westfield for nearly thirty years. He was a broad, slow man with an expression of settled melancholy, as though the whole vegetable kingdom had disappointed him privately. He listened to the rose-garden scheme with his cap in both hands and his eyes directed toward a pattern in the carpet.

“Well?” said Mrs. Anstruther.

“Yes, ma’am.”

“You understand what I want done?”

“Yes, ma’am. You wants the old place cleared.”

“Entirely cleared.”

“There’ll be the laurels, ma’am.”

“The laurels are to come out.”

“Some of them roots goes a long way.”

“Then they must be followed.”

“And the little bit of building.”

“It is not a building. It is a heap of rotten wood.”

Collins rubbed one thumb upon the other.

“There was a summer-’ouse there once, ma’am.”

“So I gather.”

“Built very strong, they do say.”

“It cannot have been very strong if nothing remains but three boards and a post.”

“No, ma’am. Not now.”

There was a pause. Mrs. Anstruther had learned that pauses of this sort were a form of resistance with Collins, and she refused to encourage them.

“You will begin on Monday.”

“If it’s agreeable to you, ma’am, I’d sooner leave that centre post.”

“Why?”

“Well, ma’am, it’s drove in wonderful deep.”

“Then you must dig deeper.”

“It ain’t in the way, not precisely. Standards could go round it. Ivy might be trained up.”

“I do not want ivy trained up a decayed post in the centre of my rose garden.”

“No, ma’am.”

“Is there any practical reason for leaving it?”

Collins considered the adjective.

“Practical, ma’am?”

“Yes. Does it support anything? Is it a boundary mark? Does it cover a pipe?”

“Not as I knows on.”

“Then remove it.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

He made no movement to go.

“Was there something else?”

Collins looked toward the window, though what assistance the window could afford him was not apparent.

“My grandfather, he worked here under old Mr. Wilkins. He used to say as that place wasn’t one to meddle with.”

“Your grandfather’s opinions on landscape gardening are not binding upon me.”

“No, ma’am. Certainly not. Only he did say—”

“What did he say?”

Collins swallowed.

“He said what was put down there was put down with a purpose.”

Mrs. Anstruther smiled.

“Drainage, very likely. You may go.”

At luncheon she gave the Colonel an account of this conversation. He was amused, though not, she thought, sufficiently so.

“The servants in these old places always have a story,” he said.

“He did not produce one. He merely suggested that his grandfather objected to roses.”

“Possibly his grandfather had known the place before the summer-house was pulled down.”

“Do you know why it was pulled down?”

“No. Wilkins did it, I believe.”

The Wilkins family had occupied Westfield for forty years before the Colonel’s uncle bought it. Of the former household, only Miss Mary Wilkins remained in the neighbourhood. She lived with a widowed cousin twelve miles away and was occasionally met at archæological meetings, garden parties, and other places where gentlewomen with good memories are apt to be useful.

As it happened, Mrs. Anstruther met her that week at the opening of a village reading-room.

Miss Wilkins was a slight, grey-haired woman whose manner combined timidity with a curious exactness. She remembered the names of servants dead for half a century and could identify every bedroom in houses which had since been rebuilt.

After the speeches, Mrs. Anstruther drew her aside.

“I have been wanting to ask you about Westfield. Do you remember a summer-house near the churchyard?”

Miss Wilkins’s expression altered so quickly that Mrs. Anstruther was sorry she had spoken. The change was not dramatic; she merely ceased to look like an elderly lady at a public function and appeared, for one instant, much younger and much more frightened.

“Yes,” she said. “I remember it.”

“I am clearing that piece of ground for roses.”

“Are you?”

“There is scarcely anything left now. Only some woodwork and a post.”

“A post in the middle?”

“Yes.”

Miss Wilkins put down her teacup.

“You are not taking that up?”

“Certainly. Why not?”

“I think—I should leave it.”

“That is precisely what Collins says. But nobody gives me a reason.”

Miss Wilkins looked about them. The chairman of the parish council was explaining a new system of lending tickets to the Rector, and two ladies were disputing the proper thickness of tea for invalids. Nothing could have been more public or more secure. Yet Miss Wilkins lowered her voice.

“I can tell you what happened to us there, if you care to hear it. It may be nothing. Children are impressionable, and we had been reading unsuitable books.”

“I should like to hear.”

“My brother Frank and I used to play in the summer-house. It was an ugly place even then: octagonal, with trellis-work over the windows, and painted a colour which may once have been green. There were seats all round. The centre post went up through the roof and ended in an iron ornament. It was always damp. One could smell wet leaves even in July.

“We disliked it, but for some reason we were always daring one another to go there. Frank was two years older than I. One afternoon we had been quarrelling, and I told him he was afraid to sit alone in the summer-house until I fetched him. He said he would stay for an hour. I went away and forgot him.”

Miss Wilkins paused.

“When I remembered, it was nearly dark. I ran back. Frank was asleep on the floor, or so I thought. I shook him. He woke screaming, and struck at me.

“Afterward he said he had dreamed that he was in a great room full of people. He could not see their faces clearly, but he knew they hated him. There was someone above him asking questions. Frank tried to answer, only the words came out wrong, and each answer made the people laugh. At last a voice said that he had been found guilty.

“He was taken out. He said there was snow underfoot, and a bell sounding. A clergyman walked beside him, reading, but the wind turned the pages too quickly. There was a high wooden thing before him. He knew he was to die, though he could not understand what crime he had committed.

“He remembered one sentence very clearly. Someone close to him said, ‘This is justice.’ Frank told me that the voice was pleased.”

“Poor child,” said Mrs. Anstruther. “He must have read an account of an execution.”

“That is what my father said. Frank had been forbidden to look at a book of old trials in the library, and had of course looked at it. But there was another thing.”

Miss Wilkins folded her gloves with great care.

“When I went to wake him, I heard someone speaking inside the centre post.”

“Inside it?”

“I put my ear against the wood. It was foolish, but children do foolish things. The voice was very low and breathless. It said, ‘You pull, and I’ll push.’ Then it laughed.”

The little speeches from the platform had ended, and chairs were being moved. The scraping noise caused Miss Wilkins to start.

“I told my father that night. He went to the summer-house with a lantern and two of the men. They found nothing. But next day he ordered the building demolished. The centre post could not be got out. They dug round it and put ropes on it. One rope broke, and a labourer hurt his shoulder. My father told them to cut it off at the level of the floor and leave the rest.”

“And did he explain why?”

“No. He was not a man who explained himself to children. But old Barlow, who had been butler to my grandfather, saw the work being done. He said, ‘Better so, sir. He’s fast enough while that holds.’ My father was very angry and dismissed him soon afterward.”

“Who was ‘he’?”

“I never knew.”

Mrs. Anstruther thanked Miss Wilkins. She did not repeat the story to Collins, because it would only encourage him, but she told her husband as they dressed for dinner.

“A nightmare produced by forbidden reading,” she concluded. “And the voice was probably wind in a crack.”

“Probably,” said the Colonel.

“You don’t sound convinced.”

“I am perfectly convinced that children have nightmares and wooden posts crack.”

“Then you agree that there is no reason to preserve the thing.”

“I don’t see how that follows.”

“My dear Henry, one cannot plan a garden round every childish fancy that happened in 1860.”

“No, naturally not.”

“Collins begins on Monday.”

In fact, Collins did not begin on Monday. He reported that one of the men had cut his hand, that the small tools required sharpening, and that the barrow wheel was not fit to be trusted. On Tuesday it rained. On Wednesday Mrs. Anstruther went down herself and found the men transplanting currant bushes in another part of the garden.

This produced a brief and decisive interview.

By Friday the laurels had been grubbed up, the rotten seats burned, and the surviving walls reduced to a pile of bricks. The ground looked raw and unexpectedly large. The post remained.

It was not an imposing object. About three feet of dark wood projected from the soil, grey at the top where it had been sawn through, and deeply channelled down the sides. I cannot say what timber it was; experts in such matters are commonly positive until shown the thing itself. Collins declared it to be oak, while the carpenter called it chestnut. Both agreed that it was extraordinarily sound.

A trench had been dug around it to a depth of nearly four feet without reaching the end.

“Whoever put that in meant it to stop,” said the carpenter.

“Or meant something else to stop,” muttered one of the labourers.

Mrs. Anstruther, who had come to inspect the operation, heard him.

“What do you mean, Andrews?”

“Nothing, ma’am.”

Collins glared at the man.

They fastened a chain about the post and attached it to a small traction engine borrowed from a neighbouring farm. The first pull merely tightened the chain. At the second, the post moved perhaps half an inch. There came from underground a sound like the tearing of coarse cloth.

One of the horses in the adjoining paddock broke into a gallop.

“Again,” said Mrs. Anstruther.

Collins wiped his forehead.

The engine strained. Earth crumbled inward. The post rose slowly, black and glistening below the soil, and of a length that no one had expected. When at last it came free, it fell on its side with a heavy, hollow report.

At that moment a cry sounded from the shrubbery.

It was not loud. Several of those present afterward described it as the call of an owl, though owls are not usually heard at eleven o’clock on a bright spring morning. Collins dropped the chain.

“What was that?” Mrs. Anstruther asked.

“Bird, ma’am,” said the carpenter quickly.

Collins said nothing. He stared into the hole. At the bottom, mixed with clay, lay a few blackened fragments which might have been charcoal, and something whitish which Andrews said was lime.

The post was cut into lengths and burned. It did not burn readily. A thick, sour-smelling smoke crept close to the ground and drifted toward the churchyard.

That evening Collins was taken ill.

The doctor called it a gastric seizure complicated by shock. Collins, however, insisted that he had been struck from behind while locking the tool-house. There was no bruise, and no one had been near him. He was put to bed over the gardener’s cottage kitchen, where he lay sweating and asking his wife whether the doors were barred.

The first nocturnal disturbance occurred two days later.

The Colonel woke at about half-past one and heard voices on the lawn. At first he supposed that men were coming home from the public-house by way of the estate, a liberty he intended to correct. He went to the window.

The moon was behind cloud, and he could distinguish only the gravel sweep, the cedar stumps, and the pale line of the terrace. No one was visible.

The voices continued. They were distant, yet somehow numerous. One would rise above the others in a sharp question, followed by a muttering answer and a sudden movement of the whole sound, like a crowd shifting its feet.

He opened the window.

At once the voices ceased.

Then, from the direction of the churchyard, came three long cries. They resembled the call heard when the post was pulled up, but were nearer and more human.

The Colonel shut the window.

He said nothing at breakfast. Mrs. Anstruther, however, remarked that trespassers had been in the grounds.

“I heard them after one,” she said. “A great many men talking.”

“Did you?”

“Surely you heard them.”

“I heard something.”

“We must tell Parkins to watch the west gate.”

“Yes.”

“And there was an owl making a perfectly intolerable noise.”

“An owl?”

“What else could it have been?”

The Colonel buttered his toast.

That afternoon he walked to the clearing. The trench had been filled, and the first rose beds marked out with pegs and string. A load of manure stood nearby. Everything was practical, agricultural, and reassuring.

Nevertheless, he found himself counting the pegs. There were twelve round the central bed and one in the middle. He pulled up the centre peg and threw it into the shrubbery.

He could not have explained why.

The following night he dreamed.

He was standing in a room crowded to suffocation. The air was hot and smelt of damp wool and tallow. Faces pressed toward him, but whenever he attempted to look directly at one, another shoulder or hat intervened.

A man sat above him beneath a carved canopy. The man’s face remained in shadow. Only the hands were clear: large white hands, moist at the wrists, resting upon a red book.

“Did you hear him speak?” the judge asked.

The Colonel tried to say no.

“Yes,” said his own voice.

“Did he confess his treason?”

“No,” he attempted to answer.

“Yes,” said the voice again.

A delighted whisper travelled through the court.

The judge leaned forward. The shadow did not move from his face.

“You have heard the witness. Let justice proceed.”

The Colonel was seized from either side. He could not see who held him, but the grip was cruelly strong. He was dragged down steps and through a narrow passage. Outside, snow was falling.

This was what frightened him most afterward: not the court, nor the sentence, but the snow. Each flake remained separate and visible. Some settled upon the black sleeve of the clergyman walking beside him. The clergyman held a little book before his face, but the pages were blank.

They passed between lines of people. The crowd made no sound now. All watched a high platform ahead.

“Why?” the Colonel asked.

The clergyman turned one blank page.

“Because he wishes it.”

“Who?”

The clergyman looked back toward the building they had left.

A bell began to toll.

On the scaffold stood a broad figure in scarlet. Its face was covered, but the Colonel knew that beneath the covering it was smiling.

He woke with the bedclothes knotted round his arms. The room was bitterly cold, and the last vibration of a bell seemed to pass through the window glass.

At breakfast he looked ill enough for Mrs. Anstruther to notice.

“You have had a bad night.”

“Rather.”

“Was it the owl again?”

“No. A dream.”

He told her part of it.

As he spoke, her brisk expression became fixed.

“Henry, have you discussed Miss Wilkins’s story with anyone?”

“No.”

“Are you certain?”

“Of course.”

“You have reproduced Frank Wilkins’s dream.”

“So it seems.”

“Not merely the general idea. The snow, the clergyman, the execution.”

“I suppose you told me those things.”

“I did not tell you about the bell.”

“I may have supplied a bell. Executions often have them in books.”

Mrs. Anstruther was silent.

Then she said, with an air of having found a satisfactory drawer in which to put the matter:

“It is an instance of transferred impression.”

“Is it?”

“Miss Wilkins told me. I told you. The details remained in my mind though I did not consciously repeat them, and you received them.”

“How?”

“I do not pretend to know. Thought-reading has been demonstrated under controlled conditions.”

“Not usually through the breakfast table.”

“There is no need to be flippant. In any case, it is far more reasonable than supposing that a piece of timber gave you a nightmare.”

The Colonel agreed that it was. He spent the morning at the golf club and did not return until tea.

By then Collins was worse. He refused to remain in the cottage and had been moved to his sister’s house in the village. His wife reported that he had heard someone walking round the building all night, stopping at each window in turn.

“Fox, most like,” she added without conviction. “Though a fox don’t speak.”

“What did it say?” asked Mrs. Anstruther.

Mrs. Collins twisted her apron.

“He wouldn’t tell me, ma’am. Only once, when he was near off his head, he says, ‘I never pulled. It was them as pulled. You go to the house.’”

Mrs. Anstruther sent soup and ordered that the second gardener supervise the rose beds.

The next evening she dined with the Rector. The Colonel had gone to London for a regimental dinner and would not return until late on the following day. Mrs. Anstruther was not nervous of being alone; indeed, she enjoyed the orderly quiet of the house when her husband was absent.

She left the Rectory shortly before ten. The night was warm but windless. There was no moon. The footpath from the church crossed the west lawn and passed close to the new garden.

The Rector offered a lantern, which she declined. Westfield’s upper windows were visible beyond the trees, and she knew every turn.

As she came beside the churchyard wall, she heard a murmur behind her.

She stopped. The sound stopped also.

It might have been leaves, though no leaves moved. She went on.

At the entrance to the clearing something brushed along the far side of the laurels. It kept pace with her for six or seven steps.

“Who is there?” she called.

No answer came.

She was annoyed with herself for calling. Servants heard such things and embellished them. She walked faster.

The path narrowed where two shrubs, not yet removed, leaned toward one another. Beyond them lay the pale new earth of the rose beds. In the centre stood a short peg.

Mrs. Anstruther was certain that no peg had been left there.

Then the shrubbery sighed.

A face looked out.

It was level with her own, though the body belonging to it was hidden. It was much too large. That is the first thing she remembers.

She remembers that it was pale, not with the clean pallor of illness but with the grey whiteness of meat long kept from the air.

She remembers perspiration running from the forehead in thick drops.

She remembers the eyes. They were closed.

She remembers the mouth hanging open, and one yellow tooth in the lower gum.

The face pushed farther between the leaves. Its closed eyes turned toward her.

It whispered:

“You pulled.”

Mrs. Anstruther tried to step back. A twig caught in her sleeve. The mouth widened.

“I’ll push.”

She remembered no more until she found herself on the drawing-room sofa, with the housekeeper holding salts under her nose and a footman attempting to pour brandy into the corner of her mouth.

She had been discovered lying near the terrace by the kitchen maid, who had heard, from her bedroom, what she described as an owl screaming in the house.

Mrs. Anstruther suffered a fever and remained in bed for four days. When the Colonel returned, she told him that she had fainted from over-fatigue. The housekeeper told him more, and on the fifth day Mrs. Anstruther described the face.

There are experiences which make argument appear not merely useless but indecent. The Colonel did not mention thought-reading.

He wrote instead to a friend at Cambridge, Dr. Leyton, whose interests included local history, ecclesiastical custom, and those less reputable branches of archæology which respectable scholars investigate while denying that they do so.

Leyton came to Westfield on the following Saturday.

He examined the clearing, the churchyard, the parish registers, and the few estate papers preserved in the muniment room. He asked whether any part of the post remained. A charred length was found among rubbish behind the tool-house.

The wood bore several shallow grooves, not accidental and not, in Leyton’s opinion, ornamental. On one side were traces of lettering. After cleaning, he made out three characters: Q N M.

“That might stand for anything,” said the Colonel.

“Certainly,” said Leyton. “Which is why it may be worth attending to.”

The decisive evidence came from the county archæological society, though not from any published paper. In a box of unsorted transcripts, Leyton found the diary of the Reverend Ambrose Hill, rector of Westfield from 1691 to 1724.

Several pages concerning the estate had been copied by a nineteenth-century antiquary. They referred to Sir Matthew Vey, Baron Vey of Warden, who had acquired Westfield after retiring from public life.

Vey had been Lord Chief Justice during a troubled reign. His career was notorious. He had converted trials into performances, encouraged doubtful witnesses, bullied juries, and displayed a particular pleasure in pronouncing death upon those who had offended the Crown or himself. Even allowing for party hatred, the reports make uncomfortable reading.

After a change of government he escaped prosecution but lost office. He withdrew to Westfield, professing penitence and refusing visitors.

The official account stated that he died peacefully in 1698.

Hill’s diary suggested otherwise.

“March the 14th,” Leyton read aloud. “‘Was called after midnight to the Hall, where my Lord was found in the west chamber, dead in great disorder. His bed was torn, and he had bitten through the tongue. Those attending declare that for an hour before the end he cried continually that the prisoners were at the door, and demanded the sentence on himself. No persuasion availed.’”

“There is more,” said Mrs. Anstruther.

Leyton turned the page.

“‘March the 20th. Unquiet reports multiply. Voices heard in the west field; lights crossing the church ground; cattle refusing the lower pasture. Widow Ames saw a great person in scarlet standing at her door, his eyes shut, and fell into fits.’”

Mrs. Anstruther put down her cup.

“Did she describe the face?”

“Not here.”

The entries continued. Servants deserted the Hall. Travellers reported being followed by an unseen crowd. Two children became ill after dreaming that they had been condemned in a court. The church bell rang without hands upon the rope.

In April, Hill consulted three neighbouring clergymen, among them one Dr. Poynter, formerly of a college in Oxford and, according to Leyton, learned in practices not set out in visitation returns.

On the night of April the twenty-ninth, the four men conducted an office in the west field.

Hill did not describe the rite. He wrote only:

“By mercy, and with great peril, the oppressor was brought beneath and bound. The stake was driven over him, marked as directed. We were charged that it remain while wood endureth, and that another be set in its stead should decay appear. Q.M.N. The place must not be opened.”

“What does Q.M.N. mean?” asked the Colonel.

Leyton looked toward the garden.

“Quieta movere nefas, possibly. ‘It is impious to disturb things at rest.’ Though your letters appeared in another order because part of the inscription has been destroyed.”

“And the summer-house?”

“Built later to cover the stake, I should think. Practical people often preserve a prohibition more readily if they can turn it into a garden feature.”

Mrs. Anstruther said, “But the post had been cut off before. Why was nothing released then?”

“Because the embedded portion remained. Your workmen removed it completely.”

“And burned it.”

“Yes.”

No one spoke for a little while.

From outside came the harmless clatter of a mowing machine on the lawn. The ordinary sound was welcome.

“What is to be done?” asked the Colonel.

Leyton did not answer immediately.

“I am a historian,” he said at last. “Historians are very good at telling people what ought not to have been done. Our qualifications for reversing it are less certain.”

The Rector was consulted that afternoon. He had at first been inclined to attribute the disturbances to suggestion, illness, and the well-known melancholy of the west field. The diary changed his manner.

In the oldest parish register, whose opening leaves were warped by damp, he found a note written in a hand later than Hill’s but earlier than any surviving churchwarden’s account. It had been cramped into the lower margin beneath the burials for 1698:

“Let no man, upon hope of profit, improvement, or vain knowledge, dig in the ground westward of God’s acre where the old house standeth. What is there laid is not dead as honest men are dead. Quieta non movere.”

The Rector read it twice.

“Why was I never shown this?”

“Because,” said Leyton, “warnings in registers are generally treated as scribbles until after they become useful.”

They attempted no second laying. Leyton advised that the ground be closed, the hole reopened as little as possible, and an iron post driven where the wooden one had stood. Whether iron possessed any particular virtue he would not say; but it was durable, and durability had plainly been valued by his predecessors.

The work was carried out in daylight by men brought from another county. While they drove the iron down, voices were heard beyond the churchyard wall. The men supposed a funeral was taking place, though the churchyard was empty. One labourer dropped his hammer and would not retrieve it. There were no other incidents.

The Anstruthers left Westfield before midsummer.

It was explained that Mrs. Anstruther required a warmer climate and that the Colonel had long wished to rent a place nearer his club. The rose garden was never planted. Nettles took the new beds, and the shrubs closed gradually about the iron post.

Collins recovered, but refused to return to the Hall. When asked what had struck him outside the tool-house, he would say only that a man condemned by his betters had no call to complain, and then look confused, as if the words were not his own.

For some months the Rector continued to hear reports. There were cries at night, movements in the west field, and once the sound of a bell under snow, though no snow had fallen. These diminished with time.

I asked him, many years afterward, whether he believed the thing had been secured again.

He said he hoped so.

Then he showed me the old register. The warning was faint, and one corner of the page had been eaten away, but the final words remained legible.

“People translate them as ‘Do not disturb quiet things,’” he said. “That is near enough. But ‘settled things’ may be better. A thing need not be quiet in itself merely because, with trouble, it has been made to keep still.”

Outside the vestry window, beyond the yews, the west field lay in evening shadow. As we closed the book, something gave a long cry among the shrubs.

The Rector waited until it had ceased.

“An owl,” he said.

But he did not look toward the window.

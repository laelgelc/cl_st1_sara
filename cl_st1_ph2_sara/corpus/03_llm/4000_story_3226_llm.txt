### THE BLACK STROKE

Sir Henry Marquis once said that a criminal act is rarely hidden by darkness; it is hidden by the false daylight which the criminal constructs around it. He meant, I think, that the guilty man does not merely conceal a fact. He surrounds it with other facts—innocent, visible, and carefully arranged—until the mind accepts the arrangement as natural.

My father, Pendleton, expressed the same idea in another fashion.

“The devil,” he said, “is an ingenious workman, but he cannot make a perfect joint. Somewhere the false piece will fail to fit the true.”

I remember the phrase because he used it on the morning after Peyton Marshall was buried, when we stood in the ruined library at Cedar Hill.

It was a noble room, extending across the southern end of the house, with three long windows opening upon a terrace of white stone. Beyond the terrace lay a formal garden of box and clipped yew, and beyond the garden the low blue summits of the Virginia hills. The morning was brilliant; rain had fallen in the night, and every cedar and magnolia shone as though varnished.

The disorder within the room was, therefore, the more shocking.

The doors of a Florentine cabinet stood open. Drawers had been pulled out of a long mahogany secretary and overturned upon the carpet. Papers lay among books, medals, yellow letters, ivory seals, coins of uncertain antiquity, and little figures of bronze and jade. A Japanese crystal, as large as an orange and without flaw or bubble, had rolled against the brass fender. A lacquered writing-box lay open on the floor; but its trays were empty.

The room had been searched thoroughly and by a man who knew what he sought.

Peyton Marshall had been a traveler, a man of letters, and a dreamer. He bought objects because they pleased some delicate instinct in him, and kept them because each was attached to a place, a person, or a memory. He had gone to Europe in his youth, wandered through India and China, and returned at forty to his Virginia estate with a mild contempt for money and a profound affection for beautiful things. Yet in this confusion not one valuable object appeared to be missing.

It was searched for Peyton Marshall’s will.

Lewis, the family lawyer, stood by the secretary. He was a thin man in black, with a narrow, legal face and restless fingers. He had the air of one who had been compelled to abandon the firm floor of statutes and precedents for some swamp where the feet found no support.

Young Julian Marshall stood at one of the windows.

He was scarcely twenty-one, fair-haired, slender, and very pale. Grief had refined his face almost to delicacy, but the mouth was his father’s—generous and firm. He had not spoken since we entered. He looked into the wet garden, although I do not think he saw it.

The fourth man in the room was Anthony Gosford.

He was an Englishman of perhaps forty-five years, low in stature, neatly made, and dressed with a correctness which the long journey and the humid Virginia morning had not disturbed. His face was pink, his eyes very pale, and his mouth a straight hard line. A small seal ring was upon the little finger of his left hand, and he turned it continually with the fingers of the right.

My father walked through the room without touching anything. He was a large man; his shoulders seemed almost to fill the doorway when he entered, but his movements were quiet and his voice habitually level.

He paused before the secretary.

“When was this room last seen in order?” he asked.

“Yesterday afternoon,” Lewis replied. “I was here at four o’clock.”

“And the door?”

“Locked.”

“By whom?”

“By me. Mr. Marshall kept his important papers in the lower drawer. After the funeral I came here with Julian, intending to examine them. We were interrupted, and I locked the room.”

“What interrupted you?”

Lewis glanced at Gosford.

“Mr. Gosford arrived.”

The Englishman ceased to twist his ring.

“I came,” he said, “in consequence of a communication received from the late Mr. Marshall.”

“What communication?”

“A private letter.”

My father looked at him.

“So private that it cannot be shown?”

Gosford’s color deepened.

“I did not say so, sir. I object merely to the tone of the question. The letter concerned a testamentary provision which Mr. Marshall had made in my favor.”

“How large a provision?”

Gosford hesitated.

“Fifty thousand dollars.”

The sum, very considerable in that day, seemed to remain in the room like the report of a pistol.

Young Marshall turned from the window.

“That is false,” he said.

Gosford regarded him with an expression of grave indulgence.

“My dear young gentleman, grief has unsettled your judgment.”

“My father never intended to leave you fifty thousand dollars.”

“He informed me that he had done so.”

“My father informed me otherwise.”

Lewis lifted a hand.

“We shall gain nothing by accusation. The question is whether there was a will, and, if so, what it contained.”

“There was a will,” Gosford said. “I witnessed Mr. Marshall place it in that drawer three weeks ago.”

My father looked at the lower drawer of the secretary. It had been forced with some flat instrument, and the brass lock hung by one screw.

“You witnessed him?”

“I was present when he completed it.”

“Did you write it?”

“No.”

“Did you read it?”

“Not as an attorney, if that is your meaning. Mr. Marshall read aloud the clause concerning me.”

“What business had you with Peyton Marshall’s will?”

“My business was Mr. Marshall’s confidence.”

“What business had you with his confidence?”

The repetition was mild, but Gosford’s fingers stopped on the seal ring.

“I had rendered him services in England,” he replied. “Services of an intimate and confidential character.”

“What services?”

“Matters affecting his investments.”

“What investments?”

“Mr. Pendleton,” Lewis interrupted, “Mr. Gosford is not compelled to disclose private transactions merely because the room has been entered.”

My father turned his head.

“No. But if a man comes across the ocean to claim fifty thousand dollars under a missing document, he must expect curiosity.”

Gosford smiled without altering the hard line of his mouth.

“Curiosity, certainly; insolence, no.”

My father appeared to consider the distinction.

“Who knew that the will was in this drawer?” he asked.

“Mr. Marshall, Mr. Gosford, Julian, and myself,” Lewis replied. “Possibly a servant saw us here, but I cannot say.”

“Who had access to the key?”

“I retained it.”

“And it remained in your possession?”

Lewis drew a key from his waistcoat pocket.

“It did.”

My father examined it and returned it.

“The lock was not opened with the key,” he said. “Who first entered this morning?”

“I did,” Lewis answered. “At half past seven. The door from the hall was still locked. I entered by my key and found the room in this condition.”

“The windows?”

“As you see them.”

Two were fastened. The third, nearest Julian, stood partly open.

My father crossed the room and examined it. The window descended almost to the terrace. There were scratches on the painted sill; below, upon the white stone, lay fragments of dark earth.

“The entrance is plain enough,” said Gosford. “The thief came from the garden.”

My father looked out.

The flower bed below the terrace had been trampled. A heavy impression, resembling the heel of a boot, was visible in the damp border; another appeared near the hedge.

“I see,” my father said.

The words, in his mouth, did not indicate agreement. They signified only that an object had entered his attention.

He descended to the terrace. We followed him. The tracks crossed the wet garden irregularly, passed through an opening in the box hedge, and ended at the gravel drive. There the marks vanished.

Gosford put his fingers together with a judicial air.

“A burglar came from the road, crossed the garden, entered at the window, searched the secretary, obtained the will, and departed by the same route. The thing is regrettably simple.”

“Simple things are sometimes true,” my father said.

“And sometimes?”

“Sometimes they are constructed for simple minds.”

The Englishman’s pale eyes narrowed.

“Do you accuse me, sir?”

“Of having a simple mind? No.”

Lewis turned away abruptly, and I saw that he was concealing a smile.

My father knelt beside the nearest footprint. He did not measure it. He touched the earth at its edge, then looked at the clipped grass between the impressions.

“The man who made these tracks,” he said, “had a remarkable power of flight.”

“What do you mean?” Lewis asked.

“He stepped in the flower beds and nowhere else.”

“The marks would not show on the grass.”

“Not as a print. But wet soil adheres to leather. A boot lifted from this bed would leave crumbs upon the grass. There are none.”

He returned to the terrace and studied the fragments on the sill.

“The earth is from the bed below,” he said. “It has not been carried through the room. There is no trace upon the carpet.”

Gosford shrugged.

“A careful burglar may have removed his shoes.”

“And replaced them to climb through the window?”

“A criminal may act strangely.”

“A criminal may act as he pleases,” my father replied. “But nature acts in sequence. Wet earth on the sill, then none on the carpet; footprints in the beds, then none between them—these are pieces cut by hand. Their edges do not join.”

Young Marshall had grown even paler. His hand was clenched against the window frame.

My father looked at him.

“Where is the will?” he said.

Lewis made an exclamation.

Gosford stepped forward.

“You address the wrong man!”

“I address the man who opened the window, scattered the earth, made the impressions with a boot held in his hand, overturned the drawers, and carried away the document.”

Julian’s lips moved, but no sound came.

“Julian,” Lewis said, “for God’s sake—”

The young man faced us. For a moment his expression was one of terror; then the fear went out of it, and there remained only exhaustion.

“I took it,” he said.

The Englishman’s breath escaped in a soft hiss.

Lewis gripped the back of a chair.

“You took your father’s will?”

“Yes.”

“Where is it?”

Julian did not answer.

Gosford turned to Lewis.

“You hear the confession. I demand that the proper authorities be notified immediately. The offense is theft, housebreaking, destruction or concealment of a testamentary instrument—”

“It is not housebreaking,” my father said.

“What?”

“He lives in the house.”

“It is criminal concealment!”

“That may depend upon what he concealed.”

Lewis’s face was distressed.

“The law does not inquire into his reasons, Pendleton. A will is a legal instrument. To remove it with intent to suppress it is a crime.”

My father bent and picked up the Japanese crystal from beside the fender. It filled his palm with a cold globe of light.

“What is a crime?” he said. “A violation of law?”

“Certainly.”

“That is convenient for the law. But justice existed before the statute book. A crime is a violation of justice—something behind the law, Lewis, something the law attempts, with varying success, to reach.”

“And when every man judges that justice for himself?”

“Then we have disorder. But when a rogue hides behind a legal paper, we have disorder also—only written in a fine hand and tied with red tape.”

Gosford laughed shortly.

“An admirable philosophy for the defense of a thief.”

Julian turned upon him.

“I am not a thief!”

“You have admitted taking the will.”

“I took it because it was false.”

“False?”

“My father promised that Cedar Hill and the greater part of his property should come to me. He told me so a month before he died. He said he had made a bequest to you because you had assisted him abroad, but it was not to diminish my inheritance materially.”

“What sum did he name?” my father asked.

“Thirty thousand dollars.”

Gosford’s face did not change.

“Your memory is convenient.”

“My memory is exact. He said thirty thousand. Yesterday, when Mr. Lewis unlocked the drawer, I saw the will beneath a packet of accounts. Before I could take it up, a servant came to say that Mr. Gosford had arrived. Mr. Lewis left the room and locked the door. But there is a second key. It was my father’s. I found it in his dressing case.”

“You entered at night?” Lewis asked.

“Yes.”

“And read the will?”

“Yes.”

“What did it say?”

Julian looked at Gosford with a strange mixture of anger and disbelief.

“It left him fifty thousand dollars in one clause—and all the residue of the estate in another.”

Lewis staggered as though physically struck.

“All the residue?”

“Everything except small legacies to the servants and five thousand dollars to me.”

There followed a profound silence.

Cedar Hill, its lands, horses, houses, investments, and mills represented a fortune. The specific bequest of fifty thousand dollars was heavy; the residuary clause was ruin.

Gosford spread his hands.

“I did not draw the document. I cannot be responsible for the disposition of a dead man’s property.”

“You told us only of the fifty thousand,” my father said.

“I had no wish to humiliate the young gentleman by proclaiming his disinheritance.”

“Compassion sits oddly upon you.”

“I shall not submit to these remarks!”

“Then do not. Where is the will, Julian?”

The young man crossed to the mantel. Above it hung a portrait of Peyton Marshall as a youth, and below the portrait stood an ivory carving of Saint George. Julian lifted the carving, pressed a narrow panel, and drew from the recess behind it a folded document secured with ribbon.

“I meant to burn it,” he said. “I could not.”

My father took the paper and laid it upon the library table.

Lewis approached with the solemnity of a priest at an altar. He untied the ribbon and unfolded the sheets. The instrument was in Peyton Marshall’s own hand, firm and rather old-fashioned. His signature appeared at the bottom, followed by those of three witnesses. Two were neighbors known to us; the third was a physician who had since gone to New Orleans.

Lewis read in silence. As he proceeded, the sorrow in his face increased.

“It is valid in form,” he said finally.

“And in substance?” asked my father.

“The law permits a man to dispose of his property as he wishes.”

“Even irrationally?”

“Even wickedly.”

Julian put both hands upon the table.

“My father did not write that!”

Lewis pointed to the signature.

“It is his hand.”

“I know the signature. I know the writing. But he did not mean this thing.”

“Meaning unexpressed is nothing to a court.”

“I tell you he promised me!”

“My dear boy, a will revokes promises.”

Gosford drew a slow breath. His confidence returned like color beneath his skin.

“This scene is painful,” he said, “but it has at least established the facts. Mr. Marshall made his decision deliberately. The resentment of an excluded heir cannot invalidate it.”

My father had said nothing. He stood at the end of the table with the crystal in his hand, turning it so that the morning light broke within it. Presently he placed it upon the document.

The glass enlarged the writing beneath it grotesquely. Fine pen strokes became bars; the texture of the paper appeared as a network of pale fibers.

Gosford watched him with open contempt.

“Do you hope to read the dead man’s intention in a crystal?”

“No,” my father replied. “Only the living man’s workmanship.”

He moved the globe slowly along the lines. The rounded surface distorted the words at the edge, but those directly beneath its center stood out with extraordinary clearness.

Lewis leaned over.

“What do you see?”

“Nothing yet.”

The Englishman resumed turning his seal ring.

My father passed over the first page, then the second. At the clause of specific bequest he stopped.

“I see,” he said.

Again the phrase altered the atmosphere of the room.

The clause read:

_I give and bequeath to my friend Anthony Gosford, in acknowledgment of valued services, the sum of Fifty Thousand Dollars._

My father moved the crystal until it stood above the first letter of the amount.

“What is the difference,” he asked, “between a written figure three and a written figure five?”

Lewis frowned.

“In ordinary hands?”

“In Peyton Marshall’s hand.”

The lawyer examined other figures in the instrument. Dates appeared in several places; there was a bequest of three thousand dollars to an old servant and another of five thousand to Julian.

“Marshall wrote the old commercial forms,” Lewis said. “His three has a flat upper line and two descending curves. His five begins with a downward stroke, then a bar across the top.”

“Look here.”

Lewis bent over the crystal. I came beside him.

The word _Fifty_ had been written out, but above it, in the margin, appeared the numerical sum: $50,000. It was this figure my father examined.

Under the magnification the initial numeral seemed no longer a natural character. Its upright stroke was thick and black; the upper bar crossed it with an angular stiffness. Beneath, however, the lower curve swept outward in a manner more appropriate to the figure three. The whole resembled a black lath nailed across the uprights of an older frame.

Lewis seized the document and carried it to the window.

“By Heaven!”

Julian followed him.

“It was thirty!” he cried. “I knew it!”

Gosford did not move.

My father set down the crystal.

“The figure was three,” he said. “A vertical stroke and upper bar were added to convert it into five. The word itself has also been changed. A heavy initial has been laid over the first letters, and the termination adjusted. The alteration is skillful to the unaided eye; under enlargement it is crude.”

Lewis examined the line for a long time.

“There is erasure beneath the word,” he said. “Very slight—but there.”

Young Marshall’s face brightened with a sudden and painful hope.

“Then the whole document is false?”

“No,” Lewis replied. “Not necessarily.”

The hope vanished.

Gosford stepped to the table.

“At last,” he said, “we approach common sense.”

My father looked at him.

“You are singularly composed for a man whose legacy has been tampered with.”

“Because there has been no unlawful tampering.”

“No?”

“Mr. Marshall made the correction himself.”

Julian struck the table with his fist.

“That is a lie!”

Gosford turned on him sharply.

“Must I endure this?”

“You will endure what is necessary,” my father said.

The Englishman drew himself up.

“Very well. Three days after executing the document, Mr. Marshall reconsidered the specific bequest. He altered the sum from thirty thousand dollars to fifty thousand. I was present.”

Lewis stared at him.

“Why was the correction not attested?”

“It need not have been, if made before final acknowledgment.”

“You said three days after executing it.”

“I used the term in a general sense. The instrument had been drafted and signed privately, but had not yet been acknowledged before the witnesses.”

“You previously said you were present when he completed it,” my father observed.

“I was.”

“Now you say it was completed in stages.”

“Legal papers often are.”

“What instrument did he use?”

Gosford’s eyes flickered toward the lacquered writing-box on the floor.

“His pen.”

“What ink?”

“How should I remember?”

“You witnessed an alteration which increased your bequest by twenty thousand dollars, yet you do not remember?”

“I remember no trivial details. Mr. Marshall had several inks.”

Lewis spoke gravely.

“An alteration by the testator before attestation may be valid. If the witnesses signed after the change, their signatures authenticate the instrument in its altered form.”

Julian looked at him in despair.

“You believe Gosford?”

“I say only what a court may believe. The physical alteration proves nothing by itself. Your father might lawfully have made it.”

“And the residuary clause?”

Lewis turned to it.

That clause left “all lands, tenements, securities, moneys, goods, chattels, and whatsoever residue” to Anthony Gosford. No alteration was visible. The hand was Peyton Marshall’s.

My father read it once, then again.

“Julian,” he said, “did your father write every word of this document?”

“I think so.”

“Think carefully.”

The young man pressed his hands against his forehead.

“He had been ill. Mr. Gosford came often during the last six weeks. They were closeted here for hours. My father said there were old claims upon some English funds—claims Gosford was helping him resolve.”

Gosford nodded.

“Precisely.”

“Did your father discuss the will after it was written?”

“Yes. On the terrace. He said that Gosford had earned a generous remembrance. I asked what he called generous. He answered, ‘Thirty thousand, Julian; enough to reward a friend, not enough to injure my son.’ Those were his words.”

“A recollection,” Gosford said. “Nothing more.”

“My father was not a liar.”

“No one calls him one. A sick man may change his mind.”

“He would have told me.”

“Perhaps he had discovered some defect in your character.”

Julian’s hand moved, but my father caught his wrist before the blow fell.

“No,” he said.

The word was quiet. The young man obeyed it.

My father turned to Gosford.

“You have placed yourself in an interesting position.”

“I have placed myself nowhere.”

“You assert that Peyton Marshall altered the figure in your presence.”

“I do.”

“If the alteration is his, your statement saves the will. If it is not his, your statement identifies the forger.”

Gosford smiled.

“A neat verbal snare, Mr. Pendleton; unfortunately, it closes upon nothing. You cannot prove who made a pen stroke.”

“Perhaps not with words.”

At this moment there came a jerking movement beyond the open hall door, followed by the impatient tapping of a stick. A shaggy head appeared, then the stooped and angular figure of Doctor Gaeki entered the room without announcement.

He was a small man, though his untamed iron-gray hair made him seem larger. His eyebrows projected like thickets above black, penetrating eyes. His coat was badly fitted; one cuff bore a yellow stain, and his pockets bulged with notebooks, bottles, string, and instruments whose purpose no reasonable person could have guessed.

He had attended Peyton Marshall in his last illness and had loved him with the irritable loyalty peculiar to men who conceal affection beneath abuse.

“You begin without me,” he said.

“You were not invited,” my father replied.

“That is why I come early.”

He thrust his stick into a corner, peered at the disordered room, and uttered an exclamation in a language unknown to me.

“Who makes pig market here?”

“Julian.”

The doctor looked at the young man.

“Ah. Badly done. Too much furniture, too little intelligence.”

“I was not attempting a real robbery,” Julian said.

“No? Then excellent.”

He came to the table and looked down at the will.

“This kills Peyton again?”

“It attempts to,” my father answered.

Gaeki’s black eyes darted from the paper to Gosford.

The Englishman had recovered his legal composure.

“I protest against the admission of every casual person into what is essentially a confidential proceeding.”

The doctor leaned near him.

“You have a pink face,” he said. “Liver?”

“My health is no concern of yours.”

“Good. Then when it fails, call another physician.”

Lewis coughed into his hand.

My father indicated the altered sum.

“Can you distinguish one ink from another?”

Gaeki snatched up the crystal, examined the numeral, and grunted.

“With eye, perhaps. With chemistry, yes.”

Gosford intervened.

“You propose to damage the will?”

“A pin point,” said the doctor. “Less damage than one fly.”

“I forbid it.”

“You forbid?”

“As principal beneficiary, I have an interest in preserving the instrument.”

“And as what else?” my father asked.

The Englishman’s lips tightened.

Lewis examined the paper.

“We cannot conduct a reckless experiment. If the ink can be tested without materially defacing the will—”

Gaeki flapped one hand.

“Lawyers! Always they wish truth, but truth must not wrinkle the paper.”

He emptied his pockets upon the table. There appeared a brass forceps, two needles, a folding lens, strips of white cloth, a little porcelain plate, and four tiny stoppered bottles. He arranged them with abrupt precision.

“What inks?” my father asked.

“Many. Iron gall, logwood, lampblack, aniline—new commercial rubbish. Each gives itself away if asked proper question.”

Gosford laughed.

“This is absurd. Mr. Marshall kept several inks in this room. Even if the added stroke differs from the rest, he may have used another bottle when he made the correction.”

“Just so,” Lewis said. “A difference would not prove a different hand.”

“Not alone,” my father agreed.

He looked around the library.

“Where are Marshall’s inks?”

Julian pointed to the lacquered box.

“He kept them there.”

The box lay open and empty, although the compartments had been shaped to hold three small bottles. My father picked it up.

“The burglar took no coin, no ivory, no jade; but he took the ink.”

Gosford shrugged.

“Or a servant removed it.”

“When?”

“I cannot say.”

My father turned to Julian.

“Did you?”

“No. I overturned the box, but it was already empty.”

“Was it empty yesterday?”

“I did not notice.”

Lewis shook his head.

“I recall one bottle upon the secretary when I came after the funeral. A square bottle with a silver cap.”

“Where is it now?” my father asked.

No one answered.

Gaeki had begun to examine the writing. He placed the will flat, scraped from an inconspicuous portion of the original numeral a particle so minute that it resembled dust, and transferred it to the porcelain plate. Then he took a second particle from the heavy added stroke.

Gosford watched every motion.

“This proves nothing,” he said. “Nothing whatever. Mr. Marshall may have changed inks. Pens run dry. Bottles are replaced. Men do not interrupt a testamentary correction in order to preserve chemical uniformity.”

“True,” my father said.

The agreement disturbed him more than contradiction.

Gaeki put a drop of clear fluid upon the first particle. It dissolved slowly into a brownish stain. Upon the second he placed the same fluid. The speck flushed violet, then faded toward blue.

The doctor showed us the plate.

“Different.”

Gosford spread his hands.

“As I predicted.”

“Wait,” said Gaeki.

He uncorked another bottle.

The Englishman’s pale eyes fixed upon it.

My father saw the look.

“You know the reagent?” he asked.

“I know nothing of this mountebank performance.”

“Yet you knew when the important bottle appeared.”

“I object to your manner.”

“What business had you with Peyton Marshall’s writing materials?”

“Again that question! What business—what business! I had the business of any guest who writes a note, addresses a letter, or makes a memorandum.”

“You wrote in this room?”

“Frequently.”

“With your own pen?”

“Sometimes.”

“With your own ink?”

A fractional pause followed.

“I carry a traveling case.”

My father crossed to the chair on which Gosford had placed his coat and gloves. Beside it stood a narrow leather dispatch case with brass corners.

“May I examine it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because it is private property.”

Lewis looked troubled.

“We have no authority to search it.”

My father nodded.

“That is true.”

He did not touch the case. Instead he picked up the Japanese crystal and held it before Gosford’s face. The inverted image of the Englishman’s pink cheeks and pale eyes floated within it, grotesque and diminished.

“Then we must continue our crystal-gazing,” he said.

Gosford made a contemptuous sound.

My father set the globe over the residuary clause.

It enlarged the loops, crossings, and minute hesitations of Peyton Marshall’s hand. He moved it from word to word. Presently he called Lewis.

“Look at the phrase ‘Anthony Gosford.’”

The lawyer bent down.

“What of it?”

“Compare it with the same name in the specific bequest.”

Lewis did so.

In both places the name was written in Marshall’s hand—or seemed to be. Yet beneath the crystal there appeared a faint roughness around certain letters, as though the paper had been disturbed before the final ink was laid down.

“Erasure?” Lewis whispered.

“Yes.”

“But what was written originally?”

“That I cannot recover by sight.”

Julian came close.

“Could the clause have named me?”

“It may have named anyone. The present words are not written over blank paper.”

Gosford’s composure broke.

“This is mere conjecture! You magnify the fibers of old paper and discover whatever fraud your prejudice desires.”

Gaeki grinned, showing strong, irregular teeth.

“Paper remembers,” he said.

He placed a drop from the second bottle upon a strip of cloth, then with a moistened needle touched one disturbed fiber at the edge of Gosford’s name. A pale red coloration spread through the scraped place. He tested an untouched word in the same line. That stain remained brown.

“What does it mean?” Julian asked.

“The original ink throughout is iron gall,” said the doctor. “Old preparation. Peyton used it for years. The added marks contain an aniline dye—commercial violet base. Sold commonly only in recent time. This over-writing here is same as black stroke in the five.”

Lewis straightened.

“The same ink?”

“Same reactions.”

Gosford replied quickly.

“Then Mr. Marshall made both alterations at the same sitting.”

“Perhaps,” my father said. “And afterward the convenient bottle disappeared.”

“He may have thrown it away.”

“A dying man climbed from his bed, removed every modern ink from the writing-box, carried the bottles out of the library, and left no word of the wholesale change by which he disinherited his son?”

“He was not confined continuously!”

“No. But you were continuously near him.”

“This is not proof.”

My father’s level voice became colder.

“No. It is the outline of proof. Let us fill it in.”

He turned to Doctor Gaeki.

“When did Peyton cease to write?”

The doctor answered at once.

“Eight days before death. Paralysis began in right hand.”

Lewis started.

“The will is dated six days before his death.”

“The will was drafted earlier,” Gosford said. “The date was inserted later.”

“By whom?” my father asked.

“Mr. Marshall.”

“With a paralyzed hand?”

“The weakness came and went.”

Gaeki struck the table with the stopper of his bottle.

“It did not go. I tested every day. After Tuesday he could not hold spoon, pen, nothing. Before Tuesday his hand firm. This will signed before Tuesday, whatever date says.”

My father looked at the document.

“And the witnesses?”

Lewis considered.

“They may have signed on the date shown. Marshall’s own signature could have been placed earlier. That is irregular, but the witnesses might not have known.”

“Precisely,” my father said. “The paper was prepared and signed while Marshall could write. Later, after the paralysis, alterations were made and a false date inserted. Gosford then brought in witnesses to acknowledge the document, representing it as complete.”

The Englishman’s face had lost its pinkness.

“A chain of suppositions!”

“Let us test one more link.”

My father turned to Julian.

“Bring me every letter your father received from Gosford.”

The young man crossed to a cabinet untouched in his counterfeit search. From a pigeonhole he took a packet tied with blue ribbon. There were seven letters, all written on thick foreign paper.

My father opened them one by one. The script was narrow, upright, and exact. Gosford looked on with a smile that had become fixed.

“You will not find Peyton Marshall’s hand in my letters,” he said.

“No. I shall find your ink.”

Gaeki uttered a pleased grunt.

He selected a word from the final letter, touched its terminal flourish with his needle, and applied the clear acid. The trace flushed violet, then faded toward blue.

He repeated the test upon the altered five.

The colors were identical.

“Same commercial ink,” he said. “Same concentration, same violet dye, same gum. Not merely same kind—very likely same bottle.”

Gosford laughed, but there was no life in it.

“Hundreds of bottles may contain the same preparation.”

“In London, perhaps,” my father said. “How many in this county?”

No one answered.

My father went to the leather dispatch case.

“You refused to open this because you carry that bottle now.”

The Englishman moved suddenly. His hand shot toward the case, but Julian was nearer. He caught it up and passed it across the table.

Gosford sprang after him. My father seized the man by the shoulder and flung him back with such force that he struck the mahogany secretary. A small key dropped from his waistcoat.

Gaeki picked it up.

“Private property,” he said, and opened the case.

Within were papers, a traveling pen, sealing wax, wafers, and a square bottle with a silver cap.

Lewis recognized it.

“That is the bottle I saw upon Marshall’s secretary.”

Gosford leaned against the cabinet. The hard line of his mouth had become gray.

“You cannot establish ownership of a bottle.”

“No,” my father replied, “but you have established fear of it.”

Gaeki removed the silver cap. The ink within appeared black, with a violet film at the glass.

He made the three tests side by side: ink from Gosford’s bottle, ink from the altered numeral, and ink from the overwritten name in the residuary clause. Under the acid each gave the same swift violet flare and the same blue dissolution. The original text remained brown.

The doctor looked up.

“One family,” he said. “All born in this bottle.”

Lewis placed his hand over the will.

“Then the specific bequest was thirty thousand dollars.”

“And the residuary clause?” Julian asked.

Gaeki examined the erased fibers more closely. By oblique light, and with the crystal above them, ghostly lines emerged beneath Gosford’s name. Lewis traced them slowly.

“To my son,” he read. “It said, ‘to my son, Julian Peyton Marshall.’”

Julian closed his eyes.

For the first time that morning Gosford was wholly silent.

My father gathered the sequence in his deliberate manner.

“Peyton Marshall intended to reward you with thirty thousand dollars and leave the residue to his son. You altered the specific sum to fifty thousand. Then you erased Julian’s name in the residuary clause and substituted your own. Because the original will was in Marshall’s hand, the fraud would escape casual examination. You caused the instrument to be witnessed after the alteration, and you removed the inks from the room lest a comparison be made. Marshall’s illness protected you; his confidence gave you opportunity; death, you believed, would close his mouth.”

Gosford lifted his head.

“You cannot prove that Marshall did not direct the changes.”

“The paralysis proves he could not make them.”

“He could direct another.”

“Then why did you deny remembering the ink? Why conceal the bottle? Why steal Marshall’s ink from the writing-box? Why describe yourself first as present at the completion, then at a correction, then at an acknowledgment? The false pieces multiply, Gosford; but their serrated edges never meet.”

The Englishman looked toward the open window. Outside, the garden lay shining under the sun. His counterfeit footprints darkened the flower beds like crude symbols upon a clean page.

Julian moved between him and the terrace.

Gosford’s shoulders dropped.

“I advised Marshall for years,” he said. “I recovered money he would otherwise have lost. He trusted me more than the boy. The estate would have been wasted here among horses and old books.”

“It was not yours to rescue,” my father said.

“He owed me!”

“He gave you thirty thousand dollars.”

“A gratuity!”

“A fortune to an honest man.”

The sentence ended the matter. Gosford seemed to understand that no further legal phrase, no qualification, no polished denial could restore the false daylight he had built around his act. He sat down, very slowly, amid the papers he had hoped to possess.

Lewis went into the hall to send for the sheriff.

Julian stood before his father’s portrait. He did not weep; but the rigidity that grief and shame had placed upon him gave way, and he rested one hand upon the mantel.

“I doubted him,” he said.

“No,” my father replied. “You doubted a paper.”

“I stole it.”

“You preserved it.”

“I staged this shameful burglary.”

“That was badly done.”

Doctor Gaeki nodded with satisfaction.

“Very badly.”

Julian almost smiled.

My father folded the will carefully.

“A just act may violate the law,” he said, “but it remains dangerous to confuse private passion with justice. You were right about your father and wrong in your remedy. Remember both things.”

Lewis returned to the room.

“The original dispositions can be established,” he said. “The chemical evidence, the erasures, the paralysis, and Gosford’s possession of the ink will be sufficient. The court may reject the alterations and admit the will as first written—or declare it invalid, in which case Julian inherits under the law. In either event, Gosford receives nothing.”

“Not thirty thousand?” Julian asked.

Lewis looked at the Englishman.

“A beneficiary who forges a will cannot safely demand its genuine bounty.”

Gosford covered his face with one hand.

Gaeki began replacing his instruments in his pockets. He held the little porcelain plate toward the window, examining the stains with professional irritation.

“What is it?” I asked.

He scowled.

“A measure of good acid wasted upon one bad man.”

Then he thrust the bottle into his coat, recovered his stick, and went out across the terrace without another word.

My father remained beside the library table. The Japanese crystal lay before him, bright and innocent, gathering the disordered room into its polished globe—the torn papers, the open drawers, the false will, the defeated Englishman, and young Julian beneath his father’s portrait.

It had been purchased in Nagasaki as an ornament. Peyton Marshall had valued it because a temple priest once told him that perfect crystal held nothing hidden within itself. He had repeated the saying to me years before, smiling at its simplicity.

Yet on that morning the crystal had performed no miracle. It had foretold nothing and summoned no spirit from the dead. It had merely enlarged what men had failed to see: a heavy stroke across a numeral, torn fibers beneath a name, the imperfect workmanship of fraud.

That was all my father ever required of Providence—that truth should leave some physical edge exposed.

“The devil’s shop is clever,” he said as we went out into the clean light of the garden, “but its workmen are always in haste.”

Behind us Lewis closed the library door, not to conceal the crime, but to preserve its evidence.

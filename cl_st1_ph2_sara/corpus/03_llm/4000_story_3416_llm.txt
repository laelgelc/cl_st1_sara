# The Egg of the East

When Herbert Loring determined to exhibit his Eastern curiosities, he did not announce the fact in the newspapers, nor did he permit the secretary of the Meridian Club to mention it in the monthly circular. He caused thirty-six invitations to be engraved upon a particularly thick and smooth paper, and sent them to thirty-six persons whose presence, in his opinion, would give dignity to the occasion without placing too great a strain upon its resources.

This discrimination was necessary. Loring did not own a hall, and although he was a man of comfortable means, he had not yet reached that degree of wealth at which a hall may be hired without consideration of its size, its ventilation, or its price. Moreover, his exhibition was of an experimental character. He wished to have among his spectators persons who were intelligent, observant, and sufficiently well bred to refrain from examining any object which he did not invite them to examine.

His list included two physicians, a professor of comparative anatomy, a retired judge, three ladies who had travelled in Egypt, an elderly general who had served in India, and several gentlemen whose opinions on art, science, and foreign politics were frequently delivered at dinner tables. There were also certain persons whose chief qualification was that they would speak of the exhibition afterward. Loring was not insensible to reputation; he merely preferred that reputation should appear to overtake him.

Several persons who were not invited expressed strong doubts concerning the value of the curiosities, while one gentleman at the Meridian Club declared that there was nothing in the East which could not be purchased in Paris. As this gentleman had never gone farther east than Vienna, his opinion was received with the respect usually accorded to knowledge derived from principle rather than experience.

The exhibition was to take place on a Thursday evening in the small theatre attached to the Lyceum Rooms. This theatre had eighty seats, a stage of moderate depth, two dressing rooms, and machinery by which a moon, a storm, or a sylvan cottage could be produced with equal facility and without much resemblance to nature. Loring hired the building for the entire day, although his guests were not expected until nine o’clock. He did this in order that there might be no haste in arranging the apparatus.

At ten in the morning he arrived with six cases, two trunks, a mahogany cabinet, and a long narrow box which could not be carried upright through the stage door. He was accompanied by a Japanese assistant named Ito, who had been in his service for three years and who possessed the useful faculty of obeying directions without appearing to form an opinion of them.

By noon the stage had been cleared. The painted garden and the sylvan cottage were removed, the wings were covered with dark cloth, and a black velvet curtain was hung across the rear. In the centre stood a table, also covered in black. To the right was a shallow brass brazier; to the left, a lacquered chest, ornamented with birds and flowers in dull gold. Above the stage, and concealed by a strip of drapery, Loring placed an arrangement of glass tubes, reflectors, metal powders, and small electrical devices which he had designed himself.

He examined every connection twice. He measured the distance from the foremost row of seats to the table. He directed Ito to sit in six different parts of the house while he altered the lights. Finally, he placed upon the table a little ebony pedestal no larger than a lady’s workbox.

At half past six he ate a light dinner in one of the dressing rooms. He had ordered a chop, toast, and a bottle of claret, but he drank only one glass of the wine and left half the chop untouched. He was not ordinarily a nervous man. He had spoken before learned societies, had descended into a volcanic crater under circumstances of considerable inconvenience, and had once spent a night in a ruined temple with a priest who had expressed an intention of poisoning him. Yet on this evening he twice consulted his watch while it was still in his hand.

The reason was not wholly scientific. Among the thirty-six persons invited was Miss Edith Starr, to whom he had been engaged for six months.

Edith had listened to many descriptions of the coming exhibition, although Loring had been careful not to tell her what its principal marvel would be. She knew that he had devoted nearly two years to an investigation which he called, according to his mood, “psychical optics,” “directed perception,” or “the practical conquest of the visual faculty.” She had laughed at the first two names and objected to the third.

“The visual faculty may not wish to be conquered,” she had said.

“My dear Edith,” Loring had replied, “faculties do not wish. They act.”

“Then the person possessing them may wish.”

“That,” said Loring, “is a philosophical distinction.”

“It seems to me a very plain one.”

He had kissed her hand and changed the subject. Loring had a sincere admiration for Edith’s mind, particularly when it did not interfere with his conclusions.

At a quarter before nine the guests began to arrive. Their wraps were taken in the vestibule, their names were quietly requested by an attendant, and they were shown into the theatre. Each found upon his seat a small printed programme:

> **PRIVATE EXHIBITION OF CERTAIN ORIENTAL PHENOMENA**  
>   
> I. Illuminations of the Court of Heaven  
> II. The Egg of Reversal  
> III. Explanatory Remarks, if desired

The words “if desired” were Loring’s. He had found that explanations are most highly valued when there is a possibility that they may be withheld.

The first two rows filled quickly. The judge sat between one of the physicians and a widow of distinguished social connections. The professor of comparative anatomy had brought a notebook, which he opened upon his knee. General Broughton took a seat near the aisle and immediately began to tell a lady behind him that he had seen something very like the promised illuminations at Benares in 1868. As nothing had yet been shown, his recollection could not be disputed.

At five minutes past nine, thirty-four of the thirty-six guests were present. One of the absentees was an elderly lady whose carriage had broken down. The other was Edith Starr.

Loring stood behind the curtain and looked through a narrow opening. He saw the vacant chair in the second row. It had been placed between Mrs. Selwyn and Edith’s uncle, Mr. Amos Starr, who had arrived alone and appeared unconcerned. Loring waited three minutes; then five.

Ito approached him.

“Begin now?” he asked.

Loring looked again at the empty seat. The audience had ceased to talk with ease and had begun to talk with patience. A delay which flatters a public assembly may irritate a private one, for each member of the latter knows that somebody of no greater importance is being waited for.

“Yes,” said Loring. “We will begin.”

The house lights were lowered gradually. A little current of expectation passed through the assembled company. When the theatre was dark, except for a pale blue lamp upon the stage, Loring came from behind the curtain.

He wore ordinary evening dress. There was nothing of the professional conjurer in his appearance, and this fact reassured those who would have objected to being deceived by a paid entertainer, although they were quite willing to be astonished by a gentleman.

“My friends,” he said, “I shall offer no preliminary account of the objects which you are about to see. Certain of them were brought from the interior of Asia; others were reconstructed by me from descriptions contained in manuscripts which, to the best of my knowledge and belief, have never been translated. If the exhibition is successful, it will speak for itself. If it is not successful, no speech of mine will improve it.”

This modest statement was received with applause.

“The first display,” he continued, “is an imitation—necessarily imperfect—of the illuminations formerly produced at the winter court of the Emperor Chien Lung. I must ask you not to look away from the stage while the lights are in motion. Their sequence is important.”

He raised his right hand. The blue lamp went out.

For one instant there was complete darkness. Then high above the stage appeared a point of crimson light. It opened like a small flower, divided into five streams, and vanished. A green star sprang from the brazier, ascended without smoke, and broke against the black curtain in a shower of silver sparks. From the left came three violet circles, revolving within one another; from the right, a golden wheel, which spun more and more rapidly until every spoke had melted into a ring of fire.

The spectators leaned forward.

The stage was now crossed by slender flights of light which seemed to pursue one another, to mingle, and to separate. Blue arrows darted upward and returned as drops of scarlet flame. A white blaze spread across the darkness, but instead of dazzling the eye it softened into a delicate radiance, in which appeared shapes like towers, bridges, trees, and flying birds. These dissolved into a sparkling mist. The mist contracted, coruscated, and became a globe of intense green, upon which the eye rested with singular pleasure.

Loring stood beneath it, motionless, with one hand extended.

The globe began to beat like a luminous heart.

At every pulsation it changed colour—green, gold, blue, rose, white—and with every change there came from some concealed instrument a low musical note. The notes were not arranged as a melody; nevertheless, they produced an impression of order, as if they belonged to a tune which the hearer had forgotten but was upon the point of remembering.

The professor’s pencil dropped upon his notebook. He did not pick it up.

The globe widened until it appeared to fill the stage. It became a sheet of amber brightness, through which innumerable sparks rose and fell. Then, in the middle of the blaze, a black spot appeared. It grew steadily larger. The sparks rushed toward it and disappeared therein. The amber field narrowed about it, the surrounding lights were drawn inward, and all at once the theatre was dark.

No one applauded.

The silence lasted perhaps four seconds, but to Loring, who was counting them, it seemed considerably longer.

Then a lamp burned upon the table. Its light was warm and clear. Loring stood beside the ebony pedestal, and upon the pedestal was a small box of red lacquer.

The audience drew a collective breath.

“The object which I shall now have the honour of showing you,” said Loring, “was entrusted to me by a monk of the Monastery of the Seven Precipices. Whether it was made there, I cannot say. The monk himself did not know. He told me that it had been in the monastery for more than four hundred years, and that during this period it had been opened only nine times.”

At that moment Edith Starr entered the rear of the theatre.

Her delay had been caused neither by a broken carriage nor by feminine indifference to the hour. An accident in the avenue had blocked the way, and after waiting ten minutes she had left her cab and walked the remaining distance. The attendant in the vestibule, believing that no one should be permitted to enter during the exhibition, had attempted to detain her. Edith, however, knew that a vacant seat awaited her, and she passed him with a quiet decision which made resistance seem discourteous.

She opened the rear door just as Loring spoke of the four hundred years.

The theatre was dim, and she could not at first distinguish the faces before her. She saw Loring upon the stage, the black-covered table, the brazier, the lacquered chest, and the little ebony pedestal.

She did not see any red box upon the pedestal.

Supposing that it was too small to be visible from the doorway, she moved along the wall toward a chair in the last row. There she sat down without attracting attention. Loring did not see her. Every face in the theatre was turned toward him.

“The box,” he continued, “is itself of interest, although its interest is secondary. You will observe the seven figures upon the lid. They represent the Seven Masters of Change.”

He lifted his hand above the pedestal, placed his fingers as if upon a lid, and raised them.

Edith looked more closely. There was no box. His fingers closed upon empty air.

The audience bent forward with perfect attention.

“You see,” said Loring, “that the interior is lined with pale green silk. In the centre lies the egg.”

There was a little murmur of admiration.

Loring stooped and appeared to take from the nonexistent box an object which he held between his thumb and forefinger.

“It is larger than the egg of a pigeon and smaller than that of a domestic fowl. Its colour is not white, although at first you may call it so. Look more carefully. You will perceive a faint opalescent tint—a touch of blue upon one side, of rose upon the other. As I turn it, the colours change.”

He slowly moved his empty fingers.

“Ah!” whispered Mrs. Selwyn.

The judge adjusted his spectacles.

General Broughton nodded gravely, as a man may nod when a phenomenon confirms something which he has always known but never mentioned.

Edith sat upright. She glanced at the people nearest her, wondering whether this was an arranged pleasantry in which she alone had not been instructed. But there was no laughter. The professor gazed at Loring’s hand with an expression of absorbed inquiry. One of the physicians was unconsciously stroking his beard. Mr. Amos Starr had his lips parted like a child at a peep show.

Loring placed the supposed egg upon the bare pedestal.

“This egg,” he said, “has no value as an egg. It cannot be eaten. It cannot be hatched by warmth. If placed under a hen, it remains unchanged; if broken—and I am informed that the experiment has been tried—it is found whole upon the following morning. Its peculiarity is disclosed only when it is touched by the Wand of Alternation.”

He turned toward the lacquered chest.

Edith could see that the chest was real. He opened it and withdrew his hand.

In that hand there was nothing.

“This wand,” he said, holding the empty hand horizontally, “is made from the central rib of a leaf which grows upon the northern slope of Mount Omi. Notice its extreme thinness, its black colour, and the small band of copper near the handle.”

The spectators noticed all these things.

Loring brought the imaginary wand close to the imaginary egg.

“It is important that the first touch should be exceedingly light.”

He tapped the pedestal.

A sharp little sound was heard. Whether it came from the wood or from some mechanism beneath the table, Edith could not determine.

“Look!” said Loring.

The company looked.

A hairlike crack had appeared at the larger end of the egg. It was so fine that at first one could scarcely distinguish it from the delicate variations of colour upon the shell. Loring turned the pedestal a little, and the crack became more visible.

“There,” he said. “It lengthens.”

The crack travelled slowly downward.

“See it divide. One branch passes to the left; another to the right. The shell is not breaking from pressure. Something within it is coming to life.”

There was a second tap.

The top of the egg lifted.

A tiny yellow beak appeared in the opening, withdrew, and appeared again.

A lady in the front row uttered a little exclamation, after which she smiled apologetically, although no one looked at her.

“Patience,” said Loring. “The creature has been asleep a long time.”

The beak struck the shell. Once. Twice. Three times.

At the third stroke a fragment fell upon the green silk which lined the top of the pedestal. Then a small, damp head emerged. Its eyes were shut. Its feathers clung together, and its neck trembled under an effort which seemed too great for so fragile a thing.

“Come,” said Loring softly. “Come out of your four hundred years.”

The shell rocked. A foot appeared, absurdly large in proportion to the body. The head rose; the wings struggled; the shell separated into two unequal parts, and between them lay a newly hatched chick.

The assembled company applauded.

The applause was not loud. The creature appeared too young to endure noise, and several spectators, having accepted its existence, immediately accepted a responsibility for its nerves.

Edith saw only the ebony pedestal.

She looked at Loring. His eyes shone with excitement, but his control was perfect. With the tip of the nonexistent wand he made small guiding motions above the empty wood.

“The chick,” he said, “is at present no different from any other chick of its age, except that it has no proper age. In another minute the distinction will become apparent. I shall feed it.”

From beneath the table he took an actual shallow dish. Into this he poured a few grains from a paper packet. Edith could see the dish and the grains.

He set the dish beside the pedestal.

The chick lifted its head. It saw the food, swayed upon its legs, and stepped out of the broken shell. At first it pecked uncertainly, striking the rim of the dish; then it found a grain and swallowed it.

“Observe,” said Loring.

The chick ate a second grain.

It grew.

The change was slight, but unmistakable. Its legs lengthened; the damp down upon its back dried and thickened. It ate again. A shadow of brown appeared upon its wings.

Another grain, another change.

“Each particle of food,” said Loring, “contains for this creature not merely nourishment, but time. To us, a grain is a grain. To the bird, it is a week.”

Peck. Swallow. Change.

Peck. Swallow. Change.

The chick was now as large as a man’s fist. Its motions had lost their first helplessness. It stood firmly, looked about the theatre, and gave a thin, questioning cry.

The audience laughed with delight.

The cry had been perfectly audible. Edith heard it as clearly as the others; but, looking toward the wings, she caught sight of Ito beside a small instrument furnished with reeds and bellows. His hand moved, and the chick cried again.

Loring tapped the table with his wand.

“Continue,” he commanded.

The bird obeyed. It ate more rapidly.

Yellow down gave place to feathers. The wings lengthened and folded themselves with increasing neatness against the body. The beak hardened. The feet became strong and angular; little scales appeared upon the legs. A blush of red showed above the eyes.

“Three months,” said Loring.

The bird pecked.

“Five months.”

It pecked again.

“Eight months. You see the beginning of the comb.”

The professor of comparative anatomy fumbled for his pencil, failed to find it, and continued to stare.

The comb rose red and serrated upon the bird’s head. Wattles unfolded beneath its beak. The tail feathers, at first short and inconspicuous, pushed backward in a dark-green curve. The brown of the wings deepened into russet and gold. The breast became black, glossy, and full.

“Behold,” cried Loring, “the completed bird!”

Upon the pedestal stood a magnificent rooster.

It was nearly three feet high. Its eye was bright, its comb erect, and its feathers seemed polished. It turned its head sharply to one side, then to the other, surveying the audience with that mixture of suspicion and self-approval which belongs to the male of its species and is not unknown elsewhere.

A roar of applause filled the theatre.

The rooster lifted itself, beat its wings, and crowed.

General Broughton sprang to his feet.

“By Jove!” he cried.

The bird crowed again, louder than before. The widow of distinguished connections put her fingers to her ears and laughed. The judge applauded with both hands above his head. Mr. Amos Starr called, “Again!” The physician who had stroked his beard now gripped the arm of his chair.

Loring smiled, and for the first time that evening his anxiety left him. His experiment had succeeded. Thirty-four persons of education and consequence saw a rooster where no rooster existed, heard it through a mechanical contrivance which, without the vision, would have deceived no one, and responded to it with the unguarded enthusiasm of children.

Only Edith, sitting unseen at the back, watched his empty hand hover above an empty pedestal.

Loring waited until the applause subsided.

“You have seen birth and growth,” he said. “The process is not complete. In the ordinary course of nature, development proceeds in one direction. The egg becomes the bird; the bird does not become the egg. But the law governing this object is not the ordinary law.”

He raised the wand.

“The first tap awakened the creature. The second hastened its growth. The third will reverse it.”

He struck the pedestal.

The rooster ceased to crow.

It stood rigid, with one foot raised. Then the lifted foot descended. The proud tail sank a trifle; the comb diminished; the body appeared to contract.

A murmur passed through the audience.

“Look!” cried Loring. “Do not lose an instant of it. The year returns upon itself.”

The bird grew smaller.

Its long curved tail feathers shortened and straightened. The black breast lost its breadth. Gold retreated from the neck; the wattles drew upward; the comb folded down into a narrow red ridge.

“One year,” said Loring. “Ten months. Eight months.”

At each phrase the creature diminished.

“Six months. Four.”

It was a young fowl now, awkward and unfinished. Its feet seemed too large; its wings too small. It gave a weak cluck, and the audience leaned nearer.

“Two months.”

Feathers softened into down.

“Three weeks.”

The chick tottered.

“One day.”

It sank between the fragments of shell. The fragments stirred, rose at the edges, and closed about it. For a moment the shell was cracked; then the lines ran backward, joined, and disappeared. Once more the egg lay whole and opalescent upon the green silk.

No one moved.

Loring lowered the wand.

“The egg must now be returned to its box,” he said. “If left exposed, the transformation might recommence without control.”

He took it between his fingers and placed it in the imaginary box. He lowered the imaginary lid.

The theatre burst into applause.

Men who prided themselves upon reserve shouted their approval. Ladies waved their programmes. The judge declared that he had never seen anything comparable to it, although the general immediately named three things he had seen in India which were not only comparable but, under different conditions, superior. The professor demanded permission to inspect the shell. Both physicians began speaking at once.

Loring bowed.

“I regret,” he said, “that the object cannot be handled. Contact with an unprepared person might destroy its equilibrium.”

This explanation, being impossible to test, was generally considered satisfactory.

The house lights were raised, not suddenly, but in three slow stages. The spectators blinked, looked at one another, and began to talk with great animation. On the stage Loring replaced the nonexistent box in the real lacquered chest and shut the lid.

Edith remained in the rear row.

She had come prepared to be entertained, perhaps astonished, and certainly pleased by Loring’s success. Instead she experienced a cold uneasiness which increased as those about her expressed their delight. It was not the trick itself that troubled her. She had seen conjurers and understood the convention upon which their art depended: the performer promised deception, and the audience consented to be deceived. Here no such compact had been made. These men and women believed that they had judged with their own eyes. Yet their eyes—or something more intimate than their eyes—had been managed for them.

Loring descended from the stage. He was surrounded at once.

“My dear fellow,” said the judge, “you have overturned half the laws of biology.”

“Only half?” asked Loring pleasantly.

The professor pressed forward.

“I must have the dimensions of the shell. At the beginning I estimated the longitudinal axis at two and one-quarter inches, but after the reversal it appeared a shade larger.”

“I shall make the measurements available,” said Loring.

“Was the food chemically prepared?” asked a physician.

“In a certain sense.”

“Did you say Mount Omi?” cried General Broughton. “I knew a fellow who went there in ’seventy-two. Lost two fingers.”

Mrs. Selwyn touched Loring’s sleeve.

“Mr. Loring, I confess that when the poor little head came out, I could scarcely breathe. And when it grew—well, I shall dream of it.”

“I hope,” said Loring, “that it will crow at a reasonable hour.”

This was repeated and admired.

For nearly twenty minutes he answered questions without answering them. He accepted congratulations, declined requests for immediate repetition, and promised a second exhibition at some indefinite date. Throughout this time he did not see Edith. Her chair in the second row remained vacant, and whenever he glanced toward it his pleasure lost a little of its brightness.

At last the guests began to depart. They carried away programmes, recollections, and strong opinions. In the vestibule the judge said that no principle known to jurisprudence could apply to the phenomenon. The professor lamented the restrictions upon examination. General Broughton recalled that the Tibetan bird he had once seen was larger. Mrs. Selwyn announced that the chick’s first cry had been the sweetest sound imaginable.

As they passed Edith’s place, none spoke to her. Some looked directly at her without recognition. Their attention, so violently concentrated upon the stage, had not yet entirely recovered its customary freedom.

Mr. Amos Starr was among the last to leave. Edith stepped into the shadow beside the wall while he passed. She did not wish to explain herself to him. He was a kind man, but his kindness operated most readily after breakfast and least readily in matters connected with engagements.

When the outer door closed, Loring turned to Ito.

“That is all,” he said. “Pack the upper apparatus first. Leave the table.”

Ito bowed and disappeared behind the curtain.

Loring stood alone in the aisle. His triumph was complete, except for the absence of the one person whose approval he most desired.

Then Edith came forward.

He saw her and gave an exclamation of pleasure.

“You are here! I thought you had not come.”

“I came late.”

“How much did you see?”

“The egg.”

His face brightened.

“Then you saw the essential part. Where were you?”

“In the last row.”

“That was unfortunate. The position is not the best, although the range appears to have been sufficient. Tell me—what did you think of it?”

She stopped several feet from him.

“There was no egg,” she said.

Loring did not answer at once.

“There was no little red box,” she continued. “There was no green lining. There was no shell, no chick, no rooster. There was no wand in your hand.”

His expression changed, but not to confusion. It became intensely interested.

“You saw nothing?”

“I saw you. I saw the pedestal. I saw the dish and the grain. I saw Ito at his machine. I saw thirty-four sensible persons applauding an empty table.”

Loring grasped the back of a seat.

“Edith, at what moment did you enter?”

“You were telling them that the box was four hundred years old.”

“Then you missed the illuminations.”

“Yes.”

He drew a long breath. The last uncertainty vanished from his face, and in its place came the satisfaction of a man whose calculations have been verified by an accidental but decisive test.

“It is perfect,” he said.

Edith looked at him.

“What is perfect?”

“The experiment. Your late arrival supplies the control I lacked. I had thought of placing one person behind a screen during the first display, but the arrangement might have attracted attention and impaired the general result. You missed the lights; therefore you were not influenced. You saw the stage as it actually was. The others saw precisely what I directed them to see.”

“Influenced?”

“Hypnotized, if you prefer the familiar term, though it is not altogether accurate. Ordinary hypnosis acts upon individuals and commonly requires direct preparation. My process acts upon a group. The coloured intervals, the recurrent tones, and the contraction of the final field establish a temporary receptivity. After that, a carefully ordered verbal image is accepted as visual fact.”

He spoke calmly now, almost academically. The theatre, the hour, and the woman before him had become parts of a successful demonstration.

“The mind does the greater portion of the work,” he continued. “That is the beauty of it. I did not need to create the rooster in detail for each spectator. I supplied the principal characteristics—colour, growth, movement—and every person completed the image from his own memory. No two of them saw exactly the same bird, but each believed he saw the bird upon the pedestal.”

“And the sound?”

“A simple reed apparatus. Without visual preparation it would be obviously artificial. With the image established, the mind joined sound and appearance. Even the tapping served its purpose. Rhythm confirms suggestion.”

Edith’s face had become very pale.

“You did this deliberately.”

“My dear Edith, I have worked upon little else for two years.”

“You invited them for this purpose.”

“Naturally. I required an audience.”

“Did they know they were to be hypnotized?”

Loring paused.

“The word would have alarmed them unnecessarily.”

“Then they did not know.”

“They knew they were to witness an exhibition.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No,” he said, with a trace of impatience. “They did not know the method. If they had known it, resistance would have been almost inevitable, and the experiment could not have been made.”

Edith turned toward the stage. Ito had removed a portion of the black drapery, revealing bare boards and a discoloured wall. The ebony pedestal remained under the lamp. It seemed smaller now.

Loring took a step toward her.

“You must understand that no harm has been done. The condition lasts less than an hour. By tomorrow their recollection of the smaller details will be uncertain, although the general impression will remain. No will has been weakened; no faculty has been injured. I have proved merely that perception may be directed by scientific means.”

“You made them believe what was false.”

“For a few minutes.”

“You entered their minds without permission.”

“That phrase is theatrical.”

“The performance was theatrical. The act was not.”

He frowned slightly. Praise, objections, and requests for evidence he had expected. Moral condemnation appeared to him irrelevant, like criticism of the frame when one had painted a new picture.

“Edith,” he said, “every public speaker directs thought. Every painter compels the eye to accept a flat surface as depth. Every novelist causes the reader to grieve for persons who never lived. I have only carried an established principle farther.”

“A novelist does not tell me that his people are standing in my room.”

“That is a difference of degree.”

“It is a difference of honesty.”

He walked to the edge of the stage and back again.

“You are disturbed because you were outside the effect. Had you arrived in time, you would have found it beautiful.”

She turned sharply.

“Yes. Had I arrived in time.”

He stopped.

“You meant me to see it as they did.”

“Of course. I reserved the best seat for you.”

“You meant to hypnotize me.”

The word, spoken now without inquiry, seemed to strike him with greater force than it had before.

“I meant you to share the experience.”

“Without telling me what you were doing.”

“There could have been no experience if I had told you.”

“Then my knowledge was less important than your success.”

“That is unfair.”

“Is it?”

He approached her, and his voice softened.

“You know that I would never injure you.”

“I know that you intended to take possession of my judgment for your experiment.”

“For a harmless exhibition.”

“You decided it was harmless.”

“I am competent to decide.”

“For yourself, perhaps.”

“Edith, surely you do not imagine that I would use this power against you.”

“You intended to use it upon me tonight.”

“Not against you.”

“Without me, then. Around me. Through me. Choose whatever preposition makes you comfortable.”

For the first time Loring felt anger, although he believed it to be sorrow.

“You are putting an ugly construction upon a scientific achievement because the circumstances of your arrival allowed you to see its mechanism.”

“No,” she said. “The circumstances of my arrival allowed me to see you.”

He stared at her.

She went on, and now her words came neither loudly nor rapidly, but with an accumulation which left him no place to interrupt.

“For six months I have believed that you respected my mind. You have praised it often enough. You have asked my opinion when the matter was one in which my opinion could not hinder you. I thought that when you withheld your plans, you did so to give me pleasure by surprise. But you withheld them because knowledge would have enabled me to resist. You wished me to sit there, in the chair you had chosen, while your lights and sounds prepared me to believe whatever you said.”

“Only the egg.”

“How should I know that?”

He made a gesture of protest.

“How should any of us know?” she continued. “You told them there was a box, and they saw a box. You told them there was an egg, and they saw an egg. You told them a living creature came from it, and they pitied the creature. You made it grow, and they wondered. You made it shrink, and they obeyed you with their eyes at every step. They laughed when you wished them to laugh. They applauded when you wished them to applaud. And you stand here pleased because it was perfect.”

“I cannot deny that I am pleased by success.”

“That is precisely what frightens me.”

“Frightens you!”

“Yes. If you had been ashamed when you found that I knew—if you had said, ‘I was carried away; I did not consider what right I had’—I might have believed this ended with tonight. But you are not ashamed. You are delighted. My escape from your influence interests you because it proves the influence upon the others.”

“Escape is an absurd word.”

“It is the right word.”

He was silent. The sounds of packing came from behind the curtain: the closing of a case, the scrape of metal, the careful tread of Ito upon a ladder. These practical noises, which had earlier reassured Loring, now seemed to emphasize the emptiness of the theatre.

Edith removed her glove from her left hand. Upon it was the ring he had given her, a clear stone in a narrow gold setting. She drew it off.

Loring’s face altered.

“What are you doing?”

“I am returning this.”

He did not take it.

“You cannot mean that.”

“I do.”

“Because of an experiment?”

“Because of what the experiment has shown me.”

“It has shown you that I can cause a visual impression in a prepared audience.”

“It has shown me that when you can control people without their knowledge, you do it; that when they cannot resist because you have kept the truth from them, you call their helplessness necessary; and that when one of them happens not to be controlled, you regard her as a useful confirmation.”

“You are not one of them. You are Edith.”

“That did not save me from being invited.”

He looked at the ring in her palm.

“I love you,” he said.

“I believe you do.”

“Then how can you suppose—”

“I suppose nothing. I saw.”

“I would never make you do anything contrary to your happiness.”

“You would first decide what my happiness ought to be.”

“No!”

“You have decided what I ought to see. The rest is only a longer performance.”

This answer silenced him, not because he accepted it, but because some part of him recognized its relation to thoughts he had never put into words. He had imagined their married life with great distinctness. He had selected a house, planned a study, considered journeys, and determined that Edith’s occasional tendency toward social obligations could be moderated without difficulty. He had not called these plans control. He had called them arrangements.

She held out the ring once more.

“Take it, Herbert.”

He took it.

For several moments neither spoke. Then Loring said:

“If you leave now, under the influence of agitation, you may regret it.”

“I am under no influence but my own.”

The sentence was not loud, yet it seemed to fill the empty theatre more completely than the applause had done.

He bowed. His manners remained to him when argument did not.

“Shall I call a carriage?”

“No. Mine is waiting.”

He accompanied her to the vestibule. The attendant brought her cloak, and Loring placed it about her shoulders. The act was performed with the customary care, but both were conscious that custom had already become history.

At the outer door she turned.

“I hope,” she said, “that you will never exhibit the egg again.”

“There was no egg,” he replied bitterly.

“No,” said Edith. “That is what I shall remember.”

She went out.

Loring returned to the theatre. Ito had taken down the glass tubes and was packing them in cotton. The table stood bare beneath the lamp. On the ebony pedestal there was neither box nor egg, neither broken shell nor living bird. There had never been anything there.

Loring sat in the first row and looked at the empty stage.

His experiment had exceeded every expectation. The selected lights had subdued resistance; the tones had established rhythm; the spoken image had become visible; an entire company had accepted impossibility as fact. He had proved that thirty-four minds might be guided by one mind, provided they were not warned of the guidance.

Only the unprepared observer had seen the truth.

And the truth, which had produced no chick, no rooster, and no magic egg, had nevertheless hatched the one consequence Herbert Loring had not imagined.

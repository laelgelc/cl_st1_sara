### THE SILVER CLASPS

There are men to whom honesty is profitable, just as there are men who can wear white linen at their work and not soil it; but among us—Semka, Mishka, and myself—the honourable and the advantageous nearly always clashed, and when they did, the advantageous generally had the stronger fists.

We lived then in what had once been the office of a glass factory. The factory itself had burned down long before our arrival, leaving two brick chimneys, a heap of greenish slag, and several walls which stood upright from habit rather than strength. Our office possessed half a roof, one door, no windows, and a stove whose smoke came out wherever it pleased. When the wind blew from the river, soot descended upon us with such tenderness that in the morning each man could see upon his blanket the outline of his own body. During rain we shifted our sleeping places according to the dripping of the ceiling. Semka maintained that this was better than paying rent because it developed observation.

“Suppose you live in a proper house,” he would say. “You lie where you like. Your brain rots. Here—drop!—you move. Another drop!—you think. That is education.”

Semka was small, dry, and quick. His face looked as though it had been made from a piece of old leather and then sharpened at the nose. He never owned anything that could not be put into his pockets, and what he did own was often of doubtful origin. Life, according to him, was an innkeeper who watered the vodka, a contractor who cheated in the reckoning, a policeman who struck first and asked questions afterward. Therefore, he argued, an intelligent man ought to strike, cheat, or water something whenever the opportunity came.

He called this not dishonesty but “keeping the balance.”

Mishka was altogether different. He was large in the shoulders and loose in the limbs; he walked as if each leg had received separate instructions. A dirty yellow beard spread over his chest, and above it shone two gentle blue eyes, always surprised, even when nothing surprising had happened. He had a habit of smiling before he understood a joke, out of politeness, and afterward, when he understood it, of becoming sad. Drink made him affectionate. Hunger made him philosophical. Religion made him uneasy.

“There must be something, brothers,” he would say at night, when the stove smoked and none of us had eaten. “You can’t say there’s nothing. A man is born, suffers, steals perhaps—well, not steals exactly, but takes—and dies. What for? Eh? There must be an account somewhere.”

“There is,” Semka answered. “At the tavern. And they won’t give us credit.”

I myself was neither as clever as Semka nor as good as Mishka. I had sufficient conscience to be troubled by a bad action, but not always sufficient courage to avoid it; and I had enough intelligence to see that Semka’s philosophy was crooked, though on cold evenings, when the stomach complained, its crookedness seemed less important than its usefulness. We were friends because nobody else had much need of us.

Our occupations changed with the weather. We unloaded flour, dug drains, carried furniture, chopped wood, repaired fences badly, and assisted drunken carters to find horses which were often standing beside them. Once we painted a shop sign, though none of us could paint. The merchant, seeing the result, refused payment and set his dog on us. Semka threw the paint pot at the dog; the dog became blue, the merchant became furious, and we escaped with seven kopeks, a brush, and a lifelong prejudice against commerce.

In summer we ate more, because there was work. In winter we slept more, because there was not. Spring was the worst: the streets turned to black porridge, boots opened at the toes, roofs leaked, and employers, having spent the winter warm and fed, suddenly remembered economy.

It was in such a spring that we met the old woman.

We had gone to the market at dawn and stood with the other casual labourers beside the flour warehouse. There were thirty of us and work for perhaps six. Every man tried to appear stronger than he was. Chests were thrust out, shoulders squared, coughs suppressed. The contractors walked along the row, poking arms, examining hands, and speaking of men as peasants speak of sick horses.

“You three,” said a voice behind us. “Can you pull down a bath-house?”

We turned.

The old woman was small and dressed entirely in black, though the mud had decorated the lower part of her skirt with grey spots. Her head shook continually, not from fear but from age and indignation. A sharp nose projected beneath heavy spectacles; behind the thick glass her eyes appeared tiny and stern, like two officials at distant windows. She held a black umbrella over one shoulder, although there was no rain.

“Pull down?” Semka repeated. “We can pull down anything.”

“Can you build?”

“That costs more.”

“I do not ask whether it costs more. I ask whether you can.”

Semka looked at the sky.

“With God’s help,” he said piously, “everything can be built. But pulling down is our special gift.”

The old woman disliked him at once. I saw it in the way her spectacles flashed. Yet we were the only three men who had not already been hired, and necessity, which makes companions of thieves and saints, made her speak to us.

“The bath-house is rotten. It must be taken apart carefully. The sound boards are to be stacked. The bricks are to be cleaned. The rubbish is to be carried beyond the garden. Afterward the well must be cleaned.”

“Three roubles,” said Semka.

“One rouble and twenty kopeks.”

“For three men?”

“For the work.”

Semka laughed without mirth.

“Grandmother, for that money we can sit here and preserve our health.”

“You are not preserving it now.”

This was true. Mishka had been coughing since February, Semka’s left eye was swollen, and I had tied sacking around one boot to keep the sole attached. Truth, however, has never lowered the price of labour.

They bargained. The old woman invoked poverty, widowhood, fair dealing, the cost of timber, and the judgment of God. Semka invoked the size of the building, the depth of the well, the weakness of our stomachs, and the current price of bread. At last they agreed upon two roubles, a midday meal, and a glass of tea each. Semka demanded half a rouble in advance.

“No.”

“Then we may die on the road.”

“You may,” said the old woman. “It is not far.”

We followed her.

Her house stood at the end of a lane beyond the cemetery, behind a leaning fence. It was old but solid, painted the yellow of stale butter, with green shutters and a porch whose steps had sunk unevenly into the earth. The garden surprised us. From the road one saw only the fence and the dusty roof, but inside grew apple and cherry trees, currant bushes, lilac, nettles, burdock, and tall grass shining with the last of the morning dew. The place had been neglected into abundance. Branches crossed one another as though whispering; damp leaves gave off a sweet, dark smell; and over everything stretched a clean blue sky in which not even a chimney smoked.

For a little while we stood silent.

A man who lives among ruins acquires a strange respect for leaves. A brick falls because mortar loosens; a beam rots because rain enters; a boot breaks because leather is poor; but every spring a tree, without wages and without command, covers itself afresh. Mishka took off his cap.

“Fine,” he murmured. “God’s world, eh?”

“Mosquitoes too,” said Semka. “His world is complete.”

The bath-house stood at the far end of the garden. It had sunk on one side, and moss covered the roof. One corner was held up by a post; another had apparently decided that support was unnecessary. Near it was the well, roofed with warped boards. The water below smelled of mud and iron.

The old woman gave us an axe, two crowbars, a spade, a rope, and instructions enough to demolish a cathedral.

“Do not split the long boards. Do not break the bricks. Do not tread on the currants. Do not throw dirt near the gooseberry bushes. Do not swear.”

“Anything else?” asked Semka.

“Do not mock old age.”

“That comes under swearing,” he replied.

She crossed herself, sighed, and went back to the house.

We began.

A rotten building is easy to destroy if one does not care what remains; but to take it apart carefully is another matter. Every board which looked sound was nailed in six places, and every board which came loose at a touch crumbled in the hand. The roof showered us with dust, beetles, dry moss, and the nests of creatures which objected noisily to eviction. Semka climbed upon a beam and issued orders. Mishka pulled where he should have pushed. I attempted to save the bricks and bruised my fingers.

After an hour the bath-house looked no less solid, while we looked much older.

“Work intelligently!” cried Semka from the roof.

“What does that mean?” Mishka asked.

“It means don’t work like yourself.”

A plank gave way under Semka, and he disappeared to the waist. Mishka laughed so violently that he dropped the crowbar on his foot. He then cursed. The old woman appeared at once, as if the forbidden word had rung a bell in her room.

“Who spoke filth?”

“The roof,” said Semka, struggling among the rafters. “Your roof has no upbringing.”

She stared at us, shaking her head.

“Lost men,” she said. “All three lost.”

“Not lost,” Mishka answered mildly. “We know where we are.”

Toward noon she brought us food: black bread, boiled potatoes, salt, and a bowl of cabbage soup in which small yellow circles of fat floated at respectful distances from one another. To us it seemed a feast. We sat under an apple tree, black with dust, and ate so quickly that the old woman watched us with something almost like alarm.

“Chew,” she commanded. “A Christian must not eat like a wolf.”

“A wolf does not get soup,” said Semka.

Mishka nudged him.

“Don’t answer, Semyon. She feeds us.”

“I observe only natural history.”

After the meal the old woman did not leave. She sat on a low garden bench with her hands folded over her umbrella. For some minutes she watched us drink tea from chipped glasses. Then she asked:

“Do you pray?”

Mishka lowered his eyes. I looked at Semka, and Semka looked into his empty glass.

“When there is cause,” he said.

“There is always cause.”

“Then we should never finish the bath-house.”

“You joke because your souls are dark.”

“Dust,” said Semka. “It enters everywhere.”

The old woman’s little eyes fixed themselves upon Mishka. Perhaps his beard, his blue gaze, or his habit of appearing guilty even when innocent suggested material more suitable for salvation.

“And you?” she asked.

“I?” Mishka scratched his ear. “I believe, of course. That is—I do not disbelieve. My mother believed strongly. She had an icon of Saint Nicholas, with flowers. Paper flowers, but fine ones. When she died, my brother took it. He had a house, you see, and I had nowhere to put it.”

“Do you know the Scriptures?”

“I’ve heard them.”

“What have you heard?”

Mishka considered.

“About the rich man and the needle.”

“The camel.”

“Yes, the camel with the needle. A difficult business.”

The old woman stood up and went into the house. Semka watched her.

“She will bring more soup,” Mishka whispered hopefully.

“She will bring salvation,” said Semka. “It is cheaper.”

Indeed, she returned carrying a large Bible bound in dark leather. It had thick yellow-edged pages and two silver clasps, chased with little vines. The metal shone where generations of fingers had touched it. She settled herself on the bench, opened the book, and beckoned us.

“Rest for half an hour. Listen.”

“We have rested,” Semka protested.

“You will work better after receiving the Word.”

“The Word does not pull nails.”

“Come here.”

There are orders which a man resists because they are harsh, and others to which he submits because resistance would require more effort. We sat on the grass. The old woman adjusted her spectacles and began to read.

Her voice was dry, solemn, and unexpectedly strong. She read about affliction, mercy, the proud being cast down, the poor being comforted, and men bearing one another’s burdens. The leaves moved above us. Somewhere beyond the fence a cart rattled, then faded away. Bees worked in the cherry blossom. The breeze turned one corner of a page, and the old woman pressed it down with a brown finger.

At first I listened. The words, though familiar, appeared different in that garden. We were dusty, homeless, and uncertain of supper; yet here it was written that such people might be seen, might even be remembered. For several minutes I experienced a sweetness which was half hope and half fatigue.

Then I began to yawn.

Semka lay on one elbow, plucking grass and examining the roots. His eyes wandered from the old woman’s face to the book, from the book to the silver clasps, and from the clasps toward me. When he caught my look, he frowned as if I had accused him unjustly of something he had not yet done.

Mishka alone listened with his whole body. His mouth hung slightly open. At every mention of sin he nodded; at every promise of mercy his eyes grew moist. Once he sighed so loudly that the old woman paused.

“Does it touch you?” she asked.

“It does,” said Mishka. “Especially about burdens. That’s exactly right. Everyone has one. Some two.”

Semka rolled onto his back.

“Read about wages,” he said.

She shut the Bible sharply.

“You mock holy things.”

“No, grandmother. Wages are holy to a hungry man.”

“You are of the devil.”

“If the devil paid regularly, half the town would work for him.”

Mishka seized Semka’s sleeve.

“Enough! Can’t you be quiet? She reads well.”

The old woman looked upon Mishka with approval and upon Semka with the desire to see lightning employed accurately. Then she resumed. Presently the warmth, the food, and the droning words overcame me. I do not say I slept, for a worker does not sleep in paid hours; I merely closed my eyes and ceased for a time to be aware of the world.

When I opened them, the reading had ended. The old woman was carrying the Bible toward the house. Semka stood near the bench, stretching. Mishka remained seated, staring into the grass.

“Well?” Semka asked him.

Mishka smiled awkwardly.

“It is good.”

“What is?”

“All of it. A man hears such things and feels—well, you know—lighter.”

“Then you can carry the bricks.”

We worked until evening. Whether the Scripture improved us I cannot say, but the bath-house came down. We stacked the boards, scraped mortar from the bricks, and carried seven barrow-loads of rubbish beyond the garden. The old woman inspected everything, discovered twelve faults, corrected our stack twice, and at last paid us.

Semka counted the money.

“You promised two roubles.”

“There are two.”

“The meal was not deducted?”

“I do not rob labourers.”

“No offence. People surprise us.”

She gave each of us a small piece of bread for the road. To Mishka she added two boiled potatoes.

“You have a receptive heart,” she told him. “Do not follow bad companions.”

Mishka looked at Semka and me, troubled.

“They are not so bad,” he said. “Only externally.”

We left by the garden gate. Evening had cooled the lane; the sky above the cemetery was pale green, and rooks swung heavily toward the trees. Semka walked ahead, whistling. After we had gone some distance he stopped beside a ditch.

“Now,” he said, “let us examine Providence.”

From inside his shirt he brought out the two silver clasps.

Even in the poor evening light they gleamed.

Mishka’s face changed first to astonishment, then fear, then sorrow.

“Where did you get those?”

“Found them.”

“Where?”

“On a book.”

“What book?”

“You saw it.”

Mishka stared at him.

“You took them from the Bible?”

“I rescued them from idleness.”

I felt a coldness in my stomach. Theft was no novelty among us, though we preferred to steal from institutions, merchants, contractors, unattended carts, and persons whose boots showed no cracks. The old woman’s silver, however, seemed a mean object to take; and because it had fastened the book from which she had read about mercy, the meanness stood out more clearly.

“You fool,” I said. “She’ll know it was us.”

“She will suspect the devil. She already introduced us.”

“Give them back,” said Mishka.

Semka put the clasps into his pocket.

“Why?”

“Because.”

“A powerful argument.”

“You mustn’t take from such a book.”

“Is silver holier when screwed to leather?”

“That isn’t the point.”

“What is the point?”

Mishka’s lips moved. Thought came to him slowly, and Semka, who could invent ten reasons while another man searched for one, took pleasure in waiting.

“She read to us,” Mishka said at last.

“We did not ask her.”

“She fed us.”

“That was in the agreement.”

“She trusted us.”

“She watched us every minute.”

“It will grieve her.”

Semka shrugged.

“Grief purifies. She said so herself.”

Mishka looked to me for help. I knew Semka would not return them merely because we preached at him. Moreover, my share of the wages was already warming my pocket, and I was thinking of sausage.

“What will you do with them?” I asked.

“Sell them.”

“To whom?”

“To an admirer of church art.”

“You mean Yashka the fence.”

“A businessman should not be insulted for his clientele.”

Mishka followed him along the lane, arguing. He spoke of God, the old woman’s age, the Last Judgment, and ordinary shame. Semka answered each objection with cheerful precision. God, he said, had made silver but had not established private ownership of clasps; the old woman’s age meant she had less time in which to miss them; at the Last Judgment all metal would melt; and shame was an illness caused by insufficient food.

At the edge of town, beside a row of sheds, Mishka caught Semka by the arm.

“Sell them to me.”

Semka stopped.

“You?”

“Yes.”

“With what money?”

“My wages.”

Semka’s eyes narrowed.

“How much?”

“Whatever they’re worth.”

“They are worth a rouble.”

“You liar,” I said. “They’re thin as fish scales.”

“Artistic work. Antique. Sacred association.”

“Forty kopeks,” said Mishka.

“Eighty.”

“Fifty.”

“Seventy, and I lose by friendship.”

They settled at sixty-five kopeks. Mishka handed over nearly all his share of the day’s wages. Semka gave him the clasps, biting one first to demonstrate value. Mishka wrapped them in his bread and pushed the parcel deep into his coat.

“What now?” Semka asked.

“I’ll return them.”

“Tonight?”

“Tomorrow.”

“Why not tonight?”

Mishka glanced back toward the darkening lane.

“I am tired.”

“Conscience keeps office hours, then.”

“Go to the devil.”

We bought sausage, bread, onions, and a bottle of vodka. Mishka had money only for a herring tail, which he divided with us all the same. Back at the factory office we lit a fire from broken crate boards. Smoke filled the room, rain began tapping through the roof, and Semka, made tender by drink, offered to buy back one clasp at a reduced price.

Mishka refused.

Later, when Semka slept, Mishka took out the parcel and examined the silver in the firelight.

“Tell me,” he said, “will she be glad?”

“Probably.”

“She’ll understand I didn’t take them?”

“If you explain.”

“But suppose she thinks I took them and became frightened?”

“She may.”

He sighed.

“A good action is complicated. A bad one—you do it and it’s finished.”

This observation pleased him so much that he repeated it twice, then placed the clasps inside his boot for safety and lay down. In the morning he forgot them there, put on the boot, and walked three steps before crying out.

The day was wet. No employers came to the market, and by noon we had earned nothing. Hunger returned with the punctuality of a tax collector. Semka proposed that Mishka postpone restitution and lend him twenty kopeks on the security of the clasps.

“No.”

“Then at least come with us to the docks. They may need men.”

“I’ll go to her first.”

“You’ll miss work.”

“I know.”

“And spend the day walking.”

“I know.”

“And receive nothing.”

“I know!”

Semka shook his head sadly.

“There is a stubbornness in virtue which makes it almost as unpleasant as vice.”

Mishka set off alone.

We did find work at the docks—three hours carrying damp sacks for a merchant who deducted money because one sack had split before we touched it. At sunset Semka and I went to the cheap inn near the cattle market. It occupied a cellar. The ceiling dripped, the tables leaned, and the air consisted of tobacco smoke, wet wool, fried onions, and the sour breath of men who had ceased expecting tomorrow to improve upon today.

We ordered tea, bread, and one small bottle. Semka had just begun explaining that the merchant at the docks was a greater thief than himself because he owned scales, when Mishka appeared in the doorway.

He looked exhausted. Mud reached his knees. His beard was wet, his coat hung open, and his blue eyes had the dazed expression of a dog returning from a long and unsuccessful errand. He saw us, came to the table, and dropped onto the bench.

“Give me bread,” he said.

I pushed the plate toward him. He ate two pieces without chewing.

“Well?” Semka asked.

Mishka raised a finger.

“Wait.”

He ate another piece, drank my tea, then looked around for more.

“Did you return them?” I asked.

“I returned them.”

“And?”

Mishka closed his eyes.

“Brothers,” he said, “never do good on an empty stomach.”

Semka leaned back and laughed.

“Tell us.”

Mishka wiped his mouth with his sleeve.

“I went there. The gate was shut. I knocked. No answer. Knocked again. A dog barked somewhere, though yesterday I saw no dog. I thought, ‘Perhaps that is a warning.’ But then she came. She looked through the gate and said, ‘What do you want?’ I said, ‘I’ve brought something.’ She said, ‘What?’ I said, ‘Open and I’ll show you.’ She said, ‘Show me through the crack.’ Can a man show his soul through a crack?”

“The clasps would fit,” said Semka.

“Be quiet. She opened. I took them out and said, ‘Here, grandmother.’ She looked. Her head began shaking twice as fast. ‘Where did you get them?’ she asked. I said, ‘From a comrade.’ She asked, ‘Which comrade?’ Well, I didn’t want to betray you.”

“You had paid for the privilege,” Semka said.

“So I said, ‘It does not matter.’ Then she cried, ‘It was the little black one!’”

Semka was offended.

“I am not black.”

“You were black with roof dust.”

“A temporary complexion.”

“I told her you had made a mistake.”

“Good.”

“I said perhaps the clasps had fallen off and you had found them.”

“Excellent.”

“She asked why, if you found them, you sold them to me.”

Semka frowned.

“You explain too much.”

“I became confused! She kept looking through those spectacles. So I told the truth—but gently. I said you had taken them not from wickedness, but from need and wrong understanding.”

“Wrong understanding of silver?”

“That’s what I said. Then she asked why I bought them. I told her I wanted to return them. She asked how much I paid. I said sixty-five kopeks. She said they were worth perhaps twenty.”

Semka looked thoughtful.

“She does not appreciate workmanship.”

Mishka struck the table with his palm.

“That’s not all! I expected—well, I don’t know what I expected. She might say, ‘Thank you, Mikhail. You are poor but honest.’ Or perhaps give back ten kopeks—not that I wanted it! I swear I did not. But what did she do? She took me into the parlour.”

Here he shuddered.

The old woman’s parlour, as Mishka described it, contained dark furniture, portraits of dead relations, pots of geraniums, an icon lamp, a clock that ticked like a beetle, and enough crocheted covers to conceal every useful object. She made him sit on the edge of a chair while she examined the Bible.

“The leather was torn where the clasps came off,” Mishka continued. “She stroked it and sighed. Then she said, ‘You have done a righteous deed, but your soul remains in peril.’ I said, ‘That is possible.’ What else could I say? She asked whether I drank. I said, ‘Sometimes.’ She asked whether I stole. I said, ‘Not as a profession.’ She asked whether I attended church. I said, ‘When circumstances allow.’ Then she asked about women.”

Semka poured vodka into the glasses.

“And what did you say?”

“What could I say? I said women also happen according to circumstances.”

I confess that even the waiter laughed at this.

“She did not laugh,” Mishka went on. “Not once. She said the Lord had sent me to her. I thought: perhaps. A man cannot know. Then she brought the Bible.”

“The same Bible?”

“The same, only without clasps. It looked reproachful. She read to me about thieves. Then about drunkards. Then about fornicators. She found everything.”

“She had an index,” said Semka.

“At first I listened. Honestly, I did. There were good words. About one sinner repenting, and heaven rejoicing. I thought, ‘That is pleasant. Let heaven rejoice. I have walked far.’ But she went on. She explained each verse. Then she asked what it meant. I answered badly, so she explained again.”

“Did she feed you?” I asked.

Mishka’s face became solemn.

“That is the heart of the affair. On the table stood a plate of little pies.”

“With what?”

“I don’t know! Meat, cabbage, mushrooms—does it matter? Pies. Round, brown, shining with butter. I could smell them. She did not offer one.”

Semka clicked his tongue sympathetically.

“She read about the bread of life,” Mishka said, “while ordinary bread stood two steps away.”

This was so unjust that we all drank.

“Perhaps the pies were old,” I suggested.

“They were warm! Steam came out of one where the crust had cracked. I watched it until my eyes hurt.”

“What happened then?” asked Semka.

“She said I must abandon bad companions. I told her you were not really bad. She said the little black one had desecrated holy Scripture. I said you were a fool, certainly, but not altogether lost.”

“Thank you.”

“She said I must save you. I said, ‘How?’ She said by example. I said you did not notice examples. She said by exhortation. I said you answered back. She said by prayer. I said perhaps prayer was best because it could be done at a distance.”

Semka nodded approvingly.

“Sound theology.”

“Then she began telling me to find regular work. As if work were a mushroom and I had only to bend down! I told her there were thirty men at the market and six jobs. She said a diligent man always finds occupation. I asked whether she had another bath-house.”

“What did she say?”

“She became angry.”

The picture was clear to me: the old woman upright beside her clasp-less Bible, Mishka sagging on the chair, the warm pies between them, mercy discussed at great length while hunger sat uninvited but very attentive.

“She told me poverty was no excuse for sin,” Mishka continued. “I said I knew that. Then she said sin was often the cause of poverty. I asked about my mother, who had worked all her life and died owing money. That was wrong of me, perhaps. She took off her spectacles, wiped them, and said I was rebellious.”

“You?” I said.

“I was hungry.”

Hunger has overturned empires; it could certainly make Mishka rebellious.

“She read again. The room grew dark. The clock struck four, then five. Every time I tried to rise, she said, ‘One moment; this is for your salvation.’ At last I said, ‘Grandmother, forgive me, but if my salvation takes much longer, my body will die before it receives the benefit.’”

Semka bent over the table, choking with laughter.

Mishka smiled reluctantly.

“She did not like that either. I asked for leave to go. She told me to kneel before the icon. I knelt. My trousers were wet from the road, my stomach was making noises, and the floor was hard. She prayed. She prayed for me, for you two, for drunkards, thieves, unbelievers, negligent workmen, and all who use unclean speech. That took time. Then she made me promise not to drink.”

The bottle stood between us. Mishka looked at it with anguish.

“Did you promise?” I asked.

“I promised to struggle.”

“A broad promise,” said Semka.

“I struggled all the way here.”

“And now?”

Mishka held out his glass.

“I have been defeated.”

We filled it.

“But did she thank you?” I asked.

Mishka considered.

“At the gate she said, ‘There is good in you, Mikhail. Do not bury it.’ Then she gave me this.”

From his pocket he took a small folded paper. On it, in faded print, was a picture of the Last Judgment: angels above, flames below, and tiny naked sinners looking very uncomfortable.

“No pie?” said Semka.

“No pie.”

“Not even a potato?”

“Nothing.”

Semka took the paper and studied it.

“You paid sixty-five kopeks, walked six versts in mud, lost a day’s work, endured an inquiry, a sermon, an examination, and prayer—and received a picture of fire.”

“Yes.”

“That is dear.”

For a while we were quiet. Around us men shouted over cards; a woman carrying bowls pushed between the tables; from the kitchen came the hiss of fat and the smell of onions. Mishka chewed bread slowly now, his first hunger blunted. His face, for all its fatigue and mud, had recovered its usual mildness.

Semka turned the Judgment picture upside down.

“Listen, Misha,” he said. “You are an idiot.”

“I know.”

“No, a complete idiot.”

“Possibly.”

“But you did well.”

Mishka looked at him suspiciously.

“Are you mocking?”

“I mock everyone. It saves time. Still, you did well.”

“Then give me back my sixty-five kopeks.”

Semka recoiled.

“Virtue must be voluntary.”

“At least half.”

“You bought an experience.”

“I did not want an experience. I wanted the clasps.”

“And gave them away. Commerce cannot survive such instability.”

Mishka called him a louse. Semka replied that a louse at least held fast to what it acquired. Mishka reached across the table; Semka moved the bottle to safety; I ordered a plate of fried potatoes before friendship could become expensive.

When the potatoes came, peace returned. We ate them with bread, dipping both into the grease. Semka contributed money for another small bottle, which was as close as he ever came to repaying a debt not enforced by violence.

Later, warmed by vodka and the crowd, we discussed the matter philosophically.

“The old woman is not bad,” Mishka said. “Only she has eaten before she teaches.”

“That alters religion greatly,” said Semka. “A full man speaks of eternity. A hungry man asks when.”

“She did see good in you,” I reminded him.

“Yes, after six hours.”

“Good is hard to see under dirt,” said Semka.

Mishka rubbed his beard.

“Still, when she read in the garden yesterday—it was fine. The leaves, the wind, all quiet. I thought a man might become different.”

“You became poorer,” Semka said.

“For one day.”

“And tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow I may become different.”

“After breakfast?”

“Preferably.”

We laughed. Yet I remember that moment more clearly than many solemn events which were supposed to improve me. Mishka, ridiculous and weary, had done what neither Semka nor I would have done. He had bought stolen silver with money needed for food, walked through mud to return it, defended the thief who had cheated him, endured instruction from the person he had helped, and come away without even a pie. He did not call himself honest. Had anyone called him so, he would probably have objected. He knew too much about his own life—about petty lies, drunkenness, laziness, and occasions when unattended property had changed hands. But goodness, which sometimes avoids respectable houses because it dislikes being examined, had entered him blindly, stumbled about, and paid too much.

The old woman, too, was not wicked. She guarded her Bible, her garden, her salvation, and other people’s souls with the same anxious severity. She knew the passages concerning mercy, hunger, burdens, and mutual comfort; she had even marked some of them with blue ribbon. But when mercy arrived at her gate in torn boots, smelling of mud and vodka, she did not recognise it until she had questioned it for two hours, and afterward she tried to make it respectable.

As for Semka, he stole the clasps, cheated Mishka in selling them, and nevertheless spent his last kopeks that night on potatoes for us all. Men like us were made not of one metal but of rubbish swept from many workshops: a little iron, a little tin, a shaving of silver, much rust. Society preferred to weigh the rust, because rust was abundant and scales were cheap.

The inn began to close. The waiter wiped the tables by pushing crumbs onto the floor. Outside, rain tapped the cellar window. We had nowhere better to go, but at last even homeless men must return home.

Semka lifted his glass.

“To Mishka’s salvation,” he said.

“No,” Mishka objected. “That affair is not concluded.”

“To the clasps, then.”

“They are concluded.”

“To the old woman’s pies,” I suggested.

Mishka sighed.

“May they be granted peace.”

We drank.

Then we went out into the wet night and walked toward the ruined glass factory, Semka in front avoiding puddles, Mishka behind stepping into every one, and I between them, carrying the bread we had saved for morning. The wind blew from the river through our clothes. Far away a church bell sounded once, uncertainly, as though it too had forgotten the hour.

“Brothers,” Mishka said after a while, “do you think she will have the clasps put back?”

“Certainly,” said Semka.

“They will cost her money.”

“That is the moral consequence of property.”

Mishka pondered this.

“And do you think she will pray for us tonight?”

“She promised.”

“That’s something, eh?”

“It costs nothing,” Semka replied.

We walked on. Behind us the tavern door shut; before us stood the broken chimneys, the leaking roof, and another uncertain day. Nothing in our condition had improved. Mishka was poorer, Semka no wiser, and I no more courageous. Yet the queer affair was finished, the silver had returned to the sacred book, and for that night at least we were together, fed, and not entirely ashamed of one another.

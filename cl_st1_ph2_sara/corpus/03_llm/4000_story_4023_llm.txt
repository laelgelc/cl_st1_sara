### The Rope of Sorrow

One time, and Hanrahan wandering the roads west of Kinvara, he heard the crying of a fiddle from a house beyond the bog. It was a wet night, with a low moon slipping between the clouds, and there was a smell of turf-smoke in the air and the small cold smell that comes before frost. He had walked since morning, and there was mud on his broken boots and the grey of his stockings showing through, and he had but a few pennies and a little verse in his head that would not shape itself rightly.

But when he heard the fiddle he turned from the road, for he was never able to pass music or company, whether there was welcome for him or no welcome.

The house belonged to Martin Hehir, a farmer having three cows, a strip of oats, and a meadow going down towards the bare hills. There were neighbours gathered there that night, for the hay was saved and the weather had held, and there was whiskey on the table and cards laid out by the wall. Two boys were dancing in the middle of the floor, and a red-haired girl was keeping time with her hands, and old women were talking together beside the fire.

Martin Hehir saw Hanrahan at the door, with the rain shining upon his ragged coat.

“A welcome before you, Hanrahan,” he said. “Come in from the darkness. There is room near the fire, if there is not much else.”

“There is more wealth in a warm hearth than in a king’s treasury,” said Hanrahan, “and more nobility in a full cup than in a bishop’s blessing.”

At that Martin laughed, and he put a glass into Hanrahan’s hand. But Bridget Hehir, Martin’s wife, looked narrowly at the stranger, for she had heard of his wandering and his drinking, and of the songs he made for women and the curses he made against men who crossed him. She said nothing, because it is not wise to give offence to a poet of the Gael, and she let on to be stirring the fire.

Hanrahan drank, and the warmth came back to him. He drank again, and the room grew bright in his eyes. Then he saw Oona, Martin’s daughter, standing beyond the dancers.

She had been in the shadow by the dresser, but now the fire sprang up and laid its light upon her face. Her hair was dark and loosely tied, and there was a quietness in her that was not like the other girls. Her hands were brown from work, but they moved as gently as leaves when she gathered the empty cups. She had no grand dress on her, only a blue woollen skirt and a white kerchief, but Hanrahan thought there had never been a queen clothed in the like of them.

He put down his glass.

“Who is that girl?” he asked.

“You are in the house five minutes only,” said one of the young men, “and already you have forgotten the whiskey.”

“I would forget the sun itself,” said Hanrahan, “if she stood between me and its rising.”

The young men laughed, but Oona lifted her eyes to him.

“That is our Oona,” said Martin Hehir, and there was pride in his voice. “And she has enough work without poets putting idleness into her head.”

“It is not idleness I would put there,” said Hanrahan. “It is remembrance.”

“Remembrance of what?” Oona asked.

“Of the place you came from before you were born.”

The room grew quieter at that. Even the fiddler let his bow hang loose. Bridget Hehir turned from the hearth and said, “Girls have enough to remember in this world without being set thinking of another.”

But Oona came a little nearer.

“What place is it?” she said.

Hanrahan looked into the fire as if he saw a road opening among the red embers.

“It is a country where no roof is blackened by smoke and no field is bare after harvest. There are trees there of rowan and apple, and blossoms upon them together, though one belongs to spring and the other to autumn. The sea is as smooth as a silver shield, and the islands move upon it like cattle going home in the evening. No one grows bent there, and no hand loses its strength, and no beautiful face is covered over with clay.”

“You have never been there,” said the red-haired girl.

“I have gone near it,” said Hanrahan.

“How near?”

“As near as a dream is to waking. As near as the moon is to the well that holds its image.”

One of the boys laughed. He was a strong fellow named Colman Burke, and he had been watching Oona all the evening.

“If it is so fine a country,” he said, “why did they not keep you? They might have given you a coat without holes in it.”

There was laughter at that, and Hanrahan’s face darkened. But Oona did not laugh. She looked at the grey stockings showing above his boots, and pity came into her eyes.

“A man may come poor from a king’s country,” Hanrahan said, “if he leaves his treasure behind him. And a man may sit in a rich house and own nothing but the hunger of his heart.”

Then he turned to Oona.

“If you and I went out from this house, Oona Hehir, the night would open before us. The road would not be long under our feet. The stars would be candles carried for us, and the moon would be a white hound running before us. And when every dancer in Ireland had fallen weary, you and I would still be dancing.”

“There is no man in the house must dance with her but myself,” said Colman Burke, getting to his feet.

“You may dance with her feet,” said Hanrahan, “but it is I could set her heart dancing.”

There might have been anger then, but Martin Hehir called for another tune, and the fiddler began quickly. Yet Oona did not take Colman’s hand. She sat beside Hanrahan near the fire, and he told her of the old kings and the women of the Sidhe, and of white cities seen at sunset where no city had stood at noon. He told of ships going over the sea without sail or oar, and of wells that gave wisdom to whoever drank from them, though sorrow came after the wisdom.

As far as her mother could hear, Oona spoke hardly a word. But she leaned towards him, and when he lowered his voice, she leaned nearer still.

Bridget Hehir came to Martin and said under her breath, “Put him out.”

“I will not,” Martin said. “A poet’s word might blacken the oats in the field.”

“Then stop his tongue.”

“What lock would stop running water?”

Bridget went to the neighbour woman, Maire Loftus, who was broad in the body and sharp in the understanding.

“He will bring the girl away,” Bridget whispered.

“He has nowhere to bring her but the roadside.”

“The roadside is far enough.”

Maire watched Hanrahan for a while. He had a drop of drink taken by then, and pride was growing in him with every word. Oona’s cheeks were red from the fire, and there was a strange brightness in her eyes.

“Ask him for a song,” Maire said. “While he sings, I will think of something.”

So Bridget filled his glass, though it went against her heart, and said, “We have heard talk enough of that country. Let us hear its music, if you know it.”

Hanrahan stood up. The room was still, and the wind could be heard feeling along the thatch. He looked at Oona and began to sing:

> Beyond the foam, beyond the rain,  
> There stands a house without a door;  
> The dead go in and rise again,  
> And age lies sleeping on the shore.  
>   
> There hazel burns with scented flame,  
> And cups of amber overflow;  
> Queens with the morning in their name  
> Dance where the deathless roses grow.  
>   
> No bell is rung for burial there,  
> No lover turns from lover’s side;  
> The blossom lives upon the air,  
> The green leaf does not fall with pride.  
>   
> Come, heart of mine, come out of clay,  
> Come where the years have lost their breath;  
> The golden dancers tread the day  
> In that immortal land past death.

When the song was ended, no one spoke for a time. The fire settled, and a coal broke softly in two.

Then Oona rose.

“I will go with you,” she said.

Her mother caught hold of the dresser.

“You will do no such thing.”

“I will go only to see the road he speaks of.”

“The road he speaks of is hunger and wet grass.”

Hanrahan held out his hand. “Let her choose. The sun does not ask leave of the hill before it rises.”

“You have bewitched her,” Bridget said.

“If truth is witchcraft, let the priests burn the morning.”

Oona had taken one step towards him when Maire Loftus clapped her hands.

“There now!” she cried. “And I near forgot the rope.”

“What rope?” Bridget said, though she knew there was none.

“The hay rope for the stack. Your man said it must be twisted before morning, or the west wind will have the thatch from it.”

Martin looked at her, but Maire gave him such an eye that he held his peace.

“There is no man here knows the old way of twisting,” Maire went on. “Not Colman Burke, with all the strength he boasts of.”

“I know it,” said Colman.

“You do not. You would make a rope loose enough for geese to walk through.”

Hanrahan laughed. “There was never a rope twisted in Connacht I could not better.”

“Poets’ hands are soft,” Colman said.

Hanrahan spread his hands before the fire. “These hands have cut rushes, lifted stones, carried turf, and fought men twice your size. Bring the hay, and I will show you.”

Maire fetched an armful from beside the wall and divided it into long strands. She gave one end to Hanrahan and kept the other herself.

“You must twist and step backward,” she said. “There is no room otherwise.”

“I need no woman to teach me rope-making.”

“Then show us.”

He began twisting, drawing the dry stems together between his palms. Maire turned her end and moved after him, and Hanrahan stepped backward across the floor. He talked while he worked, boasting of ropes he had made for boats on the Shannon and halters for wild horses in Mayo, and the company gathered about to watch him.

“Keep it tight,” Maire said.

“Was there ever weakness in anything my hands held?”

“Backward with you.”

“I am going backward only that the rope may go forward.”

Oona stood beside the hearth, troubled and uncertain. Bridget came behind her and put both arms around her waist.

“Let me go,” Oona said.

“It will pass from you.”

“It will not pass.”

“It will. Look at his coat. Look at his boots. He would have you sleeping beneath hedges.”

“I would sleep beneath the stars.”

“You would sleep beneath rain.”

Hanrahan was near the doorway now. The door had been opened without his seeing, and beyond it lay the black yard and the misty road.

“More hay,” he called.

“There is plenty,” said Maire. “Backward now. Backward.”

He took another step, and another, and then his heel struck the threshold. Before he understood, he was outside. Maire dropped the rope, Bridget pushed the door, and Martin set the wooden bar across it.

Hanrahan beat upon the boards.

“Open!”

No one answered.

“Open, Martin Hehir, or there will be neither calf nor grain under your roof this year!”

Martin crossed himself, but Bridget said, “A curse spoken in drink has short legs.”

“Oona!” Hanrahan cried. “Come out to me!”

Oona struggled against her mother. “Let me go! You gave him welcome.”

“We gave him fire and drink, and he would steal the light out of the house.”

Hanrahan called again, but the fiddler had begun a reel at Maire’s bidding. Colman Burke seized Oona’s hand, and the others drew around her, clapping and stamping until the little house shook. Whether she danced willingly or no one could say afterward, but Hanrahan heard the quick tune and the heavy feet, and he heard laughter also, though it is likely not all of them were laughing.

He stood with the unfinished rope hanging from his hand. Then he threw it down and went away.

The mist had thickened, and the moon was hidden. He walked beyond the last houses and down towards the strand, where the tide was far out and the weed-covered stones shone faintly. There was no sound but the sea moving in its sleep and the thin crying of plovers over the wet ground.

Hanrahan sat upon a rock and sang the song again. But the immortal country seemed far from him now, and the words fell dead between his lips. Behind him, as he thought, the fiddle was still playing, though the house could not be seen.

“Oona,” he said, and the plovers answered him.

Then a white shape came through the mist.

At first he took it for a woman in a long cloak, and then for a wave standing upright, and then for one of the queens he had sung of in the house. Her hair was pale as bog-cotton, and there was a crown upon her that might have been silver or might have been the foam of the sea.

“So this is Hanrahan,” she said, “who would lead mortal women into the Country of the Young.”

“You know my name,” he said.

“All proud men are known to us.”

“Are you come to bring me across the sea?”

She laughed, and her laughter went over the flats like ice cracking.

“You were called before, and you would not come. The bright women called you from the hill, and you chose a warm fire and a cup among men. You were weak, Hanrahan. You were weak.”

“I chose my own road.”

“You chose every road and kept to none.”

The mist shifted, and she was gone. But something moved at his feet.

It was the rope.

He knew the dry hay of it, and he saw the marks his fingers had made in the twisting, though he had left it before Hehir’s door. It lay in a coil beside the rock, stirring a little as if breath were passing through it.

He stooped to take it, and it slipped around his wrist.

The touch was cold and wet.

He pulled, but the rope tightened. It was no longer a little hay rope. It was thick as the cable of a ship and scaled like a fish beneath his hand. It wound about his arm, his breast, his knees, and still its length came crawling out of the mist.

Then he saw it was a great water-worm, blind and shining, with a mouth opening in the dark beyond the tide. Its coils went around the rocks and the hills and the scattered houses, and one coil lay about the house where Oona was dancing. Another passed around Kinvara, another about all Connacht, and another beyond sight under the sea.

Hanrahan struggled, and the creature tightened.

The sky bent down. He saw that the stars were not stars at all, but wet gleams upon the worm’s black skin, and that the whole world was caught inside its turning.

Voices spoke from the mist, women’s voices, old and young together.

“He would not come when he was called.”

“He chose the mortal fire.”

“He desired the immortal blossom.”

“He put his hand to love and drew it back.”

“Let him have neither one nor the other.”

Hanrahan cried out, “I have made songs that will outlive kings!”

“Let the songs live,” said the voices.

“And let him wander.”

“Let him hunger.”

“Let him remember.”

Then one voice, colder than the others, spoke close beside his ear:

“Because you refused the women of the hills when their hands were open, no mortal woman will give you rest. You will go from door to door, and music will draw you in, and pride will drive you out. You will sit beside warm fires and carry winter within you. You will kiss living lips and taste the clay of the grave.”

“The cold in his heart,” said the voices.

“The cold, the cold.”

“Till the clay takes him.”

“Let him die, let him die, let him die.”

Hanrahan fell upon the stones.

When morning came, a fisherman found him sleeping above the tide-mark, his coat white with frost and a short piece of hay rope twisted around his hand. The fisherman woke him and asked what had happened, but Hanrahan would tell him nothing.

He looked inland, where smoke was rising from the houses, and then towards the sea, where the sun made a golden road upon the water.

After that he wandered on, and there are some say he never again sang the song of the immortal country in a house where a young woman was listening. But whether it was that time or another time the cold entered his heart, no one can be certain.

It is certain only that he kept the rope.

And on lonely nights, when drink had left him and no fire would warm him, he would take it from under his coat and feel it moving in his hands.

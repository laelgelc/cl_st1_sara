“His own shoe upon his own head”—such is the saying, Sahib, and the wisdom thereof is broad as the Grand Trunk Road. Yet what fault lies in the shoe if the head comes beneath it? I am Afzal Khan, son of Dilawar Khan, once constable of the Sirkar, and now a poor man with three children who cry for bread and a wife whose tongue is sharper than a new tulwar. Therefore I beseech the Presence to appoint me messenger, orderly, gatekeeper—whatsoever office brings seven rupees monthly and the shadow of protection.

A bad character? Nay, Sahib! Who has poured this poison into the ear of authority? I am known from Howli to the tehsil as a man of clean heart. If certain enemies say that I fled from justice upon a Government mare, they speak one half of one quarter of the truth; and a quarter-truth, as the Presence knows, is the father of a lie.

I will tell all. Then the Sahib may judge whether I am not honest.

In those days I served at the Howli Thana under Havildar Ram Baksh, a man of experience, with a belly like a grain jar and thoughts as subtle as the tracks of a snake on stone. There were six constables besides myself, but one had fever, two slept greatly, and the remainder performed according to opportunity. The country was troubled by dacoits—men without respect for law, save when law was properly explained to them.

Therefore Ram Baksh made an arrangement.

Was that corruption? O Sahib, do not strike the word so quickly! It was a peace treaty. The dacoits robbed to the east on Mondays and to the west after the moon had risen. We, being servants of the Sirkar, pursued them toward the north or south, according to the weather. Thus no man was wearied, the records showed great activity, and tranquillity was maintained.

Sometimes, when the Assistant Commissioner demanded arrests, the dacoits sent us a cousin of small value, or a fellow who had quarrelled over the division of plunder. We put irons upon him, and Ram Baksh wrote: “After a pursuit of four kos, the accused was captured at peril of life.” All was neat. The prisoner received food; the officers received praise; the true dacoits continued their business; and the Empire, Sahib, rolled onward like a well-oiled cart.

Bribes? Nay, gifts of friendship—two goats at the Feast, a basket of mangoes, sometimes a silver bangle whose owner could not be found. Shall a policeman insult a respectable man by refusing his mangoes? That way lies rebellion.

Our life at Howli was peaceful. By day we reclined beneath the neem tree upon charpoys and watched the melon beds beyond the wall. Those melons were sweeter than sugar cane and cool as water from a deep well. By night we slept, one man keeping watch unless sleep overcame him also. The rifles stood in the rack, six in number, and the great station record book lay beneath Ram Baksh’s pillow, because he said no thief could steal from under the head of Government.

It fell upon a night of such heat that even the lizards gasped. The moon was thin, the dogs were silent, and all honest men slept. Toward dawn Ram Baksh rose, desiring water. Then came a cry that shook the rafters.

“The rifles!”

We leaped from our charpoys. The rack was empty. Six rifles of the Sirkar—gone! There remained only a broken cleaning-rod and one strap.

“The book!” cried I, for I possess a quick intelligence.

Ram Baksh clutched beneath his pillow. The book also had departed.

It was here; it was there; it was gone. No door broken, no wall climbed, no sentry slain. We stared one at another, and our mouths became dry. For rifles may be replaced, Sahib, but the record book contained many matters: names altered, dates improved, confessions made by men who had never seen the Thana, and accounts of cartridges which, by the Mercy of Heaven, had gone elsewhere.

Ram Baksh sat in dust and beat his brow.

“We are dead men,” said he. “Yunkum Sahib will eat us uncooked.”

Know, O Presence, that Yunkum Sahib was Assistant Commissioner of that district, called by the people the Tiger of Gokral-Seetarun. He slept little, rode far, believed no report, and asked questions from the bottom upward. It was said he could smell a falsehood sealed in three envelopes. Also he travelled upon a devil-carriage of three silver wheels, noiseless as a ghoul, which moved when he beat it with his feet. Thus he arrived before the dust had warning.

When Ram Baksh remembered this, sweat fell from him like rain.

Then, behold, wisdom entered his heart.

“We have been attacked,” said he.

“But we have not,” said young Lal Singh, who was simple.

Ram Baksh struck him behind the ear. “Now thou hast been attacked. Let the rest follow.”

Aha! There was a man fit to command armies. At once we laboured. We broke the lock of the rear gate from within. We overturned two stools, smashed three cooking pots, and cut the mosquito curtains with bayonets. We fired shots through the roof and one into the wall, though that bullet, by misfortune, passed through the Havildar’s box and spoiled his festival turban.

Next came footprints. We ran round the compound barefoot, in boots, and in sandals; but they appeared too much like police footprints, because such they were. Therefore we fetched Ram Baksh’s mare and led her backward through the dust. Lal Singh pressed a wooden bowl into the earth to imitate the track of an elephant, until we explained that dacoits seldom brought elephants secretly to a police station.

“There must be blood,” said Ram Baksh.

No man spoke.

“There must be wounds.”

Still no man spoke.

Then Ram Baksh pricked his arm and showed us how small was the pain. We admired him greatly. He thereupon ordered me to cut Lal Singh upon the shoulder, Lal Singh to cut Ganda Mal upon the thigh, and Ganda Mal to strike me lightly over the head. Ganda Mal, being a man against whom I had testified in the affair of the missing onions, struck with enthusiasm. I fell among the cooking pots and saw seven moons.

Yet there was not sufficient blood. So we bought a goat from a widow behind the mosque.

Bought, Sahib—certainly bought! Payment was entered afterward under “information from secret agents.” We killed the goat, sprinkled its blood upon the veranda, dragged a blanket through it, and made handprints on the walls. There was smoke and breaking and blood and trampled earth. Even I, who knew the truth, trembled to behold the ferocity of the attack.

Now an attack requires attackers. Therefore we arrested Karim the potter, who had refused Ram Baksh credit; old Daud, whose nephew had complained to the tehsil; two brothers who owned the best melon field; a wandering barber; and a holy man who laughed when I slipped into a drain. We found upon them one rusty sword, two pruning knives, a brass spoon, and a child’s wooden pistol.

Evidence, Sahib, is like dough. In trained hands it assumes proper form.

The prisoners denied everything. Such obstinacy! We encouraged remembrance by the approved methods of the countryside—standing in the sun, sitting upon uncomfortable surfaces, and certain questions addressed through a split bamboo. Before noon the barber confessed that he had led two hundred dacoits through the rear gate, though he afterward confused the matter by confessing that he was the King of Delhi.

Ram Baksh wrote the report. What a report! Dacoits innumerable; shots like monsoon hail; policemen fighting breast to breast; the Havildar wounded while defending the record book; myself pursuing the enemy for three kos despite blood streaming into my eyes. We all swore to it. Why does the Sahib move his eyebrows? An oath is an oath, and we swore together.

At sunset came a whisper from the road: “The Tiger!”

Yunkum Sahib entered the compound on the devil-carriage, without horse, groom, bell, or mercy. Dust lay upon his boots, but not upon his face. He looked at the broken gate. He looked at the prints, including the elephant marks which Lal Singh had forgotten to erase. He put one finger into the goat’s blood and smelled it.

Then he smiled.

That smile, Sahib, was colder than an empty cooking pot.

Ram Baksh saluted and began: “At the third watch, your honour, not fewer than three hundred armed villains—”

“Four hundred,” whispered Ganda Mal.

“Not fewer than four hundred villains surrounded the Thana. We resisted for two hours.”

Yunkum Sahib examined the roof.

“They fired downward?” said he.

Ram Baksh replied that dacoit bullets, being fired by criminals, followed unlawful paths.

The Sahib heard all. He questioned each man separately. Our accounts agreed in every important particular, because Ram Baksh had beaten them into us. It is true that Lal Singh said the moon was full while I said there was no moon, and Ganda Mal declared the attack happened before midnight though he had been snoring beside me until dawn. These are small differences caused by battle.

Then the prisoners were brought. Karim’s face was swollen. Old Daud could not stand. The holy man saw Yunkum Sahib and began to laugh again. I disliked that laughter.

Yunkum Sahib said nothing. He ordered us all to the dak bungalow.

There, in the bathroom, beside the tin tub, stood the six rifles.

Upon the soap box lay the station record book.

Ho! Ho! Even now the wonder returns to me. Ram Baksh made a noise like a buffalo swallowing mud. Lal Singh sat down. As for me, I understood instantly—for, as I have said, my intelligence is quick.

Yunkum Sahib himself had come in the night upon that noiseless wheel-devil. He had entered by the open rear gate, passed the sleeping sentry, lifted the rifles from their rack, and drawn the book from beneath Ram Baksh’s head. Perhaps Ram Baksh had even turned over to assist him. The Tiger had carried all away and waited to see what tale we would manufacture.

He had expected negligence, Sahib. We gave him conspiracy, false evidence, false oaths, torture, wrongful arrest, destruction of Government property, misuse of ammunition, and the slaughter of a widow’s goat. It was a full basket.

Yunkum Sahib spoke softly: “Put them in irons.”

Now, when authority speaks softly, wise men run.

The others watched the rifles. I watched the door. Outside stood Ram Baksh’s mare, saddled, for he had intended to ride magnificently before the prisoners. I slipped beneath the arm of the chowkidar, sprang upon the mare, and departed in the direction of Afghanistan.

The mare died after twenty-nine miles.

Did I steal her? Sahib, consider: she belonged to a convicted man and carried an innocent one away from danger. Moreover, when she died I removed the saddle lest thieves take Government property. I would return it even now, if I knew where it had been sold.

Ram Baksh and the others received imprisonment. I alone escaped, which proves that Heaven distinguished between us. Since then I have lived honestly—except for hunger, which compels many adjustments. My children are thin; my wife curses the police, the dacoits, the British Government, and chiefly me.

Therefore employ me, Presence. Who better for a messenger? I know roads, reports, thieves, constables, mares, and the minds of Havildars. I sleep lightly now. I can swear strongly. I speak true talk even when truth is injurious to me.

Why does the Sahib laugh?

Seven rupees, then—six, and food? Nay, do not call the guard. There is no need. I also am of the household of the Sahib.

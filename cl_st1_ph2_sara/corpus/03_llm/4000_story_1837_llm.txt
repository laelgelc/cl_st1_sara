### THE SPLENDOUR OF EUPOMPUS

It was Emberlin who first explained to me the meaning of that cryptic sentence in Ben Jonson’s *Discoveries*: “Eupompus gave splendour to Art by Numbers.”

I had often read the sentence, and had as often allowed it to pass into that hospitable lumber-room of the memory where an educated man keeps quotations which he cannot explain but may someday have occasion to repeat. Jonson’s prose is full of such adamantine fragments. They have the air of inscriptions surviving from temples whose gods, rites and even architectural principles are forgotten; and I confess that I had never felt more curiosity concerning Eupompus than concerning the cook of Petronius or the more conscientious of the Neoplatonists.

Emberlin, however, was incapable of leaving a mystery alone. He telephoned to me one wet November afternoon and said, without preliminary civility:

“I have made a discovery about *Discoveries*.”

He paused, plainly expecting thunder.

“Not the authorship?” I asked.

“The authorship! My dear fellow, do not be Meredithian. No—the passage on Eupompus.”

“Of course.”

“You remember it?”

“Perfectly.”

“What does it mean?”

I told him that I had always supposed it meant what it said.

“That,” he replied, “is the last refuge of the unencyclopædic. Come at six.”

Emberlin inhabited three rooms above a maker of ecclesiastical vestments in Bloomsbury. The situation suited him. Through his windows one saw, according to the hour, either a grey wall, a yellow moon, or the upper half of an angel in a shop-sign; below, copes and chasubles slept in violet tissue-paper, while above them Emberlin constructed those aerial systems which were his chief luxury and, I rather believe, his only regular exercise.

His drawing-room was too crowded with books to appear untidy, for disorder ceases to offend when it becomes absolute. On the mantelpiece stood a bronze hand, a cracked astrolabe, two Chinese monsters, and a photograph of Mallarmé which Emberlin had framed upside down because, he said, it improved the forehead. Manuscripts occupied the chairs. The piano had not been opened for eleven years, though Emberlin was perpetually composing an opera upon Raymond Lully. Its libretto, he assured me, was complete except for the words.

He was at that time forty-two, though his face, which had preserved the innocence of a child and acquired the complexion of a bad winter, made any estimate hazardous. His eyes were bright, mobile and almost indecently intelligent. His conversation did not illuminate a subject so much as abolish the surrounding darkness. To pass an evening with him was to see the common universe—that hotch-potch of bills, pavements, appetites and parliamentary intelligence—dissolve, while in its place arose a crystal Eden of correspondences, analogies and delicious impossibilities. He could connect Giordano Bruno with the etiquette of umbrellas, or deduce from a spoon the melancholy of the late Roman Empire. That his conclusions were frequently false seemed a criticism of fact rather than of Emberlin.

He had, besides, the vanity proper to those who delight rather than succeed. Praise embarrassed him if it was general, but he accepted the minutest admiration with the gravity of a monarch receiving tribute. I had learned to humour him. When he showed me a poem of four lines, written in three alphabets and entitled *The Bronze Syllable*, I praised the caesura. There was no caesura, but the compliment nourished him for a week.

On the evening in question I found him seated before the fire, with a huge folio supported on his knees and a decanter at his elbow.

“You are late,” he said.

“By three minutes.”

“Two minutes and forty-seven seconds.”

I did not then observe the precision.

He poured me sherry and tapped the folio.

“This,” he said, “is Gaspard van Zuyler’s *De Artibus Numeralibus apud Alexandrinos*, Leyden, 1663.”

“Not the familiar Zuylerius?”

For a moment he looked almost offended.

“The elder Zuylerius, naturally. The familiar Zuylerius was his nephew and wrote on symbolic falconry. This is infinitely rarer. The British Museum copy disappeared in 1891, perhaps from shame. I found this in the library of Sir Andrew Pell, between a veterinary manual and the sermons of an uncle.”

“And he explains Eupompus?”

“He does not explain him. He reveals him. There is a difference.”

Emberlin leaned forward. The fire threw his large shadow over the books; and presently, in that room scented with dust, sherry and the ecclesiastical dyes from below, he began the history of Eupompus.

The man whom Jonson names so casually was, according to Zuylerius, an Alexandrian painter of the second century after Christ—not, Emberlin warned me, the earlier Eupompus of Sicyon, with whom he has been confused by scholars who deserve confusion. He enjoyed in youth a prodigious reputation as a portraitist. Governors, merchants, rhetoricians, courtesans and successful poisoners sat to him. He was especially admired for hands. One celebrated portrait represented the financier Cassianus counting gold; the fingers were so cunningly painted that three slaves, successively accused of stealing one of the coins, were tortured before the optical deception was discovered.

Eupompus received enormous fees, maintained a villa near Canopus, and might have died respectable had he not, at the age of thirty-nine, begun to count.

“The origin of the habit,” said Emberlin, “is disputed. Zuylerius attributes it to an afternoon spent waiting in the Baths of Hadrian. Cassiodorus Niger—whose authority is dubious but piquant—says that Eupompus counted the eyelashes of a dancing-girl and found the number prime. Personally, I accept the mosaic hypothesis.”

“What is that?”

“He observed that the floor of his studio contained twelve thousand nine hundred and sixty tiles.”

“Did it?”

“Almost certainly not. But the discovery, whether accurate or imaginary, transformed him.”

At first Eupompus merely introduced numerical exactitude into his pictures. If he painted a banquet, there were precisely forty guests, eighty sandals, two hundred fingernails and one hundred and seventeen olives. A hunting scene contained nine dogs, three boars, twenty-seven spears and three hundred and sixty-five trees, one for every day of the Egyptian year. His patrons were delighted. The enumeration of objects lent an agreeable solidity to ownership; a rich man, Emberlin remarked, likes to know not only that he possesses abundance but exactly how much abundance he possesses.

Soon, however, Eupompus became contemptuous of portraiture. The individual face seemed to him vague, accidental and morally disreputable. A nose could be long or short; a mouth might smile today and decay tomorrow; but Seven remained Seven even if Alexandria burned and the gods went bankrupt.

He began his first great philarithmic canvas, *The Black Swans*.

“It measured,” said Emberlin, consulting the folio, “ninety cubits by twenty-one.”

“Where did he hang it?”

“He did not hang it. He built a wall for it.”

Upon this Cyclopean surface Eupompus painted thirty-three thousand black swans advancing across a silver lake. They were arranged in eleven divisions of three thousand, each division subdivided into flocks of three hundred, thirty and three. No two birds occupied precisely the same attitude, though all possessed the same dimensions. In the foreground eleven dead swans floated upon their backs, their feet raised like black arithmetic signs against the water. The work required seven years and consumed so much lamp-black that the Alexandrian undertakers petitioned the prefect.

“What did it mean?” I asked.

Emberlin smiled pityingly.

“My dear fellow, it meant thirty-three thousand.”

The picture caused a sensation. Pilgrims came to count the swans, though no two pilgrims reached the same total. The more devout began again. One senator spent eighteen months before the canvas and died at the twenty-ninth thousand, whereupon his heirs accused Eupompus of concealing additional birds. The painter refused to defend himself, merely observing that error is personal but Number divine.

His disciples called themselves the Philarithmics—Lovers of Number. They maintained that counting was the purest form of contemplation because it alone permitted the mind to touch reality without soiling it by emotion. Beauty, courage, love and justice were, they said, regional superstitions; but four apples remained four even if the apples were sour, stolen, painted or theological. Their daily exercises included counting pebbles, breaths, roof-tiles, syllables, raindrops and the visible teeth of strangers. The advanced student was expected to count simultaneously in opposite directions.

Eupompus next painted *The Orchard of Proclus*. It represented one thousand and twenty-four trees arranged in quincunxes, each bearing a number of fruits corresponding to its distance from the central fountain. The fountain itself had sixteen basins, from which sixty-four streams descended by two hundred and fifty-six channels into a square pool. There were no people in the orchard. Humanity, Eupompus explained, would have ruined the progression.

After the orchard came *The Citizens under the Stars*: eight thousand four hundred persons standing in groups which reproduced the principal constellations. The rich formed Orion; slaves composed the Dragon; philosophers, being few and mutually hostile, were used for the smaller stars of the Lyre. Every citizen was painted with his mouth open, as if uttering his own number. From a distance the multitude resembled a yellowish cloud. From near at hand it was intolerable.

By then Eupompus had ceased to sell his works. Indeed, it was difficult to find buyers with adequate walls. He abandoned his villa and lived in an immense warehouse, sleeping beside a counting-board. His beard reached his girdle; his nails grew curved and translucent; and he ate beans, not from ascetic conviction, but because they were convenient to enumerate.

His doctrines also enlarged. Number, which had begun as a discipline of art, became the substance of the cosmos. The visible world was merely Number wearing a coloured cloak. Matter was interrupted arithmetic. Time was the counting of change; space, the counting of intervals; music, the counting of vibrations; virtue, the proper addition of acts. Even the gods were compelled to submit. Isis was Two because she was mother and widow; Serapis was Nine for reasons now unhappily lost; the supreme God was Zero and Infinity together—an identification which disturbed certain priests but delighted the tax-collectors.

“Neoplatonism?” I suggested.

“Neoplatonism is a fog through which one sometimes sees the moon. Eupompianism was an account-book in which the moon had been entered twice.”

His later paintings grew less representational and more alarming. One consisted solely of red squares diminishing toward a white centre. Another displayed six million blue points on a field of ochre. In *The Migration of the Units*, ten thousand naked figures walked from left to right, gradually losing their human features until the last was a perpendicular stroke. The work was praised by an elderly grammarian as the final conquest of accident.

There was also the amphitheatre.

Here Emberlin lowered his voice, though only the photograph of Mallarmé could have overheard us.

The picture showed a circular arena containing ninety-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine Cyclopes. They occupied ascending tiers, one above another, each creature naked, hairless and provided with a single enormous eye. All those eyes were fixed upon the solitary figure in the arena: a small, ordinary man with two eyes. He stood with his arms hanging helplessly, conscious that his superfluous organ made him monstrous. The Cyclopes neither threatened nor applauded. They merely counted him.

I confess that the conception troubled me. There are absurdities which, if pursued with sufficient logic, become nightmares; and the lonely biped under that arithmetic scrutiny seemed to express something in modern existence which I had no wish to understand.

“Was that his masterpiece?” I asked.

“No. His masterpiece was never completed.”

In his sixty-third year Eupompus announced *The Totality*. This was to contain every possible object, event, relation, thought and number: the universe displayed not as chaos, but as an exact inventory. He ordered a circular building three hundred cubits in diameter. At its centre he prepared a panel of polished ivory representing the Fons Deitatis, from which concentric rings would proceed through Pure Number, intelligible form, celestial mechanism, animal life, human history, private memory and the uncounted dust of futurity.

“How does one paint every possible event?”

“With difficulty,” said Emberlin.

Eupompus worked for fourteen years. He completed Zero, Unity, the first nine primes, two-thirds of Geometry, five planets, the concept of thirst, all red insects, and the left side of Cleopatra. He had begun Remorse when the catastrophe occurred.

One morning two of his most faithful disciples, Ptolemon and Bion, found him counting the blows of a hammer upon the floor. Whether they interrupted, contradicted his total, or offered to assist is not known. Eupompus killed both of them with the hammer—Ptolemon with seven blows, Bion with nine—then climbed the unfinished dome and threw himself into the court below.

Thus, Emberlin concluded, the splendour which Eupompus gave to Art by Numbers was regrettably transient. His followers dispersed. The warehouse burned; the great canvases perished; the word “Philarithmic” survived only in three corrupt manuscripts and one abusive marginal note. Ben Jonson, who had encountered some late epitome of Zuylerius’s lost Greek source, preserved the sentence without comprehending its magnitude.

“And that,” Emberlin said, closing the folio, “is my discovery about *Discoveries*.”

The room had grown dark. On the table stood two glasses, the decanter, and a small heap of walnut shells which Emberlin had arranged in rows while speaking.

“It is a remarkable history,” I said. “Do you believe it?”

He stared at me.

“Belief is a vulgar relation between the mind and fact. I have demonstrated it.”

“But why did Eupompus kill himself?”

“Madness, obviously.”

“And the disciples?”

“A regrettable complication.”

We dined afterward at a little restaurant in Soho, where Emberlin embarrassed the waiter by asking how many peas accompanied the sole. He explained that he wished merely to test Zuylerius’s assertion that modern Europeans had lost the instinct of quantity. I thought nothing of it. Emberlin’s curiosity had always been omnivorous, and if he chose to digest his fish numerically, it was no concern of mine.

Four months later he was a Philarithmic.

The transformation did not announce itself dramatically. There were no black swans, no warehouse, no murders. He simply began to count everything.

He knew that there were eighty-six paving-stones between his door and the corner, one hundred and forty-two from the corner to the tobacconist’s, and six hundred and eleven from the tobacconist’s to the British Museum—unless he crossed obliquely, in which case the number was reduced by nineteen. He had calculated the average footsteps required to traverse Russell Square in rain, sunshine and intellectual agitation. He informed me that the staircase to his rooms contained forty-seven steps, though one was “metaphysically ambiguous” because of a landing.

His bathroom tiles numbered two thousand three hundred and four. He had counted them so often that he could chant their arrangement while bathing. His bedroom possessed seven hundred and ninety-one visible objects if dust-particles were excluded, and an indefinite but approachable number if they were not. He measured the growth of his fingernails, the oscillations of the gas-flame, the beats of his heart before and after coffee, and the hairs remaining upon his head. The last inquiry, being repeated nightly, depressed him but did not cure him.

Conversation became impossible. If one mentioned Shakespeare, Emberlin replied that the plays contained a certain number of commas. The Alps suggested cubic mileage; love, the pulse-rate; death, the probable sum of human corpses. When I praised a sunset, he estimated the angle occupied by the red cloud. When Mrs. Pelham announced her engagement, he asked how many letters had passed between the lovers. She answered, with some spirit, that there were things which could not be counted. Emberlin looked at her with infinite compassion.

His old learning did not disappear at once. It contracted. That splendid intellect, formerly a palace with windows opening upon every century, became a narrow corridor lined with numbered doors. Mallarmé, Bruno, Lully and Petronius were still admitted, but only after submitting their statistics. Poetry was “syllabic distribution”; painting, “enumerated pigment”; music, “audible proportion.” He abandoned his opera because the chorus refused, in imagination, to consist of exactly two hundred and fifty-six voices.

We remonstrated. Ferris appealed to his humour; Dr. Latham to medicine; I, less wisely, to pride. He defeated us all by counting our arguments. Mine, he said, contained twelve propositions, of which seven were repetitions, three sentimental, one insolent and one unverified.

Once, in desperation, I demanded whether he had reflected upon the end of Eupompus.

“Constantly,” he replied. “The accounts are inaccurate. Seven blows and nine blows make sixteen; but the hammer was found to bear eighteen stains.”

There was no reasoning with him. Reason itself had become diseased.

I visited him for the last time in June. He was sitting on the floor amid thousands of white beans, sorting them into little heaps. The curtains were drawn against the distraction of daylight. His face had grown puffy and vacant; his bright eyes moved mechanically from heap to heap. He did not rise.

“What are you doing, Emberlin?”

“Approaching certainty.”

“About what?”

“The beans.”

He placed another bean upon a heap and whispered, “Four hundred and nine.”

I remembered the man who had once transformed an evening into a crystal Eden; who could touch the dullest fact and make it flower into paradox, history and laughter. That thaumaturge had vanished. In his place crouched this dreary animal, occupied by an operation which a child could perform, an idiot repeat, and a machine surpass.

On the wall above him he had written, in large black characters:

**EUPOMPUS GAVE SPLENDOUR TO ART BY NUMBERS.**

I left without saying good-bye.

It is customary to pity such obsessions as maladies, and no doubt pity is proper when one considers the sufferer’s helplessness. Yet I confess that my pity for Emberlin was mingled with a disgust so violent that even now I cannot wholly excuse it. To see a rich mind impoverish itself is worse than to see a miser starving beside gold; for the miser at least preserves the treasure, whereas the fanatic melts it down to manufacture his chains.

I have since reconsidered the death of Eupompus. Emberlin had said he killed his disciples and himself because he was mad. I rather believe the contrary.

For years Eupompus had been mad peacefully, industriously and with the approval of his school. Madness painted the swans, planted the quincunxes, marshalled the constellated citizens and assembled the ninety-nine thousand nine hundred and ninety-nine Cyclopes. Madness built systems, founded a terminology, attracted commentators and called itself Pure Number. It was patient, logical and complacent.

But perhaps, on that final morning, as he stood beneath the unfinished Totality with the hammer in his hand, sanity returned.

Perhaps he saw—not gradually, but in one intolerable flash—the fourteen wasted years, the rotting canvases, the beans, the tiles, the solemn disciples counting their breaths; saw Art reduced to inventory, God to a cipher, the universe to a ledger, and his own magnificent intelligence gnawing numbers like a rat gnawing bones. Perhaps Ptolemon and Bion approached him chanting the sacred progression. Perhaps their faces expressed the serene idiocy of perfect converts.

Then he struck them.

And, having destroyed what he could of Eupompianism, he climbed the dome and destroyed its author.

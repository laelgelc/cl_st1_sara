I was fifteen then, going on sixteen, and there wasn’t anything in Kentucky I cared about half so much as a thoroughbred horse. I reckon that sounds foolish to people who have never stood by a training track before sunup and heard a horse breathing in the dark, but I can’t help it. A horse could give me a feeling in my throat that no person ever did. I don’t mean a plow horse, though they are all right in their way, or one of those high-kneed carriage horses rich women like. I mean a thoroughbred, with his skin shining and his legs made clean and hard, and something held back inside him.

That held-back thing was what got me.

When I was nine, I ate near half of one of Father’s cigars because old Lem Barlow told me tobacco would stunt my growth and I had a scheme to become a jockey. It made me so sick they laid me on the porch with a wet rag over my face. Mother cried and Father laughed until she commenced jawing at him. I never got small enough for a jockey. By fifteen I was five feet nine and still going, but I learned everything I could about horses anyway.

I knew pedigrees the way our preacher knew the begats. I could tell you which mare threw which colt, which grandsire had wind, which family liked mud, and which horse would quit when another looked him in the eye. I knew all the big races and what weight the winners carried. I studied the racing sheets until the paper wore soft at the folds. Sometimes at night I would shut my eyes and see bright silks going around a turn, and I could smell trampled grass though there wasn’t anything in my room but old socks and the camphor Mother kept in the wardrobe.

So when I got the scheme to go to Saratoga, it did not seem wrong to me. It seemed necessary.

There was four of us: me, Virgil Tate, little Jim Pruitt, and Clarence Moseby, who everybody called Clary although he was bigger than any of us. Virgil was the only one with much sense, which was bothersome because he kept using it against the scheme.

“You know how far Saratoga is?” he said.

“Far enough to have racing worth seeing.”

“You know your daddy will skin you?”

“He has got to catch me first.”

“He’ll only wait till you come back.”

That was true, but I did not want to hear it.

Jim was ready because Jim would go anywhere if he thought there might be food. Clary came because he had been reading about tramps and believed hopping freight trains was manly. It was my scheme, though. I want that understood. If we had got killed under the cars it would have been my fault, and if it turned out glorious, that was mine too.

We cut out from Beckersville after dark with two blankets, three tins of sardines, a loaf of Mother’s bread I stole out of the pantry, and four dollars and eighty cents among us. I left a note saying I had gone north on important business and would return before school. I thought that sounded dignified. Later I learned Mother kept it and cried on it until the ink ran.

Hopping freights was not like Clary’s books said. There was no moonlit loafing in clean boxcars while some wise old tramp told stories. There was cinders in our eyes, boards hard enough to crack your bones, and brakemen who would kick you off without waiting for the train to stop. We rode in a car that smelled of onions, another that had carried hogs, and once between two heaps of black coal where Jim’s face got so dirty only his teeth showed.

In Ohio a brakeman found us.

He had a lantern and a red neck and a fist like a ham. Virgil jumped down and ran. Clary tried to explain we was traveling to Saratoga for educational purposes, and the man caught him by the pants and threw him into weeds. Jim crawled beneath the car and stayed there until the train moved, which near took off his arm. I don’t like remembering that part. We laughed about it later, but it wasn’t a joke when it happened.

We slept behind a feed store and woke wet with dew. We ate the last bread. Jim said he was starving, which he had said every hour since Cincinnati. By the time we got into New York State our clothes was tore and our faces looked mean enough that women drew their children aside.

But we made Saratoga.

It was morning when we walked into town, and the first thing I noticed was the smell. There was horses in it, and frying bacon, coffee, wet leaves, cigar smoke, and manure. I tell you it was lovely. Those things might not sound lovely separated out, but all together they was racing. My legs quit aching. Even Virgil stopped complaining.

There were automobiles by the hotels and colored silks in shopwindows and men reading racing papers on the sidewalk. Stable boys went past us with buckets. A little bay colt was being led across the road, dancing sideways and switching his tail, and I stood there grinning until Clary shoved me out of the way of a motorcar.

We did not have money enough to get into the track more than once, and we had nowhere to sleep. That was when I thought of Bildad Johnson.

Bildad came from Beckersville and had cooked for Mr. Washburn’s stable before going north. He was a big dark-skinned man with gray at his temples, and he could fry potatoes so you wanted to eat the pan. Father had once helped him when a horse trader tried to cheat him, so I figured he might help us. At least I hoped he would not wire Father before we got to see a race.

We asked all over the stable quarter until somebody pointed us toward a low shed behind Barn Sixteen. Bildad was standing over an iron skillet with a towel on his shoulder.

He looked at us, and then he looked again.

“Well, Lord preserve the wicked,” he said. “If that ain’t Mr. Henry Bell’s fool colt.”

“My name is Thomas.”

“I know your name. Fool colt is what you are.”

He made us stand in a row and tell how we got there. Virgil told the truth except for parts that made us look worse. Bildad listened with his arms crossed. When Virgil finished, he said he ought to box all our ears and telegraph home.

“But you won’t?” I asked.

“I ain’t decided.”

Then Jim stared at the skillet, and Bildad seen him. He gave a sigh that came up from his shoes.

“Sit down,” he said. “A hungry boy’s too pitiful to punish proper.”

He fed us potatoes, eggs, bacon ends, and coffee with condensed milk. Jim ate until I feared he would disgrace Kentucky. Bildad kept watching me over the stove.

“You come all this way for horses?”

“For Sunstreak and Middlestride.”

At that he quit looking stern.

Everybody who knew racing was talking about those two. They were both Kentucky horses, which made it more important. Middlestride was a long dark chestnut with a white mark down his face and the hindquarters of a locomotive. He broke slow and sometimes ran like he had forgot why he was there, but in the stretch he came on until the earth seemed to be pushing him.

Sunstreak was different.

I had never seen him, but I knew his pictures. He was a bright bay, almost gold when the sun caught him, with a small star and black legs. He had won seven races without once looking used up. People said his trainer, Jerry Tillford, kept him under wraps and that nobody knew how fast he could truly go.

“They run Saturday,” I said. “The Champion Mile.”

“I can read,” Bildad said.

“Which one do you favor?”

Bildad turned bacon in the pan. “Middlestride’s a mighty horse.”

“That ain’t an answer.”

“It’s the answer you’re getting.”

Bildad hid us in a room behind the kitchen where sacks of flour and potatoes was stored. We slept on straw pallets and had to keep low when owners and track officials came around. In return we carried water, scrubbed pots, peeled vegetables, and kept our mouths shut. Or we was supposed to. Jim could not keep his shut even asleep.

Those days before the race were the finest I ever had, or they would have been if what happened afterward had not reached back and spoiled them.

We woke in the black morning while stars were still out. The stable aisles had lanterns hanging in them, and horses put their heads over the doors when they heard feed buckets. You would hear a hoof strike wood, a chain rattle, a man speaking soft. Then the sky got gray and the horses went toward the track, steaming a little in the cool air.

We watched workouts from behind a hedge where Bildad said nobody would bother us. Jockeys crouched over necks. Trainers stood with watches. You could hear horses coming before you seen them, a growing drum on the ground, and then they went by with nostrils red and manes flat, and the sound stayed inside your chest after they were gone.

Clary liked the gamblers best. He followed them around listening to odds and came back saying things like, “The smart money is on Lady Banner,” when he had no money and wouldn’t know smart money if it bit him.

Virgil watched everything with a worried look, as if enjoyment was another trap I had laid for him.

Jim became acquainted with every cook in three barns.

I only wanted Sunstreak.

I seen Middlestride the second morning. They led him past after a gallop. He was bigger than I expected and a little rawboned, with sweat dark along his neck. He walked with his head down and one hind foot dragging just enough to make you wonder, but his eye was quiet. I liked him. You could not help liking him. There was something square about him, like a man who speaks slow because he means to be exact.

That afternoon I seen Sunstreak.

He came from between the barns with a boy on his back and Jerry Tillford walking alongside. I knew Jerry from photographs too. He was about forty, narrow through the waist, with a brown, weather-cut face. He did not wear a fancy suit or carry himself like the owners. His hat was pushed back and there was dust on his boots.

Sunstreak walked on a loose rein.

That was all. He was only walking. But I knew.

His coat had a light under it. His ears moved at every noise, but his body stayed easy, and the muscles along his shoulder went and came like water under silk. He looked modest. That is a funny thing to say about a horse, but he did. He was not showing off. It was as if he possessed some tremendous private fact and did not need to tell it.

My throat commenced aching.

I have had that happen three times with horses. The first was when I seen a mare foal in Father’s pasture. The second was Sunstreak. The third does not matter here.

Jerry laid one hand on the horse’s neck. Sunstreak turned his head a little, not enough to touch him, but enough to show he knew exactly where Jerry was.

Then Jerry looked up and seen me.

I was standing where I had no business standing. I expected him to curse or call a guard. Instead he kept his hand on Sunstreak and looked straight at me.

There was a shine in his eyes.

I don’t mean tears, and I don’t mean the sun, though the sun was low behind me. It was knowledge. That is the only way I can put it. He knew what he had beneath his hand, and he knew I knew too. For one second it did not matter that I was a runaway boy with dirty clothes or that he was Jerry Tillford, trainer of the finest horse alive. We was only two people understanding the same lovely thing.

I did not speak. He did not speak.

Then he smiled, hardly at all, and went on.

I stood there after they disappeared.

“Well?” Virgil said behind me.

“He knows.”

“Who knows what?”

“Jerry. He knows about Sunstreak.”

“Seeing he trains him, I would expect it.”

“You don’t understand.”

“No,” Virgil said. “I surely don’t.”

That’s what I’m writing about. At least I reckon it is. I knew what Jerry’s eyes meant then. I would have sworn it before a judge. Months later I did not know anything.

Saturday came clear and warm. Bildad got us through a service gate inside a wagon under empty baskets. It was a joke to him, but not to me. My heart was beating before we even climbed out.

The grandstand was already filling. Women wore bright dresses and men had straw hats. Bookmakers shouted. Programs snapped. Cigars made a blue haze under the roof. The track lay smooth and brown, and the infield grass looked so green it hurt.

We stayed near the rail where Bildad told us, though Clary wandered off to make a bet and lost thirty cents on the first race. He said his horse got shut off. Every losing gambler says that or something like it.

I scarcely watched the early races. They was just ordinary horses, though one ordinary horse could have been the pride of Beckersville. The Champion Mile was sixth on the card. By the fifth race I could not eat. Jim took my sandwich, looking ashamed for nearly half a second.

Then the horses came to the paddock.

Middlestride entered first. People clapped. His red coat was polished until every line of him showed. He looked somewhat clumsy walking, long through the body and heavy behind, but I knew what would happen when he got room. He would gather himself and come.

Sunstreak came last.

The noise changed. It did not get louder right away. It dropped, like people had drawn one breath together. Then it rose.

He was lovely.

His bay coat shone copper and gold. His black mane lay braided. The saddle looked like nothing on him, and the jockey’s pale-blue silk seemed only a little patch of sky that had settled there. Sunstreak walked once around the ring, quiet as a horse going to water, but you could see the power held in him. It was like the river above Niagara, which I had only seen in a picture then. The water is smooth there, but you know the falls are waiting.

Jerry walked beside him.

He checked a strap and spoke to the jockey. Then he stood at Sunstreak’s head, looking into his face. The shine was there again. I seen it from across the paddock. Tender and proud and scared, all at once.

I thought of Father when my little sister first walked across the parlor, and how he held his hands out though she never reached him. I reckon that is the nearest I can get. Jerry looked like Sunstreak belonged to him in a way money could not buy. Like he had built every breath in the horse and loved each one.

“He’ll win,” I said.

Virgil asked, “How do you know?”

“I know.”

The bugle sounded.

They went onto the track, seven horses altogether, but only two mattered. Middlestride jogged stiffly. Sunstreak moved light, stepping high for three strides, then settling. At the post one horse acted up and backed away. The crowd fretted. I dug my nails into my palms.

They lined.

The barrier went.

Middlestride broke last.

A gray horse took the lead, with Sunstreak third along the rail. They went around the first turn bunched close. I could not breathe right. People were shouting numbers. Clary screamed, “Move him out!” though the jockey could not hear and would not have listened.

At the half Sunstreak still ran third. He did not look fast. That was the terrible part. The gray in front was driving and another horse was reaching, but Sunstreak seemed to be going where he pleased, his head steady, the rein slack enough to tremble.

Middlestride was fifth and coming.

They hit the far turn.

The jockey on Sunstreak lowered his hands.

That horse changed.

He did not lunge or fight. He just let the hidden thing loose. One moment he was boxed behind two horses, and the next he slid between them like a bird dog flattening through broom grass. His stride opened. The whole track looked too little for him.

Middlestride came outside, big and pounding, passing horses one by one.

For a second they were nearly even entering the stretch, Sunstreak at the rail and Middlestride wide. Kentucky against Kentucky. The grandstand made one solid roaring sound.

I cried. I don’t care who knows it.

Middlestride was running with all his honest heart, neck stretched, mouth open. He gained for three strides. Then Sunstreak’s jockey looked beneath his arm and moved his hands once.

Sunstreak went away.

Not by much at first, and then by daylight. He crossed the finish three lengths in front. Middlestride was second, five lengths ahead of the rest.

There was a pause before the time went up.

Then people shouted louder than during the race.

He had broken the world record for a mile.

Clary hit me on the back. Jim yelled with his mouth full of somebody’s peanuts. Virgil laughed as if he had gone crazy. I leaned over the rail and shook and cried, because I had known and Jerry had known, and now everybody knew.

When Sunstreak returned, Jerry pushed through the crowd to him. Men grabbed Jerry’s arms and slapped his back. The owner was waving his hat. Photographers crowded close. But Jerry only reached for the bridle and put his face near Sunstreak’s cheek.

The shine in his eyes was brighter than before.

I thought, There is a man who has done one clean thing perfectly.

I would have followed him anywhere then.

That was why I went that night.

Bildad gave us stew after dark. Everyone in the stable quarter was talking about the record. Men who had bet Middlestride said he would win next time. Men who had bet Sunstreak claimed they had never doubted. Jerry’s name ran through every conversation.

Near ten o’clock I seen him leave with three men. They had been drinking. One was a heavy owner with a red face, and another wore a checked suit. Jerry walked between them, laughing.

I told Virgil I was going out.

“To do what?”

“Nothing.”

“That’s generally when you do the worst thing.”

I went anyway.

I thought perhaps there would be more celebration and I might stand near Jerry. I imagined somebody would ask him how he trained Sunstreak, and Jerry might see me and remember the moment by the barn. Maybe he would let me hear. I had started thinking I could be a trainer, seeing I was too tall to ride.

The men went toward town. I followed far enough back that they wouldn’t see. They stopped twice to drink out of a bottle. Jerry was unsteady now. Once he sat on a hitching rail, and the others pulled him up.

They turned down a side street where the houses got narrow and tired-looking. There was paper and horse droppings in the gutter. A piano played somewhere, but it was out of tune. Women laughed behind a window.

The men went into a yellow house with red curtains.

I knew what kind of place it was. We had one house outside Beckersville that boys talked about, though none of us admitted going near it. Father once called it a sorrowful place and said any man who bragged about visiting one was lower than the women in it. I did not understand all of that, but I knew the yellow house was rotten.

I should have left.

Instead I went around the side.

There was a little yard with dead flowers and broken bottles. The air smelled of spilled beer, perfume, garbage, and something sweet gone bad. A window stood open above a porch roof. Light came through it.

I climbed a drainpipe and pulled myself onto the roof.

Inside was a parlor with red wallpaper. The paper peeled by the ceiling. There was a sofa, three chairs, and a crooked picture of naked women pretending to be angels. Jerry and the others sat with four women. Bottles stood on the table.

One woman had red hair. She was big through the hips and shoulders, and for one foolish second she put me in mind of Middlestride. Then she laughed, showing a black place between her teeth, and the likeness was gone. Middlestride was clean. There was nothing clean in that room.

Jerry was talking loud.

“That horse was nothing when I got him,” he said.

One of the men laughed. “Nothing worth sixty thousand dollars.”

“He had legs. That’s all. Legs and speed without sense. He’d of burned himself out as a two-year-old.”

The red-haired woman leaned against Jerry. “But you fixed him, darling.”

“I made him,” Jerry said.

My hands tightened on the window ledge.

He said it again.

“I made Sunstreak. The owner paid bills. The boy rubbed him. But I made him what he is. He ain’t got a breath I didn’t teach him to take.”

It was not true. Jerry had trained him, yes. He had guarded his strength and taught him to wait. That was fine. But he had not made Sunstreak. No man made the light under that skin or the heart that carried him down the stretch. Jerry knew that. I had seen him know it.

“He loves me,” Jerry said. “That horse would lie down and die if I told him.”

The men cheered. One spilled whiskey on the rug.

I felt hot all over. I wanted to climb through and hit Jerry, though he could have whipped me easy. He was taking the lovely thing and making it cheap. He was turning Sunstreak into something he owned like a watch.

Then the red-haired woman took his face in both hands.

“Do you love me like your old horse?” she said.

Everybody laughed.

Jerry looked at her.

The shine came into his eyes.

It was the same shine.

I tell you it was. Do not say I was too far away or that the light played tricks. I was close enough to see sweat by his hair. He looked at that woman the same way he had looked at Sunstreak in the paddock, tender and proud and hungry, as if they shared some secret nobody else could touch.

Then he kissed her.

I dropped from the roof.

I landed badly and cut my knee on a bottle. Somebody shouted from inside, but I ran. I went through alleys and across a street without looking, and an automobile missed me by inches. The driver cursed. I kept running until the fine houses and hotels were behind me.

At the barns I found Virgil awake.

“We’re leaving,” I said.

“When?”

“Now.”

“Did something happen?”

“No.”

“You’re bleeding.”

“I fell.”

He watched me. Virgil always did have too much sense.

“What did you see?”

“Nothing. Wake the others.”

Bildad caught us before we reached the gate. He stood in the dark with his nightshirt tucked into his pants and asked where we thought we was going.

“Home,” I said.

“At midnight?”

“Yes.”

He looked at my knee and then at my face. “You boys steal something?”

“No.”

“Somebody after you?”

“No.”

“What happened?”

“Nothing happened.”

That was a lie. Everything had happened.

Bildad argued, but I would not stay. At last he packed bread, cold chicken, and apples into a flour sack. He gave us two dollars and told Virgil, not me, which freight yard to try.

Before we left he put his hand on my shoulder.

“Horses will be here in the morning,” he said.

I jerked away. “I know it.”

He had not accused me of anything. I was still angry at him.

The trip home took four days. I hardly spoke. Clary complained we was leaving at the best time. Jim asked if I was sick. Virgil waited until the second night, when we lay in an empty car rocking south, and then he said, “Was it Jerry Tillford?”

I sat up.

“How did you know?”

“You followed him.”

“I never said I did.”

“You didn’t need to.”

I thought of telling Virgil. But if I told it, the thing would become common. He might laugh, or worse, he might understand it in some way I could not.

“He lied about Sunstreak,” I said.

“What kind of lie?”

“I don’t want to talk.”

So we didn’t.

Father met me at Beckersville station because somebody from the yard telephoned that four scarecrows claiming to be local citizens had come off a freight. Mother cried and held me. Father looked like he meant to kill me, but Mother would not turn loose.

He did whip me later. Not bad. I think by then he was too relieved to put his heart in it.

For a while everybody asked about Saratoga. Clary told stories until we had ridden on top of trains through thunderstorms and fought six brakemen. Jim spoke mostly of food. Virgil said little.

I talked about Sunstreak’s race. I could not help that. When I described him moving between the horses on the turn, the old ache came into my throat. But before I finished I would remember the yellow room and Jerry kissing that woman, and the ache would turn sour.

Sunstreak raced twice more that year and won both times. I read the accounts. I cut out one photograph and then threw it into the stove. A week later I regretted it and searched the ashes, which was foolish.

It is winter now. I am sixteen.

Sometimes Father takes me to Mr. Washburn’s farm, and I stand by the pasture fence. The yearlings come nosing at my coat. They are still lovely. I have not quit knowing that. On cold mornings their breath drifts white and their coats grow rough, and they look at you plain and honest. A horse does not claim he made the grass or the speed in his own legs. He is what he is.

But racing does not smell the same in my mind.

Before Saratoga I could think of manure and coffee and cigar smoke together, and it was all lovely. Now that rotten perfume comes into it. The yellow wallpaper gets behind the green track. Sometimes even Sunstreak’s bright coat seems touched by the light from that room, and I hate Jerry for doing that.

I know men go to women. I am not a baby. I know a man can be drunk and brag. Father says a man’s mouth often outruns his character when whiskey gets hold of him. Maybe Jerry loved the horse one way and wanted the woman another way. Virgil would probably say it is simple.

It is not simple to me.

The shine was the same.

If Jerry had only been ugly with Sunstreak, I could understand. If he had beaten him or treated him like machinery, then Jerry would be rotten and the horse clean, and that would be all. But Jerry knew. That is the trouble. He knew the power held quiet under Sunstreak’s skin. He knew enough to wait for it and care for it and bring it whole to the day when it could break the world’s record. He loved the horse like a father loves a child.

Then he carried the same look into that foul room.

I can’t make it out.

Maybe the shine did not mean what I thought. Maybe lovely and clean are not the same thing. Maybe a person can understand something fine and still be rotten somewhere else. But if that is true, how do you know what to trust in him?

I keep seeing Jerry’s hand on Sunstreak’s neck. I keep seeing the woman’s hands on Jerry’s face.

They are joined together now, and I cannot pull them apart.

What did he do it for?

Merchant Vahl had never made a speech in his life, unless one counts those brief and forcible addresses which he delivered every Saturday evening to the warehouse clerks upon the sin of leaving string on the floor. Yet on the day when his only son returned from the University, with a black coat, a pale forehead, and opinions upon nearly everything, the merchant determined to propose a toast.

The dinner was a considerable one. There were present Judge Krag, Solicitor Bentsen, the Adjunct from the Latin school, two captains, three elderly merchants with their wives, and a young lady who played the pianoforte with more expression than accuracy. Madame Vahl had prepared twelve dishes, according to the ancient Norse principle that hospitality consists in compelling one’s friends to eat twice as much as is good for them. Rhine wine, port, and a recherché liqueur from Hamburg stood upon the sideboard; and the merchant, who had already confused the Rhine wine with the port, was in a state at once elevated and disquieted.

His son, Henrik, sat at his right hand. He had been absent three years, and during that period had acquired, together with some Latin and political economy, the agreeable conviction that the world was waiting to be explained by him. His father looked at him often, with pride not unmixed with astonishment. It seemed scarcely possible that this long-haired young man, who spoke familiarly of constitutions and called certain respected historians “obsolete,” was the same child whom he had once chastised for sailing his mother’s workbox in the rain-barrel.

When the roast had been removed, Merchant Vahl began to tap his glass with a knife.

The guests became attentive; indeed, several of them raised their glasses at once, for experienced diners know that the first sign of a peroration should never be neglected. Madame Vahl grew pale. She knew her husband’s virtues, which were numerous, and his limitations, which she had been obliged to learn by heart.

“My friends,” said the merchant, “I—I have desired to say—I mean, on this occasion—”

“Take your time, Vahl,” said the solicitor benevolently.

This advice made matters worse. The merchant glared at him, drank what he supposed to be Rhine wine, and began again.

“My friends! We are assembled beneath my roof to welcome home my son—my Henrik—from the University of our Fatherland. There he has pursued knowledge; there he has associated with distinguished professors; there, in short, he has become one of those upon whom the future depends.”

Henrik lowered his eyes modestly, while taking care that everybody observed him do so.

“We fathers,” continued Vahl, finding unexpectedly that he possessed both a subject and a voice, “are too prone to believe that wisdom was born with us and must die with us. It is not so. The world advances! The son must stand upon the father’s shoulders; he must see farther, think more clearly, act more nobly. If our children do no more than repeat us, then we have laboured in vain.”

Judge Krag nodded gravely. He had heard almost the same sentiment at a public banquet the previous week and had approved it then also.

“We must not fear their independence,” cried the merchant. “No! We must rejoice in it. Let them correct our errors; let them cast aside our prejudices; let them outgrow us! For what does the gardener desire? Not that the young tree should remain forever beneath the old, but that it should rise—higher, stronger, and nearer heaven!”

Here the young lady’s mother whispered, “Beautiful!” and Madame Vahl, whose apprehensions had by now changed into tears, pressed her napkin to her eyes.

“I drink, therefore,” concluded the merchant, “to The Young! To their courage, their knowledge, their liberty—and to the day when they shall surpass us!”

Every glass was emptied. Henrik embraced his father; the ladies were moved; the Adjunct declared that the speech did honour not only to the paternal heart but also to the century. Merchant Vahl sat down amid acclamations, astonished at his own eloquence and sincerely persuaded of every word he had uttered. This sincerity must be admitted in his favour. Men do not always lie when they proclaim principles which they will repudiate ten minutes later; they merely speak from one part of their nature and act from another.

After dessert, the ladies withdrew to the drawing-room, and the gentlemen remained with their cigars. This custom is said to descend from the sea-kings, though no sea-king was ever known to smoke a Havana; historical facts of this kind are most secure when they cannot be investigated. Presently, however, Madame Vahl opened both doors and insisted that the gentlemen join the ladies, lest her curtains, her cushions, and her female guests should all become smoke-dried together.

Coffee was served, and the conversation turned, by that easy descent common after dinner, from poetry to taxation and from taxation to the late ministry.

“The ministry fell,” said Judge Krag, “because it offended the landed interest.”

“Certainly,” said Vahl. “Everybody knows it.”

Henrik, who had hitherto listened with the amiable impatience of one allowing error time to expose itself, leaned forward.

“Pardon me, Father, but that was not the cause.”

There was a small silence.

“What was not the cause?” asked Vahl.

“The opposition of the landed interest. The decisive vote concerned the commercial tariff, and the coastal deputies divided against the government.”

“I think,” said the merchant, smiling, “that I remember the circumstances.”

“No doubt; but the division lists were published. Professor Rosing analysed them in his lectures.”

“Professor Rosing!” exclaimed Captain Dahl, who had never attended a lecture and therefore entertained a natural suspicion of professors.

Merchant Vahl’s smile became more elaborate.

“My dear Henrik, lectures are excellent things, but public affairs are not learned from a desk. I was in the capital that very week.”

“But the date you mentioned was two months earlier.”

“Are you correcting my dates?”

“I am only saying that the ministry resigned in March, not January.”

Several guests examined their coffee-cups. Solicitor Bentsen coughed behind his hand, and the young lady at the pianoforte struck three notes without knowing it.

Vahl’s colour deepened.

“You return after three years and tell me what I myself witnessed?”

“If you witnessed it in January, Father, you witnessed something else.”

A snicker escaped one of the captains. It was instantly suppressed, but suppressed laughter, like a mouse behind wainscoting, is often more disturbing than laughter openly admitted.

“Henrik,” said Madame Vahl, “your father naturally knows what he is speaking about.”

“The lists are quite clear, Mother.”

“The lists!” cried Vahl. “Always lists, books, professors! Do you suppose a little university learning authorises you to contradict your elders before their friends?”

Henrik stared at him.

“You said only just now that the young ought to correct the errors of the old.”

The words were accurate; and accuracy, at an inconvenient moment, is one of the least pardonable forms of insolence.

“I said nothing about impertinence.”

“I was not impertinent.”

“You imply that I do not know the month in which an event occurred under my own eyes.”

“I imply only that you are mistaken.”

“Only!”

“And I am right.”

There are households in which the phrase “I am right” may be received as a contribution to discussion; the Vahl household was not one of them. Madame Vahl set down the coffee-pot. The Judge frowned judicially, though without having heard the evidence; and Solicitor Bentsen attempted mediation by remarking that, as the proverb said, a father might be wrong in the calendar and right in the almanac. Nobody understood him, which frequently happened with the solicitor’s proverbs.

Merchant Vahl rose.

“So this is the liberty we have been celebrating! A son returns to his father’s house in order to give him lessons before his guests.”

“You invited independence.”

“I invited no such exhibition!”

“You toasted it.”

“Be silent!”

Henrik became very pale. Youth can endure injustice better than humiliation, chiefly because it does not yet know how common both are.

“I will not say I am wrong when I am right,” he said.

“Then you may leave the room.”

The old Adjunct, Master Lind, had watched the dispute through half-closed eyes. He was Henrik’s former teacher and had once beaten him for confusing Hannibal with Hasdrubal, though he now remembered only that the boy had possessed an unusually honest face. Teachers generally forget their punishments sooner than their pupils do; they have more of them to remember.

He rose, placed a hand upon Henrik’s arm, and said:

“Come, my dear boy. The evening is warm.”

They went through the glass doors into the garden. Behind them the company began, in cautious murmurs, to repair the shattered dignity of the host. Madame Vahl wept; Merchant Vahl declared he had never been so insulted; and Judge Krag observed that universities were useful institutions, provided young men did not remain at them too long.

The garden descended towards the fjord. The sunset lay red upon the water, the birches were motionless, and from the drawing-room came the first uncertain measures of a nocturne. Henrik walked rapidly, while the Adjunct, who suffered from his knees and from no illusions, followed more slowly.

“It is monstrous,” cried the young man. “Before everyone he praises truth, progress, freedom—and the instant I speak the truth he calls it disrespect!”

“Yes,” said Master Lind.

“Then you agree with me?”

“Entirely.”

Henrik stopped. Agreement, when obtained too easily, is sometimes disappointing.

“Yet you led me away.”

“I feared that, if you remained, you would prove yourself right beyond all possibility of forgiveness.”

“But ought truth to yield to vanity?”

“Ought? No. Does it? Every day.”

“My father did not mean one word of his speech.”

“On the contrary, he meant every word while he was speaking. That is the most ordinary and the most curious part of it.”

Henrik looked towards the lighted windows. His father’s figure passed across them, broad, agitated, and patriarchal.

“Then men are hypocrites.”

“Sometimes. More often they are merely divided. Your father loves youth as an idea. Youth as a son who corrects his dates is another matter.”

“And you think this natural?”

“I think it human, which is not precisely the same thing, though it is commonly used as an excuse.”

They reached the little arbour at the foot of the garden. Master Lind sat down with relief. Old teachers are believed to delight in walking while they impart wisdom; in reality they prefer a bench (a rarer thing with old teachers than is generally supposed).

“When we are young,” he continued, “we serve truth because we possess nothing else. Later we acquire a house, a name, a position, a wife who fears scenes, clerks who bow to us, and children who know more recent facts. Then truth becomes a guest whom we are glad to honour, provided he does not sit in our chair.”

“I shall never be like that,” said Henrik.

“Of course not.”

“I mean it. If one day I have a son, and he knows better than I, I shall rejoice. I shall listen to him. I shall never demand respect at the expense of reason.”

“No?”

“Never! Age has no privilege against truth. The coming generation must be freer than ours; otherwise mankind goes backward. I would rather be corrected a thousand times than silence an honest conviction.”

The sunset illuminated his pale face; the fjord glowed behind him; his hand was raised, and his voice had that transporting resonance which comes to young men after wine, injury, and a noble generalisation. Anyone might have been impressed but Master Lind.

The teacher regarded him affectionately.

“My dear Henrik,” he said, “in twenty-five years you will sit at the head of this table. You will have your father’s warehouse, your father’s house, perhaps even his waistcoat. Your son will return from the University and correct you before a judge and two captains. You will then discover that independence is admirable in the abstract and intolerable at dessert.”

“I shall not.”

“You will.”

“I swear I shall not.”

Master Lind rose and took his arm.

“Come,” he said. “We must return before they forgive one another without us.”

Twenty-five years later Henrik Vahl, merchant, civic councillor, and father of a university student, proposed at a family dinner a toast to the fearless intellect of the new generation. Before the evening ended, he ordered his son from the room for contradicting him about the date of a treaty.

And the teacher was right.

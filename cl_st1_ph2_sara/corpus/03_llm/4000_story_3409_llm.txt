# Pomona’s Account of the Grange

When it first became necessary for me to leave Rudder Grange for a period of three weeks, I regarded the prospect with an uneasiness which Euphemia, although sharing it to a reasonable extent, endeavored to persuade me was out of proportion to the occasion.

The journey was not one of pleasure. Certain matters connected with my business demanded my presence in a city sufficiently distant to make daily travel impossible, and Euphemia, who had relatives in the neighborhood of that city, proposed to accompany me. This arrangement was in every way agreeable, except that it involved the abandonment, for twenty-one consecutive days, of our garden, poultry, cow, horse, house, barn, furniture, preserves, implements, and general interests to the direction of Pomona.

Pomona was young. I do not know that any exact statement of her age had ever been made to us, and I did not care to press the subject, because she had once informed Euphemia that people who asked too many questions about ages generally had something to conceal regarding their own. But she possessed the strength of a young woman, the judgment of a middle-aged one, and, at times, the independence of a municipal government.

She had come to us as a servant, but the title never wholly described her. She cooked, swept, milked, fed the chickens, assisted in the garden, opened the front door, discouraged peddlers, and expressed opinions upon everything that occurred within sight of the house. If she thought an opinion was especially valuable, she expressed it upon things that had not yet occurred.

Euphemia trusted her.

“If anything happens,” my wife said, as we sat beneath the pear tree discussing the journey, “Pomona will know what to do.”

“That,” I replied, “is exactly what troubles me.”

Still, there was no alternative. Our hired man, Jonas, was to sleep over the barn and attend to the heavier work; but Jonas had an infirmity which rendered him less useful in emergencies than in ordinary agricultural labor. When confronted by anything unexpected, he went to ask somebody what ought to be done. If the barn had caught fire, I believe Jonas would have driven three miles to inquire whether he should pump water or save the harness.

Pomona, on the other hand, never asked anybody what ought to be done. She did it, and permitted the propriety of the act to be considered afterward.

For several days preceding our departure, I prepared written instructions. I drew up a list showing the proper hours for feeding the cow, the horse, the chickens, the ducks, the two pigs, and the dogs. I described the treatment of the tomato vines in dry weather; I marked the days upon which the asparagus bed might require attention; and I explained that no stranger, under any pretext whatever, was to enter the house, barn, garden, orchard, or poultry-yard.

Pomona listened while I read these instructions.

“You needn’t worry about strangers,” she said. “If they come where they ain’t wanted, they’ll find out.”

This answer did not relieve me, but Euphemia smiled.

Our household dogs were two in number. Lord Edward was a setter of excellent disposition but imperfect discipline. He believed that every person who entered the grounds came expressly to see him, and he welcomed visitors with an enthusiasm which had several times injured their clothing. The other dog, who belonged properly to Jonas, was a small yellow animal called Peter. Peter possessed none of Lord Edward’s friendliness and little of his size; but he compensated for these deficiencies by a fixed suspicion of mankind and a bark which no reasonable person could long endure.

On the morning of our departure I walked about the property with the solemn interest of a man who is taking leave of his native land. I examined the fastenings of the barn, tested the pump, looked at the cow, counted the fowls, and impressed upon Pomona the necessity of closing the west gate at sunset.

“Supposin’ somebody wants to come in after sunset?” she asked.

“No one whom we desire to see will come after sunset,” I answered.

“Then I won’t let ’em in,” said Pomona.

At the railway station, Euphemia discovered that she had left a small package upon the hall table. I was secretly gratified by this evidence that anxiety was not confined to myself, although I did not say so. Pomona, who had driven us to the train, returned for the package at a speed which caused me to regret that I had not included in my written instructions a paragraph upon the treatment of horses.

We remained away twenty days.

The business which had demanded my attention was concluded sooner than I had anticipated, and, as neither Euphemia nor I found city life an adequate compensation for separation from our home, we determined to return one day before the date announced in our letters.

The prospect was delightful. During the last part of the journey we spoke continually of Rudder Grange. Euphemia wondered whether the late peas had done well, whether the speckled hen had brought off her brood, and whether Pomona had remembered to air the guest chamber. I wondered whether the young apple trees had been watered, whether Jonas had properly repaired the pasture fence, and whether any unauthorized persons had crossed the premises.

It was nearly four o’clock when we left the railway at our station and hired a conveyance to take us home. As we approached the familiar road, Euphemia leaned forward and watched for the first view of the chimneys.

“How peaceful it looks!” she said.

At that moment the driver turned into the lane, and we discovered that the outer gate was secured by a chain and padlock.

This was unusual.

I descended and examined the obstruction. The chain was a new one. The padlock was also new, and no key in my possession would open it. The driver suggested that we go around by the lower road and enter through the orchard gate. We adopted this course, although I was already conscious of painful apprehensions.

The orchard gate was locked.

We continued along the road until we reached the small entrance near the barn. This gate had not only been locked but strengthened with two boards nailed diagonally across it.

“There has been some dreadful trouble,” said Euphemia.

I did not reply. The driver, who had begun by exhibiting a neighborly interest in our circumstances, now looked at me as though I might be a person attempting to enter property which did not belong to me.

We discharged him and passed our trunks over the fence. Then, with considerable difficulty and some injury to my coat, I assisted Euphemia across. We left the baggage under a tree and proceeded toward the house.

At every step our alarm increased. The lawn bench was gone. The iron chairs which ordinarily stood upon the porch were gone. The rustic table beneath the maple had disappeared. The wheelbarrow, the watering pots, the movable chicken coops, and even the grindstone had vanished. The windows of the house were closed, the shutters fastened, and no smoke came from the kitchen chimney.

“It has been robbed,” said Euphemia.

I hastened forward. On the front door was nailed a large placard, printed in heavy black letters:

**THIS PROPERTY, WITH ALL BUILDINGS, STOCK, CROPS, AND HOUSEHOLD EFFECTS, WILL BE SOLD AT PUBLIC AUCTION FOR UNPAID TAXES. TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED.**

There are moments when the human mind, although ordinarily active, refuses to perform its functions. I read that notice twice before its significance reached me. Then I turned to Euphemia.

“Unpaid taxes!” she exclaimed. “But you paid them!”

“I certainly paid every demand presented to me.”

“Could there be some other tax?”

“No just government could invent another.”

We stood before the door in silent consternation. I was attempting to determine whether it would be better to seek the tax collector, a lawyer, or the sheriff, when the door opened about six inches and Pomona looked out.

Her expression was one of surprise, but not alarm.

“Well!” she said. “You’re back.”

“Pomona,” cried Euphemia, “what has happened?”

“Nothin’ much,” she replied. “I didn’t expect you till to-morrow.”

I pointed to the placard.

“What does this mean?”

She opened the door a little farther.

“That? That’s all right. There ain’t no auction.”

“Then why is it there?”

“I put it there.”

I confess that this answer, although it removed one apprehension, immediately produced several others.

“Did you lock the gates?” I asked.

“Yes, sir.”

“Did you remove everything from the grounds?”

“Everything I could lift. Jonas helped with the grindstone.”

“Where is Jonas?”

“Gone to his sister’s. I told him he might go yesterday, ’cause there wasn’t nothin’ more for him to do till you got back.”

“Where are the animals?”

“All safe.”

“The furniture?”

“What I moved is safe.”

“The garden?”

“Never looked better.”

“Why,” asked Euphemia, “does the house appear to have been abandoned, and why have you announced that our home is to be sold for taxes?”

Pomona considered the question.

“It’s a long story,” she said.

“Then begin it,” I replied.

She looked beyond us toward the orchard.

“You didn’t leave the trunks outside, did you?”

We admitted that we had.

“Then we’d better fetch ’em in before it rains. There’s a cloud comin’ up.”

This practical consideration could not be disregarded. Pomona produced the keys of the gates from a pocket containing so many other objects that their immediate discovery appeared almost miraculous. We brought in the baggage, and she conducted us through the house.

Everything was in excellent order. The rooms were clean, the furniture polished, the pantry well supplied, and the plants thriving. The removed objects had been stored in the barn, woodshed, attic, cellar, and spare bedroom. The animals were well and, except for a remarkable air of watchfulness in the dogs, seemed properly contented.

“Now,” said Euphemia, when we had taken off our traveling things, “tell us what occurred.”

“I wrote it down,” said Pomona.

“You wrote it down?”

“Yes’m. I thought, if anything happen’d while you was gone, you’d want to know it. And it ain’t always easy to remember everything in its right order. So I made it into a novel.”

I had known that Pomona was fond of reading. She preferred works in which orphaned heroines, concealed wills, Italian counts, underground passages, and poisoned goblets appeared in generous quantity. I had not known that she aspired to authorship.

“Where is this novel?” I asked.

She produced a school copybook from beneath the cushion of a chair.

“It ain’t finished,” she said. “The last chapter was goin’ to tell how you came home and everything turned out. But you come too soon.”

Euphemia and I sat at the dining-room table, while Pomona placed herself near the window. She opened the book, cleared her throat, and began to read in a voice unlike her usual one. It was slower, deeper, and designed, apparently, for the utterance of grave historical facts.

“‘Chapter the First,’” she read. “‘The Depart-ure of the Man-si-on’s Lord and Lady.’”

“Pomona,” I said, “you need not read all of it. Tell us what happened.”

“But it don’t sound near so good that way,” she answered.

Euphemia requested her to continue, and she did so.

“‘The sun had arose in golden splendor over the verdant pathways of Rudder Grange, when two forms might have been seen departing from its hospitable portals. They was the noble master and his fair companion, bound upon a journey into distant regions, where business and relations summoned them. Behind remained Pomona, the youthful but undaunted guardian of the domestic hearth, little recking of the dark events which Fate was even then preparing to occur.’”

She paused.

“That means you went away,” she explained.

“We understand,” I said.

“‘For one day and one night all remained calm. The feathered tribes consumed their frugal repasts; the mild-eyed cow cropped the herbage; and no sound disturbed the peaceful scene, except Peter, who barked most of the time without no reason. But upon the second morning a sinister vehicle approached the man-si-on.’”

“What sort of vehicle?” asked Euphemia.

“A lightning-rod wagon,” said Pomona, dropping her literary tone. “There was a man drivin’ it, and rods and points and wire and all sorts of things stickin’ out.”

She returned to her manuscript.

“‘From this vehicle descended a vile intriguer in a linen coat. His brow was bold, his speech deceivin’, and in his hand he bore implements of destruction.’”

“Was he armed?” I asked.

“He had a brace and bit.”

“That is not generally considered an implement of destruction.”

“It would be if somebody bored into your roof with it.”

There was reason in this, and I permitted her to proceed.

The lightning-rod man, it appeared, had received an order to equip a house in our neighborhood. He had confused the directions and had arrived at Rudder Grange under the conviction that he was expected. Pomona told him he was mistaken. He consulted a paper, looked at the house, and told Pomona that young women did not always understand business arrangements made by their employers.

“I says, ‘They didn’t order no rods.’

“He says, ‘My instructions says a gray house past the second bridge, with a barn west of it.’

“I says, ‘There’s three gray houses past the second bridge.’

“He says, ‘This is the one I was directed to.’

“I says, ‘It ain’t.’

“He says, ‘I can’t spend my time arguin’ with hired girls.’

“I says, ‘Then you’d better spend it drivin’ away.’

“And then,” Pomona continued, “he got into his wagon, and I thought he was goin’. But he only drove around to the back, where I couldn’t see him from the kitchen.”

The man had brought two assistants. While one unloaded rods and ladders, the other climbed to the roof of the side porch. Pomona heard the ladder scrape against the gutter and went out.

“I told ’em to stop, and they laughed. The head man says they’d put the rods up, and when you got home you’d thank ’em for not lettin’ a foolish servant interfere with the safety of the property. Then he went up the long ladder himself.”

“What did Jonas do?” I asked.

“He’d gone to the mill.”

“And Lord Edward?”

“He wanted to help carry the rods.”

This was entirely consistent with Lord Edward’s character.

Pomona first attempted to remove the ladder. The two assistants prevented her. She then went into the house, shut the doors, and reflected.

“‘The undaunted maiden,’” she read, “‘now perceived that peaceful words was vain. Her home was being invaded by mercenary miscreants, and above her devoted head sounded the fatal boring of the roof.’”

Here she looked at me.

“He’d begun by then. I could hear him.”

“What did you do?” Euphemia asked.

“I got the bulldog.”

We had no bulldog.

Pomona explained that a large brindled animal had appeared at the back door on the previous evening. It wore a broad collar without a name and had accepted food with an air suggesting that it had never doubted its right to receive it. Pomona had tied it in the woodshed until its owner could be discovered.

“He looked like he’d know what to do with lightning-rod men,” she said. “So I took him out.”

The dog’s appearance caused the two assistants to reconsider their duties. One retreated to the wagon. The other climbed the ladder. Pomona fastened the bulldog at the foot of it.

“Then I took the ladder away from the porch,” she said.

“But the man was on the roof!” cried Euphemia.

“That was what the ladder was for.”

“What did the other two men do?”

“They wanted to put it back.”

“And you prevented them?”

“The bulldog did. He’d got excited by then.”

The assistants discussed the matter from the wagon, while their employer addressed Pomona from the roof. His observations, according to the novel, were “language of such a sulphurous complexion that the summer atmosphere seemed darkened thereby.”

“He called me names,” Pomona explained. “But that wasn’t my lookout. A person on somebody else’s roof ain’t in no position to be particular.”

The siege continued for nearly half an hour. The lightning-rod man demanded his ladder. Pomona required him to promise that he and his assistants would leave the property. He refused. His assistants suggested compromise, but did not approach the dog. The dog, who enjoyed the proceedings, stationed himself beneath the eaves and kept his attention fixed upon the roof.

At length the man attempted to descend by way of the porch post. The bulldog objected. The man returned to the roof with such speed that, in Pomona’s account, “his garments fluttered like the pinions of a panic-stricken eagle.”

“What became of his horse and wagon?” I asked.

“I sent ’em home.”

“You did what?”

“The men wouldn’t go without him, and he wouldn’t come down while they was there, ’cause he was ashamed. So I untied the horse, turned the wagon around, and gave it a slap. The assistants ran after it.”

“Did they catch it?”

“I don’t know. It went pretty fast.”

The chief salesman, watching his vehicle disappear, became more reasonable. He agreed to remove all the rods already placed upon the roof and to depart without charge or argument. Pomona restored the ladder but retained the bulldog as security.

The man descended, gathered his tools, removed a metal point which he had fixed to a chimney, and climbed down again. He then discovered that he had neither horse, wagon, nor assistants.

“He says, ‘How am I expected to get away?’

“I says, ‘Walk.’

“He says, ‘My property’s gone.’

“I says, ‘It went down the public road. You can look for it.’

“He says he’d have the law on me.

“I says, ‘You can bring all the law you want, but don’t bring no more rods.’

“Then he went. He looked back twice, but the dog burst into bark-ter, and he didn’t stop.”

Pomona had written six pages concerning the departure of the “defeated marauder.” She was persuaded to omit them.

When the lightning-rod man had passed out of sight, she attempted to return the bulldog to the woodshed. The animal, however, had undergone a change. Before the affair he had been a hungry stranger; afterward he regarded himself as commander of the premises.

He would not allow Pomona to approach the kitchen door.

“At first,” she said, “I thought he was only playin’. Then I found out he wasn’t.”

She tried the front door. The bulldog ran around the house and arrived before her. She tried the cellar entrance, a window, and the porch roof. Each attempt increased his determination. He stationed himself where he could watch the principal entrances and expressed violent disapproval whenever she came near.

“But you were outside the house!” said Euphemia.

“Yes’m.”

“For how long?”

“Three days.”

Euphemia laid both hands upon the table.

“Where did you sleep?”

“In the barn the first night. The second night I slept in the hammock till it rained, and then I went to the barn. The third night I didn’t sleep much, ’cause I was figurin’ how to get in.”

“What did you eat?”

“Eggs, mostly. I could get into the henhouse. And there was milk.”

“How did you milk the cow?”

“The usual way.”

I did not consider further inquiry useful.

During these three days the bulldog guarded the house with a fidelity which would have been admirable had he admitted any distinction between burglars and the person responsible for feeding him. Pomona attempted conciliation by throwing bread, potatoes, and a piece of pie within his reach. He ate each offering and resumed his hostility.

“I knew there was meat in the pantry,” she said, “but I couldn’t get to it. So I traded eggs to a boy goin’ by for a piece of beef. He wanted to know why I didn’t go in the house, and I told him we was airin’ it.”

With the beef she gradually induced the bulldog to enter the woodshed, where she shut him in. She then regained possession of the kitchen, cooked him a substantial meal, and, through a series of cautious negotiations extending over the afternoon, restored their former relations.

“By supper-time,” she said, “he’d found out I was the one that cooked.”

This circumstance would, in many cases, have established peace permanently; but there remained the problem of the other dogs. Peter distrusted the bulldog from the beginning and barked at the woodshed door until he lost his voice. Lord Edward, with his customary hospitality, wished to make the new arrival’s acquaintance, but showed signs of reconsidering this wish whenever the bulldog looked at him.

Pomona believed that three dogs, properly united, would provide an admirable defense for the property. Three dogs divided into opposing parties, however, promised only confusion.

“I thought they’d ought to settle which one was boss,” she said.

“Surely,” Euphemia said, “you did not allow them to fight.”

“I didn’t allow ’em at first. That’s why they kept wantin’ to. Every time I tied one, the other two went around lookin’ insultin’. So I says to myself, ‘Let ’em have it out, and then they’ll know.’”

“Pomona!”

“I didn’t let Peter into it. He ain’t built for fightin’. He’s built for noise.”

The contest between Lord Edward and the bulldog took place in the orchard. Pomona selected an open space where neither animal could become entangled, removed Lord Edward’s collar, and stood ready with a bucket of water.

“‘The hour had now arrived,’” she read, “‘when the rival monarchs of the brute creation should decide by mortal combat which was to reign over the peaceful domains. On one side stood the tawny stranger, his bosom swelling with unconquerable rage. On the other was Lord Edward, graceful but determined, while Peter occupied a distant eminence and encouraged both parties by frantic outcries.’”

The fight began.

Pomona’s literary account was exceptionally full at this point. It included comparisons to lions, gladiators, armies, thunderstorms, and “two ships colliding on the crimson ocean of war.” Euphemia objected to nearly every sentence. I myself experienced considerable indignation, because Lord Edward, whatever his faults, was our dog, and it seemed to me that no household servant had the right to arrange for him a trial by combat.

“Was he injured?” I demanded.

“Not much. His ear was chewed some, but it’s all healed now.”

“And the bulldog?”

“He got a scratch on his nose.”

“Then Lord Edward lost?”

“Yes, sir. But he didn’t lose bad. He found out what he needed to know.”

At the height of the conflict, when Pomona was preparing to use the water, a visitor entered the orchard. He was the Reverend Mr. Barncastle, pastor of a church which Euphemia and I occasionally attended. Mr. Barncastle was a serious man, much concerned with the moral tendencies of the community, and the spectacle before him produced an effect which Pomona described as “righteous horror mingled with personal caution.”

He did not approach the dogs.

“What is the meaning of this disgraceful exhibition?” he called.

Pomona, whose attention was occupied, answered that it meant the dogs were settling matters.

“Who authorized it?”

“Nobody had to. They was goin’ to do it anyhow.”

Mr. Barncastle replied that he had often feared the growth of brutal amusements among the less instructed classes, but had not expected to encounter a deliberately arranged dogfight upon my premises. He asked whether I approved of such proceedings.

This question placed Pomona in a difficult position. She knew that I did not approve; but she also felt that an admission of this fact would make her solely responsible and, more importantly, might injure my reputation by suggesting that brutal amusements flourished at Rudder Grange without the master’s knowledge.

“I says, ‘He ain’t here.’

“He says, ‘That does not answer me.’

“I says, ‘He don’t generally speak about dogfights.’

“He says, ‘I should hope not.’

“And then the bulldog rolled Lord Edward over, and the minister climbed onto the fence.”

“What did you say to him?” Euphemia asked.

Pomona hesitated.

“I said you was thinkin’ of bein’ a vestryman.”

I had never thought of being a vestryman.

“Why did you say that?” I asked.

“Well, I had to say somethin’ respectable right off. He was lookin’ at the dogs, and then at the house, and then at me, as if the whole place was goin’ to perdition. I thought a vestryman was about as far from dogfightin’ as I could get.”

Mr. Barncastle’s attention was immediately diverted. A vacancy had recently occurred in the vestry, and the selection of a suitable person to fill it had become a matter of importance. He asked Pomona whether I had expressed a serious desire for the position.

“I told him you’d spoke of it confidential.”

“I never spoke of it at all.”

“That made it more confidential.”

Euphemia covered her face, but I saw that she was laughing.

Pomona continued. Mr. Barncastle said that, although the choice did not rest entirely with him, he should be gratified to learn that I felt called toward greater usefulness in church affairs. He had sometimes thought me insufficiently active, but he had never doubted my respectability. Pomona assured him that respectability was one of my strongest points.

“What precise words did you use?” I asked.

“I said you was as respectable as anybody, when you had your good coat on.”

This qualification appeared unnecessary.

The minister then looked again at the dogs. By that time the battle was over. Lord Edward had withdrawn beneath a tree, and the bulldog stood in possession of the field. Peter continued to bark, but from a safe position.

Pomona explained to Mr. Barncastle that the contest had not been an amusement but a necessary domestic regulation.

“He says, ‘Violence can never be a proper regulation.’

“I says, ‘You didn’t live here with them dogs.’

“He says, ‘Kindness and moral influence would have accomplished more.’

“I says, ‘You can try it if you want.’

“Then the bulldog looked at him, and he said he was late for a pastoral visit.”

Before leaving, Mr. Barncastle promised to consider my name in connection with the vacant office.

Thus, in my absence and wholly without my knowledge, I had been transformed from the proprietor of a dogfighting establishment into a candidate for ecclesiastical distinction.

“What became of the bulldog?” asked Euphemia.

“He’s here.”

“Here!”

“Yes’m. He and Lord Edward get along first-rate now. Lord Edward don’t bother him, and he don’t bother Lord Edward. Peter bothers both of ’em, but they don’t mind Peter.”

“Where is he at present?” I asked, glancing toward the door.

“Under the kitchen table, likely.”

I looked beneath it. The dog was there. He regarded me steadily, but, as Pomona sat near him, he made no objection to my presence.

“His name’s General,” she said. “I named him after the fight.”

I decided that the question of General’s permanent residence could be postponed.

“Is this all?” I asked. “Does the novel now explain the tax notice?”

“Not yet,” said Pomona. “That comes in the chapter about the tree man.”

She turned several pages.

“‘Chapter the Seventh. The Deep Designs of the Arboreal Tempter.’”

The tree salesman had first appeared ten days before our return. He represented a nursery and wished to sell Pomona fruit trees, flowering shrubs, grapevines, ornamental evergreens, and a new species of berry which, according to him, combined the virtues of the strawberry, raspberry, currant, and peach.

Pomona informed him that she did not own the place and could buy nothing.

“He says, ‘I’ll wait for the proprietor.’

“I says, ‘He won’t be home for two weeks.’

“He says, ‘Then I’ll call in two weeks.’

“I says, ‘You needn’t.’

“He says he was sure you’d be interested, because no intelligent owner would leave a property in its present unimproved condition.”

“What unimproved condition?” I asked.

“That’s what I asked him. Then he pointed out places where he could put things. He wanted to cut down the lilac bushes, move the grape arbor, and plant a row of Norway spruces right through the asparagus bed.”

I felt an immediate and personal resentment toward the man.

Pomona ordered him from the grounds. He went as far as the road, but remained there, making notes. The next day he returned with catalogues. Peter announced him before he reached the gate, and General added a deeper warning. The salesman did not enter, but stood outside and called to Pomona.

“He said he had thought of a plan for the whole place. I told him we already had a plan.

“He said the present arrangement was primitive.

“I said it suited us.

“He said people never knew what suited ’em till an experienced man told ’em.

“I said General was experienced with men.”

At this, the salesman departed. But he came again the following morning. This time he brought a young assistant, who measured the road frontage while the salesman sketched the house and grounds.

Pomona released Peter. She did not release General, because, as she remarked, there was no reason to waste a strong dog upon a weak man when a weak dog would do. Peter pursued the assistant into a ditch and retained possession of one trouser cuff.

The salesman threatened legal proceedings. Pomona returned the cloth and advised him not to leave pieces of himself where dogs could get them.

“He come back the next day,” she said.

“Why?” Euphemia asked.

“He said perseverance was the foundation of business.”

The man’s perseverance was extraordinary. He called on foot, in a buggy, and once in company with a gentleman whom he introduced as a landscape architect. He pushed catalogues beneath the gate. He sent a circular through the post. He offered a discount. He offered two discounts. He declared that the berry which combined the properties of four fruits was almost sold out, although Pomona observed that he appeared to have enough of it to talk about forever.

At last he told her that he intended to remain in the vicinity until the proprietor returned. He had learned the date of our expected arrival from the station agent and meant to call upon us before breakfast.

“I knew you wouldn’t like that,” Pomona said to me.

“I should not.”

“So I tried to think of somethin’ that would keep him away. Dogs didn’t do it. Locked gates didn’t do it. Tellin’ him no didn’t do it. And Jonas said if we hurt him, there might be trouble.”

“I am glad Jonas recognized that possibility.”

Pomona’s first plan was to place a sign upon the gate announcing the presence of scarlet fever. Jonas objected that such a notice might bring the health authorities. Her second plan was to announce that a dangerous bull was loose upon the property; but we owned no bull, and the salesman had seen the cow. She then considered displaying a placard stating that the house was haunted, but feared that this might attract rather than repel visitors.

“Then,” she said, “I thought of taxes. Everybody’s scared of taxes, and nobody wants to buy trees for a place that’s goin’ to be sold.”

She visited a printing office in the village and ordered the placard. The printer asked by what authority she announced a sale. Pomona replied that she lived upon the property and knew what she was doing. This statement, while true in its separate parts, was misleading as a whole.

When the notice was ready, she fastened it to the front door. To support the impression it created, she removed all movable objects from the grounds, closed the shutters, stopped using the front rooms, locked the gates, and put boards across the barn entrance.

“What about the animals?” asked Euphemia.

“I kept ’em back of the house. The cow’s in the lower pasture, the chickens are in the far yard, and I only let the dogs out when nobody was lookin’.”

The scheme succeeded. The tree salesman arrived, read the notice from beyond the gate, and stood for several minutes in apparent reflection. Then he pushed his catalogue between the bars, took it back, and walked away.

“He didn’t even ask no questions,” Pomona said triumphantly. “That was the first time.”

“Did no one else ask questions?” I inquired.

“A few people stopped. I told ’em it wasn’t a good time to visit. Mrs. Drover came to see if the taxes was really unpaid, but I let General look out the window, and she went home.”

“And you intended,” said Euphemia, “to restore everything before we arrived?”

“Yes’m. Jonas and me was goin’ to put it all back this afternoon and take the sign down after dark. Then to-morrow morning the place would look just the same, and the tree man wouldn’t know nothin’ till you was safely inside.”

“Except,” I observed, “that the entire neighborhood may now believe us ruined, dishonest, or both.”

Pomona appeared surprised.

“They won’t believe it long after they see you.”

This confidence in my mere appearance was flattering, although not wholly reassuring.

She turned to the last written page of her novel and resumed her ceremonial voice.

“‘Thus did the youthful guardian, surrounded by vile intriguers, savage beasts, religious dignitaries, and the dark clouds of unpaid taxation, preserve inviolate the sacred trust committed to her hands. But little did she dream, as she removed the grindstone beneath the pale beams of the moon, that the rightful owners was even then rushing homeward one day ahead of time, and that before another sun had sunk in the crimson west—’”

“That is where I stopped,” she said. “I was goin’ to finish it to-night.”

For a few moments neither Euphemia nor I spoke. During the reading of Pomona’s narrative, my emotions had passed through so many conditions that I found it difficult to determine which properly belonged to the present moment.

She had unlawfully driven away a tradesman’s wagon; she had detained another tradesman upon our roof; she had remained excluded from our house for three days by a dog which did not belong to us; she had arranged a combat between that animal and Lord Edward; she had informed a minister that I desired church office; she had published a false announcement concerning my financial affairs; and she had caused Rudder Grange to resemble the abandoned estate of a bankrupt.

On the other hand, the lightning-rods had not been installed. The roof had sustained no serious damage. The animals were healthy. The garden had been watered. The house was cleaner than when we left. No property had been stolen. The tree salesman had been defeated. Even the dogs had attained, by methods which I could not approve, a stable form of government.

Euphemia looked at me, and I looked at her. We had often found that in domestic affairs no advantage resulted from one of us expressing an opinion before the other was ready to share it.

At length she turned to Pomona.

“Did you have a very difficult time?” she asked.

Pomona considered.

“No’m. Not after General and Lord Edward got settled.”

“Were you frightened?”

“Not exactly. I was mad when the rod man bored the roof, and I was hungry the second day General kept me out. But things come out all right.”

“And you truly thought you were protecting the place?”

“What else would I be doin’?”

There was no answer to this.

I told Pomona that the tax notice must be removed at once; that every person who had heard of the supposed sale must be informed of its falsity; that no future tradesman was to be imprisoned upon any part of the house; that vehicles were not to be dispatched without their drivers; that dogs were not to be encouraged to fight; and that under no circumstances was my name to be proposed for an office, religious or secular, without my knowledge.

She listened attentively.

“All right,” she said. “But what if they choose you vestryman anyway?”

“They will not.”

“Minister Barncastle seemed pretty set on it.”

The following morning proved that she had some reason for this belief. Mr. Barncastle called before breakfast. He came partly to inquire into the report of our unpaid taxes, which had reached him through Mrs. Drover, and partly to inform me that, after consideration, he had spoken favorably of my willingness to serve the church.

It required a long conversation to correct both errors. I explained that the placard had no legal significance. He expressed relief. I explained that I had never announced a desire to become a vestryman. He expressed disappointment.

“But your young woman was most explicit,” he said.

“Pomona’s explicitness,” I replied, “is not always connected with the facts in the customary manner.”

He reflected upon this. Then he remarked that a man who inspired such loyalty in his dependents might possess qualifications for useful service, whether he knew it or not. Before leaving, he asked me to think seriously upon the matter.

Thus Pomona’s fiction, even when corrected, retained a certain influence upon reality.

Later in the day we restored the grounds. Jonas returned from his sister’s and helped carry out the benches, chairs, tools, coops, and grindstone. The shutters were opened, smoke once more rose from the kitchen chimney, and the false placard was burned. Several neighbors passed slowly along the road while this work was in progress, but I made no effort to detain them. I felt that any explanation offered voluntarily would merely furnish additional material for discussion.

The tree salesman did not return. A week later, however, I received by post a catalogue upon which was written: “Should the property remain in private hands, I shall be pleased to submit revised plans.”

I burned this also.

General remained with us. We advertised for his owner, but no one claimed him. He accepted me after Pomona formally presented us, and he accepted Euphemia at once. Lord Edward deferred to him in matters concerning bones, doorways, and places near the stove, but otherwise maintained his dignity. Peter continued to insult both large dogs from positions of comparative safety. The system, although not one I would have established, worked with surprising smoothness.

As for Pomona’s novel, Euphemia persuaded her to finish it. The final chapter described our return, our “overpowering astonishment,” and the “triumphant vindication of faithful innocence.” I was represented as clasping Pomona’s hand and declaring that no monarch of antiquity had ever possessed a more trustworthy guardian. I did not remember making this statement, but I allowed it to remain, because by that time I had learned that revision of Pomona’s imagination was a labor likely to produce little practical benefit.

We had intended, before our journey, to make another trip later in the season. We now abandoned the plan.

This decision was not prompted by distrust. On the contrary, we had acquired an enlarged opinion of Pomona’s abilities. We knew that, if left alone, she could defend the house, regulate the animals, discourage commerce, influence the clergy, and alter the public standing of the property without requiring assistance from us.

It was because we had acquired this enlarged opinion that we resolved, with complete agreement and no necessity for prolonged discussion, not to leave Rudder Grange again for a very long time.

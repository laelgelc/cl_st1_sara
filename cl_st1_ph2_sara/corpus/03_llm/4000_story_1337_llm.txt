# THE SOWER OF THE WILDERNESS

The border has vanished. Where, within the memory of men yet living, the wolf crossed the dim trail at noon, and the smoke of a solitary cabin rose through leagues of unbroken woodland, there are now populous villages, fenced meadows, iron roads, and towers whose bells divide the day with civilized precision. The great trees have fallen; the deer-path has become a highway; the ford, once known only to the hunter and the wandering tribes, is spanned by masonry; and the river, which bore the silent canoe or the rude flatboat of the emigrant, turns the wheels of manufacture and reflects at night the lamps of cities.

With the wilderness, much of its true history has also disappeared. The louder incidents have survived. We remember the rifle-shot, the midnight alarm, the ambuscade, the blockhouse, and the sanguinary exploits of men whose courage, though often unquestionable, was chiefly destructive. Violence writes its annals in characters of blood, and therefore catches the eye of posterity; while patience, charity, and creative labor, moving quietly through obscure places, leave records which the careless historian passes by. A tree is less startling than a battle, though the tree may nourish generations after the smoke of battle has been scattered by the wind.

It is the purpose of this sketch to recall one of those modest lights which shone far from the centers of wealth and learning, and whose pure radiance has been almost lost in the sensational glare of frontier tradition. The hero of our narrative founded no state, commanded no army, and slew no enemy. He wore no uniform except such garments as pity or chance bestowed upon him; he owned little land, carried little money, and slept more frequently beneath the branches than beneath a roof. Yet, during nearly half a century, he traversed the forests of Ohio and Indiana, planting the seeds of future abundance, consoling the afflicted, protecting the helpless, and preaching, in his strange but persuasive manner, the brotherhood of all created life.

His name was Jonathan Chapman. The people called him Johnny Appleseed.

There is good reason for believing that Chapman first entered the Ohio country in the spring of 1801, though old settlers differ concerning the exact month and route of his arrival. Some place him upon the upper waters of the Muskingum; others remember traditions of his appearance near the Ohio River, where he obtained apple seeds from the cider presses of western Pennsylvania and descended in a canoe. The discrepancy is of small consequence. We know that he was then a young man of about twenty-six years, spare in figure, hardy beyond ordinary endurance, and already possessed by that singular purpose which afterward gave unity to his wandering life.

The reader who consults a map of the old Northwest Territory will better understand both the magnitude and the beneficence of his undertaking. From the Ohio northward to Lake Erie, and from the Pennsylvania line westward toward the Wabash, extended an empire of forest, interrupted by streams, marshes, Indian villages, military posts, and here and there a pioneer clearing. Settlement did not proceed in an even line. It advanced along rivers, paused at unfavorable lands, leaped forward upon military roads, and sent lonely households far beyond the protection of compact communities. Chapman’s plan was to precede this advancing population, selecting fertile bottoms and sunny slopes, enclosing small nurseries against deer and cattle, and planting apple seeds in readiness for families who might not arrive until years afterward.

Other men followed settlement with merchandise, law, speculation, or strong drink. He went before it with seeds.

The seed itself was easily obtained. At the cider mills about Pittsburgh, and at various presses along the Monongahela and Ohio, the dark kernels accumulated in heaps and were thrown aside as refuse. Chapman gathered them into leather bags, coffee sacks, or whatever receptacles could be procured. Sometimes he descended the river in a small boat, towing his precious cargo through shallow water; sometimes he carried it upon his back over trails where the projecting roots bruised even feet hardened by years of travel. He planted not at random, as childish fancy has sometimes represented him scattering handfuls of seed upon the winds, but with a practical knowledge of soil, moisture, exposure, and the habits of browsing animals.

A suitable spot having been found, he cleared it with such rude tools as he possessed, placed the seeds in orderly beds, surrounded the ground with brush, and left the nursery under the care of the nearest settler, who was to sell or distribute the young trees and preserve, if he were able, some small account of the proceeds. After one or two seasons Chapman returned, repaired the fence, pruned the seedlings, and supplied new stock. Thus his apparently aimless wanderings were connected by an intelligent design. From nursery to nursery, through hundreds of miles of forest, he moved like the humble superintendent of an immense garden whose owners had not yet arrived.

The old settlers say that his first appearance was not readily forgotten. His clothing varied with the bounty of those among whom he traveled, but it was commonly composed of cast-off garments, selected for service rather than symmetry. A coffee sack, with openings cut for the head and arms, sometimes served as a shirt. A pair of trousers too large for him might be secured by cord or strips of bark. He was frequently barefoot, even when frost silvered the leaves and thin ice formed along the margins of the creeks. At other times he wore one shoe, having given its mate to some poorer or less hardy traveler.

Upon his head he carried, according to a tradition too firmly established to be omitted, a small tin vessel which served the double office of cap and cooking utensil. Whether he wore this strange covering continually may reasonably be doubted; but that he sometimes placed a tin pan or mush-pot upon his head is attested by several independent recollections. His dark hair, afterward streaked with gray, fell about his shoulders; his beard was thin; his eyes were keen, bright, and remarkably gentle. Children, frightened at first by the rags, soon discovered the kindness beneath them, and followed him as they might have followed some beneficent character out of Scripture.

There was nothing slothful in his poverty. He did not neglect his person because labor was disagreeable, nor wander because he lacked perseverance. No man upon the border endured more fatigue or accomplished more of his chosen work. His destitution was voluntary. Convinced that superfluous possessions burdened the spirit and deprived others of necessities, he reduced his wants to the narrowest limits. Corn meal stirred with water, a handful of berries, roasted potatoes, or bread given at a cabin door supplied his table. The hollow of a tree, a bed of leaves, or the bare puncheon floor furnished his lodging. Yet those who knew him best declare that he was not gloomy. His life, though austere, appears to have been serenely happy.

An incident related by a family living near the Licking River illustrates both his fortitude and his charity. Chapman came to their cabin during a November storm, his feet bleeding from frozen ground and sharp stones. The settler, indignant at such unnecessary suffering, gave him a stout pair of shoes and required him to put them on before supper. Johnny submitted with a grateful smile. Early the next morning he continued his journey. Before noon the settler overtook him upon the trail and found him barefoot again.

“Where are the shoes, Johnny?” he demanded.

“I met a man whose feet had not learned the forest,” was the answer. “Mine have.”

That explanation, whether given in those exact words or improved by repeated telling, contains the man.

Children were the first to recognize him and the last to forget. When he approached a cabin, carrying his seed bags and a bundle of tattered books, a shout often ran through the clearing. He could fashion whistles from willow, tell where wild honey might be found, imitate the calls of birds, and describe distant rivers to boys and girls who had never traveled ten miles from home. From his pockets came bits of bright cloth, curious stones, dried berries, and, when the season permitted, apples chosen from older orchards. But his greatest gift to childhood was attention. He listened to their small griefs with a gravity which many persons reserve for matters of property or politics.

One woman, whose recollections were taken down late in life by a schoolmaster in Richland County, remembered Chapman’s visits to her father’s cabin. “We had no books,” she said, “except the Bible, a spelling book, and what Johnny brought. He would sit by the fire after supper, with the children about his knees, and read as if the Lord stood just beyond the chimney. His voice was high at first, but when he spoke of mercy it became soft, and even the baby ceased crying.”

His religious opinions were derived from the writings of Emanuel Swedenborg, the Swedish philosopher and seer, whose doctrines found few regular expositors in the backwoods. Chapman carried portions of those writings beneath his clothing or in his seed sacks. Books were costly and scarce; he therefore separated them into chapters or signatures and left a fragment at each cabin, promising to exchange it upon his return. In this manner a single volume performed a circuit of many miles, passing through hands roughened by the ax, the plow, and the rifle.

He announced these papers with a phrase which became familiar throughout the settlements.

“Here is news right fresh from heaven,” he would say, taking from his breast a worn and weather-stained page.

His theology was not always understood. Indeed, it is doubtful whether certain hearers comprehended more than the solemnity of his manner and the charity of his conclusions. He spoke of the correspondence between visible and spiritual things; of the Divine Essence descending through degrees of creation; of the inward meaning of Scripture; and of a future life in which the affections determine the true habitation of the soul. Such doctrines, delivered beside a cabin hearth while rain beat upon a bark roof and wolves cried from the opposite ridge, must have seemed strange enough. Yet he possessed an untaught eloquence, and when he passed from speculation to mercy, humility, and justice, even the most uncultivated listener understood him.

His preaching was never reserved for the Sabbath, nor did he seek a pulpit. A stump, a log, the bank of a river, or the floor of a crowded cabin served his purpose. He did not thunder against sinners with the fierce satisfaction then common among itinerant exhorters. Rather, he pleaded with them. All life, he taught, had proceeded from God, and cruelty toward the humblest creature injured the soul of him who practiced it. The strong must protect the weak; abundance imposed an obligation; and religion without tenderness was a lamp without oil.

At a camp meeting in central Ohio, according to an oft-repeated account, a richly dressed missionary was denouncing the extravagance of frontier women. He wore polished boots, fine linen, and a brocaded vest, and demanded in a loud voice where one might now find the primitive Christians who went barefoot and took no thought for raiment. Chapman, who had been sitting quietly among the listeners, advanced in his ragged clothing, placed one bare foot upon the platform, and said:

“Here is your primitive Christian!”

The congregation laughed, and the missionary, after one glance at the extended foot and another at his own boots, found it prudent to change the subject. Johnny did not repeat the triumph. Malice formed no part of his humor, and he was perhaps sorry for the embarrassment he had caused.

His reverence for animal life approached, in some particulars, the scruples of the Brahmins. He ate little or no flesh, and objected to hunting when it was undertaken merely for sport. A rattlesnake might be driven away, but should not, if escape were possible, be killed. A wolf was dangerous because hunger made him so; a bear defended its young according to the law of its nature; even the mosquito, whose theological purpose many frontier men would have been glad to question, possessed its allotted place in creation.

One evening, while encamped upon a prairie, Chapman built a small fire to warm himself. Mosquitoes and other insects flew into the flame and were destroyed. He immediately extinguished it, though the night was cold, saying that he could not purchase his comfort at the cost of their lives. On another occasion he discovered that the hollow log in which he had intended to sleep was occupied by a bear and her cubs. He withdrew without disturbing them and spent the night in the snow. These incidents have the coloring of legend; nevertheless, they agree so perfectly with well-established actions that there is no sufficient cause to reject them.

His tenderness was not confined to wild creatures. Neglected horses, broken by brutal labor and abandoned as worthless, continually excited his compassion. He bought them for a few dollars, or obtained them by promising a future payment which the owner seldom expected to receive. He then placed them in good pastures, treated their wounds with herbs, and, when they had recovered, gave them to poor families upon the condition that they should be kindly used. More than one Indiana farmer began his modest prosperity with a horse rescued by Johnny Appleseed.

There was a practical side to this benevolence which deserves notice. Chapman knew the medicinal plants of the forest and had learned, by observation rather than schooling, something of the treatment of men and animals. He used poultices of elm, willow, and plantain; he understood the cleansing of wounds; and he could often distinguish curable exhaustion from incurable disease. His judgment was not infallible, but in remote cabins, where the nearest physician might be forty miles away, his arrival was welcomed as that of nurse, preacher, and friend.

The Indigenous inhabitants of the country treated him with a consideration seldom accorded to white wanderers. While hunters, surveyors, and traders sometimes entered their villages under suspicion, Chapman passed freely among them. His abstinence from weapons, his simplicity, and his evident reverence for the natural world appealed to qualities which frontier prejudice too often refused to acknowledge in the native character. He learned enough of several dialects to make his intentions known, shared his food without bargaining, and listened when their elders spoke.

It has been asserted that certain tribes regarded him as a man protected by the Great Spirit. This may be an embellishment; but it is generally conceded that he traveled unharmed at periods when few white men would have ventured alone beyond the settlements. He carried no rifle. A staff, a seed sack, and his religious papers were his ordinary equipment. If he feared death, he concealed the fear beneath an extraordinary confidence in Providence and in the good faith of those whom others called enemies.

During the War of 1812, when the frontier was shaken by rumor and actual violence, this confidence became of signal service to the settlers. After the surrender of Detroit, isolated families throughout northern Ohio expected attack. Cabins were abandoned; cattle strayed in unfenced clearings; women and children crowded into blockhouses; and every broken branch in the night seemed the footstep of a hostile warrior.

Chapman, moving between native villages and white settlements, learned of approaching danger and carried warnings through the forest. One summer night he reached a cabin near Mansfield and found the family asleep. He beat upon the door, crying that they must leave at once for the blockhouse. The father, uncertain whether the report was true, hesitated to abandon his livestock and ripening corn.

“The corn can grow again,” said Johnny. “The little ones cannot.”

The argument prevailed. The family dressed hastily, and Chapman guided them along a dark trail to safety. Without resting, he turned toward the next clearing. Tradition represents him traveling through the whole night, stopping at scattered cabins, crossing streams swollen by rain, and refusing food until every household in the threatened district had been warned. Whether the danger on that particular occasion was as immediate as fear supposed is less important than the courage of the messenger. No trumpet proclaimed his mission; no military order required it. Compassion sent him.

He was equally ready to defend the native people against injustice. If a frontier company planned revenge upon an entire village for the act of an unknown warrior, Chapman remonstrated with a boldness surprising in one so gentle. He distinguished guilt from race at a time when stronger and more educated men refused to do so. To him, the red child and the white child were alike recipients of life from the same eternal source. This belief, which should have been commonplace among Christians, was then sufficiently rare to appear eccentric.

When peace returned, he resumed the quieter campaigns of cultivation. His nurseries extended along the Muskingum, Mohican, Walhonding, and Black Fork; into the counties afterward known as Richland, Ashland, Knox, Wayne, and Crawford; and thence westward toward the Maumee and the valleys of Indiana. Land records preserve occasional traces of his ownership, though they cannot reveal the full extent of his labor. He sometimes purchased a small tract, established a nursery, and sold the young trees at a few cents each. More frequently he occupied unused ground by permission or custom, relying upon the future honesty of settlers.

Payment was the least important part of the transaction. If a farmer had money, Johnny accepted it. If he had no money, a little corn meal, a garment, or a promise answered as well. If he had nothing, the trees were given. Chapman reasoned that the children of a poor family required apples no less than the children of a rich one, and possibly required them more. The settler who afterward prospered might discharge the debt; if not, Providence possessed larger accounts than those kept by men.

He sometimes accumulated more money than his simple wants demanded, but surplus wealth did not remain long in his hands. A widow’s taxes, medicine for a sick child, seed corn for a family whose crop had failed, or the purchase of an abused horse soon consumed it. Thus he was continually engaged in business and continually poor. The auri sacra fames—that accursed hunger for gold which has driven men across oceans and over the rights of their brethren—found no lodging in him.

The apples grown from seed were not usually the sweet, uniform fruit known to later horticulture. Many were sour or small, though useful for cider, preserves, vinegar, and livestock. Chapman distrusted grafting, regarding it as an injury to the tree and perhaps as an improper interference with natural order. Here his tenderness misled his judgment. The grafting knife, rightly used, does not torture the plant in any meaningful sense, and the improved orchard owes much to the art he opposed.

Yet it would be unjust to measure his service solely by the flavor of individual apples. The pioneer orchard had value beyond its harvest. It marked permanent occupation. The cabin might be temporary, the cornfield exhausted, and the fence burned for fuel; but the orchard represented years of expectation. Beneath its branches children played, marriages were celebrated, neighbors gathered at harvest, and the old sat in declining life. The apple tree softened the severity of the clearing. In spring it spread a cloud of fragrant blossoms against the black stumps; in autumn it offered fruit when other stores were low; and through winter its cider or dried slices brought variety to the rough table.

Chapman planted, therefore, not only trees, but domesticity and hope. He carried into the wilderness one of the oldest symbols of human habitation. Where his nurseries flourished, the future household was already foreshadowed. The forest still stood in primal silence, yet within the guarded enclosure slept the possibility of laughter, shade, harvest, and remembrance.

His errors, like his virtues, traveled with him. He is charged with introducing dog-fennel into parts of Ohio, having scattered its seed under the belief that the plant possessed valuable medicinal properties. The weed accepted the invitation with greater enthusiasm than the settlers desired, spreading along roads and through neglected fields. Farmers who blessed Johnny for his apple trees were less devout when uprooting his fennel. He heard their complaints with humility, though he never wholly surrendered his favorable opinion of the plant.

This touch of impracticality completes rather than injures the portrait. Perfect wisdom does not wander the earth in a coffee sack. Chapman was ignorant of much that educated horticulturists understood, credulous concerning certain remedies, and obstinate in peculiar opinions. But his mistakes sprang chiefly from misplaced benevolence, not self-interest. Even the troublesome weed had been intended as medicine.

His physical endurance became legendary. He crossed icy streams with his seed bags held above his head, walked scores of miles without food, and penetrated thickets which tore his garments and skin. In summer his bare feet were hardened like leather; in winter he sometimes warmed them in the ashes of a deserted fire. He knew the stars, the direction of prevailing winds, the habits of clouds, and the voices of night birds. When trails vanished beneath snow, he followed the contours of ridges or the downward course of streams. Rarely lost, he was at home everywhere and possessed a house nowhere.

It would be a mistake, however, to picture him always solitary. The frontier was a vast neighborhood, and Johnny belonged to it more completely than men whose farms were separated by only a rail fence. He appeared at sugar camps in spring, at cabins during corn husking, at mills where settlers waited for meal, and at rivers where emigrant wagons were ferried across. News traveled with him. He knew who had married upon the Tuscarawas, whose barn had burned near the Scioto, where fever afflicted a settlement, and which western lands had recently been opened.

His arrival often brought the world to an isolated household. Yet he carried no gossip calculated to injure. If compelled to speak of misconduct, he spoke as one grieved rather than delighted. The weaknesses of others awakened in him neither surprise nor superiority. He believed the soul to be a field in which good seed might still be planted, however neglected the ground.

Several accounts describe his encounters with rough men who attempted to make sport of him. A tavern idler might snatch the tin vessel from his head or ask whether apples grew in heaven. Johnny’s reply was usually so grave, mild, and unexpected that the laughter turned against the questioner.

“Will there be orchards in paradise?” one man demanded.

“There will be whatever the Lord finds good for His children,” Chapman answered. “But no man will own the shade.”

The sentence passed from cabin to cabin, acquiring polish as it traveled; yet, authentic or not, it expresses his habitual contempt for selfish possession.

Women of the settlements regarded him with mingled affection and anxiety. They mended his garments, provided him with stockings, and urged him to eat more substantial food. He thanked them and obeyed for a time, but the new coat was soon given to a shivering stranger, the stockings disappeared upon the feet of some child, and the carefully prepared meat remained untouched. Mothers trusted him because children were safe in his company. No suspicion of impurity ever attached to his name. His affection was diffused over all humanity and fixed possessively upon none.

There were rumors that in youth he had loved a woman who married another, and that disappointment drove him into the wilderness. Romantic tradition frequently invents a broken heart to explain any life which refuses ordinary ambition. No trustworthy evidence supports the tale. Chapman himself appears to have believed that marriage belonged to a later spiritual state, or that Providence would provide a companion in heaven. Whatever the origin of his celibacy, he bore it without bitterness and never spoke lightly of domestic happiness in others.

As the years passed, the wilderness receded. Trees fell by thousands; roads widened; frame houses replaced log cabins; orchards matured where Chapman had once guarded seed beds with brush. Towns arose around mills and crossroads. The people who had welcomed him as a strange young traveler now received him as an old friend. Children who had sat at his knees brought their own children to hear him. His hair grayed, his shoulders bent slightly beneath the sacks, but the clear eyes and rapid stride remained.

Civilization brought comforts which he had helped prepare, yet it also brought conditions uncongenial to his spirit. Land became valuable. A nursery once left under a neighbor’s care was enclosed by a purchaser who questioned the planter’s title. Lawyers demanded papers; surveyors corrected old boundaries; merchants sold improved trees; and men began to laugh more harshly at a barefoot preacher carrying Swedenborgian pages. Chapman did not quarrel with progress, but when a district became settled he felt that his work lay farther west.

His farewells were simple and moving. He visited the familiar cabins, now enlarged or weatherboarded, exchanged religious tracts, inspected old orchards, and asked forgiveness for any injury he might have done. Families pressed money upon him. He accepted enough to purchase seed or discharge an obligation, then distributed the remainder before he had traveled many miles. Women wept; men who considered themselves too stern for tears followed him to the edge of the clearing; children walked beside him until called home.

One evening, upon leaving an Ohio settlement where he had labored for many years, Chapman stood beneath an apple tree then broad enough to shade the cabin roof. The woman of the house reminded him that he had planted it when she was a girl.

“No,” he replied, touching the bark. “The Lord planted it. I only put the seed where it could hear Him.”

Then he turned westward.

Beyond the settled portions of Ohio lay Indiana, still clothed in immense woods and watered by the St. Joseph, the Maumee, the Eel, the Tippecanoe, and the upper branches of the Wabash. There, during his later years, Chapman established new nurseries and renewed the ancient pattern of his life. He followed native paths, military roads, and river margins; visited Fort Wayne and scattered communities around it; slept beneath sycamores of enormous girth; and went forward always a little in advance of the ax.

The westering sun is an image too obvious for the close of an aged wanderer’s life, yet in Chapman’s case nature herself seems to have arranged the symbol. He had moved for nearly half a century in the direction of sunset. Behind him spread orchards, farms, villages, and graves; before him, until almost the last, stood the forest. His road was the road of the nation, but his conquest was without violence. He subdued no people. He demanded no surrender. He entered the wilderness bearing life in a sack.

By 1847 he had reached the age of seventy-two. Exposure, hunger, and ceaseless travel had not wholly broken his constitution, though those who saw him observed that his step was less certain and his hair almost white. In early spring he came to a settler’s house in northern Indiana. The day had been mild, but toward evening a cold rain swept through the trees. Johnny asked for shelter, a request rarely made and never refused.

The family received him gladly. He ate a little bread and milk, dried his garments before the fire, and inquired about their children, their neighbors, and the young orchard near the cabin. After supper he drew from beneath his coat several worn pages and announced, with the old brightness in his eyes:

“Here is news right fresh from heaven.”

He read of humility, charity, and the eternal uses of created things. The storm passed while he was speaking. Rain ceased upon the roof; the clouds opened in the west; and a pale beam of sunset entered through the small window, resting upon the old man’s face. Our informant, who received the account from one of the children then present, declares that the family afterward remembered not so much the words as the expression with which they were uttered—a look of repose, as though the speaker already perceived the country toward which he had so long been traveling.

Before lying down, he prayed. He asked protection for the household, mercy for the suffering, wisdom for rulers, peace between peoples, and gentleness toward every creature that breathed beneath the Divine care. There was no petition for himself.

He slept upon a blanket near the hearth.

In the morning they found him still, his hands folded upon his breast and his face undisturbed. Death had entered without violence, like a quiet messenger unwilling to awaken the house. The wanderer who had slept beneath storms, in snow, beside dangerous animals, and within hearing of hostile camps, had at last found a shelter from which no charity could call him forth.

They buried him near the scene of his final labor. No marble monument was required. The region itself had become his memorial. Along rivers and roads, upon old homesteads and deserted clearings, trees of his planting remained. Some were already ancient; others had sprung from orchards descended from his nurseries. Their branches leaned above cabins, shaded graves, and dropped fruit into the grass where grandchildren played.

History may question the precise location of every nursery, the literal truth of every anecdote, or the exact distance traversed upon some night of danger. Such inquiries are proper. Affection must not be permitted to falsify the record. Yet when all doubtful ornament has been removed, the essential figure remains: a poor man devoting his strength to benefits he would seldom live to enjoy; a religious enthusiast whose faith produced kindness rather than persecution; a traveler accepted by peoples divided through fear; a protector of children, animals, strangers, and enemies; a planter in an age too much celebrated for destruction.

There are heroes whose names are associated with the taking of cities, the overthrow of nations, and the multiplication of graves. Their monuments rise in public squares, and the dates of their victories are taught to every child. Jonathan Chapman’s campaigns were marked by rows of seedlings; his victories ripened slowly; his trophies were gathered in baskets; and the only blood habitually shed in his service was his own, from feet wounded upon the frozen trail.

Which achievement most benefits mankind—the field made desolate in an hour, or the orchard brought to fruit through years of patient care? The answer need not diminish legitimate martial courage. It merely restores to the roll of honor another and gentler fortitude.

Johnny Appleseed had no expectation of earthly fame. Had sculptors proposed to raise his image, he would probably have asked that the money be spent for shoes, seed, or bread. Had historians praised him in his presence, he would have spoken of Providence, or turned aside to rescue some beetle from their path. His humility was not the trained modesty of one secretly conscious of applause. He cared little for the opinion of the world because his attention was fixed upon duties too immediate to permit vanity.

His life bore fruit according to its kind. The seeds he planted became trees; the trees gave food and shade; his acts of mercy became traditions; and those traditions, repeated beside winter fires, softened the temper of a frontier too often represented as merely fierce. The child who heard that Johnny spared a snake might hesitate before tormenting a harmless animal. The farmer who remembered his generosity might grant another season to a debtor. The traveler who saw one bare foot in the snow might share his second coat.

Thus goodness propagates itself by methods as quiet as the growth of roots. The smallest deed descends into hidden soil, and years afterward some unrecorded branch bends beneath its fruit.

When spring comes to the valleys through which Chapman passed, the apple trees lift their blossoms like pale clouds against the tender leaves. Their fragrance moves over fields where the great forest once stood, crosses roads unknown to the first settlers, and enters houses bright with comforts they could scarcely have imagined. Few who breathe that perfume remember the ragged sower. Yet remembrance is not the measure of influence. Rivers do not preserve the names of all the springs that fill them; history, like the greater current, swallows a thousand modest tributaries while bearing their strength toward the sea.

Let us, then, rescue this one name from forgetfulness—not because the man was flawless, nor because every fireside legend must be accepted as fact, but because his governing purpose was noble and his endurance extraordinary. Beneath the meanest apparel there may dwell a royal charity. Beneath an eccentric doctrine there may burn a truth neglected by the orthodox. In a barefoot wanderer, mocked by the worldly and pitied by the prosperous, there may exist that rare heroism which creates, protects, and asks no reward.

From the insect at his dying fire, upward through the wounded horse, the frightened child, the impoverished settler, and the threatened village, Chapman’s compassion formed one unbroken chain, whose highest link, in his belief, was fastened to the throne of God. Whether posterity accepts all his visions is immaterial. It cannot wisely reject the mercy they produced.

The warrior’s drum is silent; the frontier rifle rusts above the chimney; the blockhouse has fallen; and many proud names of that stirring age are now no more than letters upon a weathered stone. But each returning spring renews the emblem of the old planter’s life. The dark bough flowers. The flower yields fruit. The fallen fruit gives back its seed to the earth.

And so, while an apple tree blooms beside a western cabin, the gentle mission of Johnny Appleseed is not ended.

# The Young Hunters of Hemlock Mountain

“Billy, can you keep a secret?”

Billy Harper looked cautiously toward the kitchen door before answering. This was characteristic of Billy, who never accepted a secret until he had made certain that no grown person was likely to hear it.

“I guess so,” he said. “What is it?”

Tommy Reed drew a small, much-worn book from beneath his jacket. On its red cover a gentleman in buckskins was represented aiming a rifle at a bear, while another gentleman, apparently wounded in seven places, waved a knife from the top of a rock.

“We’re going hunting,” said Tommy.

“Who is?”

“You and me.”

“When?”

“Now.”

Billy opened his round blue eyes.

“Does your mother know?”

“Of course she doesn’t. Hunters don’t ask their mothers every time they start for the wilderness.”

This statement seemed reasonable as applied to the heroes of Tommy’s book, but less so when applied to two boys standing behind the corncrib of Mr. Reed’s mountain farm. Tommy was eleven, Billy not quite twelve; and although each considered himself old enough to face wolves, Indians, bears, robbers, and any other danger likely to be met in a properly arranged adventure, neither had ever slept a night away from home.

Billy scratched one bare ankle with the toe of the other foot.

“What’ll we eat?” he asked.

This, too, was characteristic. Tommy thought first of glory; Billy thought first of dinner.

“Prog,” replied Tommy grandly.

“What sort of prog?”

“Meat, and fish, and berries, and things. There’s plenty in the woods.”

“But we haven’t got any meat yet.”

“We’ll shoot it.”

“With what?”

Tommy produced a bow, made from a bent hickory branch, and three arrows of unequal length. One was tipped with a rusty nail, one with tin cut from an old spice box, and one had no point at all.

Billy examined the weapons without enthusiasm.

“You couldn’t shoot a cow with those.”

“We don’t want a cow. We want deer. A deer’s easier because it’s wild.”

This explanation did not wholly satisfy Billy, yet adventure was tugging at him. The woods climbed green and cool behind the farm; beyond them rose Hemlock Mountain, blue in the August haze. Somewhere among those trees brooks ran under mossy stones, trout hid in brown pools, and bears—according to old Mr. Mullin—left tracks as large as dinner plates. It was difficult to remain beside a corncrib when such a country waited.

“What do we take?” Billy asked.

Tommy had considered this matter. From his pockets he brought forth a piece of fish-line, four hooks, a box containing seven matches, a jackknife with one blade, a brass button, two yards of twine, a candle end, six buckshot, and a little bag of salt. Billy contributed a dull knife, a bent spoon, three matches, a red cotton handkerchief, and a whistle.

“We need blankets,” said Billy.

“Real hunters sleep on boughs.”

“What if it rains?”

“They sleep under rocks.”

“What if there aren’t any rocks?”

“There always are.”

“What about lunch?”

Tommy pointed toward the back porch, where three covered baskets stood waiting. Mrs. Reed and Mrs. Harper, who was spending the day helping with preserves, intended that two of these should go to the men mowing the upper field. Tommy had heard his mother say so.

“We’ll take the little basket,” he whispered. “It’s full of bread and chicken and apple pie.”

Billy’s fears vanished at once.

“Jolly!” he said.

Five minutes later the young hunters slipped through the orchard, crossed the pasture below the barn, crawled under the rail fence, and entered the woods. Tommy carried the basket and bow. Billy carried an old tin pail, which he insisted might be useful for berries, water, fish, or anything else requiring a pail. Both boys had their jackets tied about their waists, though the afternoon was warm.

The forest received them pleasantly. Sunshine fell in golden patches between the leaves. A brook babbled somewhere below, and a squirrel, disturbed by their passage, ran up a chestnut tree and scolded them from a safe height. Ferns brushed their knees; blackberries shone beside the path; and now and then a blue mountain rose between the trunks, promising still greater wildness beyond.

“Don’t make so much noise,” Tommy warned. “You’ll scare the game.”

“I ain’t making noise.”

“You stepped on a stick.”

“So did you.”

“I meant to. Hunters sometimes make a noise to fool the animals.”

Billy looked doubtful but said no more.

For an hour they marched briskly uphill, though the basket grew heavier and the celebrated wilderness proved to contain a surprising number of briars. Tommy led them away from the wood road because no hero in any of his books had ever followed an ordinary road when he could plunge through trackless forest. Consequently they climbed over fallen trunks, scrambled across stony gullies, tore their trousers on bushes, and once returned to the same white birch after traveling in what Tommy declared had been a straight line.

At last Billy sat down upon a log.

“I’m hungry.”

“It isn’t noon.”

“It must be. It feels two o’clock inside me.”

Tommy, who was also hungry, consented to halt. They chose a pleasant place beside the brook, where a flat rock served as a table. Billy knelt close as Tommy opened the basket.

Then both stared.

There was no chicken, no bread, and no apple pie. The basket contained three balls of blue yarn, a half-knitted stocking, two papers of darning needles, a roll of old linen, and a pair of Mr. Reed’s gray socks.

For several seconds the brook made the only sound.

“You took the wrong basket,” said Billy.

“You said this was the little one.”

“I didn’t.”

“You looked at it.”

“I didn’t say it had chicken.”

“You said ‘Jolly!’”

“I meant it was jolly if it did.”

Tommy turned over the yarn, as if a sandwich might be concealed beneath it. None was.

“Well,” he said at length, “a true hunter can live on the country.”

“Can he eat socks?”

“We’ve got berries.”

Billy looked at the blackberry thicket. It had appeared abundant before lunch, but it now seemed a poor substitute for chicken and pie. Nevertheless, the little fellows picked until the tin pail was half full, pricking their fingers and staining their mouths purple. They ate every berry, then searched for more. By the time they finished, their hunger was not gone, but it had been persuaded to wait.

“We’ll catch trout,” said Tommy. “Then we’ll have a tip-top supper.”

The brook was narrow, but below a ledge it spread into a dark pool overhung by alders. Trout could be seen darting along the bottom like brown shadows. The boys cut two poles, tied on their lines, and hunted beneath stones for worms. Billy objected to touching them, so Tommy did that part while Billy held the pail.

Fishing occupied most of the afternoon. Tommy’s hook caught first in an alder, then in his own sleeve, and afterward in Billy’s hat. Billy lost his bait four times and declared the trout were stealing it on purpose. Yet patience, or hunger, finally brought its reward. A spotted fish flashed in the sunlight at the end of Tommy’s line, and another, smaller but equally precious, was captured by Billy.

They shouted so loudly that any deer within a mile must have taken warning.

The next problem was fire. Of their ten matches, two broke, three went out, and one burned Tommy’s finger. The seventh lighted a nest of dry birch bark, and soon a little flame ran among the twigs. Tommy cleaned the trout with his knife, though not as neatly as a cook might have done, and laid them upon green sticks across the fire.

One side burned black while the other remained almost raw. Ash blew over them; one fish fell into the coals; and Billy, attempting to rescue it with the spoon, dropped the spoon also.

“They smell good,” he said hopefully.

They did smell good, for hunger is the best seasoning in the world. The young hunters ate with fingers and knives, scraping away ashes, bones, scales, and such portions as seemed too lively to swallow. There was not much left when they had finished, though neither fish had been larger than a boy’s hand.

Billy leaned back against a tree.

“I wish we had the pie.”

“You always think of eating.”

“I don’t always. Sometimes I think of sleeping. Where are we going to sleep?”

“We’ll make camp farther up.”

“Why farther up?”

“Because hunters never camp the first place they stop.”

There was no sound reason for this rule, but Tommy had delivered it with authority. They put the yarn and stockings back into the basket, stamped out the fire, and continued into the forest.

The sun was low when they found what Tommy called a camp. It was a hollow beneath several pines, carpeted with brown needles and protected on one side by a shelf of rock. The brook ran near enough to furnish water. The pines sang softly overhead, and a woodpecker tapped somewhere in the quiet woods.

Billy looked about.

“Where are the beds?”

“We make ’em.”

The making of beds required more labor than either expected. Pine boughs do not arrange themselves, however frequently they may do so in books. The boys hacked at branches with their dull knives, dragged them to the hollow, and laid them in two heaps. Tommy then leaned sticks against the rock for a shelter. The sticks fell twice; the third construction remained upright because Billy held it.

When darkness began gathering between the trunks, the camp looked poor enough, but it was their own. This cheered them. They lighted the candle end, ate a few late berries, and lay down with their jackets over their shoulders.

For ten minutes Tommy talked of the adventures they would have in the morning.

They would climb Hemlock Mountain, discover a cave, shoot a deer, smoke the meat, and perhaps meet a band of robbers. Billy asked how many robbers there might be.

“Six,” said Tommy.

“What’ll we do if they all have guns?”

“Capture ’em.”

“How?”

“Wait till they’re asleep.”

“What if they don’t all sleep together?”

Tommy did not answer. His pine bed had proved full of knobs, and a root pressed into the middle of his back.

The boys shifted, sighed, turned over, shifted again, and discovered that August nights in the mountains are colder than August days. The cheerful forest had changed with the light. Every tree had become taller, every shadow deeper, and every small noise important. A twig snapped beyond the brook. Something rustled among the leaves. Far off, an owl called.

“What was that?” Billy whispered.

“A rabbit.”

“How do you know?”

“I heard lots of rabbits.”

The owl called again.

“Was that a rabbit too?”

“No. That was—another hunter.”

“It sounded like ‘Who.’”

“He was calling his dog.”

Billy pulled his jacket over his head. Presently the cold drove him closer to Tommy, and Tommy, though he complained that Billy’s knee was sharp, did not move away. Thus the first night of their glorious expedition passed in short dozes, long wakings, and many brave explanations of noises neither understood.

Morning sunshine restored the woods to friendliness. The brook sparkled, birds sang, and the night’s terrors appeared foolish. The boys washed their faces, drank from the stream, and set out in search of breakfast.

They found raspberries, two sour apples under a wild tree, and a patch of wintergreen. Billy ate the wintergreen leaves because Tommy said they were good for hunger. They were not, but they freshened his breath.

Afterward the boys made a trap.

Tommy had read of deadfalls and snares, and his knowledge, though confused, was impressive. They found a hole at the foot of a rocky bank, set a box-shaped arrangement of sticks before it, and baited the contrivance with half an apple. A flat stone was balanced above, ready to fall when the bait was touched.

“What lives there?” Billy asked.

“Foxes, maybe.”

“The hole isn’t big enough for a fox.”

“Then a mink.”

“What if it’s a skunk?”

Tommy paused.

“We’ll come up on the windward side.”

“Which side’s that?”

“We can tell when we get there.”

Having entrusted the trap with the important duty of providing dinner, they returned to the brook and caught one more trout. It was even smaller than the fish of the evening before and more thoroughly burned. Billy ate the larger share because, as Tommy said, he complained the loudest.

Near noon, while they were following a ridge, they heard a crack among the brush. Tommy seized his bow.

“Deer!” he whispered.

Billy stepped behind him.

The brush parted, and a brown animal appeared. It was not a deer, but a heavy-bodied creature with small ears and a blunt nose. It sat upright and looked at the boys with grave surprise.

“Shoot!” Billy breathed.

Tommy fitted the nail-pointed arrow and drew the string. Before he could let fly, the creature dropped to all fours and vanished under a log.

“What was it?” asked Billy.

“A young bear.”

“It didn’t have a bear face.”

“Then it was a beaver.”

“There ain’t any water there.”

“A woodchuck, maybe.”

The last guess happened to be right, though neither boy knew it. They rushed forward, shouting, and the frightened animal, finding its hole blocked by a stone, dodged beneath another log. Tommy thrust in a stick. Billy stood ready with the basket. After much scrambling, poking, yelling, and leaping backward, they succeeded in driving the creature into a hollow stump, where it was for the moment a prisoner.

“We’ve trapped him,” cried Tommy.

“What do we do with him?”

Tommy had not thought so far. The woodchuck scratched furiously inside the stump.

“We could cook him.”

“Who’ll clean him?”

“You.”

“I cleaned the pail.”

“That ain’t the same.”

“No, and I won’t.”

The debate might have continued if a dry voice behind them had not said, “Seems to me the first thing is to find out what you’ve caught.”

The boys whirled.

A tall man stood on the ridge. He wore a faded felt hat and carried a real rifle over his arm. At his heels sat a black-and-tan hound with solemn eyes.

Tommy lowered his bow. Here at last was a genuine hunter, yet instead of feeling like a brother of the wilderness, he became suddenly aware of his torn trousers, dirty face, and three absurd arrows.

The man looked at the hollow stump.

“Woodchuck,” he said. “Old one, too. What are you calculating to do with him?”

“Eat him,” said Tommy, though with less confidence than before.

“Got a kettle?”

“No.”

“Got grease?”

“No.”

“Ever dress a woodchuck?”

Tommy was silent.

The hunter’s mouth twitched.

“Then I expect the woodchuck’s got the better chance of eating dinner.”

He lifted the stone from the burrow entrance and stepped aside. After a moment the animal shot out of the stump, crossed the open ground, and disappeared into its hole.

Billy watched it go with relief.

The hunter seated himself on a log.

“Where are you fellows from?”

“Reed’s farm,” Tommy answered.

“That’s eight miles by the ridge road.”

“We came through the woods.”

“So I judged. Your folks know you’re here?”

“We’re on a hunting expedition,” said Tommy.

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Neither boy replied. The dog came forward to sniff the basket. Billy wished the basket held food instead of stockings.

The hunter unstrapped a leather bag from his shoulder and opened it. Within were bread, cold venison, cheese, and two thick molasses cakes. Never had provisions appeared so splendid. Even Tommy’s pride could not resist them.

“Maybe,” said the man, “two hunters might help me lighten this load.”

Billy sat down before the sentence was finished.

The meal was conducted with little conversation, for hungry boys attend seriously to business. Billy ate until his cheeks rounded and his eyes brightened. Tommy tried to take smaller bites in the presence of the hunter, but forgot his dignity when he reached the second cake. The dog received the scraps and afterward rested his chin upon Billy’s knee as if the two had been friends for years.

“My name’s Amos Grey,” said the hunter. “What are yours?”

They told him.

“I’ve seen you at Reed’s farm. Your mothers’ll be worried.”

“We can take care of ourselves,” Tommy said.

“So I see. You’ve got one tin pail, three arrows, a stocking basket, and enough dirt on you to plant beans.”

Billy laughed before he remembered that the joke concerned them.

Mr. Grey’s eyes were kind, although his voice remained dry.

“There’s a bear been ranging this side of Hemlock,” he continued. “Not likely to bother a man, but a hungry bear and a couple of boys might misunderstand each other. Better go home.”

“We’re going to climb the mountain first,” said Tommy.

“Mountain’ll keep.”

“We ain’t afraid.”

“That’s useful when a fellow knows what to do. When he doesn’t, a little fear sometimes saves him trouble.”

Tommy colored. He admired Mr. Grey and disliked being advised by him, two feelings which boys and older people can carry together very comfortably.

The hunter rose and whistled to his dog.

“Follow this ridge east till you strike the logging road. Turn right and keep downhill. It’ll bring you within half a mile of Reed’s place. Don’t leave it.”

“We won’t,” Billy promised.

Tommy said nothing.

Mr. Grey studied him for a moment, then nodded and walked away. The hound followed, looking back once before the trees hid them both.

“We ought to go home,” Billy said.

“We will—after we see the trap.”

“What trap?”

“Our deadfall.”

“It didn’t catch anything.”

“How do you know?”

“I guess animals know better.”

Tommy took up the basket.

“A hunter always visits his traps.”

Thus a sensible promise to follow the logging road was put aside for the sake of a flat stone, half an apple, and a hole whose location neither boy clearly remembered.

Before setting out, however, Tommy wished to try his bow. He had been somewhat humbled by the appearance of Mr. Grey’s rifle, and he wanted to prove that his own weapon was not merely an ornament.

A thrush flew from a low branch and alighted on a gray boulder. It was a harmless little bird, brown-backed and speckled at the breast, and it tilted its head as if listening to music beneath the earth.

“Watch me,” Tommy whispered.

He fitted the tin-pointed arrow. Billy caught his arm.

“Don’t. It ain’t good to eat.”

“I’m not going to hit it.”

The string twanged.

The arrow struck the bird.

For an instant the thrush fluttered upon the stone, then fell among the ferns. The boys ran forward. Tommy had expected the bird to fly away; in his imagination, shooting had always been followed by triumph, pursuit, or supper. He had never considered the small warm body, the trembling wings, or the bright eye fading under his gaze.

Billy knelt beside it.

“You killed it.”

Tommy said nothing.

“It wasn’t doing anything,” Billy added softly.

“I know.”

The forest seemed strangely still. A moment before Tommy had felt like the boldest marksman on the mountain. Now the bow in his hand was a foolish, cruel toy, and he would gladly have broken every arrow if that could have made the thrush rise again.

People often discover the wrong in an action after it can no longer be undone. That is one reason a good heart must be taught to think before the hand acts. Tommy had not meant to be wicked; he had only wished to imitate the hunters of his books. But make-believe cruelty had become real pain, and the lesson hurt.

He picked up the bird carefully.

“What’ll you do?” Billy asked.

“Bury it.”

With his knife Tommy dug a little grave beneath a fern. He lined it with leaves, laid the thrush inside, and covered it with earth and two flat stones. Billy helped without speaking.

“Don’t tell anybody,” Tommy said when they stood.

“I won’t.”

Tommy pushed the bow through the basket handle and did not touch it again that day.

They started toward their trap. At first both believed they knew the way. It lay, Tommy said, beyond a split hemlock, below a ledge, near three white birches. Unfortunately the mountain possessed a great many hemlocks, ledges, and white birches. The boys found one split tree, but no hole; then a hole without a ledge; then three birches beside a brook they had never seen.

“We came from that way,” said Billy.

“No, we didn’t.”

“I remember that crooked pine.”

“All pines look crooked if you stand wrong.”

They climbed a slope, crossed a ravine, and descended on the other side. The sun slipped lower. Every turn which ought to have brought them to the trap led instead to more trees, more ferns, more rocks, and more winding brooks.

“Let’s find the logging road,” Billy urged.

“That’s what I’m doing.”

“You said the trap was this way.”

“The road’s beyond it.”

“You don’t know.”

“I do too.”

But Tommy did not. He hurried because he was afraid, and the faster he hurried, the less carefully he looked. Billy followed because he was more afraid of being alone than of being lost.

The afternoon faded. Shafts of light withdrew from the forest floor and climbed the trunks before disappearing. The birds quieted. Cool air settled in the hollows, and once more the pleasant woods began to change beneath the boys’ anxious eyes.

“We’re lost,” Billy said at last.

“We’re only turned around.”

“That’s the same thing.”

“No, it isn’t. Lost means you don’t know where you are.”

“Do you?”

Tommy did not answer.

They shouted. The mountain returned faint echoes, but no human voice. They shouted again until their throats hurt. A crow flew over the treetops, croaking as if it knew how foolish they had been.

“We’ll build a fire,” said Tommy.

Their match box contained three matches. The first went out in damp leaves. The second broke. With the third they lighted a scrap of linen from the basket, then fed the flame with birch bark and twigs until a cheerful blaze sprang up.

For a while the fire encouraged them. They ate the last handful of berries, drank from a nearby spring, and gathered wood. Yet Mr. Grey’s warning returned to Billy.

“What if the bear smells the fire?”

“Bears are afraid of fire.”

“What if this one isn’t?”

“All bears are.”

“Mr. Mullin said it chased his calf.”

“A calf can’t build a fire.”

Billy considered this distinction, but did not find it comforting.

As darkness thickened, the boys heard something moving above them on the slope. Leaves crackled. A branch snapped. Then came a heavy sniffing sound.

Billy seized Tommy’s arm.

“That’s him.”

“It’s probably a deer.”

“Mr. Grey said there was a bear.”

The unseen creature moved again.

Tommy’s courage, though genuine, was not proof against an animal he could not see. Beside the fire stood a tall maple with low branches.

“Climb,” he ordered.

Billy needed no urging. He caught the lowest limb, scrambled, slipped, kicked Tommy in the shoulder, scrambled again, and pulled himself into the tree. Tommy followed with the basket tied by its handle to his belt.

They climbed twenty feet before choosing two branches that forked close together. Below, the fire burned like a red eye. Beyond it all was blackness.

The creature on the slope came no nearer. It may have been a porcupine, a deer, or even a raccoon; but to the boys it remained a bear, and an imaginary bear in the dark is sometimes more alarming than a real bear by daylight.

Tommy wedged himself against the trunk.

“Don’t go to sleep,” he said.

“I won’t.”

“If one keeps watch, the other can rest.”

“You keep watch first.”

“All right.”

For five minutes Billy asked whether Tommy was awake. For five minutes more he explained that his branch was narrow. Then his voice became drowsy, his head sank against the trunk, and cautious Billy fell asleep.

Tommy watched the fire die. The moon rose behind the pines and looked through the leaves upon the two little hunters roosting like owls. Tommy thought of his bed at home, with the patchwork quilt and the window near the apple tree. He imagined his mother calling him to supper. He remembered the thrush beneath the fern. These thoughts were not heroic, but they were honest.

Presently his eyes closed.

Some time later a scream awakened him.

“Tommy! Tommy! I’m falling!”

Tommy gripped his branch and stared downward. Billy’s place was empty.

“Where are you?”

“Here! Oh, pull me up!”

A pale shape swung below the branch. Billy had rolled in his sleep. His belt, catching on a broken limb, had held him, and now he dangled several feet beneath Tommy like a very frightened apple. His toes found no support; his hands clawed uselessly at the trunk.

“Hold still,” Tommy ordered.

“I can’t! I’m coming loose!”

Tommy crept along the branch, but it bent under him. Billy hung too low to reach, and when Tommy tried to descend, bark crumbled beneath his bare foot.

“Can you climb your belt?”

“No.”

“Catch the branch.”

“I can’t.”

Tommy tore the twine from the basket and tied it to the fish-line. The cord reached Billy, but it was too weak to pull him. Billy caught it nevertheless and held on as if it were a ship’s rope.

“I’ll go for help,” Tommy said.

“Don’t leave me!”

“I have to. You’re stuck fast. The belt’s strong.”

“It feels like it’s breaking me in half.”

“I’ll come back quick.”

“Suppose the bear comes?”

Tommy looked down at the dark ground.

“He can’t climb with you hanging there.”

This was intended as comfort, but Billy began to cry.

Tommy descended, scraping his knees and tearing one sleeve. On the ground he looked up. In the moonlight Billy’s round face appeared between leaves.

“Keep shouting,” Tommy called. “I’ll find somebody.”

Then he ran.

It is hard to find a lost person in mountain woods, and nearly as hard for a lost person to find anyone else. Moonlight silvered the rocks but made the shadows blacker. Tommy dashed downhill because farms usually lay in valleys. He stumbled over roots, splashed through a brook, fought through laurel, and scrambled across fallen trunks. Behind him Billy’s calls grew faint and vanished.

Tommy called too. No answer came.

He climbed a ridge, expecting lights beyond it, and found only another dark valley. He descended that valley, crossed a marshy place, and lost one shoe in the mud. After recovering it, he pressed on, limping, shivering, and trying not to imagine the bear following.

A patch of moonlight opened ahead. Tommy hurried toward it. The ground seemed level, covered with leaves. He took three running steps, and on the fourth there was nothing under him.

He dropped through rotten branches, struck a sloping wall of earth, rolled over twice, and landed in a heap of dry leaves.

For a while he lay still. Then he moved his arms, his legs, and his head. Nothing was broken. He sat up.

He was in a deep, round hole.

Years earlier, hunters had dug the pit for bears, covering it with poles and leaves. No bear had fallen into it, and the trap had long been abandoned. Rain had washed the sides smooth; weeds grew around the rim; and the old covering had weakened until one small boy was sufficient to bring it down.

Tommy tried to climb. The earth crumbled under his fingers. He sprang for a root, missed it, slid backward, and filled his shoe with dirt. He tried the other side with the same result.

“Help!” he shouted.

The pit returned a muffled little echo.

Tommy shouted until he was hoarse. Then anger seized him. He kicked the wall, tore at roots, flung leaves, and raged like a little bear caught in the very trap intended for one. But anger cannot make a pit shallow. At last exhaustion quieted him.

The leaves at the bottom were dry and deep. The pit kept off the wind. Tommy curled himself against the earthen wall and put his hands under his cheek. He intended to rest only a moment.

Within minutes he was asleep.

At Reed’s farm, meanwhile, nobody was sleeping.

The boys had not been missed at noon. Mrs. Reed supposed they were helping in the upper field, while Mrs. Harper believed they had gone to the brook. It was not until the men returned without their lunch that the baskets were examined and the truth began to appear.

“The small basket’s gone,” said Mrs. Reed.

“So’s Tommy’s bow,” said Billy’s mother.

A search of the barn, hayloft, orchard, pasture, and creek bank brought no boys. Near the rail fence Mr. Reed found two sets of tracks entering the woods.

By sunset three farmhands and both fathers were searching the lower slopes. Neighbors came with lanterns. Mrs. Harper walked from room to room, putting on her shawl, taking it off, and listening whenever a dog barked. Mrs. Reed blamed herself for not watching more carefully, then blamed Tommy’s adventure books, then blamed nobody and began preparing food for the searchers.

Old Mr. Mullin arrived on a gray horse, carrying a lantern in one hand and a long gun in the other.

“Wal,” he said, “boys is boys, and woods is woods. Put the two together, ’specially in berry time, and somebody’s got to go fetchin’.”

“Have you seen them?” Mrs. Harper asked.

“No, ma’am, but I’ve seen bear sign up Hemlock way. Big bear, too.”

This information did not improve anyone’s spirits.

“Don’t speak of bears,” said Mrs. Reed sharply.

“Wal, I ain’t sayin’ the bear’s seen the boys. Likely he’s mindin’ his business and they’re enjoyin’ theirs. Still, I’ll load both barrels.”

The first search party returned near midnight without news. Lanterns moved through the orchard like tired fireflies. Men called from ridge to ridge. The mothers waited at the kitchen table, where the untouched food grew cold.

Then a dog barked in the yard.

Amos Grey entered with his hound at his heels.

“I met the boys near Cedar Ledge around noon,” he said. “Fed them. Told them to take the logging road home.”

“They never came,” Mr. Reed answered.

Mr. Grey’s expression changed.

“Which way were they headed?”

“They were talking of a trap,” Mrs. Harper said. “Billy makes traps behind our henhouse. Oh, Mr. Grey, can your dog find them?”

“Maybe. They handled my food bag, and the dog took a liking to the round one.”

“Billy,” whispered Mrs. Harper.

Mr. Grey asked for something the boys had worn. Tommy’s cap and one of Billy’s old shirts were brought. The hound sniffed them, circled the yard, then trotted toward the woods with his nose low.

A new party set out: Mr. Grey, the boys’ fathers, Mr. Mullin, two farmhands, and several neighbors. The mothers remained behind because the ground was steep and someone must be there if the boys returned on their own. It was the hardest duty of all.

The hound followed the trail slowly at first. In the pasture he lost it among cattle tracks, found it at the fence, and moved faster beneath the trees. The men carried lanterns, ropes, blankets, food, and guns. They climbed to the brook, discovered ashes from the first cooking fire, and beside it found Billy’s bent spoon.

“They were here,” said Mr. Harper, holding it.

“Wal, that spoon don’t look overfed,” observed Mr. Mullin. “Likely neither was Billy.”

No one laughed much, but the familiar thought of Billy’s appetite brought a little comfort.

Near dawn they found the pine camp, then the remains of another fire. The hound circled the place and whined. From there the trail divided. Mr. Grey studied the ground.

“They climbed that maple.”

He raised his lantern. High among the branches hung something pale.

“Billy!” Mr. Harper shouted.

A weak voice answered.

“Is that you, Pa?”

Within moments the tree was surrounded. Billy had ceased swinging and now hung limply by his belt, one foot caught in the fish-line. His face was wet with tears, and he was too tired even to be ashamed of them.

Mr. Grey climbed with a rope. He fastened it beneath Billy’s arms, cut the belt, and lowered him gently. Mr. Harper caught his son and held him so tightly that Billy complained he could not breathe.

“Where’s Tommy?” everyone asked.

“He went for help,” Billy said. “A long time ago. Maybe the bear got him.”

“What bear?”

“The one below us.”

Mr. Grey examined the leaves near the dead fire.

“No bear tracks.”

“There was something.”

“A deer, likely.”

Billy’s relief was mixed with indignation. To suffer half a night in a tree on account of a deer seemed unfair.

They wrapped him in a blanket and fed him bread and warm milk from a bottle. After three bites he remembered his friend.

“You’ve got to find Tommy. He saved me. He tried to pull me up, and then he went all alone.”

“We’ll find him,” said his father.

One farmhand took Billy home while the others followed Tommy’s trail. This was more difficult, for he had run wildly. The hound led them through the brook, up one ridge, down another, across mud where a small shoe had plainly been dragged free, and into a thicket of laurel. The sky lightened from black to gray. Birds began calling, and the pines separated against a pale eastern glow.

Then the dog raced ahead and barked.

The men found him standing at the edge of the old bear pit.

Mr. Reed reached it first. He looked down with such fear that for a moment he could not speak.

At the bottom, on a bed of leaves, Tommy lay curled in peaceful sleep.

“Tommy!” his father called.

The boy stirred, rubbed his eyes, and sat up. He saw faces looking over the rim—his father’s, Mr. Grey’s, Mr. Mullin’s, and several others framed by the morning sky.

“Did you get him?” Tommy asked.

“Get who?”

“The bear.”

Old Mr. Mullin gave a great laugh that startled birds from the neighboring trees.

“Wal, sonny, the only bear we’ve got is the one in the bear pit, and he don’t look dangerous before breakfast.”

They lowered a rope. Tommy tied it around himself, and the men hauled him out, dirty, scratched, hungry, and otherwise unharmed. His father did not scold him. He only put both arms around him, which made Tommy feel smaller than any scolding could have done.

“Is Billy all right?” Tommy asked.

“He is. You did bravely to go for help.”

Tommy glanced at the pit.

“I didn’t get very far.”

“You went.”

Mr. Grey handed him a piece of bread and meat. Tommy ate while the men examined the old trap.

“Had a soft landing,” said the hunter. “Luck’s been kinder to you than you deserved.”

“Yes, sir,” Tommy answered.

This reply pleased Mr. Grey more than a boast would have done.

The walk home became a procession. News traveled ahead, and by the time they reached the farm, neighbors lined the yard. Mrs. Reed ran through the gate and clasped Tommy until the remaining breath was squeezed from him. Billy, washed and bandaged and seated importantly in a chair, waved from the porch.

“Hollo!” he called. “I told ’em you saved me.”

Tommy was given hot cakes, eggs, milk, fried ham, and a quantity of apple pie large enough to satisfy even Billy. Everyone wanted the story. Billy related how he had hung “a hundred feet” above the bear, while Tommy corrected the distance to twenty. Mr. Mullin declared that both boys had shown “considerable talent for gettin’ into places no sensible body would pick.”

By afternoon Tommy had become the hero of the adventure. He had shared his food, climbed a dangerous tree, tried to rescue Billy, and gone alone through the dark for help. The bear pit, softened by repetition, became deeper; the moonlight became dimmer; the supposed bear grew larger. Tommy listened while neighbors praised his courage, and he did not deny it, for he was still a boy and praise is pleasant.

Yet he knew certain things they did not.

He knew that the expedition’s supplies had been stockings and yarn. He knew that trout tasted poorly when half raw and half ashes. He knew how cold a pine bed became at midnight, how narrow a branch felt above dark ground, and how little a bold speech helped when one was lost. Most of all, he remembered a brown thrush lying among the ferns.

That evening he and Billy sat on the orchard wall. The sun sank red beyond Hemlock Mountain, and the distant forest appeared once more inviting, full of green paths and shining brooks.

“Do you suppose,” Billy asked, “we’ll go hunting again?”

“Maybe.”

“When?”

Tommy considered. Only two days before, he would have answered, “Tomorrow,” and begun planning at once. Now he swung one bare foot against the stones and watched the mountain darken.

“When we’re older,” he said.

“How much older?”

“Old enough to carry the right basket.”

Billy nodded gravely.

“And a blanket,” he added.

“And enough matches.”

“And pie.”

Tommy smiled.

“Yes, pie too.”

A thrush sang from the apple tree. Its clear notes fell through the warm evening, and Tommy’s smile faded. Billy glanced at him but said nothing, for he had kept the promised secret.

Tommy never told the grown people about the little grave under the fern. The lesson belonged to him alone, and perhaps it was the most valuable thing he brought home from Hemlock Mountain. He still loved adventure books; he still meant, one day, to fish wild brooks, climb high ridges, and camp beneath the pines. Courage had not been frightened out of him, nor should it have been. But courage was beginning to make room for judgment, and ambition for kindness.

He resolved that when he was old enough to hunt, he would first learn the woods, carry proper food, heed good advice, and never mistake cruelty for bravery.

And whatever game he might pursue in years to come, he would never again lift bow or gun against a harmless singing bird.

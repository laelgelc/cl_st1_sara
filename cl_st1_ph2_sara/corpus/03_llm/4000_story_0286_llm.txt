# The Kingdom of the Egg

Before my father became ambitious he was, I have been told, a cheerful man. I never knew him in that condition and have sometimes doubted that it existed, although my mother, who was not given to embellishment, always insisted upon it. She said he used to sing while pitching hay and could sleep anywhere—under a wagon, in a mow, or with his head on a sack of seed corn. He laughed at the hired men’s jokes and spent his wages on tobacco, suspenders, and lemon drops. On Saturday nights he went into Fremont and stood about before the feed store with other farmhands, arguing about horses and the proper time for planting oats. He did not own a horse, and the oats were never his, which perhaps made his opinions easier to maintain.

In those days he worked on farms along the Sandusky River. He was large in the shoulders, narrow in the chest, and already losing his hair. The baldness began before he was twenty-five and advanced steadily, as if some pale moon were rising over his forehead. Later, when care had sharpened his face and given him a habit of looking suspiciously at small objects, his smooth head made him seem older than he was. But in the beginning, my mother said, the baldness amused him. He would bow to women at church picnics and invite them to admire the progress of civilization.

My mother taught school in a one-room building called Beech Grove, although there were only two beeches near it and one was dead. She boarded with the farmer for whom my father worked. Her room was upstairs at the end of a passage where seed catalogues, broken chairs, and hickory nuts were stored in winter. Every morning she walked three-quarters of a mile to the schoolhouse carrying a dinner pail and a small brass bell.

She came from a family that had never possessed anything but plans. Her father had intended to buy a threshing machine, then to study law, then to go west, but died while repairing a gate. Two of her brothers had talked of entering business in Toledo and became section hands instead. From these men my mother inherited no money, but she inherited the fixed conviction that life ought to lead somewhere. She was incurably ambitious.

That was my father’s undoing.

I do not mean that my mother was harsh. She did not command him to grow rich or compare him aloud with bankers and storekeepers. She merely believed in him. There are men who can endure contempt, sickness, weather, and insufficient food, but who are destroyed by being believed in. My father was such a man.

They were married in the parlor of the farmer’s house. My mother wore a brown dress she had sewn herself, and my father borrowed a coat from a hired man who was shorter in the arms. A Methodist preacher spoke of building a home and taking one’s proper station in the world. The phrase stayed with my mother. A proper station was, in her mind, a place one reached by intelligence and work, whereas the railroad stations known to us were places where one waited in cold rooms for trains that came late.

During the first year of their marriage they lived in two rooms over a harness shop. My father continued to hire himself out by the month, but my mother disliked seeing him come home with manure on his boots and his neck burned red by the sun. When I was born she stopped teaching. After that the desire to rise in the world became more urgent. It was no longer for herself or even for my father. It was for me.

I lay in a wooden cradle beside the stove, making helpless noises, and already bore the responsibility of changing our family’s destiny.

It was then that poultry entered our lives.

A man traveling through the county sold my father a booklet entitled *Profit from Poultry on Five Acres*. On its yellow cover stood a white hen of noble proportions, surrounded by chicks. Behind her were a painted house, two apple trees, a windmill, and a gentleman in a straw hat who appeared to be counting money. The booklet explained that eggs were demanded by hotels, hospitals, restaurants, railway dining cars, growing children, invalids, and the better class of household. A hen, properly managed, was not a bird but an investment with feathers.

My mother read the booklet in the evenings. She read aloud figures concerning Leghorns, Plymouth Rocks, Brahmas, and Minorcas. She learned about bone meal, grit, ventilation, trap nests, and the extraordinary profits to be gained from selling day-old chicks. My father listened while mending harness or rocking my cradle with his foot. I imagine that at first he listened as he might have listened to a sermon on the New Jerusalem. The city of promise was distant, but its gates were open, and all that was needed to enter was a flock of sensible hens.

They rented a poor farm six miles south of Tiffin. The soil was clay and had been worn out by corn. A house leaned upon the land, a barn leaned upon the house, and several acres of thistles stood upright with an appearance of prosperity denied to everything else. Because the ground could be had cheaply, my parents considered this an advantage.

The chicken enterprise began with three hundred eggs purchased from a breeder in Indiana. My father ordered an incubator from a company in Buffalo. It was a square wooden cabinet with pipes, trays, a thermometer, and a lamp beneath it. The advertisement said that any child could operate it. This proved unfortunate, since no child was available except me, and I was still occupied with learning to sit up.

For twenty-one days my father watched the machine. He turned the eggs, filled the lamp, adjusted the flame, examined the thermometer, and rose twice each night to make certain that science had not gone out. My mother kept a notebook in which she recorded temperature, humidity, wind direction, and passages from the manufacturer’s instructions.

On the twenty-second day fourteen chicks appeared. Six died before noon. Three more died during the night, having mistaken the water pan for a place of rest. Of the five survivors, one had a twisted neck and walked backward, another possessed only one useful leg, and a third grew to maturity but never laid an egg, being eventually discovered to have been a rooster.

This was our beginning.

For ten years chickens occupied my parents. They did not merely keep chickens; they served them, studied them, warmed them, dosed them, cursed them, and buried them. Chickens filled our mornings with shrill demands and our nights with apprehension. They fell ill from dampness and died from heat. They froze in winter, smothered in corners, ate bits of glass, developed swellings, went blind, pecked one another to death, and were carried away by foxes, rats, hawks, owls, and boys.

A chicken, looked at casually, has an air of simple-minded innocence. It walks about nodding and scratching, and one may suppose that nature has made it plain and manageable. This is an error. No creature is more ingenious in the discovery of death. Give a chicken clean water, balanced food, warm housing, and a fenced yard, and it will search until it finds a rusty nail, an open drain, a poisonous weed, or another chicken’s eye.

My father read poultry journals. Their pages were filled with men who had begun with twelve hens and in three years owned brick houses. There were photographs of white buildings arranged in rows, of egg rooms containing numbered crates, and of solemn breeders standing among birds so healthy that they seemed a superior race. Testimonials declared that failure came from ignorance, poor sanitation, improper selection, lack of enterprise, excessive enterprise, or neglect of one of several powders sold by the journal’s advertisers.

My father bought the powders.

There was Red Comb Tonic, Imperial Louse Destroyer, Hen-Vigor, Quick-Grow Mash, Reliable Worm Expeller, and a black liquid called Mortality’s Master. The labels showed energetic fowls escaping from the grasp of death. In our yards death remained calm and businesslike.

I grew up among feathers and arithmetic. At breakfast my mother calculated the cost of feed against the value of eggs. At supper my father announced how many hens had failed to lay. They spoke of twenty cents as other people speak of honor, love, or eternal life. Twenty cents a dozen might save us. Eighteen cents was ruin. At twelve cents my father sat silently with his hands on his knees.

In spring we hoped. In summer we worked. In autumn we calculated. In winter we borrowed.

The incubators multiplied. After the first one came a larger machine with two lamps, then a red-painted machine advertised as self-regulating, then a used kerosene model brought from Findlay on a wagon. Each contained within it a new future. Each smoked, overheated, chilled, or produced weak chicks with damp heads and questioning eyes.

My father’s face changed during those years. The cheerful hired man disappeared. He acquired a narrow, anxious smile and the habit of rubbing his bald head with the heel of his hand. When entering a chicken house, he bent forward as though approaching a sickbed. If he saw a hen standing apart with its feathers puffed out, his shoulders fell.

“Another one,” he would say.

My mother never answered. She took a shovel.

There were victories, certainly. For several weeks in one remarkable spring the hens laid so many eggs that crates stood along the pantry wall. A grocer in Tiffin agreed to buy them at a good price. My mother washed every egg in warm water and packed them in clean straw. The next morning a wheel came off the wagon on the county road. Two crates broke. Before my father could return with another wagon, the sun grew warm. The grocer claimed the remaining eggs were stale and reduced the price.

I remember my father coming home with yolk dried on his boots.

The most curious result of the poultry years was his museum. Chicks were sometimes born with four legs, two heads, missing eyes, exposed organs, or beaks that crossed like scissors. At first my mother carried these beings away quickly, not wanting me to see them. My father, however, began to preserve them.

He bought alcohol at the drugstore and placed the malformed chicks in fruit jars. There was a pale one with two bodies joined at the breast, a black one with three feet clustered beneath it, and another whose head was turned completely backward, as if it had entered the world already regretting the journey. The jars stood on a shelf in the cellar. My father labeled them in pencil: “Double,” “Three-Leg,” “No Wings,” “May 6,” and, in one case, “Very Rare.”

Why he did this I cannot say with certainty. He claimed that a medical college might buy the specimens. Sometimes he imagined charging admission at the county fair. Once he wrote to a museum in Cleveland and received no reply. I fancy that the jars reassured him. They proved that failure was not always his doing. Here, pickled and displayed, were mistakes made before the chick had breathed, errors committed in darkness by nature herself.

The creatures frightened me. When sent to the cellar for potatoes I ran past the shelf, but I always looked. Their sealed eyes, yellow claws, and floating bodies suggested that they knew something about our family and were waiting for me to understand it.

At the end of ten years we owed money to the feed merchant, the bank, the incubator company, and my mother’s cousin in Norwalk. Our best hens developed cholera. They died in groups, greenish at the comb, and were burned in a pit behind the barn. The smoke drifted across the fields for two days.

After that my father quit poultry.

One cannot say he surrendered, since ambitious people do not surrender. They begin again in another place.

A railway junction called Pickett stood seven miles away. It consisted of a depot, a water tower, two grain elevators, a coal shed, six houses, and a building that had formerly been a saloon. Passenger trains on the north-and-south line stopped there, and travelers sometimes waited to make connections with the eastbound local. The railroad men said a factory might be built nearby. Factories were always about to be built in places like Pickett. Their invisible chimneys smoked over half the small towns of Ohio.

My mother decided we should open a restaurant.

The former saloon could be rented cheaply. Downstairs there was a long room with a counter and six stools, a kitchen in back, and two small tables by the window. Upstairs were four rooms in which we could live. A faded sign over the door said SAMPLE ROOM. My father painted over it in white and printed PICKETT LUNCH in red letters. Beneath that my mother added HOME COOKING.

We sold the farm equipment, paid what debts we could, and moved on a rainy day in March. The chickens went to an auction, but the museum came with us. My father wrapped each jar in newspaper and packed the collection in a wooden egg case. Thus the results of our first enterprise traveled carefully into the second.

I was eleven then and thought Pickett nearly a city. Trains passed close enough to shake the water in the pitchers. Telegraph wires hummed above the road. Men in blue overalls rolled milk cans along the platform, and twice a day the station agent put out a little green truck piled with mail sacks. After the isolation of the farm, these activities seemed evidence that the world was going somewhere.

My mother cooked. She made pies, fried ham, boiled coffee, peeled potatoes, and baked beans in a brown crock. My father waited on customers. I swept the floor before school and carried coal from the shed. At first we expected crowds.

The crowds did not come.

The fast trains went through without stopping. Passengers on the locals had already eaten or preferred to wait. Railroad men drank coffee, but railroad men expected it strong, hot, and nearly free. Farmers came in on wet days and sat for an hour over a five-cent cup. Two clerks from the grain elevator bought pie, although one of them later married and began taking his noon meal at home.

Our restaurant possessed the physical arrangements of prosperity without prosperity itself. There were glass domes for cakes, nickel-plated napkin holders, a cigar case, a cash register with brass keys, and a sign advertising orange soda. My father polished the counter every morning until it reflected his troubled head. Then he stood behind it waiting.

Waiting became his occupation.

My mother’s ambition did not leave her, but it became quieter. She saved string, turned collars, and made soup from bones. At night she studied advertisements for roadside eating houses. One article explained that personality brought trade. Customers, it said, did not return merely for food. They returned for a smile, a joke, a friendly word, and the feeling that the proprietor was glad to see them.

My father read the article many times.

He decided to become cheerful.

This was a difficult undertaking. Natural cheerfulness may be no more admirable than brown eyes, but artificial cheerfulness is a heroic labor. My father practiced smiling before the mirror in the kitchen. The smile exposed his teeth and made his eyes anxious. He memorized jokes from an almanac. When a customer entered, he hurried forward and said, “Well, well, here we are!” as if both he and the customer had survived a flood.

Usually the customer said, “Coffee.”

My father asked where he was headed, what line of work he followed, whether he thought rain likely, and whether he had ever considered the intelligence of the common hen. At the last question my mother would stop moving in the kitchen.

The chickens had not released him.

He kept the jars in a cupboard beneath the cigar case. On slow afternoons he brought them out and dusted them. He believed travelers might be interested. A commercial man who sold farm pumps once stared at the two-headed chick for several minutes, and my father talked of that success for a week.

“People want something different,” he said. “You have to catch their attention.”

My mother looked at him. “They might want pie.”

“Pie isn’t different.”

“No,” she said. “It isn’t.”

The disastrous night occurred in November. I will first tell what I saw afterward, because that is how the event came to me.

I had gone to bed in the small front room above the restaurant. Wind drove rain against the windows, and freight cars coupled in the yard with great iron crashes. Sometime after midnight I awoke. At first I thought one of the trains had struck the building. Then I heard my father climbing the stairs.

He came into the kitchen carrying an egg.

His shirtfront and trousers were smeared with another egg that had broken over him. Yellow yolk shone in the lamplight. A piece of shell clung to his vest. His face was white, except around the eyes, and his mouth worked as though he were chewing something he could not swallow.

My mother sat at the table in her nightdress with a shawl around her shoulders. She had been waiting for him.

He crossed the room and placed the unbroken egg in a saucer. He did this very gently. Then he lowered himself into a chair, leaned forward, and put his head on his arms.

My mother went to him. She did not ask what had happened. She placed one hand on the bare crown of his head and stroked it slowly.

Presently he began to weep.

It was the first time I had seen a grown man cry. I stood in the doorway, hidden by the dark passage, and watched his shoulders shake. My mother continued stroking his head. On the table between them the egg rested in its saucer, white, whole, and composed.

For years that was all I knew. Later I learned the cause from fragments spoken by my mother, from a remark made by the station agent, and from my own imagination, which may have supplied too much. Still, I believe the account that follows is substantially true.

That evening the northbound passenger was delayed by a washed-out culvert. A young man came into the restaurant at about ten. He was perhaps twenty-two, neatly dressed, and on his way to Toledo, where he worked in an office. He carried a leather case and wore a gray hat with a narrow band. The station agent told him the train would not arrive before midnight.

My mother had gone upstairs with a headache. I had gone to bed. My father was alone.

The young man ordered coffee and a ham sandwich. My father made the sandwich, set it before him, and rang fifteen cents on the register. Rain streaked the windows. The depot platform was empty. For a while neither man spoke.

My father decided that this was his opportunity.

“Well, well,” he said. “Looks as if the railroad has caught you.”

The young man smiled politely.

“Going far?”

“Toledo.”

“Business?”

“Yes.”

“What line?”

“Insurance.”

“Insurance,” my father said. “That’s a coming thing.”

The young man drank his coffee.

My father wiped the counter. He wanted the stranger to like him. I think that desire must be understood. For months he had stood in an empty restaurant, armed with jokes, friendliness, and a polished counter, while the world passed on the tracks outside. Here at last was a traveler held captive by rain and railroad misfortune. Two hours lay before them. Friendship, admiration, and perhaps fame might arise in two hours.

“You ever see an egg stand on end?” my father asked.

The young man looked at him. “Can’t say I have.”

My father brought an egg from the kitchen.

There is an old belief that any difficult act, once performed by a celebrated person, becomes easy and therefore ought to be performed by everyone. My father had read that an explorer stood an egg on its end by flattening the shell slightly. He placed the egg on the counter and tapped it.

It fell over.

He tapped it again. It rolled toward the young man, who stopped it with one finger.

“Counter isn’t level,” my father said.

He tried another place. The egg fell. He wet the end, rubbed it against his sleeve, and made a small bed of salt, though the salt rather defeated the purpose of the demonstration. At last the egg stood.

“There,” he said.

The young man nodded. “That’s pretty good.”

The words encouraged my father. Tragedy often enters by means of encouragement.

“There are plenty of things about eggs people don’t know,” he said. “Take incubation. Now, an egg looks simple. But it isn’t. It’s one of the deepest things in nature.”

The young man glanced toward the clock.

My father held the egg between thumb and forefinger. He explained the germ, the yolk, the white, the shell, the air cell, and the twenty-one days required for a chicken to prepare itself for disappointment. He spoke of temperature and moisture. As he talked, his nervousness increased. Words came too quickly. Sweat appeared on his bald head.

Then he remembered a trick involving the hand.

A fresh egg, he said, could not be crushed if held lengthwise in the palm and squeezed evenly. The arch of the shell resisted pressure. He had read this in a poultry journal and had demonstrated it successfully to me once, although the egg used then may have been boiled.

“Try it,” he urged.

The young man declined.

“Go ahead. Put it in your hand like this.”

“I’d rather not get egg on my clothes.”

“It won’t break. That’s the point.”

My father placed the egg in his own palm. He wrapped his fingers around it and began to squeeze. At first his expression was confident. Then surprise entered it. The egg did not break, but neither could he crush it. His arm trembled. He squeezed harder, and the young man watched.

My father’s face reddened. What had begun as an exhibition of natural law became a contest. The egg, without movement or sound, resisted him.

At last he stopped.

“You see?” he said, breathing hard. “Can’t be done.”

The young man laughed a little—not cruelly, I think, but because the sight of a bald restaurant keeper straining against an egg had become comic.

My father heard the laughter.

He might still have escaped. He might have put the egg away, poured more coffee, or spoken of the weather. Instead he thought of the museum.

“I’ve got things here you never saw,” he said.

He knelt and opened the cupboard beneath the cigar case. One by one he placed the jars on the counter.

The young man stared.

In the yellowish alcohol floated the failures of our chicken farm: the chick with two heads, the chick with the backward neck, the four-legged chick, and the pale unfinished creature labeled “Very Rare.” Dust had collected under the rims of the lids. One jar had lost alcohol, so that a claw projected above the surface.

“Nature does funny things,” my father said.

“Yes,” said the young man.

“This one here would have been worth money if it had lived.”

“Would it?”

“Exhibitions. Scientific purposes. People pay to see what’s unusual.”

The young man moved his plate away from the jars.

My father noticed the movement and became more eager. He told the history of each specimen. He described the incubators, the lamps, the thermometers, and the night when forty chicks suffocated because a ventilator stuck. He told of the hen cholera and the broken wagon wheel. Ten years of defeat, held silent in him, poured out before a stranger who had asked for coffee.

As he talked, he handled the jars lovingly. They were his witnesses. They proved he had ventured, studied, worked, and suffered. They proved that nature was treacherous and that his losses possessed meaning.

The young man’s expression changed from curiosity to discomfort. He looked again at the clock.

My father saw that he was losing his audience.

“Wait,” he said. “I’ll show you something else.”

He took the egg he had tried to crush and placed it on the counter. From a drawer he brought a vinegar bottle, a piece of rubber tubing, a cork, and a small knife. There was, he explained, a method of removing the contents of an egg through one small hole and then forcing the empty shell into a narrow-necked bottle. Once inside, the shell could be expanded. The result would astonish anyone.

Whether such a trick is possible I do not know. My father believed it was.

He pierced one end of the egg. The knife slipped and made a crack. A thread of white ran over his fingers.

“That one was thin-shelled,” he said.

He brought another egg.

The young man rose. “I think I’ll go over to the depot.”

“No need. It’s cold there.”

“I don’t mind.”

“This won’t take a minute.”

My father’s voice had become pleading. The young man sat down again out of kindness or embarrassment. My father pierced the second egg and began blowing through the rubber tube. Nothing came out. He enlarged the hole. Yolk spattered onto the counter.

He laughed, but it was his practiced laugh, the one from the mirror.

“Stubborn fellow,” he said.

The young man smiled despite himself.

My father blew harder. His cheeks swelled. The tube slipped. The egg shot from his hand, struck his chest, and burst against his vest. Yolk slid down his shirt and trousers.

For a moment there was silence.

Then the young man laughed.

I have wondered often about that laugh. Perhaps it was no more than the involuntary release of a tired boy who had been shown preserved monsters and detained over eggs after midnight. Perhaps he tried to stop. Perhaps, once begun, it grew because my father stood so still, his hands held out, his face astonished and yellow shell clinging to his vest.

My father’s whole life was in that moment: the yellow booklet, the rented farm, the incubators, the dying chicks, the powders, the debts, the painted restaurant sign, the practiced smile, the empty stools, and my mother upstairs still believing in him.

He picked up the remaining egg.

“You think that’s funny?” he said.

The young man stopped laughing.

“You come in here and make sport of a man?”

“I didn’t mean anything.”

“A man tries to show you something—tries to be friendly—and you laugh.”

“I said I didn’t mean anything.”

My father raised the egg.

The young man stepped toward the door. My father threw. The egg passed beside the gray hat and broke against the doorframe. The young man went out into the rain, crossed the road, and did not look back.

My father stood alone among the jars.

The restaurant smelled of coffee, ham, alcohol, and raw egg. On the counter the malformed chickens floated in their separate prisons, bent and doubled, incapable of surprise. Rain beat upon the windows. Across the road the signal changed from red to green.

My father slowly returned the jars to the cupboard. He wiped the counter. He gathered the broken shells and dropped them into the stove. Then, noticing one egg left beside the vinegar bottle, he took it upstairs.

That was the egg I saw him place in the saucer.

By morning he had washed his clothes. My mother scrubbed the doorframe. No one spoke of what had happened. At breakfast my father fried the unbroken egg and put it on my plate.

I could not eat it.

Not long afterward the factory that had been promised for Pickett was built twelve miles away in another town. Passenger service was reduced. We kept the restaurant for three more years, each year poorer than the one before. My mother took in sewing. My father worked part-time at the grain elevator and no longer tried to entertain customers.

The jars remained in the cupboard until one froze during a bitter winter night and cracked. My mother ordered them thrown away. My father carried the box behind the coal shed. He dug a hole in the hard ground and buried the specimens together.

I watched from an upstairs window. When he had covered them, he stood with the shovel in his hands, looking down. Snow began to fall upon his bare head. After a while my mother went out without a coat and brought him inside.

Years later I left Pickett. I went to Columbus, then east, and achieved enough of what my mother called rising in the world to understand its cost without escaping its attraction. I have sat in offices above busy streets and watched men destroy their peace over promotions, inventions, houses, and plans. I have heard cheerful voices practiced for customers and seen smiles put on like aprons. Whenever someone speaks to me of unlimited opportunity, I think of my father’s hand closing around an egg.

It is easy to say he failed because he chose badly. Chickens are uncertain, restaurants depend upon location, and a man unsuited to jokes should not become an entertainer. Such explanations are sensible and therefore incomplete. My father’s true error was more ordinary. He believed that effort must persuade the world. He believed that if he worked, studied, saved, smiled, and tried again, the stubborn shell of things would yield.

The shell did not yield.

Still, I cannot despise him. On that night, after anger had spent itself, he carried one egg upstairs and set it down gently. That gesture has remained with me more faithfully than his rage. He could throw an egg at a stranger, but he could not crush the one in his hand. He had been defeated by it too many times. It contained breakfast, profit, birth, deformity, promise, and the old round argument concerning which thing came first and whether beginnings are anything more than endings not yet understood.

My mother stroked his bald head. My father wept. I stood unseen in the passage.

And on the table, in a cheap white saucer, lay the smooth and perfect cause of everything.

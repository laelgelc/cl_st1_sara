# The Black Ledger of Ephraim Pike

There lies, some miles westward of Boston, a low tract of country which the oldest maps distinguish by no name, though every farmer within a day’s ride once knew it as Hangman’s Mire. Why it came by this title is disputed. One account derives it from a smuggler who was found suspended from an oak there, with his boots full of Spanish coin; another from three witches executed during an epidemic of public virtue; while a third, supported by a schoolmaster of uncommon learning and no judgment whatever, maintains that the place resembles a gallows when viewed from the steeple of Roxbury church. As no one has ever been known to climb that steeple merely to inspect a swamp, the first two opinions may be considered equally credible.

The mire was a dismal piece of ground. Black alders leaned over pools that never reflected the sky; tufts of coarse grass grew upon treacherous islands; and fallen trunks, green with moss on the upper side and rotten as bad promises underneath, lay across channels of brown water. In dry weather the mud swallowed a shoe; in wet weather it swallowed the wearer also, unless he possessed unusual activity or had already sold himself to some power interested in preserving him. Owls lodged there by day, carrion birds by night, and frogs kept up such solemn disputations that a traveller might imagine a convocation of divines buried beneath the ooze.

Tradition says that a pirate named Silas Rook hid a quantity of treasure in this swamp during the reign of Queen Anne. Rook had sailed under seven flags, betrayed six captains, and hanged the seventh with his own sash; he therefore enjoyed a reputation for enterprise. After burying his plunder beneath a blasted hemlock, he was lost at sea, though one version declares that his ship arrived safely and only the crew was lost—a distinction characteristic of nautical history. The treasure remained, for it was guarded, not by any honest Christian watchman, but by that ancient superintendent of ill-gotten property who has long held mortgages upon a considerable part of the human race.

Near the edge of this mire, in a stone house whose chimney seldom smoked, lived Ephraim Pike and his wife, Hepzibah. They owned forty acres, though the greater portion seemed ashamed of the connection and produced nothing. A few savins struggled beside the door; two apple trees bore leaves but no fruit; and a lean horse gnawed the rails of its own enclosure with the melancholy perseverance of a debtor consuming his last security.

Ephraim was a narrow-chested, sharp-nosed man, with pale eyes that measured everything they looked upon. He could estimate the value of a neighbor’s coat before greeting the wearer and compute the waste of candle grease during family prayer. He had inherited a tolerable farm and reduced it, by economy, to barrenness. He begrudged seed to the field, fodder to the cattle, mortar to the walls, and food to himself; yet, as he begrudged all these things still more to his wife, he considered his conduct prudent.

Hepzibah was of a more active parsimony. What Ephraim withheld, she seized; what he locked away, she searched for; and what she could neither seize nor discover, she broke in the hope of finding money concealed within it. She was tall, raw-boned, and possessed of a hand that had left its print upon cupboards, servants, peddlers, and once upon the selectman of the town, who afterward conducted public business from the opposite side of the road.

The married life of this worthy pair was one continual audit. They hid provisions from each other, counted the eggs separately, and marked the level of the cider barrel with chalk. If a hen disappeared, Ephraim accused Hepzibah of eating it; Hepzibah replied that a husband who starved his wife had no title to poultry; and the quarrel commonly ended with Ephraim in the yard and some article of furniture following close behind. Their neighbors heard these contests at a respectful distance and judged, from the vigor of the combatants, that famine had not yet carried off either.

It happened one November afternoon that Ephraim, returning from Boston by a path he believed shorter, entered Hangman’s Mire. The day had been close and gray. A copper-colored light lingered between the clouds and the distant hills, but in the swamp dusk had already collected under the trees. Ephraim picked his way from root to root, using a thorn stick to test the ground and muttering over the injury done to his shoes.

The path, if path it could be called, wound deeper among the pools. Here a dead pine stood upright, stripped of bark and polished by weather; there a great oak had fallen and lifted its roots like the claws of some drowned beast. Ephraim observed that several of the largest trees bore names cut deep into their trunks. One was marked “Magistrate Bell”; another, “Deacon Plum”; a third, “Captain Jabez Crowl.” The trees appeared sound at a distance, but where the bark had split, the heartwood was black and crumbly.

At the foot of a hemlock lay a newly cut branch, and beside it rested an axe of uncommon size.

Ephraim paused. A man sat upon a stump a few paces away, though Ephraim would have sworn that the stump had been empty the instant before. The stranger was broad-shouldered and dressed like a woodsman, in rough boots, leather breeches, and a dark woolen shirt. His face was neither young nor old. It was begrimed as if with charcoal, but two eyes shone through the blackness with a red, steady intelligence. He carried no powder horn, no game bag, and no Christian expression.

“You are late abroad,” said the stranger.

“So are you,” replied Ephraim.

“I have business here.”

“So have I. Mine is to get out.”

The man smiled and tapped the axe handle. “Few are in such haste when they find my tract of timber.”

“Your tract?” said Ephraim. “This mire belongs to Widow Ransom.”

“She holds the deed aboveground. I hold an older title.”

Ephraim looked again at the marked trees. “You have been cutting valuable wood without leave.”

“Valuable?” The woodsman struck the trunk marked with Deacon Plum’s name. The bark rang hollow. “This one has flourished thirty years on public esteem and widow’s money. It is nearly ready for the fire.”

“Deacon Plum is alive and sits in the first pew.”

“He sat there yesterday,” said the stranger. “He will lie elsewhere tomorrow.”

The woodsman thrust the blade into the trunk. A swarm of pale insects poured from the wound.

Ephraim was not a man readily troubled by omens, particularly when they concerned another person. Still, he drew back.

“Who are you?” he demanded.

“I have many names. The sailors call me Black Reef. The Indians who hunted these woods had another. In Salem I was once known as the Learned Stranger, because certain magistrates consulted me more faithfully than they consulted mercy. Churchmen name me in sermons; merchants meet me in their countingrooms; and politicians deny my acquaintance while carrying my letters in their pockets.”

“I have heard of you,” said Ephraim.

“No doubt. I have heard of you also.”

This pleased Ephraim more than prudence permitted him to show. “Then you know I am not easily imposed upon.”

“That is precisely why I waited. A fool may be cheated by any man, but it takes an artist to bargain with Ephraim Pike.”

The stranger turned and pointed beyond the trees, where a low mound rose above the reeds.

“Silas Rook buried four iron chests under that bank. Spanish pistoles, French louis, church plate from Havana, emeralds from a bishop’s ring, and enough silver to make every honest man in Massachusetts dishonest before breakfast.”

Ephraim’s mouth tightened. “Pirate tales.”

“I supervised the burial.”

“Then why did you not take it?”

“I have no use for money. I use those who have use for it.”

This distinction was subtle enough to impress Ephraim. He stepped nearer.

“What would you require?”

“Certain services. A name in my ledger. A promise, signed in the customary fashion. In return, the treasure and my assistance in placing it to profitable employment.”

“What employment?”

“There are several. You might fit out a ship to the coast of Africa and traffic in human misery.”

Ephraim frowned. “A hazardous trade. Cargo dies, crews mutiny, and Parliament is always talking.”

The stranger’s eyes glimmered with amusement. “Then you might arm a privateer.”

“Too much powder wasted.”

“Or open a gaming house.”

“Gamblers sometimes win.”

The woodsman laughed. It sounded like an axe biting dry wood. “You are difficult.”

“I am careful.”

“Then lend money in Boston. Hard times are coming. Land will change hands; merchants will overreach; speculators will build fortunes upon wind and require coin to keep them afloat. Advance a little, demand much, and take all.”

Ephraim considered this in silence. It struck him as a profession at once safe, respectable, and cruel; three recommendations rarely united in a single occupation.

Yet he was unwilling to close a bargain before consulting the one person whose wishes he most enjoyed opposing.

“I will think on it,” he said.

“You may.”

“And if I refuse?”

“Someone else will accept. There is never a scarcity of candidates.”

The last glow had vanished from the sky. Ephraim turned toward the path, but after a few steps looked back. The stump was empty. The axe remained; the branch lay beside it; and from far within the mire came a measured chopping.

He reached home after midnight, scratched, muddy, and missing one buckle. Hepzibah met him with a candle in one hand and a pewter ladle in the other.

“Where have you been?”

“Abroad.”

“With whom?”

“No one.”

“Then no one has cost you a buckle.”

Ephraim attempted to pass, but she seized his coat and searched the pockets. She found only a dry black leaf he did not remember collecting. The instant she touched it, it crumbled into soot.

Her suspicions, instead of being quieted, became theological.

By morning she had extracted the greater part of the story—not because Ephraim told it willingly, but because concealment between husband and wife is difficult when the wife is prepared to employ household implements. When she learned of the treasure, her indignation exceeded all bounds.

“You refused?”

“I postponed.”

“You came home without one coin?”

“I came home with my soul.”

“A fine piece of property! It feeds no hens, pays no taxes, and has never done a day’s work since I knew you.”

“The terms require reflection.”

“They require a man, which is why you found them troublesome.”

All that day she pursued him with arguments, reproaches, and the broom. She represented the treasure as already theirs and his caution as theft from the marital estate. Ephraim, who had been almost decided to accept, hardened immediately against it. He declared that no wife should send her husband to damnation merely to improve her furniture; Hepzibah replied that the furniture stood in greater need of salvation than he did.

The dispute continued three days. On the fourth morning she disappeared.

At first Ephraim supposed she had gone to a neighbor’s to denounce him, a charitable practice in which she frequently indulged. By dusk, however, he discovered that two silver spoons, a brass candlestick, his mother’s wedding brooch, and the small bag of coins beneath the hearthstone had disappeared also. He then understood that Hepzibah had gone to propitiate the black woodsman upon her own account.

She returned late that night, empty-handed, with her cap torn and an expression capable of souring milk at twenty paces.

“Well?” asked Ephraim.

“He will not treat with me.”

“Perhaps he dislikes your manner.”

“He says the offer was made to you.”

“An honorable adherence to terms.”

“He shall alter them.”

The next morning she went again, carrying the silver teapot, a length of homespun, and the lean horse’s only decent blanket. These things had, at various times, been hidden from Ephraim and were consequently of more value than he had suspected. She did not return.

On the second day, Ephraim began to feel uneasy for the teapot. On the third, he ventured to the edge of the mire and called her name. The only answer was the croak of a raven.

Several neighbors organized a search, though most remained on dry ground and offered advice. Near the hemlock they found deep marks in the mud, as if a furious struggle had taken place. Coarse gray hairs clung to a thorn bush; whether they came from Hepzibah or her antagonist was debated with some warmth. One of the silver spoons lay bent double. A huge hoofprint was discovered beside a small crater of scorched moss, and from a low branch hung Hepzibah’s checked apron.

Ephraim climbed up and took it down. It felt unusually heavy. He untied the corners, expecting the household valuables, but found only a blackened liver and a handful of stiff bristles.

Some declared that the devil had carried off Hepzibah. Others maintained that she had carried off the devil, since neither had afterward been seen in a cheerful condition. A hunter reported hearing shrieks, curses, and blows throughout the night, followed by a voice crying for quarter in a tone not recognizably female. The most current account is that the woodsman won after a hard contest, though it cost him a quantity of hair and very nearly an eye.

Ephraim mourned with moderation. He regretted the spoons, the brooch, the candlestick, the teapot, the blanket, and finally, after some effort, his wife. Yet he could not deny that the house had grown wonderfully peaceful. He slept without barricading the door, found food where he had left it, and enjoyed undisputed possession of the chimney corner. Gratitude for this deliverance disposed him favorably toward the power responsible.

He therefore returned to the mire.

The black woodsman sat upon the same stump, sharpening his axe. A long scratch crossed one cheek.

“You have had company,” said Ephraim.

“A vigorous woman.”

“Did she leave any property?”

“None that you may reclaim.”

“And herself?”

“Still less.”

Ephraim considered it poor policy to press the inquiry.

“I am prepared to discuss your proposal.”

“Your soul for the treasure.”

“Not so fast. The treasure must be proved, weighed, and delivered free of expense.”

“It shall.”

“I will not engage in piracy.”

“Agreed.”

“Nor gaming.”

“Agreed.”

“Nor the African trade.”

The stranger raised an eyebrow. “A sudden delicacy?”

“I dislike the uncertainty.”

“Call it conscience if you wish. Men commonly give an agreeable name to their least profitable objection.”

“I will lend money,” said Ephraim. “But the returns must justify the risk.”

“You shall ask four percent a month.”

“Six.”

“Five.”

“Six, payable in advance, with penalties for delay.”

The woodsman studied him with professional admiration. “Egad, Pike, I begin to fear for my own reputation. Six, then. You will lend upon land, ships, shops, harvests, inheritances, and expectations. When distress visits a man, you will call yourself his friend.”

“I will require security.”

“You will take twice the value.”

“Three times, if his need is urgent.”

“And show mercy?”

“Whenever it pays.”

At this the stranger produced a book bound in dark hide. Its pages were crowded with names, some large and ornamented, others cramped into margins; there were judges, captains, merchants, tavern keepers, smugglers, pious widows, impious deacons, and several persons described as reformers.

“What is the term?” asked Ephraim.

“Until called.”

“Too vague.”

“All mortality is vague.”

“I prefer a date.”

“You will receive notice.”

“How much?”

“Enough to put your affairs in order, if any order can be made of them.”

Ephraim argued, qualified, amended, and disputed until the moon rose. At length the bargain was concluded. What ceremony confirmed it is uncertain. Some say Ephraim signed in blood; others that he merely shook hands, which, between men of business, has often accomplished the same result. He left the swamp with a little black purse that never emptied so long as the money drawn from it was used to entangle another.

Within a year a brass plate appeared beside a narrow door in Boston:

**EPHRAIM PIKE  
ACCOMMODATIONS IN MONEY  
TO PERSONS OF CHARACTER AND PROPERTY**

The word “accommodations” attracted the needy, and the qualifications excluded no one who possessed anything worth taking.

The times favored him. Peace had left merchants with idle ships, war rumors raised the price of everything, and a new company proposed to extract copper, pearls, and medicinal salt from a tract of land where nothing had previously been extracted but mosquitoes. Men bought shares with borrowed money and sold them to other men who borrowed more. Farmers mortgaged their fields to become merchants; merchants pledged their cargoes to become gentlemen; gentlemen endorsed one another’s notes in the belief that mutual insolvency was a species of wealth.

Through this general prosperity Ephraim moved like a spider through a dancing school.

He opened his door early and closed it late. No applicant was dismissed merely because repayment was impossible; impossibility improved the security. A merchant with a ship overdue received fifty pounds upon a warehouse worth five hundred. A farmer whose children were ill obtained ten pounds upon his orchard and house. A widow borrowed enough to bury her husband and thereby enabled Ephraim, in due course, to bury her title to the farm.

He was courteous to desperation. He called every borrower “friend,” pressed a chair upon him, inquired after the family, and regretted that the hardness of the times compelled such severe conditions.

“I would gladly make the rate less,” he would say, dipping his pen, “but money is timid.”

“Six percent a month will ruin me,” protested one young tradesman.

“Then repay promptly and escape ruin.”

“I need three months.”

“Take six. It is unwise to be hurried.”

“But the penalties—”

“Are intended to encourage punctuality. Sign here.”

The tradesman signed. Ephraim shook his hand, wished him success, and six months later owned his shop.

His fortune grew hand over hand. Bonds filled his drawers; mortgages crowded his iron chests; deeds accumulated until he possessed claims upon half the county and the gratitude of no one in it. He built a mansion upon a hill overlooking the road to Boston. Its front displayed twelve tall windows, but only four rooms were finished. He ordered carved stone for the entrance and refused to pay the mason after it arrived. He kept a coach for dignity and starved the horses for economy; thus his establishment presented wealth in front and famine behind, which some considered an accurate model of society.

Respectability followed riches, as it generally does when it cannot arrive first. Ephraim became “Mister Pike” in the newspapers. He subscribed five shillings toward a new meetinghouse, upon condition that the amount be publicly announced as five pounds. He lent silver plate for charitable dinners and charged for scratches. He was consulted about currency, trade, public morals, and the causes of poverty, upon all of which he spoke with the authority of a man who had profited by their confusion.

If a bankrupt cursed him, sober citizens replied that contracts must be honored. If a family was turned into the snow, the event was called an unfortunate necessity. If Ephraim purchased their house for one-tenth its worth, this proved his sagacity. There is no alchemy like success: it converts cruelty into firmness, theft into finance, and a full purse into a certificate of virtue.

Years passed. The marked trees in Hangman’s Mire fell one by one. Magistrate Bell died during a banquet given in praise of temperance. Deacon Plum was found to have confused the church treasury with his private account, but as he expired before the confusion could be corrected, a Boston paper declared that a pillar had been removed from Israel. Captain Crowl, who had boasted of carrying the gospel to foreign ports, went down with a cargo of rum, muskets, and forged Bibles.

Ephraim read these reports uneasily.

His own hair whitened; his back bent; his hands shook when he counted coin, though they steadied remarkably when receiving it. Sleep became difficult. At night he heard branches scraping his shutters even when there was no wind. Sometimes a horse galloped along the road and halted before his gate, but when the servants looked out, nothing stood there except a patch of scorched earth.

He began to reflect upon the next world. His reflections resembled his dealings in this one: he wished to secure the greatest return at the smallest cost.

He joined the church with sudden fervor. No man sang louder, prayed longer, or frowned more severely at sleepers. He attended three services on the Sabbath and criticized the minister for not providing a fourth. He learned whole chapters of Scripture, choosing chiefly those that condemned other people. He subscribed to societies for the suppression of vice and urged penalties against Sabbath breakers, drunkards, gamblers, idle laborers, and women who wore bright ribbons.

The congregation admired his zeal. A few remembered the widows and farmers, but memory is an uncomfortable faculty and is therefore little exercised in polite assemblies.

Ephraim carried a large Bible beneath his arm wherever he went. He kept a smaller one in his coat pocket, as a merchant keeps a reserve note for emergencies. One lay open upon the desk in his countingroom, commonly upon the verse concerning forgiveness, and served admirably to mark his place while he calculated compound interest.

It is said that he caused his coach horses to be shod backward, believing the black collector might follow tracks but could not reason. Another story says he buried a Bible beneath the threshold and placed horseshoes over every door. A third insists that he negotiated with a traveling preacher for a written guarantee of salvation and withdrew when the preacher refused to warrant the result. These reports may be exaggerations; but fear, like wealth, is seldom displayed without some vulgar ornament.

For all his devotion, Ephraim’s business did not soften. On the contrary, he became more exacting, perhaps because he regarded every profitable act as provision against an uncertain future. He balanced his piety against his extortions as if heaven kept books by double entry.

One afternoon in early spring, a man named Caleb Vane came to the countingroom. Vane had once possessed three mills and a fine house by the river. Flood, illness, and an unfortunate speculation in imported glass had reduced him to one mill, a sick wife, and seven children. Ephraim held the mortgage on the remainder.

Rain beat against the windows. The sky over Boston had turned the color of lead, and thunder muttered beyond the harbor.

Caleb stood before the desk, hat in hand.

“I have come to ask another month.”

“The payment was due yesterday.”

“My mill wheel broke in the flood. Give me time to repair it, and I can pay.”

“Time is costly.”

“I have already paid twice what I borrowed.”

“You have paid interest. The principal remains untouched.”

“My house is pledged.”

“And the mill.”

“If you take both, my family has nowhere to go.”

Ephraim turned a page of the ledger. “That is not entered in the agreement.”

Caleb leaned over the desk. “When I first came, you called yourself my friend.”

“A business expression.”

“You said you wished to save me.”

“From immediate inconvenience. I did so.”

“You have drawn my earnings for five years.”

“Proof of my patience.”

Outside, lightning whitened the street. A gust forced smoke down the chimney, and the candles burned blue.

Caleb’s face altered from pleading to despair. “Have you no mercy?”

Ephraim’s fingers closed upon his pen. “Mercy does not discharge a bond.”

“Then keep the mill and leave us the house.”

“The house is better security.”

“My children were born there.”

“A sentimental circumstance, but not a legal one.”

Caleb struck the desk. “You have made your fortune from men’s misery.”

“I made it from their signatures.”

“You have taken bread from the hungry.”

“I lent them money to buy it.”

“You will answer for this.”

Ephraim glanced at the open Bible. Its pages stirred though the windows were shut.

“I am prepared to answer to any lawful authority.”

“To God, then!”

A violent knock sounded at the outer door.

Both men became still.

The clerk had gone home early because of the storm. The servants were at the mansion. Ephraim and Caleb were alone.

The knock came again—three heavy blows, measured and deliberate.

Ephraim snatched up the small Bible and thrust it into his pocket. He laid one hand on the larger volume.

Caleb said, “Someone is at the door.”

“Let him wait.”

A third knock shook the brass plate outside and jarred dust from the beams.

Ephraim’s fear turned, as fear often does in hard men, to anger.

“Take your whining elsewhere!” he cried at Caleb. “The devil may carry me off if I have made one penny unjustly!”

The door flew open.

A blast swept through the room, extinguishing every candle but one. In the doorway stood the black woodsman, unaltered by the years. Rain did not wet his clothes. The scratch upon his cheek had healed, though a faint pale line remained to commemorate Hepzibah.

“You called,” he said.

Ephraim clutched the Bible. “I called no one.”

“You invoked me with admirable distinctness.”

“It was a manner of speaking.”

“I have waited long enough upon your manners.”

The street behind him was empty except for a great black horse. Sparks flew from its nostrils and its hooves smoked upon the wet stones.

Ephraim backed against the desk. “Our agreement included notice.”

“You have had it. Every gray hair was a notice. Every funeral bell was a notice. Every midnight hoofbeat was a notice. You chose not to enter them in your books.”

“I require time to settle my accounts.”

“They are settled.”

“I have church obligations.”

“They know you better elsewhere.”

“I carry Scripture!”

“So did many of my best clients.”

Ephraim raised the great Bible like a shield. The woodsman glanced at it.

“That belongs to Deacon Plum’s estate,” he observed. “You took it with the house.”

Before Ephraim could reply, the stranger seized him by the collar. The smaller Bible fell from his pocket; the larger slid across the mortgage papers and closed with a clap.

Caleb sprang aside. Ephraim kicked, clung to the desk, and cried out for sheriff, minister, magistrate, and any other authority that might possess jurisdiction over the infernal. The woodsman dragged him through the door as easily as a farmer drags a sack of meal.

The black horse knelt. In an instant Ephraim was thrown across its back, facing the tail, his coat skirts over his head and his hands still clutching a bundle of unsigned foreclosure papers. The woodsman mounted behind him.

“Stop!” shrieked Ephraim. “The bond is not witnessed!”

“It has had witnesses enough.”

The horse leapt forward.

Caleb ran to the doorway. He saw it gallop through the rain, striking fire from the stones, overturning a constable, scattering market baskets, and passing the meetinghouse just as the bell began to toll without a hand upon the rope. It sped westward beyond the lamps of Boston, over fields and frozen ruts, with thunder breaking above it and Ephraim’s cries growing fainter in the wind.

Several persons later claimed to have seen the riders. A tavern keeper swore that Ephraim offered a hundred pounds for rescue, then two hundred, then attempted to reduce the sum when no one accepted. A farmer heard him reciting prayers backward. A child declared that a large book flew after the horse like a black-winged bird, shedding burning pages.

At Hangman’s Mire the horse left the road and plunged among the trees. Blue flames rose from the pools. The branches bent together behind it, and from the deepest part of the swamp came one last cry—not of repentance, according to those who heard it, but of protest concerning the interest.

At that same instant lightning struck Ephraim’s mansion.

The servants escaped, though one returned at considerable risk to save a ham he had long suspected his master intended never to eat. The unfinished rooms burned first; then the carved entrance, the empty stables, the starved coach, and all the respectable magnificence of the place went up in a single roaring blaze. By dawn only the chimneys remained, standing among the ashes like monuments erected by creditors.

Caleb and several officials examined the countingroom the following morning. Every bond and mortgage had become a strip of black cinder. The iron chests contained chips of rotten wood, fish scales, broken glass, and round stones stamped with the faces of dead kings. Ephraim’s purse held dried leaves. His silver melted into gray puddles that smelled strongly of the swamp.

The horses in his country stable were found to be skeletons covered with hides. His coach collapsed when touched. The deeds to farms and houses bore no names, only brown stains like muddy fingerprints. Thus the fortune which had been gathered from so many ruined households dispersed in less time than it had taken to sharpen a pen.

Caleb Vane recovered his house and mill, though he afterward avoided speculation and was consequently considered a man of limited ambition. The other debtors reclaimed what they could. As for the church, it replaced Ephraim’s pew cushion, erased his name from the list of benefactors, and preached the next Sabbath upon the inscrutability of Providence without mentioning compound interest.

To this day, old people say that a black horse may sometimes be heard near Hangman’s Mire when thunder gathers over Boston. A thin man rides upon it backward, holding a ledger from which the pages flutter like scorched leaves. He stops travelers, asks the current rate of interest, and offers ready money upon excellent security. Those who answer are apt to lose the path; those who refuse hear a woodsman laughing among the hollow trees.

It would be imprudent to credit every particular of so ancient a story. Ledgers decay, mansions burn, and wealthy men disappear without supernatural assistance. Yet the swamp remains; the pools are still black; and many a flourishing reputation, when struck in the right place, has been found rotten at the heart.

The moral, therefore, may stand even if the horse does not: he who builds his house from another man’s ruin should not complain when the collector knocks at his own door.

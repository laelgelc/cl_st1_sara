### THE BRIGHT FLOWERS

“You have been a long time on the road,” said the young doctor.

He had come out upon the hospital veranda to smoke, and, seeing that I was awake, stopped beside my hammock. It was evening. Beyond the white railings the sea lay smooth and dark, and from the town came the faint jingling of a tram-bell, a civilized, agreeable sound which made one think of clean collars, coffee, and persons who know beforehand where they are to sleep.

“How do you know I have been on the road?” I asked.

“Your feet.”

“My feet are slanderers.”

He laughed. He was a good young man, pink in the face and serious about humanity; the sort of man who, after looking at a scar, desires to know the history of the knife. I had many scars, and consequently he regarded me as a volume in several parts.

“You were a tramp?” he asked.

“Peradventure.”

“And you never killed anyone?”

The question was innocent. He had been discussing crime with the assistant surgeon, who maintained that a murderer could always be known by his ears. Mine are small, almost aristocratic, and had evidently awakened doubt.

“No,” I said. “At least, not with my own hands.”

He ceased smiling.

The hammock swung a little. Somewhere under the veranda a sick man coughed with the regularity of a clock. The doctor threw away his cigarette, then changed his mind, picked it up, wiped the sand from it, and smoked again. Youth loves mankind, but it is economical with tobacco.

“What does that mean?” he asked.

“It means exactly what it means. If you have four and twenty hours to spare, doctor, I will tell you how a man was murdered under the bright flowers of heaven, and why none of us was guilty.”

“None?”

“None separately. Collectively, perhaps—but society is a large coat, and bloodstains vanish upon it.”

He frowned. I shut my eyes, for the sea air was cool and pleasant, and began.

---

We had left Perekop in July: Savka the soldier, Ilya who called himself a student, and I.

What I called myself at that period varied with circumstances. To charitable ladies I was a ruined clerk; to peasants, a discharged schoolmaster; to policemen, when invention would have been wasted, I was nobody. All these descriptions contained some truth, though not enough to embarrass me. I had once copied documents, had once corrected a village deacon’s spelling, and was indisputably ruined. Thus, by joining fragments, one may manufacture a respectable biography.

Savka had served in an infantry regiment and possessed a discharge paper, an old cap without a visor, shoulders fit for carrying a gun or breaking a door, and a nature as generous as it was dangerous. He would give you his shirt in the morning and knock out your teeth in the afternoon for losing one of its buttons. He was broad, red-haired, hairy in the nostrils, and walked with that rolling assurance which remains in a soldier after the government has ceased to feed him. When drunk, he wept over his mother; when sober, he cursed her for having brought him into an ill-arranged world.

Ilya was a different article. He was no older than twenty-two, but hunger had shrivelled him as heat shrivels an apple forgotten on a window-sill. His face was narrow, yellow at the temples and grey around the mouth; his eyes were greenish, dry, and watchful. He wore one boot. On the other foot he had constructed, out of sacking and wire, an object which he declared was a sandal of the ancient Greeks.

“Then the ancient Greeks,” Savka told him, “were swine.”

“They conquered half the world.”

“With that on their feet?”

“Not precisely.”

“Then don’t lie.”

Ilya said he had studied law in Kharkov. Perhaps he had. He knew Latin phrases, hated professors by name, and could explain with much bitterness the rights of property, especially when the property belonged to somebody else. Yet he never spoke of examinations or books, and once, when I asked him the difference between Roman and criminal law, he replied that a hungry man had no need of either. This answer struck me as so learned that I did not question him again.

We three had met outside Perekop because misfortune, like bad weather, drives creatures of various breeds under one shed. Savka had been turned out of a tavern for attempting to pawn the landlord’s own samovar. Ilya had slept in a ditch and awakened without his cap. I had exchanged my last waistcoat for a loaf which proved, upon mature examination, to consist largely of clay. We shared that loaf, abused the baker, and thereafter considered ourselves comrades.

Do not smile, doctor. Comradeship has been founded on less.

We were going south. Why south? Because the road ran there, because the air smelt of salt, because in the north we had already failed, and because a Jew with blue spectacles had told Savka that labourers were needed near Simferopol. Whether the Jew existed I cannot swear. Savka had obtained the information while drunk and could remember neither the place, the work, nor the rate of wages; but the south lay before us, broad, yellow, and promising, while behind us stood Perekop with its police, dogs, and charitable citizens. We therefore marched.

The Crimean steppe in July is a frying-pan prepared by God for the correction of vagrants. The road ran white across it, trembling in the heat, sometimes ascending a low swell from which we saw—further on—another low swell, another stretch of road, another emptiness scorched pale as bone. Grasshoppers rattled in the grass. Lizards darted beneath stones. Far away a wagon would appear, no bigger than a beetle, and crawl for an hour toward us; when it arrived there was generally an old Tatar in it, deaf to Russian eloquence, or a colonist who had already been robbed and was resolved not to repeat the experience.

We went.

At noon the light descended upon our skulls like a hammer. Our shadows shrank under our feet. The sky was a blue metal basin, the steppe a brass plate, and upon this handsome service we were three flies whom no one had troubled to brush away.

We had eaten nothing since the previous morning.

There are, as philosophers know, several kinds of hunger. There is the tender hunger of the gentleman who lifts the lid from the soup and says, “Again cabbage?” There is the disciplined hunger of the monk, supported by bells, doctrine, and an excellent supper expected after sunset. There is the poetic hunger of young lovers, who forget luncheon and remember it in their memoirs. Ours belonged to a fourth category. It occupied the whole body. It sharpened the smell, thickened the tongue, weakened the knees, filled the mouth with sourness, and set a small, laborious animal clawing behind the navel.

At first we talked continually of food.

Savka described army porridge as though recalling a mistress: its steam, its greasy hollows, the fragments of meat discovered at the bottom. I remembered baked carp with sour cream. Ilya spoke only once.

“Black bread,” he said.

“What with it?” asked Savka.

“Nothing.”

“Idiot. At least put an onion with it.”

“No.”

“A cucumber?”

“No.”

Savka spat into the dust.

“A student! They teach them to eat bread without onions, and afterward wonder why the empire is going to ruin.”

Later we ceased speaking of food, because every word made the stomach contract. We walked with open mouths. Dust coated our teeth. My feet were blistered, Savka’s neck had turned the colour of brick, and Ilya’s antique sandal shed pieces of civilization at every verst.*

\* A verst is somewhat more than a kilometre, though to an empty stomach it may equal ten.

Toward afternoon clouds appeared in the east, white and enormous. They rose one upon another like mountains of whipped cream.

“Milk,” Savka muttered.

“Clouds,” said Ilya.

“Who asked you? Milk. And that red on the edge is cranberry vinegar. I saw such a dish at a general’s wedding.”

“You were never at a general’s wedding.”

“I guarded the gate.”

“That is not being at the wedding.”

“I smelt it. That’s better than your learning.”

We went further on.

Once we found beside the road the rind of a melon. It was green, curled, and already inhabited by ants. Savka pounced upon it, scraped away the insects with his thumb, and bit into the white inner skin. Ilya watched him. I watched Ilya watching him. There are glances in which all political economy may be read.

“Share, brother,” I said.

Savka chewed, ashamed and furious.

“There’s nothing.”

“Then you lose nothing by sharing.”

He cursed, broke the rind into three pieces, and gave us each one. It was dry and bitter. We sucked it to threads.

Food, doctor, is commonly treated as a domestic detail. Men write verses to wine, women, liberty and battle; they erect monuments to generals, inventors, martyrs, even critics. But a crust which passes down a famished throat does more for the human soul than all their marble and music. For one instant the universe consents to your existence. The jaws move; saliva returns; warmth spreads beneath the ribs; you are reconciled with law, family, fatherland and God. Then the crust is gone, and you become a revolutionist again.

At sunset we came to a well. Or rather, to a hole containing six inches of warm water, two drowned beetles, and the reflection of a sky too magnificent for it. We lay down and drank on our bellies. Savka immersed his whole beard. Ilya drank slowly, lifting the water in his cap, for hunger had not destroyed his caution.

Nearby stood a stone trough, a broken wheel, and three poplars so thin that they seemed ashamed of pretending to give shade. There had perhaps once been a station or shepherd’s hut, but nothing remained except a circle of blackened earth and a few pieces of dung. We searched with the thoroughness of archaeologists. Savka found a horseshoe nail. I discovered a rag. Ilya, after poking beneath the trough, brought out half a walnut shell.

He looked at it a long time.

“Any nut?” Savka asked.

“No.”

“Then why look?”

“Because there was one.”

Night descended rapidly. On the steppe it does not creep from corners as in a town; it drops from the whole sky, blue at first, then violet, then black, until earth becomes a vast dark platter under a cupola sown with stars. The heat left the ground. A smell of wormwood rose around us, dry, bitter and wholesome. From very far off came the barking of a dog, then another answered, and for a while the invisible villages spoke to one another across the darkness.

We collected dung and stalks for a fire. Savka had three matches. The first went out in the wind. At this he made a noise such as the Church might properly reserve for the last trump. The second flared, scorched his finger, and died. Before striking the third, he crossed himself.

“O Lord,” he said, “Thou knowest we are unfortunate men and not damned chemists. Be merciful.”

The third match lit the dung.

Savka immediately began chanting in his hoarse bass: “Glory to Thee, Who hast revealed the light—”

“Stop,” said Ilya. “You’ll frighten it.”

“What?”

“The fire.”

We crouched around the miserable flame. It smoked, stung our eyes, and gave almost no heat, but we loved it. Savka stretched out his hands and began again to talk of porridge. Ilya sat with his knees drawn to his chin, lips pressed together. The flames made hollows in his cheeks and points of green light in his eyes. He looked less like a student than a small, ill-tempered saint painted on a tavern wall.

I lay on my back and contemplated the stars.

They were countless. Some burned white, others blue; one near the horizon pulsed red as if wounded. In those days I possessed a ridiculous susceptibility to beauty. It has since been cured by hospitals, railway waiting rooms and the conversation of educated people, but then a star could still make me melancholy. I thought of towns shining beyond the steppe, of tables laid behind curtains, of women cutting bread without knowing that every crumb was observed by eternity. Above us those bright flowers opened in silence; below, three vertebrate animals waited to see which would first bite the others.

“Listen,” Savka said.

We listened.

At first there was only the wind moving through the wormwood. Then came a faint scrape, a pause, another scrape. Footsteps.

“A man?” I whispered.

“Or the devil,” Savka replied.

“The devil rides,” said Ilya.

“Who told you?”

“Go and ask him.”

The sound drew nearer along the road. Scrape—pause—scrape. Whoever came walked with difficulty. We could not see him, but the darkness itself seemed to gather around the noise.

Savka rose. Hunger had made him weak, but he spread his feet as on parade and picked up the horseshoe nail, which was not a formidable weapon except in imagination. Ilya slipped behind the trough. I remained by the fire, being perhaps the bravest, perhaps only the slowest.

“Who goes?” Savka called.

The steps stopped.

“Who’s there?” answered a voice.

It was a man’s voice, thin and cracked. We heard hurried breathing.

“Come on,” Savka said. “We don’t eat people.”

This pleasantry was less convincing than he intended.

A figure entered the uncertain ring of firelight: a man of forty or forty-five, tall but stooped, with a sack over one shoulder and a carpenter’s saw wrapped in cloth under his arm. His beard was fair, sparse, and wet with sweat. He wore a cotton shirt, dark at the neck, trousers tucked into cracked boots, and a cloth cap pulled low. His face shone strangely. At first I thought it grease; then I saw his lips trembling and understood that he had fever.

He saw the three of us.

In the selfsame instant his right hand flew inside his shirt. Something glittered.

“Back!” he cried.

A revolver.

We became attentive.

It was an old weapon, small and rusted around the cylinder, but hunger does not render a bullet more digestible. Savka lowered the nail. I raised my hands with that urbanity which distinguishes me in difficult situations. Ilya remained invisible behind the trough.

“Peace, brother,” I said. “We are travellers.”

“I know your sort.”

“Then your education is complete. Put the pistol away.”

“Don’t come nearer!”

His arm shook so violently that the muzzle described circles. This was alarming; a steady man shoots where he intends, but fever may confer freedom upon the bullet.

Savka sniffed.

“You’ve got bread,” he said.

The man’s eyes shifted toward him.

Under the sweat and sickness there appeared an expression more primitive than fear—the expression of an animal whose food has been scented by others.

“I have none.”

Savka sniffed again, delicately, like a dog before a pantry.

“You’ve got rye bread. And onion.”

“Go away.”

“Where?”

“Anywhere. For Christ’s sake, don’t touch me.”

At this invocation Savka was offended.

“Why drag Christ into it? Did He give you the bread?”

The carpenter tried to pass around us. We moved—not deliberately, you understand, but as iron filings move when a magnet approaches. He turned with the revolver.

“I’ll fire!”

“Fire, then,” Ilya said from the darkness.

The carpenter started. He had not counted the voices accurately.

“Who’s that?”

“A student,” said Savka. “Harmless when fed.”

The man began to cough. It bent him double, though the pistol remained clutched in his hand. He coughed until his knees struck together and saliva hung from his beard. When he straightened, his eyes were full of water.

“I’m ill,” he said. “Ague. Three days. I’m going home.”

“Where’s home?” I asked.

“Ak-Mechet.”

“That’s far.”

“I know.”

“Wife?” Savka asked.

The man nodded.

“Children?”

“Two girls.”

“How old?”

“Six and nine.”

This domestic inventory should have humanized him. It did, to an extent. I pictured two fair-haired girls waiting by a window, and for perhaps half a second I was touched. Then the smell of bread came from his sack, warm though it could not have been warm, substantial, sacred. The imaginary daughters faded.

Savka licked his lips.

“We also have relatives,” he said.

The carpenter swayed. His eyes travelled over our faces, measuring. He knew. A respectable person imagines a robbery begins when the robber takes out a knife; in truth it begins when hunger looks at property and property looks back.

“Stay there,” he said.

He put the revolver between his knees, still aiming vaguely, and fumbled at the sack. From it he drew half a round loaf. The sight of it caused me physical pain. Its crust was dark and cracked, with flour in the creases. One end had been cut and showed the dense grey crumb.

The carpenter tore off a piece. His hands shook.

“Catch!”

Savka caught it.

We fell upon him—not upon the man, at first, but upon the bread in Savka’s hands. He ripped it with his fingers. I received a piece no bigger than two matchboxes; Ilya emerged and snatched another. We ate.

We did not chew properly. The bread scraped my dry mouth, lodged in my throat, descended with magnificent slowness, and struck the stomach like a spark in cold tinder. My eyes closed. Tears came into them—not moral tears, doctor; digestion is also capable of lyricism. Beside me Savka grunted. Ilya swallowed so quickly that his narrow throat jumped.

“More,” Savka said.

“That’s all I can give.”

“You have more.”

“My children—”

“You said you were going to them, not feeding them on the road.”

“It is for home.”

“There’s food at home.”

“There is nothing at home!”

The carpenter’s voice broke. The revolver shook in his fist.

We had each eaten perhaps two ounces. This did not pacify hunger. It awakened it. The little animal in the belly, which had lain exhausted, lifted its head and tore at the bars.

Savka stepped forward.

“Give us half.”

“No.”

“A third.”

“No!”

“Then a Christian share.”

“I have given you bread.”

“You threw us a dog’s piece.”

“I have girls waiting! My wife! I’ve been working at the salt sheds. They owe me money. I got only this, a little flour, two fish—”

“Fish?” Ilya said.

It was the first word he had spoken in several minutes. Softly spoken, but the carpenter heard and looked at him with hatred.

“I have no fish.”

“You just said two.”

“I’m fevered. I don’t know what I say.”

“You know where your pistol is.”

The man raised it. Savka stopped.

Now, had he fired into the air, we might have scattered. Had he shot Savka, we might even have become moral. But he was sick, frightened, attached to his life, and burdened by the thought of wife and daughters; consequently he did nothing useful. His finger trembled on the trigger. We saw that he did not wish to kill.

That knowledge killed his advantage.

“Listen, master,” I said reasonably. “You have food and we have none. You are one; we are three. Nature has made an error in distribution. We propose to correct it without legal formalities.”

“I earned it.”

“Undoubtedly. And we have earned hunger.”

“By idleness!”

Savka’s face changed.

“Idleness?”

“I know you. Tramps. Thieves.”

Savka’s fist closed around the nail.

“I served the Tsar seven years.”

“And now you rob sick men.”

This was accurate, but tactless.

Savka sprang.

The shot went off with a flat, enormous crack. In the open steppe it seemed to strike the sky. A spark leapt from the muzzle. Something hissed past my ear.

Then we were upon him.

I say “we” without false refinement. I caught his wrist. Savka struck him under the jaw. Ilya seized the sack. The carpenter kicked, twisted, cried out, and for an ill man displayed indecent strength. His feverish body was hot through the shirt. He smelt of sweat, dust, sawdust and fear. The revolver went off again into the ground. Savka roared a curse. I bent the wrist backward until the fingers opened, and the weapon dropped.

The carpenter fell. Savka put a knee on his chest.

“Don’t kill him,” I said.

Observe my humanity.

“Give me the pistol,” Savka panted.

I picked it up.

Ilya had already opened the sack. He drew out the half-loaf, two onions, two dried fish, a small cloth bag of flour and a lump of yellow sugar.

The treasure of emperors has caused less emotion.

Savka released the carpenter and lunged for the food. I thrust the revolver into my belt, where it burned against the skin, and took the loaf. For a moment all three of us snarled at one another.

“Equal,” said Ilya.

“You grabbed first,” Savka said.

“Equal.”

“I fought him.”

“So did we.”

“I could break you in two.”

“You could try.”

The carpenter rolled onto his side, coughing. A dark line ran from his lip into his beard. No one attended to him. Justice occupied us.

We divided the bread into three portions, using the carpenter’s knife. Savka demanded the largest because he was largest; Ilya maintained that equality concerned persons, not stomachs; I suggested that the cutter take the last piece, an ancient method whose wisdom becomes evident only when one holds the knife. We quarrelled. We measured. We shifted crumbs from one portion to another. The carpenter watched us on his elbow.

“For God’s sake,” he whispered. “Leave me some.”

We did not answer.

“Brothers, I gave you bread.”

Savka bit into an onion. Its sharp smell filled the night.

“Brothers!”

“Don’t whine,” he said with his mouth full. “A man should have courage.”

The carpenter stared at him. Something in his face—astonishment, perhaps—made me uncomfortable.

“We will leave you the flour,” I said.

Ilya looked at me.

“The flour is his,” I explained.

“All of it?”

“A little.”

“You cannot eat flour without water,” the carpenter said. “The well—where is the well?”

“Behind you.”

He crawled toward it.

We ate.

How shall I describe that supper? Words were invented by satisfied men. They have terms for sauces, vintages and shades of silk, but only the one blunt word for the resurrection of a starving body. We ate the bread. We tore the dried fish with our teeth, swallowed skin, bones and salt. We split the onions; their juice burned the cracks in our lips and ran down our wrists. Savka, having no knife, gnawed the sugar and passed it grudgingly. I held a yellow splinter on my tongue until it dissolved into light.

We ate.

The carpenter reached the trough and drank. His arms gave way once, and his face nearly fell into the muddy water. He returned crawling, collected the flour bag, and sat beyond the fire. The night had grown cold. He shivered so violently that his teeth knocked.

“Give me the pistol,” he said.

Savka laughed.

“To shoot us properly this time?”

“I must travel. There are wolves.”

“Then explain to the wolves about your daughters.”

The man bowed his head.

We ate until there was nothing. Not a crumb. Ilya licked the inside of the sack. Savka scraped scales from the cloth and put them into his mouth. I chewed an onion root, stringy and full of grit.

With food inside us, our tempers improved. Warmth spread through my limbs. The stars became beautiful again; the fire seemed companionable; even the carpenter ceased to be an enemy and resumed, in a limited way, his membership in mankind.

“What is your name?” I asked him.

“Yegor.”

“Patronymic?”

“Semyonovich.”

“A carpenter, I see.”

He glanced at the wrapped saw.

“I make doors. Frames. Coffins.”

Savka crossed himself.

“Don’t say that at night.”

“I make what they order.”

“Have you made many?”

“Many what?”

“Coffins, devil take you!”

“For children, mostly. Fever last spring.”

Savka threw a stalk into the fire.

“Shut your mouth.”

Yegor gave a strange little smile, not pleasant.

“You are afraid of words.”

“I fear God.”

“You robbed me.”

“God is not bread.”

There was much theology in that reply.

Yegor’s fever increased. He alternately hugged himself and loosened his shirt. Sweat ran down his chest, then he shivered, though the night was not yet truly cold. He spoke of Ak-Mechet, of a house roofed with reeds, of his wife Anna, of the girls, Masha and little Dunia. He had been away six weeks at the salt sheds repairing carts. The contractor had cheated him, the foreman had beaten him, and he had walked two days with the fever. This recital ought to have established a moral difference between him and us, but in fact it established kinship. He too had been cheated, beaten, unpaid and driven onto the road. His error consisted merely in possessing supper when we met him.

Misfortune is a strong cement, my brothers, but it sets badly. It binds men while the rain falls; at the first patch of sun they pry one another loose with knives.

“Come nearer the fire,” I said.

Yegor hesitated.

Savka shrugged.

“Let him. We’re not beasts.”

So Yegor came closer.

We arranged ourselves around the flame. Savka gave him a rag to wrap around his shoulders; it was the same rag I had found beside the well, and Yegor accepted it with gratitude. Such are the delicate compensations of life: first we stole his food, then we bestowed rubbish, and his thanks restored our self-respect.

He asked for the revolver again.

“No,” I said. “In the morning.”

“Swear.”

“I do not swear over weapons.”

“I bought it in Kerch. There are robbers on the road.”

Savka chuckled.

“You found them.”

Yegor looked at us one after another. When his gaze rested on Ilya, it stopped. Ilya sat opposite him, knees up, green eyes shining between the fire and darkness.

“You,” Yegor said.

“What?”

“You took something else.”

“No.”

“There was money in the sack.”

“There was flour.”

“In the lining. Two roubles.”

Ilya’s face did not alter.

“You’re fevered.”

“I sewed it there myself.”

“Search me.”

Yegor began to rise. Savka pushed him down, not violently.

“Enough.”

“Let me search him.”

“Lie still.”

“My children—”

“Your children again!” Savka exploded. “Have they no other occupation but to sit in your mouth? My mother had seven. Did she whimper over each kopek? A man loses money and at once makes orphans of the whole province.”

Yegor clenched his jaw. Ilya stared into the fire.

I had seen him put nothing into his clothes, but I had not been watching continuously. I suspected him. Yet suspicion among comrades is like a flea under a blanket: troublesome, certainly, but one does not throw away warmth on its account.

“Perhaps the lining tore,” I suggested.

“It did not.”

“You may search the sack at daylight.”

“The money was there.”

“Then it will remain there.”

Ilya smiled with one corner of his mouth.

“Unless money walks.”

Yegor cursed him. It was a weak, elaborate curse involving his birth, his soul, his learning and several diseases. Savka objected to one phrase on religious grounds. Their voices rose. I put a hand on the revolver and commanded peace, feeling for the first and perhaps last time the dignity of government.

“Sleep,” I said. “At dawn we settle everything.”

“Give me my weapon,” Yegor repeated.

“At dawn.”

He lay down, still muttering.

Savka stretched beside the fire and began to sing. He always sang when fed, and because the army had taught him only marching songs and funeral responses, his gratitude sounded either murderous or ecclesiastical. That night he chose a hymn. His bass wandered over the steppe:

“Rest with the saints, O Christ, Thy servant—”

“Stop it!” Yegor cried.

Savka raised himself.

“I’m singing for my soul.”

“Sing something else.”

“I know nothing else.”

“Then have no soul.”

Savka considered striking him, then laughed. A minute later he was asleep.

Ilya sat awake. He was mending his sacking sandal with a piece of wire, bending close to the fire. His thin fingers worked patiently. Yegor watched them.

“You really a student?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Of what?”

“Men.”

“What have you learned?”

“That they ask too many questions.”

Yegor turned away.

The fire sank. We had little fuel left, and no one wished to walk into darkness for more. The stars seemed lower, sharper; the Milky Way crossed the sky like flour spilt by some prodigal housewife. In the grass, unseen creatures clicked and whispered. Once a bird cried with a human note, and Yegor jerked upright.

“Dunia?”

“It’s a bird,” I said.

He groaned and lay down.

I ought to tell you that I was troubled. Not by the robbery—let us preserve proportion—but by the man’s fever. His breathing rasped. Sometimes he muttered to his wife, apologizing for the flour; sometimes he begged an invisible foreman not to strike his hands because they were a carpenter’s livelihood. Once he said clearly: “Masha, shut the door. There are men outside.”

This pricked me.

I took off my coat, which was more hole than cloth, and spread it over him.

“You’ll freeze,” he murmured.

“I am accustomed to it.”

“Thank you, brother.”

That word, after what we had done, should perhaps have burned me. It did not. On the contrary, I felt noble. Man is an ingenious bookkeeper: one coat lent may be entered against one loaf stolen, two fish consumed, a wrist twisted, a life threatened—and the account declared balanced.

I lay near Savka. The revolver remained in my belt. I intended to keep watch, but warmth, food and exhaustion argued otherwise. My eyes closed.

Before sleep took me, I saw Ilya still sitting upright.

“Sleep,” I told him.

“Soon.”

“What are you thinking?”

“Nothing.”

“That is difficult.”

“Not for everyone.”

He looked across the fire at Yegor.

I remember this distinctly. I do not embellish it, nor will I claim that his gaze warned me. At the time it seemed merely wakefulness. Afterward every small thing became a prophecy.

The stars blurred. The steppe tilted. I slept.

---

Morning on the steppe is shameless.

In a town, murder may hide under roofs, behind shutters, in alleys where dawn comes grey and exhausted. But there the sun rose clean out of the earth, enormous and scarlet; the eastern sky turned gold, the grasses shone with dew, larks climbed singing into the blue, and every stone, stalk and spider-thread stood revealed with intolerable innocence. It was a morning fit for the first day of creation.

Savka woke me by pulling at my shirt.

“Get up.”

His voice was low.

I opened my eyes. The fire was ash. My coat lay on the ground. Ilya was gone.

At first this meant nothing. Men step aside in the morning. Then I saw that the sack was gone, the flour was gone, and my belt was empty.

“The pistol,” I said.

Savka nodded.

“Student?”

“Who else?”

We both turned toward Yegor.

He lay on his back three paces from the ashes. One arm was bent under him, the other extended with the palm upward. His cap had fallen off. In the brilliant light his face appeared extraordinarily calm, almost polished, except that the tongue protruded a little between the teeth.

Around his neck was a dark, narrow mark.

For a while neither Savka nor I moved.

A lark sang above us with furious happiness.

“Yegor,” I said.

There was no answer.

Savka went to him, squatted, and touched the chest. Then he snatched his hand back.

“Cold?”

“No. Warm.”

“Alive?”

Savka bent his ear to the mouth. His red beard brushed the dead man’s cheek.

“No.”

He looked at me. I looked at him.

“Strangled,” he whispered.

The word seemed absurd under that sky.

There was wire near Yegor’s shoulder, twisted into a short loop. The same wire, I think, with which Ilya had mended his sandal. The rag had been pulled from Yegor’s back. His shirt was torn open, and the lining of the sack—or perhaps a strip cut from it—lay beside him. His pockets had been turned inside out. Even one boot had been removed and then discarded; apparently it did not fit the ancient Greek.

Savka stood.

“I’ll kill him.”

“He has the revolver.”

“I’ll kill him with my hands.”

“First catch him.”

We searched the ground. Ilya’s tracks ran south from the well, sharp in the damp dust. One footprint was from the boot, the other a confused lattice from the sandal. After a hundred paces they joined the road and became difficult to distinguish.

Savka began walking fast.

I caught his arm.

“Where?”

“After him.”

“He has been gone hours.”

“I’ll find him.”

“And when you do?”

“I’ll squeeze his student throat until learning comes out.”

“Suppose the police find us first.”

He stopped.

That was the word which restored reason.

Police.

There lay a dead carpenter. We had his blood on Savka’s cuff from the blow; my coat had covered him; our footprints circled the corpse; we had taken his food, seized his pistol, and passed the night at his fire. The actual murderer had vanished with the weapon and money. We remained: a discharged soldier with no destination, and a man whose occupation was difficult to state politely.

A magistrate might have found our narrative diverting.

Savka stared toward the south. His fists opened and closed.

“We didn’t do it,” he said.

“An excellent argument. Courts are built to honour excellence.”

“You saw me sleeping.”

“I did.”

“And I saw you.”

“Did you?”

He turned sharply.

“What do you mean?”

“Nothing. Did you see me all night?”

Savka’s face darkened.

“You think I—”

“I think no such thing. I am illustrating the weakness of testimony.”

“You were beside me.”

“And Yegor was beside us.”

Savka crossed himself and glanced at the corpse.

The dead man’s open hand gleamed in the sun. Ants already moved near his wrist, busy and without legal prejudice.

“We ought to bury him,” Savka said.

“With what?”

“The saw.”

“To dig?”

He looked around. The earth was hard and stony. We had no shovel. A grave deep enough to hide a man would have required half a day, perhaps more, and every minute increased the chance of wagon, shepherd, patrol or curious peasant.

“We can drag him from the road,” I said.

“Where?”

“There’s a hollow beyond the trough.”

“That isn’t burial.”

“No. It is geography.”

Savka swore at me, at Ilya, at the steppe, at Yegor for having died, at God for permitting educated men to mend sandals with lethal wire. Then he began to weep.

Do not misunderstand him. Savka could break ribs without anger and steal with an easy heart; but death touched his superstition, and betrayal touched his sentiment. He had called Ilya comrade. This, more than the murder, wounded him.

“Little snake,” he sobbed. “We shared bread. I gave him the bigger piece of fish.”

“You did not.”

“I meant to.”

He knelt beside Yegor and pulled the tongue back into the mouth. It slipped out again. Savka tried once more, then covered the face with my coat.

“Leave that,” I said.

“It’s yours.”

“Exactly.”

He looked at me with disgust but removed it.

We dragged Yegor by the arms. His heels left two lines in the dust. The body was not heavy; he had been almost as starved as we. Yet dead weight has a peculiar obstinacy. The head bumped over stones. Once the cloth around the saw caught on a root, and Savka shouted at Yegor as if the man were inconveniencing us deliberately.

In the hollow grew tall wormwood. We laid him there.

His pale hand remained visible. Savka covered it with earth and dry grass. I found the cap and placed it over the face. That was our funeral office.

Savka stood at the edge of the hollow.

“We should say something.”

“Say it.”

He removed his cap. His lips moved, but he could remember only fragments: “With the saints—where there is no sickness—nor sorrow—nor sighing—”

He stopped.

“There was sickness,” he said. “There was sorrow. And sighing enough.”

“Those are said to be absent afterward.”

“How do you know?”

“I don’t.”

He replaced his cap.

We searched once more for anything useful, because reverence does not abolish necessity. The saw was good but too conspicuous. We left it. In Yegor’s discarded boot Savka found a copper coin. He looked at it, hesitated, and put it in his pocket.

“For candles,” he explained.

“Certainly.”

We drank from the well.

The water tasted of mud and iron. Yegor’s flour had spilled beside the trough, a white patch trodden into the dirt. Savka scraped some into his palm, mixed it with water and swallowed the paste. I did the same. We neither spoke nor looked at one another.

Then we went south.

We went quickly at first, expecting every sound to be a pursuit. A cart creaked behind us and we hid in the grass until it passed. Two peasants rode by on horses; Savka crossed himself so violently that one of them laughed. At noon we saw three black birds circling far back over the well.

“Crows,” Savka said.

“Perhaps.”

“Over him.”

“Perhaps.”

“He had daughters.”

“Yes.”

“Shut up.”

It was he who had mentioned them.

Further on, the heat returned. The road burned beneath our feet. Hunger, temporarily silenced, began its patient work again. By afternoon Yegor’s death had become less immediate than the blisters on my heels. This may shock you, doctor, but pain has its hierarchy, and the body is a despot who issues decrees every minute. A corpse two leagues behind cannot compete forever with a stone in one’s boot.

Savka continued to look for Ilya’s tracks. Sometimes he would point to a mark and say, “There!” Then the mark proved to be from a peasant boy or a woman’s shoe or a dog. Once we saw a solitary figure far ahead and ran until our hearts hammered; it was an old beggar carrying reeds. Savka asked whether he had passed a young man, thin, one boot, one sack sandal.

“Yesterday,” said the beggar.

“Which way?”

“North.”

“That’s impossible.”

The beggar shrugged.

“Then south.”

Savka threatened him. The beggar raised his reeds like a spear and cursed us with admirable invention. We went on.

Toward evening Savka said, “You knew.”

“Knew what?”

“That he would do it.”

“No.”

“You saw him looking.”

“So did you.”

“I slept.”

“So did I.”

“You’re clever.”

“I have always tried not to be punished for that.”

He walked in silence.

“Maybe you helped him,” he said at last.

I laughed. Not because it was amusing, but because if I had struck him we should have become two corpses or one.

“Then why am I here without the money?”

“To fool me.”

“My dear Savka, you do not possess enough to reward the labour.”

He gripped my shoulder.

“Swear by God.”

“I never call God as witness where a former soldier’s intelligence is concerned.”

He struck me. Not hard; a flat blow across the mouth. Blood came from my split lip.

I picked up a stone.

For a moment we stood in the road, he with his fists, I with the stone, both starving again, both ready to divide the remaining universe by violence. Above us the evening clouds were rose-coloured; a flock of cranes passed high in the west, calling to one another. Their formation was exact, their purpose common, their direction known. It was an offensive spectacle.

Then Savka lowered his hands.

“Go on,” he said.

I dropped the stone.

We went.

That night we slept twenty paces apart.

On the following morning he gave me half the copper coin to buy water from a shepherd. There was no water for sale, but the gesture restored comradeship. By noon we were again calling each other brother. Three days later, near Dzhankoy, Savka stole eggs from a cart and was caught. He knocked down the driver; I knocked down the driver’s son; we escaped with six eggs, of which two were broken in my pocket. We sucked them raw. We laughed. Thus the progress in morals continued.

We never saw Ilya again.

Years afterward, in Odessa, I met a clerk who resembled him: narrow face, pressed lips, greenish eyes. The man wore spectacles and a respectable coat, and was copying petitions in a lawyer’s office. I followed him for an hour. At last he turned and asked what I wanted.

“Did you once study law in Kharkov?” I said.

“No.”

“Did you ever possess only one boot?”

He called a policeman.

It may have been Ilya. It may have been another educated man. Nature repeats those faces whenever she requires a knife with a handle.

As for Savka, he died in a prison infirmary, I heard, after striking a warder with a soup bowl. Or perhaps he went home to his mother and became a village watchman. Both endings are equally suitable, and in our class of society suitable endings are more common than verified ones.

Yegor? His wife waited, I suppose. For a day, then two, then a week. Perhaps someone found the body. Perhaps the crows completed the investigation. Perhaps Masha and Dunia grew up and were told that their father had deserted them. That would have been respectable society’s final contribution: first to cheat him of wages, then to leave him to robbers, then to slander his memory.

Yet do not think I accuse society in order to acquit myself. I am more subtle than that.

We took his bread. Yes.

We seized his weapon. Yes.

We listened while he spoke of his children and ate the food intended for them. Yes.

Then we let him warm himself at our fire, covered him with my coat, and went to sleep. Ilya strangled him. Or perhaps—I see the doubt in your young medical eyes—Savka did. Or I did, and have constructed Ilya from moonlight, wire and a missing revolver. Memory is a witness easily bribed.

But suppose the account is true. Where does guilt begin?

With Ilya’s hands?

With ours, when we rushed Yegor?

With the contractor who withheld his wages?

With the baker who sold clay as bread?

With the police who drove us from Perekop?

With hunger, which has no hands but borrows ours?

At what point, doctor, does a man become a beast? When he kills? When he steals? When he smells another man’s bread and thinks first not of daughters but of crust? Or when, afterward, lying comfortably in a hospital hammock, he arranges the whole affair into philosophy?

I opened my eyes.

The young doctor was standing where he had stood at the beginning, but his cigarette had gone out. The sea beyond the veranda had vanished into darkness. Lamps shone along the quay, small and orderly. Under us the sick man coughed again.

“You say none of you was guilty,” the doctor said.

“I say hunger is a large accomplice.”

“That does not absolve you.”

“Did I request absolution?”

“You could have saved him.”

“Possibly.”

“You could have kept watch.”

“Possibly.”

“You could have returned and told the police.”

“Most certainly. They would have thanked me with irons.”

“You are excusing cowardice.”

“My dear doctor, names are the luxury of those who have eaten dinner. Cowardice, prudence, theft, redistribution, murder, necessity—the belly has a poor vocabulary. It says only: live.”

“And conscience?”

“Conscience speaks afterward, when the belly permits conversation.”

He leaned upon the railing.

“You feel remorse.”

It was not a question. Young men are very positive where the souls of others are concerned.

I considered lying to please him. A tear, a tremor, a hand over the eyes—these are small fees for the respect of the humane. But the night was pleasant, and I was tired.

“I remember,” I said. “That is not the same thing.”

He looked at me with aversion.

This pleased me more than it should have.

“The carpenter was a victim,” he said. “You were robbers. The student was a murderer. There is a difference.”

“There was a difference in degree. Not in kidney.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means we were all fashioned from the same poor meat, frightened by the same darkness, driven by the same whip. Yegor would have shot us to keep his bread. We would have killed him to take it. Ilya merely arrived first at the conclusion toward which the rest of us were crawling.”

“No,” said the doctor sharply. “That is false.”

“Peradventure.”

He went inside.

The hammock moved under me, to and fro, to and fro. From the ward came the clean smell of carbolic acid and linen. Somewhere a spoon struck a cup. Beyond the town, beyond the lamps and roofs, the same ancient stars were opening over the sea—the bright, indifferent flowers that had shone upon Yegor’s dead face, upon Ilya’s flight, upon Savka’s tears, upon my prudent departure.

I watched them through the railings.

We were beasts, all of us; beasts of one litter, though some had collars and some sores, some held revolvers and some medical diplomas. Misfortune had starved us, fear had taught us, and the road had stripped away those little embroidered garments which men call honour, faith and law. Perhaps beneath them there had once been souls. I will not dispute it. A naked man is not always handsome, but at least he is difficult to deceive.

And yet—since you insist upon morality—when I remember that morning, it is not Ilya whom I see first, nor Savka, nor even Yegor with the wire about his neck.

I see the open hand in the sunlight.

Empty.

Still asking.

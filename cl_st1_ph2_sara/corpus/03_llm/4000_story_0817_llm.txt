The house of Madame Carambeau stood at a dignified remove from the street, behind an iron fence which terminated in spear-points and which had discouraged, for more than half a century, beggars, vendors, dogs, and all strangers not provided with a proper introduction. It was a broad Spanish house, yellowed by heat and age, with a deep gallery running around it and green shutters that excluded the afternoon sun. Beyond the courtyard lay a garden where orange trees, myrtles and great glossy-leaved plants flourished in a rich disorder which Madame called arrangement.

From the upper gallery one could see the levee, the brown Mississippi moving with immense indifference, and, farther off, the newer quarter of the city spreading itself in lawns, pale villas and insolent fresh paint.

Madame did not approve of that quarter.

She did not approve of its architecture, its language, its people, its manner of cooking rice, its habit of opening windows at improper hours, nor its women, who wore hats in the morning and spoke to men with a freedom which Madame regarded as a national infirmity. Americans, she declared, had no repose, no traditions, no sauces and no respect for their elders. They were noisy in pleasure, abrupt in grief, careless in religion and capable of drinking coffee from large cups.

Her dislike had not softened with age. It had become compact, polished and serviceable, like the black mahogany cane that rested beside her chair.

Madame Carambeau was seventy-eight. Her figure was small and erect; her hair, beneath its cap of fine lace, retained a surprising abundance of iron-gray; and her eyes, cold, dark and observant, could compel a servant to remember what had not been said. She was imperious with the healthy and tender with the sick. She could reduce her daughter Cécile to tears by remarking that a custard was without character; yet she had sat three nights beside an old cook whose lungs were failing, and had fed him broth from a silver spoon with her own hand.

She had been estranged from her son Henri for ten years.

The cause was simple. Henri had married an American woman.

He had not, Madame admitted, committed the offense in ignorance. He knew his mother’s opinions. He knew the history of the Carambeaus, the value of a name, the obligations of blood and the lamentable consequences of rashness. Nevertheless, he had married Miss Mary Harding of Ohio, whose father manufactured something in Cincinnati and whose mother was said to have addressed Madame, in a letter, as “Dear Mrs. Carambo.”

There are wounds which nature may heal, and there are wounds inflicted by misspelling.

Henri had come once to plead with her.

“Ma mère,” he had said, “you condemn her without knowing her.”

“I know she is American.”

“She is my wife.”

“That is your misfortune, mon fils.”

“And if I have children?”

Madame’s face had not altered.

“They also,” she replied, “will be American.”

He had gone away then, and she had not seen him since. She knew that he lived in the expanding quarter beyond Canal Street, in a house with a lawn—an exposed and barbarous arrangement—but she never passed that way. If his name was spoken, she became occupied with her embroidery. If Cécile began, “Henri wrote—” Madame answered, “Plaît-il?” in a tone which made further intelligence impossible.

It was therefore unfortunate that Gustave’s birthday occurred in December.

Gustave was Cécile’s youngest boy, and Cécile, who possessed a weak chin, a warm heart and a terror of displeasing everybody, had invited twenty children to the house when Madame had distinctly understood that there would be twelve.

“They are little children, maman,” Cécile explained.

“They are twenty little children.”

“Some may not come.”

“They will all come. Children accept what their parents refuse.”

They did come. They arrived in carriages, in buggies, under the guidance of nurses and breathless mothers. They invaded the courtyard, filled the gallery and converted the grave old rooms into regions of shrill pursuit. There were paper caps, sugared fruits, tin trumpets and a cake iced to resemble a fortress. A small boy broke the arm of a porcelain shepherdess. Another attempted to discover whether goldfish could live in lemonade.

Madame endured eleven minutes.

Then she rose, took her cane, and withdrew to her own chamber at the end of the gallery, where the shutters were half closed and the white curtains moved gently in air perfumed by orange flowers. Her broad chair stood near the window. On a little table were her spectacles, a vial of smelling salts, a prayer book and a bowl of water in which floated three magnolia leaves.

Here the noise came softened by distance. It was still detestable, but no longer personal.

Madame had begun to read when the door opened.

A little girl stood upon the threshold.

She was perhaps six years old, slight, fair-haired and dressed in white, with a blue sash slipping from her waist. One stocking had fallen about her ankle. Her cheeks were brilliantly flushed, and her eyes possessed that bright, unnatural clearness which Madame recognized at once.

The child looked at her without fear.

“You are the old lady,” she said.

Madame lowered her book.

“I am Madame Carambeau.”

“I am tired.”

“Then return to your nurse.”

“I do not know where she is.”

“That is characteristic,” observed Madame, though of whom it was characteristic she did not specify.

The child crossed the room, swaying a little. Before Madame could call, protest or ring the bell, the small creature laid one burning hand upon her black silk skirt, climbed into her lap and rested her head against the lace fichu at her breast.

Madame became very still.

The child gave a faint sigh. Her body trembled, then relaxed with complete confidence against a person who had spent ten years proving herself inaccessible.

“Cécile!” called Madame sharply.

No one heard. A drum was being beaten in the courtyard.

She touched the child’s forehead, lifted one eyelid, felt the quick pulse at the throat and rang with violence. Zélie, the housemaid, appeared.

“Send for Doctor Lemaire. Tell Cécile to stop that infernal music. Bring fresh linen, warm water and the small bottle from the locked cabinet—not the large one, imbécile. And find this child’s nurse.”

Within a quarter of an hour the birthday fortress was abandoned, the drum silenced and the guests hurried away with favors clutched in their hands. No one knew precisely to whom the little girl belonged. She had entered with a group of children from a neighboring house; one nurse thought she belonged to another nurse, who had departed under the same impression. The child herself could give no useful account. When asked her name, she whispered something that sounded like “Loulou,” then turned her face into Madame’s shoulder.

Doctor Lemaire came, rubbed his glasses, pronounced the fever serious but not alarming, and approved every measure Madame had already taken.

“She must be kept quiet,” he said.

“She shall be.”

“And watched through the night.”

“I had not intended to leave her in the street, Doctor.”

He bowed. He had known Madame for forty years and was not sensitive.

They placed the child in the great curtained bed, between cool linen sheets smelling faintly of lavender. Madame undressed her, folded the small garments and noted, with disapproval, that the nightdress supplied by Cécile was trimmed too elaborately for illness. She bathed the hot face with orange-flower water, measured drops of medicine, cooled the child’s wrists and sat beside her with a palm-leaf fan.

At midnight the fever rose.

The little girl tossed and spoke in broken phrases of candles, a red ball, a hymn and a father who had promised to return before Christmas. Once she cried out for her mother. Madame’s hands paused; then she bent and gathered the child close.

“Hush, petite. I am here.”

“You won’t go?”

“No.”

“Promise?”

“I promise.”

The child’s fingers closed around hers.

Madame kept the promise. Through the long hours she remained beside the bed, commanding silence from the household and obedience from the fever. Her sternness found proper employment in illness; it opposed disorder, waste and danger with equal vigor. When the medicine was due, the child received it. When the linen grew damp, it was changed. When Cécile suggested that maman should rest, Madame looked at her until Cécile withdrew.

Toward dawn the child slept. The gray light revealed Madame’s face, weary and composed, and the little hand still clasping one of her fingers.

The next day brought rain. It fell steadily upon the gallery roof and blurred the garden into masses of green and silver. The search for the child’s family continued, but confusion multiplied. One servant carried a message to the wrong street; another returned with the intelligence that a family named Brown had lost a dog. Madame declared that modern civilization had abolished both memory and responsibility.

Yet she did not seem impatient for the mystery to end.

The child woke near noon and asked for water. Her fever had lessened. She studied Madame solemnly.

“You look cross,” she said.

“I am not cross.”

“Papa looks cross when he is sorry.”

“I am not sorry.”

The child considered this.

“Do you speak French?”

“Naturally.”

“I know bonjour.”

“That is a beginning.”

“And voulez-vous.”

“A doubtful continuation.”

“Papa speaks French. Mamma does not, except when she sings.”

Madame stiffened slightly, but the child had already reached for the lace at her wrist.

“Is this yours?”

“It was my mother’s.”

“Did she love you?”

Madame looked toward the rain-darkened garden.

“Yes.”

“I love you,” said the child, and closed her eyes.

The declaration entered Madame’s guarded heart without permission. It did not knock, present credentials or state its ancestry. It entered like a seed borne over a wall, lodging in a narrow fissure where no gardener had intended growth.

For two days she nursed the child. During that time something began, quietly and against all regulation, to put forth roots.

At first there was only doubt, a pale shoot barely distinguishable from weakness. Could an American child be gentle? Could she submit to medicine without uproar, say her prayers without ostentation and prefer broth to cake? From doubt grew mistrust—not of the child, but of certain conclusions Madame had kept dusted and arranged for many years. Mistrust thickened into inquiry; inquiry unfolded, leaf by leaf, toward truth.

Madame resisted the process by correcting the child’s pronunciation.

On the second evening the fever broke. The little girl woke hungry and demanded toast. Madame allowed half a slice.

“Tomorrow,” the child said, “I must sing at church.”

“You will do nothing of the kind.”

“It is Christmas morning.”

“You will remain in bed.”

“But Mamma will be frightened.”

That fear, at least, was soon relieved. Near sunset a distraught nurse arrived in a hired carriage, having traced the child through three households and a confectioner’s shop. She was red-eyed, incoherent and so overcome by Madame’s manner that she supplied explanations without identities. The child’s parents, she said, had been searching everywhere; the father had been called out of the city the day before; the mother was nearly ill with terror.

Madame would not permit the child to be moved until Doctor Lemaire returned. When he pronounced it safe, she wrapped her patient in a wool cloak and carried her herself to the carriage.

The little girl clung to her.

“Come see me,” she whispered.

“Where?”

But the nurse, fluttering with blankets and apologies, called an address which was lost beneath the wheels, rain and her own lamentations.

“I shall find you,” Madame said.

The child kissed her cheek.

That night Madame slept badly. The room seemed too large, the silence empty. On the pillow lay one yellow hair, fine as silk, which she discovered, regarded severely and placed inside her prayer book.

On Christmas morning the household assembled early. Cécile found her mother dressed in black satin, with her best lace collar and a bonnet ornamented by a modest spray of violets.

“You are going out, maman?”

“I am.”

“To mass?”

“No.”

Cécile waited.

“I am going to an American church.”

Cécile sat down.

Madame’s coachman, when informed, turned completely around upon the box.

“To Saint Luke’s,” she repeated.

He drove as one who had lost confidence in landmarks.

Saint Luke’s stood among open lawns and rose beds washed clean by the rain. Its doors were garlanded with cedar. Madame entered beneath the curious gaze of people who made room for her with a politeness she had not expected and therefore found faintly irritating.

The service was simple. Children sang. Madame searched their faces, but the fair-haired little girl was not among them. She heard words concerning peace, forgiveness and the coming of light into closed places. The minister spoke without elegance, but not without sense.

When the congregation rose, Madame remained seated for a moment.

Then she ordered the coachman to drive to Henri’s house.

She knew the address. She had always known it.

The house offended fewer principles than she had anticipated. It was pale, yes, and stood upon an open lawn, but roses bloomed along the gallery and the windows were shaded gracefully. Before the carriage had quite stopped, Henri appeared at the door.

For ten years Madame had prepared many speeches for this meeting. None was available.

Henri descended the steps slowly. He was older, broader, and there was gray at his temples. Yet he was still the boy who had come home wet from the river, the young man who had danced beneath the orange trees, her son.

“Ma mère?” he said.

She extended both hands.

“Henri.”

He took them, and whatever he had meant to say was lost. He bent his head over them.

A woman came from the house—a fair, gentle-faced woman with anxious eyes. Behind her darted a little figure in white.

The child stopped. Madame stopped.

Then the little girl cried, “My old lady!” and flew down the steps.

Madame caught her.

Henri stared from his daughter to his mother. His wife covered her mouth with one hand.

“This is your child?” Madame demanded.

“My daughter Louise,” Henri answered.

The child’s arms tightened about her neck.

The seed had flowered. It flowered all at once, beyond caution, beyond argument, opening through every hard conclusion she had cultivated; and Madame, who had feared that truth would diminish her, discovered that it left room in the heart by breaking its walls.

She kissed Louise, then looked at Mary.

“You are my son’s wife.”

“Yes, Madame.”

“You have suffered much anxiety.”

Mary’s eyes filled.

“I owe you my child’s life.”

“No. You owe me nothing. Henri owes me ten years.”

Henri laughed unsteadily.

“You sent me away, ma mère.”

“Because you were stubborn. You have always been stubborn.”

He kissed her.

They returned together to the old house for Christmas dinner. Cécile wept, Gustave shouted, the servants hurried to add places, and the mahogany table lengthened to receive what pride had so long excluded.

Louise sat beside Madame and demanded another spoonful of sauce.

“You must learn French,” Madame told her.

“Will you teach me?”

“Certainly.”

“And I shall teach you English.”

Henri looked down to hide a smile.

Madame observed him.

“You find that amusing?”

“Not at all, ma mère.”

“It is well. The child must have both languages and both families. I have never believed in prejudice.”

Henri raised his glass.

“No, ma mère,” he said gravely. “Never.”

Outside, the river moved beneath the winter sun; the garden leaves trembled; and through the opened doors came the voices of children, the clatter of dishes and the old familiar sounds of a house no longer closed against the world.

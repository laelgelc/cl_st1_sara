Prince Padema’s Descent

Prince Padema maintained that fortune had used him shamefully.

This was true, if it may be called ill fortune to thrust a dagger into the wrong gentleman. The Prince had intended only to chastise a young musician of low degree who had grown insolent in the matter of a certain lady. Unfortunately, on a wet Venetian night, beneath an arcade where masks, cloaks and shadows rendered all men cousins, he had struck instead the nephew of the Doge.

The nephew died with admirable promptitude. Padema’s intended victim escaped with equal dispatch, and the lady, who had been the cause of the disagreement, retired to a convent and told everything she knew.

Thus the Prince found it prudent to leave Venice.

His Highness was not without friends. Aristocratic friendship, when properly nourished, will survive many offences, though it seldom survives a request for money. A Florentine cousin lent him a deserted palace overlooking a narrow street near Santa Croce; another nobleman supplied letters bearing a false name; and Pietro, his valet, obtained two horses, three cloaks and sufficient gold for a prolonged sojourn.

Before dawn the Prince had exchanged the glimmering canals of Venice for the dusty road south.

“I have been treated abominably,” he said as the walls of Florence rose before them.

Pietro, who had helped clean the dagger, made no answer.

Padema’s quarters occupied the uppermost floor of the palace. The rooms were spacious, neglected and furnished with those vast chairs in which dead ancestors seem to continue sitting invisibly. A cracked Madonna watched from a niche. The faded curtains smelled of incense and rain. Outside, an iron balcony clung to the wall like an eagle’s nest, so high above the paving stones that Pietro crossed himself when first he looked down.

The Prince declared the place a prison.

He was accustomed to marble halls, illuminated boats, hidden gardens, gaming tables and women who concealed their faces only in order to reveal them more effectively. Florence appeared to him severe. Its walls were too solid, its streets too straight, and even its bells seemed to reprove him.

More grievous still, his departure had interrupted an absorbing amour.

It was not the first such interruption Padema had suffered, nor would it have been the last had his habits permitted longevity. For three days he was inconsolable. On the fourth he observed the woman on the balcony beneath his own.

The lower balcony projected from a modest house adjoining the palace. It was enclosed by ironwork as delicate as black lace. A pot of white roses stood beside the railing, and above the door burned a little votive lamp whose flame trembled even when the evening air was still.

The woman came out at sunset.

Padema saw first the whiteness of her hands. Then she raised her face, and the Prince, leaning over his railing, forgot Venice, the murdered nobleman and the injustice of fate.

She was young, though not girlish; pale, though not frail. Her hair was black and gathered simply at the nape, and her eyes, when she glanced upward, seemed darker than the street below. She wore no jewels. Her gown was of grey silk, severe in cut, yet the very absence of ornament appeared to Padema a refinement prepared especially for his taste.

She started when she perceived him and withdrew at once.

This retreat convinced him that she had been struck by his appearance.

“Pietro,” he said, “who lives below us?”

“A Venetian lady, Highness.”

“Married?”

“I do not know.”

“You should.”

Within two hours Pietro knew that she called herself Madonna Livia, that she had arrived six months before, that she lived with an elderly servant named Pepita, and that no husband had been observed. She received few visitors and went out only to Mass.

“A widow, then,” said Padema.

“Possibly.”

“An exile?”

“So the fruit-seller believes.”

Padema smiled. Exile was a bond more intimate than kinship. Two Venetians in Florence were already halfway to an understanding.

The next evening he waited upon his balcony. The woman appeared somewhat later, carrying a book. At sight of him she hesitated.

“Madonna,” he said softly, “must two children of Venice behave like strangers beneath a Florentine sky?”

She lowered her eyes.

“I do not know you, signore.”

“That is a misfortune easily repaired.”

“For some misfortunes,” she replied, “repair arrives too late.”

She went indoors.

The Prince was delighted. Beauty without resistance is a meal without wine, and Padema preferred his pleasures seasoned with difficulty. Her melancholy phrase suggested a bereavement; her lowered eyes suggested innocence; her return the following evening suggested everything else.

Evening after evening they spoke across the space between their balconies. Padema gave the false name from his letters but did not conceal his rank for long. A prince may disguise his face more readily than his title, which he carries about him like perfume.

She told him little. Venice had become painful to her. She had lost one whom she loved. She wished to live quietly. The world frightened her.

Padema found these confessions enchanting. He spoke of destiny, solitude and the strange cruelty which drives two wounded hearts from the same beloved city only that they may meet beneath a foreign moon. It was a speech he had employed before, with alterations suitable to weather and geography.

Sometimes she listened from behind the white roses. Sometimes she trembled and bade him cease. Once she wept.

The tears persuaded him that victory was near.

There remained, however, the practical difficulty of access. The street door of her house was watched by a porter who slept little and gossiped much. Padema could not risk discovery in Florence; the agents of Venice had long arms, and the dead nephew of a Doge possessed relatives of extraordinary vigilance.

“Let me come to you,” he whispered one evening.

“It is impossible.”

“No door is impossible.”

“This one is. Pepita alone knows that I speak with you. If the porter saw—if anyone learned—”

“No one shall learn.”

She pressed her hand to her breast.

“It cannot be.”

The Prince had often noticed that women repeat impossibility when they wish a man to exhibit ingenuity.

“There is the balcony,” he said.

She looked up sharply. Between his railing and hers was a vertical distance of some twenty feet; beneath both yawned another seventy feet of darkness and stone.

“You would climb down?”

“For true love, I would descend from heaven.”

In his heart he considered this declaration somewhat excessive, but her breath caught so prettily that he forgave himself.

“You would be killed.”

“Not with a stout rope.”

“No. I will not hear it.”

She vanished, leaving the roses stirring in the dusk.

On the following night she did not appear. Nor on the next.

Padema passed these evenings in a state of irritation which he termed anguish. On the third, the votive lamp burned late, and at length the woman came forth with a veil over her hair.

“You have made me very unhappy,” she said.

“Then let me devote my life to repairing the wrong.”

“You do not know what you ask.”

“I ask nothing but to kneel at your feet.”

He had no intention of remaining there long, but tactical humility is the ladder of desire.

She turned away. Her shoulders moved.

“Do not weep,” Padema murmured.

“I am afraid.”

“Of me?”

“Of myself.”

This answer was satisfactory.

At last she explained her design. On the next evening Pepita would send the porter to carry a message across the city. At midnight the house would be quiet. Padema must lower himself toward her balcony by a rope fastened to his own railing. When he came within reach, she and Pepita would draw him inward. Before dawn he must return in the same fashion.

“A rope,” she repeated, looking down into the street. “But it must be very strong.”

“It shall hold ten men.”

“Fasten it securely.”

“To the staunchest railing.”

“And bring no servant into the secret.”

Padema agreed, though he intended to use Pietro for the unromantic portions of the enterprise. Princes readily promise secrecy on behalf of those who serve them.

Pietro procured a coil of silk-bound hemp from a dealer near the Mercato Vecchio. The valet examined the knots, tested the fibres and carried it upstairs beneath his cloak.

“It will hold ten men,” he reported.

“So I said.”

“Highness, the height is considerable.”

“My dear Pietro, I have descended by ropes before.”

This boast referred to an adventure in Padua involving a senator’s wife, two mastiffs and a burning stable. Padema suppressed the details, for a servant need not be encouraged to measure his master’s experience too accurately.

At eleven on the appointed night, clouds concealed the moon. The narrow street below lay empty except for a shrine lamp glowing red in a wall and a cat that passed noiselessly from one doorway to another. Florence seemed carved from black stone.

Padema dressed in dark clothes, removed his rings and buckled a small dagger beneath his doublet. It was not the dagger he had used in Venice. That instrument, through no fault of its own, had become too conspicuous.

Pietro secured the rope to the balcony railing and pulled upon it with all his strength.

“It is safe,” he said.

“Go to bed.”

“Shall I not wait?”

“No.”

Pietro glanced over the railing. “If your Highness slipped—”

“I do not slip.”

The valet bowed and withdrew. His obedience, like most obedience, was composed partly of discipline and partly of a desire not to be responsible.

At midnight the door beneath the lower votive lamp opened, and the woman stepped upon her balcony. Pepita stood behind her, small and bent, holding a candle shaded by one hand.

“All is quiet,” the woman whispered.

Padema raised the coil.

“I shall lower the loose end. When it reaches you, hold it away from the wall. Otherwise I may strike the stones.”

She looked upward. Her face was a pale oval in the darkness.

“I am trembling.”

“With happiness?”

“With anticipation.”

The rope slid over the ironwork, uncoiling softly. Its end descended past her balcony. She leaned out and caught it with both hands. Pepita set down the candle and helped her draw it taut.

“Come,” she whispered.

Padema climbed over his railing.

For one instant he sat astride it, the palace behind him and the abyss before him. A wiser man might have been troubled by this posture. But wisdom is frequently only vanity instructed by failure, and Padema had not yet failed often enough.

He wrapped his legs around the rope and began to descend.

The first yards were easy. His gloved hands moved steadily, his boots braced against the wall, and the scent of the woman’s roses rose toward him. Below, she watched without speaking.

When he had descended halfway, his foot slipped on a smooth stone. His body swung outward.

The rope creaked.

Padema stifled an oath and clung tightly. For several moments he rotated slowly above the street, like the body of a man who is hanged. Then he recovered his balance.

“Hold the end firmly,” he whispered.

“I hold it.”

He descended another few feet. Her balcony was now nearly level with his knees.

“Reach for me,” he said.

She did not move.

“Madonna?”

Pepita had disappeared into the room. The candle remained upon the floor, its flame small and upright. By its light the woman’s face appeared altered—not in feature, but in meaning. The softness had gone out of it. Her eyes were calm.

“Lower,” she said.

“I am low enough. Take my foot.”

“A little lower.”

Padema obeyed. His waist came level with the iron railing.

Now he perceived that the rope did not merely hang past her balcony. Its loose end passed between two iron bars and around the lower rail. She held it in both hands, using the balcony itself as a brace.

“Pull me in,” he said.

“No.”

He smiled uncertainly.

“This is a strange moment for modesty.”

“I am not modest.”

The rope tightened beneath him. He attempted to climb, but found that he could not raise himself. Each upward movement was checked by the tension she maintained below.

“Release the end.”

“No.”

“Do not play, Madonna. My arms are tiring.”

“I know.”

The night seemed suddenly colder. Far away a bell struck the quarter-hour. Padema looked upward. His own balcony hung above him, remote as a battlement. The rope ran over its edge and vanished into darkness.

“What are you doing?”

The woman leaned closer.

“Do you remember Meela?”

The name passed through his mind without alighting.

“Meela?”

“From Venice.”

“I know many people in Venice.”

“She was seventeen. She wore a blue ribbon at her throat. Her father kept the glass-house on Murano.”

A memory stirred: a summer night, a garden by the lagoon, a slim girl who had believed three promises in succession. Padema remembered her less clearly than he remembered the blue ribbon.

“She was a charming child,” he said cautiously.

“She was my sister.”

His Highness was appalled.

The full recollection came reluctantly. Meela had refused him at first. Then she had loved him. Later she had become troublesome, speaking of marriage, honour and a child that had not lived long enough to be born. One night she had followed his gondola in a smaller boat. There had been rain, a quarrel, an overturned lantern. He had ordered his boatman to row on.

By morning they found her body caught beneath the posts of a landing stair.

“It was an accident,” he said.

“She called to you.”

“The storm was loud.”

“You heard her.”

“I could not save her.”

“You would not.”

His hands tightened on the rope.

“My dear lady, grief has disordered your reason. Whatever happened, I deeply regretted it.”

“You did not attend her burial.”

“I was absent.”

“You were at a masque.”

Padema looked down. The street was very far below. Its paving stones showed faintly through the darkness, irregular and pale.

“Listen to me,” he said. “I was young. I was thoughtless. If I wronged your family, I will make restitution.”

“With gold?”

“With anything. A house in Venice. Land. Jewels. I will obtain a pardon and return. You shall come with me.”

“You still offer me Venice.”

“I offer you my name.”

“You did not offer it to Meela.”

“I offer marriage.”

This was the greatest promise in his collection, and he had never before produced it without seeing hope awaken in a woman’s eyes.

She merely adjusted her grip upon the rope.

“It is useless,” she said.

He tried again to climb. At once she drew the loose end backward. The rope pulled against the bars, pinning his weight below the balcony line. His boots scraped the wall and found no hold.

“You cannot remain there long,” she said. “Your hands will open. If they do not, mine will.”

“Pepita!” he called softly. “Pepita, come here!”

The old servant appeared in the doorway.

“I will give you fifty ducats,” he said.

Pepita looked at him with an expression of mild disgust.

“A hundred.”

She turned her back.

Padema’s voice sharpened. “Do you know who I am?”

“Yes,” said the woman. “That is why you are here.”

He reached for his dagger, but to free one hand was impossible. The motion set him swinging. The rope twisted, turning him slowly toward the void and back toward the wall.

“You murdered a nobleman,” she continued, “and fled from Venice. In Florence you lived under a false name. Tonight you attempted to enter a woman’s chamber by the balcony. Your rope failed, or your hands failed. No court will inquire very closely.”

Padema stared at her.

She had arranged not only his death but its explanation. This offended him almost as much as the prospect itself. It was his privilege to convert crime into accident; he had not imagined that the art could be practised against him.

“I loved Meela,” he said desperately.

The woman’s face hardened.

“No.”

“I swear it.”

“No.”

“I shall found a chapel for her soul.”

“No.”

“I repent.”

At this she almost smiled.

Above them, from the Prince’s balcony, came a slight metallic sound. The knot had shifted against the railing.

Padema felt it through the rope.

“Wait,” he whispered.

The woman began to let the loose end slide through her hands.

He dropped several inches.

“No!”

She stopped.

His breath came quickly. Sweat made the rope slippery beneath his gloves.

“For the love of God,” he said.

“My sister asked you in God’s name to turn back.”

“It is not the same.”

“No,” she agreed. “You are not drowning.”

He began to plead without art. He promised estates he did not own, influence he no longer possessed, tenderness he had never felt. He promised exile, confession, marriage and a lifetime at her feet. The formulas of seduction deserted him one by one, and naked terror spoke in their place.

She listened until he was silent.

Then she said, “Meela also promised everything.”

The rope slipped another inch.

Padema looked once toward the high balcony from which he had descended. Its ironwork traced a black pattern against a break in the clouds. For a moment the moon shone upon it, delicate as an etching and utterly beyond his reach.

The woman released one hand.

He cried out.

She covered her ears with that hand and opened the other.

The rope ran swiftly through the bars.

Then it was gone.

She did not look down. Pepita came forward, drew her gently inside and closed the balcony doors. The little votive lamp continued to burn beside the white roses.

In the morning they found Prince Padema in the narrow street, still grasping a length of rope.

Pietro testified that his master had dismissed him. The porter had seen no visitor enter the house below. The magistrate, upon learning the dead man’s assumed name and nocturnal purpose, raised his eyebrows and declared the affair lamentable.

By noon it was called an accident.

The woman heard the bells from a shuttered room. She sat without speaking while Pepita placed the blue ribbon of Meela in a small wooden box.

Outside, Florence went about its business. The shrine lamps paled, the merchants opened their doors, and high above the street an empty rope moved gently in the morning wind.

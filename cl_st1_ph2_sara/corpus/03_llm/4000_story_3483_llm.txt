### The River Beyond the Rain

There are persons who maintain that a story must stand upright upon two respectable legs—Probability and Fact. If a king goes away, they ask for his route; if a princess marries, they inquire into her age, caste, settlement and witnesses; if a dead boy returns to life, they close the book with the air of magistrates dismissing unreliable evidence.

I have no quarrel with such persons. In the market-place they are useful. They know the true weight of mustard oil, the correct fare of a carriage, and the number of witnesses required to prove that a neighbour’s mango tree leans across one’s wall. But no fairy tale has ever entered the heart through a properly stamped affidavit.

At seven years old I understood this.

I understood, moreover, that a headache which arrives at the hour of lessons is not necessarily less real because it departs when the tutor does. Learned men may dispute this. I can only state that on a certain stormy evening in Calcutta my forehead became the seat of an affliction so grave that the multiplication table could not safely approach it.

All afternoon the clouds had been gathering over the city. By dusk they hung low and black, and the rain came slanting across the courtyard, beating the plantain leaves, overflowing the broken spouts and making the dim lanes shine beneath the lamps. The wind pushed wet fingers through every crack. Doors groaned. Somewhere a shutter banged with the persistence of a creditor.

My tutor arrived in spite of the weather.

This seemed to me an injustice beyond human remedy. If thunderbolts could not protect a boy from grammar, what purpose did thunderbolts serve?

He entered with his umbrella streaming, his shoes muddy, and his book wrapped beneath his shawl. The book was dry. I noticed this with despair. A hopeful child may imagine that rain has compassion; but, alas, nor had the rain any compassion for me.

I was sitting beside the lamp with my chin on my hand. When my mother came in, I closed my eyes and pressed two fingers against my temple.

“What is the matter?”

“My head,” I whispered.

The tutor looked at me suspiciously. He had the kind of wisdom which sees through everything except childhood.

“Where does it ache?” my mother asked.

“Everywhere.”

This answer, being comprehensive, left no opening for argument.

She touched my forehead. It was not hot, but mothers possess a mercy beyond thermometers. She turned to the tutor and said, “Let it pass to-day. The weather is bad.”

The tutor hesitated. Duty wrestled with the desire to reach home before the streets became rivers. Duty was defeated.

“You must revise the lesson to-morrow,” he said.

I received this sentence with the resignation of one reprieved from execution but reminded that mortality remains. As soon as his umbrella disappeared through the outer door, my pain began to loosen its grip. By the time I heard his sandals splash beyond the gate, I was almost well enough to pursue my grandmother.

She sat in the inner room upon a low bed, sorting betel leaves. A brass lamp burned beside her. The mosquito curtain, tied up for the evening, hung in white folds from the wooden frame, and the rain filled the pauses of the house with a steady murmur.

“Grandmother,” I said, laying my head upon her lap, “tell me a story.”

“Your head aches.”

“A story will cure it.”

“What story?”

“One with a king.”

“There are many kings.”

“The king who went away.”

“They all go away sooner or later.”

“The one who came back.”

Grandmother smiled. She knew perfectly well which king I meant, though no such king had ever been mentioned before.

She put aside the betel leaves, settled her shawl, and began.

“There was a king—”

“Which king?”

“The king of a country very far from here.”

“How far?”

“So far that even the birds flying toward it forgot where they were going.”

This satisfied geography.

The king, she said, had a queen, a palace, seven white elephants, nine hundred horses and storerooms filled with gold. He possessed everything except a son.

Here modern understanding may pause to question why a daughter did not suffice. But the king was not modern understanding. He was a king in a grandmother’s story, and therefore he had got everything except the very thing he wanted.

The queen had given birth to a daughter whose eyes were like rain-washed leaves and whose little hands shone with golden bracelets. The king kissed the child, ordered drums to be beaten, distributed sweetmeats to the poor and remained dissatisfied.

At last he said, “I shall go into the forest and practise austerities until the gods grant me a son.”

The queen wept.

“How long will you be gone?”

“Twelve years.”

“That is a long time.”

“To one who waits, yes. To one who sits beneath a tree and counts prayers, perhaps not.”

So the king exchanged silk for bark-cloth, gave his crown to the minister, and went away. The princess was then so small that she slept with one fist closed around her nurse’s finger.

Twelve years in a fairy tale do not require twelve years to pass. They are lighter than twelve drops of rain. The princess grew. Her hair fell below her waist; her anklets chimed through the palace passages; when she stood at the lattice, the moon itself seemed to have found a companion.

The king, meanwhile, remained beneath his tree. Whether the gods granted him a son, whether the ants climbed over his feet, whether he regretted the hardness of the ground—these matters my grandmother did not explain. A child does not demand accounts from the gods. It is only later that we put spectacles on our doubts and call the result wisdom.

At the end of twelve years the king returned.

The city gates were hung with garlands. Conch shells sounded. The queen came forth with a golden tray, and behind her stood the princess, veiled in blue.

The king looked at the tall young woman and frowned.

“Who is she?”

The queen thought he was joking.

“Do you not know?”

“I have never seen her.”

“She is your daughter.”

“My daughter was a child.”

“Twelve years have passed.”

The king looked again. Time had done its work without first obtaining his permission, and this offended him. Perhaps austerities teach many things, but they do not always teach a father that little girls grow up.

“She must be married at once,” he declared.

The queen asked, “To whom?”

The king, still angry with time, said, “To the first man I see from my eastern window at sunrise.”

Now, if this decree appears rash, remember that kings in stories are surrounded by ministers precisely in order that they may ignore advice. The minister bowed. The queen sighed. The princess lowered her eyes. Nobody mentioned that the first man might be a beggar, a thief, or the palace washerman returning a bundle of clothes.

That night the queen ordered the road beneath the eastern window to be swept. She sent messengers secretly to summon princes, scholars and noblemen before dawn. But the storm had broken bridges, swollen streams and delayed every splendid person.

At sunrise the king opened the shutters.

A small Brahman boy was gathering dry sticks beside the palace wall.

He was seven years old.

At this point I lifted my head from my grandmother’s lap.

“Seven?”

“Seven.”

“As old as I am?”

“Perhaps one month younger.”

“What was his name?”

“If I tell you, you will forget it.”

“I will not.”

“Then his name was Kanu.”

“What did he do with the sticks?”

“He carried them home for his mother’s fire.”

“And then?”

“The king’s guards seized him.”

Here I protested that they should not have seized him too roughly, and Grandmother assured me that one guard held the bundle while another lifted Kanu as gently as a basket of mangoes.

The boy was brought before the throne. His sacred thread crossed his narrow chest; his hair was untidy; one knee was scratched.

The king said, “You shall marry my daughter.”

Kanu stared.

“I must take the sticks home.”

“They shall be sent.”

“My mother will be waiting.”

“She shall come to the palace.”

“I have school.”

“You may go after the wedding.”

This last arrangement appeared reasonable to me, though it did not greatly improve Kanu’s fortune.

The court priest examined the almanac and discovered, as court priests often do, that the very hour forced upon them by events was the most auspicious hour in a hundred years. The drums sounded. Sandalwood was burned. Kanu was bathed, dressed in silk, covered with ornaments and seated beneath a canopy, where he looked less like a bridegroom than a child who had strayed into a ceremony and was too polite to escape.

The princess came wearing gold and crimson. She was tall; he barely reached her shoulder.

“Did she mind?” I asked.

“She obeyed her father.”

“But did she mind?”

Grandmother considered.

“She looked at Kanu and saw that he was frightened. Then she did not mind so much.”

That answer opened no wound in justice, and the wedding proceeded.

Afterward the princess took Kanu to a palace of seven wings, one for each day of the week. In the first wing were fountains; in the second, singing birds; in the third, shelves of sweetmeats; in the fourth, ivory toys; in the fifth, peacock-tail fans and mirrors framed in silver; in the sixth, books and writing boards; and in the seventh, her own rooms, where lamps burned in niches and the floors were cool white stone.

She cared for him as one cares for a precious and troublesome child. She saw that he ate, sent him to school, rubbed oil into his hair, and told him stories when thunder shook the shutters.

Thus another twelve years began to pass, though not all at once.

Every morning Kanu went to the school of the Brahman teacher. At first the other boys mocked his silk coat and palace servant. Later they grew accustomed to these things. But they never grew accustomed to the princess.

Sometimes they saw her waiting behind the carved lattice when Kanu returned. Sometimes she came to the garden in a blue sari to remind him that he had forgotten his lunch. She was still young and so beautiful that the boys lost their places in their books when her anklets sounded.

One day a schoolmate asked, “Kanu, who is that lady in your house?”

Kanu answered, “I do not know.”

That evening he asked the princess.

“Who are you?”

She was arranging flowers in a brass bowl.

“I shall tell you when you are older.”

“When?”

“Next year.”

The next year came. Schoolboys do not forget a promised secret, especially when it does not belong to them.

“Who is she?” they asked. “Your sister? Your aunt? A goddess? A queen in hiding?”

Again Kanu asked.

“Who are you?”

She was grinding sandalwood upon a stone.

“To-day there is too much work,” she said. “Ask me after the spring festival.”

The spring festival passed beneath clouds of coloured powder. Kanu asked again.

“After the rains.”

The rains came and went. The palace roofs shone, the tanks overflowed, frogs held noisy assemblies in the garden.

“After the harvest.”

The harvest ended.

“After your sacred lesson.”

The lesson was learned.

Years gathered around him. His shoulders broadened; his voice changed; the silk clothes no longer seemed borrowed. Yet the question followed him from boyhood as faithfully as a shadow.

“Who is the beautiful woman in your palace?”

At last Kanu came home in anger. He threw down his books.

“You always say to-morrow. You always say after this festival or that season. I will not eat, sleep or go to school until you tell me.”

The princess looked at him for a long time. Perhaps she saw the seven-year-old stick-gatherer still standing inside the young man. Perhaps she saw that time, which had once astonished her father, had now come quietly into her own house.

“Very well,” she said. “To-night I shall tell you.”

She prepared the seventh wing herself.

She bathed and put on a blue sari thin as evening mist. She braided her hair with jasmine. She laid a new coverlet upon the bed, scattered champak and tuberose over the pillows, and placed golden lamps around the room. Perfume rose like invisible music. Outside, rain began to fall.

But among the flowers a serpent had hidden itself.

It was small, dark and cold. No servant saw it. No lamp revealed it. It waited beneath the jasmine as death waits beneath all our arrangements, though children are not obliged to know this.

Kanu entered.

The princess stood by the bed.

“Now,” he said, smiling, “tell me. Who are you?”

She reached for his hand.

The serpent struck.

Kanu cried out once and fell among the flowers. Before the princess could call the guards, before the physicians could bring powders or priests could mutter charms, the colour left his face.

“He died,” said Grandmother.

“No,” I said.

The rain beat loudly upon the roof.

Grandmother stroked my hair. “The serpent’s poison was strong.”

“No. He did not die.”

“In stories, too, people sometimes die.”

“Not Kanu.”

She tried to continue: the princess wept, the palace lamps went out, messengers ran to the king—

I sat up.

“What then?”

“They prepared sandalwood for the funeral.”

“What then?”

“The princess took off her ornaments.”

“What then?”

My voice had become dangerous. Grandmother knew that she stood before the court of childhood, where death may be accused but is never permitted to win merely because it has facts on its side.

She calmly altered the universe.

“They did not burn him,” she said. “The princess forbade it.”

I lay down again, though I remained watchful.

“What did she do?”

“She placed his body upon the broad stem of a banana tree. Beneath his head she put the blue cloth she had worn on their wedding day. Around him she arranged the flowers, but she searched every one to make certain that no serpent remained. Then, at midnight, she set the raft upon the river.”

The river was swollen with rain. It carried Kanu past ghats where lamps trembled on the steps, past sleeping villages, past bamboo groves bent beneath the wind. The princess walked along the bank until reeds and darkness stopped her. Then she stood with her hands clasped while the little raft vanished.

“What then?”

“Far downriver lived a magician.”

“A good magician?”

“Would I send Kanu to a bad one?”

The magician was sitting beneath a banyan tree, drawing circles in ash. He saw the banana stem caught among the roots and found the young man lying upon it, pale beneath the flowers.

He listened at Kanu’s heart.

“He is not dead,” said the magician. “He has only gone behind the shut door.”

The magician kindled a fire of neem leaves. He set seven bowls of river water around it, one for each wing of the palace. Into the first he dropped sandalwood, into the second a grain of rice, into the third a gold flower, into the fourth a peacock feather, into the fifth a child’s writing reed, into the sixth a thread from blue cloth, and into the seventh one tear which the princess had shed upon Kanu’s hand.

“How did he get the tear?” I asked.

“Magicians have their means.”

This was true.

He spoke incantations until the river turned quiet. He touched water to Kanu’s forehead and said, “Return.”

Nothing happened.

He touched water to Kanu’s lips.

“Return.”

Kanu breathed.

He opened his eyes and asked, “Where is the princess?”

The magician smiled. “Now you know who she is.”

Kanu rose, stepped from the banana stem and walked back along the river. At sunrise he reached the seven-winged palace. The princess was still standing at the gate in her blue sari.

“Who are you?” he asked one last time.

She answered, “I am your wife.”

And because he was no longer seven, he understood.

Here my grandmother’s voice became mixed with the rain. The brass lamp had burned low. Someone loosened the mosquito curtain, and its white folds descended around us like the walls of a misty palace. I wished to ask whether Kanu returned to school, whether his companions believed him, whether the serpent was punished. But these questions lost their sharpness before they reached my lips.

My counterfeit headache had entirely disappeared. In its place came sleep, the only magician whose incantations no child doubts.

Looking back now, I can see a hundred absurdities in the tale. I can summon physicians against the poison, lawyers against the marriage, reformers against the king, and scholars against the banana stem. They may all speak. They may produce their documents. But they arrive too late.

That rainy evening remains beyond their jurisdiction.

For I had been carried, like Kanu, upon the dark river. The storm moved outside the mosquito curtain; my grandmother’s hand rested upon my head; flowers from an impossible palace drifted through my dreams. Death itself had appeared, but only as a door which a magician knew how to open.

In the morning I awoke to light on the wet leaves and the cries of vendors in the lane. My tutor would come again; multiplication had survived the storm. Yet I, too, had returned from a country beyond the rain, where kings make foolish vows, princesses wait without growing weary, and no beloved child is surrendered forever to the serpent among the flowers.

*[Sam Lawson sat on the sunny side of a stone wall in the blueberry pasture, with three boys round him, their baskets no fuller than when they came, though their mouths and fingers were blue enough to show where the berries had gone. Sam had a grass-stem between his teeth and his old straw hat tipped over one eye.]*

“Wal, boys,” said Sam, “there’s some folks can manage a house, and there’s some can manage a doctrine, and it ain’t often the Lord sees fit to put both gifts into one person. I’ve known women that could turn out mince pies light enough to float, and yet couldn’t tell Jonah from Jeroboam; and I’ve known ministers that could take the horns of the altar and the beasts in Revelation and the seventy weeks of Daniel, and range ’em all in rows as straight as seed-onions, but couldn’t bile an egg without crackin’ the kittle.

“Parson Carryl was one o’ that sort. He knew every by-path from Genesis to Revelation, and could fetch up a text from any part o’ Scripture as handy as Mis’ Deacon Peabody could fetch a stocking-needle out o’ her apron pocket. He was a tall, grave man, with white hair risin’ over his forehead, and eyes that looked right through common things and on into eternity; but the consequence was, he commonly stumbled over the common things.

“His wife, Mis’ Carryl, she’d always seen to them. She was one o’ your thin, resolute women, tough as a cart-whip and smart as a steel trap. She could keep the parson, two hired men, a bound boy, fourteen hens, a cow with a cross disposition, and the whole female prayer-meetin’ in their proper places, and never raise her voice above what she called a Christian tone. Folks said Parson Carryl ruled the church; but Mis’ Carryl ruled the parsonage, and, between you and me, boys, the church too, considerable.

“But at last she was took down with the slow fever, and, though she fought it as if ’twas a disorderly hired man, she couldn’t turn it out o’ doors. She died along in March, when the snow was slushin’ off the eaves and the roads was knee-deep with mud.

“The parson preached the Sabbath arter the funeral. He took his text about bein’ a stranger and pilgrim on the earth, and there warn’t a dry eye in the meetin’-house. He spoke calm, but his face looked sort o’ emptied out, like a house when all the furniture’s been sold at vendue. The fact was, he’d loved Mis’ Carryl in his way, though he’d been so used to her keepin’ life smooth under his feet that he no more thought how she did it than you think how the ground holds you up.

“Now there’d been a young woman helpin’ Mis’ Carryl that last year—Huldah Greene. Everybody called her Huldy. She was left without father or mother when she warn’t more’n sixteen, and Mis’ Carryl had took her in, partly out o’ charity and partly because she knew a good pair o’ hands when she saw ’em.

“Huldy was nothin’ of a helpless, hangin’-vine kind. She was straight and strong, with brown hair that had a glint of gold where the sun struck it, and clear gray eyes that could look mild as June rain or steady as a frost, ’cordin’ to what there was to be looked at. Her cheeks had that sort o’ color you see on the shady side of a Baldwin apple, and when she laughed—wal, I ain’t goin’ to talk foolish, boys, but there’s laughs that make a room seem warmer, same as openin’ the oven door on bakin’-day.

“She could spin, weave, sew, churn, bake, brew spruce beer, make soft soap, dip candles, nurse a sick body, quiet a fretful baby, and tell by the smell when a preserve kettle was one minute from scorchin’. Work rolled off from her like water off a duck’s back. She never seemed hurried, yet everything was done. I’ve seen women make more commotion over one batch o’ pies than Huldy made over a parish supper.

“So, arter Mis’ Carryl died, Huldy stayed on. There warn’t no particular agreement at first. She jist stayed because there was the parson, poor innocent soul, wanderin’ round among funeral gloves, condolence letters, and jars of things the neighbors had sent in. He’d open the pantry door when he meant to go upstairs, and once he carried a clean shirt to the barn and laid it in the haymow, under an impression it was a sermon he was goin’ to study.

“Huldy put the house to rights, answered the door, fed the men, kept the fires, sorted the dead lady’s clothes, and moved soft-footed through the rooms, singin’ low to herself. The parson would come down and find his breakfast hot, his stock brushed, his sermon paper laid by his plate, and his spectacles—most wonderful of all—where he could put his hand on ’em.

“‘Huldah,’ says he one mornin’, ‘the dispensations of Providence, though mysterious, are attended by mercies.’

“‘Yes, sir,’ says Huldy.

“‘I had not supposed,’ says he, lookin’ at a buckwheat cake as if it was a theological proposition, ‘that the domestic machinery could continue in operation with so little visible disturbance.’

“‘No, sir,’ says Huldy, and turned her head to hide a smile.

“He hadn’t the least notion that she’d been up since four, skimmed six pans o’ milk, kneaded bread, mended the kitchen pump, doctored the cow, and sent the bound boy back to wash his neck. He thought domestic machinery went of itself, like the planets, only quieter.

“Wal, now, this state o’ things might have continued peaceable if there hadn’t been a parcel o’ women in the parish that felt the Lord had laid on ’em the oversight of other folks’s concerns. There always is sich persons. They’re born with a spiritual spyglass under one arm and a moral yardstick under t’other, and they never can be easy till they’ve measured everybody and sighted every speck on the horizon.

“The head one was Widder Pipperidge. She was a little peaked woman with snappin’ black eyes, a nose hooked like a hawk’s beak, and a mouth that shut like a steel purse-clasp. She wore bombazine ever since Cap’n Pipperidge died, though there was them that said she’d had her widow’s weeds cut and ready while he was only ailin’. I don’t say that was true. A lie travels in light shoes, boys, while truth is pullin’ on its boots; and I never help a lie along unless I know exactly where it’s goin’.

“Mis’ Pipperidge had expected, arter a decent season, that Parson Carryl might be led to see the advantages of a mature companion. She had a good feather bed, twelve silver teaspoons, two hundred dollars at interest, and a disposition that never allowed anybody near her to remain unconscious of their faults. She’d more’n once observed that ministers needed wives who understood the responsibilities of the position, and she said ‘position’ in a way that made it sound like the seat next the throne.

“So one afternoon she and Mis’ Deacon Blodgett and Miss Asphyxia Smith went up to the parsonage. Huldy set out pound cake, quince preserve, and the best India tea, and the three of ’em ate enough to prove they had no prejudice against her cookin’. Then, when she’d gone out, Mis’ Pipperidge began.

“‘Dear Parson Carryl,’ says she, foldin’ her hands, ‘we have felt some concern.’

“‘Indeed?’ says the parson.

“‘Only affectionate concern,’ says Mis’ Blodgett.

“‘Entirely disinterested,’ says Miss Asphyxia, who hadn’t been disinterested in anything since she was weaned.

“‘The dear young person you retain,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge, ‘appears amiable, but she is inexperienced.’

“‘Huldah?’ says the parson. ‘She has conducted the household with remarkable regularity.’

“‘Surface regularity,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge. ‘Young persons make a show. But prudence, foresight, supervision—these come only with years.’

“‘And trials,’ says Mis’ Blodgett.

“‘And observation,’ says Miss Asphyxia.

“‘You, dear sir,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge, leanin’ forward, ‘should exercise personal oversight. A master ought to know the condition of his estate. Livestock, stores, expenditures, winter provision—these cannot safely be abandoned to a girl.’

“The parson looked solemn. He’d never thought of himself as havin’ an estate before. The word pleased him. It sounded patriarchal, like Abraham.

“‘There is force in your observation,’ says he.

“‘We knew you would see it,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge, and they all went away with their mouths pursed up in righteousness.

“Next mornin’ the parson says to Huldy, ‘Huldah, I have concluded that it is incumbent upon me to acquaint myself more particularly with temporal affairs.’

“‘Yes, sir,’ says Huldy.

“‘I shall begin with the poultry.’

“‘Yes, sir,’ says she, and there was a little twinkle in her eye, but the parson never saw twinkles unless they were in the original Hebrew.

“Now, there was a great bronze tom-turkey struttin’ round the yard. He was an awful proud critter, with a head all blue and red, and a tail he spread out like Deacon Grimes’s wife’s best fan. He’d gobble at the hired men, chase the boy, and look at the parson as if he’d been waitin’ years for a chance to dispute doctrine with him.

“The turkey hens had been layin’ careless-like in the grass, and Huldy had collected two dozen eggs in a basket. The parson found ’em and stood considerin’.

“‘Huldah,’ says he, ‘these eggs should be subjected to incubation.’

“‘Yes, sir.’

“‘Where are the female birds?’

“‘One was took by a fox, sir, and the other’s wanderin’. I was cal’latin’ to set the speckled hen on some.’

“‘That appears an unnecessary deviation from nature,’ says he. ‘The eggs are turkey eggs; consequently a turkey should hatch them.’

“‘Yes, sir,’ says Huldy, in that quiet way of hern.

“‘The male bird,’ says the parson, ‘is larger, possesses greater warmth, and should therefore be capable of covering a larger number. I have often remarked upon the unequal distribution of domestic duty among the lower creation.’

“Huldy bit her lip.

“‘I shouldn’t wonder but what it had been remarked on before,’ says she.

“‘Bring me a box,’ says he.

“Wal, boys, I wish you could have seen the next half hour. The parson put a bushel basket on its side in the barn, filled it with hay, and arranged the eggs as careful as if they’d been members of the congregation. Then he went after the tom.

“The tom warn’t in a receptive state of mind.

“The parson advanced slow, sayin’, ‘So, so, my creature,’ and holdin’ out corn. The turkey reached for the corn, but when the parson reached for the turkey, there commenced sich a flappin’, gobblin’, runnin’, dodgin’ and dust-raisin’ as that barnyard hadn’t seen since the ministerial association dined there and Deacon Grimes backed his wagon over a beehive.

“The parson’s coat-tails flew. His white hair stood up. The tom went between his legs and upset him into the bean-poles. Then the hired man, Enoch, come runnin’, and the bound boy come runnin’, and Huldy stood in the kitchen door with her apron to her mouth.

“‘Secure him!’ cried the parson.

“Enoch made a dive and got one wing. The boy got the other. The parson took the legs. The tom used his neck and language. Between ’em they carried him to the barn, upside down, spread out, and furious enough to have voted against infant baptism.

“They set him on the eggs.

“He got up.

“They set him down again.

“He got up quicker.

“Then the parson fetched an old splint basket, clapped it over him, and put a flat stone on top.

“‘There,’ says he, breathin’ hard, ‘instinct must now resume its proper operation.’

“Instinct resumed operation, sure enough. That turkey went round and round under the basket, fussin’ and fuzzlin’ and wuzzlin’, and every turn he made there was a little crunch. At last he h’isted the basket, stone and all, come out backward, dragged it three rods, flew over the woodpile, and landed in the rain-barrel.

“When they got him out, there warn’t enough of them eggs left to make a teaspoonful of custard.

“Huldy didn’t say a word. She wiped the yolk off the parson’s sleeve, brushed the hay out of his hair, and told Enoch to let the turkey alone. The parson looked mournful, but dignified.

“‘Huldah,’ says he, ‘the resistance of the natural creature to moral responsibility is more deeply rooted than I had supposed.’

“‘Yes, sir,’ says Huldy.

“Next Sunday he preached from the text, ‘The whole creation groaneth and travaileth,’ and made a powerful application about Adam’s fall. Everybody said it was one of his most experimental sermons. Only Huldy and Enoch knew how much experiment had gone into it.

“But she hadn’t lost all the eggs. She’d kept eleven aside, mistrustin’ theology when it got too near the hen-house, and she slipped ’em under the speckled hen. That old hen took to ’em with Christian patience, and in due time she come sailin’ out with ten little turkeys behind her, every one as pert as a grasshopper.

“Mis’ Pipperidge heard about the first eggs, but not the second.

“‘I feared as much,’ says she. ‘Waste, confusion, no mature judgment.’

“Wal, a month or so after that came the pig business.

“There was an old well near the kitchen ell that had gone dry, and the stones round the top had begun to cave. Huldy told the parson it was dangerous, and he agreed it should be secured. She hired Jabez Turner to make a stout square curb with a hinged cover, and he’d jist finished it, all new pine boards, when a drover came along with a young pig Huldy had agreed to buy.

“The parson had been advised by Mis’ Pipperidge that every proper country household ought to fatten pork. He came out of his study while Huldy was in the garret sortin’ wool, and there stood the pig tied to the fence, squealin’ in a voice that seemed to accuse the whole human race.

“The parson looked at the pig. Then he looked at the new square structure by the kitchen.

“‘Ah,’ says he, ‘Huldah has provided a pen.’

“Jabez had left the cover tilted back to let the new boards dry. The parson untied the pig.

“‘Come, creature,’ says he. ‘Your habitation is prepared.’

“The pig didn’t see it so. He backed, twisted, wound the rope round the parson’s legs, and started for the garden. The parson followed in short hops, exhortin’ him to moderation. They went through the asparagus bed, over a row of young cabbages, and round the well three times. At last the parson caught the pig under the forelegs, lifted him—squealin’, kickin’, and wrigglin’—and dropped him over the curb.

“There was a silence.

“Then, from considerable depth, came one splash and a long, surprised squeal.

“The parson leaned over.

“‘Dear me,’ says he.

“The well warn’t dry after all. There was six foot of muddy water in the bottom.

“Just then Huldy came out.

“‘Where’s the pig?’ says she.

“The parson pointed down.

“‘Huldah,’ says he, ‘there appears to have been a misconception as to the purpose of this enclosure.’

“Huldy looked into the well. The pig was swimmin’ round, snout up, meditatin’ revenge.

“She never stopped to scold. She ran for a rope, called Enoch and Jabez, fetched an old grain basket, and, after a deal of contrivin’, they lowered Jabez halfway down till he got the pig into the basket. When they drew it up, that pig come over the edge like a cannonball, shook muddy water over everybody, bit Enoch’s boot, and headed for the parlor.

“Huldy caught it with a tablecloth jist as it got under the sofa.

“Now, boys, a pig in a tablecloth ain’t a dignified object, and a minister standin’ by with green slime on his best breeches ain’t much better. But Huldy only says, ‘There, there, poor little cretur,’ as if the pig had been the victim of doctrinal error—which, in a manner, it had.

“She put it in a wash-tub for the night, covered the well, and next day had Jabez build a real pen behind the barn. Then she drove over to Seth Barlow’s and bought another pig, because, says she, ‘One pig alone gets lonesome, and lonesome things don’t thrive.’

“When the parson saw the two of ’em bedded down in clean straw, he stood a long while lookin’.

“‘Huldah,’ says he at last, ‘I perceive that special aptitude is a significant element in the ordering of Providence.’

“‘Yes, sir.’

“‘Your aptitude for these concerns is plainly greater than mine.’

“‘I’ve had more practice, sir.’

“‘Henceforth,’ says he, ‘I shall confide the temporal administration to your discretion.’

“‘Yes, sir,’ says Huldy.

“And from that hour he never interfered with a turkey, pig, pump, pickle jar, hired man, seed catalogue, or soap kettle. He’d found his limitations, which is more’n most folks do if they live to be ninety.

“Wal, then that place began to flourish. Huldy had a gift for makin’ things grow. She planted pinks and hollyhocks along the front walk, trained morning-glories round the porch, and set a row of sweet peas under the study window. The bean vines climbed higher, the cucumbers bore thicker, and even the old pear tree, that hadn’t done anything but creak for six years, put out blossoms and gave three baskets of pears.

“She managed Enoch and the boy without screechin’. Some women drive hired help like oxen with a goad, and then wonder why they kick over the traces. Huldy would say, ‘Enoch, when you’ve got the south field mowed, I’ve a notion there’ll be a cherry pie wantin’ somebody to help it disappear.’ Enoch would mow as if angels was countin’ every swath.

“The bound boy, Lem, had been considered lazy, wicked, and probably destined for the gallows. Huldy discovered he couldn’t see farther than the end of his nose. She got him spectacles from a travelin’ peddler, and after that he could distinguish weeds from onions, which improved his moral character amazin’.

“She kept the accounts in a little brown book, and no tin-peddler nor flour agent could cheat the parson by so much as a brass button. Old Silas Doolittle once brought a load of damp wood and swore it was seasoned maple.

“Huldy lifted one stick.

“‘Silas,’ says she, ‘this tree had leaves on it Tuesday.’

“‘Now, Huldy—’

“‘You can take it back and dry it, or you can leave it at green-wood price.’

“Silas looked at her, saw there warn’t no gap in the fence, and took green-wood price. Arter that the men round town said Parson Carryl’s housekeeper had eyes in the ends of her fingers.

“And all the while she was singin’. She sung over the churn, over the ironing, out in the garden, and up garret among the herbs. Sometimes it was hymns, and sometimes old ballads, and sometimes no words at all, only a tune floatin’ round like a bobolink over a meadow.

“The parson’s study window was open into the garden, and her voice would come in with the smell of sweet peas and new hay. His sermons changed. They’d always been sound—sound as a dry codfish and about as nourishin’ to common appetites—but now they began to bud and blossom. He preached of the lilies, and folks could almost see Huldy’s white lilies by the porch. He preached about the bread of life, and there was a warmth in it like fresh loaves on the kitchen shelf. He spoke of the peace that passeth understanding, and his voice had a softness it hadn’t had in Mis’ Carryl’s day.

“Folks noticed.

“‘The parson is growin’ wonderfully spiritual,’ says Mis’ Deacon Blodgett.

“‘There are influences,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge, with a long draw on ‘influences.’

“‘What influences?’ says Miss Asphyxia.

“‘I should be sorry to say,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge, which was her common way of sayin’ anything she particularly wanted heard.

“Now, there was to be a great association meetin’ at the church in September, with ministers from nine towns, delegates, deacons, singers, and enough relations to consume a winter’s provision in one afternoon. The parsonage was to entertain twelve overnight and feed near forty at dinner.

“That was the chance the parish women had been waitin’ for. They appointed themselves a committee to inspect preparations. Mis’ Pipperidge said it was not distrust, but assistance.

“They come on Thursday—Mis’ Pipperidge, Mis’ Blodgett, Miss Asphyxia, and Mis’ Zeruiah Pennel, who had a nose for dust and could find a cobweb in a thunderstorm.

“Huldy received ’em pleasant.

“‘We thought,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge, ‘you might be overwhelmed.’

“‘Thank you,’ says Huldy. ‘I’m gettin’ along.’

“‘So young a person cannot understand the scale of such hospitality,’ says Mis’ Blodgett.

“‘No, indeed,’ says Huldy. ‘Won’t you look round and tell me what I’ve forgot?’

“That took a little wind out of ’em, but they begun.

“They looked in the parlor. The brass andirons shone fit to dazzle you. The curtains was washed, stretched, and white as snow. There warn’t dust enough on the mantel for a flea to write his name in.

“They went to the spare chamber. Sheets smelled of lavender, quilts lay smooth, wash-pitchers were full, and on each bureau was a little nosegay.

“They tried the pantry. Huldy had pies in rows—apple, mince, pumpkin, and quince; hams boiled; chickens dressed; dough risin’; cakes covered; pickles, preserves, and jellies ranged by color like church windows in the sun.

“Mis’ Pennel run one finger along the top shelf.

“Nothin’.

“She looked behind the clock.

“Nothin’.

“She opened the wood-box, though what she expected to find there I never knew. The sticks was laid straight.

“‘Have you remembered mustard for the boiled ham?’ asks Mis’ Blodgett.

“‘Made yesterday,’ says Huldy.

“‘Extra lamp-wicks?’

“‘In the blue drawer.’

“‘Warm bricks for the beds?’

“‘There’s eight by the hearth.’

“‘Feed for visiting horses?’

“‘Enoch’s put four bushels of oats in the granary.’

“‘A place for wet wraps if it rains?’ says Mis’ Pipperidge, with a kind of triumph.

“‘Hooks are up in the back entry, and there’s two clothes-horses ready,’ says Huldy.

“Then Mis’ Pipperidge opened the cellar door.

“‘I suppose,’ says she, ‘the cellar may not have received attention.’

“It had. The potatoes was sorted, apples barreled, preserves labeled, crocks covered, floor swept, and the very cobwebs looked as if they’d been warned off the premises.

“The women came upstairs again, pretty dry in their minds after so much huntin’ and no game.

“‘You have done—creditably,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge, as if she was awardin’ a prize to a child.

“‘Thank you,’ says Huldy.

“‘Of course,’ Mis’ Pipperidge goes on, ‘we trust you have not neglected the higher duties in this absorption with material things.’

“‘I hope not,’ says Huldy.

“‘Young women,’ says Miss Asphyxia, ‘are sometimes so anxious to make themselves indispensable that they forget humility.’

“Huldy’s color rose a little.

“‘Would you like another piece of cake?’ says she.

“They all did.

“The association came, and everything went smooth as cream. Forty-three persons sat down, and nobody waited, nobody lacked, nothin’ burned, and the coffee held out. Huldy moved among ’em in a brown dress with a white kerchief crossed over her bosom, fresh and quiet, and the visiting ministers praised the dinner till Parson Carryl felt as if he’d prepared it through some extension of pastoral authority.

“One old minister from North Beulah says, ‘Brother Carryl, you are favored in your household.’

“‘I am indeed,’ says the parson, and his eyes followed Huldy as she went through the doorway.

“Mis’ Pipperidge saw that look.

“Now, jealousy, boys, is like a burr in a horse’s tail. The more it gets switched, the tighter it twists. The women couldn’t fault Huldy’s work, so they begun to fault her motives.

“First it was said she had arranged the parson’s books more often than necessary. Then somebody noticed she put flowers on his desk. Then Miss Asphyxia declared she had heard Huldy singin’ ‘Come, Thou Fount’ in a manner too expressive for a kitchen. Mis’ Blodgett observed that the parson took two cups of tea when Huldy poured it and only one elsewhere. By the time the story had gone from the sewing circle to the Thursday lecture, Huldy was supposed to be settin’ snares for the parson with every doughnut.

“‘I have nothin’ against the girl,’ Mis’ Pipperidge would say. ‘I sincerely hope I am mistaken. But the difference in age! The sacredness of his office! The opportunities of a person under his roof! Dear me, one trembles.’

“And she trembled all over town, tellin’ everybody why.

“Huldy didn’t hear at first. Busy folks are commonly the last to know what idle folks say about ’em. But one afternoon she went to carry broth to old Aunt Nabby, and, comin’ past the vestry, she heard voices through the open window.

“‘Of course she means to marry him,’ says Miss Asphyxia.

“‘If she can,’ says Mis’ Blodgett.

“‘A young woman who had proper delicacy would have left months ago,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge. ‘Remaining under such circumstances speaks for itself.’

“Huldy stood still. The covered bowl was warm in her hands, but she went cold all over. Then she walked on and carried the broth to Aunt Nabby, set the room to rights, and listened to the old lady complain about her rheumatism for half an hour. That was Huldy. She never made another person pay for the hurt she’d received.

“But that night she didn’t sing.

“The parson noticed. He was tryin’ to write a sermon on rejoicing always, and somehow it wouldn’t rejoice.

“Next day Huldy was quiet. The day after, quiet still. She went about with a steady face, but the house seemed different. A fire may burn, boys, but if the kettle don’t sing, you feel there’s somethin’ gone wrong.

“At last the parson says, ‘Huldah, are you unwell?’

“‘No, sir.’

“‘Has some difficulty arisen with the household?’

“‘No, sir.’

“‘Has Enoch again neglected the gate?’

“‘No, sir.’

“‘Then something has disturbed you.’

“She folded a towel very exact.

“‘I’ve been thinkin’ I’d better leave, sir.’

“The parson laid down his pen.

“‘Leave?’

“‘Yes, sir. Come November, maybe. Mis’ Elderkin over in South Parish needs somebody.’

“‘But you are needed here.’

“‘There’s others can do.’

“‘Not as you do.’

“‘They might, with time.’

“The parson took off his spectacles, wiped ’em, put ’em on, and looked at her. ‘Huldah, have I given offense?’

“‘Oh, no, sir! Never.’

“‘Then why should you alter an arrangement which has proved beneficial?’

“She tried to answer, but her mouth trembled. At last she says, ‘Folks are talkin’.’

“‘Folks are generally talking,’ says the parson.

“‘About me—and you.’

“He looked puzzled.

“‘They say I’m stayin’ here to—to catch you. They say I’m makin’ myself necessary on purpose. They say it isn’t proper.’

“The parson’s face changed. First he looked astonished, then grieved, then stern in the way he looked when rebukin’ doctrinal error.

“‘Who has said this?’

“‘It don’t matter.’

“‘It matters insofar as false witness is prohibited by the ninth commandment.’

“‘Please don’t ask me, sir. I couldn’t stay after knowin’. Mis’ Carryl was good to me, and you’ve been good to me, and I’d rather go than bring shame on the house.’

“She turned away then, and the parson saw a tear fall on the clean towel.

“Wal, he didn’t answer quick. He rose and walked to the window. Outside, the garden was in late bloom. Huldy’s asters stood purple by the wall, the sweet peas climbed the strings, and the speckled hen went by with three half-grown turkeys she still considered babies. Beyond was the pigpen, neat and tight, with two pigs sleepin’ in the sun. Everywhere he looked was something Huldy had tended into comfort.

“Then it came over him—not all to once, maybe, but as daylight gets into a room—that he didn’t merely need the bread she baked, the accounts she kept, nor even the singin’ that helped his sermons. He needed Huldy. He loved the turn of her head, the sound of her step, her sense, her courage, her gentleness, and the way life grew wherever she laid a hand.

“He turned round.

“‘Huldah,’ says he, ‘do you find the difference in our ages an insuperable objection?’

“She looked up, startled.

“‘Sir?’

“‘I am aware that I am not young. I am also aware that my acquaintance with temporal affairs is deficient. These deficiencies are facts and should be plainly stated.’

“‘I never minded your age,’ says Huldy, and then turned red clear to her hair.

“‘As to propriety,’ says the parson, comin’ a little nearer, ‘there is one arrangement which would place your continuance here beyond reasonable objection.’

“Huldy’s hands tightened on the towel.

“‘If,’ says he, and now the great preacher that could address eight hundred people without a tremor began stumblin’ like a boy recitin’ his first piece, ‘if your regard for me is not wholly—wholly professional—or domestic—and if you could esteem—if you could consent—Huldah, will you become my wife?’

“She looked at him. The clock ticked. A bee bumped against the window-pane. Out by the barn the tom-turkey gobbled, as if objectin’ to the whole proceeding.

“‘Are you askin’ because of the talk?’ says she softly.

“‘No,’ says the parson. ‘The talk has served only to show me what I should have known without it. I ask because this house without you would again be empty, and because my life—’ He stopped, drew breath, and says, very simple, ‘Because I love you, Huldah.’

“Then Huldy smiled through tears.

“‘Yes, sir,’ says she.

“Now, boys, you’d suppose a man, havin’ been accepted, might kiss the woman. But Parson Carryl stood there lookin’ relieved and happy, and says, ‘I trust I shall justify your confidence.’

“Huldy waited.

“He picked up his spectacles.

“She waited still.

“At last she says, ‘Parson Carryl?’

“‘Yes, Huldah?’

“‘I don’t believe Mis’ Carryl would have thought it improper for you to kiss the woman you’re goin’ to marry.’

“‘My dear,’ says he, and learned something no theological seminary had ever taught him.

“They didn’t tell the parish. Huldy said if folks had taken the trouble to invent the courtship, they needn’t be burdened with arrangin’ the weddin’. So the parson rode over to North Beulah, spoke to his old friend Dr. Marsh, and the next Tuesday mornin’ Huldy put on a dove-colored dress she’d made quiet in her own room. Enoch drove ’em over in the chaise, and Dr. Marsh married ’em with his wife and Enoch for witnesses.

“They came home by sunset. Huldy went straight into the kitchen and saw to the biscuit, because marryin’ don’t keep bread from burnin’. But that evenin’ the parson sat with her on the porch, and there warn’t much sermon writin’ done.

“Sunday mornin’ the meetin’-house was full. Rumor had got tired for lack of fresh feed, and Mis’ Pipperidge was watchin’ the door to see whether Huldy would have the face to come in after all the ‘concern.’

“The first bell rang. Deacon Blodgett stood in the aisle. Miss Asphyxia craned her neck. Mis’ Pipperidge sat stiff in the front side pew, her black eyes sharper than carpet tacks.

“Then the door opened.

“In walked Parson Carryl, dignified as ever, and Huldy beside him on his arm, wearin’ her dove-colored dress and a white bonnet with one small sprig of myrtle. They came up the aisle together.

“You could feel the congregation stop breathin’.

“The parson led Huldy into the minister’s pew, leaned down, and said something that made her smile. Then he went into the pulpit.

“Mis’ Pipperidge’s mouth opened. It shut. It opened again. Mis’ Blodgett dropped her hymnbook. Miss Asphyxia whispered, ‘What does it mean?’ loud enough to be heard three pews off.

“Arter the opening hymn, the parson looked over the congregation.

“‘Before proceeding to the discourse,’ says he, ‘I have a personal announcement. On Tuesday last, Huldah Greene and I were united in marriage by the Reverend Doctor Marsh.’

“There was a rustle like wind through dry corn.

“‘We have chosen to make the fact known here,’ he went on, ‘among our friends and parishioners, whose interest in the subject has, I understand, been unusually active.’

“Huldy looked down. Enoch coughed into his hand. I saw Deacon Grimes bite his lip till his chin shook.

“The parson preached that day from the text, ‘Whoso findeth a wife findeth a good thing.’ It was a warm, grateful sermon, with more earthly sense in it than some folks expected. He spoke of companionship, faithful service, kindness without display, and the duty of judging no heart by idle report. He never named Mis’ Pipperidge, but by the time he got through, she looked as if every word had found her pew without need of directions.

“Arter service everybody crowded round.

“‘How perfectly unexpected!’ says Mis’ Blodgett.

“‘Not altogether,’ says Miss Asphyxia, who’d predicted everything except that it would happen.

“‘My dear Mrs. Carryl,’ says Mis’ Pipperidge, takin’ Huldy’s hand between two fingers, ‘I trust you will be equal to the responsibilities of your new position.’

“Huldy smiled.

“‘I’ve had some practice with the house,’ says she.

“Then Mis’ Pipperidge turned to the parson. ‘You have certainly surprised us, sir.’

“‘Have I?’ says he, mild as milk.

“‘The parish has naturally felt a great interest.’

“‘Yes,’ says the parson. ‘I perceived that.’

“‘There has been—perhaps—some conversation.’

“‘So Huldah informed me.’

“Mis’ Pipperidge’s nose went a mite higher. ‘I hope you do not consider that any person has exceeded the bounds of proper concern.’

“The parson looked at her over his spectacles, and there was a little light in his eye that Huldy had put there.

“‘Not at all, madam,’ says he. ‘Since the parish appeared determined to talk about Huldah and me, I concluded it was only charitable to give them something worth talking about.’

“Wal, boys, that’s the whole on’t.”

Sam Lawson leaned back against the wall and pulled his hat over both eyes.

“Was Mis’ Pipperidge mad?” asked the smallest boy.

“Mad?” said Sam. “Lordy massy, she was sweet enough to preserve crab-apples in. That’s how some folks shows it. She called on Huldy the next week and gave her a worked pincushion with ‘Bless Our Home’ stitched crooked across it.”

“And did the parson ever meddle with the pigs again?” asked another.

“Never. He baptized babies, buried sinners, married fools and wise folks, preached election, free will, sanctification, and the final perseverance of the saints; but when it come to livestock, he held his peace. Experience had convicted him.

“And Huldy?” said the third boy.

“Oh, Huldy lived happy enough. She filled that old house with flowers and children and music, and the parson’s sermons kept on growin’ humaner every year. Folks came miles to hear him. They said he’d got a new understandin’ of grace.

“But, as I was a sayin’ when you boys switched me off the track, there’s more’n one kind of wisdom. Book wisdom can tell you why the stars move, and practical wisdom can tell you where not to put a pig. A household—or a church, for that matter—needs both.

“Now hadn’t you better pick some blueberries? Your mothers sent you up with quart pails, and between the three of you I don’t see enough berries to spot a dumplin’. Stories is fillin’ to the mind, but they don’t make much sauce for supper.”

The *Martha Jane* dropped down with the last of the ebb, her brown sail taking what little wind came over the marshes. Behind her, at the end of a long painter, the boat dragged and nodded through the grey water.

Captain Rudge stood at the helm. He was a broad, heavy man, red about the eyes from want of sleep, with a split in one sea-boot and a voice which carried from bank to bank. The mate, Ned Pell, was forward, coiling a line which had already been coiled twice.

“Make a worse job of it if you can,” said Rudge.

Pell made no answer.

“You hear me, you sulky lump?”

“I hear.”

“Then look alive. We ain’t carrying passengers.”

Pell put down the coil and went to the lee rigging. For three days they had scarcely spoken except to quarrel, and every order had become an insult before it reached him. He was a spare, dark young man, quick with his hands and slow to forget a word. The skipper had called him fool, dog, and dockside loafer since breakfast; and the day before, at Gravesend, had knocked a mug from his hand because the tea was cold.

The river widened. A chill rain began, very fine and straight, dimming the low banks and putting a skin upon the deck.

“Ease that sheet,” called Rudge.

Pell eased it.

“Not all of it, murdering idiot!”

“You said ease.”

“I said ease, not let go. D’you want her in the mud?”

Pell hauled in again. The skipper watched him, his thick lips moving.

“I’ve shipped bad mates,” he said, “but never one as needed his mother aboard to wipe his nose.”

The mate turned.

“Leave my mother out of it.”

“Ho! He can talk.”

“Leave her out.”

“I’ll say what I please aboard my own barge.”

Pell’s face had gone white. There was a handspike lying beside the windlass, wet and black at one end. He stooped and picked it up.

Rudge left the helm to the lashings and came forward two steps.

“Put that down.”

The rain tapped on the sail.

“Put it down, d’you hear?”

The captain reached for him. Pell swung once, blindly and with both hands. The handspike struck above Rudge’s ear with a dull sound. His knees bent; he staggered against the rail, caught it, and for an instant hung there looking astonished. Then the barge rolled under the press of sail, and he went backward into the river.

Pell stood with the handspike across his palms.

The skipper’s head showed once in the wake. His mouth opened, but the water filled it. He disappeared in a trough.

The mate dropped the handspike. It struck the deck and rolled into the scupper.

He ran aft. The helm was swinging loose, and the *Martha Jane* had begun to come up into the wind. Pell caught the tiller, his breath jerking in his throat. Behind, the empty water heaved under the rain. There was no sign of Rudge.

“Skipper!” he shouted.

Only the sail answered, slatting once before filling again.

Pell stared at the painter. The boat was fifty feet astern, rising upon a ridge of black water. Something moved beside it. A great hand came over the stern; then another. Rudge’s face appeared, streaming and bloody. He clung there, kicking. With a violent heave he rolled into the boat and lay in the bottom.

The mate’s grip loosened upon the helm.

Presently Rudge sat up. Blood ran from his hair to his cheek and thence upon his soaked collar. He felt the wound, looked at his red fingers, and then looked along the painter at Pell.

Neither spoke.

Pell made as if to shorten the line.

“Leave it!” roared the skipper.

His voice came thinly over the water, but there was no weakness in it.

“Skipper—”

“Leave that painter alone!”

“You’ll freeze.”

“I’d sooner freeze than have you finish the job.”

Pell held the barge on her course. The lights upon the bank faded one by one, and the rain thickened into darkness. Astern, Rudge sat in the boat with his back against a thwart, never taking his eyes from the barge.

After half an hour Pell called again.

“Come aboard. I won’t touch you.”

“Not yet.”

“I was mad.”

“You’ll have time to get sane in gaol.”

The mate said nothing.

“I’ll give you to the police at Littleport,” continued Rudge. “Attempted murder, that’s what. I’ve seen men sent off for less.”

“You can come aboard.”

“When I’m ready.”

The boat dipped out of sight and rose again. Rudge found the baler and emptied water over the side. Then he removed his neckerchief and bound it round his head. His movements were slow, but deliberate. Once the painter tightened with a crack, and Pell turned, thinking it had parted. The skipper laughed.

“Thought you was rid of me?”

“No.”

“Liar.”

Night settled on them. The banks became two deeper strips of blackness; the barge moved between them with the water hissing at her sides. Pell tended the helm, took in a little sail, and lighted the riding lamp with hands which shook so badly that he broke two matches. Whenever he looked astern, Rudge was there.

At ten o’clock the skipper shouted for food.

Pell put bread, cold meat, and a flask into a canvas bag. He bent a small line to it and paid it down the painter until Rudge caught it.

“I don’t want none of your kindness,” said Rudge.

“It’s your own meat.”

“That’s different.”

He ate everything and drank from the flask. Afterward he wrapped the canvas bag round his shoulders and crouched low in the stern.

Pell thought of slipping the painter. He thought of it whenever the darkness hid the boat. A knife lay in the locker beneath his hand; one stroke would do it, and by morning the tide might carry Rudge ten miles away. But he saw again the two hands reaching over the stern. He shut the locker.

Near midnight the wind freshened. The *Martha Jane* leaned until water ran along her lee deck. Pell took in sail alone, stumbling on the wet hatch-covers and looking aft between every pull. Rudge watched him labour and gave no advice. When the barge was shortened down and steady again, the skipper spoke.

“You’ll tell ’em it was an accident, I suppose.”

“No.”

“What’ll you tell ’em?”

“The truth.”

Rudge considered this.

“Won’t help you.”

“I know.”

“You cracked my skull and left me drowning.”

“I know.”

“No mercy,” said Rudge. “Not a grain.”

The mate returned to the helm.

Towards morning the rain ceased. Pell’s clothes were stiff upon him, and his eyes burned. Behind, the skipper began to move about the boat. He drew in the painter hand over hand, pausing whenever the barge pitched. Pell watched but did not interfere.

The boat came under the stern. Rudge stood upon the forward thwart, seized the rail, and tried to climb. His first effort failed. On the second he got one elbow over. Pell stepped forward to help.

“Stand off!”

Rudge kicked, heaved, and came over the rail like a landed fish. He lay on deck breathing heavily. Then he rose, took the helm from Pell, and struck him across the mouth with the back of his hand.

“That’s for now,” he said. “The police’ll do the rest.”

“Ay, ay.”

The skipper went forward, found the handspike in the scupper, and picked it up by the dry end. He examined the dark stain upon it.

“Evidence,” he said.

He wrapped it in a piece of sailcloth and put it in his bunk. Then he cleaned and bound his wound, changed his clothes, and made tea. He gave none to Pell until he had drunk two mugs himself.

“Littleport by evening,” he said. “I know the sergeant there. Fine big cell he’s got.”

Pell drank his tea standing.

The dawn came pale and cold. A low mist lay over the water, with the tops of reeds showing through it. The skipper’s face had swollen on one side, and the bandage made his head seem larger than ever. He was in good spirits now. Every hour he found a fresh description of the sentence awaiting the mate.

“Seven years, maybe,” he said. “Maybe ten. Depends if the magistrate likes your face.”

Pell worked without answering.

At noon the sun showed faintly, and the mist lifted. They passed a string of empty barges and a dredger at anchor. Rudge ate cheese and onion at the helm.

“See that?” he said suddenly.

Pell looked ahead. Something dark rolled in the trough between them and the bank. At first it seemed a bundle of clothes. Then an arm came clear of the water.

“Man overboard,” said Pell.

“Life-belt! Quick!”

The mate snatched the belt from the rail and threw it, but the floating man made no movement. The tide bore him past the belt, face downward.

“Bring her round,” said Rudge.

Pell ran to the sheets. The skipper thrust the helm over, and the *Martha Jane* came ponderously into the wind. The sail shook; blocks rattled; the boom swung.

“Boat,” said Rudge.

Together they hauled the boat alongside. Pell jumped in with the boathook while Rudge paid out the painter.

The man was already twenty yards astern. Pell pulled until his back cracked, reached him at last, and caught his collar with the hook. The body turned. Its face was blue-grey, the mouth open and the eyes shut.

“Easy!” shouted Rudge. “Don’t tear him loose.”

“He’s gone.”

“Get him in.”

Pell hooked an arm over the gunwale and pulled. The boat listed dangerously. Rudge hauled on the painter, bringing both boat and burden against the barge. He came down the side, and between them they dragged the man aboard.

“Anchor,” said the skipper.

They let go, took in sail, and carried the stranger below. He was a seaman, perhaps forty, with a fresh cut across one palm and tar upon his trousers. No breath stirred him.

“He’s dead,” said Pell.

“You a doctor now? Strip him.”

They stripped off the wet clothes and rolled him in blankets. Rudge rubbed the man’s chest with a rough towel while Pell worked his arms up and down. Water came from the stranger’s mouth.

“That’s it,” said Rudge. “Again.”

For twenty minutes they laboured. Nothing happened.

Pell’s shoulders ached. “It’s no use.”

“Again, I said.”

They went on. The cabin grew hot from the stove, and sweat stood on both their faces. Rudge knelt with his bandage slipping over one eye, cursing the stranger for a lazy hound and ordering him to breathe.

Suddenly the man gave a small cough.

Pell stopped.

“Again!” cried Rudge.

The chest moved. There was another cough, deeper than the first, and a shudder ran through the body. The skipper sat back upon his heels.

“There,” he said. “Told you.”

They gave the man a spoonful of hot spirits. He choked, swallowed, and opened his eyes for a moment.

“Where—?”

“Never mind where,” said Rudge. “You’re not dead. That’s enough for now.”

Half an hour passed. They weighed anchor and made sail again. The rescued man lay in Rudge’s bunk, breathing noisily, while the handspike, still wrapped in sailcloth, rested beneath it.

Pell took the helm. The skipper remained below, warming broth and changing the man’s blankets. When he came up, he looked tired.

“You sit with him,” he said. “If he wakes, gimme a shout.”

Pell went below.

The stranger slept. Once he muttered a woman’s name; once he began to shake so violently that Pell held him until it passed. The bundle beneath the bunk had worked partly into sight. Pell could see the black end of the handspike.

When Rudge relieved him, he followed the mate’s glance.

“Safe enough,” he said. “Police’ll want it.”

Pell nodded.

“No mercy,” added the skipper.

“Ay, ay.”

They took turns through the afternoon. The stranger revived slowly and at last told them that he had fallen from a timber lighter while clearing a fouled line. His name was Hobb, and he had a wife in Limehouse. Rudge told him to save his breath.

Towards dusk the lights of Littleport showed ahead, yellow and blurred in the damp air.

The mate saw them and became very quiet.

“Nearly there,” said Rudge.

“Yes.”

“Sergeant’s station just past the quay.”

“I know.”

“You won’t try running?”

“No.”

Rudge looked at him, then at the lights.

They brought the *Martha Jane* alongside without a word wasted. Pell sprang ashore with the line. Rudge checked her stern, and together they made the barge fast. Below, Hobb called weakly for water.

The skipper put on his coat and cap. He touched the bandage at his temple, then went to the bunk and drew out the wrapped handspike. For a moment he stood with it in his hand.

Pell waited by the hatch.

Rudge pushed the weapon deep beneath a coil of old rope.

“Go below,” he said. “See to him.”

The mate did not move.

“Well?”

“Ain’t you going to fetch them?”

“Fetch who?”

“The police.”

Rudge buttoned his coat.

“If anybody asks about my head, we had a row down Wapping. Three dock fellows set on us. You understand?”

Pell stared at him.

“And mind you say you gave a good account of yourself,” continued Rudge. “I won’t have people thinking I ship a useless mate.”

There was a thickness in Pell’s throat. He tried to speak, failed, and looked away toward the quay.

Rudge stepped ashore.

“No mercy,” he called back. “Not a grain.”

Then he turned and walked between the warehouses without once looking round.

Pell stood listening to his boots until the sound was lost among the carts. He drew one deep breath, shut his eyes, and opened them again.

From below came Hobb’s faint voice.

“Water, mate.”

Pell went down to him.

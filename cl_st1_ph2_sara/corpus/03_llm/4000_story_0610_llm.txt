I

At half-past nine on a Tuesday morning George Peel, the rising young sculptor, was eating kidneys in a pale blue dressing-gown while a considerable part of London had already performed enough labour to justify lunch.

The room in which he ate them overlooked Regent’s Park, and was furnished with that severe elegance which costs much more than mere comfort. There were grey walls, black chairs, a silver coffee-pot, two Japanese prints, and a small bronze figure by George himself, entitled *The Awakening of Labour*. Labour, represented by a young woman without sufficient clothes, was awakening beautifully upon a marble pedestal. George, represented by George, remained seated.

Opposite him Mary Peel administered porridge to their son.

The son, also George, but known as Georgie in order that the world might distinguish between genius and infancy, was eleven months old. He had his father’s brow, his mother’s eyes, and his own astonishing disregard for the decencies of life. At this moment he had placed one hand in the porridge and was considering whether the silver spoon might not be more profitably employed upon the table.

“Another letter from Uncle Samuel,” said Mary.

George looked at the envelope beside his plate.

“Is it?”

“You know it is.”

“I thought it might be a commission.”

Mary did not answer. A wife learns early which of her husband’s statements are intended for belief, which for admiration, and which merely to keep the air occupied. She wiped Georgie’s fingers. Georgie immediately replaced them in the porridge.

The envelope was long, grey, and austere. Samuel Peel’s handwriting had the disciplined appearance of having renounced pleasure in youth and regretted nothing since. Even the stamp seemed conscious that public expenditure ought to be watched.

George opened the letter with a fruit knife.

“My dear Niece,” he read aloud. “He always begins with you when he means me.”

“Go on.”

George went on.

Samuel acknowledged Mary’s request for a temporary accommodation of two hundred pounds. He regretted that he could not accede to it. He had already made, during the five years of her marriage, an annual contribution of five hundred pounds toward the maintenance of her household. He had done this, he said, because he respected art, because Mary was the only child of his late sister, and because he had understood that a modest assistance would enable her husband to establish himself in an honourable profession.

George paused.

“Well, it has,” he said.

Mary glanced around the room. It was undeniable that he had established himself. The question was whether he could continue to inhabit the establishment.

The letter proceeded. Samuel had lately observed, in a London weekly, an account of Mr. George Peel’s celebration following the award of the International Gold Medal for Sculpture at Brussels. The report had mentioned “champagne flowing freely in the spacious studio,” together with music, distinguished guests, and supper at midnight. Samuel could not reconcile such ostentation with a request for further help.

“Champagne!” George exclaimed. “It was not even good champagne.”

Mary looked at him.

“That is not perhaps the strongest answer.”

“It shows the report was inaccurate.”

Georgie struck the table with his spoon. He had no views upon journalism, but strongly supported noise.

Samuel concluded that, since five hundred pounds a year appeared to encourage habits neither prudent nor dignified, the allowance would in future be reduced to three hundred pounds, payable quarterly. He remained Mary’s affectionate uncle, Samuel Peel.

For several seconds George held the letter motionless.

Men no longer gasp from astonishment. They ceased to do so, with cloaks and swords, about two centuries ago. George said:

“Well!”

Mary rescued the coffee-pot from Georgie.

“What shall we do?”

“Do? My dear girl, there is nothing to do.”

“Then why are you looking like that?”

“Like what?”

“As if you had found a crack in a statue after the exhibition had opened.”

George folded the letter with exaggerated precision. He was thirty-three, handsome in a broad and rather unfinished manner, with fair hair that fell into the proper disorder without assistance. Critics had called his work vigorous, elemental, modern, classical, revolutionary, and sound. This diversity of opinion had pleased him. No critic had yet called it remunerative.

“He can’t reduce it,” George said.

“He has.”

“He can’t morally reduce it.”

“He has not reduced it morally. He has reduced it by two hundred pounds.”

“That old paragraph! I told Pemberton not to mention the champagne.”

“You told him twice, in front of six people.”

“A man must keep himself before the public.”

“And his creditors?”

“Especially his creditors. Credit depends upon confidence.”

Mary considered this maxim. It had financed their curtains, a motor excursion to Brighton, three months of a French cook, and the carved stone fireplace in George’s studio. Its authority was therefore not negligible.

At that instant the maid entered with two more letters upon a tray. One bore the crest of a West End dealer. George seized it.

The dealer regretted that, owing to prevailing uncertainty in the market, Sir John Farleigh had decided not to proceed with the proposed memorial group for his country estate. He hoped, however, to retain the sketches for future consideration.

The second letter came from a firm of builders demanding payment of forty-seven pounds, eighteen shillings, and sixpence.

Georgie dropped his spoon.

The spoon fell into the milk-jug, an event which no competent historian could connect with the financial crisis, though it expressed the general situation perfectly.

Mary said, very quietly:

“How much have we?”

“Immediately?”

“Altogether.”

George rose and walked to the window. Outside, the park displayed that air of untroubled solvency peculiar to public property.

“There are assets,” he said.

“What assets?”

“My work.”

“Yes.”

“The Brussels medal.”

“We cannot eat it.”

“It is solid gold.”

“It is gilt bronze. You told me so.”

George turned. A less gifted man might have been embarrassed. George, who had made a profession out of giving shape to awkward material, merely altered the shape.

“This is temporary,” he said. “All artistic careers have a period of compression. It precedes the great expansion.”

Mary fed Georgie the last spoonful of porridge.

“The rent is due on Friday.”

“Exactly. A crisis is often the necessary condition of a decisive idea.”

“And have you one?”

“No,” said George. “But it is only half-past nine.”

II

The circumstances which had brought a rising young sculptor, an heiress’s niece, and an infant with porridge on his left ear to this distressing conjunction must now be explained. Money troubles are never simple. If they were simple, people would avoid them, and half the most respectable households in England would lose their chief intellectual occupation.

Mary’s father, an amiable solicitor, had died when she was seventeen, leaving less than had been expected and more confusion than was decent. Her mother survived him by three years. Samuel Peel, the mother’s elder brother, then assumed the responsibilities of family guardian with a solemnity which suggested that Providence had appointed him by registered post.

Samuel lived at Bursley, a manufacturing town in the Midlands. He owned several rows of cottages, shares in two pottery concerns, a large square house named Elm Lodge, and opinions upon nearly everything. He was a widower, a deacon, a temperance advocate, a trustee of the Municipal Institute, and, at the time of our story, a candidate for the Town Council. He was sixty-two. His collars were high, his boots were polished, and his charity was exact.

He had meant Mary to marry a dependable man—preferably a solicitor, a manufacturer, or a person connected with insurance. Instead she married George.

George’s father had been a schoolmaster with romantic tastes and no capital. George, having modelled the head of a school porter at the age of sixteen, was sent to London, where he won prizes, borrowed money, and acquired friends. He had talent. Let us admit this at once, for otherwise the whole comedy becomes too easy. His portrait busts possessed force; his figures had movement; and when he spoke of mass, rhythm, and the obedience of stone, he spoke with knowledge. Unfortunately stone did not always obey, and clients obeyed even less.

George met Mary at an exhibition. She admired a small bronze of his called *Youth Defying Circumstance*. George admired her profile, her composure, and—after discreet inquiry—the existence of Samuel Peel.

Their marriage was opposed by Samuel during four exhausting interviews and accepted during the fifth. Samuel eventually offered Mary five hundred pounds a year, declaring that this was not an allowance to George, but a provision for his niece. George agreed emphatically. A provision for one’s wife is wholly different from an allowance to oneself, particularly when paid into a joint account.

Five hundred pounds was then a handsome sum. It remained a handsome sum throughout the marriage. But handsome sums, like handsome men, may become unreliable if asked to do too much.

George had begun with the reasonable theory that an artist must work. He next adopted the more advanced theory that an artist must be seen to work. Finally he reached the mature conviction that an artist’s visible circumstances were part of his work. A sculptor in lodgings might produce masterpieces, but no millionaire wished to climb three flights of stairs to discover them. Therefore George leased a large studio. A patron expected wine; therefore George supplied wine. Critics expected confidence; therefore he dressed well. Society expected originality; therefore he gave suppers at inconvenient hours.

Every expenditure was a professional necessity until the bill arrived, when it became an investment.

For a time the principle succeeded. George made a bust of Lady Fancourt, a memorial angel for a family in Surrey, and a bronze athlete which the public described as audacious because it was unclothed and critics described as derivative because they had seen Greeks. His name appeared in newspapers. Photographs showed him in a white coat, standing amid heroic torsos and buckets. He was spoken of as “the rising young sculptor, George Peel.”

To rise is admirable; but a man cannot indefinitely rise without arriving somewhere, and the newspapers never stated where George had arrived.

The Brussels medal was the summit of his publicity and the beginning of his insolvency. He celebrated because celebration was expected. The celebrated were expected because there was champagne. The champagne merchant, having read of the international honour, allowed generous credit. Thus prestige nourished credit, credit nourished champagne, and champagne nourished prestige—a beautiful circular system, lacking only money.

Mary understood more of this than George supposed. She did not enjoy debt, but she enjoyed George; and a woman in love may support a theory long after she has ceased to believe it. She also knew that Samuel’s allowance had made possible not merely luxuries but George’s genuine work. Without it, he would have been forced to produce sentimental children, civic busts, and decorative nymphs for suburban gardens. George despised such things in conversation, though he would have accepted commissions for all three by return of post.

The loss of two hundred pounds a year was therefore not a reduction. It was an avalanche.

For two days George behaved with impressive normality. He went to the studio, rejected a proposed alteration to a clay figure for being “commercial,” lunched at his club, and asked the secretary whether any letters had arrived for him. None had. On Thursday he called upon two dealers. One had gone to Paris. The other was in, but regretted it.

That evening George and Mary held a council of war in the drawing-room. Georgie, who had not been invited, attended in a woollen nightgown and attempted to eat a corner of the builders’ account.

“We can move,” Mary said.

“From here?”

“To somewhere cheaper.”

“Impossible.”

“We can reduce the servants.”

“We have already reduced them.”

“We have a cook and a housemaid.”

“A cook is not a servant. A cook is the basis of civilization.”

“We could sell the motor.”

“It is not ours.”

“Then that economy has already been achieved.”

George paced between the fireplace and *The Awakening of Labour*.

“What did you say to your uncle in your letter?”

“That we had exceptional expenses and needed two hundred pounds.”

“Did you tell him about the Farleigh commission?”

“It had not been refused then.”

“Did you appeal to his affection?”

“I said I hoped he would help me.”

“Too mild. Samuel must be approached through his principles.”

“He has a great many.”

“Exactly. Somewhere among them there will be a weak one.”

Mary folded her sewing.

“You are not to quarrel with him.”

“My dear, a quarrel would be fatal. He would enjoy it. Samuel has been preparing moral arguments against me since our engagement.”

“You called him a provincial Pharisee at Christmas.”

“Privately.”

“He was in the hall.”

“That was architectural misfortune.”

The following morning George received a note from his bank. It was polite, brief, and devastating. Politeness from a bank is frequently the last attention before burial.

At lunch he ate nothing. At tea he ate three scones and suddenly asked:

“When is the Bursley election?”

“Next month.”

“And Uncle Samuel is standing for the South Ward?”

“Yes.”

“Temperance and Municipal Economy?”

“Yes.”

George stared into the fire.

“The Tiger,” he said.

“What about it?”

“Is it still opposite the market?”

“I suppose so.”

“Respectable?”

“Quite.”

“Bar?”

“Of course it has a bar. It is a hotel.”

George smiled.

Mary knew that smile. It had preceded their marriage, the Brussels celebration, and the purchase of a twelve-foot block of Carrara marble which still stood untouched in the studio because George’s inspiration had altered after delivery.

“What are you thinking?”

“I am thinking,” he said, “that Samuel is not a hard man. He is only a man who must be enabled to do right without appearing weak.”

“How?”

“Pack for Bursley.”

Mary looked at him.

“With Georgie?”

“Especially with Georgie.”

From the hearthrug Georgie sneezed. He had no notion that he had just been appointed to a leading political rôle.

III

George’s plan, when disclosed, seemed to Mary outrageous, theatrical, uncertain, and expensive. These were not, in George’s mind, objections. They were the ordinary characteristics of any enterprise worth undertaking.

“We cannot go to the Tiger merely to annoy Uncle Samuel,” she said.

“My dear Mary, we are going there because we can no longer afford London.”

“We cannot afford a hotel.”

“The Tiger is cheaper than ruin in Regent’s Park.”

“Not much.”

“Appearances must be maintained.”

“I thought appearances were the cause of the trouble.”

“They are also the cure. Poison and medicine are often the same thing in different bottles.”

Mary declined to admire this observation.

George proposed that they should take sufficient luggage to imply permanence, the perambulator to guarantee visibility, and the child because leaving an eleven-month-old infant alone in London would expose them to criticism from quarters more formidable than Samuel. George would inform the servants that he had obtained industrial work in Bursley. He would say the same thing in Bursley itself. A rising young sculptor abandoning Art for the potteries was a tragedy; a nephew by marriage driven into a public-house hotel by family parsimony was a scandal; and Samuel Peel, candidate for the South Ward upon a platform of temperance, domestic virtue, and efficient relief, could not endure both at once.

“The Tiger is not a public-house hotel,” Mary protested. “It is perfectly respectable.”

Of course it is perfectly respectable. The Tiger had bedrooms with mahogany furniture, a dining-room where cold beef appeared under a silver cover, and a hall porter whose dignity equalled that of an ambassador from a minor power. But respectability is a delicate fabric. To a temperance reformer, any building containing a bar is morally all bar; and to the public, a bar is always the most memorable part of a respectable hotel.

“Uncle Samuel will see through you,” said Mary.

“He has never yet seen through me.”

“He reduced the allowance.”

“He saw the champagne. That is different.”

“And if he refuses?”

“Then I shall seek employment.”

“In Bursley?”

“In the industries.”

George pronounced “the industries” as an explorer might have pronounced “the interior.”

“What can you do in a pottery?”

“Sculpture is modelling. Pottery is modelling. I may transform English ceramics.”

“You do not know anything about firing.”

“I shall begin with design.”

“Do you know anything about design for pottery?”

George frowned.

“A man of general artistic training—”

“No, then.”

“No specialist permits that question to be answered simply.”

Mary walked to the window. She was distressed, but not so distressed as she had been before George conceived the plan. Activity, however absurd, gives poverty a temporary air of adventure.

“And what am I to say?”

“The truth.”

“Which truth?”

“That Samuel’s remarks about the champagne wounded you profoundly.”

“They did not.”

“They will, on reflection.”

Mary laughed. This encouraged George more than agreement would have done.

They left London on Saturday. George travelled first class because he might meet someone, and because third class, in his opinion, represented panic. The luggage went separately. Georgie occupied more space than either parent and accepted none of the responsibility.

During the journey George elaborated his future in the potteries. He would first secure a position at four pounds a week; within six months he would become art director; within two years the firm would issue a Peel Series of modern ware; in five years—

“In five years,” Mary said, “Uncle Samuel will be sixty-seven.”

George stopped.

“That is not relevant.”

“It is the most relevant fact in our finances.”

George rebuked the observation as cynical, chiefly because it was true.

Bursley received them under a yellow-grey afternoon sky. Kilns and chimneys rose beyond the station; carts rattled; the air contained smoke, clay, and commercial purpose. George, who had been born in a cathedral town and educated in London, surveyed the place as if industrial England had been arranged for his inspection.

The Tiger’s omnibus carried them to the market square. Their arrival was observed by a tobacconist, two shop assistants, a retired cashier, the hotel porter, and Mrs. Dain of Grove Street, who had known Mary’s mother. In London this would not have constituted publicity. In Bursley it was the first edition.

George registered in a large hand:

**Mr. and Mrs. George Peel, Child and Nurse—London.**

They had no nurse. George explained that the entry was anticipatory; Mary explained that it was false; the hall porter, who heard neither explanation, judged that the nurse had missed the train.

By six o’clock it was known in three streets that Samuel Peel’s niece had come from London with a baby and no nurse and was staying at the Tiger. By eight, George had abandoned sculpture. By nine, he had lost all his money. On Sunday morning, according to a report in the chapel vestibule, he had been offered a place painting beer signs.

Samuel Peel heard nothing until Sunday afternoon. Men of importance are often the last to receive local intelligence because everyone assumes they already possess it.

He was in his study reviewing an election address. Above the mantelpiece hung a portrait of his late wife, who had been gentle, humorous, and wholly incapable of preventing him from becoming Samuel Peel. On the desk lay proofs bearing the heading:

**PURITY, ECONOMY, AND THE HOME.  
VOTE FOR PEEL.**

His housekeeper entered.

“Mr. Peel, is it true Miss Mary’s staying at the Tiger?”

Samuel looked up.

“What?”

“With her husband and the baby.”

“Certainly not.”

“Mrs. Dain said she saw them arrive.”

“Then Mrs. Dain was mistaken.”

“She spoke to Miss Mary.”

Samuel removed his spectacles. He did not gasp. He said:

“This is perfectly ridiculous.”

The housekeeper waited.

“Why should they stay at the Tiger?” he demanded.

“I couldn’t say, sir.”

“They have a home in London.”

“So I understood.”

“And relatives here.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Then it is perfectly ridiculous.”

By Monday morning Samuel had confirmed the ridiculous fact. He sent no message. To send a message would imply concern; to call at the Tiger would imply approval; to invite them to Elm Lodge would imply surrender. Samuel was not a man who surrendered before luncheon.

At eleven he went to the Town Hall on business. He chose a route which avoided the Tiger. Bursley’s streets, unfortunately, had been laid out before his candidature and did not cooperate. At twelve-fifteen, emerging into the crowded market square, he saw George Peel advancing toward him with a perambulator.

George wore a dark coat, spats, and an expression of grave endurance. He looked less like a ruined artist than any ruined artist whom Bursley had ever hoped to see.

“Samuel!” he called.

Samuel could not escape without running, and candidates do not run in market squares unless pursued by animals.

“George,” he said. “I understand you are here.”

“Yes. Mary too.”

“So I am informed.”

“Can’t stop. Appointment at one of the works.”

“What works?”

“I’ll explain later. Hang on to this pram a sec.”

Before Samuel had decided whether the phrase was a request, a command, or evidence of insanity, the handle of the perambulator was in his hands.

“George!”

“Only a minute.”

George darted into the Tiger.

Samuel stood.

Inside the perambulator, Georgie opened his eyes and beheld a large grey face under a hard hat. He considered this personage without enthusiasm. Samuel looked down at the child. He had not held, wheeled, fed, washed, or negotiated with a baby for thirty years. He believed that infants, like foreign nations, ought to be treated kindly but kept under proper administration.

“Good child,” he said.

Georgie’s lower lip advanced.

“Now, now.”

Georgie began to cry.

IV

A baby crying in solitude is a domestic inconvenience. A baby crying in a crowded marketplace under the charge of a municipal candidate is a public statement.

Heads turned. The tobacconist came to his doorway. Two women paused beside a vegetable stall. A cabman grinned.

“Got a new job, Mr. Peel?” he called.

Samuel pushed the perambulator gently. It struck the curb.

Georgie yelled.

Samuel pulled it back. One wheel mounted the boot of a butcher’s boy, who apologized—out of respect for Samuel, not from any conviction of guilt.

“Whose little ’un?” asked a woman.

“My niece’s,” Samuel said.

The answer was a tactical error. A man wheeling an unknown baby may be philanthropic; a man wheeling his niece’s baby outside a hotel has family complications.

“Ah!” said the woman.

No syllable in the English language contains more biography than a properly delivered “Ah!”

Samuel looked toward the Tiger’s door. George had vanished. Through the glass he could see the hall, the stairs, and a waiter carrying bottles. Bottles! At twelve-twenty in the day, under the very shadow of his election posters.

Georgie continued.

Samuel pushed the perambulator up and down. His movement attracted more notice than stillness had done. From a distance he now appeared to be deliberately promenading the infant. A fellow trustee of the Municipal Institute approached.

“Morning, Peel. Grandchild?”

“No.”

“Thought you had none.”

“I have none.”

The trustee peered under the hood.

“Fine lad.”

“He is my niece’s child.”

“Staying with you?”

“No.”

“Ah!”

Again the syllable.

“They are temporarily at the Tiger,” Samuel added, seeking clarity and achieving disaster.

The trustee raised his eyebrows.

“At the Tiger?”

“Only temporarily.”

“Of course, of course.”

There are occasions when “of course” means that nothing in the universe is less credible.

George reappeared at last, adjusting his gloves.

“Thanks,” he said. “Knew you wouldn’t mind.”

“What is the meaning of this?” Samuel demanded in a low voice.

“Of what?”

“Leaving this child with me in the public street!”

“I had to see a man.”

“In the Tiger?”

“He was waiting there.”

“What man?”

“Industrial connection.”

Georgie, recognizing his father’s voice, ceased crying. This sudden silence suggested that Samuel had been personally responsible for the previous uproar.

“You will take this perambulator at once.”

“Gladly. You ought to come in and see Mary.”

“I shall do nothing of the sort.”

“Very well.”

George took the handle.

Samuel hesitated. He could feel the market observing the quarrel. Not openly; Bursley had manners. The town merely purchased vegetables at a much slower rate.

“Why,” Samuel asked, “are you staying at an hotel when you have relatives in the town?”

George looked surprised.

“We did not care to presume.”

“You might have informed me.”

“After your letter?”

“My letter concerned finance.”

“It concerned champagne.”

“It concerned extravagance.”

“Mary took it personally.”

“Why should Mary take it personally?”

“You addressed it to her.”

Samuel’s lips tightened.

“This is perfectly ridiculous.”

“Quite,” George agreed. “But feelings often are.”

“What is this nonsense about industrial work?”

“No nonsense. I’ve done with sculpture.”

Samuel stared at him.

“You have won an international medal.”

“A medal is not a meal, Samuel.”

The phrase was prepared, but Samuel did not know that.

“I understood commissions were plentiful.”

“So did I. That was the mistake.”

“You have been living beyond your means.”

“Not now. We shall live in Bursley. Cheap rooms, perhaps. I can design tiles, sanitary ware, mantel ornaments—whatever the old town requires. Something in the honest industrial line. Four pounds a week would be wealth.”

Samuel glanced about him. The trustee was still near the cab rank. The woman who had said “Ah!” was selecting onions without looking at them. Above the Tiger entrance hung a gilded animal of uncertain zoology and unmistakable publicity.

“You cannot remain at this hotel,” Samuel said.

“It is perfectly respectable.”

“That is not the point.”

“It has a very good table.”

“That is not the point either.”

“And Mary likes the room.”

“George, do not be absurd.”

“I don’t care twopence where we stay. I’m merely considering her. She’s upset.”

“About a reasonable letter?”

“I said it was reasonable.”

“You did?”

“Entirely. You may be hard up yourself. Investments fluctuate. Public men have expenses. Five hundred a year is a great deal.”

Samuel coloured.

“I am not hard up.”

“I’m glad.”

“The reduction was a matter of principle.”

“Exactly. We respect principle.”

Samuel regarded him. George’s expression was open, manly, and false.

“And you propose to abandon your profession?”

“No choice. Prestige doesn’t pay the milkman. I’ve been up a gum-tree for months.”

“And Mary?”

“She’ll manage. She always does.”

This simple tribute, unlike George’s calculated phrases, affected Samuel. He was fond of Mary, though his fondness had to pass through so many committees of judgment before reaching her that it often arrived disguised as advice.

“Where is she?”

“Upstairs.”

“With no nurse?”

“We dismissed the nurse.”

“You wrote that the child required special care.”

“All children require special care. Georgie requires it noisily.”

As if invoked, Georgie began to beat the side of the perambulator with a wooden ring.

Samuel made a decision.

“Take me to Mary.”

George did not smile. It was among his genuine artistic gifts that he knew when a composition would be ruined by an additional touch.

They entered the Tiger together, Samuel walking half a pace ahead to indicate that he was not being led. The hall porter bowed. Two commercial travellers rose from chairs. Behind a glazed partition the barmaid caught sight of Samuel Peel proceeding toward the staircase beside a perambulator. Her professional training enabled her to preserve silence until he had passed.

Then she said, “Well!”

Upstairs, Mary occupied a sitting-room overlooking the square. She had been waiting for forty minutes, during which she had repented the scheme, defended it, condemned George, excused Samuel, and unpacked Georgie’s woollen shirts. When Samuel entered, she stood.

“My dear Mary,” he began.

“Uncle.”

“What is all this?”

Mary looked at George. George looked at Georgie. Georgie looked at Samuel’s watch-chain.

“We had to come somewhere,” Mary said.

“But why here?”

“George hopes to find work.”

“In Bursley?”

“He says there may be an opening in pottery design.”

Samuel turned upon George.

“There is no opening.”

“Then I shall make one.”

“You know nothing of the trade.”

“I learn rapidly.”

“You cannot drag Mary and the child about the country while you learn rapidly.”

George appeared wounded by the phrase “drag about,” though it was a fair description of the railway journey.

“We cannot afford London,” Mary said.

“I never suggested you could not afford London.”

“The allowance—”

“Three hundred pounds is not poverty.”

“No,” Mary agreed. “But it does not cover the rent.”

“Then the rent is too high.”

“It is.”

Samuel was checked by this ready admission. He had expected defence and had prepared an attack. Agreement left him holding the weapon awkwardly.

“George’s studio is part of his profession,” Mary continued. “Or was.”

“Was?”

“He is giving it up.”

Samuel walked to the window. Below, the market circulated round its new information. He could already imagine the account. Peel’s niece at the Tiger. Husband ruined. Baby handed about in street. Family quarrel. Champagne. Temperance. Election. The human mind, particularly the Bursley mind, could connect these items with a rapidity denied to logic.

“There need have been no question of giving up sculpture,” he said.

George remained silent. This was difficult, and therefore impressive.

“I intended only,” Samuel continued, “to mark my disapproval of ostentation.”

“You marked it very clearly,” Mary said.

She spoke without reproach. Samuel would have preferred reproach. Quietness made his action appear, for the first time, not principled but consequential.

“The newspaper may have exaggerated,” he conceded.

“It did,” George said. “The champagne did not flow. It stood about in glasses and became warm.”

Samuel ignored him.

“Nevertheless, there must be economy.”

“Certainly,” Mary said.

“And proper accounts.”

“Yes.”

“And no more midnight entertainments.”

George moved slightly.

“Within reason,” Samuel added, because even Samuel understood that an artist forbidden all entertaining might cease to be an artist and become merely unemployed.

Georgie leaned from Mary’s arms toward the watch-chain. Samuel, after a hesitation, allowed him to grasp it. The child pulled with the concentrated force of a dock labourer. Samuel’s dignity descended two inches.

“You cannot stay here,” he said.

“We can look for rooms tomorrow,” said George.

“You will do nothing of the kind. You will come to Elm Lodge.”

Mary’s face altered.

“All of us?”

“Of course all of you. I cannot have my niece living in an hotel.”

“The Tiger is perfectly respectable,” George murmured.

Samuel turned.

“Will you stop saying that?”

George stopped.

Within an hour their luggage was being transferred to Elm Lodge. This speed was not due to George, Mary, Samuel, the hotel, or the railway company individually. It was due to scandal, which remains the most efficient transport service in provincial England.

At dinner Samuel laid down conditions. George was to return to London on Wednesday. He was to retain the studio for three months while seeking commissions. The allowance of five hundred pounds would continue, subject to economy. As to the requested two hundred pounds, Samuel would advance it—not as a gift, not as an encouragement to extravagance, and certainly not because pressure had been applied, but in order that immediate obligations might be honourably met.

“Thank you,” Mary said.

George said nothing for a moment, which was his finest performance of the week.

“I shall repay you,” he said at last.

Samuel looked at him.

“No date was mentioned,” George added.

The two hundred pounds was paid by cheque on Wednesday morning. George placed it in his pocket with the grave air of a statesman receiving a treaty. He kissed Mary, admonished Georgie to respect his mother, thanked Samuel for hospitality, and departed for London by the eleven-ten.

Before the train reached Rugby, he had planned a new series of small bronzes which could be produced cheaply and sold through a Bond Street dealer. Before Euston, the bronzes had become a major artistic movement. By the time he entered his studio, he had decided to hold a modest supper to announce them.

It would not, he assured himself, involve champagne.

Samuel won the South Ward by eighty-three votes. Whether the incident at the Tiger harmed him cannot be established. His supporters maintained that the sight of a public man managing an infant in a crisis proved his practical humanity. His opponents maintained other things, usually after closing time.

The allowance continued at five hundred pounds. George’s new series sold moderately well. He did not repay the two hundred pounds that year, nor the next; but he modelled a remarkably forceful bust of Samuel for the Municipal Institute, and Samuel, after objecting to the mouth, secretly admired it.

Bursley remembered longer than either man desired.

For years the barmaid at the Tiger—who became in due course the landlady—would tell favoured customers how Councillor Peel had once marched through the hotel with a baby, after being found wheeling it outside the bar while his artistic nephew hid within.

This was inaccurate in four particulars.

It was therefore accepted as history.

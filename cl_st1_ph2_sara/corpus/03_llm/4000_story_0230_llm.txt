### The Beetle Who Deserved Golden Shoes

There was once an Emperor who possessed a horse more splendid than any other horse beneath the sun. It had carried him through smoke and sword-strokes when kingdoms hung upon an hour; it had leaped a broken bridge while the enemy thundered behind; and on the day of victory it had borne the Emperor home with a torn bridle, a blood-stained saddle, and such a calm look in its large brown eyes that all the court wept and shouted at once.

Therefore the Emperor ordered golden shoes for the horse.

They were not made of solid gold, for gold is too soft for roads, but they were of strong iron overlaid with gold, and they flashed whenever the horse lifted its feet. The master smith fitted them in the royal stable, while ministers, grooms, pages, and two poets stood near and praised the Emperor’s gratitude.

A black beetle also lived in that stable. He belonged to an ancient family, as he frequently said, though no one had asked him; and because he had been born beneath the Emperor’s own roof, he considered himself attached to the court.

When he saw the golden shoes, he hurried to the smith.

“I must be shod too!” he cried.

“With what?” asked the smith.

“With gold, of course. Pray do not pretend ignorance. If the horse receives four golden shoes, I ought at least to receive six.”

The smith stared at him, then laughed so loudly that a hammer fell from the bench.

“Thou!” he said. “Golden shoes for thee!”

“Why not? Have I not six feet? Do I not live in the imperial stable? Have I not walked under the imperial horse?”

“That may be,” replied the smith, “but the horse carried His Majesty through battle.”

“And I,” said the beetle, swelling with indignation, “have carried myself through life, which is no small matter.”

The smith lifted him with two fingers and set him outside the door.

“There is thy shoeing,” he said. “It costs nothing.”

Our beetle was deeply offended. Persons conscious of their own greatness are often wounded by the smallest misunderstanding.

“This court has become corrupt,” he declared. “Merit is ignored, and size is everything. I shall depart upon a secret embassy and seek my fortune elsewhere. When they discover whom they have lost, it will be too late.”

He took no leave of the horse, the groom, or the other beetles, though taking leave is considered a polite thing to do. He merely spread his wings and flew away.

He came first to a garden where the morning sun shone through the leaves, and every drop of dew held a little rainbow. Red tulips stood like cups of flame; white lilies breathed sweetness; and the gravel walks ran between beds of blue, gold, and violet.

Two ladybirds sat upon a rose.

“What a glorious world!” said one.

“How good it is to live among fragrance and colour!” said the other.

The beetle landed heavily on the path.

“Fragrance?” he repeated. “I smell nothing useful.”

The ladybirds looked at him.

“Perhaps you have come from far away,” said the first.

“From the Emperor’s stable,” replied the beetle. “I am accustomed to royal surroundings.”

“How grand!” cried the second. “Then surely the gardens there are finer than this.”

“Gardens!” said he. “No respectable creature lives upon flower beds. Give me a warm corner, a sound roof, and something properly decayed. These blossoms are painted rubbish. They neither shelter nor nourish a person of consequence.”

“But see how the sun shines!” said the ladybirds.

“The sun is extremely forward,” answered the beetle. “It shines upon everybody without distinction. That alone proves its lack of breeding.”

He marched away, while the ladybirds whispered that travellers were curious people. The beetle heard them and decided that garden insects were jealous of court society.

By noon the sun grew hot. He crossed a smooth lawn, which to him was a vast green wilderness, and every blade of grass stood higher than a tree. He stumbled among roots, fell into a footprint, climbed out, and complained continually.

“This country has no roads,” he said. “One cannot expect civilisation so far from the palace.”

At last a cloud covered the sky. Thunder rolled; rain descended in great silver rods; and the beetle was struck upon the back by drops as large, in his opinion, as millstones. He ran beneath a broken flowerpot, where a frog was already sitting in the wet earth.

“Delightful weather!” cried the frog. “It refreshes the world.”

“It is an outrage,” said the beetle, shaking his wings. “The heavens should be better governed.”

“The rain fills the ditches,” continued the frog, “and makes the grass sing.”

“Grass does not sing.”

“It does if one understands it.”

“Then I am thankful not to understand it,” replied the beetle. “At court we have music performed by musicians.”

The frog blinked.

“Hast thou truly lived at court?”

“I was born in the Emperor’s stable. Golden shoes are worn there.”

“By thee?”

The beetle coughed.

“That is a delicate political matter. I left before certain arrangements were completed.”

The rain entered beneath the flowerpot and formed a puddle around his feet. The frog splashed joyfully, but the beetle hurried away, calling the place unfit for a gentleman.

Night overtook him near a hedge. The wind shook the branches, and the storm returned with such fury that leaves flew like green birds and twigs came tumbling down. Our traveller was rolled over three times, buried in mud, and driven into a crack beneath an old board.

There he found two earwigs and their many children.

“Welcome, stranger,” said the mother earwig. “It is crowded, but there is always room for one more in bad weather.”

The little earwigs pressed close together and stared politely.

“Are all these yours?” asked the beetle.

“Every one,” replied the mother proudly. “See how straight their feelers are! And such clever pincers!”

The father earwig nodded.

“The youngest already knows how to hide beneath bark,” he said. “We expect a brilliant career.”

The beetle examined the children.

“They look exactly alike.”

“Not to a parent,” said the mother.

“One has a noble forehead,” said the father, “and another has uncommon depth of character.”

“They have no rank,” replied the beetle. “Birth is everything. I myself come from the imperial stable.”

“How fortunate,” said the mother. “Then perhaps thou hast children at court.”

“I have been too occupied with public affairs.”

“Or a wife?”

“A person in my position must be cautious. Many marry for title.”

The earwigs exchanged a glance, but gave him the driest corner and shared their supper with him. In the morning the storm had passed, and the air was clean and bright.

“You may remain until your wings are dry,” said the mother.

“I could not possibly,” answered the beetle. “Family life is noisy, and your young people display no respect for distinctions.”

He went away without thanking them.

Beyond the hedge lay a ditch where the rainwater had gathered among reeds and scraps of straw. Here he discovered three beetles of his own kind, all black, round, and shining. They were rolling a fine ball through the mud and appeared contented.

“At last!” cried our friend. “Respectable society!”

The others greeted him and asked where he had come from.

“The Emperor’s stable,” he replied.

They stopped rolling.

“Indeed!” said the eldest. “We have heard of the Emperor.”

“Most creatures have,” said the traveller modestly. “He and I occupied the same establishment.”

“Was it pleasant?”

“Until favouritism destroyed everything. A horse received golden shoes, while I, whose feet are more numerous, received none.”

The three beetles agreed that this was unjust, particularly when he explained the matter several times. They knew nothing of battles or bridges, but they understood feet.

“Remain with us,” said the eldest. “The mud is excellent, and yonder heap has been improving for weeks.”

The beetle inhaled.

“There is a truly civilised fragrance here,” he said. “The world may yet be saved.”

Among the ditch beetles was a young maiden with a polished back and graceful feelers. She admired travellers, and he admired being admired. Before evening he had told her about the palace, the smith, the golden shoes, the garden, the storm, and his secret embassy. He omitted his fall into the footprint and made the frog appear an ambassador.

“You must have been in terrible danger,” she said.

“Danger and I are familiar companions,” he replied. “Yet I do not speak of it.”

He had done little else for two hours.

The maiden’s mother invited him to supper. Her father asked about his intentions. Our beetle, warmed by good food and attention, placed one forefoot upon his heart.

“My intentions are serious,” he declared. “I seek a companion worthy to share my name, my travels, and, when justice is restored, my position at court.”

Thus they became engaged; and because beetles do not require long preparations, they were married the next morning beneath a dock leaf. The guests ate heartily, the bride looked happy, and the bridegroom spoke at length about responsibility.

For two days all went well. On the third, his wife said:

“We must choose a home. The bank near the willow is safe, and in time there may be children.”

“Children?” repeated the beetle.

“Certainly.”

“And a fixed home?”

“Naturally.”

He became thoughtful. A fixed home sounded less like a royal progress and more like work.

“My dear,” he said, “a great public duty calls me.”

“What duty?”

“It is confidential.”

“When wilt thou return?”

“That also is confidential.”

He slipped away while she was speaking to her mother. He told himself that a person destined for golden shoes could not spend his life in a ditch. Besides, he had never explicitly promised to remain, except during the wedding, and words uttered at weddings are often too splendid for daily use.

He flew until he reached an orchard. There two learned men were collecting insects. They wore spectacles, carried boxes, and spoke in grave voices.

“A fine specimen!” cried one, catching him beneath a net.

“Common enough,” said the other.

“Common!” shouted the beetle, though they could not understand him. “I am attached to the imperial court!”

The men placed him in a box beside a blue butterfly and a long-legged fly.

“This is imprisonment without trial,” said the beetle.

The butterfly trembled.

“They may pin us to a board,” it whispered.

“Impossible. Diplomatic persons are protected.”

But when the lid opened and a great pin glittered overhead, our friend forgot diplomacy. He pushed beneath a fold of paper, found a narrow crack, squeezed through it, and dropped into the grass.

“An escape arranged by my own intelligence!” he cried as he flew away. “The Emperor would have been proud.”

He next entered a hothouse through a broken pane. Within, the air was soft and heavy; vines climbed to the roof; grapes hung like green lamps; peaches glowed among the leaves; and strange flowers opened crimson mouths.

A sleepy caterpillar lay upon an orange leaf.

“This is tolerable,” said the beetle. “The climate has clearly been prepared for my arrival.”

“It is always summer here,” replied the caterpillar.

“And no rain?”

“The gardener provides only what is needed.”

“Then he is superior to the heavens. I may take up my abode here.”

He found a warm place beneath a tub and slept magnificently. For several days he ate, wandered, and informed the inhabitants that he was travelling in disguise. Yet the hothouse was swept every morning, and one day the gardener discovered him.

“Out with thee!” cried the man, tossing him through the door.

The beetle landed upon his back.

“Again!” he exclaimed. “Every government persecutes merit.”

Before he could right himself, two boys came running.

“What a splendid black beetle!” said the elder.

“Let us make him a sailor,” said the younger.

They carried him to a pond, which was a lake to the boys, an ocean to the beetle, and merely a pond to the ducks. At the water’s edge floated a little boat with a white sail and a mast no thicker than a stem of grass.

The boys tied the beetle to the mast with a thread.

“I protest!” he cried. “I am no sailor. I am a court official on a secret mission!”

“He waves his legs,” said the younger boy.

“He is saluting,” said the elder.

They pushed the boat from shore.

A light wind filled the sail. The toy vessel glided over the water, and our beetle hung against the mast, furious and terrified. Waves no larger than a child’s hand rose around him like mountains; a floating leaf seemed an island; and a carp’s nose, appearing for one instant, resembled the head of a sea monster.

“This is the reward of greatness!” he groaned. “Abandoned by my country, betrayed by marriage, imprisoned by scholars, expelled from paradise, and now sent to sea without provisions!”

The boat drifted farther out. The boys ran along the bank, shouting, but soon another amusement drew them away. Evening came. The wind changed, the sail drooped, and the little vessel rocked among the reeds.

A duck swam near and looked at the beetle.

“What art thou doing there?” she asked.

“I command this ship.”

“Then why art thou tied up?”

“It is a naval custom.”

The duck dipped her head beneath the water and emerged with something good to eat.

“Come ashore,” she said.

“I cannot leave my post.”

“Very well.”

She swam away. The beetle called after her, but dignity, once spoken aloud, is a troublesome cord in addition to any other.

The sky darkened. Cool mist crept over the pond, and the reeds whispered together. Our friend thought of the royal stable: its warm corners, its familiar smells, the regular footsteps of the grooms. He even thought of his wife in the ditch, though only long enough to decide that she ought somehow to have prevented this misfortune.

“The world is wicked,” he said. “No creature has suffered as I have. If I perish, the Emperor will erect a monument, provided he hears the truth.”

The boat bumped against a reed, swung free, and drifted on. A drop of water struck the beetle’s head.

“This is the end,” he murmured. “I forgive nobody.”

The following morning, three girls came to gather water flowers. One wore a blue ribbon, one a red ribbon, and the youngest carried her shoes in her hand.

“Look!” cried the youngest. “The boys have left their boat.”

“There is something tied to it,” said the second.

“A poor beetle!” exclaimed the eldest.

They drew the boat ashore with a branch and carefully loosened the thread.

“How cruel to bind him so!” said the girl with the red ribbon.

“He must have travelled all night,” said the youngest.

“He is alive,” said the eldest. “See, he moves.”

The beetle stood upon the girl’s palm, wet, stiff, and offended.

“At last,” he said, “a deputation from the Emperor.”

The girls could not understand his words, but they admired his polished wings and set him upon a broad leaf in the sunshine.

“Fly home, little fellow,” they said.

The warmth entered his body. He stretched one leg, then another, opened his wing-cases, and rose into the air.

He flew over the pond, over the orchard, and beyond the garden. Whether he chose the road or the wind chose it for him cannot be said; travellers generally give themselves credit for both. He passed hedges and roofs, chimneys and fields, until at last he saw towers flashing in the afternoon light.

“The palace!” he cried. “My mission is complete.”

An upper window stood open. He flew through it, crossed a chamber where curtains billowed, passed down a corridor, and came at last to the royal stable.

There stood the Emperor’s horse, quiet and noble, with its golden shoes newly polished. The beetle descended upon its mane.

The horse turned its head.

“Who is there?”

“It is I,” replied the beetle. “I have returned from foreign service.”

“Indeed,” said the horse. “Hast thou travelled far?”

“Across deserts of grass, through tempests, among savage tribes, into captivity, through tropical kingdoms, and over the ocean. I have married, commanded a ship, escaped learned enemies, and been received by distinguished ladies.”

“That is much for one small traveller.”

“Small!” said the beetle. “Thou shouldst say concentrated.”

The horse resumed eating its hay.

The beetle crawled down the mane and saw the golden shoes gleaming below. For a moment his old grievance stirred within him; but then a new and far more pleasing thought occurred.

“Now I understand everything!” he exclaimed.

“What dost thou understand?” asked the horse.

“Why thou receivedst golden shoes. It was on my account.”

“On thy account?”

“Certainly. The Emperor knew that I belonged to the stable. He wished to honour me, but court etiquette prevented him from placing gold directly upon my feet; therefore he placed it upon thine. Thou art, so to speak, the pedestal.”

The horse said nothing. A horse that has crossed a broken bridge under arrows sometimes knows when silence is best.

Our beetle settled into his former corner, where everything was warm, familiar, and satisfactorily fragrant. He told the stable beetles that foreign courts had begged him to remain, that scholars had attempted to preserve his portrait, that he had refused command of a fleet, and that the Emperor’s horse wore golden shoes as a public recognition of his services.

“And what hast thou learned from travel?” asked an old beetle.

“I have learned,” replied our friend, “that the world is not so bad when one’s true importance is finally understood. Travel broadens the mind.”

Then he tucked his six unshod feet beneath him and went contentedly to sleep.

And this was perfectly true: his mind had broadened so far that it could now contain the whole world, provided the whole world was about himself.

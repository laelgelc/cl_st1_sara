# The Ghost of the *Idaho Lass*

## I

Paa lies in latitude 11° 18' south and longitude 143° 2' west, or thereabouts; for the Admiralty chart upon which I first saw it marked gave no soundings within forty miles of the place, and represented the island itself by no more than a fly-speck surrounded by a broken oval of reef. It is one hundred and sixty miles, as nearly as may be, from its nearest inhabited neighbour, and consists of three low ridges of coral rock, enclosing a lagoon into which no vessel drawing more than eight feet may safely pass except at the top of spring tide.

Yet, in the year of which I write, that insignificant spot was worth considerably more than half a million dollars.

The reason was guano.

For perhaps eight thousand years—it may have been eighty thousand; I repeat what was told me—certain sea-birds had resorted to Paa in such numbers that, viewed from offshore, they resembled a cloud lying permanently upon the island. The deposit resulting from their occupation had accumulated in places to a depth of thirty-five feet, and an analysis made at Honolulu showed a percentage of nitrogen which brought the estimated value of the whole to a figure that made Cyrus Ryder remove his cigar from his mouth and stare for several minutes at the opposite wall.

I was present when he received the chemist’s report.

Ryder’s office occupied two rooms over a ship-chandler’s establishment near the water-front. The stairs were dark; the walls were grimy; the windows had not been washed since the preceding administration; and every chair, desk, pigeon-hole, and map case had acquired the same varnish of tobacco smoke, sea-coal dust, and commercial anxiety. Yet business to the amount of millions had passed through those two rooms. Ryder himself, when the occasion demanded, could be as persuasive as a revivalist, as close as a money-lender, and as sudden as a shark.

He read the report twice, folded it carefully, and put it in the inside pocket of his coat.

“Dixon,” he said, “there’s a proposition in that.”

I knew the word. Everything with Ryder was a proposition. A stranded bark was a proposition; a mortgage upon a cannery was a proposition; a broken syndicate, a doubtful concession, or a bankrupt line of steam packets was a proposition. I asked him whose island Paa was.

“Mine,” said he.

It appeared that a trader named Wilkins had landed there from the brig *Morning Star* in 1878, had nailed a tin plate bearing his name and the date to the largest tree—which happened to be only seven feet high—and had taken possession under the American Guano Islands Act. Wilkins afterwards died at Apia, leaving, among other effects, the log of the *Morning Star*, a rough survey of Paa, and his interest in the claim. These had passed from hand to hand until Ryder acquired them in settlement of an account for three hundred and forty dollars.

For nine years the claim had lain in one of Ryder’s tin boxes. Then a Honolulu agent, hearing that a German firm had sent a schooner to inspect Paa, recommended an analysis. The sample had been obtained, the analysis made, and the proposition had suddenly ripened.

There was, however, a difficulty.

Captain Jens Petersen, a Norwegian then commanding the steam whaler *Valkyrie*, had learned of the deposit. Petersen contended that Wilkins’s occupation was invalid, the tin plate having disappeared and no permanent settlement having been made. He had already purchased a small schooner at Papeete, and was fitting her out with lumber, corrugated iron, water tanks, six labourers, and a representative of the Norwegian consul. If he reached Paa first, erected buildings, hoisted his flag, and maintained occupation, international law—according to Ryder’s understanding of it—might develop an alarming respect for accomplished facts.

“How long before he sails?” I asked.

“Ten days, maybe twelve. We can be out in seven.”

“We?”

Ryder turned his chair and called through the partition.

“Hardenberg!”

There was a noise in the outer office as of some heavy article being upset, followed by the appearance of a man nearly six feet three inches in height, with red hair, enormous hands, and shoulders which seemed to have been designed for some larger body. Behind him came Strokher, lean, loose-jointed, and melancholy, with a yellow moustache. Last of all entered Ally Bazan, the colonial, a little brown, active fellow from Sydney, who wore gold rings in his ears and could talk continuously for twenty minutes without either arriving at a point or appearing to breathe.

The three were known along the front as the Three Black Crows. They had sailed together in the *Glenlivet*, had deserted together at Callao, had been arrested together in Valparaiso, and had once brought a dismasted sugar bark from Fiji to San Francisco with a jury-rig which was still spoken of admiringly in certain forecastles. Ryder trusted them, which was unusual; and they trusted Ryder, which was inexplicable.

“Boys,” said Ryder, “I’ve got a proposition.”

“Then God ’elp us,” observed Ally Bazan.

Ryder ignored him. He spread the chart upon the desk, flattened it with an ink bottle, a revolver, and two ledgers, and set forth the case. The schooner *Idaho Lass*, then lying in the Oakland Creek, was to sail for Paa within seven days. Hardenberg was to command her, Strokher to act as mate, and Bazan to go as boatswain and general factotum. I was to keep the expedition’s accounts and see to the legal papers. Upon reaching Paa we were to land material for two houses, leave four men in occupation, make a survey, and bring back five tons of guano for public assay.

“If you beat Petersen,” Ryder concluded, “there’s five thousand dollars among you. If you don’t, there’s wages.”

“Wot sort o’ place is this Paa?” demanded Bazan.

“An island.”

“Much fever?”

“No inhabitants.”

“Cannibals?”

“No inhabitants, I tell you.”

“Snakes?”

“Coral island. No snakes.”

“Spirits?” said Bazan.

Ryder stared at him.

“Spirits,” repeated the colonial, “ghosts, fetches, bogies, an’ them white things wot sits on a feller’s chest in the night?”

“There’s guano,” said Ryder. “That’s all there is.”

“Same colour,” said Bazan gloomily. “At a distance.”

We accepted the proposition.

## II

The *Idaho Lass* was a fore-and-aft schooner of one hundred and nine tons register, eighty-four feet between perpendiculars, and twenty-three feet in the beam. She had been built at Eureka for the coast lumber trade and was as sturdy a little vessel as ever buried her lee rail in a norther. Her timbers were sound, her copper no more than eighteen months old, and her sails, though patched, sufficient for the passage. But she had lain idle for six weeks; her running rigging needed renewal, her pumps were foul, and the main hatch leaked.

We began work on a Monday morning.

The haste of our fitting-out was such that no man kept to his proper duty. I painted the names upon water casks, checked stores, copied depositions, and twice went aloft to reeve signal halyards. Hardenberg drove the caulkers like slaves. Strokher inspected provisions, rejected half of them, accepted them upon a reduction of price, and then complained that we should all die of ptomaine poisoning. Bazan was everywhere: in the forepeak, at the masthead, under the floor of the galley, or over the side on a stage, singing mournful songs of transportation and shipwreck.

By Thursday night the work was advanced enough for Hardenberg, Strokher, and me to sleep aboard. Bazan had already done so on the two previous nights, declaring that shore lodging took the heart out of a sailor and six bits out of his pocket. Our four island labourers had not yet joined us.

The schooner lay moored head and stern in the creek. A flour warehouse rose upon one side; upon the other were mud flats, dark piles, and the lights of the railroad yards. The tide had turned at eleven o’clock and ran out softly, rubbing driftwood against the schooner’s bends. There was no wind.

At about half-past one I was awakened by a light beating noise overhead.

I lay in the after cabin, opposite Strokher. Hardenberg had his bunk beyond the little table. The sound came again.

Tap—tap—tap.

“Halyard block,” muttered Strokher.

“Can’t be,” said Hardenberg out of the darkness. “No wind.”

We listened.

Something moved upon the deck.

Now I do not pretend to more courage than I possess. A noise aboard ship at night has always impressed me differently from the same noise ashore. On land one may attribute it to a dog, a shutter, a passer-by, or the settling of a house. But a ship is a small world; one knows every man and every loose article within it, and when an unexplained footfall crosses the deck at two in the morning, one feels that the account has one item too many.

Hardenberg slipped from his bunk. I followed him, and Strokher, after some hesitation, followed me.

We came up the companion without a light.

The night was clear overhead, though a bank of vapour had gathered low upon the mud flats. The warehouse wall shut off the city lamps, leaving the schooner in a dimness through which the masts rose like the hands of an unilluminated clock. The rigging was etched in thin black lines against the stars.

At first we saw nothing.

Then Hardenberg laid a hand upon my arm and pointed.

A figure was climbing the foremast.

It was already above the gaff jaws, moving slowly and without sound. Something white or gray enveloped it from the head downward, so that no face, hands, or feet could be distinguished. It climbed as a man climbs who knows the rigging, yet with an extraordinary gliding movement, and when it reached the crosstrees it seemed for an instant to hang there in the air.

Strokher’s teeth clicked together.

“Who’s that?” Hardenberg called.

The figure made no answer.

“Come down out o’ that!”

Still it did not answer. It moved out upon the foreyard—or rather upon the wire lift where no sane man would have trusted himself—and then disappeared behind the rolled tops’l.

We waited.

The tops’l shook.

The gaskets came away one after another.

The canvas dropped with a soft, heavy flap, and before any of us spoke the figure had descended to the deck. It passed forward, bent at the pin rail, and began to haul upon the halyards.

I heard Strokher whisper, “Lord preserve us!”

Up went the tops’l. Not hurriedly, but hand over hand, steadily and skilfully, until the yard was mastheaded and the sail hung pale above us in the still air.

The figure next cast loose the jib, ran it up, belayed the halyard, and went toward the forecastle.

At that moment Hardenberg sprang down from the quarterdeck.

The apparition vanished.

I mean precisely that. One instant it was before us, a gray upright shape by the fore hatch; the next there was nothing but the hatch itself, the heel of the bowsprit, and a coil of manila line.

We searched the deck, the forepeak, the galley, and the hold. We found no one.

Bazan was asleep in the forecastle.

He lay in his bunk with both blankets pulled over his head and had to be shaken three times before he woke.

“Wot’s afire?” he cried, sitting up. “Where are we? Don’t put me in the boat! I can’t swim more’n two mile.”

Hardenberg dragged the blankets away.

“Did you hear anything?”

“Only you blokes stampedin’ about like bullocks.”

“Have you been on deck?”

“Me? Look at me. I’m asleep.”

His shirt and trousers were beside the bunk; his feet were bare and apparently clean. Yet the tops’l and jib were set.

We lowered them before daylight.

## III

At breakfast Strokher explained that there was nothing extraordinary in what we had seen.

“It was fog,” said he. “Fog twisting round the mast. Moonlight on fog. I’ve seen fog take the shape of a horse, a woman, a church steeple, and once the late Captain Sanderson of the *Annie M.*, who owed me eleven dollars.”

“Fog don’t cast off gaskets,” said Hardenberg.

“A monkey, then.”

“Where’d a monkey come from?”

“Warehouse. There might be a monkey in the warehouse.”

“Wearing a sheet?” I asked.

Strokher became offended.

“I’m giving you scientific explanations, Mr. Dixon. If you prefer ghosts, say so.”

Bazan had listened with his cup halfway to his lips. As the story proceeded, the little fellow’s eyes became rounder and his complexion lost its colour.

“A gray thing?” he said. “No ’ead?”

“We couldn’t see one,” I replied.

“Climbed the fore?”

“Yes.”

“Set the tops’l an’ jib?”

“Yes.”

He put down the cup.

“I’m not goin’.”

Hardenberg laughed, though not very naturally.

“You’re going if I tie you to the windlass.”

“No, I ain’t. I know them things. Me uncle Bill seen one aboard the *Mary Thistle* off Cape Leeuwin, an’ three days after, the cook died, the skipper broke ’is leg, the cargo shifted, an’ every last soul was drownded except Uncle Bill.”

“Then how’d he know what happened after the cargo shifted?” Strokher asked.

“He was drownded later,” answered Bazan. “In a pub at Woolloomooloo, through fallin’ in a water-butt. It were a warnin’. That there last night was a supercargo.”

“A what?”

“A ghost wot sails with a ship. Horrid supercargo, they calls ’em. Don’t eat, don’t sleep, don’t sign articles, an’ always turns up when there’s death ahead.”

The phrase “horrid supercargo” was Bazan’s own invention, I suspect, but it remained with us. Before noon every workman aboard had heard of it. One caulker left his maul and refused to return. The cook, who had been engaged that morning, demanded a month’s wages in advance. Even Ryder, when he came over in the afternoon and found us discussing the matter, seemed less amused than angry.

“Proposition!” he exclaimed. “There’s your proposition—some loafer playing tricks to delay us while Petersen gets away.”

He offered fifty dollars for the detection of the trickster and ordered us to keep watch.

That night the four of us remained on deck until midnight. Nothing happened. At twelve-thirty Bazan announced that his eyes were dropping out of his head and went forward to his bunk.

The tide turned. A little fog drifted in through the piles. From across the creek came the far-off striking of a locomotive bell. We watched and waited.

At ten minutes after one Strokher caught my sleeve.

“Fore rigging,” he breathed.

The figure was there again.

As before, it was wrapped from head to foot. As before, it climbed without hesitation, reached the crosstrees, cast off the tops’l, and let the canvas fall. In the fog it appeared larger than any man, and its white covering fluttered about it like grave-clothes.

Hardenberg had brought a belaying pin. He gripped it in both hands.

“Now,” whispered Strokher, “when it comes down.”

The thing descended.

It dropped the last six feet to the deck without a sound and walked toward the pin rail. Hardenberg advanced. I was behind him, carrying a lantern whose slide was still closed.

“Hold!” he shouted.

I opened the lantern.

For the fraction of a second the light struck the figure. I saw folds of whitish cloth, a dark hollow where the face should have been, and one arm extended toward the halyard. Then the lantern went out. Whether the draught extinguished it or my own thumb moved the slide, I cannot say. There was a rush past us, very light and swift; Strokher cried out that something had touched his neck; and when we got the lantern relighted the deck was empty.

The forecastle door stood ajar.

Inside, Bazan slept with his face to the bulkhead.

This time there were spots of mud on the forecastle floor, but there was mud everywhere aboard, and they proved nothing. We turned out the hold. We searched the lazaret, the chain locker, the sail room, and the narrow space beneath the galley flooring. Hardenberg even opened the flour warehouse and roused its watchman. No stranger was found.

By morning the jib and tops’l had been raised a second time.

You may believe that the matter had begun to trouble us. Daylight reduced it to an absurdity; darkness restored every detail. We could speak loudly at breakfast, draw plans, set traps, and explain away what we had seen; but after midnight the memory of that hooded head above the crosstrees acquired a force not to be argued down.

Strokher maintained that the rival Norwegians were responsible.

“A man could swim across from the piles,” said he.

“Naked?” asked Hardenberg.

“His clothes are the white thing.”

“An’ how does he get into the fo’c’s’le without waking Ally?”

“Drugs him.”

Bazan, hearing this, immediately declared that he had been drugged.

“I knew it,” he said. “Tongue like the bottom of a parrot’s cage, an’ me ’ead full o’ bells. It’s either Norwegians or the horrid supercargo. I’d sooner it was Norwegians, but I don’t believe it.”

Ryder hired a private watchman. The watchman arrived at six in the evening, inspected the schooner, and was found at eleven in a saloon half a mile away. He asserted that a severe internal pain had compelled him to seek medicinal brandy.

We contrived an experiment for the following night. Fine black thread was stretched across the forecastle door and over the fore hatch. Flour was sprinkled on the deck about the mast. A small brass bell was fastened to the tops’l halyard. Hardenberg stationed himself in the longboat, Strokher crouched behind the galley, and I took the quarterdeck. Bazan remained with me until nearly two o’clock, talking without cessation.

“I don’t say as I’m afraid,” he said. “Fear ain’t in it. Wot I say is, a man’s got a right to choose ’ow he dies. If I’m to be ’anged, ’ang me. If I’m to be shot, shoot me respectable. But I don’t fancy bein’ took up a mast by a cold party in a bedsheet an’ dropped into the Pacific where it’s four mile deep an’ all squids underneath.”

At two he went forward.

Nothing happened for perhaps twenty minutes.

Then the little bell on the halyard gave one thin note.

The figure stood at the foot of the mast.

I had not seen it approach. It was simply there. The flour showed pale around it; the black thread across the hatch was unbroken. It lifted both arms, took the ratlines, and mounted.

Hardenberg rose from the longboat.

The apparition stopped.

For several seconds man and thing faced each other—the one on the deck, immense and rigid, the other ten feet above him, gray cloth stirring in the faint night air.

Then Hardenberg threw the belaying pin.

It passed through the loose covering, or seemed to pass through it, struck the mast, and fell to the deck.

The figure came down upon him.

Hardenberg gave a roar and went backward into the longboat, upsetting a bucket and two oars. Strokher bolted from behind the galley; I ran forward; and in the confusion the apparition escaped us again.

The tops’l was half set. The thread across the forecastle door was broken.

Inside lay Bazan, apparently asleep. Flour marked the floor near his bunk, but his feet were under the blanket. The white covering of the ghost was nowhere to be found.

At five in the morning Hardenberg sat in the cabin with his elbows upon the table.

“I’ll take a ship through fire,” he said. “I’ll take her round the Horn in winter. I’ll stand up to any living man. But I don’t sail with that.”

Strokher rubbed the back of his neck.

“My lungs,” said he, “have been bad for months. Salt air brings on haemorrhage. I noticed it yesterday.”

“You never had bad lungs,” said Hardenberg.

“It’s hereditary.”

“Your father was hanged.”

“On my mother’s side.”

Bazan said nothing. He packed his sea chest.

## IV

Ryder came aboard at nine and found the Three Black Crows on the wharf.

Hardenberg had resigned. Strokher had developed such an alarming pulmonary condition that he could scarcely speak above a whisper, though five minutes earlier I had heard him shouting at a drayman. Bazan sat upon his chest with his arms folded, prepared, as he expressed it, to be chopped into bait before setting foot again on that “floating family vault.”

Ryder began with persuasion, continued with threats, and ended with financial argument.

He doubled the bonus.

Hardenberg shook his head.

He offered seven thousand dollars.

Strokher coughed behind his hand.

He proposed ten thousand, half payable before sailing.

Bazan got off his chest, but only to move it farther from the schooner.

At last Ryder turned to me.

“What about you, Dixon?”

I confess that I hesitated. I did not believe in ghosts. I do not believe in them now. But disbelief is a broad daylight accomplishment, and the *Idaho Lass* had to be sailed by night as well as by day. Moreover, without Hardenberg and Strokher we could not take her to sea. I told Ryder as much.

He looked from one to another of us, his face gray with rage.

“Petersen sails tomorrow,” he said.

No one answered.

“Ten thousand dollars,” he repeated. “For three weeks’ work.”

Still no one answered.

“Then go to the devil, the lot of you.”

Bazan, deeply injured, replied that it was precisely to avoid that destination that we had resigned.

Ryder tried for two days to engage another crew. But the story had travelled. Sailors who would have shipped for a whaling voyage of three years, or for the Arctic in a rotten sealing schooner, refused the *Idaho Lass*. One man accepted, took his advance, saw the foremast after dark, and deserted before breakfast. Another came aboard drunk, declared himself on intimate terms with every ghost in Christendom, and was found half an hour later swimming toward the wharf.

Petersen sailed from Papeete on the fourteenth.

The *Idaho Lass* remained in Oakland Creek.

Three weeks later a trading steamer reported that the Norwegian flag had been raised over Paa, that two frame houses and a storage shed had been erected, and that Petersen had left eight men, a launch, water condensers, and sufficient provisions for six months. His occupation was recognised provisionally by his government. Ryder protested at Washington; Washington asked for documents; the documents were sent, lost, found, referred, copied, and referred again. Meanwhile Petersen formed the Paa Phosphate and Guano Company in Christiania with a nominal capital of two million kroner.

Within eighteen months he had shipped eleven thousand tons.

Within two years he sold his controlling interest and retired.

I have heard that he afterwards owned a villa near Bergen, another at Nice, and a steam yacht of four hundred tons. Ryder received nothing. His tin plate had rusted away, his claim was disputed, and the only result of his proposition was a file of correspondence tied with red tape and a bill for the fitting-out of the *Idaho Lass*.

As for the Three Black Crows, they went their several ways. Hardenberg took a berth in the *Dauntless*, Strokher recovered from his hereditary lung disease in time to join him, and Bazan shipped as boatswain in a small inter-island packet. When they met, which was often enough, they spoke of the *Idaho Lass* with lowered voices and complete agreement. Time, far from weakening their conviction, added embellishments. After one year the ghost had fiery eyes. After eighteen months it had carried a coffin up the mast. Bazan eventually remembered that he had heard it calling his name in the voice of his drowned uncle Bill.

I said little. I had no explanation to offer.

Then, almost exactly two years after Petersen reached Paa, I saw the horrid supercargo again.

## V

At that time I was employed by a marine insurance concern whose offices stood near the same water-front. One November evening, having worked late over the papers of a damaged wheat ship, I found myself too tired to walk home and accepted a bunk aboard the company’s inspection cutter, the *Petrel*. She lay at the end of Meiggs’s Wharf, where the water was deep enough for steamers, and had been fitted with a small cabin aft.

By chance Ally Bazan was aboard.

He had come ashore that afternoon from the packet *Merry Andrew* and, finding no cheap lodging, had obtained permission from the cutter’s keeper to sleep in the forecastle. I met him as I crossed the deck. He was unchanged except for a blue tattoo upon his left hand and a new gold ring in his ear.

“Mr. Dixon!” he cried. “Well, strike me pink! Still keepin’ out o’ ghost ships?”

We talked for half an hour. Bazan gave me the history of every vessel, captain, cook, gale, fight, and illness he had encountered since our last meeting, omitting only such details as might have been relevant. At ten he went forward. I retired soon afterwards.

The night was cold and clear, with a light offshore wind. Shortly after two o’clock I woke to the sound of a block striking the mast.

Tap—tap—tap.

The blood in my body seemed to stop.

I sat up.

A foot moved overhead.

For perhaps half a minute I remained motionless, listening. Then I put on my shoes, took the cabin lamp, and climbed the companion.

The wharf lights shone across the cutter’s deck. At the foot of the mast stood a figure wrapped in white.

It held the throat halyard of the mainsail.

Even before it moved I recognised the loose shrouding, the hidden head, the dreadful, deliberate posture. There before me, after two years, stood the ghost of the *Idaho Lass*.

But the light was better now.

The white covering was a sheet.

Below its edge I saw two small bare feet.

The figure began to haul upon the halyard. Hand over hand it worked, with the sure, automatic movement of an able seaman. The gaff rose six feet, ten feet, fifteen. The sheet had been wound about the head and shoulders, but one brown arm slipped free as the figure reached for another pull.

Upon the back of that hand was a blue tattoo.

I set down the lamp and caught Bazan by the shoulders.

He uttered a shriek that brought the cutter’s keeper running from the wharf. The halyard flew through the block, the gaff dropped, and Bazan collapsed in a tangle of sheet and rope.

For some moments he stared at me without recognition.

“Wot?” he gasped. “Where am I?”

“Setting the mainsail.”

“Me?”

“You.”

“I was asleep.”

“I know.”

He looked at the sheet, at his bare feet, and at the half-hoisted sail. Then a change came over his face—a slow, horrible understanding.

“Mr. Dixon,” he whispered, “don’t you go sayin’—”

“Did you ever walk in your sleep before?”

He sat down upon a coil of rope.

After a long silence he admitted that he had.

As a boy in Sydney he had often been found wandering at night. Once he had climbed to the roof of his mother’s house wrapped in a tablecloth. Aboard the *Glenlivet* he had twice gone aloft while asleep, though no one had told him until afterwards. On the *Merry Andrew*, only three months before, the cook had caught him trying to loose the foretops’l at midnight with a blanket over his head.

“Why the sheet?” I asked.

He rubbed his forehead.

“Always takes somethin’ with me,” he said. “Blanket, sheet, sailcloth—anything. Don’t know why. Maybe I’m cold.”

“And you remember nothing?”

“Nothin’. Not a blessed thing.”

The cutter’s keeper, who had heard only part of this, began to laugh. Bazan turned upon him savagely.

“You shut your face, or I’ll knock it round to the back of your ’ead.”

Then he seized my coat.

“You won’t tell ’Ardenberg?”

I said nothing.

“Nor Strokher?”

I continued to say nothing.

His grip tightened.

“Think, Mr. Dixon. Ten thousand dollars! That’s wot they’ll think. Petersen an’ ’is island. They’ll murder me. ’Ardenberg’ll tie me in a sack an’ use me for a buoy. Strokher’ll talk me to death first. Don’t tell ’em. I didn’t know. Strike me blind, I didn’t know!”

And indeed he had not known.

At once every circumstance aboard the *Idaho Lass* became plain. The ghost appeared only after Bazan had gone to the forecastle. It was never seen by him. It knew the rigging, chose the sails uppermost in a sailor’s habits, and vanished toward the very bunk in which he was afterwards found asleep. The flour marks, the broken thread, the silent climbing, the bare feet, the shroud—all were explained by the little colonial sitting before me with the cutter’s sheet still wound about his waist.

I made him lower the mainsail properly. Then we went below, where the keeper produced coffee and, in spite of Bazan’s threats, suffered repeated fits of laughter.

“Wot I don’t understand,” said Bazan, after a long meditation, “is why I was frightened of meself.”

“That,” I told him, “is a commoner condition than you suppose.”

I have never informed Hardenberg or Strokher. Ryder died some years ago, and no disclosure could restore Paa to him or remove Captain Petersen from his villa near Bergen. The Three Black Crows still meet occasionally, I believe, and still relate the story of the haunted schooner. Hardenberg swears that his belaying pin passed through the apparition’s body. Strokher has supplied it with blue fire about the head. Bazan, I am told, is the most impressive witness of the three.

He alone never saw the ghost.

And if, when he tells the tale, his voice trembles more than the occasion seems to require, you and I know the reason.

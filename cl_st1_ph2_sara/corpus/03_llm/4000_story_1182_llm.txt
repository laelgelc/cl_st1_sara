## THE WRECK IN GREEN’S COVE

At low water the harbor had an air of having withdrawn from its own affairs. The little coasting schooners, which at flood-tide rode erect and consequential before the village, now leaned upon the brown flats as if overcome by weariness; skiffs lay stranded among ribbons of weed; the black stakes of the fish-weirs marched nowhere across the glistening mud; and under the wharf, where the green water commonly whispered and slapped, there were only dank stones, the slow dropping of brine, and a smell compounded of tar, salt hay, dead shells, and ancient timber.

Beyond the wharf the harbor widened toward the islands. On the eastern side rose a gray shoulder of granite, furred with spruces and scarred by the weather; westward, a line of white houses looked down from the hill, their dark roofs bent like the hoods of old women watching a funeral. Between these shores, near the head of Green’s Cove, something vast and black protruded from the flats.

It had once been a ship.

The tide had entered it through a hundred seams and departed through a hundred more, leaving its ribs filled with pools in which minnows flashed and crabs went sidewise among iron bolts. One side of the hull had fallen outward. The other, lifted high and curved against the sunset, resembled the jaw of some fabled sea-monster whose flesh had been consumed while its bones remained to frighten children. Grass grew along the keel. A young birch had rooted where the forecastle had been, and every summer a pair of swallows nested beneath a warped beam which no living carpenter could have set straight.

Captain John sat upon a pile-head and watched the wreck with a visiting boy beside him.

He was a small, compact man, built as though the weather had pared away all that was unnecessary. His beard was white and cropped close; his skin, to the rims of his pale eyes, had the grain and color of seasoned oak. He wore a blue coat polished at the elbows, sea-boots although he had not been afloat that day, and a battered felt hat which he removed only in church, at meals, or when speaking of the dead.

The boy had come from Boston to spend August with an aunt whose house stood above the harbor. For a fortnight he had sailed in Captain John’s yawl, dug clams with him, and listened to fragments of local history in which French privateers, drowned ministers, Spanish gold, and uncommon large haddock were mingled without regard to rank or probability. That evening, as the sun descended into a narrow band of saffron beneath a bank of cloud, he pointed to the hulk.

“What ship was that?”

Captain John did not answer at once. He bent forward, resting his hard hands upon the knob of his stick. Far outside, beyond the purple islands, a ground-swell moved against the ledges with a sound so low that it seemed less heard than remembered.

“That?” he said finally. “Why, that’s the Alcazar. Leastways, Alcazar’s what she was born, an’ the Stranger’s what Cap’n Green tried to make her. But names stick to ships closer’n paint. You can scrape ’em off the stern, but you can’t git ’em out o’ the timbers.”

“Was she wrecked here?”

“Ayuh. Wrecked here, or come home here. Accordin’ to how you look at it.”

He glanced at the boy, then toward the village, as if to make certain that no elder authority had arrived to challenge his account.

“I’ll tell ye,” he said. “Only you mind this: there’s what I know, an’ there’s what my father told me, an’ there’s what folks guessed after Cap’n Green was gone. Them three kinds o’ truth git braided together in an old harbor, same’s strands in a hawser. You can’t always pick one out without unlayin’ the whole rope.”

The tide, turning beyond the bar, had begun to send narrow silver threads into the channels. Captain John watched them creep around the wreck.

“’Twas in eighteen hundred an’ twenty-seven,” he began, “come late September, when Green found her. Ephraim Green he was, though scarce anybody called him Ephraim, not even his wife. He’d been away to the Banks in the Sea Flower, a stout little pinky, an’ was workin’ home from the east’ard when the fog shut down off Isle au Haut.

“You know fog out there? No, you don’t. Boston fog’s smoke with water in it. This fog was the reg’lar article—thick enough to lean against, an’ cold, with a little wet breath runnin’ through it. Couldn’t see from the mainmast to the jib-boom. They was feelin’ along under short sail, ringin’ the bell an’ listenin’ for surf, when somebody for’ard sings out there’s a cliff risin’ dead ahead.

“Green jumps up, looks, an’ orders the helm hard over. But the cliff comes with ’em.

“Then the fog thinned one minute, an’ there she stood.”

Captain John lifted one hand slowly, palm outward, and in his gesture the vanished ship seemed to rise again beyond the mudflats.

“She was a great foreign-built vessel, black-sided, high in the waist, with her spars all abroad an’ her yards cocked every which way. One royal was hangin’ in rags. Her forecourse bellied once, though there wa’n’t wind enough to stir Green’s whiskers, an’ then fell slack. No flag. No hail. No man at the rail. She come out o’ the fog bow fust, mighty slow, like she’d sighted the Sea Flower an’ meant to have a look.

“Green hailed her in English. Then he hailed in French, an’ after that in Spanish. He knew considerable Spanish, did Green, though nobody recollected where he’d got it. Not a sound come back except a door bangin’ aboard of her—bang, pause, bang—an’ a loose block tappin’ aloft.

“Now any sensible skipper’d keep clear of a craft like that till daylight. Derelicts’ll stave ye in, an’ dead ships is cunnin’. They don’t answer the helm, but they know enough to lie across your course. Green, though, he studied her. She was coppered, you see, an’ her lower masts looked sound. Salvage on a ship that size would make a poor man comfortable an’ a comfortable man proud.

“‘Lower away,’ says he.

“His mate, old Abner Torrey, asked if they hadn’t better wait for the fog to lift.

“‘Lower away,’ Green says again.

“So they put Green an’ four men in the boat. They pulled under her lee, though she hadn’t rightly got a lee, driftin’ as she was. The weeds hung from her copper in long hair, an’ there was marks along the side—scratches an’ dents, as if boats had beat against her an’ broke loose. They found a ladder trailing. Green went up fust.

“That much Abner told my father with his own mouth. Abner wa’n’t one to garnish a yarn. He had no more imagination’n a marline-spike, an’ not half so much play in his features. But he said when Green put his hand on that ladder he stopped. Just for a breath, mind ye. Then he looked up along the ship’s side, an’ his face went the color of tallow.

“‘Know her, Cap’n?’ says Abner.

“Green turned on him sharp. ‘Know her? How should I know her?’

“‘No reason,’ says Abner. ‘Only you looked—’

“‘Come aboard,’ Green says, an’ over the rail he went.”

The sun had dropped lower. Its red light entered the broken hull before them and lay there like fire in a furnace without heat.

“The deck was littered but not storm-swept,” Captain John continued. “That’s what troubled ’em. A cask had rolled against the capstan. There was clothes here an’ there, a shoe by the main hatch, a red kerchief knotted to a pin. The longboat was gone, an’ two quarter-boats likewise; falls cut, not broke. She’d been abandoned deliberate.

“They went aft. The cabin door was the one they’d heard. Green caught it an’ fastened it back.

“Inside, she was grand—too grand for honest freight, Abner always said. Mahogany lockers, brass lamps, a table inlaid with little stars, an’ painted panels showin’ palms, yellow houses, an’ ladies walkin’ under balconies. There was mold over everything. A silver cup stood on the floor, green-black with tarnish. Papers had been took or burned, except scraps in a language Abner couldn’t read. In one room they found a woman’s shawl, fine as cobweb, an’ in another a child’s wooden horse with one wheel off.

“Water was what they looked for fust. Sailors always do. They sounded the tanks an’ opened casks. Dry. Every blessed one. One breaker had been chopped apart, likely to lick the damp out o’ the staves.

“‘Died o’ thirst, then,’ says Abner.

“‘They left,’ says Green. ‘Boats is gone.’

“‘Left some while ago.’

“‘No concern o’ ours. Find the log.’

“But there wa’n’t any log.

“They searched the galley. No food to speak of. A handful o’ rice under a locker, a bone, some spoiled meal alive with worms. Flies had been there, though the cold had finished ’em. Little heaps of ’em lay in the corners like pepper.

“All this while none of the men wanted to open the main hatch.

“They couldn’t have told ye why. It wa’n’t fastened. The gratin’ was in place, an’ a tarpaulin had been thrown over it, tied down hard. But there was a smell risin’ through—old by then, not the fresh smell o’ death, but sweetish, dry, an’ sick, like a vault opened in summer.

“Green stood over it a long spell.

“‘Below there!’ Abner called.

“No answer.

“‘Fetch a bar,’ says Green.

“They cut the lashings. Took three of ’em to pull the tarpaulin off, for it had stuck to the coaming. Under it the hatch was cross-battened. Not to keep water out, neither. The battens was on the upper side, nailed down so what was below couldn’t lift the gratin’.

“Abner says Green begun swearin’ then, low an’ steady.

“‘What is it?’ one o’ the men asked.

“Green told him to hold his tongue.

“They pried up the battens. Every nail squealed. When the hatch come free, a breath rose out o’ the hold—warm, Abner swore, though the day was cold enough to numb your hands. Their lanterns wouldn’t burn proper at fust. The flames stretched thin an’ blue.

“Then they went below.”

Captain John stopped.

Across the cove a woman called children in to supper. Her voice traveled clearly over the flats; a door closed, a dog barked twice, and smoke climbed from a chimney into the windless dusk.

The boy waited until he could bear it no longer.

“What did they find?”

Captain John looked down at him almost reproachfully.

“I’m comin’ to that. A story ain’t a mack’rel to be split from nose to tail in one swipe.

“The hold had been divided into shelves,” he said. “Long platforms, narrow, one above another, with iron rings set in the uprights. There was chains, short lengths mostly, an’ shackles no bigger’n would fit your wrist.

“An’ there was bones.

“Men’s bones, women’s bones, little children’s bones. Some lay separate where the flesh had gone an’ the ship’s roll had scattered ’em. Some was still held together by rusty iron. Down in the lowest part, where bilge-water had stood, they was heaped close, as if the livin’ had crawled upon the dead for one last inch o’ air.

“A slave ship she’d been. There wa’n’t any mistakin’ that.”

The boy turned toward the hulk. A gust had entered the harbor, and the grass along its keel bent all one way. For an instant the upright ribs appeared crowded with thin figures.

“How many?” he whispered.

“Abner said he couldn’t count. Green wouldn’t let him try. Maybe forty. Maybe a hundred. There’d been more aboard, likely, when she sailed from Africa. Some sold, some dead at sea, some carried away by the boats—who knows? The ones there had been shut below when the crew left.

“Think o’ that. The tanks go dry in the tropics. Men fight for a spoonful. The crew knows the boats won’t hold all. So they take water, compass, bread, an’ arms. Then they nail down the hatch over them they stole from their own country, an’ pull away.

“Mebbe they told themselves the captives was dead already. Men tell themselves queer things when there’s profit behind ’em an’ judgment ahead.

“Abner climbed out an’ was sick over the rail. Two others wouldn’t go below again. Green stayed.

“He took the lantern an’ walked the length o’ that hold alone.

“Now here’s where talk begins. Abner said when Green come up, he looked twenty years older. Said there was dust on both knees, as though he’d knelt. Said, too, that Green had found a small brass disk on a chain an’ hid it in his coat. Abner never saw it plain, so that might be moonshine. But he heard Green say a name.

“‘Alcazar,’ Green says.

“Not read it off her bell, mind ye; they hadn’t found the bell. Not off her papers, for there wa’n’t any. He just come up out o’ that hold an’ says ‘Alcazar’ as natural as you’d name your mother’s kitchen.

“‘What’s that?’ says Abner.

“‘Her name.’

“‘How d’ye know?’

“Green looked at him. ‘I seen the board in the cabin.’

“There wa’n’t no board in the cabin. Abner went back an’ hunted.”

“Had Captain Green been on her before?” the boy asked.

Captain John rubbed his chin.

“That’s been asked nigh sixty years. Green had made voyages when he was young that nobody knew much about. He shipped out of Portland, then out o’ Havana, then vanished three year. Came home with money enough to buy a share in two vessels, but no yarns. Sailors without yarns is suspicious to start with. He’d been on the Guinea coast; that was known. He spoke Portuguese along with his Spanish. Once, when Parson Bell preached against the slave traffic, Green got up in meetin’ an’ walked out so sudden he knocked over a foot-stove.

“But proof? No. Proof’s a landward article, kept dry in boxes. At sea it washes overboard.

“Some said he’d served aboard the Alcazar. Some said he’d been mate an’ had helped pack human souls into that hold like shingles in a crate. Some said he was one of the men who went off in her boats. An’ some said folks hated Green because he was rich an’ close-mouthed, an’ made the rest after he died.

“I know only this: he feared that ship from the minute he touched her, an’ he wanted her worse’n any man ever wanted a vessel.”

Green set a prize crew aboard the derelict and passed a hawser from the Sea Flower. The fog remained through the night. The two ships moved together in a blind gray chamber, the smaller towing the greater, though at times, Captain John said, the line hung slack and the Alcazar seemed to advance of her own will. Men aboard the Sea Flower heard knocks under the abandoned ship’s deck. The prize crew heard footsteps aft. By sunrise one sailor had taken to the rigging and refused to descend until the mainland was sighted.

“They brought her here,” Captain John said. “Right through the harbor gate, with half the village runnin’ down to look. She was the biggest craft ever come in, then or since. Her topmasts went above the church steeple when she swung at anchor.

“Green’s wife, Marthy, stood on their porch. She had sharp eyes. Folks said she looked at the ship, then at her husband, an’ went inside without a word.

“The bones was buried on the outer hill, beyond the old graveyard wall. Not in consecrated ground; there was argument over that. Lord, how folks can argue when kindness is waitin’! Parson Bell said them poor creatures had been carried outside the law of men, but never outside the mercy of God. He read Scripture while they put the bones down in three long boxes. Green didn’t attend.

“Then he went to work on the ship.

“He bought out the other salvage shares. Mortgaged the Sea Flower, mortgaged his house, borrowed from his wife’s brother, an’ hired every caulker from here to Thomaston. They stripped the shelves from the hold, burned ’em on the beach, drew the spikes, scoured the timbers with lime, an’ smoked her with pitch. Still the smell come back whenever the weather turned warm.

“He renamed her the Stranger.

“‘Bad luck to change a ship’s name,’ Abner told him.

“‘Bad luck belongs to fools,’ says Green.

“He had STRANGER painted across her stern in gilt. Next mornin’ the S had blistered, though the paint below was sound. They done it again. Then the T peeled. After the third try, Green had the letters carved. But fishermen kept callin’ her Alcazar, an’ so did the old women, an’ so did the children who’d stand ashore after dark an’ dare one another to hail the bones aboard.

“Green wouldn’t sell her. He could’ve cleared his debt and more, for she was sound under the rot. Live oak knees, teak rails, copper fastenin’s. Built to last. That was the wicked joke of it. Men that owned her had spent good money makin’ a prison strong.

“All winter Green labored. You’d see a lantern movin’ in her cabin till two in the mornin’. Marthy sent for him; he wouldn’t come. Once she went aboard an’ found him asleep at the table with an empty rum bottle an’ a chain laid out before him.

“‘Ephraim,’ she says, ‘sell this ship.’

“‘She’s mine.’

“‘She’s killin’ you.’

“‘She’s mine,’ he says again.

“Marthy looked around the cabin. ‘No,’ says she. ‘I think you’re hers.’

“That come from Mary Spofford, who was Marthy Green’s niece an’ heard it herself. Mary was a girl then.”

At the name, Captain John’s voice altered so slightly that the boy did not at first notice it. The roughness remained, yet some gentler current moved beneath it, as a deep tide may run under harbor ice.

“In May,” he continued, “the Stranger cleared for New York with granite, spars, an’ smoked fish. Green took command. Couldn’t get a full crew locally, so he shipped men from Portland. Abner wouldn’t go.

“‘I boarded her once,’ he says. ‘That’s my share.’

“They had fine weather past Monhegan. Then, off Cape Cod, a gale come up from the south’ard. Not a proper season for it, but weather don’t consult almanacs. It struck at midnight.

“The Stranger behaved like no ship them men had sailed. She’d fall off with the helm up, then luff till the canvas shook. She rolled her lee rail under an’ hung there. Blocks tore loose. A topsail sheet whipped one man overboard. Another was crushed when a spar came down. The third died below—broke his neck fallin’ from a berth, though the ship was steady at the time.

“Three dead in one night.

“Green stood by the mizzen and shouted at the vessel. Not the crew—the vessel.

“‘You won’t have me!’ he says. ‘D’ye hear? Not yet!’

“They heard him plain between the gusts.

“At dawn the gale quit. Just quit, like a door slammed. Sea still high, clouds runnin’, but no wind. The Stranger lay among the waves, groanin’ in every timber. The crew said there was moanin’ below, too, under the cargo.

“They made New York. Soon as the lines was fast, every man walked off. Green had to hire stevedores to discharge her. Word spread along the waterfront. Sailors would come to stare but wouldn’t sign. Somebody found her true name in a shipping list—Alcazar, out of Bahia, reported missing with master, crew, and cargo. Cargo, it said. That was all. Cargo.

“Green stayed aboard near a month. He drank. He’d always taken his ration, but now rum was his watchmate. At night he saw people gather on the quay. When he went up, there’d be nobody. He heard scratching at the cabin panels. He tore one down and found an old iron ring behind it, set into the frame.

“At last he got six men and a boy to bring her home in ballast. Paid double wages. They arrived in October, Green standin’ at the rail thin as a belayin’-pin, his beard gone white on one side.

“Marthy had died while he was away.”

Captain John removed his hat.

“No message reached him?” the boy asked.

“Messages don’t always catch ships. An’ mebbe hers did. Mebbe that was one o’ the voices he heard.

“After the buryin’, Green went aboard again. Wouldn’t sleep in his house. Wouldn’t visit neighbors. He kept the Stranger anchored in the cove, though winter was comin’ and the exposed moorin’ unsafe. There was better chain available, but he wouldn’t buy it. Used gear out of the Sea Flower—old hemp cable, half worn through.

“Greed,” Captain John said, replacing his hat. “Folks talk as though haunting does all the labor. Mostly a man gives his ghost tools.”

The last light had left the upper sky. The tide moved swiftly now, surrounding the Alcazar’s ribs, rising through the gaps and pouring into dark compartments with a hollow chuckle. From the village came the first pale squares of lamplight.

“It happened the third o’ December. Snow in the mornin’, rain by noon, an’ after dark a northeast gale that drove the sea right over the outer ledges. Every vessel in harbor put out extra anchors. The Stranger had one bower down an’ that poor old cable.

“About nine, the cable parted.

“People heard it go—a sound like a cannon. She swung broadside, struck a schooner, carried away the schooner’s bowsprit, then drifted up the harbor. There was Green and four men aboard. They tried to let go the spare anchor, but the ring jammed. Tried to make sail; the canvas split. Every gust drove her nearer the western shore.

“She grounded once and came free. Grounded again where you see them ribs, though the beach was farther out then. The sea broke over her. Her rudder went. Mainmast cracked.

“Men come with lanterns. They rigged a line from shore. One by one Green’s crew got into the riggin’ and come down when the wreck heeled near enough. The cabin-boy was last but Green.

“‘Come, Cap’n!’ they yelled. ‘She’s breakin’ up!’

“Green stood by the mainmast. No hat, no coat. Sea pourin’ round his boots.

“‘Throw him the line!’ somebody shouted.

“They threw. It landed across his arm. He took hold. For a second they had him.

“Then he cast it off.

“Mary Spofford was on the shore with a lantern. She saw his face. Said he looked past all of ’em, out toward the harbor mouth, as though another shore had opened there—hot an’ white, with palms behind it, mebbe, an’ people waitin’ on the sand.

“‘Come ashore!’ Mary called.

“Green shook his head.

“‘I can’t,’ he says.

“The wind tore the words away, but she heard the next.

“‘She come for me. I got to go with her.’

“Then the mainmast fell.

“The ship rolled to port, lifted, an’ seemed to spring forward though she was hard aground. A sea went over her from stern to bow. When it passed, Green was gone.

“They found his body two days later outside the harbor, wedged between rocks under the cliff. One hand was shut so tight they had to force the fingers open. Inside was a little brass disk, black with age. There was marks on it nobody here could read.

“After that the Stranger worked higher on the beach every gale. Folks stripped what they could—riggin’, copper, knees, spars. But they left the deep timbers. Too much labor, an’ some things ain’t cheap even when they’re free.

“So she rotted where she lay. Stranger in the custom papers, Alcazar in everybody’s mouth.”

Captain John fell silent.

The wreck before them had changed with the tide. No longer stranded and helpless, it floated in appearance, its broken side hidden by black water, its remaining ribs rising like a row of oars. The young birch trembled upon it. Somewhere within, an old plank moved and uttered a long, low complaint.

“Do you think it was really haunted?” the boy asked.

Captain John considered this with the seriousness he might have given to wind or current.

“What’s hauntin’?” he said. “A dead man walkin’? Chains clankin’ where there ain’t no chains? Mebbe. But there’s other kinds.

“Suppose Green had been aboard her years before. Suppose he’d heard them people beggin’ under the hatch. Suppose he’d helped nail it down, then took his place in a boat with water at his feet. He might sail to the end o’ the world, change his coat, buy a house, sit in meetin’, an’ hear that hammer all the same.

“Then one foggy day the very ship comes bow-on out o’ nowhere. Empty of all but what he remembers. He could leave her, but he don’t. He takes her in tow. Calls her his prize. Scrapes her, paints her, gives her a new name, same as he’d done with himself. Yet she follows. Follows him south, follows him north, lies outside his door, takes his money, takes his men, takes his sleep, till at last she breaks loose an’ comes ashore with him standin’ on her deck.

“Haunted? Ayuh, I call it haunted.

“But not by timbers alone. Her an’ Green belonged together, same as a man an’ his sins. He thought he was towin’ her. All the while she was towin’ him.”

They remained on the wharf until the tide covered the flats and the harbor became whole again. A bell sounded from a schooner beyond the point. Voices crossed the water and were lost; a night-heron flew low over the cove, uttering its harsh, solitary cry. Outside the islands, the ground-swell continued its ancient murmur against the ledges, indifferent perhaps, or only patient.

Captain John rose stiffly.

His cabin stood at the far end of the village, close under the hill. The boy walked with him there. In the dusk they passed gardens smelling of damp earth, sheds where nets hung like gray veils, and dooryards in which women paused to exchange good-nights. Once an old woman called, “That you, John?” and he answered, “Who else’d it be?” with such practiced severity that she laughed after them.

The cabin contained a bunk, a stove, two chairs, a shelf of books swollen by sea-air, and a narrow table under the window. Everything was neat. On the wall hung Captain John’s sextant, a faded chart of the West Indies, and the half of a painted name-board on which only the letters MARY could be read.

The boy had seen it before but had supposed it came from some vessel.

Captain John lighted a lamp. Its glow fell through the window upon the yawl moored below, a modest, able boat with no name painted on her stern.

“What do you call your yawl?” the boy asked.

“Don’t call her nothin’,” said Captain John, busying himself with the wick. “Names is apt to make a craft vain.”

“But you said names stick to ships.”

“Did I? Well, an old man says too much toward sundown.”

He set bread and cheese upon the table. Then, noticing that the boy still looked at the painted board, he turned it face to the wall; but he did so gently.

“Was Mary Spofford the girl who saw Captain Green die?”

“Ayuh.”

“What became of her?”

Captain John stood with one hand upon the board.

“She married,” he said. “Went west for a spell. Came home a widow. Died twelve year ago come February.”

He looked from the window. The yawl lifted at her mooring, listening beneath them while the tide whispered along her planks.

“Best hand with a lantern I ever see,” he added. “Never let the wind blow it out.”

He said no more. Yet afterward, whenever the boy heard Captain John hail the yawl in fog or darkness, he noticed that the old man did not call her Boat, nor Yawl, nor any sailor’s rough convenience of speech. He called softly, as one might call across a widening distance to someone who had once stood on a storm-beaten shore with a faithful light in her hand.

And below the hill the unnamed vessel answered only by the creak of her rope, while farther out, in Green’s Cove, the tide entered and departed from the bones of the Alcazar.

## THE EYE OF NELSON

Vanquished when he met the women; invincible when he met the men—such, in the briefest compass, was Horatio Nelson.

This maxim, like most maxims, is not entirely true; but neither is it entirely false, and therefore it possesses every qualification necessary for immortality. The ladies conquered Nelson repeatedly, though never permanently. The gentlemen, on the other hand, found him a difficult person to persuade, even with cannon. If he lost his heart in every civilised port, he commonly recovered it before sailing; but when he lost an eye in Nicaragua, he retained the loss to the end of his days, and made better use of the missing article than many men make of two.

The manner of that loss is not generally known. Historians, who love dates but distrust adventures, have passed over it with a sobriety bordering upon ingratitude. Yet the affair explains not only the eye, but the man; and since the man afterwards became an admiral, a peer, a statue, and almost a British weather-sign, we may be pardoned for returning to the time when he was merely a thin, ardent, twenty-two-year-old naval officer with two bright eyes and no discretion whatever.

In the year 1780 Great Britain sent an expedition against the Spanish possessions in Nicaragua. There was a war on, of course. In the eighteenth century there was nearly always a war on; and when there was not, statesmen employed themselves in discovering reasons why there should be.

Colonel Polson commanded the enterprise. He was brave, methodical, and magnificently ignorant of the country into which he marched. Under him were sailors, soldiers, adventurers, fever, rum, and young Horatio Nelson. The plan was to ascend the San Juan River, capture Fort San Carlos, advance upon Lake Nicaragua, overthrow the Spanish power, enrich the Crown, confound the enemy, and return home to applause. Plans of this description are admirable until the shooting begins.

At first all went charmingly. The British boats moved up the river between walls of tropical verdure. Parrots screamed encouragement, monkeys offered criticism, and mosquitoes attached themselves to the expedition with a loyalty which no subsequent disaster could shake. Here and there a Nicaraguan patrol observed the red coats, reflected upon the uncertainty of military glory, and retired with a celerity which Colonel Polson described as cowardice and the patrols probably called strategy.

“The men won’t stay and fight us,” complained Nelson.

“They will when we reach the fort,” said Polson.

“Then we had better reach it quickly.”

This was Nelson’s view of most geographical problems.

The farther the expedition advanced, the easier the conquest appeared. Villages were deserted before the leading boats came in sight. Storehouses yielded maize, fruit, and occasional bottles of Spanish wine. The river was smooth, the enemy invisible, and the whole invasion began to resemble a marine picnic conducted in uniform. Officers talked confidently of promotion. Soldiers discussed plunder. Sailors speculated upon the quality of the tobacco. Only the surgeon looked grave, but surgeons are paid to do that.

Fort San Carlos stood near the point where the river issues from the great lake, and Colonel Polson, having no desire to run his flotilla under unknown cannon, ordered a halt below the stronghold. Reconnaissance was necessary.

It was also slow.

Nelson waited for an hour, which to him was approximately equal to a month. He examined the river, the forest, the distant line of the fort, and the officers engaged in considering who ought to go and ascertain what lay ahead.

“If they debate until sunset,” he said to himself, “the Spaniards will have time to build another fort.”

Now Nelson’s impatience was not the ordinary impatience of a man who dislikes delay. It was a consuming conviction that Providence had made the world too leisurely and expected Horatio Nelson to hasten it. He therefore stepped ashore without permission, slipped into the jungle, and went alone to reconnoitre.

The forest swallowed him within twenty paces.

Great trees rose like the pillars of some pagan cathedral, their trunks bound with creepers and their crowns woven into a roof through which the sunlight descended in green and golden shafts. Orchids burned among the shadows. Broad leaves brushed his shoulders; unseen wings fluttered overhead; insects whirred, clicked, chirped, and complained. Between the trees ran dark alleys and luminous arcades, some ending in thickets, others opening suddenly upon pools where the sky shone like blue metal.

Nelson moved lightly, a pistol in each hand and his sword loose in its scabbard. He had been warned of snakes, jaguars, hostile scouts, and poisonous plants. Of these he feared the scouts least and the plants most, because a scout could be fought.

He had gone perhaps half a mile when he heard a twig break.

He stopped.

Nothing moved.

Then came a faint rustle on his left. He turned noiselessly and saw, beyond a curtain of leaves, a figure standing upon a low bank above the river.

It was a girl.

She stood in silhouette against the shining water, one hand holding aside a branch, the other shading her eyes as she watched the British boats below. She wore a white blouse, a dark skirt, and a red scarf drawn about her waist. Her black hair was braided and coiled behind her head; but one loosened strand lay against her cheek, where the heat had coloured the skin like bronze warmed by sunset.

Nelson, who had entered the jungle to discover cannon, forgot cannon immediately.

He watched her for several seconds. She was slight, erect, and so intent upon the flotilla that she did not hear him approach. At her girdle hung a narrow stiletto in a silver sheath. Nelson observed the weapon, the little brown hand resting near it, the curve of her cheek, and the long lashes bent toward the river.

Duty recovered itself sufficiently to whisper that she was a spy.

Romance replied that she was beautiful.

Nelson compromised, as young officers have done in every age, by capturing her.

He sprang forward and caught her wrist just as she seized the stiletto. She twisted with the quickness of a hummingbird, struck at his face with her free hand, and tried to drive her knee into a portion of his anatomy not protected by naval regulations.

“Softly!” he cried. “You will injure yourself.”

“It is you I endeavour to injure!”

“Then I withdraw the objection.”

She struggled fiercely, but Nelson was strong, and after a moment he contrived to imprison both her wrists in one hand. With the other he removed the stiletto.

The blade flashed upright between them, thin and bright as a silver serpent.

“An uncharitable little instrument,” he observed.

“It would have been charitable if it had reached your heart.”

“My heart is unfortunately upon the other side.”

“You have none.”

“Madam, we have known one another for less than a minute, and already you judge me with the confidence of an old friend.”

She ceased struggling and looked at him.

Her eyes were black, lustrous, and very angry. Her lips were cherry-red, though their present expression suggested that cherries may occasionally contain gunpowder.

“Release me,” she said.

“Certainly.”

Nelson released her right hand.

She stared at him. “Release me!”

“I have done so.”

“You still hold my left.”

“You did not specify both.”

“You are a liar.”

“No. A sailor.”

“There is a difference?”

“A liar deceives for profit. A sailor merely endeavours to improve a dull conversation.”

She looked down at the stiletto in his hand. “Give me my knife.”

“That would be discourteous.”

“You stole it.”

“Captured it.”

“You stole me also, perhaps?”

“I captured you too.”

“Then I am a prisoner?”

“Most decidedly.”

“To whom?”

Nelson smiled. “At present, to me.”

She drew herself up. Though scarcely sixteen, she accomplished the movement with the dignity of a queen receiving an ambassador she intended to hang.

“I am Donna Rafaela Mora,” she said, “daughter of Don José Mora, commandant of Fort San Carlos. If you harm me, every gun in the fort will answer.”

“Then I shall take great care not to harm you. I am Lieutenant Horatio Nelson of His Britannic Majesty’s Navy.”

“I know.”

This checked him.

“You know?”

“I have watched you since yesterday.”

“Have you, indeed?”

“You stand in the bows when the boats advance. You wave your sword too much. You speak before your superiors have finished speaking, and when they give an order you obey it only if it pleases you.”

Nelson blinked. “Your military observations are remarkably exact.”

“I was taught to count men, boats, guns, and fools.”

“And into which category do I fall?”

“You have two pistols,” she answered.

He laughed. “Come, Donna Rafaela, you cannot hate us so bitterly. We have sailed an immense distance to pay our respects.”

“You burned San Bartolomé.”

“A regrettable misunderstanding.”

“You took cattle from the villages.”

“An urgent negotiation concerning provisions.”

“You are invading my country.”

“Spain is at war with England.”

“I am not Spain.”

“Nor, as it happens, are you Nicaragua.”

“My home is here.”

There was no ready jest to that, and Nelson, who possessed more honesty than prudence, did not attempt one.

He glanced toward the river. Through the trees he could see fragments of the British flotilla—oars flashing, red coats moving, sunlight striking gun-barrels. Above and ahead, beyond a bend, stood Fort San Carlos.

“What were you doing here?” he asked.

“Gathering flowers.”

“With a stiletto?”

“The flowers are dangerous.”

“And why were you counting my boats?”

“I admire boats.”

“How many do we have?”

“Twenty-three.”

Nelson had asked chiefly to test her. The answer was correct.

“And men?”

“More than you will take back.”

“A patriotic estimate. How many men are in the fort?”

She smiled for the first time. It changed her face altogether, and our young friend, who should have become more suspicious, became less.

“Three hundred and forty,” she said.

“Infantry?”

“Two companies.”

“Artillery?”

“Enough.”

“How many guns?”

“Count them when they fire.”

“I had rather count them before.”

“Then go nearer.”

“Will they shoot?”

“If they see you.”

“And if they do not?”

“They may shoot also. My father dislikes Englishmen, whether visible or not.”

Nelson studied her. “Your father commands the fort?”

“Yes.”

“He knows our strength?”

“I know it.”

“That was not my question.”

“It was my answer.”

Their talk had become a bout with invisible rapiers, and Nelson enjoyed it enormously. Unfortunately, the lives of several hundred men depended upon his remembering that he had not come ashore to amuse himself.

He gestured with her stiletto toward the fort.

“You will accompany me.”

“No.”

“As my prisoner.”

“No.”

“You have a poor understanding of captivity.”

“You have a poor understanding of women.”

That, as the reader knows, was perfectly true; but truth is seldom persuasive when spoken at the proper moment.

Nelson took her by the hand and led her along a narrow path ascending through the forest. She resisted for six paces, then followed quietly. Her docility ought to have alarmed him more than her violence had done.

Above the trees the fort appeared by degrees: first the flagstaff, then the pale stone wall, then the dark mouths of cannon projecting through embrasures. It occupied a rocky height commanding the river, square, stern, and apparently capable of making the marine picnic extremely expensive.

Yet it was very still.

No sentry moved upon the parapet. No order rang from within. The Spanish colours stirred lazily in the heated air, but of the three hundred and forty defenders not one showed so much as a hat.

Nelson crouched behind a fallen trunk and watched.

Rafaela stood beside him.

“Your garrison is bashful,” he murmured.

“They are waiting.”

“For what?”

“For you to come within range.”

“How far do your cannon carry?”

“Farther than your boats float.”

“What ammunition?”

She hesitated just sufficiently to make the answer seem reluctant.

“We had little proper shot. My father ordered balls made from river clay, baked hard and packed with nails, chain, and scraps of iron.”

Nelson raised his brows. “Clay cannon-balls?”

“You laugh.”

“I was imagining the Royal Navy defeated by pottery.”

“When the ball strikes, the clay breaks.”

“And the iron flies?”

“In all directions.”

“By Jove! A basket of butcher’s knives delivered by artillery.”

“If you prefer that description.”

“It is a monstrous contrivance.”

“It was my idea.”

He looked at her with fresh admiration. “Then remind me never to quarrel with you.”

“You have brought an army to my door.”

“Ah, but that is a national quarrel. It need not interfere with private esteem.”

She made an impatient movement. He still held her hand, lightly now, and she looked down at their joined fingers.

“You promised to release me.”

“I promised no such thing.”

“You said ‘certainly.’”

“I released one hand.”

“You twist words like vines.”

“Words, Donna Rafaela, are much safer to twist than necks.”

“Are English officers always so dishonourable?”

Nelson flushed. She saw it and knew that she had found the exposed point in his armour.

“Dishonourable?”

“You hold a girl who cannot fight you.”

“You fought admirably.”

“Then give me a weapon.”

“What would you do with it?”

“Prove that I am not afraid.”

The stiletto gleamed between them. Nelson considered it.

“You may have your knife when I deliver you to Colonel Polson.”

“I will not go to him.”

“You have no choice.”

“Then keep the knife. But release my hand.”

“And see you vanish into the jungle?”

“I give my word not to run.”

“Your word as a Spanish lady?”

“As Rafaela Mora.”

He released her.

She did not run. Instead she rubbed her wrist, gazing at him with mingled resentment and triumph.

“There,” he said. “You see that English honour survives.”

“You require much applause for a small virtue.”

“So do most nations.”

She pointed to his pistols. “Give me one.”

“Impossible.”

“You are afraid.”

“My dear young lady, a moment ago you wished to put a knife through my heart.”

“The pistol may miss.”

“A consoling distinction.”

“You have two.”

“That is because enemies sometimes come in pairs.”

“You say I am your prisoner. A prisoner cannot be dangerous.”

“Not even when she asks for a loaded fire-arm?”

“I want to see if English pistols are as poor as English promises.”

This was childish, absurd, and transparently provocative. Nelson knew all three things. He also knew that he could seize the pistol before she raised it, that he had retained her knife, and that no one in the fort had stirred for half an hour.

Besides, she was watching him.

A young man may resist an army. Being watched is another matter.

“I shall make an exchange,” he said. “Your stiletto for one pistol.”

Her eyes brightened. “Agreed.”

He handed her the stiletto hilt first. She slipped it into her sash. Then he drew one pistol, checked the priming, and offered it butt foremost.

Rafaela took it.

For the smallest portion of a second they stood smiling at each other.

Then she leapt backward, levelled the weapon, and fired at his head.

The explosion cracked through the forest. Smoke burst between them. The ball tore through Nelson’s hat and clipped a lock of hair above his temple.

Rafaela stared.

Nelson’s remaining pistol was already aimed at her breast.

Neither moved.

“Well,” he said at last, “English pistols appear to miss.”

Her face had gone pale, but her chin remained high. “I warned you.”

“You did. I mistook the warning for flirtation.”

“That was your mistake.”

“One of several, I begin to suspect.”

The smoke drifted away through the trees.

Nelson looked toward the fort.

There was no shout. No drumbeat. No sentry appeared. Most significant of all, no cannon answered the shot. A loaded pistol had been discharged within easy range of the walls, yet Fort San Carlos remained as silent as a tomb after closing time.

Suddenly the whole matter seemed plain.

“There are no men in that fort,” he said.

Rafaela’s expression did not alter, but he felt the truth in her stillness.

“They are waiting,” she replied.

“For Judgment Day, perhaps. Your garrison has fled.”

“My father is there.”

“Your father may be. Three hundred and forty men are not.”

She said nothing.

Nelson stepped nearer. “You told me the fort held three hundred and forty.”

“It did.”

“Did?”

“You asked how many were in the fort.”

“No. I asked how many men the fort had.”

“And it had three hundred and forty.”

He burst out laughing. “You twist words like vines.”

“I had an English tutor for five minutes.”

He should have been angry. Instead he bowed.

“Donna Rafaela, I salute you.”

“I have not surrendered.”

“No. But your fort has been abandoned, your shot has failed, and my boats will be under those walls within the hour.”

“My father remains.”

“Then persuade him to surrender. I will guarantee his life and yours.”

“He will not surrender.”

“He cannot defend the place alone.”

She looked at him strangely. “You believe defence is a matter of numbers?”

“Usually.”

“Then bring your numbers.”

Nelson mistook this for bravado. Bravado was a language he spoke fluently and admired even in an enemy.

He took the empty pistol from her and returned the stiletto to her sash.

“You may go.”

“You release me?”

“Entirely. Both hands, this time.”

“Why?”

“Because the fort is deserted, because I do not make war upon girls, and because I should be sorry to see you under guard.”

“You will be sorrier when you see me again.”

“Then I shall look forward to it.”

She turned and ran uphill.

At the edge of the trees she paused. Sunlight fell upon her white blouse and red scarf. For an instant she seemed less like a girl than the bright emblem of some dangerous country—small, proud, and impossible to possess.

“Horatio Nelson!” she called.

“Yes?”

“You should have kept my knife.”

Then she vanished.

Nelson returned to the river in the best of spirits. He had discovered that the fort was abandoned, acquired precise information concerning its ordnance, survived an assassination, and met the prettiest girl in Nicaragua. Few reconnaissance officers can show so profitable a morning.

Colonel Polson received him with mixed emotions.

“Where the devil have you been?”

“Reconnoitring, sir.”

“Alone?”

“It saved discussion.”

“You disobeyed orders.”

“No order had yet been given.”

“You might have been killed.”

“I was nearly killed.”

Polson stared at the hole in his hat. “By whom?”

“The commandant’s daughter.”

“Good God.”

“A charming girl, though impulsive.”

Nelson then reported that the garrison had fled. He described the silent walls, the unanswered pistol-shot, the absence of sentries, and the clay cannon-balls packed with scrap iron.

“Clay balls?” said Polson.

“An ingenious economy.”

“And you are certain the place is empty?”

“As certain as one may be without breakfasting inside.”

The colonel examined the fort through a glass. Nothing moved upon the parapet. The flag hung listlessly. No smoke rose from the interior.

The flotilla advanced.

So far, so good—or so bad, as the case may be.

The boats rounded the bend in two columns, oars dipping together. Marines sat with muskets upright between their knees; sailors crouched at the sweeps; officers stood conspicuously where officers believe they inspire confidence and actually provide targets. Nelson occupied the bow of the leading boat. His torn hat had been replaced, but not his cheerfulness.

The fort loomed above them.

Still no movement.

The first boats entered the narrow channel beneath the eastern wall. Nelson could see weeds growing between the stones and black swallows nesting near the embrasures. One cannon projected almost directly overhead.

“Deserted,” said an officer behind him.

At that instant the cannon fired.

The world became flame, smoke, clay, iron, and screaming.

The baked ball burst as it struck the leading boat. Nails, chain-links, jagged hinges, musket fragments, horseshoe ends, and every murderous scrap which Spanish ingenuity had collected flew across the crowded deck. Men fell in heaps. Oars shattered. The gunwale dissolved into splinters. The boat swung broadside to the current.

A second cannon roared.

Then a third.

The apparently dead fort erupted with a fury beyond anything a full garrison might have produced. Clay balls broke over the flotilla like thunderbolts stuffed with cutlery. One tore through three boats in succession. Another burst against a mast and swept an entire rank of marines into the river. The water, peaceful a moment before, boiled with wreckage and men.

“Back!” shouted someone.

“Forward!” shouted Nelson.

Both orders were obeyed, with the natural result that the boats collided.

Nelson leapt onto a thwart, waving his sword.

“Pull for the landing! Get under the guns!”

A clay ball struck the bow.

He saw it coming—not as a ball, but as a reddish blur surrounded by flying fragments. The shell burst against the stem. A strip of iron spun from the cloud and hit him above the right cheek.

There was a white flash, a sensation of enormous pressure, and then half the world disappeared.

He fell among the dead and wounded.

From the parapet, scarcely visible through the smoke, a white-clad figure moved from gun to gun.

There were no three hundred and forty men in Fort San Carlos. There were not thirty-four, nor even three. Don José Mora lay within the commandant’s quarters, bedridden by fever and too weak to stand. His soldiers, convinced that resistance was hopeless, had abandoned the fort during the night.

But Donna Rafaela had remained.

She had loaded the guns before going out to spy upon the British. She had measured the ranges, laid the fuses ready, and stationed slow matches beside the embrasures. Now, with her father calling hoarse encouragement from his bed, she ran along the parapet like a small avenging spirit, applying fire to each prepared piece in turn.

One girl commanded the battery.

The British could not believe it; and disbelief, though excellent in theology, affords little protection from artillery.

Boat after boat was smashed. Men trying to swim ashore were swept downstream. Powder caught fire in one transport, which exploded with a solemn boom and showered the river with burning timber. The surviving officers attempted to rally the flotilla, but there was scarcely a flotilla left to rally. What had begun as an advance became a struggle to escape the channel.

Nelson, bleeding and half-conscious, tried to rise.

“Leave me,” he said.

No one did. British sailors will ignore many sensible orders, particularly when one of their own officers gives them. Two seamen dragged him into the bottom of a damaged boat while a third hacked free the tangled oars.

As they drifted beyond the bend, another cannon fired. The shot fell short, struck the water, and skipped once before sinking.

Then the fort was hidden by trees.

Of the proud little expedition which had advanced beneath the walls, scarcely a handful escaped unwounded. The river carried away hats, muskets, broken planks, red coats, and the more perishable hopes of Colonel Polson. The marine picnic was over.

Nelson awoke after dark beneath a canvas awning. His head was bandaged, his mouth tasted of blood, and pain beat through his skull in time with his pulse.

The surgeon sat beside him.

“How many?” Nelson whispered.

The surgeon told him.

Nelson shut his left eye; the right was already beyond shutting.

“The fort?”

“Not taken.”

“Of course not.”

After a while he asked, “Who fired the guns?”

The surgeon hesitated.

“A prisoner says there was only one defender able to stand.”

Nelson turned his bandaged face.

“One?”

“The commandant’s daughter.”

For several seconds he was silent. Then, faintly, painfully, he laughed.

“She said I should have kept her knife.”

Rafaela Mora did not pursue the retreating British. She had no troops with whom to pursue them, and by morning she had neither strength nor ammunition. But she had held Fort San Carlos. More than that, she had destroyed a flotilla which experienced soldiers had considered sufficient to seize it.

The King of Spain heard of the exploit. Kings are sometimes slow to hear of suffering but wonderfully quick to hear of glory, especially when the glory may be rewarded at moderate cost. His Catholic Majesty granted Donna Rafaela a military rank, a decoration, and a pension for life. She had defended a royal fortress when its garrison would not; and even the War Office, that last stronghold of masculine astonishment, was obliged to admit the fact.

As for Nelson, he recovered, though his right eye did not.

He returned to the sea leaner, fiercer, and perhaps a trifle wiser. It would be pleasant to say that he never again underestimated a woman, but history will not permit us so extravagant a conclusion. He did, however, cease laughing at improvised ammunition, silent forts, and young ladies who asked to borrow loaded pistols.

Years passed. The thin lieutenant became Captain Nelson, then Admiral Nelson, then the terror of every enemy fleet unfortunate enough to share his horizon. His lost eye added something to his appearance and much to his legend. Men trust a commander who has left visible pieces of himself upon the road to glory.

At Copenhagen, when a signal was made ordering him to retreat, Nelson lifted his telescope to his blind eye.

“I really do not see the signal,” he said.

The jest has travelled round the world. It is quoted whenever courage disobeys caution and wins.

Yet behind that famous moment stands another: a green river under a tropical sun, a silent fort, a girl in a red scarf, and a clay cannon-ball packed with scraps of iron.

For had Rafaela Mora not taken one of Nelson’s eyes, he could never, in the hour of decision, have failed to see so magnificently.

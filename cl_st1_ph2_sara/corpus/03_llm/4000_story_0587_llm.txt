### The Measure of a Man

He who travels with Pierre “Feroce” Beaulieu accepts, whether he knows it or not, a wager with calamity. Pierre broke trail as he broke horses, dogs, and the peace of drinking places—by a prodigious expenditure of muscle and profanity. He was broad from shoulder to shoulder, deep in the chest, and thick of limb; his black beard mounted almost to his eyes, and his eyes themselves were black, quick, and arrogantly alive. Men called him Feroce because he delighted in hazards which sensible men went miles to avoid.

Willard Fenwick, who had hired him to cross from the Tanana country to the coast, was of another pattern. He was slight, narrow-wristed, and pale beneath the tan won during three seasons in the North. His sleeping-robe weighed nearly as much as he did. His voice was quiet, his clothing fastidious, and he could quote dead philosophers while mending a dog-harness with rawhide.

Pierre regarded these accomplishments as harmless eccentricities.

“Books,” he said, “dey is good for make de fire, if de paper not too shiny. But when de trail get bad—By Gar!—you want meat on de bone.”

They were swinging down a hard-packed river at the time, the dogs stretched flat in their traces and the sledge runners singing against the frost with the thin metallic whine of cutlasses being whetted. The sun, a flattened crimson wafer, hung just above the southern hills. All the world scintillated. Frost-dust rose from the malamoots’ paws and drifted backward in a veil of powdered diamonds.

Willard, running beside the gee-pole to warm himself, smiled.

“You confuse strength with endurance.”

Pierre roared. His laughter startled a raven from a spruce snag half a mile away.

“Strength—endurance! You make two word where one word do.”

“Not at all. A powerful man may spend himself quickly. A weaker man may continue because he has trained his mind to command his body.”

“De mind!” Pierre struck his fur cap with one mitten. “Dis little t’ing in de head, she tell de belly not be hungry, eh? She tell de leg not be tire’? She say to de frost, ‘Monsieur Frost, you not bite me, because I am heducate’?”

“The mind has done precisely such things. Soldiers have marched after their feet were bleeding. Athletes have finished races when their muscles were exhausted. Men in the Roman arena—”

“Roman?” Pierre interrupted. “I know no Roman. I know Baptiste Roman at Circle. He freeze dead with two gallon whisky under his coat.”

“That was hardly the example I intended.”

“Pah! You wait. De Nort’ she give you chance to show dis will-power. Den we see if de mind pull de sled.”

The North heard him. The North hears many boasts, but answers only those which amuse it.

For six days the trail was good, and life coursed strongly in men and dogs. They crossed white rivers laid between black ranks of spruce, climbed windy benches where the snow had been polished blue, and slept under skies so clear that the stars seemed nails driven through the dark firmament. Pierre sang old river songs as he trotted, and the dogs, well fed and magnificent in their winter coats, flung themselves joyously against the breast-bands.

Then came the overflow.

Water, forced up through a crack in the river ice, had spread beneath the snow for half a mile. The lead dog broke through first. In an instant the team was wallowing belly-deep in black water while the thin upper crust collapsed about them with a tinkling like shattered glass.

Pierre sprang among them, shouting and striking with the butt of his whip. Willard cut traces. They dragged the dogs one by one to solid ice, hauled out the sledge, and made camp while the wet harness stiffened into fantastic shapes.

The temperature fell that night. By morning each dog’s feet were cased in ice. They thawed them with the warmth of their hands, wrapped the pads in strips torn from a blanket, and went on. But the damage had been done. At every halt the snow beneath the team was spotted with blood.

Two days later the sky closed.

There are storms that rush like armies, heralded by cloud-banners and the artillery of wind; and there are storms that grow imperceptibly, until a man wakes to find the universe abolished. This was of the latter breed. A fine snow began before noon. By dusk the opposite bank had vanished. By midnight the nearest dogs were grey blurs at the ends of the traces, and the silence, thickened by innumerable falling flakes, oppressed like a weight.

They camped in a grove of dwarf spruce. The snow rose over the tent, over the stacked harness, and finally above the backs of the huddled dogs. For four days it fell. They dug out the stove-pipe every few hours, and when the heat thawed the floor beneath them and the little sheet-iron stove began to sink, Willard remarked that they would shortly require a writ of habeas corpus.

Pierre stared, then shook his head.

“Dis is what de book do. You get bury alive, an’ you make de lawyer joke.”

The delay consumed food. The dogs ate their full rations on the first two days, half on the third, and nothing on the fourth. When the storm ceased, the country had been remade. Hills, sloughs, islands, and trail were submerged beneath an efflorescent whiteness without line or shadow. The men wallowed waist-deep on snow-shoes, breaking road for the team. The dogs struggled five steps and lay down. Their injured feet left a pink chain behind them.

Always the whiteness; always the listening silence. The sun shone, but gave no heat. At night the cold smote the timber until trees split with reports like rifles.

Pierre ceased singing.

They were depending upon a cache at the mouth of Little Moose Creek. It contained flour, bacon, beans, dried fish, tea, and dog-feed enough to carry them through the mountains. Pierre had made it himself in the autumn, raising the platform ten feet between four spruce trunks and covering the provisions with green poles.

On the third afternoon after the storm, he saw the two forked trees and shouted. Even the dogs understood. They lifted their heads, and a feeble agitation passed down the team.

But there was no platform.

The poles lay scattered. The lashings had been cut. A torn flour sack, stiff with frost, protruded from the snow.

Pierre stood motionless. Then he burrowed like a beast, flinging snow with both hands. He found an empty bacon rind, a handful of beans, and one gum-boot which was not his.

“Indian?” Willard asked.

Pierre examined the cut thongs.

“White man. Indian take what he need. Dis t’ief take all.”

He lifted his face to the sky and poured forth a magnificent denunciation. He cursed the unknown thief’s ancestors, descendants, liver, heart, and ultimate lodging in eternity. His voice rolled among the trees until the dogs pricked their ears.

When he had finished, Willard divided their remaining food. Nine pounds of flour, a little tea, three candles, and perhaps two pounds of bacon remained. For the dogs there was nothing.

“We abandon the extra robe, the tent, and everything not indispensable,” he said. “We can reach Roper’s road house in eight days if the pass is open.”

“Eight day on dat?” Pierre kicked the flour bag. “An’ de dog with no feed?”

“Then we must make it seven.”

“Ah! De mind, she make one day disappear.”

“If necessary.”

Pierre’s teeth showed in a humourless grin.

“Bon. Now begin de school.”

They left the weakened sledge and packed what they could upon a narrow toboggan. For two days the dogs hauled it. On the third, the wheel dog could not rise. Pierre killed it with one blow of the axe, and the others devoured the warm flesh, hide and all. The following morning they went forward more briskly, but the improvement did not last.

Hunger in its first stage is noisy and importunate. It fills the imagination with roasted meats, hot bread, bubbling stews; it protests, bargains, and gnaws. Later it becomes a dull tenant of the body, no longer clamouring because it has begun to consume its lodging.

The men ate once each day. A spoonful of flour stirred into hot water, a shaving of bacon, and tea without sugar composed the meal. Pierre watched Willard’s portions with wolfish attention, though he never took more than his share. Willard, in turn, observed that the great guide’s cheeks had hollowed and that the skin under his beard had grown loose.

They entered the foothills. Here wind had stripped the ridges and packed the gullies. The snow changed beneath every step—powder, crust, blue ice, powder again. On an incline above a frozen creek, Willard’s snow-shoe slipped. His right foot plunged between two buried rocks and remained there while his body twisted downhill.

He heard a small, dry snap.

Pierre freed him. The ankle was not broken, but the moccasin had torn, and before they made camp the foot had lost sensation. They rubbed it with snow, then warmed it gradually beneath Pierre’s shirt. Feeling returned in red-hot needles, subsided, and returned as a profound ache.

“Freeze,” Pierre said. “Not too bad—mebbe.”

Two toes were waxen the next morning.

“Your mind tell dem be warm?” Pierre asked, but the old mockery was faint.

“My mind tells the rest of me to walk.”

He walked.

They killed another dog that evening. There remained four: gaunt, low-headed creatures whose ribs moved beneath their coats like fingers under cloth. Pierre had ceased beating them. He spoke to them softly now, calling each by name, coaxing where once he had commanded. Cruelty is often only the impatience of abundant strength; when strength ebbs, kinship begins.

On the horizon rose two black pinnacles, close together and slightly curved, as if some prodigious ass lay buried beneath the mountains with only the tips of its ears protruding.

“The Ass’s Ears,” Pierre said.

Willard had heard of them. Native travellers watched those peaks for weather. If vapour streamed from the western ear, snow was coming; if both ears smoked, wise men sought timber and waited.

A pale streamer floated from the western pinnacle.

“Wind,” Pierre muttered. “Bad place for wind.”

“There is shelter in the pass?”

“Some willow. Little rock. After dat, open for t’irty mile.”

“Then we camp here.”

Pierre looked back at the food sack, which had become no more than a folded rag around two handfuls of flour.

“Camp, we eat. We eat, we not have. We go.”

This was sound reasoning, though it came from the body and not the schools. They went.

By afternoon the second ear had put on its plume. Gauzy vapours spun upward, dissolved, and re-formed in long white streamers. The air was strangely mild. Their breath no longer smoked. Even the dogs showed uneasiness, lifting their noses toward the west.

The pass narrowed between walls of violet stone. Wind whispered over the heights, withdrew, then returned with a faint susurration, as though some enormous creature sniffed at them from beyond the range. The sky above remained blue.

“Faster,” Pierre said.

They could not move faster. Willard’s frozen foot had swollen until every step drove a nail of pain through the ankle. Pierre stumbled from weakness. The dogs leaned against the traces, fell, rose, and leaned again.

At dusk they found a hollow behind a rock shelf and spread the remaining robe. No wood grew there. Their stove was useless. They ate the last bacon raw and saved the flour for morning.

Then Pierre complained of sand in his eyes.

By midnight he could not open them. The glare of the previous days, multiplied from earth to sky and sky to earth, had burned his sight away. His lids swelled purple; tears froze in his beard.

“Snow-blind,” Willard said.

“I know what she is!” Pierre snarled. “You t’ink because I got no college I not know my eye?”

Willard cut two narrow slits in a strip of black cloth and bound it over Pierre’s face. At dawn the guide could distinguish light from darkness, but no shape.

They harnessed themselves beside the dogs. Willard led. Pierre followed with one hand on the toboggan line, placing his snow-shoes where Willard ordered. The wind had risen. It came in long breaths, warm at first, then rapidly colder, pouring through the pass like air released from the lungs of interstellar space.

At noon the sky disappeared.

Clouds boiled over the western wall. The Ass’s Ears vanished, and with them the last visible landmark. Snow lifted from the slopes before a flake fell from above. It streamed horizontally, seething about their knees. The wind sucked at their clothing, probed under their hoods, and cut the exposed skin of their cheeks like well-honed steel.

“Back to de rock!” Pierre shouted.

“Too late! We’re beyond it!”

“Den down—down de pass!”

The gale struck.

It did not increase; it attacked. One instant they stood in a world of flying snow, the next in a white maelstrom that abolished direction and distance. The dogs were rolled together. The toboggan overturned. Willard fell upon his frozen foot and screamed, though the wind tore the sound from his mouth before he heard it.

They righted the load and crawled. Standing was impossible. Wind—wind, with full hate in it; wind that hammered, shrieked, and drove powdered ice through the seams of their garments. Their faces became masks. Their mittens stiffened. Each breath was a knife descending into the chest.

Pierre, blind and magnificent even in ruin, hauled upon the line.

“Dis way!”

“You don’t know!”

“I smell de valley!”

“You smell nothing!”

Pierre laughed horribly.

“De mind, den! Ask de mind!”

They dragged forward because motion was less immediately fatal than rest. Minutes lost their boundaries. An hour might have been a day, or ten steps an hour. It is in such black, aimless times that thought becomes distorted. The intellect, proud architect of orderly conclusions, turns maundering child and plays with broken toys.

Willard remembered a dinner in Boston. He saw crystal, white linen, the red shell of a lobster. His mother asked why he wore snow-shoes in the dining room. Pierre was giving a lecture in Latin. The lead dog sat at the table and politely requested salt.

Then he became lucid and found himself on hands and knees, digging for the trail with his mittens.

“Pierre!”

“Oui.”

“Are the dogs there?”

“Some.”

The line held two. The others had vanished—cut loose, perhaps, or buried within yards of them. There was no searching. The world ended at arm’s length.

They abandoned the toboggan and took the robe, flour, matches, and axe. Soon the flour was lost. Willard remembered Pierre carrying it; later Pierre’s hands were empty. Neither knew when it had gone.

Night came, discernible only because the whiteness turned grey and then black. They found a drift packed against a boulder and burrowed into it. Willard dug with the axe while Pierre clawed with his hands. They rolled into the cavity with the two dogs and drew the robe over the entrance.

The snow saved them by burying them.

Inside, the wind became a remote thunder. Their breath warmed the hollow until water dripped from the roof. One dog lay against Willard’s back, trembling without cessation. The other licked Pierre’s ice-coated beard.

“Tomorrow,” Willard said, “the storm may break.”

“Tomorrow,” Pierre answered, “we make dog soup.”

The dogs heard no irony.

They slept in spasms. Willard dreamed that Hunger had become a narrow-faced man who sat upon his chest and patiently ate his heart with a silver fork. Cold crept in behind Hunger, thin-fingered and courteous, asking permission to take the feet first. He argued with them, citing law and precedent. They listened solemnly and continued their meal.

He woke to Pierre shaking him.

“Come. De cave she close.”

The roof had sagged nearly to their faces. They dug out into a dawn of dirty tumult. One dog was dead. They ate a portion raw because no fire could live. The surviving animal ate also, and thereafter followed them without harness.

For two days the storm drove them eastward. They slept once beneath an overhang and once in the lee of a ridge where the snow covered them as they lay. Willard’s foot no longer hurt. This alarmed him more than pain would have done. The leg felt detached, a burden belonging to some dead companion he had promised to carry.

Pierre’s blindness deepened. He saw only a red glow by day and black, black eyes floating before him at night. He cursed them. He struck at them. Once he tore away his bandage and ran directly toward a cliff.

Willard caught his parka.

“Let go! Dey watch me!”

“There’s nothing there.”

“Black eye! You see!”

“I see the road house,” Willard lied. “Smoke. Down the valley.”

Pierre became quiet.

“How far?”

“Ten miles.”

This figure had no basis. It served because ten miles is a comprehensible torment, whereas uncertainty is infinite.

They descended.

The gale weakened, but cold followed it. The clouds ripped apart, disclosing a green and terrible sky. Peaks leaned above them, fluted with snow. Far down the valley a dark line marked timber, and beyond the timber, perhaps twenty miles away, rose something that might have been smoke.

Willard stared until his eyes watered.

“Roper’s,” he whispered.

“Smoke?” Pierre asked.

“Yes.”

“Mebbe cloud.”

“Yes.”

They moved toward it.

The surviving dog died before noon. Pierre wanted to stop and eat it, but Willard knew that if they stopped they would never rise. He promised they would return. Pierre protested, wept, and then forgot.

By afternoon his great body began to fail. He walked with both hands on Willard’s shoulders, driving the smaller man into the snow at each stumble. At last he fell and did not rise.

“Up,” Willard commanded.

“No.”

“We’re close.”

“No close. You lie good, M’sieu’ Willard. You learn dat in college?”

Willard struck him across the face.

Pierre smiled. “Ah. De little man got muscle.”

“Get up.”

“My eye gone. My belly gone. My leg gone. All Pierre gone.” He rolled onto his back. Snowflakes settled in his beard. “You go. Tell dem Pierre Feroce, he die ver’ brave.”

“You’re not going to die brave. You’re going to die like a lazy fool within half a day of shelter.”

“Dat is good too.”

Willard pulled him by the collar. Pierre’s body moved six inches and settled deeper. The effort filled Willard’s vision with crimson sparks.

Reason calculated. He could not carry Pierre. To remain was to die with him. To leave him exposed was murder. Shelter—there must be shelter.

He removed one snow-shoe and began to dig.

The snow beneath the crust was loose and dry. He excavated a trench beside Pierre, rolled the unconscious man into it by levering him with the axe handle, and packed snow around his body. He laid the robe over Pierre’s face, leaving a narrow breathing space, then raised a marker: the axe thrust upright through one snow-shoe, with Pierre’s red sash knotted to the handle.

“Warm,” Pierre murmured from the white grave. “By Gar, I am warm.”

“You are four miles from Roper’s,” Willard said, though he did not know whether it was four or fourteen. “I’m going for help.”

Pierre had already entered bleak dreams.

Willard turned toward the smoke.

Thereafter he ceased to be a man in the customary sense. He was an intention inhabiting damaged flesh. The body rebelled; the intention dragged it onward. He counted steps to one hundred, forgot the number, began again. He fell and lectured himself upon the necessity of rising. He crawled, stood, hopped upon the better foot, and crawled once more.

The valley broadened. Timber rose about him. Spruce branches caught at his clothing like the hands of the dead. Once he embraced a tree, convinced it was a stove-pipe, and called through it for coffee. Once he dug at a patch of blue shadow, believing it a door.

Night came. The cold grew scintillant and absolute. Stars blazed between the trees, cruel in their indifference.

He found the trail by accident—a narrow depression packed by sled runners. At its edge lay fresh dog-droppings, frozen but not yet buried. This evidence of life inspired him more profoundly than any sunrise.

“Mind,” he said aloud. “The mind commands.”

His knees folded.

“The mind,” he repeated from the snow, “commands the body to get up.”

The body refused.

He laughed. The laughter became a cough, the cough a sob. He thought of Pierre beneath the drift, blind and breathing through a slit beneath the robe.

“Then,” Willard told his body with elaborate politeness, “you may remain here, and I shall proceed without you.”

The absurdity pleased him. Cackling, he rose.

A light appeared between the trees. It vanished when he blinked and returned when he moved. Yellow, warm, squared by a window-frame—it was no star.

The road house stood upon a bench above the river, logs chinked with moss, smoke lifting from the chimney. Three dog teams lay curled outside. Willard climbed the bank on hands and knees. At the door he attempted to knock, but his mitten merely brushed the wood.

Inside, chairs scraped.

The door opened, and a great flood of lamplight poured over him. A bearded man shouted. Behind him someone exclaimed in horror.

Willard remained upright long enough to speak.

“Pierre Beaulieu. In the pass. Red sash on an axe—snow-shoe above the drift. Four miles, perhaps six. He’s buried, but alive.”

“Good Lord! Who are you?”

“The mind,” Willard said, smiling with cracked lips, “commands the body.”

Then the body, having received too many commands, resigned its office.

They put him on a bunk and cut the moccasin from his swollen foot. Men harnessed the freshest team. Roper himself took the gee-pole, while two prospectors carried shovels, blankets, hot stones, and brandy. They found Pierre after midnight, seven miles away, the red sash snapping beneath the stars. His breathing-hole had nearly closed. When they dug him out he cursed them for interrupting a banquet in Montreal.

Both men survived.

Willard lost two toes and kept a white scar across one cheek. Pierre’s sight returned slowly, first as painful light, then as shapes swimming in milk, and finally as the ordinary world, which he greeted with a solemn oath and three bowls of stew.

During convalescence they occupied adjoining bunks in Roper’s loft. Visitors came to hear the tale, and Pierre, once sufficiently restored to possess an audience, told it often.

“You see,” he explained one evening to six teamsters and a mail carrier, “some man, he got only de muscle. When muscle finish, dat man finish too. But M’sieu’ Willard an’ me—we is different.”

Willard turned his face toward the wall.

Pierre tapped his forehead importantly.

“We is heducate mans. We got de mentality. We can stan’ de ’ardsheep because de mind she command de body. Dat is philosophy.”

“Is that so?” asked Roper.

“Certainement. I always say.”

From the adjoining bunk came a muffled sound.

Pierre looked over. “You laugh, M’sieu’ Willard?”

“No,” Willard answered. “I was merely reflecting upon the triumph of education.”

Pierre nodded, wholly satisfied.

“By Gar,” he said, “she save us both.”

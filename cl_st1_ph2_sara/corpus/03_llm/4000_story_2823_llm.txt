### The Meaning of Horror

We had dined at the Villa des Mimosas, above the darkening sea. The windows stood open, though it was November, and the mild air entered with the smell of salt and damp leaves. On the table, among the empty coffee cups, the liqueur glasses shone like little lamps. Four gentlemen smoked in silence; the red points of their cigars floated in the drawing room like watchful eyes.

Someone had spoken of an execution witnessed in Spain, and another of a railway accident in which twenty persons had been burned.

General de G——, who sat deep in an armchair, his hands crossed upon the handle of his cane, shook his head.

“You confuse the terrible with the horrible,” he said.

The magistrate opposite him smiled.

“Is not death horrible?”

“Not always. Death may be violent, hideous, painful; it may be terrible without being horrible. A shell bursts in a battery and kills six men. That is terrible. A child falls into a furnace. That is terrible. But horror contains something more—something obscure, abnormal, impossible to classify. It troubles not only the heart, but the intelligence. One asks why, and there is no answer. One sees men cease to be men, yet continue to speak, march, obey, and reason.”

He lifted his glass, looked through the amber liquid, and set it down untasted.

“I have known horror twice.”

Nobody interrupted him. Outside, a wave broke against the rocks with a long, withdrawing murmur.

“The first time,” he continued, “was during the retreat of 1870.

“Our army no longer resembled an army. It was a multitude moving by habit, an exhausted human mechanism whose broken pieces still dragged one another along. For three days we had marched through snow. The roads were white; the fields were white; the sky itself seemed made of dirty snow. Men stumbled with stiffened knees; some had wrapped their feet in rags, some had lost their knapsacks, some their rifles. Blood marked the road behind many of them in little frozen footprints.

“The cold completed what defeat had begun. No one spoke except to curse. The officers no longer commanded; they merely walked at the head of men who happened to follow them. Every sound from the rear produced panic. A cart overturned, and someone cried, ‘The Prussians!’ A horse broke its traces, and three companies ran into a field.

“Toward evening we reached a village consisting of perhaps thirty houses, a church and a cemetery. The inhabitants had fled. We found doors open, fires dead upon the hearths, soup frozen in a pot, and one cradle overturned beside a bed. The soldiers entered everywhere. They tore up cupboards, split furniture for fuel, searched lofts and stables, and fought over handfuls of oats.

“I had obtained a room in the mayor’s house and was trying to write a report when a tumult rose in the street. It began with a shout, then swelled suddenly, as such noises do among frightened men.

“‘A spy! A spy!’

“I went out.

“In the middle of the road a traveler stood surrounded by perhaps two hundred soldiers. The person was tall, wrapped in a long brown coat, with a fur cap pulled over the ears and a black woolen scarf hiding the chin. Snow covered the shoulders. No one could say where the stranger had come from. One man declared he had seen signals made toward the woods; another had found a map; a third swore the prisoner spoke German.

“I pushed through the crowd.

“‘Who arrested this person?’

“Ten voices answered. Each man accused the others of having begun it.

“I questioned the traveler.

“‘Your name?’

“No reply.

“‘Where are you going?’

“No reply.

“‘Your papers?’

“The prisoner raised both hands slowly, as though not understanding, or perhaps as though too exhausted to understand. A soldier struck those hands down with the butt of his rifle.

“‘To the wall!’ someone shouted.

“Then all repeated it.

“‘To the wall! To the wall!’

“You know how a crowd takes possession of a phrase. It becomes an order because everyone cries it, though no one has given it. The prisoner was pushed toward the cemetery. I tried to stop them. I called for the captains, for the guard, for the gendarmes. But rank had vanished. There were no captains, no companies, no discipline—only cold, hunger, fear and the intoxicating certainty of having found a victim.

“The stranger stumbled. The men kicked the brown coat, beat the shoulders with ramrods, struck the fur cap from the head. I saw gray hair, cut short and matted with snow. The prisoner uttered a low cry. It was not a word; it sounded like the complaint of an animal under a cart wheel.

“At the cemetery wall they formed a line.

“‘Aim!’

“There was no officer. A corporal cried the command because it pleased him to do so.

“‘Fire!’

“Perhaps thirty rifles went off. The stranger turned halfway round and fell against the snow. The rest of the men, excited by the discharge, fired without aiming. They reloaded; they fired again. Some approached until the muzzles almost touched the body. They circled it as mourners circle a coffin, but instead of holy water each offered a bullet.

“Then a rifle cracked from behind the church. Nobody knew who had fired.

“‘The Prussians!’

“In an instant they fled. The executioners ran through the village, abandoning packs, pots, blankets and weapons. Men who had just shown themselves bold before a motionless prisoner threw themselves into ditches and crawled behind walls. Within two minutes the road was empty.

“I remained with two gendarmes.

“Night had come. We approached the body. One gendarme struck a match. The flame shook in his fingers and went out immediately. We saw only the brown cloth, torn and blackened.

“He struck another.

“The head had become a shapeless, blood-dark mass. The face no longer existed. One hand was closed under the chest; the other lay open on the snow. It was thin, pale, and wore a plain gold ring.

“‘Search the pockets,’ I said.

“We worked by matches, one after another. Their small flames illuminated a button, a strip of skin, a patch of red snow, and then darkness fell again.

“We found a purse containing fourteen francs and three German coins; a crust of bread wrapped in paper; a bone-handled knife; two keys; a little prayer book; a handkerchief embroidered with an M; a railway ticket from a town already occupied; and, inside the waistcoat, a folded letter stained so completely that we could read only a few words: ‘battery,’ ‘my son,’ and ‘God protect you.’

“The elder gendarme drew back the coat. He remained bent over the corpse for several seconds. Then he looked at me.

“‘General,’ he said, ‘it is a woman.’

“Yes, a woman. An old woman, perhaps sixty years of age. She had bound her breast flat under a man’s waistcoat. Her hair had been cut; her clothes were those of a poor clerk or schoolmaster. We never learned her name. We never knew whether she was French, Alsatian or German. We never knew whether she was mute, deaf, mad, or merely numb with fear. Perhaps she was seeking the son whose letter she carried; perhaps he served in an artillery regiment beyond our lines; perhaps he was already dead.

“That uncertainty was the worst. Had she been a spy, one might have condemned the haste, the cruelty, the irregular execution; yet the mind would have found a cause. But she had crossed the snow alone, disguised and silent, carrying a letter against her heart, and two hundred men had destroyed her because one among them had cried a word.

“I had seen battlefields. I had stepped over men without faces and horses opened by shells. I had ordered batteries to fire upon villages. I had not wept.

“That night, beside that unknown woman, while the gendarme struck match after match and the snow covered her, I wept.”

The General paused. No one moved. The smoke of our cigars had gathered beneath the ceiling in a pale layer.

After a moment the magistrate said softly:

“And the second time?”

“The second was in Africa,” replied the General. “There was no cold, no crowd and no sudden madness. There were only heat, hunger and calculation.

“An expedition of eighty-seven men had gone south to survey a route between two military posts. They had camels, guides, interpreters, ammunition, flour for forty days, dried dates, medicines and twelve goats. The tribes received them peacefully. Chiefs came beneath white banners, accepted gifts, drank coffee with the officers and promised water.

“On the seventeenth night they attacked.

“The camp was surrounded before dawn. The guides disappeared; the camel drivers cut the tethers; shots came from the dunes. Most of the detachment died beside the baggage. The survivors broke through to the west—twenty-one soldiers, a lieutenant, a surgeon and three native auxiliaries. They saved their rifles, six cartridge boxes, two water skins and a sack of meal.

“One of the auxiliaries led them to a spring. The water looked clear. They drank, filled the skins and marched on. Before sunset the vomiting began. The surgeon said the spring had been poisoned with a desert plant. Two men died during the night; five could no longer walk in the morning.

“They left them beneath a cloth stretched between rifles.

“From that point the account becomes exact, because the lieutenant kept a notebook. I read it afterward. He recorded directions, hours, distances, deaths and distributions of water. On the first day, each man received one cup. On the second, half a cup. On the third, the skins were empty.

“The heat was not like fire. Fire attacks; that heat possessed. It entered the skull, remained behind the eyes, dried the tongue until speech became impossible. The sand reflected the sun upward, so that men seemed to march between two suns. Their belts loosened. Their lips split. They ceased to sweat.

“They killed the goats. Then they ate the meal dry. Then they chewed leather straps softened under stones. At night they sucked rifle barrels for the coolness of the metal.

“Hostile riders followed at a distance, visible each morning as black points upon the horizon. The survivors could not remain together, for a single rush would destroy them. They therefore spread across nearly a league, each man walking alone, with two hundred paces between him and the next. Thus they could watch one another, yet no one could speak to his neighbor.

“On the sixth day a soldier named Rigal fell behind. The man ahead of him, Perrin, turned back. The others saw the two figures meet. They saw an arm rise, something flash, and one figure fall.

“No one returned to interfere.

“At sunset Perrin came to the lieutenant carrying Rigal’s rifle and cartridge pouch. He said only:

“‘He was dead.’

“But there was blood on his knife.

“They had eaten nothing for two days. The surgeon examined the corpse. The men stood around him without speaking. No order was given, and yet a procedure established itself. The dead man was divided. Each received a portion according to rank no longer, but according to need. Even Perrin received his share.

“They ate at a distance from one another.

“Afterward they resumed their march.

“Hunger returned. It always returned. That is the frightful part. Shame passed, disgust passed, grief passed; hunger returned.

“Two days later another man disappeared during the night. His boots were found beside the ashes. On the following morning the survivors divided what remained of him. Three days later they drew lots, but the man selected refused to submit and shot the surgeon. They killed him, then divided both bodies. After that they abandoned the lots.

“They continued as irreconcilable allies. Each feared the others; each depended upon them. They marched far apart by day, gathered at dusk, counted cartridges, watched hands, slept in turns, and when one became too weak they observed him with the patient attention of wolves.

“The notebook ended with this sentence: ‘Nine remain. Water estimated two days distant.’

“Only three reached the post.

“One was the lieutenant. He had lost the power of speech. Another died within an hour of drinking. The third recovered, but whenever food was placed before him, he divided it into equal portions and arranged them in a circle.”

The General leaned toward us.

“In the snow, men killed because they were together. In the desert, they killed because they were alone. The first obeyed a cry; the others obeyed an appetite. Both acted reasonably, according to the new law of their misery.”

He looked from face to face.

“You have asked what is horrible. Is it death? Blood? Suffering? No. It is the moment when the familiar soul disappears, while the human form remains; when men invent a necessity, submit to it, and afterward cannot explain whether they were executioners, victims, or merely witnesses.”

The sea continued to murmur below the villa. On the table, the liqueur glasses still shone pleasantly beneath the lamps.

No one touched them.

By the time Hanrahan came again into the villages lying under Slieve Echtge, there was more white than brown in his hair, and the red cloak he had worn in his proud days was faded to the colour of withered fern. He had been through many counties since the spring when he last saw the mountain, singing at weddings, disputing with priests, sleeping in barns and under hedges, and giving away what little silver came to him, for a poet’s hand is quick to open and slow to close. But now his songs were fewer, and when people asked for the verses that had once stirred young men to fighting and young women to tears, he would look towards the door, as if another singer were calling him from beyond it.

He went from Feakle to Caher, and from Caher by the roads above Lough Graney, where the water at evening is like a shield dimmed with breath, and wherever he went he heard some whisper of the hidden people. A child had seen white feet among the rushes; a herdsman had heard hunting horns under the ground; an old woman at Ballinakill said there were lights travelling every night from the lake to the mountain, though no hand carried them. Hanrahan listened, and he would sit long after the tale was done, turning his blackthorn stick between his knees.

“It is not the dead that are calling you,” a smith said to him. “The dead have patience.”

“Maybe they have,” said Hanrahan, “and maybe it is patience I have been hearing.”

He had once thought the world was made for desire and song, and that all sorrow could be shaped into words and so mastered; but age had taught him that sorrow has roots below speech, and that desire, when it cannot find what it seeks among living faces, goes searching in mist and running water. Often now, when he crossed a lonely place, he would feel that the common world had grown thin as old cloth, and that a brightness moved behind it.

One evening, when a yellow moon was rising beyond the shoulder of Slieve Aughty, he came to the edge of a little wood. There were hazel bushes there, and ash trees with their leaves already blackened by autumn, and beyond them a brown stretch of bog leading towards a lake without a house upon its shore. He had been told of a widow living in a townland farther on, who might give him a corner by the fire, but the path divided among the trees, and one branch was maybe a road and maybe the dry bed of a stream.

As he stood considering, he heard a thin voice singing.

It was not a song he knew, though he had known more songs than any man between Galway and the Shannon. The tune rose and fell upon two or three notes, and the words came broken by the wind:

“I am young on the hill,
I am fair on the water,
I am beautiful, beautiful,
And sorrow is my daughter.”

An old woman came out from under the hazels. She was bent nearly double, and her grey hair hung through holes in a rag that had once been a shawl. Her feet were bare and black with bog water; one eye watered continually, and the other shone small and sharp under a red lid. She carried a crooked stick, and a sack of scraps was tied about her waist.

Hanrahan knew her, though he had not seen her for many years.

“You are Winny Byrne,” he said, “that begged from door to door before I had a white hair on me.”

She lifted her head, and her face wrinkled itself into laughter.

“I am Winny Byrne to those that have eyes for rags,” she said. “But I am young where the leaves never fall. I am beautiful, I am beautiful.”

The people said the Sidhe had taken her wits one May Eve, when she slept inside a ring of thorn trees. Some said she had followed a music into the mountain and had come back before morning old and withered, though she had been a smooth-faced girl at sunset. Others said she had always been crazed, and that poverty puts stranger thoughts into the mind than fairy music does. Hanrahan had heard both tales, and believed neither and both, as poets will.

“Where are you going in the darkness?” he asked.

“To my house, where four queens are waiting and a king has forgotten his name.”

“And where is your house?”

“Follow the one that is beautiful.”

She turned from him and went lightly among the trees. Though her body seemed frail enough for the wind to carry, she moved faster than he did, and the sack at her waist made no sound. Hanrahan followed, at first because he feared she would lose herself in the bog, and afterwards because the song drew him, and there was in it some promise he had been waiting for since youth.

The moon went behind a cloud. The path narrowed between pools that glimmered like dark eyes, and the rushes whispered though there was no wind. Once he called to her to wait, but she only cried, “I am beautiful,” and her white hair showed far ahead of him like foam travelling on a black river.

Then the ground failed beneath his foot.

He dropped into a bog drain choked with stones and water, striking his shoulder against one bank and his head against the other. For a while he knew nothing but the cold that filled his clothes. When he raised himself, there was blood upon his face, his left arm hung useless, and his stick had gone from him. He called Winny Byrne, but no answer came except the crying of a bird out on the lake.

He crawled from the drain and went on, for a pale light was moving before him, low over the bog. Sometimes he thought it was Winny’s hair, and sometimes a lantern, and sometimes the moon reflected in water, though the moon was still hidden. At last he saw a cabin crouched against the slope, so low that the heather almost covered its roof. It had no window, and the door was shut, but smoke came weakly through the thatch.

Hanrahan reached the wall and sank down beside it. He had no strength to knock. The stones were warm from the day, and he leaned his wounded head against them, hearing within the house the small snapping of a turf fire and the murmur of women’s voices. He thought Winny had gone in and left him there; but after a time it seemed to him that the wall had become clear as brown water, and he could see through it.

Four old women sat around a table, playing cards by the light of a rush candle.

The first had a face hard and red as beaten copper, and when she laid down a card there was the sound of shields struck together. The second was broad and dark, with gold rings on every finger, and she gathered the tricks to her breast as if gathering kingdoms. The third had eyes covered by a white film, yet she knew every card before it was shown, and letters moved like ants upon her dress. The fourth was little and smiling, with berries in her hair and wine upon her lips, and she sang softly whenever she won.

“You have cheated me of Courage,” cried the copper-faced woman.

“And you have hidden Power in your sleeve,” said the woman with the rings.

“Knowledge was mine before either of you was born,” said the blind one.

The smiling woman scattered her cards. “What is Courage without Pleasure? What is Power without Pleasure? What is Knowledge without Pleasure?”

They began quarrelling, and their voices were the voices of storms beating from four quarters of the sky. Cards flew about the cabin, but as they fell they became red leaves, black feathers, silver fish, and sparks of fire. Hanrahan tried to ask them who had won, for it seemed to him that his life depended upon the answer, but his tongue lay heavy in his mouth.

Then the wall was stone again, and there was silence.

He did not know whether he slept after that or only went deep into pain, but he woke with Winny bending over him. The moon had come free of the clouds, and in its light her face was old and pitiful.

“You followed too slowly,” she said.

“You led me into the drain.”

“I led you to my door.”

She dragged him inside with a strength he would not have looked for in her, and laid him on a heap of dried rushes. There was little in the cabin: a black pot hanging above the fire, a flat stone beside the hearth, a knife with a broken handle, two wooden noggins, and a long staff leaning in a corner. A crust of oaten cake was on a shelf, and a few potatoes lay under sacking.

Winny washed his wounds with water she had drawn from a spring, and bound his shoulder with strips torn from her own petticoat. She gave him warm water from the pot, with herbs bitter enough to wake a corpse, and all the while she crooned that she was young and beautiful. Hanrahan would have laughed at another time, but her hands upon him were smooth and cool, and when he looked at them from the corner of his eye they seemed not the hands of an old beggar but long pale hands such as queens have in old songs.

For three days he lay there, or it may have been longer, because no sunlight entered the cabin and Winny had no reckoning of hours. Sometimes she went away and returned with sticks, roots, or a little milk begged from a distant house. Sometimes she sat by the fire rocking herself, answering voices Hanrahan could not hear. He grew weaker. The wound on his head burned, his breath came short, and the pain in his shoulder spread like cold iron through his side.

Yet there were hours when he felt no pain at all, for music sounded outside, so faint at first that it might have been reeds touching one another, but afterwards full of harps, bells, and innumerable joyful voices. He asked Winny who was passing over the bog.

“Those that are never weary,” she said.

“Are they the people of the hills?”

“They are the people of every place that is broken.”

At night pale lights gathered under the door. They came through cracks in the thatch and moved along the floor like moonbeams under water. Shadows stood beyond the fire, tall and slender, and though he could see no faces, he saw their arms, white as river foam, lifting and falling in time to the music. Once he heard laughter so sweet that he wept, remembering every woman he had loved and every face forgotten before its beauty had faded.

On another night he saw the four card players again, but now they stood in the corners of the cabin. Courage held a burning branch, Power wore a crown of black iron, Knowledge carried a book fastened with seven locks, and Pleasure had a child asleep against her breast. They looked at Hanrahan as if each claimed some portion of him.

“He was mine when he made songs for battle,” said Courage.

“He was mine when the people repeated his words,” said Power.

“He was mine when he sought what no priest could teach him,” said Knowledge.

Pleasure laughed. “He was always mine.”

Hanrahan raised himself and cried out that none had ever possessed him, but they faded before the words were spoken, and Winny, crouched beside the ashes, told him he had been raving.

Towards the end, rain came beating on the roof, and water ran down the walls. Winny fed the last sods of turf to the fire. Hanrahan’s face had grown grey, and his eyes were large and shining. He heard the joyful voices again, but among them came a ringing and clashing, as if swords met in battle beneath the floor.

“The music of Heaven,” he whispered.

“It may be Heaven to one and war to another,” said Winny.

The shadowy beings filled the cabin. He saw them strike at one another with blades of pale flame, and every stroke sent a shower of stars through the darkness. Some had beautiful faces and some had faces eaten away by sorrow, but the same light burned in them all. Hanrahan thought of men he had known who died in ditches, of women bent over sick children, of fools mocked at fairs, and of wanderers turned from warm doors; and it seemed to him that the immortal world had always dwelt nearest to those whom the common world cast aside.

Then a great stillness came, like the silence in the heart of a lake.

His eyes turned to the things about the hearth.

The black pot widened until its rim circled the whole cabin, and within it moved stars, waters, harvests, unborn creatures, and all the plenty the heart can desire but never keep.

“The Cauldron,” he said.

The flat stone shone white beneath the ashes, and from its depth came a crying as of kings chosen and kingdoms overthrown.

“The Stone.”

The broken knife lengthened in the firelight, and its edge was clear as winter lightning.

“The Sword.”

The staff in the corner put forth green leaves, and its point reached beyond the roof into a sky crowded with hosts.

“The Spear.”

Winny rose slowly. “You have seen them.”

“I have named them.”

“But have you asked?”

All his life Hanrahan had asked questions of roads, women, books, priests, wine cups, and the changing moon, and had forgotten each answer when another desire came upon him. Now there was only one question, and it had waited behind every song he made.

“Who are you?” he said.

The old rags slipped from Winny Byrne as darkness slips from water at sunrise. She stood tall before him, neither young nor old, and her hair was bright with a brightness that was not gold or silver but both together. Her face held all the beauty he had followed through the world, and all the grief that beauty had brought him. Yet beneath that face he still saw the beggar’s wrinkles, the red eyelid, the toothless mouth, for the two appearances were one, and neither could destroy the other.

“I am one of the lasting Voices,” she said, “and we make our dwelling among those that are broken, and among those that are dying, and among those the wise call mad. We are hated because we are beautiful, and loved because we are terrible. You heard us in the hills and mistook us for wind. You saw us in women and mistook us for desire. You found us in sorrow and made songs so you would not know our names.”

The cabin opened about her into a depth of blue air. Hands reached from it, pale hands bearing lights. Some lights were red as torches carried before a bride, and some were small and still as candles about a coffin. They circled Hanrahan until his poor bed of rushes seemed a marriage bed and a bier together.

“I am beautiful,” said Winny. “I am beautiful.”

“And what do you want of me?” he asked.

“What was mine before your first song and will be mine when the last mouth forgets it.”

She bent over him and touched his lips.

“You are mine forever.”

The fire went out, or the whole visible world burned out like a candle in the morning; and Hanrahan heard once more the clashing that he called heavenly music, and the joyful voices that had neither beginning nor end. He smiled, though the blood had left his face, and his hand moved as if seeking a harp that was not there.

At sunrise Winny Byrne came out of the cabin in her rags, bent and barefoot, with the sack tied at her waist. She took the road towards Lough Graney, singing in her thin voice that she was young on the hill and fair on the water. A turf cutter saw her from a distance, but when he called she went on without turning, and from that day no one in those parts saw her again.

Two days later the turf cutters found Hanrahan lying on the rushes, with one hand upon his breast and the other stretched towards the cold hearth. There was no mark of fear on him. They carried him down from the bog upon a door taken from its hinges, and word went through the villages that the great wandering poet was dead.

They gave him an honourable wake, though he left neither money nor kin to provide it. Old men repeated his verses, young men argued over the roads he had travelled, and women who had once been girls when he sang beneath their windows came to look upon him. There were candles at his head and feet, and some said the flames burned strangely pale when midnight passed.

They buried him where Slieve Echtge could be seen beyond the fields, and every house sent someone to follow the coffin. But there were a few who, as the clay struck the lid, heard music far off upon the mountain, and one old woman said it was only the wind among stones.

Maybe it was the wind, and maybe it was not.

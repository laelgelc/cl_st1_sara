### The Silk Umbrella

Mme. Oreille was known among her relations for her economy, among the tradesmen for her bargaining, and by her husband for a severity in money matters which made the loss of a ten-centime piece seem like a family bereavement.

She was a little woman, neat, quick, and dry, with narrow shoulders and a forehead crossed by three permanent wrinkles. She moved with short, hurried steps, as if every minute spent walking cost her something. She kept the household accounts in a black notebook, washed ribbons instead of replacing them, turned worn collars, saved string, and reproached the cook for peeling potatoes too thickly.

M. Oreille, who was a principal clerk in the Ministry of Public Instruction, submitted to this discipline. He had learned that peace could be bought only by buying nothing.

For two years he carried an umbrella which had cost eight francs and a half. It was made of bluish cotton, now faded to the color of old ashes. Three of its ribs were bent; the handle had been glued twice; and the cloth, shrinking between the seams, showed little pale windows whenever it was held against the light.

Each morning, when rain threatened, he tucked this object under his arm and went to the ministry.

His colleagues had begun by smiling. Then they made remarks. At last, familiarity having destroyed restraint, they insulted the umbrella openly.

“Here comes Oreille with his lightning conductor.”

“Don’t open it indoors, my dear fellow. Something may fall out.”

“Is it insured?”

One young clerk offered him five francs for it, explaining that he wished to donate it to a provincial museum. Another, on a very wet day, put a saucer beneath it in the cloakroom, with a card reading: FOR THE LEAKS.

M. Oreille pretended not to care. But he suffered. He was a mild man, sensitive to ridicule, and the jokes followed him into his office, where the umbrella stood in a corner like a disreputable relative whom nobody dared dismiss.

At dinner one evening he said cautiously:

“My umbrella is becoming rather worn.”

Mme. Oreille looked up.

“Rather worn? What do you mean by rather worn?”

“Well—the cloth is thin.”

“It keeps off the rain.”

“Not entirely.”

“You came home dry.”

“My left shoulder was wet.”

“Your left shoulder! Turn the umbrella a little to the left.”

He said no more.

A week later he tried again.

“The wind broke another rib today.”

“Then have the rib mended.”

“They say it is not worth mending.”

“They say! Who says? An umbrella maker, of course. He wants to sell you a new one.”

M. Oreille lowered his eyes to his soup.

“The men at the office laugh at it.”

This was a mistake. His wife stiffened.

“Ah! So we must ruin ourselves because a pack of fools laugh? Let them laugh. Does their laughter cost you anything?”

“It is unpleasant.”

“Unpleasant! You are paid to work, not to parade. Your father went out in all weathers with a cane.”

“My father lived in the south.”

“That has nothing to do with it.”

The discussion ended in a storm. Mme. Oreille declared that men had no sense, that government clerks were peacocks, and that respectable homes were destroyed by vanity. M. Oreille went to bed with the conviction that he would carry the old umbrella until it collapsed over his head.

It did collapse three days later.

A gust caught it on the Pont Royal and turned it inside out. The cloth split along two seams, one rib sprang loose, and the sliding ring became fixed halfway up the stick. M. Oreille reached the ministry holding above him a blackened skeleton which cracked and fluttered like a wounded bird.

His colleagues greeted him with applause.

That evening he laid the wreck on the dining-room floor.

Mme. Oreille examined it in silence. She opened it, closed it, pulled the torn cloth, tested the stick, and counted the ribs. Her expression was that of a doctor who refuses to admit the death of a patient.

“At most,” she said, “it requires new cloth.”

“The frame is broken.”

“One rib.”

“Four.”

She counted again and discovered four.

Then she sat down.

“How much does a proper umbrella cost?”

He hesitated.

“Perhaps eighteen francs.”

She stared at him.

“Eighteen francs!”

“A good silk one.”

“Silk! Do you intend to attend weddings under it?”

“The others all have silk umbrellas.”

“The others! Always the others! If the others jumped into the Seine, would you need a silk life belt?”

He attempted to explain that a man in his position ought to appear respectable; that the head of his department had himself remarked on the umbrella; that the jokes were becoming intolerable; and that a solid silk umbrella would last for years.

The last argument impressed her. Longevity was the only form of luxury she understood.

“For years?” she asked.

“Ten, perhaps.”

“Ten?”

“At least, if it is well cared for.”

She calculated. Eighteen francs divided by ten made one franc eighty per year. The cotton umbrella, at eight francs and a half, had survived barely two years, not counting repairs. It was possible, therefore, that silk represented economy. This discovery pained her, because it obliged her to spend money immediately in order not to spend it later.

For two days she considered the matter. On the third she entered an umbrella shop, examined seventeen models, disputed every price, rejected ivory, horn, silver, imitation silver, carved wood, polished wood, and anything described as “fashionable.” She finally purchased for eighteen francs a large umbrella covered with thick black silk and fitted with a plain, strong handle.

She brought it home wrapped in paper and placed it on the table.

“Here,” she said. “Look at it.”

M. Oreille’s face brightened.

“It is excellent.”

“It ought to be. It cost eighteen francs.”

He opened it.

“Not in the room!” she cried. “You will damage it!”

He closed it at once.

“You will leave it in the cloakroom at the ministry. You will not lend it. You will not use it as a walking stick. You will keep it away from stoves. If there is only a little rain, you will wait beneath a doorway. If there is wind, you will take an omnibus.”

“But the omnibus costs—”

“An umbrella costs eighteen francs.”

He promised everything.

The following morning he departed proudly, carrying the umbrella with the careful stiffness of a man entrusted with a flag. His colleagues noticed it at once.

“Oreille has inherited!”

“A silk umbrella!”

“Has somebody died?”

He smiled, pleased despite himself, and placed it in the cloakroom. At six o’clock he took it home.

Mme. Oreille was waiting.

“Give it to me.”

“What?”

“The umbrella.”

She opened it beneath the lamp and inspected every panel.

Suddenly she uttered a cry.

“What is that?”

Near the edge of the silk was a small round hole with a brown border.

M. Oreille bent over it.

“A spark, perhaps.”

“A spark? What spark?”

“I don’t know.”

“You smoked beside it!”

“I do not smoke cigars.”

“Then someone else smoked beside it. You left it near a smoker. I told you to take care! One day! It has lasted one day!”

The hole was no larger than a lentil, but Mme. Oreille contemplated it as if a cannonball had passed through the roof. She pushed her finger toward it, withdrew it for fear of enlarging it, and began to walk around the table.

“Eighteen francs! Eighteen francs thrown into the fire!”

“It can be patched.”

“Patched! A new umbrella patched!”

M. Oreille endured half an hour of accusation. He was charged with negligence, weakness, stupidity, and secret hostility toward household property. At last he escaped to bed.

The next evening he returned late and tried to slip the umbrella into a cupboard.

His wife saw him.

“Bring it here.”

“There is no need.”

“Bring it here!”

He obeyed.

This time there were five holes.

They were scattered across two panels, neat, black-rimmed perforations such as burning cigar ash might make. Mme. Oreille counted them twice. Then she turned pale.

“Five.”

“Yes.”

“Yesterday there was one.”

“I know.”

“Now there are five.”

“I can see that.”

“Then tomorrow there will be twenty! In a week there will be no silk left!”

She seized the umbrella and shook it in his face.

“They are doing it deliberately.”

“Who?”

“Your office people.”

“That is impossible.”

“They laughed at the old umbrella. Now they are burning the new one. They are jealous.”

“Jealous of an eighteen-franc umbrella?”

“People are jealous of anything.”

M. Oreille admitted that the umbrella had stood near several coats and canes, that the clerks often smoked in the passage despite regulations, and that one of them had perhaps carelessly knocked off his cigar ash. But he refused to accuse anyone.

His wife demanded that he lock the umbrella in his desk.

“It will not fit.”

“Then carry it with you.”

“From room to room?”

“Yes.”

“I should look ridiculous.”

“You already look ridiculous. You paid eighteen francs to have holes burned in silk.”

The quarrel became terrible. Doors shook. The cook listened from the kitchen. M. Oreille, pushed beyond endurance, declared that he would never again carry any umbrella, even if it rained ink. Mme. Oreille replied that he would carry this one until only the handle remained.

For several days the damaged umbrella stood in the entrance hall, wrapped in an old towel. No one touched it. When rain fell, M. Oreille wore a heavy coat and arrived at the ministry wet. His wife, who could not bear the sight of the object and could bear still less the thought of replacing it, asked two repairers for estimates. One wanted six francs, the other eight. Both said that the entire cover must be changed, since patches would show.

Six francs! Eight francs! Added to eighteen!

She suffered physically from these figures. She lost her appetite and woke at night thinking of the holes.

On Sunday afternoon her friend Mme. Lormel came to visit. Mme. Lormel was a broad, cheerful woman who spent money with ease and gave advice with greater ease still. She listened to the story, examined the umbrella, and laughed.

“You look as if your house had burned down.”

“It is no laughing matter. Eighteen francs, and now eight more.”

“Why should you pay?”

“Who will pay?”

“The insurance company.”

Mme. Oreille blinked.

“What insurance company?”

“Yours. You are insured against fire, aren’t you?”

“The apartment is.”

“Well, the umbrella was burned in the apartment.”

“It was burned at the ministry.”

Mme. Lormel shrugged.

“Then say it was burned here.”

Mme. Oreille drew back, shocked but attentive.

“I could not.”

“Why not? Fire is fire. A match falls. The silk catches. You put it out. That happens every day.”

“But there are only holes.”

“Make them larger.”

Mme. Oreille stared at her friend. Her small face became grave. The idea entered her mind slowly, not as dishonesty, but as a possible method of restoring balance to the world. She had paid premiums for seven years. Never had she claimed a sou. The company possessed hundreds of thousands of francs. Perhaps millions. What was eight francs beside such sums?

Still she objected.

“They may ask questions.”

“Answer them.”

“They may send an inspector.”

“Show him the umbrella.”

“They may refuse.”

“Then you will have lost nothing.”

This final argument decided her.

After Mme. Lormel left, Mme. Oreille locked the door, drew the curtains, and brought out the umbrella. She placed it on the kitchen table beside a box of matches.

At first she could not act. Her heart beat violently. She imagined policemen, judges, prison, public disgrace, her husband dismissed from the ministry. Then she thought of the eighteen francs.

She struck a match.

The first went out. The second burned her fingers and dropped on the floor. The third she held beneath one of the damaged places. The silk curled, smoked, and opened into an irregular wound. A bitter odor filled the kitchen.

She coughed, opened the window, then enlarged two more holes. The work alarmed and fascinated her. Soon the umbrella appeared to have survived a small bombardment. One panel was badly scorched; another had a long brown tear where she had attempted to join two holes.

“That is enough,” she murmured.

Then, fearing it was not enough, she burned one more.

At dinner she told her husband what she intended to do.

M. Oreille put down his fork.

“You are going to defraud the insurance company?”

“Defraud! What a word! We pay them every year.”

“The umbrella was not burned here.”

“It could have been.”

“But it was not.”

“They owe us more than this. Calculate the premiums.”

“I refuse to be involved.”

“You need not be involved. You never are, when anything useful is done.”

He saw the dangerous color rise in her cheeks and withdrew from the argument. Before going to bed, however, he said:

“If you are arrested, do not mention my name.”

She did not sleep.

By morning her courage had diminished. She dressed carefully in a black gown, put on her best bonnet, wrapped the umbrella in brown paper, and set out for the insurance office.

As she approached the boulevard, she walked more slowly. The bundle under her arm seemed enormous. Passersby glanced at it—or she believed they did. In front of the company’s building, which had a wide entrance, brass plates, and stone steps, she continued past the door.

At the next corner she turned back.

Again she passed the entrance.

The third time she stopped, read the company’s name, adjusted her bonnet, and entered with the desperate air of a soldier running toward guns.

A clerk behind a railing asked what she wanted.

“I have had a fire.”

He looked up.

“Your name, madame?”

“Mme. Oreille.”

“Address?”

She gave it.

“Extent of damage?”

“An umbrella.”

The clerk paused.

“An umbrella?”

“A silk umbrella. A very fine one.”

He directed her upstairs.

The staircase seemed endless. On the first landing she considered fleeing. On the second she sat for a moment, pretending to retie her shoe. At last she reached a large office in which three men were discussing a factory fire involving five hundred thousand francs.

Mme. Oreille stood by the door, clutching her bundle.

One of the men, a gray-haired manager with a courteous expression, noticed her.

“May I assist you, madame?”

“I—I have come about a loss.”

“Certainly. Please be seated.”

She sat on the edge of a chair.

The manager finished giving instructions concerning warehouses, machinery, and stock. Then he turned to her.

“What property was damaged?”

She unwrapped the umbrella.

“This.”

The three men looked at it. One coughed. Another bent over a document.

The manager maintained his gravity.

“Was your residence damaged also?”

“No, monsieur. I acted quickly.”

“Very fortunate.”

“Yes, monsieur.”

“How did the accident occur?”

Mme. Oreille had rehearsed her answer twenty times, but the words now deserted her.

“It was—a match.”

“A match?”

“My husband. No, not my husband. That is, he was there, but it was I—or perhaps the cook. Someone had lighted a match, and it fell.”

“Into the umbrella?”

“Yes, monsieur. The umbrella was open.”

“In the apartment?”

“Yes, monsieur.”

“Why was it open?”

“To dry.”

“Had it been raining?”

She felt herself redden.

“A little.”

The manager glanced toward the window. The previous week had been exceptionally dry.

“Please continue.”

“The match fell. I did not see it immediately. Then there was smoke. Flames! Great flames! I seized the umbrella. I beat it against the floor. I might have been burned alive.”

One of the younger officials examined the silk.

“The fire appears to have started in several places.”

“The sparks flew,” she said quickly. “In every direction.”

“Extraordinary sparks.”

“It was a very bad match.”

The manager pressed his lips together.

“And what sum do you claim?”

She hesitated. This was the essential point.

“Eighteen francs.”

“For the umbrella?”

“It cost eighteen francs.”

“But it is not destroyed.”

“It is ruined.”

“It could be repaired.”

“The repair will cost eight francs.”

“Then the loss is eight francs.”

She had expected this and was ready.

“No, monsieur. The loss is eighteen. It was new. I will not carry a burned umbrella.”

“Insurance restores damaged property, madame. It does not necessarily replace it.”

“But without the fire it would be new.”

“Certainly.”

“Therefore the fire has taken a new umbrella from me.”

“It has damaged one.”

“It has destroyed its newness.”

The manager smiled despite himself.

“That is a subtle distinction.”

“It is not subtle. It is eighteen francs.”

They argued for ten minutes. Mme. Oreille repeated the price, the age of the umbrella, the quality of the silk, the violence of the flames, the speed of her intervention, and the total innocence of the household. Whenever the manager attempted to speak of depreciation, she returned to the premiums paid over seven years.

“At three francs a year,” she cried, “that makes twenty-one francs already, and you have never given me anything.”

“Insurance is not a savings account.”

“It is certainly not.”

One official turned away to hide a laugh.

The manager finally said:

“We can authorize the reasonable cost of repair upon presentation of the tradesman’s bill.”

“How much?”

“Whatever is reasonable.”

“Eight francs?”

“If eight francs is necessary.”

“I want that in writing.”

He looked at her with growing respect. She had entered trembling; now she sat upright, her hands planted on her knees, her eyes sharp and steady.

“Very well.”

“And if it costs ten?”

“We shall examine the bill.”

“No. I do not wish to climb these stairs again. They are very tiring.”

“Have it properly repaired, madame. We will pay the cost.”

“The whole cost?”

“The whole reasonable cost.”

“Write ‘the whole cost.’”

The manager surrendered. He wrote an authorization, signed it, and handed it to her.

She read it slowly, moving her lips.

“It does not say what kind of silk.”

“You may use a suitable silk.”

“The same quality?”

“At least.”

“And the frame?”

“The frame was not burned.”

“It has been weakened by the heat.”

“Was it?”

“It may have been.”

He added that any parts demonstrably damaged by the fire could be restored. Mme. Oreille folded the paper, placed it in her purse, and rose.

Her timidity had vanished. She wrapped the umbrella calmly.

“You have acted fairly, monsieur,” she said. “I shall remain insured with your company.”

“We are honored, madame.”

She descended the stairs with quick, light steps.

Instead of going to either of the modest repairers who had quoted six and eight francs, she crossed the boulevard and entered the finest umbrella shop in the district. Glass cases displayed silver handles, carved ivory knobs, imported canes, and umbrellas whose prices were not written on them.

A salesman approached.

“I wish this umbrella re-covered,” she said.

He opened it and raised his eyebrows.

“It has suffered.”

“There was a fire.”

“So I see.”

“I want the best silk you have.”

He showed her a durable black fabric.

She rubbed it between her fingers.

“Is that the finest?”

“We have a heavier quality, madame, but it is expensive.”

“Show it to me.”

He brought out another roll, dense, smooth, and lustrous.

“Better,” she said. “But is it the strongest?”

“We also have a double-woven English silk.”

“Use that.”

The salesman looked again at her plain dress and carefully mended gloves.

“It will cost twenty-six francs, madame.”

She handed him the insurance authorization.

“That does not matter,” she said. “Make it as fine and as strong as possible. And replace anything that may have been weakened by the fire.”

“The ferrule, perhaps?”

“If there is the least doubt, replace it.”

“The handle?”

She examined the handle, which was perfectly sound.

“Is there a better one?”

“We have ebony.”

“Put on ebony.”

“With a silver ring?”

She considered for one second.

“Yes,” she said. “A thick one. The insurance company is paying.”

Frances came home from Liverpool in the afternoon, when the August heat lay flattened over the fields and every hedge seemed to have gathered dust into its leaves. She walked from the station because there was no trap waiting. The road, white between the banks, dazzled her; tar softened beneath her shoes, and the iron hoops of the gates burned scarlet where the sun struck them.

She carried a little brown case. It was not heavy, but before she reached the common it had dragged her shoulder down, and her gloved hand felt swollen round the handle.

Anne had come to meet her by the footpath. She appeared above the bracken suddenly, bare-headed, her blonde hair roughened by the wind, though there was scarcely any wind below. She wore an old khaki skirt and carried a basket with two rabbits in it, their feet stiffly projecting under a cloth.

“You’re late,” she said.

“I came by the train I said.”

“Ay, but it was late.”

“Then I am not late.”

Anne looked at her sister’s pale, heated face, and took the case without asking. She was five years younger, broader in the shoulder, and had the comfortable, unquestioning strength of one who had never expected life to arrange itself delicately for her.

“Did you see him?” she asked.

Frances pulled off one glove finger by finger.

“Yes.”

“And?”

“He is engaged.”

Anne said nothing. She shifted the case to her other hand and turned towards home. The two rabbits knocked softly against the basket as she moved.

Frances waited for sympathy, but when none came she laughed.

“You might say something.”

“What’s there to say?”

“That you are sorry, perhaps.”

“I am sorry if you are.”

“That is generous.”

They walked a little way. The common dipped before them, brown and purple, with hollows of burnt grass and low bushes whose blackish berries had shrivelled before ripening. Beyond it, the stubble rose towards the village in bands of yellow glare. A few cattle stood near the pond, their legs dark in the shallow water, their heads bowed as if in penitence.

“Is she pretty?” Anne asked.

“I don’t know.”

“You saw her?”

“I saw a photograph. She has pearls and a long neck.”

“Like a goose?”

“Not in the least like a goose.”

Anne considered this.

“What did he say?”

“He said he hoped I should like her.”

“And will you?”

“I shall adore her. I shall embroider her initials on everything I possess.”

Anne gave a short laugh. Frances disliked her for laughing and was grateful to her at the same instant.

They reached a thorn tree which cast a small, defective shade. The earth beneath was dry and powdered with the dung of sheep. Frances sat down abruptly, leaning her back against the trunk. She took off her hat and laid it in her lap. The hat had looked pretty in Liverpool; here its grey ribbon appeared affected and limp.

“I can’t go farther yet.”

“You’ll get ants on you.”

“I don’t care.”

Anne put down the case and basket. She sat with her knees raised, her brown forearms resting upon them, and looked across the common. Frances closed her eyes.

At once Liverpool returned: the hotel lounge with its ruby carpet, the palms in brass pots, Jimmy bending over her hand with a tender concern that had never ripened into tenderness. He had been kind. That was the intolerable thing. He had ordered tea and moved the little silver strainer towards her; he had spoken of Evelyn’s music, Evelyn’s people, Evelyn’s charming ignorance of the north. All the while he had watched Frances with his clear, mild eyes, asking her to help him make the occasion graceful.

She had helped. She had even smiled.

Now a grasshopper sprang against her skirt, and she shuddered.

“I suppose you always knew,” she said.

“Knew what?”

“That he would marry someone else.”

“I reckoned he might.”

“Because no one could marry me?”

“Nay. Because he never seemed set on it.”

Frances opened her eyes. The sky above the common was nearly white, emptied by heat. A hawk hung in it, no larger than a dark nail.

“He cared for me.”

“Appen.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means appen he did.”

“You are hateful.”

Anne plucked a stalk of grass and bit the pale end.

“I never liked him,” she said.

“Of course not. He spoke properly and kept his nails clean.”

“So does parson.”

Frances turned away. The objects about her had acquired a cruel distinctness: the split casing of a chestnut under her hand; a strand of sheep’s wool caught on a thorn; the yellow dust in the seams of Anne’s boots. Each thing persisted, complete and needless, while the life Frances had secretly composed for herself fell to pieces without making a sound.

For years she had loved Jimmy in futures. Next winter he would understand. At Christmas he would come. When his prospects improved, when his mother ceased objecting, when Frances had a better dress, when they were both older and less afraid—then his half-smiles and half-confidences would join into certainty. She had lived among these postponed arrivals until they seemed more substantial than any day she actually inhabited.

Now he was engaged to a woman with pearls.

Anne sat up.

“What’s that?”

There was a movement in the thin grass, not the glide of a snake nor the quick neat flight of a mouse, but a blunt, laborious disturbance of the soil. A small mound lifted, cracked, and subsided. Then, as if pushed upward by the heat itself, a mole broke through.

It came into the light foolishly. Its black fur had a bluish bloom, soft as soot beneath glass. Its pointed nose worked with desperate delicacy; the pale hands, spread sideways, paddled at the hard surface. It moved in a crooked rush, stopped, opened its pink mouth, and rushed again.

“Oh!” Frances said. “Poor thing.”

Anne was already on her knees.

“Don’t touch it.”

“It’ll spoil the pasture.”

“What can that one little creature spoil?”

“More than you’d think.”

Anne dropped her hat over it. The felt twitched and travelled an inch. Frances leaned forward, suddenly intent. Beneath the crown there was a frantic scratching, minute yet violent, like rain trapped in a box.

“Let it go,” she said.

“I want to see it.”

Anne slipped her hand under the brim. A moment later she cried out and flung the hat aside.

The mole lay exposed, its mouth wide, its blind head lifted. One of Anne’s fingers showed a bright bead of blood.

“It bit me!”

“Of course it bit you. You were crushing it.”

“I weren’t crushing it.”

The mole turned upon itself. Its hands, absurdly broad and human, seized nothing; its body shone black in the sun. It seemed naked despite its fur, all appetite and terror, thrust into a world for which it had no eyes.

Anne caught up the rabbit basket.

“No,” Frances said.

Anne brought the wooden rim down.

There was a soft blow. The creature contracted.

Anne struck again, more accurately. The small mouth remained open, but the body no longer answered it. One forefoot was doubled under; the other rested palm-upward upon the dust.

For several seconds neither sister moved.

“You beast,” Frances whispered.

“It bit me.”

“You could have let it go.”

“And have it digging?”

“It was alive.”

“So’s everything till it’s dead.”

Anne sucked her finger. She was disturbed less by the killing than by Frances’s gaze, which made her feel large and clumsy. Yet beneath this discomfort she remained sure. A mole in a pasture was damage; a bite was pain; a blow ended both. Things were vexatious chiefly when people refused to finish them.

Frances stared at the little black body. She expected pity to rise in her again, sharp and luxurious. Instead something in her went level.

The chestnut casing, the wool on the thorn, the shrivelled berries—suddenly they were merely things. The hawk was gone. The sun still pressed on her forehead, but it no longer accused her. Even Jimmy’s engagement seemed to have moved away from the centre of existence. A mole had struggled, bitten, and been killed. There was no appeal beyond the fact.

“Give us your hand,” Anne said.

Frances bound the bitten finger with her handkerchief.

“You’ve spoiled it with blood.”

“It’ll wash.”

“It was a good handkerchief.”

“Then you shouldn’t have offered.”

Frances laughed, wearily and without resentment. They left the mole beside the thorn tree, a small black knot upon the immense brown common, and went home.

Near the lane they met Tom Smedley coming through a gate. He had a fork over his shoulder and his shirt-sleeves rolled high. The sun had darkened his face and arms, but under the brim of his hat his eyes looked unexpectedly light. He was not handsome in the manner Frances admired. His nose was too broad, his mouth uncertain when he smiled, and his clothes always seemed to contain traces of whatever work he had last done—straw, flour, horsehair, the green smell of bruised stems.

He stopped when he saw her.

“Well, Fran. Back from Liverpool?”

“So it appears.”

“Had a good time?”

“Magnificent.”

Anne said, “Jimmy’s got engaged.”

Tom’s hand tightened on the fork handle. He looked at Frances, then at the road.

“Has he, now?”

“To a girl with a long neck,” Anne continued.

“Anne!”

“Well, she has.”

Tom attempted a laugh.

“Then he’s suited hisself, I reckon.”

“Perfectly,” Frances said. “We must all be glad when people suit themselves.”

He heard the brittleness in her voice without knowing what to do with it. He had wanted Frances for so long that his desire had lost the shape of hope and become a condition, like weather in the bones. Jimmy belonged to that upper, polished region from which letters came and motorcars descended. Tom had resented him without daring to despise him. Now that the man was removed, Tom felt not triumph but a rough alarm. The empty place beside Frances seemed more dangerous than when occupied.

“What’s happened to your finger, Annie?” he asked.

“A moudiwarp bit me.”

“A mole,” Frances said.

“Ay, a moudiwarp. Did you finish him?”

“I killed it with basket.”

Tom grinned. “That’s my lass.”

Frances looked at him.

“Is it admirable to kill moles?”

“If they’re in pasture.”

“Suppose they are on the common?”

“They don’t know boundaries.”

“Like men.”

Tom shifted the fork. Anne looked from one to the other, interested.

“Could you kill one?” he asked Frances.

She smiled. “Why should I?”

“Thought you were too tender.”

“Did you?”

“Ay.”

“Speak nicely, Tom.”

He flushed beneath the brown of his face.

“I said, I thought you were too tender.”

“That is not what I meant.”

“What did you mean, then?”

“You know perfectly well.”

“Nay, I don’t.”

“She means don’t say ‘ay’,” Anne explained.

Frances gave her a look, but Tom laughed. His laughter changed him; it came from his chest and made him for an instant easy and young.

“All right,” he said carefully. “Yes, Miss Frances. I believe you are too tender-hearted to kill a mole.”

“You believe nothing of the kind. You think I am useless.”

“I never said useless.”

“You think I can do nothing but wear gloves and go to Liverpool.”

“I’ve seen you milk.”

“Once.”

“And you let bucket over.”

“The cow kicked it.”

“The cow knew.”

Anne burst out laughing. Frances felt a small, hot pleasure at Tom’s attention. It was not the pleasure she had known with Jimmy, made of waiting, interpretation, and inward music. This was closer and more humiliating. Tom’s eyes rested on her mouth, then moved away. His body was conscious of hers, and that consciousness passed between them like the smell of warm iron.

She said, “I could kill your mole.”

“Mine?”

“Any mole you choose.”

He lowered the fork from his shoulder.

“I’d not choose one for you.”

“Because you are afraid I might do it.”

“Because I don’t want you crying over it after.”

“I should not cry.”

“You near cried over Annie’s.”

“That was before.”

Before what, neither asked.

Tom looked at her, puzzled and stirred. The hard little challenge in her voice was not permission, yet he took strength from it. He had never known how to approach her softness; perhaps hardness offered a road.

“Bring me one, then,” he said.

“Dead?”

“It won’t stay in your pocket alive.”

“I don’t put presents in my pocket.”

“Give it me at our gate.”

“And what will you give me?”

The question silenced them. Anne bent to investigate a loose strap on the rabbit basket, though nothing was wrong with it.

Tom’s face became grave. “Appen I’ll think of summat.”

“Perhaps,” Frances said, “you had better begin.”

She took her case from Anne and walked on. She did not turn, but she could feel Tom watching her until the lane bent.

That evening her mother spoke of Jimmy over the cold mutton. She called his conduct unfortunate, though nobody could say he had promised anything. Frances agreed. She passed the mustard, discussed the price of damsons, and went upstairs before dusk.

Her room looked west over the orchard. Fruit hung among the leaves, plums with a dim violet bloom, apples green on one side and flushed on the other. Wasps entered through the open window and struck repeatedly against the pane, unable to understand the invisible refusal.

Frances undressed slowly. In the mirror her shoulders appeared thin and white above her shift. Jimmy had once touched one shoulder while helping with her coat. She had preserved the touch for three years, taking it out secretly and warming herself by it.

Now she tried to summon it and felt almost nothing.

Instead she saw Tom’s forearms, brown and scratched, and the pause after she had asked what he would give her. Shame moved through her, followed by a heavier sensation which was not shame. She lay down, but sleep would not come. Under the orchard, beneath roots and fallen fruit, small blind creatures travelled in darkness, perfectly occupied.

The next morning she took Anne’s old boots and went out alone.

Dew lay in the shade, though the open fields were already hot. She crossed the common to the place where the first mole had died. Its body was gone. Only a flattened mark remained in the dust, and beside it a few blue-black hairs clung to a stone.

For half an hour she saw nothing. Then near the lower meadow she found fresh ridges running through the grass. She stood over them with a spade taken from the tool shed. The absurdity of her position made her smile. She, who had gone to concerts with Jimmy and corrected Tom’s speech, waited beside churned earth to murder an animal for courtship.

The ground trembled.

She drove in the spade too soon. Soil broke apart, showing a narrow, polished tunnel. Nothing moved. She waited again, her palms damp on the handle.

Far off, a cart creaked along the road. Larks rose invisibly, pouring sound into the white sky. The meadow smelled of dry clover and animal warmth. Frances’s grief seemed no longer a wound but an exhaustion settled throughout her limbs.

Another mound lifted near her boot.

She struck.

The blade entered earth with a thick sound. A black body rolled from the broken tunnel, not cut but stunned. It began at once to labour away, its nose jerking, its hands tearing at the grass. The light appeared to torture it. Its mouth opened upon a tiny scarlet interior.

Frances raised the spade.

For a moment she could not bring it down. The creature’s blindness was horrible because it did not know her; it struggled against heat, air, roots, the whole bright weight of the upper world, but not against Frances. She might have been fate, or weather, or nothing.

“Go on,” she said.

Whether to the mole or herself, she did not know.

It found the lip of its tunnel. One pale hand disappeared into safety.

Frances struck with the flat of the spade.

The body leaped once. She struck again.

Then it was quiet.

She stood breathing through her mouth. There was no thunder, no accusation. A butterfly passed over the grass, yellow as a falling petal. The cart continued along the road. Her own hands looked ordinary.

She wrapped the mole in a dock leaf and carried it to Tom.

He was in the yard mending a hurdle. When he saw her come through the gate, his hammer stopped in mid-blow. She wore no hat; loose strands of hair stuck darkly to her temples. Earth marked the front of her dress, and she carried the green parcel in both hands.

“I have brought your present,” she said.

Tom set down the hammer.

She unfolded the leaf on the stone wall. The mole lay there, compact and black, its snout bloodied with soil.

“You did that?”

“Yes.”

“Yourself?”

“No, I persuaded it to die from admiration.”

He did not smile. He looked from the mole to her face. Something in the offering troubled him: its ugliness, its intimacy, the deliberateness of her coming. He understood, without being able to put understanding into words, that she had crossed some distance for him, though perhaps not towards him alone.

“Why did you do it, Fran?”

“You told me to.”

“I were teasing.”

“You should be careful.”

“Ay.”

“Talk nicely.”

“Yes,” he said. “I was teasing.”

She folded the dock leaf over the body again.

“Do you want it?”

“Nay.”

“Then I have wasted my morning.”

“Don’t.”

“Don’t what?”

“Don’t make nowt of it.”

“Nothing,” she corrected, but softly.

He came closer. The yard was still. Behind the stable door a horse shifted and breathed; flies moved upon the warm bricks. Frances could smell sawdust on Tom’s clothes and the bitter green sap of the leaf between her fingers.

“Will you come out wi’ me Sunday?” he asked.

She looked beyond him towards the fields. Stubble glittered on the hill; the hedges were dark seams, and above them the sky stood pale and vacant. Somewhere beyond those fields were the railway and Liverpool and Jimmy arranging his gracious future. The thought reached her faintly, like a bell heard under water.

“Where?”

“Anywhere you like.”

“You will hate that.”

“Then anywhere I like.”

“I may hate that.”

“We’ll both be suited.”

She laughed once. Tom did not. His face had grown strained, and she saw how much he feared her answer. The discovery gave her power, but the power was joyless. She could hurt him. She could also cease resisting.

“You know why you are asking me now,” she said.

“I’ve asked before.”

“Not like this.”

“No.”

“Because Jimmy is engaged.”

“Because you’re here.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It’s all I’ve got.”

He put his hand on the wall near hers. He did not touch her, yet she felt enclosed by his nearness. Unlike Jimmy, he had no elegant escape prepared, no words that would preserve them both from embarrassment. He stood before her with his desire like a farm tool held awkwardly in bare hands—heavy, useful, dangerous.

“We’d have to tell your mother,” he said.

“So soon?”

“She’ll hear.”

“From Anne, certainly.”

“And I want it proper.”

Frances looked down at the dock leaf. A dark moisture had begun to stain it from within. She thought of the mole under the earth, blind but whole; then in the light, frantic; then still. She thought of all the hopes she had fed in secrecy, and how eagerly they had burrowed towards a future that did not exist.

“Yes,” she said.

Tom’s hand closed over hers.

She did not lift her eyes. His palm was rough and hot; the pressure frightened her by its certainty. She had imagined surrender as a radiant thing, the final answer to long refinement of feeling. This was not radiant. It was earth, heat, blood under a leaf, a man who said “nowt” and wanted her enough to be clumsy.

“We’ll tell her tonight,” he said.

“Yes.”

Her voice sounded extinguished, as though another woman had answered from deep inside her.

Yet when his thumb moved once across her knuckles, a slow pleasure went through her—dark, involuntary, almost cruel. She let the wrapped mole slip from her other hand to the dust. Neither of them stooped to retrieve it.

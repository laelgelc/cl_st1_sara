The rain had been falling since the middle of the afternoon, at first with deliberation, and afterward with fury. It beat the bare pecan branches, ran in silver cords from the eaves, and turned the road before Doctor John-Luis’s house into a brown, shining confusion in which no respectable buggy would willingly have ventured.

Doctor John-Luis sat before a hickory fire, his slippers extended toward the blaze and his spectacles pushed high upon his forehead. He was a small, well-preserved gentleman of sixty-eight, with a round body, a narrow white beard, and hands that had retained their delicacy through forty years of opening medicine bottles, feeling pulses, and accepting fees when they were offered.

He was reading a paper three days old, which is not necessarily the same thing as reading old news in the country. The storm had reached a point at which he found it satisfactory. His coffee had been good; Marshall had latched the shutters; and the doctor had no patient whose condition, so far as he knew, was likely to require him to leave the fire.

There came a knock at the side door.

It was not loud. Indeed, the rain might have invented it. But after a moment it came again.

“Marshall!” called the doctor.

Marshall appeared from the dining room, drying a cup with a towel. He had served Doctor John-Luis since both men were young enough to believe that age would improve other people.

“Somebody knocking,” said the doctor.

“I hear ’em.”

“Well?”

Marshall looked toward the side hall. “Reckon they knows how to knock, they knows how to wait.”

The knock came a third time.

“Open the door,” said Doctor John-Luis. “A person might drown while you are reasoning.”

Marshall opened it, and a gust of rain entered first. Behind the gust stood a boy.

He was perhaps twelve years old, perhaps fourteen; poverty had made the calculation uncertain. His trousers were rolled above his ankles, one suspender crossed his chest, and his shirt clung to him like a second and much colder skin. A ragged hat drooped over one eye. Mud covered his feet and climbed almost to his knees.

“What do you want?” asked Marshall.

“I don’t want nothing,” said the boy. “I come to get out the rain.”

Doctor John-Luis leaned forward.

“Bring him in. Close that door. Mercy, Marshall, must you freeze the whole parish to examine one child?”

The boy entered without embarrassment, though he left a pool upon the floor large enough to establish his recent history. Marshall took his hat. The doctor pointed to the fire.

“Stand there. Not too close.”

The boy stood too close. Steam rose from his shoulders and trousers. Had he been an apple, he might have roasted; being a boy, he merely steamed and watched the doctor with black, lively eyes.

“Have you eaten?” asked Doctor John-Luis.

“Yes, sir.”

“What?”

The boy considered. “I eat yesterday.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“It’s all I eat.”

“Marshall, give him bread and milk.”

Marshall turned away.

“And a little meat,” added the doctor.

Marshall paused.

“Cold meat,” the doctor said, as if correcting an extravagance into which someone else had betrayed him. “And perhaps an egg. He is growing.”

The boy’s eyes followed Marshall.

“What is your name?”

“My name, it’s Mamouche.”

“Mamouche what?”

“Just Mamouche.”

“No person is just anything. Who are your people?”

“My people’s dead.”

“All of them?”

“My maman dead. Papa, I don’t know. Gran’mère dead long time.”

“And your grandfather?”

The boy wrinkled his forehead. “Théodule Peloté. He dead too.”

Doctor John-Luis took off his spectacles, although he had not been looking through them.

“What did you say?”

“My gran’père, Théodule Peloté. Folks say I favor him when I’m bad.”

The doctor stared. Many years withdrew from the room. The hickory fire became another fire, in another house, before which three young people had once sat while rain moved over the same fields: Théodule Peloté, handsome, reckless, laughing; Stéphanie Galopin, who could reduce both men to silence by lifting one dark eyebrow; and John-Luis, who had believed himself too sensible to be wounded by anything so ordinary as love.

He had loved Stéphanie. Perhaps it is not strictly true that he had never ceased loving her. A bachelor, especially an elderly bachelor, is entitled to arrange his memories so that they furnish his solitude becomingly. Still, when Stéphanie married Théodule, Doctor John-Luis had gone all the way to New Orleans for six months, though he afterward claimed the journey had been undertaken for professional study.

“Your grandmother,” he said, “was Stéphanie Galopin?”

“Yes, sir. You knowed her?”

“I knew her very well.”

Mamouche looked doubtful. It seemed impossible that this neat old gentleman had ever known anyone except Marshall and his coffee.

The food arrived. The boy sat on a splint-bottom chair and ate bread, milk, cold beef, an egg, and two pieces of cane syrup cake. Doctor John-Luis watched him with an attention he would have condemned in another man.

“Give him more milk,” he said.

“He had two cups.”

“Then give him a third. There is no moral law against it.”

Mamouche ate without haste but without interruption. When he had finished, he wiped his mouth with the back of his hand, received a napkin from Marshall, and wiped it again.

“Where were you going?” asked the doctor.

“Nowheres special.”

“Where did you sleep last night?”

“In Mr. Boudreaux’s hay.”

“Did Mr. Boudreaux know?”

“He knowed this morning.”

“And what did he say?”

Mamouche grinned. “I didn’t wait till he finished.”

The grin was Théodule’s. It appeared suddenly, with the same invitation to forgive what had not yet been confessed. But the boy’s gaze, when the doctor questioned him more closely, was steady and intelligent; that was Stéphanie.

“You may sleep here tonight,” said Doctor John-Luis. “Tomorrow we shall discuss what is to be done.”

“I can work.”

“I do not doubt you can. The question is whether you will.”

“I will if somebody watches me.”

Marshall made a noise that might have been a cough.

A bed was prepared in the small room behind the kitchen. Doctor John-Luis himself brought an old nightshirt and stood by while Mamouche, wrapped in a blanket, crossed the passage to receive it. The nightshirt reached his heels.

“You will remain until morning,” the doctor said. “Do you understand?”

“Yes, sir.”

“And you will not leave the house without speaking to me.”

“No, sir.”

That answer, although grammatically uncertain, was accepted as a promise.

The rain continued. Doctor John-Luis returned to the fire, but not to his newspaper. He remembered Théodule’s fiddle at the harvest dances; Théodule’s horse tied inside the schoolhouse as a joke; Stéphanie’s indignation, which had always selected the correct offender, even when evidence did not. He remembered her saying, “John-Luis, you are kind, but you make a ceremony of it, and that spoils half the kindness.”

He smiled at the fire.

The house had not seemed so quiet before the boy entered it. Now every silence suggested a sleeping child.

In the morning the rain had stopped. Pale sunshine lay upon the wet orchard, and the drops on the orange leaves flashed like little mirrors. Doctor John-Luis came into the dining room in a fresh collar.

“Where is he?” he asked.

Marshall poured the coffee.

“Gone.”

“Gone where?”

“If he’d left a direction, I reckon I could tell you.”

Doctor John-Luis set down his cup.

“What is he doing?”

“Running, most likely.”

The kitchen window had been raised. On the sill lay the old nightshirt, neatly folded according to Mamouche’s conception of neatness. Nothing had been taken. Not bread, not money, not even the ragged hat, which Marshall had set near the stove to dry. Mamouche had gone bareheaded into the wet morning.

“He is ashamed,” said the doctor.

“Of what?”

“Of being treated kindly.”

Marshall looked at his master. “That’s a mighty educated kind of shame.”

Doctor John-Luis did not answer.

For several days he was irritable. He complained that the coffee was weak when it was strong, that the fire smoked when it did not, and that Marshall moved his belongings. Marshall, who had not moved them, began moving them out of self-respect.

Then, one morning, the doctor announced that he had reached a decision.

“This house requires a boy.”

“House been standing eighty years without one.”

“I do not mean for the house. I mean that I have certain obligations. A man in my position should educate some worthy child.”

Marshall glanced at him. “A little ragged one, maybe?”

“Any worthy child. I am speaking on principle.”

“Yes, sir. Principle about so high?” Marshall held his hand four feet above the floor.

The doctor ignored this.

Candidates appeared.

The first was the son of a distant cousin, a solemn boy named Alcide who folded his hands, lowered his eyes, and replied “Yes, sir” with such perfection that the doctor distrusted him before luncheon. He ate one biscuit and refused syrup.

“A boy who will not eat syrup at thirteen,” said Doctor John-Luis afterward, “has either a bad stomach or a secret.”

The second candidate was healthy, honest, and industrious, but he whistled through his teeth. He whistled while feeding the chickens, while closing doors, and once during grace.

“I seek companionship,” said the doctor, “not a mockingbird.”

The third was intelligent, but corrected the doctor’s pronunciation of a Latin plant name. The correction was wrong.

The fourth had excellent manners and wiped his shoes three times upon the mat, yet he teased the old setter until the animal hid beneath the buggy.

“No,” said Doctor John-Luis. “Cruelty is not improved by clean shoes.”

Marshall brought no fifth.

Meanwhile, trouble moved through the neighborhood. Gates disappeared after dark and were found hanging from oak trees. Mr. Savarin’s buggy had been placed backward in his shed. Three geese belonging to Widow Nicaud were painted with blue laundry marking, an indignity from which the geese recovered more rapidly than the widow. A bell was tied beneath Father Antoine’s mule; the mule, offended by its own music, entered the churchyard at a gallop during catechism.

The most serious affair concerned Doctor John-Luis’s orchard gate. It vanished on a Saturday night and was discovered Sunday morning stretched across the little bridge half a mile away. Marshall brought it home in the wagon.

“This is not mischief,” declared the doctor. “It is persecution.”

“Gate don’t know the difference,” said Marshall.

“I shall offer five dollars to the person who identifies the culprit.”

“Five dollars for a gate you already got back?”

“Five dollars for justice.”

The notice was posted at the store. It produced many accusations and no culprit. Every mother suspected the son of another mother; every boy possessed an alibi supplied by boys whose own alibis required support. Doctor John-Luis raised the reward to ten dollars after someone put molasses on the schoolmaster’s saddle.

Then rain returned.

It began one evening with a low mutter beyond the cane fields. By dark it rattled against the shutters and ran from the roof just as it had on the night Mamouche first arrived. Doctor John-Luis sat before the same hickory fire. He was not thinking of the boy; at least, he had been not thinking of him steadily for more than an hour.

Hoofbeats sounded in the yard. Men shouted. The side door opened without ceremony, and Marshall entered with a lantern.

Behind him came Mr. Savarin and the parish constable. Between them, wet, muddy, and grinning uneasily, stood Mamouche.

“We caught him,” said Mr. Savarin. “Caught him taking my south gate off the hinges.”

Mamouche’s grin disappeared when he saw the doctor.

Doctor John-Luis rose.

“You?”

“Yes, sir.”

“You removed my orchard gate?”

“Yes, sir.”

“You hung the Boudreaux gate in the oak?”

“Yes, sir.”

“The blue geese?”

Mamouche looked almost proud. “Yes, sir.”

“The schoolmaster’s saddle?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Father Antoine’s mule?”

Mamouche hesitated. “I tied the bell. I didn’t tell him to run in the churchyard.”

The constable produced several objects from a sack: a screwdriver, a length of rope, two brass hinges, an apple, and a silver spoon.

“There now,” said Mr. Savarin. “The little rascal’s a thief besides.”

“I ain’t no thief!” cried Mamouche.

The warmth left his face. He sprang backward, but the constable held his shoulder.

“That spoon came from Widow Nicaud’s,” Mr. Savarin said.

“It didn’t. She give it to Miss Célestine with some jelly, and Miss Célestine throwed it at her rooster when he got in the gallery. I picked it up to bring it back.”

“At night?”

“I wasn’t going there yet.”

Doctor John-Luis took the spoon. “Did you steal the hinges?”

“No, sir. Them’s from the old shed by the bayou. Roof fell in before Christmas.”

“The apple?”

Mamouche looked surprised. “I stole that.”

Mr. Savarin gave a triumphant grunt.

“From my orchard,” Mamouche added, looking at the doctor.

The doctor examined him over his spectacles. Mud, rain, fear, and defiance struggled upon the boy’s face. Théodule would have laughed and lied. Stéphanie would have confessed and then argued with Heaven concerning the punishment. Mamouche, possessing both inheritances, had selected confession with qualifications.

“I believe him about the spoon,” Doctor John-Luis said.

“You believe that tale?” demanded Mr. Savarin.

“I do.”

“What about the reward?”

“You identified the culprit. You shall have it.”

He went to his desk and counted out ten dollars. Mr. Savarin accepted the money with the solemnity of one making a sacrifice to civic order.

“And the boy?” asked the constable.

“I will take responsibility for him tonight. Tomorrow I shall speak with Father Antoine and the others. Any damage will be paid.”

Mamouche stared at him.

When the men had gone, Marshall took the boy to the kitchen, washed the mud from him, and supplied dry clothes. The same splint-bottom chair was placed before the same fire. Bread, meat, and milk appeared without discussion.

Mamouche did not eat.

“That is serious,” said Doctor John-Luis. “You were willing to confess to tormenting half the parish, but you are afraid of a plate of beef.”

“I wasn’t tormenting ’em.”

“What were you doing?”

Mamouche searched for an answer adequate to the pleasure of a gate rising cleanly from its hinges, the secret journey through wet grass, and the glorious astonishment of upright people in the morning.

“Just making things different.”

The doctor sat opposite him.

“Why did you leave my house?”

Mamouche bent his head. “You was good to me.”

“That is a strange cause for flight.”

“I been telling you my people and eating all your food. Then you give me a bed like I belonged here. I didn’t belong. If I stayed, maybe you find out I’m bad.”

“And so you left before I could discover it?”

“Yes, sir.”

“A very clever arrangement. You then spent your freedom removing gates.”

Mamouche’s mouth twitched, but he did not smile.

“Listen to me,” said Doctor John-Luis. “You have been idle, troublesome, disobedient, destructive, and occasionally absurd. You have also told the truth when a lie might have served you better. You were ashamed of kindness because you had not learned how to receive it. That is not goodness, Mamouche; but neither is it corruption.”

The boy looked up.

“I knew Théodule Peloté,” the doctor continued. “He could charm a bird from a tree and then throw his hat at it for amusement. I knew Stéphanie Galopin. She had more sense than Théodule and me together, though I did not always admit it. You have his restlessness and her eyes. With education, work, prayer, and supervision—especially supervision—you may become a man they would recognize without embarrassment.”

Mamouche swallowed.

“What you want me to do?”

“I want you to live here. I want to educate you and raise you as my own child.”

The doctor’s voice had grown low and husky, which annoyed him. He cleared his throat.

“You will not be imprisoned. The door is there. If you prefer the roads, the haystacks, and the removal of agricultural barriers, you may go. But if you remain, you will obey me, attend Mass, study, work, and leave gates where Providence and their owners placed them.”

Mamouche looked toward the door. Rain whispered against it.

Then he looked at the fire, the food, Marshall, and Doctor John-Luis.

“I stay,” he said. “If you sure.”

“I am seldom more sure.”

“That ain’t what folks say,” Marshall murmured.

Mamouche ate.

The first weeks were not free from difficulty. He went to school; he weeded the vegetable garden; he learned that Latin possessed even more rules than Doctor John-Luis. He returned Widow Nicaud’s spoon and apologized to Father Antoine’s mule in the presence of Father Antoine. The gates remained upon their hinges, though Mamouche sometimes paused before a particularly easy one with the abstract admiration of an artist denied his profession.

One morning Doctor John-Luis came to breakfast and noticed the boy’s chair empty.

“Where is he?” he asked.

Marshall poured the coffee.

“Out by the shed.”

“What is he doing?”

“Praying.”

“Praying?”

“Says he got a powerful temptation to put Mr. Savarin’s buggy on top the cane wagon. He’s saying Hail Marys till it passes.”

Doctor John-Luis hid a smile behind his cup.

Marshall added, “I told him work passes it quicker. Got him splitting kindling between prayers.”

Through the window came the measured fall of the hatchet. Now and then it stopped, and a boy’s voice could be heard hurrying reverently through the familiar words.

Doctor John-Luis turned toward the hickory fire. He thought of Théodule, who would have moved the buggy first and prayed afterward; he thought of Stéphanie, who would have made the prayer severe enough to frighten temptation from the parish.

Mamouche, evidently, intended to do both things in his own fashion.

And because Doctor John-Luis was prejudiced, as old friends and new fathers are permitted to be, he believed this fashion the best of the three.

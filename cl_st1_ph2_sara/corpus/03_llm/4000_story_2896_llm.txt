The morning upon which my tragedy was murdered—for murder it was, though the weapon employed was no more heroic than a goose-quill—I rose at eleven, having passed the night in that species of wakefulness which is sleep embittered by consciousness. On the table lay the journal. I had folded it so that the fatal paragraph was concealed; yet I knew it was there, as the Spartan mother knew the fox beneath her son’s garment.

“Mr. Helmstone,” said the critic, “has mistaken obscurity for depth, inflation for dignity, and a constitutional dislike of punctuation for poetic fire.”

There followed other enormities. My heroine was “a vaporous young person”; my tyrant, “a cross landlord in a purple cloak”; and my invocation to Greece—which I had believed capable of making the bones of Pericles ring in their urn—was pronounced “six pages of sonorous weather.”

I paced Broadway like a dethroned emperor who has neglected to change his boots. The populace moved about its petty business. Carts rattled; clerks laughed; ladies examined ribbons. Nobody stopped me to say, “There goes Helmstone, whose Prometheus of the West has been misunderstood by an age of brass.” The sun itself shone with insulting impartiality.

At the corner I encountered Standard.

Standard had the calm eye of a man who never wrote anything longer than a receipt and therefore considered himself entitled to advise authors. He surveyed me, my black cravat, my Byronic hair, and the newspaper clenched beneath my arm.

“You have seen it?” I demanded.

“Seen what?”

“The assassination.”

“Ah? Somebody killed?”

“My poem.”

“Then it can’t have been immortal.”

There are jests which touch the heart like poisoned stilettos; there are others which merely reveal the jester’s want of soul. I assigned Standard’s observation to the latter class.

“Laugh,” I said. “Rome laughed while Cicero bled.”

“Cicero talked while Cicero bled, if accounts are trustworthy. Come to the circus.”

“The circus!”

“Exactly. Horses, sawdust, a fat fellow in spangles, and a clown who has never invoked Greece.”

“Standard, I stand upon the ruins of my hopes.”

“You’ll stand more comfortably in a box.”

He took my arm. In the acquiescent apathy of despair I allowed myself to be conducted toward that temple of vulgar astonishment where men hang by one foot to obtain applause which poets solicit on both knees.

Near the entrance we were joined by a short, full-bodied person in a faded brown coat and a beaver hat so grievously bunged that it seemed to have survived an inquisition. His hair, where it escaped beneath this calamity of a hat, was almost white; but his complexion was ruddy, his gray eyes clear, and his mouth possessed that easy mobility which belongs either to children or to men who have outlived the necessity of posing.

“Hautboy,” said Standard, “this is Helmstone, the poet.”

“Glad to know you, sir,” cried Hautboy, grasping my hand. “A poet! Well, that’s capital. You must tell me what to read. I’m a poor hand at choosing books.”

Humility, when carried to this degree, is sometimes offensive. I bowed with such mournful benignity as a condemned genius may bestow upon an innocent mechanic.

Inside, Hautboy’s delight began before the performance. The lights, the horses, the scarlet coats, the very smell of orange-peel and sawdust appeared to him so many private benefactions. A boy dropped a paper trumpet upon his hat; Hautboy restored it with a smile. A woman obscured half his view with a feather; he shifted his chair without spleen. When the clown entered and fell over a stool, Hautboy laughed—not the harsh cachinnation of the mob, but a mellow, rolling laugh, savoured like a ripe peach.

Observe the injustice of Heaven! Here was I, with an imagination populous as Athens, incapable of pleasure; while this jiggumbob fiddler-looking fellow drew happiness from a painted nose.

“Very droll,” he said, wiping his eyes. “Did you see how he looked at the stool, as if the stool had plotted it?”

“The obvious,” I replied, “has always been the resource of popular art.”

“Ah?” said Standard.

“The multitude,” I continued, “requires physical symbols. It cannot comprehend inward ruin. Let that clown represent a soul broken by criticism; let him fall, not over a stool, but from the empyrean of hope—who would laugh then?”

“Depends how he fell,” said Hautboy. “That stout man behind us would laugh if Socrates slipped on a cabbage.”

Presently a young equestrian made three attempts to leap through a paper hoop and failed twice. Hautboy applauded the third success warmly, yet without the frantic bellowing of those who mistake noise for judgment.

“She has courage,” he observed, “but her left hand is stiff. The gray horse knows more of the business than she does.”

At once the ringmaster spoke to her, she relaxed the hand, and the gray horse carried her through beautifully.

I turned upon Hautboy with some surprise.

“You understand horses?”

“A little. Music teaches one to watch time, and animals keep it better than men.”

His remark, though unadorned, was not contemptible. During the remainder of the entertainment I studied him. He laughed readily, but never before the point; admired dexterity, but was not dazzled by danger; pitied failure without patronising it; and distinguished, with quiet exactness, between the tumbler who possessed strength, the rider who possessed grace, and the clown who possessed only red paint.

What was the explanation?

I found it immediately. He had common sense.

Genius? What! Genius is an eagle chained to a rock and fed by critics upon its own liver. Common sense is a comfortable poultry-bird scratching worms in the dooryard. Hautboy was happy because he had never climbed; content because he had never desired; sane because the gods had mercifully denied him wings.

After the circus Standard proposed supper. We went to a crowded eating house, descended three steps, passed between steaming dishes and clamorous waiters, and secured a small table beneath a lithograph of General Washington which gravy had rendered melancholy.

Hautboy ate with appetite. This, too, I set down against genius. I myself ordered oysters, contemplated them tragically, and sent them away untouched.

“You don’t like oysters?” asked Hautboy.

“I like them. But there are hours when the body’s demand for nourishment becomes an impertinence.”

“Ah?” said Standard.

“Something has happened,” said Hautboy kindly.

“My last work,” I answered, “has been received with brutality.”

“Bad notice?”

“Bad! Sir, when Aristarchus corrected Homer, Homer had centuries with which to answer him. I have only Thursday.”

Hautboy considered this.

“Was the fellow right?”

I stared.

Standard buttered his bread.

“My good man,” said I, “that is precisely the question which no artist can ask while the wound is fresh.”

“No, perhaps not. But after it heals?”

“After it heals, the artist discovers that criticism proceeded from envy, ignorance, or indigestion.”

“Then there’s no harm done,” he said cheerfully.

No harm done! My midnight vigils, my magnificent juvenility consumed in composition, my heroine vaporised, my tyrant reduced to a landlord—and this ruddy philosopher found no harm done!

“You possess,” I said, “a temperament enviably defended against ambition.”

“Do I?” said Hautboy.

“He means,” Standard explained, “that you never wanted anything worth having.”

“Oh, I’ve wanted a great many things. Once I wanted a blue coat with silver buttons so much that I dreamed of it for a month.”

I smiled with mournful superiority. The blue coat explained him. Some natures aspire to Parnassus; others to buttons.

When supper was ended, Hautboy invited us to his rooms.

“They’re five flights up,” he warned us, “and not handsome. But I’ve a bottle of tolerable punch, and perhaps Helmstone will read us some of his poem.”

“I think not,” I replied. “Prometheus shall not again be bound for the entertainment of mankind.”

“Then I’ll fiddle to you.”

We climbed. With every flight the public world dropped farther beneath us: its lamps, equipages, journals, applauses, and assassinations. Hautboy’s chamber was small, low-roofed, and scrupulously clean. A bed stood behind a curtain; three chairs disagreed as to shape; books and music occupied a shelf; and over the mantel hung a little child’s shoe in a glass case. I did not ask its history. Even in my egotism I perceived a silence there which deserved respect.

Hautboy mixed the punch, removed his battered beaver, and took up a violin whose varnish was worn pale beneath the chin.

“What shall it be?” he asked.

“Something merry,” said Standard.

“Something true,” said I.

“Same thing, sometimes,” said Hautboy.

He drew the bow.

At the first phrase I altered my posture. At the second I forgot to maintain it. What had seemed a homely room expanded, not in dimension but in significance; the cracked ceiling lifted like dawn; the common lamp became a star domesticated for our use. His instrument did not whine, glitter, caper, or declaim. It spoke.

First came a country measure, so fresh that I smelled clover and saw girls in white sleeves passing under elms. Then the melody deepened. Youth entered it—ardent, vain, immortal youth—bearing laurel crowns in both hands and certain that the universe had assembled to observe its arrival. Trumpets sounded afar; doors flew open; multitudes cried one name. The name I could not hear, yet the glory of it struck me with an almost personal pang.

Then, quietly, the crowds withdrew.

The trumpets became an echo, the echo a question, the question nothing. The laurel dried. The doors closed. A solitary figure remained, not broken, not accusing Heaven, but listening. Presently he heard what applause had drowned: a kettle singing, rain on a roof, a child laughing in another room, a friend’s step upon the stair. Around the ruined temple of Fame there crept a vine; upon the vine opened roses; and beneath those roses the forgotten man sat playing to himself, with all creation for an audience.

My splenetic soul capitulated.

Hautboy ended on a note so simple that silence seemed its continuation.

“Sir,” I said, after an interval, “you have genius.”

“Ah?” said Standard.

“Hautboy,” I continued, “forgive me. I mistook your serenity for limitation. That performance contained passion disciplined by intellect, sorrow purified by art, and—yes—something Orphic.”

“Glad you liked it,” said Hautboy. “The middle part went a little flat.”

“Who taught you?”

“Oh, one and another.”

“His modesty,” I said aside to Standard, “is positively monstrous. Such gifts concealed in a fifth-story room! Why has he never appeared before the public?”

Standard raised his eyebrows.

“Never?”

“Never, indubitably. Had the world heard him, it could not forget.”

“Ah?”

That syllable began to irritate me.

“Hautboy,” said Standard, “shall I tell him your true name?”

“His true name?” I cried.

Hautboy laughed. “If you think it will amuse him.”

Standard named a name.

The glass in my hand trembled. I had heard it from my father, from old musicians, from those who remembered the great fever of twenty-five years before: the miraculous child who had conquered three capitals; the little Apollo carried through cheering streets; the prodigy before whom princes had bent, ladies wept, and grave men disputed as Greeks might have disputed over a new god. Engravings of that cherubic face had hung in ten thousand parlours. Fortunes had been showered upon him. Poets—worse poets than I, doubtless—had crowned him in verse. Then the name had vanished.

I looked at the gray hair, the full, short figure, the serene eyes, the bunged beaver on the table.

“You!” I whispered.

“So they tell me,” said Hautboy.

“But you were famous!”

“Very.”

“Rich!”

“For a while.”

“Adored!”

“Till Master Betty came through—or somebody else. There is always somebody else.”

“And now?”

“Now I teach scales to six girls and four boys, copy music when I can get it, play at dances when I’m asked, and pay my rent with reasonable punctuality.”

I rose and walked about the chamber. Here, then, stood the catastrophe which I had spent my life fearing: Fame departed, wealth dissolved, the public in love with another face. Yet the victim was not raging upon a heath. He had enjoyed the circus. He had eaten supper. He had restored a paper trumpet to a child.

“Do you never suffer?” I demanded.

“Certainly. I have rheumatism in wet weather.”

“I mean from neglect.”

“Whose neglect?”

“The world’s!”

Hautboy’s eyes twinkled.

“My dear sir, the world attended to me so much when I was a boy that I have no complaint if it attends to its own affairs now.”

“But the contrast—the summit and this room!”

“The summit was draughty,” he replied. “This room is warm.”

Standard poured himself more punch.

“Would you not return,” I persisted, “if one tremendous triumph were offered you?”

“To play? Gladly.”

“To be acclaimed!”

“Oh, that. Applause is pleasant enough. So are peaches. But a man who cannot be happy without peaches should not blame winter.”

I sank into my chair. My system of philosophy—constructed through years of exquisite mortification—fell with less dignity than the circus clown over his stool. I had believed unhappiness the certificate of genius, ambition its pulse, envy its inevitable fever, and fame its only cure. Yet here was genius after fame, genius stripped of heralds, equipages, purses, and paragraphs; and it remained not merely alive but merry.

I thought of my tragedy, that intolerable fate of nine hundred lines. Had I loved it? Perhaps. Had I loved truth, beauty, Greece, mankind? Possibly. But beneath these august attachments I discerned a small, hungry creature craving to see the name HELMSTONE printed large, craving whispers in drawing rooms, engravings in shop windows, a laurel crown—provided always that the leaves were counted by reporters.

Hautboy tucked his fiddle beneath his chin.

“Another tune?”

“Yes,” I said humbly. “Something elementary.”

The following morning I built a fire. Into it went my tragedies, odes, invocations, apostrophes, and the unfinished epic in twelve books whose hero was transparently myself with better legs. Prometheus resisted combustion, but I broke him across the tyrant’s longest speech and thrust him in with the poker.

Then I went out, purchased a fiddle, climbed five flights to Hautboy’s room, and became his most ambitious—and, I trust, his least celebrated—pupil.

“THE CEDAR CHEST”

You have asked me often enough, girls, how I first met your Uncle George—or “my George,” as Lottie will have it—and I suppose this rainy evening is as good as another for telling it. Only you must not expect a love-story at the beginning, with roses and moonlight and a young man waiting by a gate. There was moonlight, to be sure, but it fell on a woodshed window; and the first time I saw George Brett, he had one boot in his hand and was preparing to creep through a kitchen like a burglar.

But I am beginning in the middle, which old people are always accused of doing.

It was the summer I was nineteen, and a hard summer on our farm. The hay had been poor, the early potatoes took blight, and then a windstorm shook down half the apples before they were the size of walnuts. Father walked through the orchard afterward and said he did not know what we should do with so much fruit. That was his way. If every apple had stayed on the branches, he would have complained that we had no barrels enough.

Still, we girls knew there was trouble. The life-insurance premium was due, Tom needed new boots, and Mother had been saving the sugar as if each spoonful were silver. I had learned dressmaking, but there was scarcely any work near us, and the shoe factory at Tunkett had laid off twenty girls. So when I saw an advertisement in the county paper for “a capable young woman as housemaid and child-minder; good home; Protestant preferred,” I read it twice and then carried it to Mother.

“You’re capable,” said she, “and I hope you’re Protestant.”

“Where is Lanton?” Father asked.

“Forty miles by railway.”

“Forty miles!” said Father, as if it were the other side of China.

Yet he ‘most cried when he consented, poor dear, and Mother did cry, though she turned away and pretended the onions troubled her. I wrote to Mrs. Bowles in my best hand, telling her I could cook plain food, wash, iron, mend, and care for children. Girls, learn to cook plain food. A person may live without embroidery, and even without algebra for considerable periods, but no household is improved by heavy biscuit and burned oatmeal.

Mrs. Bowles answered kindly. Her husband met me at Lanton station one hot July afternoon. He was a tall, quiet man with a brown beard and a look as if he had not slept enough for several years. He took my carpetbag, asked whether the journey had tired me, and then drove me three miles in a dusty buggy without saying ten more words.

The Bowles place stood on the edge of the village, a square white house with green blinds, a neglected garden, and a long shed joining the summer kitchen. The lilacs had grown over the path, and two small faces watched me from an upstairs window. Before we reached the steps, the front door opened and the children came tumbling out—a boy of six named Jamie and little Barbara, who was four and had one stocking falling down.

“Are you the new Annie?” Barbara asked.

“My name is Nora.”

“Can you make toast that isn’t black?”

“I believe I can.”

“Then I shall love you,” she said solemnly.

That told me something of what had gone before.

Mrs. Bowles was upstairs in a pleasant room, though it had been allowed to grow close and disorderly. She was not old—perhaps thirty-five—but illness had made her face thin and transparent, and her hands looked like little white leaves upon the counterpane. She held one out to me.

“I am ashamed to receive you here, Nora,” she said. “Everything has gone wrong since Annie left. Two girls came after her, but one stayed four days and the other only two. I fear you’ll turn about before your trunk is unpacked.”

“I’m not so easily turned,” I told her.

She smiled, and I loved her from that moment. There are people who ask for service as if they were conferring a favor by allowing you to work for them; Mrs. Bowles made every kindness seem a gift received with wonder.

Well, I will not weary you with all I found, though Sue likes housekeeping matters and would hear every particular. The bread-box was green in the corners, the flour bin had no cover, and a pan containing what might once have been gravy stood behind the stove with a spoon upright in it. The dish towels were sour, the children’s cups were under the sofa, and there was no clean cloth for Mrs. Bowles’s tray. I opened the pantry and shut it again to gather courage.

Mr. Bowles appeared in the doorway.

“Bad?” he asked.

“Not past saving.”

He nodded, as if I had given him important medical news, and went away.

I tied on an apron, opened every window, built up the fire, and put water to heat. I scrubbed the bread-box, scalded the jars, found half a ham bone and made broth, and cut the scorched parts from a loaf until I had enough decent bread for toast. Jamie and Barbara followed me like two hungry chickens.

“Annie said children should eat in the yard,” Jamie informed me.

“Did she?”

“She said we cluttered the kitchen.”

“You may clutter that end of it if you keep your fingers out of my bowl.”

By supper-time the kitchen smelled of soap, broth, and baked apples; the children were clean; and Mrs. Bowles had eaten half a cup of broth and one thin slice of toast. I set fresh water, a bell, and a shaded lamp beside her bed and pinned a towel over the cracked place in the washstand pitcher so it would not scratch her hand.

When I went downstairs, Mr. Bowles stood looking into the bread-box.

“I’ve not seen the bottom of this for months,” he said.

Then he paid me my first compliment by taking three helpings at supper.

Those first weeks were busy, but happy in their way. Mrs. Bowles grew stronger enough to sit by the window, Barbara’s stocking remained where a stocking ought, and Jamie ceased hiding crusts under the dining-room rug. There was preserving to do, ironing enough for a hotel, and always some lost thing to find. Once Mr. Bowles asked where in Tunkett the clothes-line had gone, and we discovered that Jamie had cut it into harnesses for imaginary horses.

I missed home terribly at night. The whistle of the late train would come across the fields, and I would think of Mother folding her work, Father latching the barn, and Tom whistling on the back step. But useful work is a good cure for homesickness—not a perfect one, girls, but better than sitting at a window and watering your sorrow.

Mrs. Bowles talked to me when I brushed her hair. It was from her I first heard about Annie Pike.

“She was not a bad girl,” Mrs. Bowles said. “She had no mother from the time she was eight, and her father cared more for the tavern than for his children. Annie was bright and pretty, but restless. She wanted ribbons, journeys, excitement—anything she had not got.”

“Why did she leave?”

“We dismissed her. Mr. Bowles found that she had taken money from his coat.”

“Did she confess?”

“She wept and returned it. I wanted to give her another chance, but she grew angry when we spoke to her. She went away saying we would be sorry.”

Mrs. Bowles sighed, and I saw she was sorry already—not for herself, but for Annie.

By September she could walk from the bed to the chair. Mr. Bowles had business in Millford concerning a parcel of land, and because his sister was coming to stay the following week, he thought he must go then or lose the chance. He disliked leaving his wife, but she said, “Nora is more dependable than half the men in Lanton,” which pleased me more than I let anyone see.

There was money in the house—nearly eight hundred dollars, I afterward learned—which Mr. Bowles was to carry to Millford for the purchase. He kept it in a tin dispatch-box in his study. Why he did not deposit it at the bank I cannot tell you; men sometimes make surprising arrangements and then say women have no heads for business. He meant to take the early train, but a telegram delayed his appointment, and so the money remained while he went to a farm six miles away to settle another matter. He expected to return after dark.

That afternoon, just as I was paring peaches, Annie Pike came to the kitchen door.

I knew her at once from Mrs. Bowles’s description. She was handsome, with black eyes, red cheeks, and curls arranged more elaborately than suited a weekday visit. Her dress was cheap but showy, and she wore a silver-colored brooch as large as a biscuit.

“You’re the new girl,” she said.

“I am Nora Dane.”

“I’m Annie. I used to live here.”

I asked her in, for one cannot leave a visitor standing on the step, but something in her manner made me keep the table between us.

She glanced toward the hall. “Mrs. Bowles still abed?”

“She is resting.”

“And Mr. Bowles?”

“Out on business.”

Her eyes moved quickly to mine. “I left a parcel in the shed chamber when I went away. Some winter things. I came to fetch it.”

That chamber was over the long shed and reached by narrow stairs from the summer kitchen. We used it for trunks, old furniture, dried herbs, and such odds and ends. The low windows had simple hooks, and one of the hooks was broken. I had noticed it while looking for preserve jars and had meant to ask Mr. Bowles to mend it.

“What sort of parcel?” I asked.

“A brown one, tied with string.”

“I have seen none.”

“I know where it is. I’ll not trouble you.”

She went up the stairs before I could answer. I heard her moving about overhead, then the scrape of something heavy. Presently she came down with nothing in her hands.

“Couldn’t find it,” she said. “Maybe I’ll come another day.”

Her face was flushed. She smiled too brightly, bade me good afternoon, and went out by the yard gate.

Now, girls, suspicion is an ugly thing when it grows merely from dislike. But observation is not suspicion, and I had observed that Annie’s shoes were dusty when she entered and clean at the toes when she descended, as if she had knelt on the chamber floor. I had heard a window raised, though the day was cool. And she had looked three times toward the passage leading to Mr. Bowles’s study.

I put down my knife.

“Nora,” called Mrs. Bowles from upstairs.

I went to her, carried Barbara with me, and sat until she was comfortable. Jamie was playing in the front room. Nothing had happened. Nothing might happen. Yet I could not rid myself of the feeling that Annie’s business was unfinished.

At five I gave the children their supper. At six I locked the outer doors. At half after six the light began to fade, and the shed seemed to stretch longer and darker beyond the summer kitchen. I remembered the broken window hook.

I told Jamie to stay with his mother, took off my shoes, and crossed the kitchen in my stocking feet.

Do not ask me why I went alone. I was nineteen, and nineteen is sometimes brave because it has not yet learned all the reasons for fear. Besides, I thought I might find only that Annie had opened a window for some foolish purpose. I did not then imagine a robber hidden in the house.

The stairs to the chamber were steep and unpainted. I climbed slowly, avoiding the third board, which creaked. At the top was a door with a large old-fashioned keyhole. It stood nearly closed, but not latched.

Through the crack I saw the last gray light lying across the floor. The trunks and chairs stood about in strange shapes, like crouching animals. The window at the far end was open an inch.

Then a pebble struck the glass.

I scarcely breathed.

A second pebble came, and the window rose. Annie’s head and shoulders appeared above the sill. She climbed in, then leaned out and whispered, “Now!”

A man’s hands caught the ledge.

He pulled himself through without a sound. He was broad in the shoulders, with a cap drawn low, and when he turned I saw a dark, hard face and teeth that showed between his lips like a wolf’s. Annie caught his sleeve.

“You’re to wait,” she whispered. “The man may be back before nine.”

“And the sick woman?”

“She can’t rise, and the children are babies. The new girl’s a country fool.”

That country fool, girls, was kneeling six feet away with both hands pressed over her heart to keep it from shaking her body.

“Where’s the money?” the man asked.

“In the little study, in a tin box. I heard Bowles tell the doctor he had it. When they’re asleep, come down. The back door key hangs by the clock.”

“And if the girl wakes?”

Annie was silent.

He took her chin roughly. “If she wakes?”

“Frighten her. Don’t hurt her, Lew.”

He laughed under his breath.

At that moment I understood more than the words told me. Annie was afraid of him. Whatever vanity or anger had brought her there, she had gone farther than she wished and did not know how to turn back.

The man looked around. “Where do I wait?”

“In that cedar chest. She never comes up here at night.”

The cedar chest was enormous, made to hold blankets, and it stood beneath the sloping roof. Annie raised the lid. He climbed in, grumbling, and she lowered it almost shut.

“I’ll leave the window unhooked,” she said. “Come out after eight.”

She crossed the chamber and slipped downstairs.

I flattened myself behind the door as she passed. There are instants that last as long as a winter. I could smell dust and cedar; I could hear Annie’s skirt brush the wall; and from inside the chest came one slow, impatient movement.

When her footsteps reached the kitchen, I crept to the railing and listened. The outer door opened. Closed. The yard gate clicked.

Then I moved.

The cedar chest had an iron hasp, and the padlock hung open from it. I had used that lock only the week before when Barbara insisted there were mice in the blankets. I crossed the room, lifting my feet carefully. The chest lid rose the width of a finger.

I dropped the hasp.

The man struck upward just as I snapped the padlock shut.

The whole chest leaped.

I did not wait. Then I ran!

Down the stairs I flew, through the kitchen, into my shoes without fastening them, and up to Mrs. Bowles. I must have been white, but I kept my voice steady.

“Mrs. Bowles, I must go to the Bretts’. There is a man locked in the cedar chest.”

She looked at me once and understood that I was not raving.

“Take Jamie and Barbara.”

“I can go faster alone. Lock the room after me. Do not open it for anyone but Mr. Bowles, Mr. Brett, or George Brett.”

“Nora—”

“I shall be quick.”

I kissed Barbara, though she stared at me in astonishment, and ran downstairs. The chest was thundering overhead now—bang, bang, then silence, then a dragging sound. I snatched the back-door key, locked the door outside, and flew across the field.

The Brett farm was nearly a mile away by the road and perhaps half that by the pasture, if one crossed the brook and climbed two stone walls. Twilight had fallen. The cows lifted their heads as I passed, and the wet grass caught at my skirts. I ran like a spirit flying or a wind blowing; at least that is how I remember it, though I daresay I looked more like a frightened hen.

The brook soaked one shoe. I lost the other at the second wall and left it there.

Lights shone in the Brett kitchen. I reached the door and struck it with both hands. A young man opened it.

He was tall, with fair hair, grave gray eyes, and his shirt-sleeves rolled above strong brown wrists. That was George. No, Lottie, I did not think him handsome. I thought only that he looked capable.

“There is a robber in our cedar chest,” I gasped.

He did not laugh or waste one second asking whether I was certain.

“Father!” he called. “Bring the rope.”

Old Mr. Brett came from the sitting-room, spectacles in hand. I explained while George put on his boots. Mrs. Brett wanted me to remain, but I could not leave Mrs. Bowles and the children. Mr. Brett’s lame knee prevented his running, so George said he would go ahead with me and his father could follow with the hired man.

“You’ll stay behind me,” George said as we crossed the pasture.

“I know the house.”

“And I know men.”

That was almost all we said.

When we reached the yard, the house looked peaceful and bare beneath the rising moon. Girls, it lay so peaceful—every curtain still, the porch silver, the children’s upper window glowing softly—that I could scarcely believe evil had entered it. Then came a crash from the shed chamber.

“He’s breaking out,” I whispered.

George removed his boots.

That is why he had one boot in his hand when I first truly saw him. We entered through the kitchen window, because I had locked the door and dared not make noise with the key. George moved like a cat. He took the poker from beside the stove and motioned me toward the hall.

A board cracked overhead.

Then another.

The man had broken the chest lid or torn out the hasp. We heard his feet on the chamber floor.

“Go upstairs to the family,” George whispered. “Lock yourself in.”

I went as far as the hall, but I did not obey entirely. George has reminded me of that for forty-seven years.

The shed stair door opened.

A shadow slipped into the kitchen. Moonlight came through the keyhole and laid a silver finger across the floor. The man paused, listening. George waited behind the pantry door.

The robber took one step.

George sprang.

There was a crash, a curse, and the poker flew under the table. The man twisted free and struck George against the wall. I saw his evil eyes and those wolfish teeth; then he drew something bright from his pocket.

I seized the nearest object, which happened to be the dishpan, and flung it.

It struck his arm. The knife clattered down.

After that I cannot tell you precisely what occurred, because the room swayed and my sight went black around the edges. There was pushing, pushing, a chair broke, and somebody’s elbow struck the clock. Then George had the man face downward on the floor with one arm behind him.

“Rope,” said George, breathing hard.

As if Providence had timed it, Mr. Brett and the hired man entered with a lantern and a length of clothes-line.

“Well,” said Mr. Brett, looking at the broken furniture, “I see you began without us.”

They tied the robber securely and sent the hired man for the constable. Mrs. Bowles called my name from above, and I ran to her. The children were crying, but safe. She put both arms around me and held me as if I were her own daughter.

Mr. Bowles returned before the constable left. He heard the account in silence, went upstairs to his wife, and afterward came to me in the kitchen. I was sweeping broken crockery, because one must do something after an adventure.

“Nora,” he said, “I trusted you with my house.”

“Yes, sir.”

“You’ve returned it to me with one chair less and everything else safe.”

Then he shook my hand. That was a great speech for Mr. Bowles.

The robber was a fellow named Lewis Crane, who had been suspected of theft in two towns. He went to prison. Annie’s name did not come out that night, for I had told no one I recognized her, only that I had seen a woman admit him. George knew there was more to the matter, but when I asked him to leave the telling to me, he did.

The next afternoon I found Annie at her aunt’s little house beyond the railway. She opened the door, saw me, and nearly fell.

“He said he wouldn’t hurt anybody,” she whispered.

“I heard you tell him not to.”

Her face crumpled. For all her ribbons and bold looks, she seemed then no older than Barbara might have seemed in ten years—motherless, foolish, and terrified.

I did not excuse her, girls. Mercy is not pretending wrong is right. I told her plainly what might have happened to Mrs. Bowles and the children, and I made her understand that fear of disgrace did not release her from setting matters straight. But I promised that if she would break with Lew’s companions, seek honest work, and repay what she had once taken, I would help her.

For a time I visited every week. Mrs. Bowles, who guessed more than I said, found sewing for her. Later Annie went to live with a pastor’s family in another town. She became a good nurse, and when her father was ill, she cared for him faithfully to the end. No one there knew the cedar-chest story, and there was no need they should.

As for George—yes, Sue, I am coming to him. He stopped at the Bowles house the next day to ask after my nerves, and the day after that to repair the broken window hook. Then he came to mend the chest, though it was past mending, and later he brought me the shoe I had lost by the stone wall. It required a surprising number of visits to return one muddy shoe.

Three years afterward he became my George.

So that is the story. If there is a lesson in it, you may choose the one you like best: keep your bread-box clean; mend broken window fastenings; do not suppose courage means having no fear; and never decide that a foolish young person is lost beyond recall.

But I think the truest lesson is this—that when people trust you with what is helpless and dear to them, you may discover strength you never knew you had. I was only a hired girl in a wet stocking, scared nearly out of my senses. Yet Mrs. Bowles and the children were mine to guard.

And, girls, that made all the difference.

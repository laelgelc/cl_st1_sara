The three women who had just passed them wore the composed melancholy of persons who had received bad news in excellent handwriting.

“They look extraordinarily depressed,” said the Baroness, turning to watch their collective progress beneath the plane trees. “The youngest one especially.”

“That is Veronique Calthorpe,” said Clovis. “She has every reason to look depressed. She has ruined the family prospects by an act of almost Christian solicitude.”

“One generally ruins only one’s own prospects by Christian solicitude.”

“Veronique has always been advanced. The other two are her sisters, Agnes and Marion. They used to be known as the Three Graces, though no one now remembers who started the libel. Their present expression is due to Aunt Dorothea.”

“Dead?”

“Not in the least. That is the trouble.”

The Baroness settled herself more comfortably in her chair.

“A living aunt may be depressing,” she observed, “but she is at least capable of alteration.”

“Aunt Dorothea has altered,” said Clovis. “She has altered from a moderately rich, thoroughly manageable woman into a moderately poor, entirely unmanageable one. The process has been expensive, but educational. Veronique paid for the education indirectly.”

“Tell me.”

“Dorothea Penning was for many years one of those poor relations whom Providence distributes among comfortable families to prevent them from taking comfort for granted. She had a small income, a large capacity for gratitude, and hats which suggested that millinery had been revealed to her in a dream and imperfectly remembered on waking. The Calthorpe girls were kind to her in a regulated fashion. They sent her pheasants when pheasants were no longer wanted at home, invited her to stay during weeks in which no useful purpose would have been served by keeping a spare bedroom empty, and provided her with advice in quantities which made material assistance almost unnecessary.

“Roger, who was their cousin and Dorothea’s nephew, behaved differently. He had no advice to give, and very little money, but when he had any he spent some of it on her. He took her to inexpensive restaurants where the food was bad and the company exhilarating, introduced her to people whom Agnes described as ‘not quite our sort,’ and once pawned a hunting watch to pay for an operation on Dorothea’s Pekingese. The operation was unsuccessful, but Dorothea never forgot the watch.

“Then an Australian cousin, who had quarrelled with every relation except Dorothea because he had never met her, died and left her a fortune.”

“How much?”

“Enough to make affection a serious occupation. Agnes, Marion and Veronique immediately folded their collective wings about her. They chose her house, her servants, her carriage and, whenever possible, her opinions. They guarded her from unsuitable people, unsuitable charities and unsuitable securities. In return Dorothea was expected eventually to leave them the greater part of her money, an arrangement which had not been stated, because stated arrangements are apt to sound like arrangements.

“Unfortunately, she announced that Roger was to receive an equal share.”

“The Pekingese’s revenge,” said the Baroness.

“Possibly. Agnes said it was monstrous that good money should be placed within reach of a man who regarded Ascot as a sacrament and Monte Carlo as a second home. Marion said Roger would dissipate it among horses, cards and women with foreign names. Veronique said least, which was understood to mean that she felt most deeply. In point of fact she was thinking.”

“A dangerous economy of speech.”

“Especially in families. Roger’s conduct offered material enough. He gambled whenever he possessed money and occasionally when someone else possessed it; he knew every bookmaker of consequence and several of no consequence whatever; and his ideas on financial reserve were summed up in the belief that Tuesday generally arrived before Wednesday. But he had one disconcerting merit: he had been kind to Dorothea before kindness became remunerative.

“The sisters attempted moral siege. Agnes spoke of ruined homes, Marion of wasted lives, and Veronique of the quiet happiness that comes from knowing one’s capital is invested in sound industrial concerns. Dorothea listened amiably and asked whether sound industrial concerns were more amusing than baccarat.

“That question alarmed them. It suggested an intellectual curiosity where they had hoped for moral repugnance.

“Roger went that winter to a French resort called Valméri, a place possessing sea air, pink hotels, Russian invalids who danced until four in the morning, and a casino of sufficient respectability to make vice look like municipal enterprise. Veronique conceived her plan. They would take Aunt Dorothea to Valméri. She would see Roger in his native depravity, watch him staking sums which ought to have been earning three and a half per cent, and return to England resolved to save him from an inheritance.

“It was an admirable plan,” said the Baroness.

“All admirable plans contain one assumption which is wrong. This one assumed that Aunt Dorothea would dislike gambling.

“The journey began encouragingly for Dorothea and badly for everybody else. The Channel was rough. Agnes and Marion retired early to separate cabins, each maintaining afterwards that she had been perfectly well but had wished to avoid the distressing spectacle of the other passengers. Veronique remained on deck from motives of duty and was sick from motives of nature. Dorothea ate cold chicken, drank champagne, and conversed with a Belgian commercial traveller whose French, she said, was refreshingly intelligible.

“At Valméri the sisters discovered that their aunt spoke the language rather better than they did, tipped with a magnificence which secured obedience before they could issue instructions, and preferred lunching on the terrace to taking supervised drives along the coast. They had brought a Baedeker, several respectable novels and a quantity of protective intention. Dorothea had brought curiosity.

“Roger found them at the Hôtel des Ambassadeurs. He looked sunburnt, solvent and pleased to see his aunt, three conditions which the sisters regarded as almost offensively misleading.

“‘My dear Aunt Dot,’ he said, ‘this is top-hole. I thought Valméri was the last place Agnes would select for spiritual refreshment.’

“‘We came for the climate,’ said Agnes.

“‘So does everyone. The climate is in the casino after dinner.’

“Dorothea laughed. Veronique observed that casinos were depressing places in which foolish people lost good money.

“‘Not always,’ said Roger. ‘Sometimes they lose bad money. It goes just as quickly.’

“That evening Veronique suggested that they should walk through the gaming rooms. She had the air of proposing a visit to the Roman catacombs—historically instructive, morally fortifying and unlikely to encourage imitation. Dorothea agreed at once.

“The casino was brilliantly lit, elaborately gilded, and crowded with the sort of people whom Agnes habitually excluded from her drawing-room and read about with interest in the newspapers. At the baccarat table Roger was losing gracefully. His opponent, a Levantine banker with a pearl shaped like a small onion, was winning without charm. Dorothea watched for ten minutes.

“‘Does Roger always lose?’ she asked.

“‘Practically always,’ said Veronique, with subdued triumph.

“‘Then someone else practically always wins.’

“‘The bank wins in the end.’

“‘The bank looks very dull. I should have thought winning might have cheered it.’

“Presently Roger came over and explained roulette. Agnes protested that no useful purpose would be served by making the explanation too lucid. Dorothea listened, placed five louis on seventeen, and won.

“The number seventeen had no significance for her; she said afterwards that it looked lonely.

“The sisters received the success with less enthusiasm than if she had lost. Veronique pointed out that beginners were often favoured by a treacherous run of luck.

“‘How hospitable,’ said Dorothea, and staked again.

“She left the casino sixty pounds richer and several years younger in expression. At breakfast she sent for the financial papers and ignored them in favour of a little manual entitled Practical Systems for the Roulette Table. Agnes developed a headache.”

“I suppose they took her away?”

“They tried. But taking away a rich aunt is not like taking away an umbrella. Dorothea had engaged a sitting-room for the season, ordered a motor-car, and discovered a chef who understood lobster in three languages. Moreover, Roger had introduced her to a society of agreeable wastrels who treated age as an irrelevant statistic. A Bulgarian princess taught her chemin de fer; an American widow instructed her in the ordering of cocktails; and a retired Portuguese diplomat, whose past was said to be almost entirely confidential, told her that she had the hands of an empress.

“Agnes and Marion called them adventurers.

“‘Very likely,’ said Dorothea. ‘One never seems to meet adventurers at home. It is probably the climate.’

“Her first losses were small enough to be described as experience. Her second were larger and had to be described as temporary. Then came a night at baccarat when she lost three thousand pounds to the Levantine banker. Veronique sat beside her with the expression of a guardian angel obliged to witness an unsuitable marriage.

“‘You must stop,’ she whispered.

“‘My dear, I have just begun to understand it.’

“‘You have lost three thousand pounds.’

“‘That is what made understanding so urgent.’

“The next morning Agnes summoned Roger and demanded that he use his influence.

“‘Aunt Dot never obeyed me when she was poor,’ he said. ‘Why should she begin now that she can afford not to?’

“‘You encouraged her.’

“‘I explained the rules. Veronique brought her to the table.’

“There are moments when exact truth is an indecency. Veronique left the room.

“For a fortnight Dorothea recovered a portion of her losses and entertained twice as many people to celebrate the recovery. She gave a supper at which ortolans, truffles and a vintage champagne performed the humble offices usually entrusted to conversation. A violinist from Budapest played on the terrace, and a Russian count broke a chair while demonstrating a Cossack dance. Dorothea declared it the pleasantest evening she had ever known.

“‘It cost nearly four hundred pounds,’ said Marion the following day.

“‘Then pleasantness is cheaper than I supposed,’ said Dorothea. ‘I spent thirty years being bored for nothing.’

“That remark marked the end of her moral dependence.

“The sisters now adopted financial defence. They wrote to her solicitor, her banker and an elderly bishop who had once confirmed her. The solicitor replied that Mrs. Penning was legally capable of managing her affairs; the banker observed that her account remained substantial; and the bishop sent a pamphlet on temperance, having confused the issue but preserved his usefulness.

“Dorothea sold some railway shares. Agnes spoke of security. Dorothea replied that railways were merely a means of reaching places where one could spend money and saw no reason why they should monopolise it. She sold an industrial holding after winning heavily at trente-et-quarante, bought a diamond spray from a bankrupt archduchess, and subscribed to Roger’s racing stable.

“‘Horses eat money,’ said Veronique.

“‘So do nieces,’ said Dorothea, ‘but horses run occasionally.’

“That was repeated all over Valméri by luncheon time. I had it from a woman who heard it from the Portuguese diplomat’s valet, so there can be little doubt of its authenticity.”

“And Roger?” asked the Baroness. “Did he profit?”

“Moderately. He attempted, rather more often than his cousins expected, to restrain Dorothea. When she proposed staking ten thousand francs on a single coup he advised five thousand. When she wished to buy a second racehorse he recommended one with four sound legs. His moderation made him appear almost domestic. Dorothea became devoted to him.

“The sisters, meanwhile, resembled three hens who had persuaded a duck to enter a pond in order to demonstrate the dangers of water. They clucked on the bank while Dorothea swam into depths beyond the reach of poultry.

“Agnes was the first to retreat. She announced that her household required her. Marion reminded her that the household had managed during six weeks of absence.

“‘That is why it requires me,’ said Agnes.

“She returned to England, carrying a present from Dorothea of a roulette rake mounted in silver. Marion remained another ten days, sustained by the belief that deserting a fortune in danger was like leaving a sinking ship while the treasure-room remained unlocked. Then Dorothea purchased a villa overlooking the bay and engaged the American widow as a temporary companion. Marion went home on the ground that the English spring was particularly beautiful that year, though it was sleeting at Dover when she arrived.

“Veronique stayed longest. Her sense of responsibility was strengthened by the fact that the responsibility was hers. She watched Dorothea sell more investments, lose heavily, win noisily, dine late, acquire unsuitable intimates and learn to smoke small gold-tipped cigars. Every morning Veronique appeared with a headache and a plan of retrenchment; every evening Dorothea appeared without a headache and with a plan of amusement.

“At last Dorothea sent for her.

“‘My dear Veronique,’ she said, ‘you are looking ill.’

“‘I have been anxious about you.’

“‘That is extremely unselfish, but it does not suit your complexion. You must go home.’

“‘I cannot leave you among these people.’

“‘Roger is here.’

“‘Roger is one of them.’

“‘Precisely. That prevents loneliness.’

“Veronique spoke of duty, affection and the sacred obligation of protecting those who did not recognise their peril. Dorothea listened with the patience of one who has learned to wait while the croupier pays other people.

“‘You brought me here to see Roger gamble,’ she said at last. ‘I have seen him. He loses with great good humour, wins without preaching, and never pretends that money is sacred while wondering when someone else will die and leave it to him. I find that restful.’

“Veronique left Valméri next morning. Her departure had the quality of a retreat from Moscow, except that Moscow remained behind, extravagantly over-illuminated and serving supper.

“Dorothea has since become a confirmed gambler. She no longer has a fortune in the sense understood by prudent nieces, but she possesses a reduced income, a villa full of noisy friends, an interest in three racehorses, and the conviction that seventeen is a misunderstood number. Roger visits her frequently. She has altered her will, though no one knows in whose favour. This uncertainty is the only portion of her wealth that has increased.”

“And the sisters?” asked the Baroness, glancing again towards the three retreating figures.

“They calculate that, had they left Dorothea alone, she might have lived quietly for twenty years and died rich. Instead she is living noisily and may survive them all through sheer occupation.”

“That explains why they look their age.”

“No,” said Clovis. “Looking one’s age is a physical misfortune. Looking depressed is generally an intellectual achievement. Veronique’s distinction is that she has earned both.”

The youngest of the three women turned at that moment, as though conscious of being discussed. Her face held the frozen reproach of a prophet whose prediction has been fulfilled in precisely the wrong manner.

“You are right,” said the Baroness. “She does look the most depressed.”

“She should,” said Clovis. “It was her idea to show Aunt Dorothea how good money could be lost.”

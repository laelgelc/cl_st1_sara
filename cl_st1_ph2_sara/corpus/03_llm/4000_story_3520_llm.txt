### The Snow That Remembered Us

When I look back, I cannot find one Christmas by itself. They have packed together like flakes against a window, each with its own small shape, yet all making the same bright drift. Ask me whether it was the year of the red sledge, or the year Uncle Glyn swallowed a threepenny bit, or the winter the sea froze in the horse troughs, and I shall tell you yes, certainly, it was that year; though perhaps it was three years, or seven, or no year that ever appeared on a calendar.

But there was always snow.

It came over our Welsh town from the dark shoulder of the hills and smothered the chimneys, softened the chapel roofs, and laid a clean hand on the crooked streets. It filled the alleys where dustbins slept, buttoned the harbour boats in white, and turned the beach into a cold country without footprints. At night the lamps shone inside their glass bubbles, and the snow went endlessly past them, as though the stars had broken loose and were drifting down to sea.

“Was it really always snowing?” you ask.

Always.

“It doesn’t now.”

That is because you are looking out of the wrong window.

The Christmas I remember best—which is to say all of them—began with Jim Davies and me crouching behind Mrs. Pugh’s wall, armed with six snowballs apiece and awaiting cats. We did not dislike cats. We admired them as enemies. They were quick, secretive, yellow-eyed creatures from the jungles behind the fish shop, and we were northern hunters in scarves, patient and frost-whiskered, prepared to risk our lives for glory.

Jim had made a snowball so hard it could have sunk a battleship.

“This one’s for the big ginger,” he whispered.

“You’ll never hit him.”

“I hit him Tuesday.”

“That was a flowerpot.”

“He was behind it.”

We waited. The snow ticked softly from branch to branch. Somewhere a shovel scraped. Somewhere a woman called a boy who pretended not to hear. Beyond the roofs, the sea rolled its iron-grey back and sent up a far-off boom.

Then smoke rose from the Protheros’ house.

At first we thought it was only the chimney coughing. But the smoke thickened, black as burnt pudding, and pushed from an upstairs window. A pane cracked. Orange light leapt in the room.

“Fire!” shouted Jim.

“Fire!” I shouted louder, because I had seen it second.

We ran to the front door, hammering with our fists and kicking the brass plate. Mr. Prothero opened in his nightshirt, though it was nearly noon, with one slipper on and shaving foam over half his face.

“Your house is on fire!”

“So it is,” he said, looking over his shoulder.

Then everybody began to move at once. Mrs. Prothero came downstairs carrying a cage with no bird in it. Their daughter Enid carried the bird in her hands. Mr. Prothero tried to put on his coat over his nightshirt and became trapped in the sleeves. A neighbour arrived with a bucket, another with a saucepan, and Mrs. Pugh brought a vase of artificial daffodils, having misunderstood the emergency.

Jim and I threw our snowballs through the broken window.

“Stop that!” cried Mr. Prothero.

“We’re putting it out!”

“You’re breaking the furniture!”

“The furniture’s on fire!”

We raced to the police box and telephoned the brigade, taking turns to give the address, each correcting the other until the fireman on the telephone threatened not to come at all. But presently the engine clanged into our street, red and magnificent, with brass knobs, black boots and a bell that scattered every dog, child and aunt for half a mile. The firemen unrolled their hose like a great stiff serpent. Water burst against the house. Steam rose. Tiles slipped. People offered advice.

“More to the left!” called Mr. Pugh, who had never put out anything larger than a candle.

“Mind the curtains!” cried Mrs. Prothero.

The upstairs room hissed, spat and surrendered. A wet fireman climbed down the ladder, his helmet shining and his face streaked with soot.

Miss Prothero, who had stood throughout the battle clutching a small handbag, stepped forward politely.

“Would any of you gentlemen care for something to read?”

The fireman looked at her. Rainwater dripped from his nose.

“No, thank you, miss.”

“I have a very nice book about birds.”

“The canary!” cried Enid.

And only then did everyone discover that the bird had escaped and was sitting on the fire engine, singing as though the entire performance had been arranged for its benefit.

“Did the house burn down?” you ask.

No. Only the back bedroom, two curtains, a mattress, and Mr. Prothero’s best trousers.

“What happened to the canary?”

It lived to be a hundred.

“Canaries don’t.”

That one did. Now let me get back to Christmas morning.

We woke before waking was allowed. The house was black and breathing, full of sleeping uncles and hidden parcels, and the cold had drawn forests on the windows. I lay listening for bells, for reindeer, for the careful boot of wonder on the roof. What I heard was my father snoring, the kitchen clock chopping the darkness into seconds, and my brother whispering from the next bed:

“Is it morning?”

“Yes.”

“How do you know?”

“I can feel it.”

We crept to the window. Below us the street was unbroken snow, blue in the early light. Chimneys stood in rows like old men waiting for breakfast. The sea was invisible, but it announced itself with low thumps behind the houses. Then, at the far end of the street, two postmen appeared.

They came bent beneath sacks, climbing through drifts that reached their knees. They were explorers carrying news from the outer world, white-shouldered and red-faced, their boots squeaking, their breath smoking before them. At each door they vanished under holly and reappeared lighter by cards: robins, bells, churches, fat children on impossible sledges, and cottages with more snow than roofs could ever hold.

Our bell rang.

The whole house erupted.

Feet struck floors. Doors banged. An uncle asked where he was. An aunt laughed in the wrong room. We thundered downstairs in nightclothes and found the front hall heaped with parcels, envelopes and wet footprints. The postman accepted a glass of something medicinal from my father and carried the medicine onward through the town.

Then came presents.

There were useful presents, which were punishment in coloured paper: stiff collars, brown socks, vests thick enough to armour a pony, handkerchiefs with initials, and a knitted cap that gripped the skull like a determined octopus. There were useless presents, which were treasures: a tin trumpet, a rubber crocodile, a box of soldiers missing their captain, a puzzle of the British Empire, a whistle shaped like a bird, a magnifying glass for burning holes in newspapers, and a mechanical mouse that ran in circles until Auntie Bess stood on it.

There were books I would read, books I promised to read, and books written by improving men who believed boys wanted to know the history of bridges. There were sugared almonds, toffees like amber paving stones, chocolate coins, pink mice, striped sticks, sour balls, coconut ice and one golden orange smelling of a country where winter had never learned the address.

Paper flew. String knotted itself around chair legs. Trumpets trumpeted, drums declared war, whistles summoned invisible dogs, and the mechanical mouse died bravely beneath the piano.

By nine o’clock the relatives had begun arriving.

They came out of the snow in layers: uncles broad as wardrobes, aunts fragrant with lavender, cousins with damp hair, babies packed into blankets, and old people who seemed to have been alive before Christmas was invented. They stamped in the passage, dropped umbrellas, kissed everybody incorrectly, and asked whether we had grown.

Uncle Glyn had a laugh that started in his boots and burst from his waistcoat. Uncle Idris slept whenever furniture offered itself. Auntie Bess whispered scandal loudly. Aunt Megan drank sherry from a thimble-sized glass and became increasingly fond of the piano. Cousin Dilys pinched. Cousin Huw lied. Great-Aunt Nansi wore a fox around her neck, complete with paws and glass eyes, and I spent the morning expecting it to wake.

From the kitchen came the clang and steam of the feast. Saucepans rattled their lids. Fat snapped in the oven. Knives chopped, taps gushed, plates collided, and my mother moved through it all with pink cheeks and flour on her hair, commanding potatoes, rescuing gravy and banishing children.

“Out.”

“We’re only watching.”

“Watch somewhere else.”

“Can we have one sweet?”

“You’ve had forty.”

“Those were presents.”

At last the dining-room doors opened, and there stood dinner, stretching farther than geography. The turkey occupied the centre like a browned and glorious emperor. Around it gathered ranks of potatoes, hills of stuffing, shining peas, carrots, sprouts, onions, bread sauce, cranberry sauce, gravy in steaming boats, and sausages curled like sleeping tails.

We sat. We pulled crackers. Paper hats bloomed on solemn heads. Tiny explosions jumped between our hands. The jokes were read aloud and became less funny each time, which made Uncle Glyn laugh more. My father carved while everyone advised him. Plates were loaded until no pattern could be seen beneath the food.

We ate through weather and time.

The turkey diminished to architecture. Gravy crossed borders. Sprouts rolled under chairs. Babies slept. Dogs prayed beneath the table. Then came pudding, dark, blazing and crowned with blue fire, followed by mince pies, trifle, nuts, cheese and fruit no one had strength to touch.

Uncle Idris fell asleep with his paper crown over one eye. Uncle Glyn loosened three buttons. Aunt Megan announced that she had never felt better in her life and immediately knocked over the sherry.

“Did Uncle Glyn swallow the threepenny bit then?” you ask.

Not that year.

“You said he did.”

Another Christmas. Or perhaps it was a sixpence. Memory gives excellent presents but keeps no receipts.

In the afternoon the rooms grew golden and drowsy. Firelight moved on the wallpaper. Smoke from pipes curled around the paper chains. The grown-ups settled into chairs and became a parliament of snores, coughs and murmurs. One uncle read the same paragraph for an hour. One aunt counted stitches with fierce devotion. Aunt Megan played two bars of a hymn on the piano, forgot the third, and began again in another key.

We children escaped.

Outside, the day had turned pale and enormous. Snow rounded every wall and buried the garden gates. Our town, usually shabby with rain and gulls, had been transformed into a northern kingdom, a fortress of white roofs and blue-shadowed lanes. The chapel wore a frozen crown. The harbour lamps shivered. The beach huts became tombs of explorers lost in ice.

Jim and the others waited at the corner: Dai Thomas, small and fearless; Billy Evans, who carried string for emergencies; and Huw, who said he had once fought a wolf near Swansea.

“That was a sheepdog,” said Jim.

“It was dark.”

“It was noon.”

“It was dark to me.”

We slid down Constitution Hill on trays, boards, sacks and one door borrowed from a shed. We attacked the girls’ snow castle and were driven back by ammunition containing stones. We built a snow giant with a coal-buttoned coat and seaweed hair. We tracked the prints of a mysterious beast, which turned out to be Mrs. Pugh’s spaniel, though Huw maintained it was a young hippopotamus.

Then we walked through town, five conquerors with red ears and wet socks.

Behind curtains, families glowed around fires. Pianos tinkled. Wireless sets crackled. Cutlery clinked in kitchens. From the chapel came the heavy practice of an organ, each chord rolling down the street like a dark cart. Far out in the bay, a ship called once, and the hills returned the call in a softer voice.

At the sweet shop window we stared at jars we could not afford. At the harbour we threw ice at the tide. At the cemetery gates we dared one another to touch the stone angel, and Dai did, though he ran away before it could seize him.

By evening our pockets were wet, our gloves had become cold rags, and the first lamps were coming on. This was the hour for carols.

We went from house to house, singing loudly where the curtains were bright and softly where dogs were known to live. Doors opened. Coins dropped into Billy’s cap. We were given mince pies, slices of cake, two apples, a handful of nuts and, by an old gentleman who disliked music, strict instructions to go away.

At the end of a lane we found a house none of us knew.

It stood behind black laurels, tall and unlit. Snow lay on its steps without marks. No smoke rose from the chimney. Its windows reflected nothing. Even the town seemed to have drawn away from it.

“Empty,” said Jim.

“Haunted,” said Huw hopefully.

“Then you knock,” I told him.

We opened the gate. It gave a long rusty cry. The garden was silent beneath the snow, every shrub crouching. We climbed the steps and gathered before the door.

Billy knocked.

No answer.

He knocked again.

From deep inside came a sound, not a footstep, not quite. A dragging hush, then stillness.

“We should sing,” whispered Dai.

So we began. Our voices were thin at first, shaken by the cold and the house, but soon we grew bold. We sang of stars and shepherds, of night made bright, of a child in a stable and peace descending over the troubled earth. The words climbed the door and trembled in the eaves.

Then another voice joined us.

It came from behind the door: low, cracked and wandering, an old voice or a woman’s voice or the voice of the house itself, singing the same carol half a line behind. It knew the tune but not our time. It rose when we fell. It held one note after we had stopped.

We stood frozen.

The voice continued alone.

Jim ran first. We all claimed afterward that someone else had. Down the steps we flew, through the shrieking gate, along the lane, our boots striking sparks from the hard snow. Behind us the voice seemed to follow, stretched through the dark like a thread. Huw lost his cap. Billy lost the money. Dai lost one shoe and did not notice until we reached the chapel.

“What was in the house?” you ask.

An old woman, perhaps.

“Only an old woman?”

There is no such thing as only an old woman in a dark house at Christmas.

When I came home, breathless and snow-plastered, the windows were bright as ovens. The door opened before I touched it, and warmth rolled out with the smells of toast, smoke, oranges, coffee and the remains of dinner.

“Where have you been?”

“Caroling.”

“Why are you missing a glove?”

“A ghost took it.”

“Take off your boots.”

Inside, the relatives had revived. Uncle Idris was asleep in a different chair. Aunt Megan had found the third bar of the hymn and was now searching for the fourth. Plates of sandwiches appeared. The turkey returned in thinner form. There were pickles, cake, biscuits, nuts and steaming cups. Everybody talked across everybody else, building one enormous conversation no single person understood.

Then the piano began properly.

My mother played, and Aunt Megan sang. Uncle Glyn sang too deeply, my father too late, the cousins too loudly, and Great-Aunt Nansi moved her lips several words behind. We sang hymns, old songs, sea songs, foolish songs with choruses that grew faster each time. The walls seemed to breathe with us. The fire clapped. The windows shook. Outside, snow pressed its quiet face to the glass.

One by one the younger children were carried away. The babies went first, warm bundles smelling of milk. Then the cousins vanished upstairs, protesting. At last I was sent to bed.

From my window I could see the town descending toward the harbour, every lamp and curtained room set in snow. The chapel windows glowed dimly. A ship’s light moved beyond the breakwater. Music drifted from neighbouring houses—piano notes, laughter, a burst of singing, then silence, then singing farther away. The sounds seemed to cross the roofs on soft feet.

Snow fell without hurry, covering our tracks, the sled marks, the footprints of postmen, the place where the fire engine had stood, the road to the dark house, the whole day with all its noise and crumbs and miracles.

I lay under the blankets while the house settled around me. Below, a last uncle laughed. A door closed. The fire murmured in its grate. I said my prayer for everyone I knew, and for some I did not, and for the canary, and for the lonely singer behind the door.

“Were you frightened?” you ask.

Not then.

The snow kept falling over the chimneys and the harbour, over the sleeping aunts and uncles, over Jim’s house and mine, over the sea that never slept. All the town was near, shining and hushed, held for one moment inside the winter night.

And before the final music faded, I slept.

By seven the snow had covered the yard gate. By eight it had erased the road.

I stood in the courtyard with my instrument bag between my boots and the carriage waiting behind me, its two high wheels white to the hubs. Ten miles away, according to the messenger, a young man was dying. Ten miles are not much in summer. In such weather they are a province.

My horse had fallen dead that afternoon. There had been no warning. It lowered its head over the manger, shuddered once, and became an object to be removed when there was time. I had spent the remaining daylight sending Rosa from farm to farm with requests. She returned each time alone.

“They need their horses for the morning,” she said.

“For what?”

“For living, they say.”

Naturally. A doctor’s journey is considered necessary only after it has been completed. Before that it is an inconvenience.

I lifted the lantern. Snow struck its glass with small dry taps. Rosa stood beside the carriage in a thin shawl, holding the useless harness. Her hair was wet at the temples. She was seventeen, diligent, and accustomed to my temper, which perhaps explains why I had ceased to notice how much I demanded of her.

“We cannot go,” she said.

“We must.”

“With what horse?”

The horse—the horse. There was the carriage, the bag, the coat, the doctor and his duty. Only the animal was lacking, and because of that one omission all the rest became absurd. I kicked at the door of the old pigsty, a structure unused since the spring floods. The boards gave inward at once.

A warm smell breathed out.

At first I saw only darkness, then two pale eyes, very low to the ground. A man crawled from the sty on hands and knees. He was broad across the shoulders and wore a leather cap. Behind him, as though drawn from the earth by a chain, came two enormous horses, their flanks steaming, their heads lowered to pass through the narrow doorway. They unfolded themselves in the yard. Their legs seemed too long, their chests too deep. Snow melted before their hooves.

“Harness them,” I said.

The man grinned.

Rosa stepped backward. He caught her around the waist before I understood his intention and pressed his face against hers. She cried out. When she tore herself free, a red wound, semicircular and exact, showed on her cheek.

“You brute!” I raised my bag to strike him.

He ducked without haste and began fitting the harness. He knew every buckle, though he had never seen my carriage. The horses stood obediently, breathing clouds over his shoulders.

“You will come with me,” I said. “I cannot drive these animals.”

“No.”

“You brought them.”

“They know the road.”

Rosa had reached the house door. She held one hand to her cheek and pulled at the handle with the other. The door had swollen in the damp and would not open.

The groom watched her.

“You will come,” I repeated.

He placed my bag in the carriage, lifted me by the elbow as if I were a child, and settled me beneath my fur coat. His strength made protest ridiculous. I should have climbed down. I should have taken the iron bar from beneath the seat. I should at least have shouted to Rosa to run around the house and escape through the kitchen window. Instead I arranged the bag between my knees. Habit is the cowardice of orderly men.

The groom turned toward Rosa.

“Stay away from her,” I said.

He showed his teeth. “Giddy up.”

The courtyard split apart. The gate flew past, though I had not seen it open. Snow became a white wall, then no snow at all, and the carriage stood in another yard beneath a low roof. The journey of ten miles had taken less than a breath. Before I could turn, the horses thrust their heads through the windows of the house, one on either side, and gazed into the sickroom.

I heard, faintly and impossibly, the cracking of my own front door far away.

Rosa!

But the patient’s family had surrounded me. The father took my bag, the mother took my coat, a sister seized my wrist and drew me inside. Their gratitude was so urgent that it resembled arrest.

The room was suffocating. A stove burned red in the corner. Smoke hung under the ceiling. The young man lay on a narrow bed, his face pale but not unusually so. He reached toward me as soon as I approached.

“Doctor,” he whispered, “let me die.”

Patients often make such requests when they do not expect to be obeyed. I put two fingers to his pulse. It was steady. His skin was warm, not fevered. His pupils reacted properly to the lamp. I listened to his chest and heard nothing beyond the ordinary labor of a frightened heart.

He was not dying. He was scarcely ill.

I felt relief, but it was a bitter relief, for now the whole journey revealed itself as needless. Rosa had been abandoned, the groom had been delivered to her like a reward, and I had crossed ten miles of storm to tell a healthy boy to drink broth.

“You will live,” I said.

He closed his eyes. “That is what I fear.”

The mother leaned over me. “Well?”

“There is no immediate danger.”

The father frowned, as if I had confessed incompetence. The sister held up a bloodstained towel.

“You have not examined him properly,” she said.

Doctors are always accused of insufficient examination when the desired illness cannot be found. I opened my bag. The instruments shone in their velvet grooves, precise, innocent, and completely useless. Outside, the horses breathed against the panes. Their eyes were clouded blue, but they watched me.

At home the groom would be forcing the door. Perhaps Rosa had reached the pantry. There was a bolt on the pantry door, though a weak one. Why had I not repaired it when she asked? Twice she had asked. Or had she asked three times? Such details become clear only after they can no longer help us.

The family remained around the bed.

“Again,” said the father.

I examined the patient again.

This time, turning him toward the lamp, I saw on his right side a discoloration no wider than a coin. I bent closer. The skin seemed to loosen under my gaze. What I had taken for a shadow opened petal by petal into a wound as broad as my hand.

It was rose-colored at the edges, darker toward the center, and in its depth black-red, like wet stone in a mine. Fine threads of tissue crossed the cavity. Blood gathered on them without falling. At the bottom, where no bottom ought to have been, pale worms moved together. Each was thick as my smallest finger. Their heads were pink, their bodies white, and when the lamp touched them they lifted themselves from the flesh as flowers lift toward morning.

The boy watched my face.

“You see it now,” he said.

Yes, I saw it. A flower, a pit, a mouth dug into the body. No instrument in my bag had authority there. No powder, no knife, no compress. A wound of that depth would have brought fever, collapse, delirium; yet his pulse remained calm. Clinical facts stood on one side, the wound on the other, and the wound was stronger.

The mother began to weep with satisfaction. The father nodded. The sister smiled at me as if I had finally remembered my profession.

“Cure him,” she said.

I cleaned the edges. I touched the surrounding skin. I pretended to calculate. In truth, I knew he would die. Whether in an hour or in a year I could not say, but the wound was death already established in him, furnished and occupied.

“We must keep him quiet,” I said. “Fresh linen. Cool water. No smoke in the room.”

They listened gravely to these small instructions, and because they were small, despised them.

A murmur had begun outside. Villagers crowded the yard with lanterns. Children pressed their faces to the windows beneath the horses’ jaws. Someone rang a handbell. The sound was thin and cheerful.

I closed my bag.

“I have done what is possible.”

The father blocked the door.

“What is possible?” he asked.

“What can be done.”

“That is not what we sent for.”

They wanted the other thing, naturally. The thing beyond training, beyond nature, beyond any agreement made between a village and its doctor. They had not called me through the storm to hear about limits.

The children began to sing.

“Doctor, doctor, dressed in white,  
Close the wound before daylight.  
If the wound remains instead,  
Lay the doctor with the dead.”

A simple tune. Easy to remember. The adults joined on the second verse.

I appealed to the father. “Control them.”

“They are only children.”

Hands seized my shoulders from behind. My coat was already gone; now they removed my jacket, waistcoat and shirt. I tried to protect my trousers, an undignified struggle, but numbers settled the question. They stripped me completely. The sister folded my clothing with domestic care and placed it on a chair. My fur coat lay across the far end of the room, near the window, close enough to see every dark hair of its collar, too far to reach.

The song continued.

A village places extraordinary trust in a doctor once it has made him naked.

They lifted me onto the bed. The young man moved toward the wall to make room, though there was scarcely room for one. His wound faced me. Its heat touched my belly. The worms stirred in their pit, pleased perhaps by company.

The family withdrew. The door shut. Through the windows the horses stared in, solemn as officials.

For a while neither the patient nor I spoke.

Then he said, “You are not a doctor.”

“I am the district doctor.”

“That is an appointment, not an answer.”

His breath smelled of milk. Up close he looked younger than I had thought. A trace of down lay along his jaw. His eyes were clear and hostile.

“You came without help,” he said.

“I came as quickly as I could.”

“You were brought.”

“That changes nothing.”

“It changes everything.”

I wanted to explain the dead horse, the snow, the refusal of the neighbors, the pigsty, the groom. Above all I wanted to explain Rosa. To whom was the explanation owed—the boy, Rosa, myself? I had been taken from one victim and delivered to another, and because I carried a medical bag everyone supposed this movement had meaning.

“Your wound is serious,” I said.

He laughed once. “Now you speak honestly.”

“But not hopeless.”

“You lie poorly.”

Hope is a treatment prescribed when other treatments have failed. I told him the tissues around the wound were healthy. I said his circulation was strong. I spoke of resistance, observation, proper nursing. Each phrase dropped between us like a dry leaf.

“You are beside me because they threatened you,” he said. “I am beside you because I cannot rise. Which of us is the patient?”

“You are feverish.”

“You said I had no fever.”

The room had grown hotter. Sweat ran along my ribs. The wound shone, beautiful and foul. For a moment I imagined climbing into it, descending past the worms into that red mine where duty, weather and blame could not follow. Then one of the horses struck the outer wall with a hoof.

The window!

I sat up. The boy did not stop me. Outside the singing had moved around the house, perhaps toward the kitchen. I crossed the bed, stepped onto the sill and pushed between the horses’ heads. They drew back to admit me.

My carriage waited below.

I dropped into the snow, climbed onto the seat and caught the reins. My clothes remained on the chair. My fur coat remained by the window. I could see the sister standing beside it now, one hand resting on its sleeve.

“Give me my coat!” I shouted.

She smiled and closed the shutters.

The horses turned from the house. I expected the violent leap that had brought me there. I braced my feet, gripped the seat and cried the groom’s command.

Nothing.

They moved at a walk.

“Go on!”

Their heads sagged. The traces hung loose. One hoof rose, travelled forward, descended; then another. Snow gathered on their backs. The carriage wheels entered the drifts with the patience of millstones.

Behind us the children resumed their song, but altered it:

“Naked doctor, ride away,  
Night is long and roads will stay.  
Could not heal and would not die—  
Let him cross the winter sky.”

My instrument bag had fallen open. Forceps, probes and bottles rolled over the floor with each turn of the wheels. I tried to reach my coat, forgetting it was not there. My hand closed on air.

Cold first tightened the skin, then seemed to enter beneath it. I crouched behind the bag. The reins burned my fingers. Surely the supernatural strength of the animals was not exhausted; surely they were withholding it. They had carried me outward in an instant because the groom wished to be alone with Rosa. Now there was no need for speed.

Rosa, Rosa. Her cheek marked by his teeth, her hands at the swollen door. I had exchanged her for a patient I could not save. No—exchange implies consent, calculation, a bargain. I had simply allowed the stronger will to arrange us.

Perhaps she had escaped. Perhaps the groom had only wanted shelter. Such rationalizations are warm rooms one builds in the snow, without walls and without roofs.

The road stretched ahead, barely visible between fields. Ten miles. At this pace, ten thousand.

By morning the villagers would hear that I had fled naked from a dying boy. By noon another doctor would occupy my district. He would use my waiting room, my bottles, perhaps my bed. He would find my horse dead in the stable and complain that I kept a disorderly house. The groom would tell him Rosa had gone away. Everyone goes away eventually; thus every crime can be described as departure.

I whipped the horses with the reins. They did not notice.

“Home!” I shouted.

One turned its head slightly, showing me its pale eye.

The snow thickened. There were no houses now, no trees, no ditches, only a white plain under a white sky. My carriage moved across it with ceremonial slowness. I could no longer feel my feet. The bag slipped from my knees, but I clutched it again. A doctor must not lose his instruments.

That is our instruction: answer the bell, take the bag, accept the horse provided, enter the house, find the wound. If there is no wound, look again. If there is one, cure it. If it cannot be cured, offer hope. If hope is rejected, offer yourself. Then escape, if you can, without clothes, without honor, without return.

The false alarm had been answered. That was my crime. Yet had I refused, the boy might truly have been dying; indeed, he was dying. Duty has two doors, and behind both stands accusation.

The horses continue. The wheels continue. Somewhere behind me my house is being broken open, my servant is calling, my practice is passing into other hands. Somewhere ahead lies home, but distance no longer decreases merely because one travels.

I sit naked above the great wheels, holding my useless bag. Snow settles over my shoulders like a second, colder skin.

“Home,” I say once more.

The horses lower their heads.

There will be no homecoming.

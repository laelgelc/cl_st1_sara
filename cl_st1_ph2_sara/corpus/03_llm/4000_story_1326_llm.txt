# The Last “Well”

I had gone to the grounds that September afternoon to see New York win a ball game, and I came away scarcely remembering which club wore my colors.

The crowd was already pouring through the gates when I saw Old Well-Well laboring up the incline. I knew him at once. Everybody who had followed baseball along the eastern circuit knew that mountainous figure and the three-part yell which had rolled over grandstands for thirty years.

“Well!—Well!—Well!”

It was no common shout. The first “Well” rose deep and challenging; the second burst broader, like a gun from a fort; the third went up and out, a stentorian blast that seemed to strike the roof, rebound across the diamond, and scatter echoes among the distant houses. Pitchers had turned on the slab at the sound of it. Rookies had grinned, veterans had lifted their caps, and bleacher crowds had taken it up in vain attempts at imitation.

But the man climbing before me could not have frightened a sparrow.

He leaned upon the arm of a younger fellow, stopping every dozen steps to breathe. His shoulders were still tremendous, but his coat hung loosely from them, as canvas hangs from a dismantled mast. The red had gone from his broad face. His cheeks sagged, his lips were gray, and beneath the brim of his old felt hat his forehead shone with cold moisture.

“Easy, Mr. Weller,” said his companion.

Old Well-Well shook him off impatiently.

“I’m all right, Joe. Don’t fuss. Ain’t I been waitin’ six years for this?”

Then he saw me looking and managed a smile.

“Last game of the season?” I asked, chiefly because I could think of nothing else.

“Maybe,” he answered.

The young man beside him looked away. That one movement told me what the old fellow had left unsaid.

We entered together, and by chance—or by the management’s forethought—our seats were adjoining, low in the grandstand and a little beyond third base. Old Well-Well sank into his chair with a sigh. For several minutes he sat bent forward, breathing through parted lips. Yet when the Philadelphia players came trotting from their bench to warm up, he straightened as if a hand had drawn a cord through his spine.

“Where is he?” he demanded.

“There—center field,” Joe replied. “Number twenty-one.”

I followed the old man’s stare and saw a tall, slim youngster loping over the turf. He moved with that deceptive ease which belongs only to men born for speed. He did not run so much as stream. His shoulders scarcely swayed; his feet touched the earth lightly; and when the ball came arching toward him he circled under it as naturally as a swallow wheels beneath an insect.

“That’s Burt,” Old Well-Well said.

There was pride in the two words, but something more than pride—an old hunger satisfied after long denial.

“My sister’s boy. Raised him after she died. Taught him to throw before he could spell Philadelphia. They said he was too light. Said he couldn’t hit a curve. Said he’d never stick in the big league. Wot d’ye tink o’ him?”

Before I could reply, Burt caught a high drive near the wall and returned the ball to the infield. It came low and straight, no rainbow toss, and cracked into the third baseman’s glove.

“Peacherino!” cried somebody behind us.

Old Well-Well’s hands closed upon his knees. For one instant his chest expanded. I expected the famous cry.

His lips formed the first word.

No sound came.

He coughed instead—a dry, tearing cough that doubled him in his seat. Joe gripped his shoulder, and I saw alarm leap into the young man’s face.

“You promised the doctor,” Joe whispered.

“I promised I wouldn’t yell,” the old fellow muttered when he had recovered. “I ain’t yelled, have I?”

“You’d better not try.”

Old Well-Well turned his eyes toward center field.

“I got to see him once,” he said. “Just once, Joe. If the right time comes.”

The band blared; the home players took the field; hats flew; papers fluttered; and the grandstand, filling to its uppermost rim, stirred like a cornfield before a hard wind. New York had brought two thousand rooters, myself among them, and our section thundered when the first batter strode to the plate.

Philadelphia’s pitcher was a lean southpaw named Kline. He worked cautiously and badly. A single over short, a sacrifice, and another single put New York ahead before many of us had settled into our seats.

“One to nothin’!” shouted a red-faced man. “That’s the game! Put it on ice!”

Old Well-Well paid no attention. He watched Burt.

The next batter, Hanley, drove a vicious liner into the gap between center and right. At the crack of the bat both runners were off, and we New Yorkers rose with them. The ball seemed ticketed for the fence.

Burt started across.

Run! He was fleet as a deer. He crossed behind the right fielder, stretched out, and for several strides appeared not to touch the ground. Then he leaped. His gloved hand flashed above his head. Ball and glove met at the last possible inch.

The catch saved two runs.

The crowd broke with a roar. Even the New York benches applauded that piece of fielding. Burt came down, whirled, and threw toward first. The runner, certain of the hit, had rounded second. The ball shot across the diamond, struck the baseman waist-high, and completed the double play.

“Nothin’ to it!” howled a Philadelphia voice.

Old Well-Well was on his feet.

His great frame trembled. His eyes blazed in the pale ruin of his face. Again his mouth opened, and again I waited for that battle cry which I had heard in healthier years shake windows behind the stands.

“Well—”

It came only as a husky breath.

Joe pulled him gently down.

“Save it,” I said, though I had no right to speak.

The old man looked at me, and I divined that he understood more than the words. He nodded.

“Not big enough yet,” he whispered.

Philadelphia tied the score in its half of the second on a double, an infield out, and a long sacrifice fly. Burt, batting seventh, waited in the circle with two men gone. When his turn came he swung at the first pitch and sent a hard grounder directly at the shortstop. Out by a step.

“Too anxious,” said Old Well-Well. “He’ll learn.”

He spoke as a craftsman, not a partisan. He had taught the boy to hold a bat, to shorten his stride, to watch the pitcher’s hand; and now, though death itself seemed seated beside him, he could still rebuke a bad choice of pitch.

The third and fourth innings passed swiftly. New York got two men on in the third, but Kline struck out the clean-up hitter. Philadelphia threatened in the fourth and failed. Burt came up with one out and none aboard. This time he took two balls, fouled off a fast one, and lined the fourth pitch over second.

“A hit!” cried Old Well-Well.

Burt rounded first hard. The center fielder fumbled the carom for a fraction of a second, and that fraction was enough. The rookie kept going.

“Hold it!” yelled the first-base coach.

Burt did not hold it.

The throw came high. He slid under the tag at second in a cloud of red dust, his fingers hooked upon the bag.

“Safe!”

Old Well-Well struck me across the arm. He had forgotten me, the grandstand, his sickness, everything except the streak of youth rising from the dust.

“See him judge it? See him? That boy knows!”

A grounder to third advanced Burt no farther. The next man lifted a fly to medium left. Burt tagged and raced for third after the catch.

“He can’t make it!” shouted a New Yorker.

He made it standing.

Yet another fly ended the inning, and Burt was left there. He jogged to center without outward annoyance, but Old Well-Well sank back, exhausted by the useless venture. His face had taken on a waxen stillness. Joe produced a small bottle and held it beneath his nose. The old man pushed it away.

“Don’t bother me with medicine when there’s a game.”

I began to feel sure that he had not come to see merely one more contest. He had come to finish something. Perhaps all his years of coaching the boy through lots, minor clubs, cheap hotels, sore arms, slumps, and rejection had led to this afternoon. Burt had reached the great arena. Old Well-Well must witness the proof with his own eyes—and, if fate offered one magnificent occasion, he must send the boy his old salute.

In the fifth, New York scored again.

A scratch hit, a base on balls, and a sacrifice put runners on second and third with one down. Philadelphia drew the infield close. The batter chopped toward short. The runner from third broke with the pitch. The shortstop fielded cleanly but chose first, and New York led, two to one.

“Smart ball!” cried our section.

The next batter singled to center.

The runner on second rounded third and came home. Burt charged the ball. He did not pause to set himself. Scooping it on the run, he threw across his body.

From where we sat, the white sphere seemed to skim the grass. The catcher shifted left, braced, and received it with a report like a pistol shot. The runner slid. Dust flew.

“Out!”

The umpire’s arms cut downward.

For half a heartbeat there was astonished silence; then the Philadelphia crowd exploded. Men pounded one another. Women waved parasols. Boys capered in the aisles.

“What an arm!” somebody gasped.

Old Well-Well did not rise. He could not. But his hands lifted from his knees and hovered before him as though blessing the distant player. Tears gathered in his eyes.

“That throw,” he said to me, “I taught him that. Don’t throw where the catcher is. Throw where he’s got to be.”

Once more his chest swelled. Once more his throat labored. No cry came. Only a faint, strangled syllable.

“Well—”

His hands fell.

Joe bent close. “Enough. We’re leaving.”

“No.”

“You can’t stand it.”

“I can stand till he’s done.”

The sixth brought Philadelphia level. Their first man tripled along the right-field line and scored on a single. Score tied, two to two. The crowd, which had been muttering, awakened into noisy confidence.

“Now we got ’em!”

“Knock the lid off!”

“Where’s your ice, New York?”

But neither club could break through. In the seventh Burt made another catch, this one with his back to the plate, racing toward the wooden fence while the whole stand held its breath. He caught the ball over his shoulder, struck the barrier with one hand, and turned smiling.

Old Well-Well laughed—a deep but nearly soundless laugh. It shook him cruelly. When it ended he sat with closed eyes.

“Did he hold it?” he asked.

“He held it,” I answered.

“Sure. He always holds ’em.”

In Philadelphia’s half Burt beat out a roller to short, stole second on the first pitch, and went to third when the catcher’s throw bounded into center. There he remained while two batters struck out and a third grounded weakly to the box.

Failure! Groans rolled through the stands.

Burt trotted out again. From center field he glanced toward our section. I wondered whether he could distinguish his uncle among those packed rows. Old Well-Well lifted his hat. The rookie raised one hand in answer.

That small exchange affected me more than all the cheering. I had known of the old man’s illness, as most baseball people did. The newspapers had said his heart was worn out, that physicians had forbidden excitement, that he had left his bed against orders. Did Burt know how near the end stood? His wave had been gay, but he lingered a moment before turning away.

The eighth went scoreless. The ninth began under a lowering sky, though the afternoon still held. New York opened with a double. A bunt moved the runner to third. Kline, after pitching the game of his life, weakened and gave a base on balls.

One out. Men at first and third.

The Philadelphia manager replaced Kline with a right-hander named Doyle. The New York crowd beat feet upon the planks.

“Bring him in!”

“One little hit!”

“Here’s the ball game!”

The batter drove a fly to center—deep enough, I thought, for the run. Burt settled beneath it. The man on third crouched, waiting. The catch was made.

The runner dashed home.

Burt’s throw came like a white flame. Straight, swift, sinking at the last instant. The catcher took it before the plate and swept his glove across the runner’s chest.

Out!

Double play!

The grandstand became surf in a storm. Old Well-Well lurched upright, clutching the rail. His face was transfigured. The first syllable burst from him, stronger than before but broken.

“Well!”

Near us, several people turned. They recognized the beginning.

He fought for the second call. His throat convulsed. The sound died.

A woman behind us said, “Why, that’s Old Well-Well!”

Recognition passed along the row. Men ceased shouting to watch him. He lowered himself slowly, ashamed and furious.

“Not yet,” he panted. “Wasn’t the time.”

Philadelphia failed in the ninth, and the game went to extra innings.

Ten!

The number traveled around the park. Shadows lengthened over the outfield. Vendors had ceased their cries. Hundreds stood in the aisles, unwilling either to sit or depart.

New York put one man on in the tenth. No score.

Philadelphia got two on with one out, and our pitcher escaped by forcing a pop-up and a grounder. No score.

Eleven!

Burt opened Philadelphia’s half with a clean single. The crowd demanded a bunt. The manager ordered one. The batter popped it into the pitcher’s hands, and Burt barely returned to first. Another man struck out. On a hit-and-run Burt reached second, but the following batter flied to right.

No score.

Twelve!

By then the game had ceased to seem ordinary. Every pitch carried the weight of an inning; every foul drew a groan; every pause became an inbreathing silence. Partisanship weakened beneath fatigue and admiration. New York men applauded Philadelphia fielding; Philadelphia men saluted a fine stop by our third baseman.

Old Well-Well had grown terribly quiet.

His breathing was scarcely visible. His big hands lay open on his thighs. Once Joe spoke to him and received no answer. I leaned closer, fearing he had died there unnoticed, but his eyes remained fixed upon the distant young man.

“Mr. Weller,” I said.

He turned slowly.

“Twelve,” he murmured.

“Yes.”

“He’s got more in him.”

In the thirteenth New York struck.

The first batter singled past third. The next sacrificed. Then came a base hit into left, too sharp for the runner to score. Men on first and third, one out.

Doyle struck out the next man with three curves.

Two out.

The New York catcher, a stocky veteran named Mills, hit a grounder through the box. The runner from third scored. The man from first tried for third and was thrown out, but the damage had been done.

New York, three; Philadelphia, two.

Our section erupted. Hats sailed. A man climbed upon his seat and screamed, “Game on ice! Game on ice!” until an usher dragged him down.

Old Well-Well never moved.

Joe touched his wrist. “We’re going now.”

“No.”

“Burt may not even come to bat.”

“He will.”

Philadelphia’s eighth man led off the bottom of the thirteenth. Darkness gathered beneath the grandstand roof. Across the field the scoreboard showed that merciless row of figures and, at the end, the one-run deficit.

The batter struck out.

The pitcher’s place came next, and a pinch hitter strode up. He fouled two, took a ball, and then sent a lazy fly to left.

Two out.

All New York rose. One out from victory.

Philadelphia’s lead-off man stood between Burt and the plate; Burt was due second. The batter worked the count full. The crowd quieted until the slap of the catcher’s mitt could be heard from end to end of the park.

Ball four.

A stir ran through the seats.

The next batter chopped toward third. It was a difficult chance. Our third baseman charged, gloved the ball barehanded, and threw while off balance. The runner beat it by half a step.

Two men on. Two out.

And Burt came to bat.

Old Well-Well rose without aid.

I shall never forget that ascent. It was not the rising of a man from a chair; it was the last elevation of a fallen giant. His knees shook. His shoulders sagged. He seized the rail in both hands and dragged himself upright, inch by inch, until he stood towering above those around him.

Burt walked from the circle, knocked dirt from his spikes, and looked toward us.

He saw the old man.

Of that I am certain.

For a moment the bat rested across his shoulder. Then he faced the pitcher.

“Give him one!” cried a boy.

“Bring ’em all home, Burt!”

“Attaboy, kid!”

The pitcher threw low. Ball one.

The next pitch cut the outside corner.

Strike one.

Old Well-Well’s fingers whitened upon the rail.

The third pitch came high and fast. Burt swung under it.

Strike two!

A roar broke from the New York section. One strike! One pitch! The game balanced upon it.

The fourth offering was a curve that missed by inches.

Two and two.

Then came the pause—the long, impossible pause in which twenty thousand people ceased to breathe. The pitcher looked in. Burt crouched. The runners edged from their bases. Old Well-Well stood beside me, pale as carved stone, his lips moving soundlessly.

The pitcher wound.

The ball came.

Crack!

Burt’s bat met it full.

The ball rose in a tremendous arc toward left-center, carrying beyond the infielders, beyond the racing shortstop, beyond the reach of the left fielder. The center fielder sped across. For an instant it appeared he might catch it. He stretched both hands.

The ball struck the turf three yards before him and bounded toward the wall.

The runner from second scored.

Three to three!

The runner from first rounded third. The coach whirled his arms.

“Come on! Come on!”

The outfielders collided, recovered, and chased the ball. Burt flew past first. The winning runner came home standing.

Philadelphia, four to three!

The game was won—but Burt had not stopped.

He rounded second like a shadow swept by wind. The center fielder hurled the ball toward the infield. Burt reached third before the relay man had it. The third-base coach tried to hold him, but the youngster tore through the sign and started for home.

Then the whole spectacle dilated before my eyes.

Burt running.

The relay turning.

The catcher planting one foot beside the plate.

The crowd rising in one vast upward surge.

Old Well-Well drawing breath.

The throw came home, high and wide. The catcher leaped. Burt launched himself headfirst. Glove, runner, dust, plate—all vanished in a brown cloud.

The umpire sprang aside and flung out both arms.

“Safe!”

For one fraction of time there was no sound. It was as if the great crowd had drawn the whole world’s breath into its lungs.

Then Old Well-Well shouted.

“Well!”

The first call boomed over the grandstand—deep, tremendous, reborn.

“Well!”

The second struck the field like thunder. Players halted. Heads turned. Men in the bleachers tore off their caps.

The old man swayed. His face shone with a joy beyond pain, beyond caution, beyond the overmastering truth of death. He gathered the last remnant of his mighty frame, lifted both arms, and hurled his identity into the evening:

“WELL!!!”

That final blast rolled beneath the roof, swept across the diamond, and seemed to ring against the city itself. The crowd knew it. From every quarter came the answering cry:

“Well!—Well!—Well!”

Again! Louder! Thousands beating it out!

“Well!—Well!—Well!”

Burt had risen from the plate. He stood motionless, dust covering his uniform. At the first call he stared toward us. At the second he began to run. At the third he flung aside his cap and raced from the field.

Old Well-Well watched him come.

He smiled.

Then his hands slipped from the rail.

I caught one arm; Joe caught the other; but the enormous body folded between us and bore us to our knees. People drew back. Someone cried for a doctor. The game’s triumphant tumult broke into a hundred confused sounds—questions, commands, sobs, stamping feet.

Frenzied, flinging action—wild chaos—shrieking cries!

And through it all Burt came leaping up the steps.

“Uncle!”

He thrust men aside and dropped beside the old fellow. Old Well-Well lay pale and motionless, his head supported upon my coat. His eyes were open. They found Burt immediately.

The boy seized his hand.

“You saw it?” he cried. “You saw me, Uncle?”

The old man could not speak. His breath fluttered once. But his fingers closed faintly around Burt’s, and the smile remained upon his face.

A doctor forced his way through, listened at the broad chest, and called for room. The crowd hushed. That silence moved outward in rings until even the distant bleachers seemed to understand. Men who had never met Old Well-Well stood bareheaded. New York players lingered before their bench. Philadelphia’s team gathered below our section, looking up.

The ambulance attendants came with a stretcher.

They carried him down the aisle slowly. Burt walked beside him, still holding his hand. The old man appeared already carved into some peaceful monument—huge, white, immovable—except for his eyes.

Those eyes were conscious.

Those eyes were smiling.

At the gate Burt bent close, and perhaps the old man heard once more the crowd murmuring his famous call—not shouted now, but spoken softly, tenderly, passed from row to row like a farewell:

“Well—well—well.”

The ambulance bell rang. The doors closed. It moved away through the parting multitude, bearing the dying giant and the boy who had justified all his faith.

Only then did I look back at the scoreboard.

Philadelphia had won, five to three.

I had entered the grounds a New York man. I left with my hat in my hand, hearing above the fading roar that last great cry which had been victory, pride, benediction, and farewell.

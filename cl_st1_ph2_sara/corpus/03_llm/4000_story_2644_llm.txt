### The Chained Sepulcher

It is perhaps fortunate that the windows of this chamber command no prospect of the western hills; for though the physicians assure me that a glimpse of green fields conduces to tranquillity, I know too well what lies beyond those bland declivities, where the elder elms close ranks above a sunken path and the ground descends, by imperceptible degrees, toward the Hyde sepulcher. The attendants call my avoidance a symptom. My father, whose grief I cannot behold without tenderness, calls it shame. Yet neither physicians nor father has slept beneath that hill, nor heard, through several feet of mould and masonry, the low festal murmur of those who have forgotten daylight.

I shall therefore set down the circumstances of my confinement, not in expectation of release—for I have ceased to crave the liberties of men who mistake blindness for wisdom—but because certain facts, lately established by one whose testimony cannot be lightly impugned, have altered my case from a medical curiosity to something darker. Let the reader judge whether I am mad. I ask only that he judge after hearing all.

My name is Jervas Dudley. I was born to wealth, educated largely in solitude, and early afflicted, as my family expressed it, with an imagination too active for health. Tutors came and went, finding me courteous but inattentive; other children wearied me with their games, their quarrels, and their inability to perceive that a wood at twilight contains more society than a crowded drawing-room. I preferred books whose paper had browned and whose bindings exhaled the faint sweet odor of dust, leather, and slow corruption. In those volumes I found companions who spoke without interruption and belonged to centuries more congenial than my own.

Our estate bordered a tract of neglected woodland once attached to the seat of the Hydes, a family extinguished—so the neighbourhood believed—when their mansion burned in 1768. On my mother’s side there ran some attenuated current of Hyde blood, though the connexion was deemed too remote to boast and too disreputable to investigate. The Hydes had been wealthy, imperious, and given to pleasures which the parish records described with ecclesiastical caution. Their last generations were remembered for gaming, wine, duels, and certain nocturnal assemblies concerning which old people lowered their voices.

The mansion had stood upon a broad rise above the marsh; but in my childhood no wall remained higher than a man’s knee, and briars obscured even these poor remnants. Only the cellar endured, a black depression choked with nettles and fallen stones. Some distance away, in the wooded slope, survived the family tomb.

I first beheld that place when I was ten.

It was an afternoon in late October, grey without rain, when the colourless sky seemed less an expanse than a vast lid pressed over the earth. Wandering beyond the limits prescribed by my nurse, I crossed a brook, passed through a thicket of hazel, and came upon two stone pillars leaning toward one another like conspirators. Beyond them a path descended betwixt yews of extraordinary age. Their roots writhed above the soil, and their branches, interlacing overhead, admitted only a green and submarine light. The air was cold, though the day elsewhere had been mild; and with every step I detected more strongly a fragrance compounded of wet leaves, ancient stone, and that mineral chill which belongs to deep wells and unopened cellars.

At the path’s end lay the tomb.

It had been cut into the hillside and faced with granite blocks, whose joints were padded by moss of a luxuriant and almost phosphorescent green. Above the doorway, upon a weather-eaten lintel, appeared the Hyde arms: a winged hourglass surmounted by a skull. The door itself was of oak plated with iron, and across it hung a chain secured by a monstrous padlock, brown with rust yet formidable. Brambles had drawn a curtain over the lower stones. Pale fungi clustered at the threshold.

I cannot communicate the sensation with which I approached. Fear had little part in it. Rather, I felt the astonishment of a traveller who, after years among barbarous nations, hears his native tongue unexpectedly spoken. The place recognised me. The damp wall, the chained portal, the mouldering escutcheon—all possessed a familiarity more intimate than memory and more solemn than affection.

I placed my hand upon the iron.

From within came a sound.

It was slight: perhaps the falling of grit, perhaps the movement of a small creature; yet in that instant I conceived, with a certainty beyond reason, that someone had stirred on the farther side. I withdrew, but not far. For an hour I sat beneath an elm, watching the door whilst dusk collected among the trunks. When at last my nurse’s cries reached me, I suffered myself to be led home, though I looked backward until the yews concealed the entrance.

After that day the tomb became the axis of my existence.

I visited it in every season. In winter I brushed snow from the heraldic carving; in spring I watched black water ooze between the stones; in summer I lay among fern and foxglove whilst insects hummed above the chained door; and in autumn I listened to acorns falling upon the roof with hollow, sepulchral knocks. Often I spoke aloud, though I shall not repeat the names I used nor the answers which may have arisen within my own mind. There are communications that lose truth when exposed to common language.

At first my family supposed the habit childish eccentricity. Later, discovering that I sometimes remained until midnight, they endeavoured to prevent it. Gates were locked; servants were instructed to follow me; my books were examined for corrupting influences. These measures accomplished nothing. I knew passages through the woods invisible to coarser eyes, and could lie motionless beneath laurel whilst a searcher passed within arm’s reach. The tomb had become my chapel, my school, and, in a sense I did not yet comprehend, my home.

The chain mocked me. I studied its links, tested the padlock, and searched the leaves for some hidden key. Once I attempted to file the iron, but the harsh noise filled me with such sacrilegious horror that I desisted. It was not for me to violate that threshold by force. If admission were intended, the tomb itself would provide the means.

During adolescence my devotion assumed a scholarly form. I ransacked parish histories, legal abstracts, diaries, correspondence, and mouldy packets preserved in our attic. The Hyde genealogy proved tangled, for its members had seldom displayed patience with marriage laws or baptismal regularity. Nevertheless, I traced my descent through Anne Hyde, who in 1711 had married one of my maternal ancestors after a quarrel with her family. Of the final Hydes, the most notable was Jervas, born in 1737, only son of Sir Geoffrey Hyde and heir to the mansion.

Little was recorded of him in respectable documents. In private papers he emerged vividly: handsome, learned, prodigal, scornful of religion but terrified of cremation, which he regarded as the annihilation of the soul’s last earthly refuge. He drank enormously, composed indecent verses in a vigorous Augustan manner, and affected fashions already antiquated in his own youth. Rumour charged him with sorcery, though this accusation appears to have arisen from his astronomical instruments and his fondness for reading at unsuitable hours. He perished in the fire that destroyed the mansion. No identifiable body was recovered.

I was troubled by his Christian name.

Jervas is not common, and my father could give no reason for bestowing it upon me. He maintained that it had occurred to my mother, who died before I could question her. When I discovered that no other Jervas appeared in the intervening branches of our family, the coincidence impressed me deeply, though even then I lacked the courage to shape my suspicion.

At twenty-one I began keeping vigils beside the sepulcher. I would leave the house after the household slept, carrying no lantern, for moonlight filtered sufficiently through the yews and darkness itself had grown friendly to me. Beneath a certain elm, whose roots formed a natural couch, I rested facing the door. Sometimes I read; more often I watched.

It was during these nights that dreams came—not the confused pageants familiar to ordinary sleep, but coherent experiences possessed of sequence, texture, and recollection. I moved through panelled rooms lit by innumerable candles; I heard a spinet beneath laughter; I knew the location of chambers long demolished and could name portraits reduced to ashes a century before my birth. Men in satin coats greeted me with coarse affection, whilst ladies, powdered and patched, rebuked my tardiness. On waking I retained scraps of conversation, scandalous genealogies, the flavour of unfamiliar wines, and melodies absent from every collection I consulted.

My father’s explanation was simple. Having read excessively of the Hydes, I dreamed of them.

This account might satisfy, had my dreams contained only what I had read. But I knew things afterward confirmed in papers previously sealed. I described to an antiquary a mural concealed behind the mansion’s northern wall and depicting Diana surprised at her bath. Excavation uncovered fragments of painted plaster corresponding to my description. I repeated an obsolete nickname for the western meadow, which an aged lease proved authentic. I identified, among a dealer’s miscellaneous miniatures, the likeness of Sir Geoffrey’s sister Euphemia, though her identity had been lost to the dealer and was established only by comparison with a privately owned portrait.

Such incidents caused uneasiness. My father forbade further research, and Dr. Haverill, then our family physician, spoke to me in the soft, insulting fashion by which medical men disguise a verdict already reached. I answered with restraint. It is dangerous to display agitation before those who profit by naming it.

The key came to me in my twenty-fifth year.

One evening, after rain, I slept beneath the elm and dreamed of a hand extended from the tomb. It was white, narrow, and adorned with a black-stoned ring. In its palm lay a long tarnished key. A voice, which I recognised though I cannot say from where, instructed me to seek beneath the third step of an attic staircase in our house.

I awoke before dawn. Mud stained my clothing, and my hair was full of leaves. Returning home by a servants’ entrance, I climbed to the disused eastern attic. The staircase there was steep, its boards warped with age. Beneath the third tread I found a hollow.

The key lay within.

It was wrapped in the remains of a silk handkerchief and coated with green corrosion. I did not ask how an instrument belonging to the Hyde vault came to be hidden in the Dudley house. Certain explanations suggest themselves, none suitable for the serenity of the daylight world.

That night the padlock opened at my touch.

The chain fell with a subdued clang, and the door, after resisting as though the earth itself held it, swung inward upon a breath of air so cold and old that my candle guttered blue. Before me descended six steps, moist and glistening. I crossed the threshold.

The chamber beyond was circular, built of sweating stone, with alcoves radiating from its walls. Coffins occupied most of these recesses: some leaden, some wooden, some collapsed into heaps of boards and whitish dust. Tarnished plates gave names and dates. At the centre stood a stone table upon which rested a cup, a cluster of decayed flowers, and a book swollen by damp. Cobwebs hung from the low roof in grey festoons, stirring though no wind entered.

The odor was formidable but not repellent. It spoke of long enclosure, of spices exhausted by centuries, of rust, mould, and that final neutrality into which silk, bone, pride, and beauty alike decline. I breathed it as another man might breathe the air of his birthplace.

One recess remained unsealed. Within stood a sound coffin of black wood, its lining strangely free of corruption. Upon the silver plate was engraved a single word:

JERVAS.

I cannot relate all that followed. There were tears, though I am not prone to them; there was an invocation learned in no book; and there was, after much hesitation, repose. I lay down in the empty coffin and slept until the approach of dawn.

From that time my visits became frequent. I entered the tomb two or three nights each week, locking the door behind me lest some profane wanderer interrupt my communion. In the coffin I enjoyed a sleep deeper than any bed had granted. Often I woke with memories not my own: the pressure of a sword-belt at my hip, the sting of snuff, the warmth of a woman’s hand, the roar of companions demanding another bottle. My solitude diminished. Indeed, I began to find the society of living men unbearably thin.

Changes in my manner attracted notice. I used words my family considered affected, though they rose naturally to my lips. My handwriting altered. I acquired an appetite for old Madeira and once astonished a visiting scholar by correcting his quotation from a ribald poem printed anonymously in 1759. I had never seen the poem. He demanded my source, but I could offer none without inviting ridicule.

More disquieting was my fear of fire.

Formerly I had regarded flame with ordinary caution; now the flare of a match produced nausea, and a large hearth filled me with scarcely governable dread. I ordered candles snuffed before sleeping and refused to pass the kitchen when its ovens were lit. This weakness humiliated me, for it belonged not to my temperament but to his—to Jervas Hyde, whose body had been denied the ancestral vault by fire.

Yet with fear came gaiety. I, who had scarcely tasted wine, began to drink with robust and ceremonial zeal. Alone in the library, I filled a silver cup and sang verses whose origin I could not discover:

> Fill high, my lads, the bishop’s bowl,  
> And drown the sexton’s warning;  
> The Devil may collect the soul,  
> But flesh shall wait till morning.  
>   
> Then kiss the wench and dice the rent,  
> Let sober saints revile us;  
> For every crown was wisely spent  
> If midnight finds us stylish.  
>   
> Drink deep before the candles die,  
> Laugh loud though graves be near us;  
> We’ll cheat the worms with Burgundy,  
> And toast the fiends that fear us!

My father overheard this performance. The next day he informed me, with visible pain, that I had sung in a dialect associated with the district a hundred years before my birth. He begged me to abandon the tomb. I answered—too harshly, I confess—that a man cannot abandon his own grave.

Thereafter I was watched.

A servant named Briggs followed me on several nights, but I eluded him. Or so I believed. He later gave testimony which my physicians esteem highly, asserting that I never opened the vault, but merely slept outside beneath the elm. Consider, however, the conditions: darkness, dense foliage, distance, and an observer predisposed to accept only what comforted his master. I could pass through the chained door swiftly, replacing chain and lock from within by a method I need not describe. Briggs saw an outline beneath the tree and assumed it mine. Whether that outline was cloak, shadow, or something which occupied my accustomed place whilst I descended, I leave undecided.

The culminating event occurred on the night of August 5th, during a storm remembered throughout the county.

The day had been oppressive. Clouds amassed in coppery tiers above the western horizon, and the woods lay unnaturally still, as if each leaf awaited a command. After dinner I felt a summons stronger than any before it. My blood beat in a cadence that seemed echoed from beneath the hill. Despite my father’s precautions, I escaped through a conservatory window and crossed the grounds.

As I approached the Hyde estate, lightning flickered without thunder, disclosing the trees in livid tableaux. The ruined cellar shone intermittently among weeds. Rain began in drops large and warm as tears.

Then I saw lights.

Where the demolished mansion had stood, windows gleamed in three stately rows. Walls rose darkly against the storm; chimneys, gables, and balustrades possessed the solidity of remembered architecture. Carriages crowded the drive. Horses tossed plumed heads, and liveried servants hurried beneath lanterns, though neither hooves nor wheels disturbed the nettles through which I walked.

The great doors opened for me.

Within, heat and radiance struck my face. The entrance hall blazed with candles reflected in gilt mirrors. Portraits lined the staircase; I knew every countenance. Music surged from the ballroom, mingled with the clink of glasses and laughter at once elegant and cruel. No one questioned my arrival. A footman bowed and called me Mr. Hyde.

I wore, I perceived, a coat of wine-coloured velvet embroidered in silver. Lace covered my hands. A sword hung at my side, and on one finger gleamed the black-stoned ring I had seen in my dream.

The revel exceeded every earthly festivity. Gentlemen wagered fortunes on the fall of cards; masked women danced with clergymen; a judge crawled beneath the supper table in pursuit of a duchess’s slipper. Wine flowed across linen already stained with wax and fruit. At the head of the board sat Sir Geoffrey Hyde, enormous and purple-faced, pounding time to a song with the pommel of his cane.

He hailed me as his son.

I answered him as father.

What followed cannot wholly be entrusted to paper. I drank. I sang. I embraced persons dead before my grandsire’s birth, and exchanged recollections of events no history records. There were jests concerning a chapel, a poacher, and a sealed room beneath the nursery—things I need not name. The company demanded my old song, and I mounted the table to oblige them, kicking aside silver dishes whilst ladies applauded.

Outside, the storm broke.

Thunder shook the house. Candle flames streamed horizontally, though the windows were closed. For one suspended moment every face turned toward the ceiling. I saw beneath powder, rouge, and beauty the grey fixity of the charnel-house.

Lightning descended.

The western wing erupted in fire.

Panic dissolved the revel. Curtains flamed upward; portraits blackened; molten wax rained from chandeliers. The guests did not flee like living people. Some laughed, some continued dancing, and some stood with arms extended as fire passed through their garments and revealed the bones beneath. Sir Geoffrey raised his cup and drank whilst his hair burned.

I alone was terrified.

Not death—but burning! To be consumed, scattered, rendered incapable of burial; to have no corporeal tenement, no last chamber among my kindred! I cried that the tomb awaited me. I begged them to carry my body there. Hands seized me, flaming hands, and voices roared that I had escaped once but would not escape again.

I leapt from the table and ran through smoke. Corridors altered as I traversed them. Doors opened upon brick, stairways descended into flame, and from every wall the Hyde escutcheon grinned in crimson illumination. My coat caught at the sleeve. The smell of scorched velvet became the smell of flesh.

At last I reached the cellar. Stones fell behind me. In a niche near the floor I glimpsed the miniature portrait of a young man, and though fire ringed the frame I snatched it up. The painted face was my own.

The roof collapsed.

When consciousness returned, rain beat upon me. I lay in the actual ruined cellar, half submerged in muddy water, whilst my father, Briggs, and several neighbours restrained me. No mansion stood above us. Only lightning-struck trees writhed against the sky.

They say I raved of burial, protested that my body must not be burned, and called my father Sir Geoffrey. They say I spoke in an accent so archaic that portions were unintelligible. My clothing was not velvet but the ordinary dark suit in which I had left home; yet one sleeve was scorched.

In my hand lay the miniature.

Lightning had split a section of cellar wall and exposed a shallow cupboard. From this recess, they insist, the portrait had fallen. It represented a young gentleman in eighteenth-century dress. Upon the reverse, in faded ink, were the initials “J. H.” The resemblance to me was so exact that my father dropped it upon first seeing the face.

This evidence did not secure belief. On the contrary, it hastened my confinement.

For some weeks I remained under guard at home. During that period my father visited the tomb with Dr. Haverill and two workmen. They reported that the chain remained in place and that the padlock, when broken open, showed corrosion proving it had not moved for many years. No one had entered. I had never entered. My nights had been passed beneath the elm, where Briggs had repeatedly observed me sleeping and muttering.

When I demanded that they search for the coffin marked with my name, my father refused. The broken lock had been replaced; the vault, he said, contained only the coffins expected from the family records. I became violent—not from madness, but from the horror of being denied my appointed resting-place—and was brought to this institution.

The doctors have woven a neat fabric from the threads I gave them. A solitary child develops an obsession; antiquarian reading supplies material for dreams; dreams become convictions; a coincidental name encourages identification; inherited nervous weakness produces somnambulism; lightning, ruins, and a concealed portrait complete the hallucination. My altered speech is imitation. My knowledge is cryptomnesia. My scorched sleeve touched some natural flame, though none burned in the flooded cellar. The key, which vanished after my seizure, never existed.

I admit that their arrangement possesses elegance. Falsehood often does.

Months passed. My former self returned sufficiently to satisfy the attendants, though I have not relinquished what I know. I no longer sing at night. I do not demand wine. I permit a lamp to burn in my chamber, albeit at the farthest corner. These concessions are called improvement.

Then old Briggs came to me.

He had served our family for fifty years and was neither fanciful nor courageous. My father had dismissed him after finding that he smuggled letters into the asylum at my request. Before leaving the district, Briggs resolved to settle the matter of the tomb for himself. One moonless night he carried tools to the sepulcher, broke the new padlock, and entered.

His account differed in several details from mine. He found no cup upon the central table, no flowers, and no book. Several coffin plates bore names I had incorrectly remembered. Yet in the western recess, behind a fall of cobweb and rotted tapestry, stood an empty coffin of black wood.

Its lining was uncorrupted.

Its silver plate bore the name JERVAS.

Briggs had touched the letters. He had measured the coffin. Fearing that his word would not suffice, he removed the plate and brought it to me concealed beneath his coat.

It lies now under the loose board below my bed.

Thus does the matter rest. I shall not display the plate to Dr. Haverill, for he would call it theft, forgery, or some relic planted by my own hand before confinement. Men devoted to disbelief possess explanations as inexhaustible as faith. Nor shall I trouble my father, whose remaining years deserve whatever peace ignorance can bestow.

But you, reader, who have followed me beneath the yews and through the flaming halls, will understand why this white chamber oppresses me more than any crypt. The tomb was never a prison. It was a promise.

At times, when the institution sleeps, I hear revelry beyond the walls: a remote spinet, the stamp of dancing feet, the laughter of those who drink after death has closed the taverns of the world. Perhaps these are but nocturnal noises magnified by longing. Perhaps Jervas Hyde survives only as an ancestral blemish in my blood. Perhaps I am neither possessed nor reborn, but merely one of those unhappy visionaries who mistake the architecture of the mind for the masonry of earth.

Yet the coffin waits.

The attendants may confine my present flesh; the physicians may annotate each word I speak; my father may chain the sepulcher with iron enough to anchor a ship. None of them can alter the inscription.

When this borrowed heart has ceased its labour, I shall go home.

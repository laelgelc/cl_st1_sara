### The Adventure of Mr. Wedderburn

There is a singular promise in the dry and unconsidered body of an orchid. Your tulip declares itself in the bulb, your rose in the cutting; but this brown fragment, warped like a diseased knuckle and wrapped perhaps in a little foreign moss, may produce anything from a flower of no merit whatever to a marvel capable of disturbing the taxonomy of Europe. It may ruin its purchaser, poison his gardener, or—by the fortunate conjunction of novelty and a sufficiently pronounceable name—confer upon him a modest immortality.

Mr. Winter Wedderburn understood this promise better than most men.

He was a shy, lonely, rather ineffectual gentleman of forty-six, possessed of a small private income, a red-brick house near Haslemere, and that constitutional good health which, in the absence of enterprise, may itself become a grievance. Nothing had ever happened to him. He had never been wrecked, robbed, shot at, married, divorced, prosecuted, lost in a fog, or bitten by anything more considerable than a gnat. Even his illnesses had been decorous and without sequel. His existence moved between breakfast, the London train, certain botanical acquaintances, his orchids, tea, and bed.

His cousin and housekeeper, Miss Emily Wedderburn, considered this tranquillity a blessing for which he showed an almost impious want of gratitude.

“You talk as though misfortune were a distinction,” she said one morning.

They were at breakfast. Wedderburn had neglected his toast and was reading a catalogue from Stevens’s auction rooms, making small marks beside the more obscure lots. Miss Wedderburn, who had already finished her coffee and was calculating the sugar required for six pounds of damsons, watched him over the silver pot.

“Not misfortune precisely,” he said. “Experience.”

“Experience is generally another name for something one ought to have avoided.”

“Take Batten, for instance.”

“I would rather not.”

“He has had a remarkable life. He broke his collar-bone in the Pyrenees, had yellow fever at Lagos, fought a duel in Paris, and was divorced before he was thirty-five.”

“And now,” said Miss Wedderburn, “he has indigestion, no money, and a wife in Bayswater who writes to newspapers.”

“Still, things have happened to him.”

“A great many things happen to people who are careless.”

Wedderburn folded the catalogue and stared beyond the coffee urn with the mild melancholy of a man excluded from catastrophe.

“I sometimes feel,” he said, “that before I die something ought to happen to me.”

Miss Wedderburn rang for the breakfast things.

“Something will, if you go into London without your umbrella. The glass is falling.”

He went nevertheless, carrying the umbrella, and returned by the five-forty with a wooden case under his arm and an expression of subdued triumph on his face.

The orchid sale had included the remnants of a collection made by one Arnold Renshaw, a man of uncertain education and considerable endurance, who had penetrated a swampy district beyond the upper Orinoco. Renshaw had not returned. His Indian assistant—who had returned only so far as Trinidad—reported that the collector had been discovered one morning among the roots of a great tree, dead and strangely bloodless. Leeches, fever, a vampire bat, or the combined effects of the climate and native rum had each been proposed. The assistant himself died before a more satisfactory account could be obtained.

From Renshaw’s cases came orchids rare, damaged, incorrectly labelled, or quite unknown. They had therefore been purchased with unusual eagerness.

“This,” said Wedderburn, opening his box upon the dining-room table, “was attached to the packing in which they found the larger specimens. Nobody could identify it.”

Miss Wedderburn leaned forward reluctantly.

On a bed of grey fibre lay a brown shrivelled mass, from which projected several flattened cords of dead-looking root. It was ugly even beyond the customary ugliness of dormant orchids. One portion suggested a spider shamming death; another, the hand of an infant preserved too long in spirits.

“You paid money for that?”

“Only seven shillings.”

“It looks rotten.”

“It may be.”

“And what is it?”

“That is the point. It may be an undeveloped Epidendrum, though the rhizome is unusual; or perhaps some aberrant form allied to Catasetum. One of the rootlets appears almost prehensile.”

“It looks like fingers,” said Miss Wedderburn.

Wedderburn touched one of them tenderly.

“There is no reason why aerial roots should not exhibit considerable modification. Darwin showed how extraordinarily specialised orchids may become in relation to fertilisation. Given a peculiar environment—”

“I dislike it.”

“You cannot dislike a plant you have known for less than a minute.”

“I can dislike most things at once, if they deserve it.”

He carried the specimen to the hothouse, where he fixed it in a slatted basket among his rarer plants. The hothouse was his sanctuary: a long, low building attached to the south wall, with misted panes, hot-water pipes, and a green, humid atmosphere that caused Miss Wedderburn’s spectacles to cloud whenever she entered. Under Wedderburn’s care, orchids from India, Brazil, Madagascar, and the Malay Archipelago hung in ordered ranks. Their labels, their seasons, their preferences in temperature and moisture were known to him with an intimacy he had never achieved with mankind.

The unknown orchid received a place of honour.

For several weeks it did nothing. Wedderburn inspected it each morning before breakfast and again after tea. He adjusted its shade, altered its drainage, and disputed with Mr. Hodges, the odd-job man, over the proper degree of ventilation. Miss Wedderburn, when she entered to call him to meals, continued to declare that it resembled a dead spider.

Then, one wet morning in November, the plant stirred.

A small green point had appeared upon the rhizome. Wedderburn summoned his cousin from the jam cupboard to observe it.

“You see?” he said. “Life.”

“I see a pimple.”

“It is a growth bud. And here—look at this rootlet. It has extended nearly an inch.”

Miss Wedderburn regarded the pallid cord. Its extremity had divided into five delicate processes, which curved against the basket slat.

“Fingers,” she said.

“Rootlets.”

“It is holding on.”

“That is what roots are intended to do.”

“Not like that.”

Wedderburn smiled with unusual confidence. “My dear Emily, one must not apply moral prejudice to vegetable structures.”

Nevertheless, he remained in the hothouse for nearly an hour after she had gone, contemplating the orchid with a pleasure in which scientific curiosity and personal vanity were agreeably combined. If the plant proved new, he might describe it before the Linnean Society. Wedderburnia renshawi had, he thought, an excellent sound; although Wedderburnia mirabilis was perhaps better. He imagined a coloured plate in a botanical journal, and beneath it his name in small but permanent type.

The orchid grew with astonishing rapidity. Its leaves emerged broad and dark, with a peculiar oily lustre. More aerial rootlets descended from the basket, pale at first and afterwards faintly purple, turning and attaching themselves wherever they met wood, iron, or brick. Their movements were, of course, too slow to be perceived; yet from one visit to another their positions altered in a manner suggestive of stealth. One coiled about a hot-water pipe. Another hung free in the air, bent at the end like an inquiring finger.

Wedderburn took measurements.

“It gains nearly three inches in twenty-four hours,” he said at dinner.

“Then it will be in the drawing room by Christmas.”

“The rate cannot continue indefinitely.”

“I hope not. I found one of its tentacles touching the door.”

“Roots, Emily.”

“Tentacles.”

He helped himself to potatoes with a faint air of injury.

“At any rate, something is happening.”

“So is the damp in the spare bedroom, but you have not measured that.”

Early in December the plant developed an inflorescence. The stem rose thickly from between the leaves and bore a cluster of swollen buds, each sheathed in a translucent skin. Wedderburn’s excitement became difficult to conceal. He woke before daylight, carried a lantern to the hothouse, and returned with wet slippers and reports upon the buds. He wrote to two botanical acquaintances, though without giving sufficient detail to permit either of them to forestall him. He consulted works upon tropical pollination, nectaries, and insect-trapping plants; and he spoke several times of the deceptive labellum of Ophrys and the extraordinary devices by which Nature secured the transport of pollen.

“Nature,” said Miss Wedderburn, “would do better to leave insects alone.”

“My dear Emily, Nature is not a district nurse.”

The buds enlarged until each was almost the size of a hen’s egg. Their colour changed from green to ivory, and through the enclosing membranes there showed veins of orange and violet. At the same time a faint odour appeared in the hothouse. It was agreeable at first, resembling vanilla mingled with new-cut hay; but it lingered in the throat, and after ten minutes Miss Wedderburn complained of giddiness.

Wedderburn professed to feel nothing.

“You are pale,” she told him.

“The temperature is rather high.”

“And the smell is stronger.”

“That is natural before anthesis.”

“I do not care what it is called. You must open the upper ventilator.”

“The buds require humidity.”

“You require air.”

He compromised by opening the door for four minutes, timing the interval with his watch.

On the following afternoon the first flower opened.

Wedderburn discovered it shortly after three o’clock and called aloud, but Miss Wedderburn had gone into Haslemere on household business and Hodges was repairing a fence beyond the orchard. Thus the triumph was his alone.

The flower was magnificent. Three broad sepals of milky whiteness spread backward from a labellum of golden orange, intricately folded and streaked with bluish purple. Within its throat stood a curious fleshy column, glossy and crimson at the tip. The whole bloom measured nearly nine inches across. A second flower was opening beside it, and behind these the remaining buds strained against their coverings.

Wedderburn stood with his hands clasped behind him, breathing quickly.

The air was heavy with perfume. It seemed not merely to fill the hothouse but to occupy it, to lie in visible layers among the leaves and baskets. Its sweetness was rich, warm, and almost material. He thought of honey heated in wine, of tropical nights, of fruits splitting in the sun. Beneath the sweetness there was another odour, faintly salt and metallic, which he could not immediately place.

“Extraordinary,” he whispered. “Quite extraordinary.”

One of the free rootlets hung near his shoulder. Its divided extremity stirred, or appeared to stir, in the warm current above the pipes.

Wedderburn moved closer. He wished to examine the column, for its structure seemed unlike that of any genus known to him. The purple markings upon the labellum converged in narrow lines, leading inward. He bent until his face was almost level with the bloom.

The perfume intensified.

A mist came over the glass. He took off his spectacles and wiped them, but the mist remained. The white sepals expanded enormously; the orange throat became a fiery tunnel, receding and swaying. He was aware that his heart beat with unusual slowness.

“I must open—” he said.

His hand did not reach the ventilator. The floor tilted softly beneath him, and the rows of orchids rose in a green wall. He made one uncertain step, caught at the bench, and fell among the pots.

The flower remained open above him, splendid and exultant in the steaming air.

* * *

Miss Wedderburn returned at half-past four with a parcel of candles, two ounces of nutmeg, and the information that the price of butter was outrageous. She called from the hall. Receiving no answer, she removed her bonnet, put away the nutmeg, and went to the study.

Wedderburn was not there. His teacup stood empty upon the tray.

She called again.

The house was quiet except for the kitchen clock and the faint tapping of rain upon the windows. Then she became aware of the perfume. It had escaped from the hothouse and drifted along the passage, sweet and oppressive.

Miss Wedderburn set down the candles.

“I knew it,” she said, though she did not yet know anything.

At the hothouse door the odour met her like warm breath. Through the misted glass she saw the great white flowers, luminous in the dusk. She also saw one of Wedderburn’s shoes projecting from beneath the bench.

She opened the door and entered.

For several seconds she did not understand what lay before her. Wedderburn was on his back, his face very white, his mouth open. The orchid’s aerial roots had descended about him in a pale, intricate net. One crossed his forehead; two were fastened at his throat. Others encircled his wrists and clung to the backs of his hands. Their divided tips had flattened against the skin into little crimson discs.

Then she saw that the roots were swollen. A red colour travelled through their translucent tissues in slow pulses towards the basket.

She caught Wedderburn by the coat and pulled. The roots tightened.

The perfume surged about her. The floor became uncertain, and a delicious weariness passed through her limbs. For an instant she wished only to sit down among the warm leaves and close her eyes.

Instead she bit her lower lip until it hurt.

“Air,” she said.

She staggered to the door, flung it wide, and tried the upper ventilator. It had jammed. She seized a terracotta pot and hurled it at the roof. The pot rebounded, showering bark and fibre. She snatched up an iron staging hook and struck again.

A pane cracked.

She struck a third time. The glass burst outward, and cold rain drove through the gap. She smashed the adjoining pane, then another. The winter air rushed down into the hothouse. Leaves thrashed; baskets swung upon their wires; the great flowers trembled.

The narcotic sweetness weakened.

Miss Wedderburn returned to her cousin. She tore at the root across his forehead. It clung with the tenacity of a leech. Bracing one foot against the bench, she pulled until it snapped. The broken end writhed against her sleeve and dripped a thick scarlet fluid.

She recoiled, then caught another.

One by one she ripped them loose. Some yielded with a soft sucking sound; others stretched like cords. Wherever they left Wedderburn’s skin there appeared a round, bleeding wound. The longest root was fixed beneath his collar. She broke two fingernails dragging it free.

When the last sucker released him, the rootlets lifted and groped blindly through the disturbed air.

Miss Wedderburn seized Wedderburn beneath the arms and tugged. He was not a large man, but unconsciousness had given him the discouraging weight of wet laundry. She dragged him to the doorway, his heels striking pots from the lower shelf.

The roots followed.

One whipped about his ankle. Another caught Miss Wedderburn’s skirt and tightened so sharply that the cloth tore. She let go of her cousin, grasped the orchid’s basket with both hands, and wrenched it from its hook. The wire snapped. Plant, moss, roots, and flowers descended together upon the flags.

She caught the slatted basket and dragged it after Wedderburn into the garden.

At that moment Hodges came round the corner carrying a spade. He saw Miss Wedderburn, hatless and bleeding at the lip, hauling an insensible gentleman across the path while a monstrous flowering plant trailed behind them in a confusion of red-stained ropes.

His mouth opened.

“Don’t stand there,” she said. “Fetch Dr. Reeves.”

Hodges dropped the spade and ran.

The rain fell heavily now. The white flowers collapsed beneath it, their orange throats filling with water. The rootlets writhed feebly upon the gravel. Miss Wedderburn dragged her cousin beyond their reach, tore away the remnants that still adhered to his clothes, and sat beside him in the mud until he gave a shallow sigh.

Dr. Reeves arrived within twenty minutes and was scandalised by nearly everything: the blood loss, the broken glass, the delay in sending for brandy, and the botanical fragments adhering to his patient’s collar. He dressed the circular wounds upon Wedderburn’s face, neck, and hands and ordered him to bed. There followed stimulants, beef tea, hot bottles, and a firmness from Miss Wedderburn that made medical instruction superfluous.

For two days Wedderburn lay pale and quiet. On the third he asked after the orchid.

“It is dead,” said his cousin.

“Entirely?”

“I should hope so.”

“Where is it?”

“On the ash heap. Hodges used paraffin.”

Wedderburn considered this with genuine regret.

Exposed to the cold, the plant had decayed with extraordinary rapidity. Its leaves became black and pulpy; its aerial roots shrank into grey threads; and the magnificent flowers dissolved into a malodorous slime. Dr. Reeves preserved a section of root in spirits, though he afterwards complained that it discoloured the solution and could not be induced to exhibit any recognisable vascular arrangement. The specimen was eventually misplaced by his assistant.

Within a fortnight Wedderburn was downstairs again, wrapped in a dressing gown and taking an interest in toast. The wounds resembled leech bites and promised to leave only slight scars. Miss Wedderburn had the hothouse repaired, though she insisted upon larger ventilators and forbade the purchase of any plant whose history included an unexplained death.

Wedderburn accepted these conditions with surprising meekness.

Yet he was not chastened. On the contrary, illness had made him bright and garrulous. He described the opening flower to Dr. Reeves, to Hodges, to the vicar, and to two botanical friends who came from London expressly to hear the account. He speculated upon carnivorous adaptation, tropical scarcity, the attraction and anaesthetising of animal prey, and the possible relation of the orchid to Renshaw’s bloodless body in the swamp.

“I had always believed,” he said one evening, as Miss Wedderburn poured his tea, “that something remarkable might happen to me.”

“You nearly died.”

“Yes,” said Wedderburn, touching with satisfaction one of the little scars upon his wrist. “That is what makes it remarkable.”

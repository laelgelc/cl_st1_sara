# THE SECOND BULLET

## I

In the year 18— my regiment was quartered in the little town of R—, a place which possessed neither commerce, society, nor even a tolerable inn. Those who have never spent a winter in a provincial garrison may imagine that military life supplies its own amusements. This is true in the capital, where reviews, theaters, suppers, and intrigues divide an officer’s attention; it is less true where the arrival of a traveling apothecary furnishes conversation for three days.

Our mornings belonged to drill. We dined with one another or at the tavern, whose German proprietor served every dish under a different name and with the same brown sauce. In the evenings we played faro, drank punch, and quarreled over matters which, by daylight, would not have appeared worthy of disagreement. We had no married men among us. The district contained several landowners, but they were either too old, too economical, or too suspicious of uniforms to receive us frequently. Thus our society consisted almost entirely of ourselves.

One man alone, though not of the regiment, exercised over us an influence which none of us cared to acknowledge. He was called Silvio. Whether this was his family name I never learned. He appeared to be about thirty-five, which to me, then scarcely twenty-two, seemed an age already touched by experience. He had formerly served in the hussars; why he had resigned no one knew. He lived in a poor house at the edge of the town, with one old soldier for a servant, and went always on foot in a black overcoat whitened at the seams. Yet when he entertained us, champagne flowed as freely as if it had come from a colonel’s cellar, and his table, though plain, was never mean.

His books were chiefly military memoirs, novels, and works on campaigns. He lent them willingly and never asked for their return; on the other hand, he was equally careless about returning those he borrowed. His chief occupation was pistol shooting.

The walls of his dining room were as full of holes as a honeycomb. A narrow shelf above the stove held a collection of pistols in perfect order, and on a nail near the window hung an ace of diamonds through which he had sent five bullets, one upon another. At thirty paces he could cut the pip from a playing card; at twenty, he extinguished a candle without touching the brass socket. Once, when a fly settled on the wall during dinner, one of our youngest officers laughed and challenged him to hit it. Silvio took up a pistol, asked the officer whether the fly was dear to him, and fired. The insect disappeared; the plaster showed a fresh white scar.

We regarded his skill as something supernatural. Had he offered to shoot a pear from my cap, I believe I should have placed it there without hesitation. His conversation was usually abrupt, but when it concerned duels he became attentive. He had known celebrated seconds, notorious bullies, and men whose courage failed in the interval between an insult and sunrise. He spoke of them without enthusiasm and without contempt. If asked whether he himself had fought, he answered that he had; but he gave no particulars. We concluded that some unhappy victim had fallen by his hand, and our imagination supplied what his silence withheld.

His face was pale and marked by smallpox. His dark eyes had an unpleasant steadiness when anything displeased him. There were moments when his countenance seemed almost gentle; they passed quickly. He laughed rarely, and then not always at what amused the rest of us.

In spite of his reserve, I became more attached to him than did my comrades. Youth loves mystery because it has not yet learned how often mystery conceals only vanity, misfortune, or an unpaid debt. I supposed Silvio to be the hero of some obscure history. His apparent indifference to money, comfort, and rank completed his power over me. He treated me with a kindness which, though never expressed warmly, distinguished me from the others; and I valued it as a mark of confidence from a superior nature.

An incident occurred which for a time altered the opinion of the regiment.

A new lieutenant, B—, had recently joined us. He was a tall, good-looking young man, rich, loud, and sincerely convinced that a fine horse and a ready laugh excused every indiscretion. Silvio disliked him at once, perhaps because B— had the habit of treating every silence as an invitation to a joke.

One evening about ten of us were supping at Silvio’s house. The champagne had been more abundant than usual. After supper cards were brought, and our host, contrary to his custom, consented to keep the bank.

Silvio played with perfect exactness. He never disputed, never explained, and never forgave a mistake in the account. When a punter calculated wrongly, Silvio corrected the figure in chalk and continued the deal. It was his habit, and we were accustomed to it.

Lieutenant B—, who did not know this peculiarity, imagined that Silvio had made an error to his disadvantage. He objected. Silvio silently changed the total. B— objected again, speaking with greater warmth. Silvio took the chalk and restored the first figure.

“You have counted my stake twice,” said the lieutenant.

“No,” replied Silvio, dealing the cards.

“You mean that I cannot add?”

“I mean only that the account is correct.”

B— laughed and appealed to us. No one answered. His face reddened. Taking the brush used to clear the chalk from the table, he wiped away the figure. Silvio wrote it down once more.

This calmness, which should have ended the matter, enraged the lieutenant. A heavy brass candlestick stood at his elbow. He seized it and threw it at Silvio.

Our host bent his head. The candlestick struck the wall and fell among the bottles. Two of them broke; the cards were covered with wine.

We rose at once.

Silvio remained seated. His face had become so white that the scars upon it seemed dark.

“My dear sir,” he said, “have the goodness to leave my house, and thank Providence that this occurred here.”

His voice was low. No man who heard it doubted what would follow.

B— had sobered immediately. He muttered that he was prepared to give satisfaction whenever it might be demanded, bowed to us, and went out.

The game could not continue. We took our caps and withdrew, speaking in subdued voices of tomorrow’s inevitable event. The question was not whether the lieutenant would be killed, but whether Silvio would choose twelve paces or permit him the doubtful favor of fifteen.

The next morning B— remained in his quarters. He wrote several letters and arranged his affairs. By noon we learned that Silvio had received his apology.

We did not believe the report. That evening it was confirmed.

Silvio did not fight.

At first we sought some explanation favorable to him. Perhaps the matter had not been represented correctly; perhaps the lieutenant had made a submission so complete that a duel became impossible. But the truth was simple. B— had apologized. Silvio had accepted the apology.

The officers were embarrassed in his company. Courage is a quality which military men readily forgive a man for displaying and never forgive him for lacking. A reputation built over years may be injured by a gesture of one second. We no longer spoke before Silvio with the same ease. The younger men avoided meeting his eyes. B—, having passed several days in expectation of death, became more cheerful than before and told the story against himself. Little by little the affair ceased to be discussed, but it was not forgotten.

Silvio perceived the change. He showed no resentment and continued to receive us, though fewer came. I alone could not behave naturally toward him. My imagination had placed him so high that his fall disturbed me almost as if I had been deceived by a friend. I was ashamed of my disappointment, yet unable to conquer it. He noticed my constraint and on two occasions attempted to speak with me; I answered respectfully but coldly. After that he left me to myself.

Several months passed.

One afternoon a messenger brought Silvio a sealed packet. He tore it open in our presence, read the first lines, and dismissed us with unusual haste. The following morning we received invitations to dine with him for the last time. He was leaving R— that night, perhaps forever.

We found his room in disorder. Pistols lay open upon the table; books and clothes had been thrown into a trunk; papers burned in the fireplace. The walls, stripped of several targets, looked more miserable than before. Silvio was cheerful, or made an effort to appear so. He gave us an excellent dinner. Corks struck the ceiling, and our glasses were never empty. Whatever reserve had lately existed between him and the regiment disappeared at the approach of separation. We recalled old jokes, drank to campaigns we had not fought, and promised to meet again in places none of us expected to visit.

At last Silvio looked at the clock. His telega stood ready. We embraced him one after another. When I held out my hand, he detained it.

“I should like to speak with you,” he said. “Remain a moment.”

The others departed. I stayed behind.

Silvio closed the door and returned to the table. For some time he filled and emptied his glass without speaking.

“You thought poorly of me,” he began. “Do not deny it. When that young madman threw a candlestick at my head, you expected me to kill him.”

I attempted to reply.

“Let us be honest. You wondered how a man who had made a profession of pistols could swallow an insult in the presence of his friends.”

I lowered my eyes.

“You were right,” he continued. “Such an insult cannot be forgiven. I did not forgive it. I merely postponed the account. But I could not expose myself to that boy’s bullet. I have no right to do so. My life does not yet belong to me.”

He rose and walked from the stove to the window. His movements had the restless regularity of a tiger in its cage.

“I owe a shot,” he said, “and I have owed it for six years.”

He took from a writing case a red cap with a gold tassel. It had once been handsome, but time had faded the cloth. About an inch above the embroidered band was a round bullet hole.

“Do you see this?”

I nodded.

“You know that I served in the hussars. In my regiment I was first in everything that makes young fools admire one another. I drank the most, played the highest, quarreled the quickest. I commanded fashion, if fashion may be said to exist among men whose boots smell of the stable. The colonel condemned me; the officers imitated me. I had fought several duels, and one had made some noise. Whether I possessed courage I did not ask. No one else asked it.

“Then a young count joined us. I will call him Count V—. Fortune had exhausted herself in his favor. He was rich, well born, intelligent, handsome, and cheerful. He spoke French better than our adjutant, rode any horse, sang tolerably, danced admirably, and lost money with such grace that those who won from him felt obliged to like him. Women received him as if they had been waiting for his arrival. Men forgave him because he appeared not to notice his victories.

“In a week he had displaced me.

“I hated him.

“At first I attempted to overwhelm him by extravagance. He surpassed me without effort and without intention. I told stories; his were better. I made jokes; he laughed first and thus robbed them of malice. I insulted people; he reconciled them. What in me was labor seemed in him nature. Worst of all, he treated me with sincere friendliness.

“I could have forgiven contempt. Kindness was intolerable.

“At a ball given by a Polish landowner, I saw that the hostess, with whom I was then foolishly enamored, paid greater attention to him than to me. I whispered something offensive in his ear. He turned and struck my face.

“We reached for our swords, but the ladies fainted, or pretended to faint, and we were separated. That same night we arranged to meet at dawn.

“The morning was clear. I arrived with three seconds and found him already there, accompanied by one officer. He was in full uniform. He held his cap in his hand; it was filled with cherries.

“We measured twelve paces. I insisted that we draw lots for the first shot. My anger was such that I feared my hand might not be steady if I fired after him. Fortune gave him the first number.

“He aimed and fired. The bullet passed through my cap.”

Silvio placed the red cap on his head. Through the hole I could see a lock of dark hair streaked with gray.

“My turn had come. His life was in my hand. I looked at him, seeking at least some trace of agitation. He stood before me selecting cherries from his cap. He ate them one by one and spat the stones in my direction. One stone rolled almost to my boot.

“His composure enraged me. What satisfaction, I thought, can there be in taking the life of a man who values it no more than the stem of a cherry? I lowered my pistol.

“‘You do not seem prepared for death,’ I said. ‘You are eating breakfast.’

“‘You do not inconvenience me,’ he answered. ‘Have the goodness to fire. But take your time. The shot remains yours.’

“I asked our seconds to remember his words. Then I declared that I would not fire that morning.

“He shrugged his shoulders.

“‘As you please,’ he said. ‘I shall be at your service.’

“We parted. I resigned my commission and withdrew here. Since then not a day has passed on which I have not imagined our second meeting. Now the hour has come.”

He handed me the letter he had received. It had been written by an agent in Moscow. Among several items of business was a brief announcement that Count V— was shortly to marry a young lady of great beauty and fortune.

“You understand,” said Silvio. “Six years ago he cared for nothing. Today he is about to possess rank, wealth, love, and domestic happiness. We shall see whether he receives my bullet with the same appetite.”

I felt a chill, though the room was warm.

“Do you mean to kill him on his wedding day?”

“His wedding has already taken place. I shall not be so theatrical.”

“But if he refuses?”

“He cannot refuse.”

“And if he again meets you calmly?”

Silvio smiled.

“Then perhaps I shall fire.”

The servant entered to announce that the horses were ready. Silvio fastened his shabby overcoat, placed two pistols in a traveling case, and embraced me.

We went into the yard. The night was dark. A single lantern hung from the telega, illuminating one wheel and the steaming flanks of the horses. His trunk had been tied behind with rope.

“Farewell,” he said.

“Shall I hear from you?”

“If there is anything worth hearing, you will hear it.”

He took his seat. The driver cracked his whip, the bells sounded, and the vehicle disappeared beyond the last houses of R—.

I remained at the gate until the noise died away. At twenty-two one believes that every story must end with a death. I was certain that before many days Count V— would be dead, or Silvio would be. Yet weeks passed, then months, and no news reached us.

Shortly afterward my regiment was transferred. Other events occupied me. The lieutenant with the candlestick distinguished himself in the field and received a decoration. Several of our companions married; two were killed. Silvio’s name was sometimes mentioned over a bottle, but no one knew what had become of him.

His unfinished shot remained in my memory like a tale interrupted at its most important line.

## II

Five years later family circumstances obliged me to leave the army and settle upon my estate in the district of N—. I exchanged the noise of the regiment for an old house, neglected fields, and the company of a steward who stole from me with an air of wounded honesty.

At first rural life amused me. I rode over my land, examined barns, discussed seed with peasants, and made plans which required three times my income. By autumn I had discovered that agriculture is less poetical when one is responsible for it. In winter my occupations diminished further. I read the same journals until I knew the advertisements by heart. I played billiards alone, taking both colors, and found myself cheating one side in favor of the other. The nearest respectable neighbor lived more than twenty versts away.

Our district, however, contained one estate which was spoken of with particular admiration. It belonged to Count V— and his wife. They spent most of their time in Petersburg, but one summer I learned that they had arrived at their country house.

The name awakened an old memory. I did not know whether this was Silvio’s count; there were many men of that initial, and I had never heard the full name. Nevertheless, curiosity joined the natural desire for society, and I resolved to call.

The count’s estate offered a striking contrast to my own. The road passed between clipped lindens and ended before a broad stone house. Servants appeared before I had descended from my carriage. In the entrance hall stood marble busts, suits of armor, and flowering orange trees in tubs. Nothing was ostentatiously new; everything seemed to have belonged there long enough to acquire the dignity of custom.

The count received me in his library. He was a man of about thirty-five, with agreeable features and manners at once easy and attentive. There was no effort in his courtesy. He asked about my service, named several men I had known, and recalled an action in which my former regiment had taken part. Within ten minutes I felt as though I had been a frequent guest.

The countess entered soon afterward. She was younger than her husband and still remarkably beautiful. Her expression had that quiet openness which artists often give to figures intended to represent innocence, though in life it is less common. She spoke kindly of the neighboring families and reproached us for having neglected her since her arrival.

We went through several rooms while the count showed me paintings collected by his father. I understood little of art but considered it a social duty to pause before the largest canvases. There were Italian saints, Flemish taverns, severe family portraits, and landscapes in which blue mountains receded behind impossible ruins.

In a small drawing room I noticed a Swiss view: a dark lake, a bridge, and snowy peaks under a pale evening sky. The picture itself was not remarkable. What arrested me were two bullet holes near the center, one directly above the other, separated by no more than an inch.

“That is fine shooting,” I said.

The count looked at me quickly.

“Do you shoot?”

“A little. In my regiment a man was thought respectable if he could strike a card at twenty paces.”

“And could you?”

“Sometimes. But I once knew a marksman who would have considered such a shot childish.”

The countess, who had been arranging flowers, turned toward us.

“What was his name?” asked the count.

“Silvio.”

The count’s face altered. The countess let a rose fall from her hand.

“You knew Silvio?” he said.

“Very well. We were quartered together, or nearly so. He told me a strange story before leaving R—. He was going to seek a man who owed him a shot. I never learned the end.”

The count glanced at his wife. She had become pale.

“You may learn it now,” he said. “The man was I. Those two bullets are the conclusion.”

The countess laid her hand on his arm.

“Must you tell it?” she asked softly.

“My dear, our guest already knows half, and perhaps the darker half. Besides, so many years have passed.”

She withdrew her hand and sat by the window. The count took me back to the Swiss landscape.

“You have heard how our quarrel began?”

I repeated as briefly as possible what Silvio had told me: the rivalry in the regiment, the ball, the slap, the duel, the pierced cap, and the cherries.

The count listened with an expression in which embarrassment and displeasure were mingled.

“He omitted nothing,” he said. “At twenty-one I believed indifference the highest form of courage. It did not occur to me that a man might eat cherries because he was vain and because three other men were watching him. Silvio understood vanity; he suffered from it himself.

“Well, you know that he reserved his fire. Six years passed. I heard nothing from him, but I did not forget. The thought that somewhere a man carried a loaded pistol which, in a moral sense, was directed at me, followed me even when I laughed at it. Before a battle I sometimes wondered whether Silvio would be cheated of his privilege. At a gaming table, when fortune went against me, I would think: at least he cannot complain that I have nothing to lose. Such ideas came rarely, and I dismissed them.

“Then I married.

“We came here immediately after the wedding. I had known happiness before, or what I called happiness; but it had been the happiness of movement, success, and appetite. This was different. I began to make plans for years ahead. A man does not fear death properly until he has arranged the furniture of his future.

“One evening I went riding alone. My wife had remained at home because of a headache. On returning, I saw a traveling carriage before the entrance. A servant told me that a gentleman waited in my study and had refused to give his name.

“I entered.

“Silvio stood near the fireplace.”

The count paused. His wife looked out of the window, though the garden offered nothing at that moment but a motionless fountain and two peacocks upon the gravel.

“He had changed,” the count continued. “His face was thinner, and there were gray hairs at his temples. He wore the same sort of black overcoat you described. On the table lay his pistol case.

“‘You did not expect me, Count,’ he said.

“I confess that I did not answer immediately.

“‘The shot remains mine,’ he continued. ‘I have come to discharge it. Are you ready?’

“There was no witness. We were alone in my house. I closed the door.

“‘I am ready,’ I said.

“He took out a pistol and examined the lock. His hands were perfectly steady.

“I placed myself before him. At that moment I thought of my wife, who might enter at any instant; of the absurdity of being killed beside my own writing table; of a hundred insignificant things—a letter unanswered, a horse ordered for the next morning, the fact that my boots were dusty. Fear does not always announce itself nobly.

“Silvio watched me.

“‘You are not eating cherries today,’ he said.

“I felt the insult, and it restored me somewhat.

“‘Fire,’ I answered.

“He raised the pistol, then lowered it.

“‘No,’ he said. ‘It is too easy. You are unarmed, and I dislike execution. Besides, our former arrangement gave chance a voice. Let us return to it. We will draw lots once more. The first shot shall belong to whoever receives it.’

“‘You have no obligation to offer that.’

“‘I am aware of my obligations.’

“He tore two scraps from a letter and rolled them between his fingers. One he marked. I drew.

“The first shot was mine.”

Here the countess covered her eyes.

“I told Silvio that fortune appeared determined to protect him. He smiled and stood before the picture you see there. It then hung in my study. I took the pistol.

“I had fired coolly at men in battle, but battle is generous: smoke, noise, motion, and necessity share the responsibility. In that quiet room there was only Silvio’s face and my own decision. I wished to miss, yet I feared that an intentional miss would be called cowardice. I aimed too quickly and pulled the trigger.

“The bullet struck the picture.

“Silvio looked over his shoulder.

“‘A little high,’ he said. ‘Now it is my turn.’

“He cocked his pistol.

“I cannot describe the minutes that followed. He did not aim at once. He walked about the room, examining the furniture, the books, the windows, as if deciding where my blood would cause the least inconvenience. Then he stopped before me.

“‘This is curious,’ he said. ‘The first time, you waited for my bullet while eating cherries. Today you have lost your appetite.’

“‘Have the goodness to finish,’ I replied.

“‘You are in haste. Formerly you advised me to take my time.’

“He lifted the pistol.

“At that instant the door opened.”

The countess rose abruptly.

“I had learned that the stranger was Silvio,” she said, addressing me for the first time since the story began. “My husband had once told me of the duel. I ran through the rooms. The servants tried to stop me. When I entered, I saw him facing the pistol.”

Her voice trembled, but she continued.

“I threw myself before my husband.

“‘Is this your wife?’ Silvio asked.

“My husband tried to move me aside. I would not let him.

“‘Yes,’ I said. ‘I am his wife. If he has offended you, forgive him. Do not kill him before me.’

“Silvio looked at us without lowering the weapon.

“‘Countess,’ he said, ‘your husband has already fired at me twice. He once put a hole through my cap and has now spoiled this landscape. I have been unusually patient.’

“I understood nothing except that my husband was alive and might die. I knelt.

“‘Rise, Masha,’ my husband said. ‘This gentleman wishes to see whether I can meet death calmly. Do not deprive him of his amusement.’

“I had never heard him speak in such a voice. I clung to him and begged Silvio again.”

The count took up the account.

“I was ashamed—ashamed of her entreaties, ashamed of my fear, ashamed that Silvio should witness both. I ordered her to leave. She refused. I turned upon Silvio.

“‘Will you fire, sir, or must I ask whether you have come here merely to frighten women?’

“He smiled in a manner I shall never forget.

“‘That is enough,’ he said. ‘I have seen what I wished to see. You will remember me. I leave you to your happiness.’

“He uncocked the pistol.

“For a moment none of us moved. I could not believe that the affair was ended. My wife still held my arm.

“Silvio placed the pistol in its case. At the door he stopped and glanced at the picture.

“‘You fired poorly today, Count,’ he said. ‘Permit me to correct your shot.’

“Before I could answer, he turned, fired almost without aiming, and went out.

“The report brought the servants running. We heard his carriage depart while the room was still full of smoke.

“His bullet made the upper hole.”

We approached the painting. The holes were so near that a large coin would have covered both.

The countess touched the frame but not the canvas.

“He could have killed you,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And he wished you to know it.”

“Yes.”

She left the room.

The count and I remained before the landscape. I recalled Silvio’s bullet-riddled walls, his faded cap, and the cherry stone rolling through the grass to his boot. His revenge had been exact. He had not taken the count’s life; he had taken from him forever the memory of having waited for death without concern. Whether this was mercy or cruelty I could not decide.

“Did you ever see him again?” I asked.

“Never,” said the count. “A year later I heard that he had gone abroad. I made inquiries, but no report was certain. Men like Silvio leave many rumors and few addresses.”

We returned to the library. The count spoke of other things, and at dinner the countess was composed. No reference was made to the two shots. Yet whenever the conversation paused, I thought of the Swiss landscape in the adjoining room and of the two small holes above the painted lake.

I remained three days at the estate. The count was an excellent host; the countess treated me with gentle attention. On the morning of my departure, the count accompanied me to the steps.

“You knew him better than I did,” he said. “Tell me—was he satisfied?”

“I believe so.”

The count looked toward the windows of the small drawing room.

“Then he was fortunate,” he replied. “Few men are satisfied by revenge after waiting six years.”

I could think of no answer.

My carriage drove down the linden avenue. At the turn of the road I looked back. The house shone quietly in the morning sun. A gardener was trimming the hedge, and a child’s white cap appeared for an instant upon the terrace. Silvio had spared not merely a man, but an entire future of such ordinary scenes. Whether he understood their value, I do not know.

Some years afterward, during the disturbances in Greece, I read an account of a body of foreign volunteers who had fought near a mountain village against superior Turkish forces. Their commander, a Russian known among them by an Italian name, had been killed at the head of the detachment. The description might have applied to Silvio. Later, a traveler assured me that it was indeed he.

There was no certainty. His body had not been recovered.

I never heard his name again.

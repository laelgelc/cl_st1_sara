### THE CHIMNEY AT SPARROCK’S MILL

There were no illustrated weeklies in Oldtown when I was a boy; no railway brought us the morning’s gossip while it was yet warm from the press; no lecturer came twice a month to explain the stars, the pyramids, the constitution, and the domestic habits of the Patagonians; and no travelling company transformed the upper room of the tavern into a palace, a prison, or the blasted heath of Macbeth. Even books, though held in reverence, were not so abundant that a child might bury himself among them and forget his neighbours. We were therefore obliged to draw our entertainment from one another, and I am not sure that we were greatly to be pitied.

In those days every household had its traditions, as every hearth had its particular blaze and every garden its own ancient apple-tree. There were tales of the French wars, and of ships driven upon the black ledges east of the harbour; tales of wolves following a solitary rider through the forest; of ministers who had wrestled with the adversary in visible shape; of children lost in snow and guided home by lights which no mortal hand carried; of treasures buried under a three-forked pine; and of dead men who could not rest because some wrong, forgotten by the living, lay between them and the grave.

These narratives were not told in the hasty, naked manner in which one now reads an occurrence in a newspaper. They belonged to winter; to the snapping of great logs; to the slow roasting of apples; to the whir of the spinning-wheel; to the measured click of knitting-needles; and, above all, to the chimney-corner, where the narrator sat with his face in light and his back toward those shadowy regions from which all marvels seemed to proceed. A good storyteller did not fling out his facts like corn to poultry. He approached them by woodland paths, stopped at every house on the road, inquired after the grandparents of all concerned, discussed the weather, the state of religion, the price of rye, and the natural perversity of mankind, and arrived at his catastrophe only when the youngest hearer had drawn his stool close enough to touch the narrator’s knee.

Of all who frequented my grandmother’s hearth, none understood this art better than Sam Lawson.

Sam was a spare, loose-jointed man, with a long nose, mild blue eyes, and a mouth that appeared grave until a story took possession of him, when one corner of it would begin to twitch in advance of the humour. He came on errands which no one had sent him upon, borrowed tools he seldom returned, and brought news which generally required correction; yet he was welcome, for he carried about with him the whole unwritten history of Oldtown.

On the evening of which I speak the winter had closed upon us with uncommon severity. Snow had fallen since noon, and by sunset it was sweeping level with the lower panes. The wind came roaring over the pines, struck the eastern side of the house, rattled the shutters, searched at the doors, and then went complaining round the corners because it could not get in. Far beyond the woods, though we could not hear it distinctly, we knew the Atlantic was raging upon its iron coast; and that knowledge made the secure room, the red curtains, the shining brass andirons, and grandmother’s great braided rug seem doubly warm and precious.

My brother Bill and I had been watching the snow climb the windows when Sam Lawson entered, powdered white from cap to boots, carrying a small parcel of dried codfish for Aunt Lois.

“Why, Sam!” cried grandmother. “What possesses you to be abroad such a night?”

“Wal,” said Sam, stamping in the entry, “I’d sot out, Mis’ Badger, afore it come on so masterly thick; and arter a man’s sot out, there ain’t no sayin’ where he’ll stop. Wind’s kind o’ cantankerous. Tried to take my cap three times, and got it once; but I reasoned with it, and fetched it back.”

“You mean you picked it out of the drift,” said Aunt Lois.

“Jes’ so. Reasonin’ with the wind and pickin’ your cap out of a drift amounts to much the same thing, when all’s said.”

He disposed himself beside the hearth, holding first one long boot and then the other toward the blaze. We boys immediately began our assault.

“Tell us about the ghost at Sparrock’s mill,” said Bill.

“No, now,” said Sam, looking solemnly into the fire. “That ain’t a proper story for boys.”

“We’ve heard worse,” I urged.

“Shouldn’t wonder. Boys contrive to hear the worst of most things without half tryin’. It’s one o’ the mysterious providences.”

“We are not afraid,” said Bill, edging nearer me.

“Course not. Never knew a boy that was afraid in a room with six grown folks, two candles, a hickory fire, and the family dog under the table.”

A gust struck the chimney and sent a hollow cry through it. Bill glanced upward.

Aunt Lois, who was slicing apples for pies, said, “Don’t fill their heads with nonsense, Sam.”

“Who’s a-fillin’ ’em, Lois? They’re full now—full as a tick. I was merely sayin’ the tale wa’n’t suitable.”

Grandmother smiled over her knitting. “You have told it here before, Sam.”

“Wal, mebbe I have, and mebbe I hain’t. There’s stories a man tells so often he can’t remember whether he’s told ’em, dreamed ’em, or only calc’lated to tell ’em. But this here account come straight from Cap’n Eb Sawin, and Cap’n Eb wa’n’t what you’d call an imaginative man. He could sail a schooner, shoe a horse, set a broken finger, and argue foreordination; but he couldn’t invent nothin’.”

“He could drink cider,” observed Aunt Lois.

“In moderation,” said Sam. “That is, moderate accordin’ to the size of Cap’n Eb. A quart pitcher looked small alongside of him.”

“Did he see the ghost?” I demanded.

Sam lowered his voice.

“He see somethin’.”

At that Aunt Lois made an impatient sound; but grandmother sent me to the pantry for the cider jug, and Bill brought a pan of apples. When Sam had taken a long drink, wiped his mouth upon the back of his hand, selected the largest russet, rejected it for another, and turned his chair until one shoulder rested against the warm brick, he began.

“Wal, ye see, this happened in the great snow of ’twenty-nine—or mebbe ’thirty. I’ve heerd reasons on both sides, and there was a Widow Peabody who held out for ’twenty-eight till the day she died; but she was ninety-six, and arter ninety a person’s dates are apt to shake loose and travel round promiscuous. Anyhow, it was in the old days, afore the Sparrock mill got burnt—tore down, I should say, though burnin’ would ha’ been a sight healthier.

“Cack Sparrock lived there alone. His Christian name was Zaccheus, but folks had wore it down to Cack, same as a stone gets wore down in a brook. His father, Old Man Sparrock, had built the mill on Hemlock Run, where the road to Dunbury crosses the north branch. There was woods all round it then—black spruce, hemlock, and pine so thick a fox could go from Oldtown to Canada and never show his head to daylight.

“Cack had been a likely enough youngster once. That’s what everybody said, and everybody is always charitable to a man arter he’s gone thoroughly bad. He’d worked at the mill, sung bass in the meetin’-house, and courted Prudence Mather for the better part of two years. Then Prudence married a tinsmith from Portsmouth, Old Man Sparrock died sudden, and Cack took to drink.

“Now I don’t say Prudence’s marryin’ the tinsmith caused Cack’s drinkin’. A man that wants a cause can generally find one without employin’ a woman. But Cack grew slouchy and wild. He let the wheel rot, patched the roof with birch bark, and sold meal that had more gravel than nourishment in it. Folks stopped goin’ there, and by the time Cap’n Eb happened along, the place was no more a proper mill than I’m a bishop.

“But afore all that—nigh on fifteen year afore—there used to travel through Oldtown a peddler named Jehiel Lommedieu.”

“Lommy-do?” said Bill.

“Lom-me-dieu,” Sam repeated with dignity. “French name, though he wa’n’t French to speak of. His people come from somewhere up York State, or mebbe down Rhode Island. I’ve forgot which, but there’s trustworthy folks in both places.

“Jehiel was a little, brisk man, with black eyes, a striped waistcoat, and a pack near as big as a meetin’-house pulpit. He carried needles, ribbons, combs, spectacles, jackknives, pomatum, hymn-books, tin whistles, court-plaster, buttons, and them little brass clasps women are forever needin’ and men never know the name of. He could mend a watch, pull a tooth, write a love letter, or sing a song in a foreign tongue. He slept wherever night overtook him and paid his way fair.

“Everybody knew Jehiel. Mis’ Deacon Titcomb bought a tortoise-shell comb of him that lasted forty year and would ha’ lasted forty more if her granddaughter hadn’t set on it. My own mother—no, come to think, it was my aunt Nabby—bought a paper of pins. Anyhow, they was in the family.

“One October, Jehiel come through on his usual round. He had done uncommon well, for he was collectin’ money owed him and carried considerable silver in a belt under his shirt. He took supper at the tavern and told Landlord Pettigrew he meant to go by Sparrock’s mill, cross through Dunbury, and be back in Oldtown afore Thanksgivin’.

“‘Save me a slice of your turkey,’ says he.

“‘I’ll save ye the drumstick,’ says Pettigrew.

“‘Then I’ll be here to beat it,’ says Jehiel.

“He was a jokin’ little crittur. Next mornin’ he shouldered his pack and went north. Folks see him pass the Widow Sykes’s place. A woodcutter met him within two mile of Sparrock’s. And that was the last natural sight ever had of Jehiel Lommedieu.

“Thanksgivin’ come, and the drumstick went cold. Christmas come. Snow fell, snow melted, and no Jehiel. There was inquiries made in Dunbury, Portsmouth, Portland, and every place along his route. Some said he’d gone west. Some said he’d took ship. Some said he’d been robbed by strangers. Old Man Sparrock allowed that Jehiel had stopped at the mill for a drink of water and then gone on toward Canada.

“Wal, there it rested. A man with no fixed home is easy lost. If he don’t come back, each town thinks he’s in the next one, and by the time folks begin to wonder, his tracks have been tramped over by all creation.

“Old Man Sparrock died the next spring.”

“Suddenly?” asked grandmother.

“Drefful sudden. Fell in a fit, accordin’ to Cack. There was them who said he’d been ailin’, and them who said he hadn’t; and Dr. Moseley said it might ha’ been apoplexy, though he didn’t see the body till it was laid out and had never been over-confident in diagnoses made through a coffin lid.

“After that Cack lived alone at the mill, except for his jug, which was steady company. And there was one person that went to see him now and again—a woman called Ketury.”

At the name, the wind gave another long cry about the eaves, and Sam paused to turn his apple stem.

“Was she the witch?” I whispered.

“Folks called her so,” he replied. “Folks will call a lone woman anything rather than admit they don’t understand her.

“Ketury belonged to the remnant of the old people that had camped and planted along the river before our grandfathers’ grandfathers come disputin’ the ownership. She was old—or looked old—and lived in a bark-roofed cabin nigh the cedar swamp. She gathered roots, made baskets, doctored sprains, and knew where every berry grew from here to the coast. If a cow sickened, some said Ketury had overlooked it; but if a child had fever, they slipped through the woods after dark to fetch her herbs. That’s mankind for ye: ready to hang wisdom at noon and borrow it by midnight.

“She didn’t talk much. She’d look at a person as if she was readin’ somethin’ written behind his face. Cack feared her; that much was known. Once, when he was drunk at the tavern, he swore she had stood outside the mill three nights runnin’ and pointed at the chimney. Folks laughed, and Cack threw a mug at the wall and wouldn’t say no more.

“Wal, years went by. Then come the great snow.

“Cap’n Eb Sawin had been over to Dunbury to buy a pair of oxen. He was called Cap’n because he’d gone two voyages to the Banks, though he spent most of his life farmin’. Eb was six foot two in his stockings, broad as a barn door, and not easy scared. He’d faced squalls, bulls, constables, and a church disciplinary committee, and come off tolerable cheerful from all four.

“He started for home too late in the day. The sky was low and yaller, and the air had that still, waitin’ feel which any sensible man knows means snow. Eb knew it too, but a man who has just bought oxen always thinks Providence will hold the weather till he gets ’em home.

“By three o’clock the flakes was comin’ thick. By four the road was gone. The wind rose and drove the snow level through the woods, so the trees seemed marchin’ past and the drifts leaped up like white beasts. The oxen turned their backs to it and wouldn’t stir. Eb unyoked ’em, got ’em under a ledge, and tried to find the road afoot; but every direction looked like every other direction, which is one of the disagreeable habits of a snowstorm.

“He wandered till after dark. More’n once he fell into drifts to his waist. The wind yelled in the pine tops, limbs cracked, and the snow come so sharp it cut his face. At last he see a pale blink through the trees. He made for it, stumbled over a raceway, and found himself at Cack Sparrock’s mill.

“Cack opened the door after a powerful sight of knockin’. He had a candle in one hand and a bottle in the other.

“‘Who be ye?’ says he.

“‘Eb Sawin.’

“‘What d’ye want?’

“‘I want to come in afore I freeze solid enough to be set up for a milepost.’

“Cack looked over his shoulder and seemed minded to shut him out. But Eb put one boot inside the door, and there ain’t many doors can be shut contrary to a Sawin boot.

“The room wa’n’t invitin’. The mill stones hadn’t turned for years, and flour dust lay gray over everything like grave mould. The windows was stuffed with rags. Rain had leaked down the walls and froze in long streaks. There was one smoky fire on the hearth, and the chimney above it was broad enough for a man to stand in.

“Eb brushed himself off, drew up a stool, and asked for somethin’ warm.

“‘There’s rum,’ says Cack.

“‘Rum ain’t warm.’

“‘Feels warm.’

“‘Wal,’ says Eb, ‘that’s a theological question I don’t purpose arguin’ in this weather.’

“He took enough to hearten himself, though not half what Cack took. Then Eb told him about the oxen. Cack said they’d be dead by mornin’. Eb said they wouldn’t. Cack said all creatures died. Eb said that was no reason to hurry ’em. So they went on disagreein’ sociable enough for a spell.

“But Cack was restless. He’d drink, listen, get up, try the door, and stare at the chimney. Every time the wind come down it and flung smoke into the room, he’d jump.

“‘What ails ye?’ says Eb.

“‘Nothin’.’

“‘Then ye’ve got a mighty active kind o’ nothin’.’

“‘This is a bad night,’ says Cack.

“‘I’ve noticed.’

“‘Things travel on such nights.’

“‘Snow travels,’ says Eb. ‘It’s travelled into my neck and both boots.’

“Cack didn’t laugh. He leaned toward him.

“‘Did ye ever hear of a dead man walkin’?’

“‘Never heerd one walk so I knew he was dead.’

“‘They come back,’ says Cack. ‘If they ain’t buried proper, they come back.’

“Eb didn’t like the look of him. He judged Cack was gettin’ either crazy or quarrelsome, and neither state improves with rum. So he said, ‘You go to sleep, Cack. I’ll keep the fire.’

“Near eleven Cack dropped into a chair, but he didn’t sleep natural. He muttered and clawed at his collar. Once he cried, ‘Father, don’t put him there!’ and then woke up shakin’.

“Eb made out the words, though he didn’t then know their meanin’.

“Outside, the storm grew worse. It banged against the mill, roared through the broken wheel, and squealed under the eaves. The old building creaked like a ship labourin’ in seas. Sometimes the great chimney sucked upward with a hollow rumble; sometimes a gust beat down, scatterin’ ashes and sendin’ blue smoke twistin’ through the room.

“Then, close upon midnight, there come three knocks at the door.

“Thump—thump—thump.

“Cack lifted his head.

“‘Did ye hear that?’

“‘I ain’t deaf,’ says Eb.

“Again it came.

“Thump—thump—thump.

“Now, no person could rightly have been abroad. Eb himself had barely won through while he had daylight and strength. By midnight the drifts was over the fences, and the woods so blind with snow a crow couldn’t ha’ flown ten rods without hittin’ a tree.

“‘Don’t open it,’ says Cack.

“‘Might be some one lost, same as me.’

“‘No livin’ soul could get here.’

“‘Then,’ says Eb, ‘the dead can’t complain of the cold.’

“He took the candle and went to the door. Cack caught his coat, but Eb shook him loose and lifted the latch.

“The wind struck like a cannon blast. It blew out the candle, flattened the fire, and filled the room with snow. In the doorway stood Ketury.

“She had no cloak, only a dark wool blanket drawn over her head and shoulders. Yet the snow did not seem to lie on her. Her hair was loose, her face looked the colour of old copper, and her eyes shone black in the dark.

“She stepped in without askin’.

“Cack crawled backward till his chair hit the wall.

“‘You!’ says he.

“Ketury shut the door. Then she walked to the hearth and held her hands above the coals. She didn’t shiver and she didn’t speak.

“Eb relit the candle.

“‘How’d ye come through that snow?’ he asked.

“She looked at him and said, ‘The wind makes a road.’

“‘It covered mine,’ says Eb.

“Ketury turned to Cack.

“‘It is time.’

“‘No,’ says Cack.

“‘Long time,’ says she. ‘Too long.’

“‘Get out!’

“‘I came to call him.’

“Cack gave a sort of strangled yell. ‘There ain’t nobody here!’

“Then Ketury lifted her face toward the chimney.

“The fire had sunk low. Above it the chimney mouth stood black—black as a hole chopped into midnight. The wind moaned far up in it; and underneath the moan Eb thought he heard a faint scratchin’, like a rat behind stone.

“Ketury raised one hand.

“‘Come down,’ she said.

“Cack covered his eyes.

“Nothin’ happened.

“‘Come down,’ she said again. ‘Come down! Let’s see who ye be.’

“There came a rumblin’ overhead. Mortar sifted onto the hearth.

“Eb looked up. He wasn’t a man given to fancies, and he said afterward—said it to Parson Willet, Deacon Brown, Justice Haynes, and my own uncle’s wife’s brother—that at first he supposed the chimney was fallin’. He stood ready to jump.

“Somethin’ white showed in the soot above the fire.

“Then two feet came down.

“Human feet they looked, hangin’ side by side, toes downward, with old brass buckles glimmerin’ where the shoes might once have been. There was no body above ’em that Eb could see. Jest two feet, comin’ slow out of the chimney.

“Bill,” said Sam suddenly, “don’t clutch my sleeve so. Ye’ll stretch it.”

“I wasn’t clutching,” Bill answered, immediately letting go.

Sam drank cider and continued.

“Those feet descended till they hung a yard above the fire. Ketury never moved her eyes.

“‘Come down,’ says she. ‘Come down! Let’s see who ye be.’

“Two legs followed the feet.

“They was thin, gray legs, in striped breeches, and where they joined at the top there wa’n’t nothin’—no more body than there is to a pair of tongs. They moved a little, too, as if steppin’ down stairs that nobody else could see.

“Eb said it was the legs that troubled him most. A full ghost is what a person expects, if he expects anything; but a pair of legs, conductin’ business on their own account, goes against reason and good order.

“Cack began to pray. He didn’t pray connectedly, havin’ neglected the practice. He mixed up the Lord’s Prayer, the multiplication table, and a verse about Nebuchadnezzar.

“Ketury spoke the third time.

“‘Come down, come down! Let’s see who ye be.’

“There come the middle of a man—a waistcoat, a brown coat whitened with mould, and a leather belt around the middle. It settled upon the legs, though Eb couldn’t see how it fastened. The coat was ripped at the breast, and through the rip there showed a dark stain.

“Cack screamed, ‘I never struck him!’

“Eb turned on him. ‘Struck who?’

“But Cack only whimpered and beat his head against the wall.

“The wind roared. Ashes whirled up. Ketury’s blanket streamed behind her, though the door was shut.

“‘Come down!’ she cried louder. ‘Come down! Let’s see who ye be.’

“Two arms slid from the chimney, one after t’other. They joined the shoulders. One hand hung open. The other was shut tight, as if holdin’ somethin’.

“Now there stood a man’s shape over the fire—feet, legs, body, and arms—but no head.

“Eb, practical as he was, found that objectionable.

“‘If ye’ve called him this fur,’ says he to Ketury, ‘ye might as well finish. I don’t favour leavin’ a job half done.’

“She looked once at Eb, and he said afterward there was grief in her face—not witchcraft, nor triumph, but grief as old and hard as winter.

“Then she stretched both hands to the chimney and gave the call once more.

“‘Come down! Come down! Let’s see who ye be!’

“There was a great scrapin’ and rattlin’ high in the bricks. A round shadow rolled forward. Slowly, slowly, the head descended—first black hair stiff with soot; then a white forehead; then shut eyes; then a small, sharp face.

“The head settled upon the shoulders.

“The eyes opened.

“Cack fell on his knees.

“‘Jehiel!’ he yelled. ‘Jehiel Lommedieu!’

“The apparition turned its face toward him.

“Eb knew the peddler’s likeness, though it had been fifteen year. He’d bought a razor of Jehiel once, and maintained it was the only honest razor ever sold by a travelin’ man.

“The ghost lifted its closed hand. The fingers opened, and a little brass button dropped onto the hearth with a click.

“Then it pointed upward—straight up the chimney.

“Cack crawled toward Eb.

“‘Don’t let him touch me! Father done it! Father done it for the money!’

“The ghost kept pointing.

“‘He come late,’ Cack sobbed. ‘Snow was fallin’. Father knew he had silver. He struck him with the mill hammer. I held the lantern—that’s all! I held the lantern! Father made me help. We took the pack apart and burned what we couldn’t sell. We hid him in the chimney where it narrows above the smoke shelf. Father said fire and soot would eat him, and no man would ever know.’

“Eb seized Cack by the shoulder.

“‘Is the body there now?’

“‘Yes! O Lord, yes! He’s been there all these years. I hear him scratchin’ when the wind blows north. I hear the buckles knockin’ on the brick. Ketury knew. She found the silver button by the raceway, and she knew.’

“Eb looked toward Ketury.

“But at that instant the ghost turned from Cack and faced the chimney. It seemed to come apart the same way it had come together, only quicker. The head rose, the arms flew upward, the body stretched thin, and the legs streamed after, as smoke streams up a flue.

“Ketury stepped into the hearth.

“‘Wait!’ cried Eb.

“She lifted her blanket across her face.

“A blast came down the chimney with a roar like the whole Atlantic tumblin’ onto the roof. The candle went out. The door burst open. Sparks, snow, soot, and ashes spun together till Eb could neither see nor breathe.

“When the blast passed, the fire was dead.

“Ketury was gone.

“Cack lay on the floor.”

Sam stopped.

For a moment none of us spoke. The storm had lessened, but snow whispered dryly against the panes, and somewhere in the house a board gave a small, distinct crack.

“What did Captain Eb do?” I asked.

“Did?” said Sam. “Wal, he didn’t set there till the ghost come down for a second visit. He got Cack onto the bed, lit the lantern, and waited for daylight. Cack alternated between prayin’, confessin’, and callin’ for rum, but Eb wouldn’t give him none. Said he’d had revelation enough out of one bottle.

“At dawn the storm abated. Eb dug his way to the road, found the oxen alive under the ledge—showin’ Cack didn’t know as much about oxen as he did about murder—and fetched help.

“Justice Haynes, Parson Willet, Dr. Moseley, and six or seven others come to the mill. Cack made a formal deposition. The justice wrote it all down, though his ink froze twice and Parson Willet had to warm the bottle inside his coat. Cack confessed that Old Man Sparrock killed Jehiel for near three hundred dollars; that he himself helped lift the body into the chimney; and that fear of his father first, and fear of punishment afterward, had kept him silent.

“Parson Willet exhorted him to repentance, sayin’ mercy might be found even at the eleventh hour.

“Cack said, ‘It’s later than that.’

“He begged them to pull down the mill, especially the chimney. Before sunset he took a fit and died.”

“From guilt,” said grandmother softly.

“From drink,” said Aunt Lois.

“From both, mebbe,” Sam replied. “Guilt’s thirsty work.

“Anyhow, the deposition was signed, witnessed, and sealed. Cap’n Eb made his mark—he could read print but had small patience with writin’. Then the selectmen ordered the old mill tore down.

“They started with the chimney. About eight feet above the hearth the masons found a hollow where bricks had been took out and put back careless. Inside was the skeleton of a man, doubled close. There was remnants of a brown coat, a leather money belt cut open, two brass shoe buckles, and a striped waistcoat. In one pocket they found three needles rusted together, a bit of tortoise-shell comb, and a pewter thimble.

“Landlord Pettigrew identified the waistcoat. Mis’ Deacon Titcomb identified the comb as same pattern as hers. Eb identified the razor—”

“How could he identify a razor in the chimney?” Aunt Lois interrupted.

“Wal,” said Sam, blinking, “mebbe the razor was found in Cack’s chest. I never claimed the chimney had pockets enough for the whole peddler’s pack. What I mean is, evidence accumulated. Evidence has a way of accumulatin’ round a story if ye give it time.

“They buried Jehiel in Oldtown churchyard, and Parson Willet preached from the text, ‘There is nothing covered, that shall not be revealed; neither hid, that shall not be known.’ Folks come from three towns to the funeral. As for Ketury, some said they saw her standin’ among the pines while the grave was filled. Others said she had gone north before the great snow. Nobody ever found her cabin occupied again.

“And that,” Sam concluded, “is the full, true, and circumstantial account of the chimney at Sparrock’s mill, jest as Cap’n Eb told it to me, except what I’ve forgot, and such little particulars as naturally get improved by age.”

Aunt Lois set down her paring knife with decision.

“The skeleton may have been found,” she said, “and Cack may have confessed. But that business of the ghost coming down in pieces is folly. Captain Eb was snow-blind, half frozen, and sitting all night with a drunken man. Cack had rum, and I dare say Eb drank more than he admitted. They fell asleep, dreamed the whole apparition, and frightened one another awake.”

“Where did Ketury come from?” asked Bill.

“She may have come before the storm and been hiding in the mill.”

“And how did she go away?”

“By the door, like anybody else.”

“What about the blast?”

“The door blew open.”

“What about the button?”

“There are always buttons lying about untidy houses,” said Aunt Lois.

Grandmother folded her knitting in her lap.

“You may explain every part separately, Lois, and yet leave the whole unexplained. The confession was true; the body was found; and if Providence chose a strange instrument to uncover blood-guiltiness, it is not for us to say the instrument was impossible.”

“Providence does not need to send people down chimneys by instalments.”

“Wal,” said Sam, “Providence has a sight more ways of conductin’ business than you or me has had occasion to examine.”

Aunt Lois gave him a sharp glance. “Do you yourself believe every word you have told?”

Sam regarded the glowing bed of coals. His face assumed the expression of a man called upon to settle a question which had perplexed philosophers and divided churches.

“Lois,” he said at last, “there’s two sorts o’ folks in this world. There’s them that believes everything, and goes through life forever bein’ took in; and there’s them that believes nothin’, and don’t get no comfort out o’ what’s true. Now my practice has always been to keep a happy medium. I believe enough of a story to enjoy tellin’ it, and disbelieve enough to keep from losin’ sleep.”

“That is a convenient doctrine.”

“Most useful doctrines are.”

“You have embroidered Captain Eb’s account till there is more embroidery than cloth.”

“Mebbe. But cloth without embroidery is apt to be poor Sunday wear.”

Grandmother laughed; even Aunt Lois’s mouth softened, though she tried to hide it by gathering the apple peelings. The wind had dropped, and the house settled again into its familiar security. Bill ventured away from my side and went for another apple. The dog came out from under the table, stretched, and yawned, as if to signify that ghosts were of no importance to creatures with a sound conscience.

Sam rose and wrapped his muffler twice about his neck.

“You are not going home through that snow,” grandmother said. “You will sleep in the kitchen chamber.”

“Wal, I ain’t particular. That chamber’s tolerable warm, though the clothespress creaks in a manner I don’t wholly approve.”

“It creaks because the floor is uneven,” said Aunt Lois.

“Course. Everything has a reason, accordin’ to Lois.”

“Most things have.”

Sam put one hand on the door latch and looked back at her. The mischievous corner of his mouth began to rise.

“Speakin’ o’ reasons, Lois, did your mother ever tell these boys why you won’t pass the old buryin’-ground alone after sundown?”

Aunt Lois turned so quickly that the pan of apples nearly slipped from her hands.

“Sam Lawson!”

“Or what it was,” he continued mildly, “that followed ye from Deacon Titcomb’s huskin’ when you was nineteen, and left three wet footprints on the doorstep, though there hadn’t been rain for a fortnight?”

“There was no such thing.”

“Jes’ so,” said Sam. “No such thing at all. Only I’ve always noticed that things which never happened are commonly the ones folks are most anxious not to have mentioned.”

Grandmother looked over her spectacles. Bill and I stared at Aunt Lois. Outside, beyond the red curtains and the circle of firelight, the snowy road ran white between the dark pines, past the church, past the old burying-ground, and away toward regions in which the histories of the living and the unrest of the dead were not yet wholly divided.

“Aunt Lois,” I said, “what followed you home?”

But Aunt Lois took up the candle, declared that children who asked foolish questions ought to be in bed, and marched us from the room without another word.

Behind us we heard Sam Lawson draw his chair again toward the hearth.

“Wal, Mis’ Badger,” he said to grandmother, “seein’ as I’m to sleep here, I might’s well have jest one more mug o’ cider. Snow stories is powerful dryin’.”

## The Minister’s Night Voyage

When the Reverend Samuel Bax died, in the winter of 1889, at the age of seventy-one, the people of Troy discovered that they had loved him more than they had commonly allowed. This discovery, as often happens in small towns, expressed itself in wreaths, lowered blinds, minute recollections of his virtues, and a complete suspension of those criticisms which had accompanied him through fifty years of useful labour.

His widow, Nance, survived him by six days.

She had sat beside the dead man for one night, declining alike the attentions of her daughters, the consolation of elders, and the beef tea of Mrs. Penberthy; and towards morning she had laid her hand upon his forehead and said, in the hearing of the youngest grandchild, “Iss, lad. I’ll not keep ’ee waiting long.”

On the following Tuesday they were carried together up the steep street from the harbour. Troy had seldom witnessed so large a funeral. The drapers closed their shutters; the pilchard boats in the Pool flew their colours at half-mast; and even Ruan, across the water, sent a deputation which arrived in two boats and walked behind the family with a dignity that Troy considered almost presumptuous.

The text preached over them was: “Lovely and pleasant in their lives, and in their death they were not divided.”

The young minister, who had never known them except as two white-haired persons inhabiting the square grey house beside the chapel, spoke with a tenderness which did him credit. He called their union “a marriage made in Heaven,” and the congregation sighed. Some among the elder hearers remembered circumstances which qualified the phrase; but none contradicted it. Fifty years confer an authority before which inconvenient beginnings grow modest and retire.

I, who tell the tale, will not dogmatise upon the precise share taken by Heaven in that marriage. It may have designed the end; but the truth may be told that Nance Trewartha contrived the beginning.

This happened fifty years earlier, in 1839, when Samuel Bax was twenty-one years old, possessed of a new black coat, a tolerable education, a high opinion of the ministerial office, and no knowledge whatever of women or boats.

Troy, though no more than a congregation of slate roofs heaped about a crooked harbour, looked down upon Ruan. It did so literally, from the northern side of the water, and morally, from every point of view. Troy had three merchants, two surgeons, a bank which opened twice weekly, an Independent chapel with a gallery, and a mayor whose chain was believed to be silver. Ruan had fishermen, nets, brown sails, tarred sheds, and a reputation for settling disputes without the preliminary expense of law.

Between them ran the harbour, crossed in daylight by a penny ferry.

It would seem difficult to found a social gulf upon a penny; yet human ingenuity has accomplished more with less. A Troy maid might wed a Ruan fisherman, but her mother would lower her voice when naming the match. A Ruan man might grow wealthy, build himself a square house, subscribe handsomely to chapel funds, and dine upon mahogany; Troy would still recall that his grandfather had gone barefoot to the mackerel grounds. The two places exchanged fish, gossip, labour, sweethearts, and insults, but never equality.

In March of that year the Independent chapel required a minister. The former pastor, after twenty-eight years of theological conflict with his deacons, had accepted a call to Bodmin, where, as he wrote in his farewell letter, “a wider field of Christian concord awaits me.” Troy understood this to mean that the Bodmin stipend was larger by fourteen pounds.

Several candidates preached trial sermons. One whispered, one shouted, one leaned so far over the pulpit that the children expected him to fall; but Samuel Bax, newly issued from an academy at Exeter, pleased almost everybody.

He was tall without being gaunt, pale without appearing sickly, and his hair, which was brown and abundant, rose from his forehead in a manner suggesting intellect to the matrons and romance to their daughters. His hands were long, his linen clean, his boots newly blacked. He had a somewhat grave mouth, but smiled readily enough when praised; and if he inclined his head while listening, it was partly from humility and partly because he knew that his profile was good.

For his trial sermon he took the text, “Launch out into the deep.”

No one afterwards failed to observe the appropriateness.

He preached for forty-seven minutes. He thumped the desk eleven times, raised his right hand twenty-three times, and quoted a Greek word which no person present understood, though Mr. Prowse the chemist said afterwards that the pronunciation had been excellent. The congregation remarked also a small darn at the edge of his left cuff, proving either poverty or thrift, according to the observer’s temper.

In the third pew from the front sat the daughters of Troy: Miss Prowse, with four hundred pounds; the two Misses Rundle, whose uncle owned warehouses; Betsy Couch, pink and practical; Grace Treloar, thin, devotional, and given to tracts; and several others who had been brought to chapel by conviction, curiosity, or maternal pressure. By the second hymn each had ascertained that Samuel wore no mourning ring. By the sermon’s end they had divided his merits, defects, prospects, and probable salary amongst them.

Behind these young women sat Nance Trewartha of Ruan.

She had crossed by the penny ferry that morning in a blue gown and a bonnet whose ribbon she had ironed herself. Her father owned two fishing luggers and commanded one of them; which made him a substantial man in Ruan, though in Troy he remained Trewartha the fisherman. Nance was his only daughter. She kept his house, mended his accounts where he had spoiled them, could row a boat against tide, and had once carried a half-drowned boy from the surf under the eastern cliff.

She was twenty, dark-haired, deep-bosomed, straight as a mast, and beautiful in a fashion too decided to be called pretty. Her eyes were grey when quiet and almost black when angry; her hands, though shapely, were brown with work. Troy young women pitied those hands, and Troy young men did not.

During the sermon Nance examined Samuel closely. She noted the clean parting of his hair, the nervous movement of one finger against the pulpit Bible, the breadth of his shoulders, the frayed cuff, the absence of a ring, and a small white scar beside his chin. She listened to his doctrine also, but not so continuously.

When the congregation poured into the street, the young women collected near the chapel gate.

“He’s very young,” said Grace Treloar.

“He’ll improve,” said Betsy Couch.

“His voice needs strengthening,” observed Miss Prowse, whose father had already invited Samuel to tea.

“He looked this way twice,” said the younger Miss Rundle.

“He looked at the clock,” said her sister.

Nance stood a little apart, tying her bonnet strings. Betsy, who liked her in private and despised Ruan in public, nodded.

“Well, Nance, what do you think of our new minister?”

“You haven’t got ’en yet,” said Nance.

“We shall. Father says the deacons are agreed.”

“Then I think he’ll do.”

The girls laughed, not loudly, but with that delicate unanimity by which one class instructs another in its place.

“Do?” repeated Miss Prowse.

“Iss. He’ve got a good head, and his chest’ll broaden.”

“You examined him like a horse.”

“No,” said Nance. “I know more about horses.”

This answer disturbed the harmony. Grace Treloar asked whether Ruan chapel was without ministers that its women must come hunting to Troy.

“We’ve ministers enough,” Nance replied, “and men too, for them that want ’em.”

She went down to the ferry before her cheeks betrayed her. Halfway across she looked back. Samuel Bax stood above the chapel steps receiving congratulations. Miss Prowse’s father had him by the elbow; Miss Prowse herself waited beside a shining carriage; the Rundles hovered near; and even Grace Treloar had discovered a question concerning infant baptism which required a private answer.

Nance watched until the ferry bumped against Ruan quay.

“New preacher?” asked the ferryman.

“Iss.”

“Any good?”

“He’ll do,” she said again.

But that evening, while gutting mackerel for her father’s supper, she considered the enemy. Troy possessed money, leisure, polished speech, drawing rooms, pianos, and mothers skilled in the science of invitations. Nance possessed a blue gown, strong arms, a father who disliked preachers, and the ability to sail anything in the harbour. Waiting, she perceived, would be foolish.

Samuel accepted the call.

Three weeks later he arrived by coach on a raw April afternoon, accompanied by two trunks, a case of books, an umbrella, and a conviction that Providence had opened his path. The deacons met him at the inn. They conducted him to the chapel, the vestry, and finally to the lodging selected for him in the house of Mrs. Vellan, a widow whose garden descended by terraces to the harbour.

The town lay under a pale sunset. Chimneys and masts cut the western light; gulls wheeled above the fish quay; the tide, flooding between the piers, lifted the moored boats one by one as though waking them. Across the water Ruan climbed the opposite hill in a disorder of whitewashed cottages, net-lofts, alleys, and smoke.

Samuel paused at Mrs. Vellan’s gate.

“A curious place,” he said.

“Picturesque,” corrected Deacon Prowse. “You will find the people warm-hearted, Mr. Bax, though requiring firmness. The fishing class especially.”

He indicated Ruan.

“Do they attend our chapel?”

“Some cross over. We make no distinctions in the sight of God.”

This was true on Sundays and less invariably so during the week.

At supper Samuel met several leading members of the congregation and two of their daughters. Miss Prowse poured tea. Betsy Couch handed bread. Grace Treloar inquired about missions. Mrs. Vellan watched all three and formed conclusions adverse to each. By ten o’clock Samuel was tired, flattered, and content. His books had been arranged; his first sermon lay prepared; his future seemed to stretch before him as a straight, ascending road with a respectable congregation on either side.

The wind had fallen. A thin moon showed above Ruan hill, and frost sharpened the garden paths. Samuel opened his bedroom window and looked towards the harbour. He could hear the tide turning stones below the wall.

Someone tapped at the garden door.

At first he supposed it a branch. The sound came again: three rapid knocks, a pause, then two.

He took his candle and descended.

Mrs. Vellan’s servant slept in the attic; the widow herself had retired with a headache after the excitement of entertaining a minister. Samuel crossed the dark parlour, entered the kitchen, and drew back the bolt.

A young woman stood outside.

Her dark cloak was dusted with moisture, and the moonlight behind her made a pale border about her head. She breathed quickly, as though she had been running.

“Mr. Bax?”

“Yes.”

“You must come. Quick.”

He lifted the candle. “What has happened?”

“Mrs. Tregenza, over to Ruan. She’s dying, and asking for ’ee.”

“I do not know Mrs. Tregenza.”

“She heard ’ee preach. Said she couldn’t die easy without ’ee.”

There are summonses which a young minister imagines often and almost desires, not from hardness of heart, but because grave duties lend immediate dignity to an untested office. Samuel saw himself beside the poor woman’s bed, speaking courage to a departing soul while rough fishermen listened with tears in their eyes.

“Has no one sent for your own minister?”

“She’ll have none but you.”

“Wait while I wake Mrs. Vellan.”

“No time. The ferry’s shut, but I’ve a boat below. She may be gone before we land.”

Her urgency conquered his prudence. He fetched his overcoat, pocket Bible, and hat. On returning he found her already halfway down the garden.

“Who are you?” he whispered as they descended.

“Nance Trewartha.”

He remembered the dark girl in the third pew, and also, vaguely, some laughter outside the gate.

The garden ended at a narrow landing where a boat rose and fell against green-stained steps. Nance jumped aboard, cast off the painter, and held out a hand. Samuel ignored the hand and stepped in with care. The boat moved from under him, causing him to sit abruptly upon a thwart.

“Mind yourself,” said Nance. “You’m no sea legs yet.”

“I have travelled by packet.”

“Iss? Sit quiet, then.”

She shipped two sweeps and pulled them beyond the garden wall. It was a small open boat, broad amidships, with one mast and a bundled lug-sail. Samuel could see little besides Nance’s face, the white loom of the sweeps, and black water slipping past. Troy’s lamps trembled behind them.

The ferry lay moored for the night. Beyond it a forest of spars shifted upon the tide.

Nance rowed strongly. No wasted movement disturbed the rhythm of her body; she bent, pulled, and recovered with an ease so complete that Samuel, who had never seen a young woman perform hard labour without apology, watched in astonishment.

“You should have brought someone with you,” he said.

“Who?”

“Your father. Or a brother.”

“Father’s at sea. I’ve no brother.”

“It is not suitable for you to be abroad alone at this hour.”

“Then ’tis lucky I’ve got you.”

This was unanswerable in the circumstances. Samuel cleared his throat.

They passed the last moored lugger, but instead of bearing across towards the Ruan lights, Nance continued down harbour.

“Is Mrs. Tregenza’s house near the mouth?”

“A little way.”

The tide caught them. Soon the lights of Troy and Ruan drew together behind, becoming one scattered constellation upon the hillsides. Ahead, the harbour opened between two dark headlands. A longer movement lifted the boat.

“Are we going outside?” Samuel asked.

Nance rested on her oars. “Can ’ee sail?”

“I have had no occasion to learn.”

“Can ’ee swim?”

“Not well.”

“How far is not well?”

“I can remain afloat.”

“In a bath?”

He heard laughter in her voice and stiffened. “This is hardly the time for levity.”

“No,” she said. “Maybe not.”

She stepped forward, raised the little mast, and shook out the sail. It opened with a crack. The boat heeled so suddenly that Samuel grasped both gunwales.

“Sit to windward. No, t’other side. That’s right.”

“You said we were going to a dying woman.”

“So we are.”

“Then why are we sailing away from Ruan?”

Nance made no answer. She hauled the fore-sheet, put the tiller down, and sent the boat between the harbour piers.

The sea received them.

It was not rough, but it was alive in a manner the harbour had not prepared Samuel to understand. Long swells advanced out of darkness, lifting the boat towards the stars and letting it slide again into black hollows. The breeze, hardly felt ashore, pressed cold against his cheeks. Behind them the harbour mouth narrowed.

Samuel looked from the distant lights to Nance. “Where does this Mrs. Tregenza live?”

Nance steadied the sail.

“There’s no such place,” she said.

For several moments the hiss of water made all the conversation.

“What do you mean?”

“I told you a lie.”

He stared at her. “No one is ill?”

“No.”

“No one sent for me?”

“No.”

“Then turn back at once.”

Nance pushed the tiller over a fraction. The sail drew harder, and the boat went on.

“Did you hear me?”

“Iss.”

“I command you to return.”

“You can’t command a boat.”

“I command you.”

“That’s worse.”

Samuel rose. The boat dipped, the sail flapped, and Nance seized his coat.

“Sit down, you foolish man! You’ll have us over.”

He sat, less in obedience than from terror. The water, passing within a hand’s breadth, appeared black and bottomless.

“This is an outrage,” he said. “It is criminal. What can you intend?”

Nance looked ahead. Her face had lost its mischief; under the moon it appeared calm, almost severe.

“I mean for ’ee to marry me.”

Samuel supposed for an instant that the sea had disordered his hearing.

“You mean—what?”

“I love ’ee. I’ve loved ’ee since you stood up in the pulpit, and I mean for ’ee to marry me.”

“You cannot love a person you have seen once.”

“Twice now.”

“This is madness.”

“Maybe.”

“It is wickedness.”

“Maybe that too.”

“You have enticed me here by a falsehood, exposed us both to danger, and placed my character—my sacred office—in peril. Turn back immediately.”

“No.”

“How far do you propose to go?”

“Far enough.”

“For what?”

“Till you say yes.”

The simplicity of it silenced him. He looked towards land. The twin headlands were already behind them, and the coast stretched in broken darkness under the moon. No vessel showed nearby; no voice could reach them. Troy, his chapel, his trunks, his future, and the deacons who guarded it had shrunk into an uncertain glow astern.

“You cannot keep me at sea indefinitely.”

“No. Wind’ll shift by morning, likely.”

“And then?”

“We’ll run west.”

“For how long?”

“I brought bread.”

He turned upon her. “Do you understand what you are doing? If we remain away all night and return together, my reputation is destroyed.”

“So’s mine.”

“That does not excuse it.”

“No.”

“My congregation will dismiss me.”

“Then you can preach at Ruan.”

“This is not a jest!”

“I’m not jesting.”

He believed her. The conviction was more alarming than the boat, the darkness, or the cold.

Nance sat with one hand upon the tiller and the other holding the sheet. Her cloak had fallen open at the throat. Strands of black hair whipped across her cheek. She seemed not an ignorant girl conducting an absurd adventure, but some sea-born power acquainted with winds, tides, and purposes beyond his control.

Samuel disliked this impression and resisted it.

“You expect to secure a husband by ruining him?”

“I don’t want to ruin ’ee.”

“You have used the word love. Is this your understanding of it?”

“No,” she answered slowly. “But ’tis my way of getting time.”

“Time?”

“With the Troy girls after ’ee, when should I speak? Miss Prowse’ll ask ’ee to supper, and the Rundles’ll ask ’ee to tea, and Grace Treloar’ll save heathens beside ’ee till you’m promised before midsummer. They’ve parlours and money and fine talk. I’ve a boat.”

“Therefore you abduct me?”

“Iss.”

Despite his anger, the word struck him as comic. A laugh rose and was suppressed. To laugh would diminish the enormity of his position, and Samuel was not yet willing to surrender that advantage.

“You know nothing of me.”

“I know enough.”

“What do you know?”

Nance considered. “You’m vain.”

He gasped.

“You liked them watching ’ee in chapel. You liked Deacon Prowse saying your sermon was powerful. You’ve got a scar by your chin; you bite your finger when you lose your place; your left boot turns in a little; and you preach too long.”

“This is insolence.”

“Your shoulders are good. You’m kind, I think, though I haven’t proved it. You believe every word you preach, which isn’t true of all ministers. You’d be handsome if you got some weather on ’ee. And there’s no ring on your hand.”

“You examined me like a horse.”

“No,” said Nance, “I know more about horses.”

This time the laugh escaped him. It was brief and unwilling; nevertheless she heard it.

“Turn back,” he said, with less force.

“Say you’ll marry me.”

“I shall say nothing of the kind.”

“Then we’ll sail.”

They sailed.

The moon climbed clear of a bank of cloud, laying a cold road across the water. Inland the fields rose faintly behind the cliffs, divided by hedges invisible except where frost caught them; seaward there was nothing but the slow, breathing dark. The boat ran close-hauled, shouldering the swells. Now and again spray crossed the bow and flashed like powdered silver.

Samuel’s anger, having no fresh material, settled into misery. He imagined the dawn at Mrs. Vellan’s house: the empty bed, the open garden door, the alarm. By breakfast the town would know; by noon it would improve the facts; by evening he would have eloped with three Ruan women and stolen the communion plate. He saw Deacon Prowse’s injured face, Miss Prowse’s outraged purity, the chapel door closed against him. His first charge would end before his first sermon.

“Ruin,” he murmured.

Nance heard. “I’m risking the same.”

“You appear indifferent.”

“I’m not.”

“You can return whenever you choose.”

“So can you.”

“I cannot sail the boat.”

“Then you’m not choosing, are you?”

There was a truth in this so offensive that he rejected it at once.

“You take advantage of my ignorance.”

“Iss.”

“A more modest woman would be ashamed.”

“I am ashamed.”

Her voice altered. Samuel looked at her.

She had not moved, but the hand upon the sheet trembled. He understood for the first time that courage might contain fear without being defeated by it. Until then he had regarded her as shameless because shame had not stopped her; now he perceived that she had brought shame, terror, hope, and her whole future aboard, and was sailing with them because desire was stronger.

“Nance,” he said, more quietly, “look at me.”

She did not.

“Look at me.”

“I must watch the sail.”

“You have managed it without watching for the past hour.”

She turned her head.

The grey eyes which had confronted him so boldly were wet. Not with the decorative moisture fashionable in sentimental prints, but with two angry tears which the wind drove sideways towards her temples.

“I know ’tis wrong,” she said. “D’you think I don’t? I know what they’ll call me. I know what you’ll call me. But if I’d waited decent, you’d never have seen me. Not seen me proper. I’d be Trewartha’s girl from Ruan, smelling of nets, and you’d bow at the ferry and go dine with Miss Prowse.”

“I would not judge a person so narrowly.”

“You did.”

He began to protest and remembered that, until the garden door opened, he had forgotten her existence.

Nance went on. “I don’t ask ’ee for money. We’ve enough. I can work. I can keep house and accounts; I can nurse sick folk, row, sail, mend, cook—though pastry’s not my best. I’m strong. Look at me.”

The phrase, returning upon him, had the force of a command.

He looked.

Her cloak had slipped farther back. The wind pressed her dress against the long line of her body; her head was bare now, for the hood had fallen, and her dark hair streamed over one shoulder. She held the boat as though it were part of herself—firmly, lightly, without fear. Behind her the sail curved pale against a sky crowded with stars.

Samuel had seen beauty in engravings, in phrases, in the pale daughters of respectable houses; he had admired it with judgment. This required no judgment. It entered him suddenly and disturbed everything.

“Look at me,” she repeated. “I’m sound. I’m not lazy nor false—except tonight, and I’ll carry that lie to judgment. I’ll be true to ’ee all my days. If sorrow comes, I’ll stand by. If they turn ’ee out, I’ll feed ’ee. If you’m sick, I’ll tend ’ee. If you hate me tomorrow, I’ll take ’ee home and never trouble ’ee after. But tonight I wanted one chance under God’s sky for you to see me.”

A shooting star went down beyond her shoulder, so bright that Samuel turned instinctively to follow it. The firmament seemed alive, innumerable fires breathing in the blackness. He had preached that the heavens declared glory; but no academy had instructed him what to do when glory took the form of a fisherman’s daughter demanding marriage in an open boat.

“Nance,” he said, “you speak as though affection alone were sufficient.”

“What else?”

“Suitability. Character. Agreement in faith. Mutual esteem. The approval of families.”

“Father’ll roar, but he’ll come round.”

“That was not my meaning.”

“I’m Independent.”

“This is no time for flippancy.”

“Seems the only time we’ve got.”

He looked away, but there was nowhere safe to rest his eyes. The stars accused his smallness; the sea mocked his authority; the coast, remote and indifferent, offered no assistance. His destiny, which at sunset had resembled a straight road arranged by Providence and deacons, now appeared to be running before the wind in a boat commanded by Nance Trewartha.

He thought of the Troy girls. Miss Prowse had money and composure; Betsy Couch had sense; Grace Treloar had principles. None of them would have lied about a deathbed, stolen him from his lodging, and threatened to sail to the horizon. This was greatly in their favour.

Yet none of them would have dared so much for him.

Vanity had its part in what followed. The truth may be told. A man of twenty-one may resist a woman’s beauty, deplore her methods, and condemn her grammar; but to discover that she has staked her good name, her safety, and perhaps her life upon the chance of obtaining him is a compliment difficult to regard with perfect sobriety.

“You said you loved me after seeing me once,” he said.

“Iss.”

“What did you love?”

“Your face first.”

He was disconcerted by her honesty.

“Then the way you preached. Then I thought how you’d look when you’m older. Then all the rest I don’t know yet.”

“That is not love. It is imagination.”

“Love has to start somewhere.”

“And if the reality disappoints you?”

“I’ll bear it.”

He smiled despite himself. “You allow no advantage to me.”

“You can bear me.”

The boat mounted a larger swell. For one suspended instant Nance and the sail stood against the entire starry heaven; then they descended together. Something in Samuel descended also—pride, perhaps, or fear, or merely the belief that life could be kept within channels by correct behaviour.

He said, “Come here.”

“I can’t leave the tiller.”

“Then I shall come to you.”

“You’ll capsize us.”

But he had already moved, crawling with undignified care along the wet thwart. Nance watched him, scarcely breathing.

He reached her. Up close he saw the salt on her lashes and the quick pulse in her throat.

“This does not signify consent,” he said.

“No?”

“No.”

He kissed her.

It was his first kiss, and therefore not accomplished with the practised elegance which Miss Prowse might have expected; but Nance received it without criticism. She dropped the sheet, put both arms around his neck, and kissed him with such energy that the sail shook loose and thundered overhead.

The boat swung broadside.

“Mind!” she cried, thrusting him away. “Unfasten that sheet—quick! Duck your head!”

The boom swept across. Samuel ducked, fumbled with the rope, and nearly lost his hat. Nance seized the tiller, hauled the boat round, and laughed aloud.

“What are you doing?” he demanded.

“Going home.”

He stared at her. “Why?”

“You kissed me.”

“I did not agree to marriage.”

“Near enough.”

“It is nothing of the kind.”

“Then we can talk on the way.”

And, with a competence that reduced his objections to air, she put the boat about and headed for the harbour.

The return was swifter. Wind and tide, which had seemed engaged in carrying Samuel towards destruction, now combined to restore him to respectability. The coast travelled beside them; the headlands opened; and presently the lamps of Troy and Ruan separated once more upon their opposing hills.

They spoke little. Nance’s boldness had drained away with the necessity for it. She kept her eyes upon the sail. Samuel, chilled and bewildered, sat amidships and considered what had occurred.

Before dawn they entered the harbour. Frost shone on the quays, and every house was dark. Nance lowered the sail among the sleeping boats and took up the sweeps. They glided under Mrs. Vellan’s garden wall without challenge.

At the steps she held the boat steady.

Samuel climbed out. When he turned, Nance was still below him, one hand on the painter. Her face looked suddenly young.

“No one saw,” she whispered.

“Apparently not.”

“I’ll say nothing.”

“That is essential.”

“Iss.”

He waited. She did not move to follow.

“Well?”

“I’ll let ’ee go.”

The words cost her more than the voyage.

“You said you would not return until I promised.”

“I lied once. I won’t force a promise out of ’ee. You kissed me because of the stars, maybe, or because you pitied me. Morning’s different.”

Samuel looked eastward. A pale seam had opened above Ruan hill; roofs and chimneys were separating from the darkness. In an hour Troy would wake, and with it would wake propriety, ambition, caution, and every argument against her.

He ought to accept the release.

Instead he said, “I am not certain that I wish to be released.”

Nance lifted her face.

“You’ll marry me?”

“I said nothing so definite.”

“You will.”

“You have an intolerable habit of deciding my answers.”

“Iss,” she said, and smiled.

Three months later they were married.

The congregation was indignant; but indignation, though nourishing, does not always secure unanimity. Deacon Prowse opposed the match, Miss Prowse developed a contempt for youthful preaching, and Grace Treloar observed that Delilah also had been strong-willed. Yet Samuel retained his pulpit. His sermons shortened; his chest broadened; and under Nance’s management the ministerial household spent less, gave more, and entertained without bankruptcy.

Troy never wholly forgave her origin, though after twenty years it began to boast of her character, and after forty forgot that it had ever objected. Ruan forgave Samuel sooner. He learned to swim, but not to sail; Nance declared one miracle in a marriage sufficient.

They lived together for fifty years. Whether the match was made in Heaven, upon the sea, or in the determined heart of Nance Trewartha, I shall not decide. It is certain only that Samuel, who went out one night to guide a dying stranger, returned before dawn having lost his command of the voyage and found the companion of his life.

And when at last they were carried together above the harbour, while Troy and Ruan followed in one procession, the young minister read that in their death they were not divided.

The old people heard him gravely.

A few remembered the boat.

# The Three Locks of Råbäck

In the autumn of 1897, when the house called Ashgrove, at Woolminster in Sussex, came into my possession, I found myself obliged to undertake alterations of a more extensive character than either the surveyor or I had anticipated. The eastern portion, which dated from about 1810, was sound enough; but an earlier building had been incorporated into it, and the timber of this nucleus—an inn, as I subsequently learned, once bearing the sign of the Crown and Feathers—had suffered equally from damp, beetle, and the injudicious removal of two supporting walls.

I mention these circumstances because they led to a discovery which, though without value to the historian, has afforded me a subject upon which I have spent more time than I can readily justify.

Behind the panelling of an upper room, enclosed in a narrow cavity beside the chimney, the workmen found a tin travelling-case. It was much corroded, and contained a quantity of papers, some tied with green tape, others thrust loosely into a leather portfolio. Most had belonged to a Mr Thomas Wraxall, who occupied the house for a brief period in 1863 and died there in circumstances not wholly forgotten in the village.

His papers comprised journals, memoranda, hotel accounts, railway tickets, copies of inscriptions, and notes intended, apparently, for a projected volume upon certain Swedish families of distinction. There were also letters addressed to him, none of later date than September 1863, and a pocket-book in which the last entries were made in pencil and with increasing irregularity.

I have arranged and abridged the documents. Where Wraxall gives evidence at first hand I have generally allowed him to speak for himself; where his notes are repetitive, illegible, or occupied with matters irrelevant to the case, I have summarized them. Swedish names have in two instances been altered, since the family concerned still exists; and the manor which Wraxall calls Råbäck shall retain that designation here, though I believe it to be fictitious. My purpose is not to connect a respected house with a narrative which may, after all, admit of some ordinary explanation.

Thomas Wraxall was born in 1818, educated at Winchester and Trinity College, Cambridge, and called to the Bar, though he appears never to have practised. He possessed a modest independence and contributed accounts of foreign travel to several periodicals. His book on the lesser châteaux of the Loire enjoyed some reputation; a later work upon the painted churches of Carinthia did not. He was unmarried, methodical in his arrangements, courteous in society, and addicted to solitary journeys. A Cambridge acquaintance describes him as “the most inquisitive man alive, not from vulgar prying, but because every locked door presented itself to him as an editorial omission.”

This defect, if defect it was, should be remembered.

Wraxall crossed from Hull to Gothenburg in June 1863. He intended to collect material for a study of Swedish noble houses under Christina and Charles XI, and had obtained letters to librarians, clergy, and landowners. By the middle of July he had reached Vestergothland, where he heard that at Råbäck there existed an unexamined collection of family papers relating to the De la Gardies.

The manor stood, according to his description, at the northern end of a long lake, about nine English miles from the nearest posting station. The road, after leaving the town, passed between oat-fields, entered a dark tract of fir and birch, and descended toward water of an unusual stillness. On the left rose the manor, a pale, many-windowed building with two square wings and a steep copper roof; on the right, separated from it by a meadow and a low stone wall, stood the parish church.

Behind both was the wood.

Wraxall’s first account of the place is in his accustomed travel-writing manner and contains little that need detain us. He praises the white water-lilies, the bell-shaped tower of the church, and the courtesy of his landlord, Herr Nielsen, who kept the inn at the southern edge of the demesne. His interest was quickly fixed upon the churchyard.

“The church,” he writes, “is small, consisting of nave, chancel, a wooden porch on the south, and a northern chapel built by the De la Gardie family in 1684. The porch door bears, in rude carving, a representation of the Last Judgement. Our Lord is enthroned above; beneath, three angels blow trumpets of a decidedly military pattern, while the dead issue from their coffins with expressions which, even allowing for the carver’s limited skill, cannot be termed hopeful. The devils are more competently executed. One in particular, at the lower left, smiles broadly as he draws a crowned figure by the ankle into a kettle.

“The northern chapel is not entered from the church. It has its own door, plated with iron, opening from the churchyard. Its roof is black copper, and its two windows, set high in the wall, are furnished with iron grilles. Nielsen tells me it contains the äldre grevarnas grafvar—the tombs of the older counts—but adds that no member of the household has entered it within his memory.

“This, I take it, is not quite accurate, since the key must be kept somewhere and the place cannot be wholly free from repair.”

That last sentence is characteristic.

Wraxall found the steward at Råbäck willing to admit him to the family archive. He was assigned a room in the western wing and allowed to work daily between nine and four. The collection consisted chiefly of account-books, leases, household inventories, and letters of no exceptional importance. A series of estate calendars, however, brought repeatedly before him the name of Count Magnus Gabriel De la Gardie—not the well-known statesman of that name, but a cousin, born, as Wraxall calculated, in 1606 and dead in 1679.

The count’s portrait hung in the great hall. Wraxall describes it with some care.

“The face is narrow and yellowish, the eyes pale, the moustache long and drooping. He wears black armour relieved by a crimson sash, and rests his right hand upon the head of a great dog. I say dog because the painter plainly intended an animal; yet the head is too small for a mastiff, the ears are hidden, and the forefoot visible at the count’s knee resembles, if anything, a naked human hand. The background contains a tower, a red evening sky, and three birds flying away. On the frame, in Latin:

“MAGNUS DE LA GARDIE, DOMINUS DE RÅBÄCK, QUI LONGE PEREGRINATUS EST ET DOMUM POTENTIOR REDIIT.

“‘Magnus De la Gardie, lord of Råbäck, who travelled far and returned home more powerful.’

“I asked the steward where the count had travelled. He replied, ‘In the East.’

“‘To Constantinople?’

“‘Farther.’

“‘To the Holy Land?’

“At this he looked toward the portrait and said, ‘Not to a holy place.’”

The account-books supported the tradition that Count Magnus had been feared rather than loved. Fines for trespass, wood-cutting, absence from labour, and failure to attend church were exacted with unusual severity. A cottager who allowed one of the count’s deerhounds to stray was imprisoned for five weeks. Two tenants accused of fishing in reserved waters disappeared from the rolls without the customary record of eviction or death. In 1647 the estate paid for rebuilding a cottage belonging to one Anders Björnsen, “the former habitation having been consumed.” A marginal note in another hand added: “He was within.”

There was also a payment for ironwork:

“To Master Olaf Sten, for three locks of uncommon strength, with chains, plates, and staples, for the lord count’s stone house after death.”

Wraxall copied this and placed beside it a question mark.

His inquiries were not well received. The steward admitted that there were stories, but called them old women’s talk. Herr Nielsen crossed himself, though a Lutheran, and changed the subject. The deacon, a young man who spoke some English, advised Wraxall to study Count Magnus’s contributions to agricultural improvement.

“Did he make any?” Wraxall asked.

“No,” said the deacon. “But it would be safer if he had.”

This reply seems to have encouraged rather than checked him.

On 23 July Wraxall found, among papers relating to the rebuilding of the manor chapel, a folio sheet written in Latin and Swedish. It had once been sewn into a book, and the left margin was torn away. His translation runs as follows:

“Journey undertaken by M. G. D. L. G., alone, in the year of grace 1635. From Råbäck to Lübeck, thence by ship, and through the imperial lands to Venice; thereafter to Smyrna and by routes not entered here. His declared purpose was to seek the city called Chorazin, upon which Our Lord pronounced a curse, and there to salute the Prince of the Air and receive one of those who wait in that wilderness.”

The final line, written more faintly, appeared to say:

“He returned at midsummer. Another returned with him.”

Wraxall knew, of course, the denunciation in the Gospel: *Woe unto thee, Chorazin*. He also knew the medieval tradition—more picturesque than authoritative—that in the ruins of that city certain spirits were confined until the end of the world. He searched the manor library and found in an imperfect sixteenth-century demonology a reference to a *peregrinatio nigra*, or Black Pilgrimage, made by those who desired knowledge of hidden things. The pilgrim must renounce baptism, walk a prescribed circuit among accursed places, and bring home a servant “whose loyalty is not love, but participation.”

The passage is underlined in brown ink. Against it someone had written the letter M.

At this point Wraxall’s journal ceases, for some pages, to be a scholarly record and becomes a series of questions:

“What exactly was brought back?

“Why three locks?

“Is ‘stone house after death’ the mausoleum?

“Who has the key?”

He obtained the key on the following day. How he did so is not altogether clear. He says that the old sexton, after much reluctance, allowed him to borrow it until sunset; but elsewhere there is a note implying that the key was left on a peg in the vestry and that no permission was sought. It was an object about seven inches long, with a complicated ward and a bow shaped like a crown.

The De la Gardie mausoleum, as Wraxall calls it, occupied the northern chapel. Its interior was twenty feet by fifteen and crowded with monuments. Light entered through the two high windows and fell chiefly upon the dust. There were four sarcophagi of grey stone, three mural tablets, two kneeling effigies in white marble, and a wooden hatchment so decayed that the arms could scarcely be distinguished. Iron coronae, intended for candles, hung from the vault.

Count Magnus’s sarcophagus stood apart at the eastern end. It was of black marble, severe in design, without recumbent figure or Christian emblem. Upon the lid lay a metal plate engraved with the count’s arms and the single word:

PRAESTOLATUR

Wraxall translates this as “He waits,” though “He awaits” is equally possible.

Three iron bands passed around the coffin, and each was secured by a padlock. The locks were large, black with age, and unlike one another. One bore a cross; one a seven-pointed star; the third had no device.

On the wall above were two engraved panels. The first represented a forest road. A man ran among the trees, looking backward, while a figure in a cloak pursued him. Between them moved something lower to the ground, covered by a hood. The running man’s mouth was open. The cloaked pursuer’s mouth was also open, but, as Wraxall remarks, “in a manner suggestive not of exertion but of amusement.”

The second panel showed a chamber with barred windows. A corpse lay upon the floor. A tall man stood in the doorway, and beside him crouched the hooded thing. From beneath its covering projected an appendage, long and pale, divided at the end into several narrow points.

Wraxall made a rubbing of both plates.

He then examined the locks. They appeared firm. He did not touch them, or says that he did not; yet his entry concludes:

“I spoke aloud—an absurd habit, but solitude encourages it—and said: ‘Well, Count Magnus, you are protected from your admirers. I should have liked to meet you.’

“There was a movement overhead, probably a bird upon the copper roof.”

That evening he questioned Herr Nielsen concerning the count. The conversation is reported in detail, and I condense only the opening.

Nielsen denied that Magnus had practised magic. Wraxall produced his copy of the Black Pilgrimage memorandum. Nielsen refused to read it. Wraxall then asked whether the count had been accompanied by a servant after returning from the East.

“A little man?” said Nielsen.

“I did not say little.”

“No. You did not.”

“Then there was such a servant?”

“There was no man.”

“What was there?”

Nielsen looked for some time at the stove, though the night was warm. At length he said:

“My grandfather’s grandfather told of two who came to the count’s gate after midnight. One was the count. The other wore a grey covering from its head to the ground. It was no higher than a child of eight years. The dogs howled, and afterward they would not enter the courtyard.

“But such things are foolishness.”

“Did this servant have a name?”

“The count called it *min följeslagare*—my companion.”

“And what did the country people call it?”

Nielsen did not answer.

Wraxall persisted. The innkeeper finally said, “They called it the one that laughs.”

There follows, in the journal, an account which Nielsen claimed to have received from his grandmother. Its date would have been about 1650.

A landowner named Hans Thorbjörn, whose farm bordered the Råbäck demesne, brought an action against Count Magnus over the diversion of a stream. The court at Skara found for Thorbjörn. The count said nothing publicly, but told a servant that no man took water from him twice.

Some weeks later Thorbjörn visited a neighbour and remained late. The shortest path home crossed the edge of Råbäck wood. His friends urged him to take the high road, but he laughed and set out at eleven.

Not half an hour later, the household heard him screaming.

The farmer and two servants carried lanterns to the road. The screams moved among the trees, now near, now far off, though always approaching the stream. Between the cries came another sound.

“What sort of sound?” Wraxall asked.

Nielsen replied: “Laughing.”

“Human laughter?”

“No.”

“How, then, could your grandmother say it was laughter?”

“Because she was human.”

The searchers reached a clearing. There they saw Thorbjörn run across the moonlight. Behind him walked a tall man in a broad hat and long cloak. He did not hurry. Beside him went a short thing covered with a hood. When Thorbjörn stumbled, the little one sprang forward, and the lanterns went out.

The searchers fled.

At dawn Thorbjörn was found against an oak. He was alive for several minutes but could not speak. The skin of his face had been drawn downward so that, in Nielsen’s phrase, “it hung like wet cloth.” There were marks upon the tree above his head, five parallel furrows cut deeply into the wood.

The body was buried without being shown.

Wraxall notes that the story has recognizable folktale elements and cannot be accepted as evidence. He nevertheless records a second tradition. Two brothers employed in Count Magnus’s stables were suspected of theft and confined overnight in a granary. In the morning one was dead and the other mad. The survivor could say only that a small person had entered beneath the door.

That night Wraxall dreamed of the mausoleum.

He says he stood in the chapel while something knocked from within the black sarcophagus. The knocks came in groups of three. After the third group, a voice said—not from the coffin but immediately behind him—“You wished to meet me.”

He woke at two and found the window open. The wind had scattered his rubbing of the engraved panels across the floor.

On 25 July he returned to the mausoleum. His stated purpose was to verify the wording of the inscription, though he had already copied it twice. The day was overcast, and the chapel darker than before. He noticed at once that one of the three padlocks had opened. It was the lock bearing the cross. It hung from its staple, and the iron band was loose upon the floor.

Wraxall examined it. Neither shackle nor body was broken. Pressure upon the mechanism did not close it, nor could he discover any rust or defect sufficient to account for its release.

He fetched the sexton, who became violently agitated and refused to enter. The old man looked from the threshold and said in Swedish, “One word, one lock.”

Wraxall asked what this meant.

The sexton replied, “You have spoken to him.”

“I remarked upon the tomb. Nothing more.”

“How many times?”

Wraxall could remember only his words of the first visit. He dismissed the incident as a failure of an ancient mechanism, though his journal betrays less confidence than his conversation can have done.

The next several days were occupied by research at the manor. Here he found further references to the count’s cruelties, but nothing conclusive regarding the pilgrimage. A private calendar recorded his return in June 1636—one year later than the loose memorandum—and added, “His lordship will no longer receive the sacrament.” Another entry, dated 1659, noted payment to “two men who cleansed the north room after the judgement upon the poacher.” The sum included replacement of plaster and burning of bedclothes.

Wraxall’s notes grew increasingly untidy. Questions were written in the margins, crossed out, and repeated. Twice he found himself on the path to the churchyard without recalling why he had left the manor. On the second occasion he was carrying the mausoleum key.

“I had been working,” he says, “upon an inventory of plate, and the next thing I knew I was under the lych-gate. I cannot account for the interval. There was no faintness, nor had I taken wine at luncheon. The key was in my left hand.

“It would be absurd to attach importance to this. Preoccupation is sufficient explanation.”

He entered the mausoleum.

The second lock, that marked with the star, remained closed; but it was warm to the touch. Wraxall stood beside the coffin for some minutes, experiencing, as he puts it, “a sensation of expectation not wholly my own.” He then said aloud:

“Count Magnus, if any account of your journey exists, I wish you would show it to me.”

A faint metallic click came from the coffin.

He left at once. Outside, he found the sexton’s young granddaughter watching him from behind a gravestone. When he approached, she ran away.

That evening the second padlock was found open.

The sexton would not touch it. The deacon came, accompanied by the steward, and both inspected the coffin from the entrance. The steward proposed that the lock be replaced. No one volunteered to do it. Eventually the chapel was shut, the key placed in a drawer in the vestry, and the churchwardens summoned to consider the matter.

Wraxall was asked to return his own key, which he had apparently retained under a misunderstanding. He did so, but not before making an impression of it in sealing wax.

This detail, included without apology in his journal, indicates the degree to which his curiosity had overcome ordinary scruple.

During the following week he sent to Gothenburg for a locksmith and meanwhile continued his investigations. In the manor library he discovered a letter from the chaplain who attended Count Magnus in his last illness. It was addressed to the bishop and had never been sent.

The chaplain wrote that the count did not fear death but gave particular instructions for his burial. The body was to be placed in a stone coffin before sunset. Three locks were to be secured, each by a different man, and the keys cast separately into deep water. No prayer was to be spoken after the lid was closed. No one was to address the dead by name within the chapel.

The final paragraph was damaged, but enough remained:

“For he says that invitation is stronger than iron, and that if any living man shall desire his company freely and thrice declare the same, then no bond laid by priest or smith shall avail. I believe his mind disordered. Yet last night, when I watched at the farther door, I heard a child laughing in the corridor, though no child is kept in the house.”

Wraxall copied the letter at full length.

He did not leave Råbäck.

It may seem extraordinary that an educated man, possessing both the warning and the means of escape, should remain. Yet the papers make his motives tolerably plain. He did not fully believe the danger, and he believed still less that he could abandon an inquiry at the moment when it promised an answer. Fear and fascination, far from cancelling one another, had become allied. He wanted to disprove the legend; he also wanted to see what happened next.

His final visit occurred on 4 August.

The locksmith had not arrived. Rain fell throughout the day, and Wraxall worked until the servants came to close the archive. He dined alone at the inn. At half past eight Nielsen heard him go upstairs; at nine he came down wearing his travelling coat and asked whether the churchyard gate was locked.

Nielsen said that it was not.

“Then I shall take a turn,” said Wraxall.

“In this weather?”

“I have left a memorandum at the manor.”

The manor lay in the opposite direction.

Wraxall’s own account begins again after this conversation.

“I had no intention of visiting the chapel. I must insist upon that. Yet I found myself at the north door, and in my pocket was the duplicate key made from the wax impression. I unlocked the door.

“The rain upon the copper roof was loud enough to conceal other small noises. My candle burned badly. The first two locks lay open beside the coffin. The third remained in place.

“I approached to within an arm’s length. I observed that the lid was not fitted as closely as I had supposed. There was a black line along its western edge, perhaps one eighth of an inch wide.

“I said, ‘Count Magnus, I have come a long way to learn your history.’

“This was not, I think, an invitation.

“The candle sank suddenly.

“I then heard a sound resembling a sigh from within the coffin. I cannot be positive. Rain, wind, and the resonance of the vault may produce curious effects.

“I said, ‘I would give a great deal to see you, Count Magnus.’

“The third lock fell upon the pavement.”

Here the handwriting changes. The remainder is in pencil:

“The band moved. Not fell—was drawn aside.

“Lid rising at foot.

“Something underneath, darker than chapel.

“Smell of shut earth and burnt hair.

“Behind me—laughing.”

Wraxall remembered nothing between that moment and his arrival at the inn. Nielsen found him beating upon the door shortly before ten, soaked through, without hat or candle. He had lost the chapel key. His left sleeve was torn from shoulder to wrist, and on the skin beneath were five narrow red lines, though he denied having caught his arm upon a branch.

He demanded a carriage. None could be obtained until morning. He therefore spent the night in the common room, refusing to go upstairs and insisting that every door and shutter remain fastened.

At dawn he left for the posting station. He abandoned two trunks, taking only his papers and a small valise. The landlord’s account, receipted in full, has survived.

The next portion of the case must be reconstructed from the pocket-book.

Wraxall travelled by carriage to the railway and took the afternoon train toward Gothenburg. At the first station after Skara, while looking from his compartment, he saw two persons upon the platform: a tall man in a dark cloak and broad, old-fashioned hat, and a second figure, very short, wrapped in a grey hood.

He had seen them before.

He does not say when. The sentence is underlined twice.

At Falköping the same persons stood beyond the ticket barrier. They did not board the train in his sight. Nevertheless, as the train moved away, he noticed the short figure looking from the last carriage.

The hood concealed its face.

Wraxall reached Gothenburg on 7 August and secured passage upon a steamer sailing the next evening. His hotel register shows that he changed rooms twice, first because the window overlooked an alley, and again because a connecting door could not be locked. A servant reported that he asked repeatedly whether any “foreign nobleman with an attendant” had arrived.

On boarding the steamer he counted twenty-eight persons ahead of him on the gangway. At dinner, however, the purser’s list contained only twenty-six passengers, and twenty-six appeared at table.

Wraxall counted them three times.

“Two absent,” he wrote. “No cabins assigned? Purser says impossible. Tall one stood near forward hatch. Little one behind coils of rope. No one else appeared to notice.”

The weather in the Kattegat was calm. Shortly after midnight Wraxall heard a scraping outside his cabin. It travelled slowly along the door, paused at the latch, and descended almost to the floor. He placed a trunk against the door and remained awake until daylight.

In the morning there were five scratches upon the paint.

A fellow-passenger, Dr Whitcombe of York, later recalled meeting Wraxall on deck. He seemed ill and asked whether Whitcombe believed that “a legal invitation could be withdrawn.” Receiving an answer concerning contracts, Wraxall laughed and said the matter was not a legal one.

He then asked whether a person pursued by enemies abroad would be safe after crossing into England.

Whitcombe replied that English law afforded protection.

“Yes,” said Wraxall, “but what of English soil?”

At Hull, Wraxall again saw the two figures. His notes become brief:

“Twenty-six landed. I counted twenty-eight on quay.

“They pass customs without speaking.

“No luggage.

“The tall one’s face pale—perhaps only beard. Hat entirely wrong for present fashion.

“The other leaves no sound when walking.”

He travelled to London, where he stayed for three nights at a hotel in Jermyn Street. Two letters written there survive. One is to his solicitor and concerns ordinary financial matters. The second, addressed but apparently never sent to the Swedish deacon, reads:

“I fear that in my levity I spoke three times, though I can positively recollect only two expressions that might be interpreted as a wish. The distinction may not be material. Is there any formula of dismissal? Search the chaplain’s papers. Above all, learn whether the coffin was found open and whether anything remained within.”

No reply could have reached him, for he left London the same day.

He chose Woolminster because, as he wrote, it was “small, inland, unconnected with my family, and served by no railway.” The Crown and Feathers stood at the edge of the village, with fields on three sides and a lane in front. Wraxall engaged the upper parlour and adjoining bedchamber under the name of Wilson. He asked the landlord, Samuel Perrott, whether the house possessed a back staircase. On learning that it did, he had its door nailed shut.

The recollections of Woolminster were collected by me in 1898, when three persons still lived who remembered the event. The clearest account came from Mrs Alice Pullen, then eighty-six, who in 1863 had been a maid at the inn. I reproduce part of her statement, preserving something of her manner of speech.

“He were a civil gentleman, but mortal frightened. Never out after sundown, and allus looking down the lane. He’d have me count the folk in the taproom, and when I said eleven he’d say, ‘Count again, Alice.’ So I counted, and there was eleven.

“On the second evening he asked if any strangers had come. I told him no, only Mr Gooden the carrier and two drovers from Hailsham. He said, ‘No foreign gentleman? No child?’

“I said we’d had no children in the house.

“Then he caught hold of my wrist and said, ‘It is not a child.’

“I didn’t like that. His hand was cold as a bit of cellar stone.”

On the third day a boy reported seeing two strangers upon the church road: a very tall gentleman dressed in black and a little person beside him. The little one wore a hood despite the heat.

Perrott went out to offer them lodging, but found no one.

At dusk Wraxall saw the figures from his window. He came downstairs carrying a chair, wedged it beneath the front-door latch, and ordered every person in the inn to remain inside. Perrott objected that customers would be arriving. Wraxall offered him ten pounds to close the house for the night.

They compromised at five.

There were seven persons within: Wraxall, Perrott and his wife, Alice Pullen, a groom, and two farm labourers who had been drinking in the taproom. Wraxall counted them. He then asked each to sit where he could see him.

At ten, footsteps approached along the lane.

They were slow and regular. Those present heard them stop before the house.

A second sound followed: a light pattering close to the wall.

No knock came.

After several minutes Wraxall relaxed sufficiently to drink brandy. He told Perrott that if anyone asked for him by name, the landlord must deny that he was there.

At half past ten a voice outside said, very distinctly:

“Mr Wraxall.”

Mrs Perrott began to cry.

The voice did not repeat the name. Instead, something moved beneath the parlour window. Alice Pullen, who was nearest, later said that she saw the top of a grey hood rise level with the sill, though the sill stood six feet from the ground. There was no face within it—only, as she expressed herself, “a sort of dull wetness.”

The candles went out.

In the confusion Wraxall ran upstairs. This was the worst action he could have taken, for it separated him from the others and placed him in a room from which there was no exit except the door and window. Perrott followed, but before he reached the landing the upper parlour door slammed.

He heard Wraxall turn the key.

For perhaps a minute there was silence.

Then Wraxall began moving furniture. A table was dragged across the floor and thrust against the door. The bedstead followed. Perrott called to him, but received no answer.

From outside the inn came three heavy knocks.

Not upon the door. They seemed to strike the wall of the upper room.

After the third, the window glass broke.

Wraxall screamed.

The witnesses agreed upon the character of that scream, but their descriptions add little. It continued longer than a single breath should have permitted and ended in a bubbling sound. During it another sound was audible—higher, quick, and interrupted by little gasps.

Alice Pullen called it laughter.

Perrott and the groom forced the door at dawn. They had not attempted it sooner. No blame should, I think, attach to them.

The room was in disorder, but the barricade remained against the door. The window was shattered inward, although the outer wall showed no marks of ladder or climbing. Wraxall’s travelling-case lay open, and his papers had been thrust into the cavity beside the chimney, from which they were recovered thirty-four years later.

As to the body, I shall be brief.

The coroner’s inquest was held that afternoon. Of the twelve jurors, two fainted upon entering the room; one was sick; and four refused to look a second time. The surgeon, Mr Harcourt, gave evidence for less than five minutes and afterward destroyed, or said that he destroyed, his written notes.

The verdict was “Death by the Visitation of God.”

It was observed, though not entered in the formal record, that the dead man’s face had been displaced in a manner no fall, blade, or animal known in Sussex could satisfactorily explain. There were five deep marks upon the wall above him. They began nearly eight feet from the floor.

No money was stolen.

No stranger was traced.

Wraxall was buried under his assumed name. His solicitor later identified him, and a small stone was added bearing his proper initials. I visited the grave in 1898. The inscription was weathered, but legible. Someone had scratched beneath it, with a nail or knife, a single Latin word:

PRAESTOLATUR.

I do not know when this was done.

There remains one further document. In 1901 I received, through the assistance of a Swedish antiquary, a copy of an entry in the Råbäck parish register dated 6 August 1863. It states that the northern burial chapel was opened by the churchwardens after complaints from the sexton. Count Magnus’s sarcophagus was found with all three locks unfastened and the lid displaced.

The coffin was empty.

This statement is followed by a later annotation, in another ink, declaring that the remains had probably been removed during repairs in the eighteenth century. No record of such removal has been found. The explanation is reasonable, but not complete.

When Ashgrove was demolished, I instructed the workmen to preserve the panelling of Wraxall’s room. On the inner face of one board, invisible until the wall was opened, were five long scratches. They had been made from within the sealed cavity.

The foreman attributed them to rats.

I accepted his opinion, and the board was burned.

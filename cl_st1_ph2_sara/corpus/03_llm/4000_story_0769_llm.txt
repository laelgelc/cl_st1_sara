### What the Silence Kept

The letter announcing my aunt’s arrival was written by my uncle in an ink so diluted that several words had faded into the bluish paper, as though the Nebraska sun, reaching eastward across fifteen hundred miles of winter country, had already begun to bleach them. Miriam would come on Thursday, he said, upon a matter connected with the estate of her mother’s cousin; the property was inconsiderable, but signatures were necessary, and Miriam, being the only surviving claimant, must appear before an attorney in Boston. He added that she had not been well since corn husking, and asked me to see that she did not mislay her return ticket.

I read the letter twice at my breakfast table, between the coffee urn and a dish of oranges, while sleet tapped delicately at the windows and wheels hissed along the wet pavement below. Then the room, with its books, its framed prints, its polished brass, withdrew from me. I saw instead a low house crouching under a dun sky; cattle tracks frozen into the dooryard; a windmill gesturing frantically above the sheds; dishcloths stiff as bark upon a line; and my aunt, carrying two pails through driven snow, her skirt bound about her knees with a strip of sacking.

It had been nearly six years since I had seen her, and twenty since, as a boy of sixteen, I had left my uncle’s farm. Yet the news of her coming disturbed me beyond what affection alone might have accounted for. It was as if some old debt, long entered in a forgotten ledger, had suddenly been presented for payment.

Before she became my uncle’s wife, Miriam Ashford had taught music in Boston. I possessed no reliable image of that young woman, though from a daguerreotype, a few remarks of my mother’s, and certain relics surviving in Nebraska, I had constructed one: a straight, eager girl in a dark silk dress; abundant hair arranged in smooth wings above her temples; fine hands resting upon a square piano; an expression at once ardent and severe. She had studied in Leipzig for two years and had returned with notions too ambitious for the modest female academy that employed her. She played Bach at recitals, read German poetry, and could distinguish, so my mother told me, every instrument in an orchestral score.

Then, at thirty, she met my uncle Caleb, who had come East to purchase agricultural machinery. He was handsome, reticent, and possessed of that western gravity which in a drawing room may be mistaken for depth. Within four months they were married. Within six, she had followed him to a homestead in a county where the railroad had not yet arrived, where timber was scarce, water uncertain, and the nearest piano stood eighty miles away in the parlor of a hotel.

What she had expected of the frontier I cannot imagine. Perhaps she saw herself as some domestic Orpheus, bringing order and song into a raw world; perhaps love simply placed a veil over distances which, once traveled, could never be traveled back. She exchanged her students for hens, her concert programs for seed catalogues, her black silk for calico faded by lye and weather. She bore six children, buried two, survived drought, hail, blizzards, prairie fire, and a grasshopper year during which the insects ate even the handles of the hoes. By the time I came into her keeping, whatever rebellion had once disturbed her was already deep underground.

I was nine when my parents died, and my uncle took me west. For seven years his farm was the circumference of my existence. I learned to break clods, carry swill, turn the grindstone, search for calves in ravines, and sleep through the percussion of sleet upon the roof. It was my aunt who taught me everything not required by necessity. At night, after the bread had been set to rise, the milk strained, the lamp chimneys cleaned, and the last shirt removed from the ironing board, she heard my lessons at the kitchen table. From her I received my first Latin, my first glimpses of ancient history, my first confused apprehension that mankind had lived richly and disastrously before Nebraska was surveyed.

She had brought one instrument west, a small rosewood melodeon whose bellows wheezed and whose ivory keys had yellowed like old teeth. During my earliest years she sometimes played hymns upon it after supper. Later, when work increased and the instrument began to fail, it stood closed in the parlor beneath seed sacks, newspapers, harness buckles, and once, through an entire autumn, a kit of mackerel. Still, she taught me the notes from a torn primer, drawing staves with a carpenter’s pencil upon wrapping paper. Her swollen fingers, split at the knuckles from cold and washing soda, moved over the table while she made me beat time.

“Not like a flail, Julian,” she would say. “Listen inwardly first.”

What could inward listening mean to a child who knew music chiefly as wind worrying the stovepipe, rain upon sod, the quarrel of coyotes, and the long, level roar of corn in an August gale? Yet somehow she made me understand that sound might contain proportion, memory, even mercy. When at last I escaped—as I then thought of it—to school in Lincoln, and afterward to the university and the East, I carried with me more of her defeated instruction than I knew.

On Thursday morning I went to the station early. The western train arrived under a roof of smoke, emitting passengers in successive gusts: commercial men with leather cases, women shepherding children, soldiers, immigrants, old people stunned by travel. I failed at first to recognize my aunt. She had descended before I saw her and stood beside a pillar, guarding a battered telescope valise with one foot.

She appeared smaller than I remembered, though perhaps it was only that the immense country behind her had once enlarged her in my childish sight. She wore a man’s brown overcoat, too broad in the shoulders and too short at the wrists, over a purple dress trimmed with black braid. Her bonnet, crushed by two nights in the train, was secured beneath her chin by green ribbons of a shade ordinarily found in cheap confectionery. One black cotton glove covered her left hand; the right was bare and grasped a rolled newspaper, a lunch basket, and several claim checks.

Her face shocked me. It had been seamed and darkened until the skin resembled leather repeatedly soaked and dried. The cheeks had fallen in; the upper lip was drawn and colorless; one eyelid drooped from an inflammation contracted, she later explained, while nursing a sick calf in a storm. Yet her pale gray eyes remained clear, and when they found me there passed through them a light so swift and youthful that for an instant I saw the musician of the daguerreotype imprisoned within this weather-beaten figure.

“Julian,” she said, and kissed me shyly, smelling of wool, wood smoke, and the apples she had carried in her basket.

I took her parcels and made some foolish remark about her having brought provisions enough for an Arctic expedition.

“We were delayed once near Creston,” she answered seriously. “And Caleb thought there mightn’t be meals on the train.”

In the cab she looked anxiously at the traffic, not with pleasure or curiosity, but with the strained attention of one trying to remember something omitted.

“I left instructions about the red cow,” she said. “She’s to be kept from the west lot. The wire’s down by the creek.”

“No doubt Uncle Caleb will attend to it.”

“He might not notice. And Ruth forgets the salt. The young calves scour if they get too much skim milk.” After a pause she touched my sleeve. “I wrote it on the flour bin.”

Her body had reached Boston, but the governing part of her still moved through sheds and pens, counting eggs, measuring meal, testing the wind. Even in my comfortable rooms she could not at first be persuaded to remove the overcoat. She sat on the edge of a chair, her valise at her feet, and watched the fire as if it were burning wood needed elsewhere. When my landlady brought tea, my aunt gathered the crumbs carefully into her palm and asked whether the leaves might be dried and used again.

The legal business occupied Friday morning. By noon the inheritance, after fees, amounted to less than the price of her journey. She accepted this without complaint. On our way home we passed a bill announcing a Saturday afternoon concert devoted to Wagner. The name struck me with the force of a summons. I remembered a rain-soaked evening in Nebraska when, finding an old newspaper wrapped around lamp wicks, she had smoothed it and read aloud a notice of a performance of *Tannhäuser*. She had stopped midway, carried the paper to the window, and stood there long after the light was gone.

I bought two seats.

When I told her, she looked first embarrassed, then alarmed.

“I’ve nothing suitable, Julian.”

“You have yourself.”

She glanced down at the purple skirt, upon which years of alterations had left a geography of seams.

“Folks here dress different.”

The truth of this had already troubled me. I had observed, with a shameful exactness, the white thread at her cuff, the stain upon her bonnet ribbon, the heavy farm shoes that made her tread unevenly. I imagined the glances of women in velvet and men accustomed to judging by surfaces; and although I despised them in advance, I was not innocent of their judgment. Gratitude and vanity contended in me, and vanity, even defeated, left its stain.

On Saturday she permitted my landlady to press her dress and lend her a plain black mantle. At one o’clock we drove to the concert hall through a clear, cold brilliance. Snow lay in the Common; the elms lifted dark branches against a porcelain sky; sunlight flashed upon windows and carriage lamps. My aunt sat rigidly upright, her gloved hands crossed over a small beaded bag. Once, passing a row of brick houses on Beacon Street, she turned so quickly that the green ribbons trembled.

“I used to know someone there,” she said.

“Shall we stop afterward?”

“No. She’d be dead.”

The hall was already filling when we arrived. Warmth, perfume, damp fur, the subdued rustle of programs, the gleam of varnished wood and brass—all that civilized abundance which I accepted as the natural setting of an afternoon—seemed to close around my aunt with an almost physical pressure. She moved beside me clumsily, turning her shoulders as she did when passing between barrels in the cellar. A young woman in pale blue looked at her bonnet and whispered to her companion. I felt my aunt’s hand tighten on my arm, though whether from noticing the insult or merely from fear of the steps, I could not determine.

Our places were near the front. The stage, crowded with chairs, desks, music stands, and instruments, appeared to her, I thought, less like a sanctuary than a complicated machine whose purpose she had forgotten. She stared at the kettledrums, the harps, the grouped violins, the golden mouths of the horns; but when I named them, she gave no sign of interest. She unfolded her program, examined it upside down, then laid it in her lap.

Could thirty years erase a language once spoken fluently? Did those instruments remain for her living voices, or had they become no more significant than the parts of a reaper displayed to a townsman? I had imagined that her entrance into the hall would be a return, a miraculous recovery of territory; now I feared I had brought her only to the boundary of a country from which she had been exiled too long.

The players entered. Their tuning rose about us—fragments, questions, bright metallic assertions, thin cries of the reeds, the deep throat-clearing of the basses. To me that disorder had always promised delight. To my aunt it seemed painful. Her mouth contracted. Her right hand closed over the left, hiding the great joints and broad, flattened nails.

Then the conductor lifted his baton.

The first sustained chord of the prelude was so quiet that it seemed not produced but uncovered, as one uncovers beneath dust the edge of a buried inscription. My aunt did not move. The melody gathered itself from the strings, advanced, retreated, and returned with other voices clinging to it; the brass entered solemnly, distant at first, like standards seen beyond mist. Still she sat with her eyes fixed upon the stage, her face vacant in its attention.

I watched her more closely than I watched the orchestra. A pulse appeared in her temple. Her lips parted. When the violins rose, her fingers began to move upon her skirt—not beating time, not quite shaping notes, but traveling with an old mechanical certainty, as though beneath the purple cloth lay the keyboard of the lost melodeon, or another keyboard, brilliant and responsive, in a room she had inhabited before any of us knew her.

Recognition came to her not as joy but as a wound.

She bent forward. The green ribbons hung loose against her cheeks. At the first great outpouring of the brass, she drew a breath that shook her whole frame, and I felt, rather than saw, the tears begin. They slipped down without distortion of her features, silently, continuously, like water overflowing a vessel left beneath steady rain.

I dared not speak.

Where had the music taken her? To Leipzig, to some student lodging smelling of coal smoke and wet stone; to a recital room in Boston where young women turned pages under gaslight; to the evening when my uncle first stood awkwardly beside her piano? Or was it carrying her not backward but outward, into that immeasurable realm of unrealized life which opens before us only when it is too late to enter? Her face told me nothing I could translate. It registered neither one memory nor one grief, but the pressure of too many.

For my own part, the orchestra only sharpened Nebraska. In the surging strings I saw wheat laid flat beneath a storm; in the horns, the copper west after sunset; beneath the tread of the basses, wagons moved through spring mud. I saw my aunt at the churn, at the wash boiler, at the sickbed; lifting bread from the oven with burned wrists, patching overalls by a lamp that smoked, walking at midnight to the barn while sleet cut her face. Those hands now trembling beside me had washed my hair, bound my sprained ankle, guided my blunt pencil through Latin declensions, and placed my fingers upon the yellow keys. They had made a road for me with labor so constant that I had mistaken it for the ordinary earth.

Between the selections she wiped her face with a handkerchief, folded it into a smaller square, and asked in a whisper, “Do you hear this often?”

“Not this program. But concerts, yes.”

“How often?”

“In the season—perhaps every week.”

She looked at me then. There was no accusation in her eyes; that would have been easier to bear. There was only wonder, and behind it the terrible arithmetic by which she measured my years of abundance against her own starvation.

“Ever since you came back East?”

“Nearly.”

She turned away before the applause ended.

The next music began with tumult. The orchestra became wind and black water, a vessel laboring under a torn heaven; waves rose in the lower strings, the brass flung out signals, and the violins drove forward like rain. My aunt’s hand seized mine. Her grip, hardened by ax handles, pails, and the wringing of heavy linen, was painfully strong.

Once, long ago, she had told me the tale of the wandering mariner condemned to seek a harbor he could never keep. She had been rolling pastry at the time, and flour whitened her arms to the elbow. I had thought it only another legend, akin to Ulysses and Perseus, one of the stories with which she illuminated winter evenings. Now I wondered whether she had understood it even then as I could not: the curse not merely of wandering, but of seeing rest, beauty, home, and being compelled to pass them by.

Her tears continued. She made no effort now to conceal them. The woman in pale blue, seated across the aisle, looked over once and then lowered her gaze with an altered expression.

During the intermission people rose, talked, and displayed themselves. My aunt remained seated. Around her flowed silk, broadcloth, laughter, greetings, the hundred easy gestures of those accustomed to leisure. She appeared among them like a figure dug from an ancient field, scarred by tools and weather, its original features barely discernible, yet possessing through mutilation a gravity the unbroken statues lack. She asked no questions about the performers. Instead she said, “Caleb will be feeding by now.”

“Yes.”

“It’ll be dark early.”

“Yes, Aunt Miriam.”

“The stove smokes when the wind’s north.”

Her words fell one by one into the brilliant hall: feed, darkness, smoke. They were not irrelevancies, as I first supposed, but the terms of the sentence awaiting her.

The final selection was quieter. A single theme passed through the orchestra and was given back transformed; it moved from instrument to instrument as light moves across separate windows at sunset. Under its influence my aunt seemed to lose the rigidity that had sustained her. Her shoulders sank. She leaned toward the stage with an expression almost hungry.

The music rose, not violently, but with the immense assurance of something released from mortal limitation. It filled every hollow of the hall, touched the gilded balconies, trembled in the chandeliers, and entered, I believed, those sealed chambers within her where for thirty years no voice had been permitted. What dormant growths stirred there? Music may lie long in a human being as moss lies gray and brittle upon a stone, indistinguishable from death until rain discovers its color. Yet is revival always mercy? To awaken an appetite where no food can be given, to restore sight at the door of darkness—what kindness is that?

At the last chord she lifted both hands slightly, then let them fall.

Applause broke over us. The conductor returned, bowed, departed, returned again. Chairs scraped; programs fluttered; the audience began to pour into the aisles. I stood and held out her mantle.

She did not see it.

“Aunt Miriam, we must go before the crush.”

She sat bent forward, staring at the emptying stage. Musicians were closing their cases. A man carried away the conductor’s desk. The harps, stripped of human hands, stood remote and shining.

I touched her shoulder.

She caught my wrist with both hands. Her face, which had endured births, graves, winters, hunger, and all the blunt injuries of necessity, broke at last.

“No,” she sobbed. “Please, Julian. I don’t want to go.”

The words were scarcely louder than a child’s. Yet I knew that it was not the cold street she feared, nor my rooms, nor even the westbound train waiting two days hence. To go was to return to the station platform under the iron roof; to the long passage across Ohio, Illinois, Iowa; to the narrowing towns and widening spaces; to the branch line where the cars smelled of coal dust and livestock; to Caleb waiting in a buffalo coat beside the wagon. It was to return to the fallen wire, the red cow, the scouring calves, the smoking stove, the bread sponge, the churn, the endless shirts. It was to descend again beneath the silence that had covered her, now knowing fully what the silence concealed.

Most of the audience had gone. Programs lay among the vacant seats like pale leaves after a festival. On the stage a workman extinguished lights one by one, and each darkened globe seemed to remove some coast from the world.

I sat down beside her.

Beyond the high doors, carriages rattled, newsboys called, and the city continued in its wealth of sound. But before us the stage widened and dimmed until it became, to my guilty imagination, the Nebraska plain under evening: mile after mile of colorless grass, the cattle tracks frozen hard, the windmill turning above the sheds, and one low farmhouse already waiting for her beneath the enormous, unanswering sky.

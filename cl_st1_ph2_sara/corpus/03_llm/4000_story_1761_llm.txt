It was Rulledge who asked whether any of us had ever had a presentiment which afterward justified itself; and he asked it at an hour when presentiments, if they have the civility of other social phenomena, ought to be in bed.

We had remained late in Minver’s rooms after a dinner which he had given less from hospitality, as he said, than from a wish to determine whether Rulledge’s appetite was a moral defect or merely a physical misfortune. The experiment had proved inconclusive. Rulledge had eaten everything, including, by a confusion of plates which he afterward maintained was premeditated, a small omelet intended for Wanhope; and he was now stretched in the largest of Minver’s bamboo chairs, with that expression of spiritual vacancy which in him commonly followed material abundance.

The fire had sunk to a red cavern under the ashes; beyond the Venetian blinds the city, muffled in a wet March snow, made itself heard from time to time by the slipping hoofs of a cab-horse or the melancholy clangor of a street-car bell. Wanhope had been speaking—professionally, but not offensively so—of certain nervous impressions which precede illness; and Rulledge, who could never let psychology alone until he had degraded it into psychics, seized upon the word “precede.”

“But that is just it,” he said. “Why should not the mind precede an event as well as the nerves precede a fever?”

“Because,” Minver answered, “the event may be going in another direction.”

“You know what I mean. A warning—a distinct sense that something is going to happen.”

“Something is always going to happen. The universe owes most of its reputation for veracity to that circumstance.”

Rulledge turned from him with the wounded patience of one long persecuted for his openness to higher things. “Wanhope understands me.”

“I understand the terms of your question,” Wanhope said. “That is not necessarily the same thing.”

“Then put it in your own terms.”

Wanhope joined the tips of his fingers and looked into the fire. He had the faculty of imparting scientific dignity to attitudes which in another man would have seemed only comfortable. “A presentiment may be regarded as an emotional state for which consciousness has not yet supplied an adequate cause. The cause may afterward be found in an event, and then the emotion appears prophetic; or the event may merely select itself from a multitude of occurrences because it fits the emotion. We remember the successful coincidences and forget the failures.”

“I don’t forget mine,” Rulledge said.

“No,” Minver assented. “Your failures have the advantage of occurring three times a day, at regular meals.”

Rulledge refused the diversion. “Suppose a man knows that a particular calamity is to happen?”

“That would no longer be a presentiment,” Wanhope returned. “It would be knowledge, or delusion.”

“Or both,” Minver suggested. “The categories are not mutually exclusive in Rulledge.”

I had noticed that during this Minver had ceased to sketch with a charred match upon the back of an envelope. He had made, apparently without intending it, the profile of a woman, and then had crossed it with two impatient lines. His habitual irony was so constant that one learned to distinguish its degrees; and I thought the ridicule with which he met Rulledge’s insistence had become defensive.

“You have had one,” I said to him.

He glanced at me. “One what? Omelet? Never. I respect my friends’ property.”

“A presentiment.”

“My dear fellow, I have had a thousand. I have one now that if we encourage Rulledge he will tell us his.”

Rulledge sat upright. “Mine is perfectly well authenticated.”

“Then spare us. Authenticated mysteries are the dullest. They have all the discomfort of fact and none of the liberty of fiction.”

“But you have known such a case,” Rulledge persisted. “I can see it.”

“You can see the mushrooms you ate for dinner. Their psychical intimations are notoriously vivid.”

Wanhope smiled, but he did not help Minver away from the subject. “There is no reason,” he said, “why you should not tell us your case, if you have one. You need not accept any interpretation of it.”

“That is the psychologist’s magnanimity,” Minver remarked. “He leaves you your facts after he has taken away everything that made them worth having.”

He rose and pushed the ashes together with his foot. For a moment I thought he meant to dismiss us; but he came back to his chair, and, sitting somewhat outside the circle of the lamp, said: “Very well. I will tell you something which happened when I was a boy. I warn you that I remember it with the accuracy peculiar to childhood—which is to say, I remember perfectly what I felt, imperfectly what I saw, and probably invent half of what I was told afterward. If that satisfies Wanhope’s standards—”

“It is better than most evidence,” Wanhope said.

“It is much better,” Rulledge declared.

“That was not implied.”

Minver waited until Rulledge had subsided, and then began.

“My uncle Felix was my mother’s eldest brother. He was in the dry-goods business in a small town which was then in the West, though it has since, I believe, been pushed eastward by the growth of the country. The town stood upon a canal, and had a courthouse with four white pillars, a hotel called the National, and a public square where the cows enjoyed rights which were afterward taken from them by civilization. My uncle’s store fronted the square. His house was two streets behind it, among maples and locusts, in a neighborhood where everybody knew when everybody else bought a new parlor carpet.

“He was a melancholy man, but not an unhappy one. The distinction is important, though Rulledge will not perceive it.”

“I perceive it perfectly,” Rulledge said.

“Then conceal the effort. My uncle had what the family called forebodings. No one treated them very seriously, least of all my aunt, who had married him with a cheerful courage and managed him with a tenderness which must have been almost invisible to him because it was so constant. He would wake in an uncommon depression and say the day had a black look; or, if one of the children was late from school, he would be certain there had been an accident at the mill-race. Usually the child appeared while he was explaining why recovery could not be hoped for.

“He knew this foible in himself, and was ashamed of it. That made it worse. He had the double action of mind which Wanhope will appreciate: the feeling first, and then the intelligence standing apart and condemning the feeling without being able to weaken it. He was like a man frightened by a shadow who knows it is a shadow, but cannot persuade his heart to behave with the enlightenment of his reason.

“My Aunt Lydia was his opposite—or, perhaps, merely his complement. She had bright brown hair, which she wore in long curls on Sundays and in a net on weekdays; I remember the curls with absurd distinctness, because when she stooped to kiss me they touched my face. She played the melodeon, kept geraniums through the winter, and had a laugh which began softly, as if she were afraid it might hurt someone, and then escaped her altogether. Their daughter, Clara, was a year older than I; there were two younger boys, who do not enter the matter except as part of the warm, noisy background from which I have never been able to detach the event.

“We lived twenty miles away, and visited them twice a year. To me their house represented magnificence. The parlor had black hair-cloth chairs, each with a white crocheted thing pinned to its back; there was a marble-topped table bearing a glass dome full of wax flowers; and over the mantel hung portraits of Uncle Felix and Aunt Lydia, painted at the time of their marriage, when the artist had given him the complexion of a mulatto and her the figure of an extinguished candle. At night the furniture assumed a moral and almost hostile character. If I had heard the grown people talk of dreams or warnings before I went upstairs, every chair became an attentive person, and the wax flowers, under their glass, seemed to be preserved from some funeral which had not yet occurred.

“The talk was not infrequent, for this was before we had wholly ceased to believe in anything. People had given up witches, in respectable circles; ghosts were already under suspicion; but dreams, warnings, second sight, death-ticks, and the turning of portraits to the wall still retained a suburban standing between religion and superstition. My uncle’s presentimentalism—if I may coin a word bad enough for the thing—was a family entertainment. They teased him about it, and he submitted with a sad smile that made me secretly take his part.

“The episode I mean occurred in the autumn when I was eleven. My mother and I were staying at his house because she had been ill, and the country air was thought beneficial, though the town had a tannery, two flour mills, and a canal whose fragrance disputed the medicinal quality of the atmosphere. Uncle Felix was preparing to go to New York to buy goods. Such a journey was then considerable. One went by packet to the lake, by steamer eastward, by rail where there was rail, and by whatever further machinery Providence and the transportation companies had imperfectly arranged. A man leaving for New York was followed by the imagination of his family over distances which have since been abolished.

“For a week before his departure my uncle was oppressed by a depression different, my mother said, from his ordinary ones. This point comes to me through her; I could not then have distinguished one shade of adult misery from another. To a child, grown people are either in good humor or unjustly severe. But I remember him moving about the house with a sort of absent gentleness. He would stop in the doorway and look at Aunt Lydia while she sewed, or lay his hand on Clara’s head and leave it there until she laughed and pulled away. At meals he forgot to eat; and once, when my aunt asked if his coffee was cold, he answered, ‘No, no, Lydia; nothing is wrong,’ with such solemnity that even I knew he was not speaking of the coffee.

“The evening before he started, the trunks stood strapped in the hall. His sample case was beside them, and his greatcoat, with a cape to it, hung from the antlers of the hat-rack. It rained; the drops ran down the windows, blurring the lamps outside, and the tow-path bell came faintly from the canal. My aunt was darning one of his woolen stockings. She had been rallying him upon forgetting a list of articles she wanted from New York, when he suddenly said:

“‘Lydia, I ought not to go.’

“She looked up, but kept the stocking drawn over her hand. ‘Then don’t go, Felix.’

“‘I must. The business depends upon it.’

“‘Mr. Haskins could go.’

“‘Haskins does not know the houses. Besides, I have made the appointments.’

“She waited; she knew he had not finished.

“‘I shall never come back,’ he said.

“The words, as I remember them, fell into the room with no theatrical effect. He did not lift his voice; there was no thunder, and the lamp did not flicker. I have perhaps supplied the rain too liberally because rain belongs to such scenes; but I am certain of the quietness. My aunt put down the stocking.

“‘Felix, you have said that before.’

“‘Not like this.’

“‘No,’ she said after a moment. ‘Not like this.’

“My mother sent Clara and me upstairs, which naturally insured our hearing more than if we had remained. We sat on the landing, behind the angle of the banisters. I could see my uncle’s boots and the lower part of my aunt’s dress, but not their faces.

“‘It is as if something had already happened,’ he said. ‘Not as if it were coming, but as if it had happened and I were traveling toward the knowledge of it.’

“That phrase made a particular horror for me. I imagined a calamity waiting somewhere upon the road, perfectly accomplished, like a bridge washed away, while my uncle advanced toward it in ignorance. For months afterward I could not see a distant turnpike without thinking that some completed disaster lay just beyond the hill.

“My aunt said: ‘If you believe that, stay.’

“‘And ruin the season? Throw the store into confusion? Make myself ridiculous to everyone—and to myself? What can I say? That I have a feeling?’

“‘You need not say anything.’

“‘I should know why I stayed.’

“Then there was a silence, and I heard the darning scissors click as she picked them up. ‘You have had feelings before, dear,’ she said.

“‘Yes. That is why I am going.’

“He left at dawn. The packet passed at six, and we all went down to the canal to see him off. The sky had cleared, but the world was sodden and gray; the tow-path shone like brown glass, and the horses steamed in the cold air. The packet was a long, narrow boat with a low cabin and curtained windows. It seemed to me then a vessel fit for adventure, though I suppose it smelled of damp wool, lamp oil, and humanity.

“Uncle Felix kissed us all. When he came to my aunt, he held her a long while, without embarrassment before the boatmen or the passengers. She whispered something; he shook his head and smiled. It was the saddest smile I had ever seen, and because I was a child I thought sadness in a smile must be a proof of prophecy.

“He went aboard. The line was cast off; the horses leaned into their traces; the water folded heavily away from the bow. He stood on the after deck, looking back. My aunt waved her handkerchief until a bend of the canal hid him, and then she remained with the handkerchief lifted after there was nothing to wave to.

“That is the part I witnessed. What happened on the journey I heard from him afterward, and from my mother, who heard it from both him and others; so you may discount it by whatever percentage skepticism demands. Only leave enough for a story.”

Rulledge made a motion of impatience, but Minver ignored him.

“My uncle’s oppression did not lighten with departure. Usually the act which commits us to a dreaded course brings some relief; choice is over, and the mind, released from its alternatives, accommodates itself to motion. With him the reverse occurred. Every mile appeared to confirm the doom, though it did not define it. On the canal packet he sat chiefly outside, despite the cold, watching the monotonous procession of tow-path, willow, lock, warehouse, and dripping field. At each stopping-place he had an impulse to go ashore and return; at each he argued himself forward.

“He afterward said that the feeling had no object. This is essential. He did not at first believe the packet would sink, that the horses would run away, that robbers would attack him, or that he would die of illness. The mind, however, has a constitutional dislike of unsupported emotion; it endeavors to provide every feeling with a fact, as society provides every respectable man with an occupation. Before the first day was over, he had imagined accidents enough to stock a newspaper.

“At night, in the narrow berth, he heard the water washing the hull and thought of drowning. When the boat stopped unexpectedly, he thought a collision had occurred. When it moved rapidly, he believed they were approaching one. Yet beneath these provisional terrors lay the original pressure, unchanged and unappeased, as if it knew that none of the accidents invented for it was the right one.

“He reached the lake safely and took a steamer eastward. There a commercial acquaintance joined him, a Mr. Bostwick, who sold hardware and possessed that invaluable temperament which regards another man’s anxiety as an opportunity for anecdote. My uncle confessed enough of his distress to be laughed at. Bostwick prescribed brandy, cards, and a better breakfast; and for some hours, under the coercion of companionship, my uncle was almost cheerful.

“On the second night there was a fire.

“It began among freight near the forward stairway. The lake was rough, the wind drove the smoke aft, and for perhaps twenty minutes the confusion was serious. Bells rang; men shouted contradictory orders; women appeared from the cabins half dressed, carrying bandboxes, children, or nothing at all. My uncle and Bostwick helped pass buckets. Then someone cried that a woman was trapped below.

“My uncle went down with one of the deckhands. They found her overcome by smoke near the cabin passage—an elderly woman, very heavy, and difficult to move. Between them they dragged and carried her up. My uncle’s coat was scorched, his hands were burned, and for a little while it seemed that the flames had gained beyond control. But the crew cut away the burning freight and smothered what remained. By dawn the danger was over.

“Now you would suppose that a man who had traveled under a conviction of calamity, and had survived so plausible a fulfillment, would either claim his presentiment as triumphantly verified or dismiss it as discharged. My uncle did neither. While the passengers were congratulating one another, and Bostwick was telling everyone that Felix had saved the woman single-handed—he had not—my uncle sat looking at his burned palms with a despair greater than before.

“Bostwick said, ‘Well, there’s your warning accounted for.’

“‘No,’ my uncle answered.

“‘No? If you want anything better than a fire at midnight in half a gale, you are particular.’

“‘That wasn’t it.’

“‘How do you know?’

“‘Because it is still with me.’

“Then, after a silence, he said: ‘I shall never see my wife again.’

“That was the first direction the foreboding took. Until then it had been diffused, a dark weather of the mind. After the fire it concentrated itself upon separation from Aunt Lydia. But even this meaning was ambiguous. To say that he would never see her again was not necessarily to say who would die; and, since he was the traveler, exposed to the hazards of strange roads, strange hotels, machinery, weather, and disease, his imagination fixed upon himself as the threatened person.

“He wrote to her from the next city. The letter came before he returned and was afterward kept by Clara. I saw it years later. It was affectionate but not alarming. He said he had been in some danger, that Providence had spared him, and that she must not be uneasy if reports of the fire reached home. There was a postscript asking her to kiss the children for him. The handwriting of that postscript sloped downward until the last words almost touched the edge of the paper.

“He could have turned back then. He had passed the chief difficulties of the journey, but return was possible. Yet the conviction that he would die had now produced a singular resignation. So long as the foreboding was formless, he struggled against it; when he had interpreted it as his own death, he became calm. The mind had found a direction for its terror, and preferred the worst certainty to uncertainty.

“He completed his business with unusual method. He selected goods, revised contracts, settled old accounts, and wrote full instructions for his partner. Bostwick afterward said he had never known a man more composed or more exact. But this composure was the industry of farewell. My uncle bought gifts for the children: a jointed wooden acrobat for one boy, a miniature drum for the other, and for Clara a little workbox of rosewood with a mother-of-pearl keyhole. For my aunt he bought a brooch, a spray of enameled forget-me-nots. It was a deplorable piece of sentiment even for that period.”

“It was beautiful,” Rulledge protested.

“It was blue,” Minver said. “Let us not confound the categories.

“In New York his depression became less visible but more absolute. He went about among warehouses and countingrooms; he ate when reminded; he slept several hours a night; he answered questions rationally. At the same time, everything he saw acquired for him the pathos of a last sight. The crowded streets, the omnibuses, the shop lamps shining in rain, even the enormous hotel register on which he wrote his name, appeared to belong to a world from which he had already been excluded. He told my mother later that he felt like the ghost of himself, permitted for a few days to finish neglected errands.

“On the return journey he met delays but no dangers. A broken rail detained the train for three hours; the passengers grumbled, and my uncle regarded the interruption as perhaps the appointed moment. Nothing followed. A riverboat grounded on a bar and remained there until morning. He thought, ‘It will be here.’ The boat floated free. On the lake a storm rose, but the steamer made harbor. Each false climax left the conviction untouched.

“Do you see the cruelty of it? A coward might have exhausted himself in successive panics; my uncle, who was not a coward, had to maintain, hour after hour and day after day, the quiet consent of a condemned man whose execution is repeatedly postponed without reprieve. He ceased trying to guess the instrument. He merely believed that death stood somewhere between him and home.

“At the last railway junction he was met by Haskins, his partner, who had come on business and was surprised to find him looking well. My uncle asked at once whether all was right at home.

“‘Perfectly,’ Haskins said. ‘I had a letter from the store yesterday.’

“‘And Lydia?’

“‘I suppose she is well. No one said otherwise.’

“This reassured him for perhaps an hour. You must remember that messages then traveled slowly and irregularly; absence was a real region, not a temporary failure of communication. He had had letters from Aunt Lydia in New York, but none for several days. He explained the silence by his changing addresses, yet it remained in his mind.

“The final stage was again by canal. He boarded the packet in the afternoon. By calculation he would reach home shortly after sunrise. It was a mild October night, with a thin moon and mist over the fields. He did not sleep. He sat near the stern while the other passengers settled below, and watched the familiar country appear by degrees: a covered bridge, a limekiln, an orchard belonging to a man he knew, the black bulk of the mill. With every landmark the dread increased.

“Near dawn the packet passed the cemetery. He could distinguish the pale shafts among the trees, and he looked for the place where he supposed he would be buried. This is one of the details I distrust, because it is too appropriate; but I remember his saying it, and I remember the chill it gave me. Beyond the cemetery the first roofs of the town showed through the mist.

“He was still alive.

“The packet entered the long straight reach. The courthouse cupola caught the sunrise; smoke rose from chimneys; a dog ran barking beside the tow-path. My uncle stood with his bag in his hand. He had reached the point where the survival of each minute seemed an anomaly. When the boat touched the landing, he stepped ashore slowly, as if the earth itself might be the trap.

“No one waited for him. That was not strange, for the packet was early. He declined the porter’s offer to carry his bag and walked home. The streets were scarcely awake. A baker was taking down his shutters; two schoolboys, though it was Saturday, were rolling a hoop; somewhere a woman beat a carpet. These common sights affected him almost unbearably. He had imagined the town continuing after his death; now he moved through it, and it had not noticed that he was doomed.

“He turned into his own street.

“From the corner he could see the maples before the house, then the white fence, then the upper windows. The curtains of his bedroom were drawn. He told himself Aunt Lydia was still asleep. His heart rose with a hope so violent that it was scarcely distinguishable from fear. He walked faster.

“Then he saw people at the gate.

“There were three women from neighboring houses and old Dr. Morse’s buggy tied to the fence. Before anyone spoke—before, he said, he could clearly recognize a face—the foreboding changed.

“That was his word. It changed.

“All the way home he had expected his own death. At sight of the people by the gate, the conviction left him so completely that he knew in the same instant that he was safe. But the oppression did not lift; it turned, as a compass needle turns, and fixed itself elsewhere.

“‘Lydia is dead,’ he said.

“One of the women came toward him. She began, ‘Felix—’ but he put out his hand.

“‘Do not tell me. I know.’

“My aunt had died shortly after midnight.

“She had been perfectly well when she went upstairs. Clara was in her room helping lay out the things for my uncle’s return—the clean linen, his slippers, and a vase of late roses which they had brought in from the garden before frost. Aunt Lydia complained suddenly of pain, sat down on the bed, and lost consciousness almost at once. The doctor came, but there was nothing to be done. They called it a seizure of the heart.

“My mother was with her. Her last conscious words, if the family memory did not afterward shape them to the event, were: ‘Felix will be home in the morning.’

“He came into the house without weeping. Clara ran to him, and he held her; but he still carried the traveling bag in one hand, as if some part of him had not understood that the journey was over. The gifts were in it. He gave them to the children weeks afterward, except the brooch. That he put in Aunt Lydia’s coffin.”

For some moments no one spoke. The city noises, which had withdrawn while Minver talked, seemed to return: a wagon labored through the soft snow; a door slammed in the street below; from a distant elevated train came a brief iron thunder.

Rulledge was the first to break the silence. His voice had lost its usual satisfaction in mystery. “And he had felt it from before he left?”

“So the family believed.”

“And she was well then?”

“Entirely.”

“Had there been heart trouble before?”

“None that anyone knew of.”

“Could he have received some message—some hint on the way?”

“Not unless the psychical post had better service than the governmental one.”

Rulledge accepted the rebuke meekly. “But the moment he saw the people—he knew?”

“He said he knew.”

Wanhope had not moved. “There are several distinguishable elements,” he began.

Minver groaned. “He is going to dissect my aunt.”

“No. The emotional sequence is more interesting than any supernatural interpretation. First, an undirected depression; then an effort of the intelligence to supply possible dangers; then, after the fire, a localization of the fear in permanent separation from his wife; next, by an egocentric process natural to the traveler, the assumption of his own death; and finally, upon the visual stimulus of the neighbors and the doctor’s buggy, an instantaneous correction.”

“Correction?” Rulledge cried. “He said it before they told him!”

“Certainly. The sight was enough to suggest death in the household.”

“But why hers?”

“Because she was the emotional center of the foreboding.”

Minver leaned back in the shadow. “There, Rulledge. Science permits the miracle, provided it may rename all the parts.”

Wanhope did not resent this. “I do not say the explanation is sufficient. It is possible that states of profound affection involve perceptions which we do not yet know how to isolate. What is called presentiment may someday be classified as the obscure reception of indications transmitted through channels now unsuspected.”

“Telepathy,” Rulledge said eagerly.

“I did not say telepathy.”

“You meant it.”

“I meant what I said—an increasingly rare distinction in this company.”

I asked Minver what became of his uncle.

“He lived twenty-six years after that. He never married again. His forebodings ceased almost wholly, which is curious, if you like. Perhaps the great one exhausted the faculty; perhaps grief gave him something definite to be sad about and cured him of indefinite sadness. He prospered, grew stout, and became rather cheerful in old age. But he would never discuss the journey before strangers. Once, when I was grown, I asked him whether he believed his feeling had really concerned Aunt Lydia from the beginning.

“He said: ‘I don’t know. I thought it concerned me. Perhaps love is stupid even when it is warned.’

“That is all.”

“No,” Rulledge said, “it isn’t all. Did the clocks stop?”

Minver stared at him.

“In the house,” Rulledge explained. “When she died. Clocks often stop.”

“My dear Rulledge, even death could not have stopped my aunt’s kitchen clock. It gained eleven minutes a day and regarded winding as an impertinence.”

“Was there no other sign? A picture falling, a dog howling, a dream—”

“There was a rosebud found on the stairs,” Minver said gravely.

Rulledge bent forward. “Really?”

“No. But I felt that your emotional necessities required one.”

Rulledge sank back with dignity. “You always spoil everything.”

“I have spent my life trying to spoil the things you improve.”

Wanhope looked at Minver with a mild attentiveness. “You spoke of the matter ironically from the beginning, but you have remembered it with unusual fullness.”

“Memory is the most sentimental of the faculties. That is why we should distrust it.”

“Yet you believe your uncle.”

“I believe he suffered.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is the only part that matters.”

The answer silenced us again, though Minver at once seemed ashamed of having made it. He rose, turned up the lamp, and exposed us to one another’s faces with an effect almost indecent after the half-darkness in which we had listened.

Rulledge also rose. His expression was thoughtful; but as he buttoned his coat, thought gave way to another impulse of equal sincerity.

“Is there anywhere near here,” he asked, “that one could get a sandwich at this hour?”

Minver regarded him with restored affection. “Your immortality is the only psychical fact of which I have never doubted. Nothing can destroy what continually renews itself from the bill of fare.”

Rulledge said that we might laugh, but the demand of the physical nature was itself mysterious; and he went away through the snow in search of food, carrying, as he always did, his soul safely embedded in his appetite.

Wanhope followed soon afterward, promising to send Minver a monograph on anticipatory emotion. Minver promised not to read it. I remained a moment at the window. Below, Rulledge had stopped under a lamp to question a cabman, while the snow passed slantwise through the circle of light and vanished into darkness.

“Do you think,” I asked, “that your uncle really knew?”

Minver lowered the blind.

“If he did,” he said, “it was knowledge of a very useless kind. It told him nothing in time to save her, and everything in time to torment him.”

He took up the envelope on which he had drawn the woman’s profile, looked at it, and dropped it into the fire. The crossed lines blackened first; then the face curled inward, brightened at the edges, and was gone.

“But,” he added, as he opened the door for me, “that objection has never prevented knowledge from being true.”

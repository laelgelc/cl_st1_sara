### The White Carnation

In Red Butte it was generally admitted that old Mr. Shirley owned the bank, that Jay Ellington Harper was its cashier, and that Theodosia Shirley ran it. The first was a legal fact, the second appeared upon the brass plate, and the third was known to every cattleman who had ever asked an extension on a note.

Theodosia was called Tommy because the West, which wastes little time upon unnecessary syllables or distinctions, had found the longer name inconvenient. She was tall, brown, quick of motion, and carried her head as if the wind had been blowing against it from childhood. At twenty-four she could post a ledger, judge a steer, mend a bicycle chain, and tell a liar before he had finished clearing his throat. These accomplishments were not considered feminine in Red Butte; nevertheless, they were considered useful, which is a higher compliment in a new country.

Jay Harper, on the other hand, was feminine in none of the accepted particulars, but he had several gifts which women are expected to value more than arithmetic. He was handsome, softly spoken, beautifully shaved, and always wore a white carnation in his coat. He could dance through a whole evening without crushing a slipper, and his manner of handing a woman into a buggy made her feel that transportation was an act of homage.

As a cashier he was a misfortune.

“Jay,” Tommy said one afternoon, leaning over his shoulder, “you’ve put Mr. Cramer’s payment on the debit side.”

Harper adjusted his eyeglasses and looked distressed.

“So I have.”

“And carried the balance wrong.”

“I fear that is true.”

“And forgot to charge interest.”

Harper turned on his stool. “Tommy, must you enumerate all my crimes at once?”

“No. I’ve got work to do.”

She corrected the ledger while he watched her with grateful admiration. He admired her precisely as one admires a fine bridge or a trustworthy revolver—with confidence, affection, and no very clear desire to marry it.

Tommy knew this, although she had not always known it. She loved him with that private, humiliating fidelity which sometimes fastens upon competent women, perhaps because Nature enjoys putting bad paper among good securities. She had once believed that her usefulness to Jay must end in happiness. At nineteen one can mistake dependence for devotion, particularly when the dependent person has gray eyes and says “Thank you” beautifully.

Then Mr. Shirley sent her East for two years.

“My girl,” he had said, “you know banking, but you don’t know the world.”

“I know the part that borrows money.”

“There are other parts.”

“I’ve never seen ’em at the bank.”

Nevertheless she went, and returned with improved French, broader opinions, three trunks of garments she seldom wore, and Jessica Bell.

Jessica had been Tommy’s dearest friend at the eastern school. She was small and fair, with delicate wrists and a way of looking at ordinary objects as though they had been mentioned in poetry. She wore pale gowns, used violet scent, and had never been compelled to decide anything more serious than whether blue ribbons became her.

The West received her with enthusiasm. It likes fragility when fragility arrives with its own return ticket.

The seven Old Boys, who sat every evening behind the plate-glass window of the Occidental Hotel, pronounced her “a little white rabbit,” and competed in sending her flowers. Men who had not changed their collars for county court put on coats to call at the Shirleys’. Even the wind, meeting her the first morning, merely lifted her veil and passed on, doubtless abashed.

Jay fell in love with her before luncheon.

He had gone to the Shirley house to welcome Tommy home and had brought his customary carnation, so white and stiff that it looked as if it had been audited. Jessica came down the stairs in a gray traveling dress, paused upon seeing him, and put one hand to her throat.

Tommy, who was behind her, saw the whole business completed in that pause.

“Jessica,” she said cheerfully, “this is Mr. Harper. He keeps father’s bank from becoming too dull. Jay, this is Miss Bell. Don’t either of you look so scared. You’ll get used to it.”

Harper bowed.

Jessica blushed.

Tommy went upstairs to remove her hat and called herself a fool in the privacy of her room.

During the following month Red Butte enjoyed the romance in installments. Jay took Jessica driving. He brought her carnations. He read to her on the Shirley veranda, although neither of them attended closely to the book. Jessica said the plains were “vast and solemn”; Jay agreed that they awakened noble thoughts. Tommy, passing with a bundle of statements, observed that the plains chiefly awakened thirst and jack rabbits, but the lovers forgave her.

There was justice in their attraction. Jay and Jessica belonged to the same ornamental order. They believed in twilight, graceful speeches, and the moral value of music. Both disliked harsh voices and practical jokes. Both were liable to headaches when the weather changed. If a bill came due, they suffered; if a sunset occurred, they recovered.

Tommy belonged to another species.

“You don’t think him strong,” Jessica said one evening, with faint reproach.

They were in Tommy’s room. Jessica lay among cushions while Tommy cleaned the bearings of her bicycle upon a newspaper.

“Jay’s strong enough in his line.”

“What is his line?”

“Being Jay.”

“You laugh at him.”

“I laugh at everybody I’m fond of. It’s cheaper than flowers.”

Jessica looked dreamily toward the window. “He understands so much.”

“He understands things that don’t require understanding.”

“Tommy!”

“Well, he does. Moonlight and poetry and the color of your eyes. Put a protested draft before him and his soul leaves his body.”

Jessica sat upright. “You are unjust.”

“No. I’ve balanced him for five years.”

The color rose in Jessica’s face, and Tommy bent lower over the wheel. After a moment Jessica asked very softly, “Were you ever—did you ever think of him differently?”

Tommy spun the wheel and listened to its thin humming.

“I thought the moon was made of something valuable when I was little,” she said. “Folks get educated.”

It was not an answer, and because Jessica was gentle she did not insist. Because she was in love, however, she believed it.

Soon afterward Harper left Mr. Shirley’s bank to establish one of his own at Barstow, twenty-five miles west. The change was encouraged by Mr. Shirley, who liked Jay but preferred to like him at a distance, and by Tommy, who drew up the papers, examined the securities, selected the safe, and arranged his correspondents.

“You’re making me independent,” Jay told her.

“I’m making father’s ledgers independent of you. That’s not quite the same.”

He smiled, but his face sobered when he looked at her. They were alone in the bank after closing. The gas jets hissed above them, and outside a hot wind drove dust against the windows.

“Tommy, you have always been extraordinarily good to me.”

“Don’t get grateful. It never leads to anything sensible.”

“I have sometimes wondered—”

“Then quit it. Wondering is bad for business.”

He stood beside her desk, turning the white carnation in his buttonhole. “You hide yourself behind jokes.”

“Safer than hiding behind collateral. Somebody might examine it.”

“I believe I understand you better than you permit me to say.”

Tommy closed the ledger.

Her heart had begun to beat in the old foolish way, and she despised it for taking orders from a man who could not total a column. She looked up. Harper’s expression was tender, uncertain, and serious enough to deceive any woman who had not spent five years correcting his mistakes.

Then Jessica’s laugh floated in from the street below. Harper turned instinctively toward the window.

Tommy saw it and was cured—not of loving him, which is rarely managed so promptly, but of expecting profit from it.

“You understand me beautifully, Jay,” she said. “That’s why you’d better catch the six o’clock train.”

He left next morning for Barstow, where his bank opened with flowers in the window, walnut fittings, and a capital somewhat more impressive upon paper than in the vault. For three months it prospered. Harper sent reports full of confidence and telegrams short of punctuation. He came to Red Butte on Sundays and spent most of the day with Jessica.

Yet he did not ask her to marry him.

His hesitation was partly financial, partly temperamental, and partly due to Tommy. A man may be drawn toward softness while still looking over his shoulder at strength. Harper felt that Tommy understood his defects and could protect him from their consequences. Jessica understood none of them, which was much pleasanter but less reassuring.

The matter might have remained thus until everybody was old had not the Barstow bank been attacked by a rumor.

It began on a Thursday, when a sheep buyer’s check was delayed in collection. By Friday morning somebody said Harper had refused payment. By ten o’clock a dozen depositors were at the counter; by noon there were fifty. Harper paid steadily, but fear has a larger account than any cashier can satisfy. At one-fifteen he telegraphed Mr. Shirley:

RUN ON BANK. NEED FIFTEEN THOUSAND BEFORE THREE OR MUST CLOSE.

Mr. Shirley was in Denver. Tommy received the message.

She read it once, glanced at the clock, and opened the vault. There was cash enough. There was no train until evening, the telegraph could not transmit currency, and the only available horse had gone lame that morning.

Twenty-five miles separated Red Butte from Barstow.

“Get me a satchel,” she said to the clerk.

Jessica, who had entered to meet her for luncheon, stared. “What has happened?”

“Jay’s bank is being pulled to pieces by a pack of frightened sheep.”

“Is he in danger?”

“Only of failure.”

“To Jay that would be terrible!”

“It’s never entertaining.”

Tommy thrust packets of bills into a leather bag and locked it. Her mind moved with its usual cold precision. The Barstow road was level for eight miles, then rose across the bluffs, then dropped through open cattle country. On a good day she could bicycle it in two hours. This was not a good day. The thermometer outside Miller’s drugstore registered one hundred and four, and the southwest wind was coming up.

“I’ll ride over,” she said.

Jessica caught her arm. “I shall go too.”

“You’ll do nothing of the kind.”

“He may need me.”

“He needs fifteen thousand dollars.”

“I can carry some.”

“You can barely carry your parasol when it’s open.”

Jessica’s eyes filled, but her chin, to Tommy’s surprise, remained firm. “If you go alone and anything happens— Tommy, please. I love him.”

That settled it. Love has been permitted to excuse less reasonable expeditions.

Ten minutes later they wheeled their bicycles from the alley behind the bank. Tommy wore a divided brown skirt, stout boots, and a man’s linen collar. Jessica had changed into a pale cycling costume which had been designed in Boston for shaded avenues and male admiration. The money was divided between two satchels and strapped to the handlebars.

The seven Old Boys emerged from the Occidental.

“Where you bound, Tommy?” called Colonel Hanks.

“Barstow.”

“In this heat?”

“The heat hasn’t got a bank account.”

Doc Peters examined the sky. “Wind’ll be dead against you.”

“Then it won’t get lonesome.”

The Old Boys watched them mount.

“Little one won’t make ten miles,” said the Colonel.

“Love’ll carry her,” suggested another.

“Love never carried anything uphill,” Doc Peters replied. “It generally rides.”

They left town to the dry clicking of their chains.

For the first six miles Jessica did well. Excitement brightened her eyes, and the road ran straight between fields bleached almost white. Locusts screamed in the cottonwoods. The sun hung like a brass shield over the plain, and each turn of the wheels seemed to wind the heat tighter around them.

Then the wind struck.

It came over the open country with the sweep of cavalry, full of dust, straw, and the hot smell of cattle. Their skirts snapped. The bicycles swerved under them. Tommy lowered her head and pushed, feeling the leather bag tug at the handlebars.

Behind her Jessica cried out.

Tommy turned. “Keep straight! Don’t fight every gust.”

“I can’t breathe.”

“You’re breathing enough to tell me.”

They rode on.

At the foot of the bluffs the road began to climb in long red curves. Heat trembled above it. The distant buttes bent, lifted, and performed solemn serpentines against the sky. A herd of cattle stood hidden in a hollow until the riders were nearly upon them; then horned heads rose through the grass like something conjured from the earth.

Jessica dismounted.

“I must rest.”

“Two minutes.”

Tommy held both bicycles while Jessica sat beside the road. Her face had lost its pretty color, and dust streaked the violet-scented collar. She looked no more made for the country than a lace handkerchief made for pumping a well.

“Drink,” Tommy ordered.

Jessica obeyed and tried to smile. “I am a dreadful burden.”

“You’re no heavier than you were at luncheon.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Yes. Save your breath.”

They started again, walking the steepest mile. The road scorched through their soles. Grasshoppers sprang against their skirts. At the summit Tommy looked at her watch.

Two-ten.

Fifteen miles remained.

They mounted for the descent, but Jessica’s hands trembled so violently that the front wheel wavered. Twice she nearly fell. At the bottom she stopped, slid from the saddle, and sank into the yellow grass like a little white rabbit shot in flight.

Tommy dropped her bicycle.

“Jessica!”

“I’m not fainting,” Jessica whispered, with the obstinacy of one plainly doing so.

Tommy loosened her collar, shaded her face, and held the water bottle to her lips. There was no house in sight. The road ran empty between low swells of burnt grass. Far away, a windmill winked and disappeared.

“Listen,” Tommy said. “You can’t go on.”

“I must.”

“You mustn’t. There’s a cottonwood draw half a mile back. You’ll wait there.”

“And Jay?”

“I’ll attend to Jay.”

“No.” Jessica gripped her wrist. “Take my money. Take all of it. Don’t wait.”

Tommy looked at the delicate face, gray now beneath the dust. Jessica had failed physically, as everyone knew she would; but there are various sorts of courage, and the little creature had spent all hers without complaint until there was none left.

“You’re a better sort than you look,” Tommy said.

Jessica gave a weak laugh. “Is that praise?”

“From me it’s a testimonial.”

Tommy dragged her into the meager shade of the draw, wet her handkerchief, and made her lie down. Then she fastened both packets of money to her own bicycle.

“If a wagon comes, stop it. If it doesn’t, stay still. I’ll send somebody back.”

“Tell Jay—”

“I’ll tell him his bank’s safe.”

“Tell him—”

But Tommy had mounted.

The final fourteen miles remained afterward in her memory as heat, noise, and the recurring sight of the front wheel. The wind pressed upon her like a hand. Her mouth dried until swallowing became painful. Once the chain slipped, and she repaired it with fingers blackened by oil while precious minutes ran through her head like figures in a ledger.

Two-forty.

She rode harder.

The cattle country rolled around her, enormous and indifferent. A steer broke from a ravine and galloped beside the road, tossing its head; for fifty yards they raced, the animal frightened, the woman furious, both pursued by dust. Then it wheeled away.

At the last rise she saw Barstow: a water tower, two grain elevators, the depot, and the bright line of roofs trembling in the heat.

The bank clock showed three minutes to three when Tommy came down Main Street.

A crowd packed the pavement. Men in shirt sleeves shouted through the open doors. Women waited with passbooks clutched in gloved hands. Inside, Harper stood behind the counter, pale and correct, paying out the last small bills.

Tommy pushed through.

“Here,” she said, dropping the satchels upon the counter. “Open your other window.”

Harper stared at her. “Tommy!”

“Don’t make a speech. Pay ’em.”

The sight of the money did what reason, honor, and Harper’s beautiful assurances had failed to do. The crowd quieted. A cattleman who had demanded his deposit began explaining that he had never personally doubted the bank. Others decided they could wait until Monday. Within twenty minutes the run was over.

Harper came around the counter.

His collar had wilted, his hair was disordered, and the white carnation hung crushed from his coat. For once he looked almost useful.

“You rode from Red Butte?”

“Part of me did. The rest blew here separately.”

“You saved me.”

“Your depositors saved you. They quit asking for their money.”

He caught both her hands. “Tommy, I have been blind.”

“Frequently.”

“I know now what you are to me. I have tried to deny it because—because you are so unlike other women.”

“That’s a handsome opening.”

“I need you. I have always needed you.”

There it was at last: the proposal she had once expected, the confession she had rehearsed hearing in foolish hours, and it came with Jessica lying under a cottonwood beside the road.

Tommy withdrew her hands.

“Where is she?” Harper asked suddenly.

“Ten miles east. Fallen by the wayside on your account.”

“Jessica came?”

“She carried half the money until she couldn’t carry herself. She wanted me to leave her there and save your precious walnut fittings.”

His face changed. Tenderness, alarm, and something simpler than either swept away the gratitude he had mistaken for love.

“Is she ill?”

“She’s exhausted.”

“I must go to her.”

“Needless to say.”

He snatched his hat and called for a buggy. At the door he turned. “Tommy—what I said—”

“You were excited. Bankers get that way when there’s money in sight.”

“But I meant—”

“No, Jay. You meant you need a cashier. Hire one. You love Jessica.”

He stood silent.

Tommy smiled, although the effort cost more than the ride.

“She’s your sort, and you’re hers. You can read poems to each other and mislay the household bills. Go get her before she wakes up and realizes she has sense.”

A buggy rattled to the curb. Harper hesitated only once; then he hurried out.

As he moved, the crushed carnation fell from his buttonhole to the floor. He did not notice it.

Tommy remained in the emptying bank. Outside, the town repeated the story already: how Miss Shirley had ridden twenty-five miles against the wind; how Harper had never been in real danger; how everybody had known the bank was sound. Success produces a remarkable number of original believers.

She stooped and picked up the flower.

It was bruised and dusty, brown at the edges, but one inner fold remained white. For a moment she held it against her palm. Her face, which had defied heat, wind, distance, and fifteen thousand dollars in unstable currency, altered very slightly.

Then she laughed once, without amusement.

“Men,” she said, addressing the ledgers. “They can’t even drop a thing where it’ll be overlooked.”

There was a small iron grate behind Harper’s desk, though no fire burned in June. Tommy tossed the carnation into it. The flower struck the bars, rested white against the black iron, and slipped through.

Then she rang for a clerk, asked for water, and began to balance the bank.

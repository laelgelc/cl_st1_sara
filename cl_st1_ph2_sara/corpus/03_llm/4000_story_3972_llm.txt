### THE PRIVATE HONOUR OF SPIDER BUFFIN

Spider Buffin was a small man with large grievances. Nature had denied him height, weight, education, influence, and nearly every other instrument by which a citizen may impose his opinions upon society; but she had given him an excellent memory for injuries.

His hobby was revenge.

Other men collected stamps, bred pigeons, or spent Sunday morning in bed. Spider corrected enemies. A careless elbow in a public-house, a disputed half-crown, a smile insufficiently respectful—these things entered his mind and remained there, gathering interest. He might forget a face, a name, or where he had put a stolen purse, but he never forgot an affront.

His profession was picking pockets. It was a calling for which his light fingers, narrow shoulders, and complete absence of moral speculation peculiarly fitted him. Yet it was not his profession that brought him into collision with Constable Keating. It was his hobby.

Alf Mercer had offended Spider in the matter of a greyhound and seven shillings. The greyhound had never belonged to either of them, and the seven shillings had been stolen from a third party, but these legal refinements did not affect the sacred nature of Spider’s claim.

For three weeks he brooded. For three weeks he followed Alf through Clerkenwell. For three weeks he carried in his coat a short length of lead pipe, not as a weapon—Spider disliked ugly words—but as an instrument of correction.

Then, on a wet Tuesday night, he found Alf alone beneath a railway arch.

The opportunity was perfect. Cromwell at Dunbar, Napoleon at Austerlitz, and Spider Buffin under the railway arch had this in common: each perceived that Providence had arranged matters with a view to immediate action.

Spider advanced.

“’Ullo, Alf,” he said.

Alf turned.

“’Ullo, Spider.”

“Remember that greyhound?”

“No.”

This was worse than insolence. It was atheism.

Spider produced the pipe and had reached the second movement of his argument when a vast hand descended upon his collar. He was lifted from the ground with the calm ease of a housewife removing a kitten from the milk.

Constable Keating was six feet three inches high and wide in proportion. His face was pink, his manner genial, and his belief in the general soundness of British institutions had never been disturbed by anything he had seen while serving them. He could have eaten Spider, but refrained from doing so because the regulations made no provision for it.

“Now then,” he said. “What’s all this?”

“Private business,” said Spider, revolving helplessly.

“So was the Gunpowder Plot, I dessay. Drop that.”

Spider dropped the pipe.

Alf Mercer, whose gratitude had limits, went away rapidly. Spider watched him vanish and transferred to Constable Keating the entire moral debt of the transaction. A revenge frustrated is not a revenge abolished. It merely changes its address.

At the station Spider maintained a dignified silence, except when asked his name, occupation, residence, age, and intentions, on all of which subjects he lied with fluency. The magistrate gave him three months.

Thus the Government, which frequently assists its citizens in ways they do not appreciate, provided Spider with a season of quiet seclusion at the public expense.

For thirteen weeks he thought of Constable Keating.

He remembered the grip upon his collar.

He remembered Alf’s retreat.

He remembered the grin of the constable who had searched him.

Above all, he remembered that Keating, while leading him to the station, had advised him to “turn over a new leaf.”

There are phrases which embitter a lifetime.

Spider emerged from prison thinner, paler, and spiritually refreshed. He had made a vow. Constable Keating should be corrected. Not crudely, not hastily, but with the solemn care appropriate to a Holy Mission.

The first obstacle was practical. Spider knew Keating’s beat, but a policeman on duty possesses a helmet, a whistle, colleagues, and the organised approval of the State. To attack him under these conditions would have been brave, but inconvenient. Spider required his private address and some knowledge of his habits.

He therefore adopted friendship.

The first advance was made outside the Blue Cow, where Keating stood at ten o’clock one evening, contemplating the public with benevolent suspicion.

Spider approached humbly.

“Evenin’, Mr Keating.”

Keating looked down.

“Well, well! Buffin, isn’t it? Out again?”

“Um.”

“Keeping straight, I hope?”

“Um.”

“That’s the thing. Honest work, regular hours, a clean conscience. Nothing like it.”

Spider had tried all three and had found much to say against them, but he nodded.

“Bin thinkin’,” he said.

“Good habit.”

“About what you said. New leaf.”

“Ah!” Keating beamed. “Knew there was good in you.”

Spider, who knew exactly what was in him, was startled by this discovery.

He visited Keating again two nights later and bought him half a pint. It injured Spider to spend money on a policeman, particularly as the money had been removed that afternoon from a lady in Holborn; but revenge, like education, requires investment.

Within a fortnight Keating regarded him as a reformed character. He spoke to Spider of honest labour, the blessings of domestic life, and his own experiences with rheumatism. Spider listened, saying “Um” at points which appeared to require intellectual participation.

In return Keating supplied, without knowing it, the desired facts. He lived in Doughty Mews with his widowed sister. He went off duty at eleven on Thursdays. He walked home by way of Little Warner Street because the main road was noisy. His sister left a chop for him in the oven.

The Holy Mission had acquired a timetable.

Unfortunately, friendship with a policeman is a luxury which few men in Spider’s position can afford. Members of the Frith Street Gang began to notice his conversations with Keating. They noticed also that Spider had lately escaped arrest during two minor disturbances, though this was due less to official favour than to his superior speed.

The matter was discussed at the rear of the Britannia public-house.

Sid Marks presided. Sid was a short, square man whose eyes possessed the restful expression of two gun-barrels. He was known as the great Sid, partly from respect and partly because those who had called him anything else were no longer socially active.

With him sat Otto the Sausage and Rabbit Butler.

Otto had earned his title from a physical resemblance which grew more marked when he wore his brown overcoat. Rabbit Butler’s name was not due to gentleness. His front teeth projected, and in moments of emotion his nose moved rapidly.

Spider was summoned.

He entered to find the three men contemplating him with the grave attention usually accorded to condemned machinery.

“Sit down,” said Sid.

Spider sat.

“See much of Keating?” asked Sid.

“Who?”

“The slop.”

“What slop?”

Sid sighed. A man who wishes to survive interrogation should not insult the intelligence of the tribunal beyond what courtesy demands.

“Big Keating. Pink face. Feet like meat safes. The copper you been drinkin’ with.”

“Friend o’ mine,” said Spider.

Otto made a moist sound.

“A friend,” repeated Sid.

“Um.”

“Police friend?”

“Um. No.”

“Looks police.”

“’E ain’t police when ’e’s off duty.”

This observation, though technically adventurous, produced no comfort.

Rabbit leaned forward.

“You narkin’, Spider?”

“No.”

“You looks like you’re narkin’.”

“I ain’t.”

“Why’re you sweet on Keating?”

Spider hesitated. To reveal his plan would be to lower revenge from the plane of personal honour to that of common gossip. Moreover, if Sid knew that Spider intended to assault a policeman, Sid might forbid it on business grounds.

“Reformin’,” he said.

Silence followed.

There are statements so improbable that argument merely weakens one’s opposition to them.

Sid tapped the table.

“Listen, Spider. We don’t like narks.”

“Nor me.”

“We don’t like people as drinks with narks.”

“Nor me.”

“We don’t like people as says ‘nor me’ too often.”

Spider refrained from agreeing.

“You got till Friday,” said Sid. “Then we decides.”

“Decides what?”

Rabbit smiled, revealing the architecture responsible for his name.

“Whether you goes on breathin’ regular.”

Spider departed with the uncomfortable conviction that events were pressing upon him. Today was Thursday. The Holy Mission must be performed that night. Once Keating had been corrected, even Sid could hardly maintain that Spider was a police informer. Broken policemen do not commonly provide character references for their assailants.

At half-past ten Spider took up his station in Little Warner Street.

It was a bitter night. The wind moved round corners with professional malice. Spider stood beneath a broken lamp, his hands in his pockets and his soul supported by principle. In one pocket was a stone wrapped in a stocking. He had selected this equipment after much thought. The stone provided authority; the stocking supplied delicacy.

The street was narrow and dark. A cat crossed it, paused to inspect Spider, and withdrew as if unwilling to become involved.

At eleven the distant clock struck.

At five minutes past, footsteps sounded.

Spider straightened.

But the footsteps belonged to two men, not one. They approached from the opposite direction and halted in a doorway twenty yards away. One was broad and tubular. The other moved his nose.

Otto the Sausage and Rabbit Butler.

Spider froze.

Their presence could mean only one thing. Sid had sent them after Keating. Perhaps the gang had learned that Keating had been asking questions. Perhaps Otto and Rabbit hoped to test Spider. Perhaps they were engaged upon a Holy Mission of their own.

Whatever the reason, their conduct was intolerable.

Keating belonged to Spider.

He had thought of him for thirteen weeks. He had endured prison food, sermons, cold weather, and the purchase of half-pints. He had listened to descriptions of Keating’s sister’s rheumatism and a complete account of how Keating once arrested a drunk in Islington. No man could acquire a clearer moral title to another man’s skull.

To have Otto and Rabbit arrive now was like seeing strangers enter one’s garden and dig up a treasure one had spent months locating. It was piracy. It was plagiarism. It was infringement of patent.

Spider’s anger became almost pure.

Another tread entered the street: slow, heavy, official.

Constable Keating appeared at the corner, out of uniform, wearing a dark overcoat and bowler hat. Without his helmet he seemed less like a fortress, though still more than sufficient for Spider’s ordinary requirements. He walked contentedly, thinking of his chop.

Otto slid from the doorway.

“Got a match, mate?”

Keating stopped.

“Certainly.”

He reached into his pocket.

Rabbit came behind him.

The first blow landed with a dull crack. Keating staggered. Otto struck him in the stomach. Rabbit seized his throat. There was a gasp, a scuffle, a shoe scraping stone. Keating tried to shout, but Rabbit’s arm tightened.

Spider watched in outrage.

These men were spoiling everything.

The honourable course would have been to wait until they had finished, then correct them later. But Spider’s mind, never designed for simultaneous problems, became overheated. He forgot Sid. He forgot the gang. He forgot that Keating was his enemy. He remembered only that Otto and Rabbit were stealing his revenge.

He sprang from the shadows.

“’E’s mine!” he screamed.

Otto turned, astonished.

Spider struck him behind the ear with the stone in the stocking.

Otto sat down abruptly, like a statesman accepting office. Rabbit released Keating and lunged. Spider ducked. Rabbit’s fist met Keating’s shoulder. Keating, recovering his breath, drove one large hand into Rabbit’s waistcoat and projected him against the wall.

Then all civilisation dissolved.

Whistles. Feet. A growl from Otto. Rabbit’s teeth gleaming. Keating’s bowler rolling in the gutter. Spider clinging to Otto’s back. Otto backing into brickwork. A window opening above. A woman calling, “Police!” though the largest object in the struggle was already one. Another whistle. Running boots.

When order returned, Rabbit lay face downward, Otto sat dazed against a doorstep, and Keating leaned upon a wall breathing like an elderly locomotive.

Spider stood in the middle of the street with the stocking in his hand.

Keating stared at him.

“Buffin!”

Spider saw the position and disliked every part of it.

“Um.”

“You saved my life.”

“No.”

“You did!”

“Didn’t.”

“You came at them like a tiger.”

Spider glanced at Otto and Rabbit. Their expressions indicated that zoological comparisons would be remembered.

Two uniformed constables arrived. Keating placed a hand upon Spider’s shoulder, this time affectionately.

“This man,” he announced, “is a hero.”

Spider closed his eyes.

Respectable life had trapped him.

The next morning the newspapers improved the affair. “EX-CONVICT’S BRAVERY” appeared in one. Another preferred “REFORMED MAN ROUTS THUGS.” A third, possessing greater literary resources, described Spider as “a slight but lion-hearted figure whose courageous intervention undoubtedly prevented tragedy.”

The Frith Street Gang read newspapers.

At the police court Otto and Rabbit stood in the dock with the stern patience of men awaiting a temporary legal inconvenience. Spider was called as a witness.

He had spent the night considering his duty. His first duty was to himself. His second was to prevent Sid Marks believing him an informer. His third, which existed only in theory, was to tell the truth.

He entered the witness-box.

The magistrate was an elderly gentleman with silver hair, a gold watch-chain, and a faith in moral transformation. Keating gave his evidence simply. He had been attacked without provocation. He would have been overwhelmed but for Buffin, who had flung himself upon the assailants with no concern for personal safety.

Spider was then invited to identify the prisoners.

He looked at Otto.

Otto looked at him.

He looked at Rabbit.

Rabbit’s nose moved once.

“Never seen ’em,” said Spider.

The magistrate peered over his spectacles.

“You have never seen these men?”

“No, sir.”

“Yet Constable Keating states that you struck the larger prisoner.”

“Never touched ’im.”

“Why, then, was he found beneath you?”

“Coincidence.”

The magistrate frowned. He was accustomed to falsehood, but preferred it with more furniture.

“Did these men attack the constable?”

“No, sir.”

“Who did?”

“Nobody.”

Keating spoke from behind. “Come now, Buffin. No need to be afraid.”

Spider turned with pain in his eyes.

“I ain’t afraid.”

“Quite right,” said the magistrate warmly. “Your actions prove that. But modesty must not be permitted to obstruct justice.”

Spider reflected that justice appeared perfectly capable of obstructing itself.

The magistrate continued.

“You are reluctant, no doubt, to send fellow creatures to punishment. That does credit to your heart. But the court has heard ample evidence. Your courage, your self-sacrifice, and your extraordinary humanity form a combination which I wish were more common.”

Spider lowered his head. To the court this appeared modest. In reality he had seen Sid Marks at the back of the room.

Sid’s face was expressionless.

That was the worst expression it possessed.

The magistrate leaned forward.

“You have had, I understand, an unfortunate past?”

“Um.”

“But you have redeemed it. Let this be an example. No man is beyond hope. Beneath the roughest exterior may burn the pure flame of duty.”

Spider’s exterior was not particularly rough, and the only flame within him was a desire to set fire to the building.

Keating shook his hand when the case was over.

“Proud of you, lad.”

“Um.”

“Come round Sunday. My sister’ll be pleased to meet you.”

“Um.”

“You’re a different man.”

“Um.”

This was true. Spider was now a man with perhaps two hours to leave London.

He departed by a side door. During the magistrate’s address, when all eyes had rested upon the paragon in the witness-box, Spider’s fingers had encountered the end of the gold watch-chain. Habit is the quietest of teachers. The watch now lay in his waistcoat pocket.

He went first to his lodging. His possessions were not numerous, but poverty does not prevent sacrifice; it merely makes packing easier. He collected two shirts, a razor, a pair of boots, a silver cigarette case belonging formerly to a commercial traveller, and a photograph of a young woman whose identity he had forgotten.

At a pawnbroker’s in Gray’s Inn Road he disposed of the cigarette case, the better boots, his overcoat, and the magistrate’s watch.

The pawnbroker examined the watch.

“Family property?”

“Um.”

“Your family?”

“Sort of.”

The sum offered was an outrage against commerce, ancestry, and precious metal, but Spider accepted it. Sid’s decisions, unlike railway trains, did not wait for passengers.

At King’s Cross he purchased a ticket to the farthest place whose fare appeared reasonable. Then he changed his mind. Railway stations might be watched. London, according to his present calculations, contained Sid Marks, Otto the Sausage when released, Rabbit Butler when released, and Constable Keating with an invitation to Sunday dinner. No city could support so many dangers without injury to the health.

Spider descended into the Tube.

A train roared from the tunnel, bringing warm wind, sparks, and the promise of elsewhere. He entered a carriage and sat beside a stout gentleman reading the evening paper.

On the front page was Spider’s photograph beneath the words:

HERO OF LITTLE WARNER STREET.

The stout gentleman glanced from the photograph to Spider. Spider turned up his collar.

The train moved.

At the next station the gentleman discovered that his wallet had gone. By then Spider Buffin, redeemed citizen, public benefactor, and pure flame of duty, was already changing lines.

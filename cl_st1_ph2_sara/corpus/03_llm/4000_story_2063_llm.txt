There is nothing, according to Henry, that so assists a man in discovering his affection for a woman as the suspicion that another man has discovered it first.

“A woman may be all that’s desirable,” said Henry, “and a man may know it—in a general way, as he knows the Louvre is full of pictures; but unless somebody else begins pushing towards the entrance he’ll very likely postpone his visit. Competition puts him forrarder. It’s the same with bargains, religions, and the last train home.”

We were sitting outside a little café near the Luxembourg Gardens, where Henry, in earlier and more active days, had been a waiter. He had not lost the manner. Though no longer responsible for my refreshment, he continued to watch my glass and the passing public with equal solicitude.

“That is a cynical view of love,” I said.

“It’s a practical view,” answered Henry. “Cynicism is only practical sense spoken by a chap you don’t like. Besides, I’m not saying competition creates the article. There must be something there to begin with. You can fan a spark, but you can’t fan a brick. I once saw a case—Tom Sleight and Marie Godselle.”

“French?”

“Not quite all,” said Henry.

He waited until a cab had rattled past, then leaned back and folded his hands upon his waistcoat.

“Marie was French by arrangement. By birth she was Mary, which is different. Her father was a French Canadian who had settled in New Hampshire and married an American woman; so Mary had inherited a little of three nations and trusted none of ’em. She spoke French from her father, English from her mother, and plain truth from herself when she happened to be in the humour for it.

“She was fourteen when she married Tom Sleight.”

“Fourteen!”

“There you are,” said Henry. “You interrupt before I’ve had time to say it was quite legal. Leastways, they told me it was, and the people whose business it was to object apparently didn’t. New Hampshire was a broad-minded place in those days concerning other people’s mistakes.

“Tom was nineteen, maybe twenty. A sailor-man. You know what a sailor-man is at nineteen. If you don’t, you’ve been more fortunate in your acquaintances than most. He’d been ashore six days, and in those six days he’d fought three men, bought a dog, lost the dog, promised to take Mary round the world, and married her secretly. That’s pushing on, even for a sailor.

“They were married by an elderly minister living some miles out of the town. Tom had persuaded Mary that her relations would come round afterwards, as relations always do in the calculations of people who aren’t going to have the trouble of bringing ’em. He gave her an onyx brooch before they started. It was an ugly little thing—a black oval with a white line across it, set in gold thin enough to have been ashamed of itself. But he’d bought it with his own money, and she wore it under her cloak while the minister was joining them.

“‘Now you’re Mrs. Sleight,’ says Tom when they came out.

“‘Am I different?’ says she.

“‘Entirely,’ says he.

“She believed him. At fourteen you believe a man when he tells you you’re entirely different. Later on you generally know better, though you may prefer him to continue saying it.

“They were to meet next morning and arrange how the world was to be informed. Tom’s ship was sailing in three days, but he meant, as I understand, to return from his voyage loaded with wealth, whiskers, and parental approval. That night, however, he went into a drinking saloon with two shipmates. There was a disagreement concerning a card game, though Tom afterwards maintained it concerned a lady. A sailor likes his brawls to have a romantic origin. Anyhow, somebody hit him with a belaying-pin, which was a nautical touch the saloon-keeper happened to keep behind the bar.

“Tom was carried unconscious to his ship. The captain, who was short of hands and long in authority, had him put below. By the time Tom knew his own name they were out at sea and New Hampshire was a grey line behind them.

“‘Why didn’t he go ashore?’ I asked.

Henry looked at me patiently.

“Ships,” he explained, “when they are out at sea, don’t commonly stop to let a young man walk home because he has remembered he’s married. Tom wrote. He wrote to Mary; he wrote to the minister; he wrote to a ship-chandler who knew her father; and when they reached Liverpool he wrote again. Not one answer came.

“There were reasons. The first letters went astray because he addressed them to Mrs. Thomas Sleight, and no such person was known at the Godselles’ house. One reached Mary’s father, who opened it, understood enough to become violent, and burnt it. The minister had died between marrying them and receiving Tom’s inquiry—a want of consideration, but elderly gentlemen will do it. The ship-chandler had moved to Boston, and Tom’s second lot of letters were sent from ports where the post was treated as a speculation rather than a service.

“Mary, meanwhile, had waited the next morning; then the next afternoon; then for three days. After that she learnt Tom had been carried aboard drunk and injured, which became, in the telling, merely drunk. His ship had sailed. No message had come to her. Her father got enough of the truth out of her to know there had been a marriage, and he made what you may call the most of it.

“‘He is ashamed of you,’ he told her. ‘He married a child for sport, and now he has run away.’

“That was cruel.”

“It was,” said Henry. “Cruelty generally is when it happens to be accurate in the one place where it oughtn’t to be. Tom hadn’t run away on purpose; but from Mary’s point of view, if you understand, the difference was administrative.

“Her father took her to Montreal. Later they went to France, where he had relatives near Dieppe. He died when she was eighteen, leaving little except debts and a conviction that all sailors were scamps. Mary had already acquired that conviction for herself, so there wasn’t much in the way of inheritance.

“Tom came back after two years. He searched. I’ll say that for him. He went to the Godselles’ old house, advertised, questioned everybody who could remember the family, and found the minister’s widow. She produced a page from her husband’s register, which proved him married but didn’t tell him where his wife was. He spent money and temper, both freely. Then his ship sailed again.

“After a while the searching became less vigorous. That happens. A grief may remain heavy while the arms carrying it grow tired. Tom told himself Mary must be dead; then that she had gone away because she wished to forget him; then that, being only fourteen, she had perhaps not regarded the business seriously. A man can generally find a theory that leaves him room to dine.

“He went on sailing. He became mate, then had a difference with a captain, then tried trading on his own account, then lost most of what he had in a cargo that got itself wet. At twenty-eight he inherited his father’s estate in England, which wasn’t a great estate, but it was enough to make work seem an affectation. So he left the sea, bought clothes, and came to Paris to learn how a gentleman spends an income without looking as if he is learning.

“That was when I knew him.”

“At your café?”

“At my café. Rue des Écoles—not one of the grand places where the waiters despise you in six languages, but a cheerful little establishment with tables under an awning, red cushions, and a proprietor who watered the wine only when business was bad. I was head waiter, though Monsieur Baptiste called me second waiter when wages were discussed.

“Marie worked there. Mary, as I’ve told you, but Marie to us. She was twenty-seven then, tallish, dark-haired, and not what painters call regular. Painters like a face they can measure; men prefer one they can remember. She had grey eyes that looked blue when she was amused and almost black when she wasn’t. She could carry six glasses in one hand and make a customer apologise for spilling one at another table.

“She had been in Paris four years. She told people she was unmarried.”

“She was not, strictly speaking.”

“Strictly speaking,” said Henry, “is the manner in which nobody speaks about marriage. She had married, certainly. Whether she had ever been married—in the sense of sharing breakfast, expenses, and opinions—was another question.

“Tom came in one wet evening in April. He had put on weight since New Hampshire, grown a moustache, and lost the scar near his temple under his hair. Mary knew him before he had taken off his gloves.

“He didn’t know her.

“You think that unlikely?”

“I was about to say—”

“You needn’t. A girl of fourteen in a cotton dress and a woman of twenty-seven in a Paris café are two different propositions to a man. Besides, Tom had spent thirteen years remembering Mary as a child. Memory is a poor portrait-painter. It keeps the eyes and changes everything round ’em.

“Marie went white. I saw it. Then she caught hold of the back of a chair and asked him what he would take.

“‘Coffee,’ says Tom, ‘and cognac.’

“‘Together?’

“‘Not in the same cup,’ says he.

“That was his idea of a joke. She brought the things and stood looking at him.

“‘Have we met?’ he asks.

“‘Would you remember if we had?’ says she.

“‘I hope so.’

“‘Then we have not,’ says Marie.

“She went into the service room and dropped a spoon. Marie never dropped spoons. I followed her.

“‘Who is he?’ I asked.

“‘My husband,’ says she.

“‘Ah,’ I says. ‘Does he know?’

“‘You heard him.’

“‘It’s a bit complicated.’

“‘Not at all. He has forgotten me.’

“‘That simplifies his side of it,’ I said.

“She looked as if she might throw the spoon, so I went back to Tom.

“He came again the next day, and the day after. At first it was because he liked the coffee. Men often begin with coffee when they mean the waitress. By the end of the week he was taking meals he didn’t want at hours inconvenient to digestion.

“Marie served him without favour. If he tried to talk, she became occupied. If he grew silent, she asked whether he was unwell. This encouraged him. Nothing encourages a man so much as being made to suppose encouragement is difficult.

“Then there was young Jules, a clerk from a bookseller’s shop, who had admired Marie for six months without getting forrarder than borrowing matches. Marie began to smile at Jules.

“‘You have a pleasant smile,’ Tom told her one afternoon.

“‘Monsieur Jules thinks so,’ she said.

“‘Monsieur Jules is easily pleased.’

“‘Then perhaps he will make a good husband.’

“Tom stirred his coffee for some time, though the sugar had vanished.

“‘A bookseller’s clerk?’ says he.

“‘A man’s a man.’

“‘That depends on the man.’

“‘And who is to decide?’

“‘A woman, apparently,’ says Tom.

“After that he doubled his attentions. He brought flowers, which Marie placed upon Jules’s table because, as she explained, there wasn’t room on Tom’s. He gave her chocolates, and she shared them with an elderly commercial traveller from Lyons. The traveller had no teeth worth mentioning, but he had gallantry, and gallantry will undertake what teeth cannot.

“‘She’s flirting with the whole place,’ Tom complained to me.

“‘Not quite the whole place,’ I said. ‘She’s done nothing with the proprietor.’

“‘He’s married.’

“‘So I believe.’

“‘What does she see in that long-faced clerk?’

“‘Maybe she sees he’s looking at her.’

“‘I look at her.’

“‘Then maybe she sees two men looking at her.’

“That didn’t comfort him.

“Now, mind you, Marie was not enjoying herself as much as she pretended. There are women who can make a man jealous for the pleasure of seeing him miserable; and there are women who do it to learn whether he is capable of being miserable. Marie belonged to the second sort, though on busy evenings she may have borrowed something from the first.

“She wanted to punish him. That was natural. She wanted him to recognise her without being told. That was unreasonable, but also natural. Most of all, she wanted to find out what kind of man he had become. She had carried the thought of him for thirteen years as one carries a locked box—sometimes hating the weight, sometimes wondering what is inside. Now the box had walked into the café, ordered cognac, and failed to know her.

“‘He is inconstant,’ she told me.

“‘To whom?’

“‘To his wife.’

“‘By falling in love with you?’

“‘He believes I am another woman.’

“‘But you know you aren’t.’

“‘That makes it worse.’

“‘For him or for you?’

“She said there were times when my English logic was vulgar.

“Tom, for his part, became serious. He ceased wearing the brighter of his waistcoats, which in a man of his temperament was equivalent to entering a monastery. He followed Marie with his eyes. He waited outside after closing, and she sent Jules home beside her. He invited her to the theatre; she said she disliked theatres, then went the following night with a party including the commercial traveller from Lyons.

“‘He must be sixty,’ Tom said.

“‘His heart is younger,’ says Marie.

“‘His knees aren’t.’

“‘I am not marrying his knees.’

“At last Tom asked me whether she was engaged.

“‘Not that I know of.’

“‘Does she care for any of those fellows?’

“‘A waiter’s paid to know what a customer wants,’ I said, ‘not what a woman does. There wouldn’t be money enough.’

“‘Do you think I have a chance?’

“‘There’s all the makings of a good husband in you.’

“He brightened.

“‘You really think so?’

“‘Makings aren’t the finished article,’ I said. ‘There’s the makings of soup in a butcher’s cart.’

“He called me an infernal pessimist and tipped me five francs. Tom was generous when contradicted. Agreement he took as his due.

“One night, after the shutters were up, Marie remained behind. I was counting the spoons, there having been an English family in, and with English families you count the spoons before and after from habit rather than accusation. Tom knocked at the glass. Marie let him in.

“He stood there with rain on his shoulders, looking less polished than usual and more honest.

“‘Marie,’ says he, ‘I can’t go on like this.’

“‘Then stop,’ says she.

“‘You know what I mean.’

“‘A woman never knows what a man means until he has said it, and after that she only knows what he has said.’

“‘I love you.’

“She turned away. I could see her face in the mirror, but Tom couldn’t. For one second she looked fourteen. Then it passed.

“‘How long?’ she asked.

“‘How long what?’

“‘How long have you loved me?’

“‘From the first evening.’

“‘Before Monsieur Jules?’

“‘Yes.’

“‘You did not show it.’

“‘I didn’t understand it.’

“‘And Monsieur Jules explained?’

“‘Confound Monsieur Jules!’

“‘That is not an answer.’

“‘Very well. Seeing those fools round you made me realise—’

“‘That another man wanted what you had overlooked.’

“‘I had not overlooked you.’

“‘No,’ she said, very quietly. ‘You did not look at all.’

“He missed that, naturally. A man in love hears the general music but loses some of the words.

“He told her about his estate, his prospects, the house in Sussex he might buy, the travelling they could do. A sailor-man makes love with his hour of departure always before his mind. He wants the matter settled, the luggage labelled, and the lady aboard before the tide changes.

“Marie listened. Then she asked:

“‘Have you ever loved another woman?’

“Tom stopped.

“There are questions to which a man’s hesitation is the honest part of his answer.

“‘Yes,’ he said.

“‘And what happened?’

“‘It was long ago.’

“‘That is when most things happened.’

“‘I was a boy.’

“‘Were you married to her?’

“He looked at me. I had become extremely occupied with the spoons.

“‘Who told you?’ he asked.

“‘No one. You have the face of a man who is remembering a duty.’

“‘I believed her dead.’

“‘Believed?’

“‘I had no proof.’

“‘Then she may be alive.’

“‘She may.’

“‘And you ask me to marry you?’

“‘The marriage may not have been valid.’

“Marie smiled, but not pleasantly.

“‘When it suited you, was it valid?’

“‘Yes.’

“‘Then it remains valid until something more useful than your wishes makes it otherwise.’

“Tom began walking up and down between the tables. He told the story—not all at once, but with additions, defences, and explanations. He described the secret wedding, the fight, the ship sailing, the letters, his searches. He admitted he had grown tired. That was the best thing he said.

“‘I failed her,’ he finished. ‘Not willingly at first. Afterwards—perhaps. I let time do what I hadn’t the courage to do myself.’

“Marie’s hand went to her collar, where there was no brooch.

“‘What was her name?’

“‘Mary Godselle.’

“‘Was she pretty?’

“‘She was a child.’

“‘Children may be pretty.’

“‘She had grey eyes.’

“‘Blue?’

“‘Sometimes.’

“Marie glanced at me.

“‘And if you found her?’ she asked.

“‘I don’t know.’

“‘You must know before you speak to me again.’

“‘If she has made another life—’

“‘You must find it.’

“‘If she hates me?’

“‘You must hear her say so.’

“‘If she wants money?’

“‘You must give it.’

“‘And if she is dead?’

“‘You must know.’

“Tom stood still.

“‘And afterwards?’

“‘Afterwards,’ said Marie, ‘you may come back and tell me what sort of man you found yourself to be.’

“He left next morning for America.”

I interrupted Henry at this point.

“Surely she had only to tell him.”

“Of course,” said Henry. “And a magistrate has only to tell a prisoner the sentence without putting on a wig; but people like ceremonies where their feelings are concerned. Marie didn’t merely want Tom. She had wanted him at fourteen and had seen what came of that. She wanted evidence.

“Besides, she had been deserted—mistakenly, accidentally, through a belaying-pin and the post office, but deserted all the same. You can explain to a woman that a wound was accidental; it doesn’t make the scar ornamental.

“She guided him, though.”

“How?”

“Carefully. Before leaving Paris he came to the café every day, asking questions. Marie recalled things she was not supposed to know.

“‘The Godselles had relatives in Montreal, perhaps?’ she would say.

“‘I never heard so.’

“‘Canadians often have relatives in Montreal. It is one of the things Montreal is for.’

“Or she’d mention Dieppe.

“‘A French Canadian family might return through Dieppe. Ships go there.’

“‘How do you know?’

“‘I read.’

“She also told him to write to the parish priest in a village outside Dieppe—the very village where she and her father had lived. She said a customer had once spoken of the place. The customer was herself, but Tom did not ask.

“He sailed from Havre. The first letter came from New York three weeks later, addressed to Marie at the café. She read it in the service room, twice, then gave it to me.

“‘My dear Marie,’ it began. ‘I have reached New York and go north tomorrow. I have thought much of what you said. You were right.’

“‘That’s panning out well,’ I said. ‘A man who begins a letter by saying a woman was right has either improved or wants something.’

“‘Read on,’ says she.

“He had found the old ship-chandler in Boston. The man remembered Mary’s father taking her away after what he called a scandal. He thought they had gone to Canada. Tom was travelling to Montreal.

“The next letter said he had discovered a cousin of Monsieur Godselle. The cousin remembered them, but had quarrelled with the father and knew only that they had sailed for France.

“‘He is coming nearer,’ I said.

“Marie folded the letter.

“‘Yes.’

“She wasn’t triumphant. That surprised me. For months she had been pushing him along the road; now that he was coming she looked round for somewhere to hide.

“A third letter arrived from Dieppe. Tom had found the village and an old woman who remembered Mary. The old woman said Mary’s father was dead and Mary had gone to Paris to work.

“‘He asks for the address,’ Marie said.

“‘Will she give it?’

“‘She has forgotten.’

“‘Convenient.’

“‘She is eighty-three.’

“‘At eighty-three,’ I said, ‘forgetfulness becomes almost a profession.’

“Tom remained in Dieppe a fortnight, questioning people. He sent Marie pages about Mary. It was queer reading. He had begun the journey for Marie’s sake, but Mary grew real to him as he searched. He wrote of the child waiting at the meeting place; of his letters burnt or lost; of the shame she must have borne alone. He did not excuse himself so much now. Travel may broaden a man’s mind, but finding out the consequences of his conduct broadens it quicker.

“Marie answered every letter. Short answers, mostly.

“‘Continue,’ she wrote.

“Or, ‘You have not yet found her.’

“Once she wrote, ‘Do not pity her too much. Pity is a form of vanity when we use it to show ourselves how tender we are.’

“‘That’s hard,’ I told her.

“‘He was hard.’

“‘At nineteen.’

“‘I was fourteen.’

“You couldn’t get round that.

“Then came news from Paris itself. Tom had returned without calling on us. He had employed an inquiry agent, a little dried-up fellow who could discover anything except who owed him money. The agent traced Mary from Dieppe to Rouen, from Rouen to Paris, and through two dressmaking establishments, a lodging-house in Montparnasse, and a family where she had been companion to an invalid aunt. After that the trail stopped at the café district.

“Tom wrote that he believed his wife still lived in Paris.

“Marie took to making mistakes. She gave a German professor the proprietor’s luncheon and charged Jules for three cognacs he had not drunk. Jules paid, thinking it an advance in their relationship.

“‘You must finish it,’ I said.

“‘I am afraid.’

“‘Of Tom?’

“‘Of Mary.’

“‘You are Mary.’

“‘That is what I am afraid of.’

“I understood her, though not immediately. Marie had made a life. It wasn’t a grand life, but it was hers. She earned her wages, chose her friends, and could shut her door upon the world. Mary Sleight was a girl who had waited by a frozen road for a husband who didn’t come. To put on Mary again was to put on the waiting.

“Tom’s next letter was different. He had learned from the inquiry agent that a woman answering Mary’s description had once sought legal advice about a missing husband. She had made no application to declare him dead and had never attempted another marriage.

“‘Why not?’ Tom wrote. ‘If she hated me, she might have freed herself. If she cared, why did she not search? I don’t understand her.’

“‘That’s reasonable,’ I said.

“‘Is it?’

“‘No man understands a woman merely because he married her. Marriage provides opportunity, not ability.’

“Marie wrote him a longer answer that night.

“She told him a woman might neither hate nor forgive; might refuse to search because she had searched once in every face; might remain married not from hope, but because a promise, even a foolish promise, had become part of her pride. Then she told him to walk the Tuileries Gardens the following Tuesday at four o’clock, beside the long basin, and to look for a woman wearing an onyx brooch.

“He came to the café on Monday.

“I hardly knew him at first. He was thinner and brown from travel, and that smooth, well-fed look he’d brought to Paris had worn off. He asked for Marie.

“‘She isn’t here.’

“‘Will she come?’

“‘Not today.’

“He sat at his old table.

“‘I may have found my wife,’ he said.

“‘So I heard.’

“‘Marie told you?’

“‘She tells me most things after she has decided I can’t prevent them.’

“He looked towards the service-room door.

“‘I’ve thought of nothing but Mary for weeks. Yet every road towards Mary seems to have been pointed out by Marie. I don’t know what I feel any longer.’

“‘That needn’t trouble you,’ I said. ‘A man’s feelings aren’t improved by his knowing their names.’

“‘If Mary wants me back, what am I to say to Marie?’

“‘The truth.’

“‘Which truth?’

“‘Ah,’ I said. ‘That’s your difficulty.’

“He took a little case from his pocket and opened it. Inside was an opal brooch set with small seed pearls.

“‘I bought this for Marie before I left. I meant to give it to her when I came back free.’

“‘And now?’

“‘Now I have no right.’

“He shut the case.

“‘Will you give it to her?’

“‘No.’

“‘Why not?’

“‘I’m a waiter, not Providence.’

“In the end he left it on the table. I carried it to Marie that evening.

“She opened the case and watched the opal change colour under the lamp.

“‘It is beautiful,’ she said.

“‘More expensive than the other, I should judge.’

“Her head came up.

“‘What other?’

“‘The onyx one.’

“‘How do you know about that?’

“‘You told me.’

“‘I did not.’

“‘Then you meant to. With women, one has to count intentions among the facts.’

“She went to a little box and brought out the onyx brooch. It was as I’ve described: black, severe, old-fashioned, and not worth much to anyone except the two people whose lives it had helped to confuse.

“She laid the opal beside it.

“‘He gave this to Marie,’ she said, touching the opal. ‘He gave that to Mary.’

“‘Yes.’

“‘He loves Marie.’

“‘He says so.’

“‘He is seeking Mary.’

“‘He has found her, if she’ll stand still.’

“She walked to the window. It was raining, as it had rained on the night Tom first came in. Paris does its best work in drizzle; it gives people an excuse for not seeing clearly.

“‘Suppose he is disappointed,’ she said.

“‘In which of you?’

“‘In Mary. He remembers a child.’

“‘He fell in love with the woman.’

“‘He thinks they are different.’

“‘So do you, half the time.’

“She was silent.

“‘Suppose,’ she said, ‘he feels trapped.’

“‘He has travelled several thousand miles towards the trap.’

“‘For Marie’s sake.’

“‘And for Mary’s.’

“‘He may choose out of duty.’

“‘Most husbands do from time to time. It doesn’t prove they’ve ceased choosing from affection on other days.’

“She picked up the opal brooch and held it against her dress. It flashed green and rose in the lamp-light. Then she fastened the onyx beside it.

“‘I could wear both,’ she said.

“‘You could.’

“‘Then he would understand at once.’

“‘Maybe.’

“‘Why do you say it like that?’

“‘Because understanding at once is not always the same as discovering. If you wear both, you tell him. If you wear only the onyx, he must look at your face and find Marie for himself.’

“She stood before the mirror. The two brooches were side by side: the opal, all changing colour and Paris brightness; the onyx, dark and plain, the poor little token of a sailor lad who had possessed more haste than sense.

“‘Which should I wear, Henry?’ she asked.

“‘The onyx.’

“‘Not the opal?’

“‘No.’

“‘Why?’

“‘Because he gave the opal to the woman he wanted,’ I said. ‘Let him find out for himself that she is the wife he came to make amends to.’

“She unfastened the opal and put it back in its case.

“Then she pinned the onyx at her throat, took up her gloves, and went out to meet him in the Tuileries.”

The road beyond the meeting-house had been softening since noon, though the snow still lay in gray-white strips along the stone walls and under the hemlocks. In the open fields the earth showed sodden and dun-colored, with here and there a black ridge where the ploughing of the autumn before had risen through the thaw. Mrs. Trimble’s wagon went heavily. The wheels gathered such thick rings of clay that Rebecca Wright had twice proposed getting down to find a stick, but each time the old horse, after a moment’s consideration, had pulled them free.

“He knows we’ve started home,” said Mrs. Trimble. “There ain’t nothin’ gives a creatur’ more strength than knowin’ where it’s bound. I felt so myself all through Mis’ Barlow’s address. She meant well, but when she got to the foreign heathen the third time I begun to think of my kitchen fire.”

Rebecca laughed. She sat very straight, holding the ends of her black woolen shawl together with one hand. Her bonnet was a little too youthful in shape for her grave, narrow face, but she had bought it because the ribbons were good and could afterward be turned.

“I don’t know but we’ve heathen enough nearer by,” she said. “I always think so when there’s a meetin’ to tell us what folks ought to do in places nobody present has ever seen.”

“Now, Rebecca, you’re severe. There was eight dollars and thirty-seven cents taken.”

“So there was, and Deacon Lunt put in a five-cent piece twice, because the first time he took out a dime by mistake.”

Mrs. Trimble turned upon her companion a look of reproachful amusement.

“You see too much,” she declared. “It’s a gift, but I don’t know’s it makes you any happier.”

“I never expected gifts to do that,” said Rebecca.

They had come from the afternoon meeting of the Women’s Aid Society, held in the vestry because the church itself was still damp from the winter and smelled strongly of fresh plaster. There had been reports, prayers, a paper concerning missions, and a supper of baked beans, brown bread, and seven kinds of cake. The women had remained late, comparing the spring prospects and the sicknesses of the neighborhood; and now the sun, already low, shone coldly under a bank of cloud.

Mrs. Trimble was a comfortable woman, broad in face and person, well wrapped in a fur cape which had belonged to her mother. Her husband owned good upland pasture, a woodlot, and two houses in the village besides the one they occupied. It was not that she thought herself rich; there were people in town who kept a hired girl the year round, while Mrs. Trimble had assistance only at haying and during preserves. Yet she had never lacked dry wood, sound shoes, or the certainty of tomorrow’s dinner, and these blessings had grown so customary that she seldom named them, even in prayer.

They passed the burying-ground, where the snow had melted first from the southern slope. The thin slate stones stood in uneven rows, and one recent mound looked raw and lonely.

“They’ve set up Elder Bowden’s stone,” said Mrs. Trimble.

“It leans already.”

“Land’s settled under it, I presume. I thought the elder’s funeral was real affecting. When Parson Dow said the dead should rise, Mis’ Cummings begun to cry out loud.”

“She always does. I couldn’t help thinkin’,” Rebecca added, “that if all those old Bowdens got up together, packed as close as they are in that lot, they’d come like pole beans.”

“Rebecca!”

“Well, they would. I don’t say it irreverent. Resurrection’s got to be practical, same as anything else.”

The wagon creaked down a long incline. At its foot the road forked, the better-traveled way turning toward the village, while a narrow branch went across low meadows to the northern farms. Mrs. Trimble drew up.

“If we went by the Janes place,” she said slowly, “we could see Ann and Mandana Bray. It ain’t above a mile and a half farther.”

Rebecca’s quick eyes turned toward her.

“I was wonderin’ if you’d thought of ’em.”

“I have thought of ’em a good deal, but I ain’t been to see ’em since they was carried over there. I’m ashamed to own it. There’s always been somethin’—snow, or sickness, or the road broke up, or company expected.”

“There’s generally somethin’ between us and what we ought to do.”

Mrs. Trimble flicked the reins lightly. “We’ll go.”

The old horse appeared less certain of the new direction. The road was narrow and badly drained, and in places the wheels sank to the hubs. Water stood in the cart-ruts, reflecting a pale sky. Beside a flooded pasture a flock of sheep crowded upon one dry rise of ground, their wool dark and ragged from the winter. Farther on, a deserted house leaned toward its fallen ell, and dead morning-glory vines still clung about the kitchen window in a frail brown net.

“I always connect morning-glories with the Brays,” said Rebecca. “Ann had ’em clear over the shed roof. Blue ones, mostly.”

“And that white rose by the front door,” Mrs. Trimble answered. “I had a slip from it once, but it didn’t live. Their place was the prettiest little spot on the crossroad. Poor creatures! It went under the hammer for less than the new horse sheds cost.”

“The town didn’t buy the horse sheds neither,” said Rebecca. “Folks paid by subscription because everybody wanted ’em. The Brays was everybody’s business, so they come to be nobody’s.”

Mrs. Trimble made no answer. She knew the history, as all the town knew it, but facts which have been often repeated may remain light and harmless until some sudden circumstance gives them their proper weight. Ann and Mandana Bray had lived all their lives in a small farmhouse left by their father. They had owned twelve acres, two cows, a few apple trees, and furniture which, though plain, had been kept with care. Mandana had been lame since childhood; Ann, the stronger and more capable, had worked indoors and out until an overturning wagon injured her shoulder and arm. Debts followed, then illness, then the sale. At the public auction their feather beds, chairs, dishes, and even the old brass candlesticks had been scattered among neighbors who spoke compassionately while they bid.

Because neither sister could maintain herself, the selectmen had arranged for them to be boarded by Mr. Silas Janes, whose offer was the lowest. The arrangement was called economical and temporary. It had now lasted sixteen months.

“I heard Mis’ Janes was a proper kind of woman,” said Mrs. Trimble, as if answering some inward accusation. “She come from over Ridgeton way. I don’t know her well.”

“She’s proper enough, maybe. A board can be proper, if it’s planed straight.”

“Silas has had hard luck. Two cows died, and his oldest boy went off.”

“He’d have stayed if there’d been anything to stay for. Look there.”

They had gained the top of a low hill. Below them stood the Janes farmhouse, a large, unpainted structure with a sagging roof and a chimney from which no smoke appeared. The barn doors hung unevenly. A broken sled lay on its side near the woodpile, though the woodpile itself was hardly more than a scattering of wet sticks and old fence rails. Three hens scratched in the mud by the doorstep; one had lost most of its tail feathers and lifted its feet as if the ground offended it.

There had once been an orchard behind the house, but half the trees were dead. The remaining branches showed black against the snow in the hollows.

“It looks discouraged,” Mrs. Trimble said under her breath.

“House and folks together, I expect.”

They drove into the yard. No one appeared until Mrs. Trimble called; then the door opened, and a thin woman in a faded dress looked out. Her hair was dragged tightly back, and a baby rested against one shoulder. Two other children peered from behind her skirts.

“You’re Mis’ Janes?” Mrs. Trimble asked pleasantly. “I’m Mis’ Trimble, from the village, and this is Rebecca Wright. We thought we’d stop and see the Bray sisters.”

Mrs. Janes shifted the baby. Her face showed neither welcome nor refusal, but only the guarded weariness of one to whom every arrival may bring a new demand.

“They’re in the back room,” she said. “You can go round the end. The kitchen’s all tore up. Seth spilled the lye, and I ain’t had a minute.”

“We won’t trouble you,” said Mrs. Trimble.

“They’ve been middlin’. Ann’s arm’s bad again. There ain’t much anybody can do for it.” She glanced at the wagon and then at the visitors’ clothes. “Town pays me when it gets round to it. I don’t know’s you’ve heard, but we ain’t received a cent since January.”

“That’s too bad,” Mrs. Trimble answered, though the words seemed feeble.

“It takes victuals to keep folks, sick or well. Silas says he’ll speak to the selec’men once more, and after that he won’t. I can’t make meal out o’ promises.”

The baby began to cry, and Mrs. Janes closed the door with an apologetic abruptness.

For a moment the two visitors sat still.

“She looks poorly,” said Mrs. Trimble.

“She looks pinched down to the bone.”

“I don’t believe she means to be hard.”

“No. She ain’t got enough strength left to mean much of anything.”

They drove around the north end of the house, where the yard narrowed between a wall and a thicket of leafless lilacs. A separate door opened into what had perhaps been a bedroom or shed-room. Its lower boards were rotted, and a strip of old carpet had been tacked along the sill to keep out the wind. Rebecca climbed down first. Her shoes sank into the thawed earth, and she held the wheel while Mrs. Trimble descended.

There was no step. A flat stone lay before the door, half buried in mud.

Rebecca knocked.

For a few seconds there was silence; then they heard a chair move, a weak exclamation, and a voice whose brightness startled them.

“Why, somebody’s come! Mandana, somebody’s come to see us!”

The door opened only partway. Ann Bray stood within, holding it with her left hand. She was a small woman, delicately made, with white hair smoothed under a clean cap. Her right arm was bound close to her side by a strip of flannel, but her face shone with pleasure.

“Mis’ Trimble! And Rebecca Wright! Well, if this ain’t a sight for sore eyes! Come right in—come right in out o’ the damp. I’d have opened quicker, only this door drags, and I ain’t so handy as I used to be.”

Mrs. Trimble entered and stopped before she had crossed the threshold. The room was large enough, but almost empty. Its plaster was cracked, the floor bare and uneven. A small stove stood near the chimney, with one leg supported by a brick; the fire within it was so low that no warmth reached the farther corners. There were two narrow bedsteads, a deal table, three mismatched chairs, a wooden box, and a shelf bearing a few dishes. A patched quilt hung over one window, though the afternoon was not yet dark.

Mandana sat close to the stove, wrapped in a brown shawl. One of her feet, twisted by her old lameness, rested upon a low stool. She had Ann’s fine features, but her expression was sharper, and her eyes were clouded.

“I knew voices,” she said. “I can’t see who ’tis till they get near.”

Rebecca went to her and took both her hands.

“It’s Rebecca. I’d have come before if I’d been half as good a friend as I called myself.”

“Set down and don’t begin confessin’,” said Mandana. “We ain’t ministers. Ann, give Mis’ Trimble the chair with a back.”

“They all have backs, dear.”

“Well, give her the one that don’t pitch.”

Ann laughed as if Mandana had made the happiest joke imaginable. She took Mrs. Trimble’s cape and laid it carefully upon the bed, smoothing it with her sound hand.

“We’re so pleased,” she said. “I was tellin’ Mandana only this mornin’ that spring would bring somebody. Folks can’t travel while the drifts last, and the road’s been dreadful. You had an awful time gettin’ here, didn’t you?”

“Not so bad,” Mrs. Trimble replied.

“It’s bad enough,” said Rebecca. “I’ve got most of the north road on my shoes.”

Ann looked down. Upon her own feet were men’s worn cloth slippers, much too large, with pieces of string tied about the ankles. Mandana wore one old leather shoe and one stockinged foot wrapped in flannel.

Mrs. Trimble saw, and after that she seemed to see everything at once: the stove door, which would not shut; the damp stains upon the wall; the single comb on the shelf; a loaf no larger than her fist, wrapped in a towel; the cracked pitcher with its handle tied on; the hands of the two sisters, fine and almost transparent with cold.

“Your fire’s low,” she said.

Ann looked quickly toward the stove.

“It generally draws better. This is a kind of dull day.”

“There ain’t wood,” Mandana said.

“Now, sister—”

“There ain’t. She needn’t tell Mis’ Trimble the chimney’s dull. A chimney can’t burn ashes.”

“We’re expectin’ some,” Ann explained. “Silas has been busy, and the snow made haulin’ hard.”

“He brought us half a basket Monday,” said Mandana. “Wet alder and one piece of birch. Ann dried it in the oven, and the oven smoked.”

“Silas has had more than his share of trouble,” Ann said gently. “His horse is lame, and the children have all had colds. Mis’ Janes don’t get any rest, day nor night.”

Mrs. Trimble sat stiffly in the unreliable chair. She wished to ask many questions, but shame made them difficult. It seemed impertinent to inquire how little these women had, when their whole poverty stood visible before her.

“You didn’t get to meeting through the winter?” she asked at last.

Mandana gave a short laugh.

“We ain’t been to meetin’ since we come.”

“I thought perhaps somebody carried you.”

“Who?”

Ann hastened to soften the question. “There ain’t many teams goes by, and it would be a great deal of trouble. I don’t know how I’d manage with my arm, either. We hear about the sermons. Elvira Page sent us a paper Christmas, and there was a real good piece in it.”

“About resignation,” Mandana said.

“It was comfortin’.”

“To them that wrote it, likely.”

“Mandana gets low-spirited toward night,” Ann explained, still smiling. “She always did feel the cold. At home we had the south windows, and the sun would come in over the geraniums.”

Rebecca walked to the small window not covered by the quilt. The glass was blurred and dirty, but she could see the desolate orchard and the fields beyond. The room faced north and east; no full sunlight could reach it except at the longest summer mornings.

“I recollect your geraniums,” she said. “And the box of mignonette.”

“I saved some morning-glory seed,” Ann answered eagerly. “It’s in a paper in my workbag. I don’t know’s I shall have a place to put it, but seeds keep hope a long while. Father used to say you could carry a whole summer in your apron pocket.”

Mrs. Trimble bent her head.

“What did you do with your workbag?”

“Why, it’s here.” Ann brought from beneath the bed a faded green bag, its drawstrings carefully mended. “This is one thing we kept. There wasn’t much room in the wagon when we moved, and the auction took most everything. We each had a bundle and the feather tick, but the tick proved too wide for these bedsteads, so Silas sold it. He said he’d account to the town.”

“He gave us two dollars,” said Mandana. “A new tick cost six.”

“It wasn’t new.”

“It was ours.”

The last two words fell quietly, and there was no answer to them. Outside, one of the hens gave a harsh cry. A child ran past the window, dragging a stick along the weather-beaten boards.

Rebecca sat down beside Mandana.

“I bought your two sprigged cups at the sale,” she said abruptly.

Ann looked at her with glad surprise. “Did you, now?”

“I did. I hadn’t any business to. I’ve cups enough, and I knew they belonged with you. I was mad at the whole affair, and then I went and bid like the rest.”

“You always admired those cups,” said Ann. “Mother had six to begin with, but only two lasted. Little brown branches, and a pink flower shaped like a star.”

“With gold round the edge,” Mandana added.

“The gold’s most gone.”

“I can see ’em plain,” Mandana said. “I see old things better than new ones nowadays.”

“You’re goin’ to see those cups again,” Rebecca told her. “I’ll bring ’em.”

“Oh, no,” Ann protested. “They’re yours fairly. We were pleased you had ’em. We said so afterward.”

“I didn’t have ’em fairly. I had ’em legally, which ain’t always the same. I’ll bring ’em tomorrow.”

Mandana’s mouth trembled. She turned her face toward the stove.

Ann began to busy herself at the table. “You’ll have a little refreshment. We’ve got some crackers, and I believe there’s a mite of peach left. Mis’ Janes let us have hot water after dinner, so I can warm it again and make tea.”

“You mustn’t,” said Mrs. Trimble. “We’ve just come from a supper.”

“Then you won’t need much,” Ann answered cheerfully. “But you can’t come such a road and go away without tastin’ anything. ’Twouldn’t seem friendly.”

She lifted the stove lid with difficulty. Beneath it were two or three red coals. From the wooden box she took a handful of twigs no thicker than knitting needles.

Mrs. Trimble rose. “Let me do that.”

“No, no; I’m used to my left hand now.”

But the visitor took the twigs and laid them on the coals. The box was empty afterward except for bark and dust.

“Is that all your kindling?” she asked.

“There’s more outdoors,” Ann said.

“There ain’t,” said Mandana.

“Perhaps Silas used it. Well, he’ll cut some.”

Rebecca looked at Mrs. Trimble, and the look passed between them like a spoken pledge.

Ann set out four saucers. There were only two cups, one white and cracked, the other yellow earthenware without a handle. She poured a little tea into each and then, after a moment’s thought, brought two small bowls from the shelf.

“We often drink out of these,” she said. “They hold the heat real well.”

The crackers, six in number, were placed upon a plate. Last of all Ann took from a dark corner a small preserve jar. At the bottom lay one peach, broken into pieces, in a little amber syrup.

“Our last of the season,” she said with innocent pride. “They kept beautiful. I always used to put up forty quarts, but these was given us by Mrs. Eastman. Mandana, do you recollect the year our tree bore seven bushels?”

“Eight, near enough.”

“Father said seven.”

“Father never counted what dropped beyond the wall.”

Ann divided the peach among four saucers, adding syrup to make the portions equal. There was hardly a spoonful apiece.

Mrs. Trimble found herself unable to speak. She had eaten at the church supper until she had declined a second piece of frosted cake. The memory of long tables, crowded plates, and women pressing food upon one another seemed suddenly indecent. She looked at the fragment of peach before her and then at Ann, who stood waiting with anxious hospitality.

“Do taste it,” Ann urged. “I’m afraid the jar may have caught a little air.”

Mrs. Trimble ate. The peach was sweet and faintly spiced, and no richer food had ever humbled her.

“It’s excellent,” she said.

Ann’s face brightened. “I knew it was. Mandana thought it had worked, but she can’t smell so well as she used.”

“My smell’s good enough. I said the cover was loose.”

“You two always kept the best preserves in town,” Rebecca said.

“That was because we had time,” Ann answered. “If a woman has children underfoot and men comin’ in hungry, she can’t watch syrup by the minute. Mis’ Janes works hard from before light. I don’t believe she’s sat down to eat a meal in peace since we come.”

There was no bitterness in her voice. Her compassion, offered from the cold room to the woman in the crowded kitchen, had the simplicity of habit. Mrs. Trimble felt it as a rebuke, though Ann intended none.

“How does your arm pain you?” Rebecca asked.

“Oh, it ain’t much by day.”

“It keeps her awake,” said Mandana. “She walks the floor, and when she can’t walk she sets by the stove, if there’s a fire.”

“The cold makes it ache some.”

“Has the doctor seen it lately?”

“Not since we left home. Doctor West said rest would help it.”

“Rest without warmth’ll cure about as much as warmth without food,” Rebecca declared. “You ought to have it looked to.”

Ann smiled. “Doctors cost money.”

“The town’s responsible.”

“The town,” Mandana said, “is a great creatur’ with forty hands and no head. One hand points to another till you’re dead.”

“Now, Mandana.”

“It’s true. Selec’man Pike come here in November. He stood in the door and said, ‘You comfortable?’ Ann said yes, because he’d kept the door open and the wind was blowin’ on us. Then he said Silas was to have another twenty-five cents a week, and we never heard no more. I asked for shoes. He said there ought to be a pair somewhere.”

“Perhaps he forgot,” Ann said.

“He remembered election day.”

Mrs. Trimble’s cheeks had grown red. Her husband had praised the selectmen only the week before for keeping the poor account lower than that of the neighboring township. She herself had listened and approved. Economy was a word that carried a good sound in public discussion; here in the Bray sisters’ room it meant the last twigs in a box, a wrapped foot, and two women who had not seen their church for more than a year.

“What became of your shoes?” she asked, though she dreaded the answer.

“Mine wore out,” Ann said. “These slippers was Silas’s. He was goin’ to throw ’em away, and Mis’ Janes thought I might make ’em do indoors. Mandana’s good shoe split.”

“I’ve got the split one under the bed,” Mandana said. “Ann tried to sew it, but she can’t pull waxed ends with one hand.”

“What size do you wear?”

Mandana named it.

“And you, Ann?”

“Oh, I don’t need shoes till the ground dries.”

“What size?”

After a pause, Ann told her.

Rebecca repeated both sizes, as if entering them in a ledger of the mind.

For a little while they talked of other things. Ann asked after every neighbor by name: who had married, whose child had been ill, whether the church repairs were finished, if the choir still sang too quickly, and whether old Captain Dole continued to sleep through the sermon and wake at the benediction. Her interest had neither greed nor complaint in it. News was one of the few possessions that could be brought into the room without leave from the selectmen, and she received each bit with delight.

“Did they keep the old pulpit?” she asked.

“Yes,” said Mrs. Trimble. “They painted it.”

“What color?”

“Sort of drab.”

“The committee calls it fawn,” Rebecca added.

“Fawn!” Mandana exclaimed. “I never see a deer the color of cold gravy.”

Ann laughed until she had to put her hand to her shoulder. Pain crossed her face, sharp and sudden, but she turned it away.

“The choir rail’s white,” Mrs. Trimble continued, “and they’ve moved the organ.”

“Then Mis’ Fogg can’t see who comes in late,” said Mandana. “That’ll take half the value out of worship for her.”

Even Mrs. Trimble laughed, though tears were near. The old familiar wit made the bareness of the room harder to bear. These women had not ceased to belong to the life of the town; the town had merely put them where it need not see them.

Rebecca described the meeting from which they had come, including the missionary paper and Deacon Lunt’s coin. Ann listened with shining attention.

“I always liked the Aid,” she said. “Folks get more done when they work together.”

Mandana turned toward her sister, though her clouded eyes could not clearly find Ann’s face.

“They work for them that’s far enough off,” she said.

Ann’s composure faltered. “Don’t, dear.”

“I ain’t blamin’ ’em. I wouldn’t have folks blamed. I only get tired.”

Her hands, knotted with age, began to tremble. She drew the brown shawl over her face, but it did not hide the first low sob. Ann went to her at once and, with the awkward tenderness permitted by her injured arm, patted the thin shoulder.

“You’re cold,” she murmured. “That’s all. You’ll feel better when the weather settles. Spring’s most here. We’ll plant the seeds, and maybe somebody’ll carry us to meetin’ Easter.”

“I don’t want to die here,” Mandana said from beneath the shawl. “That’s all I’ve been thinkin’. I want to see our room once more—the south room—and the lilac by the pump. I don’t care if I die the next day.”

The three women were silent. The feeble fire snapped once and fell together. A draft lifted the edge of the quilt over the window.

Mrs. Trimble could remember the Bray house perfectly now. It stood empty on the crossroad, having been bought by a man from out of town who intended to use the land but had done nothing with it. The house was small and weathered, yet sound enough. She remembered the kitchen floor scrubbed white, the braided rugs, the clock with brass weights, the sprigged cups, and Ann coming from the garden with her apron full of beans. She remembered, too, that she had attended the auction for curiosity, had bought a little stand for less than its worth, and had gone home congratulating herself on a bargain.

Ann looked up, still keeping her hand on Mandana’s shoulder.

“You mustn’t mind her,” she said. “She don’t often give way. We’re better off than some. There’s a roof, and Mis’ Janes brings what she can. I know the town’s had heavy expenses. The bridge went out, and there was the smallpox scare.”

Mrs. Trimble rose suddenly and walked to the uncovered window. The cloud bank had broken in the west. Across the low fields the descending sun shone beneath it, throwing a clear golden light through the dirty glass. For one brief interval the poor room was transformed. The cracks in the wall became fine dark lines; the yellow bowl and the four saucers gleamed upon the table; Ann’s white hair shone around her face like an aureole. She stood beside her weeping sister with the last of the peach still uneaten in her saucer and an expression of such patience that Mrs. Trimble’s indignation seemed coarse beside it.

A sentence of Scripture came to Mrs. Trimble—not from the afternoon address, but from some older hearing: where two or three were gathered together, there was a Presence among them. She had always imagined such words fulfilled in churches, at prayer meetings, or beside respectable deathbeds. Yet if holiness could dwell anywhere, it dwelt in this room, where there was not wood enough for the night.

The light faded almost as quickly as it had come.

“We must be goin’ before dark,” Rebecca said softly.

Ann’s face showed disappointment, but she did not urge them. “Of course. The road by the meadow’s bad after sundown.”

Mrs. Trimble put on her cape. While Rebecca helped Mandana settle her shawl, Mrs. Trimble crossed to the wooden box and looked into it once more, as if the twigs might have increased. Then she examined the stove, the bedding, and the loose carpet at the threshold. Her movements had become precise.

“I’ll send wood tonight if I can find anybody to haul it,” she said.

Ann began to protest.

“You needn’t say a word. I’ve got a shed full of dry maple, and more in the lot.”

“We couldn’t pay—”

“You won’t.”

Mrs. Trimble spoke so firmly that Ann stopped.

Rebecca tied her bonnet strings. “I’ll be here tomorrow with the cups and shoes. I’ll fetch Doctor West too, or know the reason why.”

“He won’t come all this way,” said Mandana, lowering the shawl from her face.

“He’ll come if I sit in his buggy and drive him.”

A faint smile appeared at the corners of Mandana’s mouth.

Ann followed the visitors to the door.

“You’ve done us a sight of good,” she said. “I don’t mean the things you’re talkin’ about. Just comin’. It makes a difference to be remembered.”

Mrs. Trimble took her left hand in both of hers.

“We’ll remember better after this.”

Outside, the air had sharpened. Mrs. Janes stood near the corner of the house with an armful of broken boards. She looked at the visitors anxiously, perhaps expecting criticism.

“I was goin’ to carry them pieces in,” she said. “They’ll burn some.”

“Have you an axe?” Mrs. Trimble asked.

“Silas took it to the north lot.”

“I’ll have wood sent. Some for your kitchen too.”

Mrs. Janes’s guarded face changed. For an instant she looked very young and near tears.

“I ain’t asked charity,” she said quickly.

“No more have they. This ain’t charity. It’s what ought to have been done.”

“The town’s behind with us.”

“I’ll speak to the town.”

Mrs. Janes shifted the boards. “Ann’s too good. She gives the children half what’s carried to her, and then Mandana scolds. I can’t always spare things. I’ve tried, but there’s days when I don’t know what any of us is goin’ to eat. Silas ain’t shiftless like folks say. He’s beat out.”

“I know,” Mrs. Trimble answered. “We’ve let too much rest on this house.”

They climbed into the wagon. Ann remained in the doorway, holding the patched covering aside. Mandana’s dim figure could be seen behind her. Rebecca waved, and Ann waved in return until the wagon turned past the dead orchard.

For the first mile neither visitor spoke. The horse found his way through the ruts, stopping once where water had spread across the road. Twilight gathered in the hollows, and a woodcock flew up from the wet meadow with a whistling of wings. Behind them the Janes house sank out of sight.

Mrs. Trimble’s silence was unusual. Rebecca respected it until they reached the hill above the burying-ground. Then she said, “Well?”

“I’ve been thinkin’.”

“I supposed you had.”

“I’ve been thinkin’ what I shall say to my husband first, and then to the selectmen. That Bray house is empty.”

“So I hear.”

“Ezra Penniman owns it. He don’t want the house; he wanted the twelve acres to join his pasture. I believe he’d rent it for almost nothin’ if the town kept the roof in repair.”

“He might.”

“He will, after I talk to his wife.”

Rebecca nodded. The indirect path was sometimes the straightest in town affairs.

“The sisters could go back,” Mrs. Trimble continued. “They’d need somebody to bring provisions and do the heavy work. Ann can manage more than you’d suppose, if her arm’s tended, and Mandana can help with what she sees. The town pays Silas Janes now. Let it pay rent instead, and hire the Perkins girl two mornings a week.”

“The Perkins girl needs the money.”

“They’ll have dry wood. I’ll see to the first cord myself, and Mr. Trimble can’t object unless he wants to explain why. We can get their things back.”

“Some of ’em.”

“All that can be found. I bought the little stand.”

“I know you did.”

Mrs. Trimble accepted the rebuke. “I’ll return it. Mis’ Eastman has one of the bedsteads, and the Foggs bought the clock. The clock may be harder.”

“Mis’ Fogg says it loses.”

“Then she can lose it back again. We’ll raise money if folks won’t give the things. There was enough spent on cake today to buy six chairs.”

“Seven, if they’re auction chairs.”

Mrs. Trimble did not smile. “I sat there, Rebecca. I listened to what we owed folks across the ocean, and I never once thought of Ann and Mandana in that room.”

“You thought of ’em enough to turn down the road.”

“Late.”

“Late ain’t never, unless a person makes it so.”

The village lights had begun to appear, warm squares behind curtains. At one house supper smoke rose from the chimney, and the smell of frying salt pork drifted into the road. Mrs. Trimble drew a long breath.

“I shall call on Doctor West before I go home,” she said. “Then I’ll see Ezra Penniman. Tomorrow morning I’m going to the selectmen’s office.”

“They don’t meet till Friday.”

“They’ll meet when I find ’em.”

“That sounds more likely.”

“And you’ll come?”

“I said I would. I’ll bring the cups, and I’ve a pair of low shoes Ann can wear till new ones are made. Mandana’ll need hers altered.”

“We’ll take measurements. I’ll ask Hannah Cobb to make them; she understands a lame foot. And they shall go to church Easter, if four men have to carry them.”

“Ann won’t like four men fussin’.”

“Then two women will do it.”

The old horse quickened his pace as the road grew firmer. Mrs. Trimble sat upright, the reins gathered in her capable hands. Her comfortable life no longer seemed an accusation merely; it had become a means. She possessed a full woodshed, a husband whom others listened to, a horse and wagon, acquaintance with every family in town, and the habit of being heard when she chose to speak. Until that evening these things had lain about her like furniture in a prosperous room. Now she counted them as tools.

Rebecca looked back once toward the dark northern road.

“Ann gave us the last peach,” she said.

“I know.”

“She’d have given it if she’d known there’d never be another.”

“I know.”

“They mustn’t be made to feel beholden.”

Mrs. Trimble’s voice was steady. “It’s we that are beholden.”

They passed the meeting-house. Its newly painted windows reflected the final strip of sunset, and the repaired steeple rose pale above the elms. On the vestry steps two pans, forgotten after supper, had collected rainwater and dead leaves.

“The town’ll say it can’t afford all you’re plannin’,” Rebecca remarked.

“The town can’t afford not to.”

“It’s bore a good deal of what it couldn’t afford, if nobody pressed it.”

“I shall press it.”

Rebecca settled her shawl and gave a small, satisfied nod.

At the next corner they separated, Rebecca walking the short distance to her house while Mrs. Trimble drove on toward Doctor West’s. Before she stepped down, she put one hand on the wagon seat.

“I’ll have the cups wrapped at eight,” she said. “They’ve stood on my parlor shelf long enough.”

“I’ll come for you at half past.”

“Make it eight. We’ve lost time already.”

Mrs. Trimble turned the horse. The doctor’s house showed a lamp in the office window, and she could see his shadow moving behind the shade. She drove directly to his gate.

Behind them, far beyond the village and the wet fields, the Bray sisters’ room had fallen dark. Yet Ann had kindled the last twigs, and the two women sat close together beside the small returning flame, speaking of morning-glories, sprigged cups, and the south room at home.

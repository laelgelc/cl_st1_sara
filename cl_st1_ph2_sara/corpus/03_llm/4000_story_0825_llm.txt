The announcement occupied four lines in the least conspicuous column of the Plymdale Chronicle, between an offer by which every annual subscriber might receive, without additional charge, a coloured representation of Christmas in the Holy Land, and the advertisement of Mr. Binns, monumental mason, who undertook to supply sorrowing families with urns, angels, broken columns, and “all other appropriate emblems of affection and permanence.”

It was, therefore, suitably placed.

“On the 17th instant, at the Registry Office, Eleanor, only daughter of the late Augustus Gail, solicitor, to Charles Faraday, M.A., lecturer in history at Plymdale University.”

There was nothing in the type to distinguish this union from the marriages of clerks, widows, dissenting grocers, and persons who had reasons for preferring a civil ceremony. Yet Eleanor, on reading the paragraph after breakfast, saw it expand until it seemed to cover the page, the town, and perhaps the moral surface of England. She had never before appeared in print. Her thoughts, though extensive, had not been reported; her refusal to attend Mrs. Belward’s garden party had escaped journalism; even her paper upon “The Economic Bondage Implicit in Feminine Accomplishments,” read before the Plymdale Society for Social Inquiry, had been dismissed by the Chronicle in the phrase “other contributions followed.”

Now she was married, and the public knew it.

The public, indeed, knew more than the Chronicle had said. Plymdale always did. It knew that Eleanor had worn grey at the ceremony; that no relative had given her away; that Charles had not promised to endow her with all his worldly goods; that they had returned separately from the Registry Office, he to deliver a lecture on the territorial arrangements of the Treaty of Utrecht, she to purchase a travelling inkstand. It knew, finally, that the married pair did not propose to live together at once.

It was uncalled for!

It was improper!

It was indecent!

These judgments, emitted in drawing-rooms whose curtains had been discreetly lowered during less lawful domestic episodes, possessed all the force which society obtains when it condemns in public what it tolerates in private.

The truth was less dangerous and more remarkable. Eleanor Gail and Charles Faraday loved each other; and, being intelligent, generous, and young enough to regard experience as the servant rather than the master of principle, they had determined not to spoil love by marriage. They would marry, certainly, because legal institutions, however imperfect, had tiresome powers over property, reputation, railway journeys, and hotel registers; but they would preserve within marriage that complete personal liberty which unmarried friendship assumes without praise.

Each was to remain a separate soul. Neither was to demand an account of hours, companions, letters, tastes, opinions, or migrations. Affection was to be spontaneous, confidence voluntary, fidelity spiritual rather than police-regulated. Eleanor would not become Charles’s subordinate because a registrar had made an entry in a book; Charles would not become Eleanor’s jailer because ancient custom had handed him a key.

“If I care for you only because I cannot escape you,” Eleanor had said, “my caring is worthless.”

“And if I trust you only while I can observe you,” Charles replied, “my trust is an insult.”

This was admirable, and so should we say. The fact that human beings have often made a poor use of liberty is no argument for chains, though it has furnished the makers of chains with their most profitable advertisement.

Eleanor was twenty-seven, with grave dark eyes, a mobile mouth, and a mind which had suffered from insufficient employment as an unopened flower suffers in a shaded room—not dramatically, perhaps, but by a slow pale distortion of energies intended for sunlight. Her father had believed women capable of learning everything except what might permit them to earn a livelihood. Consequently, she knew Italian songs, elementary botany, ecclesiastical embroidery, and the capitals of several countries, but had never been allowed to pursue one study long enough to become formidable in it.

After Mr. Gail’s death, a small independence and a large indignation descended upon her. She read economics, history, political philosophy, physiology, and those novels which respectable reviewers describe as dangerous when they perceive that their wives have understood them. Charles met her at the Society for Social Inquiry and loved, first, the courageous exactness with which she contradicted Professor Nevinson, and afterwards the quick colour with which she apologized for having enjoyed it.

Charles was thirty-four, broad-shouldered, brown-bearded, genial, and endowed with that optimistic confidence in reason which is often found in men who have not yet been seriously contradicted by their own emotions. He believed in liberty, education, municipal reform, the higher employment of women, lower taxation upon books, and the ultimate perfectibility of society, provided society would consent to attend his lectures. His goodness was genuine; so was his vanity; and the latter commonly wore the former’s hat.

It was Charles who urged Eleanor to go to Paris.

“You read French,” he told her, “as though every sentence had been put under arrest. You must learn to speak it, to hear it, to think in it.”

“And leave you?”

“Why not? I return to Plymdale for the Michaelmas term. You stay six months with Madame Vauclerc. You will have the museums, the theatres, the lectures—and Paris itself.”

“But people will say—”

“My dear Eleanor, if we begin consulting people, we may as well ask Mrs. Belward to take charge of our consciences.”

Thus, ten days after the wedding, Charles returned alone to his rooms near the university, while Eleanor established herself in the household of Madame Vauclerc, a widow possessing a severe regard for grammar and a lenient one for conduct. Plymdale was scandalized afresh. It had understood elopement, separation, estrangement, and even desertion; but a bride deliberately sent to acquire an irregular verb exceeded the town’s moral vocabulary.

Their letters began immediately.

Charles wrote on university paper, in a large forward-leaning hand. He described his lectures, his colleagues, the rain, the irritating slowness of the library committee, and the small triumph of having persuaded three students that history had occurred before they were born. Eleanor wrote from a narrow desk overlooking a courtyard where a plane tree, two cats, and a washerwoman competed for possession of the available atmosphere. She described the Louvre, the Luxembourg Gardens, the voice of an actress at the Théâtre-Français, her struggles with the subjunctive, and the agreeable shock of discovering that Frenchwomen crossed a street as though Providence had promised to stop the traffic.

The letters were frequent, intimate, and free. They were not those dutiful bulletins by which separated married persons prove that nothing has happened. They were continuations of a single conversation, delayed by geography but enriched by expectation. A thought formed in Charles’s mind on Tuesday reached Eleanor on Thursday and returned altered, enlarged, contradicted, or illuminated by Sunday. Their intellectual companionship acquired across distance a new delicacy, as certain scents become perceptible only when the flower itself is absent.

For two months their experiment appeared wholly successful.

Then Charles dined with the Beatons.

Margaret Beaton was a timid advocate of female emancipation who wore divided skirts when no one was expected and changed into conventional silk before visitors arrived. She believed women should possess votes, professions, and complete domestic authority, although she trembled when required to dismiss a dishonest servant. Her daughter Kitty, at nineteen, had none of her mother’s theories and all of the power for which those theories pleaded.

Kitty was golden, rounded, indolent, and pretty with the ruthless completeness of a peach. She had never read a page of political economy, but controlled the Beaton household by a Napoleonic distribution of smiles, silences, kisses, and headaches. Margaret demanded justice; Kitty obtained the best chair. Margaret discussed equality; Kitty selected the guests. There are many roads to sovereignty, and philosophers do not always choose the shortest.

Charles, who had known Kitty as a child with untidy stockings, was surprised to discover her transformed. He reported the surprise to Eleanor with the candour demanded by their principles.

“You would scarcely recognize little Kitty Beaton,” he wrote. “She has become extraordinarily lovely—a creature all gold and rose, with that peculiar freshness which belongs to youth before it has learned to mistrust admiration. I found myself looking at her more often than was necessary for conversation, and I tell you this because concealment, even concerning a trifle, seems inconsistent with everything we have promised each other. Beauty is beauty, whether one is married or not; and surely we need not become blind in order to remain faithful.”

Philosophically considered, the passage was impeccable.

Eleanor read it three times.

The first reading produced a sensation so slight that she mistook it for amusement. At the second, a small cold instrument appeared to pass beneath her breastbone. At the third, Kitty Beaton—whom she had formerly regarded as a harmless and even rather vacant child—rose before her imagination enriched with every beauty Charles had mentioned and several he had omitted.

Gold and rose!

Freshness!

He had looked at her more often than was necessary.

Eleanor folded the letter, unfolded it, and held it toward the bougie, as though some invisible qualification might emerge under heat. None did. Charles had been candid. He had trusted her intelligence. He had paid tribute to beauty without compromising love. She repeated these truths with the conscientiousness of a physician administering a medicine which the patient obstinately refuses to absorb.

For the remainder of the evening Paris became hateful. The laughter in the courtyard was vulgar; the bells were noisy; the plane tree had a diseased appearance; French civilization itself seemed guilty of keeping her from Plymdale. Misery, issuing from four innocent sentences, spread like smoke through the city and discoloured even the moon.

It is right to say that Eleanor despised her distress. Jealousy belonged to the old enslaved woman, whose economic dependence compelled her to guard the man upon whom bread and position depended. She, Eleanor, was emancipated. She possessed money, opinions, a passport, and a return ticket. Her love for Charles was founded upon reciprocal liberty. If his eye rejoiced in Kitty’s beauty, that was a movement of aesthetic perception, not moral treachery.

Unfortunately, the old enslaved woman, who ought by all sound reasoning to have been dead, stirred within her grave and demanded particulars.

How had Kitty been dressed?

Had Charles sat beside her?

Had she looked at him?

Had Margaret left them alone?

And what, precisely, constituted “more often than was necessary”?

Reason entered armed rebellion against instinct, deployed arguments, occupied strategic heights, and issued several noble proclamations. Instinct, without answering one of them, poisoned the wells.

Eleanor’s next letter was short.

She had attended a lecture. The weather was damp. Madame Vauclerc sent her compliments. She hoped Charles was well.

He read it with concern, but without comprehension. A second letter was scarcely warmer. In a third, Eleanor discussed the restoration of a picture at the Louvre for two pages and did not say that she missed him.

Charles asked whether she were ill.

“No,” she replied. “I am perfectly well.”

This answer has alarmed men in every language.

He wondered whether Paris tired her, whether Madame Vauclerc had offended her, whether his own letters had become tedious, whether prolonged separation, though intellectually defensible, might not impose unnecessary discomfort. He did not, however, connect Eleanor’s coldness with Kitty. His conscience acquitted him so promptly that he failed to observe the nature of the charge.

By January the university term had ended, and Charles travelled to Paris. He informed Eleanor of his arrival only a day before it occurred, partly because he wished to surprise her, partly because he feared she might forbid the journey out of devotion to their system, and partly because the best of men are not wholly above the pleasure of appearing suddenly at a door.

Madame Vauclerc received him beneath a chandelier which gave less light than might reasonably have been expected from so much glass. She was a compact woman in black, with bright eyes and a moustache too slight to be masculine but too decided to be ignored.

“Madame Faraday is in the salon,” she said. “She has a visitor.”

The information ought not to have signified. Eleanor was free to receive a visitor. Charles mounted the stairs repeating this truth with the calmness of a defendant who knows the law is on his side and suspects the jury is not.

He heard voices. Eleanor’s came first, animated and low; then a man laughed.

Charles entered.

Eleanor stood near the window. The cold afternoon had coloured her cheeks, and a nervous brightness animated her eyes. Beside the mantel was a young man of perhaps thirty, tall, narrow-waisted, dark-haired, and ornamented with a moustache of a kind rarely produced in English universities. His clothes possessed that harmonious negligence which requires both time and calculation. One hand held his hat; the other rested upon a portfolio.

For the smallest fraction of a moment Eleanor looked dismayed.

The expression vanished. She crossed the room and offered both hands.

“Charles! You are here already.”

“The train was punctual.”

What follows an unexpected reunion between husband and wife cannot always be performed gracefully before a handsome stranger. Eleanor seemed uncertain whether to kiss Charles, present the visitor, explain the portfolio, or conceal it. She therefore did none of these things at once, and the pause, though brief, acquired the psychological comprehensiveness of an hour.

The stranger bowed.

“Monsieur Faraday?”

Eleanor recovered herself.

“Yes—Charles, this is Monsieur Lucien Armand. Monsieur Armand—my husband.”

Armand’s expression conveyed pleasure, respect, intelligence, and the faintest possible amusement. Charles disliked him immediately.

“I have heard much of you,” said the Frenchman.

Charles wondered from whom, how much, and in what circumstances. He replied that he was honoured.

Eleanor glanced at the portfolio. Armand glanced at Eleanor. Between those two glances passed something which Charles could not interpret, and the inability irritated him more than an unpleasant certainty might have done.

“Our matter,” Armand said, “can be completed on Thursday?”

“I think so,” Eleanor answered. “I shall send a note.”

“And tomorrow?”

“Tomorrow at two, if that is still convenient.”

“Perfectly.”

Charles looked at the fire.

Armand bowed again, expressed delight at the meeting, hoped for another, and departed with a grace which made Charles’s English silence appear heavily upholstered.

“Well,” Eleanor said when the door closed, “you have surprised me.”

“I intended to.”

“And I am glad—very glad.”

She embraced him then, warmly enough to dissolve any ordinary doubt. But jealousy is not an ordinary doubt. It is an incipient cyclone which draws its power from every breeze sent to disperse it.

“Who is Monsieur Armand?”

“A man I have met here.”

“So I perceived.”

“He is an artist.”

Charles recalled the portfolio. The explanation was natural and somehow unsatisfactory.

“What is to be completed on Thursday?”

Eleanor’s hands moved quickly among the cups on the tea table.

“Oh, a little affair—a commission.”

“Yours?”

“Yes.”

“And tomorrow at two?”

“I must see him.”

“Alone?”

The word escaped before principle could intercept it.

Eleanor looked up. Surprise came first; then pain; then a composure so careful that it announced the existence of both.

“I suppose so. Does it matter?”

“No. Certainly not.”

“Charles—”

“My dear Eleanor, we agreed that neither should account to the other for companions or occupations. I have no question to ask.”

He had already asked three. But a philosophy is not considered broken merely because one trips over it.

Eleanor poured tea. The silver cover rattled against the pot.

Their first evening together was affectionate, constrained, and absurd. They spoke of the journey, the university, Madame Vauclerc, the price of rooms, French pronunciation, and the cleaning of pictures. Each avoided Monsieur Armand with such determination that he became the principal presence in the room. Had he been seated between them, smoking a cigar and displaying his moustache, he could scarcely have occupied more space.

Before leaving for his hotel, Charles asked what Eleanor planned for the next day.

“Madame Vauclerc and I shall go out in the morning. In the afternoon—”

“Yes?”

“I have the appointment I mentioned.”

“With Armand.”

“With Monsieur Armand.”

“Of course. Then I shall amuse myself.”

Eleanor hesitated.

“I could postpone it.”

“Why should you?”

“No reason.”

“Then do not.”

Their eyes met. Each saw in the other an appeal which pride translated as defiance.

At two the following afternoon Charles stood before a carved Assyrian relief in the Louvre and hated it. He had determined to use Eleanor’s engagement as an opportunity for solitary enjoyment. A husband less enlightened might have followed her, questioned Madame Vauclerc, or accidentally passed the house at the appropriate hour. Charles would visit museums. He would demonstrate, if only to himself, that trust was not a phrase but a capacity.

The Assyrian king, receiving tribute from lines of conquered persons, appeared smug.

Charles passed into another gallery. Italian Madonnas regarded him with a serenity founded, he reflected sourly, upon the absence of handsome French portrait painters from the sacred narrative. A Venus offended him by resemblance to Kitty Beaton. Two landscapes were merely paint. The whole institution, celebrated as a treasury of civilization, seemed a long corridor arranged to prevent a man from seeing what concerned him.

He went outside.

Paris was bright with that hard winter brilliance in which every wheel, window, and wet paving-stone flashes separately. Crowds moved along the Rue de Rivoli; women lifted their skirts from the mud; flower sellers offered violets; a regiment of omnibuses conducted noisy warfare with the fiacres. Charles walked without direction, pursued by pictures more vivid than any he had refused to admire in the Louvre.

Eleanor beside a studio window.

Eleanor laughing.

Armand bending over her hand.

The portfolio opened.

The door closed.

Each vision was unsupported by fact and furnished in detail.

He reminded himself that Eleanor had spent months alone in Paris and might have received twenty handsome men without his knowledge. This thought, intended as reassurance, sent the blood through his veins like fire. The doctrine of reciprocal liberty, so noble when surveyed from the armchair of abstraction, became less hospitable when one was required to inhabit it.

At half past three he found himself in the Avenue des Champs-Élysées. He did not know why he had gone there. A man may travel a considerable distance under the guidance of motives whose existence he would indignantly deny.

Then he saw Eleanor.

She was in an open fiacre, despite the cold, wrapped in dark fur, her face bright beneath a small hat. Beside her sat Lucien Armand. They were speaking eagerly. Armand said something; Eleanor laughed and turned toward him with the unguarded delight Charles had once believed reserved for himself—not by compact, certainly, but by one of those assumptions which survive unnoticed beneath the ruins of convention.

The fiacre passed close.

Eleanor did not see him.

Charles stopped on the pavement. For one magnificent and disgraceful instant, civilization withdrew from him. He wished to seize the reins, drag Armand from the carriage, demand explanation, assert ownership, break the portfolio over the artist’s well-shaped head, and perhaps appeal to a legal institution he had described repeatedly as barbarous. Jealousy, that ancient savage whom Charles had supposed educated out of existence, sprang upright in his blood wearing no garment of philosophy whatever.

The carriage vanished among others.

A stout gentleman collided with Charles and apologized. Charles glared as though the gentleman, too, had violated his marriage.

He walked for an hour. The city darkened; lamps appeared; the cold sharpened. His thoughts alternated between accusation and self-reproach. Eleanor was free. Eleanor had deceived him. She had not deceived him, because she had promised no report. She had been secretive. Perhaps secrecy was necessary because he had shown suspicion. Perhaps his suspicion had been justified by her secrecy. Thus reason, pressed into the service of jealousy, ceased to be a judge and became an ingenious advocate capable of pleading either side with equal dishonesty.

By six he had resolved to leave Paris.

By seven he had resolved to confront Armand.

By eight he had resolved to laugh.

At nine, when he entered Madame Vauclerc’s salon, he was prepared to do all three.

Eleanor awaited him by the fire. She wore a pale dress, and her hair, loosely arranged, softened the intellectual firmness of her face. There was excitement in her manner, but also anxiety.

“You have been out all day,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Did you enjoy the Louvre?”

“Immensely.”

This was false, and Eleanor knew it.

“I wish you had come back earlier.”

“I understood that you were engaged.”

“Only for part of the afternoon.”

“With Monsieur Armand.”

“Yes.”

“I saw you.”

The words emerged with a bluntness which shattered the delicate structure of their evasions.

“In the fiacre?”

“In the fiacre.”

A faint smile touched her mouth. Charles interpreted it as guilt, amusement, tenderness, triumph, and cruelty before it disappeared.

“You looked very happy,” he said.

“I was.”

“That is evident.”

“Charles, what do you mean?”

“Nothing that I have a right to mean, according to our arrangement.”

She rose.

“Then say what you feel, not what you have a right to feel.”

This invitation, though just, was perilous.

“I feel,” he began, “that there has been concealment.”

“There has.”

“I feel that this man’s visits, your appointments, the glances between you—your drive today—require some explanation.”

“Require?”

The word stood between them with its old marital authority suddenly restored.

Charles heard it and reddened.

“No. Forgive me. I cannot require anything. But I ask.”

Eleanor regarded him. For a moment the distressed goddess and the incipient cyclone struggled visibly within her; then affection, which is wiser than either dignity or anger when it chooses to be, took command.

“Come with me,” she said.

She led him through the passage to a small room at the back of the house. It had been used for storing trunks and rejected furniture. A lamp burned on the table. Against the wall stood a large object covered by a green cloth.

Eleanor paused beside it.

“I had intended it for Thursday. Monsieur Armand wished to make one alteration, and today we went together to a dealer who has a frame he thought suitable. That was our secret.”

She pulled away the cloth.

Charles saw Eleanor.

Not the Eleanor who stood beside him, pale with agitation, but Eleanor arrested in the duality of accomplished delight and growing promise: seated near a window, a book closed upon her knee, her head lifted as if someone beloved had just entered beyond the boundary of the canvas. Armand had caught the intelligence of the eyes, the strength of the mouth, and that concealed tenderness which strangers sometimes missed because Eleanor guarded it behind thought. Paris lay faintly outside the painted window, less a city than an atmosphere of enlargement.

Charles stared.

“I wanted you to have me with you in Plymdale,” she said, “or something of me. You had said once that portraits were usually dead things, and Monsieur Armand’s work seemed alive. I meant it as a surprise.”

“My dear—”

“He brought the first sketches here because I could not always go to the studio. Madame Vauclerc was present, if that matters.”

“It does not matter.”

“It did ten minutes ago.”

Charles accepted the blow. It was deserved, though we may observe that deserved blows seldom become agreeable by recognition.

“I have been a fool,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I have been worse than a fool. I have distrusted you.”

“For one afternoon.”

“For one very long afternoon.”

Eleanor’s severity softened.

“Were you jealous?”

He looked at the portrait, which appeared capable of understanding more than either of them wished to confess.

“Hideously.”

She laughed then—not the laugh he had seen in the fiacre, but one in which relief, affection, vanity, and a small amount of vengeance were harmoniously mingled. Charles drew her toward him, and philosophy, after making a dignified attempt to remain in the room, withdrew before the more ancient eloquence of reconciliation.

Later, sitting beside the bougie while the painted Eleanor watched from the shadows, they discussed practical matters. Charles praised the portrait extravagantly. Eleanor defended Armand’s handling of the background, which Charles had not criticized. They agreed that the picture should be sent to Plymdale when the frame was ready.

“And you?” Charles asked.

“What of me?”

“How much longer must French civilization retain you?”

Eleanor looked down at her hands.

“Madame Vauclerc says my accent is still deplorable.”

“I have heard Madame Vauclerc’s English.”

“That is not relevant.”

“No. But it is comforting.”

“I had thought,” Eleanor said slowly, “that perhaps I have learned enough for the present.”

Charles waited. Her earlier ideological confidence had deserted her at the threshold of a simple desire.

“I think—I mean, don’t you think—one can continue a language without living in the country?”

“One can.”

“And there are books in Plymdale.”

“Several. I have seen them.”

“And if I came back, I need not—of course I should not wish you to imagine that I had abandoned our principle of independence.”

“Certainly not.”

“I could have rooms of my own.”

“You could.”

“Near yours, perhaps.”

“Very near.”

She looked at him.

“Or even—temporarily—the same rooms might be convenient.”

“Purely as a matter of convenience.”

“Charles, do not laugh.”

“I am not laughing.”

He was, but tenderly, and she forgave him.

Thus the great experiment, without being renounced, submitted to amendment. Liberty remained their principle; companionship became their preference. They would return together, not because Plymdale demanded it, nor because marriage compelled it, but because separation had accomplished its educational purpose and had begun to educate them in matters not included in Madame Vauclerc’s curriculum.

There remained, however, one confession.

It came late, when the fire had fallen and the sounds of the house were still. Eleanor had been silent for several minutes. Charles, who attributed the silence to contentment, was surprised when she said:

“There is something I must tell you.”

“About Armand?”

“No. About Kitty Beaton.”

Charles searched his memory.

“What has Kitty to do with anything?”

“You wrote that she was extraordinarily lovely.”

“Did I?”

“You said she was gold and rose. You said you looked at her more often than was necessary.”

Charles remembered, and with remembrance came illumination—not complete illumination, for nature seldom wastes completeness upon us, but enough to show him the outline of what he had missed.

“Was that why your letters changed?”

“Yes.”

“My poor Eleanor!”

“I was ashamed. I told myself that beauty is beauty; that marriage should not make us blind; that trust which depends upon ignorance is worthless. I repeated your arguments, and my own, and everybody’s. They were all true, and they did no good.”

“You thought I cared for Kitty?”

“I did not think it. That was the misery. I knew you did not, and I was jealous all the same.”

She spoke with her face turned from him.

“I imagined her beside you. I saw her youth, her freshness, her dreadful golden hair. I remembered that I am older, and grave, and not in the least like a peach. Every sensible idea I possessed went to pieces. Some emotions cannot be overcome by philosophy.”

Charles took her hand.

“My dear girl,” he said with affectionate superiority, “you are only a woman, after all.”

Eleanor leaned against him and did not answer. She was too happy, perhaps, to expose the imperfection of his conclusion; or she understood that self-knowledge, like French, is not mastered in a single term.

Charles looked across at the portrait, pleased to think that he would carry home both the ideal image and the living original. He had already forgotten the fire in his blood on the Champs-Élysées, the imagined crime, the savage claim, the philosophic structure collapsing at the sight of a fiacre. Men are mercifully assisted in their dignity by an excellent memory for the failures of women and a constitutional dimness concerning their own.

Outside, Paris glittered beneath the winter stars. Somewhere in the city Monsieur Armand, innocent agent of their instruction, was perhaps explaining to another sitter that a portrait required patience, secrecy, light, and an appropriate frame. In Plymdale, Mrs. Belward was telling guests that no good could come of a marriage founded upon freedom. Between these judgments sat Eleanor and Charles, closer than before, less certain, and therefore a little wiser.

Their principles had not been destroyed. They had merely encountered the human heart, which respects a theory as the sea respects a line drawn upon a map.

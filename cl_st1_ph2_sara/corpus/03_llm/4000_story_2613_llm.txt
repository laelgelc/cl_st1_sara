# THE TESTAMENT OF GEORGE ANGELL

*There are mercies which consist not in revelation, but in the failure of separate truths to recognize one another.*

I write under an apprehension which will, to any ordinary reader, appear either the last infirmity of an overwrought mind or the theatrical precaution of one who has mistaken coincidence for conspiracy. Three years ago I should myself have judged it so. I was then a man tolerably disciplined in the habits of historical inquiry, respectful of evidence, distrustful of enthusiasm, and particularly hostile to those occult speculations which flourish wherever philology is weak and imagination intemperate. Yet I have since learned that scepticism, though admirable as a guard against error, may become a veil before immensities; and that the intellect, having once pierced that veil, may envy forever the simpler blindness it formerly despised.

If these pages are found after my death, I ask that they be read as a deposition rather than as a confession. I have altered no date, invented no witness, and suppressed only those particulars which might guide rash men toward places better left unvisited. The documents from which I have drawn are to be deposited, if my executor has courage enough, in the locked antiquarian collection of the Miskatonic University Library. Whether they will remain there is another matter.

My great-uncle, Professor George Gammell Angell, Emeritus of Semitic Languages at Brown University, died in Providence during the winter of 1926–27. He was ninety-two, and though physically diminished had preserved an astonishing lucidity. His end was therefore attributed, with some reluctance, to cardiac failure induced by shock. He had been returning from a ferry landing when, according to the sole witness, he was jostled by a dark-coated sailor who immediately disappeared among the warehouses. The Professor climbed the steep street toward his rooms, staggered, and fell. There was no wound, no poison discoverable by the physicians, and no legal ground for inquiry.

As his nearest surviving relative, I inherited his effects.

Most were precisely what one might expect from an industrious scholar whose life had been consecrated to dead alphabets: annotated grammars, copies of Assyrian inscriptions, correspondence with European orientalists, and boxes of squeezes and photographs from museums whose curators had long since joined their specimens in antiquity. One box, however, differed from the rest. It was of blackened oak, recently fitted with a brass lock, and bore no title beyond a pencilled injunction in my uncle’s hand:

**NOT TO BE DESTROYED. NOT TO BE PUBLISHED.**

Within lay a grotesque clay relief, several notebooks, a bundle of press cuttings, two sealed narratives, and a large envelope marked:

**CTHULHU CULT—DREAM TESTIMONY AND RELATED MATTERS.**

The word meant nothing to me. Its letters suggested no known linguistic family, though their arrangement possessed an unpleasant appearance, as if the written form were merely a concession to organs incapable of producing the original sound.

It was the clay object, however, which first arrested me.

The tablet measured scarcely eight inches across and had plainly been modelled while wet by hands possessing technical promise but little archaeological training. Its background represented, or attempted to represent, an architectural vista of extraordinary abnormality: monoliths leaning at angles which the eye rejected though measurement could not expose their fault; doorways broader at the summit than at the base; and masses resembling towers, altars, and natural cliffs simultaneously. Above these hieroglyphics—if hieroglyphics they were—rose the figure of an entity for which familiar biology affords only treacherous analogies.

A swollen head, vaguely cephalopodan, was set upon a corpulent and scaly trunk. Numerous flexible appendages surrounded what might have been a mouth. The extremities terminated in prodigious claws, while from the back projected a pair of folded, membranous wings. Yet no catalogue of parts conveys the total impression. It was not the combination of octopus, reptile, and dragon that disturbed me, but the suggestion that these earthly shapes were imperfect recollections of something formed according to another system of life. The sculptor had somehow communicated weight, antiquity, and a malignant intelligence so vast that the little figure seemed less an image than a window opened upon abysmal magnitude.

Beneath it, in uncertain characters, appeared the phrase:

**PH’NGLUI MGLW’NAFH CTHULHU R’LYEH WGAH’NAGL FHTAGN.**

I confess that I laughed when I first read the accompanying notes. My uncle, whose judgement I had always esteemed, seemed to have spent his final years arranging the delusions of artists, policemen, sailors, and uncivilized visionaries into a structure of quite unnecessary complication. Yet as the dates multiplied, as independent testimonies converged, and as the same indescribable shape emerged from continents and centuries remote from one another, my amusement diminished. Before I had finished the first notebook, I had locked the study door.

## I. THE RELIEF IN CLAY

The sculptor’s name was Henry Anthony Wilcox, a student at the Rhode Island School of Design who occupied rooms in an old house in Thomas Street. My uncle first encountered him on 1 March 1925, when the young man appeared at the Professor’s residence carrying the still-moist tablet beneath his coat. Wilcox was then twenty-four: thin, nervous, dark-haired, and endowed with that peculiar sensitiveness which respectable physicians call temperament when they are unwilling to call it disease.

He told Angell that he had fashioned the relief immediately after waking from a dream.

The dream had begun with no visible scene, but with a sound transmitted through an immeasurable depth. It resembled neither speech nor music, but a subterranean concussion rhythmically divided into syllables. Presently there arose around him an immense city of greenish stone, drenched though no rain fell and illuminated though no sun or moon could be seen. Its geometry oppressed him. Pavements appeared at once horizontal and precipitous; walls withdrew while seeming to advance; and one could not distinguish whether certain black openings lay beneath his feet or towered leagues overhead.

From somewhere beyond the city came a voice. Wilcox could retain only two fragments—“Cthulhu” and “R’lyeh”—which he had attempted to reproduce upon the clay.

My uncle, thinking the youth had discovered or copied some archaic inscription, asked sharply where he had seen the design. Wilcox denied having seen it anywhere. The denial offended Angell, who dismissed him with unusual coldness. Yet that evening the Professor compared the relief with photographs in a much older file and sent the sculptor an apologetic note requesting his return.

Thereafter, between 2 March and 23 March, Wilcox called almost daily. Angell recorded each interview with medical exactitude, setting down pulse, temperature, duration of sleep, and the minutest alteration in the dream. The city recurred every night. Its walls grew clearer; the submerged voice became louder; and something colossal seemed to move behind a portal composed of blocks higher than the towers of Providence.

On 9 March Wilcox spoke of stars visible through a vaporous sky. Their arrangements were unrecognizable. On the 13th he reported a sensation of being observed from beneath the foundations. On the 16th he awakened crying that the angles of the masonry were alive. Three days later his landlady summoned a physician after finding him delirious, struggling against the bedclothes, and uttering the strange syllables with such violence that blood appeared at his lips.

His fever lasted until 2 April.

During that interval he described vast dripping surfaces rising through black water; a door whose opening produced a wind older than the atmosphere; and a mountainous shadow crowned with writhing appendages. The attending physician, Dr Tobey, confirmed the fever but regarded the imagery as a symptom of cerebral excitement. At noon on 2 April the patient fell into a deep sleep. He awoke after sunset, entirely lucid, and remembered nothing of his dreams.

The visions did not return.

Had this been the whole of the matter, I should have regarded it as a singular but trivial case of artistic neurosis. Angell did not. Some instinct—scholarly at first, though later perhaps less innocent—led him to investigate whether comparable disturbances had occurred elsewhere during the same period. His methods were comprehensive. He wrote to hospitals, psychiatric institutions, newspapers, universities, observatories, and acquaintances among artists and poets. He questioned those known for extravagant imagination as carefully as those reputed sober. The replies filled forty-seven pages.

Their implications were not immediately apparent, for most were negative. Businessmen, tradesmen, engineers, and persons occupied chiefly with practical affairs had slept as usual. Among painters, sculptors, composers, mystics, and the mentally unstable, however, the record was different.

A Parisian painter had produced, between 5 and 20 March, a series of canvases representing black towers above a gelatinous sea. A violinist in Prague destroyed a composition whose final movement, according to his brother, consisted of an intolerable repetition of three descending notes. In Buenos Aires a poet wrote of “the drowned capital where the first kings await the second moon.” A Boston architect dreamed of staircases ascending downward. Patients in asylums at London, Montreal, and San Francisco became violently excited, several crying out against something they believed was “pushing through.”

The dates coincided.

More disturbing were the newspaper cuttings. On 28 February an earthquake had shaken portions of the Pacific with no clearly determined epicentre. Its tremors were not severe, but instruments from Australia to South America registered peculiar secondary movements continuing at irregular intervals throughout March. Ships reported unexplained waves under calm skies. Along the coast of Chile, thousands of fish were cast ashore, many belonging to deep-water species. On a small island east of New Zealand, a trading vessel found the native settlement deserted and its canoes drawn up in a circle around a stone platform stained with blood.

There were outbreaks of collective frenzy in Haiti and Manila; a nocturnal procession in Bombay halted only after military intervention; and rumours, too fragmentary for verification, of drumming heard from the interior of Africa. On 23 March—the night of Wilcox’s worst delirium—telegraph agencies recorded more unexplained disturbances than on any other date. Then, after 2 April, the agitation ceased with remarkable abruptness.

The Professor had underlined one sentence from a London medical correspondent:

*It is as though a pressure, exerted upon certain receptive minds from a source outside ordinary consciousness, had been suddenly withdrawn.*

Against this Angell had written: **Or submerged.**

I remained unconvinced. Selective evidence may establish any marvel, and my uncle’s inquiry had plainly been directed by an expectation he did not explain. Only when I opened the older of the two files did I discover the event which had prepared him to recognize Wilcox’s tablet. It carried the date 1907 and the title:

**REPORT OF INSPECTOR JOHN R. LEGRASSE, NEW ORLEANS POLICE—WITH REMARKS BY PROFESSOR WILLIAM CHANNING WEBB.**

The report concerned an idol.

It was identical to the thing in clay.

## II. THE SWAMP CEREMONY

Inspector Legrasse’s narrative had first been given, not in a police court, but before the annual meeting of the American Archaeological Society at St Louis in 1908. He had attended carrying a stone figure seized during a raid in the Louisiana swamps, hoping that one of the assembled scholars might identify it.

No one could.

The statuette was between seven and eight inches high, carved from a dark, greenish-black material which geologists present were unable to classify. It possessed the same pulpy head, tentacled countenance, scaly body, claws, and folded wings as Wilcox’s relief. It squatted upon a rectangular pedestal covered with undecipherable characters. Despite its size, observers agreed that it conveyed a loathsome impression of enormity. One professor declared that its proportions seemed designed for monumental representation, as though the small object were a reduced copy of an effigy hundreds of feet in height.

Legrasse explained that he had taken it on 1 November 1907 from worshippers assembled beyond the inhabited bayous south of New Orleans.

The affair began when several families of fishermen reported women and children missing from settlements near the swamp. Local search parties found no bodies, but heard drums during the night—faint at first, then rolling across the water with a rhythm unlike any customary festival. Because the people of the district refused to venture farther, the police organized a detachment of twenty men under Legrasse.

They travelled by boat through channels scarcely wider than the hulls, beneath cypresses whose hanging moss obscured the sky. At intervals they found crude signs cut upon the trees: a winged shape above a group of wavy lines, or a circle enclosing several black stars. The air grew warm despite the lateness of the season. Birds vanished. Even insects seemed absent, and the only sounds were the dipping of oars and, from an uncertain distance, the steady beat of drums.

Near midnight the channel widened into a stagnant lagoon. Beyond it rose an island of firm ground which no member of the party recalled seeing on any map. A glow flickered among the trees.

The officers concealed their boats and advanced through reeds until they reached a natural amphitheatre. Legrasse’s description, though restrained, was sufficiently vivid to trouble my sleep.

At the centre stood a granite monolith perhaps eight feet high. Upon it rested the black idol. Around the stone a great fire threw upward a column of greasy smoke, while outside the firelight nearly a hundred worshippers revolved in a broken circle. They wore no uniform vestment, yet all had marked their faces and bodies with the same devices. Some crawled; some leapt with contortions suggestive of puppets jerked by unseen wires; others prostrated themselves toward the idol and remained motionless while the procession passed over them.

Beyond the monolith, suspended upside down from three trees, were the remains of those who had disappeared.

The drums ceased. A chant began.

Legrasse wrote the sounds as accurately as memory permitted:

**Ph’nglui mglw’nafh Cthulhu R’lyeh wgah’nagl fhtagn.**

I need hardly state my reaction on finding the phrase from Wilcox’s dream recorded eighteen years earlier.

The police charged. Five worshippers were killed, several wounded, and forty-seven captured; the remainder escaped into the swamp with a facility implying long familiarity with its paths. The prisoners displayed little fear. Some laughed when threatened with execution. Others swayed in silence, their eyes fixed upon the southern sky.

Only one, an aged man known among them as Castro, gave any coherent account of their belief. His testimony was secured by promises which Legrasse admitted he had no intention of fulfilling.

The cult, Castro said, did not worship a spirit or god in any human sense. It served beings older than terrestrial life, the Great Old Ones, who had descended from the stars when Earth was young. Their bodies were not wholly matter as humanity understands matter, and their minds operated across distances which annihilated ordinary distinctions between thought and place. They had built enormous cities before the first animals moved in the seas. When cosmic conditions changed, they could no longer live freely upon the world and withdrew into stone chambers beneath ocean and earth.

They were not dead.

“Death,” said Castro, “is a habit of little creatures.”

The Old Ones waited. Their tombs preserved them, while their thoughts travelled outward through dreams. Sensitive persons occasionally received fragments of those thoughts, interpreting them as visions, madness, revelation, or art. The cult had been established among early humanity by intelligences which came from the dark to instruct chosen priests. It existed in scattered groups across the globe, communicating rarely and guarding its doctrine through murder when necessary.

Chief among the sleepers was Cthulhu, entombed with his servants in the drowned city of R’lyeh beneath the southern Pacific. When the stars assumed their proper relation, the city would rise. The priests would open the seals, Cthulhu would awaken, and the world would become his dominion again.

Asked what would then happen to mankind, Castro smiled.

Men would cease to be men, he said. The restraints invented by conscience, law, and identity would dissolve. Humanity would learn the liberty of the Old Ones—the liberty to kill, exult, destroy, and transform. Voices carried through dreams would teach new ways of being, after which Cthulhu would stride between continents and the earth would flame with a happiness no sane person could survive.

The chant, Castro explained, meant approximately:

**In his house at R’lyeh, dead Cthulhu waits dreaming.**

The translation was necessarily imperfect. The word rendered as “dead” implied a condition neither life nor death, and “dreaming” signified an activity combining thought, command, and hunger.

At this point in Legrasse’s account, Professor William Channing Webb of Princeton had interrupted the meeting. Webb, an explorer and anthropologist of considerable reputation, asked to inspect the idol. After doing so, he stated that he had seen one like it forty-seven years before.

In 1860, while attached to an expedition along the western coast of Greenland, Webb encountered an isolated Inuit community whose customs differed notably from those of neighbouring settlements. Their habitations surrounded a cleft of volcanic rock, from which a warm vapour issued even in winter. They avoided ordinary hunting grounds during certain phases of the moon and preserved a cycle of legends concerning gigantic beings who slept beneath the sea.

Webb was permitted to observe, from a distance, one of their ceremonies. The worshippers assembled around a crude stone figure, sang a phrase the explorer had transcribed phonetically, and sacrificed several dogs before the entrance to the cleft. His notebook, produced at the St Louis meeting, gave the refrain as:

**Pahn-glooee ma-gal-nahf Kthooloo Reh-yah wah-nakal f’tan.**

Allowing for the different ears and orthographies of the recorders, the correspondence was exact.

Here were two cults separated by climate, language, ancestry, and thousands of miles, yet possessing the same idol, the same chant, and the same doctrine of a sleeping entity beneath the sea. No known commerce connected them. The Greenland rite was already ancient in 1860; Castro claimed that his Louisiana branch derived from ceremonies carried westward before the earliest European voyages.

The archaeologists debated diffusion, fraud, cryptomnesia, and coincidence. No conclusion was reached. Legrasse retained the idol, refusing numerous offers to surrender it to a museum. Professor Webb died in 1919. The matter survived only in private correspondence—until Wilcox, who had never met any participant and was not born at the time of the raid, modelled the same monstrosity after a dream in 1925.

I spent much of the following year attempting to dismantle my uncle’s case.

Wilcox was my first object. I visited him without explaining my relationship to Angell and purchased several sketches under the pretence of furnishing a private collection. He remembered the Professor with embarrassment but had no interest in occultism. His library contained no archaeological works, and he could neither pronounce the chant correctly nor recall its meaning. When I showed him a deliberately inaccurate copy of the Legrasse idol, he identified several errors at once, then became pale and asked where I had obtained it.

“From no place I’ve visited,” he said. “At least—not awake.”

His room contained recent studies of the city: tremendous facades emerging from waves, streets bending into impossible perspectives, and constellations arranged in forms unknown to astronomy. He had resumed dreaming after my uncle’s death, though less vividly than in 1925. The recurrence persuaded me that he might be perpetrating an elaborate deception, but I found no motive and no evidence.

From Providence I travelled to New Orleans, where Legrasse, now retired, received me with courtesy. He confirmed every important feature of his report. The idol remained in his possession, locked in an iron cabinet. When placed beside photographs of Wilcox’s relief, it revealed correspondences too minute to be accidental: a notch above the left eye, six divisions on each wing, and a peculiar asymmetry in the claws. Yet the stone figure was unquestionably old, while the clay had been modelled before my uncle showed Wilcox any image.

Legrasse advised me to abandon the subject.

“Your uncle asked too many questions,” he said. “There are people who hear when that name is spoken.”

I took this for a policeman’s taste for melodrama. I no longer do.

The press cuttings occupied me next. By consulting newspaper archives and meteorological records, I verified nearly every incident in Angell’s compilation. Some reports had been exaggerated, but the broad pattern remained. Between 28 February and 2 April 1925, a disturbance centred somewhere in the southern Pacific had coincided with a worldwide epidemic of abnormal dreams.

Thereafter I might still have retreated into uncertainty. The facts were strange, but not conclusive. Similar symbols can arise independently; witnesses may influence one another; scholars may unconsciously arrange data to gratify a theory. I had almost resolved to seal the box and return to my proper work when a scrap of Australian newspaper, found accidentally among unrelated papers, completed the structure my mind had laboured to keep incomplete.

It concerned the rescue at sea of a Norwegian sailor named Gustaf Johansen.

The date was 18 April 1925.

## III. THE VOYAGE OF THE *EMMA*

The article, from the *Sydney Bulletin* of 25 April, reported that the steam yacht *Vigilant* had arrived towing a disabled launch found adrift south-west of the Pacific islands. Its sole living occupant, Second Mate Gustaf Johansen of Oslo, had been delirious from exposure. A second man lay dead beside him. The launch belonged to the schooner *Emma*, which had sailed from Auckland on 20 February with eleven men and had not returned.

Johansen stated that the *Emma* had been attacked by an armed vessel, the *Alert*, after refusing an order to alter course. The schooner was sunk; its surviving crew boarded the attacker, killed its crew, and continued in the captured vessel. Several days later they discovered an unknown island. What occurred there Johansen refused to describe. The *Alert* was subsequently lost, and only he and one companion escaped in a boat.

The geographical position printed in the article lay near the suspected centre of the February earthquake.

More extraordinary still, the *Alert* had been manned by persons drawn from various Pacific ports who were known to police as members of an obscure religious association. Authorities recovered from the dead sailor in Johansen’s boat a small metal image. The newspaper’s description was brief—“a winged, octopus-headed figure of apparently ritual character”—but sufficient.

I wrote at once to maritime officials in Sydney and Auckland. Their replies confirmed the article while adding little. The image had vanished from police storage. The *Alert* was believed to have sailed from Dunedin, though its registered movements could not be reconstructed. Johansen had returned with his wife to Norway and declined all requests for interviews.

In October 1928 I travelled to Oslo.

The address supplied by the shipping office led me to a narrow house near the old docks, but I was too late. Johansen had died six months before. His widow, a grave and kindly woman who spoke little English, told me he had survived his voyage physically but had never again slept in darkness. He kept lamps burning throughout the night and started whenever he heard dripping water. He had written much in secret, always in Norwegian, and sealed the manuscript in a metal box.

His death had been peculiar. Returning one evening through a crowded lane, he cried out and fell. Two sailors were seen hurrying away, but no one could describe them. Physicians attributed the death to heart failure. The resemblance to my uncle’s end struck me with a force I endeavoured not to betray.

When I showed Mrs Johansen Angell’s photograph, she recognized him. A letter from the Professor had reached her husband shortly before his death.

She gave me the manuscript.

I read it that night in my hotel, aided by a dictionary and my imperfect knowledge of Danish. The prose was plain, almost brutally practical. Johansen had written not for publication but to rid himself, if possible, of memory. Certain passages were blurred where the pen had torn the paper. Others ended in repetitions or shapeless marks. I translated the narrative after returning to America, and what follows is condensed from that translation.

The *Emma*, a two-masted trading schooner of Auckland registry, departed on 20 February 1925 bound for Callao. Her crew consisted of Captain Collins, First Mate Green, Second Mate Johansen, and eight seamen. Weather remained ordinary until 28 February, when a tremor passed through the vessel though the sea was calm. That night several men dreamed badly. One awoke insisting that a bell was ringing beneath the hull.

On 1 March the compass showed small but recurrent deviations. Clouds gathered in the south, coloured at sunset by a greenish reflection for which no auroral or meteorological explanation seemed adequate. The men became irritable. Two reported hearing speech mingled with the creaking timbers.

On 12 March the *Emma* encountered the steam yacht *Alert*. Its crew signalled the schooner to turn north and threatened violence when Collins refused. The strangers displayed no recognized flag. Their gestures were frantic, and through glasses Johansen saw that several wore pendants representing a winged figure.

The attack began before dusk. Gunfire disabled the *Emma* and killed Captain Collins and three sailors. The survivors, carried alongside by a change in wind, boarded the *Alert* and overcame its crew after a savage struggle. Green assumed command. No useful charts were found, though the cabin contained navigational calculations centred upon a position not marked as land. The same position appeared on scraps of paper beside the words **R’lyeh** and **Cthulhu**.

Johansen did not know those names. This point is important. His manuscript had been sealed before any contact with me, and Mrs Johansen understood neither their spelling nor significance.

The *Emma* sank. The survivors continued aboard the *Alert*, intending to reach a shipping route. Yet contrary currents and repeated failures of the compass drew them south. The sea became littered with dead fish. A heavy odour, compounded of salt marsh, opened graves, and the interior of long-sealed caverns, hung over the water.

At dawn on 22 March, sailor Briden sighted land.

It had not been there the previous evening.

The men first mistook the dark elevation for storm cloud. As the light increased, it resolved into a coastline of black and green stone rising almost vertically from the sea. No beach, vegetation, or birds were visible. Great terraces ascended toward masses hidden in vapour. Water poured from them in cataracts, showing that the entire formation had lately been submerged.

Green consulted the charts. No island existed within hundreds of miles.

They might have fled but for the damaged engines, the adverse current, and the human compulsion to set foot upon a thing which reason declares impossible. The *Alert* approached a narrow inlet where enormous blocks formed a semblance of harbour. The crew anchored at 10:15 in the morning. At 10:40, Green, Johansen, Briden, Rodriguez, Williams, Parker, and two others went ashore.

The masonry was cyclopean beyond comparison. Every stone exceeded a house, yet the surfaces were fitted without visible joint or mortar. Carvings covered them—spirals, clusters of stars, marine organisms unknown to natural history, and repeated figures of the tentacled entity. Slime made every foothold treacherous. A mist moved among the structures with an apparent independence from the wind.

Johansen’s account became uncertain when describing the geometry. He repeatedly complained that directions would not remain fixed. A facade seen on the right would, after a few paces, appear behind and above them. One man leaned against a surface he believed vertical and fell forward as though descending a steep slope. Another stepped across an angle between two blocks and vanished to the waist in what had seemed solid shadow.

The city was not constructed for beings who perceived space as men do.

They climbed toward a vast open place bordered by monoliths. In its centre stood a portal—if that word may be applied to a black polygonal surface whose dimensions altered with the observer’s position. From one angle it seemed a doorway at the base of a cliff; from another, a hatch lying upon the ground; from a third, an aperture suspended against the sky.

Reliefs around it showed lines descending from stars, winged shapes moving among planets, and small upright creatures kneeling before a mountainous figure. Some scenes appeared less carved than fossilized, as though the stone had grown around events still occurring within it.

Parker, examining the portal, discovered a projection like a lever. Green ordered him not to touch it. At that instant the ground trembled. The men heard a sound below them—not the cracking of rock, but the inhalation of some prodigious sleeper.

They should have run.

Parker pressed the projection.

The portal opened inward, though Johansen could not say toward what direction “inward” lay. Darkness emerged from it. This was his phrase, and he underlined it twice. The blackness did not merely occupy the opening; it flowed outward like a substance, followed by a stench so ancient and intolerable that two men collapsed.

Then something began to ascend.

Johansen refused for several pages to describe it. He wrote of claws grasping the edges of the aperture; of a pulpy mass forcing itself through an opening impossibly small for it; of wings unfolding against towers; and of flexible appendages searching the air with a loathsome deliberation. At last he wrote one complete sentence:

**The idol was its portrait.**

Cthulhu had awakened.

No numerical measure can communicate the dimensions implied by Johansen’s words. The thing rose above monoliths which themselves dwarfed cathedrals. Its head rolled forward beneath its own gelatinous bulk, while the tentacles surrounding its mouth writhed across distances the sailor compared to a ship’s length. Vast membranous wings opened, dripping the ooze of the abyss. Its body possessed no stable contour; parts swelled, dissolved, and re-formed, as if ordinary matter were only a temporary suggestion imposed upon it.

Yet the worst was not magnitude or form. It was recognition. The men knew that they had seen it before—in dreams forgotten upon waking, in childhood terrors, in the instinctive recoil from deep water and starless night. The shape had lain behind the mind of the species since its beginning.

Green screamed once. A claw descended, and he ceased to exist.

The others fled. Rodriguez and Williams ran toward what appeared to be the lower side of a slanting monolith, only to fall upward along its face and disappear over an edge hundreds of feet above. Parker was overtaken by the tentacles. Two sailors reached an angular fissure through which the creature could not pass; but the fissure changed orientation as they crossed it, closing upon them like the jaws of a trap.

Only Johansen and Briden gained the shore.

Behind them Cthulhu moved through the city. Buildings shook without collapsing, for their architecture obeyed stresses unknown to human engineering. The entity advanced by walking, crawling, and a third mode for which Johansen had no word. Its chant—or thought, or breath—entered the sailors’ skulls without passing through their ears.

They reached the *Alert* and cut the anchor rope. Briden tended the engine while Johansen took the wheel. For several minutes it seemed that they might escape. The island was already descending; water boiled in the streets, and green foam rose between the monoliths.

Then Cthulhu entered the sea.

Its motion created no ordinary wave. The ocean appeared to recoil. Johansen, looking astern, saw the colossal head and wings drawing nearer, while tentacles beat the water on either side of the vessel. The engines could not outrun it.

He made his decision without consulting Briden.

Turning the *Alert* through half a circle, he drove it directly at the pursuing monstrosity.

There is in this act a simplicity surpassing heroism. Johansen had no expectation of killing the thing. He knew only that flight was impossible and that forward motion remained the last expression of human will available to him. He lashed the wheel, increased power, and watched the impossible face swell before the bow.

The ship struck.

The result was not the impact of steel against flesh, but a bursting dissolution. The prow entered the creature’s head amid a noise like innumerable bubbles exploding beneath pressure. A deluge of green-black matter swept over the deck. The wings collapsed; the tentacles broke into ropes of vapour and slime; and the vast body sagged into the sea.

Johansen turned the vessel again.

When he looked back, the scattered material was drawing together. Streamers of substance flowed against the waves; fragments united; the outline of the head rose anew. The thing was reforming.

But R’lyeh was sinking. Towers vanished one after another beneath the water. Whirlpools opened around the city, pulling the unfinished shape toward the descending portal. The stars, perhaps shifted by some minute celestial interval, no longer favoured its emergence. Cthulhu’s regenerated claws grasped at the sea, and its voice filled Johansen’s mind with a fury so enormous that he lost consciousness.

He awoke after nightfall. No island was visible.

Briden lived for six more days but never spoke rationally. He sat facing the stern and laughed whenever the sea was calm. On the seventh morning Johansen found him dead. The *Alert*’s engines failed soon afterward, forcing Johansen into the launch in which the *Vigilant* discovered him.

The manuscript ended with words written in a deteriorating hand:

*It is below again, but not as men are below the earth. It sees through sleep. Sometimes I hear the door. If the stars return to their places, the stone will not hold. I have told no one, because telling is a kind of opening.*

After translating this narrative, I compared its dates with Angell’s records.

R’lyeh rose on or about 28 February, when the Pacific earthquake occurred and the dreams began. Wilcox’s visions grew more distinct as the sailors approached the island. On 23 March, when the portal opened and Cthulhu emerged, sensitive minds throughout the world reached the height of delirium. In the early hours of 2 April, according to maritime estimates reconstructed from Johansen’s account, R’lyeh sank.

Wilcox recovered that same day.

The correspondence admits no tolerable explanation.

I have spent two years seeking one.

Astronomers assure me that the visible stars underwent no unusual arrangement in 1925; but visible position may be irrelevant, and the bodies involved may lie beyond optical detection. Geologists deny that an island of such size could rise and sink without leaving broader evidence; yet they cannot explain the recorded disturbance or why depth soundings near the coordinates disagree from one survey to another. Psychologists speak of collective delusion, though no mechanism of collective delusion can transmit an unknown word from Louisiana to Greenland, from a Norwegian sailor to a Providence sculptor, or from the ocean floor to the locked wards of distant asylums.

The evidence forms a circle. Every avenue of escape returns me to its centre.

I now understand why my uncle concealed his work. He had arrived, perhaps gradually, at the same conclusion. He knew that scattered cults preserve the memory of beings whose dominion antedates humanity; that their symbols remain unchanged across millennia; that dreams are not always private phenomena; and that somewhere beneath the southern ocean lies a city constructed when the continents were young.

I believe also that he was killed.

The sailor who jostled him was never found. Johansen, after receiving Angell’s letter, died in an almost identical manner among the docks of Oslo. The Legrasse idol vanished from police evidence in Australia, though no ordinary thief could have known its importance. Since my return from Norway, I have twice observed a man standing across from my rooms after midnight. He is not always the same man. The posture is the same.

Yesterday I discovered a smear of black, briny mud upon the inside sill of my study window.

I do not know whether the cult understands how much I possess. Perhaps these pages will be their warning. Perhaps they already wait only for a suitable occasion, preferring deaths which physicians can certify and newspapers ignore. I have arranged copies of the essential documents in several places, though I suspect precautions to be useless against an organization which has endured from prehistory and receives instruction through dreams.

There are moments when I doubt everything. By daylight the city is full of traffic, argument, commerce, and trivial urgency. Men hurry to appointments; bells mark the hours; buildings stand obediently upon their foundations. It seems impossible that beneath the same sun there could exist minds to which all human history is less than the stirring of mould upon a stone.

Then night comes.

I dream of a black sea beneath unfamiliar constellations. Far below, towers wait in green darkness. Their angles are hidden by silt, but I know they do not sleep. A door larger than any mountain bears the weight of the ocean, and behind it something thinks with a patience no human chronology can measure.

Sometimes it thinks of Wilcox.

Sometimes of the cult.

Lately it thinks of me.

The old chant returns with the pulse of the tide. I hear it in rainwater descending the pipes, in engines labouring at the harbour, in the half-articulate sounds of sleepers. I have come to believe that the words are not praise but measurement—a formula recording the relation between tomb, sleeper, and stars. When the relation is correct, no mortal lock will matter.

What, then, is mankind? We congratulate ourselves upon a few thousand years of memory, as if memory conferred ownership. We name epochs after the scraps of bone found in shallow earth, while beneath our oceans lie architectures raised by intelligences that crossed the gulfs between suns. Our sciences measure what happens to be near enough and small enough for our instruments. Beyond that narrow radius extend ages and powers before which reason itself may be no more than a local adaptation, useful to a transient animal and meaningless to the universe.

The greatest mercy is not ignorance, for ignorance may be repaired. It is incoherence—the failure of truths to assemble. Angell assembled them. Johansen supplied the final piece. I, in my arrogance, joined them and saw the picture.

Now I cannot unsee it.

Should I die suddenly, let no investigator be deceived by the absence of violence. Look to the docks, to the foreign sailors who leave no records, to the quiet men who wear small objects beneath their coats. Yet do not pursue them far. The cult is not the horror. It is only the shadow cast forward by what sleeps.

Burning these pages would accomplish nothing. Publication might accomplish worse. I therefore leave them sealed, hoping that whoever inherits them will possess the wisdom I lacked and refuse the temptation to verify what ought to remain doubtful.

For the sea keeps its monuments.

The stars proceed without haste.

And in the drowned, Cyclopean halls of R’lyeh, where green ooze drifts across the images of forgotten worlds, Cthulhu waits—not dead, not dreaming as we dream, but listening through the frail minds of men for the hour when the door shall open and never close again.

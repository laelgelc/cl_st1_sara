### THE YEARS YOU LOSE

To live forever, you must first agree to die—again and again.

One moment there is sunlight.

It lies hot upon your shoulders, flashes on the blue water of the lake, and makes Barbara narrow her eyes when she smiles at you. You are sitting on the end of the pier in your swimming trunks, your feet dangling above the water. Somewhere behind you a portable radio is playing. Barbara is saying something about Sunday.

Then the sunlight vanishes.

You are standing.

And naked.

Your feet rest on a cold, smooth floor. Your hands fly out and strike walls scarcely an arm’s length away. Four walls. No window. No door you can feel. The ceiling is perhaps eight feet above you, glowing with a pale light that seems to come from everywhere and nowhere.

“Barbara?”

Your voice comes back flatly.

You press both palms to the wall. It is warm, faintly yielding, like hard rubber. You turn once in the cramped space, then again, searching for a seam.

There is none.

Your heart pounds. You remember the pier, the lake, the date.

July 17, 1954.

You say it aloud.

“This is nineteen hundred and fifty-four.”

It sounds foolish, but saying it steadies you.

“My name is Norman Hastings. I am twenty-five years old. This is nineteen hundred and fifty-four.”

No one answers.

A line appears in the wall.

You jerk back. The line widens into a black rectangle as a section slides silently aside. Beyond it is a room, twelve feet wide and perhaps sixteen long. It contains a table, a chair, a narrow couch and what might be a closet. None looks quite right. The chair has no visible legs. The table seems molded from one piece of gray glass.

You wait.

Nothing moves.

You step through.

The wall closes behind you.

You spin and beat upon it. No result. The glowing surface has become seamless again.

Think.

You were beside Barbara. You had just asked whether she wanted to drive to Millbrook for supper. She laughed because your car had been making that noise. Then—

Then this.

Kidnapping? Hallucination? Some government experiment?

You inspect yourself, half expecting injury. Your body is exactly as you remember it: lean from swimming, a bruise on the left shin, a white scar below the right knee. Even the small cut on your thumb, acquired that morning while opening a tin, is present.

The closet opens when you touch it. Inside hangs a garment of silvery material and a pair of dark slippers. No underwear. No buttons. The garment parts down the front, then seals when you draw the edges together. The slippers shape themselves around your feet with such comfort that you resent them.

On the table lies an envelope.

NORMAN HASTINGS, it says.

The handwriting is yours.

Not merely similar. Yours.

You seize it and turn it over. Sealed. Beneath your name is a second line:

READ THIS BEFORE TRYING THE DOOR.

You look toward the only visible door in the room.

Of course you try it.

There is no handle, only a dark plate. It ignores your touch. You push, kick once, then return to the table.

Do you blame yourself?

You tear open the envelope.

Inside are twenty closely written pages.

The first sentence reads:

Dear Norman—The date is July 17, 2004.

You laugh.

It is not an amused laugh.

You read the sentence again, then examine the paper, handwriting and room as if one of them must confess to the joke.

The letter continues:

You have already looked for the signature. It is on the last page, and it is mine—ours. But if you turn there now, you will only waste time and return to this page.

You turn to the last page.

The signature is Norman Hastings.

Your signature. Changed slightly, made firmer and more angular, but yours.

You return to the first page.

I know precisely what you remember. You remember sitting beside Barbara Welles at Lake Cardon. You remember asking about Millbrook. You remember the radio playing behind you. Your next sensation was darkness, followed by the light in the chamber.

This is not because I have been watching you. I remember it.

I am Norman Hastings.

I am seventy-five years old.

Or I was until six minutes ago.

You put down the letter.

The room remains quiet. The light remains steady. Your hands do not.

“This is nineteen hundred and fifty-four,” you say.

But the words have weakened.

* * *

The old man—yourself, the letter insists—has anticipated disbelief and arranged proof.

A drawer in the table opens when you press a marked corner. Inside are photographs, documents and newspapers.

You recognize yourself in the first photograph. You are older, perhaps thirty, wearing a dark suit. Barbara stands beside you in a wedding dress.

You touch her face.

The next photograph shows Barbara holding an infant.

The third shows you alone with a boy of about four.

You stop there.

A marriage certificate gives the date as November 6, 1954. A birth certificate names Andrew Norman Hastings, born the following year.

There is also a death certificate.

Barbara Hastings.

You do not pick it up at first. The name is enough.

Then you do.

Cause of death: viral pneumonia.

Date: March 12, 1958.

Three and a half years after your wedding. Forty-six years before this room.

“No.”

It is a small word.

Only minutes ago Barbara sat beside you in the sun. Her shoulder touched yours. A drop of water hung from her hair. You were going to take her to supper.

Now the paper says she is dead.

You sit on the floating chair. It yields beneath you.

For a while there is no future, no strange room, no impossible letter. There is only a date printed in black ink and the intolerable fact that the world has gone on without your permission.

You try to picture forty-six years of Barbara not existing.

You cannot.

The letter waits.

At last you wipe your eyes with the back of your hand and continue.

I am sorry. No sentence can make this easy. I considered omitting the certificates, but you would search for her as soon as the door opened. It is better that you learn here.

Our son Andrew is alive. He is forty-eight years old, married, and has two daughters. You must not contact him yet. To him, his father is—or was—an old man. Your appearance would expose everything before you have decided what must be done.

You feel anger then. Hot, useful anger.

What right had the old man to do this? What right had he to drag you from Barbara, strip fifty years from your mind, and leave grief in their place?

But you have not been dragged from 1954. The letter says so. Barbara lived, married you, bore your son and died. You remained. You aged. You worked.

You simply do not remember any of it.

Fifty years have been cut from you as neatly as film from a reel.

* * *

You are a physicist—or will be. In 1954 you are still young enough to be called promising by men who mean inexperienced. The older Norman writes without pride that he eventually deserved the promise.

In 1981, while studying temporal asymmetry in subatomic decay, you observed an effect no accepted theory predicted. Certain particles, exposed to a shaped electromagnetic field, did not move backward through time. Their internal conditions reversed.

The difference matters.

The letter explains it with experiments.

You placed a metal cube in the first chamber. The cube had been machined that morning and deliberately scratched. After three seconds of exposure, the scratches disappeared. Analysis showed the metal restored to a condition twelve hours earlier.

You increased the field.

A tarnished silver disk became bright. A snapped wire became whole. A steel sample fatigued by repeated stress regained its original strength.

At first you believed matter was being transported into the past. But clocks outside the field continued normally, and the objects did not vanish from their own histories. The cube had still been manufactured, scratched and tested. Only its present physical state changed.

You named the process state regression.

An object inside the field is forced toward an earlier arrangement of itself.

Not an earlier place.

Not an earlier date.

An earlier condition.

The guinea pigs followed.

An elderly animal entered the chamber and emerged young. A scar disappeared. A damaged liver became healthy. The regressed animal retained behaviors learned before the selected point—but not after it.

Memory, after all, is physical. It is a pattern in living tissue. Reverse the tissue far enough and the pattern goes with it.

You pause.

The chamber behind you is four feet square.

Your cut thumb is still present because you had it this morning—fifty years ago, in the state to which your body has been returned.

You stand and cross to the blank section of wall.

The chamber is not a time machine in the sense imagined by Wells or the motion pictures. It has not carried you from 1954 to 2004.

You lived those years.

The machine has carried your body backward through its own changes.

Seventy-five to twenty-five.

Gray hair to brown. Weak heart to strong. Arthritic hands to steady ones. Every cell restored.

And every memory after this afternoon erased.

You touch your face. It feels familiar. Of course it does. It is the only face you know.

The old man is gone.

No—worse.

The old man is you.

* * *

The next pages discuss the machine’s uses.

They are obvious before you read them.

Cancer could be reversed to a stage before the first malignant cell. A ruined kidney could become healthy. Paralysis caused by injury could vanish if the body were returned to a condition before the accident.

Age itself would become optional.

At seventy, enter the chamber. Leave at thirty. At seventy again, repeat.

Immortality.

The word should thrill you.

Instead you look at Barbara’s death certificate.

If the machine had existed in 1958, you could have saved her.

If it had existed one day before she fell ill—

You crush the edge of the page.

Then comes the arithmetic.

In 2004, the world holds more than six billion people. Each year millions are born and millions die. Remove age and disease, and death becomes uncommon. Accidents, violence, perhaps certain sudden infections—but little else.

The old remain.

The young arrive.

Within decades there would be ten billion, then twenty, then more. Food, water, land and energy would fail. Governments would try to restrict use. The wealthy would buy it. Armies would seize it. Religions would divide over it. Nations would go to war for chambers that offered eternal youth.

Birth laws would follow. Enforced sterility. Secret children. Rebellion.

Or perhaps the machine would be reserved for the “valuable”: rulers, scientists, generals, those able to convince themselves that their continued lives mattered more than other lives.

Do you doubt it?

You do not.

The letter offers no dramatic prophecy, only population tables. The figures are enough.

Human beings may one day spread beyond Earth. They may learn to build self-supporting cities on other worlds. They may accept limits on childbirth without tyranny or murder. If so, the machine could become a blessing.

But not now.

The final pages ask for a decision.

Destroy it, and humanity loses the greatest medical discovery ever made.

Reveal it, and humanity may destroy itself.

Hide it, and one man must become its keeper.

You search the room again with new eyes. The machine is built into the walls. The notes, controls and essential components lie somewhere beyond them. The door remains sealed because the old Norman did not trust even himself to act before reading.

You resent him for that.

You resent his calm handwriting, his predictions, his merciless logic.

On the last page he writes:

You now believe I was a coward. You think I should have made the decision myself instead of giving it to a young man who has lost everything.

I did make it.

But I could not know whether age, fear of death or attachment to my own work had corrupted my judgment. So I returned myself to the man I was before the discovery, before ambition and regret could influence me.

You are not another judge, Norman.

You are the same judge without the evidence of fifty years pressing upon him.

Choose.

Beneath the signature are three instructions.

Speak DESTROY, and the apparatus will melt its core and burn all records.

Speak RELEASE, and the door will open, the research will be transmitted to several laboratories, and the world will learn.

Speak PRESERVE, and the room will open without transmitting anything. You will inherit the property, the concealed laboratory and sufficient funds to guard the secret.

You read the choices again.

Destroy.

Release.

Preserve.

Simple words.

* * *

You pace.

Destroying the machine feels like murder. How many children will die of diseases it could cure? How many men and women will grow feeble, blind, confused? How many Barbaras will be buried because you chose silence and fire?

Yet releasing it would not save everyone. Not for long. It would create a world where death is a privilege demanded from others.

You try to imagine trusting governments.

Trusting scientists.

Trusting yourself.

That is the sharpest point. If you preserve the machine, you will possess the means to restore anyone. Andrew, the son you do not remember. His wife. His daughters. Would you refuse them if they became ill? Could you watch Barbara’s child die while salvation stood in the next room?

The old man anticipated this too.

A final note is written across the back page:

Andrew believes the machine failed and that my work was abandoned. He must continue to believe it. I have arranged the legal transfer through an identity prepared for you. You will not meet him as his father. That is part of the cost.

You almost speak DESTROY then.

Not from wisdom. From fury.

Let the old man’s work burn. Let his traps and secrets burn with it.

But he is not here to punish.

He ended in the chamber.

You are what remains.

The machine is too dangerous to reveal and too valuable to destroy. Between those facts there is only one narrow road.

You know the choice before you admit it.

“Preserve.”

For a moment nothing happens.

Then a tone sounds inside the wall. The dark plate beside the door brightens. The door slides open upon a corridor.

Beyond it waits the year 2004.

You do not step out.

There is one more paragraph in the letter, printed beneath the fold.

Fifty years may pass before the world is ready. If it is ready, give it the machine.

If not, you know what to do.

Return to the chamber at seventy-five. Set the field for fifty years. Leave a letter.

You stare at the words.

In fifty years you will be old. You will remember a life you have not yet lived, a son you cannot claim, perhaps friends and loves still unknown to you. You will possess half a century of thought, pain and compromise.

Then you will erase it.

The young man who wakes will know Barbara as living. He will learn again that she is dead. He will hate the old man who imprisoned him. He will read the experiments, calculate the danger and arrive—if character is constant—at the same answer.

Preserve.

Again.

Perhaps in 2054 humanity will be ready.

Perhaps not.

Perhaps in 2104.

2154.

The dates march forward while you remain twenty-five, each life ending not in death but in deliberate forgetting.

You understand the opening sentence now. To live your life again, you must surrender the life you have lived. Immortality is not one endless road. It is a row of roads, each cut off from the next by darkness.

Who, then, survives?

The body?

The name?

The decision?

You fold the letter carefully. Your hands have stopped shaking.

Across the corridor a panel gleams like a mirror. You see a young naked-faced stranger in a silver garment, standing at the threshold of a century he does not know.

Behind him, reflected by the polished wall of the room, is another image of the same man. And behind that, fainter, another. The surfaces face each other, multiplying you as barber-shop mirrors do—Norman after Norman, each smaller, each farther away, each believing himself the first.

You look down that corridor of reflections until the faces are too small to distinguish.

Then you pick up the evidence of your vanished life and step into the future.

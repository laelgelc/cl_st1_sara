At nine o’clock precisely, while the curtain was descending upon the second act, Henry Latham remembered that he had forgotten something.

It was not, properly speaking, a remembrance. No image accompanied it; no name rose suddenly illuminated in the mind; no neglected appointment, unpaid account, unanswered telegram presented itself for correction. There was merely the conviction—instantaneous, complete, and extraordinarily disagreeable—that something important had been omitted.

He sat forward in the narrow stall and frowned.

About him the theatre resumed its ordinary existence. Programmes rustled; women leaned shining heads together; men escaped sideways towards the bar. The orchestra, sunk below the footlights, produced small experimental groans. Henry looked at his watch.

Nine.

But where, what, with whom?

His engagements were not of a kind that vanished. They stood in his diary in black ink, reinforced by his secretary’s memory and his own. At forty-eight he possessed the comfortable certainty of a man whose life has been entered, checked, and balanced. He sold machinery; he understood contracts, shipping, discounts, men. He was neither imaginative nor absent-minded. Imagination, indeed, he regarded as a loose screw in otherwise serviceable intelligence.

Yet the sensation remained.

He searched his pockets and read the two letters he found there. One concerned a shipment delayed at Hull; the other invited him to golf on Sunday. Neither helped him. He reconstructed the day—office, luncheon, bank, barber, dinner, theatre—and discovered no gap.

Then uneasiness moved in him again, more strongly. It seemed to rise from below thought, pressing upwards like water beneath a locked door.

“Nerves,” he decided.

The word annoyed him. His digestion was excellent, his sleep punctual, his nerves—if such things existed apart from doctors’ invoices—had never troubled him. Nevertheless, when the orchestra began and the audience settled, he found the darkened stage intolerable. The painted room, the false moon beyond its window, the voices making counterfeit grief—all oppressed him. Something waited elsewhere. Something had been overlooked, left undone.

He remained five minutes. Then, with the guilty irritation of a man leaving a dull committee, he pushed past protesting knees and went out.

The night was clear and unexpectedly mild. A taxicab carried him westward through streets varnished by recent rain. Electric signs burned above the shops; buses thundered; beneath the measured roar of traffic sounded another murmur, deeper and continuous, like a sea heard from an upper room.

Henry noticed it, and was surprised that he noticed it.

At his building the porter touched his cap.

“No messages, sir.”

“I didn’t ask if there were.”

“No, sir.”

Henry entered the lift, feeling absurdly rebuked. Of course there were no messages. Whose message did he expect? At the third floor he let himself into his flat and switched on every light in the hall.

The familiar order received him: the brass bowl for cards, the framed engraving of St Paul’s, the dark stand bearing two overcoats and three golf sticks. Nothing had altered. The sitting-room was warm; the evening papers lay folded beside his chair; whisky and soda waited on the tray. His housekeeper had drawn the curtains exactly to the central line.

He mixed a drink, lit his pipe, and sat down.

For perhaps a minute he knew relief. Here were walls, leather, firelight, tobacco—solid witnesses on behalf of reason. He opened the financial paper and read half a column about Argentine railways.

Then he lowered it.

There was someone in the flat.

The certainty did not arrive as sound. No board creaked, no handle turned. Rather, the silence had changed its quality. It was occupied. Somewhere beyond the sitting-room—perhaps in the passage, perhaps behind the closed door of his study—an attention answered his own.

Henry stood up.

“Who’s there?”

The flat gave back only the faint ticking of the clock.

He examined the rooms methodically. Dining-room, study, spare bedroom, kitchen; cupboards also, though he despised himself for opening them. The windows were fastened. The servants had gone. Nothing living shared the place with him.

And yet, when he returned to the sitting-room, the presence returned too—not nearer, because nearness did not belong to it, but more distinct. It surrounded rather than approached him. It appeared to wait at every door simultaneously, asking admission without speech.

He poured more whisky than he intended.

This was ridiculous. Fatigue, indigestion, some pressure upon the brain. He would telephone Dr Marlow in the morning. Meanwhile he would read, smoke, and behave as usual.

But usual behaviour had become difficult. The lamp seemed violent; the furniture stood about him with a heavy, obstinate lifelessness. Even the clock ticked aggressively, chopping eternity into manageable commercial fragments.

He was shivering.

Then his eye fell upon the curtains.

A silver line showed where they met. He watched it, at first merely because it was the only cool thing in the over-lighted room. It widened—or seemed to widen—as the curtains stirred above the radiator. Moonlight. There was nothing remarkable in moonlight; he had seen it on roads, on golf courses, on railway platforms. Yet now that thin brightness drew him with an urgency more powerful than fear.

He crossed the room and pulled the curtains apart.

London lay below him.

He had looked from that window a thousand times and seen roofs, chimneys, aerials, offices, the square black backs of houses. To-night he saw space. The city had lost its boundaries. Its roofs flowed away like a dark tide, touched here and there with silver; domes floated; towers rose as though from submerged kingdoms. Above them travelled clouds of an almost transparent whiteness, and behind the clouds burned innumerable stars.

Henry stared.

Beauty smote him between the eyes.

He could have used no other word, though the word had hitherto belonged to catalogues, women, and men who wore soft collars. Beauty entered now as a power—feminine perhaps, imperious certainly—breaking through shutters he had kept closed so long that he had forgotten they existed. It came with moon and cloud, with wet roofs gleaming, with the remote breathing of the streets; it came, strangely, with a scent of leaves and cold earth, though no garden lay near enough to send it.

He turned off the electric lamps.

The room opened.

Moonlight spread across the carpet in long pale bars. The polished table became a dark pool; the glass upon it held one trembling star. His possessions withdrew modestly into shadow, no longer insisting upon their price and usefulness. The presence was still there. But fear had altered. It had thinned into expectancy.

“Who are you?” he whispered.

Something touched memory.

A face peeped, as it were, from behind a curtain—not yet visible, but smiling. A voice once known hovered beyond hearing. Youth stirred remotely, absurdly: cheap rooms, tobacco smoke, arguments continuing towards dawn.

He resisted. Why should youth return? He had put it away with other unprofitable experiments. Yet memory, patient as a woman certain of welcome, remained at the threshold.

Growth—the word occurred to him.

Growth? He dismissed the word. Men grew until twenty-one; businesses grew; investments grew. A middle-aged consciousness did not enlarge suddenly at a window because the moon happened to be full.

Nevertheless, enlargement was precisely what he felt. His awareness moved outwards, upwards. It passed beyond the room, beyond the façade of the building, beyond the disciplined streets. He saw the procession of the clouds; he saw the stars between them; he saw moonlight descending in a rain of quiet fire. And he felt, not thought, that these separate splendours were gestures of one vast living thing—Night, mysterious and myriad-eyed, bending over the city with an immemorial tenderness.

The scent of leaves strengthened.

Then a name sounded within him.

Straughan.

Henry gripped the window-frame.

Philip Straughan—poor, untidy, extravagant Straughan, who painted pictures nobody bought and heard music in common noises; who could stop in a muddy lane because a green shoot had pierced a wall; who once spent his last sovereign on a blue Persian bowl and borrowed supper afterward. They had shared rooms for six months in Chelsea, before Henry entered his uncle’s firm and began the long ascent towards solidity.

Straughan had called beauty necessary.

Henry had called it decoration.

How fiercely they had argued! Straughan, thin and ardent, flinging open the window to a winter dawn; Henry demanding that he shut it and save coal. Yet affection had lived beneath their quarrels—an affection Henry had never named because naming feelings seemed to him a way of exaggerating them.

The curtain of memory drew wider.

He recalled their last evening before Straughan sailed for India. There had been wine—bad wine—and rain tapping at the panes. Straughan had received some commission involving murals for a princely house. He was full of India already: temples, red dust, bells, mango groves, Himalayan snows. Henry had laughed at half of it and envied the rest.

Then death had entered their talk, as naturally as another guest.

“If there is any road back,” Straughan had said, “I shall find it.”

“You’ll be too busy painting angels.”

“There will be no angels. There’ll be colour, though. And sound. Perhaps one comes back in the thing one loved best.”

Henry had poured the wine.

“Very well. Whoever goes first must report to the other.”

“Seriously?”

“As seriously as the subject permits.”

They had shaken hands across the table.

The memory stood complete.

For more than twenty years Henry had not thought of that promise. Letters had dwindled, then ceased. There had been rumours of travel—Bombay, Kashmir, some native court beyond Jaipur. Henry had married and been widowed; enlarged the firm; changed his clubs and his address. Straughan had passed behind the furniture of his busy life and disappeared.

Until now.

“Nine o’clock,” Henry said.

At that instant the church clocks began to strike the half-hour. The sounds crossed and mingled above London, solemn, sacerdotal, as though hidden towers celebrated a rite for which the whole bright night had been prepared.

The presence pressed close—not against his body, but against that inward door whose hinges had so long rusted. He understood its appeal now.

Let me in.

And Henry, who had locked out grief, wonder, useless delight, and much besides, made no resistance.

The change was simple. Nothing appeared. No pale shape gathered in the moonlight; no hand touched his; no remembered voice spoke his name. Yet the night poured through him. The beauty Straughan had worshipped became for one immeasurable moment his own element. He saw that loveliness was not ornament laid upon the world, but revelation shining through it; that cloud, stone, star, smoke, the cries of traffic and the quiet roofs were characters in one hierophantic script. He saw—and the practical phrase would have offended him yesterday—that the universe possessed an interior.

Tears ran down his face.

He neither wiped them away nor felt ashamed. Wonder rose in him, wave after wave, until even sorrow was transfigured. The unseen companion seemed everywhere: in the silver on the chimneys, in the wild fragrance no London garden could explain, in the deep spaces between stars. Not a ghost, then, but a gift; not an invasion, but an opening.

How long he stood there he never knew.

At last the clouds covered the moon. The city became ordinary by degrees, though never wholly ordinary again. Henry closed the window, but left the curtains wide. He went to bed without switching on the lights, and slept with the strange peacefulness of one who has kept an appointment.

During the following days he told no one. Business continued. Ships were delayed, contracts signed, telephones answered. Yet small treacheries of beauty overtook him: sunlight in a clerk’s ink-bottle, rain upon black branches, the crimson lining of a woman’s umbrella. Each time something in him widened.

Three weeks later a letter arrived bearing Indian stamps.

It was from a doctor at a hill station whose name Henry had never heard. Philip Straughan, after a brief fever, had died there at nine o’clock in the evening—nine by London time—on the night Henry had left the theatre.

The letter fell upon the desk.

For a while Henry sat motionless. Then he crossed to the window and looked out upon a grey, commercial afternoon. Above the warehouses one narrow opening showed blue.

He understood.

Straughan had kept their appointment. He had found his road back—not in shape or voice, not with any vulgar disturbance of doors and curtains, but in the splendour that had been his native speech.

He had come back in the beauty he loved.

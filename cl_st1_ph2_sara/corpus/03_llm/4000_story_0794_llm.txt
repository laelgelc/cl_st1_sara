### The Yellow Roses

Before the war the Myrovers had been numbered among the substantial families of Clarendon. They had owned land enough to satisfy any reasonable ambition, and slaves enough to cultivate it; and if their house had not possessed the magnificence which later generations, with the assistance of memory, attributed to all houses of the old régime, it had at least been broad, cool, and hospitable, with white columns in front, a grove of oaks behind, and a garden whose roses were famous throughout the county.

Colonel Myrover had gone into the Confederate army with two sons, four horses, and an orderly who had belonged to him from childhood. He returned with neither sons nor horses, and with one sleeve pinned across his breast. The orderly, having acquired his freedom without asking the colonel’s consent, came home by another road and took up land for himself. The colonel survived the new order of things by only a few years. He could face bullets with composure, but taxes, mortgages, hired labor, and the political equality of his former servants presented difficulties for which his education had not prepared him.

At his death there remained to his widow and his daughter Mary the old house, stripped of most of its furniture; twenty acres of exhausted land; a number of family portraits, which could neither be eaten nor advantageously sold; and an income sufficient, by strict economy, to maintain appearances from a distance. Appearances, in Clarendon, were of no small consequence. Poverty was unfortunate, but poverty borne with dignity might still be respectable. Work was necessary, but not every kind of work was suitable. A Myrover lady might sew secretly, take in a boarder under the name of a guest, or sell preserves through a confidential intermediary; she could not, without loss of caste, stand behind a counter, keep accounts in a public office, or instruct the children of those who had once worked in her father’s fields.

Unfortunately, the kinds of labor which public opinion permitted were precisely those for which Mary had no talent. Her sewing was neat but slow; her preserves, though excellent, could not compete with cheaper manufactures; and the spare chamber, having a cracked ceiling and a chimney that smoked in damp weather, attracted no permanent guest. Mary was twenty-six, healthy, intelligent, and conscientious. These qualities, which in a man might have been esteemed a capital, were in her situation chiefly useful in teaching her how steadily the little capital left by her father was disappearing.

When, therefore, the superintendent of the colored schools offered her a position, she received the proposal with mixed feelings. The salary was small, but regular. The occupation itself was honorable in the abstract, for teaching had always been considered proper for a lady; yet the particular pupils to be taught introduced a complication which the most liberal person in Clarendon could not entirely ignore.

Mrs. Myrover ignored nothing.

“Your father,” she said, when Mary told her of the offer, “would sooner have seen you buried.”

Mary, who knew that her father had been a brave and affectionate man, but who had also observed that the dead are often credited with opinions useful to the living, made no direct reply.

“We must live, mamma,” she said after a pause.

“We have lived.”

“We have spent nearly all that was left from the timber money.”

“There are still the south fields.”

“They are mortgaged.”

Mrs. Myrover drew herself upright. Poverty had narrowed her figure without bending it. Her black silk dress, preserved from better days and turned twice, gave forth a faint rustle of protest.

“To teach negroes!” she exclaimed. “The children of our own servants, perhaps! People whose parents have forgotten everything we did for them!”

The old lady’s recollection of what had been done for them did not include the labor they had performed for the Myrovers; memory, like other household servants, was expected to know its place.

Mary accepted the position.

For several weeks Mrs. Myrover spoke of the school only as “that place.” She would not permit Mary to enter the front parlor in the dress she wore while teaching, and required her to leave her books in the back passage. These precautions were not altogether hygienic. They expressed, with the delicacy proper to a cultivated household, the distinction between those whom Providence had placed above and those whom Providence, assisted by law and custom, had kept below.

Mary herself began her duties with a resolution to be just. She had inherited most of the prejudices of her class, but not its harsher temper. She believed that colored people were improvident, imitative, emotional, and incapable of sustained mental effort; she also believed it a Christian duty to improve them. The contradiction caused her no discomfort. It was one of those arrangements by which benevolence may enjoy all the pleasures of virtue without surrendering any of the advantages of superiority.

The school stood at the edge of town, beyond the cotton warehouses and near a low, marshy branch. It was a plain frame building, formerly used as a military storehouse. The roof leaked in two places, one window lacked half its glass, and the winter wind entered freely under the door. There were benches without backs, a blackboard polished by long use, three maps of countries whose boundaries had already changed, and a stove which smoked when there was no fire and refused to draw when there was.

On Mary’s first morning thirty-seven children waited for her, in every variety of garment and condition. Some had books; some carried slates; several had nothing but their hands. Two little boys wore coats made for grown men, with the sleeves rolled above their wrists. One girl had tied a clean apron over a dress so patched that its original fabric could scarcely be determined. They stared at Mary with the frank curiosity of children who had not been trained to conceal their interest.

She called the roll, assigned seats, and began to discover that ignorance, while inconvenient, was not peculiar to any race, and that intelligence did not always respect the distinctions maintained by Clarendon society. The pupils were noisy, affectionate, inattentive, sensitive to praise, and ingenious in avoiding disagreeable tasks. In these respects they resembled other children, though Mary did not at first carry the comparison so far.

Among the older girls was one called Sophy Tucker. She was twelve, small for her age, and very dark, with thin arms, grave eyes, and hair arranged in many narrow braids. Her mother took in washing; her father, when sober, worked at the sawmill. Sophy possessed one faded blue dress for school and a brown one for Sundays. Her shoes were too large, having been given to her by a woman whose daughter had outgrown them; but she kept them carefully blacked, and stuffed rags into the toes to prevent them from slipping.

From the first day she attached herself to Mary with a devotion which asked no encouragement and was discouraged by no reserve. She arrived before the others to sweep the floor, fill the water bucket, and wipe the blackboard. At noon she gathered Mary’s books before Mary had risen from her chair.

“Lemme tote ’em, Miss Ma’y,” she said.

“They are not heavy, Sophy.”

“I knows dey ain’t heavy. I kin tote ’em jes’ de same.”

Mary permitted the service, not because she needed it, but because refusal seemed to wound the child. Sophy walked beside her as far as the corner where the respectable portion of Clarendon began. There Mary generally took the books.

“You may go back now,” she would say kindly.

“Yes’m.”

Sophy understood the boundary, though no sign marked it. She would stand for a moment, watching Mary walk toward the avenue, and then turn homeward by the less frequented street.

In the spring she began to bring flowers. They were not the fine roses for which the Myrover garden had once been celebrated, but common blossoms gathered from narrow yards—violets, snowdrops, sprays of bridal wreath, and later a cluster of yellow roses from a bush behind the Tuckers’ cabin. The stems were sometimes short and the leaves insect-bitten, but the flowers were always fresh.

“Dey come off’n my own bush,” Sophy said proudly, presenting the first roses. “Mammy say it ain’t much account, but it blooms mighty free.”

“They are very pretty,” said Mary. “Thank you, Sophy.”

She placed them in a tumbler on her desk. Sophy looked at them all morning, and whenever Mary’s sleeve brushed the bouquet, the child smiled.

Mary became an excellent teacher. Conscientiousness did for her what enthusiasm might have done for another. She studied methods, imposed order without cruelty, and spent part of her small salary upon chalk, copybooks, and fuel. The children learned rapidly. Sophy learned fastest of all. Within a year she could read a newspaper with only occasional hesitation, write a legible hand, and perform fractions with a confidence which would have surprised several persons in Clarendon who were certain that education was wasted upon her race.

Mary’s interest in the school ceased, little by little, to be merely dutiful. She remembered the pupils’ birthdays; she visited two when they were ill; she persuaded the superintendent to repair the roof. On Christmas Eve she gave each child a small bag of candy and an orange. She did these things without abandoning the social philosophy in which she had been reared. She loved the children, but loved them as dependents; she respected their efforts, but could not quite conceive that their hopes and griefs might possess the same dignity as her own. Had the question been put plainly, she would have denied any difference in their souls before God. In the daily arrangements of life, however, she observed a great many differences which God had not specifically mentioned.

Sophy occasionally came to the Myrover house on errands. She was never admitted at the front. Dinah, an elderly colored woman who cooked for the family three days each week, received her at the kitchen door.

“You set right dere,” Dinah would say. “Don’t you go traipsin’ roun’ de yard. Ole Miss don’t like chillen messin’ wid her flowers.”

The flowers, by that time, consisted of two struggling rosebushes, several clumps of narcissus, and a bed of geraniums. Mrs. Myrover guarded them with the vigilance once extended over twenty acres of ornamental grounds.

One afternoon Mary found Sophy waiting on the back step with a parcel of copybooks which had been left at the school.

“You should not have brought these all the way,” Mary said. “It is very warm.”

“I didn’t mind, Miss Ma’y.”

Sophy’s eyes rested upon a length of pale ribbon with which Mary had tied some papers.

“You like it?” Mary asked.

“Yes’m. Hit’s mighty pretty.”

Mary cut off a piece and handed it to her. “You may have this.”

Sophy received the ribbon with solemn delight. It was not new, and one edge was frayed, but it had belonged to Mary.

“I’m gwine keep it always,” she said.

“You may use it on your Sunday dress.”

“No’m. I’m gwine keep it.”

Mrs. Myrover, appearing in the passage, saw the child and frowned.

“Mary, must the school follow you home?”

“She only brought my copybooks, mamma.”

“She might have left them at the gate.”

Sophy heard. She rose quickly.

“I’s goin’, Miss Ma’y.”

Mary flushed slightly, more from annoyance at her mother’s rudeness than from sympathy with the child’s humiliation.

“Run along, then,” she said gently. “And thank you.”

Sophy went around the house, keeping carefully to the path. At the gate she looked back. Mary had already entered the kitchen.

Prince, the Myrovers’ dog, followed Sophy to the road. He was a brown-and-white setter, old enough to have acquired discretion, but young enough to preserve an interest in whatever might be eaten, chased, or affectionately greeted. Sophy was fond of him, and Prince, whose notions of caste were imperfect, returned her regard without embarrassment.

“Go on back, Prince,” she whispered, embracing his neck. “You b’longs ter Miss Ma’y.”

Prince licked her hand and trotted obediently toward the house.

During Mary’s second year at the school, the principal of the white academy invited her to assist with the younger classes. The salary was somewhat larger, and the position would have restored her, in the opinion of Mrs. Myrover, to respectable employment. Mary considered the offer and declined it.

“The colored school needs me more,” she explained.

Mrs. Myrover received this declaration as fresh evidence of the demoralizing influence of changed conditions.

“You have become positively infatuated with those people,” she said. “They will accept everything and be grateful for nothing.”

Mary thought of Sophy’s flowers, of the clean blackboard every morning, and of thirty-seven eager faces bending over their books.

“I believe some of them are grateful,” she replied.

Her mother sighed with the bitterness of one whose world had been destroyed and who found the new one wanting in proper sympathy for the loss. Mrs. Myrover’s prejudices had been hardened by suffering. This rendered them explainable, which was not quite the same thing as rendering them just.

In early autumn Mary fell ill. She had returned from school one afternoon with a headache and a chill. By evening she had a fever. The physician called it an infection, prescribed rest and medicine, and assured Mrs. Myrover that a woman of Mary’s constitution should recover. For three days she seemed no worse; on the fourth she became delirious, and on the sixth the physician ceased to offer assurances.

The school was dismissed when news of her illness spread. Sophy came each morning to the gate and asked Dinah how Mary was.

“Po’ly,” Dinah said the first day.

“Restin’,” she said the second.

On the third she covered her face with her apron.

“Miss Ma’y done gone, honey.”

Sophy stood still. The sunlight lay warm upon the gatepost. Somewhere behind the house Prince barked once and was silent.

“When she comin’ back?” the child asked.

Dinah lowered the apron. “She ain’t comin’ back. She’s gone ter glory.”

Sophy understood then. She turned away without speaking and walked home.

Mary’s death stirred Clarendon deeply. Whatever doubts had once attached to her occupation were softened by the finality which corrects so many social judgments. The newspaper praised her devotion to an humble duty. The minister referred to her as a noble daughter of the South, who had sacrificed herself upon the altar of Christian service. Ladies who had crossed the street to avoid meeting her pupils now spoke tenderly of her labors among “the unfortunate children of our dependent race.”

Mrs. Myrover accepted their condolences, but not their interpretation.

“The work killed her,” she said. “Two years in that wretched school, breathing foul air, wearing herself out for negroes—and this is the result.”

Whether the school had contributed to Mary’s illness no one could say. The physician did not think so, but Mrs. Myrover’s grief required an object less sacred than Providence, and the colored population was available.

“No negro is to enter this house,” she told Dinah. “Not one. I will not have them crowding in to stare at her.”

Dinah, who had dressed Mary for her first party, nursed her through measles, and cooked for her during the lean years after the war, received permission to remain because the household could not conveniently dispense with her. This exception did not modify the principle.

Mary was laid in the parlor beneath the portraits of her ancestors. The old room, seldom used, had been opened and dusted. White flowers covered the coffin—lilies from the church, roses from the ladies of the academy, carnations sent by the superintendent, and a wreath bearing the words OUR BELOVED TEACHER. The wreath had been purchased by the white teachers, though the inscription might reasonably have included others.

Sophy heard that Mary was to be buried on Thursday. On Wednesday evening she went behind the cabin to the yellow rosebush. The season was nearly over, but seven blossoms remained, some fully opened and two still in bud. She cut them all. Her mother gave her a piece of damp cloth to wrap around the stems.

“Dey ain’t gwine let you in dat house,” Mrs. Tucker warned.

“I got ter carry ’em.”

“You kin carry ’em, but dey ain’t gwine let you in.”

Sophy said nothing. From a box beneath her bed she took Mary’s ribbon. She had indeed kept it always. It was creased from folding, but clean. She tied it around the stems, making and remaking the bow until it pleased her.

The next morning she washed with unusual care, braided her hair afresh, and put on the brown Sunday dress. She carried the roses before her, guarding them from dust and sunlight.

At the Myrover gate several carriages stood along the road. White visitors entered by the front walk. Sophy went around to the kitchen, where she found Dinah moving between stove and pantry, her eyes swollen with weeping.

“I brung Miss Ma’y some roses,” Sophy said.

Dinah looked at the bouquet and then toward the passage.

“Ole Miss say nobody colored ain’t ter come in.”

“I ain’t gwine do nothin’. I jes’ wants ter put ’em by her.”

“I knows, honey.”

“Miss Ma’y liked my roses.”

“I knows she did.”

Sophy waited.

Dinah wiped her hands upon her apron. “Gimme de flowers. I’ll put ’em in.”

Sophy drew the bouquet against her breast. She had imagined herself carrying the roses to Mary, seeing where Mary lay, and placing them with her own hands. To surrender them at the kitchen door seemed only a little less complete than taking them home.

“I wants ter take ’em.”

At that moment Mrs. Myrover appeared. Grief had sharpened her face and reduced her patience.

“What is this, Dinah?”

“It’s Sophy, ma’am. She brung some flowers fer Miss Ma’y.”

Mrs. Myrover glanced at the child and the small yellow bouquet.

“The house is full of flowers. We need no more.”

Sophy tightened her fingers around the stems.

“Miss Ma’y liked dese,” she said.

Mrs. Myrover’s lips compressed. “Take the child away, Dinah. I will not have a scene.”

There had been no scene, unless a child standing quietly with flowers constituted one; but Dinah knew the uses of that word.

“Come ’long, honey,” she murmured.

Sophy walked back to the gate. Prince came bounding from beneath the porch and tried to follow her, but a servant called him back. The dog paused between the house and the street, uncertain which affection duty required.

“Go ter Miss Ma’y,” Sophy told him.

Prince returned to the porch.

The funeral was to be held at Saint Luke’s Church. Colored persons were not expressly forbidden to attend services there, for Saint Luke’s prided itself upon Christian hospitality; they were expected, however, to enter by a side door and sit in the gallery. On this occasion the church was crowded. Mary’s pupils came in a body, under the care of an assistant teacher, but found the gallery nearly filled by servants and colored members of the congregation.

The usher was a very reasonable man. He explained that fire regulations did not permit additional persons upstairs, and that all seats below had been reserved for the family and their friends.

“They may stand outside,” he said. “The windows are open. They will hear quite well.”

Thus the pupils assembled beneath the long side windows. Some could hear the organ; a few could see through the raised sash. Sophy stood near a broken pane from which a triangular piece of colored glass was missing. Through the aperture she saw part of the chancel and one end of the coffin.

Above it glowed a stained-glass figure of Christ blessing the children. The figure’s hands were extended with impartial tenderness toward small faces of several conventional shades. The artist, not having lived in Clarendon, had neglected to provide separate galleries.

The minister spoke at length of Mary’s life. He praised her gentleness, her fortitude in adversity, and her willingness to labor among the lowly. He said that in heaven there would be no sorrow and no separation. Sophy listened intently. She could not see Mary’s face, nor could she make her roses pass through the narrow space in the glass. Their petals had begun to droop.

When the service ended, the assistant teacher tried to keep the children together. The coffin was carried down the aisle; Mrs. Myrover followed, leaning upon a cousin’s arm. Behind her came the relatives, the academy teachers, church officers, neighbors, and persons whose acquaintance with Mary had been slight but whose regard for funerals was considerable.

Sophy stood near the church steps. For one instant the coffin passed close to her. She moved forward with the bouquet, but a gentleman directing the procession put out his hand.

“Stand back, little girl.”

“I got flowers fer Miss Ma’y.”

“You must not interfere with the procession.”

His tone was not unkind. He merely had a duty to perform and performed it according to the established order.

The hearse moved away. Carriages followed. Mary’s pupils walked behind them until the procession turned toward the white cemetery. The assistant teacher then stopped.

“You children must go home now.”

Sophy did not go home. She waited until the others had scattered, then followed at a distance.

The cemetery occupied a green hill beyond town. An iron fence surrounded it, and the only gate opened upon a gravel drive. Near the entrance a white sign bore a notice in neat black letters:

VISITORS ARE REQUESTED TO OBSERVE THE DECORUM OF THIS PLACE. ADMISSION RESERVED TO MEMBERS OF THE WHITE RACE.

The wording was so courteous that no one could reasonably have complained of its spirit. It threatened no violence, employed no offensive epithet, and denied admission with all the politeness compatible with denying it absolutely.

Sophy read the notice slowly. Mary had taught her to read.

She watched the carriages enter. The gatekeeper knew most of the drivers and scarcely looked at them. When Sophy approached, he stepped in front of the gate.

“You can’t come in here.”

“I’s wid de funeral.”

“Doesn’t matter.”

“I got flowers fer Miss Ma’y.”

“You can leave them outside if you want.”

She looked past him. Prince had jumped from one of the carriages and was trotting beside the mourners. No one stopped him. The dog crossed the gate freely, his feathered tail raised, his bright eyes searching for the mistress who had not answered him for three days.

Sophy moved away from the entrance. She followed the fence until she reached a place from which the grave could be seen between the iron bars. She was perhaps fifty yards distant. The minister stood at the head of the open earth. Around him gathered a dark semicircle of mourners. The white coffin rested above the grave, nearly hidden beneath flowers.

Sophy knelt in the grass. The roses were badly wilted now. One petal loosened and fell upon her dress. She pressed the stems through the bars, but the bouquet was too large. The ribbon caught upon an iron point.

Inside the cemetery Prince wandered from person to person. During the final prayer he moved toward the fence, having caught a familiar scent or seen a familiar form. He came across the grass and stood opposite Sophy.

“Prince,” she whispered.

The dog thrust his muzzle between the bars. Sophy put one hand through and stroked his head.

“You kin go in,” she said. “Dey lets you go in.”

Prince wagged his tail.

Sophy looked toward the grave. The minister had closed his book. Men were preparing to lower the coffin.

She untied the ribbon, narrowed the stems, and bound them again. Then she pushed the bouquet through the bars. It fell on the grass inside.

Prince sniffed it.

“Take ’em, Prince,” Sophy said. “Take ’em ter Miss Ma’y.”

The dog looked at her.

She picked up the bouquet and placed it as far as her arm could reach. “Fetch, Prince.”

Prince knew that word. Mary had taught him to carry gloves, newspapers, and once, to the admiration of her pupils, a little basket from one end of the schoolroom to the other. He took the stems gently in his mouth.

“Dat’s right,” Sophy whispered. “Take ’em ter Miss Ma’y.”

Prince turned.

At first he seemed disposed to carry the bouquet toward the gate, perhaps supposing that any object received near a fence ought to be delivered at an entrance. Then he heard the clods of earth fall upon the coffin and saw the people gathered. He trotted toward them, the yellow roses swinging from his mouth.

No one noticed him until he reached the grave. Mrs. Myrover, lifting her veil for air, saw the dog approach.

“Prince!” she exclaimed faintly.

He passed between two mourners, came to the mound of flowers, and laid Sophy’s bouquet upon it. The pale ribbon rested against a bank of lilies. One broken yellow blossom rolled a few inches and stopped upon the dark earth.

“How touching,” murmured a lady. “The faithful creature has brought flowers to his mistress.”

Several persons agreed that it was a remarkable proof of canine intelligence. Mrs. Myrover began to weep, and the dog was led away lest he disturb her.

Outside the fence Sophy watched. From where she knelt, the mass of funeral flowers showed white against the newly turned soil; among them, scarcely visible, lay a small dash of yellow.

She remained until the mourners had gone and the workmen began filling the grave. Then she rose. Her hands were scratched where she had thrust them through the iron bars, and the damp cloth which had wrapped the roses lay empty at her feet.

Prince had been taken away in the last carriage. The cemetery gate was closed. The courteous sign shone in the afternoon sun.

Sophy looked once more toward the grave and started home. Her tribute had reached Mary at last. It had passed the house that would not admit her, the church that could find no room for her, and the cemetery whose rules excluded her. It had required, to accomplish this simple journey, the assistance of a creature to whom Clarendon conceded privileges denied to a child.

The yellow roses faded before night. The ribbon remained among the flowers until wind and rain carried it into the grass. Whether Mrs. Myrover ever learned who had sent them is doubtful. Whether Mary, had she lived, would have understood the full measure of Sophy’s devotion is more doubtful still.

But Prince had understood enough.

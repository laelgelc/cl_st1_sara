The upper bay of Grandfather’s barn was our chosen country on those long, mellow afternoons when September had begun to lay a yellow finger here and there upon the maples, though summer still lingered in the hollows and along the river. There the hay, sweetened by months of darkness and warm weather, rose in fragrant cliffs on either hand; through the broad door we saw the low meadow, with its silver threads of water, its clumps of purple aster, and its ranks of elms standing as grave and peaceful as a congregation at prayer. Below us the hens went murmuring among the chaff, now and then breaking into a confidential “craw, craw,” as if some matter of importance had been settled; and from the orchard came the lazy dropping of apples, each with a soft thud that made the silence seem deeper.

It was there that Sam Lawson found us one afternoon, my brother and me, lying upon the hay and watching motes turn slowly in the bars of sunlight.

Sam had come ostensibly to mend a rake-handle, but as he had brought neither knife, wedge, nor hammer, and as he sat down at once with his back against a beam, we understood that Providence had designed him for other employment.

“Sam,” said my brother, “did Captain Kidd ever bury any money along our river?”

Sam looked at us for some moments with the deliberate pity which age bestows upon youth when youth happens by accident to approach an interesting subject too directly.

“Wal, boys,” said he, “there’s them that says he did, and there’s them that says he didn’t; and them that says he didn’t mostly never went arter it, so their testimony ain’t wuth so much as t’ other sort. A man can set by his kitchen fire and say there ain’t no bears in the North Woods; but that don’t alter the tracks in the snow.”

“Were there tracks?” I asked.

“Not bear-tracks,” said Sam. “Money-tracks. Signs. Marks on rocks. Dreams. Divinin’-rods that would turn in a man’s hand like a trout on a line. And there was old Aunt Nabby Pease, that woke up three nights runnin’ with the taste o’ silver dollars in her mouth. That’s a cur’ous circumstance, though Nabby did take calomel frequent, and calomel will do sing’lar things to folks.”

“But did you ever dig for Kidd’s treasure?”

“Me? Lordy massy, no!” said Sam, with virtuous warmth. “I never dug for no treasure. I was present when some misguided critturs undertook suthin’ o’ that natur’; but I didn’t have no share in it. Wouldn’t. Not for kingdoms. ‘Hastin’ to be rich,’ Scriptur’ says, ‘pierces a man through with many sorrers,’ or words to that amount. I allers held that money come by honest labor was the only money a man could lay his head down alongside of and sleep peaceful.”

My brother glanced at Sam’s hands, which bore no signs of recent conflict with honest labor, but he prudently asked only, “Who did dig?”

“Wal,” said Sam, settling deeper into the hay, “to begin at the beginnin’, ye’ve got to know suthin’ about Cap’n Kidd.”

“We know about him.”

“No, ye don’t,” said Sam. “Boys never know nothin’ complete. They know the middle and want the end, but the beginnin’ they despise, though that’s commonly where the moral lays hid.

“Kidd was a respectable seafarin’ man to start with, so I’ve heerd. Wore good broadcloth of a Sunday, give to the poor when folks was lookin’, and could say grace to a dinner without peekin’ under his eyelids to see who got the breast o’ the chicken. Then he took to piracy. It was gradual, I s’pose. Sin mostly is. Fust a little suthin’ that don’t seem to signify; then another little suthin’; then, afore ye know it, ye’re sailin’ under a black flag with a knife in your teeth and the judgment day astarn of ye.”

Sam paused, folded his arms, and began in a cracked, melancholy chant:

> “My name was Captain William Kidd,  
> As I sailed, as I sailed;  
> Much wickedly I surely did,  
> As I sailed.  
>   
> “I struck a man and laid him low,  
> And where he sleeps no soul may know;  
> The tide comes in, the tide goes out,  
> And ghosts they walk the sands about—  
> As I sailed.”

“That isn’t all the song,” I said.

“No,” replied Sam; “and it’s a mercy. Caesar Peters used to fiddle thirty-seven verses of it at muster-time, and every verse was more discouragin’ than the one afore. There was one about his mother, and one about the gallows, and one about Satan waitin’ on the wharf. Caesar could draw that fiddle-bow acrost the strings till it sounded like a rusty gate swingin’ in a graveyard wind.

“Wal, Kidd sailed up and down, robbin’ ships and murderin’ folks, and when he got more gold than was convenient to carry, he buried it. That’s reasonable. Gold is heavy, boys. Folks that ain’t got none thinks it’s light as vanity, but them that has it knows better. He’d come ashore by night with a crew and a chest, and choose a likely place—a p’int, or a big tree, or a rock with a mark on it. Then he’d make one o’ the crew dig the hole. When the hole was done and the money in, Kidd would kill the digger and put him atop of it.”

“What for?”

“To guard it.”

“A dead man couldn’t guard it.”

Sam turned upon me almost severely.

“Couldn’t he? Then why don’t folks go alone through buryin’-grounds at midnight? A live watchman has to be paid, and he may go to sleep or take to drink; but a spirit will stay by treasure a hundred year, and never ast for higher wages. Kidd was a forehanded man in some respects.”

The particular hoard about which we had asked, Sam continued, was believed to lie under a great gray stone on the riverbank, two miles above the village. It was a lonely place even at noon. The river there narrowed between steep banks; ancient hemlocks leaned over the water, and the roots of a blasted pine gripped the earth like the claws of some buried animal. Upon the stone itself were three marks: a cross, a crooked line, and a hollow shaped like the print of a thumb.

“Now,” said Sam, “some allowed the cross meant religion, which Kidd hadn’t ought to have trifled with; some said it stood for ten thousand pounds; and old Deacon Barlow said the crooked line meant the ways o’ sin. But Mother Hokum said it was a shovel.”

“Who was Mother Hokum?”

“Wal, she was Hokum’s wife,” said Sam, as if the information were exhaustive. “Folks called her Mother because she had enough children to mother the county. Their house was a little brown concern back o’ the mill, with a roof that sagged in the middle and a winder propped up by a hymn-book. There was a tow-head to every crack and cranny. Look into the woodshed, and there’d be two young Hokums settin’ in a meal-barrel; lift the bedquilt, and like as not there’d be another asleep crossways at the foot. It put ye in mind of a rabbit-pen.

“Hokum himself was a mild, mournful man, with great ideas and poor execution. He could tell how to farm twenty acres, but couldn’t raise beans on one. He was allers jest goin’ to do suthin’. Monday he was goin’ to mend the fence; Tuesday he was goin’ to hire out; Wednesday he’d heard of a dog that could smell out copper mines, and by Thursday he was calculatin’ what he’d do with his profits.

“Mother Hokum, she took in washin’, spun wool, braided hats, nursed sick folks, and kep’ the family from droppin’ through the bottom of creation. She’d a tongue in her head, too. Parson Lothrop called once and told her poverty was a blessed discipline.

“‘So I’ve heerd,’ says she, wringin’ out a sheet, ‘but I notice the Lord mostly gives that discipline to them that’s got no choice, while you and Mis’ Lothrop are permitted to struggle along with two cows, a hired gal, and a smoke-house full o’ pork.’

“The parson says riches was vanity.

“‘Then,’ says she, ‘I wish Hokum had vanity enough to pay the butcher.’

“That butcher’s bill was an old affliction. Abner Sykes had sent it eleven times. The twelfth time Mother Hokum took the paper, folded it into a little boat, and set it sailin’ down the brook.

“‘There,’ says she, ‘if Abner wants his money, he can foller it to sea. Maybe Cap’n Kidd’ll pay him.’

“And that’s what set her thinkin’.”

“About the treasure?” my brother prompted.

“Jest so. She’d heard of the marked rock, same as everybody had. There was a divinin’-woman over in East Parish who said the money was there, and there was a red-haired pedler that dreamed of an iron pot three nights runnin’. Red hair is powerful for dreams, though uncertain in business. Then one fall Hokum got uncommon low. No corn, no pork, no shoes for half the children, and a note comin’ due at the bank. Mother Hokum stood him up by the woodpile.

“‘Hokum,’ says she, ‘you’ve talked about that treasure for fifteen year. Now you go and git it.’

“‘But, Mariar,’ says he, ‘it may be guarded.’

“‘You’ve got nine children,’ says she. ‘What’s one ghost more?’

“‘Diggin’s hard work.’

“‘Then git somebody that knows how.’

“So Hokum went to Toddy Whitney and Harry Wiggin. Toddy was called Toddy not because that was his lawful name, but on account of a drink he admired. He didn’t believe in spirits—not spirits without bottles, leastways—but he’d seen lights by the marked rock. Harry Wiggin had once found a counterfeit fourpence in a field and had considered himself gifted for treasure ever since. Both of ’em entered into the scheme hearty.

“But when it came to diggin’, Toddy had rheumatiz in his shoulder, Harry had a weak back, and Hokum was needed to direct. So they went after Primus King.”

Sam’s manner changed slightly when he spoke of Primus; there was in it the reluctant respect of an impractical man for one whom no amount of moonshine could deprive of common sense.

“Primus,” he said, “was the best hand with pick and spade in these parts. Strong, steady, and no more scared of a graveyard than he was of a turnip-patch. He listened while Hokum told about Kidd, the iron pot, the murdered sailor, and the ten thousand pounds.

“‘What do I get?’ says Primus.

“‘A share,’ says Hokum.

“‘A share of what?’

“‘Of the treasure.’

“‘Have you seen it?’

“‘Not yet.’

“‘Then I’ll take two dollars now.’

“Hokum argued and Toddy argued. Harry said it showed a want of faith. Primus says, ‘Faith don’t buy flour. Two dollars before I lift the spade.’

“‘But s’pose we find a million?’ says Toddy.

“‘Then you can afford the two dollars,’ says Primus.

“Mother Hokum heard of it, took the last money from the blue teapot, and paid him herself. That was wisdom. Whatever happened after, Primus’s treasure was already dug up.”

“And you went too?” I asked.

“Wal—not as a participator,” said Sam. “I went in a advisory capacity. Hokum come to me because I’d read considerable about supernatural manifestations, and because my great-uncle Peleg had owned a divinin’-rod.”

“Did the rod work?”

“It worked at turnin’,” said Sam. “Only trouble was, it turned jest as well over old boots as over silver. Uncle Peleg dug up seven boots, a stove-lid, and the skeleton of a cat, and then Aunt Jerushy chopped the rod for kindlin’.

“I told Hokum plain that buried money was Devil’s money. Spirits don’t take kindly to bein’ disturbed; and if Kidd had put a murdered man there, why, that man might feel he’d acquired rights in the property. I warned him.

“‘Sam,’ says Hokum, ‘you needn’t take a share.’

“‘I won’t,’ says I.

“‘But you might come along,’ says he, ‘and tell us what not to do.’

“That appeared Christian. A man may properly keep others from danger, even if it carries him two miles from home at midnight. I didn’t tell Mis’ Lawson exactly where I was goin’, because women are apt to confuse caution with superstition. I said I was settin’ up with a sick friend. Hokum was sick with poverty, and Toddy was commonly sick with suthin’, so it wa’n’t no lie in the gross, though perhaps it might have been improved in particulars.”

The night appointed was the last full moon of October. All day the sky had been pale and windless, and toward evening the frost sharpened the air. After supper, while the village settled into darkness and one kitchen light after another disappeared, the five men assembled behind Hokum’s woodshed.

Primus brought his tools. Harry brought a lantern, though they had agreed not to use it unless the moon failed. Toddy brought a stone jug, declaring that a swallow now and then was excellent against night vapors. Hokum brought a coil of rope, two sacks, and a list, prepared by Mother Hokum, of debts to be paid from the treasure.

“She’d put everything down,” said Sam, “from Abner’s butcher’s bill to a new meetin’-house bonnet. There was even ‘Hokum, a decent coat—if money remains.’ She understood his habits.

“We started nigh eleven. The moon was big and white over the east hill, and the leaves, what was left of ’em, shone red as old blood. The road was tolerable cheerful, but when we turned into the river path, the trees begun whisperin’ among themselves. There wa’n’t wind enough to stir a candle, but them trees whispered. Then a whippoorwill called on one side of us, and another answered from t’other.

“Harry says, ‘That’s a warnin’.’

“Toddy says, ‘Nonsense,’ and took a drink against it.

“Presently we come to the rock. Boys, it looked twice as big by moonlight. The mark on it wa’n’t a shovel then, nor a cross neither. It looked like a man with his head cut off.”

Sam paused until even the hens below seemed to listen.

“Now,” he resumed, “there’s rules in treasure-diggin’. Fust, ye mustn’t speak when once the spade goes in. Second, ye mustn’t answer if anything calls your name. Third, ye mustn’t look behind ye, no matter what comes. Most important of all, when the treasure shows, not one livin’ soul must say a word till it’s safe above ground. If ye speak, it’ll sink. That’s been proved time and ag’in.

“I explained all that. Toddy asked if whisperin’ counted. I said it did. Harry asked if groanin’ counted. I said likely it depended who groaned. Hokum asked what to do if the ghost attacked us. Primus drove his spade into the earth and said, ‘You hired me to dig. You gentlemen can settle the ghost.’

“Then we begun—or Primus begun. The rest of us stood round and assisted by lookin’ solemn.

“The dirt was tough with roots. Primus cut through ’em, steady as a clock. Chunk—scrape—thump. Chunk—scrape—thump. Every shovelful made the hole deeper, and every sound went rollin’ through the woods as if somebody under the ground was knockin’ back.

“After a spell the moon went behind a cloud. The river turned black, and a noise come up from it like a man drawin’ a long breath.

“Hokum edged near me.

“‘Sam,’ says he—forgettin’ the rule already—‘what’s that?’

“I wouldn’t answer. I jest pointed at the river.

“Toddy whispered, ‘Bullfrog.’

“Then suthin’ went who-o-o through the hemlocks.

“Harry grabbed my arm.

“‘That’s him,’ says he.

“‘Who?’ says Toddy.

“‘The murdered sailor.’

“Primus kept diggin’.

“‘Murdered sailors seem uncommon fond of owls,’ says he.

“At that Toddy laughed, but it wa’n’t a free laugh. Then he took another drink and declared he didn’t believe in ghosts, not one mite. Harry said neither did he. Hokum said he’d allers considered such things contrary to reason. And every one of ’em crowded nearer Primus.

“Down he went—down, down, down—till only his head and shoulders showed above the hole. We heard the spade strike stones, roots, gravel. Once it struck suthin’ hollow.

“Jink!

“Everybody stopped breathin’.

“Primus tried it again.

“Jink—jink!

“Boys, there’s no sound in this world like iron under a spade at midnight when ye’re expectin’ ten thousand pounds. It goes right through the ear and lays hold of the covetous part of the heart.

“Hokum dropped on his knees by the hole. Harry’s teeth begun to chatter. Toddy’s eyes looked as big as saucers.

“Primus cleared away the dirt with his hands. Presently he says, very low, ‘There’s something here.’

“I reminded him by signs that he mustn’t speak.

“He looked up and says, ‘Then how am I to tell you where to put the rope?’

“That was reasonable. Rules has to bend to circumstances.

“They lowered the lantern into the hole, with a coat thrown over it so the light wouldn’t attract spirits—or constables, for there was some question whose land we was on. We could see the curved black side of suthin’ big. Iron it looked to be. Primus scraped and scraped. There was a rim, and under the rim a kind of handle.

“An iron pot?” my brother cried.

“So it appeared,” said Sam. “A great iron pot, or kittle, or chest with rounded sides. They got the rope under the handle and hauled. It didn’t stir.

“They hauled again. Primus pried below with the pick, while Hokum, Toddy, and Harry pulled above. I stood apart.”

“Why didn’t you help?”

“Conscience,” said Sam. “Besides, there wa’n’t room on the rope.

“They tried and tried and tried. The rope strained. The handle creaked. The earth around the pot cracked loose, and from far off—or maybe from under our feet—come a groan.

“Hokum let go.

“Primus says, ‘Take hold.’

“Hokum took hold.

“Then they drawed and they drawed. Up come the pot an inch. Then another inch. Dirt rattled off it. Suthin’ inside went chink—soft and heavy. Coin, boys. Or pebbles. Pebbles will chink if they’re situated right, but nobody thought of that then.

“The moon come out sudden, shinin’ straight into the hole. There was the rim of the pot, risin’—risin’—and the rope stretched thin and hard.

“Hokum’s face was purple. Harry was prayin’ without makin’ a sound. Toddy had both feet braced against the rock. Primus stood below, heavin’ upward with his shoulder.

“Up it come—slow, slow—till the rim was level with the ground.

“And then Toddy Whitney hollered:

“‘We’ve got it!’

“At that instant the rope snapped.”

Sam clapped his hands sharply. We both started.

“Down went the pot. Jink! It struck once. Then again, deeper. Jink—jink—jink—down, down, down, as though the earth had opened its mouth and was swallowin’ it. The bottom fell out of the hole—or seemed to—and there come a laugh.

“Not a human laugh. No, sir.

“It begun low, under the roots: ‘Ho—ho—ho!’ Then it run along the bank and acrost the river and up among the trees, louder and louder, till the whole night appeared to be laughin’ at ’em.

“‘Ha—ha—ha-a-a!’

“Toddy fell backward. Harry went over the rock on all fours. Hokum jumped into the hole and clawed at the dirt with his hands. Primus climbed out considerable prompt.”

“What did you do?” I asked.

“Me?” Sam looked offended by the personal turn of the question. “I maintained myself as well as could be expected.”

“Did you hear the laugh?”

“Wal—now we’re comin’ to a p’int that needs explainin’.”

He removed a straw from his sleeve, examined it, and let it fall.

“Ye see, boys, I hadn’t slept well the night afore. Mis’ Lawson had been dryin’ apples, and the young one had the croup, and our brindle cow had got out about two in the mornin’. Then the walk, and the night air, and Toddy’s—well, never mind that—and standin’ still while Primus dug, all had a soothin’ effect.

“There was a soft place under the hemlock, banked with leaves. I set down there merely to meditate on the vanity of riches. And meditation, when pursued earnest, sometimes passes into sleep without a man bein’ aware of the exact boundary.

“So, strictly speakin’, I didn’t see the pot come up.”

“You were asleep!” my brother exclaimed.

“Not through the whole operation,” said Sam. “I seen the beginnin’, and I seen the consequences. A witness ain’t disqualified because he closes his eyes during portions not essential to his moral judgment.”

“Then you didn’t hear the laugh either?”

“I woke up with it—or with Hokum and Toddy makin’ a noise that might account for part of it. There’s owls, too, and echoes. I don’t undertake to say. A prudent man don’t explain more than Providence reveals.”

When Sam awoke, as he told us, the moon was shining peacefully over a scene in which peace had no other representative. Hokum had Toddy by the collar and was endeavoring to shake him apart.

“‘You spoke!’ Hokum says. ‘You opened your everlastin’ mouth!’

“‘How’d I know?’ says Toddy.

“‘Sam told ye.’

“‘Sam was asleep!’

“‘That don’t alter the rule!’

“Harry was kneelin’ by the hole, callin’ down into it, ‘Come back! Come back!’ which was foolish, because if it was treasure, it wouldn’t obey, and if it was a spirit, ye didn’t want it to.

“Primus sat on the marked rock laughin’.

“Hokum turned on him.

“‘What you laughin’ at?’

“Primus jingled the two dollars in his pocket.

“‘My treasure didn’t sink,’ says he.

“That made Hokum madder. He said everybody had cheated him—Toddy by speakin’, Harry by not pullin’ strong enough, Primus by havin’ no faith, and me by sleepin’ when I ought to have been advisin’.

“‘You told me I wa’n’t to have no share,’ says I. ‘A man can’t be expected to stay awake over property he’s renounced.’

“We searched the hole at daylight. Primus dug another three feet, cash in advance for the extra work. There wa’n’t no pot. There wa’n’t even a bottom such as had been there. The earth was packed and stony, jest common earth, with one old horseshoe in it and a piece of black root.

“Toddy said the root was what they’d taken for a handle. Harry said the treasure had sunk to the center of the world. Hokum said Kidd had removed it out of spite. Primus said nothing, because a man with four dollars has less need of opinions.

“They filled the hole. That took till breakfast-time. Men are allers slower puttin’ earth back than takin’ it out, particularly when there ain’t no fortune under it.

“When Hokum got home, Mother Hokum met him at the door. She looked at his clothes, his empty sacks, and his face.

“‘Did ye get it?’ says she.

“‘Toddy spoke,’ says Hokum.

“She never asked another question. She took the treasure list, turned it over, and wrote on the back an advertisement for washin’.”

“And was the iron pot truly there?” I persisted.

Sam gazed out of the barn door. The sunlight had climbed the opposite wall, and over the meadow the shadows of the elms stretched long and blue. A hen below us announced, with sudden pride, the discovery of something beneath the straw.

“Wal,” he said at last, “Hokum swore to it till the day he died. Harry swore to it when sober. Toddy swore both ways, accordin’ to whether he felt blamed. Primus said he dug up something heavy, but whether it was Kidd’s gold, an old kittle, or the natural hardness of the earth, he wouldn’t undertake to decide for no man.

“As for me, I seen enough to know that treasure-seekin’ is vanity. If they’d got that money, it would have brought quarrels, lawsuits, extravagance, and maybe the murdered sailor along with it. Hokum might have took to high livin’; Toddy surely would; Harry would have dug up the whole township. Mother Hokum alone might have made sensible use of it, but riches has strange ways even with sensible folks.

“So I’ve allers held we was mercifully delivered.”

“But,” said my brother, “you didn’t get anything at all.”

Sam rose slowly, brushing the hay from his coat.

“Wal, boys,” he replied, “I got home in time for breakfast, and I got the story. Money spends and is gone; but a good story improves with keepin’.”

He moved toward the ladder, then paused and looked back at us.

“Only,” he added, “if ye ever tell it, be particular to say I never dug. I merely went along to keep them poor critturs from doin’ anything foolish.”

Then he descended into the dimness below, where the hens received him with low, skeptical murmurs; and we remained in the fragrant hay, looking beyond the elms toward the unseen river, unable to determine whether Captain Kidd’s pot had sunk into the earth, or whether it had never risen anywhere except in the rich and inexhaustible country of Sam Lawson’s remembrance.

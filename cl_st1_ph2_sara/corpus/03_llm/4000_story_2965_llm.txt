### ALETHIA AMONG THE ELECTORS

Until the death of her aunt, Miss Alethia Debchance had lived almost entirely in the society of people who were either dead, imaginary, or under strict orders not to excite her. The late Miss Virginia Debchance had regarded the outer world much as a careful householder regards a doubtful tradesman: it might occasionally be necessary to deal with it, but there was no reason for inviting it into the drawing-room. Alethia had consequently reached the age of twenty-seven with a knowledge of human nature derived chiefly from the novels of Mrs. Webblehinton, varied by the more devotional romances of the Honourable Agnes Moystoncleugh.

In these works mankind presented no very intricate problem. Men were divisible into three classes. There was Hugo, whose pale, mocking face and inexplicable absences proclaimed a career of polished infamy; Sir Jasper, who was harsh in manner but invariably paid the mortgage in the last chapter; and Nevil, the younger son, whose frank laugh, untidy hair, and habit of rescuing retrievers from mill-streams marked him for ultimate happiness. The women, though more numerous, were even less difficult. They were innocent, designing, misunderstood, or American.

Miss Virginia had not encouraged visits from relatives. The Debchances were scattered over several counties, and had probably not been unduly depressed when Alethia, owing to her aunt’s nerves, was unable to accept their invitations. With Miss Virginia’s death, however, the nerves ceased to exercise an active veto, and Alethia found herself at liberty to investigate the family at closer quarters.

The first invitation came from Robert Bludward, a distant cousin on the maternal side, who was contesting the parliamentary division of Stranglath in the Conservative interest. He wrote in a cheerful and rather hurried hand, saying that his mother would be delighted to receive Alethia at Roper Squenderby, that the countryside was looking its best, and that an election, though noisy, was not without entertainment.

The expression “not without entertainment” seemed to Alethia almost recklessly worldly, but she accepted the invitation. She had never seen an election, and vaguely imagined it as a sustained moral conflict in which good men addressed multitudes from balconies while evil men lurked in taverns and ordered anonymous outrages. Robert’s candidature supplied a further interest. She possessed a photograph of him taken at the age of fourteen, in cricketing flannels, with an expression of defiant innocence. He had then been unmistakably a Nevil. Whether parliamentary ambition had preserved or corrupted that character remained to be discovered.

At the junction for Stranglath, Alethia changed into a local train containing two farmers, a commercial traveller, and a cage of agitated poultry. The farmers discussed the election with that absence of reserve which distinguishes men who believe the rest of the carriage to be politically negligible.

“Bludward’s an out-an’-out rotter,” said the larger of the two, with the deliberation of one announcing a scientific result. “Always was.”

“Ay,” said his companion. “A bad ’un. If he gets in, there’ll be no living in the place.”

“He won’t get in,” returned the first farmer. “We’ll put a stopper on him. Chobham’s the man.”

The commercial traveller observed that they were both alike, but this contribution, being impartial, was ignored.

Alethia sat rigidly behind a copy of *The Church Family Monitor*. The phrases she had overheard admitted of only one interpretation. Robert was a rotter—a particularly complete and established rotter—and his presence in Parliament would render life impossible throughout the division. It was true that farmers sometimes spoke with a rustic picturesqueness, but “put a stopper on him” had an unmistakable and sinister precision. Sir John Chobham, apparently the champion of law and innocence, was to be assisted by these rough but honest men against a cousin of her own blood.

For one moment family loyalty sought to raise its head; then moral fortitude pressed it gently down again. The Debchances had never permitted kinship to obscure duty. Alethia could recall no particular instance of this family austerity, but she felt certain that Miss Virginia would have approved of it in principle.

Outside the station at Roper Squenderby a motor-car waited for her. The chauffeur touched his cap and asked whether she would object to driving through the town, as Mr. Bludward was speaking in the market-place and wished to take her home with him afterwards.

Alethia assented with considerable trepidation. To pass openly into the centre of a town which had apparently resolved to put a stopper on one’s host demanded something of the uncomplaining fortitude of the early martyrs. She was sustained by the thought that early martyrs had seldom travelled in well-sprung cars.

The market-place was decorated with flags, bills, and portraits. Robert’s face, enlarged to a size which nature could never have contemplated, looked down from hoardings beneath the words BLUDWARD FOR HONESTY AND PROGRESS. On the opposite walls Sir John Chobham, an elderly man with whiskers of reassuring gravity, appealed for JUSTICE, PURITY, AND THE CONSTITUTION.

The car was delayed by a small procession, and Alethia’s attention was caught by a newspaper bill:

> BLUDWARD’S BLACK RECORD  
> THE STRANGLATH BETRAYAL  
> ELECTORS, BEWARE!

She purchased the paper from a boy whose expression suggested that he would willingly have sold either candidate’s black record at the same price. It was the *Stranglath Sentinel and County Safeguard*, and its leading article bore the title “The Man Behind the Mask.”

Alethia read:

> We have hitherto refrained, from motives of public decency, from exposing the full measure of Mr. Robert Bludward’s nefarious career. The hour for silence is past. This political adventurer, fattening upon privilege and trafficking in the miseries of the poor, now seeks to lay unclean hands upon the sacred liberties of Stranglath. Are our homes, our wives, our children, and the graves of our forefathers to be delivered over to one who has betrayed every trust, mocked every principle, and crawled through every mire of public dishonour? We ask the electors to answer at the poll.

There followed references to “the Squenderby transaction,” “the widows of Much Fallow,” and “an episode in the Argentine which cannot be printed in a family newspaper.”

Alethia folded the sheet with trembling fingers. That an episode was unprintable in a newspaper which had printed so much was evidence of almost measureless depravity. The widows of Much Fallow especially troubled her. There were probably dozens of them, impoverished, grey-haired, and still waiting for restitution. Robert, meanwhile, had written that the countryside was looking its best.

A cheer rose from the far side of the market-place, followed by groans, laughter, and the braying of a tin trumpet. Robert came through the crowd, hatless and smiling, accompanied by three men wearing blue rosettes. He sprang into the car.

“Hallo, Alethia! Awfully good of you to come. Sorry to collect you in the middle of the circus. We’ve had a capital meeting—only two dead cats, and one of them missed me.”

He laughed with an ease which, to Alethia, was more dreadful than any scowl could have been. Hugo, she remembered, had always laughed when the bloodhounds discovered the wrong body.

“You are not hurt?” she asked.

“Not a bit. Old Chobham’s people are rather feeble throwers. We must teach them cricket after the election.”

His light reference to his opponent’s approaching defeat and subsequent subjection completed the impression made by the newspaper. The boy of fourteen had disappeared. In his place sat a serpent in duckling’s plumage, genial, athletic, and stained with the tears of Much Fallow.

As the car moved off, a thick-set man thrust his head towards Robert and shouted:

“We’ll settle Chobham on Thursday, sir! Leave him to us!”

“That’s right, Jowlett,” Robert called back. “No half-measures!”

Alethia closed her eyes. Thus, in full daylight and before witnesses, arrangements were made for the removal of Sir John Chobham. The audacity of the thing was almost more appalling than its wickedness. In Mrs. Webblehinton’s novels the conspirators had at least waited for a thunderstorm and a ruined chapel.

A group of boys ran beside the car chanting:

“Chobham, Chobham, old and grey,
Bludward’ll blow your beard away!”

Robert tossed them a handful of coppers.

“Promising young blackguards,” he remarked.

To subsidise youthful corruption, and to do it with the airy good humour of a schoolboy feeding ducks, revealed a depth of character which Alethia had not encountered even in fiction. She wondered whether Sir John had been warned. Probably he went unsuspectingly from meeting to meeting, a venerable figure consecrated to Justice, Purity, and the Constitution, while Jowlett and the promising young blackguards closed round him.

Roper Squenderby was a comfortable red house standing among clipped yews. Mrs. Bludward received Alethia with the remote courtesy of a woman whose drawing-room had become an electoral office and whose cook had threatened notice because seven committee-men required supper at irregular hours.

“So glad you have come,” she said, without appearing to connect the remark with any inward condition. “You will find us very unsettled. Elections are tiresome, but Robert enjoys them.”

That a mother could speak so coldly of the perilous career into which her son had plunged seemed at first surprising. Then Alethia perceived the explanation. Mrs. Bludward knew. Perhaps she had long known. A mother’s silence may cover many things, especially when the family estate is involved.

At luncheon Robert discussed polling districts, motor arrangements, and the doubtful temper of the Nonconformist vote. Nothing in his conversation suggested remorse.

“What is the Squenderby transaction?” Alethia asked suddenly.

Robert looked puzzled, then laughed.

“Oh, that. We bought the old tannery and turned it into cottages. The *Sentinel* says I robbed the ratepayers because the council made the road.”

“And the widows of Much Fallow?”

“The what?”

“The widows whom you have impoverished.”

Robert considered.

“I believe they mean the poultry club. We stopped a subsidy. There were three widows on the committee, and one of them keeps a grocer’s shop. Have you been reading the *Sentinel*?”

Alethia admitted it.

“You mustn’t believe everything you see in an election paper,” he said. “They’ll have me poisoning wells by Wednesday. We call it the *Stranglath Smell*. Wait till you see what our side says about Chobham.”

The levity of this confession was staggering. He did not deny that accusations would multiply; he merely predicted their publication date. As for the widows, reducing a scene of organised misery to a poultry club was precisely the sort of adroit falsehood one expected from a practised criminal. The grocer’s shop had been introduced to make the victim ridiculous.

That afternoon Robert left for a meeting at Little Spleenham. Mrs. Bludward retired with a headache, and Alethia found herself at liberty to save Sir John Chobham.

She did not know his address, but the town possessed a public reading-room, where directories might be consulted without attracting notice. She walked there by a back lane, avoiding the market-place and every thick-set man who remotely resembled Jowlett.

The reading-room occupied the upper floor of a building shared by a corn merchant and a dentist. It contained several elderly men, all asleep behind newspapers, and a young clerk engaged in copying racing information into a notebook. On a central table lay the day’s journals.

Alethia searched first for a directory. Before she found one, her eye was arrested by the *Roper Squenderby Clarion*, whose poster proclaimed:

> CHOBHAM UNMASKED  
> A FOE TO EVERY HOME  
> SHALL STRANGLATH BE SOLD?

The article beneath was headed “The Grey Wolf of Reaction.”

> There are moments when silence becomes complicity. Sir John Chobham, the nominee of privilege and the obedient tool of corrupt interests, has presumed to pose before this constituency as a guardian of morality. We tear the mask from his face. Behind the venerable whiskers lurks the cold and calculating enemy of progress, the oppressor of labour, the betrayer of childhood, and the secret friend of every force that fattens upon human degradation. His public life is a trail of broken pledges; his private influence, where it can be traced, is darker still. Shall the homes of Stranglath be surrendered to this ravening hypocrite? The answer rests with every honest man.

Alethia read the paragraph twice. Its meaning could not be evaded. Sir John was not the intended victim of villainy; he was a villain of an older and perhaps more formidable school. Robert had betrayed widows and trafficked in mire, but Sir John was the secret friend of human degradation and had designs upon every home in Stranglath. The beard which had seemed so reassuring now assumed the aspect of a deliberate disguise.

She sank into a chair between the *Poultry Gazette* and the *Journal of Municipal Drainage*.

The situation was worse than any Mrs. Webblehinton had dared to imagine. In novels one wicked man might dominate a county, but Providence usually supplied an upright baronet, a courageous curate, or at least a young solicitor with evidence in a sealed envelope. Here both sides were governed by ravening hypocrites, while the inhabitants cheered impartially for one or the other. Even the boys had been drawn into the conflict. The farmers proposed stoppers; Jowlett promised settlements; newspapers tore masks from faces; mothers sat coldly among the ruins of moral law.

The outside world, which Miss Virginia had viewed with reserve, was evidently a place of continuous and highly organised infamy.

Alethia abandoned her intention of warning Sir John. One did not warn a grey wolf of the existence of another wolf. The proper course was withdrawal.

Returning to Roper Squenderby, she wrote a telegram to herself in the name of her housekeeper:

> WATER PIPE BURST STOP WEST BEDROOM FLOODED STOP RETURN ESSENTIAL.

The west bedroom contained no water pipe, but Alethia felt that an untruth committed in flight from two political monsters belonged to the more defensible branches of deception.

Robert expressed concern and offered to drive her to the station.

“So sorry your visit has been cut short,” he said. “Come again when the election’s over and everyone has stopped calling everyone else a murderer.”

Alethia regarded him with grave compassion. Even now he treated murder as a verbal amusement. She wondered whether he and Sir John, meeting after the declaration, would shake hands over the bodies of their adherents.

Mrs. Bludward kissed her without warmth and sent a maid to pack sandwiches. Within an hour Alethia was travelling homeward, carrying both local newspapers in her dressing-case as documentary evidence of her escape.

Her housekeeper received her with surprise, and the west bedroom maintained its customary dryness. Alethia offered no explanation beyond saying that conditions in Stranglath made a prolonged absence undesirable.

In the months that followed, she resumed her former life with an increased respect for Miss Virginia’s judgement and a deeper confidence in imaginative literature. Mrs. Webblehinton’s villains, once thought extravagant, now appeared almost lenient studies from nature; even Hugo had never corrupted an entire poultry club or menaced the homes, wives, children, and ancestral graves of a parliamentary division.

Of Robert Bludward’s subsequent career she heard little. He won the election by a majority of two hundred and seventeen, Sir John Chobham sent him a courteous telegram of congratulation, and the two men dined together at the Stranglath Constitutional Club before Parliament assembled. Alethia regarded these details, when they reached her, as proof of a sinister understanding.

She remained, however, sincerely grateful to the fearless outspokenness of the local Press.

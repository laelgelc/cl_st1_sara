The shadow of Palitlum climbed the wall of the smoke-house and trembled there, a monstrous thing with shoulders broad enough to carry a canoe. Palitlum himself sat small and bent beside the fire. Between his knees he held my bottle of Three Star, and whenever he lifted it the shadow lifted an enormous black club against the roof.

“It is a strong drink,” he said, after the third experiment.

“It was stronger before you began proving it.”

“True, O Hair-Face. Drink is like a chief: while his people be many he speaks loudly; when they be gone his voice is little.”

He inverted the bottle over his open mouth. One amber drop gathered, considered the descent, and fell. Palitlum smacked his lips and looked sorrowfully through the glass at the fire.

Outside, the tide moved among the beach stones with a soft clatter. Sea mist had swallowed the village, and the dogs, made uneasy by the hidden world, barked one to another from beneath the houses. Within, the cedar fire hissed, and the smoke crept low before finding the hole in the roof.

“You promised me the death of Ligoun,” I reminded him.

“I promised when the bottle was young. Now it is old.”

From my blanket-roll I brought out another bottle. At once Palitlum sat upright. Age fell from his face as a wet blanket slips from a pole.

“One cup now,” he bargained, “and the bottle when the tale is ended.”

“Half a cup now.”

“A full cup. The death of Ligoun was a great death.”

“Then half a cup should begin it greatly.”

He sighed at the meanness of white men, but held out his tin cup. I poured. He drank slowly, not because he wished to, but because he wished me to observe how slowly he could drink. Then he wiped his mouth, drew his blanket about him, and became solemn.

“Listen, O Hair-Face. Thou hast seen old men fall. They fall as rotten alders fall, with no wind upon them and no song in their branches. Ligoun did not so fall. He was an old pine, still a-shoulder to the frost and storm, and many axes were broken ere he went down.

“In those days I was not Palitlum the Drinker. I was Olo the Ever-Hungry, because my belly cried though my mouth was filled, and because my heart hungered for deeds. I could run from the salt water to the snow-line while a raven flew between two hills. I could paddle from dark to dark and laugh when the others slept over their blades. Also, I had never tasted thy Three Star.”

“Then you possessed at least one advantage over the present generation.”

“Nay. I possessed thirst and knew not its name.”

He glanced toward the promised bottle. Finding it safe beside me, he continued.

“Ligoun was chief above chiefs among the Thlinket. His name had gone north with the traders and south with the salmon. The Sitka men knew it; the Chilcat knew it; the people of the outer islands spoke it softly when the weather was black. In his youth his canoe was a wolf among deer. He came in mist, and knives awoke, and when the sun rose there were widows looking seaward.

“He had taken slaves and blankets beyond counting. He had stood before rifles when other men lay behind stones. Once, when the Stikine closed about him on a narrow beach, he broke the circle with seven men, and four came home. Again, he crossed the ice after the Copper River people, and the cold ate two toes from his left foot, but he brought back their chief’s copper shield.

“Thus was Ligoun.

“But age came, and with age came much looking. The young man sees the nearest face and strikes it. The old man looks beyond the face and sees what the blow will awaken. Ligoun traded with the whites. He entered their forts and watched men from many villages labour under one word. Their chiefs quarrelled, yet their rifles stood together. Their merchants cheated one another, yet when danger came their doors were barred as one door.

“‘Behold,’ Ligoun said, ‘the whites are weak singly. Their skins are soft, their noses freeze, and without flour they complain like children. Yet together they are strong. We are strong singly, but each house bears a knife for the next house. Therefore they increase, and we become fewer.’

“He spoke peace at the councils. Men laughed, but not loudly, for his scars were many. He said the old feuds were hungry pits into which the people threw their best sons. He gave blankets to settle blood debts. Twice he paid for killings in which his own house had no part. And to me, who followed him as a lean dog follows the butcher, he taught the Law.”

Palitlum leaned forward. The flames marked every line in his face.

“Blood for blood,” he said, “and rank for rank.

“Mark this, O Hair-Face, for white men hear the first and forget the second. If a slave kills a slave, a slave’s blood closes the account. If a house-man of name be slain, then a house-man of name must go into darkness. If a chief be killed, no common throat may pay. Honour weighs the dead and the living in one balance.

“Also, a man gains no height by killing downward. The eagle does not boast that he has taken a mouse. But he who strikes above himself climbs by the blow, if he live to speak of it. Therefore the young are hot for chiefs, and chiefs must walk beneath many eyes.”

“A system designed to keep everyone busy,” I observed.

Palitlum frowned. “It kept the memory busy. Among thy people a man writes a debt upon paper, and paper remembers. Among us the blood remembered.”

He held his hands toward the fire.

“Ligoun taught me this when I had seen seventeen summers. ‘Olo,’ said he, ‘the Law is an old canoe. It has carried us far, but there is blood in its seams. We must come ashore.’

“‘Then break it,’ said I.

“He struck me beside the head—not in anger, but because I was foolish.

“‘A law is not a paddle to be broken on the knee,’ said he. ‘Men sit within it. Break it suddenly and all are in the sea.’

“I understood him later. A little.”

The mist pressed against the house. Somewhere near the door a dog scratched, circled twice, and settled with a grunt. Palitlum’s gaze had passed beyond the flames into a hall crowded with the dead.

“It was in the house of Niblack,” he said. “Niblack was rich. He had three carved fronts to his house, six canoes fit for war, and more blankets than could be counted before hunger came. His mother’s line was ancient. His father’s line was not forgotten. He owned two copper plates, and each had a name.

“He sent messengers north and south. ‘Come,’ they said. ‘Niblack makes a feast. There shall be quass enough to drown anger, meat enough to fatten remembrance, and gifts enough that no man shall depart speaking small words.’

“So the chiefs came.

“They came in black canoes with red eyes painted upon the prows. They came under wolf signs, raven signs, killer-whale signs. The sea before Niblack’s village flashed with paddles. Rifles lay wrapped in sealskin. Knives slept beneath blankets. Men sang their house songs while the women watched from shore.

“Ligoun went because peace sits poorly at home. It must travel where anger lives. He took twelve men, and I was the youngest. We wore our best blankets, and our hair was dressed with eagle down. At his belt Ligoun carried the knife thou hast asked to buy from me.”

Palitlum’s hand disappeared beneath his blanket. It returned holding a knife in a worn hide sheath. He drew it halfway. Fire ran along the old steel, thin and cold.

The handle was dark horn, polished by use. Near the guard, three shallow marks had been cut, not decorative and not accidental.

“I offered ten bottles,” I said.

“And I answered ten times no.”

“Ten bottles of Three Star are a considerable argument.”

“An ocean is much water, yet a seal will not trade its skin for it.”

He pushed the knife home and tucked it away.

“At Niblack’s house,” he resumed, “the beams were black with the smoke of grandfathers. Lamps burned seal oil. Dried salmon hung in rows, and boxes of meat stood open. The floor was covered with clean sand, but ere dawn it was covered otherwise.

“Niblack sat at the far end beneath his crest. On his right sat Ligoun, for Ligoun was greatest of the guests. On his left sat Kakul of the Islands, whose canoe had once gone through a winter sea to take revenge. Beyond them were chiefs according to honour—Shakes the Loud, Keshan of the Split Ear, Taku-Tin who owned the White Copper, and others whose names would fill thy cup while I spoke them.”

“You may omit those who did nothing.”

“In a killing no man does nothing. He strikes, hides, remembers, or is remembered.”

This seemed reasonable, especially as the second bottle remained unopened.

“The quass was brought in a great wooden bowl,” he said. “It was dark and sour, and the smell of it walked ahead like a herald. Niblack drank first. Then he gave the bowl to Ligoun. Ligoun drank and returned it. So it went by rank, each chief receiving after the man above him.

“Now among the Yukon people there was a young chief called Opitsah the Knife. He was narrow in the face, quick in the hand, and proud beyond his blankets. He had killed three men, but none above him. Thus his name was sharp and his honour dull.

“He sat far down the line.

“When the bowl reached him, Opitsah did not take it.

“There was no sound.

“The man who held the bowl thought perhaps he had not seen. He offered it again. Opitsah looked past the bowl to Ligoun.

“‘I do not drink after an old woman,’ he said.

“It was an insult.

“Every hand beneath every blanket found what it sought. Men did not move, yet the house changed. It became like the forest when the wolf scent comes—the deer still feeding, the birds still perched, but all life listening.

“Niblack laughed. It was a little laugh, born frightened.

“Ligoun said naught.

“He sat with his hands upon his knees. His hair was grey, and the scars on his cheek were pale. He looked at Opitsah as one looks at a spark fallen upon wet ground.

“Then Opitsah stood.

“‘Ligoun speaks peace,’ he cried. ‘Ligoun gives blankets where men should give blood. Ligoun has sat so long beside white traders that his heart has become flour. See! I name him woman, and he smiles.’

“Ligoun did not smile.

“Opitsah stepped across the floor. No man hindered him, for each wished to see whether the old pine held sound wood. He stopped before Ligoun and struck him on the mouth with the back of his hand.

“Still Ligoun said naught.

“A drop of blood came from his lip.

“Then Opitsah drew his knife and stabbed Ligoun in the shoulder.”

Palitlum’s fingers tightened about his empty cup. His voice had grown quieter.

“The blood leaped to the knife. The women beside the wall drew breath, all together. Opitsah stepped back, waiting.

“Ligoun looked down at the blood upon his blanket. Then he looked at Opitsah.

“‘Thou art beneath me,’ he said.

“That was all.

“Opitsah’s face became dark. He had offered death and been told it had no value. Had Ligoun killed him, his name would have risen like smoke. Had Ligoun even struck him, men would have said he had stood against the great chief. But Ligoun measured him and found him too light.

“So Opitsah stabbed him again, beneath the ribs.

“I rose, but Ligoun’s hand held me back.

“‘Sit, Olo.’

“I sat.

“‘Art thou afraid, old man?’ Opitsah asked.

“‘I am ashamed for thee,’ said Ligoun.

“Then Opitsah cut him across the arm, and the blood ran to his fingers and dropped upon the sand.

“Ligoun turned his head toward me. His eyes were clear. ‘What is the Law?’

“‘Blood for blood,’ I answered.

“‘And?’

“‘Rank for rank.’

“‘Then where is my equal?’

“I understood.

“Across the house, beneath his crest, sat Niblack.”

The fire collapsed inward with a shower of sparks. Palitlum waited while I fed it cedar. It seemed to me that he enjoyed the interruption, not because he wished to rest, but because a tale, like a trail, is made longer by stopping at the steep places.

“Niblack understood also,” he continued. “He lifted both hands.

“‘Ligoun, this quarrel is not mine.’

“‘Thy roof covers it,’ said Ligoun.

“‘Opitsah is a dog. Kill him.’

“‘A chief does not pay for a dog with his own blood.’

“Then Ligoun stood.

“He stood slowly, O Hair-Face. Remember this. All others were quick that night. Knives were quick, rifles were quick, old hatred was quickest of all. But Ligoun stood as a tree rises out of morning mist.

“Blood ran beneath his blanket. One arm hung weak. He turned toward Niblack.

“Opitsah sprang behind him and drove the knife into his back.

“Ligoun swayed. I put my shoulder beneath his arm, and he laid his weight upon me.

“‘Now, Olo the Ever-Hungry,’ he said, ‘there is food before thee.’

“We took one step.

“Niblack called to his house-men, but they looked away. He was their chief, yet the Law was older than his name. Also, none desired to stand first before Ligoun.

“We took another step.

“Then Shakes the Loud remembered that Kakul of the Islands had killed his mother’s brother twenty winters before. Perhaps he had remembered it all evening. Perhaps the sight of blood opened the door. He drew a pistol and shot Kakul through the chest.

“Kakul fell against Keshan of the Split Ear. Keshan believed himself attacked and drove his knife into Kakul’s throat. Taku-Tin saw Keshan strike and remembered a slave stolen during the spring salmon run. He fired, but the bullet struck Shakes.

“Then all the old debts stood up together.

“A man of the Wolf house killed a Raven over a woman long dead. A Chilcat chief struck a Sitka man because of blankets withheld from a potlatch before I was born. Two cousins, who had eaten from the same dish, remembered that their grandfathers had disputed a canoe. Knives flashed. Rifles spoke, and the smoke of black powder rolled beneath the beams.

“Thus is anger, O Hair-Face. It sleeps like snow upon a mountain. A raven’s foot falls, and the valley is buried.

“But Ligoun walked.

“A bullet tore his blanket at the hip. Another entered his side. He bent, and I thought he would fall; but he breathed once and straightened.

“‘Dost thou hold me, Olo?’

“‘I hold.’

“‘Then hold as the shore holds the sea.’

“Men fought around us. Some struck at Ligoun because they feared him; some because they desired the honour; some because, in the smoke, every upright figure was an enemy. I used my knife. I was young, and the blood sang loudly in my ears.

“Opitsah came again. He darted in and cut Ligoun behind the knee. I slashed at him, but he was gone like a fish beneath dark water.

“We moved forward.

“Niblack had climbed upon the gift boxes. His crest rose behind him—a painted bird with a hooked beak—and he seemed small beneath it.

“‘Stop him!’ he cried. ‘I will give fifty blankets! I will give a canoe!’

“No man listened. Fifty blankets are warm, but they do not cover dishonour.

“Ligoun reached the first box. He could not lift his wounded leg, so I lifted it for him. He stepped up. Niblack fired a rifle from his hip. The ball struck Ligoun high in the chest and spun him half about.

“For a moment his weight crushed me to my knees.

“Niblack laughed then, loudly, because fear believed itself safe.

“Ligoun heard.

“He came upright once more.

“I tell thee, O Hair-Face, the dead were already pulling at him. His blood was behind us in a dark trail. His breath bubbled. One eye had closed where a pistol barrel had struck him. Yet he rose, and Niblack’s laughter died.

“Ligoun climbed the second box.

“Niblack dropped the empty rifle and drew his knife. It was a fine knife with a silver handle, bought from a Russian trader. But his hand shook, and fine iron cannot make a firm heart.

“Ligoun caught his wrist.

“They stood so, face to face: Niblack unhurt and trembling; Ligoun cut, stabbed, shot, and steady. Then Ligoun spoke.

“‘Thy house made the feast. Thy rank pays the debt.’

“He took the knife from his own belt—the knife I carry—and drew it once across Niblack’s throat.

“Niblack fell beneath his crest.”

Palitlum ceased speaking. The tide had turned; I could hear it sucking seaward through the stones. His cup tilted toward me almost without conscious intention.

I shook my head.

“The tale is not ended.”

“The great deed is ended.”

“The bottle was promised for the whole death.”

“White men bargain with little ropes.”

“Nevertheless, they hold.”

He grumbled, but settled again.

“When Niblack fell, Ligoun stepped down from the boxes. I do not know how. A tree, once cut through, may stand while the wind decides.

“He leaned against a roof post. The fighting weakened. Men had spent their first fury, and now they looked about to learn whom they had killed. Some crawled for the door. Some bound wounds with strips torn from blankets. The lamps had been overturned, and meat, sand, quass, blood, and feathers were mixed upon the floor.

“Opitsah stood near the centre.

“He was wounded in the cheek, but his eyes were bright. He pointed at Ligoun.

“‘Behold the woman!’ he shouted. ‘I have killed Ligoun!’

“Ligoun heard him. He smiled then, though before he had not.

“‘A mosquito may drink a chief’s blood,’ he said, ‘but it does not thereby become a chief.’

“Men laughed. Even wounded men laughed. That laughter slew Opitsah’s honour more deeply than a knife.

“Ligoun looked at me.

“‘Olo.’

“I went to him.

“‘The Law?’

“I knew what he asked. ‘Blood for blood.’

“‘And peace?’

“I had no answer.

“He nodded, for my silence was an answer.

“Then he began his death song.

“It was not loud. Ligoun had no loud breath left. Yet the house listened. He sang of the first canoe coming through fog; of the bear his father had killed with a stone-headed spear; of his mother, who had named him beneath winter stars. He sang the names of the men who had fallen beside him, lest they travel nameless. He sang that a chief must stand where his people can see him, and that death coming from the front is a guest for whom the mat is always spread.

“Last, he sang of the pine upon the high ridge. The south wind could not bend it. Snow burdened it, lightning burned it, and still its roots held the mountain. But when it fell, it fell in the teeth of the north wind and broke smaller trees beneath it.

“While he sang, Haskul of the Narrow Eyes lifted a rifle.

“Haskul was of rank—not equal to Ligoun, but no common man. Perhaps he desired the last fragment of honour. Perhaps he feared Ligoun might live. Perhaps the night had made him mad.

“He fired.

“Ligoun’s song stopped.

“The old pine fell.”

Palitlum’s head sank. For several breaths he was not the shabby mendicant who followed trading schooners for liquor, but Olo the Ever-Hungry, kneeling in smoke beside the greatest man of his world.

“And Opitsah?” I asked at last.

His head came up.

“Opitsah had killed Ligoun. Haskul’s bullet ended the song, but Opitsah’s knife had opened death’s door. Therefore he was mine.

“He saw me coming and ran. Across bodies he ran, over spilled meat and broken bowls, toward the doorway. I threw Ligoun’s knife.

“It entered beneath his shoulder.

“He fell outside upon the beach. I followed and turned him over. The mist was low, and the tide was carrying the black canoes gently against one another.

“‘I killed Ligoun,’ he whispered.

“‘Thou wert beneath him,’ I said. ‘But not beneath me.’

“Then I closed the debt.

“Afterward I returned for Haskul. Him also I killed, for though his rank was less than Ligoun’s, it was greater than mine, and the blood had made me hungry. Before dawn I struck Shakes the Loud, and later, on the trail beyond the village, I found Taku-Tin wounded and sent him after the others.

“Thus my name rose. Men no longer called me only Ever-Hungry. For a time they called me Olo Who Held the Pine.”

“And now?”

“Now fools call me Palitlum.”

“The Drinker.”

“It is not the worst name. Drink gives courage to the timid and sleep to the brave. It makes young men old, and if there be enough, it makes old men young. It warms a man while it steals his blanket, lends him songs and takes away the words, puts strength in his legs and then removes the ground beneath them. A powerful chief, this drink of thine.”

I looked at the knife beneath his blanket. “Twelve bottles.”

“No.”

“Twenty.”

“No.”

“With twenty bottles you could be young for a month.”

“With the knife I have been young for forty years.”

His answer shamed me, as it was intended to do. I pushed the promised bottle across the floor.

Palitlum seized it with no trace of ceremonial restraint. He bit the cork, worried it free, and spat it into the fire.

“Yet,” he said, raising the bottle, “forty years is a long thirst.”

He drank. The level sank; his eyes brightened; his bent shoulders spread beneath the blanket. Behind him the fire flared, throwing his shadow upward until it filled the wall—Olo again, broad and steadfast, supporting a dying chief through smoke and iron.

Then he tipped the bottle higher. The shadow tipped its vast bottle against the roof. Palitlum dwindled beneath it, old, thirsty, ridiculous, and unconquered; and while the last amber drops fell, the great black figure trembled like a pine in the teeth of the wind.

I do not defend Miss Mahala Brooks. Jeanie Deans would not have done what she did, and Jeanie, as everybody knows who has read about her, would walk barefoot through thorns rather than purchase mercy with a lie. Miss Mahala herself had always upheld Jeanie, putting her next after certain women of Scripture and a little before most women then living. Consequently, when Miss Mahala departed from her principles, she did it with her eyes open; and if there is guilt in that, it belongs to her.

Still, there are persons who will say that a lie told for love is not shaped like other lies. I shall not argue with them. I merely tell what happened.

Miss Mahala lived in the brown house beyond the meetinghouse, where the road bent toward Elder Perry’s parsonage and then wandered, as if its conscience had become uncertain, among huckleberry woods and neglected farms. Her own farm lay half a mile behind her house. She had inherited it from her father, together with a mortgage-free conscience, six silver spoons, a spotted cow, and the habit of considering herself accountable for the morals of the neighborhood.

The cow and the spoons had gone long ago. The conscience remained.

She was a narrow, brisk old woman, with keen gray eyes and a mouth that shut like the clasp of a purse. In winter she wore a green shawl; in summer she wore the same shawl folded over her arm, because weather, like human nature, could not be trusted. Her black hat had lost its crown in some ancient mishap and had been reconstructed with velvet, wire, and faith. Children fled from the hat when they saw it advancing down the road, but they commonly returned, because there were peppermints in Miss Mahala’s pocket.

She knew when Deacon Simms’s hens began laying in Widow Fennel’s haymow; she knew who thinned the cream before sending it to the creamery; she knew which young people sat in the back of the prayer meeting and whispered during the long prayer. This knowledge did not make her popular in every house, but it made her useful in all of them.

Among the objects of her watchfulness was St. John Perry, called Johnny by everybody except his father at moments of baptismal solemnity.

Elder Perry had named the boy after the beloved disciple. Miss Mahala considered this a rash use of Scripture.

Johnny had blue eyes, a brown, impudent forelock, and a look of being innocent for reasons of his own. He could climb any tree in the neighborhood, imitate every bird but the whippoorwill, and extract jam from a pantry without visibly disturbing the cloth tied over the jar. He was affectionate, particularly when affection was likely to be rewarded, and no one had ever seen him refuse his little sister Peace the larger half of anything he had lawfully obtained. As to things not lawfully obtained, the evidence was less complete.

When Johnny was seven, Miss Mahala found him in her herb garden.

The herb garden stood behind the kitchen, in neat beds bordered with clam shells. There were sage and thyme, tansy, balm, catnip, pennyroyal, and enough boneset to cure the whole county of fevers it had not yet contracted. Miss Mahala came round the corner with a pan of sour milk for the hens and saw two bare legs beneath the currant bushes.

“St. John Perry!”

The legs became motionless.

“Come out o’ there.”

Johnny emerged slowly. He had pennyroyal in one hand and catnip in the other, while his blouse bulged in a manner not intended by its maker.

“What ye doin’?”

“Nothin’.”

“Ye’re doin’ it with considerable vegetation.”

Johnny looked at the handfuls as though they had surprised him.

“What’s under yer blouse?”

“My stomach.”

“Besides yer stomach.”

“Shirt.”

Miss Mahala set down the pan.

“Johnny, I ain’t got time to peel truth off ye layer by layer. Empty it.”

He pulled out a bunch of sage, three onions, a green tomato, and a small frog. The frog, whose moral relation to the matter was doubtful, escaped toward the well.

“What did ye take them for?”

“Ma’s sick.”

Miss Mahala’s severity faltered. Mrs. Perry was delicate and often ill; she had a sweet, transparent face which made even common weariness look like a form of sainthood.

“What’s she want with green tomaters an’ a frog?”

“She don’t want the frog. That was mine.”

“Did she send ye for the herbs?”

“No.”

“Then ye stole ’em.”

Johnny regarded her attentively.

“I was goin’ to tell ye.”

“When?”

“When ye asked.”

“Tellin’ after ye’re caught ain’t askin’ before ye take. Don’t ye know the Commandment?”

“Yes’m.”

“What does it say?”

“Thou shalt not steal.”

“There! Then why’d ye do it?”

Johnny considered.

“Mebbe if there wa’n’t any Commandment, it wouldn’t be stealin’.”

Miss Mahala afterward said that this answer made her blood run cold. At the time, however, she caught him by the shoulder before theology carried him farther.

“Don’t ye trifle with Sinai! Them Commandments wa’n’t made to give ye notions. They was made to stop ’em. Why didn’t ye come to the door an’ ask?”

“You was makin’ junket.”

“How’d ye know?”

“Seen it through the window.”

“Then ye could see me to ask me.”

“You looked cross.”

“I’d look crosser seein’ ye steal.”

“Yes’m,” said Johnny meekly.

She gave him fresh sage, thyme, and pennyroyal for his mother, but made him return the onions and tomato. She also gave him a peppermint, which may appear to have confused the moral issue; Miss Mahala maintained that it sealed the lesson in sweetness.

Two weeks later she found him in her apple tree.

This time he had eaten one green apple, carried two in his pockets, and dropped four in trying to descend. Miss Mahala stood below, holding up her apron as if she expected either apples or a boy to fall into it.

“Johnny Perry, ye’re a bad boy.”

“I ain’t,” said Johnny from the leaves.

“Ye’re stealin’ my apples.”

“They ain’t ripe.”

“Unripe property belongs to folks same as ripe property.”

“They’d fall off anyhow.”

“So’ll ye, if ye don’t come down.”

He came down headfirst, reversed himself at the last branch, and stood before her with green juice on his chin.

“Yer father’ll hear o’ this.”

Johnny wiped his chin on his sleeve.

“He’s writin’ a sermon.”

“Then I’ll wait.”

“It’s about Adam.”

“Adam et what wa’n’t his, an’ look what come of it.”

“Pa says Eve give it to him.”

Miss Mahala found no immediate reply which would preserve both doctrine and the authority of woman. She took the apples from his pockets.

“What ye want ’em for?”

“Peace likes ’em with salt.”

“She’ll have colic.”

“She’s got it already.”

“Then she don’t need green apples to make it permanent. St. John, mark me: ye begin with pennyroyal an’ green apples, an’ ye end with moneyboxes, banks, an’ the State prison.”

Johnny gazed toward the parsonage.

“Do they have apples in prison?”

“Dredf’l little beside bread an’ water.”

“Mebbe they have salt.”

Miss Mahala marched him home.

Elder Perry was in his study, which was a back room smelling of old books, lamp smoke, and apples stored in the closet. He sat bent over a sermon with one hand buried in his hair. The subject was the higher criticism, a matter which had entered the parish by means of a newspaper from Boston and disturbed it out of all proportion to the number of persons who understood it.

“Elder,” said Miss Mahala, “I got to speak to ye about St. John.”

The minister raised a pale, abstracted face.

“Has he questioned the Mosaic authorship?”

“He’s questioned the ownership o’ my apples.”

“Ah. Boys do climb trees, Sister Brooks.”

“They don’t all climb mine an’ fill their pockets.”

“He must apologize.”

“He does more’n apologize. He reasons. That boy can build a ladder out o’ reasons an’ climb clean over the Ten Commandments. I caught him takin’ herbs before, an’ now it’s apples. I’m afeard he’s light-fingered.”

At this Elder Perry became attentive, but not in the desired direction.

“Light-minded, perhaps. The young are exposed to irreverence. Only yesterday St. John asked me whether Nebuchadnezzar, when eating grass, was properly a vegetarian.”

“I ain’t talkin’ about Nebuchadnezzar’s diet. I’m talkin’ about yer boy’s fingers.”

“Yes, yes. I will address the matter.”

“Address him with somethin’ he’ll remember.”

Elder Perry looked troubled. “Corporal punishment is a grave responsibility.”

“So’s raisin’ a thief.”

At that moment Johnny, who had been waiting in the passage, put his head around the door.

“Pa, Mis’ Brooks says I’m goin’ to State prison.”

“My son!”

“If I take a bank.”

“You must never take a bank.”

“I couldn’t. It’s too big.”

Elder Perry shut his eyes. Miss Mahala said afterward that she then knew the boy’s ruin was certain, because his father was no more fitted to cope with him than a hymnbook was fitted to catch a weasel.

Yet ruin did not arrive at once. Johnny grew.

He grew out of patched trousers into larger patched trousers, out of childish mischief into the long, loose-jointed silence of adolescence. His brown hair darkened; his eyes remained blue and wary. He worked when he chose with astonishing endurance and idled with equal thoroughness. He could make a sick rose bloom, set out cabbages in a line straighter than a deacon’s doctrine, and coax corn from the sandy patch behind the parsonage where Elder Perry had declared agriculture impossible. But he disliked school, shoes, indoor labor, and all questions beginning with “Why.”

Mrs. Perry grew weaker. Peace grew into a thin, golden-haired child who followed Johnny like his shadow. There was also a younger boy, Amos, whose chief talent was hunger. Money was scarce at the parsonage. Ministers’ salaries, like heavenly rewards, are sometimes promised more regularly than they are paid.

Miss Mahala continued to watch.

She watched Johnny bring his mother arbutus from the woods; she watched him mend Peace’s sled at midnight after pretending all day that he would not do it; she watched him slip a trapped rabbit loose when he believed nobody saw. These things confused her judgment, but did not silence it. A bad boy might love his family. Indeed, she reasoned, many famous criminals had probably been tender to somebody, although she could not at the moment recall their names.

When Johnny was fifteen, she caught him with his hand in her Bible.

It was a large Bible with brass clasps, kept on a little table in the front room. Between the family records and the Book of Job Miss Mahala kept four ten-dollar bills, two fives, and some silver in a flat paper packet. She did not regard the Bible as a bank, but as a place where burglars would be unlikely to seek money and where she herself could not forget the moral uses to which money should be put.

That June afternoon she had gone to the springhouse for a bowl of milk. Returning quietly because the cat had followed her, she saw Johnny through the open door.

He stood beside the Bible. The packet lay open under his hand.

For one moment neither moved. Outside, the summer world went indecently on: bees worried the clover, a jay scolded from the orchard, and sunlight lay broad and innocent on the floor. Miss Mahala felt that the blue sky itself mocked her. She had expected this; she had prophesied it; and now that her prophecy stood before her in a faded shirt, she would have given all the satisfaction of being right to be proved a fool.

“Johnny.”

He did not jump. That frightened her more than if he had.

“I didn’t take any,” he said.

“Not yet.”

“I was lookin’.”

“At money folks don’t open Bibles to see.”

He folded the packet carefully. His face had gone white beneath its tan.

“How’d ye know it was there?”

“Seen you put it there Sunday.”

“An’ ye come back when ye thought I was out.”

“Yes’m.”

“What was ye goin’ to do with it?”

“Nothin’.”

“Johnny!”

He lifted his eyes then, and the old evasive glitter came into them.

“You can’t send me up for not doin’ it.”

Miss Mahala’s first impulse was to call Elder Perry, her second to shake the boy, and her third to find a text to fit the crime. None seemed adequate. The thunders and lightnings of Sinai had been tried upon him when he was seven, and he had wanted to know about apples in prison.

“Come outdoors,” she said.

They sat on the flat doorstep. Miss Mahala sat erect, holding the bowl of milk on her knees. Johnny hunched beside the lilac bush, his long hands hanging between them. The cat drank from the bowl until Miss Mahala absently pushed it away.

“Ye was goin’ to take some.”

“Mebbe.”

“How much?”

“I dunno.”

“What for?”

Silence.

“Tobacco?”

“No.”

“Cards?”

“I don’t play.”

“Somethin’ wicked ye’re ashamed to name?”

Johnny dug his heel into the dust.

“Ma needs medicine.”

Miss Mahala looked at him sharply.

“Doctor say so?”

“He said she orter have beef tea an’ port wine an’ get away from here awhile. Pa ain’t been paid since March. Mr. Cobb says the parish’ll settle when collections come in.”

“Why didn’t ye ask me?”

“I knew what you’d say.”

“What?”

“That I’d begun with pennyroyal.”

Miss Mahala’s heart gave an uncomfortable movement.

“Well, hadn’t ye?”

“I didn’t take it then.”

“Ye intended to now.”

“Some.”

“For yer mother?”

“Yes.”

“An’ would ye have told her where it come from?”

“No.”

“Then ye’d steal an’ lie both.”

“I wouldn’t say.”

“Keepin’ still can be a lie.”

“You keep still plenty.”

“That ain’t the point.”

“It’s some point.”

She turned toward him. “St. John Perry, do ye know what’ll happen if ye take money?”

“You’ll holler.”

“I shan’t holler. I’ll send for the constable.”

His jaw tightened.

“I ain’t afraid.”

“He’ll put ye in the lockup.”

“I ain’t afraid.”

“They’ll take ye to court, an’ the judge’ll send ye to reform school, or mebbe prison.”

“I ain’t afraid.”

“They’ll shave yer head.”

He touched his hair but said, “Let ’em.”

“They’ll lock ye in at night, feed ye coarse food, set ye to work where ye can’t see woods nor fields—”

“I can stand it.”

This was going wrong, and Miss Mahala knew it. Fear had never found much purchase in Johnny. She had been talking to his pride, and his pride was willing to suffer magnificently for a crime not yet committed.

So she ceased to speak of him.

“Yer father,” she said slowly, “would have to stand up in his pulpit Sunday an’ see folks lookin’ at him.”

Johnny’s heel stopped moving.

“He wouldn’t preach.”

“Why not?”

“He’d preach if the meetin’house burned under him. But he’d know every soul there was thinkin’, ‘That’s the man whose son stole money out of a Bible.’ Deacon Simms’d look solemn. Mis’ Cobb’d whisper. The choir’d sing too soft. Yer father’d lose his place in the sermon, an’ mebbe he’d never find it again.”

Johnny stared across the yard.

“He could go to another church.”

“With folks sayin’ why he left this one?”

“He didn’t do it.”

“No. But fathers an’ mothers get punished for what their children do. That ain’t in the statute books, but it’s enforced stricter’n law.”

The boy said nothing.

“Yer mother,” continued Miss Mahala, “she’d ask me whether it was true. I’d have to tell her.”

His head came round.

“You wouldn’t.”

“I don’t lie.”

“You could say you didn’t know.”

“I do know.”

“You could say I only looked.”

“Would that be the truth?”

He dropped his gaze.

“She’s sick now,” Miss Mahala said, and her voice grew gentler against her will. “Mebbe this’d finish her. She’d lie there wonderin’ where she failed with ye. Mothers mostly lay their children’s sins at their own doors, though the children had carried ’em miles to put ’em there.”

Johnny’s mouth quivered once and steadied.

“Then there’s Peace.”

“Leave her out.”

“I can’t. She’s in. Everybody that loves ye is in.”

“She needn’t know.”

“She’d hear it at school. Some child’d sing out that her brother was a thief. Peace’d fight, likely.”

“She can’t fight.”

“She would for you. She’d get hurt; an’ after that she’d either believe ’em, which’d break her heart, or not believe ’em, which’d make her defend a lie. Which d’ye choose?”

“Shut up.”

Miss Mahala shut up.

That silence did what her words could not. The yard widened around them; the road went pale under the sun; somewhere in the woods a thrush poured out one bubble of pure music and waited, as though even a bird knew there are moments when a second note would be impertinent.

Johnny bent forward, elbows on his knees. At first Miss Mahala thought he was angry. Then she saw a dark spot strike the dust between his shoes.

“I wasn’t goin’ to take much,” he whispered.

“No.”

“Only five dollars. Mebbe ten.”

“Yes.”

“I was goin’ to get Ma things.”

“I know.”

“I could pay it back.”

“How?”

“I dunno.”

Presently he covered his face with both hands. His shoulders shook, silently and violently, and Miss Mahala looked away, because the naked grief of a boy is a private thing. She watched the cat wash milk from its whiskers. She watched a white butterfly stagger over the sage. She felt no triumph.

At last Johnny said, “I’m bad.”

“Some,” said Miss Mahala.

He gave a broken laugh that turned again into sobbing.

“I took the pennyroyal too.”

“I know ye did.”

“No. Afterward. More of it. An’ apples off Cobb’s tree, an’ two eggs from Mis’ Fennel, an’ a quarter Pa left on his desk.”

Miss Mahala winced at the quarter.

“What’d ye do with it?”

“Bought Peace a doll’s head. Her old one busted.”

“Good purposes don’t wash stolen money clean.”

“I know.”

“Do ye?”

“Yes.”

She waited.

“I won’t ever do it again,” he said.

“Folks have said that in court.”

“I won’t.”

“Nor lie?”

“Nor lie.”

“Not even by keepin’ still?”

He looked at her through wet lashes.

“You do keep diggin’.”

“Roots go deep.”

He held out his hand, awkwardly, as if pledging himself at a cattle bargain.

“I swear.”

“Don’t swear. Promise.”

“I promise I won’t steal or lie, so long as I live.”

Miss Mahala took his hand. It was calloused, brown, and trembling.

“What d’ye want to do with yer life, Johnny?”

He blinked at the change.

“Nothin’.”

“That ain’t a trade.”

“I’d like a farm.”

“Why?”

He shrugged, embarrassed by desire as he had not been by accusation.

“I like makin’ things grow.”

“What things?”

“Anything. Corn. Apples. Flowers. I could fix your south field if it was drained. An’ that old orchard ain’t dead like folks say. It needs cuttin’ back. There’s two Baldwins could bear yet, an’ the pear tree by the wall. Your soil’s sour, but lime’d—”

He stopped. Miss Mahala was staring at him.

“How long ye been trespassin’ over my farm?”

“I don’t take nothin’.”

“That ain’t what I asked.”

“Two years. Some.”

Then she saw it—not the thief she had feared, nor the prisoner with cropped hair, but a tall young man walking furrows where thistles now went to seed, pruning dead wood, setting seedlings, making the abandoned place fruitful. We are all fond of declaring a child’s nature fixed until we discover some work that can alter it. Miss Mahala, who had spent years expecting Johnny to fulfill her worst prophecy, now conceived another and took to it with equal energy.

“Would ye go to agricultural college?”

He laughed outright.

“With what?”

“My money.”

His face shut again. “I don’t want charity.”

“Who said charity? I need somebody taught to handle that farm. I can’t trust a fool with it.”

“You’d trust me?”

“If ye keep yer promise.”

He stared at the lilacs, the worn step, and finally her face.

“Why?”

“Because I choose to. The farm’s goin’ to waste. Ye can study two years, work summers, an’ when ye’re fit, take it over. If ye behave, it’ll be yours when I’m through with land.”

Johnny stood up so suddenly that the cat fled.

“You mean it?”

“I don’t generally waste breath lyin’.”

That sentence was remembered afterward.

He walked to the gate and back. He put both hands through his hair. Then, with a gesture left from childhood, he flung his arms around Miss Mahala and nearly upset her, the bowl, and all her moral dignity together.

“Here! None o’ that,” she said, holding fast to him.

She gave him ten dimes from the sugar bowl—not from the Bible—and told him he might spend them honestly. He went home by the orchard path. Before supper he gave Peace four, Amos three, and kept three himself. Peace bought blue ribbon; Amos bought molasses candy; Johnny bought a packet of late carrot seed.

Thus far the story is comfortable. Comfort seldom knows when to stop, and therefore Providence, or chance, or human stupidity, must commonly stop it.

That same evening Mr. Cobb’s store was robbed.

The thief pried open a back window and took twenty-seven dollars from the till, besides tobacco, two pocketknives, and a cheese. Suspicion traveled through the village before breakfast and had accused six persons by noon. No one suspected Johnny until Elder Perry saw the dimes.

The minister came into the kitchen after visiting Mr. Cobb and found Amos arranging three silver pieces on the table. Peace wore new blue ribbon. Johnny was in the garden.

“Where did you obtain that money?” asked Elder Perry.

“Johnny,” said Amos.

Peace nodded. “He give it to us.”

Elder Perry became very still. The past, which had been waiting patiently because ministers are no safer from memory than other people, opened before him. He saw Miss Mahala in his study years ago, severe beneath the crownless hat. Light-fingered, she had said. Pennyroyal, apples, banks, prison. He had failed to act. He had permitted an inherited or acquired tendency—he was uncertain which doctrine was more terrible—to grow beneath his roof while he wrote sermons about sins in Palestine.

“Where is St. John?”

Peace began to cry before he had accused anybody.

Elder Perry found Johnny among the cabbages. The boy was kneeling in the dirt, thinning seedlings with careful fingers.

“My son, I must ask you a question.”

Johnny glanced up and saw the expression on his father’s face. Perhaps guilt leaves the body slower than repentance enters the soul; at any rate, he went pale.

“Did you give money to Peace and Amos?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Where did you get it?”

Johnny hesitated. Miss Mahala had not told him to keep their conversation secret, but the whole affair seemed to belong behind the brass clasps of the Bible.

“Mis’ Brooks.”

“For what service?”

“No service.”

“Why did she give it to you?”

Johnny looked down.

“She just did.”

“Mr. Cobb’s store was robbed last night.”

“I heard.”

“You were out after supper.”

“I went to the south field.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

“And you cannot explain why Miss Brooks gave you money?”

“I can. I ain’t goin’ to.”

Elder Perry groaned. Johnny’s new promise of truth had not made him wise in presenting it.

The minister went directly to Miss Mahala.

She was stoning cherries on the back porch, wearing a checked apron and the green shawl, for a cloud had appeared in the west. When she saw him coming, hatless and white-faced, she knew that trouble had found the shortest road.

“Sister Brooks,” he began, “I fear a great calamity has overtaken my house.”

“Nonsense. Set down.”

“I cannot sit. Years ago you warned me concerning St. John.”

Miss Mahala’s hand stopped above the cherry bowl.

“You said he was light-fingered. You had caught him taking herbs and apples.”

“Did I?”

“You did. I disregarded you. I was absorbed in a controversy of no present significance—indeed, of possibly no eternal significance. Last night Cobb’s store was robbed. Today my children have money which St. John gave them, and he says it came from you but refuses to explain why.”

The porch darkened, though the cloud had not yet reached the sun.

Miss Mahala could have told the truth. She could have said that Johnny had intended theft and had not committed it; that he had confessed, repented, promised, and been trusted. But she saw Elder Perry’s face. He would hear only intended theft. He would question Johnny, pray over him, watch him, perhaps announce the matter in some agonized fashion to the parish committee. Trust, newly planted and tender-rooted, would be pulled up every morning to see whether it grew.

Moreover, the boy had told the literal truth. She had given him the dimes. His father did not believe him.

Miss Mahala thought of Jeanie Deans.

Then she lied.

“Elder Perry,” she said, “I never told ye any such thing.”

The minister blinked.

“You—what?”

“I never said St. John was light-fingered.”

“But I remember distinctly.”

“No, ye don’t.”

“You came to my study.”

“I’ve come there dozens o’ times.”

“You spoke of pennyroyal.”

“I brought pennyroyal for yer wife.”

“And apples.”

“There was apples in yer study closet. Ye kept ’em till the room smelled like cider.”

He passed a hand over his forehead.

“You said he would end in prison.”

“Elder, I wouldn’t say such a wicked thing of a child.”

This was not a simple lie now, but an edifice with doors and windows. Miss Mahala built on.

“You was writin’ a sermon that day. About Adam, or Moses, or them critics from Germany. Ye got notions mixed. Likely ye dreamed it.”

“Dreamed it?”

“Ministers dream awake more’n common folks do asleep.”

“But why did you give St. John money?”

“For helpin’ me plan about the farm.”

“What farm?”

“Mine. I’m sendin’ him to agricultural college, an’ if he makes good, he’s to have the place.”

Elder Perry sat down.

The anger and terror went out of him so quickly that he seemed to age and grow young in the same instant.

“You would entrust St. John with your farm?”

“I would.”

“Then you believe him honest?”

Miss Mahala looked straight at him.

“I’d trust him with every cent I got.”

This, at least, was true, and she leaned upon it.

The elder bowed his head. “Sister Brooks, forgive me. My own neglect has made me suspicious. I have been a poor father.”

“Ye’ve been a busy fool in spots,” said Miss Mahala, “but ye love yer children. Go home an’ tell Johnny ye’re sorry.”

He looked up, smiling faintly. “There is an ineffable sweetness in the restoration of faith.”

“There’s sweetness in cherries too, if ye don’t let ’em spoil while talkin’.”

He rose and took both her stained hands.

“God bless you, Sister Brooks.”

Miss Mahala pulled them away.

“Go ’long.”

He went home. He apologized to Johnny, though not elegantly; and Johnny, who could be generous when no one required it of him, forgave him before the apology was half finished. Mr. Cobb’s thief was caught two towns away with the pocketknives and most of the cheese. The dimes were spent or saved according to the characters of the three children. In time Johnny went to college.

All that happened afterward and belongs to the fruit of the matter. That night belonged to Miss Mahala.

She put away the cherries, fed the cat, locked the back door, and opened the Bible. The money still lay between Job and the Psalms. She knelt by her chair as she had knelt every night for sixty-seven years, except during measles and once when the chimney caught fire.

No prayer came.

She had always believed that a lie upset the balance of things. Truth was truth, whether it killed or cured; one did not put a thumb on the scale merely because a boy had blue eyes, a sick mother, and a little sister named Peace. Yet she had put her whole hand there. She had denied her own words, confused a good man’s memory, and made Providence responsible for a dream it had not sent.

Miss Mahala rose without praying.

She was bitter at heart, but she was not sorry. That was the worst of it—or perhaps the best; I do not defend her.

Outside, beyond the dark orchard, Johnny’s future farm lay under the stars. The old Baldwins lifted their unpruned branches, waiting. Miss Mahala stood at the window in her green shawl and looked toward them.

“Lord,” she said at last, not kneeling, “I can’t ask forgiveness yet. But Ye know where to find me when I can.”

Then she shut the Bible upon the money and went to bed, trusting—stubbornly, improperly, and with something very like faith—that God had time enough to understand the lie.

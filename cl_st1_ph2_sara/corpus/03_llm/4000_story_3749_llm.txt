### THE CITY OF REFUGE

When, in the autumn of 1914, the Belgian people came pouring across the Dutch frontier—not as travellers come, with money in their purses and a destination written upon their luggage, but as cattle flee before a prairie fire—Holland opened her doors.

Farmhouses received them; city houses divided narrow bedrooms; country mansions filled their galleries with mattresses; convents, schools, warehouses, and empty factories became dormitories. The small savings of humble Dutch families flowed out in guilders and stuivers; clothes were stripped from cupboards, blankets from beds, bread from tables. All doors were open.

But charity, like a river in flood, must be guided if it is not to waste itself. There were hundreds of thousands to feed, cleanse, shelter, and protect—old men stunned by the ruin of familiar streets, women who had carried babies for days, children who had learned to distinguish the thunder of cannon from the cracking of a burning roof. Poor, wretched creatures! Behind them lay their bleeding, ravaged land; before them stood winter.

Something had to be done.

So peaceful Holland, which hated the mailed fist because she knew the strength of the free hand, borrowed from military organization its order, its punctuality, its stern economy—and used these things not to destroy life, but to save it. On a wide heath, where the wind bent the coarse grass and the autumn rain travelled unhindered from a low grey sky, there arose a wooden settlement capable of housing thousands.

It seemed to spring up overnight—a mushroom city; yet there was no confusion in its growth. Roads were measured, drains dug, water laid on, kitchens built. Long barracks stood in rows as exact as lines upon a ledger. There were hospitals, schools, workshops, bathhouses, laundries, storerooms, chapels, recreation halls, and an isolation ward. The streets bore beloved Belgian names, so that a fugitive from Antwerp might cross the Avenue de Liège to reach the Rue de Louvain, and for one moment deceive his aching heart into believing that exile had geography.

They called it, half officially and half in gratitude, the City of Refuge.

You approach it over the wet heath, hearing first the clatter of pans from the great kitchen and then the voices of children. At the gate a sentry examines your permit. Beyond him stretches a scene at once strange and homely: women in shawls hurrying with baskets; men wheeling barrows; boys drilling under a schoolmaster; babies—always babies—wrapped in garments of every imaginable colour.

You visit the kitchen. There are boilers large enough to bathe an ox, though no ox is bathed in them; soup rises in fragrant steam, potatoes descend by the sack, and bread is counted by the thousand loaves. Then you go through the hospital, white, disciplined, and quiet; through the sewing-room, where rows of Belgian women bend over Dutch cloth; through the workshops, where carpenters and cobblers earn a little money and much self-respect.

For man does not live by soup alone. Give him food and he may survive; give him work and he remembers that he is a man.

At the school you hear French in one room and Flemish in another. On the wall hangs a map of Belgium, its familiar provinces outlined with painful exactness. The children point to towns they have left—Malines, Tirlemont, Dinant—with the pride of landowners and the solemnity of mourners. Their country has been trampled, but not erased. A frontier may be crossed by feet; it cannot so easily be crossed by roots.

And what roots were exposed in that strange human nursery! Here were peasants torn from black soil their fathers had cultivated; merchants from prosperous streets; labourers, musicians, nuns, clerks, widows, professors, thieves, saints, and ordinary people who were neither. Catastrophe had stripped away roofs, furniture, occupation, and social disguise. Character stood bare, like branches after the autumn wind.

Over this miscellaneous republic presided Commandant van der Velde.

He was portly, rosy, erect, and magnificently moustached. Discipline was not merely his principle; it was his climate. Buttons fastened themselves when he approached. Loiterers discovered urgent errands. Children removed unauthorized feet from authorized benches.

Yet his severity was mostly bark, and even that bark had a wholesome sound. He knew the capacity of every barrack, the daily consumption of every kitchen, and the number of invalids in each hospital. He fined carelessness, rebuked waste, and thundered against disorder; but he also found spectacles for an old lace-maker, tobacco for a paralysed soldier, and an extra stove for a dormitory of nursing mothers. If questioned about these softnesses, he attributed them to regulations not yet printed.

His chief nurse, Sister Margareta, understood him.

She was a spare, quiet woman with clear eyes and hands that never appeared hurried, though they were never still. Where the commandant attacked a difficulty as if it were an enemy fortification, Sister Margareta walked around it, opened the back door, and invited it to explain itself.

One November morning she entered his office.

“Commandant, I wish to speak to you about Mrs. Van Orley.”

He looked up from a report.

“The woman who disturbs Barrack Seventeen?”

“The woman who has lost her children.”

“The woman who says her children were stolen,” he corrected. “She calls for them at night. She questions every child in the street. Yesterday she entered the boys’ school during arithmetic.”

“She heard a child crying.”

“Children cry. It is one of their occupations.”

“She believes the boy may be hers.”

“She believes every boy may be hers.”

“And every little girl,” said Sister Margareta.

The commandant laid down his pen. “Exactly. The poor creature is not responsible. She must be watched.”

“She must be employed.”

“Employed?”

“At the hospital laundry. Regular hours, useful work, women near her—women who will be kind.”

“They call her mad.”

“People often call grief madness when it inconveniences them.”

He frowned at this, for it sounded suspiciously like wisdom and therefore threatened his authority.

“What evidence has she that the children live?”

“None.”

“That they were stolen?”

“None.”

“That they are here?”

“None.”

“She has nothing, then.”

“She has memory.”

The commandant rose and paced before the window. Outside, rain silvered the barrack roofs.

“What is her story?”

“In the flight from their village there was shelling. A crowd broke upon the road. She was struck and carried to a cart. When she recovered, her son and daughter were gone. Some say they were killed. Some say a soldier lifted them into another wagon.”

“And she insists—?”

“That Hendrik and Sooss are alive.”

“Hope is not evidence, Sister.”

“No, Commandant. But neither is despair.”

He made a noise intended to conclude the interview. Sister Margareta waited.

“Very well,” he said. “The laundry. On probation. If she alarms the patients—”

“She will not.”

“If she neglects her duties—”

“She will not.”

“If she interrupts arithmetic again—”

“I shall speak to her.”

Thus Mrs. Van Orley entered the hospital laundry.

She was a tall, hollow-cheeked woman whose dark hair had whitened above the temples during three months of war. At work she was silent and exact. She washed, folded, counted, and carried. But when children passed the windows, her hands stopped. In the evening she wandered between the barracks calling softly:

“Hendrik! Sooss! *Kinderen!*”

Some pitied her; some avoided her; a few laughed. There is cruelty even among sufferers, for misery does not always ennoble—it sometimes merely exhausts. Sister Margareta alone never told her to forget.

Soon afterward a railway official sent two unidentified children to the camp.

They had been discovered near a border station under the care of an old Belgian soldier who died before he could give a coherent account of them. The boy said his name was Hendrik. He might be seven or eight. The little girl, who answered to Sooss, was perhaps four. Their village name was uncertain; their family name they either could not pronounce or had forgotten.

The commandant received the news as a personal outrage.

“Children?” he shouted. “Unidentified children?”

“Yes, Commandant,” said the clerk.

“Sent here without documents?”

“Yes, Commandant.”

“Without relatives?”

“Yes, Commandant.”

“Without even a proper surname?”

“Yes, Commandant.”

“Babies!”

“The boy is nearly eight.”

“Babies!” repeated the commandant, striking the desk. “Do they imagine this office is a cradle?”

At that moment Hendrik and Sooss were brought in.

The boy stood straight, holding his sister by the hand. His cap was too large and one shoe was tied with string. The little girl stared at the commandant’s gold watch-chain.

“What is your father’s name?” he demanded.

Hendrik’s mouth tightened. “Papa.”

“His other name.”

“Papa Van—” The boy faltered. “Papa went to fight.”

“And your mother?”

“Mama.”

“Where is she?”

The child looked toward the door. “We lost her.”

Sooss pointed to the commandant’s waistcoat. “Tick-tick,” she said.

The commandant’s face altered. It changed, as Sister Margareta afterward observed, from haircloth into silk.

“This?” He drew out his watch. “This is a chronometer.”

“Tick-tick,” Sooss repeated.

“A chronometer,” he insisted, placing it against her ear.

She smiled.

Within five minutes she was sitting upon his knee. Within ten, Hendrik was examining the official seal. Within fifteen, the commandant had ordered milk, biscuits, two pairs of shoes, a blue ribbon, and an inquiry throughout every Belgian register in Holland.

Nevertheless, children required a woman’s care. He therefore marched with them to the sewing-room, Sooss holding one finger of his left hand and Hendrik carrying the commandant’s gloves.

The machines stopped as they entered.

“Ladies,” he announced, “the administration has been burdened—temporarily burdened—with two Belgian children. I require a respectable woman to take charge of them until their relatives are found.”

A stout seamstress laughed. “And will the administration provide kisses, Men’eer Commandant?”

“The administration provides necessities.”

“Kisses are necessary,” said another.

The commandant coloured. “This is not a debating society.”

Sooss, alarmed by the laughter, lifted both arms toward him.

“Up,” she commanded.

He lifted her at once.

The room rippled with delight.

“A fine nurse you make, Commandant!”

“Silence! Who will take them?”

At the far end of the room a bundle of folded linen slipped to the floor.

Mrs. Van Orley stood beside it.

She had entered from the hospital passage unheard. For one suspended instant she did not move. Her face became terrible in its hope. She stared first at the boy, then at the little girl in the commandant’s arms.

“Hendrik,” she whispered.

The boy turned.

She took one step. “Hendrik!”

His eyes widened. The commandant felt Sooss stiffen against him.

“Mama?” said the boy.

Then Mrs. Van Orley ran.

“My son! My little son! Sooss—Sooss, my baby! Mine! They are mine!”

Hendrik flung himself against her. She caught him, kissed his hair, his forehead, his torn collar, and reached blindly for the little girl.

Sooss looked into the transformed face. A child’s memory is a dark chamber, but love knows where the lamp is kept.

“Lieve moeder!” she cried.

The commandant surrendered her.

Mrs. Van Orley gathered both children to her breast. She laughed once—a broken, incredulous sound—and then sank to the floor beneath the weight of recovered happiness.

There was confusion: women crying, chairs overturned, the commandant shouting for air and water as though both were military supplies. Sister Margareta came swiftly from the passage, knelt, loosened the mother’s dress, and placed the children where her opening eyes could see them.

“You knew,” murmured Mrs. Van Orley.

“I hoped,” said the nurse.

“No,” replied the mother, clutching a hand of each child. “I knew.”

Outside, the rain had ceased. A pale beam of sunlight lay across the wooden street named for a ruined Belgian town.

The registers would be corrected; inquiries answered; reports written in proper form. The commandant would declare that the reunion resulted from efficient administration, and no one who had seen him carrying Sooss would contradict him. The women would tell the story through every barrack before supper. As for the mother and her children, the rest we will leave to them.

For there are moments when even sympathy must stand at the door.

The City of Refuge had restored no burned house, rebuilt no shattered church, and driven no invader from Belgian soil. It could not give back the dead. Yet within its plain wooden walls order had become mercy, exile had found shelter, and a woman whom sorrow had made strange was proved faithful rather than mad.

Nations, like persons, are judged not only by the enemies they conquer, but by the helpless they receive. In that bitter year the brutal power of war scattered families across Europe like leaves; but quiet Holland gathered what leaves she could into her protecting lap.

And among them were Hendrik and Sooss.

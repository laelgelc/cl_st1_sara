> **There was an old woman who lived in a shoe;**  
> **She had so many children she did n’t know what to do;**  
> **She gave them some broth without any bread,**  
> **And whipped them all soundly and put them to bed.**

Now you may suppose that the old woman had always lived in a shoe, and that all the children were her own, and that she had forgotten how to count them. But this is not so. She knew every child by name, even when three were calling her at once, two were tumbling downstairs, and another was asking where his stockings had gone.

Her name was Mistress Martha Merryweather, and in the beginning she lived in a small cottage with a red roof, four square windows, and a garden full of pinks and marigolds. She had four daughters, whom she brought up respectably, teaching them to bake, sew, sweep, and never put muddy shoes upon a clean counterpane.

In time the four daughters married and went away. Then Martha sat beside her fire and said:

“At last I shall have a peaceful old age.”

But peaceful old ages are sometimes as difficult to obtain as peaceful young ones.

Before long her eldest daughter died, leaving five children. Their father had gone to sea and had not returned, so Martha took in the five, set cots along the bedroom walls, and enlarged the cottage by building a long room at one end.

“Five children make a lively house,” she said, “but lively is not the same as unbearable.”

She rose early, combed five heads, mended ten stockings, and cooked a great kettle of porridge. The children helped in the garden, though their help was not always useful. They pulled up flowers with the weeds and watered the path instead of the beans; nevertheless, they meant well.

Just when Martha had taught them to put away their boots, her second daughter died, leaving four more children.

“Dear me,” said Martha, when they arrived with bundles, boxes, a wooden horse, and a yellow cat. “Five and four make nine. Nine is certainly more than five, but it is not ten.”

There was not room for them in the cottage, so she sent for a carpenter and bade him build another chamber beyond the first. This made the house longer and narrower. The roof curved oddly where the new part joined the old, but Martha did not trouble about appearances. A house, she said, was made to keep rain off children, and not to please strangers on the road.

She put four more cots inside, made four patchwork quilts, and purchased a larger pot. From morn till night she washed, cooked, stitched, swept, scolded, comforted, and counted.

Breakfast was generally bread-and-milk. Supper was milk-and-bread. On Sundays they sometimes had bread-and-broth, which was considered a fine change, though, when you consider it carefully, you will realize that bread had a great deal to do with all three meals.

Then the third daughter died and left three children.

“Five and four and three make twelve,” said Martha. “And twelve children can sit round a table if the table is long enough.”

The carpenter returned and built a tall room above the kitchen, with a little round window at the top. By this time the cottage had a long body, a high back, a narrow front, and a roof that bent like an ankle. Nobody noticed this particularly, for the country people were accustomed to Mistress Merryweather’s additions.

The twelve children were as cozy at night as peas in a pod, but by day they filled every corner. There were elbows at the table, knees beneath it, boots by the hearth, aprons on the pegs, and small garments flapping across the garden. Their thirty-four feet—for Martha’s two must be counted—wore away the green lawn, kicked the white gravel into the cabbages, and made a broad brown track to the gate.

Martha looked at the track and sighed.

“Children must walk somewhere,” she said.

Once more she supposed her troubles had reached their full measure. Then her youngest daughter died, leaving four children, one of them a baby.

When these appeared at the gate, Martha sat down upon the doorstep.

“Twelve and four make sixteen,” she said. “And if I have managed twelve, I suppose four more can hardly make me twice as tired.”

This was not good arithmetic, but it was brave reasoning.

The carpenter built a nursery projecting from the front and a pantry at the side. The nursery made a kind of toe, the pantry made a heel, the tall chamber stood up like the back of a boot, and the chimney rose where a shoe-button might have been.

A stranger passing along the road stopped and stared.

“Why,” he cried, “the house is shaped like a monstrous shoe!”

The children laughed, and the stranger told the innkeeper, and the innkeeper told the carrier, and the carrier told everybody from one end of the county to the other. Thus Martha became known as the old woman who lived in a shoe, although she was neither so very old nor actually inside a shoe.

You may wonder how she fed sixteen children. Martha often wondered too.

She kept hens, planted potatoes, bartered eggs for milk, and made each loaf last as long as honesty allowed. She cut the elder children’s coats down for the younger ones and joined pieces of cloth so cleverly that a patch sometimes had a patch of its own. Every child had duties. The eldest chopped wood, the next carried water, and the smaller ones gathered eggs, minded the baby, or polished spoons.

Still, they were children, and sixteen children can make more noise than one thunderstorm and continue much longer.

They slammed doors, broke cups, hid frogs in pockets, quarrelled over stools, and once sailed Martha’s best bonnet upon the duck pond. Yet she loved them all, and if one scraped a knee she bound it gently; if one woke from a bad dream she sat beside the cot; and if any stranger called them ragged, she became as fierce as a hen guarding chicks.

The baker-man came every morning in a blue cart with loaves stacked under a clean white cloth. He was a fat, impatient fellow who disliked waiting while sixteen children searched sixteen pockets for pennies.

One wet Tuesday Martha had no money ready, for the goose had swallowed a button, the baby had hidden her purse in the coal scuttle, and two of the boys were arguing about whose turn it was to fetch wood.

The baker-man stood at the door and stared into the crowded kitchen.

“Good gracious!” he said. “How many children have you here?”

“Sixteen,” replied Martha.

“Sixteen! You should send half to the poor-house and the other half to the madhouse.”

The children heard him. Martha heard him too, and her mouth became very straight.

“My grandchildren remain where they are wanted,” she said. “Leave the bread and come tomorrow for your money.”

But the baker-man tossed the cloth over his baskets.

“No money, no bread,” he said, and drove away.

The children were more offended by his words than by the loss of breakfast, though they were very fond of breakfast. That afternoon, while Martha was hanging linen behind the house, they held a council in the upper chamber.

“We shall frighten him,” said the eldest boy.

“We shall make him beg pardon,” said the eldest girl.

And because children often begin with one intention and end with quite another, they formed a plan far larger than was necessary.

They made helmets of saucepans and shields of barrel lids. They blackened their faces with soot, wrapped themselves in old blankets, and armed themselves with wooden swords, broom-handle spears, and bows that shot blunt sticks. The baby was appointed drummer and given a spoon and an empty biscuit tin.

Before dawn next morning, they hid behind the hedge beside the road. The baker-man came whistling in his cart, thinking of nothing worse than rain.

At a signal, the baby beat the tin.

Bang! Bang! Clatter!

Sixteen terrible warriors sprang from the hedge, shouting at the tops of their voices. One waved a hearth brush. Another thrust forward a cabbage stalk. Three fired blunt arrows, which struck the cart, the horse’s harness, and the baker-man’s hat.

The horse bolted.

The baker-man dropped the reins, seized his baskets, and tumbled into the road among his rolls. Then he leaped up and ran away, crying that the country was invaded.

The children cheered. The horse galloped on, the cart bounced after it, and every loaf intended for the shoe-house vanished down the road.

This was a consequence they had not considered.

They returned home quietly and removed their soot, but soot is hard to remove from ears, and sixteen guilty faces are harder to hide than one.

At breakfast Martha set an empty bread board upon the table.

“Where is the baker-man?” she asked.

Nobody answered.

“Where is the bread?”

Still nobody answered.

Then the baby, who had not learned the value of secrecy, began beating the biscuit tin and shouted, “Bang! Baker run!”

The whole tale came out. The children confessed the helmets, the hedge, the arrows, the bolting horse, and the scattered rolls.

Martha listened without interrupting.

“The baker-man spoke cruelly,” she said at last, “but his cruelty did not give you leave to become cruel and foolish yourselves. You frightened a man, endangered a horse, and lost our food. Therefore you shall eat what remains.”

What remained was broth.

It was thin broth too, made with onions, two carrots, a bone, and a great deal of water. Martha ladled it into sixteen bowls.

“Broth without bread?” asked one child mournfully.

“Broth without bread,” said Martha.

After supper she took down the switch kept above the mantel. In those days a grandmother believed that certain lessons entered more quickly through the seat than through the ears, and Martha was a grandmother of the old school. So each culprit received a sound whipping according to age and guilt, though the baby escaped with a sharp scolding, having served only as drummer.

Then she sent them all to bed before sunset.

The house was quiet at last. Sixteen children lay beneath sixteen quilts, reflecting that broth was better with bread, that bakers had feelings, and that jokes which frightened horses were not good jokes.

Next morning Martha found the baker-man at the inn. She paid what she owed and apologized for the ambush. The baker-man, whose hat still bore the mark of an arrow, apologized for his unkind speech.

“I suppose,” he said, “sixteen children require a madhouse full of work.”

“They do,” replied Martha, “but this is their home.”

After that he brought bread faithfully, and sometimes added a bag of yesterday’s buns. The children carried his baskets, rubbed down his horse, and never again sprang from a hedge in armor.

Years passed, as years will, even in a shoe-shaped house. The children grew taller, steadier, and more useful. Some became carpenters, farmers, sailors, seamstresses, cooks, and shopkeepers. Some married and built cottages of their own. One became a baker and always gave large loaves to families with many mouths.

One by one they went away, until the cots stood empty and the great kettle seemed too large.

Then Martha sat beside her fire in the strange long house with its toe, heel, and high-backed roof, and at last enjoyed the peaceful retirement she had expected so many years before.

But she was never lonely for long. On Sundays the road filled with carts, and grown grandchildren came bringing husbands, wives, babies, pies, flowers, and fresh bread. They mended her fence, weeded her garden, painted her windows, and told their own children how Grandmother Martha had fed sixteen mouths, patched thirty-two stockings at a time, and kept them all together when no one else could.

And if one of them happened to complain that his house was crowded, another would say:

“You should have seen the shoe.”

Then Martha would smile, and the baker would cut the bread, and everyone would remember with gratitude the old woman who had known exactly what to do.

## Salvage Point

The house of Will and Alice Hermann stands upon the boldest finger of rock between Penobscot Bay and the open Atlantic. It is a cheerful house, though the situation is grave; a hospitable house, though every northeast gale tries the latches; a secure house, in appearance at least, though the sea has written all about it a long and legible warning against security.

From the landward road you come upon it by degrees. First there is a yellow farmhouse in the hollow, with a red barn and six stiff poplars; then a dark ascent through pointed firs; then a sudden widening of the sky; and finally the Hermann house itself, white and square, with a round tower at one corner and a lantern-room above it, looking for all the world like a lighthouse which has resigned from public service and taken to private hospitality.

Beyond it the ledges run seaward, black when they are wet, purple when the evening falls, and silver-grey under the dry sun of September. Blue mountains lean along the western horizon; islands rise green and solid near at hand, or float like clouds farther off; and between them the tides travel in their ancient, restless roads.

Will named the place Salvage Point.

The name was appropriate in more ways than one. The grounds were full of rescued things. A splintered mast served as a flagstaff. The gate swung between two knees of oak from a wrecked brigantine. A ship’s bell, green with weather, called the family to luncheon. The bench before the western window had once been the taffrail of a barque; and under the porch stood a monstrous carved woman, gilt crown and all, who had formerly breasted the waves as the figurehead of a packet ship.

“She is the only crowned head Alice permits in the household,” Will told me.

This was unfair to Alice, who would have permitted any number of crowned heads if they had behaved themselves; but she laughed, and the wooden queen continued to stare at the bay with an expression of fluent wonder.

There were smaller relics everywhere—blocks, dead-eyes, copper lamps, brass plates, fragments of teak, a compass-box without a compass, and a steering-wheel which controlled nothing more dangerous than the vines upon the pergola. Yet the place was not aggressively rustic, not obtrusively nautical. Will had the artist’s instinct for arrangement, and Alice had that rarer domestic genius which persuades beauty to become comfortable. Broken things did not look broken at Salvage Point. They looked as if they had reached their proper harbour.

I could not visit the Hermanns without remembering that their marriage itself had been the first and best piece of salvage with which I was concerned.

This was many years before the house on the Maine coast was built, when I was rector of St. Matthew’s, in New York, and still young enough to suppose that a clergyman’s advice ought to be both final and original. Experience has corrected me. Advice is seldom final, and never original; but it may occasionally give honest people permission to do what they know to be right.

Alice Mackaye was then twenty-three, pretty without fragility, rich without vanity, and dutiful without weakness—though her father mistook the last quality for obedience.

Josiah Mackaye dealt in sugar. I do not mean that he stood behind a counter and wrapped it in blue paper. His business occupied warehouses, ships, counting-rooms, cables, and a considerable portion of his conversation. He knew the price of sugar in Java before breakfast, in Cuba before luncheon, and in London before dinner. He was not bad-hearted, but only pig-headed; and a man who has made three fortunes by trusting his own judgment naturally finds it hard to believe that his daughter may possess one.

The difficulty was Will Hermann.

Will had no money to speak of, no family influence worth mentioning, and no desire to enter the sugar trade. He was a painter and illustrator, paid his bills, kept his promises, and earned enough to live in modest independence. But in Mr. Mackaye’s social arithmetic, an artist was a proletarian with a necktie, while Alice was an heiress and therefore a branch of the capitalist system.

The young people had loved each other for two years when they came to me. During those two years Mr. Mackaye had tried command, argument, delay, travel, social competition, and the introduction of eligible young men. Alice endured the commands, answered the arguments, returned from the travel, and found the eligible young men agreeable but irrelevant.

At last Mr. Mackaye made his terms plain. If Will would enter the family business, give up painting as a profession, and submit to a probationary period of three years, the marriage might be considered.

“Might!” said Will, when he repeated the word to me. “At the end of three years he might consider it. Meanwhile I am to learn the refined art of sweetening the world at wholesale.”

Alice reproved him with a look, but there was laughter behind her anxiety.

They had come to my study on a rainy November afternoon. Will sat forward in his chair, his long hands clasped between his knees. Alice sat very straight beside the window. She had been crying, though she had repaired the evidence with that practical thoroughness which was characteristic of her.

“Do you love each other?” I asked.

Will looked astonished.

Alice answered, “Yes.”

“Are you free to marry?”

“Legally, yes,” she said.

“And morally?” Will demanded.

“That,” I replied, “is what you have come to ask me.”

It was an excellent beginning, because it gave me time to think.

Alice said, “Father has no one but me.”

“He has eight hundred employees,” said Will.

“No one in his family.”

“He had your mother,” I said gently.

Alice’s eyes turned toward the rain.

“My mother always yielded to him.”

“Was she happy?”

“She was good.”

“That was not my question.”

Alice was silent.

Now, I have no quarrel with obedience. It is a noble virtue in its proper place, particularly when exercised by other people. But honouring a parent does not mean surrendering to him the moral command of one’s entire life. A father may advise; he may warn; he may even grieve. He may not rightfully require his grown daughter to purchase his approval by the sacrifice of an honourable love.

I told them so.

Will rose and walked twice across the room.

“Then you think we should marry?”

“I think Alice must decide whether her father’s will is to be her conscience.”

“My inheritance will be gone,” Alice said.

“Probably.”

“My home too.”

“For a time.”

“And Father may never forgive me.”

“That is possible.”

She looked at Will. “Could we live on what you earn?”

“Not as you have lived.”

“That was not what I asked.”

“Yes,” he said. “We could live. I can work.”

Then, because young lovers have a tendency to make noble sacrifices in bundles, Will declared that he would release her, withdraw, disappear, and carry a blasted life into some picturesque exile.

“Nonsense,” I said. “You have not the income for a blasted life. Besides, you are proposing to jettison the very thing you came here to save.”

Alice turned. “Jettison?”

“A nautical term. To throw cargo overboard in order to save the ship.”

The technical definition steadied her. There are moments when an exact word is better than a handkerchief.

“What is the cargo?” she asked.

“Your money, perhaps. Your father’s approval, perhaps. The ship is your life together.”

Mr. Mackaye received the news of their engagement as if the price of sugar had fallen through the cellar. He threatened, denounced, reasoned, and finally announced that Alice should never receive a dollar from him.

She answered, “I hope you will change your mind, Father. But I shall not change mine.”

They were married quietly at St. Matthew’s. Mr. Mackaye did not attend. I performed the ceremony; the verger gave Alice away; Will forgot the ring until it was produced from the pocket of his best man; and the bride and groom departed in a hired cab whose off hind wheel made a noise like a revolving pistol.

They had hardships. Let us not perfume poverty. Their first flat was cold, their furniture second-hand, and Will’s commissions arrived irregularly. Alice learned to cook by experiment and to mend by necessity. Once they spent the last of their available money on medicine for the baby and lived for three days upon oatmeal, apples, and the conviction that they had done right.

But they were happy.

Will’s work improved because it had something better than ambition behind it. Alice made friends, managed accounts, and transformed each temporary lodging into a home. Two children came, then a third. There were illnesses and recoveries, failures and small triumphs, muddy shoes, unpaid invoices, birthday cakes, and all the ordinary weather of a marriage honestly undertaken.

Mr. Mackaye remained unrelenting in public. In private, I suspect, he weakened by inches. He inquired about the children through intermediaries, subscribed anonymously to a magazine which employed Will, and once bought one of Will’s paintings through a dealer at twice its proper price. Pride is a curious fortress: the defenders often smuggle provisions to the enemy.

When he died, he left Alice less than she would have inherited had she obeyed him, but far more than he had threatened to leave. There was no apology in the will. A sugar merchant does not necessarily deal in words. Yet the money was enough to give the Hermanns freedom from anxiety, and some years later they bought the point in Maine and built their house.

So Salvage Point was not merely a name. Alice had salvaged her own life from affection wrongly used as authority. Will had salvaged his art from respectability. Even old Josiah, before the end, had saved something from the wreck of his obstinacy.

All this I remembered one September evening as Will and I sat upon the taffrail bench, watching the light withdraw from the islands. Alice and the children had gone inland for a village entertainment. The wind was west, the sea long and blue, and the gulls hung above the outer ledges like scraps of torn paper.

My eye fell upon a small brass plate fixed to the porch post. It bore one word:

**JOHNNY**

“What vessel did that come from?” I asked.

“Not a vessel,” Will replied. “A motorboat. The best piece of real salvage we ever had.”

“Better than the wooden queen?”

“Much better. She cost me paint. Johnny cost me money.”

“Then naturally you value him.”

Will filled his pipe, found that he had no matches, borrowed mine, and began.

“It was seven years ago, in June. Thick weather offshore, but clearing inshore. I was painting near the south window when Alice called to me that a launch was running queerly beyond Gull Ledge.

“I took the glass. She was a twenty-five-foot motorboat, low in the stern, with a small cuddy forward. Her engine was going steadily, and she was headed northeast. There was no one at the wheel.”

“No one?”

“Not a soul. I thought at first the man aboard had stooped to work on the engine. But he did not rise. The boat kept coming.

“Now you know the water out there. First Gull Ledge, then the Pot, then a narrow gut with Black Tooth on one side and the shoal off Heron Island on the other. A careful man in daylight can take a small boat through, if the sea is easy. An empty boat ought to have smashed herself in ten minutes.

“She crossed the tail of Gull Ledge on a lift of water. Then she swung half a point, as if a hand had touched the wheel, and cleared the Pot. After that she went straight for Black Tooth.

“I said, ‘That finishes her.’

“But a wave caught her quarter and threw her through the gut. She disappeared in mist, engine still knocking away.”

Will paused to light his pipe again.

“You followed?”

“As soon as we could. Alice came, of course. There is no keeping her ashore when anything may be saved. We took our launch and picked our way along. The fog closed behind us, and for twenty minutes we saw nothing.

“Then we heard the engine.

“It had stopped knocking regularly and was racing in short bursts. We found the motorboat aground on a strip of shingle inside Heron Island, as neat as if she had been hauled up for the night. Her bow was bruised, the rudder bent, and the propeller chipped. Otherwise she was sound.

“There was no one aboard.

“We searched the cuddy. Two blankets, a coffee-pot, three lines, a box of hooks, a torn chart, some bread, tobacco, and a blue jersey. No papers. Her name had been painted on the stern, but weather and rubbing had taken most of it away. We could read only the last three letters—nny.

“We towed her home on the rising tide.”

“Was there blood?” I asked.

“None.”

“Signs of a struggle?”

“None. The fuel tank was half full. The ignition worked. The throttle had been lashed at slow speed with a bit of cord. That was the strangest thing.”

“Someone intended her to go on alone.”

“Perhaps. Or intended to free his hands for something else. We advertised in the newspapers at Rockland, Belfast, and Portland. We notified the coast guard and sent descriptions to harbours along the bay. For a fortnight no one came.

“Then, one afternoon, a boy appeared at the gate.

“He was thirteen, small but squarely built, with a tanned face and very blue eyes. His clothes were clean, though they had been mended often, and he carried a canvas bag. He looked first at me, then past me toward the boat.

“‘Is that the launch you found?’ he asked.

“‘Yes.’

“‘Her name is Johnny.’

“‘Is your name Johnny?’

“‘Yes, sir. The boat was named for me.’

“That was our introduction.

“Alice brought him inside and fed him. He had come by train, by freight wagon, and finally on foot. His father, Captain Elias Trent, was a widower and a fisherman. Three weeks before, Trent had sailed from a harbour east of us with two other men to inspect some lobster grounds and fish offshore. They had provisions for four days. None of the three returned.

“The boy had seen our notice in a newspaper wrapped around groceries.

“‘Can you prove the boat belonged to your father?’ I asked.

“He opened his bag and took out a photograph. There was the motorboat at a wharf, with the name Johnny fresh across her stern. Captain Trent stood beside her—a heavy, bearded man with one hand on the boy’s shoulder.

“‘There’s a split under the port locker,’ Johnny said, ‘and a burned place by the stove where Father dropped the lamp. The engine number is 47619.’

“Every detail was right.

“‘Where is your mother?’ Alice asked.

“‘Dead, ma’am.’

“‘And where are you living?’

“‘With my aunt for now.’

“He said ‘for now’ in a way that told us the arrangement had limits.

“I explained salvage to him. Legally, we were entitled to compensation for saving the boat and bringing her in. His face tightened, but he did not plead.

“‘How much?’ he asked.

“‘What is the boat worth?’

“‘Maybe four hundred dollars. Not now, with the rudder bent.’

“‘How much money have you?’

“He emptied his pocket upon the table. There were eighty-three cents, a knife, two nails, and a brass button.

“‘That is not all,’ he said quickly. ‘I can work. I can pay by the month.’

“Alice looked at me. You know her look. It is gentle, but it carries an order.

“I said, ‘Our salvage charge is one cent.’

“The boy stared.

“‘One cent, payable now.’

“He selected a penny from the little heap and placed it in my hand.

“‘I want a receipt,’ he said.

“That pleased me. I wrote one: ‘Received from John Trent the sum of one cent, in full payment of all claims for salvage upon the motorboat Johnny.’ I signed it, and Alice witnessed it.

“Then the boy said, ‘What about the repairs?’

“‘Those are made already.’

“They had not been, but that was a detail. We kept him with us while the village mechanic straightened the rudder, patched the bow, and attended to the propeller. Johnny worked beside him every hour. He knew engines, tides, weather, and boats; he could splice a line and handle a launch; but at dinner he blushed if spoken to suddenly, and he slept each night as if boyhood had been waiting all day to claim him.

“Did he believe his father was dead? I could not tell. He asked no foolish questions, and he made no brave speeches. Once, on the rocks, he said to Alice, ‘If Father got ashore anywhere, he’ll come back when he can.’

“Alice answered, ‘Yes.’

“That was all. Hope should not always be argued with. Sometimes it is the only plank left floating.

“When the boat was ready, I offered to send a man with him. Johnny shook his head.

“‘Father let me run her alone last summer.’

“‘Your father was with you.’

“‘Yes, sir. But he said I did all right.’

“In the end I went as far as the mouth of the bay. The weather was clear, the motor behaved beautifully, and the boy handled her better than many grown men. At the turning point he thanked me.

“‘You and Mrs. Hermann saved her,’ he said. ‘She’s all I’ve got.’

“‘No,’ I told him. ‘You have yourself.’

“He considered this soberly.

“‘Yes, sir. But a fellow needs a boat.’

“So he took the Johnny home.

“For two summers he used her for fishing and carrying parties. Then he sold her—not cheaply, either—and entered the navy. When the war came, he was boatswain’s mate on an American destroyer. He wrote Alice once from Queenstown. A good letter. Said the sea was bigger than he had expected and men were much the same size everywhere.”

“And his father?” I asked.

Will looked toward the ledges, now black beneath the first stars.

“Never found.”

“Nor the two men with him?”

“Never.”

“Was there any theory?”

“A dozen. They may have gone overboard while hauling a fouled line. One man may have fallen, and the others tried to save him. They may have taken a dory and lost the launch. There may have been a quarrel. The throttle may have been lashed while all three worked forward. But why did the boat travel alone through those ledges? Why did she reach the only safe beach for miles? Was it tide, wind, chance, or some last intention of the man who owned her?”

“You have an artist’s preference,” I said, “for mystery.”

“No. I have a sailor’s respect for it.”

The wind shifted after sunset and brought us the deep breathing of the outer sea. Behind us the lamps were lit in the house, warm squares of gold against the dark. The rescued bell, moved faintly by the wind, gave one low note.

Alice returned presently with the children, and the ordinary world came in with them—wraps, laughter, village news, and a paper bag of molasses candy. Will’s story was not mentioned again that evening.

But before I went to bed I stood for a while beneath the brass plate marked **JOHNNY**. The house rose above me, steady and bright, while beyond it the black water moved among reefs and shoals.

I like all the loose ends of a story to be tied up. It is a weakness belonging, perhaps, to the clergy, who are professionally inclined toward conclusions. Alice and Will were saved; their marriage prospered; even the Obstacle relented a little before death. The motorboat was recovered, the boy’s future secured, the penny honestly paid. These matters lie snug and shipshape in their proper places.

But Captain Elias Trent and the two men who went fishing with him were never accounted for.

Their story, I am afraid, belongs in a different compartment of the ship.

### What Would People Say?

I first knew Theodore Baines at school, where he possessed a singularly obliging nature. He would do almost anything rather than disappoint another person’s idea of him; and, as other people’s ideas were numerous and generally uncomfortable, his life was active.

At fourteen he was a small, pale boy, with a private preference for seed-cake and ginger-beer. The senior boys, however, smoked shag tobacco behind the fives court, and Theodore felt it would be discourteous to remain healthy.

I discovered him there one evening leaning against the wall with a clay pipe in his mouth. He was of a greenish colour.

“Do you like it?” I asked.

“Very much,” he replied, after an interval devoted to controlling his features. “It is soothing.”

He then went behind a water-butt and was ill.

The older boys commended his spirit and gave him beer. He disliked beer even more than tobacco, but drank it with an expression of gloomy resolution. On returning to the dormitory he was compelled to lie upon the floor, the bed appearing to him, as he explained, dangerously mobile.

“Why do you do it?” I asked.

“One must,” he answered. “All decent fellows smoke.”

I pointed out that Wilkins did not.

“Wilkins has asthma,” said Theodore. “That makes it different.”

This was the foundation of Theodore’s philosophy. Anything unpleasant became necessary the instant three other people did it.

Years later, when I met him in town, he was in love. I knew this because his collar was higher than comfort required, his boots were bright enough to signal ships, and he spoke frequently of death.

The lady was Miss Amelia Croft, a pleasant girl with substantial hair and a mother who believed that a man in love should be unable to eat. Theodore, therefore, left his dinner untouched.

“You have no appetite, Mr. Baines?” Mrs. Croft would ask, in a gratified voice.

“None whatever,” Theodore would sigh, pushing away a cutlet upon which his eyes rested with painful affection.

Amelia looked tenderly at him. The weak-eyed female cousin, who had been invited to observe the progress of the romance, whispered that it was beautiful.

I remained at the Crofts’ house one night. At two in the morning I heard cautious movements below, and, supposing burglars had entered, descended with a poker. In the pantry I found Theodore eating cold beef with one hand and blancmange with the other. A loaf, a chicken leg, and half a tart stood ready for subsequent operations.

He looked annoyed at my entrance.

“I thought you had no appetite,” I said.

“Not in front of her,” he answered. “A man who is truly in love does not eat.”

“Then what are you doing now?”

“This is different. Nobody can see me.”

He finished the tart and returned upstairs to compose a sonnet concerning the satisfaction of starving for Amelia’s sake.

They married. Theodore performed the ceremony admirably, though he afterwards admitted that his boots hurt him and that he had been thinking chiefly of luncheon. In due course Amelia prepared to present him with an heir.

On the evening of the event, Theodore was placed in the passage outside the bedroom. The nurse, a large woman with calm eyes, informed him that a husband ought to remain near at hand.

“But I can do nothing,” said Theodore.

“Of course not,” replied the nurse. “Still, it is proper.”

A maiden aunt produced a hard chair. Another relative brought him a cold chop, remarking that he would be too anxious to touch it. Theodore, who had eaten nothing since noon, looked at the chop hopefully; but the family had assembled to watch his distress, and he was obliged to turn from it.

“You must keep up your strength,” said the aunt, removing the plate.

At intervals somebody opened the bedroom door and told him not to worry. Until then he had not worried much, but repeated assurances produced their natural effect. He walked up and down the passage, upsetting a hot-water can, stepping upon a medicine spoon, and striking his head against a cupboard. Whenever he tried to sit, the nurse emerged and looked surprised that he should be capable of it.

At three in the morning a cheerful cry was heard.

“It’s a boy,” said the nurse.

“Thank Heaven,” exclaimed Theodore, with perfect sincerity. “May I have the chop now?”

The maiden aunt said that men were strange creatures.

Their married life was orderly, owing chiefly to Janet, their Scotch cook. Janet held strong views upon religion. She disapproved of Roman Catholics, Methodists, decorative waistcoats, French sauces, and staying at home on Sunday morning. Theodore and Amelia had formerly attended church when the weather was agreeable. Under Janet they attended twice every Sunday, whether the weather was agreeable or not.

“You don’t mean to say your cook makes you go?” I asked him.

“My dear fellow, certainly not. We go because we wish to.”

“And if you did not wish to?”

“Janet would give notice.”

On Sunday afternoons they conducted a class for troublesome boys; on Sunday evenings they entertained a curate whose conversation consisted chiefly of objections to dancing. Theodore loved dancing. The curate had flat feet and did not.

They also gave up cards, because Janet found a pack in the drawing-room and placed it upon the kitchen fire. Theodore said it was a misunderstanding. Janet said it was Providence.

When Theodore’s uncle died, leaving him a small estate in the country, I congratulated him.

“You will be able to live as you please now,” I said.

He looked doubtful.

A country gentleman, it appeared, could not live as he pleased. He must hunt. Theodore disliked horses at a distance and feared them at close quarters, but the county expected him at the opening meet. He purchased a scarlet coat, white breeches, and an animal called Brutus, whose previous owner assured him that a child could ride it. This may have been true if the child had been exceptionally wicked and Brutus aware of it.

I saw Theodore after his first day’s hunting. He was lying face downward upon a sofa while a servant applied lotion to portions of him.

“Good sport?” I asked.

“Excellent,” he murmured.

“Did you see the fox?”

“No.”

“The hounds?”

“For a short time.”

“What happened?”

“Brutus went through a gate. I went over it.”

“Why not give it up?”

He raised his head.

“My dear chap, I am living in the county now.”

Shooting followed. Theodore could not hit birds, but birds were occasionally hit by other people near him, and one enthusiastic neighbour put two pellets into Theodore’s hat. Another shot him gently in the leg. He bore no resentment; the man belonged to an old family.

Fishing, agricultural dinners, magisterial duties, tenants’ luncheons, and the judging of pigs occupied the remaining hours. He had imagined that landed property meant sitting beneath cedar trees with a book. He discovered that it meant rising in darkness to pursue animals he did not wish to catch, and making speeches to farmers who could not hear him.

A second inheritance brought greater trials. His fortune became large enough for public notice, and public notice decided that he should enter Parliament.

“I know nothing about politics,” he protested.

“That is no objection,” said his friends.

“Nor do I want to.”

They regarded this as modesty.

He was selected for a safe constituency, kissed several damp infants, expressed confidence in British industry, and entered the House with a majority of four hundred. For six years he voted late at night upon questions he had not heard discussed. If his party went into one lobby, Theodore went with it, though the other lobby might lead nearer home.

“What do you think of the Bill?” I asked him once.

“Which Bill?”

“The one you supported yesterday.”

He reflected.

“It is essential to the welfare of the Empire.”

“What does it do?”

“I have not had time to examine the details.”

He had, however, spent five hours dining with men who had.

Wealth next required a yacht. Theodore suffered from seasickness while looking at marine paintings, but people assured him that no man with his income could respectably remain on land. He bought the *Ariadne*, a graceful vessel containing brass, mahogany, nine sailors, and no place where he could be comfortable.

His cruises were much admired. Visitors came aboard, ate his food, praised the sea air, and departed before the vessel moved. Theodore remained, wrapped in rugs, regarding the horizon with hatred.

The yacht also furnished his one public scandal. During a Mediterranean cruise a thick-lipped undergraduate, the son of a person of importance, brought aboard a dancer from a café and introduced her as a Serbian princess. Theodore, confined below by weather, knew nothing of the matter until the young woman quarrelled with the undergraduate and threw his clothes through a porthole. A correspondent sent the facts to a newspaper, improving them sufficiently for publication.

Theodore’s name appeared beside the phrases “midnight revel,” “foreign adventuress,” and “orgy upon the high seas.” He had spent the entire evening in his cabin with a basin.

“Why not explain?” I suggested.

“One cannot discuss such matters,” he said.

The undergraduate’s father agreed, especially as silence protected the undergraduate. Theodore’s reputation suffered, while the young man was said to have displayed high spirits.

After this, fashionable people discovered Theodore. They said his face showed sorrow, refinement, and a curious modern unrest. I had always thought it showed indigestion, but fashionable people see further.

A lady who wore amber beads and never finished a sentence introduced him to the Higher Arts. Theodore ceased reading detective stories and subscribed to limited editions of books he could not understand. Their pages were uncut, which saved time. He attended private views where green cows stood beneath pink moonlight, and elongated corpses, described as studies of awakening life, leaned against purple trees.

“What do you make of it?” I asked him before one canvas.

“The colour is extraordinarily significant,” he replied.

“What does it signify?”

“The new impulse.”

The artist, overhearing him, grasped his hand. Theodore bought the picture.

Music became another duty. He sat through symphonies containing long passages in which every instrument appeared to be looking for a different tune. At the conclusion he joined those who said, “How inevitable!” though nothing in his experience had seemed less so.

There were poetic dramas where the actors whispered from behind curtains; foreign plays in which marriage was discussed with great frankness and no amusement; lectures upon the soul of furniture; dinners where nobody ate meat but everyone drank champagne; At Homes beginning at an hour when Theodore wished to go to bed; and breakfast parties ending at an hour when he wished to get up.

His days were arranged for him by women with long fingers and men whose hair fell over their collars. At eleven he inspected symbolic pottery; at one he lunched with a novelist who despised novels; at three he heard an address upon simplicity of life; at five he attended a crowded reception in honour of solitude; at eight he dined among people who spoke of sincerity; and at midnight he went to a theatre where human misery was represented without the relief of an interval.

He grew thin. Society considered this distinguished.

One spring morning I found him in his library. Outside, the sun shone; inside, Theodore was dressed for a memorial performance of a Scandinavian tragedy lasting five hours.

I proposed rebellion.

“Come with me,” I said. “We will take the train to Richmond, hire a boat, eat beef and pickles, drink beer that you need not pretend to like, and finish at a music hall. No politics, no art, no improving conversation.”

For the first time in years, Theodore’s face became that of the boy I had known before the tobacco behind the fives court.

“Could we have ginger-beer?” he asked.

“Two bottles each.”

“And seed-cake?”

“As much as you like.”

He went to the window. I could see him picturing the river, the little boat tied beneath the willows, an honest meal, and an evening in which no one would ask him what a pink moon meant. His hand moved towards the bell.

Then he noticed the cards upon the mantelpiece: the memorial performance, a duchess’s tea, a dinner for an Eastern philosopher, and an experimental opera in which the orchestra played behind the audience.

His shoulders settled.

“I should enjoy it,” he said sadly. “I should enjoy it more than anything.”

“Then come.”

He put on his gloves.

“My dear fellow,” he replied, “what would people say?”

And he drove away to spend the day doing what they had told him he enjoyed.

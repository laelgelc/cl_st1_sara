In Florence, a city distinguished in former times no less by the courtesy of its gentlemen than by the splendor of its merchants, there lived a young nobleman named Federigo degli Alberighi, whose lineage was ancient, whose manners were gracious, and whose skill in arms, horsemanship, music, and the chase caused him to be esteemed among the most accomplished men of Tuscany. Since nature had joined to these gifts a generous heart and an ardent spirit, he might have lived in prosperity and honor, had not love, which often makes the wisest imprudent and the prudent prodigal, drawn him into great and ruinous expenses.

The lady whom he loved was Monna Giovanna, considered by all who knew her to be among the fairest and most virtuous women in Florence. She was married to a wealthy citizen and guarded her reputation with such care that neither Federigo’s praises nor his magnificence could persuade her to show him any favor contrary to her duty. Yet Federigo, hoping that constancy and liberality might succeed where words had failed, arranged tournaments, gave banquets, clothed his servants richly, bestowed gifts upon poets and musicians, and maintained horses and hounds beyond what his revenues could support. Whenever an occasion arose on which he might display courage, courtesy, or expense before Monna Giovanna, he seized it eagerly, believing each new sacrifice to be an argument in his cause.

But the lady, who was neither proud nor insensible, understood his intention and regretted the waste it caused, though she would not encourage it. Thus Federigo consumed his inheritance without gaining anything except a reputation for magnificence, which, though honorable when sustained by sufficient means, becomes a dangerous ornament when purchased with a man’s substance.

It happened, therefore, that his estates were sold one after another, his creditors took possession of his house, and the friends who had filled his halls at supper found other tables when the silver dishes disappeared. At last there remained to him only a small farm in the country, whose modest income scarcely provided bread, wine, and the plain necessities of life, and one excellent falcon, the finest, as many judges declared, that had ever struck a heron above the Arno.

With these poor remnants of his fortune, Federigo withdrew from Florence. He cultivated his fields, repaired his own fences, and endured necessity without complaint. When the season permitted, he hunted with his falcon, partly for pleasure and partly because the bird sometimes supplied his table. Though poverty had altered his clothing and diminished his household, it could not teach him meanness; and if a traveler came to his door, Federigo received him with as much grace as he had formerly shown princes, offering little, but offering it freely.

Now Monna Giovanna’s husband fell ill and, perceiving that death was near, made his will. He had by his wife one young son, whom he loved tenderly, and to this child he left the greater part of his possessions, directing that, if the boy should die without heirs, the wealth should pass to Monna Giovanna. Not long afterward he died, and his widow, having mourned him with the dignity proper to a faithful wife, devoted herself to the care of her son.

As is the custom among prosperous Florentine families during the warmer months, she left the city for an estate in the country. This property happened to lie near Federigo’s little farm; and the boy, wandering sometimes with servants through the neighboring fields, became acquainted with him. Federigo, remembering whose son he was, treated him with particular courtesy, and the child soon delighted in visiting the poor gentleman, whose conversation was cheerful and whose knowledge of horses, dogs, and birds seemed inexhaustible.

Above all, the boy admired the falcon. He watched it sit hooded upon Federigo’s glove, grave as a judge; he saw it rise into the bright air, circle until it seemed no larger than a leaf, and then descend with terrible swiftness upon its prey. So greatly did the bird please him that he thought no treasure could equal it. Nevertheless, knowing that Federigo prized it beyond all his remaining possessions, he did not dare ask for it.

Before the summer had ended, the boy became ill. At first his physicians considered the sickness slight, but within a few days it grew severe, and every remedy they attempted brought little benefit. Monna Giovanna rarely left his chamber. She listened to the physicians, prayed in the chapel, and offered alms for his recovery; but, according to the common laws of other mothers, she placed more hope in any wish expressed by her child than in the gravest counsel of learned men.

One morning she sat beside him and asked whether there was anything in the world that might give him comfort.

The boy hesitated, for illness had weakened his courage without diminishing his desire. At last he said, “Madonna, if I had Messer Federigo’s falcon, I believe I should soon be well.”

The mother was troubled by these words. She knew the bird to be excellent, and she also knew that it was the sole consolation and chief resource remaining to the man who had wasted so much for her sake. To ask it seemed cruel; yet to refuse even an uncertain hope to her dying son seemed still more cruel. For a long while she answered nothing. Then, seeing the child watch her with anxious eyes, she said, “Take courage, my son. Tomorrow I shall go myself and ask Messer Federigo for his falcon.”

The promise comforted him, and Monna Giovanna spent the night considering how she might make so difficult a request without dishonoring herself or wounding Federigo. At length she resolved not to send a servant, since a favor of such weight ought to be sought in person. The next morning, accompanied by another noblewoman, she walked to the little farm.

Federigo was attending to some labor behind the house when a servant came running to tell him that Monna Giovanna stood at his gate and desired to speak with him. At first he believed that the man was mocking him; but, seeing the truth in his face, he hurried forward in his working clothes, with such astonishment and happiness that he scarcely felt the shame of appearing poorly dressed.

“My lady,” he said, bowing deeply, “no honor that could come to this house would equal the honor your presence has brought it. Had I known of your coming, I should have made such preparation as my devotion commands, though my means might still have failed to express it.”

Monna Giovanna replied, “Messer Federigo, I have come with this lady to dine with you in a simple fashion, if it does not inconvenience you. I desire to make some amends for the solitude and suffering you have endured through loving one who could not, without loss of honor, reward that love.”

Federigo answered that whatever suffering he had borne had been made noble by its cause, and that her visit repaid every loss a hundred times. He then led the ladies into his small garden, where roses grew beside herbs and vines cast a pleasant shade. Having begged them to rest there while the table was prepared, he entered the house and called the one old serving woman who remained to him.

Only then did joy give way to distress. His stores contained bread, a little wine, onions, and a few vegetables, none of which seemed worthy to set before such a lady. There was no meat in the house, and because the hour was already late, he could neither hunt nor send to a distant market. He searched each chest, though he knew them all to be empty, and found no plate, garment, or ornament that might be sold or pledged. Poverty, which he had endured calmly when it concerned himself alone, now appeared to him as the most bitter of humiliations.

While he stood uncertain, his eyes fell upon the falcon perched near the window. The bird was strong, well fed, and noble in appearance. Federigo considered that it could provide a dish worthy of his guest; and although it was dearer to him than any object left in his possession, his long habit of generosity, together with his desire to honor Monna Giovanna, overcame every other thought.

“This companion,” he said quietly, “has often served me in the field. Today it shall serve me in a greater manner.”

He took the falcon in his hands, killed it, and gave it to the servant, instructing her to pluck and roast it carefully. Then he spread upon the table his best cloth, which, though worn, was clean, and placed beside it the few pieces of earthenware that poverty had spared. When all was ready, he returned to the garden with a composed face and invited the ladies to dine.

The meal was modest in everything except courtesy. Federigo carved the bird and gave the best portions to Monna Giovanna, who ate without knowing what had been placed before her. He spoke pleasantly, avoiding every mention of his losses, and so gracefully concealed his distress that she might have believed herself received in a rich house, had not the bare walls and humble vessels testified otherwise.

When dinner was finished and the servant had removed the table, Monna Giovanna judged that the time had come to explain her purpose. She turned toward Federigo and spoke in this manner:

“Messer Federigo, if you remember my past severity, which perhaps you have called cruelty, you may wonder at my confidence in asking a favor from you. Yet I do not come for myself. If you had children, or had ever watched a beloved child suffering, you would understand how entirely a mother’s judgment may become obedient to her fear. My son is gravely ill, and he has conceived so strong a desire for your falcon that he believes possession of it will restore him. I know how precious the bird is to you, both for its excellence and because it is the last companion of your altered fortune. Therefore I cannot make this request without shame and sorrow. Nevertheless, maternal duty compels me to ask that you give it to me—not because I have any right to it, but because your nobility is greater than my deserving. In granting this favor, you may preserve the life of my son and bind me forever to gratitude.”

Federigo, hearing what she asked, realized that the falcon she sought was the very dish she had eaten. For a moment he could not answer. He turned away, and, though he had endured the loss of lands, friends, and comfort without tears, he now wept openly, not because the bird was dead, but because he could not give it to her.

Monna Giovanna supposed that grief at parting from the falcon caused his silence, and she was almost ready to withdraw her request. But Federigo mastered himself and said:

“My lady, since Fortune first made me your servant, she has opposed my desires with constant malice; yet all her former injuries were light compared with the one she has done me today. You have come to my poor house, which you would not visit when it was rich, and you ask a small gift of me; but Fortune has arranged matters so that I am unable to give it. That you may know this failure comes not from unwillingness, I must tell you what has happened.

“When I learned that you intended to dine here, I searched for food worthy of your rank and found nothing. Then, remembering the excellence of my falcon and believing it the only thing I possessed that was fit to serve you, I killed it and had it prepared for the meal. I considered it well bestowed when I saw you eat it. But now, knowing that you desired the bird alive, I grieve more deeply for my inability to serve you than I ever grieved for my poverty.”

To prove his words, he called the servant and ordered her to bring what remained. She returned with the feathers, feet, and beak of the falcon. At this sight Monna Giovanna was at first dismayed, and she reproached him gently for sacrificing so excellent a bird merely to provide one dish. Yet her displeasure soon yielded to admiration. She considered the greatness of a spirit that poverty had been unable to diminish, and she understood that Federigo, possessing almost nothing, had given all without hesitation.

She returned sorrowfully to her son and told him that the falcon could not be obtained. Whether grief at this disappointment increased his illness, or whether the appointed end of his life had already arrived, the boy grew weaker and died within a few days. His mother mourned him with that profound grief which needs no display, and for a long time she desired neither company nor change.

Because the child had left no heir, Monna Giovanna inherited the great wealth of her late husband. After a proper period, her brothers began urging her to marry again. She resisted them, being content with widowhood; but they persisted, declaring that her youth, fortune, and position required the protection and order of marriage.

At last she said, “If you insist that I take a husband, I will marry no man except Messer Federigo degli Alberighi.”

Her brothers laughed and replied, “Why choose a man who has nothing?”

She answered, “Brothers, I know that Federigo lacks money; but I would rather possess a worthy man who needs wealth than possess wealth that needs a worthy man to govern it.”

Perceiving that her resolution was fixed, and knowing Federigo’s noble birth and excellent character, they consented. Thus Monna Giovanna became his wife and placed her fortune in his hands. Federigo, taught by suffering that generosity without measure may become injustice toward oneself and others, managed their possessions thereafter with prudence, yet without ever becoming miserly. He lived with Monna Giovanna in affection and honor for the remainder of his days.

And so Fortune, after stripping him of riches to reveal his true worth, restored riches to him when he had learned how they ought to be used; from which it may be understood that nobility resides not in estates or treasure, but in the heart that remains generous when every outward advantage has been taken away.

### M’sieu Fortier’s Violin

When the last carriage had rolled away from the French Opera, carrying its little freight of silks, gardenias, white gloves, and satisfied criticism into the fog of Bourbon Street; when the gas-jets in the vestibule had blinked, fluttered, and gone out one after another; when the final echo of the tenor’s high note had ceased trembling among the gilded balconies, there remained in the deserted orchestra an old man with a violin beneath his chin.

It was M’sieu Fortier.

He played no part of that evening’s programme. He played only a phrase remembered from long ago, when his fingers had been lighter and the opera was young. The notes rose timidly at first, then warmed, unfolded, and went wandering through the empty house like children unwilling to go home. They climbed to the premières, peeped into the secondes, and lost themselves at last among the shadowy troisièmes and quatrièmes.

Then Fortier lowered his bow.

“Ah, mon violon,” he whispered, “you remember.”

His shoulders were raised, as though he were cold, though the night was mild. He placed the violin in its case with a tenderness which would have appeared ridiculous in a younger man, and perhaps sublime in no man at all except M’sieu Fortier. Then, after a courtly bow to the vacant stage, he carried it home.

Home was three small rooms on Bayou Road, occupied by M’sieu Fortier, Ma’am Jeanne, and Minesse, a white cat of such spotless dignity that she seemed always dressed for the opera. Ma’am Jeanne had a round, patient face, and the economical habit of turning yesterday’s supper into to-day’s breakfast with additions so artful that one hesitated to call them remnants. Minesse contributed nothing to the household save composure, but she contributed that abundantly.

Every evening, when Fortier returned, she met him at the door.

“Eh, Minesse,” he would say, “you t’ink dey play well to-night?”

Minesse, who had heard his opinions before, would arch her back and inspect the violin case.

“No, no; you may not scratch. Dat is not one common fiddle. Dat is—ma foi!—dat is one soul.”

The violin was exquisite. Its varnish held the dim richness of old wine, its curves had the repose of something perfected before haste entered the world, and its voice could laugh like a girl on Mardi Gras morning or sob like a widow behind closed shutters. Fortier had bought it in Paris forty years before, at a price which had frightened him for two years and delighted him ever afterward.

Mr. Courcey wanted it.

Courcey was a wealthy young gentleman whose taste was excellent and whose possessions were numerous. He knew old silver, old wine, old lace, old songs, and old men, though it may be doubted whether he understood any of them beyond the pleasure they afforded him. He had first seen Fortier’s violin after a performance of *Faust*, and from that hour it disturbed his peace.

“I will give you three hundred dollars for it,” he said.

Fortier smiled and bowed.

“Tree hundred dollar? You are ver’ kind, monsieur. But it is not for sale.”

“Four hundred.”

“It is still not for sale.”

“Name your price.”

Fortier drew the case against his breast.

“For dat violin, monsieur, dere is no price.”

Courcey laughed, not unkindly. Men accustomed to purchasing things often regard refusal as a picturesque preliminary to bargaining.

His friend Martel, who stood near, said, “Leave him his violin, Courcey. You already own enough beautiful things to make three houses uncomfortable.”

“But look at it!”

“I have looked. You see an addition to your cabinet. He sees forty years of his life.”

Courcey shrugged his shoulders. Nevertheless, whenever he met Fortier, he renewed the offer; and whenever Fortier refused, the collector desired the instrument more.

For thirty-two years M’sieu Fortier had sat among the second violins of the French Opera. He was never brilliant. In youth he had dreamed of becoming a virtuoso, of playing before kings, of hearing encore after encore until roses lay deep about his feet. But the fingers which flew in dreams moved more soberly in daylight. He had accepted his place, and in accepting it had grown faithful to it.

He knew every frayed corner of the scores, every whim of the conductors, every draught beneath the stage. He knew when a soprano would miss her entrance by the nervous way she touched her necklace; when the tenor’s cold would break upon the upper note; when the audience would forgive, and when it would not. The opera was not his employment merely. It was his weather, his calendar, his country.

Then came the Americans.

They came not with trumpets, but with papers, figures, contracts, and an admirable intention to improve everything. A syndicate assumed control. There were conversations about efficiency, freshness, enterprise, and the demands of a progressive public. The old orchestra, it was discovered, lacked vigour. Younger musicians could be engaged; some could be brought from the North; all would rehearse under a new system.

One damp afternoon Fortier received a letter thanking him for his long service.

He read it twice. Then he carried it to the window, as if daylight might alter the words.

Ma’am Jeanne looked up from her sewing.

“What is it, Fortier?”

“Dey have make one mistake,” he said.

But no mistake had been made.

For several days he polished the violin, rearranged his music, and waited for a messenger. None came. At last he put on his old black coat and went to the opera offices. He returned with his shoulders raised higher than before.

“Dey want young men,” he explained to Minesse. “Young men play loud. Dat is progress.”

Minesse blinked.

Fortier had once made cigars in summer, when the opera was closed. He now went back to the trade, sitting near a window in a shop where brown tobacco leaves lay in fragrant piles. But cigar-making, like violin-playing, asks obedience from the fingers, and his fingers had begun to tremble. The cigars he rolled were too tight or too loose. The foreman was patient for a week, impatient for another, and economical thereafter.

So Fortier worked at home when work could be had. His earnings shrank quietly, almost politely. First went the wine at dinner. Then coffee became chicory, and chicory became hot water coloured by recollection. Ma’am Jeanne discovered an astonishing number of methods for cooking beans. Minesse grew acquainted with the philosophy of small portions.

Still Fortier attended the opera.

At first he bought a good seat below, where he could watch the bowing of the new orchestra and whisper criticisms into his collar.

“Too fast,” he murmured. “Dey chase de music. Music, she is a lady; you do not chase.”

Before the season was well advanced, he moved farther back. Later he climbed to the secondes. Then came the troisièmes, where the faces on the stage were pale circles and jewels gave more information than expressions. At last he sat in the quatrièmes among boys, labourers, and old women carrying paper parcels.

There is a peculiar pain in returning as a stranger to the place where one once belonged. Yet former haunts draw us by the very wound they give. The dismissed clerk passes his old office; the sailor visits the wharf; the lover walks beneath the darkened window. Perhaps we hope to surprise the past waiting for us. Perhaps grief, like love, desires nearness.

Fortier came until he had not the price of the cheapest gallery.

Then he stood outside.

On opera nights he walked to Bourbon Street and watched the carriages arrive. Through opened doors came light, warmth, laughter, and sudden fragments of tuning: an oboe’s thin complaint, a horn’s round summons, the restless chatter of strings. When the doors closed, he remained under the faint gaslight, listening to what could not be heard.

November came with fog and a wet cold that entered through old cloth. Work was scarce. There was no money for rent, little for fuel, and on one evening nothing at all for supper.

Ma’am Jeanne said she was not hungry. Fortier said he had eaten at the cigar shop. Minesse, less courteous, cried beside the empty saucer.

The violin lay in its case beneath the bed.

All night Fortier listened to the rain and thought of Courcey.

In the morning he carried the violin to the wealthy man’s house. He walked slowly, holding the case beneath his coat to protect it from the mist. Twice he turned back. The third time he reached the door.

Courcey received him in a room crowded with beautiful objects. There were bronzes, miniatures, porcelain shepherdesses, swords which had outlived their wars, and chairs upon which no one was expected to sit. Martel lounged beside the fire.

“M’sieu Courcey,” Fortier began, “you remember dat once you wish to buy my violin?”

Courcey’s eyes brightened.

“Certainly. Have you changed your mind?”

Fortier did not answer at once. He set the case upon a table and opened it. The violin shone warmly in the luxurious room, looking, Fortier thought, already at home there.

“You offer me—how much?”

“I offered four hundred dollars.”

The old man’s face contracted. Four hundred dollars seemed an immense sum, an insult, and a temptation. He touched the worn lining of the case.

“No,” he said. “No, monsieur. I cannot take four hundred.”

Courcey misunderstood.

“Five, then.”

Fortier recoiled.

“Non! You do not comprehend. I cannot sell her for so much. If I take so much, den it is true—I have sell her. Give me fifty dollar. Only fifty.”

Martel turned from the fire.

Courcey stared. “But the instrument is worth many times that.”

“Fifty dollar,” Fortier repeated. “We have need. Ma’am Jeanne—she have no wood. Minesse—” His voice failed absurdly upon the cat’s name. “Fifty dollar is enough.”

Courcey counted the notes.

Fortier took them without looking, folded them once, and put them into his pocket. Then he bent over the violin. His fingers travelled along its sides, paused at the darkened wood beneath the strings, and closed the case.

He did not say farewell.

At the door he made his old-fashioned bow, but it was spoiled by the trembling of his knees.

When he had gone, Martel said, “Well, you have your treasure.”

“Yes.”

“You should be proud. It is not every day a man buys another man’s heart for fifty dollars.”

“You heard me offer more.”

“That makes it better, no doubt.”

Courcey frowned and lifted the violin. He drew the bow across the strings. A deep note sounded, rich and sorrowful.

“Magnificent,” he said.

Martel looked into the fire. “It did not sound sorrowful before.”

Fortier went home by a long way. In his pocket were fifty dollars. He could buy food, wood, pay a portion of the rent. But the notes seemed heavy as stones, and he could not touch them.

Ma’am Jeanne asked where the violin was.

“At M’sieu Courcey’s.”

“For repair?”

Fortier sat down.

“No. I sell her.”

Ma’am Jeanne’s hands became still.

“Why, Fortier?”

“Because we must eat.”

That evening they did not eat. The money remained in his pocket, folded once.

At bedtime his hand reached beneath the bed for the case, as it had done each night for years. It found emptiness. Minesse sat in the vacant place and washed one paw.

“Go away,” he told her sharply.

The cat stared, offended.

Fortier lay awake until dawn.

On the second day he attempted to work, but rolled one cigar around nothing and another with the leaf reversed. On the third he walked to the opera, heard the orchestra tuning, and fled before the curtain rose. On the fourth he took the fifty dollars from his pocket, spread the notes upon the table, and counted them. Then he counted them again. They remained fifty dollars, and he remained unable to spend one cent.

He began talking to Minesse.

“I have sell my child,” he said. “You understand? Suppose somebody take you away—eh? No, dat is not same. You are one cat.”

Minesse leaped into his lap.

“Pardon,” he whispered.

By the sixth day he had ceased pretending to eat. His face seemed smaller; his shoulders touched his ears. Ma’am Jeanne placed the folded money before him.

“Go,” she said.

Fortier looked at her.

“Go where?”

“You know.”

It was raining when he reached Courcey’s house. He had no overcoat, for the old one was at the pawnbroker’s, and he held both hands against his breast though there was nothing there to protect.

Courcey and Martel were again together. On seeing Fortier, Courcey rose quickly.

The old man laid the fifty dollars upon the table.

“Take it,” he said.

Courcey glanced at the notes, then at Fortier. “What do you mean?”

“I cannot. I try, monsieur. Six day I try. I do not eat; I do not sleep; I cannot go by de opera. When I close my eyes, I hear her call. I am one old fool, yes. But I have play her forty year. She know my hand. She know when I am glad, when I am ashame, when I remember France. Speech cannot say it; tears cannot say it. She say it.”

His voice sank.

“You buy one violin. Me, I sell my child.”

No one spoke.

Outside, wheels hissed upon the wet street. A carriage bell sounded and was lost in the fog.

At last Courcey crossed the room, opened a cabinet, and took out the violin. Fortier gave a little cry. He did not reach for it immediately; perhaps joy, when too sudden, also has the power to frighten. Then he gathered the instrument and case into his arms.

“Mon violon! Ah, mon pauvre violon!”

His bowed white head rested upon the wood. The violin made no sound, yet it seemed to speak more clearly than any music Courcey had heard from it.

Fortier pushed the money toward him.

Courcey shook his head.

“Keep it.”

“No, no. I return all.”

“You sold me a violin,” Courcey said gently. “I have discovered I do not want it.”

“But de money—”

“Consider it payment for the lesson.”

Fortier did not understand, but Martel did. He turned toward the window, perhaps because the room had grown too warm.

The old musician made one bow, then another, each more confused and grateful than the last. With the case held close beneath his arm, he went out into the rain.

He was still poor. The Americans still managed the opera, the young orchestra still played too loudly, and there would be beans again for supper. Nothing practical had been restored.

Yet as Fortier passed beneath the gaslights of Bourbon Street, his shoulders no longer seemed quite so high. From within the case, secure against his heart, came the faintest answering vibration, as though the old violin, awakened by his step, were already beginning to sing.

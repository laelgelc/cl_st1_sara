## The Rival of Hoyland

Dr. James Ripley had been the doctor of Hoyland for eleven years, and it was generally admitted, even by those whom he had failed to cure, that no man could have looked more fitted for the position. He was reserved, learned, unmarried, and forty-three. His shoulders had acquired that slight inclination which comes from prolonged bending over books and bedsides, while his hair, already thin upon the temples, gave him an appearance of experience which was worth quite a hundred a year to him. Country mammas trusted a man who looked too grave for flirtation, and elderly patients found consolation in a physician who appeared older than themselves.

His practice was a comfortable one. From the red-brick house at the corner of Market Street, with its three stone steps and shining brass plate, he commanded Hoyland and six miles of the country round. His consulting room, with its dark shelves, anatomical prints, and severe horsehair chairs, was as well known in the district as the church porch. At nine in the morning his chestnut mare stood before the door; at one he lunched upon whatever his housekeeper had contrived to keep warm; at two he received patients; and after dinner he read the medical journals until eleven, unless some agitated groom or breathless labourer summoned him out into the darkness.

He was a fortunate man, though he seldom thought so. Good fortune, when prolonged, becomes merely the order of nature, and Ripley regarded the whole county as a territory which Providence had assigned to him in recognition of his university honours.

Professionally, he was always at its service. Socially, he was a drop of quicksilver. He might be seen at the Hunt Ball, standing with rigid courtesy beside a potted palm; he might dine at the Hall, where he spoke of drainage to the squire and of the nervous system to the rector; but before friendliness could warm into intimacy he had slipped away. He preferred a monograph upon locomotor ataxia to the brightest conversation in Hoyland, and an evening with the microscope to the combined charms of the Misses Scully, whom their mother had presented to him in annual succession.

Thus secure in income, reputation, and solitude, he had begun to believe that change was one of those pathological conditions from which other people suffered. It was therefore with irritation, rather than alarm, that he first observed the alteration in the house opposite the post-office.

For ten years the lower windows had displayed waterproof coats and fishing tackle. Now the shopkeeper’s gilt pike and salmon disappeared; blinds of professional gravity were hung; and a square of polished brass flashed beside the door.

Ripley had driven past it twice before curiosity overcame dignity. On the third occasion he leaned from his gig and read:

**VERRINDER SMITH, M.D.  
CONSULTATIONS: 10 TO 1**

He sat back with tightened lips.

“Smith,” said he. “Verrinder Smith. Never heard of him.”

His groom, who knew neither the person nor the question, touched his hat.

“There are too many doctors,” Ripley continued. “The profession is overcrowded with men who have obtained foreign diplomas and fancy that a brass plate is equivalent to experience.”

He carried the name home with him. It disturbed his luncheon and followed him into the consulting room. After his last patient had gone, he took down the Medical Directory.

Verrinder Smith had not obtained a foreign diploma. The record was unusually distinguished: London degrees with honours, a gold medal in anatomy, a scholarship in pathology, house appointments at two large hospitals, and a recently published paper upon certain obscure degenerations of the spinal cord.

Ripley read the entry twice.

“A laboratory man,” he said at last. “No practical knowledge.”

The next morning he made inquiries. Mr. Lennox, the chemist, had heard that the newcomer possessed private means. The postmaster believed that furniture had arrived from London. Mrs. Scully’s maid reported a piano. A piano suggested a wife, and a wife suggested dinner parties, social introductions, and all those insidious methods by which an ambitious practitioner might creep into the affections of a neighbourhood.

For three days Ripley waited for the professional visit which etiquette demanded. None came.

This omission transformed irritation into resentment. It was not merely an insult to himself; it was a violation of the ancient courtesies of medicine. On the fourth afternoon he crossed Market Street and struck the new brass knocker.

A neat maid conducted him into a consulting room brighter and less solemn than his own. The books were recent, the instruments of a kind which he had seen advertised but had not thought it necessary to purchase, and upon the table stood a microscope of admirable construction. There were no pipes, no masculine boots beneath the chair, and no evidence of the supposed wife except the piano in the adjoining room.

He was examining a case of glass slides when the door opened.

A woman entered.

She was perhaps thirty, though the firm lines of her face made the estimate uncertain. She was neither pretty enough to be dismissed as frivolous nor plain enough to be forgotten. Her dark hair was drawn simply back; a pair of pince-nez hung from a narrow black ribbon; and her grey eyes, enlarged a little by the lenses, regarded him with composed attention.

“You wished to see me?”

“I called upon Dr. Smith.”

“I am Dr. Smith.”

For one instant Ripley stared at her. The whole monstrous truth stood plain before him—the honours, the appointments, the medical paper, the brass plate.

“You!” he said.

“If that is a question, yes.”

He became conscious that his hat was still upon his head and removed it. His astonishment had left him no convenient sentence. That a woman should possess some smattering of medical knowledge was already bad enough; that she should print a title upon brass and sit waiting for respectable illness to entrust itself to her was nothing short of shameless.

“I had supposed,” said he, “that Verrinder was the surname.”

“My surname is Smith.”

“And Verrinder?”

“My Christian name.”

“A singular one.”

“So my godmother believed.”

There was the faintest movement at the corner of her mouth. He disliked her for it.

“I am Dr. Ripley.”

“I know. Your plate is visible from my window.”

He waited, but she expressed neither awe nor apology.

“I understood that a practitioner newly established in a district generally calls upon his professional neighbours.”

“So I have been told.”

“Yet you did not call upon me.”

“I feared that my visit might be mistaken for a request for your approval.”

“Approval!” He flushed. “There is a question of professional courtesy.”

“Then let us be courteous now. Pray sit down.”

He remained standing.

“You have had considerable hospital experience, Miss Smith.”

“Dr. Smith, if you please.”

The correction was gently spoken, but it touched him like the point of a tenotomy knife.

“Dr. Smith, then. Hospital experience is one thing. A country practice is another. It demands endurance, tact, and a knowledge of people which cannot be learned in the dissecting room.”

“I hope to acquire it.”

“You will find the roads difficult in winter.”

“I have bought a strong cob.”

“And night work is unavoidable.”

“I sleep lightly.”

“The poorer cases are not always remunerative.”

“I am not wholly dependent upon my fees.”

Every warning fell uselessly. She was courteous while he had been rude, self-possessed while he had been angry, concise while he had allowed himself to lecture. He felt, with increasing annoyance, that she understood both his arguments and the motive beneath them.

“You must not expect assistance from me,” said he.

“I had not intended to ask it.”

“If any dangerous case should arise—”

“I shall send for the practitioner whom I consider best qualified.”

There was no emphasis upon the words, yet they struck him as an impertinence. He bowed stiffly.

“I have the honour to wish you good afternoon.”

“Good afternoon, Dr. Ripley.”

He crossed the street in a condition of cold fury. A woman! A young woman, with pince-nez and a medical degree! That the schools should have admitted her was deplorable; that the hospitals should have encouraged her was worse; but that she should plant herself in Hoyland, under his very windows, was an outrage upon science, custom, and himself.

The town received the innovation with less seriousness.

Hoyland had been satisfied with Dr. Ripley, but it was delighted with a sensation. Ladies who had deferred their maladies from motives of delicacy now discovered symptoms which required immediate investigation. The wife of the bank manager called upon Dr. Smith and returned with reports of a polished instrument case, a pleasant voice, and a method of examination which was “so very thorough.” Mrs. Scully took all three daughters, though none had been ill before breakfast. Within a week the newcomer had been invited to two dinners, a garden party, and the Thursday musical evening at the rectory.

Ripley affected contempt.

“Curiosity,” said he. “The whole thing will pass.”

But curiosity, being joined to competence, did not pass.

Old Mrs. Pengelly had suffered for years from an affection of the eye which Ripley had treated with washes and protective shades. Dr. Smith examined it, recommended an iridectomy, and performed the operation with complete success. Young Carter, the blacksmith’s son, had contracted a tendon after an accident; she divided it with one clean movement and restored the use of the foot. A railway porter, brought home after being crushed between two wagons, was said to owe his life to her prompt recognition of internal bleeding.

Each report was gall and wormwood to Ripley.

“She operates too readily,” he remarked.

“The results seem excellent,” said the rector.

“A successful result does not justify reckless treatment.”

“But what does justify it?”

Ripley changed the subject.

He had never been an operator by inclination. Country surgery offered few opportunities, and he had been content to refer difficult cases to the county hospital. Yet now he refreshed his knowledge with a zeal which would have been admirable had it not been nourished by resentment. He purchased new instruments, practised ligatures upon rubber tubing, and read until two in the morning upon abdominal surgery. His housekeeper, finding diagrams upon the breakfast table, concluded that he had become more studious than ever.

His patients, meanwhile, began to divide.

The older men remained faithful. A physician who had attended them for eleven years possessed the authority of habit, and they mistrusted innovations which required them to remove their waistcoats. But women, children, nervous invalids, and those young farmers who prided themselves upon keeping abreast of London all went to Dr. Smith. Her waiting room was sometimes full while his remained empty enough for him to hear the clock.

He told himself that fees were of no importance. It was the principle.

Then came the case of Sir Henry Wellwood’s groom.

The man had been thrown against a stone wall and lay at the lodge with a dislocated shoulder complicated by fracture. Both doctors were summoned, but Dr. Smith arrived first. When Ripley entered, heated from his drive, she had already administered chloroform and was adjusting the injured limb.

“I think you will find that impossible by traction alone,” said he.

“I agree.”

“You intend to manipulate?”

“I am doing so.”

“The fracture makes it dangerous.”

“The pressure is here, not upon the broken surface. Will you support the elbow?”

The request placed him in a cruel position. To refuse would be unprofessional; to obey was to accept the subordinate part. He supported the elbow.

“Now,” said she.

There was a soft movement, a click, and the head of the bone returned to its socket.

Sir Henry, watching from the foot of the bed, gave an exclamation of relief.

“Admirably done!”

“It was Dr. Ripley’s assistance which made the final movement possible,” said she.

Her generosity stung him more sharply than triumph would have done.

On the way home he reviewed every detail and persuaded himself that her procedure had been unsound. It had succeeded, certainly, but success was not everything. A lucky dog might win a race through the stumble of a better horse. There remained judgement, caution, the ripened instinct of years.

The district, however, saw only that the shoulder had been restored.

For the next two months their rivalry became the secret entertainment of Hoyland. If Ripley delivered a lecture to the Mechanics’ Institute upon sanitation, Dr. Smith inspected the school drains. If she procured a new inhaler, he ordered one of a later pattern. They met beside sickbeds, at cottage doors, and once in the narrow passage of the chemist’s shop, where each stepped aside so persistently that neither could pass.

Outwardly she remained untroubled. That was perhaps the worst of it. She did not speak against him; she referred cases with scrupulous fairness; she even sent him an elderly gentleman whose gout and opinions were equally beyond her patience. Ripley, who had prepared himself for intrigue, found only good manners. One can fight an enemy, but courtesy offers no exposed surface.

The decisive morning came in November.

Rain had fallen through the night, and the lanes beyond Hoyland were brown rivers between high hedges. Ripley had just finished breakfast when a stable-boy, dripping upon the hall tiles, brought word that Mrs. Gordon of Low Bracken Farm had been taken suddenly ill.

“What symptoms?”

The boy could give no coherent account. There had been violent pain, fainting, and blood. The farmer had sent him to both doctors.

“To both?”

“Yes, sir. I went first across the street, but Dr. Smith was out. Her maid said she’d send after her.”

Ripley’s lips compressed. Low Bracken lay seven miles away, and the shorter road ran over the ridge—a bad road in dry weather and a dangerous one after rain. The valley route added three miles.

“Tell Jackson to bring the gig at once.”

The chestnut mare had not finished her feed when she was put between the shafts. Ripley climbed in, wrapped the reins about his gloved hands, and drove out of Hoyland at a pace which caused two market women to leap against a wall.

It was absurd that he should hurry. He assured himself of this as the mare’s hoofs splashed through the flooded ruts. Mrs. Gordon was his patient. Her husband had acted in panic, as ignorant men did. Yet the image of a compact brown cob and a woman in a dark riding skirt persisted before him. If Smith arrived first, she might operate. She might transform another old patient into a new advertisement.

At the fork he chose the ridge road.

“Steady, Bess,” said he, and flicked the mare.

The lane climbed sharply through a cutting where the rain had loosened stones from the bank. At the top, the country opened into wet fields under a low grey sky. The gig rocked; mud sprayed from the wheels; the chestnut’s ears lay back with effort.

Then, beyond a bend, a farm cart appeared.

It occupied the centre of the lane. Ripley pulled hard to the left. One wheel mounted the bank, hung for a fraction of a second, and turned over.

There was a crash, a sense of violent rotation, and a pain so enormous that it seemed not to belong to any single part of him. When he opened his eyes he was lying in the mud. The gig rested upon its side. The chestnut, freed by the broken shaft, stood trembling a few yards away.

He tried to rise.

The attempt drew a cry from him.

His right leg lay twisted beneath him, and through the torn cloth below the knee showed a white point streaked with red. He needed no second glance.

Compound fracture.

For several minutes he remained perfectly conscious, and therefore perfectly aware of his danger. The road was deserted. The carter, whose stupidity had caused the accident, had been thrown into a ditch and appeared incapable of movement. Rain touched Ripley’s face with small cold fingers. He considered haemorrhage, shock, contamination, necrosis. The whole dismal procession of possible consequences passed before his trained mind.

Then he heard hoofs.

Not wheels—hoofs. A rider rounded the bend upon a brown cob and checked so suddenly that the animal slid upon its haunches.

Dr. Verrinder Smith sprang down.

She looked first at his face, then at the leg. Surprise passed over her features, but it did not delay her hands.

“Do not move.”

“I had discovered the wisdom of that,” he said faintly.

She knelt in the mud, cut the cloth away with a small knife, and examined the wound. Her fingers were quick and gentle.

“The tibia is through. There is little bleeding now.”

“I can see that.”

“Can you?”

“No. But I know it.”

Her eyes met his. For one instant there was almost laughter in them; then they became grave.

“We must get you under shelter. The cart has blankets. Can you endure a little movement?”

“I appear to have no choice.”

“You have several choices, but no good ones.”

She turned to the carter, who had emerged from the ditch with nothing worse than a bleeding nose. Under her directions he righted his cart and brought straw and blankets. A labourer arrived from a neighbouring field. With two poles and a horse rug she constructed a rough stretcher, secured the broken leg with splints cut from the gig rail, and superintended his removal.

Every order was clear; every movement had a reason. Ripley, white with pain, observed her as he might have observed a surgeon at a clinic. There was no fuss, no feminine agitation, no shrinking from blood or mud. She was entirely mistress of herself and, what was less agreeable, of him.

Low Bracken Farm was the nearest shelter. Thus, while racing to preserve the Gordons from his rival, he arrived upon their cart as her patient.

Mrs. Gordon’s illness proved grave but not immediately dangerous. Dr. Smith examined her, gave instructions, and returned to Ripley, who had been placed in the best bedroom. The leg required reduction, cleansing, and a more secure dressing before he could be taken to Hoyland.

“I shall give you chloroform,” she said.

“Who will administer it?”

“I will.”

“And operate as well?”

“There is no one else within reach.”

“You might send for Dr. Henshaw.”

“He lives twelve miles away. We cannot wait.”

He knew that she was right. The knowledge was bitter, but the pain was stronger than pride.

“Very well.”

She prepared her instruments upon a clean towel. The farmer’s wife, pale but obedient, brought boiling water. Ripley watched the small bright shapes arrange themselves: forceps, probe, scissors, needles. Dr. Smith removed her pince-nez, washed her hands, and came to the bedside with the inhaler.

“Breathe quietly.”

“I know how chloroform is administered.”

“I had suspected as much.”

The sweet, oppressive vapour closed over him. Her face swam above the lint, firm and watchful. He tried to count his respirations, but the numbers broke apart. The room became a green sea into which he sank slowly, passing bells that rang beneath the water. Far overhead shone a chain of golden lights. One by one they went out.

When he returned, the room was dark except for a shaded lamp. His leg felt enormously heavy, but the savage edges of the pain had been blunted. Dr. Smith sat beside the bed, her hand upon his pulse.

“Is it reduced?” he whispered.

“Yes.”

“Any splintering?”

“One loose fragment. I removed it.”

“The wound?”

“Thoroughly cleansed. I have used an antiseptic dressing.”

He closed his eyes.

“You were right not to wait,” he said.

It was the first professional concession he had made to her. She accepted it without triumph.

By evening he was carried home in Sir Henry Wellwood’s closed carriage. For the next twelve weeks the brass plate opposite the post-office might shine unchallenged. Dr. James Ripley, ruler of the sickrooms of Hoyland, lay imprisoned in his own.

At first he was an intolerable patient.

He questioned each dressing, disputed the dosage of his medicines, demanded to see his temperature chart, and once attempted to loosen the splints in order to inspect the position of the limb. Dr. Smith caught him in the act.

“If you interfere with it again,” said she, “I shall have your hands tied.”

“You would not dare.”

“I have had delirious patients restrained for less.”

“I am not delirious.”

“No. That is what makes it so discouraging.”

He glared at her. She adjusted the bandage with an unmoved face.

Yet her treatment was beyond criticism. The wound remained clean, the temperature settled, and the damaged tissues began slowly to heal. She visited each morning and often again in the evening. At first their conversations were technical and combative.

“You are keeping the dressing too dry,” he said.

“I changed it an hour ago.”

“The circulation in the foot is sluggish.”

“Less so than yesterday.”

“I think there is shortening.”

“There is not.”

“How can you be certain?”

“I measured it.”

He asked for the tape. Her measurement was correct.

As the days passed, opposition became more difficult to maintain. He had expected vanity and found none; expected dogmatism and found a mind ready to reconsider the smallest point; expected feminine softness and discovered instead that sympathy could coexist with exactness. She would listen patiently to an old woman’s catalogue of symptoms, yet cut short Ripley’s most ingenious complaint with one lift of the brows.

His enforced idleness brought other discoveries. Patients who called to inquire after him spoke gratefully of her. Mrs. Pengelly could see the church clock. The blacksmith’s boy walked without a stick. Sir Henry’s groom had returned to work. These were no longer isolated accidents which could be explained by luck. They formed evidence, and Ripley, whatever his faults, had trained himself to respect evidence.

One afternoon she found him reading her paper upon spinal degeneration.

“You have reached the least readable section,” she observed.

“The conclusions are sound.”

“That is qualified praise.”

“The microscopic drawings are excellent.”

“They were made by my colleague, Miss Durant.”

He looked over the page.

“Another lady?”

“Another doctor.”

“Are there many of you?”

“Enough to alarm you.”

“I am not alarmed.”

“No. You overturned your gig from composure.”

He stared at her, then, for the first time in their acquaintance, laughed. The laughter surprised them both. It ended in pain as he shifted his leg, but something rigid between them had broken more cleanly than the bone.

After that day she sometimes remained when the examination was done.

They spoke of their student years, of hospital teachers, of old controversies and new experiments. Ripley had lived much among books and little among minds which could meet his own. In Hoyland his learning isolated him; with her it became a bridge. She knew the continental work which he had neglected, had observed Pasteur in Paris, and possessed opinions upon nerve physiology which were bold without being careless. When they differed, as they frequently did, her contradictions exhilarated rather than angered him.

He began to notice what professional resentment had obscured: the warmth of her low voice, the humour restrained behind her gravity, the way a loose strand of dark hair escaped when she bent over the microscope. Without her pince-nez her eyes seemed softer, though not less direct. He listened each morning for the maid’s opening of the door and measured the empty afternoon by the memory of her departure.

Winter settled over Hoyland. Frost silvered the roofs, and the windows of the consulting room across the street glowed early in the dusk. From his upper room Ripley could see patients enter beneath the brass plate. The sight no longer embittered him. Indeed, when the light remained on late, he wondered whether she had eaten and whether her difficult case at Marsh End had improved.

His own patients returned gradually to him with baskets, inquiries, and confused accounts of their ailments. He advised several to continue under Dr. Smith.

The words cost him something. They also relieved him.

By January he could stand with crutches. By February he crossed the room. The fracture had united with remarkably little deformity, and he was forced to admit that he might soon resume his rounds.

“You have made an excellent recovery,” she said at the final formal examination.

“You have made it for me.”

“The patient deserves some credit.”

“Not this patient.”

She smiled and tapped the head of her stethoscope against her palm.

“You will limp when tired, but otherwise the leg should serve you well.”

“And you will no longer come every morning.”

“You no longer require a doctor every morning.”

The statement was simple, but it left the room curiously altered.

Ripley had spent three months revising his view of women in medicine. He now undertook a more difficult revision of his view of marriage. Formerly he had considered a wife incompatible with study, distracting in health and expensive in illness. But a wife who understood the work, who could discuss a diagnosis at breakfast and take a difficult confinement when he was occupied elsewhere—that was a different proposition. He imagined the two brass plates joined upon one door, the practices combined, the district served by an alliance of science and experience.

It did not occur to him that this vision might possess defects from her point of view.

A week after he resumed work, he asked her to dine. His housekeeper produced her best soup, a sole of some distinction, and a pheasant sent by Sir Henry. Dr. Smith came in a plain grey gown, and Ripley, who had never seen her without professional black, found the alteration disconcerting.

They spoke easily through dinner. Only when coffee had been brought into the consulting room did his courage become solemn.

She stood beside the fire, looking at the shelves.

“You have placed Virchow next to your old surgical dictionary,” she said. “That is progress.”

“I have made greater progress than that.”

She turned.

“Dr. Smith—Verrinder—I owe you my life, or at least my leg.”

“You owe me nothing. I did my work.”

“You have taught me how narrow and unjust I was.”

“That debt may be acknowledged, if you insist.”

“I ask you to believe that I am ashamed of my conduct when you first came.”

“I had already observed an improvement.”

Her tone was light, but he could not follow it.

“I had thought professional life incompatible with the qualities which I most valued in a woman. I know now that intellect does not exclude kindness, nor courage gentleness. I know also that my life has become—”

He stopped. Emotional expression had never formed part of his education.

She watched him very quietly.

“I should be honoured,” he continued, “if you would become my wife.”

The clock upon the mantelpiece gave a small, dry tick.

“My wife,” he repeated, “and my partner.”

For a moment her face softened in a way which sent hope through him. Then she shook her head.

“No, Dr. Ripley.”

“You do not care for me?”

“I care for you greatly.”

“Then why—?”

“Because I do not wish to marry.”

He had prepared himself for modest hesitation, for objections of income or age, even for a declaration that his former hostility could not be forgotten. This answer had not entered his calculations.

“You mean not yet?”

“I mean not at all.”

“But your practice—our work—we could unite them.”

A gleam of the old humour returned to her eyes.

“What, and unite the practices?”

“Why not?”

“Because I have no desire to exchange my work for a share in yours.”

“You would not exchange it. You would continue.”

“As your wife, in your house, among your patients, under the generous protection of the man who once believed my education shameless.”

He winced.

“That is not fair.”

“No,” she said gently. “Perhaps it is not. You have changed, and I honour you for changing. But I too have a direction for my life.”

She crossed to the table and rested her fingers upon the case of his microscope.

“I have accepted a position in Professor Lavardin’s physiological laboratory in Paris. The work begins in April.”

“Paris!”

“There are investigations there which I have wanted to pursue for years. I shall have facilities impossible in a country practice.”

“You are leaving Hoyland?”

“Yes.”

His recovered leg seemed suddenly uncertain beneath him.

“But you have succeeded here.”

“That is why I can leave without thinking myself defeated.”

“And your patients?”

“They will return to you. Some of them never truly left.”

He turned away and stared into the fire. The future which he had arranged so neatly vanished—the shared rounds, the discussions, the two names upon one plate. In its place stood his old secure life, intact and intolerable.

“Is there nothing I can say?”

“No.”

“Nothing I can offer?”

“You have offered me what you value. I am grateful.”

She came nearer and held out her hand.

“You were a generous enemy and have become a dear friend. Let us not make either truth smaller by pretending it is another.”

He took her hand. It was the same steady hand which had held the inhaler above him, guided the forceps, tested his pulse, and rebuilt, with equal precision, his leg and his opinions.

“You will write?” he asked.

“If you wish.”

“I shall wish.”

She left him standing beside the fire.

In April Dr. Verrinder Smith’s brass plate disappeared from the house opposite the post-office. The rooms were taken by a solicitor, who possessed a wife, two children, and no opinions upon physiology. Hoyland settled back into its former habits. Dr. Ripley’s waiting room filled; the chestnut mare, recovered from the accident, resumed her journeys; and his income rose beyond its old comfortable figure. Those who had predicted disaster now congratulated him upon the departure of his rival.

“You are monarch of all you survey again,” said Sir Henry. “A lucky dog!”

“Yes,” said Ripley. “Very lucky.”

He received letters from Paris at long intervals. They contained little sentiment and much science: accounts of experiments, disputes over results, and the progress of a monograph which later attracted considerable notice. He read each letter many times and kept them in the locked drawer where he had once stored unpaid bills.

The country mammas renewed their efforts. The Misses Scully were by then married, but younger candidates appeared at dinners and garden parties. Dr. Ripley remained courteous, learned, and unmarried. His hair grew thinner, his reputation greater, and his limp more marked in damp weather.

Professionally, he had gained everything he had once desired. No rival plate confronted his window. No second gig stood at the doors of his patients. Hoyland was his without dispute.

But sometimes, when the evening surgery was over and the street lay empty beneath the lamps, he would look across at the solicitor’s darkened rooms and remember a pair of grey eyes behind pince-nez, a controlled smile, and a voice which had contradicted him without fear.

Then Dr. James Ripley, who had learned late that a mind may be conquered only by freeing it, would turn back to his solitary fire and listen, in the silence, for footsteps which would never again cross his hall.

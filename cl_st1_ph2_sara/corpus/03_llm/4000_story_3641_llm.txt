### THE SUCCESS OF GEORGE BENTON

Edward Mills and George Benton were distant cousins and complete orphans, and therefore were taken in by Mr. and Mrs. Brant, who were good people. They were so good that they had reduced goodness to a rule, which saved the trouble of examining it in particular cases.

“Be pure, honest, sober, industrious, and considerate of others,” Mr. Brant often said, “and you will never lack success or friends.”

Edward listened and believed him. George listened when there was nothing more agreeable to do.

When Mrs. Brant divided a cake, Edward accepted the smaller piece, because he was considerate. George screamed for the larger one, and got it, because Mrs. Brant feared that excitement might injure his sensitive nature. If Edward broke a cup, he confessed and paid for it out of his little savings, which proved that moral training was bearing fruit. If George broke one, he denied it, accused Edward, kicked the cat, and became so overwrought that Mrs. Brant gave him candy to restore his confidence.

Edward kept his clothes neat. Consequently they lasted. George tore his, and received new ones. Edward returned punctually from school and was allowed to weed the garden. George played truant, and when he was found after dark the household embraced him, fed him delicacies, and thanked Providence for preserving him.

Thus both boys learned their lessons.

At fourteen Edward obtained work in a store. He rose early, swept the floor, kept the accounts accurately, and brought his wages home. His conduct excited no remark, because it was his natural bent. George also obtained work, but quarreled with the proprietor, took several dollars from the till, and disappeared.

The good Brants spent three anxious weeks searching for him. When he was found in a distant town, hungry and tearful, they paid his debts, bought him a suit, and gave a supper in gratitude for his return. The minister spoke feelingly of the lost sheep. Edward waited upon the guests and washed the dishes afterward.

An account of it was published.

George promised reform. He kept the promise for nine days, which was thought remarkable in one so young. A silver watch was presented to him by several benevolent neighbors as an encouragement. Edward had wanted a watch, but it was explained that he did not require encouragement.

By and by George sold the watch, drank the money, and ran away again. This distressed the Brants, but did not surprise Edward. He continued working, and in time became confidential clerk, then junior partner, in a modest wholesale business. He did not smoke, drink, gamble, lie, or borrow. He gave to the poor, subscribed to the temperance league, contributed to the Society for the Rescue of Discharged Prisoners, and paid annually for a pew in which he seldom had time to sit.

George, meanwhile, returned at intervals. Each return was more affecting than the last, because he had farther to return from. He stole from the Brants, pawned their silver, and once struck Mr. Brant; but he always wept afterward, and Mrs. Brant said there must be good in a man who could feel so much remorse.

When the Brants died, it was discovered that they had left their house, land, and savings to George. Their will explained that Edward was steady and able to care for himself, while poor George needed protection. As a final request, Edward was instructed to receive George into his business and guide him with patience.

Edward obeyed. George became a partner without putting in money, experience, labor, or anything else that might impair the purity of the arrangement.

At this time Edward loved Clara Wilton, an excellent young woman with a tender heart and a taste for difficult duties. Clara esteemed Edward highly; but when George told her that only a noble woman could save him, she saw where her duty lay. She married George.

“You would have made me happy,” she told Edward, “but he needs me.”

Edward congratulated them and paid for the wedding trip when George’s check was returned.

Edward afterward married Clara’s practical sister, Mary, who did not believe herself appointed to regenerate anybody. They were happy in a quiet way, and so nothing was heard of it.

For several months George attended the office twice a week and spoke of beginning a new life. Then he began speculating with the firm’s money. He explained that his object was to make fortunes for both families. When the losses were discovered, he borrowed on forged securities to recover them; when recovery failed, he gambled; when gambling failed, he drank. Finally he disappeared, leaving Edward to explain matters to creditors who had not the advantages of a sympathetic temperament.

The business collapsed. Edward surrendered his house, savings, furniture, and even Mary’s small inheritance. He might legally have retained a portion, but did not. This surprised the lawyers and inconvenienced his children.

Clara returned with two little ones and no money. Edward supported them along with his own family. George sent a letter saying that shame prevented his coming home, and asking for twenty dollars.

Edward sent ten. It was all he had.

For two years he worked at whatever he could find. He applied to the organizations to which he had contributed. The temperance society regretted that its funds were reserved for drunkards. The discharged prisoners’ society could do nothing because he had never been convicted. A benevolent association informed him that honest poverty was unfortunately outside its charter, though a committee would gladly investigate him if he should abandon his family or commit an offense involving moral danger.

Edward thanked them.

At length his reputation for exactness procured him a position as cashier in the National Citizens’ Bank. The salary was small, because honesty was expected in that office and was therefore not an extra service. Still, he was grateful, and by careful economy Mary managed to feed seven people with what more imaginative families would have spent on four.

While all these things were going on, George was found drunk in a gutter.

A temperance worker named Miss Peabody discovered him, learned that he had once possessed respectable connections, and determined to save him. George was washed, dressed, prayed over, and exhibited at a public meeting. He signed a pledge with a gold pen. The audience applauded for six minutes. Edward had kept the same pledge all his life, but there had never been a convenient evening for applauding him.

An account of it was published.

Subscriptions provided George with lodging, clothing, and employment. He remained sober long enough to be photographed, then took the employer’s receipts and vanished. Miss Peabody said the relapse showed how fiercely the powers of darkness contended for a valuable soul. More money was raised.

George next appeared in a police court for assault. A prison reform committee testified that punishment would destroy the faint spark of goodness still alive within him. He was released to their care, signed another pledge, and addressed a meeting on “My Battle with the Demon.”

An account of it was published.

He discovered that audiences preferred a battle with several reverses, because uninterrupted victory lacked incident. Thereafter he reformed regularly. He fell in Boston, rose in Albany, fell in Buffalo, and rose with unusual splendor in Chicago. Each resurrection brought a better suit, a larger hall, and a more respectful introduction. At last he became a professional lecturer, warning young men against the very habits from which he drew his income.

Edward sometimes saw his cousin’s portrait in the newspapers. Under it appeared the words:

GEORGE BENTON—THE MAN WHO CONQUERED HIMSELF.

Edward was too busy conquering rent, hunger, and unpaid school bills to make public engagements.

George’s fame was temporarily interrupted when he forged the name of a patron who had trusted him. He was sent up for seven years. The phrase “sent up” was used because it sounded more hopeful than “sent away.”

In prison he conducted religious services and wrote a book entitled *From Midnight to Morning*. Ladies sent flowers. Clergymen visited him. A petition declaring that society needed his message more than the prison needed his person secured eighteen thousand signatures. The governor pardoned him after eleven months.

An account of it was published.

George came out beneath an arch bearing the words WELCOME, BROTHER. He was presented with a purse of nine hundred dollars and a railway pass. Edward, who had quietly supplied Clara and her children during George’s imprisonment, received a note from the committee thanking “all unnamed helpers.”

Then came the night which completed both careers.

Shortly after midnight, three masked men entered the National Citizens’ Bank. They bound the watchman and seized Edward, who was working late over a discrepancy of seven cents. The robbers demanded the combination of the principal safe.

Edward refused.

They threatened him. He refused again. They beat him, and still he would not betray the trust placed in him. The leader removed his mask and said that recognition now made refusal useless.

It was George.

Edward begged him to go before murder was added to the rest. George, who had often been moved by appeals before audiences, was not moved by this one. He shot Edward and opened the safe with figures afterward found in a memorandum stolen from the bank president.

The robbers were captured. The money was recovered. Edward was buried.

For several days the newspapers praised his fidelity. The bank directors issued a resolution stating that his conduct had been creditable, though they considered it proper to mention that the seven-cent discrepancy had not yet been explained. They contributed fifty dollars to his widow, ten dollars of it being deducted for a wreath.

A national association of banks also opened a subscription. The total, when divided among its members, represented nearly half a cent from each institution. It was considered an impressive testimony to commercial honor.

George’s situation aroused deeper feeling. He had been convicted of murder and sentenced to hang, but many could not forget his former reforms. Miss Peabody declared that society, having failed to save him on five occasions, had no right to abandon him on the sixth. Clergymen preached upon mercy. Ladies circulated petitions. His lecture associates arranged a benefit. Children sent pennies. The newspapers printed portraits of George as a bright-faced boy, as a ruined youth, as a temperance champion, as a prisoner, as a pardoned man, and as a condemned soul standing at the edge of eternity.

No portrait of Edward was available except a faded family photograph, and it reproduced poorly.

At eleven o’clock on the night before the execution, George experienced a complete conversion. He confessed that he had been a great sinner, which was accepted as new information, forgave everybody, and announced that he would meet them in a better world. The chaplain came out weeping and told the reporters that no deathbed had ever been more triumphant.

An account of it was published.

George went to the gallows supported by two ministers. He said he was at peace. A hymn was sung. Several spectators fainted. One reporter described the dawn as laying a golden promise upon the prison wall. The mechanism performed its duty without asking for a pardon.

George’s funeral was attended by temperance delegations, prison reformers, charitable ladies, converted gamblers, and persons who had never met him but felt that they knew his heart. His grave disappeared beneath flowers. Upon the stone were carved the words:

HE SINNED, HE SUFFERED, HE REPENTED.  
MUCH WAS FORGIVEN HIM, FOR HE LOVED MUCH.  
HIS FALL WAS TERRIBLE; HIS TRIUMPH ETERNAL.

Edward’s grave stood in the cheaper part of the cemetery. Mary saved for two years to place a stone over it, but food, shoes, and fever repeatedly interfered. At last a young mason, who had once known Edward, offered to do the work at cost. Mary chose for the inscription the maxim taught by the good Brants—the rule Edward had obeyed without a holiday from childhood until death.

The mason carved:

BE PURE, HONEST, SOBER, INDUSTRIOUS,  
AND CONSIDERATE OF OTHERS,  
AND YOU WILL NEVER—

At that point Mary’s money ended, and the sentence remained so.

Public admiration for Edward revived somewhat on the anniversary of the robbery. A committee proposed a suitable memorial. Thirty-two thousand dollars was raised in his name; but it was decided that giving it to his widow and children would be merely temporary relief, whereas a building would exert an elevating influence for generations.

So they erected the George Benton Memorial Church.

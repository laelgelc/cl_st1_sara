The racing cars came down from the Wicklow roads in a storm of dust and applause, carrying the brilliance of Europe into a city which had spent the afternoon waiting for it. Along the approaches to Dublin, clerks, labourers, children and idle men pressed against hedges and walls to watch the machines flash past. For an instant each car became a coloured projectile, blue or red or white, and then vanished northward, leaving only petrol fumes, grit and the conviction that something magnificent had occurred.

In the second blue car sat Jimmy Doyle.

He was wedged comfortably between Charles Segouin, the owner, and André Riviere, the young Canadian engineer who had driven for much of the race. Behind them, filling more space than his body required, sat Villona, a Hungarian of prodigious appetite and musical gifts. The car had not won, but it had performed honourably, and Segouin received the cheers of the roadside crowd with the calm smile of a man to whom public enthusiasm was another form of capital.

Jimmy’s face was hot from the wind. Every shout seemed intended for him. The swift blue animal answered the road beneath them; its polished bonnet quivered, its machinery gave out a deep, disciplined note. This was power—real power, not the slow accumulating of sovereigns behind a shop counter. The people saw the car and saw him in it. More than once he raised a gloved hand.

Segouin leaned across Riviere and shouted something.

Jimmy laughed at once, though the wind had carried away half the words.

“What?” he called.

But Segouin was already laughing too, and it no longer mattered.

Jimmy’s father had begun life above a public house in Kingstown and had enlarged his fortunes by observing that men who drank also ate, that men who ate might be persuaded to buy provisions, and that contracts were more nourishing than patriotism. In his younger days he had spoken warmly against English influence; prosperity had cooled him. By the time Jimmy was old enough for education, the elder Doyle preferred solid connections to ardent opinions and sent his son first to a Catholic college in England and afterward to Dublin University, where Jimmy spent more money than distinction.

There had also been a period at Cambridge, brief but socially valuable. Jimmy had acquired acquaintances, neckties and a way of pronouncing the names of wines. He had learned that one could be received almost anywhere if one arrived with confidence and departed before questions arose. His father complained of the expense, yet treasured every foreign address in Jimmy’s book as evidence that the family had escaped the narrowness from which it came.

Segouin was the best of these evidences.

He was young, assured, sufficiently handsome, and unmistakably rich. He spoke of manufacturers in Paris, engineers in Liège, concessions in Belgium and a proposed motor establishment whose profits, though not yet visible, appeared immense whenever he described them. Riviere possessed the technical knowledge. Segouin possessed the scheme. Jimmy was to possess a share.

The sum under discussion was large. His father had hesitated, asked questions, objected to the novelty of the trade; but motors were the future, and the future, Jimmy felt, could hardly refuse entrance to a man who had ridden in it at fifty miles an hour. At last the elder Doyle had agreed. Better that the boy should be attached to wealthy men. Better still if those men were Continental.

At the edge of the city the car slowed. Dublin received them with trams, carts, shouting boys and rows of worn houses under a rinsed evening sky. The blue machine moved among the traffic like a foreign prince obliged to pass through a market. Villona began to hum. Riviere discussed the engine’s performance with Segouin in rapid French. Jimmy caught references to compression, heat and a damaged valve, but the rest escaped him. He nodded intelligently.

They left him near his father’s house so that he might dress for dinner.

Upstairs, while the sounds of the street entered through the window, Jimmy inspected himself in the glass. His shirt front was perfect; his waistcoat showed exactly enough; his eyes, though rather innocent-looking, were enlivened by the day. He imagined the evening ahead: the Frenchmen, the Hungarian, the talk of racing and business, the easy expenditure of money. Pots of money. He arranged his tie again and went down to receive his father’s approval.

Old Doyle walked around him with satisfaction.

“You’ll not be late?”

“No.”

“And Segouin—he’s certain about the company?”

“My dear father, it is practically settled.”

The phrase pleased them both. His father pressed several banknotes upon him, not because Jimmy had asked, but because a Doyle must not appear diminished among foreigners.

Dinner was served in a private room at an elegant hotel. Segouin had changed into evening clothes and seemed even more prosperous than before. Riviere, pale from exertion, recovered quickly under the influence of champagne. Villona ate with serene concentration, suspending his attention only when the conversation touched upon music.

A fifth man joined them: an Englishman named Routh, whom Jimmy had once met at Cambridge. Routh was broad-shouldered, neat, and firm in his opinions. He listened before speaking and, when he spoke, gave the impression that the matter had been closed.

They discussed the race, the roads, the inferiority of certain drivers. They discussed France, Ireland and England. Segouin praised Irish hospitality; Routh praised British administration; Jimmy attempted to reconcile the two with a remark about progress. For a moment the talk sharpened. The old subjects moved round the table wearing new evening clothes.

Then Villona, having finished his meal, declared that all politics were bad for digestion.

Everyone laughed. More champagne arrived.

Jimmy felt himself rising wonderfully to the company. He made a story of an incident at the race and discovered, midway through it, that the others were listening. He exaggerated the speed, imitated a startled policeman, and brought his hand down upon the table at the proper instant. The glasses rang. Segouin laughed. Even Routh smiled.

What fellows! What an evening! There were no barriers among intelligent men. Nationality itself became charming when accompanied by good wine.

After dinner they walked through the city. The streets shone under lamps; carriage wheels passed over damp stone; voices drifted from public houses. Dublin had put on the pale, temporary mask of a capital. Jimmy walked beside Segouin, speaking of the motor business. He wished to show enthusiasm without anxiety, confidence without vulgar curiosity.

“The preliminary capital will be adequate?” he asked.

“Entirely,” said Segouin. “With the right associates.”

The words warmed Jimmy. He did not ask what portion of that adequacy was to be his.

Near the theatre they met a short, energetic man in a light overcoat who hailed Segouin with delight. He was Farley, an American, lately arrived from London, with a yacht lying in the harbour. Within three minutes he had shaken every hand, heard an account of the race and insisted that the whole company come aboard.

“A race deserves a proper finish,” he said. “We’ll make one.”

The proposal seemed inevitable. They went by street car, then by train, the night breaking into successive windows of light. Villona sang under his breath. Farley told stories of New York, Boston and San Francisco, each city apparently containing larger buildings, faster fortunes and prettier women than the last. Routh challenged one of his figures; Farley doubled it.

At the harbour a sailor rowed them across black water. Lamps trembled upon its surface. The yacht waited beyond them, white-sided and silent, with a row of golden ports. Jimmy looked back at the shore. The town lay low and indistinct, its lamps scattered over the darkened mirror. Out here the air tasted larger. To be taken aboard an American yacht after dining with racing men—this was seeing life.

Farley conducted them below with proprietary speed. The cabin was warm, bright and compact. Brass fittings shone. Bottles stood ready in racks. A piano occupied one wall as improbably as a thoroughbred in a drawing-room.

Coats were thrown aside. Drinks appeared. Villona sat at the piano and struck three solemn chords, then broke into a dance tune. Segouin seized Farley by the arm; Riviere danced with Routh; Jimmy clapped time and shouted encouragement. Presently all of them were moving, colliding, laughing, correcting one another’s steps. The floor dipped gently beneath them.

Speeches followed. Farley drank to Ireland. Jimmy, touched by the compliment, drank to America. Segouin proposed France; Routh answered for England; Riviere claimed Canada; Villona lifted his glass to Hungary and, after reflection, to music. Finally they drank to Humanity, which seemed that night a select and well-connected society.

Jimmy made a speech of his own. He could not afterward have repeated it, but while delivering it he felt noble and precise. He spoke of enterprise, fellowship, the triumph of modern invention over old divisions. The others applauded. Segouin embraced him lightly.

More bottles were opened.

It was Farley who produced the cards.

“Only something small,” he said.

Cards! Of course. A table was cleared, ashtrays moved, lamps adjusted. Villona returned to the piano, declaring that cards lacked melody, but the other five sat down. At first the stakes were modest. Jimmy won twice and arranged his little heap of counters with studied indifference.

He understood the game, certainly. He had played before. Yet Farley played quickly, Routh cautiously, and Segouin with an ease that made every decision appear obvious after he had made it. Riviere watched the cards as though they were engine parts liable to reveal a fault.

Jimmy drank and lost, then won a handsome pot. Delight ran through him. He had judged them wrongly; they were too careful. One must have nerve. Money liked movement.

The game continued.

The yacht seemed motionless now, though the glasses sometimes trembled. Villona’s music wandered from dances into grand, troubled passages. Smoke gathered below the ceiling. Jimmy found it difficult to remember which cards had gone. Once he played too soon and Routh glanced at him. Another time Segouin quietly corrected the amount of his stake.

“What excitement!” Jimmy said.

Farley laughed. “That’s the spirit.”

His counters diminished. A fresh stack was pushed toward him. Someone explained its value, but at that moment Villona struck a cascade of notes and Jimmy missed the figure. No matter. He would recover. A bold play was required.

He lost again.

The banknotes his father had given him disappeared with surprising neatness. Jimmy wrote an I.O.U. Farley supplied the pencil; Segouin made room on the table. The sum looked small when reduced to ink.

Then came a run of bad cards—or bad luck—or perhaps Routh’s intolerable caution. Jimmy could no longer distinguish cause from consequence. He knew only that withdrawal would appear mean. These men dealt in thousands. His investment alone proved that he belonged among them. To stop for a few losses would be provincial.

He signed another paper.

Time broke apart. There were cards, drinks, bursts of argument, the piano falling silent and beginning again. Farley lost heavily and remained exuberant. That comforted Jimmy. An American millionaire could lose; an Irish merchant’s son could do the same. It was democratic, almost.

At one point Jimmy believed he had won. He reached for the stakes, but Segouin’s hand covered the cards.

“No, my dear Doyle.”

Routh explained. The explanation was perfectly clear and entirely incomprehensible.

“Ah! Yes,” Jimmy said. “Naturally.”

They resumed.

His head grew heavy. The faces around the table detached themselves from their bodies and floated in the smoky light: Farley red and merry, Routh composed, Riviere intent, Segouin smiling with undiminished courtesy. Jimmy began to resent that smile. He played against it, staking not money but dignity, friendship, Ireland—something enormous and undefined.

He lost.

Villona came behind him and placed a broad hand on his shoulder.

“You are tired.”

“Not at all.”

But he was very tired. Figures were mentioned. He tried to add them and discovered that the numbers would not remain still. The total could not be so great. Someone had surely counted an I.O.U. twice. He intended to examine everything later, quietly, when his mind was fresh.

Another hand. Another signature.

At last even Farley’s cheerfulness weakened. He and Jimmy were plainly the chief losers, though Farley’s losses appeared to amuse him as evidence of the evening’s scale. Jimmy had no such protection. Each laugh now sounded expensive.

The cards blurred at their edges. He sat back and closed his eyes for only a moment.

Silence had entered the cabin. The piano had stopped. He heard the faint wash of water against the hull and the dry movement of paper as Routh gathered the accounts. In that small sound Jimmy sensed the coming weight of everything: his father’s questions, Segouin’s business, the bank, the careful totals written in daylight.

He opened his eyes. A shaft of grey had appeared at the edge of the blind. It stripped the brass of its warmth and showed the glasses clouded, the table stained, the faces slack with fatigue. The yacht was no palace now, only an enclosed room carrying them nowhere.

Villona stood by the porthole. He pulled the blind aside and looked out at the colourless harbour.

“Daybreak, gentlemen,” he said.

No one answered at once.

Jimmy lowered his eyes to the papers bearing his name. Morning had not yet fully arrived, but already he could feel its judgment waiting on the shore.

### The Lights on Greywater Head

The summer afternoon lay peacefully upon Wexford Hall.

Beyond the long windows of the western drawing-room, cattle moved through the deep grass with the solemnity of a procession; a rook descended now and again from the great oak by the ha-ha; and, farther off, the Sussex hills rested under a pale blue sky. Nothing in that ordered English landscape suggested war, unless it were the unnatural absence of young men from the fields.

Within the room the war was everywhere.

Above the mantel hung the portrait of Captain Anthony Wexford in the scarlet and gold of his regiment. On a table beneath it stood his photograph in khaki, taken before the fighting at Loos. The regimental colours were worked upon the fire screen; and on the breast of the old man who stood beside the window were the ribbons of campaigns already become history.

Major Sir George Wexford had been standing there for ten minutes. His erect figure, framed by the curtains, might have been painted upon the quiet landscape behind him. Only the fingers closed behind his back betrayed him; they tightened and relaxed continually upon one another.

Lady Mary sat near the cold fireplace.

She was a small, upright woman, with silver hair arranged in severe smoothness above a face which seemed less like flesh than like an exquisite mask in plaster. Age had refined rather than softened it. Her hands, long and colourless, rested upon the carved head of her ebony stick.

Before them stood Miss Eleanor Vane.

She was an American, a Southerner by birth, and there remained in her speech the faint cadence of Virginia—a softness which could make the plainest declaration sound reflective and the most audacious almost courteous. She wore grey. Against the dark oak of the room she had the effect of a vivid portrait from which the painter had omitted every unnecessary colour.

On her left hand was Tony Wexford’s ring.

Lady Mary looked at it, and then at the girl.

“You understand, Eleanor,” she said, “that we have not asked you here upon gossip.”

“Yes.”

“The reports have been repeated, circumstantial, and supported by persons whose word we cannot disregard.”

The girl inclined her head.

Sir George turned from the window.

“Mary,” he said painfully, “perhaps I had better—”

“No, George. It must be spoken plainly.” Lady Mary’s gaze did not leave the girl. “While Tony is in France, you have been meeting a man calling himself Arthur Meadows.”

“I have.”

“You met him without introduction.”

“Yes.”

“You afterwards dined with him in London.”

“Several times.”

“And you have accompanied him alone, at night, in his motor-car.”

“Yes.”

Sir George moved, but Lady Mary remained rigid.

“Along the eastern coast?”

“Along the eastern coast.”

“Without a chaperon?”

“Quite without one.”

The old lady’s fingers pressed the ebony handle.

“Who is Mr. Meadows?”

“He said he was an American businessman.”

“Said?”

“I had no proof of it.”

“His occupation?”

“He represented himself as having interests in agricultural machinery. He spoke of tractors, harvesting devices, and motor ploughs with considerable fluency.”

“And his family?”

“I never heard of them.”

“His letters of introduction?”

“He had none that I saw.”

Lady Mary’s expression altered by no visible degree, yet the room seemed to grow colder.

“You must forgive me if I employ an unpleasant phrase. In our day, a man travelling without position, profession, or introductions, and selling machinery from town to town, would have been called a bagman.”

“I believe he would.”

“And you, engaged to our son, deliberately formed an acquaintance with this person?”

“I deliberately picked him up,” said Eleanor.

The vulgar directness of the words struck the room like a thrown stone.

Sir George’s face became grey.

The girl saw it. A movement, so slight that neither of the others perceived it, passed over her mouth. But when she continued, her voice was even.

“I don’t mean to make it appear better than it was. I went to the Carlton on purpose because I had learned he took coffee there after dinner. I arranged to be seated where he must pass me. I dropped my glove. He returned it. I allowed him to suppose that the meeting was accidental, and when he asked whether he might call, I gave him permission.”

“Good God!” said Sir George.

Lady Mary raised one hand, not to rebuke him but to impose silence upon the suffering which threatened to break established forms.

“Did you care for this man?”

“No.”

“Did he believe that you did?”

“I hoped so.”

The old man turned his back upon them.

Outside, a bird gave one liquid note. It was answered from the cedar lawn.

Lady Mary said, “This is worse than we had feared.”

“It is worse than you feared,” replied Eleanor.

The answer had a strange effect. Lady Mary looked at her sharply, as though some inflection had failed to accord with the shameless words; but the girl’s face was tranquil.

“You encouraged him?”

“Yes.”

“To what extent?”

“To the extent necessary.”

“Necessary for what?”

Eleanor looked for a moment at Tony’s portrait.

“That will take some time to explain.”

“We have time.”

“Then I must begin with Sir Henry Marquis.”

Sir George turned suddenly.

“Marquis of the Criminal Investigation Department?”

“Yes.”

“You know him?”

“I had met him once in Washington and twice in London. The second time in London he asked me whether I loved Tony.”

Lady Mary’s brows contracted.

“What possible concern was that of Sir Henry’s?”

“I thought it impertinent. I told him so. He said impertinence was the principal liberty of policemen and ambassadors, because neither could always afford good manners. Then he asked whether I would undertake something difficult for Tony’s country.”

“For Tony’s country,” repeated the old man.

“He used those words.”

The girl paused.

In the silence the gilt clock upon the mantel made three small, precise sounds.

Eleanor continued:

“Sir Henry told me nothing definite. He said there was in London a man whom they wished to observe, but observation by the ordinary means had failed. This man was watchful, solitary, and suspicious of English acquaintances. He was, however, an American—or pretended to be one—and Sir Henry believed that he might respond to an American woman who appeared adventurous, foolish, and socially unprotected.

“‘You have, if you will pardon me,’ Sir Henry said, ‘three qualifications which my department cannot manufacture. You are American, you are beautiful, and your courage is concealed by an appearance of indolence.’

“I asked him whether the appearance of folly was also natural.

“‘My dear young lady,’ he replied, ‘that can always be supplied by masculine interpretation.’

“I did not consent at once. I told him of Tony. Sir Henry knew that I was engaged; indeed, that was why he had hesitated. He said that what he required might be misunderstood, and that no written explanation could safely be given. If I entered upon the matter, I must submit to whatever was said of me until he released me.

“I asked if it concerned the war.

“He said, ‘It concerns ships that sail from England and do not reach France.’

“Then I consented.”

Lady Mary had not moved. Sir George’s face remained turned towards the girl, but disbelief, hope, and the fear of hope struggled in it.

“You could have told us,” he said.

“No, Sir George.”

“You might have given some hint.”

“Sir Henry expressly forbade it. Mr. Meadows made inquiries concerning every person with whom I was intimate. If you had known, you might have betrayed your knowledge by a kindness. Suspicion would have been safer for us all.”

The old man winced.

Lady Mary said, “Continue.”

“Arthur Meadows was about forty-five, spare, quiet, and inconspicuous. His clothes were American in cut, but not new. He had lived, he said, in Chicago, St. Louis, Baltimore, and New York. He knew enough of each city to deceive most people and never enough to be caught in some local enthusiasm. His accent was good. Occasionally a word came out too carefully, as though he had learned not merely how Americans spoke but how they were expected to speak.

“He owned a large touring-car of exceptional power. The body was plain, almost ugly, but the engine was magnificent. He was proud of the car, and prouder still of its electric lamps. They could throw a white road across the darkness for nearly half a mile.

“At first we drove near London. Afterwards he suggested longer excursions. He liked the east coast—the lonely roads above the marshes, the cliffs near Greywater Head, the flats beyond King’s Lynn. We would dine early, drive after dusk, stop sometimes upon a height, and return near morning.”

Lady Mary’s hand made a small involuntary movement.

“Alone?” she asked.

“Always alone.”

“And Sir Henry permitted this?”

“He instructed it.”

Sir George’s military mind had begun to separate the facts from their social covering.

“The lamps,” he said. “Did Meadows use them from the cliffs?”

“Yes. Sometimes he halted the car and turned it, pretending trouble with the ignition or an overheated bearing. He would flash the lamps seaward—two long, one short; three short; sometimes other combinations. I could not be certain they were signals. A motorist testing lamps might naturally swing them across the road. Nor could we seize a man because light from his car happened to fall upon the sea.

“Sir Henry needed evidence of identity and purpose. He believed Meadows was not a common agent but someone of importance. Yet there was no photograph, no fingerprint, no letter; nothing that could be offered to a court or, more important, to the departments that had already accepted Meadows’s American papers as genuine.”

“How long did this continue?” Lady Mary asked.

“Seven weeks.”

“And during those weeks you allowed us to believe—”

“I allowed everybody to believe what Meadows must believe.”

“Tony also?”

At that the girl’s composure broke for an instant. Colour moved beneath her skin and was gone.

“Tony was in the trenches. Sir Henry said no letter mentioning the matter could safely cross the Channel. I wrote to Tony as usual.”

“Without telling him?”

“Without telling him.”

Sir George looked up at the portrait over the mantel. The painted young man had his mother’s eyes and his father’s broad brow. For the first time the old soldier understood the particular loneliness that must have lain behind those ordinary letters.

Lady Mary’s voice was still austere.

“What has an apparent murder at Benton Court to do with this?”

Eleanor’s eyes rested on her.

“So you heard of Benton Court?”

“We heard that you were there at two o’clock in the morning with Meadows and Sir Henry. The place has been empty for years.”

“That,” said the girl, “was the conclusion.”

She sat only after Lady Mary made a slight gesture towards the chair. Sir George remained standing.

“It was on the fourteenth of June,” Eleanor began. “Meadows and I had dined at Rexton. We were to drive towards Greywater Head, but as we passed through Little Benton a man stepped into the road and waved a lantern. Meadows stopped within a yard of him.

“The man was Sir Henry, though he wore a rough overcoat and a soft hat. He appeared excited.

“‘I am glad to find somebody,’ he said. ‘There has been murder done at Benton Court.’

“Meadows looked at him without expression.

“‘Then you want the police.’

“‘I am the police,’ Sir Henry replied, and gave his name. ‘My own car has broken down. I came upon this affair by accident, and I require witnesses—intelligent witnesses, if Providence has granted them.’

“Meadows said that Providence was irregular in its methods.

“Sir Henry laughed and recognised me. I made the introductions. Meadows seemed reluctant, but Sir Henry touched his vanity with great skill.

“‘I have been told,’ he said, ‘that American business methods are merely applied detection. You discover what a man wants, why he conceals it, and how much the concealment costs. Come, Mr. Meadows, and tell me whether our English criminals compare favourably with yours.’

“We drove through the village and turned between two stone pillars. Benton Court stood a mile from the road, behind elms which had grown almost together above the drive. It had been unoccupied since the death of Lord Benton. The windows were shuttered; ivy covered one wing; and the stone figures upon the parapet appeared, in the moving lamps, to lean over and watch our approach.

“A local constable waited at the entrance. Beside him stood a thin man named Preece, caretaker of a neighbouring property. Preece said that while crossing the fields shortly after eleven he had seen a light in an upper window of Benton Court. Knowing the house to be empty, he approached and heard a cry. He then saw a man run out by the side door and disappear among the trees. Entering the house, he found a body upon the floor of the library. He went at once for the constable. When they returned, the body had vanished.

“The side door had been broken inward. The lock hung loose; splinters lay across the passage. In the library a candle stood on a small table. Near the hearth were dark red stains and the impression of bloody shoes leading towards a second door. A chair had been overturned. On the carpet lay a black silk scarf and a foreign matchbox.”

“Foreign?” Sir George asked.

“It bore the name of a café in Rotterdam.

“Sir Henry became magnificent. You know his manner when a theory takes possession of him. He fills the room with it. He strode about the library, pointing with a silver pencil and constructing the crime before us.

“‘The res gestae are plain,’ he said. ‘A group of foreign agents has used this abandoned house for clandestine meetings. Tonight there was a quarrel. One man was struck down—possibly stabbed. His confederates fled, one through the side entrance in such haste that he smashed the door. Afterwards another accomplice returned and removed the corpse, but not before our witness had seen it.’

“Meadows looked at the stains.

“‘Why remove the corpse and leave the blood?’

“‘Because blood is not so easily carried.’

“‘And why break a door from the outside when one has been meeting inside?’

“Sir Henry said the fugitive may not have possessed a key, or panic may have driven him to violence.

“‘Panic,’ Meadows observed, ‘is a useful junior officer. It receives the blame for every error made by a superior.’

“Sir Henry laughed again, but I saw that he was watching Meadows very closely.

“The constable led us through the evidence. Preece had observed the supposed fugitive under the trees. The man wore a brown coat, he said, and a green felt hat. There was blood on his left cuff. He was of medium height, broad in the shoulders, with a pale face and a dark moustache.

“Sir Henry asked whether Preece could identify him.

“Preece declared that he could.

“‘You see?’ Sir Henry said to Meadows. ‘We have a witness, a scene of violence, foreign associations, blood, and the removal of a body. What further does your transatlantic standard require?’

“Meadows did not answer immediately. He walked to the broken door, knelt, and examined the wood with a pocket lens. Then he lifted the lock, touched one splinter, and returned to the library.

“‘May I ask questions?’

“‘As many as you please.’

“‘And if I reach another conclusion?’

“‘My dear sir, detection advances by contradiction.’

“Meadows looked almost amused.

“‘No,’ he said. ‘It generally advances by vanity. But we may begin with the door.’”

The girl paused.

Lady Mary leaned forward slightly. Moral outrage had not disappeared from her face, but another interest, unwilling and acute, had joined it.

“What was wrong with the door?” she asked.

“Everything, according to Meadows.

“He said that a door forcibly broken by a man in haste records both the direction and method of force. If struck by the shoulder, the panel bends before the lock yields; if kicked, the blow leaves compression or dirt; if levered, there are marks about the jamb. Here the screws had first been loosened with an unsuitable tool. The wood had then been struck several times near the lock.

“‘A burglar?’ Sir Henry suggested.

“‘An amateur producing the appearance of a burglar. Observe these crescent impressions. He used the round end of a small hammer, and he was afraid of noise. The door was not broken in passion or haste; it was persuaded to look broken.’

“Sir Henry frowned.

“‘You are very positive.’

“‘The wood is more positive than I am.’

“He then turned to Preece.

“‘At what distance did you see the fugitive?’

“‘Twenty yards, sir. Perhaps twenty-five.’

“‘What light was there?’

“‘The moon.’

“Meadows asked the date. The constable supplied it.

“‘There was no moon above the trees at eleven-twenty,’ Meadows said. ‘It rose later. Was there another light?’

“Preece hesitated and mentioned his lantern.

“‘You left it by the gate,’ said Meadows.

“The man became confused. Sir Henry intervened.

“‘He may be mistaken concerning the precise colour of the coat or hat.’

“‘He is mistaken concerning all colour,’ Meadows replied. ‘At that distance, under those conditions, the cones of the retina contribute almost nothing. Vision is carried chiefly by the rods, which distinguish luminosity but not hue. A brown coat, a green hat, and red blood upon a cuff are not observations. They are decorations.’

“Sir Henry tapped his pencil against his palm.

“‘You have studied physiological optics?’

“‘Anyone who depends upon evidence must study the instrument through which evidence comes.’

“‘In the dactyloscopic bureaus of Europe, perhaps?’

“For the first time Meadows was silent a fraction too long. Then he smiled.

“‘In a machinery office in Milwaukee, Sir Henry. We sold reading lamps.’

“He crossed to the candle.

“It was a thick yellow candle standing in a brass saucer. The wick was black, and wax had run down one side.

“‘This supplied the light seen from the field?’ he asked.

“Sir Henry said it appeared so.

“Meadows put one finger near the wick, smelled it, and lifted the candle from the saucer.

“‘It did not burn here.’

“‘The wick is charred.’

“‘It has been burned somewhere; not here. A candle guttering sufficiently to produce this stream would leave warm wax in the socket and a pool below. There is none. The base has been cut with a knife and pressed into old grease. More curious still, the wax ran down while the candle was inclined at approximately twenty degrees, but it now stands upright. It has been brought in and planted.’

“‘By the murderer?’

“‘By the author.’

“Sir Henry gave a short laugh.

“‘You distinguish between them?’

“‘I begin to suspect there was no murderer.’

“The constable stared. Preece wiped his forehead.

“Sir Henry’s face had become grave.

“‘And the blood?’

“Meadows knelt beside the hearth. He touched the edge of one stain with a folded visiting-card, then asked for water. The constable brought a glass. Meadows wet the card, rubbed it against the carpet, and showed us a crimson streak.

“‘Red ink,’ he said. ‘Blood darkens towards brown as it dries and coagulates irregularly. This has soaked evenly into the fibres. It also dissolves too readily. A stationer could identify the variety.’

“‘There are footprints in it,’ Sir Henry objected.

“‘There are marks resembling footprints.’

“Meadows studied them one by one. There were six, crossing from the hearth towards the inner door. At first glance they were unquestionably the prints of a man’s boot. But Meadows placed his own shoe beside the clearest.

“‘A foot passing through liquid leaves the strongest deposit in the first step and diminishing deposits afterwards. Here the third is darker than the second, the fifth darker than the fourth. Moreover, the pressure is wrong. A walking man puts heel down, transfers weight through the outer edge, and leaves last at the toe. These impressions have equal emphasis throughout.’

“Sir Henry said, ‘Made with an empty boot?’

“‘No. An empty boot held in the hand gives too clean an edge unless weighted, and the stride becomes mechanically uniform. These vary in angle but not in length. I think something flatter was used.’

“He took the candle and held it low. Under the oblique light, small streaks became visible beside one impression.

“‘Fingers,’ he said.

“Sir Henry bent over.

“‘Fingers?’

“‘The boot sole was inked, then pressed by hand upon the carpet. Here the little finger slipped. Here is the edge of a thumb. And here—if powder were applied—we should probably find a whorl.’

“Sir Henry straightened slowly.

“‘Whose hand?’

“Meadows’s eyes rested on the silver pencil, then on the old-fashioned signet ring Sir Henry wore.

“‘Yours, I think.’

“There was profound silence.”

In the drawing-room at Wexford Hall, the silence of Benton Court seemed for a moment to return. Even Lady Mary’s breathing was inaudible.

Sir George said, “He accused the head of the Criminal Investigation Department?”

“He did more than accuse him,” said Eleanor. “He proved it.

“Sir Henry asked what grounds he had for that particular conclusion.

“Meadows pointed to a small black smudge on Sir Henry’s right cuff. It was red ink mixed with dust. Then he indicated a nick upon the silver pencil. The pencil concealed a narrow blade, and particles of yellow wax remained near the hinge.

“‘You cut the candle,’ Meadows said. ‘You loosened the lock with the blade and finished your work with some blunt instrument. You placed the marks, prompted the witness, and brought us here. Preece is your man; so is the constable. There has been no corpse, no fugitive, and no crime. The question is not what occurred at Benton Court. The question is why Sir Henry Marquis wished me to investigate what never occurred.’

“Sir Henry’s expansive manner vanished.

“He seemed to become physically smaller—not diminished, but concentrated. His eyes, which had appeared jovial and careless, were suddenly without warmth.

“‘And what answer do you propose?’ he asked.

“Meadows looked towards the door.

“At that instant two men entered from the passage. They were not constables. One had a pistol in his hand.

“Meadows did not move.

“Sir Henry said, ‘Your answer has already been given.’

“‘By proving your little comedy false?’

“‘By the manner in which you proved it.’

“Meadows’s face remained composed, but something behind it had altered. It was as if a shutter had closed.

“Sir Henry continued:

“‘An American dealer in agricultural machinery might observe that a door had been tampered with. He might even know that dried blood differs from ink. But he would not, without special training, refer naturally to European dactyloscopic bureaus, retinal rods and cones, pressure sequence in simulated footprints, or the evidential distinction between an event and its fabricated res gestae. Nor would he examine a scene in precisely the order taught twenty years ago by Professor Walther Kranz at Heidelberg.’

“Meadows said, ‘Those matters are in books.’

“‘Some are. One is not. The test of the candle inclination has never been published. It was devised by Professor Kranz and demonstrated privately to six advanced pupils. Five are accounted for. The sixth disappeared in 1911.’

“Meadows’s hand moved a little towards his coat.

“The pistol of the nearer officer lifted.

“Sir Henry said, ‘Do not. The house is surrounded.’

“Then he pronounced a name—not Arthur Meadows, but Karl Holweg Leibnich.”

Sir George drew in his breath.

“Leibnich?”

“You know it?” Eleanor asked.

“Before the war. A criminologist of formidable reputation. Wrote on forged physical evidence and the reconstruction of crime. He was attached unofficially to the German police.”

“And officially,” said Eleanor, “to a department which had no name.

“Sir Henry told us that Leibnich had vanished shortly before the war. His death was reported in South America. Sir Henry never believed it. In neutral countries there had been rumours of a German specialist who prepared identities for agents and exposed counter-espionage traps by applying criminal science to them.

“‘We knew you were signalling,’ Sir Henry said. ‘What we lacked was proof that Arthur Meadows was Leibnich. Your papers are faultless. Your accent deceived Americans. Your fingerprints do not appear in any archive accessible to us. We could have arrested a commercial traveller, but the organisation would have denied and replaced him. I wished to take Karl Leibnich.’

“Meadows—Leibnich—looked at me then.

“It was not anger in his face at first. It was incredulity.

“‘The woman?’ he said.

“Sir Henry bowed slightly.

“‘Miss Vane has assisted us.’

“He turned towards me.

“‘You knew?’

“‘Not your name,’ I said. ‘I knew about the lights.’

“‘You came to me deliberately?’

“I answered, ‘I deliberately picked you up.’

“His expression at that was dreadful, because the words he had taken as proof of conquest became proof that he had been conquered.

“He said to Sir Henry, ‘The signals prove nothing.’

“‘Not alone. But they told us where to look. Two nights ago a patrol recovered from the marsh below Greywater Head a metal cylinder containing sailing instructions, convoy alterations, and a list of recognition signals. Your fingerprints may be absent from our files, but the paper bears the impression of your right thumb.’

“Leibnich smiled.

“‘Then why this performance?’

“‘Because I wanted your mind as well as your thumb. A spy may be replaced. A master is less easily replaced. Germany has only one Leibnich.’

“For the first time Sir Henry became expansive again. He threw back his coat and put his hands in his pockets.

“‘You must forgive the flaws. The red ink was shameful, but real blood introduces difficulties of supply and sentiment. The candle was rather good. The witness was atrocious; I warned Preece not to embroider, but give an honest Englishman a lie and he will decorate it like a public-house at Christmas. As for the footprints, I made them myself, and I confess I expected better of my wrist.’

“Leibnich said quietly, ‘You allowed yourself to appear a fool.’

“‘My dear professor, appearing a fool is among the least expensive disguises and the most effective. Clever men are seldom frightened by stupidity. They cannot resist correcting it.’

“One of the officers approached with handcuffs.

“Leibnich did not resist. At the door he stopped and looked back at me.

“‘Did you ever intend to come with me to America?’

“‘No.’

“‘Did you believe anything I told you?’

“I considered that.

“‘I believed you loved your motor-car.’

“He laughed then—a single hard sound—and went out.

“Sir Henry waited until we heard the car upon the drive. Then he dropped into a chair and wiped his forehead.

“‘Thank Heaven,’ he said. ‘If he had failed to notice the wax, we might have remained here till breakfast.’

“I asked whether the business was finished.

“‘The business is finished. The consequences, I fear, are not.’

“He meant the stories concerning me. Meadows had been seen with me in London, at Rexton, at inns along the coast. There were servants, waiters, hall porters, village people. Sir Henry could arrest a spy, but he could not arrest gossip.

“I said that I did not care.

“‘You will care,’ he replied. ‘That is the disagreeable habit of gossip. One may despise it in principle and bleed from it in practice.’

“He offered to speak to you when official circumstances permitted. I asked him not to do so unless it became necessary. Tony was still in France. I thought I could wait.”

The girl’s voice ceased.

No one spoke.

Outside, the cattle had moved into the shadow of the oak. A servant crossed a distant lawn carrying a silver tray beneath a white cloth, an infinitesimal domestic figure in that enormous tranquillity.

Sir George came slowly from the window.

“All those nights,” he said, “you knew the man might be an enemy agent?”

“Yes.”

“He might have discovered you.”

“Yes.”

“You were alone with him on deserted roads.”

“Yes.”

“And armed?”

“No. Sir Henry said a weapon would be discovered if Meadows searched the car or my bag. I carried a small electric torch with a red lens. If I flashed it three times, watchers were to close in.”

“Were there always watchers?”

“Not always near enough.”

The old man looked again at Tony’s portrait.

His son was somewhere beyond the Channel among the guns and the chalk and the torn trees. For months Sir George had imagined courage only in familiar masculine forms: a trench crossed at dawn, a wounded officer carried from wire, a company held together under shell fire. He had not imagined a girl in an evening cloak sitting beside an enemy on an empty coastal road, smiling while the white lamps spoke out across the sea.

He stopped before Eleanor.

“My dear,” he began.

The words failed him.

Lady Mary rose.

Because she used the ebony stick, the movement was slow and ceremonious. Standing, she appeared smaller than Eleanor, frailer, and more ancient; yet the authority of generations remained about her like a garment.

“Why,” she asked, “did you not defend yourself when I first accused you?”

“Because I had promised not to make the matter public.”

“But Sir Henry has now released you?”

“This morning. Leibnich’s arrest is to be announced tomorrow. Sir Henry said there was no longer any danger.”

“And if he had not released you?”

Eleanor looked down at the ring upon her hand.

“I should have waited for Tony.”

Lady Mary’s face remained still.

“You were certain he would believe you?”

“No,” said the girl.

That simple answer touched what all her bravery had not touched. The old woman’s severe mouth trembled.

“No?”

“I was certain only that he had the right to hear the truth from me before I asked him to judge.”

Lady Mary turned her head towards the portrait.

“When Anthony was seven,” she said, “he broke the glass of the orangery and allowed us to believe for two days that his cousin had done it. He confessed because he could not bear the cousin’s disgrace. I thought then that honour was merely truth spoken promptly.”

She looked back at Eleanor.

“I had not understood that honour may sometimes require silence.”

Eleanor said nothing.

Sir George cleared his throat with military violence.

“Tony’s mother and I have treated you abominably.”

“No,” said Eleanor. “You treated me according to the evidence.”

“Evidence!” He made a gesture of disgust. “A few dinners, a motor-car, village tongues—”

“Evidence,” she repeated softly, “is often accurate and still untrue.”

The old man stared at her; then an unwilling smile moved beneath his white moustache.

“That sounds like Sir Henry.”

“I learned a great deal from him.”

“I trust,” said Lady Mary, “that you did not learn his manners.”

A bell sounded in the hall.

After so much strain, the ordinary note seemed extraordinary. The door opened and Baines appeared, grave with the gravity of a man to whom wars, engagements, accusations and spies were interruptions of a more permanent order.

“Tea is served upon the south terrace, my lady.”

Lady Mary inclined her head.

“Thank you, Baines.”

The servant withdrew.

For an instant the three remained where they were: the old soldier beneath his son’s portrait, the old woman beside the regimental screen, and the American girl in grey, still wearing the ring which had been judged and restored without ever leaving her hand.

Sir George crossed to Eleanor and offered his arm.

He did not offer it casually. The movement had something of parade, something of homage. His shoulders were squared, and in his face there was a pride deeper than apology.

“Will you take an old man in to tea?” he asked.

Eleanor laid her hand upon his sleeve.

Lady Mary stood before them.

By age and rank, it was her place to go first. The forms were unambiguous. She had observed them all her life and required their observance from others.

Now she stepped aside.

Then, with the deliberate grace of another century, Lady Mary Wexford gathered the black folds of her gown and made Eleanor a low curtsy.

The girl’s breath caught.

“No,” she whispered.

But Lady Mary rose and said, “Yes, my dear.”

Her mask had broken at last. Behind its aristocratic severity was an old woman who had nearly wronged the girl her son loved, and who possessed no language adequate to repentance except the language of courtesy.

“You shall go before me.”

Sir George led Eleanor towards the open windows.

Upon the terrace the tea table shone in the declining sun. Beyond it stretched the tranquil fields, the cattle, the oaks, and the blue, untroubled distances of England.

Lady Mary followed them slowly.

At the threshold Eleanor paused and looked back once at Tony’s portrait. The painted face, young and formal above the mantel, seemed no longer to preside over a judgment. It belonged rather to the company of those absent men and women whose service was performed beyond sight, in mud or darkness, under assumed names or under no name at all.

Then she went out between Tony’s father and mother.

And Baines, who had heard everything and would repeat nothing, poured the tea.

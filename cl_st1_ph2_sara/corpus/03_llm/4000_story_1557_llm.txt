### THE SOVEREIGNTY OF NOPALITO

Baldy Stokes, whose hair had withdrawn from the summit of his head but fortified itself in a red-gray hedge around the base, sat on the gallery of the Nopalito ranch house and instructed Webb Yeager in the science of monarchy.

“If I was you,” said Baldy, “I’d be king.”

Webb tilted his chair against the adobe wall and regarded the yellow plain where three thousand cattle browsed under the afternoon sun. Beyond the corrals, the mesquite blurred into violet; a windmill turned with a rusty complaint; and a Mexican boy was pursuing a rebellious calf with language too comprehensive to be printed.

“You wouldn’t last till breakfast,” Webb answered.

“That ain’t the pint. A king don’t have to last. Look at all them kings in history. Mostly they got poisoned, shot, or divorced from their heads. But while they lasted they was kings.”

“You’ve been reading again.”

“No, sir. I heard a school-teacher explain it at the Blue Light saloon. He said government was divided into monarchies, anarchies, and them republics where everybody’s boss and nobody gets the work done. Now, this here Nopalito is a monarchy. Mrs. Yeager is queen. The boys is vassals. I reckon I’m a duke, account of my age and rheumatism. And you—”

“Don’t strain yourself.”

“You’re the prince-consort.”

Webb’s tilted chair came down on all four legs.

Baldy saw that his bullet had struck somewhere, and proceeded to enlarge the wound with the rusty knife of friendship.

“A prince-consort,” he continued, “is a sort of decorated husband. He wears a fine hat, opens fairs, and don’t take no tricks. In a queen-high flush he’s about an eight-spot.”

“You’ve got three minutes,” said Webb, “to get to the bunkhouse.”

“I ain’t criticizing. A man can be happy as an eight-spot if he don’t crave to be ace. But you was born with ace notions. That’s your misfortune.”

Webb rose. He was a tall, lean, deliberate man of thirty-five, brown from forehead to boot-top, with steady eyes and the disconcerting quiet of one who knows that shouting does not increase the caliber of a revolver. He wore no ornamental equipment except a silver spur buckle, and even that had been won at a roping contest rather than purchased to confuse the female judgment.

“Baldy,” he said, “the Nopalito pays you forty-five a month to punch cattle.”

“It does.”

“Then punch ’em. Don’t reorganize the government.”

Baldy ambled away, but at the gallery steps he turned.

“If there comes a revolution,” he said, “I’m neutral till I see which side has the chuck wagon.”

This was Baldy’s political creed, and a sound one; but this is not Baldy’s story.

The Nopalito was Santa Yeager’s kingdom by inheritance, patent, purchase, pertinacity, and the consent of every bank between San Antonio and the Rio Grande. Her father, old Joaquín Valdez, had begun with eighty cows, a spring, and a title to land that rested largely upon tradition and a paper signed by a governor who had been dead seventy years. Santa, his only child, had converted these doubtful assets into sixty thousand acres, graded stock, three fenced pastures, a credit rating, and an annual profit that made cattlemen remove their hats in her presence even when indoors.

She was twenty-eight, dark-haired, straight as a lance, and handsome with that dangerous completeness which causes lesser beauties to call a woman “interesting.” She could read a brand through dust, estimate the weight of a steer across half a mile of atmosphere, detect a false figure in a ledger, and speak to a banker in the chilly dialect that bankers mistake for financial genius. She rode well, shot fairly, and never confused pity with management.

For these qualities Webb had loved her.

For these same qualities, after four years of marriage, he occasionally wished she had possessed them in moderation.

Santa, it must be said, loved Webb with an equal and inconvenient vigor. But love is not always a dove with an olive branch. Sometimes it is a range bull that discovers another range bull at the same water hole. The Nopalito had room for ten thousand cattle, forty cowboys, and innumerable coyotes, but it sometimes appeared too narrow for two competent persons.

Webb managed the herds. Santa managed everything else, including—when necessity presented itself—the herds.

At supper that night she unfolded a telegram beside her plate.

“Dunbar & Shaw offer twenty-six dollars for the six hundred two-year-olds,” she said.

Webb cut a piece of steak and laid down his knife.

“I’ve sold them to McAllister at twenty-five and a half.”

“You didn’t tell me.”

“I’m telling you now.”

“When?”

“This afternoon. Delivery at Los Pinos on the twelfth.”

Santa read the telegram again.

“Dunbar & Shaw will take them at the north siding and pay freight.”

“McAllister spoke first.”

“Speaking first is a child’s method of acquiring property. Fifty cents a head is three hundred dollars, and the freight difference makes two hundred more.”

“I gave McAllister my word.”

“You had no authority to close without consulting me.”

Webb’s eyes narrowed, though his voice remained level.

“I’m manager of the cattle.”

“You manage their condition, range, and delivery. I make the sales.”

“Then you don’t need a manager. You need a foreman.”

At the far end of the table Baldy devoted himself passionately to beans. The other men ate with the noiseless concentration of conspirators under surveillance. News travels rapidly upon a ranch, but the sight of news being born is not common, and they were reluctant to frighten it.

Santa folded the telegram into a precise square.

“The herd goes to Dunbar & Shaw,” she said.

“I sold it to McAllister.”

“And I have cancelled the sale.”

“You did what?”

“I wired him before supper.”

Webb looked at her. For one second the dining room held only the ticking of the clock and the small sinister sound made by Baldy swallowing a bean whole.

Then Santa turned toward the men.

“Baldy, you’ll start the herd for the north siding Monday morning. Pick eight riders. Mr. Shaw will meet you there.”

Baldy’s spoon hovered above his plate. He glanced at Webb, then at Santa, and perceived himself to be a humble baron summoned to choose between rival claimants to the throne.

“Ma’am,” he said carefully, “I reckon—”

“You heard your boss,” said Webb.

The sentence was spoken quietly. But chairs felt it, and plates, and the lamp chimney. Santa’s face altered no more than a good rider’s face alters when the cinch breaks.

Webb stood.

“Mr. Yeager,” she said, “we can discuss this privately.”

“There’s nothing private about it now.”

He went from the room.

Observe, if you will, how calamity prefers small hinges. Empires have turned on a gate left open, wars on a cup overturned, marriages on a pronoun spoken before witnesses. Five hundred dollars was the visible issue. Five hundred dollars will buy many useful things, but it cannot purchase back one word publicly employed.

Webb saddled his black horse while the household maintained a discretion audible at fifty yards. Baldy came to the corral carrying Webb’s slicker and bed roll.

“You ain’t aiming to light out permanent?” he asked.

“I’m riding to San Marcos tonight.”

“San Marcos is sixty miles.”

“I know where it is.”

Baldy tied the slicker behind the cantle.

“A man oughtn’t make foreign policy on an empty stomach.”

“I ate.”

“You dissected a steak. That ain’t eating. Webb, she didn’t mean—”

“Neither did I. That balances the books.”

“You could go back in and have a talk.”

“We’ve been talking four years.”

Webb tightened the cinch. From the house came no movement. Yet a curtain in an upper window was lifted half an inch, a trivial atmospheric disturbance which escaped Baldy but not the agencies of this narrative.

“You told me to be king,” Webb said.

“I was speaking allegorically. Allegory is a kind of lie that wears Sunday clothes.”

“Well, I’m resigning as prince-consort.”

Baldy rubbed his chin.

“What do I tell the boys?”

“Tell them to drive the cattle north. They heard their boss.”

Webb mounted and rode through the gate.

No one ran after him. This is cattle country. People there will pursue a stampeded steer for ten miles, but pride, being more dangerous, is generally permitted to escape.

Santa remained at the upstairs window until the rhythm of the black horse’s hoofs had diminished into the vast silence of the brush. Then she lowered the curtain and went to her office. There were letters requiring answers, invoices requiring approval, and a pump at the east well that had developed monarchical opinions of its own.

She worked until midnight.

If anything else occurred behind the closed door, let us draw over it the decent veil of bookkeeping.

Webb found employment within a week at the Los Olmos ranch, one hundred and twenty miles west. Its owner, Colonel Ransom, possessed thirty thousand acres, a white mustache, and the obsolete military title customarily bestowed by Texans upon men who have never exposed themselves to warfare.

The colonel needed a manager. Webb needed a country in which no queen held veto power. They agreed in ten minutes.

Under Webb, Los Olmos prospered. He repaired tanks, divided pastures, culled bad cows, changed the breeding stock, reduced trail losses, and persuaded the colonel that six cowboys could not perform the work of ten merely because the colonel paid them as four. Before autumn the cattle were fat, the books were sound, and Webb’s authority ran from the windmill to the county line without appeal.

He was king.

And there are palaces colder than bunkhouses.

The first month, Webb expected a letter from Santa. The second month he informed himself that he did not expect one. In the third, he began to regard the mail rider with the detached interest of a man studying meteorology. By the sixth, he had trained himself so perfectly that he could sort the colonel’s circulars, bills, newspapers, seed catalogues, and invitations without appearing to search for a handwriting he would have recognized upside down in a rainstorm.

No letter came.

Nor did Webb write one.

Thus pride, that celebrated guardian of human dignity, stood watch between two persons who desired chiefly to surrender.

At Nopalito, Santa continued to govern.

She negotiated a bank loan, bought four hundred Sussex cows, discharged a cook who had substituted rancid bacon for rebellion, and rebuilt the dipping vats. Her riders obeyed her. Buyers respected her. The ranch enlarged its profits and diminished its laughter.

Baldy alone preserved a precarious diplomatic intercourse with the absent regime. Twice he wrote to Webb. His orthography was bold and individual.

The first communication read:

> Dear Webb:  
> Everything here is normal except it ain’t. We sold high. Rain is low. The Queen is healthy and rides every day. Nobody mentions you, which is about all they talk about. If you have got any habeus corpus left you might come get it.  
> Yours,  
> B. Stokes, Duke pro temp.

Webb answered:

> Dear Baldy:  
> Attend to your cattle.  
> Webb.

The second letter arrived five months later.

> Dear Webb:  
> Your reply received and contents insulted. We got a new batch of Sussex, red mostly, with white faces and some all white. Fine stock but stuck-up like English nobility. The interregnum continues. I looked up that word and it means when there ain’t no king, which fits except for the queen. Come over sometime under a pseudonym if ashamed.  
> Baldy.

Webb did not answer this one, but he kept it.

Time now advanced in its customary military organization. Days marched by in platoons, weeks came up in hebdomadal companies, months deployed as cavalry under banners bearing the ancient device *Tempus fugit*. Winter performed a short and muddy campaign. Spring followed with bluebonnets, calves, thunder, and young men’s thoughts, though no responsibility can be accepted for the last item.

Nearly a year after Webb’s departure, Colonel Ransom decided that Los Olmos required better breeding stock.

“I want Sussex,” he announced, combing his white mustache with two fingers. “They put on weight. Fine deep bodies.”

Webb sat at the office desk studying catalogues.

“Nopalito has the best bunch in the state.”

The colonel gave him a sideways glance. He knew the geography of Webb’s marriage, as everyone did within two hundred miles, but he was too much a gentleman to mention it and too much a Texan not to know it in detail.

“Can we buy from them?”

“If the price is right.”

“Write.”

Webb wrote a formal inquiry for fifty heifers and two bulls, specifying age, condition, pedigree, inspection privileges, delivery date, and every other item behind which a man might barricade himself from the accidental leakage of sentiment. He addressed the envelope:

> Nopalito Ranch Company  
> Santa Valdez Yeager, Proprietor

After considering the final word, he struck it out. Then he restored it.

Santa’s reply arrived four days later, typed by the ranch bookkeeper.

The Nopalito could furnish sixty heifers, uniform in quality, at a quoted figure. Delivery would be made at Los Olmos without charge. Terms, pedigree, and dates were stated. The signature was written in Santa’s hand, clear and dark:

> S. V. Yeager

No personal word adulterated the transaction.

Webb accepted the offer in the name of Colonel Ransom. If commerce possesses a heart, it is kept safely locked inside the cashbox.

At Nopalito, Baldy received orders to prepare the Sussex heifers.

“We’ll cut sixty from the south pasture,” Santa told him. “You’ll start Wednesday and deliver at Los Olmos.”

Baldy blinked.

“Los Olmos?”

“That is what I said.”

“Where Webb—”

“I know where Los Olmos is.”

“Yes’m. Geography was always my strongest moral quality.”

She handed him a list.

“Take César, Johnny, Pete, and four others. The white heifer with the black ears goes too.”

“She ain’t branded yet.”

“Bring her to the small corral tonight.”

Baldy looked at Santa, but her expression offered no grazing. He pocketed the list and withdrew.

At eleven that night the Nopalito slept, with the exception of the coyotes, the night horse, a baby in the ranch house, and such human emotions as are denied the benefit of unconsciousness.

Santa came from the rear door carrying a lantern. Over her dark dress she wore a riding coat. She crossed to the small corral, where the white heifer stood separated from the herd, aggrieved by solitude and prepared to blame civilization.

Baldy waited beside the branding fire.

“You can go,” Santa said.

“I thought maybe you’d need—”

“I won’t.”

Baldy studied the coals. In them lay a small iron he had never seen among the Nopalito brands. Its mark was shaped like a heart enclosing a cross.

His auricular nerves reported facts to his understanding, which was slower but faithful.

“I’ll be at the bunkhouse,” he said. “In case the heifer declares anarchy.”

When he had gone, Santa lowered the rails and drove the animal into the chute. The heifer plunged, kicked, and expressed hereditary opposition to government. Santa set the bar, caught the animal fast, and lifted the little iron from the coals.

Now, because this is a sentimental moment and therefore in danger from explanation, we shall delay it by returning twelve years.

Joaquín Valdez had not approved of Webb Yeager as a suitor. Webb then owned one horse, two saddles, a rifle, and hopes so extensive that they required no fencing. Old Valdez considered him suitable for employment but not matrimony. He forbade him the house.

Santa, being her father’s daughter, obeyed in every respect that did not interfere with her purpose.

At the north boundary of the Nopalito stood an abandoned stone hut. When Santa wished to meet Webb there, she marked upon the white gatepost, with charcoal, a heart enclosing a cross. Webb, riding the road, would see the sign. The heart meant what hearts have always pretended not to mean. The cross admitted that the meaning came attended by difficulties.

It was their signal during six months of forbidden courtship.

Later Webb acquired cattle, old Valdez acquired tolerance, and Santa acquired Webb. The sign disappeared from use, as ladders are discarded after one has climbed to the roof.

Now the little iron glowed red in the dark.

Santa pressed it against the white heifer’s shoulder.

There came a hiss, a smell of burned hair, and the animal’s sudden frantic struggle. Santa held the iron steadily for the necessary seconds, no more and no less. When she released the chute, the heifer bounded into the corral and stood trembling beneath the stars.

On its shoulder, darkening from red to black, was the heart and cross.

Santa watched it. Then she extinguished the coals, carried the lantern back to the house, and stopped at a room beside her own.

Inside, the baby slept with both fists closed, like a small despot withholding two decrees.

Santa bent over the cradle.

“We have sent for your father,” she whispered.

But perhaps she did not say it. A narrator who spies upon midnight branding operations should retain some ethical boundary.

At dawn the Sussex herd started west.

Baldy rode point, his bald summit covered by a Stetson of geological age. César and Johnny worked the flanks; Pete dragged behind, singing to the cattle in a voice that caused even the chaparral to recoil. The white heifer traveled near the center, her new brand hidden at first by dust.

The drive required five days.

They crossed two creeks, one county road, a belt of prickly pear, and a sheep ranch of little account. On the third afternoon a storm came booming out of the northwest. The herd milled; lightning tore the sky into blue rags; and the cowboys spent two drenched hours persuading sixty aristocratic English heifers that Texas weather was no basis for revolution.

On the fifth day they sighted Los Olmos.

Webb rode out with three men to receive the herd. He wore a gray shirt and a clean hat, having selected both without reference to the visitors and therefore with extreme care.

Baldy lifted one hand.

“Morning, Your Majesty.”

“Morning, Baldy.”

The old cowboy grinned.

“You look natural. Little more prosperous around the belt.”

“You look like something the buzzards filed a claim on.”

“That’s me. Durable. We brung your cattle.”

“I can see that.”

“Then your eyesight ain’t suffered under absolute power.”

They shook hands. Neither used the occasion for embraces, being Westerners and not candidates for the stage.

Webb inspected the herd methodically. He rode among the heifers, checking legs, eyes, depth, and condition. They were as good as promised. Santa had sent him sixty of the best. This fact he accepted as a commercial detail, though it struck somewhere below the commercial district.

“Where are the bulls?” he asked.

“Coming in the wagon. One rubbed his foot raw, so we didn’t push him.”

“You did right.”

Baldy turned his horse.

“There’s your white one. Fine heifer. Santa picked her special.”

Webb glanced up.

The white animal had moved away from the others and stood broadside in the sun. Upon her shoulder, neat and dark, was the heart enclosing the cross.

Webb did not move.

The plain around him, the cattle, riders, dust, windmills, and distant ranch buildings retired for an instant beyond the jurisdiction of his senses. He saw instead a white gatepost under the moon; a girl slipping from a stone hut; black hair under a blue shawl; charcoal on his fingers; and a heart around a cross.

Baldy watched him with the innocence of an elderly fox.

“Queer brand,” he observed. “Ain’t registered, far as I know. Maybe it’s a pseudonym.”

Webb rode to the heifer. He leaned from the saddle and touched the mark.

“Who put that on her?”

“Brands don’t generally volunteer testimony.”

“Baldy.”

“Well, I didn’t. My artistic talent runs more to coffee and profanity.”

“When?”

“Night before we started.”

“Did she say anything?”

“No.”

Webb wheeled his horse toward the ranch.

“Jim!” he called to one of the Los Olmos men. “Take charge of the herd. Put them in the north pasture. Check the bulls when they come.”

The cowboy stared.

“Where you going?”

“Nopalito.”

Baldy pushed back his hat.

“Horse is give out some,” he said. “Five days’ drive.”

Webb was already stripping the saddle.

Colonel Ransom’s bay stood tied at the office hitching rail, a long-bodied animal with lungs enough for emergency and scandal. Webb transferred the saddle in three minutes.

The colonel appeared on the gallery.

“Yeager,” he said, “where are you taking my horse?”

“Nopalito.”

“Business?”

“No, sir.”

The colonel combed his mustache.

“Then bring him back when the business is over.”

Webb mounted.

Baldy called after him, “You aiming to be king?”

Webb looked once at the white heifer.

“No,” he said. “I’m aiming to get home.”

He rode westward—though, geographically, Nopalito lay east; love, however, has frequently confused directions. Let us correct the compass and say that he rode east with such velocity that dust rose behind him like a covey of quail escaping judgment.

The distance was one hundred and twenty miles. Webb covered forty before sunset, changed horses at a line camp, rode through half the night, slept two hours on a station floor, and went on at dawn. Waste not your pity upon Colonel Ransom’s bay; the animal was exchanged after the first stage and lived afterward to a respected age, stealing oats and biting children.

By the second afternoon Webb reached the Nopalito boundary.

The land looked unchanged. Mesquite held its gray-green ranks. Cattle lifted incurious heads. The windmill turned. Yet every mile of familiar ground accused him of having remembered it too well.

At the north gate he saw the old white post.

Rain and sun had scoured it clean, but his eye found the place where charcoal hearts had once summoned him. He halted only a moment.

Near the house, a rider was directing men who were loading salt blocks into a wagon. She wore a black skirt divided for riding and a white shirt. At the sound of Webb’s approach she turned.

Santa sat motionless on her chestnut mare.

The men discovered urgent employment on the opposite side of the wagon.

Webb rode up and stopped ten feet away. Dust lay on his clothes; his eyes were red from the trail; his horse breathed hard.

Santa’s face was pale, but her chin held the ancient royal line.

“Did the cattle arrive safely?” she asked.

“Yes.”

“Were they satisfactory?”

“Yes.”

“The bulls—”

“Baldy brought them.”

Silence then assumed command.

A less disciplined woman might have cried out, dismounted, or shown her heart naked in her face. Santa merely gathered the reins more closely. But the chestnut mare, receiving some message through the hands, stepped sideways twice.

Webb took off his hat.

“I saw the white heifer.”

“There was an extra brand on her,” Santa said.

“I recognized it.”

“I thought you might.”

“Did you send it for me?”

Now the veil may descend briefly, for Santa looked away toward the corrals, and what passed across her face belonged neither to ranch records nor royal proclamations.

“I sent sixty heifers,” she said. “The contract specified sixty.”

“Santa.”

At her name, spoken not as a challenge but as a homecoming, the queen’s guard failed.

She dismounted quickly. Webb was off his horse before her boots touched earth. They stood facing each other, still separated by the ridiculous width of human pride.

“I shouldn’t have countermanded your sale before the men,” she said. “I was right about the money and wrong about everything else.”

“I shouldn’t have left.”

“You wanted authority.”

“I wanted you to give it to me. That’s different from earning it.”

“I made you feel hired.”

“I behaved like I was owned.”

They had now advanced dangerously near honesty, a region in which few people can breathe for long.

Webb looked toward the house.

“Is there room for a busted prince-consort?”

Santa’s mouth trembled into a smile.

“There is no prince-consort at Nopalito.”

“No?”

“I abolished the office.”

“Then Baldy’s constitution was defective.”

“Baldy’s everything is defective.”

“Who runs the kingdom?”

Santa held out her hand.

“Come and see.”

They walked toward the ranch house, leaving the horses to two cowboys who handled the reins with eyes fixed upon remote scenery. When Webb and Santa had entered, one of the men exhaled.

“I knowed he’d come back,” he said.

“You bet you did,” said the other. “You only lost twelve dollars betting he wouldn’t.”

Inside, Santa led Webb upstairs to the room beside her own. She opened the door.

A cradle stood near the window.

Webb stopped.

The child within was perhaps three months old, round-faced and dark-haired, asleep under a thin white blanket. One hand rested beside his cheek. The other was closed with administrative firmness.

Webb’s hat slipped from his fingers.

Santa watched him. “His name is Joaquín Webb Yeager.”

Webb went to the cradle. A man may face a charging bull, a six-shooter, bankruptcy, matrimony, and a county tax assessor without visible alarm; but set before him seven pounds of sleeping son and his knees become theological questions.

“Why didn’t you tell me?” he whispered.

“I started a hundred letters.”

“Why didn’t you finish one?”

“For the same reason you never started.”

He looked at her.

“I was a fool.”

“So was I.”

“That makes two.”

“No,” Santa said, touching the baby’s hair. “It makes three.”

The infant opened his eyes. He considered Webb with the grave suspicion proper to a monarch receiving an unannounced provincial governor. Then he yawned.

Santa lifted him and placed him awkwardly in Webb’s arms.

“There,” she said. “The true king of Nopalito.”

Webb held the child as though he were valuable, explosive, and without instructions.

“He don’t look like much of a boss.”

“You hear your boss,” Santa said softly.

This time the words carried no wound.

Webb bent and kissed her.

The Nopalito men learned of the restoration before supper, news being faster on a ranch when everyone has been studiously minding his own business. Baldy returned from Los Olmos two days later and found Webb at the branding pens directing the sorting of calves while Santa sat on the fence with a ledger.

Baldy approached cautiously.

“How’s the government?”

“Constitutional monarchy,” Webb said.

“What’s that?”

“I run the cattle. Santa runs the business. When we disagree, we talk where you can’t hear us.”

“Sounds unstable.”

“The king has final authority.”

Baldy peered toward the house.

“Him? Why, he ain’t got teeth.”

“Neither have some congressmen,” Santa said, “and they govern.”

Baldy removed his hat in recognition of this argument.

“Well, Webb, you got your crown.”

“No crown.”

“Scepter?”

“No.”

“Throne?”

Webb pointed to the office, where a cradle had been placed beside Santa’s desk.

“There’s the throne.”

Baldy considered the baby, the ledger, Webb’s branding crew, and Santa’s calm smile.

“I reckon I misdiagnosed the royalty,” he said. “That happens when a man studies political economy in saloons.”

He went to the corral gate and leaned upon it.

Webb resumed his duties at Nopalito. He did not become king, and Santa did not cease to be queen. Instead they practiced that complicated dual sovereignty known to historians as marriage. Webb bought and sold cattle within agreed limits; Santa consulted him before overruling anything; and important state affairs were debated behind closed doors, where no vassal could be injured by flying titles.

There were disputes, naturally. Peace without dispute is found chiefly in graveyards and badly written romances. But neither of them again mistook victory for happiness.

As for the white Sussex heifer, Colonel Ransom kept her at Los Olmos. She grew broad and sleek, raised excellent calves, and bore upon her shoulder all her life the heart enclosing the cross.

Visitors sometimes asked what ranch used that brand.

Colonel Ransom would comb his white mustache and answer that it was not a brand of ownership.

“What is it, then?” they would say.

“A message,” replied the colonel.

And if they demanded to know what the message meant, he referred them to Webb Yeager at Nopalito, who never explained it.

But Santa knew. Webb knew. Baldy claimed to know, though his version involved habeas corpus, royal succession, and a white English duchess traveling under a pseudonym.

The heifer alone offered no interpretation.

She carried the sign beneath the Texas sun, dark upon white, a heart holding its cross—the durable evidence that, in the cattle kingdoms as elsewhere, some messages must be burned before proud people can read them.

### The Digger’s Homecoming

There are several ways of going West, but only one way of coming back when you are stumped. You go West with a new bluey, a cheque in your pocket, and opinions concerning the country; you come back with the bluey gone, the cheque gone, and the country’s opinion of you written plain on your boots. Sometimes you bring a nugget, sometimes fever, sometimes a wooden leg, and sometimes nothing except the habit of looking towards the horizon. Mitchell said that Western Australia was a good country to make money in, provided you took the money with you and left before you spent it.

There were three of us returning that time—Mitchell, the Oracle, and me—and between us we owned two shirts fit to be seen, one razor, a billy, and ninepence. The razor belonged to Mitchell, who never used it; the billy belonged to the Oracle, who had lost the lid; and the ninepence was mine until we went aboard.

We travelled steerage.

The steerage was crowded with men whom the West had corrected. There were prospectors who had lost their outfits, miners who had lost their health, contractors who had lost other men’s money, and quiet chaps who seemed to have lost the part of themselves that used to talk. One had three fingers missing, another had a scar drawn from ear to chin, and another carried his right arm as if it belonged to a dead mate. They had left Sydney at different times with brass-bound boxes, new togs, and friends to see them off; they came back together with swags, battered hats, and no wish to be met.

Yet they were cheerful enough in patches. A man may lose money, years of life, a limb, his chance, his faith in syndicates and sometimes his soul, and still grin when another man slips on a wet deck.

The elderly digger sat near the after-hatch with his arms folded. He was grey, but not weak-looking. His beard had been trimmed with shears, and the red dust of the fields seemed ground into the cracks of his hands. He looked straight ahead most of the time, though there was nothing there but a bulkhead and a coil of rope.

We noticed him because he did not join in the growling over the tucker.

The first dinner was meat with no reason for being tough except that it was steerage meat, and potatoes which had given up the struggle. The old man took his pannikin and went aft without eating.

“Crook?” asked Mitchell.

“No,” he said. “I’m right.”

A man who says he is right in that tone is generally a long way wrong.

That night the place below was like a rabbit-box at shearing-time. Every berth had two owners and three claimants; men lay under the tables, across lockers, and along the passage, while the engines kept up their infernal thinking—thinking, thinking, thinking. Mitchell had secured a lower berth by threatening to yarn to anyone who disputed it, but he found the old digger sitting on his swag by the ladder.

“Take mine,” said Mitchell.

“I’m not sick.”

“Neither am I. I’ve got a complaint that requires fresh air.”

“What complaint?”

“Ratty ideas. Doctor says a bunk makes ’em worse.”

The old man hesitated.

“Take it,” said Mitchell. “If you don’t, the Oracle will, and he snores like a cross-cut saw in ironbark.”

The Oracle said this was a lie, and started a yarn to prove that he had once kept twenty-seven men awake without snoring at all. By the time he reached the second man, the old digger had lain down.

Next morning we learnt his name was Tom Rainer, though most called him Uncle. He had been out beyond Cue on a claim that had promised everything for four years and paid just when payment was no use to him.

The Oracle drew him into talk. He could draw a yarn out of a gate-post if he sat beside it long enough.

“Married, Uncle?”

“Was.”

There was a pause. The Oracle looked away.

“Long?”

“Twenty-three year.”

Then Rainer told us, a little at a time, and mostly while watching the sea.

He and his wife had married when he was on the roads and she was in service near Parramatta. They had grafted together ever since. He had split posts, sunk dams, fenced for cockies, drove bullocks, carted wool, and prospected whenever a report came in strong enough to turn his head. She had washed, sewed, kept lodgers, and brought up the youngsters in rooms where the roof leaked and the landlord did not.

“We never had much,” he said. “But she’d make a shillin’ do the work of two. Never complained neither—not proper complaining. She’d say, ‘Tom, if ever you strike it, I want a verandah with a bit of vine over it.’ That was all she wanted.”

He went West because a mate wrote of gold showing freely in the stone. His wife had not wanted him to go, though she packed his things and stood cheerful at the railway gate.

“She seemed queer,” he said. “Not ill exactly. I had a notion to chuck it up at Adelaide and turn back. Sat there half the night thinking of it. But I reckoned it was only homesickness, an’ I’d promised the mates. A man’s always got a reason for goin’ on when he ought to turn back.”

For two years the claim swallowed every penny. Rainer worked shifts in another mine to keep his share and sent what he could home. Sometimes no letter came for weeks. Then three would arrive together, worn and thumbed, after following him from camp to camp.

The telegram came while he was out at an abandoned shaft forty miles from the township. It had been addressed wrongly, sent on to another field, returned, and hung about offices until someone remembered a grey digger named Rainer.

It said his wife was dangerously ill and asked him to come at once.

“But it was old then,” he said. “Nine days old.”

I could see him riding into camp under the brassy sky, the plain bare around him, the sun going down red through dust; then sitting by his tent in the gloaming with the telegram spread on his knee, while the stars came out in brilliant clusters over a country too wide for a man with urgent business elsewhere. I wish sometimes I did not see things so plainly when men tell them.

He had no money for the journey. His mates subscribed what they could, and the storekeeper offered credit; but before he could leave another wire reached him.

She had been dead six days.

He said this without changing his voice. His hands closed, that was all.

“The first telegram would have fetched me in time, if it had come straight,” he went on. “Maybe not in time to do anything—but in time to see her. She’d have knowed I come.”

Nobody spoke.

“The queer part,” he said presently, “was I’d posted her a letter the day before. The reef had widened, an’ the crushing looked payable. I wrote cheerful. Told her to look out for better times. Said I’d bring her over, maybe, or else come home an’ get that little place she used to talk about. She was buried when I wrote it.”

The Oracle shifted his feet and studied a bolt in the deck.

“Twenty-three year she waited for better times,” Rainer said. “An’ when they come—”

He stopped and rubbed one thumb slowly over the other.

His eldest girl had written afterwards. There were five children at home, the oldest twenty and the youngest a baby born after he went away. He had never seen the baby. He meant to stay in Sydney until the affairs were settled, then return to the claim.

“You’ll go back?” I asked.

“What else?” he said. “The stone’s payin’ now. Youngsters got to live.”

He had no heart for the West, but would go back because there was money in the reef at last. Life had held his nose to the grindstone so long that, when it lifted him for a breath, he could think of nothing except putting it down again.

After that the men looked after him without appearing to. The cook was told that Rainer had a weak stomach, which was not true, and he was served first before the fat floated cold on the stew. A shearer lent him a clean shirt for landing; a broken-down bookmaker produced a collar; someone blacked his boots while he slept. The Oracle kept yarning whenever Rainer began staring too long at the sea. Mitchell made him smoke, though the tobacco was nearly all dust and string.

The voyage seemed short until we sighted the Heads. Then it became a cruel long, long time.

Everyone changed for landing. Men who had slept in their clothes washed behind the funnel and scraped their chins with pocket-knives. They cursed Sydney, but watched for it like children.

Rainer stood at the rail in the borrowed shirt and collar. His coat shone at the seams. He had brushed his hat until the felt came through.

“Expecting ’em?” Mitchell asked.

“They said they’d meet me.”

As the steamer warped towards the wharf, Rainer searched the crowd. His eyes moved up and down, up and down, and his lips were set tight. Then he saw them.

There were four close together near a stack of cases, and the eldest girl carried the baby. She had her mother’s shape, I suppose, for Rainer caught his breath. She wore a black dress too old for her, and when she saw him she smiled—a strained, mechanical smile that came and stayed because she did not know what else her face ought to do.

Beside her stood a boy in a black suit with sleeves beyond his wrists. He kept pulling at one cuff and looking towards the water. Another girl, about thirteen, held the little one by the hand.

The little one might have been seven.

She did not smile. She looked straight at her father with large wet eyes, as though she pitied him and was afraid for him. Children can look at grief without the polite lies grown people learn.

Rainer lifted one hand. The eldest girl nodded quickly and bounced the baby, though it was quiet. The boy touched his hat, then went red. The little girl never moved.

The ship took a year to come alongside.

Rainer’s throat worked. His hand tightened on the rail until the knuckles showed white. He looked at the eldest, at the baby he had never seen, at the boy in the bad black suit—and then at the little girl.

That finished him.

He turned abruptly and went below.

Mitchell went after him.

The rest of us kept our places. No man looked at another. The Oracle began to say something about the luggage, but his voice went wrong, and he climbed onto a case to swear at a deck-hand who was doing nothing in particular.

When the gangway was fixed, Rainer came up again. His face was washed and hard, and Mitchell followed at a distance, lighting an empty pipe.

The eldest daughter reached him first.

“Father,” she said.

“Yes, Mary. Yes, lass.”

He kissed her, then touched the baby’s cheek with one finger. The boy offered his hand and Rainer shook it gravely, man to man. But the little girl stood back, still looking at him.

“Come here, Annie,” he said.

She came slowly.

“You remember me?”

She shook her head.

That cut worse than if she had cried.

“I’m your father, little lass.”

“I know,” she whispered.

Then she put both arms round his neck, and he went down on one knee on the dirty wharf and held her as though she were the only solid thing left in the world.

Mitchell picked up the swag. I took the battered portmanteau, but he gave me the swag as well and lifted the baby from Mary’s arms.

“Come on,” he said briskly. “There’s too many fools staring here. We’ll get round behind the sheds.”

He carried the baby awkwardly, with its head in the crook of his arm and its black bonnet against his unshaven chin. The child caught hold of his moustache. He let it pull.

We guided them past the carriers and cabmen, round a pile of wool-bales, to a quiet place by the customs fence. There we left Rainer with his children.

Later, as Mitchell and I walked towards George Street, I asked him why he had followed the old man below.

He looked offended.

“Followed him? Don’t get sentimental.”

“Well, you went after him.”

“I wanted a drink,” said Mitchell.

“There wasn’t any drink below.”

“There might have been.”

“And Rainer?”

Mitchell filled his pipe with the last dust from his pocket.

“A man like that,” he said, “wouldn’t want to drink alone.”

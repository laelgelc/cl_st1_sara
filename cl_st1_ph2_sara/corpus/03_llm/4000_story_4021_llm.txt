### The Song at Daybreak

Aengus, son of Forbis, had lived through youth and manhood and the greater part of age in a broken monastery upon Inish Mór, where the lake spread about the grey walls as smooth as blue velvet, save when a wind out of the west drew upon it long threads of silver. The roof had fallen from the chapel, and quicken trees grew between the stones of the cloister; but one chamber remained dry, being sheltered by an ancient yew, and there Aengus kept his books, his brazen lamps, his rods of hazel, and many coloured stones upon which were graven the names of gods whose worship had passed out of remembrance.

He was so old that his white hair seemed finer than the web of a water-spider, and his skin had the brown, wrinkled look of a leaf that has lain all winter under snow. Yet his eyes were bright and restless, and sometimes, when he bent over an enchanted crystal or listened for voices in the wind, they shone as greenly as beryl under water.

He had one servant, a boy named Declan, who was seventeen years of age and strong in his limbs, though timid among invisible things. Declan fetched water, cut turf, trapped fish, cooked porridge, swept the chamber, and rowed twice every month to the mainland for meal and salt. He prayed at morning and evening before a little wooden cross which he had set in a niche, and he never passed the ruined chapel without making the sign of salvation.

“Master,” he would say, “I do not fear the dead monks, for they were holy men; nor do I fear the angels, for they are the servants of God; but I fear the little men of the Sidhe, and the tall lake-dwellers, and those ancient ones whom you call out of the hills.”

“Because you are young,” Aengus would answer. “Youth fears what it cannot name, manhood despises it, and age seeks it with tears, knowing that visible things are but painted curtains hung before eternity.”

“Yet curtains keep out the cold,” said Declan, and he heaped another sod upon the fire.

The old man smiled, for he loved the boy.

Many strange guests came to the monastery. There came a woman with golden feathers in her hair, who neither ate nor spoke while the sun was above the horizon; a black fisher whose wet feet left no mark upon the floor; three little men in russet coats, carrying a lantern in which a green moth burned without being consumed; and once, in winter, a pale child who sat beside the hearth and caused every flame to bend towards her as rushes bend before a stream.

Declan served them all, though his hands trembled. When they had gone, he sprinkled holy water upon the threshold.

One evening in May, after the sun had gone down in a splendour of crimson islands and purple seas, Aengus called the boy to him. The chamber was dim, but through the window the lake shone like a shield of dark opal, and above the reeds a single glow-worm moved, now spreading out its little flame and now gathering it close, so that among all the fixed and precious colours of the night it alone seemed alive and perishable, as mortal hope.

“Declan,” said the old man, “the labour of my life is at its end.”

The boy looked eagerly at the shelves.

“Have you found the book that was lost when the Danes burned Clonmacnoise?”

“I have found what no book could give me.”

“Is it treasure, master?”

“It is more than treasure.”

“Then will you buy me the field beside my mother’s cottage?”

“I will buy it no longer, because you shall buy it yourself. In the leathern wallet beneath my pillow are thirty gold pieces. They are yours when my work is accomplished.”

Declan’s face brightened.

“With thirty pieces I may have the field and two cows, and perhaps a horse when the years are good. Máire’s father would not refuse me then. We would build a room beyond the chimney, and she would plant rosemary under the window, and there would be children—if God so willed it.”

“There would be children,” repeated Aengus slowly, “and youth would become manhood, and manhood age, and the children would stand about your bed while the light left your eyes.”

Declan’s gladness faded.

“It is the way appointed.”

“It is the way of beasts, birds, and men; but there are powers who have no part in it.”

He rose with difficulty and took from a chest a small vessel of pale bronze. When he lifted the lid, a fragrance filled the chamber, like roses blooming far off in rain.

“Last night,” he said, “while you slept, I burned in this vessel the leaves of the nine hazels that grow about the Well of Connla, and I spoke the names which may be uttered only when moonlight lies upon untroubled water. Then a woman of the Sidhe stood where you are standing. Her dress was blue, yet greener than the sea in its folds, and her hair moved continually, though there was no wind. She told me that shortly after tomorrow’s dawn the Immortal Powers will sing together.”

Declan crossed himself.

“I have heard fairy music from the hill, and it drew a neighbour’s son into the bog.”

“That was no more than the piping of their shepherds. This is the first music, the song that sounded before the stars were scattered and before the sun was set in its golden house. Whosoever hears it with knowledge shall cast off age as a serpent casts its withered skin.”

“And become young?”

“Young beyond youth. I shall have the swiftness of a deer, the strength of a king in battle, and a face upon which sorrow has written nothing. I shall go among the Immortal Powers, and no frost shall stiffen me, no sickness waste me, no grave close over me.”

“It were better,” said Declan timidly, “to hear the bells at Mass and be content.”

Aengus lifted his head.

“Content! I have watched men content themselves with a warm hearth, a full dish, a woman’s kiss, and children playing among the rushes. Then the hair whitened; the hand shook; the beloved face went into darkness; and all their contentment became a story told for one winter and forgotten in the spring. I have not given sixty years to fasting and vigil that I might end under a stone bearing a name the rain will wash away.”

“But, master, those who pray—”

“Pray, then, and be blessed in your fashion. I have summoned Lugh from a wheel of fire; I have spoken with the daughters of Manannán under the lake; I have seen the Dagda’s harp hanging in a hall where the years go backward. Shall I turn from the threshold because a boy is afraid?”

Declan bowed his head.

“What must I do?”

“Fill this chamber before dawn with green boughs and rushes, and gather red roses and white lilies, for such things are beloved of the singers. Lay the boughs upon the walls, strew the rushes upon the floor, and set the flowers beside me. Then go to the eastern shore and remain there until the rim of the sun is above the water. Return when every bird is singing.”

Though fear moved like cold water about his heart, Declan obeyed. He crossed to the mainland in the little curragh, while the moon hung above the hills like a lamp of pearl. From the woods he cut hazel, willow, and quicken; from a marshy meadow he gathered armfuls of sweet rushes; and from the garden of an abandoned grange he took ruby-coloured roses and lilies as pale as the hands of saints carved upon a tomb.

All night he laboured. He fixed the boughs against the cracked walls until the chamber seemed a green house beneath the sea. He strewed the rushes thickly, and their clean fragrance mingled with the roses. The lilies he placed in a shallow silver dish before Aengus, who sat motionless in his great chair, wearing a robe that had once been purple but was now no brighter than dust.

When all was ready, the old man put his hand upon Declan’s head.

“You have served faithfully.”

“I have been afraid faithfully,” said the boy, attempting a smile.

“There is greater courage in that than in many battles. Take the wallet when you return. Beside it you will find my staff of hazel. Carry it on your wedding day, and no spiteful thing will cross your path.”

“Will you not bless my children yourself?”

Aengus looked towards the paling window.

“When you return, I shall have passed beyond blessing and need.”

Declan went out sorrowfully. He descended through the cloister, where the ivy shivered though the air was still, and walked to the eastern margin of the island. There he knelt beside a thorn tree and said the prayers his mother had taught him. Behind him the monastery lay black against the sky; before him the water changed from jet to blue, from blue to violet, and from violet to the tender grey of a dove’s breast.

A lark rose from the grass. A blackbird called from the quicken trees. Then the reeds whispered together, though no wind had yet come over the lake. One by one the birds awakened, until every hawthorn, meadow, and wet stone seemed to have found a voice. Their music was not solemn, nor terrible, nor such as might shake the stars from heaven; it was near and multitudinous, full of hunger, delight, labour, courtship, warning, and praise.

The sun appeared.

Its first beam touched the monastery window, and Declan sprang up.

“He has heard it!” he cried. “He is young!”

He ran through grass jeweled with dew, across the broken cloister, and into the chamber. The green boughs trembled in the morning light; the rushes shone like threads of green silk; the roses burned upon the floor; and the lilies had opened their white cups.

Aengus sat in the great chair.

His head had fallen upon his breast. In one hand he held a rose, and in the other a lily, their crushed petals mingling red and white upon his robe. His face had not become smooth, nor had the brown of age left it. It was quiet and very old.

“Master,” whispered Declan.

He touched the thin hand. It was cold.

Upon the table lay the leathern wallet and the hazel staff, as the old man had promised. Declan took neither at first, but knelt and prayed for the soul of Aengus, son of Forbis, who had sought wisdom beneath hills and waters, among gods and fairy people, in youth and manhood and age, and had not found the simple door through which every beggar might pass.

When his prayer was ended, he closed the old man’s eyes.

“You spent your life asking immortality from those who could not give you life,” he said, “and all the while there were the sun and the fields, bread after labour, children yet unborn, and the mercy of God. Right well I know that I am no scholar; yet it seems to me you looked so long beyond the world that you did not see the world.”

He took up the wallet and staff, and went to prepare a grave in the ruined chapel, among the holy men whom he did not fear.

Outside, a thrush had perched upon the highest quicken bough. It poured forth a song so clear and abundant that the lake, the stones, the green leaves, and the frail flowers seemed to listen; and whether it sang of mortal mornings or Immortal Powers, Declan could not tell.

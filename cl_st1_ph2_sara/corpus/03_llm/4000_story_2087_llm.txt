When Mrs. Mooney obtained her separation, there was little left to divide except blame.

Her husband had inherited a provision shop near Thomas Street, with two marble counters, a brass scale, and a painted board over the door showing a red bullock. For a time he had been considered a sound man. He wore a clean apron, subscribed to the trade picnic, and could judge bacon by pressing it with one finger. Then he began to drink after closing. He borrowed from the receipts, abused customers, bought doubtful horses, and once pursued his wife through the shop with the long knife used for cutting rashers. A constable from Francis Street took the knife from him.

After that, Mrs. Mooney went to the priest, the solicitor, and the owner of the premises. In each place she told the facts in the same firm order. Her husband was put out of the business, and she was allowed a weekly sum which arrived irregularly and ceased altogether when he found employment as a bailiff’s assistant. He went about the city with a red nose and a cracked bowler, complaining that his home had been stolen from him.

Mrs. Mooney did not complain. She sold the shop fittings and took a large, discoloured house in Lower Dorset Street, where she established a boarding house for young men.

The house had a narrow hall smelling of boiled cabbage and damp overcoats. On the parlour mantelpiece stood a clock with gilt horses which lost eleven minutes every day. The dining room contained a long table, eighteen bentwood chairs, and a piano whose lower notes had gone soft. The lodgers paid fifteen shillings a week for breakfast, dinner, and a meat tea on Sundays. A chop cost extra. Boots were left outside bedroom doors on Saturday nights and returned on Sunday morning with polish in the cracks.

Most of the young men were clerks from warehouses off the quays, assistants in drapers’ shops, or travellers who came and went with leather sample cases. They called Mrs. Mooney “the Captain.” She knew who was behind with his money, who received letters in a woman’s hand, who took porter in his room, and who had pawned his good coat until quarter day. Yet she did not trouble a man for every small fault. She understood that young fellows required liberty, provided the liberty was paid for and did not disturb the house.

On Sunday evenings there was music in the parlour. Mr. Keegan, a law clerk with a high forehead, played waltzes; little O’Rourke from the seed merchants sang comic songs; and Jack Mooney supplied the choruses in a voice like a public-house door being dragged over stones.

Jack was his mother’s son in loyalty, though not in prudence. He worked at a bottling store near the docks and had a square head, short arms, and knuckles flattened by fighting. He spoke freely of what he would do to any man who insulted his sister. There was a story that he had broken a porter’s tooth outside a music hall because the porter had winked at Polly. Jack never denied the story. He merely asked which tooth.

Polly was nineteen when the boarding house opened and nearly twenty-two when her mother began to watch her seriously. She had pale brown hair that escaped from its pins, grey eyes, and a little red mouth which seemed formed for secrets. At first Mrs. Mooney placed her in a corn-factor’s office, copying invoices for twelve shillings a week. But Polly’s father began waiting outside the office on paydays, sometimes sober and tearful, sometimes drunk and violent. After he shouted through the letter slit that he had come for his own flesh and blood, the manager wrote to Mrs. Mooney.

So Polly remained at home.

She served breakfast, answered the door, and entertained the boarders in the evenings. She was not a skilled singer, but she sang songs about soldiers departing for India and girls waiting beside garden walls. While she sang, she looked upward from under her lashes as though the words had surprised her. The young men applauded warmly. More than one offered to turn the pages though there were only two.

Mrs. Mooney observed all this. A boarding house required friendliness. Young men preferred a table where there was a young woman to laugh at their stories and ask whether they took sugar. It kept them from moving to other houses. Still, friendliness had boundaries, and Mrs. Mooney knew those boundaries as accurately as she knew the number of chops delivered by the butcher.

She noticed when Mr. Doran began lingering after breakfast.

He was a clerk in the office of a Catholic wine merchant and had been employed there thirteen years. He was not young, though the careful brushing of his dark hair made him appear younger at first sight. His glasses were rimless; his cuffs were always clean; he carried an umbrella even when the sky was clear. In his early twenties he had boasted against priests and had once stood in Sackville Street arguing that religion was an arrangement between fear and property. But time, employment, and two illnesses had moderated him. He attended Mass, kept his accounts, and sent a Christmas card to his employer.

He had lived in the house for nearly three years.

At first he treated Polly as a child. He asked whether she had been to the pantomime and brought her a paper bag of sugared almonds after a commercial dinner. Then he began correcting her pronunciation when she read the evening paper aloud. She would repeat the word, laughing at herself, and he would laugh too. Presently they had private jokes. If Mrs. Mooney entered the room, the jokes ceased, though the smiles remained.

Mrs. Mooney waited.

She saw Polly carrying hot water upstairs at an hour when no hot water had been requested. She saw Mr. Doran’s hand withdraw from the kitchen doorway. One evening, returning from Benediction, she found them alone in the parlour, Polly seated at the piano and Mr. Doran standing too near the stool. Neither appeared confused enough.

Still she waited.

A foolish mother might have made an uproar at the first sign and sent the man out of the house. That would have left the girl with whispers and no result. A prudent woman allowed matters to declare themselves. Mrs. Mooney had raised two children, conducted a business, faced a drunken husband, and collected debts from men who could not meet her eye. She knew that indignation, to be useful, must be properly timed.

On the last Sunday in June, after breakfast, she called Polly into her room and shut the door.

Outside, the bells of St. George’s sounded over the roofs. A tram clanged at the end of the street. In the dining room Jack was telling someone that a Dane could carry two barrels at once, though nobody had asked him.

Mrs. Mooney sat beside the washstand. Polly remained by the door.

“Well?” her mother said.

Polly looked down.

There were questions, tears, denials that altered into admissions, and admissions softened by assurances. He had been kind. She had not meant anything wrong. They loved one another. He had said he would always be her friend. No, he had not exactly spoken about marriage. Not exactly.

Mrs. Mooney listened without interrupting. When Polly began to cry, she handed her a folded handkerchief.

“And how long?”

Polly whispered the answer.

Mrs. Mooney’s face did not change. The affair had advanced farther than she had supposed, but not farther than she could use. A line had been crossed, and society, which ignored many things provided they remained uncertain, would insist upon a payment once certainty was produced.

There must be reparation.

Mr. Doran was not a boy who could disappear to Liverpool on Monday morning. He held a respectable position. His employer, Mr. Brennan, contributed to church funds and had two sons being educated by the Jesuits. A scandal in the office would be fatal. Thirteen years of diligence might be rubbed out by a few sentences spoken in the proper ear.

Moreover, Mr. Doran was a religious man now, at least when frightened. His conscience had become more active as his prospects improved. Mrs. Mooney had seen him returning from early Mass with his prayer book beneath his arm. A man who feared for his soul and his salary was not difficult to bring to reason.

Polly had no money, certainly. Her education was ordinary, and her father was a disgrace. But she was young, pretty, healthy, and affectionate. No man could say she had pursued him beyond his will. He had lived under her mother’s roof. He had eaten her food, accepted her trust, and then behaved dishonourably.

There must be reparation.

Mrs. Mooney sent Polly upstairs and ordered the servant to clear breakfast. She remained in the dining room, watching the girl carry away the dishes. Two flies crawled about a stain of marmalade. Through the open window came the warm smell of dust and horse dung. She glanced at the clock.

It was seventeen minutes past eleven.

Mr. Doran would soon come down.

Upstairs, he sat on the edge of his bed in his shirtsleeves. His breakfast lay heavily inside him: fried bread, two sausages, and tea gone lukewarm while Jack argued about the Dane. He had tried to shave but had cut his chin. A small piece of tissue remained stuck there, trembling whenever he breathed.

He had been to confession on Saturday evening.

The priest, having heard him in silence, had made the sin appear definite and ugly. There had been questions about occasions, intentions, promises. The little shutter between them had not protected him. When he emerged, the church seemed full of watchful faces, though only three old women knelt before the side altar.

What was he to do?

He took off his glasses and polished them. At once the room dissolved: the iron bedstead, the flowered jug, the cheap holy picture above the grate. When he put them on again, every object accused him by becoming clear.

Marriage. The word had followed him all night.

He was fond of Polly. That was true. He remembered her coming to his door with a candle during the storm in April, saying the landing window would not shut. Her hair had been loose. She had worn a blue flannel dressing gown, and her bare feet made no sound on the boards. Later she had rested her cheek against his shirt and said she felt safe with him.

But was fondness enough for a lifetime?

Her grammar was careless. She said “seen” for “saw” and called a conservatory a conservatree. What would his colleagues think? What would Mr. Brennan’s married daughter think if they met after Mass? And the family: a drunken father, a brother who fought dock labourers, a mother who kept a house full of single men. People would understand at once. They would smile. They would say Doran had been caught.

Caught. The word sickened him because it fitted so easily.

Had Mrs. Mooney known? Of course she had known. Nothing entered or left the house without passing through those cold grey eyes. She could identify a lodger by his tread on the stairs and tell, from the sound of a coin laid on the hall table, whether it was a penny or a halfpenny. She had permitted the meetings, the whispering, the late candles. And now she would sit below, broad and composed, prepared to discuss honour as if balancing the butcher’s account.

Yet what defence had he?

He could not say Polly had tempted him. Even if she had—what man would confess that? She was younger. She trusted him. She had cried against his shoulder only that morning and said she would drown herself in the canal if he abandoned her. Perhaps she had meant it. At the time her grief had seemed terrible. Now, alone, he wondered whether the threat belonged to some song she sang.

He stood and fastened his collar. His fingers shook.

He might leave. His case was beneath the bed. He could pack in ten minutes, take the tram to the North Wall, and be in England by nightfall. But Mr. Brennan would receive a letter. The house would talk. Jack would look for him. And flight itself would be a confession.

His mind travelled through scenes of ruin with the precision of a railway timetable. Monday: a summons to the private office. Tuesday: dismissal. Wednesday: men in the warehouse lowering their voices as he passed. Sunday: his usual pew, empty. He imagined his name spoken by people who had never noticed him before. A clerk’s reputation was thin glass; once cracked, no amount of polishing concealed the line.

What was he to do?

A knock sounded.

“Mr. Doran?” called the servant. “Mrs. Mooney says she’ll see you in the parlour, sir.”

“Yes. Very good.”

His voice came out formally, as though he were answering business correspondence.

He put on his coat. The tissue dropped from his chin, leaving a bead of blood. At the mirror he dabbed it away and regarded his face. It seemed older than it had on Saturday morning. There were soft pouches beneath the eyes, and the moustache he had once considered distinguished now looked merely anxious.

On the landing Polly waited.

She had been crying. Her eyelids were pink and her mouth trembled when she saw him. She wore her Sunday blouse, the cream one with small pearl buttons. For an instant pity overcame his suspicion. She seemed so young, standing in the dim passage with her hands joined.

“Oh, Bob,” she whispered, “what is going to happen?”

He could not answer.

She caught his sleeve.

“You won’t let them disgrace me, will you?”

“No. No, of course not.”

“And you do care for me?”

“Yes.”

“Say it.”

“I care for you, Polly.”

She pressed her face against him. The scent of her soap, cheap violet, returned him to those evenings when the whole house had slept and the ticking clock below seemed to cover every movement. He remembered the warmth of her arm, the small frightened laugh with which she had first entered his room, the tenderness that had followed desire and had appeared, in darkness, indistinguishable from love.

“It will be all right,” she said. “Never fear.”

The words, intended to comfort him, frightened him more.

From below came the scrape of a chair. Mrs. Mooney was ready.

He loosened Polly’s hands and began to descend.

The staircase turned twice before reaching the hall. On the first landing hung a brown photograph of the house as it had appeared under a previous owner. On the second stood an umbrella jar with a crack down its side. Mr. Doran noticed these things with painful exactness, as condemned men were said to notice sunlight on a wall.

Halfway down he met Jack coming up.

Jack had removed his coat and rolled his sleeves above his elbows. A livid scratch marked his jaw. He stepped aside, though there was ample room, and looked at Mr. Doran’s collar.

“Morning,” Jack said.

“Morning.”

Jack smiled without warmth.

“There was a fellow in the bottling stores once,” he said, “that played a dirty trick on a girl. Her brother caught him behind the Custom House.”

Mr. Doran’s hand tightened on the banister.

“Indeed?”

“They carried him home on a door.”

Jack continued upstairs, whistling.

Mr. Doran descended. The parlour door stood ajar. Beyond it Mrs. Mooney sat at the table in her black Sunday dress. A notebook lay before her, though the page was blank. Beside it stood two glasses and a bottle of port kept for respectable occasions.

She raised her head as he entered.

“Good morning, Mr. Doran.”

He closed the door.

Upstairs, Polly returned to his room and sat upon the bed. For a little while she listened. The house conducted sounds badly: a cough travelled clearly, while whole conversations became murmurs. She could distinguish her mother’s low, measured voice and, once, Mr. Doran speaking quickly. Then there was silence.

Her tears dried.

A breeze came through the open window and filled the lace curtain, making it swell brightly into the room before it fell back against the sill. Outside, a barrel-organ began a waltz. Children shouted in the street. Somewhere a woman called down a price to a vegetable seller.

Polly looked at her hands. She imagined a small house in Drumcondra, perhaps, with brass numbers on the door and geraniums in the front window. Bob would return from the office at half past six. She would wear better blouses. On Sundays they would walk after Mass, and the women from the boarding house would see her and wonder how she had managed it.

There would be a dining-room sideboard. A carpet without patches. Perhaps, after a year, a perambulator.

The picture altered and brightened. Bob was promoted. They went to Kingstown for the day. She wore gloves with pearl clasps and held her skirt away from the dust. People addressed her as Mrs. Doran.

She lay back on the pillow. The room no longer seemed dangerous or sad. The warm air softened its edges, and the music from the street moved farther away until it entered her thoughts. Hopes and scenes passed before her without sequence: a ring catching the light, a white curtain, Bob’s grave face bent toward hers, a child asleep beneath a shawl.

She had nearly forgotten why she was waiting when her mother’s voice rose from the foot of the stairs.

“Polly!”

She sat up.

“Polly, come down, dear. Mr. Doran wishes to speak with you.”

Polly smoothed her hair before the mirror, pressed two fingers beneath her eyes, and smiled once to see whether the smile would hold.

Then she went down.

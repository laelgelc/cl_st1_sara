### The Absent Son

We were smoking after dinner in the library of the Marquis de Varenne. Through the half-open windows came the warm breath of the garden, and the lamps, shaded with green silk, cast a peaceful light upon our faces. We had spoken of inheritances, of ungrateful children, and of those family catastrophes which the world discovers only when a will is opened.

Someone declared that mothers forgive everything, whereas sons forgive nothing.

Then Le Brument, the lawyer, who had remained silent, removed his cigar from his lips.

“You remind me,” said he, “of the most painful commission I ever received. I failed in it, although I employed every means known to the law, the police, and private inquiry. Yet I have always believed that my failure was less shameful than the success of the man whom I sought in remaining hidden.”

We drew nearer, and he continued:

“It happened twelve years ago. I was then practicing in Paris, and already possessed a certain reputation in delicate affairs. One morning I received a letter, written in a trembling hand, begging me to call that same day upon Mme. de Courcelles, whose name you may perhaps remember. She was enormously rich, childless in the eyes of the world, and dying.

“I went at four o’clock to her house in the Rue de Lille. The apartment had that elegant simplicity which is more costly than splendor. There were no gilded ornaments, no dazzling colors; but the carpets were thick, the curtains heavy, the chairs so soft that one sank into them noiselessly. Everything seemed arranged to spare a sick person the least shock.

“A servant conducted me into a dim room. At first I could distinguish only a bed and, upon it, a white face surrounded by white hair. Two eyes, still dark and extraordinarily alive, watched me approach.

“‘You are Monsieur Le Brument?’

“‘Yes, madame.’

“‘A lawyer—a man who knows how to keep a secret?’

“‘That is part of my profession.’

“‘And how to find a person?’

“‘Sometimes.’

“She made a gesture of impatience.

“‘Not sometimes! You must find him. I will give you one hundred thousand francs if you bring me news of him before I die; fifty thousand if you discover him afterward and deliver to him what I shall entrust to you.’

“I thought her mind weakened by illness, and perhaps she guessed my suspicion.

“‘My faculties are intact, monsieur. My physician will tell you so, and my notary has prepared the necessary clause. Sit down. I have not much breath, but I have a great deal to say.’

“I seated myself beside the bed. She dismissed the servant and stretched out a thin hand toward me.

“‘I had a son,’ she began. ‘His name was Jean. He disappeared when he was seventeen. That was thirty-two years ago.’

“She stopped. Her fingers closed upon the sheet.

“‘You will ask why I did not find him then. We searched. God knows we searched! But listen, for unless you know everything you cannot speak to him in my name. You must tell him my fault; you must tell him also my punishment.

“‘I was married at nineteen to M. de Courcelles, a good man, much older than I, who treated me with courtesy and indifference. Before my marriage I had loved another—Henri de Marçay. Our families separated us because he possessed nothing, while I possessed too much. He married later, from necessity, a woman whom he did not love. I became a wife, and then a mother.

“‘When my husband died, Jean was four years old. Henri came to offer his condolences. What occurs in the human heart at such moments? Can any one explain such things? I had not seen him for six years, and when he entered the room I felt that the whole buried past had risen before me. I ought to have sent him away. I knew it. I told myself so. But I was alone, frightened by freedom, ignorant of business, surrounded by men who advised me because they desired to profit by me. He alone seemed disinterested; he alone knew the timid girl I had been.

“‘He became my counselor, my friend, and—shall I say it?—almost a father to Jean. My child adored him. Henri taught him to ride, to shoot, to swim; he watched over his studies, corrected his fits of pride, and praised his generous impulses. If Jean was ill, Henri remained beside him all night. If he won a prize, Henri was happier than I. People thought him an old friend of my husband. The world accepts appearances when appearances are properly dressed.

“‘For twelve years we preserved—outwardly—that honorable arrangement. But I was weak, monsieur. I loved him. He loved me with one of those obstinate passions which neither age nor duty extinguishes. His wife lived; divorce was impossible; marriage was impossible; everything was impossible except our love—and therefore we yielded to it.

“‘Do you judge me?’

“I answered that I was there to hear her.

“‘Yes, yes; lawyers hear, priests pardon, and society condemns. But you are a man. You know that there are hours when the whole worldly code of honor weighs less than one look from the person one has loved since girlhood. I was guilty. I do not deny it. Yet I deceived no trusting husband; I robbed no child of affection. On the contrary, Henri loved Jean as though he had been his own son.

“‘One evening—oh! that evening!—Jean had gone to dine with a school companion. Henri came at nine. I remember that it was raining, and each drop struck the windows distinctly. We talked of Jean, always of Jean. Henri had obtained permission for him to enter a military school which he desired. I was so happy that I took Henri’s hands and thanked him. He leaned toward me. I kissed him.

“‘The door was open.

“‘Jean stood there.

“‘He had returned for a book. He looked at us—first at Henri, then at me. His face became frightfully pale. I rose; I called, “Jean!” He stepped backward as if I had tried to strike him.

“‘I ran to him.

“‘“My child, listen! Let me explain!”

“‘He uttered no word. Not one! That silence has sounded in my ears for thirty-two years. He turned, crossed the antechamber, and went out.

“‘Henri followed him immediately. I remained alone, telling myself that they would return together, that Henri would explain our unhappy past, that Jean would understand, perhaps not at once, but later—after an hour, two hours.

“‘At midnight neither had returned.

“‘At one o’clock I sent the servants to the school companion’s house. Jean had not gone back there. At two I heard a carriage stop and believed it was he. I rushed into the hall. It was Henri, drenched with rain, his clothes covered with mud. He had searched the streets, the railway stations, the cafés near the schools. He had found nothing.

“‘Then began that abominable night of waiting. Each sound was his step. Each bell made me leap up. I went from the window to the door, from the door to his room. His lamp was still burning. On his table lay the book for which he had returned. Think of it! He had forgotten the book. He had seen us, and everything else had vanished from his mind.

“‘At dawn Henri went out again. The police were informed discreetly. We questioned his friends, his masters, the servants. At noon a cabman was found who had driven a young man resembling him to the Northern Railway. There the trace ended.

“‘For eight days I neither slept nor ate. After that I remember nothing. They told me later that I had brain fever and that for six weeks I spoke continually to Jean, begging him to come into the room.

“‘When I recovered, Henri was beside my bed. The sight of him filled me with horror—not because I had ceased loving him, but because his face was joined forever to my son’s departure.

“‘I said: “Go.”

“‘He asked where he should go.

“‘“Find Jean. Do not return until you bring him back.”

“‘He bowed his head and left.

“‘I have never seen him since.’

“She closed her eyes. Two tears escaped beneath the lids and moved slowly toward her temples.

“After a moment she resumed:

“‘Henri searched through France, Belgium, England, Germany, America. He employed agents and spent nearly all he possessed. Every day he wrote to me. Every day, monsieur, for twenty years! Sometimes only a line: “No news. I continue.” Sometimes ten pages, telling me of a resemblance pursued in a strange town, of a false name, of a young Frenchman reported dead in a hospital or embarked upon a vessel.

“‘I answered none of those letters. Had I answered, I should have forgiven him; and I had condemned myself never to see him again until Jean returned. Perhaps that was cowardly. We women invent punishments for ourselves and then take pride in enduring them.

“‘Twenty years after the night Jean fled, Henri died at Hamburg. A priest sent me his last letter. It contained these words: “I have not found him. Forgive me for dying before I do.”

“‘And I remained! Why did God take him and leave me? Since then I have waited alone—twelve more years. Thirty-two years in all! I have looked at every man of Jean’s age in the street. I have followed strangers because of a movement of the head, a way of walking. I have sent money to impostors. I have opened thousands of letters with trembling hands.

“‘Now I am dying, and he does not know.

“‘Perhaps he believes that I continued my guilty happiness. Tell him that I never saw Henri again. Tell him that the kiss he surprised was almost the last kiss of my life. Tell him that his room remained unchanged, that each year on his birthday I placed upon the table the gift I would have given him. They are all there—thirty-one gifts; I had not strength to buy the thirty-second.

“‘Tell him I do not ask him to love me. I ask only that he forgive me. No—not even that. Ask him to believe that I loved him more than all else, more than honor, more than Henri, more than my own soul.

“‘Will you tell him?’

“‘I will, madame.’

“‘Swear it.’

“I swore.

“She drew a sealed packet from beneath her pillow. It contained a letter for Jean, her will, and all the information that might aid me. Then she caught my wrist with unexpected force.

“‘Do you believe he is alive?’

“What could I answer?

“‘It is possible,’ I said.

“‘Do you believe he has suffered?’

“‘Certainly.’

“‘As much as I?’

“I remained silent.

“‘No,’ she whispered. ‘No one could have suffered as much. To be guilty, to repent, and to be refused even the right to ask pardon—that is hell. My son, my dear child! How cruel children can be when they believe themselves just!’

“She sank back, exhausted. I left the house with tears upon my face. She died three days later, pronouncing Jean’s name.”

Le Brument paused and relit his cigar, which had gone out.

“For two years,” he continued, “I searched. I followed every indication in Europe and America. I found a sailor who might have been Jean, a soldier killed under another name, a teacher in Quebec, a merchant in Buenos Aires. Each trail ended in uncertainty. Whether he died soon after his flight or whether he still lived, preserving his atrocious silence, I never learned.

“The fee remains unpaid, for I did not fulfill the commission. But I keep the sealed letter. Perhaps one day an old man will enter my office and ask whether Mme. de Courcelles left any message for her son.

“And if he does,” added Le Brument harshly, “I shall give him his mother’s letter. But before I give it to him, I shall look into his eyes and say: ‘You believed yourself a judge. You were only an executioner.’”

No one answered.

Outside, a carriage passed along the quiet street; then all was silent again. And we thought of those respectable houses, closed and peaceful beneath the night, within which some unknown heart was perhaps waiting, year after year, for a footstep that would never return.

### THE WIFE’S VOYAGE

“America!”

The word fell from Edward Marvel’s lips with the strange mingling of hope and despondency with which the captive names freedom while yet conscious of his chains.

He was seated near the window of a humble cottage in Devonshire. The evening was cold and cheerless; rain beat against the narrow panes, and the last light of day, struggling through heavy clouds, touched with a mournful hue the pale face of the young man. Upon the opposite side of the hearth sat Agnes, his wife, engaged in sewing. But her hands had ceased to move. She looked up anxiously.

“America, Edward?” she repeated.

“Yes,” he answered, after a painful pause. “There, at least, a man willing to labour may find labour to perform. Here I have sought employment until application has become humiliation; I have written, waited, pleaded, and been refused. I cannot endure it longer.”

Agnes laid aside her work and approached him.

For nearly two years Edward had struggled to obtain a permanent situation. He possessed education, integrity, and no inconsiderable ability; but misfortune had overtaken the mercantile house with which he had been connected, and from the hour of its failure every avenue appeared closed against him. His small savings had disappeared. Their furniture had been sold article by article, until little remained save what necessity demanded. Worse than all, the rent of their cottage and the plain food upon their table were now supplied by Mr. Elmore, the father of Agnes.

The assistance was freely given. Mr. Elmore was neither wealthy nor harsh; and he loved his daughter too well to regard as a burden what preserved her from want. But Edward’s native independence made each gift a wound. To accept bread for himself was bitter; to receive it from the father of his wife seemed intolerable.

Agnes understood his suffering, though she did not approve the pride from which much of it arose.

“Then we will go to America,” she said gently.

“We!”

A faint colour passed over Edward’s cheek.

“Certainly, dear Edward. Didst thou suppose that I would remain behind?”

“It cannot be, Agnes.”

“It must be.”

“No,” he replied, with forced firmness. “I may encounter poverty, disappointment, perhaps hunger. I may be compelled to perform labour for which neither habit nor strength has prepared me. Would you share all this?”

“Would I remain here while thou didst share it alone?”

“You are tenderly nurtured. The voyage itself may be perilous. The new country may prove less prosperous than report describes it. I cannot expose you to such privation.”

The gentle girl placed her hand upon his arm.

“Edward, when I became thy wife, did I promise to walk beside thee only while the road was smooth? Where thou goest I will go. Thy home shall be my home; thy trials shall be my trials; and if sorrow awaits thee, then by thy side is the place in which sorrow must find me.”

Her words touched him deeply; yet the very tenderness which prompted them strengthened his resolution to protect her.

“You speak from affection, dear Agnes, not from experience. A husband must sometimes decide for both.”

“And a wife,” she answered, while tears gathered in her eyes, “must sometimes remind her husband that marriage has made them one.”

Edward rose and paced the room. Long did the conflict continue between love and misguided independence; but at length he stopped before her.

“I will make one last effort in London,” he said. “If I fail there, we shall speak of America again. Meanwhile you will remain with your father.”

Agnes regarded him with eager suspense.

“And if success attends you?”

“Then I shall send for you immediately.”

“And if it does not?”

He turned away.

“Then—God direct us!”

Three days later Edward departed for London. Cheerless was the morning of separation. Mr. Elmore attempted to speak hopefully; Agnes attempted to smile; but a fearful presentiment weighed upon her heart. Again and again she entreated her husband to write fully and conceal nothing.

“Remember,” she said at the little garden gate, clinging to his arm, “there is no misfortune so terrible as uncertainty. Tell me all.”

“All,” he promised.

“And do not leave England without me.”

His eyes sank beneath hers.

“I will act for the best, Agnes.”

“That is not a promise.”

The coach was approaching. Edward embraced her hurriedly.

“Farewell, my beloved! Trust me—trust my love, if not my judgment.”

Before she could reply, he had entered the coach. She watched it until the rain and distance hid it from sight; then, covering her face, she wept as though some voice had whispered that the separation would be longer and more perilous than either dared confess.

In London Edward renewed his exertions. From morning until evening he passed through crowded streets, ascended dark staircases, waited in cold offices, and presented letters which were read carelessly or returned with formal expressions of regret. One employer found him too highly educated; another feared that he lacked practical habits; a third had already bestowed the vacancy upon a relative; while many dismissed him with that brief civility which, though it cannot be called unkind, falls heart-crushingly upon the unfortunate.

A fortnight passed. His little store diminished daily.

At last, after receiving a refusal from the only quarter in which hope remained, he stood upon London Bridge and gazed down at the turbid river. Men pressed around him, intent upon their occupations. Carts rolled heavily by; vessels moved beneath the arches; on every side appeared the activity of prosperous life, yet for him there seemed neither place nor purpose.

“America!” he murmured again.

That evening he learned that a sailing packet would leave Liverpool within two days for New York. The passage was inexpensive; indeed, so inexpensive that, after paying for it, he would possess scarcely enough to meet his first necessities on landing. But delay had become more dreadful than danger.

He wrote to Agnes.

Long, tender, and hopeful was the letter. He told her that circumstances compelled immediate departure; that his heart rebelled against exposing her to the discomforts of a crowded packet; that America was a land of promise, where energy could hardly fail to command success; and that, once established, his first joy would be to summon her to his side.

He did not state the name of his vessel. He feared that she might yet attempt to accompany him.

When Agnes received the letter, she read it once in silence, then pressed it to her heart.

“Gone!” she exclaimed. “Gone alone!”

Mr. Elmore found her a few minutes afterward, pale but composed.

“My child,” he said, when he had read Edward’s communication, “you must be patient. Your husband has acted rashly, perhaps; but his motive was affection.”

“Affection should not separate those whom God has joined,” she replied. “Father, I must follow him.”

“Follow him! Across the Atlantic?”

“Yes.”

“You do not know upon what vessel he has sailed.”

“I can learn.”

“You may not reach him for months.”

“I shall be nearer to him than if I remain here.”

Mr. Elmore looked upon the tender young creature in amazement. Her frame trembled, but her purpose was immovable. He represented the dangers of the sea, the difficulties of arriving alone in a foreign city, the possibility that Edward might have left New York before she reached it. To each objection Agnes listened respectfully; yet when all had been spoken, she answered only:

“He is alone, and may need me.”

The father’s resistance yielded. Perhaps he recognised in her resolution something higher than impulse—a devotion which Providence itself might employ for good. He supplied her with what money he could spare, and accompanied her to Liverpool. There they discovered the packet upon which Edward had sailed and learned that a steamship, departing the following morning, would probably arrive several days before it.

This intelligence seemed to Agnes a direct encouragement from Heaven.

“I shall be there to receive him!” she cried with enthusiasm.

The voyage was rough, but comparatively rapid. Often, while the vessel rose upon the dark waves and shuddered beneath their violence, Agnes lay awake in her narrow berth, picturing Edward exposed upon the same vast ocean in a smaller and less secure ship. Yet fear for herself scarcely entered her mind. She thought only of him—of his surprise, his reproach, his joy. At times she smiled; at times the tears stole silently over her cheeks.

New York at last appeared, stretching along its crowded harbour beneath the clear light of morning. To Agnes, weary and anxious, its forest of masts and busy wharves seemed the threshold of a new existence. America! The land upon which Edward had fixed his hopes now lay before her; yet he was still upon the ocean.

Through the kindness of a family with whom she had become acquainted during the voyage, she obtained a temporary home. Mr. and Mrs. Harland, though strangers when the passage began, had soon been won by the simplicity and devotion of the young wife. They aided her in making inquiries respecting the expected packet and would not permit her to await its arrival unattended.

Day followed day.

Each morning Agnes visited the shipping office; each evening she returned with the same report—no tidings. A storm had swept the coast, and grave apprehensions were expressed for several vessels. Sadly was her health beginning to suffer under prolonged suspense. Her cheek lost its colour; her eyes grew large and mournful; yet no complaint escaped her.

At length, while she sat one afternoon near the window of Mrs. Harland’s apartment, that lady entered hurriedly.

“The packet has arrived.”

Agnes sprang to her feet.

“Edward!”

“Compose yourself, dear Mrs. Marvel. The vessel encountered severe weather.”

“But he is safe?”

“I do not know. Several passengers were sick during the voyage. Some were removed immediately after landing.”

“Sick! Removed where?”

“To different hospitals, I believe.”

Agnes caught her bonnet and shawl.

“Come, then. Why do we remain here?”

At the shipping office confusion prevailed. Sailors were answering inquiries; trunks and boxes encumbered the floor; emigrants called to one another in many languages. No complete list of the sick had yet been prepared. One officer remembered a young Englishman, but could not recollect his name. Another believed that such a passenger had been taken to a hospital near the northern part of the city. A third declared that all cases of fever had gone elsewhere.

From place to place Agnes hurried, accompanied by Mr. Harland. At the first hospital they had no knowledge of Edward. At the second, a weary attendant examined a register and shook his head.

“None of that name.”

“Look again!” cried Agnes. “Edward Marvel—an Englishman—young, with brown hair and a scar upon his left hand.”

The man looked again.

“None.”

At a third institution they were told that an unknown passenger had been brought in insensible. Agnes’s heart almost ceased to beat.

“Let me see him.”

“You cannot enter the ward.”

“I am his wife.”

“You do not know that he is your husband.”

“I shall know when I see him.”

Her clasped hands, her pale face, and the anguish of her voice moved even the accustomed official. After some hesitation, he summoned a physician.

“The patient is extremely ill,” said the latter. “Excitement may prove injurious.”

“My presence will not excite him,” Agnes pleaded. “I will be silent. Oh, sir, if he should be Edward, and should die while I am kept from him!”

The physician regarded her attentively, then motioned her to follow.

They passed along a white corridor in which every footfall sounded with painful distinctness. At a door near the end, the physician paused.

“Command yourself,” he said.

Agnes entered.

Upon a narrow bed lay a man whose wasted countenance was turned toward the wall. His hair had been cut from his forehead; his eyes were closed; one thin hand rested motionless upon the coverlet. Illness had altered every familiar line, yet love required no second glance.

“Edward! Edward!”

The sufferer moved faintly.

That voice had reached him through the shadows of approaching death. Slowly he turned his head. His sunken eyes opened, wandered, and fixed themselves upon the trembling form beside him.

“Agnes?” he whispered. “No—it is a dream.”

She knelt and took his hand.

“It is Agnes—thy wife! I have come to thee.”

A feeble flush passed over his face.

“Across the sea?”

“Yes.”

“For me?”

“For whom else should I cross it?”

He gazed upon her with an expression in which wonder, remorse, and immeasurable tenderness were blended. Then, with a sigh that seemed to release the burden of many weeks, he murmured:

“My good angel!”

His eyes closed.

“Edward!” shrieked Agnes. “He is dying!”

“No,” said the physician, bending over him. “The change may be salutary rather than injurious. His pulse is stronger. But you must be calm.”

Calm! It was a difficult command; yet for Edward’s sake she obeyed. Hour after hour she sat beside him, cooling his fevered forehead and speaking only when his restless glance sought assurance that she had not vanished. Through the night his life trembled in the balance. More than once the physician feared that he was sinking; but whenever consciousness returned, Edward’s fingers closed faintly upon those of his wife.

Morning came. The danger was not past, but hope had entered the chamber.

The history of Agnes’s voyage was told only when returning strength enabled Edward to hear it. Deep was his emotion as he learned how swiftly she had acted, how she had crossed the ocean alone, how she had searched the strange city while he lay unconscious and unnamed.

“And I thought to spare you suffering,” he said.

“You caused me more by leaving me.”

“Can you forgive me?”

“There is nothing to forgive, if you have learned that I am not a child to be sheltered from every trial, but a wife who has a right to share them.”

Edward pressed her hand to his lips.

Weeks passed, and under faithful care he slowly recovered. The Harlands continued their kindness. Other friends, attracted by Agnes’s gentleness and courage, visited them and supplied every comfort which delicacy could accept. To Edward, however, dependence, though softened by gratitude, remained painful.

One evening, nearly a month after their reunion, he sat by an open window in the Harlands’ house. His face was still pale, and his strength returned slowly. Agnes was beside him, sewing as she had sewn on that mournful evening in England.

“Agnes,” he said despondingly, “what is to become of us?”

She looked up with a smile.

“We are to be happy.”

“Happy! I have no employment, little strength, and scarcely a coin which I can call my own. We are living upon the generosity of strangers.”

“Friends,” corrected Agnes.

“Friends whom we cannot always burden. America has restored my life, but has not yet fulfilled its promise.”

“Perhaps you have not asked what America has been doing while you were ill.”

He regarded her in surprise.

“What do you mean?”

“That Mr. Harland has spoken to a merchant who requires a clerk acquainted with English correspondence. The situation will be ready when you are strong enough to enter it.”

“Agnes!”

“The salary is modest at first, but sufficient for our independence. Mr. Harland has answered for your character, and I—”

“You?”

“I have answered for your industry.”

Edward rose, forgetting his weakness, and clasped her in his arms.

“My angel! You have saved my life; you have found me employment; you have turned despair into hope. What can I call you but my good angel?”

“Call me thy wife,” she answered, resting her head upon his breast. “It is a dearer name.”

“My wife, then—my noble wife!”

“And never again,” she continued, looking earnestly into his face, “never again believe that duty commands thee to face hardship alone. Pride may wear the appearance of courage, Edward, yet lead us into cruel error. A husband cannot move safely onward if he thrusts from his side the faithful heart appointed to sustain him.”

“I know it now.”

“If poverty comes, we will meet it together. If prosperity comes, together we will be thankful. If the ocean itself divides us, I will cross it.”

Edward’s eyes filled with tears.

“It shall divide us no more.”

And it did not. In the land of promise their path was not always smooth, nor was success bestowed without labour; but labour became honourable when shared, and difficulty lost half its terror when each encountered it for the other. Edward’s abilities soon gained recognition, while Agnes made their modest dwelling rich in that domestic peace which no wealth can purchase.

Thus was the independence he had sought at last obtained—not through solitary endurance, but through faithful companionship. For true marriage does not preserve one heart from suffering by condemning the other to suffer alone. It joins courage to tenderness, strength to patience, and hope to love; and where husband and wife walk side by side, even the wilderness may become a home, and a distant shore the beginning of a happier life.

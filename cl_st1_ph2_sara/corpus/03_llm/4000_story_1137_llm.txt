### The Buyer Who Bought Twice

There are working girls, and there are women who work. The distinction is chiefly one of salary, shoes, and whether the manager says “Good morning, Miss Bauer,” or “Here, you.”

Effie Bauer belonged to the second class.

At thirty-eight—thirty-five in electric light, forty-two before breakfast—she was buyer for the women’s underwear department at Feinberg & Roth’s Great Central Store. She knew the moral character of every corset manufactured between Newark and Kalamazoo. She could tell, by laying one thoughtful finger upon a seam, whether a garment would retain its virtue through three washings; and she could look at a travelling representative’s sample case and know, before he had opened it, whether his prices had gone up seven cents.

She earned forty-five dollars a week.

This sum, in the year under consideration, did not place her among the Vanderbilts, but it removed her a long way from the girl who ate crackers behind the ribbon counter and washed out her stockings Saturday night. Effie had a three-room apartment, not counting the kitchenette, where she made coffee and disapproved of cooking. She possessed a mahogany dressing table, a brass bed, twelve pairs of shoes, a seal coat that was nearly seal, and a bathroom in which no other tenant had rights.

Moreover, she had liberty.

If Effie chose to pay six dollars and seventy-five cents for a hat shaped like an overturned soup plate, no masculine citizen demanded an inventory. If she preferred dinner at Maroni’s to liver and onions at home, nobody pushed away his plate and said his mother had understood gravy. When she wanted to sleep crosswise, she slept crosswise. When she bought violet toilet water at a dollar the bottle, the only nose consulted was her own.

So much for independence.

Now bring on the complication.

His name was Gabriel Marks, though no one except the hotel clerks called him Gabriel. He was Gabe to the trade, Marks to the porter, and “that neckwear man” to buyers who had forgotten his card. Four times a year he travelled east with two enormous sample trunks containing the latest inspirations in men’s ties—stripes for the cautious, purple flowers for the reckless, and little blue horseshoes for gentlemen whose wives selected their clothes.

Gabe was forty-four, broad through the waist, shiny upon the crown, and incurably neat. He wore pearl-gray spats in winter and carried a gold toothpick which he never used but frequently displayed. His business card said:

**G. MARKS & CO.  
Distinctive Neckwear for Particular Men**

There was no company. There was Gabe, a stenographer in Chicago, and a nephew who packed badly.

For six years Gabe had taken Effie to dinner on the Thursday evening of each quarterly visit. In six years that makes twenty-four dinners, not including two lunches, one vaudeville performance, and a Sunday afternoon upon which it rained so hard that they sat in the hotel lobby and criticized the furniture.

On the twenty-fifth dinner Gabe proposed marriage.

The occasion was observed at the Café L’Aiglon, where the lights were pink enough to flatter the poultry and the women. Gabe had ordered filet of sole, though he preferred steak, because Effie had once remarked that steak was not a festive food. He waited until the waiter had removed the plates and brought two portions of ice cream wearing paper collars.

Then he leaned forward.

“Effie,” he said, “how long is this foolishness going on?”

She looked up from the ice cream.

“What foolishness?”

“You know. Me coming, you putting on a new dress, us eating fish I don’t like, and then I go to Cincinnati.”

“If you don’t like the fish, why order it?”

“That’s what I mean. Six years I order fish. A man could get anemia.”

Effie smiled. She had a pleasing smile, carefully managed. Smiles, like lace, should not be used in wholesale quantities after thirty-five.

“Well, Gabe, you’re the one with the expense account.”

“Expense account!” He glanced toward the waiter and lowered his voice. “My expense account pays for one seventy-five. This meal, before tips, is four twenty. Look at me, Effie.”

“I’m looking.”

“No, you’re looking at my tie. Forget the tie. It’s a poor seller anyhow.”

She raised her eyes to his face.

Gabe’s face was not a face over which poets would quarrel. It had a blunt nose, sensible ears, and two brown eyes whose expression suggested that they had been waiting patiently while the mouth attended to business.

“I got seven hundred dollars in the bank,” he said. “I make between thirty-eight hundred and forty-five hundred a year, according to whether the South buys silk. I don’t drink, except a highball when a customer makes me. I don’t gamble. My digestion is fair. Will you marry me?”

Effie put down her spoon.

“Gabe!”

“That’s my name.”

“Here? In a restaurant?”

“Where should I do it? In the underwear department?”

She laughed, but Gabe did not. He sat with his hands flat upon the cloth, a salesman who had placed his best line before a difficult buyer.

“Come on, Effie. We ain’t children.”

“That’s just it.”

“What?”

“We aren’t children.”

“I said so.”

“And I heard you.”

“Then say yes.”

Effie’s hand went automatically to the lace at her throat. It was real lace, reduced from nine dollars because of a water mark invisible to anyone not in the business.

“Gabe,” she said, “I like you.”

“Fine. We got a start.”

“I like you as much as any man I know.”

“Better every minute.”

“But marriage—no.”

He blinked.

“What do you mean, no?”

“I mean what no has meant since the first woman got tired of explaining.”

“Maybe it means you got another fellow.”

“Don’t be ridiculous.”

“I’m trying not to. Who is he?”

“There isn’t anyone.”

“Then why no?”

Effie sighed and drew the ice cream toward her again. Serious conversation was no reason to let good food melt.

“Because we’re twenty years too late, Gabe.”

“For what?”

“For beginning.”

“Beginning what?”

“Everything. Saving. Scrimping. Buying furniture one chair at a time. Living in a four-room flat with the elevated railway going through the parlor. Arguing over butcher bills. Wearing last year’s suit because the insurance is due. That’s all very sweet at twenty-two, when you’ve got a waist like a lead pencil and can believe love pays the rent. At our age it’s just poor management.”

Gabe stared at her.

“I make good money.”

“So do I.”

“You wouldn’t have to work.”

“That’s another reason.”

“A reason against?”

“Certainly. What would I do?”

“Do? You’d keep house.”

Effie looked at him with the expression she used when a salesman offered her a reinforced garment with the reinforcement missing.

“I have been getting up at seven-thirty for nineteen years,” she said. “I have hired girls, fired girls, opened four new departments, and made three manufacturers change their patterns. Last Christmas I sold eighty thousand dollars’ worth of merchandise in eighteen days. And you think I’m going to spend Tuesday afternoon wondering whether you want cabbage?”

“I don’t care for cabbage.”

“Potatoes, then.”

“I like potatoes.”

“There! It’s begun already.”

Gabe pushed his ice cream away.

“You talk like marriage is a jail.”

“No. In jail they furnish the clothes.”

“Look here, Effie. You’d have a nice home. I’d get off the road after a while.”

“With what?”

“I could go in the office.”

“At less money.”

“Maybe.”

“And there’d be rent, groceries, laundry, furniture, doctor bills. Perhaps children—”

Gabe brightened.

“Sure, children.”

“At our age?”

“My aunt Tillie had one at forty-three.”

“Your aunt Tillie looks like a wardrobe trunk.”

“She’s a fine woman.”

“I didn’t say she wasn’t durable.”

Gabe’s mouth twitched, but he remained loyal to the proposal.

“We could manage.”

“Of course we could manage. That’s what I object to. I’ve managed all my life. At first I managed on seven dollars a week with one good skirt and a landlady who counted the prunes. Then I managed on twelve, then eighteen, then twenty-five. Now I don’t manage. I live.”

She began ticking off her possessions upon gloved fingers.

“I have my own bath. I take taxis when it rains. I buy a good steak and eat half if I want. I go to Atlantic City for two weeks every summer and don’t have to tell anybody what the hotel costs. I can own silk stockings without explaining a run in them. Yesterday I paid eighteen dollars for these pumps.”

Gabe looked under the table.

“For shoes?”

“For pumps.”

“What’s the difference?”

“Eighteen dollars.”

He sat back, wounded by a world in which feet had become so costly.

“Effie, I wouldn’t ask what your shoes cost.”

“Not the first month.”

“Not ever.”

“The first time the gas bill came with a red notice, you’d ask if I couldn’t walk on something cheaper.”

“I’m not a cheap man.”

“No, Gabe. You’re a good man. That’s why I’m telling you the truth.”

He was silent so long that the orchestra reached the end of one selection, received no applause, and began another with a discouraged flourish.

At last he said, “You don’t love me.”

Effie’s smile disappeared.

“Maybe I could,” she said quietly. “If I were twenty-two.”

“What happens at twenty-two?”

“You don’t know arithmetic.”

He paid the check. In front of the restaurant he helped her into a taxi, though the night was clear and the subway entrance stood half a block away.

“Good-by, Effie.”

“Don’t be foolish. You’ll call me next trip.”

“Maybe.”

“You will.”

“Maybe not.”

But he did.

Three months later they ate at a Hungarian restaurant. Three months after that they saw a musical comedy. At Christmas he sent her a silver vanity case, and she sent him two neckties selected without regard to his professional feelings. Neither mentioned marriage.

Thus another year was posted in the ledger.

Then, in March, Effie contracted typhoid.

Illness is a rude costumer. It disregards the line, ignores the complexion, and has never heard that blue is becoming. For three weeks it removed from Effie her waved hair, her careful eyebrows, her straight back, her office voice, her forty-five dollars a week, and all but six uncertain pounds of her body. It left her in a white iron hospital bed looking, as she later said, like something the grocer had forgotten in the cheese case.

At first there were nurses, doctors, thermometers, ice bags, and the grave excitement that accompanies a high fever. Then came convalescence, which is sickness after the audience has gone home.

The nurses had other patients. Mr. Roth sent flowers large enough for a funeral and a card signed by his secretary. The girls from the department visited in pairs, wearing their best collars and speaking cheerfully of things Effie could not imagine caring about. Her cousin Lena came twice, kissed the air above her forehead, and explained that she could not come oftener because the children had whooping cough.

At five o’clock each afternoon the hospital changed character. Doors opened. Elevators groaned. Husbands arrived.

They came clumsily bearing oranges, newspapers, hot-house grapes, and information no sick woman needed. They tracked rain into the corridors. They whispered too loudly. They sat upon hats, dropped gloves, and asked nurses whether the doctor really knew his business. One brought his wife a jar of pickles. Another came straight from work with cold cheeks and machine oil on his cuffs and stood at the bedside turning his cap in his hands.

Effie watched them.

She had always considered husbands in the mass—like cheap umbrellas, useful in bad weather but troublesome to store. Now she began to observe the individual article.

Across the aisle lay a thin woman named Mrs. Brodsky, whose husband visited every evening at six-ten. He was a small tailor with a wet mustache and tired eyes. He never brought flowers. He brought gossip. He told his wife what the baby had spilled, what the landlord had said, how the oldest boy had lost a mitten, and how he himself had burned the soup.

“You should have seen it, Rosie,” he whispered. “A smoke like the building was insured.”

Mrs. Brodsky laughed until the nurse stopped her.

When he left, he tucked the blanket around her feet three times.

Effie had flowers on her table that cost more than the Brodskys’ weekly rent, and suddenly they looked like stock that would not move.

One gray afternoon, after rain had washed the windows and left the city beyond them the color of dishwater, Effie asked the nurse to bring paper.

She wrote to Gabe’s Chicago office.

> Dear Gabe:  
> If you are coming East soon, let me know. I have been very sick, but am better. There is something I want to say about what you asked me last year. Maybe arithmetic is not everything.  
> Effie.

Then she tore it up because “maybe” looked cheap, and wrote another.

> Dear Gabe:  
> I have changed my mind.  
> Effie.

That was better merchandise.

Gabe arrived four days later, though his regular trip was not due for five weeks. He came to Effie’s apartment, where she had been transferred to finish convalescing under the supervision of a day nurse who boiled everything, including conversation.

Effie received him in a lavender kimono with her hair in two thin braids. No buyer at Feinberg & Roth’s had ever seen her in a kimono. Indeed, the store would have doubted that she possessed knees.

Gabe stopped inside the door.

“Good heavens.”

“I know,” said Effie. “Don’t make an affidavit.”

“You look fine.”

“Gabe, I own mirrors.”

“You look—delicate.”

“I look like a boiled chicken.”

“No, no. More color.”

“Green is a color.”

He put down his hat. On the table he placed a paper bag.

“What’s that?”

“Oranges.”

“There are fourteen oranges in the kitchen.”

“These are from Chicago.”

“Do they vote differently?”

Then he saw that she was smiling, and his anxious face relaxed.

He sat beside her, too near at first, then too far away, then halfway between. For a man who sold elegance by the gross, Gabe had little personal supply in moments of emotion.

“So,” he said.

“So.”

“You wrote.”

“I did.”

“You changed your mind.”

“Yes.”

“About what?”

Effie looked sharply at him.

“You know about what.”

“Maybe I know. Maybe a man wants to hear.”

She drew the kimono closer at the throat.

“I was wrong.”

“Once more.”

“Don’t press your luck.”

He grinned.

“Effie, you mean you’ll marry me?”

“If you still want me.”

Gabe’s grin vanished.

“What kind of talk is that?”

“The reasonable kind. I’m thin as a hatpin, my hair’s coming out, and the doctor says I can’t work for another month.”

“You think I’m marrying your hair?”

“I don’t know what you’re marrying.”

“Neither do I, exactly. But I been trying six years.”

He took her hand. It was a hand he knew chiefly in gloves, firm upon restaurant tables or lifted to signal waiters. Now it felt light, dry, and defenseless.

“What happened?” he asked.

Effie turned her face toward the rain-streaked window.

“I found out what it was I’d been buying all these years.”

“What?”

“Things.”

“That’s no discovery.”

“And I found out what I hadn’t bought.”

He waited.

“Suppose,” she said, “I came home tired. Not sick—just tired. Suppose there was somebody there to say I looked bad.”

“I wouldn’t say you looked bad.”

“You did today.”

“I said delicate.”

“Same goods, fancy label. Suppose I had somebody to worry me. Somebody whose shirts I couldn’t find because he’d put them where shirts don’t belong. Somebody coming up the stairs with a step I knew. A key in the door. A wet coat in the hall. Somebody saying, ‘Effie, where’s the aspirin?’ when it was in front of his nose.”

“Usually it is,” Gabe said.

“And perhaps children making a noise over supper. One refusing milk, another kicking the table, and me saying I’d go crazy if they didn’t stop. Then, when they were asleep, I’d go in to see if they were covered.”

Her voice had changed. The department buyer was gone from it; no prices, no percentages, no sharp little pins. She was describing merchandise never displayed in any window.

“I used to think,” she went on, “that a husband meant having a man object. He’d object to the rent, the butcher, my hats, my working, my not working. But lying in that hospital, I thought—what if nobody ever objects because nobody has a right to care? What if you can be sick three weeks and the only person who asks whether you slept is paid to ask?”

Gabe looked down at her hand.

“Effie.”

“Yes?”

“I’d ask for nothing.”

“Don’t be silly. You’d ask where your clean collars were before the wedding rice was swept up.”

“I don’t wear collars separate.”

“Then you’d invent something.”

“Effie, listen. We’ll have a home. Not a palace. A good flat. Maybe near the park. You can work if you want.”

“At first?”

“Always, if you want.”

“And my money?”

“Your money is yours.”

“And my eighteen-dollar pumps?”

He swallowed.

“If they last.”

She laughed—a weak laugh, but real.

He lifted her hand and kissed it. Then, embarrassed by his own gallantry, he patted it twice as though testing a melon.

“When?” he asked.

“June, perhaps.”

“May.”

“I’ll have no hair in May.”

“April.”

“Gabe!”

“All right, June. We’ll go somewhere. Niagara Falls?”

“I have seen water.”

“Atlantic City?”

“I go there to buy stockings.”

“Then you pick. Also—”

“What?”

He cleared his throat.

“For children, if we should be lucky, I got two names I always liked.”

Effie closed her eyes.

“What names?”

“Melvin for a boy. Shirley for a girl.”

“Gabe, those are dreadful.”

“You got better?”

“Hundreds.”

“Good,” he said. “We’re arguing like married people already.”

By the time he left, the rain had stopped. Effie heard his solid tread descending the stairs. At the landing he came back because he had forgotten his hat. Then he came back again because he had forgotten to kiss her good-by.

It was, Effie thought, a very satisfactory beginning.

Six weeks later she returned to Feinberg & Roth’s.

The store received her with electric bulbs, perfume, elevator bells, and twenty-seven persons wanting decisions before she had removed her gloves. Miss Kohn had ordered three hundred dozen of the wrong brassière. The Pittsburgh shipment was short. A representative from the Perfect Form Company had been waiting since ten o’clock and had already offended two clerks. Mr. Roth called Effie “our brave little soldier,” which she disliked, and raised her salary five dollars, which she did not.

By noon color had returned to her cheeks.

By three she had reorganized a counter, rejected a line of French combinations as French only in accent, dictated twelve letters, and reduced a salesman from sixty-five cents a garment to fifty-nine and freight.

At four-thirty she went upstairs to the blouse department to see Miss Lasky.

There, upon a wax figure with no head and an enviable waist, hung a blouse.

It was dark-blue chiffon over flesh-colored silk, embroidered at the collar and cuffs with dull silver thread. The sleeves fell in the new manner; the front achieved carelessness at considerable expense. It was the sort of blouse that caused women to remember appointments in neighborhoods where they did not need to go.

Effie stopped.

“How much?”

Miss Lasky smiled the smile of one buyer who has caught another behaving like a customer.

“Twenty-nine fifty.”

“Robbery.”

“Imported.”

“From where, Hoboken?”

“Look at the handwork.”

Effie looked.

“Twenty-four,” she said.

“Twenty-seven fifty with your discount.”

“I wouldn’t pay twenty-seven fifty for a blouse.”

“No,” said Miss Lasky. “A married woman wouldn’t.”

The words landed softly. Their effect was not soft.

Effie touched the silver embroidery.

A married woman.

She saw a flat near the park. She saw Gabe at the dining table with envelopes arranged before him: rent, gas, insurance, groceries. She saw herself holding the blouse while he tried not to ask what it cost. Because he was good, he would try. Because he was human, he would fail.

Twenty-seven dollars and fifty cents was half a week’s salary.

No—her salary.

Would it remain hers? Men said such things before marriage. Afterward money flowed into one reservoir marked Ours, from which a woman drew with a tin cup and explanations.

She pictured the seal coat wearing another winter’s shine. She pictured passing taxis in the rain. She pictured cabbage, though Gabe did not like cabbage; it had become the vegetable emblem of matrimony.

Then she pictured the hospital corridor at six-ten, Mr. Brodsky’s wet mustache, and a blanket tucked three times over thin feet.

Her hand tightened upon the chiffon.

“Effie,” said Miss Lasky, “it was made for you.”

That is an expression saleswomen use with dangerous success. Nothing is made for anyone; factories are not sentimental. Nevertheless, Effie lifted the blouse from the figure and held it against herself before the mirror.

The woman in the glass was no pale invalid in a lavender kimono. She was erect, powdered, waved, and expertly fitted. Her hat cost twelve dollars. Her gloves were new. Behind her stretched counters full of bright purchasable things, and beneath her feet the great store trembled with commerce.

At that moment an office boy appeared.

“Telegram, Miss Bauer.”

Effie opened it.

> DETAINED CLEVELAND TWO DAYS STOP LOOKED AT FLAT FIVE ROOMS STEAM HEAT FIFTY-FIVE MONTHLY STOP SHALL I TAKE IT LOVE GABE

Fifty-five monthly.

Steam heat.

Five rooms.

Love.

She read it twice. Then she looked at the blouse.

“Have you a telegraph blank?” she asked.

The office boy produced one.

Effie laid it on the glass counter between a display of camisoles and a bowl of artificial roses. She hesitated only over the wording. One must not be cruel; neither should one invite correspondence.

At last she wrote:

> GABE MARKS  
> HOTEL WELLINGTON CLEVELAND OHIO  
> HAVE THOUGHT MATTER OVER ARRANGEMENT IMPRACTICAL PLEASE DO NOT TAKE FLAT LETTER FOLLOWS  
> EFFIE

She folded the blank.

“Send that right away.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

The boy departed at a run, bearing Gabriel Marks’s domestic future past the bargain petticoats and down the employees’ staircase.

Miss Lasky, who had tact enough not to inquire and curiosity enough to suffer, smoothed the blouse upon the counter.

“Shall I wrap it?”

Effie studied the silver cuffs. Outside, five o’clock whistles began calling husbands, wives, and lesser-paid women home. Inside, the lamps shone upon silk, lace, mirrors, and Effie Bauer—buyer, taxpayer, free citizen, and owner of an empty three-room apartment with a private bath.

She took out her purse.

“What sizes does it come in?”

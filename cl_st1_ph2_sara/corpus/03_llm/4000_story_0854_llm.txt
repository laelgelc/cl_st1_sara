### Bibine’s Guardian

The ladies of Saint Philomène had been considering Loka for three Thursdays.

They considered her after prayers, after the minutes, and before the distribution of flannel petticoats. Madame Frobissaint, who was a practical philanthropist, had obtained the girl from a settlement beyond Bayou Choctaw, where no constable went willingly and no priest without a guide. Loka had appeared at her kitchen door one dawn, barefoot, hungry, and carrying nothing but a wet bundle of clothes.

The ladies agreed that she must be rescued. They disagreed only as to who should endure the inconvenience of rescuing her.

“She is strong,” said Madame Frobissaint. “She can work.”

“She looks lazy,” observed another.

“Work cures laziness.”

This maxim pleased everyone. The secretary entered Loka’s name in the book, though she spelled it twice and crossed it out once. Before noon the girl had been assigned to Madame Tontine Padue, who possessed a clean house, a hardworking husband, five acres of garden, and a baby requiring constant attention. Thus benevolence, having completed its business, adjourned for refreshments.

Loka was fifteen, perhaps sixteen. She did not know. She was small, with straight black hair hacked unevenly at the neck, brown skin, high cheekbones, and gray-green eyes that seemed always fixed upon something farther away than the object before them. Her father had been a Choctaw hunter; her mother, a woman of mixed French and African blood, had washed clothes at the logging camp. Both were dead. An uncle had taken Loka in and beaten her when drunk, which was often. She had fled after he threw a kettle at her. She had not stolen the kettle, although Madame Frobissaint repeatedly praised her for this evidence of character.

At the Padues’ house, Tontine looked Loka over from head to foot.

“You know how milk a cow?”

“No, madame.”

“Make bread?”

“No, madame.”

“Wash proper?”

“I wash.”

“That ain’t the same thing. You know your catechism?”

Loka’s gaze traveled beyond Tontine to the pale road, the ditch grass, and a ragged line of cypresses in the distance.

“No, madame.”

Tontine threw up her hands.

“Ma foi! They send me a girl can do nothing.”

Baptiste Padue, repairing a harness under the gallery, laughed softly.

“She can learn, ma femme.”

“She can learn to stare, yes. That she knows already.”

Loka was set to work.

She swept the kitchen, carried water, shelled peas, gathered eggs, shook rugs, scoured pots, peeled potatoes, folded diapers, and picked stones from the rice. She did each task slowly, without protest and without visible interest. When Tontine called, she came. When Tontine scolded, she lowered her eyes. When asked a question, she waited so long before answering that Tontine often supplied the answer herself and then punished Loka for it.

“Your thoughts got to cross the bayou before they reach your tongue?” she demanded.

Sometimes Loka’s mouth moved at one corner, though not quite enough to be called a smile.

Only with Bibine did she quicken.

Bibine was eighteen months old and built like a dumpling. His hair stood up in a yellow brush; his eyes were blue and solemn until he laughed, when his whole person surrendered to the business. He laughed at spoons, dogs, bare toes, and sneezes. He laughed whenever Baptiste removed his hat. He laughed most loudly when Loka set him astride her shoulders and trotted around the kitchen table while Tontine cried, “Put down that child before you crack his skull!”

Bibine did not care that Loka had no catechism. He did not inquire whether she could make bread. He pushed damp fingers into her mouth, slept against her breast, and called her “Lo,” a title he bestowed with greater affection than the charitable ladies had shown in pronouncing her whole name.

Loka made him toys: a man from corncob and string; a boat from cypress bark; a whistle that gave one mournful note before Bibine bit it in two. She knew where the little red lizards slept under the woodpile and how to hold a firefly without crushing it. At dusk she sat on the step with Bibine in her lap and imitated the cry of a whip-poor-will. The bird answered from the thicket. Bibine trembled with delight.

“She’ll teach him nonsense,” Tontine complained.

“Maybe the bird got something useful to say,” Baptiste replied.

“You just as foolish as them two.”

Baptiste accepted this judgment calmly. He was a broad, mild man who worked from before sunrise until dark and had never discovered that labor required him to be angry. Tontine, who worked equally hard, considered anger the proper grease of industry.

On Sundays she instructed Loka in religion. The girl learned slowly. She confused the commandments, forgot the saints, and stared out the window during the account of the Flood.

“You hear me?”

“Yes, madame.”

“Then what I say?”

Loka looked at Bibine, who was trying to fit his foot into a tin cup.

“God send rain.”

“For wickedness!”

“Yes, madame.”

“Remember that.”

But what Loka remembered was rain on palmetto leaves. Rain swelling the bayou until it lifted old logs and turned the paths to shining black ribbons. Rain drumming on the abandoned sugar shed where she had once slept alone and unafraid, wrapped in moss and burlap. No one had told her then that rain was punishment.

Summer entered the parish with a white sky and a noise of insects. Heat pressed upon the roof. The garden drooped. Even Tontine’s voice seemed to travel more slowly through the thick afternoons.

One morning a neighbor came running to say that old Mère Chavan had fallen ill. Tontine packed broth and linen, gave Loka a dozen instructions concerning Bibine, repeated every one of them, and set off down the road.

“You keep him inside. You give his milk at eleven. You don’t let him by the stove. You don’t sleep. You hear?”

“Yes, madame.”

“And no foolishness.”

“No, madame.”

Baptiste had gone to the far field. The hired boy was hauling cane. For the first time since coming to the Padues, Loka was alone in the house.

She completed her work.

She rinsed the breakfast bowls and set them upside down. She swept the crumbs from beneath the table. She folded the washing. She fed Bibine his milk, wiped his chin, changed his diaper, and laid him in the cradle. She moved without haste, yet nothing was forgotten.

Then there was no work left.

The clock ticked. A fly worried the window glass. Bibine slept with one fist open beside his ear.

Loka stood at the back door.

Beyond the yard lay the kitchen garden, the fence, a field burned pale by sun, and beyond that the woods. They were not the woods of Bayou Choctaw, but distance softened the difference. The cypresses rose dark and cool. Trumpet-vine burned among the branches. Somewhere beneath that green roof there would be damp earth, sassafras, pennyroyal, and camomile bruised sweet beneath the foot. There would be springy turf by the water and old trees hollow enough to hide in. There would be turtles sliding from logs, lizards flickering over bark, and birds calling names no catechism had ever taught her.

The woods breathed toward her.

Oh, to go barefoot where the moss drank the heat! To feel mud close cool between the toes! To walk without a voice behind her—Loka, fetch; Loka, scrub; Loka, quicker now! To lie beneath a sycamore and watch the leaves turn their silver sides to the wind. To sleep when sleep came. To wake at the owls. To eat berries from her palm and steal no one’s bread.

Why had she remained?

For a bed? She had slept soundly on pine needles. For food? The bayou gave fish, crawfish, pecans, roots. Because the ladies had written her name in a book? Their ink could not hold her.

She went to the corner where her few belongings were kept. One dress. A comb missing half its teeth. A red handkerchief Baptiste had given her. She tied them into a bundle.

There was no need to return to Bayou Choctaw. She could go north, where no uncle knew her. She could follow the creek until it met the river. She could find people who asked no questions, or find no people at all.

She lifted the latch.

But there was a sound that stopped her.

Bibine had awakened. He stood in the cradle, gripping its rail, his face flushed from sleep.

“Lo,” he said.

Loka did not move.

“Lo!”

He smiled, certain that she belonged wherever he happened to be.

She crossed the room and lifted him. He smelled of milk, warm cotton, and sleep. His arms closed around her neck. For one instant she held him fiercely, almost angrily.

“I got to go,” she whispered.

Bibine patted her cheek.

“You stay here. Maman come.”

“Lo,” he repeated.

She set him in the cradle. He began to cry. Not loudly at first. He watched her with bewilderment, as though waiting for the familiar game in which she vanished behind the door and returned at once. When she did not return, his cry sharpened.

Loka stood outside with her bundle.

She took three steps toward the gate.

Bibine screamed.

She came back, snatched him from the cradle, and pressed his wet face beneath her chin.

“I can’t,” she said. “I can’t leave you cryin’ like that.”

The clock ticked. The fly still worried the glass. The woods breathed.

Loka looked from Bibine to the open door.

“Come, then,” she said. “We take lit’ walk.”

She wrapped bread in a cloth, filled a bottle with milk, settled Bibine on her hip, and went out.

At first she followed the road. Then she slipped through a gap in the fence and crossed the field, keeping to the tall weeds so no neighbor might notice. Bibine bounced against her side and crowed at grasshoppers. At the edge of the wood the earth changed under Loka’s feet. Shade cooled her shoulders. The hard line between her brows softened.

She removed her shoes and hung them by their laces around her neck.

There! The soft ground knew her. Moss rose between her toes. Ferns brushed her calves. She broke a sassafras twig and gave it to Bibine to smell; he tried to eat it. She showed him a green lizard resting flat as a leaf upon a trunk. She whistled to a cardinal, found blackberries along a sunny cut, and crushed one between Bibine’s lips. His face became purple with astonishment.

They went deeper.

Loka did not intend to go far. Every turn disclosed another remembered pleasure: a patch of mint, a fallen tree, a pool where minnows flashed, a curtain of vine hiding a hollow. The woods seemed to open room after room for her, each furnished with something she had missed.

She sat by the creek and dipped Bibine’s feet in the water. He kicked until both were soaked. He laughed at the broken sunlight, at a frog, at his own toes under the stream. Loka laughed too—a quick, clear sound that no one in the Padue house had heard.

When he grew tired, she made a bed of dry moss beneath an oak. Bibine lay with his head upon her arm, gazing up through the leaves.

“Pretty, hein?” she murmured.

“Pitty,” he agreed.

“You don’t tell Maman I brung you.”

“Maman.”

“No. You tell her bird. Tell her water.”

“Lo.”

“Yes. Tell her Lo.”

He fell asleep. Loka watched the leaves sway. For a little while the two halves of her life rested together: the wandering child she had been and the warm, trusting weight beside her. She did not have to choose. Not yet.

At the Padue house, however, choices were being made with violence.

Tontine returned shortly after noon and found the door open, the cradle empty, and Loka’s bundle gone. She called through every room. She ran to the well, the shed, the road. Within ten minutes she had accused Loka of theft, madness, ingratitude, and murder. Within twenty, half the neighbors were searching.

Baptiste came from the field white beneath his sunburn.

“She took his milk,” he said, examining the kitchen.

“What that prove? She took my baby!”

“It prove she think to feed him.”

“Pas possible! You defend her now?”

“I ain’t defending. I’m looking.”

They searched the road toward the bayou. Men rode to the ferry. Women inspected ditches and abandoned cabins. Tontine went from fury to terror and back again, as if each might save her from the other.

By late afternoon thunderheads had gathered in the west. Baptiste stood at the fence, looking toward the woods.

“She was always watching that way,” he said.

He had just called to the others when a figure appeared beyond the field.

Loka came slowly through the weeds, barefoot, her shoes hanging from her neck. Her dress was stained green. Bibine rode upon her shoulders, gripping her hair and singing to himself. A wreath of trumpet-vine sat crookedly on his head.

Tontine gave a cry and ran.

She tore Bibine from Loka, searched him for wounds, kissed him, shook him, and kissed him again. Bibine, delighted by the excitement, seized her nose.

“You wicked girl!” Tontine cried. “You devil! You steal my child and carry him God knows where!”

Loka stood with her arms hanging empty.

“I ain’t steal him.”

“You gone! Your bundle gone!”

“I was comin’ back.”

“You expect me believe that? Baptiste, hitch the wagon. Tonight—this very night—I bring her to Madame Frobissaint. Let those ladies save her someplace else.”

Loka’s face had remained dull beneath accusation. Now it changed.

“No.”

It was the first command she had spoken in that house.

Tontine stared. “What you say?”

“Don’t send me.”

“You should think of that before.”

“I was goin’,” Loka said. The words came slowly, dragged up against shame. “I tied my things. I opened the door. I was goin’ for good.”

“See!” Tontine cried.

“But Bibine call me. He cry. I put him down, but he cry like—” She stopped and rubbed the heel of her hand against her mouth. “I couldn’t leave him. I wanted the wood so bad. Bad like hunger. So I took him lit’ ’broad in the wood. Just little while. I give milk. I keep him good. I bring him back.”

“You call five hours a little while?”

Loka looked at Bibine. He stretched toward her, trumpet flowers nodding over one eye.

“Don’t send me where he ain’t,” she whispered. “I work. I learn prayers. I do quick. Only don’t send me.”

Tontine opened her mouth, but Baptiste stepped between them.

“Enough, ma femme.”

“Enough? She near killed me!”

“She brung him back.”

“She took him!”

“Yes. And if she was bad like you say, she’d have left him here and gone alone. That would be easy. But the baby cried, so she carried her trouble with her.”

Tontine held Bibine tighter.

“You make poetry when you ought to make sense.”

“I make plain sense. You can’t hitch a mule with the gear for a pony and beat him because it don’t fit. You been pulling that girl’s mouth till it bleed, then wondering why she won’t step lively.”

“I never struck her.”

“No. You got other ways.”

Tontine flushed. The neighbors discovered sudden interest in the clouds.

Baptiste turned to Loka.

“You understand you never take Bibine off without telling?”

“Yes, monsieur.”

“You understand his maman got reason to be scared?”

“Yes, monsieur.”

“You want stay?”

Loka could not answer. Bibine answered for her.

“Lo,” he said, leaning so far from his mother’s arms that Tontine had to surrender him or drop him.

Loka caught him. He clasped her neck and crushed the trumpet-vine between them.

Baptiste nodded.

“There your guardian angel, ma femme. Funny kind—fat, sticky, don’t know a prayer. But maybe he keep her straight better than all them ladies’ books.”

Tontine wiped her eyes with the corner of her apron.

“She must wash him before supper,” she said. “He got mud everywhere.”

It was not forgiveness, but in that household it served.

Rain began before dark, sudden and warm. Loka bathed Bibine in a tin basin while he slapped the water over the floor. Tontine complained of the mess, though less sharply than usual. Baptiste sat beneath the gallery, listening to the thunder.

After supper he fixed a hook beside the back door for Loka’s shoes. Tontine announced that on Sunday afternoons, if the work was done, the girl might take Bibine as far as the creek—provided she carried milk, returned before sunset, and told someone first.

Loka said nothing. Her gray-green eyes lifted toward the rain-dark woods.

Then Bibine put a wet hand on her cheek.

She turned back to him, and this time everyone saw her smile.

### The Turning of the Year

NEW Year’s Eve!

The sun had gone down behind a wall of smoke, but the heat remained. It lay over the flats and dead paddocks as though the whole country had been covered with a sheet of hot iron. The leaves of the box trees hung edgeways and still; the creek was a chain of black puddles under steep, cracked banks; and, from the ranges, a brown haze drifted slowly across the sky. Somewhere beyond them the bush was burning.

Nils came along the dusty track with his coat over his arm and his tools on his shoulder. He had walked five miles from the fencing camp. His moleskin trousers were stiff with sweat and dust, his shirt clung between the shoulders, and there was a red mark across his forehead where the hat-band had pressed.

He saw the place before him—a house, or, rather, a hut of slabs and bark, with a skillion leaning from one side and a broken paling fence about it. No light showed except a dull candle-flame through a square of scrim. The yard gate hung by one hinge. A calf was nosing among the ashes near the wood-heap, and two fowls roosted on the handle of an overturned barrow.

Nils stopped.

The milking-bails stood open. An axe lay where it had fallen beside a half-split log. Shirts and small garments, washed and grey before they were dry, sagged on the line. Near the door was a heap of crockery in a kerosene tin, with flies settling on it in the darkness.

He went through the gate and shut it carefully behind him, though there was little use in doing so.

Inside, the kitchen smelt of sour milk, smoke, boiled fat and babies. The clay floor was gritty under his boots. Greasy plates covered the table; a saucepan of burnt porridge stood on the hearth; a billy, black outside and furred white within, hung from a nail. One candle, stuck in the neck of a medicine bottle, threw a yellow light upon slab walls stuffed with rags, a shelf of cracked cups, three school slates, a broken clock and a child’s copy-book spotted with grease.

Nils put down his tools.

“Where’s everybody?”

A thin boy came from the skillion, carrying a baby awkwardly against one hip. He was twelve, though in that light he looked older about the mouth and younger about the eyes. His face was pale under the dirt. Damp hair lay in points on his forehead.

“You’re home, father,” he said.

“So I see. Where’s your mother?”

“In bed. She’s had the headache bad all day.”

“And what’s all this?”

The boy shifted the baby, who whimpered and tugged at his shirt.

“I didn’t get time, father.”

Nils looked at the table again.

“What have you been doing, then?”

“I milked, and fed the poddy, and fetched two loads of wood. Then Nell cut her foot on the tin, and Alf got out by the creek, and baby wouldn’t stop. I made some dinner, but mother couldn’t eat it. I was cleaning up when the fire went out.”

His voice was gentle and matter-of-fact. He turned his head and coughed into his shoulder.

Nils stared at him.

“You crook?”

“No, father. Only the smoke.”

“Give me the baby.”

The boy obeyed with a grateful little movement, as if a weight had been taken not merely from his arms but from his mind. Nils held the child against his dusty shirt. The baby regarded him solemnly, then began to cry.

From the bedroom came a woman’s voice.

“Is that you at last, Nils?”

“Yes.”

“You might come in. I’ve been lying here neglected all day, if it’s any concern of yours.”

He carried the baby to the doorway. The room beyond was close and nearly dark. A strip of torn mosquito-net hung from a hoop above the bed; dirty clothes were heaped upon a box; and a gin-case, swung by ropes from two rafters, served as a cradle. His wife lay among grey sheets in a calico nightdress, one arm over her eyes.

“What’s wrong?” he asked.

“What’s wrong! Listen to him. My head’s splitting, there’s been no peace, no quiet, no one to do the smallest thing for me—and he asks what’s wrong.”

“Have you taken anything?”

“What is there to take? You never think of medicine. Other women have decent homes, and husbands that care whether they live or die.”

The boy stood behind Nils.

“I gave mother the powders, father,” he said.

“You gave me one,” she said sharply. “And cold water with it. He couldn’t even boil the kettle without letting the fire out.”

“I boiled it twice, mother.”

“Don’t answer me! There—now my own children turn against me before their father.”

Nils passed the baby back to the boy and went into the kitchen. He lifted a cup from the table, saw the ring of cold tea and drowned flies in it, and gripped the handle. The china cracked in his hand. For a moment he stood with the pieces pressed together.

Then he wrapped them in an old newspaper and pushed them deep into the ashes.

“Sonny,” he said, “sit down.”

“I’ll wash up first, father.”

“Sit down.”

The boy sat on the end of a stool. Nils wet a rag at the water-bucket and wiped his face. The skin was hot. The boy shut his eyes beneath the rough touch.

“You’ve got fever.”

“I’m all right, father.”

“You’re not.”

“It’ll go off.”

From the bedroom:

“Are you making tea, or am I to lie here without a drink all night?”

Nils filled the billy. There was little water left.

“Where’d you get this?”

“Creek,” said the boy. “The tank’s empty.”

“You carried it?”

“Only two buckets.”

“Only!”

“They weren’t quite full, father.”

Nils set the billy over the coals, split kindling, and blew the red ash until the flame took. The boy watched him. His shoulders had begun to tremble, though the room was hotter than the air outside.

The younger children came in—a girl of seven with a rag tied round her foot, and a smaller boy without trousers. They stood close to their father, staring.

“Had any tea?” Nils asked.

“Tom made us bread sop,” said the girl.

“What did he have?”

She looked at Tom.

“He said he wasn’t hungry.”

“I had some, father,” Tom said quickly.

Nils cut bread from a stale loaf, scraped off the mould and put dripping on it. He made the boy eat. Between bites Tom coughed; afterwards he gathered the plates as if rising by habit.

“Leave them.”

“But they’ll be worse by morning.”

“Leave them, I said.”

His wife called again.

“Nils! Nils, for God’s sake! Must I shout myself into the grave?”

He took her tea. She tasted it and set it on the box.

“Too strong.”

He waited.

“And there’s no sugar.”

“The tin’s empty.”

“Of course it is. Everything’s empty in this place. Other people manage. Other people don’t spend all their lives away from home and come back like a black thunder-cloud because the house isn’t polished for them.”

“I never said a word.”

“You didn’t need to. I saw your face. You think I don’t see. I’ve eyes, though nobody believes I’ve sense. I’ve lain here hearing those children run wild and that boy banging pots till he nearly drove me mad.”

Nils looked toward the kitchen. Tom was putting the little ones to bed on a mattress in the skillion.

“He’s sick,” Nils said.

“Sick! He’s always sick when there’s something to do.”

“He’s done everything today.”

“That’s what he tells you. Naturally you believe him. You always take their part.”

Her voice rose thinly in the heat. The baby woke and cried in the swinging gin-case. Nils lifted it, but she thrust out her arms.

“Give her to me. I suppose I’m still her mother—unless you’ve taught her against me too.”

He gave her the child and went out.

Tom had spread a bag upon the kitchen floor for himself. He lay fully dressed, his knees drawn up, while the candle smoked above him.

“You’re not sleeping there,” Nils said.

“It’s cooler, father.”

Nils went through the back door and pushed it wide. He opened the front door also, and propped both with stones. No wind entered. The darkness outside seemed another room, larger but no freer, full of smoke and the smell of dry grass. Far off, a tree fell in the bush with a muffled crash.

Nils carried Tom’s bag into his own small room. The bed was a frame of saplings, with bags stretched across and a flock mattress flattened hard. He shook the bedding, turned the pillow and damped a cloth for the boy’s forehead.

“I’ll sleep in the kitchen,” he said.

“No, father. You have to go early.”

“That’s my concern.”

Tom sat on the bed, twisting his fingers.

“Mother’ll be angry.”

“Let her.”

The boy glanced toward the wall. Through the slabs came the murmur of his mother talking to the baby, then complaining because the child would not feed.

“Don’t quarrel with her, father.”

“I’m not quarrelling.”

“She gets bad in the head when it’s hot.”

Nils pulled off the boy’s boots. One sock had worn through, and the heel beneath was split and black with dirt.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

“It doesn’t hurt much.”

“You say that about everything.”

Tom smiled faintly. “It mostly doesn’t.”

Nils washed the foot and bound it with a strip torn from an old shirt. On a box by the bed lay the school copy-book he had seen earlier. He picked it up. Several pages were covered with laborious writing; others had sums, corrections and red marks. Across one page the teacher had written: CARELESS. DO AGAIN.

“What’s this?”

“I tried to do it last night.”

“After the work?”

“I have to hand it in.”

Nils turned the leaves. A brown stain ran through them where tea had been spilt. His jaw hardened. He seized the book as if to tear it across, but Tom reached up in alarm.

“Don’t, father! Please. I can dry it.”

Nils looked at the thin hand on his wrist. He closed the book, smoothed its bent cover and set it down.

“I wasn’t going to hurt you, sonny.”

“I know, father.”

A cry came from the bedroom.

“Tom! Where is he? I want him.”

Nils went to the door.

“He’s in bed.”

“I want my book from the kitchen shelf.”

“I’ll get it.”

“You won’t know which one.”

“Tell me.”

“The one with the blue cover—not the dark blue, the faded one. And bring the candle. I can’t lie here in the dark while everybody else makes themselves comfortable.”

Nils found three books, their covers swollen from damp. He carried them in with the candle. She rejected two, then complained that there was not enough light to read.

“If you take the candle,” he said, “the children have none.”

“What do children want with light when they’re asleep? But that’s always the way. Last consideration—always. If I asked for one hour’s comfort it would be too much.”

Tom had risen and was standing behind him, swaying slightly.

“Go back,” Nils said.

“I could hold the candle for mother.”

“You’ll do no such thing.”

“There!” cried the woman. “Hear how he speaks in front of them? Is it any wonder they’ve no respect for me?”

The baby began screaming. The two children in the skillion woke and joined in. For a few minutes the hut was full of sound—the woman’s accusations, the baby’s thin fury, Nell calling for Tom, the smaller boy frightened without knowing why. Nils moved among them without speaking. He settled the little ones, changed the baby’s rag, found the faded book, trimmed the wick and placed the candle on a saucer where its light reached both rooms.

Then silence came unevenly.

The old clock had stopped months before, but near midnight a gun cracked somewhere across the flats. A second answered from the direction of the township. Dogs barked. Someone far away shouted, “Happy New Year!” and a drunken cheer travelled weakly through the smothering dark.

The old year was finished.

Nothing stirred in the hut except a cockroach crossing the shelf and the baby sucking in its sleep.

Nils lay beside Tom, above the blanket so as not to heat him further. The boy’s breath came quickly. After a while his hand moved across the mattress and found his father’s. It rested there, light and dry.

“Father?”

“Yes, sonny?”

“Were you frightened when you were my age?”

“Likely.”

“What of?”

Nils listened to the smoke-heavy stillness.

“Things I’d forgotten by morning.”

Tom was silent.

Then: “Do you have to grow into a man?”

“If you live.”

“I don’t mean—” The boy swallowed. “Does it happen whether you want it or not?”

“Yes.”

“Were you sorry?”

Nils turned his head. In the dimness Tom’s eyes were open.

“Why are you asking?”

“I was thinking. A man has to go away to work. And he has to keep everybody. And if he can’t, they hate him.”

“No one hates you.”

“Mother does sometimes.”

“She doesn’t know what she says.”

“She says I’ll turn out useless. She says I’ll be just another burden till I’m put under the ground.”

The father’s fingers closed over the child’s.

“You mind me. You’re not useless. You’ve done more today than many a grown man.”

Tom gave a troubled sigh.

“But if I grow up, will I get angry like you?”

Nils did not answer at once.

“Maybe,” he said. “But anger isn’t all a man is.”

“I don’t want to die either.”

“You’re not dying.”

“My chest feels queer.”

“That’s fever and smoke.”

“Are you sure, father?”

“Yes.”

Tom accepted the word. His grasp loosened, tightened once, then loosened again. Presently he slept.

Nils did not. He watched the boy’s narrow face while the candle shortened in the next room. Once he rose to wet the cloth. Once he stepped outside, hoping for a change in the air, but heat came from the darkness in breaths like a furnace. The stars were hidden. Ash settled softly on the yard, on the empty tank and the clothes left hanging upon the line.

Before dawn he dressed.

Tom woke when the bed moved.

“You going now, father?”

“I must.”

“I’ll get your breakfast.”

“You’ll stay there.”

“But you can’t walk five miles on nothing.”

“I’ve bread in my bag.”

Nils tucked the blanket around him and stood looking down. There were words in him, but none that would mend a roof, fill a tank, shorten a road or keep a child in bed when cows waited to be milked.

“I’ll be back after dark,” he said.

“Yes, father.”

“Don’t work if you feel bad.”

“No, father.”

Nils knew the promise was useless. He bent, touched the boy’s hair, and left.

The eastern sky was the colour of dirty copper. He crossed the yard, lifted his tools and started along the track toward the fencing camp. His footsteps sounded heavily in the dust, regular and diminishing, as though they were counting out the days already spent and those still to come.

Tom lay still until he could hear them no longer.

Then a cow lowed at the bails.

He rose, pulled on his boots and went outside with the milk-pail. The air was already warm. His head throbbed when he bent beneath the cow, and twice he stopped because the yard moved strangely around him.

From the hut came his mother’s call.

“Tom! Tom, where are you? Bring me some tea.”

“I’m milking, mother.”

“Leave it and come at once. My head’s worse than ever. And bring the baby—she’s been fretting all night. Do you hear me?”

“Yes, mother.”

“And find my book. Not that one you brought last night—the other one. For God’s sake, must I explain everything?”

Tom set down the pail. The cow shifted and kicked dust into the milk.

He went toward the hut, pale in the first light, carrying what remained.

The New Year had begun.

# The Fourth Passenger

I have been urged, more than once, to commit the following narrative to paper; and I have as often refused. There are experiences which a man may relate by his own hearth, to familiar friends, under the cheerful influence of wine and firelight, yet shrink from setting down in that grave black and white which seems at once to challenge disbelief and solicit ridicule. Spoken words vanish with the hour and the company; written testimony remains, to be dissected by strangers, cited against one’s judgment, or explained away by persons who know everything except the facts.

Nevertheless, twenty years have passed since the night of which I speak. I am no longer the impetuous young barrister who crossed the northern moors with a gun on his shoulder and no more fear of darkness than of an adverse jury. My hair is grey at the temples; I have held offices of trust; I have examined witnesses whose lives depended upon the precision of their statements; and I know, perhaps as well as any man, the frailty of memory, the contagious nature of terror, and the innumerable disguises under which imagination presents itself as truth.

Having weighed all this, I declare that what follows is no invention.

I do not ask the reader to believe it. I merely affirm that I saw what I saw, endured what I endured, and rode, for several dreadful miles, in the company of the dead.

It happened in the winter of 18—, within the first year of my marriage. My wife and I were then staying at a small watering-place in the north of England, where she had been ordered for a change of air after an illness. I was young, vigorous, and passionately fond of sport; and as the season had been severe, with abundant game upon the higher lands, I frequently left the town before sunrise and spent the day among the hills.

On the morning in question I set out alone. The weather, though cold, was clear. A pale sky stretched above the moor; the heather showed black-purple between ridges of old snow; and here and there a tarn, hard as polished metal, reflected the colourless light. I carried a light fowling-piece, a flask, and sufficient food for the day. A farmer with whom I was acquainted had directed me towards a remote tract seldom visited by sportsmen, and had warned me, laughingly, not to lose my bearings if mist came down.

Mist did not come. Snow came instead.

At about three in the afternoon, while I was still several miles farther from the town than prudence should have permitted, the wind shifted suddenly to the north-east. The change was so abrupt that I remember turning to look behind me, as if some vast door had opened upon the horizon. A dark bank of cloud had risen there, low, compact, and moving with extraordinary speed. The last weak sunshine was extinguished; the hills became leaden; and before I had crossed the next hollow, the snow was driving almost horizontally across the moor.

I had no immediate alarm. I was, as I have said, young and strong, and had not yet learned how quickly confidence may become folly when weather and wilderness combine against a man. I adjusted my coat, took what I believed to be the southward direction, and pushed forward briskly.

Within half an hour every landmark had disappeared.

The snow did not descend in flakes, but in fine, blinding particles, stinging the face and filling the eyes, ears, and folds of the clothing. The hollows whitened beneath my feet; the heather vanished; the sheep-paths were obliterated. The sky, the ground, and the whirling air appeared composed of one substance. Twice I stumbled into shallow burns concealed by drifted snow, and the second time the water rose above my boots. I climbed a slope expecting to recognise a ridge which I had crossed in the morning, only to find another hollow beyond it, and another ascent dimly visible through the storm.

Still I went on.

The reader who has never been lost in open country may suppose that absence of roads makes no great difference where one may advance in any direction; but a moor in snow is more bewildering than a forest. In a wood, trees, streams, banks, and inequalities offer some measure of place. On the moor all distances deceive. A dark patch which seems a rock may be a tuft of heather ten yards away; a slope which appears near may lie across a mile of bog; and when snow is falling, a man may unconsciously describe a circle until his own footprints, softened and half-filled, seem those of a stranger.

Dusk came before I would acknowledge that I was lost.

I stopped at last beneath a low bank and tried to reason. The sea lay south-east; the town, as nearly as I could calculate, must be fifteen or perhaps eighteen miles distant; farmsteads were scattered along the lower valleys; and if I continued downhill I must eventually meet either a road or running water. This conclusion, though plausible, contained the fatal assumption that I could distinguish downhill from level ground. The wind confused every sensation. One moment I seemed to descend; the next I found myself panting up a rise. My wet boots grew heavy. The lock of my gun was clogged with snow. My fingers became first painful, then curiously dull.

Night gathered.

No star appeared. No cottage lamp glimmered. No dog barked. There was no sound but the wind passing over the waste with a long, hollow cry, and the faint crunch of my own steps.

For the first time I thought of my wife.

She had expected me before five. I saw her, in imagination, standing at the window of our lodging, looking into the street whenever wheels passed; then appealing to the landlady; then sending a messenger to the inn; then enduring, hour after hour, that peculiar anguish of uncertainty which is perhaps more cruel than grief itself. The vision roused me. I forced myself onward, calling occasionally, though I knew that my voice could carry no distance against the gale.

An hour later—I judge the interval only by recollection, for I had no courage to consult my watch—I became conscious of an irresistible desire to lie down.

This desire was not accompanied by fear. On the contrary, it seemed reasonable, almost luxurious. The snow appeared soft; the bank beside me offered shelter; a few minutes’ rest, I thought, would restore warmth to my limbs. I had heard of men dying from exposure in that very fashion, yet the knowledge possessed no authority. My body argued more persuasively than my mind.

I sank upon one knee.

At that instant a gust tore the cloud of snow aside, and far before me, low upon the ground, I saw a light.

It vanished at once.

I rose, uncertain whether it had been real. Then it shone again—a dull, reddish point, unlike a star, and steady despite the storm. A house! The conviction was immediate. I shouted, though no answer came, and stumbled towards it.

The light seemed near, yet I travelled a long while before its nature became clear. It proceeded from an uncurtained window in a building set against the side of a hill. The structure was long and low, built of dark stone, with outbuildings attached at irregular angles. No smoke rose from the chimneys; no yard lamp burned; no sound of cattle came from the sheds. Indeed, but for that single red window, the place might have been abandoned.

A wall emerged from the snow. Feeling along it, I found a gate hanging upon one hinge, crossed a paved yard, and reached a door so massive that it might have belonged to a gaol. It was studded with iron, and above it hung a rusty bell-pull.

I rang.

The sound clanged somewhere within, startlingly loud, and was followed by silence.

I rang again, hammered with the butt of my gun, and called out that I was lost.

At length bolts were withdrawn. The door opened a hand’s breadth, and a narrow face appeared in the interval.

The man who regarded me was perhaps sixty years of age, though hardship may have added to his years. He had coarse grey hair, small, deep-set eyes, and a skin of that weathered brown common among shepherds. A horn lantern hung from his hand. He inspected me without surprise or welcome.

“Who’s tha?” he demanded.

“My name is Murray. I have lost my way on the moor.”

“Where fra’?”

“From the coast. I have been shooting since morning.”

He lifted the lantern and looked past me into the storm.

“Tha’s getten a queer road home.”

“So I am aware. For God’s sake, let me in. I am half frozen.”

He neither moved nor answered.

“Do you intend to leave me here?” I cried.

“Master takes no company.”

“Then tell your master that a traveller is dying at his door.”

This produced some effect. The man’s eyes shifted from my face to my clothes, my gun, and the snow lying thick upon my shoulders.

“Bide there,” he said, and closed the door.

The bolts fell again.

I confess that my anger, for a moment, overcame my weakness. I struck the panels with such violence that the sound echoed through the building; but before I could repeat the assault, the door opened fully. The servant stood aside.

“Come thy ways in. But master’ll not be fashed wi’ questions.”

“I shall ask none if I am given meat, drink, and a place by the fire.”

“Aye,” said he, with a grim contraction of the mouth. “They mostly ask for nowt else—at first.”

I entered.

The door admitted me not into a passage but into a vast, low chamber which appeared to occupy the greater portion of the house. Its roof was crossed by smoke-blackened beams; the floor was paved with uneven flags; and an immense fireplace, sunk in the farther wall, contained a bank of peat from which a deep red glow rather than a flame issued into the room.

At first I could distinguish little. Then, as the servant moved forward with his lantern, objects emerged successively from the gloom, and I began to wonder into what species of household I had been received.

Near the fire stood an oak table burdened with books, papers, retorts, crucibles, coils of wire, and instruments whose purpose I could not guess. A brass telescope pointed through an aperture in the roof. Shelves climbed the walls, crowded partly with folios and partly with glass vessels containing cloudy liquids, minerals, anatomical preparations, and dried plants. There were globes, clocks, magnets, lenses, a human skull, a small electrical machine, and a cabinet filled with labelled bones. Over one chair lay an animal skin; over another, a scholar’s gown. In a corner, a stuffed eagle spread its wings above a heap of rusted machinery. The combined smell of peat, chemicals, old leather, damp stone, and something medicinal was so singular that I stopped involuntarily upon the threshold.

A man rose from the fireside.

He was tall, gaunt, and slightly stooped, with a head of remarkable character. His hair, though entirely white, was abundant; his forehead broad; his nose aquiline; his cheeks thin almost to emaciation. But it was the eyes that fixed attention. They were clear, deeply luminous, and watchful—eyes which might have belonged to a saint, a fanatic, or a madman.

He looked at the servant.

“What is this, Jacob?”

“Gent lost on t’ muir.”

“I told you that no stranger was to be admitted.”

“So did I tell him.”

“And yet he is here.”

“Aye. He’d ha’ died else.”

The master turned to me, and whatever displeasure he felt disappeared.

“You are exhausted, sir.”

“I have been wandering for several hours. If you permit me to dry my clothes and take some food, I shall be obliged to you.”

“You shall do both. Jacob, more peat. Bring bread, meat, and the brandy.”

The servant gave me a look suggesting that such generosity was unprecedented, then shuffled away through a side door.

My host placed a chair for me close to the hearth. He took my gun, assisted me to remove my outer coat, and examined my hands with a knowledge that seemed medical.

“No frostbite,” he said. “Pain will return presently, and with it a temptation to thrust the fingers towards the fire. Resist that temptation. Warmth must come slowly.”

He supplied a basin of cold water, in which he made me immerse my hands, and rubbed my arms until circulation returned. The suffering was acute, but brief. Presently Jacob brought coarse bread, cold mutton, cheese, and a stone bottle of brandy. I have eaten at famous tables since then, but no meal has ever seemed so excellent. The old man watched me without speaking until I had satisfied the first violence of hunger.

“You have saved my life,” I said.

“Jacob opened the door.”

“At your order, I presume.”

“He is not always obedient enough to wait for my orders.”

Jacob, who was replenishing the fire, glanced over his shoulder.

“Dead men pay no rent,” he remarked. “And burying’s hard work when ground’s froze.”

His master frowned.

“You may go.”

“Nay, I’ve work here.”

“You have none.”

Jacob gave the peat an unnecessary thrust, muttered something in dialect, and withdrew.

My host seated himself opposite me.

“You said your name was Murray?”

“James Murray, of the Inner Temple.”

“A barrister?”

“I was called two years ago.”

“Then you are trained to distinguish evidence from assertion.”

“So we like to believe.”

A faint smile touched his face.

“An honest qualification. How far away are you lodged?”

I named the town.

“Twenty miles by the direct road; farther by any route you could have taken across the moor. Does someone expect you?”

“My wife.”

His eyes rested upon me with grave attention.

“You are lately married.”

“Eight months.”

“And she is alone?”

“With our landlady only.”

He rose immediately and crossed to a shelf where several maps were rolled in a leather case. Selecting one, he spread it upon the table and weighted the corners with minerals.

“The main coach road lies three miles north of this house,” he said. “The night mail passes the old crossroad shortly after ten. If Jacob guides you there, you may reach your wife before midnight.”

I looked at the black windows, against which the snow hissed like sand.

“Surely no coach can run in such weather.”

“The mail runs in worse.”

“And you would send your servant out?”

“I would send him to the crossroad, no farther. He knows the moor as a fox knows its earth.”

I thanked him warmly; but, as I had scarcely recovered my strength, and as the hour then lacked some time of nine, he advised me to remain by the fire. Our conversation, which had begun upon practical matters, soon took a direction so remarkable that I must relate a portion of it, not merely because it impressed me deeply at the time, but because subsequent events invested every word with prophetic significance.

My attention had been attracted by a thick manuscript lying open beside the skull. Across its first page was written, in a fine and severe hand: *On the Persistence of Conscious Agency after Physical Dissolution*.

My host noticed my glance.

“You find the title extravagant?”

“I find it unusual in the neighbourhood of galvanic batteries and a telescope.”

“Why? The stars, the electrical fluid, the secret operations of the brain, and the survival of the principle we call life are not separate questions, except to separate minds.”

“You believe, then, in personal survival?”

“I know that consciousness is not so easily extinguished as your material philosophers suppose.”

“That is faith rather than science.”

“Is it? What do you understand by science?”

“The observation of facts, the testing of causes, and the exclusion of whatever cannot be proved.”

He leaned forward, and the firelight sharpened the hollows of his face.

“There speaks your profession. Yet in a court of law you accept testimony concerning events which you have not witnessed.”

“When credible witnesses agree, and when circumstances support them.”

“Precisely. But let ten thousand witnesses in every age testify to apparitions, warnings, voices, second sight, and the visible return of the dead, and your scientific tribunal rejects them before examination. Why? Because the testimony conflicts with an assumption. That is not reason, Mr Murray. It is dogma wearing a laboratory apron.”

I laughed, though civilly.

“Ghosts are poor witnesses. They decline cross-examination.”

“Perhaps because the living ask the wrong questions.”

He spoke without levity. There was in his manner neither the furtive eagerness of the credulous nor the vanity of one who delights in paradox. He had the calm intensity of a man defending a conclusion reached at immense personal cost.

“You have studied these subjects?” I asked.

“For thirty-five years.”

“As philosophy?”

“As fact.”

He rose and moved among the shelves, touching instruments and volumes as he spoke.

“I was once Professor of Natural Philosophy at a university whose name is of no importance. I published upon optics, terrestrial magnetism, and the influence of electrical currents upon nervous tissue. My colleagues praised me while I enlarged what they knew. When I attempted to investigate what they denied, they called me unsound.

“I had observed phenomena in mesmeric subjects which no accepted theory explained. I had received accounts, from men incapable of conscious fraud, of events seen at a distance and deaths announced before the arrival of natural intelligence. I had myself witnessed an apparition—no vapour, no dream, but a defined human figure—which communicated a fact known to no person in the room and verified the following day. I asked that these things be examined. Examination was refused. Ridicule followed. Then censure. Then exile.”

His hand closed upon the back of a chair.

“They drove me from society because I would not deny the evidence of my senses.”

There was bitterness in the words, yet a dignity also. I felt sympathy for him, though not conviction.

“You must admit,” I said, “that the senses may deceive.”

“Certainly.”

“That illness, fatigue, grief, expectation, or terror may generate images?”

“Certainly.”

“That coincidence may appear supernatural when its causes are hidden?”

“Certainly.”

“Then why interpret an unexplained impression as proof of a spirit?”

“I do not. But why interpret every such impression as proof of disease? Because one man, fevered, sees what is not present, must another man, healthy and deliberate, deny what stands before him? The senses are fallible; therefore you would discard them only when they report what you dislike. Yet through what instruments does science itself receive the universe?”

“Repetition supplies a safeguard.”

“Not always. Some events cannot be commanded to repeat themselves. A murder occurs once. An avalanche falls once. A ship founders once. Do you deny them because they cannot be reproduced in a lecture-room?”

“No; but they leave material traces.”

“And if an apparition leaves knowledge where ignorance stood before?”

“Then I should examine the knowledge.”

“But not the apparition?”

“I should reserve judgment.”

“Ah! Reserve—the respectable fortress of disbelief.”

His tone provoked me.

“You mistake caution for cowardice.”

“And you mistake limitation for law. Nature is larger than our methods. There are forces moving about us which our instruments do not measure, as there were colours before eyes opened to behold them, and music before ears existed to receive sound. What you call supernatural may be only natural truth beyond the frontier of present knowledge.”

He lifted the skull from the table and held it between us.

“Does thought inhabit this bone? Does love? Does memory? Break it, and you find no grief, no image of childhood, no moral will. Yet these things exist. If the instrument perish, must the player perish also? Your anatomist says yes because he cannot seize the player with forceps. I say he has dissected the violin and declared music impossible.”

The fire sank. Wind pressed against the house in prolonged, muffled gusts. Somewhere among the instruments a fine wire vibrated with a faint, continuous note.

I should be dishonest if I pretended that the old man’s discourse did not affect me. It did not persuade my reason; but it disturbed that easy confidence with which youth regards all mysteries as problems merely awaiting a convenient solution. The room, the hour, the storm, the strange speaker surrounded by the wreckage of his abandoned studies—all contributed to an influence which I then attributed to imagination, though I cannot now regard it as wholly accidental.

At length Jacob entered, equipped for the journey in a sheepskin coat, high boots, and a cap tied beneath his chin.

“Time,” he said. “Snow’s easing.”

My host looked at a long-cased clock.

“Half past nine. Yes.”

He wrapped my coat, now tolerably dry, about me and insisted that I take his flask. I offered payment, which he refused with a gesture of offended courtesy.

“You may repay me by remembering our conversation.”

“I shall certainly remember your kindness.”

“That was not what I said.”

Our eyes met. For one strange moment it seemed that he would add a warning; but he only held out his hand.

“If you see nothing tonight which your philosophy cannot explain, you will arrive home as you departed. If you see more—be honest.”

Jacob opened the door. A current of icy air swept through the chamber, stirring papers, flames, and shadows. I stepped across the threshold and looked back.

The old scholar stood beneath the lamp, one hand resting upon the skull, his white head shining against the darkness behind him.

Then the door shut, and I was once more upon the moor.

The snow had diminished to a sparse, dry fall, but the wind remained severe. Jacob carried a lantern in one hand and a stout staff in the other. He moved with astonishing speed, selecting a course where I could perceive neither path nor landmark. At times we passed between walls buried to their upper stones; at others we crossed naked ridges where the gale struck us with such force that I was obliged to bend nearly double. The lantern threw a small yellow circle upon the snow, beyond which all was blackness.

For some time neither of us spoke.

“What is your master’s name?” I asked at last.

Jacob appeared not to hear.

“I asked your master’s name.”

“Name’s his own.”

“Of course it is. Has he lived here long?”

“Long enow.”

“Were you with him at the university?”

The old servant gave a short laugh.

“Me? Nay. I’ve nowt to do wi’ book folk.”

“He tells me he was unjustly treated.”

“Mebbe.”

“Do you believe his theories?”

Jacob stopped so suddenly that I almost collided with him.

“What theories?”

“Concerning spirits—the dead appearing to the living.”

He lifted the lantern until its light fell upon my face.

“Best let dead folk bide.”

“That does not answer me.”

“Aye, barrister,” he said, as if the word itself explained my persistence. “Always pecking at a thing till shell cracks. And what then? Mebbe chick’s rotten.”

He resumed his pace.

After another interval we descended into a deep cutting where the wind passed overhead, leaving an oppressive stillness. A black line showed beneath our feet. It was a road, disused but recognisable, with grass growing between the ruts and snow lying in uneven patches along its course.

“Old coach road,” said Jacob.

“Does the mail pass here?”

“Nay. We follow it to crossing.”

The road wound between banks, then climbed gradually. Upon one side rose a wall of rock, stained with water; upon the other, though darkness concealed the depth, the ground appeared to fall steeply away. Broken posts stood at intervals beside the verge.

“What place is this?” I asked.

“No place.”

“It must lead somewhere.”

“Led fra’ High March to west road, once.”

“Why was it abandoned?”

He did not reply.

A few minutes later the wind opened a rent in the cloud. Moonlight, pale and watery, flowed across the road. I saw that we were approaching a sharp bend where a low stone parapet guarded the outer edge. Near its centre several yards of masonry had fallen away, leaving a black gap beyond which there was nothing—no snowfield, no opposite slope, only emptiness.

Jacob caught my arm.

“Keep in.”

“What happened there?”

“Nine year gone come March.”

“What happened?”

“Mail.”

“What mail?”

He drew me along, but I resisted.

“You mean a coach went over?”

“Aye.”

“Were people killed?”

“All.”

“How many?”

“Coachman. Guard. Three inside.”

The wind rose out of the gulf with a sound like breathing.

“How did it happen?”

“Night were black. Horses took fright, they said. Driver pulled wrong. Wheel struck stone. Over she went.”

“And the parapet was never repaired?”

“Once. Fell again.”

This answer, and the manner in which it was given, chilled me more than the wind.

“Does the present mail use the new road?”

“Aye. Crossing’s yonder.”

He pointed ahead. At a distance of perhaps half a mile, where the abandoned way met a broader turnpike, a sign-post stood black against the whitening sky.

We reached it ten minutes later. The storm had passed eastward, leaving broken clouds and a few faint stars. The new road ran north and south, hard beneath a thin covering of snow. There was no house, gate, or shelter at the junction; only the sign-post, a milestone, and a group of stunted thorn trees.

Jacob set down the lantern.

“Mail comes fra’ north.”

“And you will wait until it arrives?”

“Nay.”

“I beg your pardon?”

“Must get back.”

“You cannot intend to leave me alone.”

“Road’s plain. Coach’ll be here soon.”

“But suppose it has been delayed?”

“Then tha waits.”

“Suppose it does not run?”

“It runs.”

His confidence irritated me. I pulled out my watch and held it near the lantern. Ten minutes before ten.

“You could spare a quarter of an hour.”

“Could. Won’t.”

“Why?”

He looked first along the northern road, then back towards the old route by which we had come.

“I’m wanted home.”

“Your master did not say so.”

“Master says many things.”

He took up the lantern.

“Jacob,” I said sharply, “is there any reason why a man should not wait safely at this crossroad?”

“No reason.”

“Then why do you appear afraid?”

“Not feared.”

“You are.”

He came close to me. His small eyes, under the rim of his cap, had a look both cunning and earnest.

“When tha hears wheels,” he said, “look well afore tha climbs.”

“Do you mean there may be another coach?”

“Mebbe.”

“What coach?”

But he had already turned away.

“Jacob!”

He lifted the lantern without looking back.

“What coach?”

His voice came faintly through the wind.

“Best let dead folk bide.”

The yellow light receded down the old road, dipped behind a bank, appeared once more, and vanished.

I was alone.

I confess that my first emotion was anger. To be abandoned at a desolate junction on such a night, after the servant’s ridiculous warnings and his master’s discourse upon apparitions, seemed a calculated trial of the nerves. Had there been any choice, I would have walked along the turnpike; but I did not know whether the town lay north or south, nor how far I might travel before finding shelter. The mail remained my best hope.

I paced to and fro beneath the sign-post.

The cold, which exercise had kept at bay, soon penetrated my clothes. The clouds closed again, obscuring the moon. I could hear snow slipping from the thorn branches, the low whistle of wind among the arms of the sign-post, and once, very far away, the cry of some night bird. Otherwise all was still.

Five minutes passed.

Ten.

I consulted my watch again. It had stopped.

I held it to my ear. No tick.

The silence which followed seemed altered by that discovery. It was not merely an absence of noise, but a positive suspension—as though every little mechanism of the world had ceased together.

Then I heard wheels.

They were distant at first: a low, irregular rumble, accompanied by the faint clatter of hoofs. Relief drove every other thought from my mind. I stepped into the road and peered northward.

No lamps were visible.

The sound grew louder. Wheels grated upon stone; harness jingled; the hoofbeats came rapidly, four horses at a gallop. Yet still there was no light.

I remembered Jacob’s words—*look well afore tha climbs*—and told myself angrily that his superstition had infected me. Perhaps the coach lamps had been extinguished by the storm. Perhaps a bend concealed them.

At last, between the low hills, two reddish gleams appeared.

They did not shine upon the road as ordinary lamps would have done. They hovered dimly, obscured by mist, now brightening, now nearly vanishing, while the vehicle beneath them remained invisible. But the coach was approaching swiftly, and if I hesitated it might pass without stopping.

I moved to the centre of the road, waved my arms, and shouted.

The hoofbeats slackened. The lamps drew near. A black mass emerged behind them—the mail, high-bodied, heavy, and old-fashioned, with luggage piled upon the roof under a torn cover. The four horses were dark with sweat despite the cold. Steam poured from them, yet I heard no breathing. The driver sat hunched upon the box; the guard was beside him; both wore caped coats and low hats which concealed their faces.

“Town?” I called.

No answer.

“Do you go to the coast?”

The coach rolled past me at walking speed. One of the inside doors swung slightly upon its strap.

I seized the handle, opened it, and climbed in.

Almost at once the horses broke again into a gallop.

There were three passengers inside.

The carriage was dark except for the dull light entering through the windows, and at first I saw no more than the outlines of two figures seated opposite me and one at my side. The closeness of the air was unpleasant. It smelt of wet leather, mould, stagnant water, and something sweetish beneath those odours—something which recalled flowers shut too long in a vault.

“Good evening,” I said.

No one replied.

I was too grateful for shelter to resent their discourtesy. The seat beneath me was damp and strangely uneven; springs groaned at every movement; draughts entered through cracks in the panels. The coach rocked violently, flinging me from side to side, and I wondered that the driver maintained such speed upon a frozen road.

“I feared I had missed you,” I said, raising my voice. “The storm delayed everything tonight.”

Silence.

The passenger opposite me was a large man in a travelling cloak, with his hat drawn down. Beside him sat a woman wearing a bonnet and veil. The third passenger, at my left, seemed very young, perhaps a student or junior officer, though his coat was of an antiquated cut.

I tried again.

“Can you tell me when we reach the coast?”

None moved. None turned.

A feeling of uneasiness, at first faint and unreasonable, began to creep over me. I looked about the carriage more carefully.

The leather lining was cracked and swollen. Tassels hung from the roof, green with mildew. One window sash was broken, though no snow entered through it. The netting overhead had rotted away in strips. Beneath my boots the floor yielded with a soft, pulpy movement, and through a hole near the opposite seat I could see the road passing below—not white with snow as it ought to have been, but black, wet, and glistening.

I bent nearer the window.

Outside, the country was scarcely visible. The lamps cast no beam. Now and then a bare tree flashed past, or a wall seemed to rise within arm’s reach and disappear, but there was no snow upon either. Rain streaked the glass.

Rain!

I touched the pane. It was not merely wet; water ran down its inner surface and dripped into the mouldering frame.

A violent lurch threw me against the young passenger.

“Your pardon,” I exclaimed.

He shifted beneath the impact—not by any voluntary motion, but loosely, horribly, like an object without muscular resistance. His hat fell from his head.

I saw his face.

There are sights which no length of time can soften. I see that face now as plainly as I saw it then: the colourless skin drawn tight upon the cheekbones, the lips retracted, the half-open eyes glazed and fixed, the dark hair plastered across a forehead cut by a deep wound. A thread of dried blood ran from his temple to his collar.

I recoiled with a cry.

The large man opposite raised his head—or the jolting coach raised it for him. His hat slipped backward. One side of his skull was crushed. His mouth hung open. Mud and blackened blood stained his neckcloth.

The woman’s veil stirred.

Not with breath. There was no breath in that carriage.

The rotten gauze lifted slowly from her face, revealing features swollen by water, pale as wax beneath bruises, and two eyes wide open in an expression of infinite terror.

Dead!

All three were dead.

I sprang towards the door and dragged at the handle. It would not move. I struck the panel, shouted to the guard, and called upon the driver to stop.

No answer.

The coach increased its speed.

The wheels no longer rattled separately; their noise became one sustained roar. The vehicle plunged and swayed as if every rut threatened to overturn it. I thrust my shoulder against the door. Rotten wood cracked beneath the blow, but the lock held. I tried the other side and found the dead woman’s knees barring my way. When I touched her gown, cold water streamed from the fabric over my hands.

Then memory came upon me.

The old coach road.

The broken parapet.

Nine years ago. Coachman. Guard. Three inside.

Three inside—and now, with me, four.

I looked through the window.

Moonlight broke suddenly across the landscape, illuminating a narrow road with rock upon one side and a precipice upon the other. Ahead stood a sign-post. Not the one at which I had waited, but another, leaning drunkenly at the bend. Beyond it the parapet showed white for a moment, and in its centre yawned the unrepaired gap.

We were not travelling towards the new road.

We were upon the old one.

“Stop!” I screamed. “Stop, for the love of God!”

The driver turned.

I saw him in profile against the moon: no living face, but a blackened countenance streaming with rain, one eye torn from its socket, the jaw bound grotesquely with a handkerchief. Beside him the guard sat doubled over, his head hanging at an impossible angle.

The horses foamed and stretched into their harness.

The large corpse opposite me rolled forward until its ruined face nearly touched mine. The young man’s arm rose with the motion of the coach and fell across my shoulders. The woman stared past me towards the approaching gulf.

Then everything occurred at once.

The moon blazed out.

The sign-post leapt towards us.

The off-wheel struck a stone.

The horses swerved.

The coachman dragged upon the reins.

The leading pair reared, their eyes white, their mouths open in soundless agony.

The vehicle tilted.

For one suspended second I saw the road, the shattered parapet, the plunging horses, the revolving sky, and beneath us a vast black hollow into which the moonlight did not descend.

Then the wall of stone vanished.

The coach went over.

There was no cry from the dead.

A crashing blow—another—glass bursting inward—wood splitting—a sensation of infinite fall—

And then nothing.

When consciousness returned, I was lying in a bed.

The room was small, warm, and bright with winter morning. Curtains of familiar chintz surrounded me. A fire burned upon the hearth. I smelt lavender, broth, and the clean, sharp odour of medicines. For several moments I could not understand where I was; then a hand tightened about mine.

My wife sat beside the bed.

Her face was white from fatigue, her eyes swollen with weeping, but when she saw that I knew her she gave a cry and bent over me.

I tried to speak. Pain seized my head, my ribs, and my left shoulder.

“Where—?”

“You are safe,” she whispered. “Oh, James, you are safe!”

Later that day, when the surgeon permitted conversation, I learned what had happened—or rather, what ordinary observation could discover of it.

At dawn, two shepherds searching for sheep lost in the storm had passed below the old coach road. In a snowdrift at the foot of the precipice they found me unconscious, partially buried, with a broken collarbone, several cracked ribs, and a severe wound upon the head. My gun was discovered fifty yards away. No footprints led to the place. No recent marks appeared upon the road above, and the snow along the edge of the broken parapet was undisturbed.

They carried me to the nearest farm, where my name was found upon papers in my pocket. A messenger rode to the town. My wife came at once, and after the surgeon judged me fit to be moved, I was conveyed back to our lodging.

For three days I had fever.

During that time, they told me, I spoke repeatedly of a coach, three dead passengers, and an old man among books. The surgeon, a sensible and kindly practitioner, considered the whole matter perfectly explicable.

“You lost your way,” he said, when I was strong enough to dispute with him. “You suffered cold, exhaustion, and terror. At some point you reached the old road, fell over the unguarded edge, and struck your head. The rest was delirium.”

“What of the house?”

“A dream formed from something seen earlier.”

“I ate there. My coat was dried there. I carried a flask given to me by the owner.”

No flask had been found.

“What of the servant who guided me?”

“No one else’s tracks were discovered.”

“It snowed after we passed.”

“Exactly.”

“And the three people killed in the coach accident?”

He shrugged.

“You may have heard the story at the town, though you do not remember hearing it. Such impressions remain hidden until fever revives them.”

I insisted that I had never heard it. He smiled with the patience doctors reserve for irrational convalescents.

“At what hour did this ghostly coach take you up?”

“My watch had stopped shortly before.”

It was produced. The glass was shattered; the hands stood at twelve minutes past ten.

“That proves only the time of your fall,” he said.

“It proves that I was conscious until then.”

“It proves nothing of the sort.”

As soon as I could travel, I employed a local man to search for the farmhouse. He knew of no scholar living in that district. We followed, as well as I could reconstruct it, the route from the old road across the moor; but snow had changed the landscape, my recollection of direction was confused, and we found only a roofless sheepfold and the ruins of a stone building abandoned many years before. There were no books, no instruments, no iron-studded door, no Jacob, no trace of habitation.

This failure has been presented to me ever since as proof that the entire adventure was born in my disordered brain.

I cannot accept it.

I remember the farmhouse as I remember the chambers in which I began my career. I remember the smell of peat and chemical vapours; the brass telescope; the glass jars; the skull beneath the lamp. I remember the scholar’s thin hands and luminous eyes. I remember the coarse bread, the cold mutton, and the returning pain in my fingers as circulation was restored. I remember Jacob’s lantern moving before me over the snow. Above all, I remember the interior of that coach—not as one remembers a nightmare, confused in its transitions and fading beneath examination, but with the hard, particular, material precision of an exhibit produced in court.

The mouldering leather.

The broken sash.

The wet floor.

The young man’s wounded temple.

The dead weight of his arm.

The water running from the woman’s dress.

The driver’s mutilated profile in the moonlight.

These things have not grown dim. Twenty years have given them distance, but not uncertainty.

My wife, who is the most reasonable of women, has never asked me to alter my belief. The surgeon went to his grave convinced that he had treated a case of exposure complicated by delirium. Friends to whom I have told the story divide according to their temperaments. Some suppose that I stumbled upon the ruins of the farmhouse, slept there, and dreamed the inhabitant. Others suggest that smugglers, recluses, or wandering showmen may have occupied it temporarily. A few concede the house and servant but reject the coach. Most seize upon the injury to my head as sufficient explanation for everything.

Let them.

I have spent my life distinguishing what is probable from what is proved, what is imagined from what is remembered, and what a witness believes from what he actually knows. I know that bodily weakness preceded my experience. I know that fear may distort perception. I know that the old scholar’s conversation prepared my mind to interpret strange appearances in a supernatural fashion. I concede every argument which scepticism can honestly advance.

Yet after every concession, the fact remains.

I entered a coach at a lonely crossroad.

I sat among three passengers who had been dead nine years.

The coach repeated its final journey along the abandoned road.

It plunged through the broken parapet.

And when shepherds found me at dawn, I lay in the snow beneath the very precipice where the night mail had fallen.

There were three dead within when that coach began its journey.

There were four when it ended.

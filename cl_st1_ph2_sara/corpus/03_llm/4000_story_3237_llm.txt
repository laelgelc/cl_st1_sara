# The Rubies of the Gobi

The library in the great house in Park Lane had been contrived for the repose of men, and, like many things contrived for repose, possessed an air of almost intolerable oppression.

Its walls, burdened with books which no one now opened, rose out of the dusk like the walls of some sumptuous mausoleum. The curtains were of a red so deep that they appeared black except where the fire struck a fold and drew from it a sombre gleam; the chairs were enormous, designed for vanished statesmen whose bodies, no less than their convictions, had required a majestic accommodation; and upon the long table, under the shaded lamp, lay an atlas bound in vellum, a silver box, three unopened letters, and a little yellow flower, dry as a memory.

Miss Hester Carstair sat before the fire.

Outside, the fog moved slowly along Park Lane, passing the tall windows in faint yellow convolutions. It absorbed the lamps, deadened the wheels of carriages, and changed the occasional cry of a newspaper seller into the voice of one calling from the further side of an immeasurable water. Sometimes the whole city seemed to mutter beneath it—a profound, continuous sound, not so much the traffic of London as the distant roar of a world turning in some stellar space.

Within, there was only the delicate collapse of the coals and the ticking of a French clock whose gilded shepherdess had for nearly a century smiled with unalterable encouragement upon the passage of time.

Hester was twenty-three. She was not beautiful in the explicit manner that enables a woman to know her power before the world has had leisure to dispute it. Her face was narrow, her colouring pale, and her dark hair, parted simply above a broad forehead, made the gravity of her expression seem almost excessive. But her eyes possessed that uncertain brilliance which is produced less by happiness than by a prolonged expectation of it. They seemed always to be looking beyond the person with whom she spoke, not from inattention, but because behind every visible object she suspected the existence of another and more significant reality.

On that evening she wore grey. Across her hands, twisting between her fingers with an absent and almost painful movement, was a necklace of rubies.

They were pigeon-blood stones of an extraordinary depth and purity, linked by ancient crude gold work into a flexible chain. In the lamplight they glowed darkly, as if each contained, under its polished surface, one motionless drop from the heart of a sacrifice. They were not gay jewels. They had none of the cold, witty brilliance of diamonds, none of the green remoteness of emeralds. Their splendour was solemn. They seemed to remember temples, swords, vows, and the sealed chambers of dead kings.

Lady Grenshaw had told her that the necklace was priceless.

Lady Grenshaw knew the price of everything; and because this knowledge had been acquired through birth, widowhood, economy, and the administration of three considerable estates, she possessed it with the authority which other persons reserve for moral principles.

“Eckhart has stripped himself,” she had said. “Do not let us indulge in childish phrases, Hester. He has stripped himself. The horses at Peshawar, the bungalow at Simla, the Chinese bronzes, the Persian manuscripts, even the famous grey Arabs—all gone. I am not defending his life in India. It was absurd, extravagant, and probably worse. But a man who sells the remains of five reckless years in order to place such a thing in a woman’s hands has, at least, made his meaning clear.”

Hester had made no answer.

Her aunt had looked at her through a lorgnette which she did not require for vision but employed for judgment.

“You cannot expect a trumpet from Heaven.”

“No,” Hester had said.

“Nor can a girl in your position afford to disregard devotion because it happens to be accompanied by rank, fortune, and a tolerable appearance.”

“My position,” Hester had repeated.

Lady Grenshaw’s mouth had hardened. She disliked the repetition of her phrases, since repetition sometimes exposed in them a quality which had escaped notice on their first appearance.

“Your father left debts,” she said. “Your mother left nothing. I have given you a home for nine years. You know that I shall continue to do so. But dependence is not a career.”

No. Dependence was not a career. It was a climate: a climate of careful gratitude, regulated warmth, and perpetual small humiliations. Hester knew it thoroughly. She knew the bell which must not be rung because Lady Grenshaw disliked unnecessary service; the carriage which might be used only when it had already been ordered; the gowns which were generous gifts and therefore could never be disliked; the opinions which might be held so long as they did not disturb the symmetry of her aunt’s arrangements. She had not been cruelly treated. Cruelty would have been simpler. She had been maintained.

And now Lord Eckhart had offered her liberation.

He was thirty-four, handsome in a dark, somewhat fatigued fashion, and reputed to have spent five years in India with an extravagance which society alternately condemned and envied. Men spoke of polo ponies, hunting camps, nights at obscure native courts, and journeys made for no intelligible purpose into regions where maps were corrected by hearsay. Women spoke of other things. Upon his return to England he had appeared unexpectedly austere. He had sold the greater part of what he possessed abroad, dismissed the servants who had followed him from Calcutta, and taken modest rooms in St. James’s.

Then he had asked Hester to marry him.

She did not know whether she loved him.

That ignorance had become the central fact of her existence, and around it every lesser fact revolved with the helpless regularity of planets around a dark sun. When Eckhart entered a room, her heart moved; but it was possible that suspense, gratitude, fear, and admiration might together counterfeit the movement of love. When he took her hand, she felt the significance of the gesture; but significance is not tenderness. When he spoke of India, of dry winds over the frontier, of stars hanging close above the plains, and of temples seen at dawn through tamarind trees, she listened with an attention so intense that it resembled passion; yet she sometimes wondered whether it was not India she loved, or distance, or the heroic conception of life which his stories awakened in her.

Marriage was not an answer one gave to a question and afterward withdrew. It was the deliberate cutting of every thread except the one by which a woman bound herself to her mate. Family, poverty, fantasy, the possible unborn future—all were drawn through one narrow ring and fastened there. If the thread proved to be gold, it endured; if it proved to be straw, endurance was still demanded.

What should she do?

The rubies lay across her hands.

Their apparent history accused her. Lord Eckhart had ruined himself for them. He had taken the material substance of his adventurous life—horses, houses, manuscripts, bronzes, perhaps objects associated with dangers and affections of which she knew nothing—and transformed it into this single red chain. It was a magnificent gesture, an immense idealism. To refuse him after such a sacrifice appeared not prudence but poverty of soul.

Her father would have understood the gesture.

At the thought of Major Carstair, Hester turned toward the little flower on the table.

It was a brittle, star-shaped thing, faded to the colour of old ivory at its centre and yellow at the edges. It had come ten years before in an envelope bearing stamps she had never seen and a postmark almost obliterated by rain. There had been no letter, only the flower, pressed between two sheets of paper, and upon one sheet, in her father’s abrupt hand, the words:

*From the threshold of the desert. If I return, I shall bring a greater gift.*

He had not returned.

Major Alaric Carstair had been a military surgeon before ill health and an imprudent marriage had severed his connection with the Army. He had possessed no genius for prosperity. Money disappeared in his hands with a rapidity which seemed not careless but metaphysical, as though he had denied its reality and it had retaliated by ceasing to exist. He had invented surgical instruments which other men patented, lent money to persons who vanished, and once spent half an inheritance upon establishing a hospital in a Balkan village whose name no English official could afterward discover.

Yet he had been a great dreamer.

Hester remembered him as a tall shape entering the nursery at impossible hours, bringing the smell of rain, tobacco, chemicals, and distant railway stations. He had spoken to her not as to a child but as to an exile temporarily deprived of important news. He drew maps upon the walls, made mountains out of cushions, and explained that somewhere in the Gobi Desert there grew a plant whose distilled essence could render the human body insensible to pain without the perils of chloroform or ether.

“The old physicians knew it,” he had said. “Then came wars, and the road was forgotten. But pain has waited. Pain always waits.”

She had not understood. Nevertheless, the gravity with which he pronounced the word had entered her imagination. Pain became a vast chained creature beneath the earth, shaking cities when it moved. Her father intended to descend and master it.

He had gone first to India, afterward toward Tibet, and at last northward into the Gobi. For a little while letters came—thin, wandering letters full of difficult names and great spaces. Then they ceased. Reports followed, each contradicting the last. He had died of fever at Leh; he had entered a monastery; he had been murdered by guides; he had gone alone into the desert; he had found his plant and forgotten England.

Lady Grenshaw preferred the report of death.

“A man with a wife and child has no right to pursue mirages,” she had once said.

Hester, who was fourteen, had answered, “If the mirage could prevent pain, it was his right.”

That reply had not been forgiven. It was one of the many evidences by which Lady Grenshaw established that the Carstair folly persisted in the blood.

Now, looking from the flower to the rubies, Hester perceived the two objects as emblems of one romantic law. Her father had given comfort, safety, reputation, perhaps life itself, for a dream intended to alleviate the suffering of strangers. Eckhart had given all the brilliant possessions of his wandering years for love of her. Was one sacrifice to be sacred and the other dismissed because it demanded something in return?

The clock struck half-past eleven.

Lady Grenshaw had gone to bed. The servants moved inaudibly through remote portions of the house. Hester rose and stood with her back to the fire, her fingers linked behind her, the necklace still imprisoned between them. Above the mantelpiece hung a portrait of her aunt’s dead husband in peer’s robes. His painted gaze possessed the resigned majesty of a man who had survived both politics and marriage only by dying before either could alter him.

Hester imagined herself as Lady Eckhart.

The title disturbed her less than the intimacy. She could imagine the house, the servants, the obligations, even Lord Eckhart seated opposite her at breakfast; but when she attempted to imagine the entire and secret surrender which marriage implied, her mind recoiled. There was in him a region she had never entered. Sometimes, while he spoke with perfect courtesy, she had seen his expression close as suddenly as a door in a fortress. Behind it lay five years of which he gave anecdotes but no account. His dissipation had perhaps been exaggerated. Yet it had existed. What did a man purchase during five dissolute years? And what portion of the purchase remained in him after horses and bronzes had been sold?

A faint sound came from the hall.

She listened. There was a murmur of voices, the discreet protest of the butler, then a pause. The library door opened, and Hargreaves entered with the expression of a man compelled to announce an event which good domestic government ought to have rendered impossible.

“I beg your pardon, miss. A gentleman has called.”

“At this hour?”

“He states that he has only now arrived in London, miss, and that his business concerns Major Carstair.”

The room appeared to contract.

“What is his name?”

“Doctor Tsan-Sgam.”

The syllables were pronounced separately and with deep suspicion.

“Where is he?”

“In the hall, miss. I informed him that Lady Grenshaw had retired. He said he had not come to see her ladyship.”

Hester put the rubies upon the table.

“Bring him here.”

Hargreaves hesitated. His eyes, trained by forty years of service never to observe directly what could afterward be reported with accuracy, touched the necklace, the clock, and Hester’s face.

“Very good, miss.”

A moment later he returned, preceding a small man in European evening dress.

Dr. Tsan-Sgam might have been fifty or seventy. Age had dealt with his face as wind deals with a substance too resistant to be destroyed: it had worn innumerable delicate lines upon the surface without diminishing the essential strength beneath. His hair was black streaked with iron grey; his eyes, under narrow lids, were singularly lucid; and his hands, one of which held a square parcel wrapped in dark silk, were fine, brown, and motionless.

He bowed first to Hester, then slightly toward the room, as though acknowledging not its luxury but the invisible arrangements which had made their meeting possible.

“I ask forgiveness,” he said. “The hour is one at which a house should be permitted to close itself against events. But I leave England tomorrow.”

His English was precise, though each sentence seemed to be constructed in some hidden chamber of the mind, translated through several languages, examined for balance, and only then released.

“You knew my father?” Hester asked.

“I had that honour.”

The simplicity of the words broke through ten years of uncertainty. She caught the edge of the table.

“Is he dead?”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam looked at her for a long moment.

“Yes.”

There are griefs which do not arrive when their cause occurs. They wait outside consciousness, neither diminishing nor dying, until a word opens the door. Hester had believed her father dead; she had spoken of him as dead; she had even defended his death against the practical contempt of her aunt. But belief is a veil, and knowledge a hand that tears it.

She sat down.

“Where?”

“In the Gobi.”

“You found him?”

“His body was found. I came too late.”

The physician placed the silk parcel upon the table. He did so with extreme care, adjusting it between the lamp and the dried flower as though the precise relation of these objects possessed ceremonial importance.

“I have brought something which belongs to you.”

He unfolded the silk. Within it stood a small ivory image, perhaps nine inches high, representing a monument of curious form. Four carved pillars supported a domed roof from which sprang a flame; beneath the dome lay the recumbent figure of a man, while around the base ran minute inscriptions in several characters. The ivory had yellowed, and in its crevices remained faint traces of blue and vermilion.

Hester bent toward it.

“What is this?”

“The image of the monument erected where Major Carstair was buried.”

“Who erected it?”

“Those who understood what he attempted.”

For a while she could not speak. The miniature structure, pale and intricate beneath the lamp, had the awful authority of things reduced from immensity. It was not merely an ornament. Somewhere under a sky she had imagined since childhood, among winds which moved over distances unmeasured by English roads, the great original stood above her father’s recovered body.

She touched the ivory flame.

“Tell me.”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam glanced toward the fire. Hester indicated a chair, and he sat awkwardly upon its edge, as though antique English furniture presented a mechanical problem for which courtesy required an immediate but temporary solution.

“I must begin,” he said, “before the desert.”

“Begin wherever you must.”

“Your father came to Lhasa at a time when His Holiness was gravely ill. I speak of circumstances which have not been made public, and which even now must be expressed with care. There had been an accident. There was a great loss of blood. Our physicians possessed knowledge of many things, but not of the operation which Major Carstair proposed.”

“A transfusion?”

“Yes. That word is exact. The danger was extreme. His Holiness might die if nothing was done; he might die more quickly if the foreign physician attempted what he advised. There was discussion. There was suspicion. Your father became angry.”

A faint and unexpected smile touched Dr. Tsan-Sgam’s mouth.

“I remember that anger,” he continued. “It was not the anger of a man whose skill is doubted. It was the anger of a man who sees time being wasted while another suffers. He said, ‘You may debate my nation, my religion, my motives, and my manners after the patient is alive.’ This sentence, though difficult in translation, had force.”

“That was my father.”

“Yes. It was entirely your father.”

The words gave her a joy almost equal to pain.

“Where did the blood come from?” she asked.

“From him.”

Hester looked up.

Dr. Tsan-Sgam passed one hand slowly over his face.

“It was determined that blood should be given. There were attendants whose rank made the offer appropriate, soldiers whose courage made it sincere, monks whose devotion made it complete. But your father had reasons concerning compatibility. Some bloods, he said, might kill. He tested what he could, with instruments he had carried over mountains at a cost of much discomfort and profanity. At last he gave his own.”

“And the Dalai Lama lived?”

“He lived.”

The fire sank, and a coal divided soundlessly, opening a golden interior.

“Your father was ill afterward,” said Dr. Tsan-Sgam. “He had already endured fever. The journey had reduced him. For many days he could scarcely stand. Yet while he was recovering, he spoke continuously of the plant in the Gobi.”

Hester’s eyes went to the flower.

“He sent me that from the desert’s edge.”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam stooped and examined it without touching.

“Yes,” he said. “The colour is gone, but I know the family. It is not the plant he sought.”

“No.”

“He possessed old documents—fragments copied from a medical book destroyed many centuries ago. He believed they described a flowering growth found near salt depressions in the southern desert. Its preparation, according to the text, caused sleep so profound that the knife could pass through flesh without waking the patient, yet did not weaken the heart. He believed also that it might quiet certain convulsions and the final agony of incurable disease.”

“He meant to prevent pain.”

“He meant to wage war upon it.”

The phrase filled the library with her father’s presence. He rose before her not as the irresponsible figure of Lady Grenshaw’s judgments, not as the debtor, the failed inventor, the man who had abandoned wife and child, but as she had secretly preserved him: a great dreamer walking toward a flaming boundary because beyond it humanity’s ancient enemy might be found vulnerable.

“Why did no one prevent him?” she asked, though she knew the uselessness of the question.

“We attempted it.”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam’s fingers closed upon his knee.

“We had a fear.”

“What fear?”

“Not one fear. Many fears, joined. The season was late. The wells were uncertain. Certain guides had brought reports of moving sands. There were also men who desired the documents he carried and who believed, perhaps, that a medicine sought by governments would possess an immense commercial value. And there was another matter.”

He paused.

“What matter?”

“Your father’s strength. He had not recovered from the transfusion. His pulse was irregular. He tired after walking a short distance. I told him that to enter the desert in such a condition was not courage but the misuse of courage.”

“What did he say?”

“He said that courage, if preserved too carefully, became merely another form of cowardice.”

Hester bowed her head.

Again, it was entirely her father.

“We asked him to wait until spring,” Dr. Tsan-Sgam continued. “He answered that men under surgeons’ knives could not wait until spring. We offered an escort. He accepted two companions and dismissed six. We offered money. He accepted supplies but no payment. His Holiness sent to him a robe, a seal, and certain objects of value. He retained the seal because it might open roads. The other gifts he returned.”

“Why?”

“He said gratitude should not be converted into luggage.”

Despite herself, Hester laughed. The laugh trembled and broke, and suddenly she was weeping.

Dr. Tsan-Sgam did not attempt consolation. There is a sympathy which touches grief and thereby makes it conscious of spectators; his was of another order. He remained still while her tears fell, guarding her sorrow as though it were a flame exposed to wind.

At last she dried her eyes.

“Forgive me.”

“There is nothing to forgive.”

“Tell me how he died.”

The physician looked toward the windows, behind which the London fog pressed its yellow face against the glass.

“He crossed the first belt of dunes,” he said. “Then the second. One companion returned with fever. The other remained until the camels were lost in a storm. They walked for three days. Your father found several plants and placed them in metal cases. At a dry basin they discovered stone ruins. There he became too weak to continue.”

“Was the plant among those he found?”

“We do not know. The cases disappeared.”

“Stolen?”

“Perhaps. The surviving companion left him to seek water after your father ordered him to do so. When the man returned, Major Carstair was gone. There were tracks—his tracks and those of two horses. Afterward there was wind.”

Hester stared at him.

“But you said his body was found.”

“Three years later.”

“Three years?”

“Yes. Men crossing farther north discovered a body preserved by salt and dryness in the shelter of a ruined wall. With it was your father’s seal, his compass, part of a notebook, and a case containing surgical needles. There was also a strip of cloth bearing the name by which he was known among us.”

“And the plants?”

“No plants.”

“Then he failed.”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam’s eyes rested upon her with grave attention.

“Success and failure are words which distance employs for its convenience. He did not bring back the medicine. That is true. But he crossed boundaries others believed closed. He gave blood where distrust had forbidden aid. He caused certain physicians to study methods previously rejected because they were foreign. Later, transfusions were made which preserved other lives. Is this failure?”

“No,” she said. “No.”

“His great discovery was not made. His great impulse was not wasted.”

The distinction entered her mind with immense force. The dream might perish; the idealism which sent a man after it could multiply invisibly in others. A deed was not limited by its announced purpose. The arrow might miss the star and yet reveal, by the arc of its flight, the strength of the hand that loosed it.

She took up the dried flower and caressed one brittle petal with the tip of her finger.

“Did he speak of me?”

“In Lhasa, many times.”

“What did he say?”

“That you would understand him.”

The answer was both a gift and a burden.

“He should not have been so certain,” she whispered.

“He was certain.”

“He left me.”

“Yes.”

The word, without excuse or softening, made her raise her head.

Dr. Tsan-Sgam continued carefully: “To honour a man is not to make his injuries unreal. Major Carstair possessed a vast compassion for human suffering. It was sometimes easier for him to perceive suffering at a distance than sorrow beside him. Great dreamers may cast great shadows. Those nearest stand in them.”

Hester looked at the ivory monument.

No one had ever spoken thus of her father. Lady Grenshaw condemned him because he had neglected the practical laws by which families survive; Hester defended him because he had obeyed a greater law. But the physician’s words admitted both laws and diminished neither. Her father had been magnificent. He had also left her. The one truth did not cancel the other.

“Perhaps,” she said slowly, “I have tried to live by his conception of things because otherwise his going would seem only cruel.”

“Perhaps.”

“I have thought sacrifice must always be answered by sacrifice.”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam inclined his head, waiting.

The rubies lay between them.

Under the green shade of the lamp their red had deepened almost to black, but where the chain curved, three stones caught the light and burned.

Hester took them up.

“Do you know Lord Eckhart?”

For the first time since entering the room, the physician’s composure altered. The change was slight—a stillness within stillness, a momentary arrest like that of intricate machinery when some hidden wheel has encountered an obstruction.

“Yes,” he said.

“You knew him in India?”

“I met him there and elsewhere.”

“Was he with my father?”

“No.”

“Did he know of him?”

“He knew the principal facts.”

Hester wound the necklace once around her hands.

“Lord Eckhart brought me these rubies.”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam’s gaze descended to them. He rose from the chair, approached the table, and examined the stones. He did not touch them.

“Yes,” he said at last. “I wondered where they had gone.”

The phrase was so unexpected that Hester loosened her hold.

“You know them?”

“I know them.”

“They belonged to someone in India?”

“They were kept for many years in a treasury, though their gold work is older than the treasury and possibly older than the dynasty from which they first came. The central stones are from Burma. Two others are believed to have crossed the mountains from a kingdom which no longer exists.”

“Lord Eckhart said nothing of that.”

“What did he say?”

“He said only that they had cost him all he could give.”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam looked at her.

The fog outside seemed to thicken against the windows. Somewhere in the house a door closed, far away and softly, like the conclusion of an argument in another life.

Hester continued: “My aunt learned that he had sold everything he owned in India. His horses, his collections, his house. She believes—I believe—that he sold them to buy the necklace.”

The physician made no reply.

“You must understand,” she said. “He has asked me to marry him.”

“I understand.”

“I have not answered.”

The words, once spoken, seemed to stand between them with a nakedness more terrible than confession. She moved away from the table and faced the fire. Her fingers, linked behind her back, gripped the rubies until the old gold pressed into her skin.

“I do not know whether I love him,” she said. “That must sound contemptible.”

“No.”

“It sounds so to me. One should know. In books, in the lives one admires, there is always certainty at the great moment. My father was certain when he entered the desert.”

“He was certain of an idea. A human being is more difficult.”

“But Lord Eckhart has sacrificed everything.”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam did not move.

Hester turned.

“That is what makes refusal impossible. If he had merely offered wealth, position, protection, I might refuse them and bear the consequences. But he has given up the visible substance of five years for me. Whatever those years were—reckless, dissolute, strange—he gathered them into this one gift. Perhaps I do not love him now, but may not gratitude become love? May not admiration? Does one have the right to demand an emotion more perfect than the devotion one receives?”

Still the physician was silent.

She came nearer. The need to justify herself, to place before this grave stranger the whole architecture of her decision, had become almost desperate.

“My father believed that the value of life lay in what one surrendered to a great purpose. He abandoned safety and reputation. He gave his blood. He died alone. If I honour that sacrifice, how can I look upon another sacrifice and say that it has no claim on me because my own heart is uncertain?”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam stooped over the ivory monument. For several seconds his face was hidden. When he straightened, there was in his expression not surprise but a profound and painful resolution.

“You have placed two acts together,” he said. “The flower and the necklace.”

“Yes.”

“You believe they speak the same language.”

“They seem to.”

“And therefore you propose that your life shall become an answer.”

“What else can honour require?”

“Truth.”

The short word fell with extraordinary weight.

Hester’s hands tightened upon the chain.

“Do you think Lord Eckhart unworthy?”

“It is not my office to judge his whole worth.”

“Did he live badly in India?”

“He lived as many men live when distance has loosened the threads which bind conduct to consequence. There were horses, gaming, journeys, women, collections, enthusiasms assumed and discarded. There were also generous actions. Human accounts are rarely kept in one ink.”

“Then what do you mean?”

“I mean that gratitude may resemble love when viewed from outside. Duty may resemble love. Imagination may clothe itself in love’s garments and stand before a mirror until even the wearer is deceived. But resemblance does not create identity.”

“You speak as though I had deceived myself.”

“I believe you have been misled.”

“By my aunt?”

“Perhaps in part.”

“By Lord Eckhart?”

Dr. Tsan-Sgam looked again at the necklace.

“We had a fear,” he said quietly.

The repeated phrase awakened in her a sudden chill.

“You said that before—about my father.”

“Yes.”

“What fear had you of Lord Eckhart?”

“That an object taken from one meaning and placed within another might acquire a false history.”

“I do not understand.”

“He was asked to perform a service. Circumstances made him the most convenient messenger. We knew something of his character, but the request came from an authority he could not easily refuse. Later, when no acknowledgment reached us, we wondered whether the service had been performed.”

“What service?”

“To bring an object to England.”

Hester’s eyes dropped to the rubies. The stones seemed to move in her hands, not physically, but as constellations appear to move when the observer discovers that the map by which he named them was wrong.

“No,” she said.

Dr. Tsan-Sgam waited.

“He brought these to me after he returned. He had sold his possessions. Everyone knew it.”

“He had debts.”

“The necklace was his last possession.”

“No.”

“He told me it had cost him all he could give.”

“Words may be arranged so that truth performs the office of falsehood.”

Hester unwound the chain from her hands and laid it upon the table, but immediately picked it up again. She could not leave it still. Its weight had changed. A moment before it had been the concentrated substance of Lord Eckhart’s renunciation; now it was an unexplained thing, ancient, red, and menacing, as though the drops within the stones had become the blood of some undiscovered betrayal.

“Where did he obtain it?”

“In Lhasa.”

“From whom?”

“From a chamberlain acting under direct instruction.”

“Did he buy it?”

“No.”

“Was it payment?”

“No.”

“For something he had done?”

“No.”

Her voice rose.

“Then why was it given to him?”

“It was not given to him.”

The fire collapsed with a soft rush, sending a multitude of sparks upward into the chimney. Hester stood motionless. Beyond the windows the fog erased London; the room, the house, the whole ordered and oppressive world of Park Lane seemed suspended in a yellow void.

Dr. Tsan-Sgam spoke with extreme care.

“After your father’s body was identified, a report was sent to His Holiness. It was remembered that Major Carstair had refused every treasure offered after the transfusion. It was also remembered that he had a daughter in England. His Holiness desired that gratitude, which the father would not accept for himself, should pass to the child.”

Hester’s lips moved, but no sound came.

The physician indicated the rubies.

“Lord Eckhart never purchased the necklace. It was the Dalai Lama’s gift to you, entrusted to him for delivery.”

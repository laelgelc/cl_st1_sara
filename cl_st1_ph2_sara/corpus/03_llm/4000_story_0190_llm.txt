## GRANDMA’S BRIGHTEST BIRTHDAY

Grandma Bell was seventy-three years old on the fifteenth of October; and because in the spring she had been so ill that even Dr. Dosewell shook his head, took off his spectacles, and said, “We must hope, Madam, and pray,” the birthday seemed to us a very precious one indeed. It had pleased God to spare her, though she was still slender and white as one of the late lilies in the garden, and must not be wearied by too much joy at once. Therefore the family council decided that the fête should last all day, in small, comfortable pieces, with quiet spaces between; which was an excellent plan, though Dick and Davy declared it was like being allowed only one bite of pudding every hour.

At seven o’clock those young gentlemen crept into Grandma’s room dressed as Dawn and the Morning Star. At least, that was their account of themselves. Dick wore Aunt Tribulation’s yellow bed-curtain pinned about his shoulders, with a paper sun upon his head; while Davy had covered his nightgown with silver stars cut from the lining of a tea-chest, and carried a lantern containing no candle, because Mamma had views concerning fire.

They advanced on tiptoe, which in their case meant a succession of bumps, whispers, and suppressed giggles; then Dick flourished his arms and cried—

“Arise, venerable Grandmother! The sun awaits you!”

“The sun will have to wait,” said Grandma, opening her bright eyes, “unless he has brought my slippers.”

Davy produced them from beneath his robe, and both boys fell upon her with kisses. They had brought also a posy of autumn violets, three chrysanthemums, and one very damp fern; and when Grandma thanked them as if they had gathered flowers from the gardens of the Queen, they looked so proud that the paper sun slipped over Dick’s nose.

After breakfast came Mrs. Coobiddy with a glass of blackberry cordial, tied round with blue ribbon; old Captain Brace brought grapes from his greenhouse; Miss Phemie presented a knitted shoulder-cape; and the baker’s little daughter appeared with a sugar bird which had lost its tail in the journey. Grandma received the tailless bird with particular tenderness, and set it upon her mantelpiece, where, I assure you, it looked as consequential as any eagle.

Then came Widow Grindle, in her clean black bonnet, carrying six new-laid eggs in a basket lined with scarlet leaves.

“They are only humble, ma’am,” she said, “but you sat by my Annie when she was sick.”

Grandma’s lip trembled; yet before tears could arrive Davy asked whether the eggs were humble because the hens were poor, and Widow Grindle laughed till the basket shook.

There followed Thomas Puddle, the lame cobbler, with a pair of warm slippers; Sally from the laundry, bearing a little pot of honey; Mr. Greenbanks, who had prospered in business after Grandma lent him ten pounds when nobody else trusted him, with an armful of roses; and two small, grimy boys from Mill Lane, who brought a wooden whistle of their own manufacture. It produced a sound like a distressed peacock, but Grandma blew it once and pronounced it capital.

Thus the morning went on—gifts, greetings, rests, broth, laughter, and little naps before the fire; and it seemed to me that the day was a kind of harvest-time, when Grandma gathered in the affection she had sown broadcast through seventy-three years. Some offerings were costly, some crooked, some edible, and some quite inexplicable; but love had wrapped every one of them.

At five o’clock she was told to put on her gray silk gown and best lace cap. Aunt Tribulation fastened a spray of scarlet leaves at her breast; Mamma wrapped her in the white shawl; and Papa, offering his arm with great ceremony, announced that the evening air was favorable for a journey of uncommon splendor.

The journey extended only from the parlor to the study, but it was made in procession. Papa and Grandma led; Mamma and the aunts followed; then came three cousins carrying lamps; and last of all walked Thomas Pib, the cat, with a blue ribbon round his neck and such an expression of injured dignity that one might have supposed the whole affair arranged against his wishes.

At the study door Papa paused.

“Madam,” said he, “your court awaits you.”

The door flew open, and there stood two Indian chiefs beside a table blazing with presents.

Never, I think, were more imposing savages seen within a respectable Christian household. Dick, under the title of Roaring Buffalo Who Never Washes, wore a crown of turkey feathers, a red blanket, six necklaces, one of Mamma’s bracelets, and stripes of vermilion across his cheeks. Davy was Thundercloud Smasher of Ten Thousand Bears, though he was so small that the bears need not have been greatly alarmed. He wore a leopard-skin hearthrug, pasteboard moccasins, and feathers enough to supply a poultry-yard.

The room itself was beautiful with evergreen, scarlet and yellow leaves, lanterns, apples, and bunches of purple grapes; the fire shone, the lamps twinkled, and in the midst stood Grandma’s chair, garlanded like a throne. She stopped upon the threshold, clasping Papa’s arm, while all cried, “Many happy returns!”

For one moment her white head bent. We knew she remembered the spring, the darkened room, and voices whispering outside her door; but Thundercloud Smasher instantly gave so tremendous a war-whoop that Thomas Pib leaped upon the bookcase, and grief had no opportunity to settle.

Grandma was enthroned, and the presents were delivered, each with a poem composed by the family poet, whose name charity forbids me to reveal.

A warm footstool came first:

> When winter winds begin to blow,  
> Keep Grandma’s dear toes from the snow.

Then came spectacles in a purple case:

> Though Grandma sees our faults full well,  
> These help her read—and never tell.

A bottle of lavender-water, a new Bible marker, a jar of ginger, a fan, a workbasket, and a ridiculous nightcap with pink bows followed, each praised in verses of unequal merit. Grandma laughed until she was obliged to wipe her eyes.

At last Dick advanced with a parcel tied in nine yards of ribbon.

“This,” said he solemnly, “is offered in the name of Thomas Pib.”

The parcel contained a mousetrap.

Now you must know that Thomas Pib’s relations with mice had long been a family sorrow. He was a handsome black cat, strong, well-fed, and capable of looking thoughtful for hours together; but though mice ran behind the pantry wainscot, danced beneath the flour-bin, and once carried away half Aunt Tribulation’s sandwich, Pib caught nothing. He would sit before a mouse-hole with his tail neatly arranged round his toes; yet if the mouse appeared, he generally stared at it in grave astonishment, as if expecting an introduction.

At length Aunt Tribulation declared him an impostor, and he was given to a farmer four miles away. Pib made no protest. He merely looked ashamed and departed in a basket.

Three days later, at breakfast, a thin, muddy, exhausted creature appeared at the window, uttered one reproachful cry, and fell into Grandma’s arms. It was Pib, having crossed fields, ditches, and perhaps mountains—for the boys insisted upon mountains—to return to those he loved. From that hour nobody spoke of sending him away. He still caught no mice, but affection must count for something; and whenever a crust vanished from the pantry, Aunt Tribulation said it was fortunate that Pib had not been there, or the mouse might have frightened him.

The trap, therefore, was meant to assist him in his professional duties. As Dick explained this, Pib descended from the bookcase, sniffed the gift, and turned his back upon it.

“He says,” observed Davy, “that a gentleman never works on his grandmother’s birthday.”

More gifts came; but the last was a tiny old-fashioned locket which Mamma placed gently in Grandma’s hand. Within it lay a soft brown curl.

Grandma’s own hair was white as snow, and perhaps that is why the brown lock looked so young and warm upon her palm.

“My mother’s,” she whispered.

No one laughed then. Grandma had been motherless for nearly sixty years, yet her face changed as she bent over the curl, and for an instant she seemed not our grandmother, but a little girl missing the arms that had once held her. Mamma knelt beside her; Papa laid his hand upon her shoulder; and the boys, forgetting feathers, war-paint, and ten thousand bears, stood very still.

“My dear ones,” Grandma said presently, “how rich you make me.”

Then Davy’s pasteboard moccasin came apart with a loud crack, and he sat down unexpectedly in the workbasket. That rescued us all.

The Indian entertainment now began. Roaring Buffalo and Thundercloud Smasher danced round the throne, stamping, chanting, and brandishing wooden tomahawks. They met in terrible battle; Dick was killed and buried beneath the leopard-skin hearthrug; Davy sang a funeral hymn consisting chiefly of “Hi-yah!”; then the dead chief rose because he remembered supper, slew his enemy, pardoned him, and took him hunting.

The hunt was conducted behind the sofa, where a stuffed bearskin cushion represented a ferocious beast. Six arrows missed it; one struck Papa’s newspaper; and the bear was finally conquered by being sat upon. The chiefs returned bearing their prey, danced again, and distributed imaginary buffalo steaks to the company. Grandma applauded every disaster.

Meanwhile more guests arrived—the rector and his wife, Dr. Dosewell, the Greenbanks family, Captain Brace, Miss Phemie, Widow Grindle, and as many neighbors as could be admitted without violating the doctor’s orders. The snow-white cake was brought in, crowned with seventy-three little candles; though only seven were lighted, lest we celebrate Grandma by burning down the house. Around it stood jellies, cream, apples, grapes, little pies, syllabub, nuts, and such goodies as made the chiefs abandon the wilderness without regret.

Papa filled the glasses, and everybody rose.

“To our dear mother,” he said, “whom God has spared to us; may the light she has given others shine round her always.”

There was a soft murmur of “Amen,” and glasses rang. Grandma looked about at children, grandchildren, friends, servants, poor neighbors and prosperous ones, at Thomas Pib beneath the table awaiting cake, and at the two painted savages leaning lovingly against her chair.

“I have had more than my share of blessings,” she said.

“No,” cried Dick, “you have only had the first helping!”

So we drank to the second.

Afterward the rug was rolled back, Captain Brace played the fiddle, and everyone danced round Grandma’s chair. Even Dr. Dosewell took two steps with Aunt Tribulation, though he denied it afterward; Widow Grindle bobbed with Thomas Puddle; the chiefs performed a reel; and Thomas Pib, wearing his ribbon, was carried once round in Mamma’s arms. Then we sang old songs until Grandma’s eyes shone brighter than the lamps.

At nine the doctor ordered retreat. We escorted her upstairs, less noisily than we had brought her down, though Davy’s feathers caught in the banister and nearly pulled him backward. Grandma was tucked into bed; the locket lay upon the table beside her violets, the tailless sugar bird, and the dreadful whistle from Mill Lane.

One by one we kissed her good night. The boys came last, scrubbed free of war-paint, rosy, rumpled, and suddenly sleepy.

“Was it too much, Grandma?” Dick asked.

“No, darling.”

“Was it the best birthday?” asked Davy.

Grandma looked at us all—the sons and daughters she had reared, the children who carried her kindness forward, and the faces made especially dear because we had so nearly lost her.

“It may be my last,” she said gently, “but do not look sorrowful. If it is, you have made it the happiest and brightest birthday of my life.”

Then she drew the boys close, one on either side, and kissed them; and because love knows when to be merry and when to be still, we went softly out, leaving her in the warm firelight, with her mother’s brown curl near her white head and the echoes of our singing about her.

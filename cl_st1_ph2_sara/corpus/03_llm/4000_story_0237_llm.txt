# The Fir-Tree

Far within a pleasant forest, where the ground was soft with moss and the wild strawberries hid beneath their three green leaves, there grew a little Fir-tree. He stood in a sunny place; the air was sweet, the brook ran near him, and tall Beeches and Pines kept off the harshest winds.

Yet the little Fir-tree was not happy.

“Oh, were I but tall!” he sighed. “Were I but as great as those noble fellows yonder! Then the Birds would build among my branches, and when the Wind blew I could bend my head with dignity.”

The Sunbeams slipped between the boughs and warmed his tender green needles.

“Rejoice in us,” they whispered. “Thou art young, and the whole morning shines upon thee.”

But the Fir-tree took no notice.

“Morning!” said he. “What is morning to one so small?”

The Dew laid clear pearls upon him, and the Wind kissed his topmost shoot; a Hare sprang over him, and once, when he was very little, that offended him exceedingly.

“To be jumped over!” he cried. “Oh, the shame of it! But wait until I grow tall.”

In summer, children came from the cottages and gathered strawberries in the forest. They sat close beside the Fir-tree, their baskets between their knees, and spoke of their games and suppers. Sometimes the smallest child would say, “What a pretty little Tree!” and would touch his fresh shoots gently.

The Fir-tree disliked being called little.

“Grow, grow!” he said within himself. “Make haste! There must be something better awaiting me.”

Every spring added a circle to his stem and lifted his crown a little higher; for by the number of circles in the wood one may afterwards count a Tree’s years, though it is then generally too late to congratulate him upon them.

When autumn came, Woodcutters entered the forest. Their axes flashed between the trunks, and great Trees fell with tremendous groans. Their branches were hewn away; the naked trunks were drawn upon wagons and carried down the road.

The young Fir-tree trembled, yet he was filled with curiosity.

“Whither are they going?” he asked the Swallows. “What is to become of them?”

The Swallows knew nothing. They had their own journey to consider and were already assembling beneath the pale sky.

But a Stork, who had visited distant harbours, stood upon one leg in the marsh and nodded wisely.

“I have seen new ships,” said he, “with tall masts that once grew in these woods. They bear flags and cross the mighty sea.”

“The sea!” cried the Fir-tree. “And what is that?”

“It is larger than thy brook,” replied the Stork; and, having given so complete an explanation, he spread his wings and flew away.

The Fir-tree quivered with delight.

“To cross the sea! To carry flags! To behold strange countries! Ah, were I but large enough to be cut down! Why must growing take so long?”

“Be glad of thy youth,” said the Wind. “Stretch thy branches freely while thou canst.”

But the Fir-tree answered, “Thou hast flown everywhere, and therefore it is easy for thee to speak.”

Winter came. The snow lay dazzling white over the earth; Foxes passed like flames between the trunks, and dark Ravens called from the bare Beeches. The Fir-tree wore snow upon every branch. Sometimes it was heavy, and his back ached beneath it—as yours or mine might ache after carrying a troublesome parcel—but he did not complain of that. His complaint was always of the future, which would not arrive quickly enough.

Near Christmas, men came and cut down several young Fir-trees, some no taller than himself. They were the finest and most evenly grown; their branches were left upon them, and they were laid carefully upon wagons.

“Where are they going?” asked the Fir-tree. “They are not large enough for masts.”

The Sparrows hopped close to the wagons, chirping with importance.

“We know! We know! We have looked through the windows in town. They are set in warm rooms and dressed in wonderful splendour—golden apples, painted toys, sugar-plums, little cakes, and hundreds of lights!”

“And afterwards?” asked the Fir-tree.

“Afterwards?” The Sparrows cocked their heads. “We did not see afterwards. But nothing could be finer.”

“Oh!” said the Fir-tree. “May such happiness come to me! Better than sailing the sea, perhaps! To stand in warmth, covered with gold and lights! And then—yes, then something still grander must follow. Else wherefore should they adorn one so magnificently? What comes afterwards?”

No one could tell him.

The Sunbeams returned with spring and said, “Rejoice! Here are new buds and young Birds.”

The Fir-tree looked down at his own lengthening branches.

“I have no time to rejoice,” said he. “I am waiting.”

The Swallows came home.

“Rejoice!” they cried. “The blue sky is deep, the gnats are dancing, and the eaves are warm.”

“I would fain know what lies beyond the forest,” replied the Fir-tree.

Summer passed; autumn passed; winter folded the woods again in silence. Each year the Fir-tree grew taller and fuller, and each year he imagined that happiness stood just beyond the next season, like a bright door which would surely open if only he could reach it.

At last, when Christmas drew near, Woodcutters approached him.

“This is a handsome one,” said the first.

“Straight, thick, and well furnished,” said the second. “It will suit the great house.”

The axe struck deep into the Fir-tree.

A sharp pain ran through root, trunk, and every green branch. He had dreamed often of being cut down, but dreams conceal many inconveniences. He fell upon the earth with a cry no man heard; the familiar soil pressed against him, and he felt a strange faintness.

“Farewell,” murmured the old Pines.

But he could not answer. He was bound, dragged from the forest, and thrown upon a wagon.

The horses trotted away. Through the branches he caught broken glimpses of the place that had been his home: the brook glittering under thin ice, the mossy hollow, the Strawberry leaves, the children’s path, and the patch of sky where the Swallows had flown.

“What is this?” he thought. “Why am I sad? Is not my great fortune beginning?”

The road jolted him cruelly. At every stone his cut trunk struck the wagon; yet he comforted himself.

“I go toward splendour. One must suffer a little on the way to greatness.”

In the town there was noise enough to bewilder a forest Tree. Wheels rattled, bells rang, doors slammed, and people hurried under lamps. The Fir-tree was carried into a lofty house, up a broad staircase, and into a drawing room where portraits looked gravely from gilded frames.

Servants fixed him upright in a tub filled with sand. Green cloth was wrapped around the tub, and then the decorating began.

From every branch they hung red apples and gilded nuts. There were sugar-plums in bright papers, baskets of sweetmeats, gingerbread men, wax dolls, drums, trumpets, little horses, and strings of strawberries preserved and threaded upon straw. Tiny candles were fastened among his needles; silk ribbons fell from his boughs; and on his very highest point they placed a great golden star.

The Fir-tree scarcely knew himself.

“Ah!” he thought, “if only the Trees of the forest could behold me now! If only the Hare could see that I am no longer to be jumped over! What dignity! What brilliance! And tomorrow—what then? Surely this is only the beginning.”

As evening came, the candles were lit.

What light! What warmth! Every flame trembled like a golden flower. The Fir-tree stood in such radiance that his needles tingled.

“Take care,” whispered a Candle. “Hold still, or thou wilt burn.”

“Hold still?” said the Fir-tree. “I would not move for all the world. I am shining!”

The doors flew open, and a troop of children rushed in. They stopped with wide eyes; then they shouted, clapped, and danced around the Tree. The room filled with laughter. A little boy beat upon a drum; a girl found a wax doll; another child discovered a paper cornucopia stuffed with sugar-plums.

“How glorious!” thought the Fir-tree. “They have come to honour me!”

Yet the children soon began plundering his branches. Apples vanished, cakes were torn away, toys were unfastened, and paper bags burst with sharp little pops. A boy leaned so heavily against one bough that it cracked.

The Fir-tree felt each tug, but he believed it part of the ceremony.

“Courage!” he told himself. “Greatness has its burdens.”

When every gift had been taken, the children seated themselves on the carpet. They demanded a tale, and a plump gentleman with spectacles came forward.

“What shall it be?” he asked. “The tale of the Seven Silver Spoons, or the history of Klumpy-Dumpy?”

“Klumpy-Dumpy!” cried the children.

The gentleman settled into a chair.

“Klumpy-Dumpy,” he began, “was born at the bottom of a staircase, though no one could discover how he came there. He owned only one boot, two buttons, and a wooden spoon; nevertheless, he climbed the staircase, married the Princess, and received half the kingdom.”

“Did he truly?” asked a small girl.

“Every word of it,” said the gentleman.

The children laughed and demanded the story again. Thus Klumpy-Dumpy climbed the staircase a second time, married the Princess a second time, and received another half of the kingdom—which, had the tale continued, might have caused difficulties.

The Fir-tree listened with all his needles.

“What a beautiful story!” he thought. “One may begin at the bottom and rise to the highest glory. It happened to Klumpy-Dumpy; why should it not happen to me? I, too, have risen. I, too, wear a golden star. Perhaps tomorrow I shall marry a Princess!”

He knew nothing of marriage, kingdoms, or Princesses, but this made them all the more splendid.

At last the children were sent to bed. The candles were extinguished, one by one, and the servants left the room.

The Fir-tree stood in darkness.

“Now,” he thought, “I must rest before tomorrow’s honours. Perhaps the court will come. Perhaps I shall be carried through the streets. Perhaps I shall be replanted in a garden made of glass, where Trees wear stars forever.”

He dreamed of the forest, but in his dream the Sunbeams wore golden crowns and the Swallows carried sugar-plums in their beaks.

In the morning, servants entered.

“Now it begins!” thought the Tree.

They pulled him from the tub.

“The next splendour!”

They dragged him upstairs.

“Higher still!”

They opened a narrow door and thrust him into a dark loft beneath the roof.

Then the door shut.

The Fir-tree lay upon his side among broken chairs, dusty boxes, cracked jars, and bundles of old cloth. There was no daylight except a thin grey line through a hole in the tiles.

“What does this mean?” he wondered. “I cannot be expected to grow here. Perhaps I am hidden so that my next appearance may cause greater astonishment.”

He waited.

All day he listened for footsteps. At night the cold entered through the roof. He thought of the Christmas candles and tried to remember every flame.

“One splendid evening,” he said. “Surely such an evening cannot stand alone. Something better must follow.”

A week passed. Then another.

“Perhaps winter ceremonies require patience,” he thought.

One night, two Mice crept from behind a chest. Their whiskers trembled.

“Good evening,” said the first Mouse. “Art thou newly arrived?”

“From what pantry dost thou come?” asked the second. “Was there bacon? Were there cheeses?”

“I come from the forest,” replied the Fir-tree with dignity, “and most recently from a magnificent drawing room.”

“The forest!” cried the Mice. “Is that a cupboard?”

“No. It is a vast place where the earth is covered with moss and flowers; the Sun shines through green branches, and Birds fly wherever they please.”

“Were there cheeses?”

“No.”

“Bacon?”

“No.”

The Mice looked disappointed, but they had never heard of a forest, and therefore asked him to describe it.

The Fir-tree told of summer mornings, of the Brook slipping over stones, of Wild Pigeons cooing high above, of children gathering strawberries, and of the little Hare that had once jumped over him.

As he spoke, the loft seemed less dark. He could almost feel warm air moving through his branches.

“How delightful thou wert there!” said the first Mouse.

“How happy thou must have been!” said the second.

The Fir-tree became silent.

Happy? He had not thought himself happy. He had been too small, too impatient, too eager for distant wonders.

“Yes,” he said slowly, “perhaps it was pleasant.”

“Tell us more!”

So he told them of Christmas Eve: the candles, toys, cakes, children, and the golden star. He told them the tale of Klumpy-Dumpy, who began at the bottom of the stairs and married the Princess.

The Mice were enchanted.

“What happened next?” they asked.

“That I do not know,” said the Fir-tree. “But doubtless it was something still grander.”

Night after night they returned, bringing other Mice with them. The Fir-tree repeated his memories until every Sunbeam appeared brighter than it had ever been, every Strawberry sweeter, every child kinder. Even the Hare became dear to him.

“If I were once more in the forest,” he thought, “I would rejoice. I would spread my branches to the Sun. I would listen to the Birds. I would be happy in the present—yes, indeed!”

But immediately another thought followed:

“When they take me from this loft, I shall do all that. Perhaps they will light me again first.”

One evening two large Rats came to hear him.

“Canst thou tell a story about tallow?” asked one.

“Or bacon rinds?” asked the other.

“I can tell of Klumpy-Dumpy,” said the Fir-tree. “He climbed a staircase and married a Princess.”

“Does it concern food?”

“No.”

“Then it is a very poor story,” said the Rats, and went away.

The Mice were offended by this judgment, but after a while even they ceased to come. There were crumbs elsewhere, and novelty is a delicate meal upon which no Mouse can live forever.

The Fir-tree was alone.

“Soon,” he whispered whenever footsteps sounded below. “Soon they will remember me.”

Winter loosened its hold. Rain tapped upon the roof. Then came warmer winds, and Sparrows chattered near the tiles. A Sunbeam entered through the hole and touched one branch.

“Rejoice,” it said, just as Sunbeams had always said.

“Oh, take me out!” begged the Tree. “Only take me into the light, and I shall never again despise it!”

At last the loft door opened. Men seized him and dragged him down the stairs.

“Now!” cried the Fir-tree within himself. “Life begins again!”

He was carried into the courtyard and thrown upon the stones.

The sky was blue. Fresh grass grew by the wall. Swallows darted overhead, and the air smelled of spring.

The Fir-tree stretched toward it—but his branches would not lift. They were dry, brittle, and yellow. Most of his needles had fallen in the loft; those that remained dropped around him at the slightest touch.

Children ran into the yard.

“Look!” cried one. “The old Christmas Tree!”

“How ugly he is!” said another.

A boy saw the faded golden star still tied to the topmost twig. He tore it away and placed it upon his cap.

“There! I am a king!”

The children danced upon the Tree’s branches. Crack! crack! went the dry wood beneath their shoes.

“Do not!” thought the Fir-tree. “I carried your toys! I shone for you!”

But the children did not hear him. Why should they? Children hear many things, but seldom the regrets of old Trees.

Then the Fir-tree remembered the forest: the cool moss around his roots, the Dew trembling upon him, the Wind calling through the Pines. He remembered the little children saying, “What a pretty Tree!” He remembered the Hare, the Swallows, the Stork, the Snow, and the patient Sunbeams.

“‘Tis past,” he whispered. “‘Tis past! Why did I not rejoice when I could? Why was the day before me always worthless, and the day to come always bright? My forest is gone. My Christmas is gone. Even the Mice are gone.”

A servant came with an axe and chopped the Fir-tree into pieces.

Each blow ended a memory.

Here fell the summer morning.  
Here broke the children’s path.  
Here split the golden candles.  
Here vanished Klumpy-Dumpy, staircase, Princess, and kingdom together.

The pieces were carried into the kitchen, where a great fire waited beneath a copper kettle. The servant threw them upon the flames.

The dry wood caught at once.

“Crack! crack!” it cried.

For one instant the Fir-tree blazed more brightly than he had blazed beneath all his Christmas candles. In every flame he saw a picture: the green forest, the silver Brook, the red strawberries, the blue sky, the little Hare leaping in careless joy.

“Oh, had I but known!” he sighed.

Then the fire sank. The servants spoke of supper; the children played in the courtyard, and the boy still wore the golden star upon his cap. Soon nothing remained of the Fir-tree but grey ashes, and from ashes no new hope arose.

His forest days were ended, his splendid evening was ended, his waiting was ended; and so, too, is the tale—for every tale must end, though happiness often ends before we have learned to call it by its name.

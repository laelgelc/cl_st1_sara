# The Chivalry of Fire

There are some who imagine that chivalry departed from the world when the last knight laid aside his lance, when castles ceased to be defended by drawbridges and moats, and when men no longer rode forth in armor to succor distressed ladies or to maintain the cause of an injured prince. But the outward fashion of a virtue is not the virtue itself. Steel breastplates may rust, banners may moulder, and the names of ancient orders may become little more than ornaments in books; yet truth, honor, faithfulness, mercy, and self-devotion cannot perish while there remain human hearts capable of preferring duty to life.

Listen, then, to certain stories in which there are no enchanted castles, no giants, and scarcely a sword drawn in single combat. Their terrors are fire, gunpowder, drowning, battle, and the blind pressure of multitudes; and their heroes are soldiers, sailors, women, and humble men whose titles were written, not upon shields, but in deeds.

During the unhappy Irish rebellion of 1641, when old friendships were torn asunder, lonely houses were surprised, and neither infancy nor age was always spared by the fury of civil contention, a little child named John Edgeworth fell into the hands of the rebels. His family had been marked out for destruction; and the infant, who could neither comprehend his danger nor plead for mercy, seemed likely to share their fate.

Among the rebels was Bryan Ferral, an Irish servant who had been attached to the Edgeworth household. He knew that any open attempt to preserve the child would bring death upon himself and probably fail of its object. The rebels were suspicious; compassion toward those whom they regarded as enemies might be punished as treachery. Bryan therefore assumed an air of ferocity, seized the poor infant by the leg, and declared that he would rid them of the young heretic in a manner terrible enough to satisfy even the most revengeful among them.

He carried the child away as if for execution.

The others, deceived by his rough words and savage countenance, allowed him to pass. But when he had reached a lonely part of the bog, beyond the observation of the camp, his manner changed. He concealed the infant among the reeds and coarse grass, in such shelter as the place afforded, and returned alone. Perhaps he stained his hands or clothes with mire; perhaps he added some dreadful expression to persuade his companions that the deed had been accomplished. Whatever means he employed, his pretence succeeded.

Yet the rescue was only half achieved. A bog was no home for an infant; cold, hunger, or the rising water might destroy what the rebels had spared. When darkness had fallen, Bryan stole back, found the child alive, wrapped him as carefully as circumstances permitted, and prepared to carry him through the very camp of his enemies.

It was a fearful passage. A cry from the infant, a question from a sentinel, or one ray of light falling upon the little face might have betrayed them both. Bryan, however, moved among the rebels with the boldness of one who had nothing to conceal. The safest disguise is sometimes an undisturbed countenance. He passed from watch-fire to watch-fire, bearing beneath his cloak the life he had preserved, until at length he reached the place where the child’s mother lay concealed, and restored her son to her arms.

Here was chivalry without helmet or plume. Bryan Ferral had no hope of renown, no applauding spectators, and no reward before him. Indeed, had his purpose been discovered, his only meed would probably have been instant death. But gratitude and mercy were stronger in him than fear; and by appearing cruel for a few moments he was enabled to perform an act of most faithful tenderness.

The rescued child grew up, and became the ancestor of a family in which courage, though it did not always assume the same form, seemed to descend like an inheritance. John Edgeworth himself was high-spirited, generous, and honorable, though by no means prudent in the management of his affairs. He spent money as if fairies replenished his purse in the night, and probably believed, like many other agreeable but improvident persons, that tomorrow had been created chiefly for the payment of today’s debts. Yet beneath this recklessness there was sound principle; and his household possessed in his wife a courage steadier than his own.

One evening, during an entertainment, a servant went to obtain a candle. Candlesticks, it appears, were not immediately at hand; and the man, with that singular stupidity which sometimes turns the commonest action into a mortal danger, stuck the lighted candle into what he supposed would serve as a convenient support.

The support was a barrel of gunpowder.

Whether the barrel stood open, or whether loose powder had been scattered near the place where the candle was fixed, the danger was equally frightful. The flame burned downward; the wax guttered; and a single spark might in one instant have blown the house, its inmates, and perhaps the neighboring buildings into the air.

The servant, discovering what he had done, uttered a cry and fled. Others followed his example. Panic ran through the rooms more quickly than fire could have done; doors were opened, voices rose, and each person thought only of escaping before the explosion.

Lady Edgeworth alone retained her composure.

She went straight toward the barrel.

There is a species of courage which springs from excitement, and another which consists in perfect command over every movement at the very moment when one false motion would be fatal. Lady Edgeworth’s was of the latter and rarer kind. She did not snatch at the candle, for haste might have shaken a spark into the powder; she did not scream for assistance, since terror in others could only increase the danger. Advancing calmly, she lifted the light from its perilous socket, carried it to a place of safety, and then returned to reassure the household.

She had probably occupied fewer moments in saving them than the servant had occupied in running away; yet in those moments her life hung upon the steadiness of her hand. Let no one say that fortitude belongs only to the battlefield. To stand beside an open barrel of powder and remove from it a burning candle requires as true a spirit as ever faced cannon or cavalry.

From this family came, in a later generation, the Abbé Edgeworth, whose name is inseparably connected with one of the most solemn scenes of the French Revolution. He remained with Louis XVI when human friendship had become dangerous, accompanied the unfortunate King to the scaffold, and strengthened him in the last terrible hour. Whether we consider the priest, the monarch, or the furious multitude surrounding them, we cannot fail to honor the faithful man who did not abandon his sovereign when crown, power, and hope had vanished together. Thus the mercy of Bryan Ferral preserved a child from whom there descended one able, long afterward, to render consolation in the face of death.

Our next story returns to gunpowder.

In the year 1804 a fire broke out in a fort in Jersey. Within its walls were stored hundreds of barrels of powder, besides cartridges and other ammunition. The flames, driven by the air and fed by combustible materials, advanced toward the magazine; and it required no great knowledge to understand what must follow if they reached it. The fort would be destroyed, the garrison annihilated, and a great part of the town overwhelmed by the explosion.

Men naturally shrank from a place where every second might be their last. An enemy can be watched, resisted, or avoided; but no skill can parry the bursting of hundreds of barrels of powder. Yet Edward Touzel, a civilian, perceived that if all fled, destruction was certain, while if a few remained there was still a possibility of saving many lives.

“No matter,” was in effect the answer of his conduct, if not of his lips; “some one must stay.”

Lieutenant Lys shared his resolution; and a soldier named William Ponteney proved himself worthy of both. The three entered upon their desperate labor. Smoke rolled through the passages; heat beat against their faces; burning fragments fell around them; and beneath or beside them lay the ammunition which a single spark might ignite. They dragged away combustible matter, carried water, beat down flames, and worked where every instinct of self-preservation cried out to them to fly.

Those outside could render little assistance. They watched the smoke, expecting at any moment to see the walls rent apart and the brave men within disappear in a column of fire. But Touzel, Lys, and Ponteney did not desert their post. Again and again they approached the most dangerous point, until at length the progress of the flames was arrested and the magazine secured.

Then, and not till then, were they at liberty to consider what they had risked.

The garrison and town owed their preservation, under Providence, to three men who had deliberately remained where death appeared almost certain. It is easy after danger has passed to speak of what ought to have been done. The true test is whether, while the beams are burning and the powder lies beneath them, a man can command his limbs to advance rather than retreat.

In that same year His Majesty’s ship *Hindostan* was overtaken by fire at sea. Of all calamities that can befall a vessel, fire is perhaps the most appalling. Water surrounds the sufferers, yet may not be brought in sufficient force against the flames; the decks become furnaces; the rigging catches overhead; and the powder magazine, hidden below, threatens a destruction swifter than either sea or flame.

The alarm was given—“Fire!”—and every officer and sailor sprang to his duty. Smoke poured from the lower decks so densely that a man descending into it could scarcely see his own hand. It filled the lungs, bewildered the senses, and caused the strongest to stagger. Nevertheless, the officers repeatedly went below, striving to discover the seat of the fire and direct the streams of water upon it. Some emerged almost insensible, gasped for breath in the open air, and then attempted once more to descend.

There was no confusion of duty with safety. Boats had to be prepared without creating a panic; ammunition had to be guarded; the wounded or exhausted required aid; and all the while the fire spread through the timbers upon which every life depended. No one knew whether the next minute would bring an explosion beneath their feet.

Their exertions could not save the ship. There are occasions when courage wins safety, and others when it can win only honor; but honor is not a small victory. Discipline was maintained, the boats were lowered, and the crew escaped from the burning vessel. The *Hindostan* was lost, yet those who had committed themselves to the suffocating darkness below had not labored in vain, for their perseverance gained the time necessary to preserve their comrades.

Eight years afterward, when the British army captured Ciudad Rodrigo in 1812, a church was used as a magazine. War had left within it powder, cartridges, and scattered ammunition; and amid these materials a fire was discovered.

Captain William Jones and a sergeant entered the building. Loose powder lay where sparks could reach it; burning matter had already fallen dangerously near; and the church, instead of being a sanctuary, had become a vast mine prepared to destroy conqueror and conquered together. There was no leisure for elaborate precautions. The flames must be removed from the powder, or the powder from the flames, and the work must be done forthwith.

Captain Jones and the sergeant seized the burning materials and carried them out. They returned for more. Their clothes were exposed to sparks; their hands were scorched; and the air within the church was clouded with smoke. Each load might be the last, for the slightest ignition of the loose powder would communicate with the larger store.

Still they continued until the danger was mastered.

The deed may be told in a few lines, but one should not therefore pass lightly over it. Imagine the black grains underfoot, the red embers above them, the knowledge that running would probably save oneself while remaining might save hundreds, and then judge whether those two men deserved the name of brave. Courage is not made less noble because its instrument is a bucket or a pair of scorched hands rather than a sword.

In 1820 a yet more frightful danger called forth the virtues of a French officer named Mathieu Martinel. A barrack at Strasbourg took fire. Beneath part of the burning building was a powder store, and above it lay nine sick soldiers, helpless in their beds. Cartridges were exposed to the increasing heat; should they ignite, the fire would be carried to the powder beneath, and the whole building must infallibly be destroyed.

Those who approached were driven back by smoke and falling fragments. Martinel, perceiving what was at stake, forced his way into the barrack. He had not merely to escape the flames, but to seek the very point toward which they were advancing. The heat scorched him; sparks lodged upon his uniform; passages with which he may have been familiar had become strange in the darkness; and every cracking beam sounded like the announcement of immediate ruin.

He reached the cartridges and removed the danger before it could communicate with the powder. By this act the nine sick men—unable even to attempt their own deliverance—were saved, as were many others near the barracks.

Martinel’s courage was not an impulse peculiar to fire. On another occasion he saw a soldier carried away by a violent current toward a mill wheel. The drowning man struggled helplessly; and any one who went to his assistance must share the force of the water and the fearful danger of being dragged beneath the machinery.

Martinel sprang in.

He grasped the soldier, but the stream was too powerful to permit a direct return to the bank. Both men were swept onward. Before them turned the mill wheel, whose paddles beat the water and threatened to crush whatever passed beneath. To release the drowning man might have afforded Martinel some chance of saving himself; but he held fast. Together they were drawn under the wheel.

For a moment they vanished.

Those upon the bank must have believed both lost; but Martinel had calculated, or at least accepted, the only course left open to him. Still retaining his hold, he allowed the current to carry them beneath the terrible obstruction and rose beyond it with the rescued soldier. Bruised, exhausted, and half drowned, he had nevertheless preserved the life entrusted by chance—or rather, as a devout mind may believe, by Providence—to his courage.

Years passed, and Paris became the scene of another public calamity. A vast crowd, collected for festivity or spectacle, was compressed within streets and passages too narrow for its numbers. Those at the rear pressed forward, ignorant that those before them could not move; those in front struggled to escape; women screamed, children disappeared between the bodies of adults, and strong men found their arms pinned to their sides. The multitude ceased to be an assemblage of separate persons and became one struggling, suffocating mass.

A crowd in panic is as merciless as fire, though no individual within it intends harm. The fallen cannot rise; the weak are crushed; and every effort to escape increases the pressure upon others.

Martinel was there.

He and his comrades plunged into the mass, reached persons already fainting, and dragged them toward openings where air could be obtained. The labor was dreadful. A rescuer might himself be caught, thrown down, and trampled; yet Martinel returned again and again. He lifted children above the level of the crowd; he supported women who could no longer stand; he drew men from beneath the feet of those who were themselves struggling for life.

Mounted cuirassiers came to assist. Their appearance, which in battle might have inspired terror, was now a promise of succor. With admirable steadiness they advanced their powerful horses, not recklessly, but with such care that the crowd was gradually divided and channels were opened through it. Some of the soldiers raised children to their saddles; others leaned down to seize the exhausted; and the very strength intended for war became the instrument of mercy.

This is a circumstance which our young readers should remember. Courage does not consist merely in rushing upon danger. The cuirassiers required patience as well as boldness; for one hasty movement of a horse might have killed those whom they sought to save. Martinel, too, evinced not the excitement of a single splendid moment, but that enduring self-devotion which returns to peril after its horrors are fully known. He had faced fire, powder, a mill wheel, and now the blind force of thousands of human beings; and in each case his first thought was for another.

Our last deed belongs to a later and most melancholy page of history.

During the Indian Mutiny, the great magazine at Delhi was placed in imminent danger of capture. Within it were arms, powder, and ammunition sufficient to strengthen the insurgents and prolong the conflict with terrible consequences. Lieutenant Willoughby and the small band with him knew that they could not long defend the place against the numbers gathering around it.

Preparations were therefore made for its destruction.

To blow up a magazine while one’s enemies are entering it is not an act that permits a sure escape. The train may fail; it may burn too rapidly; the defenders may be shot before the signal can be given; or, if the explosion succeeds, friend and foe may perish together. Yet Willoughby understood that the ammunition had been committed to his charge for the service of his country, and that he must not surrender it while any means remained of preventing its capture.

The assault increased. Resistance became hopeless. Still the defenders held their appointed positions, waiting for the fatal command. At last the signal was given.

The train was fired.

In an instant the magazine, with its vast stores, burst upward in a terrific explosion. Buildings shook; smoke and flame rose over Delhi; fragments were hurled through the air; and the weapons which might have equipped an army were destroyed. Willoughby had accepted the sacrifice of his life rather than betray his trust. Though some connected with that dreadful enterprise survived for a time, he did not return to receive the praise of those whom his resolution had served.

There will always be disputes concerning wars, governments, and the passions of troubled periods; but fidelity in the face of death belongs to no party alone. We may lament the necessity while honoring the man who, believing his duty clear, paid its utmost price.

What, then, joins Bryan Ferral in the Irish bog to Lady Edgeworth beside the candle; Touzel in the Jersey fort to the officers of the burning *Hindostan*; Jones in the church of Ciudad Rodrigo to Martinel beneath the mill wheel; the cuirassiers in the Paris crowd to Willoughby at Delhi?

It is not nation, rank, or occupation. One was a servant, another a lady; some were British, some Irish, some French; some served kings, some rescued common soldiers, and some acted for helpless persons whose names history has not preserved. The bond between them is that, at the decisive moment, they valued another’s life, or an honorable trust, above their own safety.

Nor were they ignorant of the danger. True courage is not blindness. Lady Edgeworth knew what a spark would do; Touzel knew the contents of the fort; the officers of the *Hindostan* understood the smoke into which they descended; Martinel saw the mill wheel; and Willoughby knew what it meant to fire the train. Their glory lies precisely in this—that they understood and did not turn away.

The old knights promised to defend the weak, to speak truth, to keep faith, and to face death without dishonor. Their descendants need not wear armor to fulfil the same vow. Whoever protects a child at the peril of his life, whoever remains faithful to the forsaken, whoever commands fear that others may be saved, and whoever gives himself for duty possesses the essential spirit of chivalry.

For it was said by an authority higher than any code of knighthood, “Greater love hath no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends.”

And while such love survives, the age of golden deeds can never wholly pass away.

At noon the galley lay becalmed within sight of Bugia, her long red oars drawn in and ranged above the water like the folded legs of some exhausted vermin. The sea, which before sunrise had been violet, and afterward green, was now of a whiteness so dazzling that the eye could scarcely distinguish water from light. Very slowly the vessel turned upon her own shadow; very slowly the lateen sail stirred and collapsed against the yard; very slowly the tar softened in the seams and gave forth its bitter smell. Beyond, between two bare promontories, the African town rose in terraces of chalk, saffron, and burned brick, flattened by the heat until its walls, towers, palms, and little square houses appeared enamelled upon a plate. Nothing moved there. Even the gulls had withdrawn into the blue hollows under the cliffs.

Only from beneath the deck came the irregular cough of a man who had been flogged that morning.

Don Hernando de Albornoz, who was nineteen years old, possessed of three estates, two hawks, seven legitimate suits of armour, and a mistress older than himself by eighteen months, slept in the sternsheets beneath a canopy of striped silk. He had removed his gorgeret and doublet, though not his sword. One brown hand lay upon its pommel; the other rested among the black curls of Doña Elvira’s hair, holding them as a child holds the cord of a cherished toy, even in sleep.

Elvira sat beside him, cramped and sweating. Whenever she moved her head, his fingers contracted. It was not a deliberate warning, because he slept; nevertheless she understood it as perfectly as if he had spoken. He loved her. He had said so after stabbing a page who brought her a sprig of orange blossom, and again after ordering an old muleteer whipped for looking too long at her ankles. Only a good man, she reflected, could love with such fierceness. A weak man would share, or pretend not to see, or make verses. Hernando neither shared nor versified.

A drop of sweat slid between her breasts. She watched it darken the gauze of her shift and thought of a fountain in Granada where water ran perpetually from the mouths of marble lions into an octagonal basin. She imagined herself naked in that water, the green shadows of myrtle leaves wavering over her thighs. Then she imagined Hernando finding her there, and the water became warm as blood.

He woke with a little start.

“Why do you tremble?”

“I do not.”

“You were thinking.”

“Of the heat.”

He looked at her as though heat were a rival whose insolence might require punishment. His face was fine-boned and almost girlish, but the lower lip had already acquired the droop of hereditary contempt. Three generations of Albornozes seemed to hang from it: men who had burned villages for disobedience, given jeweled chalices to monasteries, and died affronted by the ingratitude of God.

“Sing,” he said.

“My throat is dry.”

“Then be dry and sing.”

Before she could obey, there sounded from the forepart of the galley a clatter, a shout, and the slap of naked feet upon the deck. Captain Bernat Vives came aft at a run, though his age, his belly, and the dignity of his office protested against running. Behind him trotted a Moorish interpreter with one torn ear and a sailor carrying a boat hook. The captain’s bald crown was purple with heat. He snatched off his cap, bowed first to Hernando, then less deeply to Elvira, and attempted speech; but for some moments produced only breath.

“Well?” said Hernando.

“My lord—a marvel. Or a calamity. The distinction depends upon who pays the harbour dues.”

“Speak without drowning.”

Captain Vives swallowed. “The Mallorcan friar. The preacher. Ramon Lully.”

“What of him?”

“They have stoned him.”

Elvira crossed herself. Hernando yawned.

“Who have?”

“The people of the town, naturally. He came again to dispute with their doctors, though they had expelled him once, twice, perhaps three times—I lose count of holy men’s follies. He preached in the market at daybreak. There was argument, then dung, then stones. A Genoese factor saw it all from a roof. They dragged the body outside the western gate and threw it into a ditch.”

“Then he is dead,” said Hernando.

“As Judas after the fig tree; as Jonah’s gourd after the worm; as my first wife after eating bad mussels.” The captain’s eyes shone. “Dead, my lord—and ours, if we are quick.”

Hernando lifted his hand from Elvira’s hair. She rubbed her scalp secretly.

“Ours?”

“A Christian martyr lying unclaimed in a ditch! Consider. Bones from the Holy Land are brought west in purses and sold joint by joint. A saint from Africa, whole and newly murdered, is another cargo altogether. Minorca would receive us with candles. Barcelona might ring every bell. In Genoa—ah!—the confraternities would tear one another’s beards for the privilege of carrying him ashore.”

“You propose theft.”

“Rescue,” said Vives. “Pious rescue. The theft comes afterward, among bishops.”

Elvira leaned forward. “Was he a holy man?”

“Holy? Madam, he was so holy that no one could endure him long. He converted kings who would not receive him, confuted scholars who could not understand his language, and crossed the sea at an age when a prudent man will not cross a courtyard after rain. He wrote more books than I have had dinners, and I am not ascetic. Saint? If stubbornness is a rung on Jacob’s ladder, he has kicked the angels out of heaven.”

Hernando’s interest remained slight, but the thought of entering a Christian port beside the body of a celebrated martyr had a polished surface upon which his vanity could see itself. People would kneel as he passed. Women would lean from balconies. His mother, who considered this voyage proof of his degeneracy, might be forced to call it providential.

“Bring him,” he said.

The captain bowed. “I have already ordered the boat lowered.”

“You asked my leave after giving the order?”

“I knew your lordship’s goodness would precede your answer.”

“Take care it does not precede you overboard.”

Captain Vives laughed with somewhat unnecessary heartiness and hurried away.

The ship awoke. Men who had lain under benches rose cursing; ropes rasped through blocks; a boat struck the water with a report like a dropped coffin. Five sailors climbed down, armed with cudgels and short knives, and the torn-eared interpreter went with them to bribe, threaten, or mislead anyone who might object. From below came the overseer’s whistle. The chained rowers were roused to move the galley closer inshore. Iron stirred, bare feet scraped, and a hundred bodies bent in darkness.

“Pull, you sons of sores!” cried the overseer. “There’s a saint waiting, and saints hate tardiness.”

A whip cracked.

The galley advanced over the shining sea. With each stroke the oars entered cleanly, vanished to their looms, and rose shedding chains of fire. Elvira watched the town grow larger. She could now see cloth awnings, a cupola glazed blue, and black marks which were perhaps people upon the wall. The sun gave everything a hard and merciless definition: every crack in the masonry, every patch upon the sail, every blister on the sailors’ shoulders. Nothing was permitted mystery except the dark between decks.

“Tell us about this Lully,” Hernando called to Captain Vives, who stood directing the boat through the shoals.

The captain glanced around. “I must attend to the coast, my lord.”

“You have a pilot.”

“The pilot is a drunkard.”

“And you?”

“A drunkard with responsibilities.” He surrendered the tiller to the pilot and came aft. “What do you wish to know?”

“How does a Mallorcan become a saint in an African ditch?”

“By a road no sensible mule would take.” Vives seated himself upon a coil of rope. He loved stories, particularly those in which he possessed more knowledge than his listeners, and most particularly when his listeners were noble enough to make contradiction dangerous. “In his youth, Ramon was a courtier. A pretty fellow, they say, perfumed, curled, and slippery as an eel in oil. He made songs to other men’s wives, gambled till dawn, and considered fasting an invention of ugly women. He had a wife of his own, which naturally increased his appetite for other people’s.”

Hernando smiled faintly. Elvira did not.

“One lady resisted him,” the captain continued. “That was her mistake. A woman’s refusal is a door, and every young fool believes doors were created to be opened. Ramon wrote verses. She burned them. He bribed her maid. She dismissed the maid. He serenaded beneath her window. She emptied a basin into the street—water, they say, though pious versions improve it to filth.”

“Was she beautiful?” Elvira asked.

“The most beautiful woman in Palma, according to Ramon, who had not yet seen the most beautiful woman in Valencia, Naples, Tunis, or whichever port he entered next. She was married, pale, grave, and devout. Her name changes with the teller. Let us call her Ambrosia, because no one else does.”

The pilot shouted. The galley swerved from a reef, and for a moment the sun flashed green through water under the hull.

“One night,” said Vives, steadying himself, “Ramon followed her to a church. She prayed; he sighed. She went home; he followed. Her servants shut the gate; he climbed a wall. She fled to her chamber; he came after her with a love song between his teeth and one boot lost among the rosebushes. At last she turned upon him.

“‘You desire me?’ she asked.

“‘More than salvation.’

“‘Then look at what you desire.’

“She tore open her gown.”

Vives paused, pleased by Elvira’s apprehension and Hernando’s sharpened attention.

“One breast was white and sound. The other had been eaten by a cancer. It was black at the nipple, red at the edges, broken into ulcers, and the flesh below it showed through like meat left too long in brine. The smell struck him before the sight. He had praised those breasts as twin apples, twin moons, twin doves—poets are forever making two things of what should content them as one—and here was his moon suppurating, his apple wormed, his dove carrion.”

Elvira pressed an arm over her bosom.

“She said, ‘This is the beauty for which you would damn us both.’

“Ramon ran. Whether he ran from sin or smell, only God can say. For three days he shut himself in his chamber. He drank no wine. He touched no woman. He saw corruption in everything: grease floating on soup, mould under bread, yellow teeth behind a smile, worms in peaches, sweat in the armpit of a dancing girl. He looked at his own hand and imagined the nails loosening from it in the grave. Then Christ appeared to him.”

“Conveniently,” said Hernando.

“Christ knows when a man has been softened by terror. After that Ramon sold his silks, abandoned court, learned Arabic from a slave, studied logic, theology, herbs, numbers, and the properties of metals. He wished to prove the faith by reason as one proves the weight of an anchor. He also practiced alchemy.”

“To make gold?” Hernando asked.

“To make himself poor. The ordinary result.”

The captain wiped his face. “He believed all things desired perfection: lead sighed to become silver, silver longed for gold, and man, who contains a fouler mixture than any crucible, might by fire and discipline be rendered incorruptible. Sulphur, salt, mercury; body, reason, love. Boil them, cleanse them, marry them in the proper vessel, and out comes wisdom—or smoke, which is what generally came from his furnace.”

“Yet he was learned,” said Elvira.

“Madam, learning is the art of naming the smoke. He wrote systems with wheels inside wheels, letters around triangles, attributes joined to virtues and virtues to elements. Pope, imam, Jew, and heathen were to gaze upon these figures and cry, ‘Of course!’ Instead they asked him to leave.”

A sailor at the rail called that the boat had reached the shore. Through the quivering light they saw the men leap out and drag it upon a strip of stones. Then they vanished beyond an outcrop.

Hernando lay down again. “Wake me when they bring him.”

Elvira looked at him. His lids closed at once, as though obedience from the world were a curtain he could draw between himself and tedium. She envied this power. Yet when she shifted away, his hand found her ankle and held it.

Captain Vives ordered preparations for the martyr. The carpenter produced two planks and protested that they had been intended for repairing the forecastle. Vives told him that a ship carrying a saint would not sink, and the carpenter replied that this was why saints commonly traveled in other men’s ships. After dispute, oaths, and reference to the carpenter’s mother, a bier was made. Sailcloth was spread over it; Elvira gave a length of crimson damask from her baggage; Hernando, without opening his eyes, contributed nothing.

The captain arranged candles at the corners.

“They will melt,” said the boatswain.

“They are ceremonial.”

“They are bending already.”

“Then let them bend reverently.”

A little cross was nailed above the head of the bier, and two sailors scrubbed bloodstains from the deck beside it. These were not the martyr’s bloodstains but those of a Turkish rower who had bitten an overseer three days earlier. The stains had resisted seawater, sand, vinegar, and blasphemy. Vives ordered the damask drawn over them.

The waiting lengthened. Heat pressed upon the ship until the mast itself appeared to bend. The town began to murmur; perhaps the market had reopened, perhaps word of the Christians’ boat had spread. On the walls appeared several white-robed figures. A horn sounded once and was silent.

Elvira tried to pray for the dead man. Instead she thought of the diseased breast. Beneath her own skin, what secret blackness might even now be flowering? She touched herself furtively. The flesh was smooth, hot, and tender. Hernando opened one eye.

“What are you doing?”

“Nothing.”

“You touch what is mine.”

She lowered her hand. “Forgive me.”

He closed his eye, satisfied. He was a good man, she reminded herself; he wished to preserve her from vanity.

At last the boat reappeared. It came heavily, four men rowing while two knelt around a long shape in the bottom. The fifth sailor stood in the bow waving his cap.

“We have him!” he shouted. “By the Virgin’s drawers, we have the old crow!”

“Mind your tongue!” cried Vives. “He is a blessed martyr.”

“He weighs like a bishop!”

The boat struck the galley. Ropes went down, and the body, wrapped in a cloak, was lifted awkwardly. As it rose, one grey hand fell free. The fingers were smashed; blood, still red, ran from them.

“Fresh,” said the boatswain approvingly. “Very fresh.”

They laid Ramon Lully upon the bier. He was an exceedingly old man, small and wasted, his beard white but clotted brown where the stones had broken his mouth. One temple was laid open. The scalp above it showed pale as parchment beneath blood and dust. His robe hung in strips, exposing ribs striped purple and black. Yet even ruined, his face retained something not of peace—for it was contracted with pain—but of attention, as if death were a difficult argument whose least proposition he intended to hear.

Captain Vives removed his cap. Sailors crossed themselves. Elvira knelt.

Then the martyr breathed.

It was a shallow breath, a catching of the throat; but everyone heard it. The boatswain jumped backward into a candle. The carpenter shouted that he had always said the man was not dead, though he had said nothing of the kind. One sailor drew his knife, perhaps from fear that a living saint was more dangerous than a dead one.

Ramon’s eyelids moved.

“Water,” he whispered.

Captain Vives stared in dismay. Life had transformed a priceless relic into a sick passenger. Then devotion, hospitality, and calculation struggled briefly in his face.

“Water!” he roared. “Do you let a saint parch, you dung-fed pirates? And not from the forward cask. The sweet one.”

Elvira held the cup. Ramon drank little; most ran through his beard. His eyes, clouded but keen, passed over her face and came to rest upon Hernando, who had risen and was fastening his doublet.

“Where?” Ramon asked.

“On a Christian galley, Father,” said Vives. “Safe among faithful men.”

Below deck the whip cracked, followed by a cry.

Ramon listened. “Faithful,” he repeated.

“We rescued you from the heathen.”

“From one ditch to another vessel.” His voice was thin, yet it carried. “That is often the shape of rescue.”

Hernando came nearer. “You are Ramon Lully?”

“I have carried that name.”

“They say you sought martyrdom.”

“I sought to speak. Other men chose the stones.”

“You will die of them.”

“Yes.”

Hernando considered this answer with irritation. Death, to his mind, ought to make people solemn, grateful, or afraid; the old man seemed merely tired.

Elvira asked, “Father, do you suffer?”

“The body suffers. I have spent many years pretending it was a servant. Now it tells me it was always a companion.”

Vives hovered near the bier. “We will carry you to Minorca. Unless Genoa offers—unless the weather advises Genoa. You shall have physicians, processions, bells.”

“No physicians.”

“Certainly not, if you prefer sanctity.”

Ramon looked toward the hard white town. “How many were hurt?”

“Of your attackers?” Hernando said. “Why care?”

“Hatred throws stones in both directions, though only one skull may bleed.”

“They wished to kill you.”

“I wished to destroy their error. Each of us named the other mercifully while sharpening a weapon.”

Captain Vives made a troubled face. This was not proper martyr’s talk. Martyrs should condemn infidels, forgive them magnificently, and die before confusing the arrangements.

Ramon’s gaze returned to Elvira. “You are afraid.”

She wanted to deny it, but his broken face made lying seem childish. “The captain told us of the lady in Palma.”

“Ah.”

“Was it true?”

The old man closed his eyes. For some time only the creaking of the galley could be heard.

“Truth,” he said at last, “is not the same metal after it has passed through memory. I desired a woman. She showed me the wound that was consuming her. Whether she bared it in anger, pity, or despair, I did not know. I believed then that God had torn a curtain before my eyes. I saw beauty rotting; therefore I called beauty false. I smelled corruption; therefore I called desire corruption. I fled from her pain and named my flight conversion.”

Vives shifted uneasily. “Yet you became holy.”

“Did I? I left a sick woman alone because her flesh instructed me more loudly than her soul. Afterward I spent sixty years trying to love mankind in the abstract.”

Elvira looked down. Hernando’s hand had settled upon her shoulder, heavy and proprietary.

Ramon continued, his speech strengthening for a little as a flame rises when the last oil reaches it. “I entered libraries, courts, cloisters, deserts. I learned words for God in tongues that cursed one another. I drew circles and ladders. I set Goodness beside Greatness, Eternity beside Power; I turned wheels of parchment and believed creation would turn with them. I sought a universal art by which no mind could refuse truth.”

“Did you find it?” Elvira asked.

“I found that men refuse most fiercely what they cannot pretend to misunderstand.”

A sailor laughed, then, seeing no one else laugh, began to cough.

“I studied the alchemists,” Ramon said. “They taught that every metal contains a hunger for gold. Lead is not evil; it is gold impeded by coldness, impurity, an ill marriage of its principles. Brass is noisy desire. Iron is anger bound into strength. Copper takes the image of whatever hand beats it. Silver reflects light but cannot keep it. Gold alone neither rusts nor stinks nor yields its nature to the grave.”

Hernando glanced at the rings upon his fingers.

“I thought the soul likewise might be refined. Burn away lust, wash out pride, separate hatred from courage, falsehood from imagination, possession from love. Heat the man in suffering, dissolve him in reason, fix him through faith; then perhaps, from the blackened vessel, there would emerge something incorruptible.”

“Gold,” said Elvira.

“Gold—or a pinch of ash.”

Below them came the measured groan of the rowers. The galley had turned from the coast; the sail, filled by the first faint breath of evening, made a dry sound overhead. Bugia began to recede, though the light still held it close.

Ramon looked at Hernando. “You love this woman?”

“She belongs to me.”

“That was not my question.”

Hernando’s face cooled. “Take care, old man.”

“Of what? My future?”

Captain Vives made a placating gesture. “Father, his lordship gave orders for your rescue.”

“Then I owe him gratitude. Gratitude is not blindness.”

Elvira felt Hernando’s fingers tighten upon her shoulder. She feared his anger; but beneath fear, shamefully, there stirred another feeling, light and cool as wind beneath a door.

Ramon said, “Young lord, possession is the counterfeit coin of love. It has love’s image stamped upon it and passes in dark markets, but bite it and you taste brass. You guard her because you fear that another man’s desire will reveal her freedom. You wound those who look at her and call the wound devotion. Yet the beast chains what it cannot cherish, and the miser locks away what he cannot enjoy.”

Hernando’s hand moved to his sword.

Elvira whispered, “He is dying.”

“All men are dying.”

“But he sooner.”

The absurdity of this distinction delayed Hernando. He let the sword alone.

Ramon turned to her. “And you call cruelty goodness because terror is easier to bear when dressed in virtue. The captive praises the lock lest she hear the emptiness outside the door. Do not hate him for his chains. Hatred is another chain, carried inward.”

“What should I do?” she asked.

The old man smiled, and blood shone between his teeth. “If I knew how another soul should live, I would be younger than I am.”

For a while he seemed exhausted. Elvira wiped his face with a cloth. Hernando stood apart, offended less by the accusation than by the fact that she had heard it. He imagined releasing her, then imagined her happiness at being released, and this was intolerable. He imagined killing the old man, but one did not stab a martyr one had rescued; the story would be difficult to tell at court.

The sun descended. Its red image lengthened toward them over the water, broke under the oars, and gathered itself again behind the stern. Shadows returned to the world. The African hills became purple and unexpectedly tender. From the galley’s wake rose a smell of salt and weeds; from the hatch, sweat, ordure, lamp oil, and men.

Ramon breathed more quietly.

“I have failed them,” he said.

“Whom?” asked Elvira.

“All whom I wished to save. The Christians desire victory more than truth. The Muslims hear conquest inside our mercy. Princes ask whether faith will enlarge their revenues. Priests defend words whose meaning they have ceased to desire. Scholars refine distinctions while prisoners rot beneath their windows. I preached love with arguments sharpened like knives.”

“You came back,” said Vives. “That counts for something.”

“Does persistence sanctify error?”

“It sanctifies sailors. Or we should never reach port.”

Ramon almost laughed. The sound became a cough.

Captain Vives knelt beside him. His broad, sunburned face had softened. For all his calculations, he was not untouched; only in him, as in most men, reverence and appetite ate from the same bowl.

“Father,” he said, “you must not forepine yourself with doubt. Consider what will be made of your death. Churches will remember you. Children will bear your name. Your books—those which can be understood—will be copied. The stones meant to silence you will proclaim you.”

“And men will kiss my bones?”

“If God wills.”

“Will they free the slaves rowing beneath them?”

Vives glanced toward the hatch. “That is another matter.”

“There is always another matter.”

A gust crossed the sea. The sail bellied; ropes snapped taut; the candles on the bier guttered though they had not been lit. Elvira felt the wind enter her sleeves and cool the sweat along her arms. At once she saw, with the cruel clarity of unattainable things, a valley she had known as a child: chestnut trees above a stream, wet stones under her feet, fern touching her knees. She had thought such coolness permanent then, a natural property of the earth. Now it seemed a paradise more remote than heaven.

Ramon opened his eyes to the darkening sky.

“When I was young,” he said, “I believed corruption lived in the flesh. Later I believed it lived in the will. Then in ignorance, then in false doctrine, then in the disorder of the world. Each time I built a furnace to burn it out. But corruption enters even the desire for purity. We love goodness, and then we wish to own it. We find truth, and then we make it a spear. We honour a holy man, and then we bargain over his bones.”

Captain Vives looked injured. “No bargaining has occurred.”

“Not yet.”

The sailors nearby lowered their heads to hide smiles.

“What remains, then?” Elvira asked.

“To continue the work without claiming the gold. To wash, burn, separate, and begin again. A soul is no coin finished by one striking. Humanity is ore without end.”

“And if nothing is perfected?”

“Then perhaps the labour is the prayer.”

Night came swiftly. One moment the western sea held a lane of copper; the next it was lead, and over it the first stars appeared. They trembled in the wake, drawing long tracks of fire which broke and renewed themselves as the galley moved northward. The African coast sank into darkness. Only a few lamps marked Bugia, small and gold as nail heads.

Ramon asked to be turned so that he might see them.

“Those men stoned you,” Hernando said.

“Yes.”

“Yet you look back.”

“I left words among them. A man always wonders what becomes of his abandoned children.”

The old missionary murmured something in Arabic. The torn-eared interpreter bent close, listened, and crossed himself, although he understood the words better than anyone present.

“What did he say?” Vives asked.

“He asked forgiveness.”

“Of God?”

“Of the town.”

No one spoke after that. Even Hernando was silent, though his silence contained no surrender, only weariness. He drew Elvira away from the bier and made her sit beside him. For a time he did not touch her. Then, almost furtively, he took her wrist. She felt his thumb above her pulse, counting or claiming each beat.

Near midnight Ramon slept. His breath came with long spaces between, and at every silence Captain Vives leaned forward, hopeful and ashamed of hope. But the breath returned. The martyr lived on, inconveniently, into the small hours.

The galley proceeded north beneath a sky crowded with stars. Sometimes the wind died and the slaves were ordered to row; sometimes it freshened and they collapsed over their benches until a lash reminded them that rest, too, belonged to their masters. The sound of blows rose through the deck on which the saint had spoken of gold.

Before dawn the eastern darkness paled. The sea became iron, then pewter, then a luminous grey. Faces emerged from shadow looking older and less guarded. Elvira had slept against a coil of rope. Hernando had covered her with his cloak, though he had tied one corner around his arm. Captain Vives stood beside the bier, calculating distances.

Ramon still breathed, but scarcely. With the first light, his eyelids lifted.

“Morning?” he asked.

“Yes, Father,” said Elvira.

“Then let me sleep.”

His eyes closed. A little serenity entered his battered face, or perhaps daylight merely smoothed the shadows. Elvira waited for wisdom to descend upon them, for Hernando’s hand to loosen, for Vives to order the rowers unchained, for the sea itself to change from base metal into gold. Nothing happened. The galley creaked. A sailor spat overboard. Someone below cursed God in three languages.

Captain Vives touched Ramon’s throat.

“Not yet,” he muttered.

He beckoned the boatswain aside, and the two went aft. They spoke softly at first; then practical anxiety strengthened their voices.

“If he dies by noon,” said Vives, “we can bear west for Minorca. The bishop there is credulous, and the harbour entrance is easy.”

“Genoa pays better.”

“Genoa does not pay. Genoa promises, processes, disputes, and steals. Yet the fame would be greater.”

“In Minorca we might keep a finger.”

“A finger? You butcher! The body must arrive entire.”

“Entire bodies rot.”

“We have salt.”

“Salted saint,” said the boatswain. “That’ll draw pilgrims.”

“Hold your filthy tongue. We might use myrrh. Is there myrrh?”

“There’s pepper.”

“Pepper is merchandise.”

“So is myrrh.”

Vives frowned toward the sleeping old man. “If he lives two days, he may die too near Sardinia. They will claim him there.”

“Throw no line ashore.”

“A death has a geography, fool. Bishops understand geography when relics are concerned.”

Hernando listened without expression. Elvira, remembering the sermon, looked toward the brightening horizon. The sun’s rim rose, pure and molten, and laid a road of gold upon the sea; but beneath that splendour the water remained salt, the chained remained chained, the possessed remained possessed, and Captain Bernat Vives, having rescued a holy man from the stones, continued to calculate with pious care the most advantageous port in which to let him die.

# The Call of the Sneeds

There are certain things which money cannot buy, though the list is less extensive than moralists would have us believe. Money cannot purchase yesterday, nor make a short man tall, nor induce a boiled egg to become fresh again. It cannot confer grandfathers upon a gentleman whose ancestors were industrious but obscure; and, as every well-informed American knows, it cannot compel two maiden ladies of established position to call upon a young woman whom they have decided not to know.

Old Josiah Druce discovered this final limitation late in life, and it shocked him profoundly.

Druce had never respected limitations. In Wall Street, where they called him the Gray Leopard, he had removed so many that it was commonly believed he had a private arrangement with Providence. He had removed competitors, directors, presidents, legislatures, receivers, injunctions, and, on one memorable occasion, the assets of a trust company. A hostile newspaper said he had begun with nothing and had kept most of it; a friendly newspaper replied that character counts for everything in American business. Both journals were substantially correct.

He was a small man with a large head, bushy eyebrows, and little eyes which seemed always to be searching for the price-mark upon whatever they observed. His tread was noiseless. His voice, except during moments of affection, was flat and dry. When thinking, he rubbed his chin with the backs of two fingers, as though sharpening an invisible knife upon it.

He owned railways, mines, steamships, newspapers, forests, judges, and one-third of a United States senator. The remaining two-thirds of the senator belonged to interests friendly to Mr. Druce, so no inconvenience resulted. He had houses in New York, Newport, London, and Paris, and a yacht named the *Mercy*, because, as he once explained, mercy was a luxury most comfortably enjoyed by the possessor.

Yet this singular man loved his only son.

Edward Druce was tall, open-faced, good-tempered, and honest. Old Druce regarded these defects with indulgence. He had hoped Edward would enter the Street and learn business, but after six months the young man confessed that he did not understand it.

“Nobody does,” said his father. “That is why there’s money in it.”

Nevertheless Edward preferred architecture, a profession in which a man puts up buildings for other men who thereafter complain of the plumbing. He went abroad, studied in Paris, returned with sketches, theories, and a wife.

The wife was Ella Vane.

Before her marriage Ella had been an artist’s model—not, as certain severe persons afterward explained, because she was wicked, but because she was beautiful and poor. Poverty is frequently mistaken for immorality by people who have never had the opportunity to compare them. Her father had painted landscapes which nobody bought, and her mother had died while still believing that somebody would. Ella, at eighteen, possessed a straight young figure, gray eyes, a cloud of dark hair, and the inconvenient habit of making pictures better merely by standing in them. She posed as St. Cecilia, Ophelia, Spring, Judith without the sword, and once as Liberty for a municipal mural paid for by a committee whose members were under indictment.

Edward saw her in a studio, loved her in a week, and married her in a month.

Society was astonished. Society is easily astonished by conduct which does not require its permission.

Old Druce was informed of the marriage by cable. He read the message twice, placed it in his pocket, and asked what the Continental Limited was selling at. Then he dictated three orders which caused a decline of six points, ruined a gentleman in Albany, and added approximately nine hundred thousand dollars to his fortune. Having thus restored his composure, he sailed for Europe.

Edward and Ella received him in a small apartment overlooking a chimney. Edward began a speech.

“Father, I know you may feel—”

“How much do you love him?” Druce asked Ella.

Ella turned pale, but she answered him steadily.

“All there is.”

“Humph. That’s too much. He ain’t worth it.”

Edward laughed. Ella did not.

Druce looked at her for a long moment. Those small, shifty eyes, which could detect watered stock through three holding companies and a Jersey charter, perceived what half the fashionable world afterward failed to see. He saw that she was frightened, proud, innocent, and wholly in love with his son.

He held out his hand.

“My dear,” he said awkwardly, “I guess I’m your father.”

From that hour Ella could have had anything in the power of Josiah Druce to obtain. She had pearls, horses, motor-cars, servants, silver, portraits, country houses, boxes at the opera, and a carved Florentine cradle before there was the least practical use for it. She had a Fifth Avenue mansion whose ballroom contained more gold than several respectable national currencies. She had gowns from Paris, hats from London, lace from Brussels, and bills from everywhere. She had a private elevator and a public reputation. Her husband adored her; her father-in-law watched her wishes with the uneasy devotion of a pirate nursing a canary.

And she was miserable.

At first Druce did not perceive it. Men who can hear a whisper in the stock market are often deaf in their own drawing rooms. But one January afternoon he found Ella alone by the library fire, with a silver card-tray on the table and tears in her eyes.

Druce stopped without noise. Stealth was natural to him; if he had entered Heaven, the Recording Angel would probably have missed him.

“What’s the matter, girl?”

“Nothing.”

“Nothing don’t make people cry unless they’re poets. Edward been bad to you?”

“Oh, no!”

“Then I’ll send for him and find out.”

She caught his sleeve.

“Please don’t. Edward has been perfect.”

Druce looked at the card-tray. There were cards from a duchess, an ambassador’s wife, three millionaires’ widows, and a famous actress who had been divorced by two of them. The tray appeared socially nourishing.

“Who hasn’t called?” he asked.

Ella lowered her eyes.

“It is silly.”

“Most important things are. Who?”

“The Misses Sneed.”

Druce rubbed his chin.

There were two Misses Sneed, Honoria and Lavinia. They were unmarried, not because no men had asked them, but because those who had asked were not Sneeds. They lived with their father in a broad brown house on Washington Square, where the furniture had grown respectable through age and uncomfortable through principle. Their great-grandfather had lent a horse to General Washington, or had declined to lend it—accounts differed, but either action was hereditary. Their mother had been a Van Something. Their portraits appeared at charity entertainments, horse shows, weddings, funerals of importance, and other occasions upon which the public is instructed who constitutes society.

They were not rich by Druce’s standard, for they possessed only four or five millions. But their money was old; that is to say, nobody remembered precisely how it had been stolen.

“Why should they call?” asked Druce.

Ella flushed.

“Because everybody follows them. If they came—if they treated me as though I belonged—then the others would stop being kind to me in that dreadful way.”

“What way?”

“As if they were doing something courageous.”

Druce frowned. He disliked courage in other people when exercised at his expense.

“Have you asked them?”

“Not directly. Mrs. Pell spoke to Honoria. Honoria said they knew nothing against me personally.”

“That was handsome.”

“She said their position obliged them to draw a line.”

“What line?”

Ella tried to smile.

“I suppose I am on the wrong side of it.”

Druce walked to the window. Below, Fifth Avenue rolled beneath the snow with its sleighs, carriages, liveries, jewels, pedigrees, mortgages, and sins, all moving uptown at a dignified pace.

“Do you want those two women here?”

“I want not to care.”

“That ain’t the question.”

“Yes,” Ella whispered. “I want them to come.”

“All right.”

He kissed her forehead and went downtown.

General Rufus Sneed had been called the General for thirty years. He had never served in any army, commanded any militia, or faced any danger more violent than a shareholders’ meeting; but the title had been given him at a political convention and was therefore beyond dispute. He was a handsome, white-mustached gentleman with a loud voice, a soft heart, and an unshakable belief in his own firmness.

He was considered honest. This reputation had frequently prevented him from making money, but it enabled him to borrow it.

His offices occupied a corner in a venerable building near Wall Street. The carpets were thick, the clerks were gray, and the waste-baskets were emptied with discretion. On the wall hung a steel engraving of his father, who had organised the Eastern & Inland Railway and had afterward explained its failure to the bondholders in a pamphlet.

Druce entered without appointment.

The General rose with every appearance of welcome and some internal alarm.

“Josiah! This is an honor. Sit down. Cigar?”

“No.”

“I’ve been meaning to look you up. How’s Edward? How’s the charming bride?”

“That’s what I came about.”

The General’s geniality tightened by one degree.

“Indeed?”

“My girl wants your girls to call on her.”

Sneed opened a silver box and selected a cigar for himself. This required consideration. He clipped the end, examined it, lit it, and blew a cloud toward the ceiling, where many difficult subjects had gone before.

“My dear Josiah,” he said, “you know I’m the most liberal man alive.”

“I’ve heard you say so.”

“I have no prejudices. None. I judge every human being on character.”

“Sure.”

“And from all I learn, Mrs. Edward is a delightful young woman.”

“She is.”

“Beautiful, accomplished, devoted—everything one could ask.”

“Then your girls can call Thursday.”

Sneed laughed rather more than the proposal warranted.

“You don’t understand women.”

“No. I’ve been busy.”

“My daughters are independent. They have their own ideas, their own responsibilities, their own—er—position. A father cannot dictate in such matters.”

“You give them money?”

“Naturally.”

“You own the house?”

“Certainly.”

“You pay their bills?”

“They are my daughters.”

“But you can’t make ’em ride twenty blocks and leave two cards.”

“It isn’t a question of physical distance.”

“It never is,” said Druce.

Sneed crossed his legs.

“Let me put it this way. If a man owns a fine horse, he may guide it, feed it, shelter it, even sell it; but he cannot compel it to drink.”

“I’d change the trainer.”

“Women are not horses, Josiah.”

“I’ve noticed they cost more.”

The General laughed again, but his eyes had become anxious.

“You mustn’t take this personally.”

“I never do. I take it in cash.”

“Now, now! The girls have nothing against your daughter-in-law. But society—good society—rests upon certain traditions. Once you break down the barriers—”

“Who put ’em up?”

“Nobody put them up. They grew.”

“So do weeds.”

“Josiah, there are standards.”

Druce leaned forward.

“Are they going to call?”

“I cannot order them.”

“Are they?”

“I’ll speak to them.”

“You’ve spoken already.”

The General hesitated.

“I may have mentioned the matter.”

“What did they say?”

“They felt—well, they felt it inadvisable.”

“Why?”

Sneed looked pained.

“My dear fellow, we needn’t be brutal.”

“Why?”

“There was her former occupation.”

“She worked.”

“Precisely.”

Druce’s eyes narrowed. The General hurried on.

“Of course there is nothing dishonorable in honest work. Nothing! Our country was founded upon it. But one need not invite it to dinner.”

“Are they going to call?”

“I am afraid I cannot promise.”

Druce rose. His face had not changed, but the General experienced the sensation of a man who has stepped upon a railway track and hears something far away.

“Too bad,” said Druce.

“Don’t misunderstand me.”

“I don’t.”

“You and I are old friends.”

“We were until ten minutes ago.”

“Josiah!”

Druce’s mouth moved in what the financial press called his smile.

“I’m joking, General. Drop in sometime.”

He went out.

By three o’clock it was known in Wall Street that Josiah Druce had visited General Sneed. By three-ten it was known that they had discussed the Midland Central. By three-twenty the Midland Central had advanced two points. At four, an evening newspaper announced that Druce and Sneed had formed a gigantic combination to control all rail communication west of Pittsburgh. Neither gentleman owned a share of the Midland Central, but this circumstance was not discovered until after several thousand persons had lost money, and was therefore unimportant.

For two days Druce did nothing.

This disturbed the Street more than action would have done. When a tiger lies still, persons of intelligence do not assume that it has entered the upholstery business.

On Monday he met General Sneed at luncheon in the Metropolitan Club. Druce approached with outstretched hand.

“General! I acted like a fool the other day.”

Sneed, who had spent the weekend wondering which of his properties was most exposed, beamed with relief.

“Not at all, Josiah. Natural feeling. Does you credit.”

“Come sit with me.”

They took a table in a corner. Druce was genial. He asked after Honoria and Lavinia, praised the Sneed family, condemned modern manners, and agreed that social barriers were essential to the republic. He even admitted that Ella’s past might properly distress ladies of delicate sensibility.

“Still,” he said, “she’s a good girl.”

“I’m certain of it.”

“And I’m too fond of her.”

“A charming weakness.”

“It’ll cost me money someday.”

The General smiled.

“That is the common privilege of affection.”

Druce rubbed his chin.

“What are you doing with Great Northwestern?”

Sneed’s hand paused over his glass.

“Why do you ask?”

“Just curious.”

General Sneed and his associates held an enormous block of Great Northwestern securities. The railway ran from valuable places to places which hoped to become valuable, and possessed land grants, terminals, mineral rights, branch lines, floating debt, unfloating debt, and a president with an optimistic vocabulary. Sneed had supported it through two bad seasons. Another decline would compel him to sell; a moderate rise would make him very rich.

“It is sound,” said the General.

“Sound things sink too,” said Druce. “Iron, for instance.”

Sneed studied him.

“Are you interested?”

“Might be.”

“In buying?”

“Might be.”

By dessert they were discussing figures. By coffee they were whispering. At half past two they had returned to Sneed’s office, and at ten minutes before four Josiah Druce agreed to purchase the General’s entire personal holdings in Great Northwestern, together with certain associated bonds and options, for a price so liberal that Sneed’s respect for friendship became almost religious.

Druce wrote a cheque.

It was a tremendous cheque. The General held it for a moment and felt the agreeable weight which figures possess when somebody else has signed beneath them.

“Just one thing,” said Druce. “Don’t put it through till Monday.”

“Monday?”

“My people are shifting balances. The money’s there, but I don’t want the bank bothered Friday. You know how clerks are.”

The bank upon which the cheque was drawn was the Atlantic National, one of the strongest institutions in the city. Its granite walls were six feet thick, its directors had white whiskers, and its advertisements contained the word “conservative” in capital letters. Sneed would as soon have doubted the United States Treasury, though unlike the Treasury, the Atlantic National had never survived an investigation.

“Certainly,” he said. “Your word is enough.”

“No business on my word,” Druce replied. “Take the cheque.”

The securities were transferred that afternoon.

Again the Street learned that Druce and Sneed had conferred. Great Northwestern leaped nine points. London cabled questions; Boston supplied answers without waiting to receive them; Chicago bought everything bearing the word “Northwestern,” including the shares of a dairy company. The newspapers declared that a new epoch of cooperation had begun between the two great financiers. One printed a cartoon in which Druce and Sneed, dressed as Roman consuls, supported the Temple of Prosperity between them.

On Wednesday Druce invited the General to spend the weekend aboard the *Mercy*.

“Run down the coast,” he said. “Fresh air. No tickers. No daughters.”

Sneed hesitated.

“I have several matters—”

“Markets can take care of themselves.”

This was the sort of statement which, coming from Druce, resembled an undertaker’s recommendation of sleep.

“When do we return?”

“Sunday night.”

“Very well. I should enjoy it.”

The *Mercy* lay in the East River, long, black, shining, and innocent as a knife in a velvet case. She could cross the Atlantic without coal, outrun most revenue cutters, and entertain twenty guests without permitting any two to overhear the others. Her captain had once served in a navy and afterward in enterprises concerning which flags were not displayed.

On Friday morning Druce and Sneed went aboard. The day was clear; the harbor flashed; tugs fussed importantly among the larger vessels; and New York rose behind them in gray towers, church spires, smoke, ambition, and unpaid interest.

As the yacht steamed down the bay, Sneed stood at the rail.

“There’s no sight like it,” he said.

“No,” Druce agreed. “Especially when leaving.”

They breakfasted, played cards, and discussed nothing of consequence. Druce was charming. He told stories of early Wall Street—how a certain bank had been saved by locking its doors, how a senator had been convinced by arguments placed in an envelope, how a railway president who insisted upon honesty had been allowed to resign for reasons of health.

At eleven o’clock the *Mercy* passed Sandy Hook.

At eleven-fifteen a broker acting for an unknown principal offered a large block of Great Northwestern.

The market absorbed it.

Another block appeared.

Then another.

The price fell two points.

At noon rumors reached the Exchange that General Sneed had secretly sold his Great Northwestern holdings to Druce and retired from the property. This was true. The Street refused to believe it, truth having no recognised standing until confirmed by a lie.

At twelve-ten it was reported that Sneed had discovered a defect in the railway’s finances.

At twelve-fifteen the stock fell six points.

At twelve-thirty Druce’s brokers began selling Great Northwestern in quantities which suggested that the railway had been discovered to terminate in the ocean. Supporting houses bought bravely. Their bravery lasted nineteen minutes.

At sea, General Sneed finished a cigar and remarked upon the weather.

“A little wind coming,” said the captain.

“Any danger?” Sneed asked pleasantly.

“Not to us,” said Druce.

At one o’clock the assault widened. Stocks known to be held by Sneed interests were thrown upon the market. Lake & Prairie collapsed. Continental Traction sagged. Western Municipal bonds, which had not moved in eleven years, moved downward with surprising agility. Money rates stiffened. Brokers ran through corridors; telephones rang; messenger boys committed collisions; elderly speculators remembered their mothers.

The heart of Wall Street beat faster, then missed a stroke.

Its electric nerve-web carried terror outward. Boston felt a pain in its left side. Philadelphia became cautious. Chicago shouted. London raised an eyebrow and sold. Paris shrugged and sold. Berlin asked for particulars and sold before they arrived. In Kansas, a farmer who had never heard of General Sneed discovered on Monday that the price of his wheat had declined because two young ladies in New York possessed standards.

At half past one a wireless message came to the *Mercy*. The operator handed it to Druce. He read it and put it in his pocket.

“News?” asked Sneed.

“Nothing important.”

They played another hand.

At two o’clock the president of the Atlantic National Bank received certain information. He immediately denied it. At two-ten he issued a statement declaring the bank absolutely sound. At two-twenty the statement reached the Street. At two-twenty-one the run began.

No institution is more sensitive than a bank which has described itself as absolutely sound. Depositors gathered before its doors. Clerks paid out money with pale efficiency. Directors arrived through side entrances. Reporters arrived through every entrance. A clergyman interviewed on the pavement said the panic showed a lamentable want of faith.

At sea, the wind increased.

The General, who prided himself upon an old sailor’s stomach acquired during three crossings to Europe, went below for twenty minutes. When he returned, the *Mercy* was pitching gently.

“Thought I saw a tug signaling us,” he said.

“Probably did,” said Druce.

“Shall we stop?”

“No.”

“It might have dispatches.”

“Captain’s orders are not to stop.”

Sneed looked surprised.

“Whose orders?”

“Mine.”

At three o’clock the Atlantic National suspended payment.

Its president explained that the suspension was temporary, technical, unnecessary, and caused entirely by malicious rumor. The doors were locked while he was speaking. A woman in the crowd fainted; a photographer moved her into better light.

The bank’s failure snapped what remained of the market’s backbone. Great Northwestern dropped twenty-seven points in fifteen minutes. Three brokerage houses failed, then five more. Sneed securities were unsalable at any price, which did not prevent their prices from being printed. Men who had entered the Exchange wealthy came out poor; men who had entered poor came out owing considerably more. One broker laughed for seven continuous minutes and was taken home.

At three-fifteen another wireless message arrived aboard the *Mercy*.

This time Sneed noticed the operator’s face.

“Anything wrong?”

“Market’s a little off,” said Druce.

“How much?”

“Some.”

“I should like to see that message.”

“No.”

The General stared at him.

“My dear Josiah—”

“Sit down.”

There was nothing loud in Druce’s voice, but Sneed sat.

“What is happening?”

“Storm.”

“In New York?”

“Mostly.”

Sneed rose again.

“Captain! Turn her about.”

The captain glanced at Druce.

“Keep your course,” said Druce.

“This is outrageous,” cried Sneed. “I demand that you put back.”

“We’re outside the Hook.”

“You invited me as your guest!”

“That’s right.”

“You have no authority to detain me.”

“Plenty of authority. Law’s the doubtful part.”

The General’s face reddened.

“Are you mad?”

“Not often.”

Sneed strode toward the wireless room. The captain stepped between them, politely.

“Sorry, sir.”

“I will send a message.”

“The apparatus is out of order,” Druce said.

“I saw it working!”

“It went out of order when you wanted it.”

For the first time General Sneed understood that he was not a guest but cargo.

He turned upon Druce.

“What have you done?”

Druce looked toward the horizon. His hands were folded over the head of his cane.

“Bought some securities.”

“You bought mine.”

“Yes.”

“With a cheque on Atlantic National.”

“Yes.”

“And the bank—”

“Closed at three.”

Sneed’s lips moved, but no sound emerged.

The Atlantic National’s collapse did not merely endanger Druce’s cheque. It rendered the cheque, for the present, a handsome scrap of paper. Meanwhile Sneed had delivered the securities. Worse, the market crash had destroyed the value of those he retained and called loans against his properties throughout the country.

“You knew,” he said at last.

“I generally know.”

“You planned this.”

Druce did not answer.

“You thief!”

“That’s an old criticism.”

“You pirate!”

Druce glanced around the yacht.

“More appropriate.”

“I’ll have you imprisoned.”

“Maybe. When we get back.”

“You have ruined me.”

“Probably.”

The General gripped the rail. The sea and sky swung about him, though the yacht was now scarcely rolling.

“Why?”

Druce’s little eyes rested upon him.

“Are they going to call?”

For several seconds the General did not understand. Then comprehension entered his face slowly, like a guest ashamed to be late.

“My daughters?”

“Honoria and Lavinia.”

“You wrecked the market—you broke the Atlantic National—you destroyed my credit—because two women would not leave cards at your house?”

“When you put it that way it sounds petty.”

Sneed looked at him with horror.

“Good God, Josiah!”

“Don’t bring Him in. He ain’t long Great Northwestern.”

The General sank into a chair.

“You devil.”

Druce’s expression remained calm, but there was no triumph in it. He seemed merely a business man who had quoted terms.

“You said you couldn’t make ’em call.”

“No man could!”

“Then I’ll ask them.”

“You cannot purchase social recognition.”

“No. That’s what makes it expensive.”

The *Mercy* continued southward all Friday night. Druce dined with appetite. Sneed did not dine. He demanded a boat, a message, a weapon, and justice, in that order, and received none of them. At midnight he attempted to persuade the captain that piracy was a hanging offense. The captain replied that many things had changed since steam.

Saturday morning brought a bundle of wireless reports, none of which Sneed was allowed to see. He paced the deck while Druce read them.

The reports were grave. The Sneed fortune, built by three generations of prudence, privilege, marriage, and selective memory, had dissolved before luncheon. Loans had been called. Partnerships had failed. Country banks connected with Sneed enterprises had closed. Great Northwestern had gone into receivership. The General’s Washington Square house was mortgaged; his Newport cottage was mortgaged; his securities were pledged; even Honoria’s famous emeralds had been placed with a trust company to support an issue of bonds.

The newspapers, with admirable fairness, blamed everyone.

One blamed the President. Another blamed foreign capital. A third blamed the public for becoming alarmed after being told for six hours that there was no cause for alarm. Druce was described as “watching events with concern.” General Sneed, being absent and therefore unable to deny anything, was accused of precipitating the panic by unloading upon his friends.

On Sunday afternoon the yacht turned north.

Druce approached Sneed, who stood hollow-eyed by the rail.

“We’ll dock after midnight.”

“Afraid to face reporters?”

“I never face reporters after dark. They exaggerate.”

Sneed said nothing.

Druce handed him an envelope.

“What is this?”

“A letter.”

“You are within speaking distance.”

“I want you to read it tomorrow with your girls.”

Sneed tore the envelope open.

“Tomorrow.”

The old financier’s voice was soft. The General put the letter in his pocket.

At one in the morning the *Mercy* docked. No reporters were present, because Druce owned the newspaper which had published the yacht’s expected arrival for Tuesday.

Sneed entered a cab and drove to Washington Square.

Honoria and Lavinia were waiting in the drawing room. They had passed through terror and reached indignation, which is much more becoming. Honoria, the elder, was tall and severe. Lavinia was shorter and equally severe, but wore better colors. Neither had slept.

“Papa!” cried Lavinia. “Where have you been?”

“On a yacht.”

“At a time like this?” said Honoria.

The General looked around the room: the ancestral portraits, the dark cabinets, the old silver, the Chinese jars, the worn carpet upon which important feet had walked for eighty years. By noon much of it might belong to strangers whose grandfathers had walked nowhere worth mentioning.

He seemed suddenly older.

“I was detained.”

“Can nothing be done?” Honoria asked.

“Druce can do something.”

The sisters exchanged a glance.

“What does he demand?” said Honoria.

The General remembered the envelope. He drew it from his pocket and broke the seal.

Druce’s letter was written in a cramped, neat hand.

> My dear General:
>
> Human prosperity is uncertain, and the richest of us may be brought low in a single day. This reflection should teach humility, soften prejudice, and encourage those acts of Christian kindness which cost nothing.
>
> I am informed that your present embarrassments are considerable. As an old friend I grieve to learn it. I am prepared, purely from affection, to return the Great Northwestern securities acquired from you, to make good my cheque notwithstanding the temporary inconvenience at Atlantic National, to support the market in your remaining properties, and to advance such sums as may be required to preserve your house and your daughters’ personal possessions.
>
> I would not insult you by attaching a business condition to these friendly offices. There is, however, a small social misunderstanding which your daughters may wish, of their own free and independent judgment, to correct.
>
> Mrs. Edward Druce receives on Tuesdays from four until six.
>
> It is a beautiful thing to see young women rise above worldly pride. We are all dust, General, and the grave recognises no distinctions, though Fifth Avenue naturally must.
>
> Yours in friendship,
>
> Josiah Druce
>
> P.S. If the girls do not call tomorrow, I shall consider that they prefer poverty. This will be educational for them, and I shall not interfere.

For a long time there was silence.

Lavinia was the first to speak.

“It is blackmail.”

“Yes,” said the General.

“It is infamous,” said Honoria.

“Yes.”

“It is vulgar.”

“Extremely.”

“Can he do what he promises?”

“Yes.”

“And if we refuse?”

The General looked at his daughters.

“The house will be sold. Your securities are gone. The Newport place is gone. The carriages, pictures, plate, and jewels will be sold. We shall probably live with Cousin Martha in Orange.”

Lavinia shuddered. Cousin Martha’s husband manufactured buttons and spoke during meals.

Honoria walked to the fireplace.

“We have always maintained that principle is above fortune.”

“Certainly,” said the General.

“We have said that social standards must not yield to money.”

“Repeatedly.”

“We cannot permit ourselves to be intimidated.”

“Of course not.”

Another silence followed.

“What does one wear for an afternoon call?” Lavinia asked.

The financial situation improved with miraculous speed.

On Monday morning Druce announced that rumors concerning General Sneed’s affairs were unfounded. As Druce had originated most of them, he spoke with authority. He further announced his confidence in Great Northwestern, the Atlantic National, the country’s banking system, and the American people. Confidence rose six points before noon.

Druce deposited funds sufficient to reopen the Atlantic National. Its directors issued a statement crediting their survival to conservative management. Great Northwestern was reorganised under a committee selected by Druce, who restored Sneed’s holdings at a valuation agreeable to both gentlemen after Sneed had ceased objecting. Loans were extended. Mortgages disappeared. The Washington Square house remained in the family. Honoria’s emeralds returned from the trust company morally purified by adversity.

Within a week the newspapers discovered that the panic had been psychological. This relieved everybody, since psychology cannot be indicted.

Druce’s part in the affair was never proved. It is difficult to prove anything against a man who owns the witnesses, has breakfasted with the judge, and can cause the prosecuting attorney’s investments to decline. A congressional committee proposed an inquiry, but the chairman subsequently accepted a railway presidency. The committee’s final report condemned reckless speculation by small investors.

Ella knew nothing of these matters.

Edward had told her that his father and General Sneed were arranging a railway transaction. She noticed that Mr. Druce spent more time than usual at the house, wandering in and out of the drawing room, moving vases, criticising flowers, and asking at what precise hour respectable ladies called.

On Tuesday at three-thirty he appeared in Ella’s sitting room.

“What are you wearing?” he demanded.

She turned from her desk, laughing.

“This.”

It was a simple gray gown.

“Got anything more expensive?”

“Several things.”

“Put one on.”

“Why?”

“No reason.”

He went downstairs and inspected the drawing room again. At three-fifty he ordered the fire built higher. At three-fifty-five he dismissed a footman for breathing pessimistically, then reinstated him because Ella liked him. At four, he stationed himself in the library, leaving the door open two inches.

Ella came down at ten minutes past four in a pale blue dress. She looked young, beautiful, and faintly puzzled.

“Are you expecting someone?” she asked.

“No.”

“Then why are you hiding?”

“I like it here.”

“You never read.”

“Books are a bad habit.”

At four-twenty a carriage stopped before the house.

Druce ceased rubbing his chin.

The front door opened. There was a murmur in the hall, the subdued ceremony of servants handling a fact of historic importance. Then the footman entered the drawing room with two cards upon a silver tray.

Ella looked at them.

Her face changed.

Druce, watching through the narrow opening, saw the color rise into her cheeks and the sudden light come into her eyes. For one moment the Gray Leopard appeared almost human, which would have injured his credit had Wall Street observed it.

The footman drew himself up.

“The Miss Sneeds.”

Honoria entered first, carrying the dignity of six generations and the knowledge that the seventh had nearly been obliged to live in Orange. Lavinia followed with a smile of exquisite correctness. Both advanced toward Ella.

Druce softly closed the library door.

Outside, the market stood firm, the Atlantic National was absolutely sound, General Sneed remained a pillar of integrity, and society preserved its standards.

The Sneed girls called on Mrs. Edward Druce.

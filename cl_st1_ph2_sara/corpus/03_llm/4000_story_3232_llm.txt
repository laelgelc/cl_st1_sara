### THE AMBASSADRESS OF CHANCE

Of the many strange causes which came before Sir Matthew Fane during his last years upon the bench, there were three which he could never recount without abandoning that judicial composure for which he was celebrated. One concerned a dead child who spoke through a clock; another, an emerald that had crossed Europe in the hollow of a pilgrim’s staff; and the third—perhaps the least supernatural, though by no means the least miraculous—was the case of Reginald Pike, butler, accused of murdering his master.

It is the third story which concerns us.

On the evening before the final hearing, Mrs. Pike sat in the private office of Mr. Horace Brindle, solicitor and advocate, and stared at a square of worn carpet between her shoes.

The carpet had once been red. Dust, soot, innumerable boots, and the acrid water of a negligent cleaner had reduced it to a colour resembling dried blood. The room possessed the oppressive warmth of a place in which no fire was visible, yet every object seemed feverish: the mahogany desk, the brass lamp, the leather volumes with their gilt titles, even the pale fingertips which Mr. Brindle pressed together beneath his chin.

Mrs. Pike was thirty-seven years old. During the last fortnight she had acquired the appearance of fifty. Her black dress was brushed but shiny; her gloves had been darned at the thumbs; and under the small veil which she had bought for her mother’s funeral six years before, her face showed the grey exhaustion of a creature that has passed beyond tears into a mental coma.

Mr. Brindle watched her without pity.

“Well?” he said.

She raised her eyes.

“I have brought what I could, sir.”

“What you could,” he repeated. “That phrase has been the death of more men than the hangman.”

From a bag clutched upon her knees she produced an envelope. It contained three sovereigns, several shillings, and a postal order for ten.

Mr. Brindle did not touch it.

“What is that?”

“Everything.”

“No.”

“Everything, sir.”

“Do not use the word with me, my good woman. Your everything varies from day to day. On Monday, eight pounds was everything. On Thursday, there appeared another five. Now we have—what? Three pounds and ten shillings?”

“And sevenpence.”

He smiled.

The brutality of certain men is violent and therefore conspicuous. That of Horace Brindle was smooth, patient and administrative. He did not strike the helpless; he merely calculated how long helplessness could endure before yielding one more coin. With a prodigious memory for debts and no memory at all for kindness, he had made a profitable career from the extremities of others.

“You engaged me,” he said, “to save your husband from the gallows.”

“I know, sir.”

“The prosecution has established motive, opportunity and possession of the key. Your husband admits that he entered the library. His cuff was stained with the decedent’s blood. He had been dismissed that afternoon. Money disappeared from the safe. What do you suppose such a defense costs?”

“I sold the furniture.”

“Sell something else.”

“There is nothing else.”

“The house?”

“It is rented.”

“Jewelry?”

“My wedding ring brought two pounds. They would not take the little brooch. The stones were glass.”

“Relations?”

“We have none who can help.”

Mr. Brindle leaned back. His chair gave a soft, luxurious creak.

“What of Pike’s mother?”

“She is dead.”

“Your father?”

“Dead also.”

“Children?”

A slight convulsion crossed her face.

“Our boy died in the fever.”

The lawyer made a movement of annoyance, as if the mortality of her family had been arranged to inconvenience him.

“Then borrow.”

“No one will lend.”

“Steal, if necessary.”

She stared at him.

“My husband is on trial for stealing.”

“Your husband is on trial for murder. There are distinctions in law, Mrs. Pike, though I do not expect you to appreciate them.”

She pushed the envelope across the desk.

“Take this. Please. I may find something more after—after tomorrow.”

“Tomorrow will be too late.”

“You said you had a witness.”

“I said I might have one.”

“The porter who saw a man in the passage?”

“The porter has reconsidered his recollection.”

“Why?”

“Memory, like advocacy, is expensive.”

She did not understand him at first. Then she understood, and her eyes widened.

“You mean Mr. Thompson paid him?”

“I mean nothing. I never mean anything without a fee.”

Percy Thompson, the murdered banker’s nephew, had attended every day of the trial. It was he who had pressed for an immediate arrest; he who had informed the police of the quarrel between Mr. Pike and his employer; he who had declared that the missing money amounted to twelve thousand pounds in bank notes; and he who, by an ostentatious exhibition of grief, had persuaded respectable observers that family affection and public justice were identical interests.

Mrs. Pike rose.

“You know Reginald is innocent.”

Mr. Brindle inspected one of his nails.

“Innocence is a private condition. Acquittal is a professional achievement.”

“You said there was hope.”

“There was money then.”

“He served Mr. Harcourt for eighteen years!”

“And was dismissed.”

“Because he would not tell a lie about the accounts.”

“So Pike says.”

“He went into the library because he heard a cry.”

“So Pike says.”

“He found the old gentleman on the floor and tried to lift him.”

“So Pike says.”

“He never opened the safe!”

“So Pike says.”

Each repetition fell like a spadeful of earth upon a coffin.

She placed both hands on the desk.

“Mr. Brindle, if you leave him now, they will kill him.”

“My dear woman, the Crown kills no one. The law takes its course.”

“But you are his lawyer.”

“Until ten o’clock tomorrow morning.”

“What happens then?”

“If the balance of my fee has not arrived, I shall withdraw.”

“You cannot!”

“I can and shall.”

“You promised.”

“My promise was conditional.”

She looked about the room as though the solemn books might intervene. They remained mute in their leather dignity. The brass lamp burned. The forgotten envelope lay between them, pathetic as an offering at the shrine of a deaf god.

“I have no more,” she whispered.

Mr. Brindle’s mouth curved.

“Then perhaps Cinderella will come to your assistance in a pumpkin coach.”

For a moment she remained motionless.

Then she took up the envelope, put it back in her bag, and went out.

Night had now arrived.

It was a February night of mist and reluctant rain. The lamps floated in the fog like tarnished moons; wheels hissed upon the wet stones; and a human tide, issuing from offices, restaurants and theatres, divided around Mrs. Pike without perceiving her. She walked east, then west, then east again. She had no destination. The shabby hotel in which she had taken a room stood three streets away, yet to return there appeared intolerable, for there waited the narrow bed, the cracked basin, the smell of the mop in the passage, and the certainty of morning.

Morning meant the court.

The court meant Reginald in the dock.

She saw him as he had looked that afternoon: the honest, mild face diminished by imprisonment; the brown hair gone white at the temples; the frayed cuff emerging beneath a coat lent by a warder. Once, when the prosecutor had spoken of “a servant’s long-nourished resentment,” Reginald had turned toward her, not to seek reassurance for himself but to discover whether she had been hurt. That look had nearly destroyed her.

She continued walking.

Shop windows appeared and vanished. Jewels glittered upon velvet. Silver dishes shone beneath electric light. A wax lady in a Paris gown inclined her head with permanent disdain. Mrs. Pike passed these things like a ghost wandering through the treasures of a world from which death had excluded her.

At a crossing, a constable caught her arm and dragged her backward.

A motorcar swept by, its horn crying out.

“You’ll get yourself killed, missus.”

“Yes,” she said.

The constable peered at her.

“Are you ill?”

“No.”

But she did not move. The current of pedestrians carried her from the crossing and deposited her against a wall beneath a great illuminated bill.

THE VOICE OF HEAVEN

From the opera house opposite came a prodigious confusion of carriages, motors, shouting attendants and brilliant people. Women wrapped in furs passed under the awning; men in white scarves gave orders with the assurance of ambassadors. Above them the façade glowed like a palace raised by enchantment out of the London fog.

Mrs. Pike had heard of Célestine Arnaud. Even the poor had heard of her. Queens sent flowers to her dressing room; poets compared her voice to moonlight upon the sea; cities waited years for the privilege of applauding her. She was Swiss by birth, French by education, and belonged by triumph to all Europe. It was said that when she sang of grief, women who understood no word of the language wept; and that when she sang of joy, old men remembered the mornings of their youth.

A side door opened. Music escaped—a single radiant chord, then silence.

Mrs. Pike remained by the wall.

The last arrivals entered. The attendants withdrew. Rain silvered the deserted pavement.

She remained.

Presently the side door opened again, and a young man in livery came out carrying a basket of white roses. Behind him walked a woman in a dark cloak lined with pale fur. She was taller than Mrs. Pike had imagined. Her hair, uncovered to the mist, had the warm gold of ripe corn; her face was not beautiful according to the frozen symmetry of fashion, but possessed that more compelling beauty which proceeds from intelligence, health and an overpowering vitality. Diamonds shone at her throat, yet it was not the diamonds that made her luminous.

She spoke to the servant, laughed, and turned toward the waiting motor.

Then she saw Mrs. Pike.

The singer stopped.

The servant opened the motor door. Mademoiselle Arnaud did not enter it. She crossed the pavement instead.

“Madame?”

Mrs. Pike shrank against the wall.

“I beg your pardon.”

“Vous êtes malade?”

“No.”

“But yes. You are ill.”

“I am only waiting.”

“For whom?”

Mrs. Pike looked across the empty street.

“I do not know.”

There are voices which inquire and voices which command; there are voices also which seem a friendly arm put around the heart. Célestine Arnaud’s speaking voice possessed, in miniature, the quality that had conquered Europe.

“Come,” she said.

“I cannot.”

“Why not?”

“My clothes—”

“Bah! The rain has no respect for clothes. Jules, the rug.”

The servant brought a rug. Before Mrs. Pike had recovered volition, the fur-lined cloak was around her shoulders, a strong hand supported her elbow, and she was seated inside the motorcar.

“Where shall we take you?”

Mrs. Pike named the shabby hotel.

The singer repeated the address to the chauffeur, but after one glance at her companion she tapped upon the glass.

“No. To the Carlton.”

“I cannot go there!”

“Why, yes, madame, if that is the way.”

“What way?”

“The way to make you speak.”

The motor moved.

Mrs. Pike saw the opera house slip behind them, then shops, lamps and faces, all streaming backward in the wet glass. The warmth, the scent of roses, the deep upholstery and the pale fur at her throat produced an impression so remote from reality that she wondered whether she had in truth stepped beneath the motor at the crossing and was now being conveyed through some merciful interval between life and death.

At the hotel, footmen received Mademoiselle Arnaud with respectful haste. Mrs. Pike hid her darned gloves in the folds of the rug. They ascended by a mirrored lift and entered a suite where lamps burned beneath silken shades, flowers overflowed from bowls, and supper waited upon a table of ivory and silver.

The singer removed her cloak.

“Now,” she said, “you will eat.”

“I could not.”

“Then you will drink.”

“I could not.”

“Then I shall be offended, and all London says I am dangerous when offended.”

A silver cup of hot broth was placed in Mrs. Pike’s hands. She drank because resistance required an energy she no longer possessed.

Célestine sat opposite.

“Your name?”

“Mary Pike.”

“And what has happened to Monsieur Pike?”

The cup trembled.

“They are going to hang him.”

The singer did not exclaim. Her great gift, beyond voice and beauty, was attention. She listened with her whole intelligence.

Mrs. Pike told the story.

Edwin Harcourt, retired banker and moneylender, had lived in almost total seclusion at Harcourt House. He was seventy-six, suspicious, irascible and immensely rich. His wife had died; his only son had been killed abroad; and his household consisted of a cook, two maids, a gardener, and Reginald Pike, who served both as butler and confidential attendant.

Percy Thompson, the son of Harcourt’s dead sister, was his nearest relation. He visited often, asked repeatedly for money, and departed as often in anger. He had debts at clubs, debts at gaming tables, debts to men who did not send polite letters.

On the afternoon of the murder, Harcourt had discovered an irregularity in certain securities. He accused Pike of having communicated information to Thompson. Pike denied it and, when commanded to support an untruth respecting the accounts, refused. The old banker dismissed him.

At nine that evening, Pike returned to the library to surrender a private key. Harcourt sat alone before the fire. The safe stood open behind a curtain in the adjoining study. There was an altercation; Pike laid the key upon a table and left.

At ten minutes past nine, while putting on his coat in the servants’ hall, he heard a cry.

He ran back.

Harcourt lay upon the floor between the desk and the left wall, stabbed beneath the left arm. Pike knelt, attempted to raise him, and stained his cuff with blood. The missing key was found in his pocket—though Pike swore he had left it on the table. The safe had been rifled. Twelve thousand pounds had disappeared.

“Who found him there?” asked Célestine.

“Mr. Thompson. He entered a moment afterward with one of the maids. He cried out that Reginald had murdered his uncle.”

“And the weapon?”

“A paper knife from the desk.”

“The old gentleman—he was sitting when Monsieur Pike left?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“At his desk.”

“Draw me the room.”

“I cannot draw.”

“Then describe, and I shall draw.”

The singer took a writing pad. Her fingers, so celebrated for their gestures upon the stage, moved with brisk precision.

“Here is the door?”

“Yes.”

“And the desk?”

“Opposite the fire, but turned a little.”

“The wall?”

“There was a narrow passage between the left side of the desk and the wall.”

“Very narrow?”

“Two feet, perhaps.”

“Could a man pass?”

“Sideways.”

“The safe?”

“Through the curtain behind the desk.”

“And Monsieur Harcourt when he was found?”

“On his right side, with his head toward the fireplace.”

“The wound?”

“Under the left arm, near the ribs.”

“Not the right?”

“No. The left.”

Célestine placed the pencil down.

“Did he lack an arm?”

Mrs. Pike stared.

“What?”

“Had Monsieur Harcourt no right arm?”

“Of course he had.”

“No injury? No stiffness? No vestige of paralysis?”

“None that I know of. What has that to do with it?”

The singer looked again at the diagram.

“It has everything to do with it.”

She rose and walked to the window. Below, the fog rolled against the lamps. When she turned, her expression had altered. Compassion remained, but it had been joined by enthusiastic purpose.

“My father,” she said, “was juge d’instruction in a little Swiss canton. As a child I knew more of footprints than dolls. He made me draw rooms, doors, bodies, knives. ‘Célestine,’ he would say, ‘people lie, but a wall does not lie. A wound does not flatter. A chair has no fear of the rich.’ I had forgotten many things. Perhaps not this.”

Mrs. Pike rose also.

“You believe Reginald?”

“I believe the room.”

“But what can the room say?”

“That depends upon who entered it, and with which hand.”

The singer asked for the newspapers. Jules brought an armful. Célestine searched the accounts of the trial until she found a photograph of Percy Thompson.

It showed a narrow-faced man of thirty, elegantly dressed, with restless eyes and a small black moustache. Dissipation had not yet destroyed his good looks, but it had loosened them, as damp loosens a painted surface. Beneath the portrait appeared his address and the observation that the bereaved nephew had attended every session.

“He presses for conviction?” asked Célestine.

“Every hour.”

“And he inherits?”

“Nearly everything.”

“Ah.”

She read further.

“Tomorrow he will be in court?”

“Yes.”

“That may be too late.”

Mrs. Pike’s hope, which had risen for one impossible moment, subsided.

“Mr. Brindle will withdraw.”

“Your advocate?”

She told of the demand for money and the threat.

The singer’s eyes flashed.

“He said Cinderella must come in a pumpkin coach?”

“Yes.”

“Then he has imagination, this monsieur, though perhaps he does not know it.”

She rang.

“Jules, telephone to the theatre. Ask Monsieur Valet to discover where Mr. Percy Thompson spends his evenings. Say that Mademoiselle Arnaud wishes to thank him for the flowers.”

“What flowers?” asked Jules.

“The flowers he will have sent when he hears of it.”

Within forty minutes an answer arrived. Percy Thompson was dining at the Imperial Club and intended to go afterward to a private gaming house in Curzon Street.

Célestine considered.

Then she smiled.

“Bring me the blue gown, the little sable, and one red rose.”

Mrs. Pike watched in bewilderment while servants moved through the suite. The singer vanished behind a door and returned transformed. The severe dark dress had given way to blue silk; jewels trembled at her ears; the sable framed her shoulders. She seemed no longer an angel of rescue but some exquisite and dangerous ambassadress from the kingdoms of pleasure.

“You will remain here, Madame Pike.”

“But where are you going?”

“To ask a murderer whether he admires music.”

“You cannot!”

“Why not?”

“He may harm you.”

“Men like Monsieur Thompson harm those who fear them. I shall not give him the convenience.”

The red rose lay on the table. She took it up, regarded it, and gave a little laugh.

“This also may be useful.”

Percy Thompson had just lost eighty pounds and was contemplating the loss of two hundred more when a servant whispered that Mademoiselle Célestine Arnaud waited in a motor outside.

No summons could have been better contrived to overcome caution. His vanity, which distress had bruised but never disciplined, expanded at once. He abandoned his cards and hurried out.

The singer received him with both hands extended.

“Monsieur Thompson! Forgive this barbarous invasion.”

He bent over her gloves.

“Mademoiselle, I am honoured beyond speech.”

“I saw your name in the newspaper. You are connected with that terrible trial?”

“My uncle was the victim.”

“Ah, ma pauvre ami! I knew only that you were a patron of music. Lady Corton said you had praised my Marguerite.”

Percy had never met Lady Corton and had heard only one act of the opera, but these deficiencies caused him no embarrassment.

“It was incomparable.”

“You are too gracious. I have been cruelly dull after the performance. Will you drive with me for ten minutes?”

“For ten hours, if you command it.”

He entered the motor.

Célestine spoke of Paris, of music, of the stupidity of London fog, of a supper she might perhaps give after the trial. Percy responded with increasing ardour. Her manner combined deference and mischief so artfully that he believed himself selected from all men, yet obliged to prove his selection deserved.

When the motor stopped before his club, she extended her hand.

“How quickly the ten minutes pass!”

“They need not end.”

“But you have friends.”

“None who matter.”

As she turned, one of her gloves slipped to the floor between them.

Percy caught it before it settled. He caught it with his left hand.

“Merci.”

He did not observe the concentration beneath her smile.

They descended. Under the club awning, the lamps shone fully upon him. Célestine held up the rose.

“You have no flower, monsieur.”

“I am desolate.”

“Then permit me.”

She stepped close and placed the stem against his lapel. Her right hand held the flower; her left pressed lightly upon his breast while she sought the buttonhole. Beneath the exquisitely cut coat she felt a stiff, bulky packet bound by what seemed to be a paper band.

Percy recoiled.

The movement was involuntary and violent.

The rose fell.

For one instant his face emptied itself of charm. Panic enveloped the cornered man; not certainty of discovery, but the terror of one whose secret has been touched.

Célestine stooped, recovered the rose, and laughed.

“My thorn has found you?”

“No—no.”

“You are pale.”

“The trial. I have slept badly.”

“Then tomorrow, when it is finished, you shall sleep.”

“Yes,” he said. “When it is finished.”

He recovered himself and raised her hand to his lips.

She drove away.

Back at the hotel, Mrs. Pike stood when Célestine entered.

“Well?”

The singer removed her gloves slowly.

“He is left-handed.”

Mrs. Pike said nothing.

“And he carries a packet here.” Célestine touched her breast. “A packet as thick as twelve thousand pounds might be if the notes were large. That does not prove it is the money.”

“But you think—”

“I think he feared my fingers more than he admired my eyes.”

“Then tell the police!”

“What shall I tell them? That a gentleman caught a glove with his left hand and carries papers in his pocket? They will answer that many innocent gentlemen are guilty of both.”

“Then there is nothing.”

“There is the room.”

Again the room. Again the incomprehensible insistence upon walls, arms, passage and wound.

“What has the left hand to do with it?”

“It has everything to do with it.”

Célestine drew the diagram afresh.

“See. Monsieur Harcourt sits here. A person enters from the door. If that person attacks from the open side of the desk, he faces Harcourt’s front or right side. To drive the paper knife beneath the left arm, he must reach across the body, past the right arm, turning the blade inward at an awkward angle. Harcourt is alive, strong enough to cry out, and has both arms. Why would he not seize the attacking wrist?”

Mrs. Pike bent over the drawing.

“But from the narrow passage—”

“Yes. Someone approaches behind the desk, close to the wall, upon Harcourt’s left. There is little room. The attacker must move sideways. A right-handed man has his weapon against the wall; his elbow is obstructed, his stroke weak and backward. But a left-handed man has his arm free. One step—one downward thrust beneath the left arm—and then he draws away toward the curtain and the safe.”

“Mr. Thompson.”

“Perhaps.”

“Why perhaps?”

“Because truth must endure more than desire. We shall make him show his hand where he cannot afterwards deny it.”

Mrs. Pike’s strength deserted her. She sank into a chair.

“Will you come to court?”

“Yes.”

“Mr. Brindle will not let you speak.”

“Then I shall be very tiresome.”

“The judge may send you away.”

“Then I shall be tiresome to the judge.”

For the first time in fourteen days, a sound escaped Mrs. Pike which was not a sob. It was almost laughter.

Célestine knelt beside her.

“You must sleep.”

“I cannot.”

“You must.”

“How?”

The singer thought. Then, very softly, she sang.

It was no aria known to fashionable London, but a shepherd’s lullaby from the canton of her childhood. Mrs. Pike understood none of the words. She understood the green stillness of the hills, the security of a lamp in a cottage window, the return of one beloved across the snow. The voice enfolded her, and within its tenderness the prison, the court, the rope and Mr. Brindle’s smile diminished like monstrous shadows before sunrise.

Before the second verse ended, she slept.

The Central Criminal Court was crowded the next morning.

Interest in the Harcourt murder had been considerable from the first. An elderly banker, a trusted servant, a vanished fortune and a bloodstained cuff were elements comprehensible to every newspaper reader. The final day brought barristers, idlers, ladies with veils, young clerks consuming peppermint, and several gentlemen whose knowledge of murder was derived exclusively from dining with assistant commissioners.

Sir Matthew Fane took his seat at ten.

He was honorable, weary and observant. Thirty years among the dramatic inventions of guilt had made him suspicious of theatrical emotion, but not insensible to human suffering. His eyes rested for a moment upon Reginald Pike, then upon Mrs. Pike, who sat beside a tall woman in a plain grey veil.

Few recognized Célestine Arnaud. Those who did communicated the discovery in whispers, until a faint disturbance moved through the gallery.

Mr. Horace Brindle rose.

He had received no further payment. The sight of Mrs. Pike’s companion puzzled him; the quality of her clothes disturbed him; but avarice, once offended, is less flexible than prudence.

“My lord,” he said, “before the proceedings resume, I regret that circumstances of a private and professional character compel me to withdraw from the conduct of the defense.”

A murmur passed through the court.

Sir Matthew frowned.

“At this stage?”

“With profound regret, my lord.”

“Has the prisoner consented?”

Brindle turned toward the dock.

“My client is aware that I cannot continue without proper instructions.”

Pike rose.

“I have given every instruction I know, my lord.”

“Do you wish counsel to withdraw?”

“No, my lord. I have no money to obtain another.”

Sir Matthew’s face hardened.

“Mr. Brindle, professional fees are not ordinarily debated in open court. Neither is a man upon trial for his life ordinarily abandoned during the concluding proceedings.”

“I appreciate the obliquity of my position, my lord. Yet I cannot permit the court to suppose that my withdrawal is solely pecuniary.”

The prosecutor looked up sharply.

Brindle continued, turning just enough for the jury to see his grave expression.

“Certain communications from the prisoner have rendered further advocacy incompatible with my duty.”

The implication was unmistakable. He could not repeat a privileged confession; therefore he suggested one. A lesser cruelty might have withdrawn in silence. Horace Brindle intended to punish poverty by helping the rope.

Reginald Pike gripped the rail.

“I confessed nothing!”

“My lord,” said the prosecutor, rising, “this is highly irregular.”

“It is worse than irregular,” said Sir Matthew. “Mr. Brindle, you will refrain from insinuation.”

“I bow to your lordship.”

“But the words had been spoken. They hovered above the jury like smoke.

Célestine rose.

“My lord, may I speak?”

Every head turned.

The clerk demanded silence.

Sir Matthew regarded the veiled woman.

“Who are you, madam?”

She lifted the veil.

Recognition crossed the court in a visible wave.

“I am Célestine Arnaud.”

Sir Matthew knew the name, though his judicial expression conceded nothing.

“And what is your interest in this cause?”

“I have an observation concerning the death of Monsieur Harcourt.”

“Were you a witness?”

“No, my lord.”

“Then upon what basis—”

“Upon the basis of the evidence already given.”

The prosecutor rose.

“My lord, however distinguished the lady may be in another sphere, this procedure is impossible.”

Célestine inclined her head.

“Why, yes, monsieur, if that is the way. But if an innocent man is condemned because the way is more important than the truth, then your way is also impossible.”

The spectators stirred. Sir Matthew lifted a hand.

“Silence.”

He studied her. Vanity was absent from her face. Excitement was present, but governed by intelligence.

“You understand that this is not a theatre, Mademoiselle Arnaud?”

“Perfectly, my lord. In a theatre, the dead man rises after the curtain. Here he does not.”

Sir Matthew almost smiled.

“What do you propose?”

“That Mr. Percy Thompson be recalled for three questions.”

Percy sat behind the prosecutor. At the sound of his name, the colour left his cheeks.

The prosecutor objected.

“Mr. Thompson has already been examined and discharged. No foundation has been laid for recalling him.”

Célestine turned.

“Then let us lay it.”

“You are not counsel.”

“No. The counsel has withdrawn.”

Mr. Brindle flushed.

Sir Matthew conferred in a low voice with the clerk. The court waited. Outside, wheels rattled over the stones; within, not a shoe moved.

At last the judge said, “In view of the extraordinary conduct of the defense, and because the life of the prisoner is at issue, I will permit the witness to be recalled upon the court’s own motion. Mademoiselle, you will address your proposed questions to me. I shall determine whether they may be put.”

Percy came unwillingly to the witness box.

He took the oath.

Célestine looked at him with the same gracious smile she had worn beneath the club awning.

“Good morning, monsieur.”

He said nothing.

She turned to the judge.

“Will your lordship ask Mr. Thompson whether he sent me flowers last evening?”

The prosecutor sprang up.

“My lord!”

Sir Matthew’s brows rose.

“What possible relevance has that question?”

“It has none. I wished only to make him comfortable.”

A ripple of laughter ran through the gallery and was instantly suppressed.

Percy’s eyes burned.

Célestine continued.

“Then ask him, please, whether he is left-handed.”

The laughter ceased.

Sir Matthew looked toward Percy.

“Are you left-handed?”

“No.”

The answer came quickly.

Célestine’s voice was gentle.

“You are under oath, monsieur.”

“I know it.”

“You caught my glove with your left hand.”

“By accident.”

“You opened the motor door with your left hand.”

“It was nearer.”

“You raised your glass with your left hand.”

“I use either.”

She turned again to the bench.

“My lord, will you request that he raise his right hand?”

Percy raised it.

Not at once. For a fraction of a second his left shoulder moved; then he checked the instinct and lifted the right. The hesitation was small, but the whole court saw it.

Célestine said, “Now tell him to take the pen upon the ledge and write his name.”

The judge did so.

Percy reached with his left hand.

Panic corrected the movement halfway. His fingers struck the pen; it rolled from the ledge and fell.

He caught it with his left hand before it reached the floor.

There came from the spectators one long inhalation.

The judge leaned forward.

“You appear to use the left hand with facility, Mr. Thompson.”

“I use both, my lord.”

“But you denied being left-handed.”

“I misunderstood.”

“No,” said Célestine. “You understood too well.”

The prosecutor objected to her tone. Sir Matthew overruled him.

“Mademoiselle Arnaud, you have established that the witness favours his left hand. What follows?”

She walked toward the table upon which the exhibits lay. Among them were plans of Harcourt House and a photograph of the library after the murder.

“This, my lord.”

She asked permission to use a pencil. On a fresh sheet she drew the room: door, fireplace, desk, curtain, safe, body.

Her drawing was passed to the judge and jury.

“The Crown says Monsieur Pike left by this door, returned, approached his master and stabbed him. But the wound was beneath the left arm. Monsieur Harcourt had no injury to his right arm. He was not helpless. If an assailant approached from the open side, the old gentleman could see him, rise, retreat or seize the wrist. A right-handed blow delivered from that position must cross the body at an oblique angle. Yet the medical witness testified that the wound passed downward and inward almost without lateral deviation.”

The prosecutor rose.

“The medical evidence stated that such a wound could have been inflicted by the prisoner.”

“Could have been,” said Célestine. “If the victim sat motionless and politely arranged himself for murder.”

A murmur of grim amusement followed.

She pointed to the narrow passage.

“But suppose the assailant came here, behind the desk, along the wall, pretending perhaps to go toward the safe. He stands at Harcourt’s left shoulder. The wall is at his own left if he faces the victim?”

Sir Matthew examined the plan.

“No. If he approaches from behind, the wall is at his right.”

Célestine’s face brightened.

“Precisely, my lord. Therefore his left arm is free. He draws the paper knife from the desk with that hand. He strikes beneath the victim’s left arm. The blow passes downward and inward. Monsieur Harcourt turns, rises, cries out, and falls upon his right side toward the fireplace—exactly as he was found.”

She took two steps beside the jury box, reproducing the movement.

“A right-handed attacker in that narrow place must either strike backward with the right hand, which gives the wrong direction, or turn his whole body against the wall, losing force and warning the victim. A left-handed man need only extend his arm.”

The courtroom had become utterly still.

“The position of the body, the direction of the wound, the wall and the victim’s sound right arm agree upon one thing: the person who struck from the concealed passage was left-handed.”

Mr. Brindle rose abruptly.

“My lord, this is conjecture of the most fanciful kind. Even if the murderer were left-handed, thousands of persons share that peculiarity.”

“Certainly,” said Célestine. “But only one left-handed person had announced his intention of visiting the house, knew the safe, inherited the estate, required twelve thousand pounds before morning, and arrived in time to accuse a servant whose key he had placed in that servant’s pocket.”

Percy gripped the witness box.

“That is a lie!”

“Which part?”

“All of it!”

“Then why did you tell the police you arrived at a quarter past nine, when the hall clock stopped at nine minutes past from being overturned during the struggle?”

“The clock was unreliable.”

“Why did the porter at your club first say you left at eight-forty, and later reconsider?”

Percy looked toward Mr. Brindle.

The lawyer’s eyes shifted away.

Célestine saw the movement.

“And why,” she continued, “did you carry last night, beneath your coat, a bulky packet which terrified you when I touched it?”

Percy recoiled from the rail.

The prosecutor stood.

“My lord, this exceeds all permission. The witness cannot be accused upon such theatrical assertions.”

Sir Matthew’s voice was grave.

“Mr. Thompson, are you carrying such a packet now?”

“No.”

The denial emerged too loudly.

“Have you any objection to showing the contents of your inner pockets?”

“Yes. I mean—this is outrageous. I am not the prisoner.”

“Not yet,” said someone in the gallery.

The judge ordered silence.

Percy stepped back.

A court officer moved toward him.

“You shall not touch me!”

He thrust his hand inside his coat.

The officer seized his wrist. Another came from beside the dock. There was a brief, ugly struggle, the tearing of cloth, a woman’s cry. Then a packet fell upon the floor.

It was thick, rectangular and bound by a printed bank band.

No one moved.

The clerk picked it up and carried it to the judge. Sir Matthew examined the band, then the notes within.

The prosecutor’s face changed.

“These appear,” he said slowly, “to bear the numbers supplied by Harcourt’s bank.”

Percy ceased struggling.

Panic left him. In its place came the collapse which follows when fear has exhausted every disguise. His shoulders sagged; his mouth opened; and the elegant young man seemed suddenly a diseased and frightened boy.

“It was mine,” he said.

No one answered.

“He owed it to me.”

Still no one answered.

“He said he would ruin me. He had written to the clubs. He was going to alter the will. I only meant to take the notes. He turned—he caught my sleeve—”

His left hand went to his face.

“The knife was there.”

Mrs. Pike made a sound like a person waking from suffocation.

Reginald stood in the dock, unable to comprehend deliverance. The warder beside him removed his hand from the prisoner’s arm.

Percy continued in broken fragments. He had entered by the garden door, knowing the household arrangements. He had heard Pike quarrel with Harcourt and seen him leave the library. He had taken the key from the table, followed the narrow passage to the safe, and been discovered. Harcourt threatened to call the police. Percy seized the paper knife with his left hand and struck. Hearing Pike return at the cry, he had slipped through the curtain, rifled the safe, then emerged after Pike was kneeling beside the body. In the confusion he thrust the key into the butler’s coat pocket.

The money he dared not place at his rooms, which the police might search; nor at his club, where creditors and thieves were equally active. He had carried it, intending to leave for the Continent immediately upon Pike’s conviction. Vanity had taken him to Célestine’s motor, and vanity had retained the coat which concealed his fortune.

Sir Matthew ordered him into custody.

Then the judge turned toward the dock.

“Reginald Pike, the Crown will offer no further evidence against you.”

The prosecutor rose.

“In light of what has occurred, my lord, the prosecution withdraws the charge.”

Sir Matthew addressed the jury, but the words were already unnecessary.

“Your verdict?”

“Not guilty.”

The phrase passed through the court like sunlight breaking into a cellar.

Mrs. Pike did not cry out. She rose, took one step, and remained standing with both hands extended. Reginald came down from the dock. For a moment the officials, spectators and advocates disappeared from their knowledge. They met in the narrow space before the benches, and he put his arms around her.

It was not decorous.

Sir Matthew looked away.

Célestine stood near the table, the red rose from the previous evening pinned now upon her own grey dress. Reporters crowded forward; officers surrounded Percy; the gallery seethed with questions and exclamations. Amid that human eddy she seemed strangely serene, as if all the prodigious commotion concerned someone else.

Mr. Brindle was not serene.

He collected his papers with unsteady hands. His professional instinct told him that tomorrow’s newspapers would not neglect his withdrawal, his insinuation or the timing of both. Worse than scandal, however, was the disturbance taking place within the secret court of his own philosophy.

He had believed in money because money had never failed him. It purchased witnesses, patience, silence, eloquence and delay. It made guilt plausible and innocence inconvenient. The poor came to him with truth, and he taught them that truth without a fee was merely noise. This faith had been hard, coherent and profitable.

Yet money had been present on both sides of the morning’s drama. It had armed Percy Thompson, corrupted memory, deserted Reginald Pike and silenced his defense. By every rule Mr. Brindle trusted, the butler should have been condemned.

Instead, a woman had stepped out of fog, descended from a motorcar, worn jewels, dropped a glove, pinned a rose, drawn a room, and defeated the entire machinery of fear.

Cinderella had come.

And the coach, though not a pumpkin, waited outside.

As Brindle reached the aisle, Célestine spoke behind him.

“Monsieur.”

He turned.

She regarded him without anger. Contempt might have comforted him, for he understood contempt and knew its commercial value. Her expression was sorrowful.

“You left your client because he was poor.”

“My arrangements were professional.”

“You told his wife that only a fairy tale could save him.”

“I may have used an unfortunate figure.”

“No,” she said. “It was fortunate. It reminded her to walk beneath the lights.”

She passed him.

Outside, the fog had lifted. A pale winter sun shone upon the court steps, the waiting motorcars and the crowd that broke into cheers when Reginald Pike appeared with his wife.

Célestine’s chauffeur opened the door of the great silver motor.

Mrs. Pike hesitated.

“We cannot ride in that.”

“Why not?”

“People will look.”

“Let them.”

“Where are we going?”

“To breakfast.”

“We have no clothes for such a place.”

Célestine laughed, and the laugh had the freshness of water among alpine stones.

“Why, yes, madame, if that is the way.”

She drew them into the motor. Reginald sat opposite his wife, still touching her hand as though to assure himself that walls, bars and accusation had truly vanished. The singer placed the fur rug over their knees.

The car moved into the morning.

From the court entrance Horace Brindle watched it go. Around him hurried reporters, policemen, barristers and curious citizens—the old, competent human tide in which he had always known how to swim. Yet for once he felt himself abandoned upon the shore.

He looked down at the red and grey stones beneath his feet.

For the first time in many years, he wondered whether power might exist without cruelty, whether intelligence might serve compassion, and whether a truth despised because it was poor could nevertheless find, somewhere in the nocturnal city, an ambassadress.

He had no answer.

But he had seen the coach.

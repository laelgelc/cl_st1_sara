### I. THE PROPER MANNER OF RECEIVING AN ARMY WHICH HAS COME TO WELCOME YOU

When Lieutenant Adrian Wren recovered consciousness he was lying upon his back in a thicket of cedar, his head supported by the exposed root of a tree and his face turned upward to the moon. There are pillows less hard, though perhaps none better adapted to the repose of a soldier.

For some minutes he regarded the sky without curiosity. It was a pale winter sky, thinly filmed with vapour; the moon, declining toward the west, had that tarnished aspect which she assumes above fields where men have lately died. A branch crossed her face like a crack in a silver plate. The lieutenant observed this with the exact but unprofitable attention sometimes produced by a blow upon the skull.

Then memory returned with military promptitude.

His regiment had advanced through the cedar brake in line of battle. There had been a confusion of commands, the metallic shriek of projectiles among the trees, and somewhere to the right the rapid discharge of a battery. He remembered seeing a cannoneer leap upon a caisson, swing his cap, and fall backward without completing the gesture. Then came a flash immediately before his eyes, an intolerable concussion, and darkness.

Wren raised a hand to his head. He found neither bandage nor wound; the hair beneath his fingers seemed singularly coarse and scant, but this circumstance he referred to blood, dust, or some other ordinary contamination of war. His hand, as he lowered it, passed through a bar of moonlight. He stared at it. It appeared narrow, brown and deeply lined.

“Fever,” he said aloud.

The voice startled him. It was low, uncertain, and had in it a dry vibration resembling the voice of an elderly man speaking in an adjoining room. The hero of this tale, being twenty-three years of age and of sound constitution, naturally attributed the phenomenon to acoustic conditions among the cedars.

He sat up. Beyond the thicket lay a cultivated field crossed by a road, and beyond the field the low undulations of the Stone’s River country rose dimly under mist. Nothing in the scene betrayed the presence of battle. No musketry sounded; no wounded man called upon God, his mother, or the hospital corps; no wheel grated in the ruts. Even the wind had abandoned the place. The silence was not peaceful: it was attentive.

Presently, at the southern extremity of the road, something moved.

A column of infantry came out of the obscurity and advanced northward. The men marched by fours, their rifles upon the shoulder, their faces colourless in the moon. At their head rode an officer whose sword hung rigidly downward, as though it had been fastened to his hand. Behind him floated a flag, yet there was no wind to stir it. The folds maintained a slow undulation of their own, like weeds beneath invisible water.

Wren rose with difficulty and stepped to the roadside.

The regiment passed within thirty yards of him. He could distinguish straps, cartridge boxes, rolled blankets, even the dull buttons upon the coats. Not one footfall was audible. The cloth of the uniforms made no whisper; the steel gave no clink. A horse moved past, setting each hoof upon the stony road with mathematical precision, and the earth denied the impact.

Wren called: “What command?”

No head turned. His own strange voice rang across the field and died among the hills.

Another regiment followed; then a battery, its six guns and their limbers gliding forward without the murmur of axle or chain. The cannoneers sat erect. Another regiment came, and another and another—each issuing from the southern haze, crossing the white reach of road, and entering the obscurity beyond. There were cavalrymen with drawn sabres; ambulances whose curtains were closely buttoned; staff officers riding in groups and seeming to confer, although their lips made no sound. All moved northward with a dreadful regularity, neither hastening nor delaying, an army governed by some allocution unheard by living ears.

The lieutenant watched until the moon sank and a weak grey illumination spread over the fields. The last files entered the mist. The mist dissolved. There was no army.

The road was empty.

His conclusion, though erroneous, was soldierly: the Union forces had been defeated and were retreating north. He had somehow lain insensible while the army passed. That no corps should have discovered him was remarkable; that an entire army should march without noise was more so; but military operations are full of irregularities, and a man who has recently received a bullet upon the head is not exacting in his demand for explanations.

He set out in search of his command.

### II. WHEN ONE HAS MISLAID HIS LIFE, A PHYSICIAN MAY BE CONSULTED

Daylight found him upon a turnpike which he imperfectly remembered. The country had suffered changes that offended him. Fences stood where no fences had been; trees of considerable diameter occupied ground which, yesterday, had been open; and a farmhouse, white and prosperous, displayed itself upon the site of a battery position. Time, like an incompetent quartermaster, had distributed the landscape without regard to former arrangements.

Near the farmhouse Wren encountered a gentleman driving a light carriage. This person, a physician by profession—as proclaimed by the black leather case at his feet and by that look of temperate fatigue which long attendance upon human frailty communicates—checked his horse.

“You appear ill, sir,” he said. “Can I assist you?”

“I require information, Doctor. What troops passed north during the night?”

The physician looked along the vacant road. “Troops?”

“A heavy column—infantry, artillery and cavalry. They were in retreat, I fear. Has Rosecrans been driven from his position?”

The doctor’s expression became professionally vacant.

“Will you tell me your name?”

“Lieutenant Adrian Wren, Ninth Ohio Infantry.”

“You are not in uniform, Lieutenant.”

For the first time Wren inspected his clothing. He wore a dark civilian coat of an unfashionable cut, trousers stained at the knee, and heavy shoes badly cracked. There was no sword at his side, no belt at his waist.

“I have been stripped while unconscious,” he said. “Possibly taken to a field hospital and discharged before my recovery. Such negligence is criminal.”

The doctor descended from the carriage. “You say that you were unconscious. From what cause?”

“A ball struck me during the engagement in the cedars.”

“Last night?”

“Yesterday afternoon, I suppose. What date is this?”

The physician did not answer immediately. Instead he took Wren by the wrist and counted the pulse. He then drew down one eyelid, contemplated the exposed surface, and passed a hand over the patient’s skull with a freedom which would have been resented under less urgent circumstances.

“There is no recent wound,” he said.

“The ball may have glanced.”

“And your regiment?”

“I have told you.”

“Yes. The Ninth Ohio.” The doctor repeated the words with the careful gentleness employed toward children and dangerous lunatics. “What engagement do you believe occurred here?”

“The battle of Stone’s River, naturally.”

“Ah.”

That monosyllable contained a diagnosis.

Wren withdrew his wrist. “Doctor, I have answered your questions. You will now answer mine. Has General Rosecrans been defeated?”

“General Rosecrans has not commanded an army for a considerable period.”

“Dead?”

“Not precisely the word I should have selected.”

The lieutenant’s patience, weakened by hunger, broke down. “Sir, your wit is ill-timed. I have seen a Federal column passing northward—thousands of men—within the last six hours.”

The physician’s eyes sharpened with genuine interest. “Seen them?”

“Distinctly.”

“Heard them?”

Wren hesitated.

“I heard nothing.”

“No drums? No wheels? No hoofs?”

“Nothing.”

“And they took no notice of you?”

“None.”

The doctor considered this account with less astonishment than was agreeable. Physicians, having made a profession of incredible statements, are seldom courteous enough to be surprised by them.

“My friend,” he said, “you are exhausted and mentally confused. Come with me. There is food at the house, and I can arrange—”

“I am not your friend.”

“Then as my patient.”

“I am not your patient.”

“That remains to be established.”

Wren stepped away. “I asked for military intelligence and received an examination. Good morning.”

He walked north, preserving as much dignity as a man may whose knees tremble and whose coat is too large across the chest. Behind him the physician called once, then again. Wren did not turn. The carriage presently rolled away, producing upon the road a cheerful and perfectly audible rattling. That ordinary sound troubled him more than the silence of the army.

### III. AN OLD SOLDIER IS ADVISED NOT TO LOOK INTO STILL WATER

By noon the sky had darkened, and a fine rain began to fall. Wren’s strength declined rapidly. His breath came short; his heart beat with an irregular, fluttering motion; the road seemed to rise before him like the glacis of a fortification.

He again touched his head. The hand which returned before his eyes was not merely dirty. The veins stood upon it in blue cords; the joints were enlarged; the nails were ridged and yellow. He turned it slowly, examining palm and back with the attention of a surveyor verifying an impossible boundary.

Months, he thought. I have lain for months in hospital.

This explanation was absurd, but it possessed the superior comfort of being less absurd than the truth. Illness, he knew, could reduce the body, bleach or darken the hair, and give to youth the temporary aspect of age. Medical literature records prodigies of alteration after fever; men have awakened from delirium speaking Latin, forgetting their wives, or believing themselves made of glass. Why, then, should a lieutenant not lose a season?

He passed a hand over his face. The cheeks were sunken; from the corners of the mouth ran deep furrows; beneath the chin hung loose folds of skin. His fingers encountered a beard, long, grey and unfamiliar.

He began to run.

After fifty yards he stopped, gasping. The rain increased. At a bend of the road, half concealed by wet shrubs, stood a stone monument. Its design was severe: a square base, an upright shaft, and upon the summit the weather-stained figure of a soldier. Moss occupied the incised letters; lichens had established upon the northern face a small grey antiquity of their own.

Wren approached and read.

The monument commemorated the soldiers who had fallen in the Battle of Stone’s River.

“That is wrong,” he murmured.

The date beneath the inscription was 1862.

Below it another inscription recorded the erection of the memorial many decades later. Wren read the figures once, twice, and then traced them with a shaking finger as if touch might correct the testimony of sight.

The man of whom we write now understood imperfectly, which is the common manner of understanding. He did not know whether he had slept beneath the cedars while years marched over him, or whether he had died there and been permitted for one night to accompany, at a distance, the immemorial retreat of the slain. But he knew that the young lieutenant was gone.

At the monument’s base lay a shallow depression filled with rain. The water was brown, yet smooth enough to return an image.

He looked down.

An old man looked up at him: a wasted face under thin white hair, the eyes enlarged by terror, the mouth open in a noiseless appeal. For an instant Wren failed to recognise the apparition. Then it raised a withered hand as he raised his own.

Recognition was immediate and complete.

He uttered a cry, staggered forward, and fell face downward into the pool. The water was scarcely four inches deep, but death, when properly instructed, has no need of depth.

The rain continued. Far off, beyond the peaceful fields and the cultivated hills, a faint roll seemed to pass through the air—not thunder, perhaps, but the wheels of invisible artillery moving northward into obscurity.

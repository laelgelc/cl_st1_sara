## Alderman Keats’s Revolver

Alderman Keats suffered from gout.

At least, this was the settled conviction of Hanbridge, and Alderman Keats had done everything that integrity could require to establish the conviction. He limped in Crown Square; he groaned upon rising from committee-room chairs; he referred with humorous resignation to “this confounded toe”; and whenever a civic dinner offered port, he drank enough of it to justify not merely gout, but almost any disease known to the medical profession.

His doctor, a dry young man from Bursley, had once suggested that the trouble might be caused by a tight boot.

“Boot!” cried the Alderman. “My dear fellow, it’s hereditary.”

There had never been gout in the Keats family. Nor, until the Alderman’s prosperity, had there been port. His father repaired boilers near the Big Pits and regarded wine as medicine—bad medicine, moreover, and dear at the price. But Alderman Keats was a self-made man, and, having made himself, he naturally felt entitled to select the materials.

He selected those of the fine old English gentleman.

The fine old English gentleman, as understood by Keats, wore broadcloth, carried a heavy gold watch, despised foreign food, swore mildly at political opponents, drank port, suffered from gout, and kept firearms. He was bluff without being vulgar, rich without admitting it, and fearless except in the matter of draughts. He called boys “young dogs,” women “my dear,” and any person beneath him in station “an honest fellow.” Keats had mastered all these accomplishments.

He had also grown side-whiskers.

The town approved. A manufacturer may build a fortune by accurate calculation, but he builds a reputation by picturesque habits. Keats’s calculations in the earthenware trade had been remarkably accurate; nevertheless, Hanbridge preferred the gout. His fellow aldermen repeated his observations about port. Tradesmen smiled when he shook his stick at them. Even his enemies admitted that he was “a character.”

His cousin, Joe Keats, did not admit it.

Joe kept a small ironmonger’s shop in Slippery Lane and had known the Alderman before the Alderman had discovered his ancestry, his gout, or the proper use of the word “indubitably.” He was a thin, tranquil bachelor who gave no sign of being impressed by anything except a badly sharpened chisel.

“You’re limping with the wrong foot,” Joe remarked one morning.

They were standing outside the Town Hall after a meeting concerning the width of a proposed cattle market entrance—a question which divided Hanbridge more profoundly than religion.

“Nonsense!” said the Alderman.

“It was the left one yesterday.”

“Gout moves.”

“So I see.”

The Alderman stared at him. Joe stared at a passing cart. It was impossible to vanquish Joe because Joe would never consent to fight.

It was this habit of mind that led to the revolver.

Alderman Keats purchased the weapon from a pawnbroker in Bursley. He did not go to Joe, though Joe sold revolvers. He said afterwards that he preferred to transact such business where he was not known; which was accurate. The weapon was large, black, and forbidding. It dragged down the right-hand pocket of his overcoat and caused the garment to hang awry, but this defect only increased his satisfaction. He liked to slap the pocket suddenly during conversation.

“Every man ought to have a revolver,” he announced at the Conservative Club.

“What for?” asked Joe, who had been admitted as a visitor.

“What for? Protection!”

“From whom?”

The Alderman waved magnificently towards the darkened windows.

“You never know.”

This answer was received with respect, because no one could deny it. You never did know. Burglars might visit Crown Square; anarchists might invade the council chamber; a mad dog might run up Slippery Lane; or the French, taking advantage of a fog, might land at Liverpool and arrive in Hanbridge before breakfast. A revolver covered all contingencies.

“Loaded, is it?” Joe asked.

“Of course it’s loaded.”

“Know how to use it?”

“My dear Joe,” said the Alderman, “I have handled firearms since I was a lad.”

This statement rested upon an occasion in 1869 when he had carried an uncle’s fowling-piece across a field. The gun had not been loaded, and the uncle had walked behind him for safety.

The Alderman called his revolver “the barker.” The expression pleased him intensely. He would say, “I’ve got the barker in my pocket,” and then glance round as though expecting the company to recall adventures upon the American frontier. Men began to believe that his commercial journeys had contained episodes unmentioned in the annual reports of Keats and Malkin, Limited.

His fame as a marksman originated one wet Thursday at the Golden Lion.

The Alderman, Joe, Councillor Bratt, and three other substantial citizens were in a private room after luncheon. The door into the corridor possessed a keyhole of the old-fashioned sort, with a brass plate. Conversation had touched upon shooting, and the Alderman stated that at twelve paces he could put a bullet through that keyhole.

“You couldn’t put a knitting needle through it at twelve paces,” said Joe.

“I’ll wager five pounds.”

“Don’t,” said Joe. “You’ll spoil the door.”

This prudent counsel was interpreted by the others as fear for the Alderman’s reputation. The table was shifted. The landlord, summoned to witness the feat, objected that the corridor might contain a chambermaid. The corridor was inspected, the opposite bedroom was locked, and the landlord—after being assured that the bullet would pass neatly through the keyhole and bury itself in a bolster—retired behind the coal-scuttle.

Keats drew the revolver.

At that moment he ceased to be a fine old English gentleman and became a frightened man holding a machine of whose intentions he knew nothing. The weapon looked much larger out of his pocket. He had never fired it. He had not even examined it closely, lest it should fire itself.

“Give over,” he said, laughing bluffly. “We’re not schoolboys.”

“Five pounds,” Joe reminded him.

The Alderman raised the revolver. His hand moved in small circles.

“You’ll have somebody’s eye out,” said the landlord from behind the coal-scuttle.

The weapon exploded.

Plaster descended. Councillor Bratt sat down without choosing a chair and struck the floor. The landlord shouted that he was murdered. Keats shut his eyes. When he opened them, the keyhole remained visible and apparently unharmed.

Joe went to the door, bent down, and examined it.

“Clean through,” he said.

The company crowded round. Nobody could find a mark upon the door, but this circumstance proved nothing to men determined upon a marvel. Joe, whose face was hidden, had observed a neat hole in the ceiling immediately over the Alderman’s head. He said no more. The landlord opened the door and inspected the opposite bedroom. No bullet was discovered there.

“It must be in the bolster,” said Councillor Bratt.

“Indubitably,” said Keats, who was pale but recovering.

By evening, the whole town knew that Alderman Keats had shot through a keyhole at twelve paces.

By “the whole town” is meant the portion of Hanbridge which sold goods on credit, attended public dinners, employed servants, or hoped one day to be invited into the mayor’s parlour. The remainder learnt of it by Saturday and improved the distance to twenty paces.

Keats was acclaimed as a dead shot. He accepted the title with becoming modesty.

“Practice,” he explained. “Nothing but practice.”

“But you said you hadn’t fired for years,” Joe observed.

“Once learnt, never forgotten.”

A week later the bullet was found.

It had passed into the ceiling, through the floor of the room above, through the seat of a cane-bottomed chair, and into a volume entitled *Domestic Happiness*, which lay upon the chair. The chambermaid discovered it while dusting. The landlord carried the book to Keats privately, being a considerate man and also desirous of having the ceiling repaired.

The Alderman bought *Domestic Happiness* for two guineas, although its published price was three shillings and sixpence.

He then sat alone in his office and contemplated the revolver.

The weapon had betrayed him. It had leapt upwards in his grasp like a demon; it had threatened ceilings, chambermaids, literature, and the established order. Yet he could not abandon it. Hanbridge now regarded the revolver as the visible token of his courage. To cease carrying it would provoke inquiry, and inquiry was the mother of discovery.

He took the cartridges from it with extreme delicacy and dropped them, one by one, into Birches Pond after nightfall.

For three days he carried the unloaded weapon. Then a new anxiety arose. Suppose he should be required to fire it? A burglar might enter his house in the presence of witnesses. A dog might go mad during a council procession. Joe might demand another demonstration.

Keats returned to the Bursley pawnbroker and bought blank cartridges.

“Starting theatrical work?” the pawnbroker asked.

“Private practice,” said Keats sternly.

Thereafter, on alternate Wednesdays, sounds of shooting issued from an abandoned shed behind the Keats works. The Alderman stood before a row of bottles, fired six blanks, and afterwards smashed two or three bottles with a coal hammer. He then invited the night watchman to inspect the results.

“Not bad,” he would say. “A shade low with the third.”

The watchman, who valued his position, spread the news. Keats’s prowess grew. He could, according to reputable testimony, shoot the neck from a bottle without cracking the body. He could extinguish a candle at thirty yards. He had once severed a rat’s whisker while leaving the rat itself free to reflect upon the warning.

Joe listened to these accounts.

“You’ll end by killing somebody,” he said.

“Blank cartridges, my dear fellow, don’t kill.”

Joe looked at him.

The Alderman realised his error.

“That is—I mean—some fools use blanks. Dangerous habit. Gives a man no confidence.”

“Quite,” said Joe.

Months passed, and the legend hardened. Keats limped, drank port, called his revolver a barker, and represented in his own person the sturdy virtues of an England which had never quite existed.

Then came the venison feast.

The mayor of Hanbridge, having received half a stag from a nobleman whose election expenses had once been discussed with sympathy, gave a dinner at the Town Hall. The venison was tough, the speeches were long, and the port was old enough to command obedience. Alderman Keats drank patriotically. At midnight he emerged with Joe and Councillor Bratt, leaning upon both more from civic emotion than gout.

Crown Square was alive with confusion.

Men were running towards the Empire Music Hall. A horse stood trembling outside the stage entrance. From within came a roar so vast and savage that the gas lamps themselves appeared to quiver.

It was an epoch-making night in the history of provincial entertainment. The Empire had secured, at enormous expense, Professor Zambello and his Royal Bengal Tigers. There were three tigers, two attendants, one professor, and a paragraph in the programme guaranteeing absolute safety. Unfortunately, two of the tigers had quarrelled during removal from their travelling cages to the stage.

They were now fighting in a passage behind the scenery.

Professor Zambello—whose real name was Simms and who had been born near Crewe—stood at the stage door with a long iron bar. His face was bleeding. An attendant was shouting for sacking, another for paraffin, and the manager for the police. None of these articles or institutions seemed immediately useful.

Keats and his companions pushed through the crowd.

“What’s amiss?” demanded the Alderman, slapping his heavy pocket.

“Tigers loose!” cried a scene-shifter.

“Not loose,” corrected the manager. “Temporarily unconfined.”

A second roar came from the passage, followed by the crash of timber.

Professor Zambello saw Keats’s movement.

“You’ve got a gun!” he cried.

The crowd turned. In that instant Alderman Keats’s reputation, patiently constructed from port, gout, broadcloth, gossip, broken bottles, and one injured work of moral instruction, became a public obligation.

“A revolver,” said Councillor Bratt proudly. “And here’s the finest shot in the Five Towns.”

“Give it me,” said the trainer.

Keats took one step backwards.

The trainer stretched out his hand. “Quick, man! If Sultan gets his teeth into Rajah’s throat, we’re done. One shot over their heads may part ’em. If not, I’ll shoot the worse one.”

The Alderman’s fingers closed over the revolver in his pocket.

Now, Keats was not a coward in all matters. He had risked money in trade, contradicted mayors, discharged foremen, and once eaten oysters in August. But physical danger, especially when accompanied by claws, distressed him. More than physical danger, however, he feared ridicule. Had he been alone, he would have confessed instantly. But Crown Square was full. The manager knew him; the policemen knew him; the scene-shifters knew him; and tomorrow the barmaids would know everything.

“I’m not certain it’s loaded,” he said.

Joe coughed.

“Not loaded?” exclaimed Councillor Bratt. “You always carry it loaded.”

“Generally. Naturally. But I may have omitted—”

The trainer seized his wrist. “Let me look.”

Keats resisted.

“It’s a delicate weapon,” he said. “Hair trigger.”

Another crash sounded. From inside the theatre came the screams of women who, though safely locked in dressing rooms, were resolved not to waste the occasion.

“For God’s sake, give it me!” said the trainer.

The Alderman looked at his cousin. Joe’s expression contained no mockery now. It was merely attentive.

Keats imagined the trainer entering the passage with the revolver; imagined him pressing the trigger at the instant when a tiger sprang; imagined the harmless detonation, the smoke, the astonishment, and what followed. He imagined, too, the newspapers: *Gallant Trainer Mauled—Weapon Proves Useless*. His own name would occur somewhere below.

He drew out the revolver, but kept hold of it.

“You can’t have it,” he said.

“Why not?”

“It’s loaded with blanks.”

There was a pause.

Even the tigers appeared, for one magnificent second, to respect it.

“Blanks?” repeated Councillor Bratt.

“Blank cartridges,” said the Alderman. “They make a report. That’s all.”

Joe asked gently, “What about the keyhole?”

Keats turned on him with tragic fury.

“I missed the keyhole! I missed the door! I missed the whole ground floor! The bullet went through the ceiling.”

The crowd received this confession in silence, and then with laughter. It began among the scene-shifters, reached the policemen, infected Councillor Bratt, and finally seized Joe, who was obliged to support himself against the mayoral carriage.

“The finest shot in the Five Towns!” Bratt gasped.

“Bottle-necks at thirty yards!” said somebody.

“Mind the ceiling!” called another.

The Alderman stood motionless, holding the useless revolver. His face had become the colour of the port for which he had suffered so much.

Meanwhile, Professor Zambello had no leisure for social criticism. He snatched a bundle of newspapers from a boy, thrust them upon the end of the iron bar, set fire to them at a gas jet, and ran into the passage. There followed roaring, shouting, a smell of singed paper, and violent hammering upon metal. Presently Sultan retired into one cage and Rajah into another, both offended but alive.

Thus were two Royal Bengal tigers separated by the evening edition.

No revolver was required.

The Alderman walked home alone. Behind him the account of the blank cartridges was already travelling through Hanbridge at a speed unattainable by tramcar. By breakfast it had reached Bursley; by luncheon, the Big Pits; and before the following evening a small boy in Slippery Lane pointed a wooden pistol at another small boy and cried, “Mind the ceiling!”

For a fortnight Keats avoided the Conservative Club. When at last he returned, nobody mentioned the revolver. This restraint was far worse than ridicule. Councillor Bratt asked solicitously whether the Town Hall roofs had been inspected. Joe offered him a catalogue of toy firearms. The Alderman bore these attentions with a dignity that was almost genuine.

He sold the revolver to the Bursley pawnbroker at a considerable loss.

“Giving up theatrical work?” asked the man.

“Change of habit,” said Keats.

And there was another change. Alderman Keats continued to wear broadcloth, to drink port when it was put before him, and to call young men “dogs” in moments of expansion. But he no longer limped in Crown Square. He no longer groaned when rising from committee-room chairs. If anyone inquired after his toe, he replied briefly that it was perfectly well.

For, though Hanbridge never perceived the philosophical connection, the gout and the revolver had belonged to the same equipment.

When the barker vanished, the gout went with it.

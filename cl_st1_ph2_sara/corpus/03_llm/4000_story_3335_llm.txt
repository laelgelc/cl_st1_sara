# THE DOCTOR’S BILL

It was near two o’clock on a raw November morning, when Dr. Elton was awakened by a violent ringing of his night-bell. The wind drove the rain in sharp gusts against the windows; the fire in his chamber had sunk to a few red embers; and the physician, who had not returned from a distant patient until after midnight, had enjoyed scarcely an hour of that repose which exhausted nature imperatively demanded.

Again the bell rang—longer and louder than before.

“Who is there?” called the doctor, raising the sash an inch or two.

“It’s me, sir—Thomas Marvel. Do come as quick as you can! Henry’s dying!”

“Dying! What is the matter with him?”

“I don’t know, sir. He breathes very strange, and his mother says he’s in a fit.”

The word *fit* settled the question. In less than five minutes Dr. Elton had exchanged his warm bed for wet boots, an overcoat, and the uncertain shelter of an umbrella which the wind seemed determined to turn wrong side outward. Mr. Marvel lived but three squares away; yet, before the doctor reached his door, his clothes were dripping, and the rain had found its way down his neck with that peculiar ingenuity which cold rain always exhibits on such occasions.

Mrs. Marvel met him in the passage, with a lamp in one hand and a handkerchief in the other.

“Oh, doctor! I’m so glad you’ve come! I’m afraid it’s all over with the dear child!”

“Where is he?”

“Up stairs. This way—do hurry! He gave one dreadful start, and then lay as still as death.”

Dr. Elton entered the chamber, approached the bed, and looked at the supposed sufferer. Henry Marvel, a pale, slender boy of eight years, was lying upon his side, with one hand under his cheek. His breathing was deep and regular. The physician laid his fingers upon the wrist, then placed his hand upon the child’s forehead.

“What had he eaten?” he asked.

“Nothing at all,” replied Mrs. Marvel, promptly.

“Only some plum-cake,” said her husband.

“And a little mince-pie,” added the grandmother, from the opposite side of the bed.

“He begged for a sausage,” said Mrs. Marvel; “but it was no larger than my finger.”

“And some roasted chestnuts,” observed Mr. Marvel, after a moment’s reflection.

Dr. Elton turned toward them.

“The child is asleep.”

“Asleep!” exclaimed Mrs. Marvel.

“Sound asleep.”

“But, doctor, did you hear him breathe?”

“I hear him now.”

“And don’t you think his breath catches?”

“No.”

“Feel his pulse again—do! It seemed to me, just before Thomas went for you, that it stopped altogether.”

“If it had stopped altogether,” replied Dr. Elton, with more gravity than the observation deserved, “Thomas would scarcely have found me of service.”

At this moment Henry stirred, opened his eyes, and looked about him in sleepy astonishment.

“Where does it hurt you, dear?” asked his mother, bending eagerly over him.

“Nowhere,” murmured the boy.

“Don’t you feel very sick?”

“No, mother.”

“Isn’t your head bad?”

“No.”

“Your stomach?”

“I’m sleepy.”

“He never talks so when he is well!” said Mrs. Marvel, turning triumphantly to the physician. “You see how heavy he is. I am sure something ought to be done.”

Dr. Elton was tempted to answer that something ought to be done with the plum-cake, mince-pie, sausage, and chestnuts; but he restrained himself. It is one of the smaller trials of a physician that he must often preserve a decorous face where another man might be permitted to laugh. He directed that the child should be allowed to sleep, recommended moderation at the breakfast-table, and was preparing to depart, when Mrs. Marvel detained him.

“Won’t you leave any medicine?”

“No medicine is required.”

“Not even a powder?”

“Not even a powder.”

“Then what shall I do if he gets worse?”

“Send for me.”

This assurance, which should have produced tranquillity, had precisely the opposite effect; for Mrs. Marvel now possessed both permission and encouragement to summon the doctor upon the slightest renewal of her fears.

At nine o’clock the same morning, while Dr. Elton was taking a hurried breakfast, another messenger arrived from the Marvels. Henry had refused his oatmeal, and complained that his mouth tasted badly. The doctor found the boy dressed and sitting by the fire, engaged in constructing a boat out of a shingle. His tongue was slightly coated, as the tongue of a child who has eaten an improper supper is apt to be. There were no febrile symptoms; the pulse was natural; the skin moist; and the countenance, to a practised eye, gave no evidence of disease.

“I knew you would find something,” said Mrs. Marvel, when he mentioned the coated tongue. “What does it indicate?”

“Indigestion.”

“Is indigestion dangerous?”

“Not ordinarily.”

“But it may become dangerous?”

“Almost any thing may become dangerous, if we take pains enough to make it so.”

Mrs. Marvel did not perceive the qualification. She desired a prescription, and, as she would have been dissatisfied without one, the doctor ordered a mild aperient, together with strict abstinence from pastry and rich food. The aperient was administered; the abstinence was not.

Thus began a course of medical dependence which continued, with little interruption, for nearly a year. If Henry sneezed twice before breakfast, Dr. Elton was requested to call. If he came home heated from play, his mother feared inflammation of the brain. If his hands were cold, his circulation was failing; if warm, a fever was approaching. A transient redness of the throat suggested scarlet fever; a pain produced by running was regarded as an internal rupture; a heavy sleep was narcotic stupor; and wakefulness, of course, betokened nervous irritation.

Nor was Henry the only member of the household whose symptoms called Dr. Elton’s skill into requisition. Mr. Marvel, after dining too freely at a public supper, experienced oppression at the chest, and sent for the physician under the apprehension of heart disease. Mrs. Marvel discovered, one Sunday evening, a stiffness in her neck, which she feared might terminate in paralysis before morning. The grandmother had long possessed a pain that travelled from shoulder to shoulder, never occupying the same place two days together, but always requiring a fresh opinion when it moved.

“Doctor,” Mrs. Marvel would say, “I know you think me anxious; but a mother cannot be too careful.”

“A mother may be injudiciously careful,” he once ventured to answer.

“That is easily said by one who has no children.”

Dr. Elton had buried two; but he did not tell her so.

The repetition of these alarms would have been merely ludicrous had it not exerted a serious influence upon the boy. His food was watched, but not regulated; his pulse was felt twenty times a day; his tongue inspected after every meal; and his natural sensations were interpreted to him as symptoms of approaching disease. He was dosed when he required exercise, confined to the house when he needed air, and kept from wholesome sleep that his mother might ascertain whether he could be readily awakened. Every complaint obtained sympathy and medicine. The consequence was what might have been expected. Henry grew timid, languid, and attentive to every movement of his body. He learned the taste of tinctures before he had learned the multiplication table, and could describe his “nervous feelings” with a minuteness that would have done credit to a valetudinarian of sixty.

Dr. Elton saw the evil and remonstrated.

“You are making the child an invalid,” he said.

“Making him an invalid!” repeated Mrs. Marvel, with offended tenderness. “Why, doctor, we do every thing in the world to keep him well.”

“You do too much.”

“Would you have us neglect him?”

“I would have you cease to frighten him. Let him run, eat plain food, sleep undisturbed, and forget that he has a pulse.”

“But suppose he should be taken suddenly ill?”

“Then send for me.”

The familiar permission restored her confidence in her own course, and the lesson was lost.

It is not necessary to trace every cough, ache, shiver, and imagined eruption through the succeeding months. They stand in Dr. Elton’s ledger with a distinctness which they never possessed in nature.

“March 4—Visit to Henry Marvel, sore throat.

“March 6—Visit; throat natural.

“March 19—Called at night; nightmare.

“April 2—Mrs. Marvel, pain in side.

“April 3—Visit; pain removed.

“April 17—Henry, supposed measles; irritation caused by woollen shirt.

“May 8—Mr. Marvel, dizziness after dinner.

“May 21—Called at eleven P.M.; Henry restless.

“June 1—Visit; Henry well.”

And so the entries proceeded, some singly, some in groups, but all representing time withdrawn from other duties, distance traversed, sleep broken, and responsibility assumed. A physician’s charge is not merely for the ten minutes during which he sits beside a bed. It is for knowledge laboriously acquired; for readiness at hours when other men rest; for judgment exercised under circumstances in which an error may cost a life; and, not least, for the burden of that anxiety which follows him from the sick-room and often sits down with him at his own fireside.

At the end of the year, Dr. Elton sent Mr. Marvel his account. It amounted to one hundred and forty-three dollars.

The bill had not been delivered two hours when Mr. Marvel entered the office, holding the paper between his thumb and finger as if it were an offensive object.

“Doctor,” said he, “there must be some mistake here.”

“I think not,” replied Dr. Elton.

“One hundred and forty-three dollars!”

“That is the amount.”

“For attending my family one year?”

“For the visits and medicines specified.”

Mr. Marvel drew a chair forward and unfolded the account.

“Here are three visits charged in one week.”

“I made three.”

“But Henry wasn’t sick three times.”

“No; I was called three times during the same attack.”

“Well, it was nothing but a little cold.”

“You feared, at the time, that it was inflammation of the lungs.”

“My wife might have been alarmed; women generally are. But you knew better.”

“I told you there was no danger.”

“Then why did you come again?”

“Because you sent for me.”

Mr. Marvel passed to another item.

“‘Night visit, five dollars.’ That is monstrous! You were only in the house fifteen minutes.”

“I was taken from my bed in a storm, and found your son asleep.”

“That proves the visit was unnecessary.”

“It was unnecessary; but I did not make it unnecessary.”

“You doctors have a convenient way of looking at these things,” said Mr. Marvel, smiling coldly. “If a man is sick, you charge for curing him; and if he a’n't sick, you charge for telling him so.”

“We are expected to ascertain the fact in either case.”

“Here is another charge for Mrs. Marvel’s neck.”

“I attended her.”

“She was well the next morning.”

“I am glad to hear it.”

“And here—two dollars for advice about Henry’s diet! Why, any body can tell a child not to eat too much.”

“Very possibly. Yet no one in your house appeared able to make the discovery until I mentioned it.”

Mr. Marvel colored.

“I don’t wish to dispute with you, doctor. We have always treated you as a friend. You have come into our house at all hours, and we have placed perfect confidence in you.”

“That is true.”

“And now you take advantage of it!”

Dr. Elton’s face changed, but his voice remained calm.

“In what manner?”

“By sending a bill that no reasonable man would pay. Why, there has not been a serious case of sickness in my family during the whole year!”

“And whose judgment satisfied you of that fact?”

Mr. Marvel rose.

“I shall pay one hundred dollars, though even that is too much.”

“The account is one hundred and forty-three.”

“Then you may collect it by law, if you choose. And I give you notice now—we shall employ another physician.”

“That is your privilege,” said Dr. Elton.

Mr. Marvel left the office with the air of a person who had resisted an imposition. At home he repeated the interview, omitting nothing except the circumstances which told against himself. Mrs. Marvel declared she had always thought Dr. Elton “fond of running up an account,” though she had never before communicated the suspicion; and the grandmother remembered that, on one occasion, he had remained less than twelve minutes. Thus the man who had been dragged from bed by their fears, detained by their questions, and censured whenever he advised less medicine, became, in the clear light of returning health and an unpaid bill, the author of visits which they themselves had demanded.

For several weeks Dr. Elton heard nothing more of the family. Henry, under the care of a new practitioner, continued to swallow medicine with encouraging regularity.

About this time a merchant named Smith arrived in the town with his daughter Clara, a young lady of eighteen. They were on their way to a northern city, but remained for a few days at the principal inn, where Mr. Smith had business with several manufacturers. On the third evening Clara complained of headache, chilliness, and weariness. Her father, supposing that she had taken cold during the journey, directed her to retire early. In the night she became feverish, and in the morning Dr. Elton was called.

The case did not, at first, present an alarming aspect; but there was a dullness in the eye, a peculiar prostration, and a disparity between the temperature of the skin and the strength of the pulse which awakened the physician’s attention. He questioned the patient closely, learned that typhus fever had lately prevailed in a village through which they had passed, and requested that she should not be removed.

“Do you think her seriously ill?” asked Mr. Smith.

“I cannot yet determine the extent of the disease.”

“But you apprehend danger?”

“I apprehend the necessity of care.”

“Doctor, she is my only child.”

The merchant’s voice trembled as he spoke. He was a man of middle age, accustomed to command himself in business, and not inclined to magnify ordinary evils; but the possibility of losing this daughter broke through his habitual composure.

“Whatever can be done,” said he, “must be done. Do not spare attendance or expense. If another physician should be called in—”

“I will advise you, should consultation become necessary.”

“And you will come often?”

“As often as the symptoms require.”

“Promise me that you will neglect nothing.”

“No conscientious physician needs such a promise demanded of him,” replied Dr. Elton, gently; “but I will neglect nothing.”

During the next four days the fever steadily developed. The tongue became dry; the pulse quick and compressible; the skin hot, with irregular remissions; and a tenderness over the abdomen indicated irritation of the mucous membrane. Soon there was difficulty of deglutition, wandering of the mind, and that profound exhaustion which makes every change of posture an effort. The rosy color left Clara’s cheeks; her eyes grew unnaturally bright; and her hands, moving restlessly over the counterpane, sought to gather objects which existed only to her disordered senses.

Mr. Smith watched these changes with increasing terror.

“What does that mean, doctor?” he would ask, following Elton from the bedside. “Why does she pick at the bedclothes? Why does she not know me? Is the fever higher? Is her pulse weaker? Tell me the truth!”

“I have told you the truth. The disease is severe.”

“But she will recover?”

“I hope so.”

“Is that all you can say?”

“It is all an honest man can say.”

The physician now visited Clara three and sometimes four times in twenty-four hours. The case required vigilance rather than violent remedies. Nourishment had to be administered in small quantities; stimulants regulated with the nicest attention to their effect; the temperature moderated; the sick-room ventilated without exposing the patient to a current of cold air; and the nervous symptoms carefully distinguished from those which might indicate inflammation. What appears to an ignorant observer a mere alteration of a few drops in a glass may involve the result of years of study and the safety of a human life.

On the ninth night a messenger came for Dr. Elton shortly after twelve o’clock. Clara had sunk into a stupor from which the attendants could not rouse her. He found Mr. Smith walking the passage, his coat buttoned incorrectly and his face almost as pale as his daughter’s.

“She is dying!” he whispered.

“Let me see her.”

The room was close and oppressively warm. A frightened servant stood at the foot of the bed; the nurse was attempting to force wine between the patient’s lips; and every article needed for use had, with singular perversity, been placed out of reach.

“Open that window at the top,” said Dr. Elton. “Remove one of these blankets. Give me the light. Not so much wine—she cannot swallow it.”

He examined the pulse, listened to the respiration, and raised one eyelid. The condition was critical, but the sleep was not the narcotic sleep which the attendants feared. After directing the necessary measures, he sat down beside the bed to await their effect.

Hour after hour passed. The inn became silent. The storm which had threatened at sunset broke over the town, and the rain beat against the shutters, recalling to Dr. Elton, by one of those involuntary associations which visit us at unseasonable moments, his midnight summons to Henry Marvel. But there was no comedy in the scene before him. A young life hung trembling in the balance; and the difference between a timely stimulant and an excessive one, between quiet and injudicious disturbance, might be the difference between recovery and death.

Mr. Smith sat near the door, watching the physician’s countenance.

“Is there any change?”

“None.”

A quarter of an hour afterward he asked again—

“Do you perceive any change now?”

“Not yet.”

“Her breathing seems shorter.”

“It varies.”

“May I speak to her?”

“It will do no good.”

“Doctor, I cannot sit still and see her die!”

“Then walk in the passage; but do not disturb her.”

The unhappy father covered his face with his hands.

“I would give all I possess to hear her call me by name once more.”

Such declarations are common in the chamber of danger, and are usually sincere while the danger lasts. Gold then shrinks to its proper insignificance. Houses, lands, merchandise, the gain of years—all are freely weighed against one pulse of the beloved sufferer. But health is a powerful solvent of tragic resolutions; it dissolves them so completely that few traces remain when the doctor presents his account.

Toward morning Clara’s skin became gently moist. Her pulse, though still feeble, lost something of its fluttering uncertainty. She swallowed without being urged, turned her head upon the pillow, and presently pronounced a few indistinct words.

Mr. Smith sprang forward.

“Did she speak? Clara! My child!”

Dr. Elton held up his hand.

“Be quiet. The change is favorable.”

“Then she is safe?”

“I did not say that.”

“But there is hope?”

“There has always been hope. There is more now.”

The merchant grasped the doctor’s hand and pressed it between both his own.

“God bless you! If she lives, I shall owe her life to you.”

“No,” replied Dr. Elton, weary from the night’s watching; “you may owe much to a good constitution, a careful nurse, and Providence. I have only used the means placed in my hands.”

For several days the issue remained doubtful. Twice there was a recurrence of alarming prostration; once a hemorrhage was feared; and for nearly forty-eight hours the patient took scarcely nourishment enough to sustain life. Dr. Elton carried the case with him wherever he went. While listening to the complaints of others, his thoughts reverted to that rapid pulse at the inn; at meals he calculated the effect of the last prescription; and in sleep he started from confused dreams in which he had forgotten some essential direction. This mental burden, invisible to the patient’s friends, is among the heaviest portions of medical service, and is never entered separately in the ledger.

At length the fever yielded. Clara recognized her father, asked where she was, and wondered why the summer light looked so strange through the curtains. Convalescence was slow, as it must be after such a disease; but the peril had passed. Color returned gradually to her lips; she was raised for a few minutes in bed, then moved to an easy-chair, and afterward permitted to walk across the room with her father’s arm around her.

Mr. Smith’s gratitude during this period appeared without limit.

“My dear sir,” he said to Dr. Elton, “I can never repay you.”

“There will be no occasion for an impossible attempt,” replied the doctor, smiling.

“You have saved my child.”

“I have attended her faithfully. We will say no more.”

“But I shall say more. I have seen what you physicians endure. Night after night—such anxiety—such responsibility! Your profession is not half appreciated.”

Dr. Elton had heard similar observations before, but he was willing to believe them again.

When Clara had recovered sufficient strength to continue the journey, the account was sent to her father. It contained the dates of forty-six visits, including six at night, together with medicines furnished and one prolonged attendance of nearly seven hours. The whole amounted to ninety-two dollars.

Mr. Smith entered the doctor’s office the next morning. He did not bring the open expression with which he had formerly greeted him. His lips were compressed, and the bill was folded into a narrow strip.

“I have received your account,” said he.

Dr. Elton bowed.

“Ninety-two dollars.”

“That is correct.”

“Correct! For less than five weeks’ attendance?”

“For forty-six visits, six night calls, medicines, and attendance during the crisis.”

Mr. Smith laid the bill upon the desk.

“I confess, doctor, I am disappointed.”

“In your daughter’s recovery?”

“You know what I mean. I had supposed you a different kind of man.”

“What kind of man did you suppose me?”

“One who would not take advantage of a father’s distress.”

Dr. Elton looked steadily at him.

“Explain yourself.”

“You knew that I was a stranger; that my daughter’s life was at stake; that I would refuse nothing. Under such circumstances a man is helpless. He must submit to whatever the physician chooses to charge.”

“Did I make unnecessary visits?”

“I am not competent to decide that.”

“Did I neglect her?”

“Certainly not.”

“Did I refuse to come at night?”

“No.”

“Did I abandon her during the crisis?”

“No; but ninety-two dollars! It is an extortionate sum.”

“What amount would you consider just?”

Mr. Smith hesitated.

“Forty dollars would be liberal.”

“Forty dollars,” repeated Dr. Elton, “for five weeks’ care in a dangerous typhus fever, with night attendance and medicines?”

“You had other patients in the house.”

“Two, whom I visited upon separate floors, and to whom separate time and judgment were given.”

“It costs you nothing to step into another room.”

“Then perhaps it costs me nothing to cross your daughter’s room.”

“That is evading the point. Professional men seem to think their time made of gold.”

“And other men seem to think it made of air.”

Mr. Smith took up his hat.

“I shall not be imposed upon.”

“Nor shall I consent to be defrauded.”

“Defrauded! That is an extraordinary word.”

“It describes an attempt to obtain labor and skill without paying their value.”

“I offered forty dollars.”

“You offered less than half the account.”

“And all that the service is worth.”

There are injuries which provoke anger, and others which produce only weariness. Dr. Elton remembered the father standing beside Clara’s bed, declaring that he would give all he possessed to hear her speak his name. He remembered the tears, the grasped hands, the blessings, and the solemn acknowledgment that medical labor was not half appreciated. The contrast was too complete for indignation.

“You may leave the account,” he said. “I have nothing more to add.”

Mr. Smith paid at last, but not until he had complained at the inn, at the stage-office, and to several merchants of the town that Dr. Elton was undoubtedly skilful, but “exorbitant in his charges.” The qualification did not soften the injury. Men who had never watched a fever, studied a symptom, or borne responsibility for a life, nodded wisely and observed that doctors generally charged according to a patient’s means. Clara, who knew only that she had been ill and afterward grew well, heard little of the dispute. Had danger returned, her father would again have sought Dr. Elton before all others; but danger had departed, and with it departed the estimate he had placed upon the physician’s services.

The cases of Marvel and Smith differed widely. In one, the doctor was summoned for ailments which existed chiefly in apprehension; in the other, he encountered disease in its most formidable shape. In one, his judgment consisted largely in doing little; in the other, every resource of knowledge, patience, and vigilance was called into requisition. Yet both ended alike. The Marvels said, “There was no sickness; therefore the visits were worth nothing.” Mr. Smith said, “The sickness is over; therefore the attendance should cost little.” Thus the physician was condemned whether disease proved trivial or dangerous—if he reassured, he had done nothing; if he cured, nature had done it all; if he failed, his skill was questioned; and if he presented a bill, his motives were suspected.

The public is too apt to regard the physician as a ministering angel while death stands at the door, and as a tradesman the moment health returns. Yet he is compelled to unite both characters. He must possess sympathy without weakness, courage without rashness, patience without indifference, and learning without pretension; but he must also buy food, pay rent, educate his children, and provide against sickness and age. His knowledge is not gathered without years of study; his readiness is maintained at the sacrifice of ease; his usefulness exposes him to contagion, fatigue, broken rest, and the caprices of fear. Are these nothing? Is the midnight ride to be forgotten because the alarm proved false? Is the anxious vigil valueless because the patient recovered?

We pay the builder for a house that shelters us, the lawyer for defending property, and the merchant for goods that perish in their use; yet too many begrudge compensation to him whose judgment may preserve the capacity to enjoy house, property, and every earthly possession. When pain wrings the body, when a child gasps for breath, when fever clouds the eye of one we love—then no distance is too great, no hour too late, no fee worth considering. “Send for the doctor!” is the universal cry. But when the cheek regains its color and the familiar voice is heard again, memory becomes strangely economical.

A word to the wise is sufficient. Let those who demand faithful attendance pay for it faithfully. Let them remember, when the account comes, not merely the minutes spent beside the bed, but the study, labor, discomfort, risk, and responsibility represented there. Gratitude which ends when danger ends is but selfishness under a tender name; and he who values the physician only while he fears the grave has not learned the common justice due from man to man.

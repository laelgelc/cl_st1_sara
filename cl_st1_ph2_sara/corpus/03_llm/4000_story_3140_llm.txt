### The First Two Dollars and a Half

If there be one form of literary enterprise against which, in the confidence of youth, I more stoutly set my face than another, it is that melancholy species of navigation known as autobiography. The voyager embarks with a mirror for compass, memory for chart, and the indulgence of his friends for favorable weather; he is expected to discover a continent, and commonly succeeds in exhibiting a fog.

“How,” I used to demand, “can a person of proper modesty write four hundred pages concerning himself?”

I had not then learned that proper modesty, like an old-fashioned travelling cloak, may be folded very small when the journey requires it; nor that one reaches an age at which four hundred pages concerning oneself appear less formidable than four lines concerning somebody else. Yet even now I approach the untried coast with reluctance. There are shoals upon it—vanity, sentiment, the treacherous sand-bar of inaccurate recollection—and I am not so good a sailor as to mistake these for open sea.

But you, my reader, who have followed me through many invented fortunes, may perhaps possess a friendly curiosity about the poor workwoman behind them. You may ask, not unreasonably, where she found her tools; who taught her to use them; what voices first stirred the silence in which stories grow; and by what solemn or ridiculous ceremony she became aware that words could be exchanged for bread, books, pictures, or even cream cakes.

Therefore, under protest, I do set my hand and seal to a few recollections. They are not a Life. Heaven forbid! They are no more than certain lamps seen down a long road—some burning steadily, some blurred by rain, one or two, I fear, lighted by imagination after memory had neglected its duty.

My first consciousness of literary ancestry took a singular form. It consisted of a silver mug, a large dictionary, and a coffin.

The mug stood upon a shelf in my grandmother’s room. The dictionary, which required the united strength of two children to lift it, occupied a table beneath the ministerial portraits. The coffin—happily less permanent as household furniture—belonged to a funeral which I attended at an age when death was not an idea, but an arrangement of black cloth, low voices, and doors opened by persons who had forgotten how to smile.

These objects attached themselves in my mind to my paternal grandfather, the Reverend Doctor Lucian Vale, whose reputation as a biblical scholar extended far beyond my small geographical knowledge. Learned men cited him in journals which I supposed to be a species of church newspaper; candidates for the ministry approached him with a respect that made their boots creak timidly; and his Hebrew grammar, bound in brown leather, had ruined many a promising afternoon for my father.

I knew none of this. I knew only that the silver mug had been presented to him by grateful students, that the dictionary contained every word in the world except the ones for which I looked, and that people who died were put into coffins even when they had written commentaries on the prophets.

Years afterward I read my grandfather’s books. They were strong, patient, honorable works, built as one might build stone bridges—each proposition fitted to its fellow, each authority tested, the whole designed to bear the traffic of serious minds. I cannot pretend that the mantle of his scholarship descended upon me. If it did, I must have cut it into ribbons and used them to tie up manuscripts. But from him, perhaps, came some respect for the exact word, some conviction that language is not a basket of autumn leaves to be tossed for color, but an instrument by which truth may be approached, though never captured without a struggle.

My other grandfather was of a different temper and a more adventurous history. Judge Ambrose Gray had less Hebrew and more thunder in him. He belonged to those grave reformers who could not pass an injustice without asking its name, ancestry, and intention; and who, having received evasive answers, took off their coats.

His house stood near one of the secret roads by which fugitive slaves travelled northward. In its cellar were a narrow chamber, a bench, two blankets, and an iron candlestick. No child in the family was forbidden to know why they were there. My grandfather held that truth, however terrible, should not be locked away from children, though preserves and gunpowder might properly be.

Many a frightened man or woman rested beneath that roof. Once, as the story went, a mother arrived in winter with a baby wrapped in part of her own skirt. The pursuers were not half a mile behind. My grandfather hid her in the chamber, saddled his fastest horse, and rode openly in the opposite direction, wearing a cloak that could be seen across three fields. The hunters followed the cloak. Before dawn the woman and child were on their way to safety.

When I first heard this, I felt that no epic hero had ever performed an action so magnificent. Achilles diminished; Hector became a person of respectable but remote interest. My grandfather, in muddy boots, riding through sleet with the whole power of wickedness galloping after his cloak, possessed my world.

He was also haunted.

The conjunction did not surprise me. A man who defied slave-catchers might reasonably be expected to entertain spirits. The haunting began, according to my grandmother, with measured footsteps in the upper hall. The family heard them after midnight: a slow approach, a pause outside the guest chamber, then a retreat toward the stairs. Doors opened without hands. A candlestick moved from the mantel to the table. Once a bed-quilt was found folded into the shape of a human figure, although no human being admitted to the folding.

My grandfather investigated with the severity he brought to legal evidence. He measured windows, examined latches, questioned servants, and sat up three nights with a loaded pistol and a Greek Testament. The ghost, either intimidated by scholarship or amused by firearms, maintained silence until he slept.

At last a travelling medium was invited to interpret the disturbance. The family assembled in the parlor around a table heavy enough to resist ordinary enthusiasm. Hands were laid upon it. Questions were asked. The table trembled, tipped, and delivered by a laborious alphabet the intelligence that the spirits desired justice, remembrance, and—after several doubtful letters—squash pie.

My grandmother supplied the pie.

“But did they eat it?” I asked, breathless, when the tale was told to me.

“No,” she replied. “Your grandfather did.”

This conclusion shook my faith in supernatural communications, but strengthened my regard for family history. For years I examined dark halls with a delicious apprehension. The Gray ghost was less a terror than a literary instructor. It taught me that a house may possess another house within it; that a footstep may be at once a sound and a question; that the unseen, whether spirit, conscience, memory, or mouse, has uncommon power over the human imagination.

Between the scholar who interpreted prophets and the reformer who entertained ghosts, there seems to have been prepared for me a divided inheritance: the wish to know what words mean, and the suspicion that they mean something more.

But ancestry, however imposing in a genealogical paragraph, is a remote government. Childhood is ruled by nearer powers. My mother was my first sovereign.

She was herself a popular writer in a day when the phrase “literary woman” was often pronounced as if it described a domestic accident. Her stories appeared in the magazines, her books passed from hand to hand in parlors and village libraries, and strangers wrote to her with that touching faith which readers bestow upon one who has given them comfort. Some asked advice about their souls; some enclosed poems; some had discovered errors in her theology; and one persistent gentleman, having read three of her novels, offered marriage upon the reasonable assumption that he understood her perfectly.

She kept his letter because it made my father laugh.

Her study was a small room at the back of the house, with two windows, a green desk, and shelves crowded beyond the intentions of the carpenter. A vase of dried grass stood beside the ink. Proof-sheets lay under a stone paperweight. There were always pins in unlikely places and a shawl over the chair, as if she had just risen and would return before one could miss her.

Yet she did not dwell apart from us in the mysterious cloud with which children surround an author. We invaded the study. We built forts beneath the desk, sailed paper boats in the wash-basin, and presented petitions at precisely the moment when a sentence had almost consented to finish itself. She could suspend a hero at the edge of moral ruin to mend a doll; she could leave a heroine kneeling in a storm while she settled a dispute concerning a red marble; she could write with one hand and rock a cradle with the other.

I do not offer this as a pretty picture. It was a beautiful one, but beauty may conceal injustice as roses conceal a wall. The world praised her industry and profited by her sacrifice. It admired the books; it admired the children; it required that neither should show the cost of the other. She herself accepted the double duty with courage, perhaps with pride. Gifted women of her generation were trained to regard exhaustion as evidence of virtue. They carried genius as if it were contraband, apologizing for it at every domestic frontier.

My mother loved us with a gaiety that made service seem natural. She also loved her work with an ardor she rarely confessed. In her there was no simple contest between the mother and the artist, as moralists sometimes arrange these matters for convenient discussion. Both were authentic; both were tender; both demanded the whole woman. The civil war was not between affection and ambition, but between two affections equally commanding.

I remember her writing late at night. The house was still; a single lamp shone beneath her door. From my bed I could hear the faint advance and retreat of her pen. Sometimes it stopped, and I knew, without knowing how, that she had leaned her forehead upon her hand. Then the sound began again.

In the morning she was at breakfast, bright and properly dressed, inquiring whether we had remembered our overshoes.

I cannot recollect her without color. She wore a canary-yellow coat one autumn, and my own was red. The two garments stand in memory more distinctly than faces: yellow moving before me along the road, red dancing behind it; yellow pausing to wait; red rushing forward through crisp leaves. It was late October, and the maples had spent themselves magnificently. My mother was expecting the birth of another child. She walked slowly, though she made a game of it and allowed me to exchange coats with her.

The yellow one reached nearly to my ankles. The red one was absurd upon her.

“Now,” she said, “we are each other.”

I considered this possible. Children, unlike philosophers, accept the transference of identity upon slight evidence. I strutted in the yellow coat, believing myself tall, wise, and beautiful. She came after me in scarlet sleeves too short for her wrists, laughing until she had to rest against the stone wall.

That is the last outdoor picture I possess of her.

She was finishing a book. There were deadlines—a hateful word, and in her case almost a literal one—proof-sheets, letters from the publisher, household cares, the approaching confinement, and the ordinary innumerable claims which fasten most fiercely upon the person least willing to refuse them. She worked when she should have slept. She assured my father that the manuscript wanted only a chapter. A chapter, in family arithmetic, has often cost more than it earned.

The baby was born. My mother lived long enough to know that it breathed and to ask whether the last proofs had been corrected. Then exhaustion, which had been waiting with a patience denied to all who loved her, took possession.

Of the funeral I recall very little in order. There was the smell of damp wool. There were black gloves, too large for me, turning my hands into solemn paws. There was a minister’s voice coming and going like wind in another room. There was the baby, white and astonished, carried to the font and baptized while the coffin stood near.

This offended me. I had not understood that death and christening could occur in the same hour. It seemed a breach of manners on the part of religion.

“Why are they giving the baby a name now?” I whispered.

“So that your mother may hear it,” some tender person answered.

For years I believed that the dead remained just within hearing, though beyond reply. Perhaps I believe it still in some province of the heart where theology has no jurisdiction.

I do not remember weeping. That fact once troubled me, until I learned that children’s grief often hides in obedience. I stood where I was put. I held the black gloves. I watched the silver plate upon the coffin. When the adults bent their heads, I bent mine. But afterward I searched for the yellow coat and could not find it.

So much for lamentation. If I write more, I shall write less truly.

My father was left with motherless children, a scholarly profession, inadequate income, and a grief upon which no household schedule waited. He met these conditions without rhetoric. It is one distinction between noble sorrow and theatrical sorrow that the first remembers breakfast.

He rose early, answered our questions, inspected our shoes, pursued his own work, and learned, with varying success, those sciences which society had informed him were not masculine. He could interpret a difficult philosophical passage before he could tie a child’s sash; but he applied himself to the sash with humility, and ultimately produced knots of great permanence.

Once he attempted to dry my stockings upon wooden stretchers. He inserted both stretchers into one stocking and spent ten indignant minutes accusing the manufacturer of dishonesty.

“This article,” he announced, “has been constructed for a leg unknown to anatomy.”

We discovered the second stocking in his coat pocket at church.

Such errors endeared him to us. They relieved the grandeur of fatherhood. Yet beneath the small confusions lay a discipline both intellectual and moral which became the climate of my mind. A child does not analyze climate. She plays in it, rebels against it, flourishes or sickens according to forces she cannot name. My father’s seriousness, his reverence for truth, his impatience with shams, his belief that thought was a form of conduct—these entered me as weather enters a growing tree.

He was exacting. Sloth offended him more than failure; evasion more than either. If I had not learned a lesson, it was useless to present a graceful account of the circumstances. He had a scholar’s appetite for facts and a theologian’s suspicion of excuses.

“But I meant to do it,” I once pleaded.

“My dear,” he replied, “the world is already overcrowded with excellent intentions. It is completed duties that remain scarce.”

I thought this harsh, and repeated it to my dolls in a harsher tone.

He did not try to manufacture a prodigy. For this I owe him gratitude. Nothing is more tiresome than the child who has been informed that she is remarkable, except perhaps the adult into whom she develops. My father expected attention, accuracy, and honesty; brilliance he left to Providence, which showed no haste in my case.

I was not precocious. Let this be recorded clearly for the encouragement of parents whose children prefer climbing trees to studying syntax. I was noisy, brown, perpetually scratched, and devoted to enterprises which ended in torn clothing. I owned a hoop, a sled, a collection of stones, three dead beetles in a pillbox, and a belief that I could jump from the barn roof if given sufficient privacy.

Books I loved, but not always as books. They were doors, shields, building blocks, hiding places. I read stories greedily and lessons reluctantly, making a distinction between nourishment and medicine which educators have not entirely abolished. I composed no epics at seven, translated no Latin at nine, and at eleven could not spell “separate” with confidence.

Yet words had begun their underground work.

My father read aloud in the evenings. His voice, grave but flexible, could make poetry enter a room as a person enters. Wordsworth came to us in this manner—not the name upon a page, but the lonely reaper singing beyond our lamplight; the daffodils moving in their golden multitude; the child by the shore carrying intimations which we children accepted more readily than the adults who wrote commentaries upon them.

My father never hurried poetry. He allowed silence after it, a respect more useful than explanation. If we asked what a line meant, he answered; but he did not tear the petals apart to prove the existence of a flower.

De Quincey arrived later, magnificently unsafe. The opium visions, the mail-coach, the clangor and darkness of those impassioned sentences seized me before I understood half their allusions. Here language did not merely state; it marched, burned, tolled bells, opened abysses. I had known that words could tell what happened. I began to perceive that they could themselves happen.

“How does he do that?” I asked.

My father looked over the page.

“By feeling intensely,” he said, “and revising severely.”

This seemed an unsympathetic answer. I preferred to imagine De Quincey pursued by winged sentences which he had only to catch. Revision had the sound of arithmetic. Nevertheless, my father’s phrase remained. Feeling and discipline; fire and form; the dream and the proof-sheet. Many years of authorship have not carried me beyond that lesson.

We read history, sermons, essays, and biography. We disputed at table. We were permitted to ask questions, though not to be impertinent under the disguise of inquiry. Doubt was not treated as a moral disease; but intellectual laziness received no such charity. My father believed that faith had nothing to fear from thought and much to fear from cant.

In the severe theological household of my grandfather, certain questions had worn stiff collars. In ours they sometimes took off their coats and sat by the fire.

Thus my literary education advanced, if advancing be the word for a creature who alternately runs, falls down, and pursues butterflies in the wrong direction. Meanwhile I told stories to my younger companions. These were delivered orally, in installments, and featured a heroine of unsurpassed courage who bore a suspicious resemblance to myself, except that she was beautiful, obedient, and able to swim.

When invention failed, I introduced a thunderstorm.

This device has not wholly disappeared from fiction.

My first written narratives were composed in copybooks intended for more honorable use. Their covers bore marbled patterns; their pages, at first, presented a purity which rebuked me. I ruined this purity with pirates, orphans, underground passages, lost wills, and virtuous persons suffering from diseases never encountered by our physician.

One heroine died of “brain fever” after discovering that she had two fathers. Another was imprisoned for fourteen years in a tower but emerged with unimpaired complexion and a complete education in French. My villains repented upon small provocation. My heroes possessed eyes of remarkable color and no visible occupation.

I concealed these productions, though not with enough care to prevent their discovery. My father found one beneath a cushion. I waited for judgment.

He read a page.

“Why,” he asked, “does this young woman faint?”

“She has just learned that Lord Algernon is not her brother.”

“Would that make a sensible person unconscious?”

“She is very sensitive.”

“So, apparently, is Lord Algernon. He has wept six times in eleven pages.”

I recovered the manuscript in wounded silence. That night I reduced Lord Algernon’s tears by half. It was my first revision under editorial pressure.

At thirteen I sent a short piece to a juvenile paper.

I cannot now decide which amazes me more: that I sent it, or that it was printed. The story concerned a poor girl who returned a purse, refused a reward, rescued a child, converted a drunkard, and became a teacher, all within the space of three columns. Its moral equipment was excessive, but its punctuation, I believe, respectable.

The envelope had to be posted without family knowledge. Not that writing was forbidden; secrecy merely lent grandeur to the event. I copied the manuscript in my neatest hand, folded it until it resembled a legal accusation, and walked to the post office with the sensation of carrying destiny beneath my cloak.

For several weeks I watched the mail. Each approaching paper caused an agitation which I disguised by appearing not to want it. At last the number came. There was my title. Beneath it stood my name—or rather the solemn initials with which I had veiled my identity, though no one acquainted with me could have failed to recognize my moral enthusiasm.

Print performed a miracle. The words which had looked thin and doubtful in ink appeared authoritative in type. Even the poor girl’s impossible career acquired dignity. I read the piece standing, sitting, by the window, under the table, and once aloud to myself in the barn.

No payment accompanied publication. I had not expected any. Fame, in my conception, was a sufficient currency, especially when confined to the family.

My father discovered the paper.

“So,” he said, “the secret author has entered literature.”

I prepared for irony, but his expression was grave.

“You have done a brave thing. Now you must learn to do a good one.”

That sentence lowered me from Pegasus to the schoolroom, where, as he knew, I belonged. Yet he placed the paper in his desk. Years later, after many productions more deserving of preservation, I found it there, folded beside one of my mother’s letters.

The first publication did not instantly establish a career. Editors did not assemble beneath our windows. Publishers remained capable of sleep. I continued my studies, tore dresses, performed household tasks with variable enthusiasm, and wrote in intervals stolen from occupations which seemed designed by Providence to prevent authorship.

There followed rejections. Ah, those early rejections! How personal they appeared, how judicial, how final! A printed slip no larger than a visiting card could darken the universe before dinner.

One editor regretted that my article was “not adapted to present needs.” I spent a day wondering what the present needed and why it had neglected to inform me. Another manuscript returned bearing no comment except a blue pencil mark whose meaning I never discovered. I interpreted it successively as contempt, encouragement, and a secret publishing symbol.

My father counseled persistence without flattery.

“An editor,” he said, “is not necessarily your enemy because he declines to become your accomplice.”

I did not appreciate this.

But I wrote again. Some inward necessity, at once shy and stubborn, had awakened. It was not yet ambition in the worldly sense. I did not dream of a study, reviews, correspondence, editions, or the numerous anxieties by which literary success proves that it is no idler. I wanted to make something that had not existed; to take a face glimpsed in a crowd, a voice overheard at dusk, a fear, an injustice, the color of a coat among fallen leaves, and give it duration.

Perhaps all authorship begins in resistance to disappearance.

At sixteen—or near that uncertain frontier—I sent a small sketch to a weekly journal. The subject was humble: an old seamstress in our town who kept a canary, suffered from rheumatism, and had once travelled forty miles to see the ocean. Nothing occurred in the story except that she saved for years to make the journey and, upon reaching the shore, was more impressed by a child’s red pail than by the Atlantic.

This time I attempted no conversion, inheritance, rescue, or brain fever. The seamstress remained poor. The ocean did not reward her. The canary, against all fictional instinct, survived.

I had begun, without knowing it, to trust a small truth.

The journal accepted the sketch. Its editor wrote three lines, one of which stated that compensation would be forwarded upon publication. Compensation! The word shone.

I told no one. Experience had taught me that a promise from an editor is a seed, not a tree. I waited. The sketch appeared, modestly placed between an advertisement for liver pills and a column concerning municipal drainage. I found it beautiful.

Then came the envelope.

Inside lay a brief note and two dollars and fifty cents.

A modern child, accustomed to figures of larger sound and smaller value, may smile. Let him. There are sums which arithmetic cannot measure. Those two dollars and a half outweighed inheritances, advances, and royalties not yet dreamed of. They were not merely money; they were evidence. The world, which had hitherto regarded my existence as a family arrangement, had taken notice of something I could do.

I spread the coins upon my table. Two silver dollars; one half-dollar. They made a little constellation in the afternoon light.

What should be done with a fortune?

Duty suggested saving. Appetite proposed cream cakes. Vanity considered gloves. Affection whispered gifts for the household. Each claim possessed eloquence. Yet beyond them all stood another desire, one so definite that it seemed almost a command.

In a shop near the square I had seen a small framed engraving after a landscape: a road curving between dark trees toward a brightness which might be sunset or dawn. A solitary figure walked upon it. The print was not valuable. I doubt whether the original artist would have recognized his intention after the engraver, printer, framer, shop dust, and my imagination had completed their collaboration. But it drew me.

I bought it.

The shopkeeper wrapped it in brown paper and tied the parcel with string. When he counted my coins, I experienced a sensation for which pride is too crude a name. I had not begged the picture, borrowed it, or received it as a reward for good behavior. No one had chosen it for me. A portion of my mind—translated into words, accepted by a stranger, and converted by mysterious commercial processes into silver—had purchased beauty.

I walked home carrying the parcel beneath my arm. The road was ordinary; wagons passed; somebody called to me; a dog investigated the package and found it disappointing. Yet the world had shifted by a degree.

At a baker’s window I paused before the cream cakes.

They had always appeared to me among the higher expressions of civilization. Their tops shone; their pale contents promised extravagance. I could have afforded one. Indeed, I could have afforded several, though not without financial recklessness.

I looked at them as a wage-earner.

They lost dignity at once.

This was my first lesson in economics: money earned by oneself acquires a moral stiffness absent from money bestowed. A sixpence from an aunt will dance away in sweets; a sixpence gained by labor sits down, crosses its arms, and asks the price of permanence.

At home I unwrapped the engraving. My father examined it.

“You have spent your payment already?”

“Yes.”

“On art?”

I heard the smile in his voice and prepared a defense.

“It is better than cream cakes.”

“Much longer lived,” he agreed. “Though perhaps less nourishing.”

He helped me drive the nail. The picture hung crooked; we adjusted it; it remained crooked in another direction. Finally he stood back and looked at the road among the trees.

“Your mother would have understood,” he said.

No more.

That evening the silver was gone, but I possessed something I had not bought: a new relation to myself. Until then I had been a daughter, sister, pupil, dependent—beloved, corrected, protected, and defined by others. Now, in the smallest possible manner, I had become a producer. My labor had crossed the household threshold and returned with a public token in its hand.

Do not mistake me. Two dollars and fifty cents did not emancipate a woman. They could not alter law, custom, education, opportunity, or the ancient convenience by which the world accepts women’s gifts while doubting their right to cultivate them. Yet the sum contained an argument. It said that imagination had value beyond amusement; that a girl’s inward life might have an outward use; that the delicate, invisible act of arranging words could enter the stern marketplace and be acknowledged there.

A first payment is a baptism more intelligible to some writers than their first publication. Print may be mistaken for kindness. Money, however little, implies exchange. Somebody who did not know my family, pity my youth, or admire my canary had judged the work worth purchasing.

From that day I dated my professional life.

It was not a triumphant life, nor a simple one. No true vocation is simple, especially for a woman taught to divide herself among obligations and then blamed for appearing divided. I was to learn how inspiration waits while a kettle boils; how grief may sharpen a sentence and dull a year; how public praise can leave private doubt untouched; how one book may consume the strength gained from another; how the head demands solitude while the heart hears every cry beyond the door.

I was to learn, too, that authorship has its ghosts. Unwritten stories pace the upper hall. Lost characters knock faintly at midnight. Dead voices move the candlestick. One may question them with scholarship, confront them with courage, and still receive no clearer command than a demand for squash pie.

Yet the ancestral figures accompanied me: the biblical scholar bending over the precise word; the reformer riding into sleet beneath his conspicuous cloak; my mother at the green desk, the cradle moving beside her; my father reading poetry into the listening room. Heredity is not destiny, as lazy philosophy sometimes declares, but it is a company of witnesses. We write before them, against them, because of them. We accept, refuse, alter, and hand onward the unfinished inheritance.

Of my mother’s books, some are now seldom read. This is the ordinary fate of books, and not a disgrace. They served their hour; they comforted persons whose names have vanished; they placed bread upon a table; they proved that a woman’s intellect might work without extinguishing her tenderness, though the attempt to satisfy every demand upon both intellect and tenderness helped extinguish her.

The yellow coat is gone. The red one is gone. The green desk passed into other hands. But sometimes, when late work has thinned the night and a sentence refuses either to live or die, I hear the scratch of another pen behind my own. I remember that bright figure moving among October leaves, carrying a child, a household, and an unwritten chapter. Then admiration struggles with anger in me—admiration for her strength, anger at the conditions which consumed it—and I lay down my pen sooner than she did.

This, too, may be inheritance: not merely to repeat a sacrifice, but to understand it.

My father’s books survive in more solemn ranks. His notes fill the margins in a hand that grew smaller as the argument deepened. Here and there he placed a question mark like a hook upon which to hang doubt. In one volume of Wordsworth I found a pressed leaf, brown now, and beside it his pencilled line: “Read aloud to the children.”

No date. None was needed.

The framed engraving survived longest of all. Its cheap wood darkened; the glass acquired a crack; the solitary traveller faded almost into the road. I carried it from room to room, house to house, year to year. Visitors sometimes asked why I kept so poor a picture among better ones.

I could not easily tell them.

How explain that the road was the first thing my work had purchased? That its uncertain brightness represented neither dawn nor sunset, but independence before I knew the word? That the tiny figure beneath the trees was a girl walking home with brown paper under her arm, too proud for cream cakes and too ignorant to be afraid?

For ignorance has its mercies. Had I foreseen the labor—the rejected pages, the mutilated proofs, the hostile reviews, the friendly ones which praised what I disliked, the necessity of beginning again after every ending—I might have spent the money at the baker’s and chosen a less exacting destiny.

But I did not foresee it. I saw only the picture.

Perhaps vocation is always recognized first in some such trivial sacrament: a coin, a tool, a key, a name printed in small type; an object which the world overlooks and the spirit accepts as evidence. Great announcements are for prophets and railway stations. Most lives alter quietly.

You asked me where my literary life began. Shall I say it began with the learned grandfather whose dictionary contained all words, though not my own? With the other, who gave shelter to hunted strangers and afterward ate the spirits’ pie? With my mother’s lamp under the door? With the yellow coat among the leaves? With my father’s voice releasing poetry into the room? With the thirteen-year-old’s impossible heroine, morally equipped to reform a continent?

All these were beginnings.

But if you require a date—and readers, like tax collectors, prefer one—I shall choose the afternoon when an editor’s envelope yielded two dollars and fifty cents, and a girl exchanged them for a crooked little road leading toward light.

The sum was small.

The road was long.

I took it.

## MRS. GRIBBLE’S INCOME

When Mrs. Gribble inherited two hundred pounds a year for life, Mr. Gribble received the news with remarkable composure.

He read the solicitor’s letter twice, folded it carefully, and placed it in his breast pocket. Then he stood before the extinguished parlour fire, with his thumbs in the armholes of his waistcoat, and regarded his wife as a man may regard an unexpected improvement to his property.

“Well,” he said, “that’s settled.”

Mrs. Gribble, who had inherited the money from an aunt whom Mr. Gribble had always described as a sour old cat, looked up from the mending.

“It seems a great deal, Henry.”

“It is a great deal.”

“Two hundred pounds every year.”

“I can read,” said Mr. Gribble.

Mrs. Gribble lowered her eyes. Her husband walked to the window and tapped the solicitor’s letter against his sleeve.

“Paid monthly,” he said. “Sixteen pounds thirteen shillings and fourpence. Regular as clockwork. There’s people with education as don’t get that.”

“It’ll be useful,” said Mrs. Gribble.

“Useful! Of course it’ll be useful. If it weren’t useful, what’d be the good of it?”

Mrs. Gribble thought this unanswerable. She resumed her work upon a stocking which had already been darned in three colours.

They had been married thirty years. During that period Mr. Gribble’s opinion, announced loudly and often, had acquired the status of household law. It had decided where they should live, what they should eat, how long the gas might burn, and whether Mrs. Gribble required a winter cloak. Mrs. Gribble had once possessed an opinion about the drawing-room paper. It had survived for nine minutes.

Mr. Gribble was a large man with a short neck, a red moustache and a belief that noise was evidence. He earned thirty-five shillings a week in the stores of Messrs. Dobson and Pike, ironmongers. He spoke contemptuously of the firm during meals and obediently to Mr. Dobson between them.

Mrs. Gribble was small, pale and accustomed to moving round furniture as though apologising to it.

The first payment arrived on the following Monday.

The postman’s knock had scarcely died before Mr. Gribble appeared in the passage.

“Letter?”

“Yes, Henry.”

“From them solicitors?”

“I—I think so.”

“Give it here.”

Mrs. Gribble surrendered the envelope. Mr. Gribble opened it with the bread knife, drew out the cheque and examined it beneath the gas.

“Sixteen pounds thirteen and four,” he said.

Mrs. Gribble came nearer.

“I thought perhaps I might have a pound of it.”

Mr. Gribble looked at her.

“A pound?”

“For a few things.”

“What things?”

“Well—boots. Mine let in rather badly. And my black dress is very shiny at the elbows.”

“Shiny!”

“Yes, Henry.”

“You want to throw away a pound because your elbows shine?”

“I thought, with two hundred a year—”

“There you go!” cried Mr. Gribble. “Money ain’t been in the house five minutes and you’re flinging it out of the window. Boots! Dresses! Next thing’ll be diamonds and a carriage.”

“I didn’t say diamonds.”

“You would’ve done. I know you. A pound! Why, a pound’s twenty shillings.”

Mrs. Gribble could not dispute the arithmetic. Mr. Gribble put the cheque in his pocket.

“This money,” he said, “must be managed.”

“Yes, Henry.”

“Managed careful.”

“Yes, Henry.”

“And if you think I’m going to let you make ducks and drakes of it, you’ve mistaken the man you married.”

Mrs. Gribble looked briefly at that gentleman, but made no comment.

Mr. Gribble paid the cheque into an account in his own name. He then purchased a new pipe, a silver-mounted walking-stick and a pair of house slippers embroidered with yellow flowers. The slippers, he explained, had been reduced from eleven shillings to seven and were therefore an economy.

Mrs. Gribble continued to wear the boots which admitted water.

For several weeks Mr. Gribble was occupied with the responsible management of the income. Responsible management consisted chiefly in sitting at the table after tea with pencil and paper, while his wife washed the tea things in the scullery.

“House rent, twelve and six,” he would say. “Coal, four bob. Meat, say eight. That leaves plenty.”

“Soap,” Mrs. Gribble called once from the scullery.

“Soap?”

“And candles.”

“Candles! What d’ye want candles for when there’s gas?”

“The gas doesn’t go upstairs.”

“Then go upstairs in daylight.”

There was no more to be said about candles.

At the end of the second month Mr. Gribble informed his wife that he intended to leave Messrs. Dobson and Pike.

“Leave?” she repeated.

“Leave.”

“Your work?”

“My place.”

“But, Henry—”

“Place!” shouted Mr. Gribble, striking the table. “Don’t call it work as if I was digging roads. I occupy a position of trust.”

“Then why leave it?”

“Because a man with two hundred a year don’t need to stand behind a counter taking orders from Dobson.”

“But it’s my two hundred.”

The room became still.

Mr. Gribble slowly put down his pipe.

“Your two hundred?”

“It was left to me.”

“And who, I should like to know, has kept a roof over your head for thirty years?”

“You have, Henry.”

“Who’s paid the butcher?”

“You have.”

“Who’s paid the rent, the coal, the rates and the water?”

“You have.”

“And now you turn round and say ‘my two hundred’!”

Mrs. Gribble’s handkerchief went to her eyes.

“I didn’t mean—”

“Language,” said Mr. Gribble, “ain’t capable of expressing what I feel.”

He attempted it for ten minutes.

Three days later he resigned his position. Mr. Dobson accepted the resignation without visible grief, though he asked whether it was to take effect at once. Mr. Gribble replied that he saw no reason why a gentleman of independent means should remain an hour longer than necessary.

On the first morning of his independence, he came down at half-past nine and rang a little brass bell for breakfast. The bell had belonged to Mrs. Gribble’s aunt.

“What are you doing?” asked Mrs. Gribble from the kitchen.

“Ringing.”

“I heard you.”

“Then why didn’t you come?”

“I’m cooking your bacon.”

“That’s no reason to leave a man ringing till his arm drops off.”

Mr. Gribble breakfasted in his shirtsleeves, read the newspaper from beginning to end, and went out to buy tobacco. He returned for dinner, slept for an hour, and complained that the house was stuffy. By the end of the week he had arranged his life upon a comfortable foundation of meals, pipes, newspapers and criticism.

Mrs. Gribble’s life required less arrangement. It began before his and ended after it.

In April she raised the subject of a new house.

The Gribbles occupied five rooms in Willow Court, a narrow turning behind a laundry. The front window looked upon a blank wall ornamented with washing lines. The back looked upon three dustbins and a population of cats. Damp marked the parlour paper in the shape of continents, and the linoleum in the passage had worn through to the boards.

“There’s a house in Elm Grove,” said Mrs. Gribble. “Number eleven. Mrs. Porter says it’s to let.”

“Mrs. Porter talks too much.”

“It has a bow window.”

“Bow windows cost money.”

“And a little spare bedroom.”

“For who?”

“I don’t know, Henry.”

“Then what d’ye want it for?”

“And there’s coloured glass in the front door.”

Mr. Gribble put down the newspaper.

“Listen to me. Stained glass don’t keep rain out better than plain. Bow windows are traps for curtains. Spare bedrooms encourage visitors. We stay here.”

“But now we have the income—”

“I have spoken.”

His voice, which had settled the matter of the drawing-room paper twenty-seven years before, echoed again through the domestic corridors. Mrs. Gribble went back to peeling potatoes.

That evening Mr. Gribble visited the Crown and Sceptre, where he paid for drinks with the air of one circulating capital. He told Mr. Wicks, the undertaker, that investments were a great responsibility.

“Two hundred a year,” said Mr. Wicks. “For life, you say?”

“For life.”

“Whose life?”

“My wife’s.”

Mr. Gribble’s hand stopped on its way to his glass.

“What?”

“It ends when she dies, I suppose.”

Mr. Wicks had said it casually. Undertakers could not always be expected to appreciate the effect of their observations.

“Dies?” said Mr. Gribble.

“Well, she won’t draw it afterwards.”

Mr. Gribble drank without enjoyment. He had hitherto regarded the income as a permanent feature of the British constitution. The idea that it depended upon Mrs. Gribble’s continued presence in the world struck him as both unreasonable and alarming.

“How old would you say she was?” he asked.

“Your wife?”

“Who else?”

Mr. Wicks considered.

“Sixty?”

“Fifty-six,” said Mr. Gribble indignantly. “Not fifty-seven till October.”

“Ah.”

“What d’ye mean, ‘Ah’?”

“Nothing.”

“Healthy woman.”

“No doubt.”

“Never had a day’s illness.”

“Excellent.”

“Strong as a horse.”

Mr. Wicks glanced at him.

“Small horse,” he said.

Mr. Gribble went home early. He found no tea on the table. Ordinarily this would have demanded immediate investigation and correction. Instead he stood in the passage, conscious of a disagreeable sensation beneath his waistcoat.

“Maria?” he called.

There was no answer.

“Maria!”

The kitchen was empty. So was the scullery. Mr. Gribble stared at the cold grate, the uncut loaf and the absence of his wife.

She must have been run over. No other explanation excused the want of tea.

He hurried into the court and almost collided with her. She was carrying a bundle of firewood.

“Where’ve you been?”

“To get sticks.”

“Sticks! At this time?”

“The fire went out.”

“You might’ve said where you were going.”

“You were asleep.”

“I might’ve woke and found you gone.”

“You did, Henry.”

Mrs. Gribble passed him and entered the kitchen. Mr. Gribble followed closely.

“You all right?” he asked.

She looked at him.

“Yes.”

“No pains?”

“No.”

“Giddiness?”

“No.”

“Shortness o’ breath?”

“No, Henry.”

He grunted, annoyed by her health and relieved by it.

For several days he watched her. She seemed absurdly fragile now that he knew how much depended on her. Every trip on the stairs threatened sixteen pounds thirteen and fourpence. Every draught entering beneath the kitchen door blew cold upon his independence. When she climbed upon a chair to reach a saucepan, he ordered her down so violently that she nearly fell.

“What are you doing up there?”

“Getting the pudding basin.”

“Break your neck for a pudding basin, would you?”

“I always get it this way.”

“You sha’n’t. Not again.”

He fetched the basin himself, muttering about reckless women.

Mrs. Gribble began to observe him in return.

The first cough occurred on a wet Thursday.

It was not a large cough. It was a modest little sound, followed by a pause.

Mr. Gribble lowered his paper.

“What’s that?”

“What’s what?”

“That noise.”

“I coughed.”

“What for?”

Mrs. Gribble seemed to consider.

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t cough without a reason.”

“I expect I swallowed wrong.”

Mr. Gribble returned to the newspaper.

Presently she coughed again.

He looked over the top of the page.

“You’ve done it again.”

“I’m sorry.”

“Don’t be sorry. Stop it.”

“Yes, Henry.”

For ten minutes she stopped. Then came a longer cough, ending in a small sigh.

Mr. Gribble threw down the paper.

“You’re ill.”

“Oh no.”

“You are.”

“I’m only tired.”

“Tired of what?”

Mrs. Gribble looked round the kitchen. Breakfast things stood unwashed beside the sink. A saucepan simmered on the range. Two shirts soaked in a tub, the passage wanted scrubbing, and Mr. Gribble’s boots waited beneath the chair.

“I don’t know,” she said.

“Well, don’t give way to it.”

“I’ll try.”

At dinner she ate little. After dinner, while carrying his plate away, she stopped and put one hand to her heart.

Mr. Gribble rose.

“What now?”

“It’s nothing.”

“What’s nothing?”

“A queer little flutter.”

“Sit down.”

“I’ve got the washing—”

“Sit down, woman!”

Mrs. Gribble sat. Her face was pale, though this was not unusual, and her eyelids drooped.

Mr. Gribble walked about the room. Two hundred a year appeared before him in funeral black, packed its bag and departed. Behind it came Mr. Dobson, holding out an apron and smiling.

“You’ll see a doctor,” he said.

“Oh, I don’t think—”

“You’ll see him.”

“Doctors cost money.”

“Never mind money.”

Mrs. Gribble looked at him with mild surprise.

Dr. Pritchard came the next afternoon. He was a thin, clean-shaven man with chilly hands and an attentive expression. He listened to Mrs. Gribble’s chest, felt her pulse and asked several questions while Mr. Gribble stood in the doorway.

“Does she look bad?” he demanded.

“I generally finish before giving an opinion.”

“You can see she’s weak.”

“I can see she is not strong.”

“There!” said Mr. Gribble to his wife. “What did I tell you?”

The doctor asked how much work she did. Mrs. Gribble gave an incomplete list. Mr. Gribble interrupted to point out that theirs was a small house, not Buckingham Palace.

“And is she often upset?” asked the doctor.

“Never,” said Mr. Gribble.

Mrs. Gribble coughed.

“Any anxiety?”

“What’s she got to be anxious about?”

Mrs. Gribble’s hand moved vaguely towards her heart.

Dr. Pritchard watched her. For the briefest moment the corners of his mouth altered.

“I should like a word with you downstairs, Mr. Gribble.”

They retired to the kitchen. Mr. Gribble shut the door.

“Well?”

“The heart is weak.”

Mr. Gribble gripped the back of a chair.

“Danger?”

“A weak heart is always to be treated seriously.”

“How serious?”

“That depends.”

“On what?”

“On her treatment. She must not be overworked. She must have rest, proper food, warm clothing and cheerful surroundings. Above all, she must not be worried or criticised.”

“Criticised!”

“Not at all.”

“But suppose she does something wrong?”

“She must continue to be wrong in peace.”

Mr. Gribble stared.

“Any sudden excitement,” continued the doctor, “any violent quarrel, any harsh words, might produce a severe attack.”

“She ain’t going to—go off the hooks?”

The doctor’s face remained professional.

“Not if you take care.”

Mr. Gribble drew him nearer.

“You see, doctor, there’s circumstances.”

“There generally are.”

“She’s got an income.”

“Indeed?”

“Two hundred a year. For life.”

“Whose life?”

“Hers.”

The doctor looked at him. Then he gave a small and, in Mr. Gribble’s opinion, entirely unnecessary grin.

“Ah,” he said. “Then you had better make her life agreeable.”

His fee was seven and sixpence.

Mr. Gribble returned upstairs with the solemnity of a man entrusted with a delicate public duty. Mrs. Gribble lay on the bed.

“What did he say?” she whispered.

“He says you’ve got to rest.”

“The dinner—”

“Never mind the dinner.”

“The shirts are soaking.”

“Let ’em soak.”

“And the stairs—”

“Let the stairs soak too.”

Mrs. Gribble closed her eyes. Mr. Gribble stood beside her, listening for signs of continued existence.

By evening he had made tea, burned the toast, broken a saucer and spilled paraffin upon the scullery floor. The next morning he interviewed two servants.

The first wanted sixteen pounds a year and every other Sunday. Mr. Gribble showed her the door.

The second, a broad girl named Ellen, wanted fourteen pounds, beer money and one Sunday a month. He engaged her under protest.

“Fourteen pounds!” he said to his wife. “For dropping plates and entertaining policemen.”

“I could manage without—” began Mrs. Gribble.

“No, you couldn’t.”

“If it costs too much—”

“What’s money compared with health?”

The phrase pleased him. He repeated it to Dr. Pritchard at the next visit.

Ellen entered the household with strong arms and no reverence for Mr. Gribble. When he complained that his egg was hard, she said it would be softer tomorrow if the hen was willing. When he rang the aunt’s brass bell, she continued blacking the range until convenient.

Mrs. Gribble remained upstairs for a week. Each day Mr. Gribble carried her a cup of beef tea, asked whether her heart was behaving, and warned Ellen not to bang doors.

At the end of the week Mrs. Gribble came down in her old black dress.

Dr. Pritchard happened to call that afternoon. He examined her and frowned at the shiny sleeves.

“She needs warm, comfortable clothing,” he said.

“She’s got clothing,” replied Mr. Gribble.

“Some of it appears to have served.”

“It’s good cloth.”

“It may have been good cloth during the late Prince Consort’s lifetime.”

Mrs. Gribble turned her face away. Her shoulders moved slightly, perhaps with a cough.

“How much?” asked Mr. Gribble.

Mrs. Gribble said that three pounds might provide a plain dress and a cloak.

“Three pounds!”

She caught her breath and put a hand to her side.

Mr. Gribble went pale.

“I mean,” he said quickly, “three pounds ain’t much. Not for a proper thing. Better make it four.”

The dressmaker came on Monday.

New boots followed the dress. A bonnet followed the boots. Mr. Gribble attempted to resist the bonnet on the ground that the old one kept rain from the head, but Mrs. Gribble developed a flutter before the argument was properly opened.

“Get it,” he said.

She got it.

By June she was stronger, though her strength varied in a manner which puzzled no one except her husband. She could spend twenty minutes choosing ribbon without distress, but the mention of expense produced immediate breathlessness. She could walk to the shops, provided she did not carry parcels. She could enjoy a chop, a custard and half a glass of port, but washing teacups caused palpitation.

Mr. Gribble became her guardian, nurse and unwilling treasurer.

One evening Mrs. Gribble mentioned Elm Grove again.

“Doctor says cheerful surroundings,” she observed.

“This house is cheerful.”

The damp patch above the mantelpiece had spread into Africa.

“There’s not much light.”

“There’s enough to see by.”

“The kitchen smokes.”

“Open the window.”

“That gives me a pain in my chest.”

Mr. Gribble stiffened.

“And the stairs are very steep,” she continued. “Sometimes, when I reach the top—”

She paused.

“When you reach the top, what?”

“Nothing.”

“What happens?”

“Only a sinking feeling.”

Mr. Gribble saw the two hundred pounds sink with her.

The following morning he inspected Number Eleven, Elm Grove. It had a bow window, a small spare bedroom, coloured panes beside the front door and brown-and-cream linoleum in the passage. The rent was five shillings a week more than Willow Court.

“Robbery,” said Mr. Gribble.

The agent observed that there were two other applicants.

Mr. Gribble took it.

The move occurred in July. He spent the day commanding the furniture van, though the men paid little attention. A wardrobe stuck on the staircase, the leg came off the dining table, and Ellen sat on an upturned bucket eating bread and cheese while Mr. Gribble demanded activity.

Mrs. Gribble arrived in a cab after everything heavy had been carried. She entered through the door with the coloured glass and stood upon the new linoleum.

“Oh, Henry,” she said. “It’s beautiful.”

“H’m. Costs enough.”

Her hand moved towards her heart.

“But worth it,” he added. “Cheerful surroundings. Doctor’s orders.”

She smiled at the light coming through the red and blue panes.

In Elm Grove Mrs. Gribble’s recovery made steady and profitable progress. She selected curtains for the bow window, since the old ones did not fit. She required a small sofa on which to rest. The spare bedroom needed a washstand, as an empty spare bedroom was not cheerful. The kitchen required shelves. The drawing room required a rug.

Mr. Gribble disputed each article and bought it.

He did not surrender without skirmishes. His authority had echoed down the corridors of thirty years, and even a weak heart could not silence it at once.

“What’s this bill?” he demanded one morning.

“Curtain rings.”

“Eight shillings for rings?”

“They’re brass.”

“Why brass?”

“They don’t rust.”

“Let ’em rust!”

Mrs. Gribble grew pale.

“I’m sorry, Henry. I thought—you wanted the house to look nice.”

“I do want it nice.”

“I’ve made a mistake.”

She sank gently into a chair.

Mr. Gribble rushed for the port.

After that the curtain rings remained brass.

The monthly cheque still arrived in an envelope addressed to Mrs. Gribble. Mr. Gribble continued to receive it, open it and place the proceeds in his bank. At first Mrs. Gribble had been too timid to object. Now she watched the ceremony with a new expression.

The money had bought her rest, clothes and a better house, but every penny still passed through his hand. If she wanted sixpence for cotton, she must explain the colour and expected length. Mr. Gribble, meanwhile, smoked a superior tobacco and had joined the billiard room at the Crown and Sceptre.

He regarded these expenses as necessary to a gentleman of independent means.

In September the cheque was late.

Mr. Gribble spent the morning at the window. At ten o’clock he criticised the postman’s pace. At eleven he suggested that the country was going to ruin. At noon he wrote half a letter to the solicitors.

The second post brought the envelope.

“Here it is,” said Mrs. Gribble.

“Give it me.”

She looked at the name.

“It’s addressed to me.”

“Of course it is. Give it here.”

Mrs. Gribble held it between finger and thumb.

“I think I’ll open it.”

Mr. Gribble laughed.

“Don’t be silly.”

“I should like to.”

“What difference does it make who opens it?”

“None, perhaps.”

“Then give it me.”

Mrs. Gribble put one finger under the flap.

Mr. Gribble rose from his chair.

“Maria!”

She opened the envelope.

For thirty years he had only needed to say her name in that tone. It had stopped purchases, visits, opinions and tears. Now it stopped nothing. Mrs. Gribble drew out the cheque and examined it.

“Sixteen pounds thirteen shillings and fourpence,” she said.

“What d’ye mean by this?”

“I’m reading it.”

“Hand it over.”

She folded the cheque and placed it in the small leather purse hanging from her waist.

Mr. Gribble’s face darkened.

“Hand—that—over.”

Mrs. Gribble looked at him. Then her lips parted and she drew one short, uncertain breath.

Mr. Gribble’s fist, which had begun a journey towards the table, halted in the air.

“You heard me,” he said, less loudly.

She breathed again. Her hand rose to her heart.

“Don’t start that,” he whispered.

“I feel—rather strange.”

“You sit down.”

“I think I’ll go upstairs.”

“No need for that. Sit here. Put your feet up.”

“I should like to lie down.”

Mr. Gribble moved aside at once.

Mrs. Gribble crossed the room slowly. At the door she paused.

“The cheque,” he said, attempting carelessness. “You’d better leave it. Might lose it.”

She looked over her shoulder.

“It will be quite safe.”

“But the bank—”

“I shall go there tomorrow.”

“You?”

“Yes.”

“Alone?”

“Ellen can come.”

Mr. Gribble opened his mouth.

Mrs. Gribble swayed.

He shut it.

“Very well,” he said. “Please yourself.”

Mrs. Gribble’s eyebrows lifted slightly.

“Please myself?”

“I said so, didn’t I?”

“In everything?”

Mr. Gribble glanced at her hand, which still rested above the legal source of his income.

“In reason.”

Her breathing became audible.

“All right!” he cried. “Have your own way in everything. Only don’t excite yourself.”

Mrs. Gribble went upstairs.

Mr. Gribble remained in the drawing room. Upon the table lay the unpaid coal bill, the doctor’s account and a bill for the new sofa. Through the bow window he could see the butcher’s boy whistling in the sunshine. The coloured glass laid a patch of red upon the new linoleum.

He sat down heavily.

It was monstrous. A man sacrificed his position, devoted himself to domestic management, endured servants, doctors, removals, brass curtain rings and four-pound dresses, and this was his reward. His own wife seized the income upon which he had built his independence. What was marriage coming to?

Still, the cheque remained in the house. That was something. She would need him to manage it. Women did not understand banks. She would probably become alarmed by morning and hand it back. He must be patient. A weak heart required indulgence.

He filled his pipe, found it had gone out, and searched for a match.

From the room above came a faint thump.

Mr. Gribble froze.

Another thump followed, then two quick ones.

He dropped the matchbox and rushed to the foot of the stairs.

“Maria!”

There was no answer.

“Maria, are you all right?”

The floorboards creaked overhead. Then came a light pattering, a turn, and three distinct taps in brisk succession.

Mr. Gribble gripped the banister.

“Maria!”

The sounds ceased.

After a moment Mrs. Gribble called down in a weak and quavering voice.

“Yes, Henry?”

“What are you doing?”

There was a pause.

“Resting.”

Mr. Gribble listened.

The pattering began again, crossed the bedroom, turned neatly by the window and returned with something very like a dancing step.

# WILLIE’S PORTRAIT

There stood, above the mantel in the pleasant family parlour of Mr. Henry Morton, a portrait which the stranger might have passed with no more than the tribute of a momentary smile, but which, to every heart beneath that roof, was dearer than the costliest production of the most celebrated master. It represented a child in his sixth year, with fair hair falling in soft curls upon his neck, large blue eyes, in whose tranquil depths innocence seemed to dwell, and lips just parted by that happy expression which belongs only to childhood, ere care has breathed upon its spirit or sorrow cast one shadow from her evil wing.

The little hands were folded upon his breast, not formally, but with that unconscious grace which no art can teach; and the painter, whether by skill or by some gentler inspiration, had caught so perfectly the tender sweetness of his countenance, that the mother often paused before it with an overflowing heart, and said,—

“It is Willie himself! I almost expect to hear him speak.”

Then Willie, who was usually not far distant, would laugh, and, running to her side, lift his blooming face toward the painted one.

“Which do you love best, mamma?” he would ask; “Willie in the frame, or Willie here?”

“The living Willie, a thousand times!” Mrs. Morton would reply, clasping him to her bosom.

“And when I am a man, will you love the little Willie still?”

“Always, my child.”

“Then you must keep him,” the boy would say, pointing to the portrait; “for I shall grow and grow, and he will never change.”

Alas! how little did those who smiled at his childish reasoning foresee the sacred meaning which his words were afterward to possess! He did not grow and grow, as his young imagination promised. The form which seemed so full of life and hope was early touched by decay; and the little Willie of the portrait remained, while the living child passed onward to that better land where youth knows no advancement toward age, because immortality knows no change.

But we must not anticipate.

Mr. Morton was a merchant of Philadelphia, known through many years for intelligence, enterprise, and strict commercial honour. His dwelling was spacious, though not ostentatious; his table hospitable, though never profuse; and the wealth which had crowned his exertions had not hardened his heart against the unfortunate. He gave without display, employed without oppression, and never forgot, in the busy calculations of gain, that the poor clerk bending over his ledger, the labourer who rolled the bale into his warehouse, and the sailor who bore his ventures across the sea, were men like himself, with hopes, affections, and immortal souls.

His wife was one of those gentle beings whose influence is felt rather than proclaimed. She had brought him no great fortune, but she had brought what fortune cannot purchase—a peaceful temper, a faithful heart, and that domestic wisdom which makes home the resting-place of the weary spirit. Their children were four: Constance, the eldest, had entered her nineteenth year; Mary and Ellen were several years younger; and Willie, the last, the only son, was the household treasure, loved, perhaps, with that peculiar tenderness which gathers around the youngest flower in the family garden.

Constance possessed more than ordinary beauty; yet hers was not the beauty which flashes upon the eye and leaves the heart untouched. Her countenance, mild and thoughtful, seemed the faithful interpreter of her spirit. Affection gave warmth to her cheek, duty firmness to her brow, and a deep religious trust lent to her eyes an expression which sorrow might soften, but could never wholly subdue. Already one had learned to prize these qualities. Edward Wilkinson, a young merchant whose rapid prosperity was equalled only by the esteem entertained for his character, had long been a frequent visitor at Mr. Morton’s house.

No formal engagement existed between Constance and himself. He had spoken no decisive word, perhaps because the confidence of mutual affection had made haste appear unnecessary, perhaps because he desired to place his business upon still firmer foundations before asking another to share his fortunes. But there are languages more intelligible than speech. The softened voice, the involuntary smile, the watchful attention, and the silent preference which turns, amid many faces, to one alone, had told their secret. Mr. and Mrs. Morton saw it, and were satisfied; Constance felt it, and was happy; while Edward, true as the needle to the pole, looked forward to the day when he might claim her openly before the world.

Thus peace dwelt with them. If there were clouds beyond their horizon, no threatening edge had yet appeared. The father went forth each morning to his prosperous business; the mother presided over her happy circle; the daughters filled the house with music and youthful cheerfulness; and Willie, now pleading for a story, now riding upon his father’s knee, and now standing before his portrait to wonder that the painted child never laughed, seemed the bright link which united all hearts in one chain of tenderness.

But earthly happiness, be it as pure as it may, is never promised permanence. Providence, which sees the end from the beginning, sometimes removes our blessings, not in anger, but that the affections which would cling too closely to the gift may rise toward the Giver.

Willie sickened.

At first there was little alarm. The physician spoke calmly; the mother watched hopefully; and the child himself complained only that he was tired. But day succeeded day, and still the fever burned. The small hand grew thin; the bright eye became unnaturally large; the voice, once so clear through every chamber, sank to a whisper. Then fear entered the household, and every ordinary sound was hushed, as if silence could delay the footsteps of the destroyer.

One evening, as the declining sun sent a pale beam through the curtains, Willie asked to be lifted from his pillow.

“I want to see my picture,” he murmured.

His father took him in his arms and bore him into the parlour. Mrs. Morton and the three sisters followed. The child looked long and earnestly upon the painted face.

“He is not sick,” said Willie.

“No, darling,” answered Constance, though her lips trembled.

“He will always look well. Mamma, you will keep him, will you not?”

The mother could not reply. She pressed her handkerchief to her mouth, as if to imprison the cry that struggled from her heart.

“Promise me,” he continued.

“We will keep it always,” said Mr. Morton, in a voice rendered firm only by a mighty effort. “It shall never leave us.”

Willie leaned his head against his father’s breast.

“When you look at him, think I am happy. I dreamed last night of children with white wings, and they asked me to come with them. Was it heaven, papa?”

“I trust so, my son.”

“And may I go?”

The father’s strength gave way. He bent his face over the child, and his tears fell upon the fair curls.

“If God calls you, Willie, you may go,” he whispered. “Your father will not hold you from heaven.”

They bore him back to his room. That night he slept quietly. Toward morning he awoke, recognised each beloved face, and smiled. His eyes then turned upward, as though they beheld what no mortal eye could see; one soft breath fluttered from his lips, and the spirit of the innocent child was among angels.

We will not dwell upon the first anguish of that bereavement. There are griefs before which description should stand uncovered and silent. The empty chair, the neglected playthings, the little garments folded by a mother’s trembling hands—these told, from hour to hour, that Willie was gone. Yet over all the house the portrait exercised a strange and soothing power. It was no longer merely the representation of their youngest child; it was the visible shrine of memory. They spoke before it in lowered tones. The mother placed fresh flowers beneath it. The father, returning from business, often stood alone before the mantel, gazing upon that calm countenance until the hard lines of care relaxed and tears relieved his oppressed heart.

If temptation assailed him, the remembered innocence of the child acted like a spell against evil. If the daughters felt disposed to murmur at some little disappointment, one glance at Willie’s tranquil face recalled a sorrow too sacred for impatience. Thus even from the grave did the child appear to influence the living; and that portrait, which worldly estimation might value at a few dollars, possessed for them a worth which gold could neither measure nor replace.

Nearly a year had passed, when calamity approached the family in another form.

Mr. Morton’s business had become extended through several cities and dependent upon correspondents whose apparent strength had never been questioned. A commercial convulsion swept over the country. Houses believed to be founded upon rock fell in a day. Remittances failed; securities became worthless; ships arrived to markets glutted with goods; and obligations, contracted when credit was abundant, matured when confidence had disappeared.

For several weeks Mr. Morton struggled with untiring energy. He sacrificed private investments, sold property at disadvantage, and borrowed wherever borrowing could be honourably employed. He slept little, ate scarcely at all, and returned each evening with a shadow deepening upon his brow. His wife saw the change, but refrained from importunate questions. Constance saw it also, and often lingered near him with some gentle attention which asked nothing and expressed everything.

At length, one night, he entered the parlour later than usual. The family were assembled there. Mrs. Morton sat near the fire; Mary and Ellen were sewing; Constance held a book, though she had not turned a page for many minutes. Mr. Morton closed the door, approached the table, and stood with one hand resting heavily upon it.

“My dear ones,” he said, “I have painful tidings for you.”

Every face was raised.

“We are not to lose another whom we love?” cried Mrs. Morton, for the heart which has once been stricken interprets every alarm through its first great sorrow.

“No, no,” he replied, going quickly to her. “Thank Heaven, we are all spared to one another. The evil is of this world only. I am ruined.”

The words were spoken calmly; but when he had uttered them, his head sank upon his breast.

“Ruined!” repeated Mary.

“Do you mean that we are poor?” asked Ellen, with the simplicity of one who knew poverty only by name.

“I mean that everything I possess must be given up. The house, the furniture, my business—all belong, in justice, to those whom I owe.”

Mrs. Morton rose and laid her hand upon his arm.

“Henry, have you acted honestly?”

He looked at her almost reproachfully.

“Can you ask me?”

“Forgive me. I do not doubt you. I ask only that I may hear the answer from your own lips.”

“I have done everything that honour required. Not one dollar has been concealed; not one preference has been secretly made; not one article shall be withheld from those who have a legal claim upon it.”

“Then we are not ruined,” she said, with a calmness that surprised even him. “We have lost money. We still possess each other, health, and an unblemished name.”

The daughters gathered around their father.

“And I can work,” said Constance. “I can teach music, or draw, or assist in a school.”

“So can I,” added Mary eagerly.

“And I will learn,” cried Ellen, though her tears began to fall.

Mr. Morton extended his arms and drew them all toward him.

“My children,” he said, “this hour has taken my fortune, but it has shown me my wealth. Had prosperity continued forever, I might never have known the treasures which God had placed in these faithful hearts.”

Then his eyes, wandering upward, rested on Willie’s portrait.

“All have not been taken,” he murmured. “We shall carry that with us.”

No one replied; there seemed no need to reply. The thought that the portrait could be separated from them had not entered a single mind.

On the following morning Mr. Morton placed his affairs in the hands of his creditors. A committee was appointed, and two trustees, Mr. Grindle and Mr. Sharp, came to take an inventory of the household property. They were not men who violated the law; unhappily, there are those who obey its letter while forgetting that humanity has claims which no statute records. Mr. Grindle was short, precise, and important, with a habit of tapping every article which he appraised, as though suspicion dwelt in his fingers. Mr. Sharp was tall, cold, and economical of words. To both, a chair was wood and workmanship, a book was paper and binding, a picture was canvas and paint. Of the memories which may cling around such objects, their tables of calculation took no account.

They passed from room to room, followed by a clerk, who wrote at their dictation.

“Mahogany dining-table,” said Mr. Grindle, tapping it. “Used. Put forty dollars.”

“Thirty-five,” observed Mr. Sharp.

“Say thirty-seven.”

The sideboard, the chairs, the curtains, the china selected by Mrs. Morton in the first year of her marriage, the piano upon which Constance had played while Willie danced beside her—each was named, judged, and reduced to a sum. The family remained above stairs, unwilling to witness the profanation, yet hearing from time to time the measured voices and the moving of familiar objects.

At last the trustees entered the parlour.

“Pictures,” said Mr. Grindle. “Six landscapes, two family pieces, one portrait of a child.”

Mr. Morton, who had accompanied them thus far with constrained composure, started.

“The portrait of the child is not to be included,” he said.

Mr. Sharp looked at him.

“Why not?”

“It is the likeness of my dead son.”

The clerk ceased writing. Even he possessed a heart, and his eyes turned involuntarily toward the sweet face above the mantel.

Mr. Grindle adjusted his spectacles.

“We are sorry for your affliction, sir; but the portrait is property.”

“It has no value to any one but us.”

“That is a question for the sale to determine.”

Mr. Morton’s cheek flushed.

“Gentlemen, you have taken all else without remonstrance. I have aided you. I have exposed every account, surrendered every security, and pointed out property which you might never have discovered. Leave me this one thing.”

“We have no authority,” replied Mr. Sharp.

“You possess discretion.”

“We possess a duty to creditors.”

“And is there no duty to sorrow?” demanded Mr. Morton. “Can men who have children place this face beneath an auctioneer’s hammer?”

Mr. Grindle’s expression became more severe, for cold natures often resent an appeal which they cannot answer.

“Feeling must not interfere with business. If debtors were allowed to reserve whatever they valued from association, little would remain for creditors.”

“Take every other picture. Take the books, the silver, the beds on which we sleep. But this portrait—gentlemen, my child looked upon it the night before he died. He asked us to keep it. I promised him.”

There was a silence. The clerk bent over his paper. Mr. Sharp looked toward the window, but his features did not soften.

“We regret the circumstance,” he said at last. “The item must be entered.”

Mr. Morton took a step forward, his hands clenched; then, as if the painted eyes of Willie had fallen upon him, he checked the anger that rose within his breast.

“Enter it, then,” he said, with deep bitterness. “The law gives you power. May God preserve you from ever learning, through your own bereavement, the value of what you seize!”

The trustees withdrew. The clerk lingered for a moment.

“Sir,” he whispered, “could it not be removed before the sale? No one need know.”

Mr. Morton looked steadily at him.

“You mean kindly, young man; but you ask me to save the image of my child by an act of dishonesty. Shall I preserve the memorial of innocence at the sacrifice of integrity? No. If I must lose it, I will lose it with clean hands.”

The young clerk bowed his head and followed the others.

That afternoon the family learned what had occurred. Mrs. Morton heard the account in silence, but when her husband repeated the words, “The item must be entered,” she turned very pale.

“They cannot mean to sell Willie!” cried Ellen.

“Not Willie, my love,” said Constance, drawing the child toward her. “Only the portrait.”

“It is the only Willie we have!” sobbed Ellen.

This artless exclamation broke down the restraint of all. The mother wept; Mary hid her face; Constance’s tears fell fast, though she still endeavoured to comfort her sisters; and Mr. Morton walked the room in that agitation which is the more terrible because pride forbids it utterance.

For a moment the temptation suggested by the clerk returned with fearful power. It would be easy, under cover of night, to carry the portrait away. The trustees might suppose it misplaced, or some friend might claim to have purchased it privately. But the thought had scarcely arisen before he rejected it.

“No,” he said aloud.

His family looked up.

“No blessing would accompany it if dishonour placed it in our hands. Willie’s face must never reproach his father. We will surrender it.”

“You are right, Henry,” said his wife, though every word cost her a pang. “God requires no sinful act to preserve even what we most tenderly love.”

The day appointed for leaving the house arrived. A small dwelling had been rented in a quiet and unfashionable street. Two plain bedsteads, a table, a few chairs, and such articles as the law permitted them to retain had been removed there. How naked and desolate its rooms appeared to those accustomed to the comforts of their old home! Yet no complaint escaped Mrs. Morton. She arranged the humble furniture with her own hands, while the daughters strove to impart cheerfulness by a flower at the window and a white cloth upon the table.

Before quitting the old house, the family assembled for the last time in the parlour. The carpets remained, the curtains hung as formerly, the piano stood open, and every familiar object seemed waiting for the life which was about to depart. Only the silence was changed. It was the silence of a home from which home itself had already fled.

Mr. Morton lifted Willie’s portrait from the wall and placed it upon a chair. One after another approached it.

Mary pressed her lips to the painted forehead. Ellen knelt and laid both arms around the frame.

“Good-bye, Willie,” she sobbed. “Strange people will take you, but you will not belong to them.”

Mrs. Morton bent over it long and silently. When she rose, her face was white, but composed.

“Henry,” she said, “replace it. I cannot see the empty space.”

He obeyed. Constance was last. She stood before the portrait, her hands clasped, and tears trembling upon her lashes.

“Dear little brother,” she murmured, “if those who possess your image should know its history, surely they will return it to us.”

“Come, my children,” said the father. “Our strength may fail if we linger.”

They went forth. Mr. Morton closed the door upon the house in which his happiest years had passed, and the sound of the lock seemed to him like the closing of a tomb.

That evening they gathered in the scantily furnished parlour of their new abode. A small fire burned in the grate, though spring was near; for misfortune often brings a chill which does not belong to the season. Mrs. Morton had prepared tea, and Constance endeavoured to speak cheerfully of the morrow. Yet every eye wandered to the bare wall above the mantel, and every heart supplied the absent face.

A knock was heard.

Constance started, for she knew it. A few moments later Edward Wilkinson entered.

He had been absent in Baltimore during the most painful weeks of Mr. Morton’s failure, and had returned only that afternoon. The contrast between the room and the home in which he had formerly met them struck him at once; but no expression of surprise or pity wounded their feelings. He greeted Mr. and Mrs. Morton with respectful affection, spoke kindly to the younger girls, and then turned to Constance.

She had risen, but remained near her chair, pale and uncertain. A new fear troubled her. Would prosperity preserve the attachment which had grown beneath prosperity? She did not doubt Edward’s goodness, yet misfortune teaches the heart to tremble even where it has trusted.

“I learned everything upon my return,” he said to Mr. Morton. “I came first to you.”

“You find us changed in circumstances,” replied the merchant, “but not, I trust, in affection.”

“Circumstances!” Edward repeated. “They are accidents which try affection; they do not govern it.”

His eyes sought Constance’s face. Her cheek, pale a moment before, became crimson.

After some conversation, Mrs. Morton, with a delicacy which affection readily understands, led the others from the room. Constance would have followed, but Edward detained her.

“Will you remain?”

She resumed her seat. For a moment neither spoke. The little fire flickered; the unfamiliar clock upon the shelf marked the silence with loud and solemn beats.

“Constance,” Edward said at length, “when I last saw you, I had much to say which I delayed. I believed time was secure, and I foolishly waited for some happier opportunity. Providence has rebuked my presumption.”

She looked down.

“I do not know,” he continued, “whether you have understood the hope I have cherished.”

“I have understood,” she answered faintly.

“And has your father’s loss changed your heart?”

She raised her eyes, wounded by the question.

“Edward!”

“Forgive me. I did not doubt you. But perhaps you may suppose that it has changed mine.”

“I would not willingly think so.”

“Then think so no longer. I loved you in your father’s prosperous home; I love you more in this humble room, because adversity has revealed more fully the strength, tenderness, and devotion of your character. Constance, I ask you to become my wife.”

Tears filled her eyes.

“You know that I am poor.”

“I know that you are rich in all which makes life honourable and happy.”

“My father and mother may need me.”

“They shall never lose you. Their claims are sacred to me because they are sacred to you.”

“My sisters—”

“Shall be my sisters.”

She covered her face.

“Oh, Edward, this generosity overwhelms me!”

“Do not call justice generosity. If I had ceased to love you because money had vanished, my affection would have been unworthy of the name. Gold may change owners in a day; the true heart remains. Tell me only—can you trust me?”

She extended her hand.

“With my whole heart.”

He bent over it, and the compact was sealed, not by passionate protestations, but by tears of gratitude and affection.

When the parents returned, Edward made his request formally. Mr. Morton heard him with visible emotion.

“You ask for my daughter,” he said, “at a time when I can give her nothing.”

“You give me Constance,” replied Edward. “What more could I receive?”

“I once hoped to place a handsome portion in her hands.”

“Her virtues are her portion.”

“And you do not fear that men will say you married into a ruined family?”

“I fear only to act beneath my conscience.”

Mr. Morton grasped his hand.

“Take her, then. May she bring to your home the blessing she has brought to mine.”

The marriage was fixed for three days later. Some may think the period brief; but grief does not always demand delay, nor is affection strengthened by ceremonious postponement. Edward urged that there was no reason for waiting, and Constance, perceiving that her union might relieve rather than increase the anxiety of her parents, consented.

The ceremony was simple and private. Constance wore no jewels; the white dress had been prepared by her mother and sisters. Yet never had she looked more lovely. Like an unfolding bud after rain, her beauty seemed rendered more pure by the tears through which happiness now shone. When she knelt beside Edward and gave him the faith of her life, her father silently prayed that the integrity which had cost him wealth might descend as a blessing upon his child.

Immediately after the marriage, Edward and his bride departed for New York, where business required his presence. He promised to return within a week. During their absence Mr. Morton sought employment, willing to accept a subordinate place in a house where he had once been treated as an equal; but men who had praised his judgment while he was prosperous now remembered only his failure. Such is too often the world’s estimation! It confounds misfortune with incapacity, and poverty with guilt.

Meanwhile the auction took place.

Mr. Morton did not attend. He could not endure to hear the household treasures called one by one, to see strangers examine the chairs his children had occupied, or to watch the uplifted hammer descend upon Willie’s portrait. He walked far beyond the city streets, and remained alone until evening. When he returned, his wife asked no question. She knew that the old home had passed into other hands.

A week later Constance came back.

Edward met his wife at the railway station with a carriage, but instead of directing the driver toward her parents’ new residence, he gave an address in so low a voice that she did not hear it.

“Are we not going to see father and mother?” she asked.

“Certainly.”

“This is not the nearest way.”

“It is the way I have chosen,” he answered, smiling.

She looked at him with gentle wonder, but made no further inquiry. The carriage moved through familiar streets, turning and turning, until Constance recognised the neighbourhood of her former home. Her heart beat violently.

“Edward,” she said, “why have you brought me here?”

He took her hand, but did not reply.

The carriage stopped before the house they had lost.

Constance drew back.

“I cannot go in,” she whispered. “Some stranger owns it now.”

“Come,” said her husband.

He assisted her to alight. The door opened before they reached it, and a servant who had formerly lived with the family stood smiling in the hall.

Constance gazed about her in bewilderment. The hall table was in its accustomed place; the familiar lamp hung above; even her father’s cane stood in the corner as though he had but just returned.

“Edward! What does this mean?”

“Enter, and you shall see.”

He led her toward the parlour. Her steps grew slow; she trembled so much that he was obliged to support her. The door was thrown open.

There sat her father and mother, Mary and Ellen, all weeping, all smiling, all unable to speak. The carpet, the curtains, the piano, the books, the chairs—everything stood as in former days. And above the mantel, touched by the quiet light of evening, hung Willie’s portrait.

Constance gave one cry.

“Willie!”

She sprang forward, then turned, and found herself clasped in her mother’s arms. For several minutes nothing was heard but sobs, broken exclamations, and blessings. Ellen danced through her tears, pointing first to the portrait and then to Edward.

“He brought Willie back! He bought him! He would not let strange people have him!”

Constance looked at her husband. The truth began to dawn upon her.

“Is this your doing?”

Edward smiled, though his own eyes were wet.

“I had faithful agents at the sale. The house and its contents were purchased for me.”

“All?”

“All. I feared that if separate bidders obtained the furniture, some article sacred to you might be lost. Therefore I instructed my agent to buy the house and everything within it.”

“And you concealed this?”

“I wished the home itself to tell you.”

She could only clasp his hands. Her emotions were too full for speech.

Mr. Morton now approached.

“Edward has done more,” he said. “He has offered me a partnership in his business.”

“A partnership!” cried Constance.

“Not charity,” Edward said quickly. “I need your father’s experience, his judgment, and the confidence which honourable men still repose in him. Capital I possess; but capital without wisdom is a ship without a pilot. He brings what I lack.”

“And you will accept?” she asked her father.

“I have accepted,” replied Mr. Morton. “He has restored not merely my house, but my opportunity to be useful and independent.”

Let us pass lightly over the scene which followed. Joy, when deep and sacred, resembles grief in this—that language cannot contain it. Mrs. Morton knelt, and the family gathered around her while she thanked that Providence which had led them through darkness into light. They had been deprived of wealth, that they might learn how little wealth is beside affection; they had been wounded by the coldness of men, that they might more perfectly understand the divinity of compassion; they had surrendered the object dearest to their hearts rather than stain their integrity, and it had been returned to them by hands made generous through love.

In the years that followed, the partnership prospered. Mr. Morton’s knowledge and established connections, united with Edward’s energy and capital, produced results beyond their hopes. Debts already honourably surrendered were afterward paid in full, not because the law demanded it, but because Mr. Morton’s conscience found pleasure in the act. Even Mr. Grindle and Mr. Sharp were compelled to acknowledge that integrity may be a better security than property.

The old house became once more the centre of domestic happiness. Children’s voices were again heard through its rooms—Constance’s children, who learned to pause before the portrait and speak softly of “Uncle Willie, who went to heaven when he was little.” Mrs. Morton’s hair grew white; Mr. Morton’s step became slower; Mary and Ellen in due time entered homes of their own; but the family circle was often reunited beneath that cherished roof.

And Willie’s portrait still hung above the mantel.

Time deepened its colours and rendered its associations more tender. It was precious as the likeness of the innocent child whose brief life had blessed them; precious as the memorial of a grief meekly borne; precious as the witness of a temptation resisted; and doubly precious because it recalled the hour when generous affection restored, with one noble act, the home, the fortunes, and the happiness of an afflicted family.

The stranger might still have estimated it only as canvas, colour, and frame. But there are treasures which no appraiser can value, no creditor justly seize, and no auctioneer transfer. Their worth is written, not in ledgers, but in human hearts. And whenever Mr. Morton looked upon the sweet, calm face, he remembered that wealth may vanish, houses may pass to strangers, and worldly friends may fall away; but innocence, honour, affection, and trust in Providence remain possessions which adversity cannot destroy and death itself can only render sacred.

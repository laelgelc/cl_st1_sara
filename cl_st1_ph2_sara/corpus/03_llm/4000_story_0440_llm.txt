### HOW VILLE-AUX-DAMES WAS WON

In the year of grace fifteen hundred and twenty-five, after the unhappy business of Pavia, where courage was plentiful but good counsel as scarce as figs at Christmas, our most Christian King Francis the First fell into the hands of the Emperor Charles the Fifth, who sent him under strong guard to Madrid. There his majesty was lodged honourably, according to the language of ambassadors, which means that the doors were bolted, the windows watched, the dishes tasted, and every bow made to him by a man carrying keys.

Now captivity is grievous to all creatures. The linnet beats its breast against the cage, the wolf gnaws iron, and even a bishop deprived of his revenues becomes melancholy. But to King Francis, whom every reader knows to have been warm in blood, quick in compliment, and as diligent in love as in war, prison had a particular bitterness. He could endure the loss of liberty, the demands of the emperor, and the sermons of Spanish councillors; but the absence of ladies reduced him to such a condition that his physicians, having inspected his tongue, urine, pulse, dreams, sighs, and the manner in which he looked at the chambermaids, pronounced his illness to proceed from excessive continence.

The French council, being informed that the king might die of virtue, assembled in haste. Some proposed war, some money, some relics, and some a treaty written so obscurely that neither prince could understand it; but at last it was resolved to send to Madrid the king’s sister, Marguerite, Queen of Navarre, a princess accomplished in Greek, Latin, theology, poetry, and those tender sciences which are not taught in the Sorbonne.

Nevertheless, because brotherly affection, when shut up in Spain, might wander beyond the hedges planted by Holy Church, the councillors judged it prudent to obtain beforehand a papal brief covering any sin of thought, word, gesture, consolation, or other emergency arising from the interview. His Holiness, after consulting six doctors and a nephew, granted the indulgence, observing that kings must be preserved for Christendom and that certain stains, when committed by crowned persons for reasons of state, could be wiped from the celestial slate with a larger sponge than is allowed to common Christians.

Thus provided with passports for the body and the soul, Queen Marguerite departed. Yet roads were long, mules obstinate, inns full of fleas, and ceremonies more numerous than stones; so that before she reached Madrid there occurred the adventure which I shall faithfully relate, having received it from a notary whose grandfather copied it from the confession of a valet who could neither read nor lie—two excellent qualifications in a historian.

Among the Spanish officers guarding his majesty was a captain named Don Hernando Lara y López, a gentleman possessing twelve quarterings, one cloak, two swords, three creditors, and no dinner upon Tuesdays. He had served in Italy, Flanders, and Africa; had been wounded seven times; and had obtained from these labours nothing but a limp and permission to stand uncovered before richer men. Yet he was shrewd, bold, and patriotically persuaded that Spain produced the best saints, oranges, horses, swords, and women under heaven.

This captain, learning the cause of the king’s languor, begged a private audience.

“Sire,” said he, kneeling, “your physicians dishonour Castile. They say your majesty lacks a remedy which grows here more abundantly than rosemary.”

“Captain,” answered Francis, “I have seen rosemary at the windows, but little else.”

“Your majesty has been badly served. There are ladies in Madrid whose glance would warm a marble apostle and whose embrace would draw a dead knight out of his tomb.”

The king sighed.

“I do not doubt their fire. I doubt their cookery. Spanish love, as I have heard, is all pepper and no sauce.”

Don Hernando reddened beneath his beard. “Sire, permit me to prove the contrary.”

“Proof,” said Francis, “is dear in these matters.”

“Then let us wager dearly. If I fail twice to present a woman who pleases your majesty better than any French mistress, let my head answer for my presumption. But if I succeed, grant me the manor of Ville-aux-Dames in Touraine, with mill, dovecote, vineyards, fisheries, and high and low jurisdiction.”

At this demand Francis laughed, for the manor was rich enough to make an abbot humble.

“You stake a head full of nothing against a fief full of wine.”

“My head is all I possess.”

“Done,” said the king.

The word, though short, was royal, and therefore longer than any contract drawn by twenty clerks.

Don Hernando first applied to the Marchioness of Peñaflor, a widow of thirty years, black-eyed, high-bosomed, and so devout that she heard three masses each morning to repair what she intended to do after vespers. She loved her country, detested France, and accepted the enterprise as other women accept a military command. At night she entered the king’s lodging by a stair used for carrying charcoal, though nothing so combustible had ever ascended it.

The marchioness began by declaring that she would conquer for Spain. Then she embraced his majesty as a grenadier storms a breach. She invoked Saint James, the Virgin, her ancestors, and several martyrs, mingling kisses with oaths, prayers with assaults, and patriotic cries with such energetic manoeuvres that the bed shook like a galley in a tempest. The king, though no coward in that field, found himself kneaded, overturned, crossed, blessed, and nearly strangled by the chain of a reliquary she wore about her neck.

At dawn she departed triumphant, leaving behind a torn curtain, two hairpins, and a sovereign bruised in several places essential to government.

“Well, sire?” asked the captain.

Francis rubbed his shoulder.

“She is a noble lady, and Spain may be proud of her; but your love resembles your Inquisition. It asks too many questions, employs too much fire, and leaves the patient grateful merely to be alive.”

The captain protested that one swallow made no summer and one marchioness no nation. Since the wager allowed him two trials, he begged three days to prepare the second. The king consented, although he advised him to send, on the next occasion, less artillery and more music.

Now Don Hernando returned to his lodging, where dwelt his wife, Doña Isabel, who was as poor as he and much better furnished. She had skin white as milk seen by moonlight; hair long, brown, and glossy, which reached below her hips; hands soft as velvet gloves warmed at a fire; a waist that yielded without appearing weak; and that agreeable plumpness which promises comfort without threatening suffocation. Her mouth laughed before she spoke, her eyes were dark and moist, and her nose—concerning which poets too often remain silent—was neither Roman enough to conquer the world nor small enough to lose itself upon her face.

Doña Isabel listened while her husband described the wager. She did not faint, scratch him, summon her confessor, or perform any of those ceremonies by which badly instructed wives prolong a simple conversation.

“You have wagered me without naming me,” she said.

“I have wagered only my head.”

“Which is mine by marriage and of little market value. But Ville-aux-Dames—is it fertile?”

“It has vineyards.”

“And a château?”

“With four towers.”

“Are the roofs sound?”

“Three are. The fourth might be repaired when we become rich.”

She considered this, arranging her hair before a cracked mirror.

“If I go, I shall not go as merchandise.”

“Never, my heart.”

“Nor as a camp-follower.”

“God forbid.”

“I shall go as the champion of Spain, and you shall remember, when you sit in your four-towered château, that your wife won the chair beneath you.”

Don Hernando swore upon his sword, his honour, and the bones of the Cid, which was convenient, since none of these objects could contradict him.

That evening Doña Isabel was conducted to the king, dressed not in jewels—for she had none—but in a gown of black silk, modest at the throat and treasonable everywhere else. She made no patriotic speech, invoked no saints, and attempted no conquest. She merely looked at Francis as though she had discovered, to her pleased surprise, that kings were men.

“Sire,” she said, “I am told you are ill.”

“Madam, I improve already.”

“Then your medicine must not be violent.”

She sat beside him and spoke first of France, asking about the Loire, the gardens of Amboise, the hunting at Chambord, and whether the women of Paris truly laughed while refusing what they intended to grant. Her voice was low; her fingers, resting near his, appeared to have no design; and when at length those fingers made a treaty with his, it was without ambassadors, seals, or demands for Milan.

What followed is not written in the official registers, which are full of battles and therefore omit the more decisive engagements. It is enough to say that Doña Isabel treated the royal melancholy neither as a fortress to be bombarded nor as a demon to be exorcised, but as chilled flesh requiring a soft cataplasm. She gave and withdrew, advanced and pardoned, yielding at the instant resistance became delightful, resisting when surrender might have grown lazy. Thus was the terrible duel conducted with so much courtesy that each combatant believed the other victorious.

The moon crossed the window; the candles shortened; twice the guard changed below; and Francis, forgetting Charles, Madrid, captivity, ransom, and Europe, discovered that Spain possessed provinces not marked upon the emperor’s maps. At dawn Doña Isabel rose, kissed his forehead without servility, and asked whether the remedy had displeased him.

“Madam,” said he, “if hell contained one woman like you, every Frenchman would damn himself before breakfast.”

When Don Hernando appeared, the king received him with a solemnity proper to the transfer of land.

“Captain, you have won. Ville-aux-Dames is yours, with its mill, dovecote, vineyards, fisheries, rents, woods, waters, meadows, and all rights belonging thereto. I confess that France has grace, but Spain, when she chooses not to murder a man, has perfection.”

The captain bowed very low.

“I thank your majesty.”

“Tell me,” continued Francis, “where did you find this miracle? Is she a duchess in disguise, a Moorish princess, or some angel temporarily excused from heaven?”

“No, sire.”

“Then what is she?”

The captain hesitated only long enough to enjoy his estate.

“Sire, she is my wife.”

“How so?”

“My wife, sire.”

The king stared at him, then laughed until the guards believed a rescue had arrived. He ordered the grant drawn that same day, saying that any man who could make his wife serve his ambition, his country, and his sovereign in a single night deserved feudal jurisdiction over somebody.

Thus Don Hernando Lara y López became lord of Ville-aux-Dames. His descendants settled in France, where the peasants, being unable or unwilling to pronounce Lara y López, shortened it first to Laralope, then Larray; and from them, unless three genealogists and a canon of Tours are liars, descend the Larrays who later furnished France with two captains, a bishop, a treasurer, and one remarkably honest tax collector who died young.

Soon afterward Queen Marguerite reached Madrid, bringing books, counsel, perfumes, papal absolution, and sisterly tenderness. She found the king restored to colour and no longer in immediate danger from chastity. He welcomed her warmly, but with that discretion which becomes princes when dispensations have been obtained at considerable expense, and told her that he was now ready to return to French customs, French negotiations, and, above all, France.

The morality is this: kings should never despise poor captains, for poverty sharpens invention; husbands should not wager what they cannot afford to lose, unless the winnings include vineyards; and popes should write indulgences in broad terms, since Providence delights in unforeseen particulars. As for queens and princesses, let no sour chronicler judge them by the narrow rule used for shopkeepers’ wives. Royal women carry kingdoms in their petticoats, treaties in their smiles, and sometimes the honour of nations in places into which historians ought not peer without permission.

This truth was afterward proved by Mary, Queen of Scots, whose beauty frightened the heretics more than an army, and whom Elizabeth of England—red-haired, jealous, unmarried, and excessively fond of other people’s consciences—treated with that cruelty which barren politics always shows toward fruitful romance. But that matter requires a fresh bottle, a stronger fire, and another chronicle.

## Mrs. Feathertop’s Extraordinary Family

Mrs. Feathertop was certainly a very pretty young hen. Her feathers were white as apple-blossoms, except where the sunshine had touched them with a creamy gold; her comb stood up as red and neat as a lady’s best bonnet; and when she walked across the poultry-yard she lifted her feet with so much consequence that one might have supposed the whole farm belonged to her.

She had been married only since spring to Mr. Gray Cock, a dignified gentleman with green-and-gold tail-feathers, a loud voice, and a remarkably good opinion of himself. He was considered quite a leader among the poultry, principally because he crowed before anybody else was awake and then took credit for the sunrise.

The old hens shook their heads over the young couple.

“She is handsome,” said Mrs. Scratchard, “but handsome feathers don’t bring up a family.”

“No, indeed,” answered Goody Kertarkut. “She has never sat on an egg in her life. Mark my words, when her serious duties begin, she will desert the nest to look at herself in the rain-barrel.”

Mrs. Feathertop heard these remarks, for gossip in a poultry-yard is generally spoken just loudly enough to be overheard.

“I should like to know,” she said to Mr. Gray Cock, “why I cannot bring up children as well as Mrs. Scratchard.”

“My dear,” replied her husband, smoothing his breast-feathers, “there is no reason in the world. Your family connections are excellent, your appearance is superior, and you have had the advantage of my society.”

This comforted Mrs. Feathertop exceedingly, though I cannot say it answered the question.

Not long afterward she became broody. She chose a nest in a quiet corner of the barn, where the hay was soft, the light was golden, and there was a crack through which she could see the azalea bushes and a small blue piece of sky. She settled herself there with an expression of gloomy grandeur and refused to be moved.

Now Master Fred, the farmer’s son, had found eight duck eggs beside the river. Their mother had been frightened from her nest by a wandering dog, and Fred, being a kind-hearted boy with more curiosity than judgment, wondered whether Mrs. Feathertop would hatch them.

“They are eggs,” he reasoned, “and she is a hen. That seems near enough.”

So, while Mrs. Feathertop was away taking a hurried breakfast, Fred slipped the eight pale-green eggs beneath her and carried away the common hen eggs which had been there before.

When she returned, she looked suspiciously into the nest.

“I thought my eggs were rather smaller,” she murmured. “But motherhood changes one’s ideas, no doubt.”

She spread her wings over them and sat down.

For three long weeks she remained faithful. She came off the nest only for food, water, and the shortest possible dust-bath. She grew pale about the comb, lost her fashionable plumpness, and became so solemn that even Mrs. Scratchard ceased laughing for a day or two.

“I never expected she would hold out,” said that lady.

“Neither did I,” said Goody Kertarkut. “But sitting is one thing. Bringing them up is another.”

Mr. Gray Cock made frequent visits to the barn door.

“My dear,” he would say, “I hope you remember that the dignity of our family depends upon the appearance of these children.”

“I remember only that I want them to be warm,” Mrs. Feathertop replied.

“Well—yes—warmth is proper, of course. But posture, voice, and social manner are also important.”

Then he returned to the yard, where he explained to everybody that his educational system began before birth.

At last, early one bright morning, Mrs. Feathertop heard a faint tapping beneath her.

“Peep?” said a little voice.

“My child!” she cried.

A shell cracked. Out came a damp, dark head with a broad bill.

Mrs. Feathertop stared at it.

“Well,” she said after a moment, “newly hatched children are seldom at their best.”

Another shell opened, and another. Before noon eight small, soft creatures were stumbling under her wings, over her feet, and across the rim of the nest.

She was a proud and happy mother. If their noses appeared unusually wide, she supposed they would improve with age. If their feet seemed too large, she remembered that children must have room to grow. Their coats were brown and yellow instead of white, but every sensible mother knows that babies do not always resemble their parents at first.

Mr. Gray Cock was called in.

He walked around the nest, looked at the little ones from the right, looked at them from the left, and then cleared his throat.

“Ahem! A strong family,” he said.

“Do you think their bills are quite right?” asked Mrs. Feathertop anxiously.

“Entirely right—at least, for the present stage of development. Their grandfather on my mother’s side had a very powerful beak.”

“But their feet—”

“Excellent foundation,” said Mr. Gray Cock. “They will stand firmly in life.”

The news soon spread, and the neighbours came to make morning calls.

Mrs. Scratchard arrived first. She put her head on one side, peered at the brood, and gave a long, low cluck.

“Bless me! What singular children!”

“They are very strong,” said Mrs. Feathertop.

“So are weeds,” answered Mrs. Scratchard.

Goody Kertarkut pushed forward.

“Mercy upon us! Look at those feet! Every toe tied to the next one! I call that a serious family affliction.”

“They run very well,” said the mother, gathering the little ones closer.

“And the bills!” continued Goody Kertarkut. “Why, they are as flat as saucepan-lids. Did you sit too heavily upon the eggs?”

Mrs. Feathertop’s eyes filled with tears.

“I sat as carefully as I could.”

“There, there,” said Mrs. Scratchard, who enjoyed consolation almost as much as criticism. “Young mothers will make mistakes. Of course, we cannot expect experience where there ain’t any.”

The visitors went away whispering that it was exactly as they had predicted.

For several days, however, the children gave their mother little cause for alarm. They ate enormously, slept warmly beneath her wings, and followed her wherever she went. They were affectionate, cheerful, and unusually fond of pushing their broad bills into wet earth.

Mrs. Feathertop undertook their education with great seriousness.

“Now, my dears,” she said, scratching backward among the straw, “this is the proper method of finding food. Observe your mother.”

The eight ducklings observed. Then they all rushed to a shallow dish of water and began dabbling in it.

“No, no!” cried Mrs. Feathertop. “Not in the water! Food is found in dust, gravel, and respectable dry places.”

The ducklings looked at her politely and went on dabbling.

Their father attempted discipline.

“Children!” he said, standing tall before them. “The first lesson in life is obedience. The second is admiration for one’s superiors. Kindly repeat after me: Cock-a-doodle-doo!”

“Quack,” said the largest duckling.

Mr. Gray Cock nearly fell backward.

“That child has an impediment of speech.”

“Quack, quack,” said the others.

“All of them!” he exclaimed. “My dear Mrs. Feathertop, this is beyond ordinary correction. We must consult Dr. Peppercorn.”

Dr. Peppercorn was the oldest and most important fowl in three neighbouring yards. He wore his feathers somewhat ruffled, as if deep learning prevented attention to dress, and he had a habit of closing one eye while speaking, which caused simple persons to suppose that he saw twice as much with the other.

He arrived at noon, followed by Mrs. Scratchard, Goody Kertarkut, and several younger hens who wished to learn what was the matter before there was anything to tell.

“A serious time in your family, madam,” said Dr. Peppercorn.

“They seem healthy,” Mrs. Feathertop replied.

“That is often the most dangerous symptom. Bring forward the eldest.”

The largest duckling was brought forward. Dr. Peppercorn examined his bill, tapped his webbed foot with a straw, listened to his chest, and asked him to crow.

“Quack,” said the patient.

The doctor shook his head.

“Precisely as I feared.”

“What is it?” whispered Mrs. Feathertop.

“A complicated case of hereditary spoonbill, combined with membranous pedal adhesion and aquatic hallucination.”

Everybody looked impressed except the duckling, who tried to eat the doctor’s straw.

“Is it catching?” asked Goody Kertarkut, stepping backward.

“Madam,” said Dr. Peppercorn, “ignorance is always catching.”

“What shall I do?” cried Mrs. Feathertop.

“The bills must be rubbed every morning with a preparation of pepper, mustard-seed, powdered eggshell, and thistle-down. The feet should be dusted with ashes. Give each patient three grains of gravel before meals and half a grain after. Above all things, keep them from water.”

“From water?” said Mrs. Feathertop.

“Absolutely. Water encourages the disease. You may observe their unnatural longing for it.”

This sounded reasonable to everybody who knew nothing about ducks.

Thus began a melancholy course of treatment. Every morning Mrs. Feathertop rubbed eight broad bills with Dr. Peppercorn’s preparation. Every morning eight ducklings sneezed pepper into her face. She dusted sixteen webbed feet with ashes, counted gravel at mealtimes, and guarded the drinking-pan so carefully that the poor children could scarcely wet the ends of their bills.

Still, their aquatic hallucination increased.

Whenever it rained, they ran out in delight. Whenever a pail was upset, they paddled through the puddle. They admired the horse-trough, followed the geese, and talked continually of a silver brightness beyond the meadow.

“That,” said the eldest one, “is the river.”

“Never use that word!” cried Mrs. Feathertop. “The doctor says rivers are fatal.”

“But it dances,” said a little sister.

“It dimples,” said another.

“It winks at us through the reeds,” said a third.

“That is how it tempts innocent children,” said the frightened mother.

She did not sleep well. She dreamed of bills growing broader than plates and feet spreading until they covered the whole barn floor. She imagined that every “quack” was a cough. If one of the children washed his face in the drinking-water, she wrapped him in hay and made him sit near the sunny wall.

The neighbours were not encouraging.

“I told you those children were peculiar,” said Mrs. Scratchard.

“What they need is firmness,” said Goody Kertarkut. “When mine were little, they never thought of swimming.”

“Were yours ducks?” asked one of the patients.

Goody Kertarkut looked offended.

“They were properly constituted chickens.”

The summer grew warmer. Beyond the meadow the river shone blue between willow leaves, and every day the ducklings gazed toward it with greater longing. They were no longer weak babies. Their legs were sturdy, their feathers smooth, and their broad bills—though a grief to the poultry-yard—were admirable tools for finding food.

One blazing afternoon Mrs. Feathertop took them walking near the orchard. She intended to keep far from the river, but a grasshopper led them to a hedge, a beetle led them through it, and a green frog, hopping just ahead, led the whole family down the sunny bank.

Suddenly the river lay before them.

It danced and dimpled and winked. It whispered among the reeds, flashed over the pebbles, and spread a cool blue road toward the distant fields.

The eight ducklings gave one cry of joy.

“Children!” screamed Mrs. Feathertop.

Plop! went the eldest into the water.

Plop, plop! went two more.

Then five small bodies followed so quickly that the river seemed to have swallowed them in a single mouthful.

Mrs. Feathertop rushed along the bank.

“Come back! You will wet your feet! Think of Dr. Peppercorn! Think of the pedal adhesion!”

But the ducklings floated as lightly as thistle-down. They paddled in circles, dipped their heads, lifted their tails, chased shining flies, and laughed in their quacking fashion.

“We can swim!” they called.

“You cannot!” cried their mother. “No child of mine can swim!”

Yet there they were, moving farther from shore.

Mrs. Feathertop ran into the shallows until the water touched her feathers. Then instinct—or perhaps common sense arriving rather late—made her stop. She could not follow. The current carried the ducklings around a bend where willow branches hid them from sight.

“They are gone!” she wailed. “My beautiful, afflicted children are drowned!”

She staggered up the bank and fell senseless beneath an azalea bush.

By good fortune, young Dr. Partlett was passing that way. He had lately returned from studying beyond the millpond, and the older inhabitants distrusted him because he had the troublesome habit of looking at things before giving an opinion.

He found Mrs. Feathertop, revived her with cool drops from a leaf, and listened to her account.

“Eight children,” she sobbed. “Broad bills—joined toes—defective voices—an incurable desire for water! I did everything Dr. Peppercorn ordered, and now they have thrown themselves into the river.”

Dr. Partlett blinked.

“Madam,” he said, “did these children come from unusually large, pale-green eggs?”

“Yes.”

“Did they say ‘quack’?”

“Constantly. It was their saddest symptom.”

“And did they swim when they entered the water?”

“Perfectly—but I suppose that was a final convulsion.”

“My dear Mrs. Feathertop,” said the young doctor gently, “your children are ducks.”

Mrs. Feathertop sat up.

“Ducks?”

“Healthy, handsome, well-formed ducks. Their broad bills are proper duck bills. Their webbed feet are proper swimming feet. Their love of water is not an illness. It is nature.”

“But I am a hen!”

“To do you justice, madam, you are an excellent hen. Nevertheless, you have raised ducks.”

At that moment voices came from the river bend.

“Mother! Mother!”

Out from beneath the willow branches paddled the eight lost children. They came ashore dripping, shining, and jubilant, each with something to show for the adventure. One had found water-snails, another tender weeds, another a fine fat insect; and the eldest carried himself with the air of a young gentleman who has discovered his profession.

“We did not drown,” he said. “We swam to the reed-bed. There is food everywhere, and we can earn our own living!”

Mrs. Feathertop looked at their broad bills, their webbed feet, and their happy faces. Then she spread her wings as wide as possible and gathered all eight about her.

“My darlings!” she cried. “You may be ducks, but you are my children.”

The news travelled faster than any previous news in the poultry-yard.

Mrs. Scratchard and Goody Kertarkut arrived before the ducklings were dry. Dr. Peppercorn came soon afterward, walking with professional haste but stopping to arrange his feathers before entering the company. Mr. Gray Cock crowed three times and announced that he had known the truth from the beginning.

“I always said,” he declared, “that their feet showed a firm foundation.”

“You said they had an impediment of speech,” Mrs. Feathertop reminded him.

“A merely technical expression, my dear.”

Dr. Peppercorn examined the ducklings again.

“Exactly,” he said. “The case has developed precisely as I anticipated. My strict prohibition against water produced in them a vigorous reaction toward it. Without that reaction, they might never have learned to swim.”

“But you said water would kill them,” said Dr. Partlett.

“Young sir,” replied Dr. Peppercorn, closing one eye, “medicine is not to be understood literally.”

Mrs. Scratchard clucked admiringly over the brood.

“I always believed they would turn out well.”

“So did I,” said Goody Kertarkut. “I remarked from the first that those feet were meant for something uncommon.”

“You called them a family affliction,” said the eldest duckling.

“My dear child, uncommon gifts are always afflictions until society learns better.”

This was thought very wise.

From that day the ducklings spent their mornings upon the river and returned each evening to the barn. They brought home snails, seeds, tender water-plants, and wonderful accounts of the world beyond the reeds. Mrs. Feathertop never learned to swim, but she often stood on the sunny bank watching them, her heart swelling with pride whenever they dived or sailed bravely against the current.

The neighbours praised her now.

“What a family!” they said. “Such independence! Such excellent water manners!”

Mr. Gray Cock lectured the younger fathers upon his educational system, which, he explained, encouraged each child to follow his natural abilities. Dr. Peppercorn wrote a lengthy paper on the successful treatment of aquatic hallucination. Mrs. Scratchard remembered having defended the children when everybody else criticised them, and Goody Kertarkut recalled predicting their success before they were hatched.

Only young Dr. Partlett said very little. He had observed that when truth arrives, it often finds all the best seats occupied by people claiming they expected it.

As for Mrs. Feathertop, she became less vain and more confident. She no longer worried because her children did not scratch, crow, perch, or behave like respectable chickens. She had discovered that a mother’s business is not to make every child resemble herself, but to keep the little creature warm and safe until its own proper gifts appear.

And if, on pleasant evenings, you had walked beside that river, you might have seen eight sleek young ducks gliding through the golden reflections, while a white hen watched from the bank and called anxiously:

“Do not stay out after sunset! Keep away from the deep current! And mind that you dry your feet!”

For Mrs. Feathertop had learned that her children were ducks; but she remained, after all, their mother.

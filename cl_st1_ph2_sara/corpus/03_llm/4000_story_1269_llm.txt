## The Obscure Virtues of Thomas Bird

At eight o’clock on a February morning the Thames flowed beneath Blackfriars Bridge with the grave, discoloured aspect of a river conscious of the burdens laid upon it. A mist, neither dense enough to conceal nor delicate enough to beautify, hung between the warehouses and the opposite shore; through it the dome of St. Paul’s appeared unsubstantial, like an architectural intention which the city had not yet found leisure to complete. Barges moved heavily with the tide. From the railway came a prolonged vibration, followed by a flight of smoke; omnibuses rolled northward with windows dimmed by breath; and along the pavements hurried that innumerable company whose punctuality supports the grandeur of London without earning so much as a line in its chronicles.

Among these clerks, porters, shopmen, warehouse boys, and young women carrying paper parcels was Thomas Bird.

No passer-by would have found reason to observe him. He was of middle height, narrow in the shoulders, pale from indoor labour, and dressed in black clothes which had long ceased to express mourning and had not yet acquired the frankness of rags. His chimney-pot hat belonged to a fashion some years departed; his boots, though carefully polished, showed creases that no blacking could disguise; and the umbrella beneath his arm had been so often rolled in haste that it resembled a weapon imperfectly wrapped for concealment. Yet his face, when the struggling sunlight reached it, had a mild openness not frequently preserved by men who go daily to offices on insufficient salaries.

Thomas was thirty-two and earned one hundred and twenty pounds a year in the counting-house of Messrs. Drayton and Peake, importers of Italian goods. He had earned ninety pounds at twenty-five, one hundred at twenty-eight, and had received his latest advancement after an illness of the senior clerk made Thomas useful during six consecutive weeks. His duties had increased since then, though his income had not; this seemed natural to Mr. Drayton, and to Thomas it seemed at least inevitable.

He occupied a bed-sitting-room in Stamford Street, where Mrs. Baines, his landlady, supplied breakfast and tea, but not dinner. The room contained a horsehair sofa, a narrow bed, a washstand with a blue basin, six books, and a framed engraving of Tintern Abbey. Mrs. Baines declared that she had never charged a gentleman for coal he did not burn, and Thomas, impressed by this remarkable honesty, did not inquire too exactly into the coal he did.

With prudence he might have lived comfortably enough. He neither drank nor smoked; theatres attracted him only in imagination; and his appetite, though sound, was without ambition. But he possessed a defect ruinous in persons of small income: he found it difficult to keep money when another human being asked for it.

His charities were not conducted upon a system. Systems, indeed, are often invented by benevolent people as safeguards against benevolence. Thomas gave a shilling to the crossing-sweeper whose wife had been confined; half a crown to a fellow clerk who had miscalculated the interval before salary-day; sixpence to an old woman because she looked cold; and, on one extravagant occasion, five shillings to Mrs. Baines’s nephew, who wished to buy tools and afterwards bought a concertina. Such failures did not instruct him. He blamed the nephew, but continued to trust mankind.

On this particular morning he had resolved to purchase boots.

The resolution had occupied him for a fortnight. Twice he had examined a pair marked seventeen shillings and sixpence in a shop near the Borough; three times he had passed the door with the requisite sum in his pocket. This delay was not indecision, but the melancholy pleasure of anticipating ownership. New boots would restore his self-respect in wet weather and render visits to the Warbecks less painful.

At the corner of Queen Victoria Street he was stopped by Miss Pritchard, daughter of a widow who occupied the floor below his own.

“Mr. Bird! How fortunate! Mamma said only last night, ‘If there is one person in London who understands a delicate position, it is Mr. Bird.’”

Thomas took off his hat.

“I hope Mrs. Pritchard is well?”

“Her health is never what it ought to be. She bears up wonderfully; but the doctor recommends nourishment, and you know what doctors are—quite regardless of expense. Our remittance has been delayed. It is too humiliating.”

Miss Pritchard possessed a sealskin muff and was understood to have declined two offers of marriage. She lowered her voice.

“If I could borrow ten shillings until Saturday—”

Thomas thought of the boots. The day was fine; his present pair might endure another month if rain did not become unreasonable.

“Certainly,” he said. “I think I have ten shillings.”

Miss Pritchard received the coins with a sigh which suggested that lending them had relieved his embarrassment rather than hers.

“You are always kind. Mamma says your nature is positively dangerous.”

This judgement was accurate, though Mrs. Pritchard intended admiration.

At the office Thomas worked from nine until six at a high desk under a window begrimed with the contributions of several winters. At one o’clock he ate bread and cheese in a narrow room behind the warehouse. At half-past three a boy brought him a message: a gentleman wished to see him privately.

The gentleman was Mr. Edmund Warbeck.

Mr. Warbeck had once been a commercial traveller, later an agent for a provincial manufacturer, and was now connected with enterprises which changed their names more frequently than their address. He dressed with an assurance not wholly justified by close inspection. His frock-coat shone at the seams, but was well brushed; his gloves were pale, his moustache imposing, and a heavy watch-chain crossed a waistcoat beneath which prosperity seemed to reside in bodily form.

“My dear Bird!” he exclaimed. “Hard at it, as usual. You fellows in the City are the real rulers of the Empire.”

Thomas led him to the staircase landing, where conferences of a financial nature could be conducted without attracting the junior clerks.

Mr. Warbeck’s manner became confidential.

“A temporary inconvenience—nothing more. Money due on Wednesday; absolutely certain. Meanwhile, a little account has presented itself in the most abrupt fashion. Tradespeople have no imagination. Could you oblige me with a sovereign?”

Thomas’s salary had been paid the previous evening. After rent, the loan to Miss Pritchard, and his ordinary expenses, he possessed two sovereigns, seven shillings, and some coppers.

“I believe I can,” he replied.

“Capital! I need not say how ashamed I am.”

Mr. Warbeck said this upon every occasion and showed, in saying it, the ease that practice gives.

Thomas handed him the coin.

“Wednesday without fail,” said Mr. Warbeck. “By the way, Alma asked when you were coming. We haven’t seen you for nearly a fortnight.”

Thomas’s pale face warmed.

“I thought perhaps Sunday evening—if convenient.”

“Always convenient, my boy. We regard you as one of ourselves.”

He clapped Thomas upon the shoulder and descended the stairs. The debt already owed by Mr. Warbeck amounted to twenty-eight pounds thirteen shillings. Neither gentleman mentioned it.

For nine years Thomas had visited the Warbecks. Their house stood in Camberwell, in a terrace whose inhabitants maintained social distinctions of extraordinary minuteness. Mrs. Warbeck spoke contemptuously of the people at Number Twelve because they took in a lodger, though the Warbecks themselves had more than once considered doing so under another description.

Their drawing-room was crowded with objects intended to certify refinement: painted fans, bead mats, a screen embroidered with irises, albums, shells, artificial flowers beneath glass, and a small bronze warrior whose sword was perpetually lost. Mrs. Warbeck altered their arrangement every few weeks, so that Thomas, entering after an interval, received an impression of costly renovation without the introduction of anything new.

Alma was their only child.

She was not beautiful in the manner that compels attention, but pleasing in the manner that rewards it; not accomplished enough to excite envy, but sufficiently musical to play songs at evening; not profound, perhaps, yet good-natured, quick to laugh, and incapable of deliberate cruelty. Her brown hair was abundant, her eyes grey, and her hands, though a little large, moved gracefully over the piano keys.

Thomas loved her.

He had begun by admiring her when she was seventeen and he twenty-six; admiration, fed by Sunday visits and nourished by solitude, had become the fixed sentiment of his life. He kept in the drawer of his desk two faded violets she had once given him at Richmond, a scrap of pink paper on which she had written the name of a song, and a photograph taken in profile because Alma considered her profile distinguished. These relics might have appeared absurd in the possession of a richer or more handsome man. In Thomas they were merely pathetic.

He never imagined that he could marry her. His salary seemed to him less an income than an argument against presumption. Alma had been educated at a ladies’ college; she dressed well, went occasionally to dances, and spoke of friends whose brothers occupied positions of increasing magnificence. Thomas’s room in Stamford Street, his mended cuffs, and his annual week at Margate stood before him like stern witnesses. Still, hope enters where reason is ashamed to follow. If Alma smiled at him with unusual warmth, he spent a wakeful night; if she asked his opinion of a book, he lived for days in a state of inward distinction.

On Sunday, therefore, Thomas put on his best collar and walked to Camberwell. Alma sang two songs, laughed at his account of Mr. Peake’s dispute with the office cat, and brought him tea with her own hands. There was also present a Mr. Lionel Barrett, a young man engaged in the wholesale manufacture of electrical fittings.

Mr. Barrett was twenty-nine, broad-chested, well shaven, and admirably clothed. He spoke of “turnover,” “expansion,” and “new premises,” with the confidence of one to whom the future had already submitted estimates. His cuffs were white, his boots irreproachable, and the pearl in his necktie had the modest size of genuine expense.

Thomas disliked him for ten minutes, then perceived that the dislike was unjust, and made laborious amends by agreeing with everything he said.

Later, while Alma played, Mr. Barrett stood beside the piano and turned the pages. Thomas sat near the door. His boots, viewed beneath the chair, looked worse than they had in the street.

A week passed before he heard from the Warbecks. Then, on returning home one evening, he found a note from Mrs. Warbeck requesting him to call at eight upon a matter of importance.

The drawing-room, when he arrived, was unlighted. Mrs. Warbeck received him in the dining-room, where one gas-jet burned above the table. She was a stout woman with an active eye and a belief that plain speaking excused whatever plain speaking inflicted.

“Sit down, Mr. Bird. You’ll take nothing? Very well. I wanted a word with you alone.”

Thomas obeyed. He supposed that Mr. Warbeck was in some financial difficulty larger than usual.

“You’ve been a friend to us,” Mrs. Warbeck began, “and no one can deny it. Mr. Warbeck thinks the world of you. Alma too. That is why I’m sure you won’t misunderstand.”

Thomas felt a pressure beneath his waistcoat.

“Mr. Barrett has been very attentive to Alma. He’s in a first-rate business and keeps his own horse—not that a horse is everything, though it shows. We have reason to believe he may speak very soon.”

“I am glad,” said Thomas. His voice was scarcely audible.

“Of course, nothing’s settled. Young men can be frightened off by a trifle. Mr. Barrett’s family are particular—rather high, indeed, for trade people. And you know how things look.”

Thomas raised his eyes.

“I’m not blaming you,” she continued. “Goodness knows, a man can’t ’elp his salary. But you come very regular, and you’re so familiar that strangers might suppose—well, might suppose there was an understanding.”

His hands closed upon the brim of his hat.

“There has never been anything of that kind.”

“I’m sure there hasn’t. Alma would have told me. Still, your clothes are not all they might be, and Barrett notices appearances. A young man with prospects does. So I said to Mr. Warbeck—he knows nothing of this conversation, mind—I said it might be better if Mr. Bird didn’t call just at present. Only until everything is arranged.”

The blow fell quietly, and Thomas received it quietly. For a moment the room seemed to contract; the gas sang above him; from the kitchen came the clatter of a saucepan.

“I understand, Mrs. Warbeck.”

“I knew you would. You’re so sensible. And there’s no offence meant.”

“None,” he said.

“You won’t mention it to Alma?”

“No.”

“Because she’s tender-hearted and might make a fuss. Girls do, without knowing what they mean.”

Thomas rose. Every movement required separate consideration: he must place his hat upon his head, find the door, descend the steps.

“Tell Alma—” he began.

Mrs. Warbeck waited.

“Tell her nothing. Good evening.”

As he passed into the hall, he heard Alma’s voice upstairs, followed by a few notes from the piano. He hesitated, his face turned toward the sound. Then he opened the front door and went out.

The night was raw, with a thin rain falling through the lamplight. Thomas had brought his badly rolled umbrella but did not raise it. He walked from Camberwell to Blackfriars without knowledge of the streets through which he passed. His boots admitted water before he reached the bridge.

At first he felt no grief—only an astonished vacancy. The Warbecks had regarded him as one of themselves; Mrs. Warbeck had said so often enough when a bill required payment or an errand demanded leisure. Yet he had been removed like an unsuitable ornament before the arrival of an important guest. He, who had lent Mr. Warbeck nearly thirty pounds, was too shabby to sit in the room purchased partly by his economies.

Resentment came, but could not support itself. He remembered Alma’s kindness, Mr. Warbeck’s genial pressure of the hand, Mrs. Warbeck’s anxious vulgarity; and because he could explain each of them, he almost forgave all three.

At Stamford Street he found Mrs. Pritchard entertaining visitors below. Laughter passed through the ceiling as he sat in darkness. His wet boots steamed before the grate. Presently Mrs. Baines entered with a candle and observed that he would catch his death.

“I forgot the rain,” said Thomas.

“You’ve got a hinfluential cold already. And there’s a letter for you.”

The letter contained Miss Pritchard’s apologies. Her remittance had not arrived; repayment must be postponed until Monday.

The following weeks established a new order. Thomas no longer went to Camberwell, but Mr. Warbeck continued to visit the office. He borrowed fifteen shillings in March and thirty shillings in April. On the latter occasion he explained that domestic expenditure had increased owing to “certain happy possibilities.”

“Alma is much occupied,” he said. “Young people, you know. But she asked after you the other day.”

Thomas bent over a ledger.

“I hope she is well.”

“Blooming! You must come round soon.”

Thomas did not look at him.

The engagement was announced in May. He read of it in a letter from Alma, written on cream-coloured paper.

“My dear Mr. Bird,” she began, and told him that Mr. Barrett had spoken, that her parents were delighted, that the wedding would probably take place in September, and that she hoped her old friend would wish her happiness. She added, in a postscript, “We have missed you on Sundays.”

Thomas read the letter three times. Then he placed it with the violets and the photograph.

He replied that evening. His answer occupied one page and contained no word which Alma could not show her mother. He wished her every happiness; he praised Mr. Barrett’s energy and good sense; he regretted that office duties had prevented his visits. When he had sealed the envelope, he laid his head upon his arms and remained so for some minutes. Afterwards he went downstairs to carry up a scuttle of coal for Mrs. Baines, whose rheumatism had returned.

Summer passed in heat, dust, and economies. Thomas bought no boots. The seventeen-and-sixpenny pair disappeared from the shop; their successors cost nineteen shillings. His coat became green at the elbows, and a crack opened across the crown of his hat. Yet during these months he contributed to a subscription for an office messenger injured by a van, paid for medicine required by Mrs. Baines, and lent two sovereigns to Mr. Warbeck, who was suffering from expenses associated with the wedding.

There were persons who called Thomas weak. By weakness they meant that he did not protect himself against appeals which they themselves resisted without effort. It is a convenient use of language. The man who yields to appetite is weak; the man who yields to pity is also weak; and thus vice and goodness are made to share a word, greatly to the comfort of vice.

The wedding took place on the seventeenth of September.

Thomas had received an invitation. It came late and was accompanied by a brief note from Mrs. Warbeck explaining that the breakfast would be confined to family, owing to expense. He might attend the church if his business permitted.

Business would have permitted. Mr. Drayton himself granted leave for the morning, remarking that weddings were foolish affairs but must be encouraged for the sake of trade. Thomas dressed at seven. He polished his boots, brushed his hat, and put on the one pair of gloves he possessed. Then, while standing before the small mirror, he understood that he could not go.

He sat at his office desk when Alma became Mrs. Barrett.

The morning was bright, almost insolently so. Sunlight entered through the dirty window and lay in a golden bar across the ledger. Carts rattled in the street; a barrel-organ played at a distance; the junior clerk laughed over a sporting paper. Thomas added columns, checked invoices, and wrote three business letters without error.

At noon he pictured the church doors opening, Alma’s veil, Mr. Barrett’s confident face. At one he knew they would be seated at breakfast. At two he imagined the speeches. The control which had endured through the morning then deserted him. He made a mistake of eleven pounds in an account, discovered it, and stared at the corrected figure until it became meaningless.

Mr. Peake, who had a red beard and unsuspected kindness, told him he looked ill.

“I have a headache,” said Thomas.

“Go home, Bird. You’re no use to us with a head.”

Thomas put on his old hat and left.

He did not return to Stamford Street. The room, with its drawer of relics, was intolerable to anticipation. He crossed the river and wandered eastward through streets in which poverty had ceased to make any effort at disguise. Children played beside gutters; women leaned from upper windows; a costermonger cried bruised fruit beneath walls stained by smoke and damp. The afternoon faded. In these districts London seemed less a city than an immense lodging-house whose landlord never repaired anything.

By seven Thomas was tired and hungry. He bought a penny roll, then remembered a small eating-house near the Commercial Road where sixpence secured meat, potatoes, and the privilege of sitting down. As he turned into a narrow street, a woman called his name.

“Mr. Bird! Oh, sir, if it ain’t Providence!”

She was Mrs. Batty, wife of a man who had once worked as an envelope addresser for Messrs. Drayton and Peake. Thomas had visited them during Batty’s illness the previous winter. He remembered a mouldy back room, three children, and a basin of gruel which had seemed the only warm object in the house.

Mrs. Batty stood in a doorway with a baby against her shoulder.

“What is the matter?” he asked.

“It’s Batty, sir. He got a job—urgent, they said, two thousand envelopes by nine to-morrow, and us without a bite since morning. Then he met Collins, and Collins says, ‘Come and have one,’ and off he goes. I found him an hour ago, and he won’t stir. If the work ain’t done, they’ll never trust him again.”

She spoke without pause, mingling accusation, apology, and despair.

“Where are the envelopes?”

“Upstairs, sir. But I can’t write the hand they want, and the baby’s bad, and there’s the others crying for bread. I don’t ask charity, Mr. Bird, but if you could lend me a shilling—only till Batty’s paid—”

Thomas possessed three shillings and fourpence. He gave her two shillings.

“Buy food first,” he said. “Let me see the work.”

The room was worse than he remembered. Damp linen hung over a chair; the paper peeled from the walls; two children sat at the table with that unnatural quiet which hunger sometimes teaches more quickly than discipline. On the floor lay a cardboard box filled with envelopes, beside a list of names and addresses.

Thomas removed his coat.

“How many has your husband done?”

“Not three hundred, sir.”

He examined the writing. It was a commercial hand, plain and rapid. Thomas could imitate it sufficiently well.

“Bring me a pen and ink.”

Mrs. Batty stared.

“You don’t mean to do ’em?”

“I can do some. Go for the food.”

She returned with bread, dripping, milk, and a small quantity of tea. While the children ate, Thomas wrote.

Mr. Henry S. Wallace, 14 Cromer Street.

Messrs. Baines & Judd, 6 Poultry.

The Reverend C. A. Winthrop, Dulwich.

Name followed name, street followed street. The pen scratched steadily. The baby coughed; Mrs. Batty poured tea; footsteps quarrelled on the staircase. Once Batty himself appeared, disordered, red-eyed, and full of injured dignity. Seeing Thomas at the table, he muttered that a man’s home was his castle.

“Then behave like the master of it,” said Thomas, with such unusual sternness that Batty withdrew.

At ten o’clock more than fifteen hundred envelopes were complete. Thomas’s fingers ached; a ridge had formed upon the second joint; his headache was gone.

“I cannot finish them all,” he said, putting on his coat. “But Batty can do the remainder in the morning if he begins at six.”

Mrs. Batty clasped her hands.

“God bless you, sir! There ain’t another gentleman—”

“Nonsense. See that he gets up.”

In the street the air had cooled. Thomas walked westward beneath a sky in which a few stars survived the smoke. He was very weary, but the weariness was clean and almost peaceful. Alma was married; the fact remained, and would remain. Yet the violence of his grief had passed. He thought with shame of the hours spent wandering, as though his private sorrow were a singular injustice in a city where children waited silently for bread.

He accused himself of selfishness, of cowardice, of a sensibility too tender toward its own wounds. He did not perceive that few men, upon the ruin of their dearest hope, would have given away most of their supper money and worked three hours to preserve a drunkard’s employment. Moral vanity was impossible to him. He felt only that he had at last behaved a little better than in the afternoon.

Near Blackfriars Bridge the first paling of dawn had begun behind the warehouses. The river moved below, sombre and patient; carts were already coming toward the markets; somewhere a whistle sounded, calling unseen labour into another day. Thomas paused, resting his sore hand upon the stone parapet.

Then he went home to mend his boots before office time.

London took no notice of him. Mr. Drayton would find him punctual; Mr. Warbeck would borrow again; Mrs. Pritchard would continue to praise his dangerous nature; and Alma, perhaps, would remember him as a kind, shabby friend of her parents. His virtues would bring him neither distinction nor prosperity, for the world is often slow to recognise the qualities by which it is quietly preserved.

But such men exist: obscure, imposed upon, a little ridiculous in the judgement of the successful; men who surrender comfort without announcing sacrifice, who forgive because resentment costs another pain, and who pass through the multitude as unnoticed as a drop in the river. If society owes more to louder persons, it owes its continuance to these. Thomas Bird was one of them.

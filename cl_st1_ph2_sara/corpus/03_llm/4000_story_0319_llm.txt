### THE PARISIAN HEAD-DRESS

There is a common saying, that whenever two friends quarrel, there must be faults on both sides. We have heard this observation repeated by grave persons, who ought to have known better, and by thoughtless persons, who could scarcely be expected to examine any received maxim before giving it their sanction. It has a fair and charitable sound; and as people are generally unwilling to undertake the labor of ascertaining facts, it enables them to pronounce judgment with very little trouble.

But is the saying true?

If one person violates confidence, and another refuses afterward to repose confidence in her, are both to blame? If one practices deceit, and the other resents—not passionately, but firmly—the dishonorable act, shall we say that the deceiver and the deceived have equally contributed to the estrangement? Such a conclusion may pass in society for moderation; but we do not consider it justice. It is a false charity which saves us the inconvenience of discriminating between right and wrong.

These reflections were suggested by a little affair which, some years ago, disturbed the harmony of a fashionable circle. The matter concerned nothing more important, in itself, than a lady’s head-dress; yet, insignificant as the occasion may appear, we think the conduct which it disclosed by no means unworthy of examination. Character is often more clearly seen in trifles than in affairs of magnitude, because in trifles people act without preparation, and betray the principles by which their daily life is governed.

The first account that reached us was substantially as follows:—

“Have you heard,” said Mrs. Loring, “that Mrs. Bates and Mrs. Tarleton no longer speak?”

“No!” replied her companion. “They were intimate only last month.”

“Very intimate; but Mrs. Bates has taken mortal offence about a cap.”

“A cap!”

“Or a head-dress—I do not know exactly which. Mrs. Tarleton admired one that Mrs. Bates had received from Paris, and had something made like it. Mrs. Bates declared that she would never forgive her.”

“How excessively unreasonable!”

“Mrs. Tarleton says she had no idea that Mrs. Bates wished to monopolize the fashion.”

“I always thought Mrs. Bates rather vain.”

“So did I; and Mrs. Tarleton is vain too. The truth is, there is generally fault on both sides.”

This last sentence closed the inquiry. It was a convenient verdict, and passed from mouth to mouth. One lady said that Mrs. Bates had flown into a passion because Mrs. Tarleton’s head-dress was thought the more becoming. Another understood that both had ordered the same article from the same milliner, and that Mrs. Bates had absurdly accused her friend of imitation. A third had been informed that the design was common in Paris, and could not properly be claimed by any body. Before a week had elapsed, Mrs. Bates was censured for jealous ill-temper, while Mrs. Tarleton was blamed only for an unlucky coincidence and, perhaps, a little want of taste.

We heard these reports, but were not satisfied with them. There was something in the quiet and usually just character of Mrs. Bates inconsistent with such childish resentment. We therefore waited until the circumstances became known from sources entitled to confidence; and as the whole transaction affords a useful illustration of the difference between an injury to vanity and a violation of principle, we will relate it in order.

Mrs. Bates was a woman of thirty-five, agreeable in person and cultivated in mind. She was not free from vanity. We have never asserted that she was. She liked a becoming dress, was pleased when her taste was commended, and had no philosophical indifference to the little distinctions of fashionable life. But her vanity was restrained by good sense and by a true delicacy of feeling. She would not purchase admiration by an act that lowered her self-respect; nor did she think that intimacy authorized one friend to appropriate whatever another possessed.

Mrs. Tarleton was more showy, more eager for notice, and much more dependent upon the applause of others. If a new ornament appeared, she must have it; if a lady was praised for the arrangement of her hair, Mrs. Tarleton altered hers the following morning; if a color became Mrs. A., or a trimming was admired upon Mrs. B., she was restless until the color or trimming had been secured. This disposition might have remained merely ridiculous had it not been united with a selfishness which made her careless of the rights and feelings of her friends.

A brother of Mrs. Bates, then residing in Paris, sent her a small box containing gloves, ribbons, and an elegant head-dress of an entirely new pattern. The article was composed of delicate lace, narrow folds of pale blue satin, and two light sprays of silver leaves. Its beauty consisted less in the richness of the materials than in the grace with which they were combined. Mrs. Bates tried it on, and even her husband, who seldom observed any alteration in female apparel, admitted that it was remarkably becoming.

“You will wear it at Mrs. Selwyn’s party,” said he.

“Certainly,” replied Mrs. Bates, looking with pardonable satisfaction into the glass. “It was made expressly for me, and I believe there will be nothing like it in the room.”

“Then I hope,” said Mr. Bates, smiling, “that no packet from Paris will arrive for any other lady before Thursday.”

The next morning Mrs. Tarleton called. The box was still upon the table, and the head-dress, which had been shown to two or three visitors, was again produced.

“Oh, my dear Mrs. Bates!” exclaimed Mrs. Tarleton; “how perfectly beautiful! Where did you get it?”

“My brother sent it from Paris.”

“And you are to wear it at Mrs. Selwyn’s?”

“Yes.”

Mrs. Tarleton examined every fold, lifted the sprays, counted the loops of satin, and then requested Mrs. Bates to put it on.

“It is charming!” she cried. “Absolutely charming! You must let me take the pattern.”

Mrs. Bates laughed. “No, my dear; not before the party.”

“Why not?”

“Because I wish, for once, to enjoy the distinction of wearing something that has not been copied.”

“But I would alter the color.”

“That would make very little difference. The design is peculiar.”

“I assure you I should not wear mine on Thursday.”

“Then you can have it copied afterward.”

Mrs. Tarleton was silent for a moment, but not convinced.

“You know,” she resumed, “that my complexion is wholly different from yours. What becomes you may not become me; and my dress maker would, of course, modify the arrangement.”

“Then your dress maker can invent an arrangement better suited to you.”

“How severe you are!”

“Not severe, I hope; only decided. You may have the head-dress itself after Thursday, if you desire it. I would rather give it to you than see two of the same pattern at the party.”

This offer was so liberal that an honorable person must have been silenced by it. Mrs. Tarleton, however, felt no gratitude. She thought only that Mrs. Bates possessed what she wanted, and would not permit her to have it precisely when its possession would afford the greatest gratification.

“Keep the head-dress,” she said, with an affected laugh. “I would not deprive you of it for the world. I merely asked for the pattern.”

“And I merely declined to give it before Thursday.”

The conversation changed; but Mrs. Tarleton departed dissatisfied.

That afternoon she visited her sister, Mrs. Raynor, and described the head-dress with much enthusiasm.

“I never saw any thing so graceful,” she said; “and it would suit my pink silk exactly.”

“Then have one made.”

“Mrs. Bates will not allow me to take the pattern.”

“Not allow you! What authority has she over the fashions?”

“She says it came from Paris, and she wishes to wear it first.”

“How selfish!”

“She even offered to give it to me after the party.”

“That is merely a method of displaying her generosity after she has enjoyed her triumph. I would not submit to such dictation.”

“Nor I,” replied Mrs. Tarleton; though, in truth, she had been doubtful until her sister gave vanity the support of resentment.

“Could you not borrow it for an hour?” asked Mrs. Raynor.

“I might say that I wished to show it to you.”

“And while it is here, Miss Leaver could take the pattern. There would be no harm in that. Mrs. Bates has no exclusive right to lace and ribbon.”

Mrs. Tarleton hesitated. She knew there would be harm in it, because she knew that the borrowing must be obtained under a false pretense. But she desired the head-dress; and when desire is permitted to argue against principle, it is seldom at a loss for plausible reasons.

“She is really unreasonable,” said Mrs. Raynor.

“Exceedingly unreasonable.”

“And, after all, she need never know.”

This last consideration decided the matter.

On the following day Mrs. Tarleton again called upon Mrs. Bates.

“My dear,” she said, “my sister is so curious about your Parisian head-dress that I have promised to show it to her. Will you lend it to me until this evening?”

Mrs. Bates looked at her for a moment, not suspiciously, but with some surprise.

“You may show it to her here, if she will call.”

“She cannot possibly come. Her little boy is indisposed, and she does not like to leave him.”

“Certainly, then; take it. But be careful of the silver sprays. They are very delicate.”

“You may trust me,” replied Mrs. Tarleton.

These four words should be remembered.

She carried the head-dress to Mrs. Raynor’s house, where Miss Leaver, a fashionable milliner, had already been requested to attend. The folds were measured, the arrangement sketched, and the precise number of leaves in each spray ascertained.

“Can you make it by Thursday?” asked Mrs. Tarleton.

“With difficulty, madam.”

“You must make it. I will pay any additional charge.”

“The silver foliage cannot be procured in time.”

“Then use pearl leaves, or white satin. But the form must be exact.”

Mrs. Raynor suggested one slight alteration, but Mrs. Tarleton objected.

“No; the beauty consists in the whole effect. I would not have it changed.”

Thus the lady who had declared that her dress maker would, of course, modify the pattern, now insisted upon an exact imitation.

The borrowed article was returned before evening, apparently uninjured. Mrs. Bates thanked her friend for taking so much care of it, and Mrs. Tarleton received the acknowledgment without any visible embarrassment. We do not say that she felt none. Conscience is unfrequently troublesome before it becomes hardened by self-justification. But she had advanced too far to retreat without the mortification of confessing her design, and she chose rather to complete the wrong than to endure that mortification.

On Wednesday morning Miss Leaver waited upon Mrs. Bates. It happened that Mrs. Bates had ordered from her a plain head-dress for an elderly aunt, and the milliner brought several ribbons from which a selection was to be made.

After the business was concluded, Miss Leaver said, with professional animation,—

“The Paris pattern is the prettiest thing I have handled this season. Mrs. Tarleton’s will be nearly finished by night.”

Mrs. Bates looked up.

“Mrs. Tarleton’s?”

“Yes, madam—the copy of yours. The pearl leaves do not equal the silver, but in every other particular it is exact. I took the pattern yesterday at Mrs. Raynor’s.”

There was a brief silence. Miss Leaver, perceiving too late that she had said something unexpected, became confused.

“You took the pattern from my head-dress?”

“Yes, madam. I understood—I supposed—Mrs. Tarleton said it had been lent—”

“It was lent that her sister might see it,” replied Mrs. Bates, quietly.

The milliner colored deeply. “I beg your pardon, madam. I had no thought of doing what was improper.”

“You are not the person whom I blame. Is the copy intended for Mrs. Selwyn’s party?”

“I believe so, madam.”

“That is all. Let us look at the gray ribbons.”

Mrs. Bates neither ordered the work to be stopped nor demanded the destruction of the copy. She had a right to expose Mrs. Tarleton at once, but she did not choose to enter into an altercation in which angry words might obscure the plainness of the facts. When Miss Leaver had gone, she put the Parisian ornament into its box and sent a note to another milliner, from whom she selected a simple wreath of white flowers for the approaching evening.

Mr. Bates, on being informed of the affair, expressed more indignation than his wife.

“Send the head-dress to Mrs. Tarleton,” said he, “with a message that since she has taken the pattern, she may as well take the original.”

“No,” replied Mrs. Bates. “That would look like an attempt to insult her.”

“Then wear it, and let every one see that hers is the copy.”

“I shall do neither. If I wear it, she may imagine that I have entered into a contest with her for admiration. I will wear something else.”

“Humph!” said Mr. Bates. “Women’s dress seems to require as much diplomacy as a treaty between nations.”

“The dress requires very little,” answered his wife; “but truth and falsehood may be concerned with a ribbon as well as with a treaty.”

Mr. Bates admitted the distinction, though he continued to grumble that a sensible woman ought not to care whether twenty other women wore the same thing.

“I do not care now,” said Mrs. Bates. “Had Mrs. Tarleton openly copied it against my wish, I should have thought her selfish and indelicate, but I might have overlooked the offence. She obtained it by telling me that she wished only to show it to her sister. That changes the character of the act.”

On Thursday evening Mrs. Tarleton entered Mrs. Selwyn’s drawing-room with a confidence heightened by the consciousness of being finely dressed. The copied head-dress had been completed with success. Several ladies gathered around her.

“How novel!”

“What an exquisite form!”

“Is it Parisian?”

Mrs. Tarleton smiled.

“The idea is French,” she said. “Miss Leaver arranged it for me.”

This answer was not, perhaps, a direct falsehood; yet it was intended to convey what was untrue, and therefore deserves no honorable defensive. She knew that Miss Leaver had not originated the arrangement, but merely copied it from property borrowed in confidence.

Mrs. Raynor stood near, enjoying what she regarded as her sister’s triumph.

“You were wise not to be intimidated,” she whispered.

At that moment Mrs. Bates was announced.

Mrs. Tarleton looked eagerly toward the door. She had prepared herself to meet the surprise, and even the displeasure, which the appearance of two similar ornaments might occasion. She expected that Mrs. Bates would be compelled either to conceal her vexation or to betray it before the company; and vanity had whispered that, as pearl leaves were softer than silver, the imitation might be pronounced the more becoming.

But Mrs. Bates wore a wreath of white flowers.

The sight produced in Mrs. Tarleton a sensation very unlike triumph. She perceived instantly that the deception had been discovered. Mrs. Bates approached with perfect composure, spoke courteously to Mrs. Raynor, and then said,—

“Good evening, Mrs. Tarleton. Your head-dress is very pretty.”

Nothing could have been more polite; nothing could have been colder. There was no emphasis upon the words, no glance toward the pearl leaves, no smile of ungenerous triumph. Yet Mrs. Tarleton would have preferred an accusation. She faltered some confused reply, and during the remainder of the evening watched Mrs. Bates with restless anxiety.

Had Mrs. Bates told any body? Would Miss Leaver repeat what had occurred? Did Mrs. Selwyn know? Every whisper appeared significant; every look seemed directed toward the copied ornament. The admiration which had delighted her half an hour before became painful, because each compliment recalled the means by which the object admired had been obtained.

The next day Mrs. Tarleton called upon Mrs. Bates. She intended, at first, to acknowledge the whole offence; but wounded pride intervened before the confession could be made.

“I suppose,” she began, “Miss Leaver told you that I had a head-dress made somewhat like yours.”

“She did.”

“I am sorry you thought it necessary to change your own.”

“I thought it best.”

“My sister persuaded me to take the pattern. I should not otherwise have done it.”

“Your sister did not borrow the head-dress from me.”

“No; but she assured me there could be no impropriety in copying a fashion.”

“The impropriety was in borrowing it for one purpose and using it for another.”

“I did show it to my sister.”

“You told me that was the purpose for which you wished it.”

“And so it was—partly.”

Mrs. Bates remained silent.

“I think,” continued Mrs. Tarleton, “that you attach too much importance to a very trifling matter.”

“The material matter is trifling.”

“Then why should it interrupt our friendship?”

“Because the act was not trifling. When you said, ‘You may trust me,’ I believed you.”

“Do you mean to say that you will never forgive me?”

“I am not seeking revenge. I bear you no malice, and I shall speak of you with propriety. But the intimacy which existed between us cannot continue.”

Mrs. Tarleton rose, offended.

“This is very severe!”

“It may appear so. Yet intimacy without confidence is only the name of friendship, and confidence, once deliberately violated, cannot be restored by pretending that no violation occurred.”

“If you had not been so determined to keep the pattern to yourself, I should never have been tempted.”

Mrs. Bates’s expression changed; not to anger, but to sorrow.

“That is an unfortunate defence. You make my refusal responsible for your deceit. I had a right to refuse; you had no right to mislead me.”

“I see that explanation is useless,” said Mrs. Tarleton, and withdrew.

Had she gone home, examined her conduct honestly, and returned with a frank acknowledgment, much might yet have been repaired. Mrs. Bates would not immediately have restored the former intimacy, but she would have respected sincere repentance. Mrs. Tarleton, however, desired not so much to regain her friend as to escape censure. She therefore began to tell the story.

To one person she said that Mrs. Bates had taken offence because she happened to wear a similar head-dress. To another she admitted borrowing it, but declared that Mrs. Bates had never expressly forbidden her to have it copied. To a third she mentioned Mrs. Bates’s offer to give her the original, but represented the offer as an insulting display of superiority. Mrs. Raynor repeated that any fashion, once seen, became public property; and Miss Leaver, anxious to excuse herself, said she had supposed the arrangement was made with Mrs. Bates’s knowledge.

Thus, at every repetition, the false pretense under which the ornament had been obtained grew less distinct, while Mrs. Bates’s resentment became more prominent. A violation of confidence was converted into a dispute about dress; a deliberate act into a misunderstanding; and a firm withdrawal of esteem into vindictive anger.

“Well,” said Mrs. Loring, after hearing both accounts imperfectly, “Mrs. Tarleton should not have copied the thing, but Mrs. Bates should not have been so particular. There are faults on both sides.”

This judgment was repeated by several mutual friends, who considered themselves charitable because they blamed both women equally. One of them undertook to reconcile the parties.

“My dear Mrs. Bates,” she said, “surely you will not sacrifice an old friendship for a head-dress.”

“I have not sacrificed it for a head-dress.”

“But the quarrel began there.”

“The discovery began there. The cause was want of truth.”

“Mrs. Tarleton says she did not suppose you would object so seriously.”

“She knew that I objected, for I had refused her the pattern twice. She therefore borrowed the article under another pretense.”

“She was wrong, certainly; but perhaps you were a little too tenacious.”

“If I had guarded the pattern through excessive vanity, that might be a fault in me. It would not make her deception less a fault in her.”

“Can you not make due allowance for temptation?”

“I do. Due allowance may prevent harshness; it does not require renewed confidence. I can forgive an act without placing myself again in the same relation to the person who committed it.”

The lady shook her head and observed that she feared Mrs. Bates was unforgiving. Yet, when asked whether she would entrust a private letter to one who had secretly shown another letter after promising discretion, she admitted that she would not.

“And wherein,” said Mrs. Bates, “does the principle differ? The thing entrusted may be lace, paper, or money; but when confidence is asked and given, honor should protect it.”

We consider this reasoning conclusive.

It may be said that Mrs. Bates was vain in wishing to appear at one party in a head-dress unlike those of her acquaintance. Be it so. The vanity was innocent, openly avowed, and involved no wrong to another. Mrs. Tarleton was at liberty to think the refusal foolish; she was at liberty to purchase any ornament she could honestly procure; she was even at liberty, after seeing the design, to imitate it from memory, though such conduct would have shown indelicate pertinacity. But she was not at liberty to request a loan for one stated purpose and secretly employ the borrowed property for another.

Nor was this the whole offence. She concealed the act, received thanks for her supposed care, claimed admiration under an equivocal account of the design, attempted afterward to transfer responsibility to her sister, and finally circulated a distorted narrative to preserve her own reputation. Each step interpreted the preceding one. What might at first have been called a weakness became, by repetition and defence, an evidence of character.

We do not think Mrs. Bates perfect. Had she exposed Mrs. Tarleton publicly, or ridiculed her copied ornament, or exaggerated the injury, we should have censured her. She did none of these things. She avoided a humiliating contest, preserved outward propriety, stated her reasons privately, and refused only that intimacy which cannot exist without esteem.

Let no one, therefore, say carelessly that there were “faults on both sides,” as though the faults were alike in nature or degree. Such language often wrongs the injured while offering no real benefit to the offender. Mercy is not blindness, candor is not indifference, and a just judgment does not cease to be charitable because it distinguishes between vanity and falsehood.

A head-dress is a trifle; but the principle which governs our conduct respecting trifles is not a trifle. We may learn whether a person is trustworthy from the manner in which she returns a borrowed ornament, repeats a private conversation, fulfills a small promise, or resists a petty temptation. Great occasions are rare, and on them the selfish may act nobly because the eyes of the world are fixed upon them; but daily incidents are the tests by which character is most safely known.

Mrs. Bates was right. She forgave the injury, but she did not restore the confidence which Mrs. Tarleton had forfeited. To have done so merely that society might praise her amiability would not have been generosity, but weakness; and to condemn her for declining such intimacy is not charity, but injustice wearing charity’s name.

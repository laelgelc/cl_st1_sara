## The Incorruptibility of Thomas Chadwick

When Thomas Chadwick became a conductor on the electric trams, Bursley received the news with mingled incredulity and satisfaction. Incredulity arose from the widespread belief that Thomas had already exhausted the list of occupations compatible with the dignity of an Englishman; satisfaction from the certainty that, in uniform and among the public, he would furnish more entertainment than any municipal band supported out of the rates.

He had been, among other things, a policeman, a rate-collector’s assistant, a traveller in patent knife-cleaners, a night watchman at an earthenware manufactory, an attendant at agricultural exhibitions, a temporary porter at a bank, a caretaker of empty houses, an insurance agent, a bailiff, a commissionaire, a dealer in second-hand bicycles, and the proprietor of a shop which sold tobacco, mineral waters, picture postcards, and nothing in particular. He had also spent three months announcing himself as a “confidential investigator,” though no one ever discovered what he investigated or whose confidence he enjoyed. His professional versatility was equalled only by his want of professional success.

Nevertheless, he looked prosperous. Misfortune had caused him to grow steadily stouter, as though adversity, finding itself unable to break him, had decided to inflate him. At sixty he possessed a broad chest, a large pale face, grey moustaches of military intention, and a magnificent manner of standing still. In repose he resembled a chief constable inspecting a riot from an upper window. In action he resembled the same chief constable after being informed that the riot had entered by the back door.

His weakness was not physical. He had a constitution capable of enduring cold, rain, indifferent beer, domestic argument, unemployment, and the smoke of five towns simultaneously. Yet he objected to night duty on the ground of his health, to early duty on the ground of his age, to split duty on the ground of justice, and to ordinary duty whenever it interfered with any civic meeting at which Thomas Chadwick considered that his presence might affect the destiny of the district. What he could not endure was discipline. He could command in a voice that made timid persons glance instinctively for an exit; he could not obey without first explaining to authority how authority had misconceived its own functions.

His dismissal from the police had been discussed for twenty years, and two explanations survived. According to Thomas, he had resigned because a free-born Englishman could not sacrifice principle to promotion. According to the police, he had been invited to resign because, while guarding a warehouse, he had left his post to address a political meeting concerning the excessive cost of guarding warehouses. Both accounts may have been true.

The tramway appointment therefore appeared at once suitable and perilous. The uniform suited him beyond criticism. The brass buttons gave official confirmation to his stomach; the cap imparted law to his forehead; and the ticket machine, suspended upon his breast, had the air of an order conferred upon him by a grateful sovereign. He entered a car not as a servant of a public utility but as a chamberlain taking possession of a small travelling court.

Passengers, in his view, did not pay fares. They submitted their pecuniary credentials for examination. He received pennies with the grave suspicion of a Chancellor of the Exchequer receiving estimates. He tore tickets ceremonially, and the snap of his punch seemed to say:

“Observe! Society may totter, ministries may perish, and the price of coal may rise; but Thomas Chadwick remains at his post.”

It was regrettable that the tramway company had not engaged him to remain at his post in quite this fashion. The company desired speed, uniformity, civility, and exact accounts. Thomas desired the public recognition of Thomas. Between these aims there could be only a provisional peace.

At that period the Five Towns were agitated by Federation. Federation meant that ancient rivalries, municipal vanities, sewage arrangements, water supplies, debts, dignities, and mayoral chains should be thrown into one political cauldron and boiled into a larger borough. Its supporters said it was inevitable, economical, and modern. Its opponents said it was monstrous, ruinous, and an insult to their grandfathers. Both sides spoke of posterity with a solicitude never shown to their immediate neighbours.

Thomas Chadwick was an Anti-Federationist. He was not merely opposed to Federation; he had suffered the idea as a personal affront. Federation would standardize the towns, and Thomas had an instinctive dislike of standardization. He knew that under a truly enlightened system exceptional men would be judged by exceptional rules, preferably rules made after consultation with themselves. He wore an orange rosette of such dimensions that, when he leaned from the tram platform, it preceded him into the street.

The company forbade political badges on duty.

Thomas wore his beneath his overcoat until controversy warmed him; then, by a movement ostensibly directed towards ventilation, he exposed it.

One wet afternoon, on a car travelling from Hanbridge to Bursley, he encountered three young Federationists. They were clerks, and therefore members of a class which Thomas distrusted because it could write quickly, add accurately, and laugh without permission. One of them was Paul Ford, a slim young man with bright eyes and a small moustache which seemed to be laughing even when its proprietor was silent.

“How do, Tommy?” said Ford, as Thomas approached.

Thomas halted. The car proceeded beneath him with a humming that suggested suppressed comment.

“My name,” he said, “is Chadwick.”

“Right oh, Tommy.”

“And while I am in the execution of my official duty, I’ll thank you to address me as ‘conductor.’”

“Certainly, conductor Tommy. Three to Bursley.”

“You’ll have three separate tickets,” said Thomas. “This is not a picnic party.”

He issued them in a manner implying that the tickets carried grave responsibilities, and that if Bursley had been properly governed the three young men would never have been allowed to reach it.

Paul Ford examined his ticket.

“Federation’ll improve these,” he said. “One ticket for all the towns. No frontiers. No customs officers. No Tommy.”

Thomas’s nostrils moved.

“Federation,” he replied, “is a scheme got up by busybodies, office-boys, and persons without a stake in the district.”

“My uncle’s got two kilns and a public-house.”

“Then he ought to be ashamed of you.”

The other clerks laughed. Thomas rang the bell with unnecessary violence, causing the driver to stop between two legitimate stopping-places.

“What’s that for?” called the driver.

Thomas did not answer. He rang twice to proceed, conveying to the whole car that errors might occur even in the best-regulated services, especially when drivers failed to understand conductors.

“You Anti-Federationists,” said Ford, “are all for rank and wealth.”

“I’m for them as has made the country what it is.”

“Who’s that?”

“Ladies and gentlemen,” Thomas answered.

At that moment the car stopped near the entrance to Sneyd Hall, and Mrs Clayton Vernon entered.

There are coincidences which a smaller man might regard as providential. Thomas regarded this one as official confirmation.

Mrs Clayton Vernon was a tall, composed woman of perhaps forty-five, dressed in quiet colours so costly that they appeared to have renounced display from sheer confidence. Her husband owned collieries, land, a celebrated collection of eighteenth-century snuffboxes, and opinions upon the lower orders. Her father had been a baronet; her mother had been the daughter of an earl; and her name occurred in the local newspaper with a frequency surpassed only by that of the weather.

Thomas knew her by sight. Everybody knew her by sight, though few had been seen by her.

His transformation was immediate. The belligerent official melted into the hereditary retainer. His back acquired a deferential curve. His hand rose to his cap with a reverence which included Mrs Vernon, her ancestors, Sneyd Hall, the Conservative Party, landed property, and the institution of afternoon tea.

“Good afternoon, ma’am.”

“Good afternoon,” said Mrs Vernon, looking first at him and then, with a slight uncertainty, at the interior of the car.

“This way, ma’am. A seat here, if you please.”

There were four vacant seats, all equally accessible, but Thomas selected one with the care of a diplomat arranging a royal marriage. He brushed from it an imaginary speck, discouraged a labourer who had shown no intention of occupying it, and steadied Mrs Vernon by the elbow though neither tram nor lady required steadiness.

“Thank you,” she said.

Thomas issued her ticket. His fingers, capable of slapping a ticket into the palm of an ordinary citizen, placed hers as delicately as though it were a title deed.

Paul Ford observed these ministrations.

“I suppose,” he said, “this is one of them as made the country what it is?”

Thomas turned upon him.

“If some people had a little more respect for their betters, the country would be the better for it.”

Mrs Vernon glanced from Thomas to the three clerks with the faint, tolerant smile of a person overhearing the political passions of children. Thomas interpreted the smile as an alliance.

“She’s a lady, she is,” he announced when, two stops later, Mrs Vernon rose.

“I beg your pardon?” said Mrs Vernon.

“I was saying, ma’am, that courtesy is due to ladies.”

“How very nice,” said Mrs Vernon.

Thomas assisted her to the platform and watched her descend. The tram moved off. He continued to watch until Sneyd Hall’s lodge gates had received her.

Paul Ford leaned forward.

“Does she pay the same fare as us?”

“The fare is a penny,” said Thomas.

“Then Federation has begun.”

Thomas scorned to reply. He began collecting from two factory girls who had already paid him. The error, when demonstrated, offended him more than it surprised him. He had been engaged upon higher matters.

At the terminus the passengers departed, the driver went for tea, and Thomas inspected the car. His inspections were severe, particularly when there was nobody present to endure them. Under the seat which Mrs Vernon had occupied he perceived a small golden object.

He stooped.

It was a purse of woven gold, heavy, elegant, and fastened with a jewelled clasp. Inside were banknotes, sovereigns, and a card bearing the name of Mrs Clayton Vernon. The contents exceeded fifty pounds.

There was not a soul in the car.

Thomas stood upright. The empty tram, with its polished rails, leather straps, advertisements for liver pills, and remote electrical humming, became suddenly the theatre of a moral crisis. Fifty pounds! To Thomas, who had seldom possessed five pounds after the payment of household expenses, it was not a sum but a landscape. It contained rent, meat, tobacco, boots, political subscriptions, and weeks during which no one might order him to ring a bell.

He closed the purse.

The idea of theft did not seriously tempt him. Thomas was honest. Moreover, he was spectacularly honest. Ordinary integrity returns a purse because it belongs to somebody else; Thomas’s integrity returned it because the universe ought not to be deprived of the spectacle of his returning it. He saw himself standing before Mrs Clayton Vernon, the gold purse upon his palm, while she discovered with emotion that incorruptibility still existed among the people.

There would be gratitude.

He did not specify to himself the form which gratitude might take. He was above calculation. But his imagination, less disciplined than his principles, suggested a five-pound note. It then rejected five pounds as almost insulting in view of the sum preserved. Ten pounds had breadth. Even ten pounds, however, might be represented not as reward but as a gift for the grandchildren. Thomas had twin grandsons, Bert and Albert, whose existence became especially vivid whenever wealthy persons were distributing benevolence.

He could have delivered the purse to the company’s lost-property office. This would have been regular, efficient, and anonymous. The mere thought was intolerable. Some clerk, probably a Federationist, might receive the credit. Mrs Vernon might send a letter beginning “Dear Sir” to the general manager. Thomas’s act would become an entry in a ledger.

No. He would restore the purse personally.

His duty ended at six. At a quarter past, washed, brushed, buttoned, and carrying the purse in an inside pocket over which his hand frequently passed, he ascended the drive to Sneyd Hall.

The house stood above the industrial plain in an atmosphere apparently manufactured for its exclusive use. Below, furnaces reddened the murk and chimneys laboured; above, Sneyd Hall maintained lawns. The contrast gratified Thomas. It showed that society had an arrangement.

A footman opened the door.

Thomas had expected a footman, but expectation did not diminish the impact. The young man’s expression suggested that all possible visitors had been foreseen except Thomas.

“Mrs Clayton Vernon,” said Thomas.

“What name?”

“Mr Thomas Chadwick.”

The “Mr” was distinct.

“Is Mrs Vernon expecting you?”

“She has lost an article of considerable value.”

The footman’s face altered by no visible degree, yet Thomas knew that he had risen slightly in its estimation.

“Wait here.”

Thomas waited in a hall containing armour, portraits, and a clock so quiet and superior that it seemed to despise time. He arranged his cap beneath his arm. In one portrait a dead Vernon, clothed in scarlet and confidence, regarded him from horseback. Thomas returned the gaze as one public servant to another.

Mrs Vernon appeared.

“Oh!” she said. “It is the conductor.”

The identification was accurate, but Thomas had hoped that his name might have preceded him.

“Madam,” he began, “in the execution of my duty this afternoon, subsequent to your leaving the company’s car, I discovered beneath the seat occupied by yourself this article.”

He produced the purse.

“My purse,” said Mrs Vernon. “I wondered where I had put it.”

Thomas offered it with a gesture at once modest and monumental.

“It contains, so far as I could ascertain without unnecessary examination, a sum exceeding fifty pounds.”

“Yes, probably.”

“I deemed it inadvisable to entrust property of that nature to ordinary channels.”

“That was thoughtful.”

She took the purse, opened it, glanced inside, and closed it again. Thomas waited for alarm, relief, astonishment, or at least a declaration that she had passed an agonized afternoon. None arrived.

“I knew it would be quite safe if I had left it on the tram,” she said.

This confidence in public morality, though admirable in principle, robbed Thomas of half his triumph. He had not rescued the purse from safety. He had merely brought it home.

“Quite safe, ma’am,” he agreed. “Where I am concerned.”

“Of course.”

Mrs Vernon smiled. Thomas expanded slightly.

“You must have something after your walk. James, will you bring this man a glass of beer?”

This man.

The words were not intended as an insult. That was their power. Thomas followed the footman into a small room near the servants’ quarters and received a glass of beer. It was excellent beer, but it was still beer. No man can convert a glass of beer into ten pounds by drinking it slowly, though Thomas made the attempt.

Mrs Vernon remained at the door, the golden purse in her hand.

“You have children, I suppose?”

“A daughter, ma’am. Married. And twin grandsons—fine lads, both of them.”

“Twins! How charming. How old are the chicks?”

“Six, ma’am.”

“Then I must send them something.”

Thomas’s moral landscape brightened. The ten pounds reappeared, reduced perhaps, but respectable.

Mrs Vernon opened the purse, searched among its contents, and brought out two silver coins.

“Here we are. Threepence each. They can buy sweets.”

She placed the coins in Thomas’s palm.

For one second his fingers did not close. The twin threepences lay upon his broad hand with an appearance of almost indecent smallness. He had returned more than fifty pounds. He had walked uphill. He had upheld the honour of the tramway service and proved the incorruptibility of the English character. Bert and Albert were each to receive threepence.

“Thank you very much, ma’am,” he said.

His dignity saved him. Dignity had often saved Thomas when money, tact, obedience, foresight, and good fortune had failed. He drank the remainder of the beer, bowed to Mrs Vernon, bowed fractionally less to the footman, and descended the drive.

The furnaces below were glowing. Their vast fires seemed employed in the manufacture of sixpence.

At home, Thomas presented the coins to the twins with solemnity. His daughter, who knew him, asked no questions. Bert immediately proposed to spend his share upon liquorice; Albert, preferring machinery, desired a whistle. Thomas informed them that the money was the gift of one of the greatest ladies in the county, and that they must remember her in their prayers.

“Can we have it now?” asked Bert.

“You have no sense of occasion,” said Thomas.

The following afternoon Paul Ford boarded Thomas’s tram.

“How do, Tommy?”

Thomas ignored the form of address.

“Heard you found Mrs Vernon’s purse.”

Thomas began his progress down the car.

“More than fifty quid in it, they say.”

Thomas turned. News in the Five Towns travelled with the rapidity of scandal and the accuracy of smoke.

“The contents,” he said, “were no concern of mine.”

“Course not. What did she give you?”

A silence of unusual alertness entered the car. Two women ceased discussing a neighbour’s confinement. A potter looked up from his evening paper. Even the driver, though separated by a glass partition and several yards of machinery, appeared morally present.

Thomas punched a ticket.

“Give me?” he repeated.

“Reward.”

“I require no reward for doing my duty.”

“No, but what did she give you?”

Paul Ford’s face was innocent. His moustache was not.

Thomas could have answered, “A glass of beer and sixpence for the twins.” The answer was true. It was also impossible. In Thomas’s conception, Mrs Clayton Vernon was a lady; a lady possessed generosity as a natural appurtenance, like a carriage drive or a pedigree. If she had rewarded the salvation of fifty pounds with sixpence, either she was not a lady or Thomas’s services were worth sixpence. Both conclusions threatened the foundations of society.

He chose a third.

“She asked whether I had grandchildren.”

“I bet she did.”

“And when I told her of the twins, she gave them a sovereign apiece.”

The sovereigns rolled magnificently through the car.

Paul Ford whistled.

“Two quid!”

“A mere token,” said Thomas, now committed to splendour. “She would have done more, but I said, ‘Madam, I am not a man to profit by another person’s misfortune.’”

“And she gave you nothing?”

“I would not accept it.”

“Not even a glass of beer?”

Thomas looked at him.

“I was offered refreshment,” he said coldly. “As between one responsible person and another.”

“Put that in your pipe and smoke it,” said one of the women to her companion, though what precisely was to be smoked remained uncertain.

Thomas passed along the car with a mien of tried and authenticated virtue. The lie disturbed him less than might have been expected. He did not regard it as a lie in the common sense. It was a correction applied to reality. Mrs Vernon ought to have given a sovereign to each child; being a lady, she had in essence done so; and if the gross material coins happened to be threepenny pieces, this was an accident unworthy of public notice.

For several days he related the incident with improvements. He did not volunteer the narrative, but he arranged that questions should become unavoidable.

“Yes,” he would say, “I could have kept the whole amount. No one saw me. But there are men whose integrity cannot be purchased.”

The number of banknotes rose slightly. The beer disappeared altogether. Mrs Vernon’s gratitude became increasingly emotional, though never uncontrolled.

“She said to me, ‘Chadwick, I wish there were more like you.’ Those were her words.”

No one could prove that they were not her words, except Mrs Vernon, and she moved in regions inaccessible to most of Thomas’s audience.

The tramway inspector, however, proved accessible. He objected to Thomas’s political rosette, to his discussions with passengers, to his delays, and to an incident in which Thomas had refused to stop for a known Federationist until the man ran twenty yards.

“You’re here to conduct the car,” said the inspector.

“I conduct it.”

“You’re here to be civil.”

“I am civil to them as deserves civility.”

“You’re to be civil to everybody.”

Thomas drew himself up.

“That may be your interpretation of civility.”

“It is the company’s.”

“The company,” said Thomas, “is not infallible.”

This exchange did not improve his prospects, but it strengthened his self-respect.

Then, on a bright morning a week after the recovery of the purse, destiny boarded the eleven-twenty car at Sneyd Hall.

Paul Ford was already inside.

He had selected the car deliberately. He had also, by a series of casual inquiries conducted with more skill than Thomas had ever displayed in confidential investigation, learned that Mrs Vernon commonly travelled into Hanbridge on Thursday mornings. Paul had no scheme beyond the general hope that truth, properly encouraged, might become amusing.

Thomas stood upon the rear platform, his cap square, his ticket machine ready, his dignity at full pressure.

Mrs Vernon entered.

He bowed.

“Good morning, ma’am.”

“Good morning. How nice to see you again.”

Thomas threw one triumphant glance towards Paul Ford. The glance said: “You observe? Greatness does not forget greatness.”

Mrs Vernon seated herself. Thomas brushed the air near her shoulder and issued the ticket.

“And how are the little twins?” she asked.

“Very well indeed, ma’am. They were deeply sensible of your kindness.”

“I hope they bought something pleasant.”

“They appreciated the gift in a proper spirit.”

Paul Ford leaned forward.

“Two sovereigns wants some appreciating.”

Thomas heard him. Mrs Vernon did not, or gave no sign that she had.

She smiled at Thomas.

“And your daughter? Is she well?”

“Quite well, thank you, ma’am. In fact, there has been a recent addition to the family.”

“Another baby?”

“A boy, ma’am.”

“How delightful! Then I owe you something for the third chick.”

Thomas perceived the danger before it arrived, but there are catastrophes which advance with the stately inevitability of municipal works. Mrs Vernon opened her handbag. Paul Ford’s eyes became reverent. The entire car appeared to hold its breath.

From a small purse she selected a threepenny piece.

“Here,” she said, extending it. “This will make things equal.”

The coin shone between her gloved fingers.

Thomas looked at it.

He might have refused it. He might have declared that the twins had received sovereigns and demanded, in the name of equality, a sovereign for the infant. He might have cast it through the window. He might have denounced wealth, rank, landed property, baronets’ daughters, and the whole social structure from the Norman Conquest downward. He might even have turned upon Paul Ford and sought relief in physical violence.

Instead he accepted the threepenny piece.

“Thank you very much, ma’am,” he said, with exquisite courtesy.

Paul Ford made a sound, small but fatal.

Mrs Vernon glanced at him.

“Is something the matter?”

“No, ma’am,” said Paul. “I was only thinking what a generous thing equality is.”

Thomas’s face became purple beneath its official pallor.

Mrs Vernon, who had not the key to the conversation and did not require it, turned again to the window.

“There are such quantities of smoke this morning.”

“Industrial prosperity, ma’am,” said Thomas.

His voice was steady. His hand, gripping the ticket punch, was not.

Paul Ford waited until Mrs Vernon had left the car. Thomas assisted her down with undiminished attention, rang the bell, and stood gazing after her as before. Only when the tram was in motion did Paul speak.

“Tommy.”

No answer.

“Tommy, was the first sovereign a silver one?”

Thomas advanced.

“You,” he said, “are a contemptible young jackanapes.”

“Threepence for the new chick,” Paul continued. “That makes him cheaper than the twins.”

“I told you before to address me in a proper manner.”

“What did the twins really get?”

Thomas’s gestures enlarged. His official leather bag trembled against his side.

“You’ve no respect. That’s what’s wrong with the rising generation. No respect for age, position, character, public service, or a lady.”

“How much, Tommy?”

“She is a lady!” Thomas thundered. “A real lady! Not one of your Federation females going about interfering with business they don’t understand. Mrs Clayton Vernon has breeding. She has consideration. She has a heart.”

“Worth threepence.”

“You dare to sneer because you’re incapable of appreciating delicacy. A gift isn’t measured by its pecuniary amount.”

“Yesterday it was two quid.”

“I did not say—”

“You said a sovereign apiece.”

“I said no such thing in the sense you mean.”

The distinction was superb, but too elevated for the audience. Laughter moved through the car. It began with Paul Ford, infected the potter, overcame two apprentices, and finally seized an elderly woman who had understood nothing except that Thomas was in distress. Once begun, it swelled beyond reason. Thomas stood in the centre of it like a monument surrounded by revolutionary crowds.

He could have retreated to the platform. He did not. He collected every fare. He punched every ticket. He gave correct change, including a halfpenny which the company might reasonably have lost in the confusion. His dignity was wounded, bleeding, perhaps mortally injured; nevertheless, it remained on duty.

At Hanbridge, Paul Ford descended.

“How do, Tommy?” he said.

Thomas’s reply was of such a nature that the inspector, who happened to be standing by the stopping-place, asked for an explanation.

The explanation lasted twenty minutes and embraced Federation, hereditary rank, the moral decline of clerks, the duties of tramway passengers, the privileges of conductors, the character of Mrs Clayton Vernon, the lost purse, the threepenny pieces, and the essential difference between truth and malicious interpretation. By the end, the inspector knew less than at the beginning, but he knew it more decisively.

A report followed.

Thomas was suspended for three days. He regarded the suspension as political persecution. Upon returning, he discovered that the company expected him to remove his orange rosette permanently, refrain from political controversy, avoid insulting passengers, ring the bell according to regulations, and accept the inspector’s authority without debate.

The demands were cumulative. Any one of them might perhaps have been endured. Together they constituted servitude.

For a fortnight Thomas attempted submission. His attempt was impressive in the way that a chained volcano is impressive. He addressed passengers as “sir” with an intonation which reduced them to criminality. He concealed the rosette, though a corner of orange ribbon continually escaped near his collar. When Paul Ford entered and said, “How do, Tommy?” Thomas merely looked at him—a look that travelled backwards through Paul’s ancestry and found no gentleman there.

But the company was modern. It had timetables, printed rules, numbered forms, efficiency returns, and the terrible conviction that one conductor should resemble another. It could not understand that Thomas Chadwick was a character. Indeed, it had no official category for characters, except under the heading “Causes of Delay.”

The end came over a dog.

A woman boarded with a small terrier. Thomas demanded the fare prescribed for dogs. The woman denied that the terrier was a dog for fare-paying purposes, on the ground that she carried it. Thomas replied that locomotion was not the test of doghood. The woman appealed to the inspector. The inspector ruled that, according to a recent company notice, small dogs carried in the arms travelled free.

Thomas had not read the notice.

He read it then, in public, and pronounced it nonsense.

The inspector ordered him to proceed.

Thomas requested a definition of “small.”

The tram stood for nine minutes while terrier, woman, conductor, inspector, driver, and passengers participated in a constitutional crisis. In the end Thomas rang the bell, but not before informing the inspector that obedience to an absurdity did not convert absurdity into wisdom.

That evening he was dismissed.

He came home carrying his cap in a paper parcel. Without the cap his head seemed private and almost vulnerable. He told his daughter that he had resigned. The tramway company, he explained, had become impossible for any man of independent spirit. It preferred machines to men, servility to judgment, and Federationists to honest citizens.

“What will you do now?” she asked.

Thomas looked towards the mantelpiece. Upon it, between two china dogs, lay the threepenny piece intended for the third grandchild. He had never delivered it. To hand it over would have required explanations, and Thomas had already furnished reality with all the explanations that dignity permitted.

“Something will offer,” he said.

Something always did. This was at once the tragedy and the convenience of Thomas Chadwick. Within a month he was seen wearing a peaked cap outside a skating rink in Hanbridge, where his task was to maintain order among boys and receive umbrellas from ladies. Within two months he had quarrelled with the manager concerning the proper distinction between an attendant and a commissionaire. Within three, he was again at liberty.

He continued to speak of Mrs Clayton Vernon.

“If you want to know what a true lady is,” he would say, “look at her. She knows character when she sees it.”

No one mentioned the sovereigns now except Paul Ford, and even Paul’s teasing lost something of its sharpness with repetition. Thomas had survived the exposure. More remarkably, his ideal had survived it too. Mrs Vernon remained generous; the gifts remained delicate; the beer remained hospitality between responsible persons; and Thomas’s return of the purse remained one of the great recorded demonstrations of British incorruptibility.

That Mrs Vernon had valued his service at sixpence, and afterwards at threepence more, was a vulgar interpretation of events. Thomas rejected it. Facts might be standardized by tramway companies, entered in ledgers by clerks, and repeated in public-houses by Federationists; but dignity was a private industry, and in that industry Thomas Chadwick admitted no inspector.

He had lost his situation, his triumph, and the confidence of several passengers. He had been publicly caught in a lie so transparent that even he could not entirely look through it. Yet he would not surrender the lady, because to surrender her was to surrender the social universe in which Thomas Chadwick, though poor, unsuccessful, and perpetually dismissed, might still figure as a person of consequence.

Thus he remained loyal—absurdly, magnificently, heroically loyal. He was a martyr to rank, to ceremony, to his own account of himself, and to the invincible threepence which he continued to describe as a sovereign of the spirit.

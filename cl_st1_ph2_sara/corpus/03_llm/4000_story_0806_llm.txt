## The House That Shouted Happiness

The last person to see Sir Aaron Armstrong alive said afterward that he had looked like the sunrise in a red dressing-gown.

This was not an original description, but Sir Aaron had exhausted originality in the matter of his own appearance. He was enormous, sanguine and silver-bearded; his face seemed illuminated from within by a furnace that had forgotten how to go out. When he laughed, which he did continually and often without visible provocation, his beard flew apart like white smoke above a bonfire. He slapped strangers on the back, forgave enemies before they had offended him, and believed that a glass of cold water taken at six in the morning was the beginning of every moral reform.

He was a philanthropist of the loud and locomotive sort. His charities came upon the poor with the rush of express trains. He founded clubs for sober cabmen, cheerful cripples and industrious widows; he opened public gardens in rainstorms and delivered lectures upon happiness in rooms where everybody was cold. His speeches were full of dawn, trumpets, clean windows and the British spirit. He had never admitted to a headache, a hesitation or a sunset.

It was therefore commonly felt that his death was almost an offence against public decency.

To murder Sir Aaron Armstrong was not merely to murder a man. It was like strangling a brass band or finding Father Christmas dead in a chimney. Yet at half-past seven on a bitter November morning, his body lay at the foot of the green railway embankment behind his house, with his neck broken and one leg twisted in a noose of rope.

The house itself stood in a narrow strip of ground between a black canal and the railway. It was tall, thin and grey, and had the appearance of having been squeezed upward by its disagreeable neighbours. Every few minutes a train shook it from cellar to chimney-pots. Cups rattled, doors muttered in their frames, and the long windows gave a faint crystalline shiver, as if the whole building were always expecting bad news.

Inspector Gilder arrived from the city shortly after eight. He was a long, composed man with a brown moustache so level that it might have been used in surveying. He had the professional courtesy of one who listened to everybody because he trusted nobody. With him came James Merton, a young reporter who had met Sir Aaron at three public dinners and had been deafened by him at all three.

They found the local constable guarding the body and looking resentfully at the railway, which had already interfered with four statements.

“There he is, sir,” said the constable.

Sir Aaron lay where the steep green bank rose behind the garden. Seen from above, the embankment was only a ridge of wet grass; seen from below, it was a giant’s club poised against the little house. The dead man’s scarlet dressing-gown had fallen open. Beneath it he wore nightclothes of an almost childish whiteness. There was blood on one temple, though not enough to explain the death. Around his right ankle the rope had tightened into a coarse brown bracelet, and the rest of it trailed upward through the grass.

Merton took off his hat.

“It doesn’t look possible,” he said. “I mean—him.”

“Death has no respect for reputations,” said Gilder.

“No, but it generally has some sense of humour.”

Gilder glanced at him.

“This may have had too much.”

They followed the rope with their eyes. It rose to a broken attic window at the very top of the house. Fragments of glass glittered on the roof of a lower extension and among the dead weeds. The window-frame had been smashed outward.

“Thrown through it?” asked Merton.

“Or went through it,” said Gilder.

The distinction was neat, but at that moment not very illuminating.

The back door opened, and a woman appeared. She was young, though the colourlessness of her face made age seem irrelevant. Her hair, her dress and even her eyes had variations of grey in them, so that she resembled an aspen seen through rain. Behind her stood a broad-shouldered man with a heavy head, Patrick Royce, Sir Aaron’s private secretary.

The woman was Alice Armstrong.

She looked once toward the red shape at the foot of the bank and swayed. Royce caught her, but she withdrew from his hands with a movement too quick to be dislike and too frightened to be modesty.

“Inspector,” she said, “have you found Magnus?”

“Your servant? Not yet.”

“He has gone.”

“So I understand.”

“He took my father’s money.”

Royce spoke over her shoulder. His voice was rough and low, the voice of a man who had spent the night swallowing words because any one of them might explode.

“The cashbox is empty. Nearly three thousand pounds. Magnus disappeared before the police came.”

“And where were you?” asked Gilder.

“In the house.”

“Where in the house?”

Royce stared at him.

“In my room.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

The inspector’s moustache remained judicially horizontal.

A train rushed past above them, turning the garden into thunder. The house trembled; the broken attic window flashed bleakly in the morning light. Through all the racket Alice’s white face remained fixed upon the body below, like a small moon that the noise of the world could neither move nor wake.

When the train had passed, Gilder said, “We had better go inside.”

The interior was warmer than the garden but less comfortable. Electric bulbs burned in the passages with a light bleaker than moonlight. Everywhere there were reminders of Sir Aaron’s public happiness: framed photographs of banquets, silver cups from temperance societies, illuminated addresses from institutions with names like the League of Smiling Labour. In one photograph Sir Aaron stood among twenty pale factory girls, his mouth opened in a jovial roar. Their expressions suggested that cheerfulness, like dentistry, was something being done to them.

“He filled the house,” Alice said suddenly.

It was the first personal thing she had said, and because it was spoken without praise or blame it sounded more terrible than either.

Merton looked at her with the clumsy compassion of youth. Royce looked at the floor.

They climbed three flights. On the second landing Gilder paused by an open bedroom. A mattress had been disturbed, and on the dressing-table stood a photograph of Alice. Someone had turned it face downward.

“Your father’s room?” he asked.

Alice nodded.

“Had he been ill?”

“Never.”

“Troubled?”

“Never.”

“Did he sleep well?”

“He said so.”

Gilder’s eyes moved from the overturned photograph to her face.

“What a man says about his health,” he observed, “is sometimes evidence about his manners rather than his health.”

“My father was always cheerful,” she replied.

The phrase had the hard, automatic sound of a lesson learned very young.

At the attic door the inspector stopped again. There was a dark smear on the white-painted handle.

“Blood,” said Merton.

“Probably,” said Gilder. “We shall let a cleverer man say so later.”

Inside, the attic was long and almost empty. Its sloping ceiling pressed down toward the broken window. A writing-table stood against one wall; a chair had been overturned beside it. Near the centre of the room lay a revolver. A half-full bottle of whisky stood on a packing-case with one glass beside it. A kitchen knife, stained red, had been pushed under a broken wooden screen. The rope ran from the shattered window across the floor and ended in a loose tangle near the chair.

For several seconds nobody spoke.

Then Merton said, “It looks as if every murderer in London had called and left his card.”

Gilder’s expression did not change, but he glanced at him with approval.

There was blood on the knife. There was blood on the edge of the table. There were deep scratches near the window and a round black mark in the carpet. When Gilder lifted the revolver with a handkerchief, he found that every chamber had been fired.

He bent over the carpet.

“Bullets went downward,” he said. “Into the floor.”

Merton joined him.

“All of them?”

“So it appears.”

“Perhaps there was a struggle for the weapon.”

“Perhaps.”

“A knife attack too?”

“Perhaps.”

“And the rope—strangling?”

Gilder followed the rope to the window.

“Perhaps.”

Merton made an impatient gesture.

“I say, Inspector, how many ways can one man be murdered?”

“Only one successfully.”

The door creaked behind them.

A small round priest had entered without being announced. He carried a black umbrella and wore spectacles that gave his mild face a look of apologetic astonishment. There was clay on the hem of his cassock and one of his shoelaces was undone.

“Father Brown,” said Merton. “I thought you were at the Deaf School.”

“I was expected there,” said the priest. “But the train was stopped, and then I met Inspector Gilder’s man, who is an old parishioner of mine. He thought perhaps I could help.”

Gilder nodded gravely. He had known Father Brown in cases where the priest’s apparent inattention had proved more useful than another man’s microscope.

“Sir Aaron Armstrong,” he said. “You knew of him?”

“Oh, yes. Everybody did.”

“A great worker for temperance.”

“So I have heard.”

“And an optimist.”

Father Brown’s eyes moved toward the whisky bottle.

“So I have heard,” he repeated.

Alice had remained near the door, supporting herself against the frame. When Father Brown saw her, something in his manner altered; not dramatically, but as a candle changes when carried from an empty room into one where somebody is dying.

“My dear,” he said, “you had better sit down.”

She seemed about to refuse. Then her gaze fell upon the knife beneath the screen.

Her lips parted.

“That was in my hand,” she said.

Royce turned on her.

“Alice!”

“It was in my hand when I woke.”

Nobody moved. Even the railway seemed, for one unnatural instant, to pause outside the walls.

Gilder spoke with careful gentleness.

“When you woke where?”

“Here.”

“In this room?”

“Yes.”

“Tell us what you remember.”

She pressed both hands against her temples.

“I heard a noise. Father shouting. Patrick was here with him. I came upstairs. There was smoke and a dreadful smell. They were struggling near the window. Father had the rope about him—or Patrick did—I don’t know. I saw something bright. I picked it up. Then there was blood. After that I remember nothing until Magnus bent over me.”

“Magnus found you?”

“Yes.”

“And the knife was in your hand?”

“Yes.”

“Where was your father then?”

She looked toward the broken window.

“Gone.”

The word was scarcely audible.

Royce crossed the room in two strides. He moved with the power and awkwardness of a buffalo drawing a child’s cart.

“She knows nothing,” he said. “She was fainting. She couldn’t have done anything.”

“Done anything?” repeated Gilder. “Nobody has yet said she did.”

“I say she didn’t.”

“You are very positive.”

“I was here.”

“Then perhaps,” said the inspector, “you can improve upon your first account.”

Royce’s large hands closed and opened.

Before he could answer, the bell rang below. It rang once, twice, and then continuously, as if someone had leaned upon it from exhaustion or malice.

The constable called up the stairs. Gilder went to the landing. A moment later he returned with a peculiar expression.

“Magnus has come back.”

The missing servant entered the attic under police escort.

Magnus was a tall, narrow man dressed entirely in black. His hair was black, his coat was black, and his hands were enclosed in black gloves, though the day was not cold enough to require them. Against the white walls he looked less like a servant than the shadow of one. His face was long and yellowish, but his eyes were peculiarly clear.

The strangest thing about him was his voice. In a house forever shaken by trains, he spoke with such exactness that every syllable arrived separately, like letters stamped upon metal.

“You are suspected of theft,” said Gilder.

“I anticipated that.”

“You fled with three thousand pounds belonging to Sir Aaron.”

“I carried three thousand pounds from this house.”

“To where?”

“The police station at Clapham.”

Gilder stared at him.

The local constable coughed.

“It’s true, sir. Message just come through. He delivered the money personally at seven twenty and asked them to seal it.”

“Why?”

“Because,” said Magnus, “Sir Aaron was dead, Miss Armstrong was unconscious, Mr Royce was absent from the room, and the outer door stood open. It appeared prudent.”

“Why did you not wait here for the police?”

“I distrust local prudence.”

The constable looked injured, but Magnus continued with the calm of a machine giving correct change.

“I had been employed to guard the money. My master was no longer able to instruct me. I therefore placed it where no member of this household could take it.”

Merton regarded him with reluctant admiration.

“You made yourself look remarkably guilty.”

“That was not my occupation. My occupation was to prevent loss.”

“And whom do you accuse?” Gilder asked.

Magnus turned his clear eyes toward Alice.

“The young lady.”

Royce took one step forward, and the floor seemed to object beneath him.

“You black devil!”

Magnus did not retreat.

“I found Miss Armstrong on the floor beside the window. She held the knife. Her dress bore blood. Sir Aaron was below. These are not theological conclusions. They are observations.”

Father Brown, who had been inspecting the revolver, looked up.

“Theological conclusions are sometimes kinder,” he said.

Magnus’s face gave no sign that he had heard.

“You saw nobody else?” asked Gilder.

“I saw Mr Royce leaving the attic as I reached the upper landing.”

This time everybody looked at the secretary.

Royce’s face changed. It did not grow pale, for it was already the dark colour of weathered brick; but some inward support seemed to be withdrawn from it.

Alice whispered his name.

He turned away from her.

“All right,” he said. “That’s enough.”

Gilder waited.

Royce looked around the attic: at the bottle, the pistol, the knife, the rope and the open square of grey sky. He looked at them not as evidence but as enemies that had surrounded him.

“I killed him,” he said.

Alice gave a small cry.

Royce continued quickly, almost brutally, as if speed could keep pity from interrupting him.

“I came up here after midnight. I had been drinking. Sir Aaron found me. He knew about Alice and me—knew I wanted to marry her. He had forbidden it. Said I was a drunken wastrel and she’d never see me again. We quarrelled. I drew the revolver. He grappled with me. It went off several times. I took the rope and tried to choke him. He got loose. There was a knife. I don’t remember everything. I struck him, and he went through the window.”

“You threw him?” asked Gilder.

“Yes.”

“With the rope around his ankle?”

“I suppose so.”

“And you left Miss Armstrong unconscious?”

“Yes.”

“With the knife in her hand?”

Royce hesitated.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

“Why did you fire six bullets into the floor?”

“Struggle.”

“Why use a rope after a loaded revolver?”

“I was drunk.”

“Why use a knife after the rope?”

“I was drunk, I tell you.”

Gilder folded his arms.

“What had you drunk?”

Royce pointed to the whisky.

“That.”

Father Brown, who had been listening with his head on one side, now walked over to the bottle. He peered at the level of the spirit. Then, to Merton’s amazement, he dropped on his hands and knees.

In that position he travelled slowly across the attic carpet, presenting the appearance of a quadruped furnished by mistake with a very comic human head. He inspected the bullet holes, crawled under the table and recovered two flattened bullets. He sniffed the carpet, examined the legs of the chair and finally stared for some time at a faint crescent-shaped stain near the wall.

Royce watched him with growing irritation.

“What are you doing?”

“Trying to believe you,” said Father Brown.

“Well?”

“I can’t.”

The secretary’s shoulders lifted.

“You call me a liar?”

“In this matter, yes. I’m awfully sorry.”

Gilder looked from one man to the other.

“You have a reason?”

“Several silly little reasons. They may add up to one sensible one.”

He rose with difficulty and dusted his knees.

“Mr Royce says he was drunk on whisky from that bottle. But the bottle is half full.”

“What of it?” said Royce.

“A great deal. A man who drinks enough to forget a murder does not commonly remember to leave half the drink for breakfast. Especially when he is quarrelling and desperate and has the whole night before him.”

“I’d had drink before.”

“Where?”

Royce was silent.

Father Brown picked up the revolver.

“And then there is this. Six shots, all into the floor. An angry man can miss another man once, or twice, or even six times, especially in books. But he does not miss downward every time. These shots were not fired at Sir Aaron.”

“We struggled for it.”

“Then the holes would wander. They do not. They are close together. Somebody pointed the pistol at the floor and emptied it deliberately.”

Royce stared at him.

The priest touched the rope.

“And this was not used to strangle anybody. There are no fibres or marks about the dead man’s neck. It ended around his leg. That is an odd place for a murderer to put a noose, but not an odd place for a man to put one if he were trying to hold somebody back.”

“To hold him back from what?” asked Merton.

Father Brown looked at the broken window.

“From that.”

A train came by with a sudden iron roar. In the attic the bottle trembled, the screen rattled, and the loose rope writhed faintly on the boards. The grey daylight beyond the window seemed to open and close like an enormous indifferent eye.

When silence returned, Gilder said, “You mean Royce tried to save him?”

“Yes.”

“From whom?”

Father Brown’s round face was grave.

“From himself.”

Alice gripped the door-frame.

“No,” she said.

It was not loud, but the denial seemed to strike the room harder than Royce’s confession.

Father Brown did not look at her.

“Perhaps we should go downstairs.”

“No,” said Royce. “Leave it.”

“That is what you hoped to do,” replied the priest. “But truth left alone does not die. It goes bad.”

Royce advanced on him.

“You don’t know anything about it.”

“I know you did not kill Sir Aaron.”

“You’ve only guessed.”

“I know you tried to prevent his death. I know you put the bullets into the floor. I know you used the rope to restrain him. I think you knocked the knife from his hand. And I know you have invented a bad confession because you are protecting Miss Armstrong.”

Alice’s eyes moved from Father Brown to Royce.

“Protecting me from what?”

Royce said nothing.

She repeated the question. “From what?”

The priest sighed.

“Inspector, may we examine Sir Aaron’s bedroom again?”

They descended, leaving the attic with its stage properties of violence. Magnus remained under guard in the passage, black and motionless. As Father Brown passed him, he said, “You were right about the facts.”

“I generally restrict myself to them,” replied Magnus.

“That is why you were wrong about the event.”

In Sir Aaron’s bedroom the priest went first to the dressing-table. He raised Alice’s photograph and looked beneath it. There lay a little heap of grey powder and a torn label from a chemist’s packet.

Gilder bent closer.

“Poison?”

“Possibly. We shall ask the chemist. But I don’t think he used it.”

He opened a drawer. Inside were newspaper cuttings about Sir Aaron’s speeches, each one praising his invincible spirits, his inexhaustible vitality and his gospel of joy. Beneath them lay a doctor’s prescription for insomnia, dated six months earlier. There were three more behind it.

Merton frowned.

“That proves he couldn’t sleep.”

“It proves at least that he did not always tell his daughter the truth about sleeping.”

Father Brown crossed to the wardrobe. Behind an array of bright waistcoats he found two empty whisky bottles and a third with a little amber liquid in the bottom.

Alice stared at them as if they had been snakes.

“My father never drank.”

“Your father preached that men should never drink,” said Father Brown. “Those statements may be opposites.”

“But he hated alcohol.”

“People often hate the sins that have learned their names.”

“He had been a teetotaller for twenty years.”

“Then perhaps,” said the priest mildly, “he took to wine in order to sadden himself.”

Merton almost laughed, then saw Alice’s face and stopped.

Father Brown continued: “It sounds flippant, but it is not. Sir Aaron had made happiness into a duty and despair into a disgrace. A man may confess anger, greed or even drunkenness; but when he has sold cheerfulness to the public, he cannot confess sorrow without appearing a fraud. So sorrow becomes secret. Secret sorrow becomes shame. And shame, shut up long enough, begins to whisper dreadful remedies.”

Alice sat on the edge of the bed.

“I would have listened to him.”

“Perhaps. But would he have spoken? He had spent half his life telling everybody that the world was improving and the other half telling himself that he was proof of it. He could not step down from his own platform.”

Gilder took up one of the hidden bottles.

“You think he relapsed into drinking and became suicidal?”

“I think he was suicidal before the drinking, and drank because even whisky seemed more honest than his speeches. Cheerfulness without humour can be a very cruel thing. Humour allows a man to say the world is absurd and himself absurd with it. Sir Aaron’s cheerfulness allowed no cracks. It was a painted ceiling where there ought to have been a sky.”

“He was good,” said Alice suddenly. “He helped thousands.”

“Yes,” said Father Brown. “That may have made it worse.”

She looked up angrily.

“Worse?”

“A man can be crushed beneath his virtues as truly as beneath his vices. He gave away money, opened homes, saved drunkards and made crowds applaud hope. Each good deed added another witness against his private misery. He must have thought: ‘If I, who teach happiness, am unhappy, then I am not only suffering—I am a lie.’”

Royce stood by the window with his back to them.

Father Brown addressed him.

“You came here late. You had arranged to meet Miss Armstrong, perhaps?”

Royce did not answer.

Alice did.

“Yes.”

“My father had forbidden him the house. We meant to leave together.”

“And Sir Aaron discovered it?”

“Yes.”

Royce turned around.

“That has nothing to do with his death.”

“It has everything to do with why you were here,” said Gilder.

Father Brown shook his head.

“No, Inspector. It has everything to do with why Mr Royce could be blamed.”

Royce’s jaw tightened.

The priest went on gently: “You came in secretly. You found Sir Aaron in the attic. He had taken the whisky there because it was the furthest room from the servants. He had a knife, a revolver, poison, and perhaps the rope.”

“The rope was already there,” Royce muttered.

“Was it? Hanging from a beam?”

Royce looked sharply at him.

Father Brown nodded as if this completed some small calculation.

“A noose from the beam, then. Knife, pistol, poison, rope and window. That is what troubled me. There are too many weapons and only one death.”

Gilder’s eyes narrowed.

“Explain.”

“A murderer generally brings one means of murder, or seizes another in confusion. A suicide in frenzy may gather every dreadful object he can find because he does not yet know which form his fear will take. The abundance is not evidence of many attacks. It is evidence of one mind running from death to death.”

The room had grown very still.

“Sir Aaron first thought of poison,” Father Brown continued. “Perhaps he tasted it, perhaps not. Then the knife. Then the revolver. Then the rope. Finally the window. Mr Royce entered while this dreadful parliament was still debating.”

Royce’s hands had begun to tremble.

“You cannot prove it.”

“No. You can.”

The secretary said nothing.

Father Brown spoke as one might speak outside a locked door.

“You saw him with the knife. You struck it away. The blood on it may be from his hand or yours. He seized the revolver. You got control of it and fired every cartridge into the carpet, making it useless. He tried the rope. You pulled it down from the beam and looped it around his leg, not neatly, because he was stronger than you expected. You were not attacking him. None of these things were weapons in your hands. They were instruments of a curious mercy.”

Royce covered his face.

Alice rose slowly.

“Patrick,” she said.

He lowered his hands. There was no defiance left in him, only exhaustion.

“Yes.”

The word seemed to come from a great distance.

“You were trying to save him?”

“Yes.”

“And I—”

Royce crossed the room.

“No. You did nothing.”

Father Brown looked unhappy.

“That won’t do.”

The secretary swung toward him with sudden violence.

“Be quiet!”

“It won’t do,” repeated the priest. “Not because it is generous, but because it is false. Falsehood is a poor bandage. It sticks to the wound and poisons it.”

“You’d rather destroy her?”

“I would rather let her grieve for what happened than go mad over what she imagines.”

Alice stood between them.

“Tell me.”

Royce’s face twisted.

“You came into the attic,” he said. “Your father and I were struggling by the window. He was shouting that I was murdering him. Perhaps he believed it then. He was not himself. The rope was tight around his leg, and I had the other end around the table. It was the only way I could hold him.”

Her eyes widened.

“I saw the rope.”

“Yes.”

“I thought—you were pulling him out.”

Royce closed his eyes.

“You took the knife from the floor.”

“I cut it.”

“Yes.”

A train began somewhere far away. Its approach grew under the silence, wheel upon wheel, like an immense decision that could not be recalled.

Alice whispered, “And then?”

“The rope gave way,” said Royce. “He lunged toward the window. I caught his sleeve, but the glass broke. He went through. You saw him fall. Then you fainted.”

The train struck the embankment above the house with a crash of sound. The windows shook; the hidden bottles clinked in the wardrobe; Alice stood motionless while the mechanical thunder passed over them and vanished into the south.

When it was gone, she said, “I killed him.”

“No,” said Father Brown.

“I cut the rope.”

“Yes.”

“Then I killed him.”

“No. You made a mistake while trying to save your father from what you thought was murder. That is not murder. It is not even sin in the sense you mean. It is a blunder, and a terrible one. But the distinction between sins and blunders is not a trick of lawyers. Heaven itself insists upon it.”

“He would be alive if I had not come.”

“Perhaps. Or perhaps Mr Royce would have lost his grip a second later. Or Sir Aaron would have found another weapon tomorrow. We are responsible for our wills, not for every shadow cast by our hands.”

She sank back onto the bed.

Royce knelt before her, but did not touch her.

“I thought if I confessed,” he said, “you would never know.”

“You would hang.”

“Perhaps.”

“And you thought that would help me?”

“I thought you could hate me. Hatred is easier.”

Father Brown shook his head.

“Nothing founded on a lie is easier for long. She would have remembered fragments. A flash, a rope, her father crying out. She would have wondered whether your confession protected her, whether she had stabbed him, whether you had murdered him, whether both were true. Imagined guilt breeds in the dark. It grows heads.”

Gilder stood by the door, his professional severity visibly embarrassed by human sorrow.

“There will have to be an inquiry,” he said. “But if the facts are as described, there is no charge of murder.”

Royce looked at him.

“I gave a false confession.”

“People have done worse things from worse motives,” replied the inspector dryly. “I may call it obstruction if I feel neglected.”

Merton had gone to the window. In the garden below, men were carrying Sir Aaron’s body toward the house. The scarlet dressing-gown was covered now, and the sheet over it had become very small against the huge green bank.

“What about the wound on the knife?” he asked.

Royce held out his left hand. There was a deep cut across the palm, partly dried and badly wrapped.

“He caught the blade,” said Alice.

“No,” Royce answered. “I caught it when I knocked it away.”

“And the blood on her dress?” asked Gilder.

“She knelt beside me when I fell. Before she cut the rope.”

“So Magnus saw the truth,” said Merton, “and misunderstood all of it.”

“Most of us do,” said Father Brown. “Facts are innocent things until people put motives into them.”

They returned to the attic once more. In daylight its horrors had become oddly shabby. The pistol was merely an empty mechanism; the knife, a dirty piece of kitchen steel; the rope, a length of common hemp. Even the broken window looked less like the mouth of death than the neglected repair of a poor landlord.

Father Brown inspected the beam. A rubbed mark showed where the rope had passed over it. On the table lay a folded sheet of paper that no one had noticed beneath the fallen chair. It was covered on one side with the beginnings of letters.

My dear Alice—

My fellow workers—

To the public—

Each opening had been crossed out. On the other side, in a large unsteady hand, Sir Aaron had written only:

I cannot keep it up.

Alice read the sentence and laid the paper down.

For the first time she wept.

There are tears that break a heart and tears that prevent it from breaking. These were of the latter sort. Sir Aaron’s smiles had filled every room of the house and allowed no one else an honest emotion; but now, with his last confession lying among the ridiculous machinery of death, his daughter was permitted to mourn him as a man rather than worship him as a sunrise.

Magnus waited on the landing.

When told the conclusion, he listened without movement.

“I accused Miss Armstrong incorrectly,” he said.

“Yes,” replied Gilder.

“I shall apologise.”

“That may alarm her more than the accusation,” said Merton.

Magnus considered this.

“Then I shall apologise briefly.”

He removed one black glove and looked at his pale hand, as if verifying that he possessed one. Then he went downstairs.

The inquiry that followed was neither wholly private nor wholly public, as such matters seldom are. Sir Aaron’s death was reported as occurring during a period of mental disturbance. The temperance societies printed solemn tributes that omitted the hidden bottles. The newspapers praised his charitable labours and used the word “tragic” so often that it became almost cheerful. Inspector Gilder preserved the facts without advertising their cruelties. Magnus delivered a five-word apology to Alice and resumed his duties until the household was dispersed.

Patrick Royce was not charged. For a time he avoided Alice, believing avoidance another form of sacrifice; but Father Brown, who had little patience with sacrifices invented by the sacrificer, put an end to that. Whether they married is no proper conclusion to a story about death, though in fact they did, much later and without brass bands.

As for Alice, she suffered. Truth is not a narcotic, and Father Brown had never pretended that it was. She dreamed for months of the rope parting in her hand and of the red figure crashing through the window into eternity. Yet the dream remained what it was: the memory of a dreadful mistake committed in love and fear. It did not become a secret tribunal in which she was alternately murderer, accomplice and monster. She learned, slowly, that grief may be carried openly, while concealed guilt multiplies in locked rooms.

Late that afternoon Father Brown remembered the Deaf School.

Merton found him at the station, sitting beneath a poster that advertised a seaside resort as the home of perpetual sunshine. The little priest was eating a currant bun and studying the timetable with an expression of profound defeat.

“You solved the whole thing,” said Merton, dropping onto the bench beside him.

“I was late for the children.”

“They’ll forgive you.”

“They are rehearsing a play. Children rehearsing a play do not forgive lateness. They have too much theology.”

Merton laughed, then grew serious.

“There’s one thing I don’t understand. How did you first know Armstrong was the suicidal one? Before the bottles and the letter?”

Father Brown brushed a crumb from his coat.

“I didn’t know. I suspected because everybody was explaining the objects and nobody was explaining the man.”

“His cheerfulness?”

“Partly. He was not cheerful in the ordinary sense. Ordinary cheerful people are sometimes sad, and know it. Sir Aaron had made a profession of never being sad. That is like making a profession of never sleeping. Nature collects the debt.”

“But surely optimism is good.”

“Hope is good,” said Father Brown. “Optimism can be merely hope ordered about by a policeman. Hope says suffering has meaning. Optimism says there must be no suffering, or at least none in respectable company. Sir Aaron did much good, but he had no room in his scheme for his own soul when it became dark.”

Merton looked down the rails. Far away, the signals shone red and green through the dusk.

“And Royce’s confession?”

“Much too murderous to be murder. He put in everything—the drink, the pistol, the rope, the knife. A true criminal generally subtracts. A false criminal adds. He was building a monster large enough to stand between Alice and the truth.”

“You still thought she ought to know.”

“Yes.”

“Even though she cut the rope.”

“Especially because she cut it. If we had hidden that, she would have spent her life hearing the cut in every silence. A known tragedy has boundaries. An unknown one has none.”

The train entered the station with a gust of smoke and noise. Father Brown rose, nearly dropped his umbrella, rescued the remainder of his bun and climbed into a third-class carriage.

Merton stood on the platform.

“Father!”

The priest leaned out.

“What?”

“What would you have told Sir Aaron, if you’d reached him in time?”

Father Brown considered. Behind his spectacles his eyes looked very old and very kind.

“I might have told him,” he said, “that God does not require us to be cheerful. Only to be honest enough to ask for help.”

The whistle blew. The train moved.

A minute later it was carrying him back toward the Deaf School, where a dozen impatient children waited to perform a comedy they could not hear. Behind him, in the tall narrow house by the embankment, the electric lights came on one by one, pale and ordinary in the gathering dark.

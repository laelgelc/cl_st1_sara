### THE TESTIMONY OF MR. VANKIRK

That the phenomena denominated *mesmeric* are genuine, I no longer regard as a proposition requiring proof. One may dispute the nomenclature; one may reject the fluid of Mesmer, the metallic tractors of Perkins, the universal sympathy of certain German physiologists, and even that supposed polarity of the nerves by which the disciples of Dupotet explain what they cannot otherwise classify; but he who, after having witnessed the characteristic sleep, the rigidity, the insensibility, the exaltation of particular faculties, and—above all—the singular dependence of the sleeper upon the volition of the operator, persists in denying the phenomena themselves, does not reason: he merely refuses cognizance.

There is a scepticism which safeguards truth, and another which excludes it. The first interrogates; the second shuts its eyes. Against the latter, argument is supererogation.

I make these preliminary observations because the occurrence I am about to record will seem incredible even to many who concede the ordinary facts of mesmerism. I shall state nothing of which I was not a witness; and, where my memory might have failed, I have followed notes made upon the spot—notes whose abruptness, repetitions, and occasional obscurity are, in themselves, assurances that they were not subsequently adapted to a theory. I have suppressed the names of two physicians, both living, whose professional reputations might suffer through association with a narrative which they privately acknowledge but publicly decline to authenticate. The name of the principal person I retain, because he has neither reputation nor safety to lose.

Mr. Elias Vankirk was, at the period of his death, fifty-three years of age. His temperament was nervo-sanguine; his intellect acute rather than comprehensive; his habits temperate; his imagination little cultivated; and his opinions, upon all theological subjects, of the most unqualified scepticism. He did not deny God with the petulance of the schoolboy, nor immortality with the ostentation of the materialist. He simply maintained that neither term conveyed to him an intelligible idea.

For approximately four years he suffered from an organic affection of the aorta, complicated, during its latter stages, by an aneurismal enlargement which produced paroxysms of suffocation and anguish. The nature of his disease had been concealed from him, although I have reason to believe that he understood it from the beginning. Laudanum gave but imperfect relief, and, as the malady advanced, became dangerous from the quantity required. At his own solicitation I attempted mesmerism.

The result was remarkable. I could not arrest the progress of the disease; yet I was repeatedly able, by inducing the mesmeric trance, to suspend the pain for periods varying from twenty minutes to eleven hours. The sleep produced was unlike natural slumber. The pulse, though weak, remained regular; the respiration became slow; the eyelids were not wholly closed; and the eyeballs, turned slightly upward, exhibited beneath the lashes a narrow crescent of lustreless white. During the deeper states he answered questions readily, but always in a voice which seemed less to issue from the chest than to vibrate, as it were, in the air immediately before his lips.

One additional peculiarity must be mentioned. In ordinary consciousness Vankirk was cautious, argumentative, and frequently indecisive. In the mesmeric state he displayed a confidence amounting almost to infallibility. He distinguished, or professed to distinguish, between knowledge obtained by ratiocination and knowledge directly perceived. Of the first he spoke contemptuously; of the second with a solemnity which, however unreasonable, never failed to impress me.

“It is not that I think more clearly while asleep,” he said to me after one experiment; “it is that I do not think at all. I see.”

I asked him what he saw.

“Relations,” he replied, after some hesitation. “In waking life we behold objects, and infer the threads which bind them. In the sleep-waking condition the objects disappear, but the threads become luminous.”

At the time I ascribed this language to cerebral excitement. I record it now because of what followed.

On the fifteenth of November, at nine o’clock in the evening, I received from Mr. Vankirk a note, written in pencil by his attendant. It contained only these words:

*Come before midnight. I shall need sleep, but not for pain. Bring paper. Ask me what you have been afraid to ask.*

I reached his residence at twenty-three minutes before ten. The night was windless and severe. A fine, dry snow had fallen during the afternoon, and the lamps along the avenue appeared buried in pale aureoles. In his chamber the fire had sunk low, by his express command, although the temperature was scarcely tolerable. A shaded lamp stood upon a small table beside the bed. Near it were a watch, a glass of water, a vial of morphia, and several sheets of foolscap.

Vankirk was propped by pillows. His countenance had undergone, since the preceding morning, a frightful alteration. The temples were hollow; the skin about the mouth had assumed a leaden translucency; and each inspiration elevated the whole framework of the chest with a laborious, mechanical movement. Yet his eyes were intelligent, and even cheerful.

“You have come,” he said. “The physicians have also come—and departed. They believe I shall not see sunrise.”

“You should not speak.”

“I have spoken little all my life, because I possessed little certainty. To-night I may possess much.”

I felt his pulse. It was rapid, thread-like, and occasionally absent beneath the finger.

“You wish to be relieved?”

“Not merely relieved. Mesmerize me deeply. Then question me.”

“Upon what subject?”

“Upon death.”

I endeavoured to dissuade him. The operation, although previously beneficial, might, in his extreme debility, accelerate the catastrophe. At this he smiled.

“You reason as though the catastrophe were distant enough to be accelerated. It is here. I hear it at the door. The only question is whether I may look through the keyhole before it enters.”

His manner admitted no further objection. At four minutes before ten I commenced the passes. The first effect was a tremor of the eyelids; next, the muscles of the jaw relaxed; finally, the difficult breathing became comparatively tranquil. At nine minutes after ten he was in the ordinary mesmeric condition. I continued the process, directing my whole volition toward a profounder sleep. At twenty-two minutes after ten his right hand, which had lain open upon the coverlet, closed slowly, although I had not commanded the movement. His face lost the aspect of illness. It did not regain health; it became rather the image of some passionless intelligence to which health and disease were equally irrelevant.

I now began my interrogation. The transcript follows. I designate myself by N., and the patient by V.

**N.** Are you asleep?

**V.** [After a long pause.] Yes—if separation from the bodily senses is sleep.

**N.** Do you suffer?

**V.** The body suffers remotely. Its pain reaches me as thunder reaches one who beholds the storm beneath him.

**N.** Can you perceive the condition of that body?

**V.** Entirely.

**N.** Will it recover?

**V.** No.

**N.** When will death occur?

**V.** It has commenced.

I confess that this response disturbed me. I looked narrowly at the pulse and respiration. Both continued.

**N.** You mean that the disease is fatal?

**V.** I mean that death is not an instant, but a progression. The organic bonds are loosening. Before the clock completes another circuit, your senses will call me dead.

**N.** And yours?

**V.** Mine will call me enlarged.

**N.** You have long doubted the immortality of the soul. Do you doubt it now?

**V.** There is no soul in the sense in which I formerly denied it.

**N.** Explain.

**V.** The word has been made to signify a substance without extension, position, divisibility, or material attribute. Such a substance is no substance; it is the negation of substance clothed in a name. Yet the error of the definition does not prove the mortality of man. We survive—not because we are immaterial, but because what is essential in us belongs to a materiality more ultimate than the organs.

**N.** You speak of a finer matter?

**V.** Fineness is comparative, and therefore insufficient. Consider iron; reduce it to particles. Reduce these, in conception, until iron becomes vapour. Pursue attenuation through gas, through the electrical medium, through the luminiferous ether—if ether be indeed the last garment perceptible to science. At every stage you have particles, however minute; each possesses relation to each; each may be divided in thought, because each occupies space. But beyond all particulate constitution lies that which cannot be divided, not through minuteness, but through absolute continuity. This is *unparticled matter*.

**N.** Is not the phrase contradictory? Matter implies parts.

**V.** Matter, as your chemistry employs the term, implies parts. Existence does not. You have mistaken an idiosyncrasy of gross substance for a necessity of Being.

**N.** What, then, is unparticled matter?

**V.** God.

The word, uttered without emphasis, produced in me an emotion disproportionate to its sound. I had anticipated some theological affirmation, but not this abrupt identification.

**N.** You define God as matter?

**V.** I define matter as that which exists. The gross atom, the nerve, the sun, the invisible medium through which suns communicate their light, and the indivisible continuity beneath all media—these are not opposed substances, but successive conditions. The final condition, without interval, boundary, or atomic constitution, is God.

**N.** Then God is unconscious extension?

**V.** Extension is a relation among limits. The Limitless is not extended, though all extension occurs within it. Nor is God unconscious. Motion in unparticled matter is thought.

**N.** Motion ordinarily requires a part moving in relation to another part.

**V.** Ordinarily—because ordinary motion is translation. Divine motion is not passage from place to place, there being no exterior place. It is modification without separation; impulse without transit; universal vibration in which no atom travels, because atoms are subsequent results of the vibration.

**N.** I do not comprehend.

**V.** You cannot comprehend by images borrowed from bodies. Yet an analogy may assist you. A man thinks, and the thought produces movement in his brain; the brain acts upon nerve; nerve upon muscle; muscle upon matter. You regard the thought as cause and the movement as effect because you perceive them successively. But suppose a medium in which willing and alteration are one event—then thought would be motion and motion creation.

**N.** Creation of what?

**V.** Of particled matter—of the innumerable temporary centres which you call worlds and beings.

**N.** Was the universe made from God?

**V.** The word *from* suggests subtraction. Nothing can be taken from omniprevalence. The universe is God thinking in forms.

**N.** Are individual minds, then, portions of the divine mind?

**V.** Portions, if the word be purged of spatial division. They are determinations—foci—knots of particular motion in the universal consciousness. Imagine a quiet ocean: winds produce waves. Each wave has contour, duration, and a movement apparently its own; yet it is not other than ocean. So the creature is a modulation of God.

**N.** If all thought is divine thought, are our errors God’s errors?

**V.** Error belongs to limitation. The universal consciousness sees all relations simultaneously; the embodied consciousness receives them successively, through organs. A narrow aperture does not corrupt the sun, although it admits only a pencil of his light.

**N.** Why should God impose this limitation?

**V.** To create individuality.

**N.** Why create individuality?

**V.** That the universal consciousness may possess experience—not merely as totality, but as innumerable centres of contrast.

Here the patient remained silent for nearly two minutes. A slight moisture appeared upon his forehead. I wiped it away, and was startled by the coldness of the skin; nevertheless, the carotid still beat, and the lips resumed their motion.

**N.** What is the purpose of embodiment?

**V.** Preparation for ultimate life.

**N.** Define ultimate life.

**V.** Organic life is rudimental. Ultimate life begins when organization has fulfilled its office and is cast aside.

**N.** We ordinarily call that death.

**V.** Because you name transformations from the discarded husk. The worm might call the butterfly’s emergence annihilation, having no conception of wings.

**N.** But the butterfly remains organized.

**V.** The analogy illustrates change, not condition. Every organ is at once an instrument and a cage. The retina gives sight, but restricts sight to those vibrations which can affect it. The ear gives sound, but denies all undulations too vast, too rapid, or too delicate for its mechanism. The optic nerve carries the star to the mind, yet it also excludes all in the star which is not light. Thus each sense confers one narrow privilege by enforcing a million incapacities.

**N.** And after death?

**V.** Perception becomes immediate. The ultimate being requires no eye, because no membrane stands between it and luminosity; no ear, because it apprehends vibration itself; no locomotion, because distance is a condition of the organized frame.

**N.** Are you saying that the dead are omniscient?

**V.** No. You confuse expanded faculty with infinitude. The ultimate creature is incomparably enlarged, yet remains a creature. God alone is entire relation. We, after organic death, enter wider cognizance, as a prisoner, released from a single chamber, may traverse a kingdom without therefore embracing the world.

**N.** Does personal identity continue?

**V.** Necessarily.

**N.** Why necessarily?

**V.** Because the experiences accumulated under limitation determine the mode of subsequent perception. If the determination vanished, embodiment would have accomplished nothing. The wave alters the shore and is itself altered by resistance; though it subsides as contour, its motion persists in the sea.

**N.** Memory, then, survives?

**V.** Not memory as a sequence of recalled pictures. The past is incorporated into faculty. A child remembers the pain of flame; a man no longer needs the image of the burn—knowledge has entered his instinct. Ultimate memory is identity perfected by all that has been endured.

**N.** You say *endured*. Is suffering essential?

**V.** Yes.

**N.** That conclusion appears irreconcilable with divine goodness.

**V.** Only because you imagine pleasure as a positive substance, capable of existing without relation. Pleasure is the perception of release, fulfilment, harmony, expansion. Without previous confinement, expansion cannot be known; without want, fulfilment is an empty term; without discord, harmony is indistinguishable from silence. Earthly suffering excavates the capacity into which heavenly joy may enter.

**N.** Then the most afflicted will be the most blessed?

**V.** Not by mere quantity of pain. Pain that enlarges apprehension prepares delight; pain that contracts the mind must first be understood, and understanding may itself be painful. Nothing is wasted. Even terror becomes, in ultimate life, an organ of sublimity.

**N.** You have said that organs are discarded. How can terror become an organ?

**V.** I employ your language figuratively. In ultimate life, qualities perform the office which organs perform here. Courage perceives what cowardice cannot; compassion enters beings inaccessible to indifference; wonder opens immensities before which the self-satisfied remains blind.

**N.** Is this heaven?

**V.** It is the commencement of heaven.

**N.** And hell?

At this question the fingers of his left hand moved convulsively. His brow contracted, not with bodily anguish, but with an expression of intense intellectual effort.

**V.** Hell is perception without fitness.

**N.** Explain.

**V.** To enter enlargement while clinging to limitation; to receive direct cognizance while desiring concealment; to feel every consequence of one’s volition, not as an inference, but as an atmosphere—this is punishment. The cruel man, while embodied, sees the wound he makes and may look away. Disembodied, he enters the wound. The false man becomes present in the disorder of every mind he has deceived. No external judge is required. Perfect perception is judgment.

**N.** Is punishment eternal?

**V.** Nothing conditioned is eternal. But duration there does not resemble time. A moral contradiction may be experienced with an intensity for which a sidereal revolution would provide no adequate measure; yet, when contradiction is resolved, what you call ages may appear a single pang.

**N.** Do all beings finally return to God?

**V.** They have never departed from God. But they may cease to resist their own divine constitution.

**N.** Do they lose individuality in doing so?

**V.** They lose isolation, not individuality. A voice does not cease to be distinct because it enters harmony. Indeed, only in harmony is its proper relation made complete.

I paused here, partly to arrange my notes and partly because the rapidity with which he had spoken seemed dangerous. The lamp flame had diminished, and I turned the wick. As the light strengthened, I perceived that Vankirk’s face no longer cast upon the pillow the shadow which, considering the lamp’s position, should naturally have appeared beneath the chin. I attributed the circumstance to some fold in the linen, and altered my own position. The shadow remained strangely faint.

**N.** Are there ultimate beings now around us?

**V.** Innumerable.

**N.** Can you perceive them?

**V.** Imperfectly—as one hears voices through a wall, knowing neither the speakers nor the chamber.

**N.** Why do we not perceive them while awake?

**V.** Because your organs defend you. You call the eye an avenue; it is also a barricade. Were the whole ocean of vibration admitted to the brain, organization would perish beneath the inundation. The senses select those few impressions compatible with rudimental existence.

**N.** Does mesmerism weaken the barricade?

**V.** It partially suspends the organic will. In common waking, the body tyrannizes over the mind by incessant messages—hunger, temperature, gravity, sound, light, position. In mesmeric sleep these importunities diminish, and the deeper faculty, ordinarily drowned by them, attains a momentary cognizance.

**N.** Is that faculty the soul?

**V.** It is the nascent ultimate body.

**N.** A body? Yet you have described existence after death as unorganized.

**V.** Unorganized does not mean formless. Organization is the arrangement of dependent parts for a special function. The ultimate body has neither members nor viscera, neither centre nor circumference in your anatomical sense; but it has a determinate mode, produced by the whole history of its earthly embodiment.

**N.** Is it material?

**V.** All that exists is material, but not all matter is atomic. Your fear of materialism proceeds from associating matter with corruption, weight, and insensibility. Yet corruption is rearrangement; weight is relation to attraction; insensibility belongs not to matter, but to certain modes of matter. The substance of God is the most material because it is the most actual.

**N.** Is God, then, a person?

**V.** More than person—not less. Personality in man is consciousness fenced within one perspective. In God all perspectives coexist. To deny Him personality because He exceeds the human person would be to deny the sea fluidity because it exceeds the drop.

**N.** Can prayer affect God?

**V.** Prayer affects the relation between the petitioner and the Whole. You cannot add information to omniscience, nor alter perfect purpose; but you may alter yourself, and thus alter the point at which perfect purpose enters you. The window does not persuade the sun to shine; it opens.

**N.** Are miracles possible?

**V.** Every event is miraculous when perceived without the anesthesia of habit. What you specifically call miracle is either an unknown law or a direct adjustment too comprehensive for the chain of secondary causation to be visible. But God does not violate law. Law is the remembered uniformity of divine volition.

**N.** Are there inhabited worlds beyond our own?

**V.** All worlds are inhabited in proportion to their capacity for relation.

**N.** Even the suns?

**V.** You suppose life dependent upon the narrow temperature compatible with terrestrial albumen. This is the arrogance of the molecule. Beings exist in flame to whom your oceans would be stone; beings inhabit densities through which iron would fall as through mist; beings whose slowest sensation occupies centuries of your chronology; beings to whom the revolution of a solar system is but the tremor of a nerve.

**N.** Have they, too, organic and ultimate states?

**V.** Whatever possesses limitation must undergo enlargement. But do not imagine the same ladder everywhere. On one sphere, rock may be rudimental consciousness and mobility its heaven; on another, light may sleep until liberated into thought. Creation is not an army marching in a single file, but an immeasurable efflorescence.

**N.** Can ultimate beings traverse space?

**V.** They do not traverse it as bodies do. Locomotion is the successive occupation of positions. The ultimate being modifies its relation, and relation determines presence.

**N.** Could such a being reach the farthest star instantaneously?

**V.** The farthest star is far only to organs. To the naked eye it is a point; to the telescope, a world; to mathematical ratiocination, a distance; to ultimate perception, a relation immediately apprehended. Yet beyond your stars lies not emptiness but an amplitude in which whole firmaments diminish to star-shadows and are swallowed. What you name the universe is perhaps no more than a luminous mote drifting in one chamber of the divine thought.

**N.** And beyond that chamber?

**V.** There is no beyond to God.

He ceased. The watch indicated fourteen minutes after eleven. I observed that the respiration, though still perceptible, had become extraordinarily infrequent—one inspiration in perhaps forty seconds. I placed a mirror before his mouth. A slight cloud formed upon it.

**N.** You predicted death before the hour completes its circuit. Do you still perceive its approach?

**V.** [Very faintly.] I perceive no approach.

**N.** Has the danger passed?

**V.** The distance has passed.

**N.** What do you mean?

No answer followed. I renewed several transverse passes, apprehending that the sleep had become too profound. His lips moved.

**V.** The bonds are nearly transparent.

**N.** Vankirk, attend to me. Are you still connected with the body?

**V.** By pain.

**N.** Where is the pain?

**V.** Everywhere that I am not.

This reply, although obscure, impressed me with a horror which I cannot justify by any logical interpretation. I leaned nearer. The eyes were now fully visible between the parted lids, yet the pupils had rolled so high that only the dull sclera appeared. The expression of the mouth, however, was one of solemn happiness.

**N.** Tell me, if you can, what death feels like.

**V.** Like being forced through oneself. The narrow life resists the wider. Every habit becomes a membrane; every fear, a hook; every love of what must be relinquished, a filament that tears. Birth is painful because a body is entered. Death is painful because a universe enters.

**N.** And afterward?

**V.** [A whisper scarcely audible.] No afterward. Succession opens into presence.

**N.** What do you perceive?

There was a pause of such duration that I believed him incapable of reply. Then there issued from his lips a voice altered beyond all resemblance to his own. It was not louder; rather, it seemed to possess no distance from my ear.

**V.** I perceive the room as an obstruction in brightness. I perceive your body as a dark mechanism, full of sparks. I perceive your thought moving before your question. I perceive the dead—

Here the voice failed.

**N.** What of the dead?

**V.** They are not dead.

**N.** Where are they?

**V.** Here is not a place.

**N.** Do they see us?

**V.** They enter what we are.

**N.** Are you among them?

At this he smiled.

It was not the smile of a man comforted, nor of a visionary delighted by his own phantasm. It possessed an awful serenity, as if the question had become meaningless to him.

**N.** Are you living?

**V.** [After an interval.] Ultimate life—

The sentence remained unfinished. A profound change passed over his countenance. The features appeared to recede; the skin tightened upon the bones; and beneath the left eye there emerged a violet stain. I called his name loudly. He did not respond.

Believing the catastrophe imminent, and reproaching myself for having prolonged the experiment, I resolved to awaken him at once. I made the customary upward passes, clapped my hands sharply beside his head, and commanded, with all the concentration of will I could summon:

“Vankirk—awake!”

For several seconds nothing occurred. Then his eyelids descended; a tremor ran from the throat to the feet; and he inhaled with a harsh, tearing sound. The eyes opened naturally and fixed themselves upon mine.

“Have I answered?” he asked.

“You have. But you must be quiet.”

“Was I right?”

“I cannot say.”

Again he smiled—that same expression, though now touched by an indescribable weariness.

“You will know,” he murmured.

His head fell backward upon the pillow.

I seized his wrist. There was no pulse. I placed my hand upon the chest; no movement followed. The mirror remained perfectly clear. At eleven twenty-nine, therefore, I considered him dead.

Yet the final circumstance admits no ordinary explanation. Before I could summon the attendant—before, indeed, a full minute had elapsed—the body became rigid. Not with the gradual contraction familiar to physicians, but instantaneously, universally, as though every fibre had been converted into mineral substance. The jaw resisted pressure; the fingers were fixed; the arm, when raised, remained extended in a position which no recent corpse could preserve. More appalling still, the temperature had fallen with impossible rapidity. His brow, which less than three minutes earlier had been moist beneath my hand, was now cold—not with the comparative coldness of death, but with an absolute and penetrating frigidity, like stone lifted from a winter grave.

The physicians arrived shortly before midnight. They agreed that death had resulted from rupture of the aneurism. Of the rigidity they would say only that unusual cases occur; of the cold, that my agitation had deceived me. Yet one of them, on touching the forehead, recoiled so violently that he overturned the lamp.

I have often reviewed the dialogue, seeking in it some evidence of imposture, delirium, unconscious recollection, or suggestion from my own mind. None satisfies me. Vankirk had read little natural philosophy and less theology. Several of his statements were opposed to opinions I then held and had repeatedly expressed in his presence. It may be said that the failing brain, excited by mesmerism, constructed an elaborate metaphysical dream; and this explanation, though inadequate, is at least pronounceable.

But one question remains.

When I asked whether he still perceived the approach of death, he answered: “I perceive no approach. The distance has passed.” From that moment his speech changed; his pulse, though I believed I detected it, may have been only the throbbing of my own finger; the cloud upon the mirror may have proceeded from my breath; and the rigidity which followed awakening had less the character of a body newly dead than of one upon which death had long imposed its dominion.

Did I awaken Vankirk to kill him?

Or—during those last tremendous minutes, while his lips discoursed of ultimate life, of immediate perception, and of the dead who are not dead—was the thing before me already a corpse, held articulate by my volition, while the intelligence answering through it spoke, not from the mesmeric sleep, but from beyond that final barricade which his own agony had rendered transparent?

I cannot determine. I know only that, when memory returns me to that chamber, I feel again beneath my palm the impossible coldness of his brow; and I seem to feel, beneath that coldness, as beneath a door pressed inward by some immeasurable multitude, the silent and patient pressure of another world.

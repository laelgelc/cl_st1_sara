### THE LEADING LADY’S DAY OFF

Not as you have seen leading ladies on a Thursday afternoon, with one white hand pressed to a heart that has been professionally broken for the nine hundredth time; not with the light caught in the jewels at her throat, and the orchestra creeping tenderly toward G minor; not with her profile turned so that even the people behind the second balcony post could see that she suffered beautifully.

Aimee Fox was crying into a hotel pillow.

She had bitten the pillowcase, too.

That is one of the differences between grief on the stage and grief in Room 36 of the Oakhurst Hotel. Stage grief leaves no tooth marks on the linen. It does not redden the end of the nose, swell the eyelids, or make a woman’s shoulders jerk inside a kimono the color of boiled cabbage. It does not taste of laundry soap.

The room contributed what it could. Its carpet was striped in brown and yellow, as if somebody had trained a regiment of sick caterpillars to march across the floor. The wallpaper showed blue roses tied with bronze ribbons, and the green iron bed crouched against it like a large, offended grasshopper. A washstand held a pitcher with a crack down its side. The pitcher seemed to know something discreditable about Aimee and to be waiting for a chance to tell.

Outside, the July sun pressed a hot palm against the window.

At half past one the manager of the Lyceum had telephoned that the electric plant had suffered a calamity involving a belt, a flywheel, and Mr. Dugan’s thumb. There would be no matinee and no evening performance. The company had received the news with joy.

The men went in groups to find cigars, pool tables, barbers, shady porches, and other men. Mr. Pell, the juvenile, borrowed a bicycle. The comedian discovered somebody who had once known his brother in Terre Haute. The property man went fishing with the hotel clerk. Even old Morley, who played fathers, found a Grand Army veteran and disappeared in a cloud of reminiscence.

Aimee remained in Room 36.

A man alone in a strange town is an adventurer. A woman alone in a strange town is a subject for discussion.

Aimee had walked as far as the drugstore. Three young men watched her buy talcum powder. The druggist called her “little lady.” A woman in a purple bonnet looked twice at her ankles. She returned to the hotel, lay down, and thought of every person who had ever forgotten her.

This is an excellent way of producing tears.

She thought of her mother, who had been dead eleven years; of her sister in California, who wrote cheerful letters about oranges and never invited her to visit; of a dog named Midge; of a husband who had lasted eighteen months; and of a dressing-room woman in Baltimore who had known exactly how to hook a waist without pinching the skin.

She thought, finally, “I want a woman.”

Then she rang the bell.

The bell-boy who answered was fourteen years old, with ears like door handles and an expression of seasoned depravity acquired from carrying ice water after ten o’clock at night.

“Is there,” said Aimee, sitting up and clutching her kimono, “a woman in this hotel?”

The boy considered.

“There’s Mrs. Brackett, the housekeeper, but she’s got neuralgia and bites. There’s Minnie in the kitchen, but she throws things. There’s Pearlie Schultz.”

“Who is Pearlie Schultz?”

“Stenographer. Office off the lobby. She’s fat, but she’s human.”

“Send her up.”

The boy looked doubtful.

“She charges fifteen cents a page.”

“I don’t want a letter. I want—” Aimee stopped. Her face collapsed. “Oh, send her up!”

He went, shutting the door with the care of one who has just been entrusted with dynamite.

Pearlie Schultz arrived seven minutes later.

She was a large young woman in a blue linen skirt and a white waist with a collar that had surrendered early in the day. Her fair hair was rolled into an abundant mound. Her cheeks were pink from climbing stairs, and under one arm she carried a stenographer’s notebook, three sharpened pencils, and a paper sack containing cherries.

Pearlie stood in the doorway and regarded the leading lady. She had heard of Aimee Fox. Our town had seen her picture in the window of the Lyceum, where she appeared to possess a waist no thicker than a cane and eyes large enough to shelter birds.

The person on the bed possessed neither quality at that moment.

“Well,” said Pearlie, “my land!”

“I’m sorry,” Aimee said. “You’re busy.”

“I was typing a letter about two carloads of hog feed. It’ll keep. What’s happened?”

“Nothing.”

“That bad, is it?”

Aimee stared at her. Pearlie put down the notebook and sat in the room’s one rocking chair, which protested in every joint but held.

“Go on,” she said. “You can tell me. I don’t know anybody you know, except Mr. Haskins, and he owes me sixty cents.”

“I’m lonely,” Aimee said.

There it was. No dead child in Act Three, no perfidious lover, no mortgage on the old homestead. Merely loneliness, which is harder to play because it has no costume and enters without a cue.

Pearlie nodded.

“Sure.”

The word did what sympathy spoken in four syllables could not have done. Aimee began crying again.

Pearlie watched for a moment. Then she rose, poured water from the cracked pitcher, dampened a towel, and laid it over Aimee’s eyes.

“You keep on like that and you’ll look as if you’d been stung by bees. Ain’t there any other woman with the show?”

“No. There was, but she left in Scranton. She married the orchestra leader.”

“Good?”

“Clarinet.”

“I mean, was it a good marriage?”

“No marriage with a clarinet is good.”

Pearlie laughed. Aimee lifted the towel and looked at her. After a second she laughed too, though the result was damp and hiccuping.

“That’s better,” Pearlie said. “Have a cherry.”

Within fifteen minutes the sack of cherries lay between them on the bed, the notebook was under the rocking chair, and Pearlie had removed her collar. At twenty minutes, Aimee showed her a new underbodice bought in Chicago. At thirty, Pearlie confessed that she made all her own and knew a pattern that required only three quarters of a yard if you did not insist on foolishness around the neck.

Aimee sat upright.

“Three quarters?”

“Cross my heart. You cut the front on the fold, like this.”

There being no paper large enough, Pearlie demonstrated upon the hotel’s laundry list. Aimee objected to the armhole. Pearlie defended it. Aimee produced a sewing kit, two bits of lace, a packet of snapshots, and a magazine clipping entitled “A New Figure in Ten Minutes Daily.” Soon they had covered the bed with intimate architecture.

You may smile. Men have been restored to an interest in life by less—by the sight of a trout stream, the smell of a cigar, or the statement that a ball club has bought a new pitcher. But when two women who have eaten alone too many times bend over a bit of lawn and argue about whether insertion should be put in straight or gathered, there are agencies at work which ought not to be mocked by anybody who has never spent Sunday in a hotel.

Pearlie learned that Aimee traveled with two trunks and a papier-mâché fireplace. Aimee learned that Pearlie had saved eighty-seven dollars toward a trip to Niagara Falls. Pearlie had once been engaged to a bookkeeper who chewed cloves and objected to her working. Aimee had once been married to a man who objected to her working and did not trouble to chew cloves.

“They always object,” Pearlie said. “Then they object to what it costs to keep you.”

Aimee nodded gravely.

They compared hair. Pearlie’s was thick but without ambition. Aimee’s had ambition but required assistance. Aimee showed her how to push the side curls forward. Pearlie showed Aimee an exercise for reducing the hips, involving a towel and a doorknob.

At four o’clock Pearlie looked at the bureau clock and shouted.

“The social!”

“What social?”

“Strawberry social. Ladies’ Aid. I’m on the coffee and dish committee. I’ve got to be at the church lawn by five, and I ain’t even washed my neck.”

Aimee’s face changed. The room, which had retreated during their talk, came marching back—the wallpaper, the heat, the swollen green bed.

Pearlie saw it.

“Come along.”

“To church?”

“To the lawn. We need help.”

“I couldn’t. They’d know me.”

“Half of ’em wouldn’t know Sarah Bernhardt unless she came with her name printed on a sash. The other half would, and then they’d talk till Christmas.”

Aimee hesitated.

Pearlie’s eyes narrowed with practical thought.

“You can be Ethel Evans.”

“Who is Ethel Evans?”

“You are. You’re visiting me from Milwaukee.”

“I don’t look like an Ethel.”

“You will when you’ve powdered that nose.”

There are invitations which arrive engraved, invitations delivered by footmen, invitations that open the doors of palaces. None ever offered more magnificence than Pearlie Schultz’s: Come wash dishes for the Ladies’ Aid and lie about your name.

Aimee sprang from the bed.

“What does one wear to a strawberry social?”

Pearlie surveyed the open trunks. Her expression became reverent.

“My land,” she whispered. “Is all that yours?”

The next forty minutes were occupied by woman’s ancient ceremony of becoming someone else while remaining entirely herself.

Aimee bathed in water brought up in three pitchers by the bell-boy, who afterward told the clerk that actresses changed skins. She arranged her hair low and soft, hiding the silver at the temples with a touch as practiced as a card player’s. She put on a pink silk slip, a white frock with little stitched panels, white stockings, and slippers absurdly delicate for a church lawn. A coral scarf gave warmth to her throat. Powder subdued the evidence of grief; rouge restored what July and the Oakhurst Hotel had stolen.

When she turned from the mirror, Pearlie gasped.

The leading lady was gone. In her place stood Miss Ethel Evans of Milwaukee—though Milwaukee had never produced her and might reasonably have resented the claim. She looked twenty-eight in the room’s bad light, twenty-five when she smiled, and at least ten years younger than she had looked at three o’clock.

“Now you,” Aimee said.

Pearlie backed away.

“Oh, no. I’m working.”

“Precisely. Sit down.”

Pearlie sat.

Aimee loosened the heavy roll of hair, combed it, puffed it at the sides, and twisted it into a knot that suggested intention instead of surrender. She added a little powder. Then she fastened at Pearlie’s throat a small pink bow made from the end of the coral scarf.

Pearlie stared into the mirror.

“Well, I’ll be switched.”

“You have beautiful eyes.”

“They’re gray.”

“Gray eyes can be beautiful.”

“Nobody ever said so.”

“Then your town is badly managed.”

Our town was not badly managed, exactly. It had six churches, two banks, a flour mill, a weekly newspaper, and a council able to discuss a hitching post for three months. But it did occasionally neglect to inform a woman that she had beautiful eyes.

The church lawn occupied half a block under elms. By five o’clock it wore strings of Japanese lanterns, which in daylight resembled collapsed vegetables but promised romance after dark. Long tables stood beneath white cloths. Strawberries glowed in bowls, cakes leaned under glass covers, and great cans of coffee steamed beside the chapel door.

The Ladies’ Aid moved through it all with the fixed smiles of generals before a battle. Mrs. Judge Willet commanded cake. Mrs. Pringle guarded the money box. Mrs. Deacon Moss arranged carnations and disapproved of their posture.

Pearlie marched Aimee into their midst.

“This is Ethel Evans, visiting me from Milwaukee. She’s dying to help.”

Aimee had not previously been aware of the fatal nature of her desire, but she smiled.

Mrs. Pringle looked at the dress, the slippers, and the coral scarf. Mrs. Moss looked at the lips, the eyes, and the absence of any visible chaperon. Mrs. Willet looked at the rows of empty tables and made the Christian decision.

“Can she serve berries?”

“I can serve anything,” said Aimee.

“Put her at Table Four.”

Now the young men of our town were not irreligious. They attended strawberry socials because they believed in fellowship, charity, and the possibility that the Williams girls might wear their pink dresses. They bought one dish of berries and remained two hours, leaning against trees in attitudes which they considered careless and which made their collars wilt.

The first to discover Ethel Evans was Sid Strang, assistant cashier at the First National Bank. Sid had a narrow waist, a wide collar, and hair so carefully parted that it seemed divided by treaty. He came toward Table Four carrying his hat.

“Good evening.”

“Good evening.”

“I don’t believe I’ve had the pleasure.”

“No,” Aimee said. “I don’t believe you have.”

Sid laughed heartily, though there was no joke and he would have been alarmed if there had been.

“I’m Strang. Sid Strang.”

“Ethel Evans.”

“Visiting?”

“Milwaukee.”

“Fine city.”

“Magnificent.”

Sid had changed trains there once at midnight and felt qualified to agree. He ordered strawberries. Then he ordered a second dish because Ethel Evans had smiled when she handed him the first. A third followed from patriotic confusion.

News traveled.

Within twenty minutes Table Four was crowded. Young Mr. Bell from the hardware store arrived without appetite and ate cake until his eyes became glassy. The dentist’s son volunteered to carry water. Two high-school seniors pooled their money for berries and stood speechless before Aimee, nudging each other like embarrassed calves.

Aimee bloomed.

That is the word, though it sounds like a seed catalogue. Her shoulders lifted. Her eyes sparkled. Her hands, trained to make the back row understand a teacup, gave every saucer distinction. She bent toward shy boys, teased bold ones, remembered names, admired neckties, and told Sid Strang that banking must demand tremendous nerve.

“It does,” Sid said, and straightened.

When the first supply of strawberries ran low, Aimee announced that no gentleman of spirit would permit Table Four to close. The hardware clerk bought six tickets. Sid bought ten. The dentist’s son galloped to the kitchen for more berries and collided with Mrs. Moss, who forgave him when she saw the receipts.

Mrs. Pringle counted money with trembling fingers.

“Pearlie,” she whispered, “your friend is remarkable.”

“She’s had experience,” Pearlie said.

“At socials?”

“With crowds.”

At seven the lanterns were lighted. Dusk softened the church bricks, the dry grass, and the faces of those who had spent the afternoon baking. The elms held pockets of purple shadow. Somebody brought a phonograph to the parsonage porch, and a tenor voice emerged from its horn, thin but romantic.

Aimee stood beneath a red lantern, and the color warmed her white dress. Men gathered around her. Married women watched, remembering or criticizing according to temperament. Girls who had first regarded her with hostility came nearer when she asked where they bought their ribbon collars.

She was not merely acting. Or, if she was, then perhaps most happiness requires a little acting before it believes itself.

Pearlie passed carrying a stack of plates.

“Having an awful time, ain’t you?”

“Dreadful,” Aimee said. “I may never recover.”

“You’ve sold forty-three dishes.”

“Forty-five. Mr. Strang is thinking.”

Sid was indeed thinking. His financial training conquered his digestion.

“Miss Evans,” he said, “if I buy the rest of those berries, could I—would you take a little walk after things close?”

Aimee looked toward Pearlie.

Pearlie’s gray eyes were amused and watchful.

“A short one,” Aimee said.

Sid bought the berries.

The social ended at nine with a profit larger than any previous profit in the memory of Mrs. Pringle, whose memory extended through seventeen socials and one oyster supper. The Ladies’ Aid thanked Ethel Evans. Mrs. Moss invited her to church on Sunday. Mrs. Willet asked how long she would be in town. Three girls kissed Pearlie and said they wished she had told them sooner about her fascinating friend.

Pearlie accepted this sudden popularity as a stout woman accepts a folding chair—with caution.

After the last plates were stacked, Aimee walked to the gate with Sid Strang, the hardware clerk, the dentist’s son, and two others who had attached themselves to the procession. Pearlie followed a few steps behind, carrying the pink bow in her throat and the evening’s secret in her smile.

At the gate Sid cleared his throat.

“There’s a nice road along the river.”

“At this hour?” said the hardware clerk. “It’s dark.”

“That’s why it’s nice,” Sid replied with unexpected honesty.

Aimee laughed. She looked from one eager face to another. Under the lantern light they were young, solemn, hopeful, and very dear. Not because she loved any of them. She did not. But that afternoon she had believed herself old, abandoned, and invisible. Now five young men were prepared to quarrel over the privilege of protecting her from a river road on which nothing more dangerous than mosquitoes had been reported for years.

She could have gone on being Ethel Evans.

She could have let Sid walk beside her and talk about Milwaukee. She could have lowered her eyes, evaded questions, and left before daylight with the town still dreaming of Pearlie Schultz’s mysterious friend.

But a leading lady knows when a scene belongs to her.

“Gentlemen,” she said, “before we walk anywhere, I ought to tell you something.”

They waited.

“My name is not Ethel Evans.”

The dentist’s son looked delighted. A false name suggested crime, romance, or both.

“It’s Aimee Fox.”

Silence.

From the lawn came the clatter of pans and Mrs. Moss calling for a missing spoon.

Sid’s mouth opened.

“Aimee Fox—the actress?”

“The same.”

“You’re at the Lyceum!”

“I was supposed to be. The machinery had other plans.”

The hardware clerk stared at her as if she had become electrically illuminated.

“But—your pictures—”

“Are seven years old.”

She smiled, not the young, uncertain smile of Ethel Evans, but Aimee’s own—witty, tired, gallant, and entirely in command.

“I am thirty-eight. I have been married, divorced, stranded twice, applauded in forty-two states, and photographed in five different kinds of moonlight. I leave on the six-ten train tomorrow morning. Also, Mr. Strang, if I ate another strawberry I should die.”

Nobody spoke.

Then Pearlie came to stand beside her.

“She ain’t lying about the berries,” Pearlie said. “I counted.”

Sid recovered first. Bank training again.

“Could we still walk to the river?”

Aimee looked at him. Then she slipped one arm through his and the other through Pearlie’s.

“We can walk as far as the hotel,” she said. “All of us.”

And so they did—Aimee Fox in white, Pearlie Schultz with her transformed hair, and our town’s young men orbiting them beneath the elms. Behind them the Japanese lanterns bobbed in the warm dark, the Ladies’ Aid counted its extraordinary earnings, and Table Four stood sticky, empty, triumphant.

At six-ten the next morning the leading lady left town.

But not as you have seen leading ladies depart—alone in a veil, pursued by violins, while some ruined lover kneels beside the track.

She leaned from the rear platform with a paper box of sandwiches under one arm and a pattern for an underbodice tucked into her handbag. Pearlie stood below, broad and steady in the sunrise. Sid Strang had sent roses. Mrs. Pringle had sent cake. The dentist’s son had sent a note containing a quotation he believed was Byron.

The train jolted.

“Niagara Falls!” Aimee called. “Next August! You and me!”

“If I save enough!”

“You will!”

Pearlie ran beside the moving car for six steps, laughing and crying at once.

Then the platform slipped away. The town dwindled. The hotel roof sank behind the trees.

Aimee sat down, opened the sandwich box, and found that Pearlie had used too much mustard.

She ate every bite.

I

Trade had gone out of Nottingham like a dishonest lodger, leaving behind unpaid bills, cold grates, and an offended landlord in every street.

Trade—mysterious word, respectable word, god and devil of the town—had stopped the hosiery frames, emptied the warehouses, shortened the bakers’ credit, and set men who had once complained of work to wandering all day in search of it. Trade had shut one factory, then another, without laying a visible hand upon either. Trade had made the public-houses noisy in the mornings and the homes silent at night. The frames, when they worked at all, said, “Shackety-boom, shackety-boom,” behind long brick walls; but now whole courts listened for that familiar pulse and heard only rain dripping from the gutters.

Into this morose atmosphere, late on an October afternoon, came a country girl with twelve geese.

The girl had walked nine miles. The geese had walked nine miles too, though their opinion of the journey had been more continuously expressed. Eleven waddled in a dirty, disordered procession, stretching their necks, opening their orange bills, and hissing at whatever portion of the world seemed immediately responsible. The twelfth was lame. It limped behind, balancing its heavy whiteness upon one tarred foot, while the girl, with a hazel switch, urged it forward by appeals which had long ago lost the character of persuasion.

“Come up, then! Come up, stupid!”

The goose did not come up. It sat down in the road.

The girl looked at it, and the goose looked at the girl. In that exchange there was no tenderness. Each had suffered through the other; each regarded the other as an invidious burden imposed by Providence, which, being absent, could not conveniently be beaten.

She was a large, brown, weary creature of perhaps twenty, though fatigue had pushed her face towards thirty. Her boots were clotted with mud, her skirt dark to the knees with rain, and wisps of colourless hair adhered to her forehead. She had started before dawn, carrying bread in a cloth and hope in the hard, practical form of twelve live geese. The bread was gone. Hope remained, but had become lame.

By the time she entered the town the fair was failing.

Torches, newly lit, insulted the twilight with greasy flames. Booth-keepers called their final bargains; cheap crockery shone under rain; a mechanical organ screamed its last brave tune against the fog. There were toys, ribbons, gingerbread, boots, knives, and the despondent remnants of farm produce. But buyers were scarce. Women counted pennies inside their gloves. Men stared, spat, and moved on. Children desired everything and received blows or philosophy.

“You can’t have what there isn’t money for,” said one mother, dragging away a boy who had fixed his soul upon a painted trumpet.

The goose girl reached the poultry quarter just as two dealers were covering their baskets.

“Twelve,” she said. “Fine birds. Who’ll give?”

One dealer squeezed the breast of the nearest goose and shook his head with professional sorrow.

“Late.”

“They’re as good now as they were at noon.”

“Ay, but noon’s not now.”

The second man examined the lame bird.

“What’s wrong wi’ this?”

“Nowt. Footsore.”

“Footsore goose is a lame goose.”

“It’ll mend.”

“So will trade, they tell us.”

The men laughed—not because the observation was witty, but because bitterness is cheaper when shared.

The girl named a price. They offered half. She refused. They turned away. She called them back and lowered her demand; but they had already discovered an absorbing concern with a rope knot. Pride kept her silent for another minute. Hunger spoke after that.

“Take the eleven, then. Leave t’ lame one.”

The first dealer glanced over his shoulder.

“Don’t want ’em.”

At six o’clock the fair began to fold itself up. Canvas drooped; poles came down; the mud, trampled all day by commerce, remained as commerce’s principal achievement. The girl stood among her geese with a rigidity more terrible than tears. She had no money for lodging, no food for herself, and nine miles of darkness behind her. Ahead lay the same nine miles again.

A group of young men passed, clean-coated, loud, and pleasantly aware that the poor existed chiefly to provide scenery. One imitated the lame goose. Another hissed. The girl lifted her heavy face, but they were already gone, taking their laughter with them.

She gathered the birds.

“Home, then,” she said.

The geese protested. Nevertheless, they turned.

II

At half past seven, Lois Vale looked at the clock and forgave Will Selby for being late.

At a quarter to eight she looked again and altered the terms of forgiveness.

She sat in her brother’s house in a drawing-room whose order had something almost ethical about it. The silver on the table was exact; the napkins possessed corners of exemplary severity; three white chrysanthemums stood in a narrow blue vase without leaning familiarly upon one another. Lois herself was dressed in grey, with a small collar fastened at the throat. Her dark hair showed no negligence. Even her hands, resting quietly in her lap, appeared accurately composed.

She was twenty-six, cultivated, handsome, and accustomed to believing that self-command was a moral virtue rather than a fortunate arrangement of nerves. She despised scenes, loud voices, emotional mendicancy, and all the untidy methods by which less disciplined persons attempted to obtain happiness. Yet she loved Will Selby, which was already an untidiness.

Her brother Jack had not returned either. Jack’s absences, however, were natural events like rain or debt; they inconvenienced without surprising. Will’s absence was an assertion.

At eight the maid removed the soup.

At five minutes past, the bell rang.

Will entered with fog on his shoulders and apology in his smile. He was a fair, slender man whose face might have been called sensitive if the curled upper lip had not so often converted sensitivity into contempt. His clothes were excellent, though damp; his eyes bright, though not entirely steady.

“My dear Lois,” he said, bending towards her hand in the true fashion of romance, “I have dined you upon expectation. A light but indigestible course.”

“You are late.”

“The clock has poisoned you against me.”

“The clock merely provides evidence.”

He laughed, and then, seeing that she did not, passed a hand across his hair.

“I can’t stay.”

Her expression changed very slightly. Will, who knew its language, saw the change and disliked himself for producing it. Disliking himself, he immediately became flippant.

“Duty calls. In boots, with a cudgel.”

“What duty?”

“The factory.”

“The factory is closed.”

“Precisely. Closed factories require more guarding than open ones. Nothing inside but machines, and nothing outside but men who hate them.”

He turned towards the fire. Its mild domestic blaze illuminated the polish of his boot and left his face uncertain.

There had been threats against Selby’s works. A reduction in wages, followed by dismissal of sixty hands, had made the family name an object upon which unemployed men could conveniently exercise their eloquence. Two windows had been broken; a watchman had been struck; anonymous letters, remarkable chiefly for bad spelling and sincerity, had promised burning.

“Your father has watchmen,” Lois said.

“My father has economised in watchmen, as in everything except indignation. Jack and I are going down.”

“Jack?”

“If he ever arrives. We shall defend property, civilisation, and several thousand obsolete frames.”

The phrase was light, but a little too carefully so. Lois looked at him. It had often occurred to her that Will’s irony was not a weapon but a curtain: he drew it whenever he feared that somebody might see him plainly.

“Have you been drinking?”

“Not enough to answer that question honestly.”

“Will.”

He turned. For one moment bravado dropped from him.

“We’re in a devil of a position, Lois. There’s no money. The bank won’t extend us. The old man says the insurance is the only sound thing left in the concern—and then looks horrified because I hear him. The men think we feast on their wages. We don’t. We lose what they never possessed.”

“Loss does not make you innocent of their misery.”

“No. Nor does their misery make me guilty of the weather.”

Outside, a cart went past with a dull grinding of wheels. The fog pressed against the panes, curious and blind.

Lois rose.

“Do you truly mean to spend the night at the factory?”

He hesitated. It was brief—less than a second—but love measures what indifference overlooks.

“Yes.”

“And if there is trouble?”

“We shall be heroic until the police arrive.”

“I am serious.”

“So am I. That is why I sound absurd.”

She came nearer and laid both hands upon his coat. The gesture ought to have been tender; her anxiety made it judicial.

“Do nothing desperate.”

His eyes met hers. In them she saw embarrassment, fear, vanity, and something more obscure which her imagination, once awakened, hurried to name.

“What do you mean?”

“You know what I mean.”

“No,” he said. “But I admire the dramatic economy.”

“Your father spoke of the insurance. The business is failing. There have been threats. If anything happened tonight—”

He freed himself, not violently.

“My dear Lois, one does not accuse one’s future husband of arson before dinner. It lowers the tone of the engagement.”

“I accused you of nothing.”

“Then your innocence is remarkably expressive.”

For a few seconds they stood apart. The room, with its silver, white flowers, and unconsumed dinner, rebuked them by remaining civilised.

Will’s lip curled; but the irony had become painful.

“I must go.”

She wished to call him back, to say that fear had spoken through her, that love invents crimes because it cannot bear uncertainty. Instead she asked:

“Where is Jack?”

“At the Crown, perhaps. I’ll collect him.”

He kissed her. It was a hurried, apprehensive kiss, performed as though tenderness itself might detain or betray him. Then he was gone.

Lois listened until the outer door closed.

Only afterwards did she remember that he had never answered her question.

III

The factory burned at midnight.

It began in the old finishing room, where dry timber, oil, fluff, and neglect had waited together for years like accomplices. A watchman saw light beneath a door; then the windows brightened; then flame ran along the roof with a rapidity which seemed less like spreading than recognition.

Bells clanged across the sleeping town.

People came out into the fog half dressed: men fastening braces, women with shawls over nightgowns, children ecstatic at catastrophe. The fire engines jolted up, red and brass and furiously inadequate. Water hissed upon brick. Glass burst outward. Smoke descended and rolled along the streets, shouldering spectators from their places.

Lois woke to hammering at the door.

The maid entered pale and incoherent.

“Miss—the Selbys’ place—it’s all afire!”

For an instant Lois understood nothing. Then she understood too much.

From the upper window she could see the sky above the northern streets pulsing red. The factory itself was hidden by roofs, but its destruction announced itself magnificently, staining the fog, summoning every chimney and steeple into black silhouette. It was as if the town held up a great cup of roaring destruction and drank from it.

Will was there.

Or he had been there.

Or he had done it.

She dressed with hands so cold that buttons became intricate. Jack had not returned. That circumstance, attaching itself to Will’s absence, gave suspicion the dreadful neatness of proof.

By the time she reached the factory street, the roof had fallen.

Flame leapt from the hollow building, exposing girders, staircases, and fragments of wall that stood for a moment in naked astonishment before collapsing. The frames within made strange metallic cries as beams crushed them. Shackety-boom had been their living rhythm; now came clang, shriek, thunder—the industrial body breaking its bones.

Mr. Selby stood behind the police line in an overcoat thrown over his nightshirt. His grey hair streamed uncovered; sparks settled upon his shoulders. Near him, clerks, foremen, and two insurance officials stared at the inferno with expressions in which grief, calculation, and suspicion had already begun their partnership.

“Where is Will?” Lois asked.

Mr. Selby turned.

“I thought he was with you.”

“He said he was coming here.”

The old man’s face altered.

“Here?”

“To guard the factory. With Jack.”

“Nobody’s seen either of them.”

A wall fell inward, sending up a storm of sparks. The crowd cried out with the pleased horror of those who are safe. For one blazing instant every face became distinct—the hungry men, the excited boys, the women who had pawned blankets when Selby’s reduced wages—and then darkness closed over them again.

A voice behind Lois said, “Insurance’ll cover it.”

Another answered, “Ay. Convenient, that.”

She did not turn. The words entered her as quietly as a knife.

All night the fire consumed evidence and manufactured rumour. By three o’clock it was commonly known that the Selbys had fired their own place. By four, Will had been seen carrying paraffin. By five, he had confessed to a barmaid in Sheffield. Poverty, which had been denied bread, fed ravenously upon scandal.

Lois returned home shortly before dawn.

She did not sleep. She sat beside the dead drawing-room fire while morning crept into the windows like a thing ashamed. The white chrysanthemums had begun to bend. One petal lay upon the polished table; this minute disorder wounded her absurdly.

What had Will said? The insurance was the only sound thing. We shall be heroic until the police arrive. Nothing outside but men who hate the machines.

And what had she said?

Do nothing desperate.

That sentence must never be repeated. It might condemn him. Worse—her vanity whispered beneath her terror—it might expose her as a woman who had recognised the crime in advance and yet allowed him to go. She saw herself questioned; saw compassionate friends remembering her engagement; saw newspapers arranging her private words into a public horror.

She determined to conceal the conversation.

The decision appeared to her first as loyalty. Then as sacrifice. Then, when she examined it more sternly, as cowardice mixed with love. Human motives do not stand in separate vessels, labelled and pure; they flow together, and conscience, dipping its cup, commonly drinks more than it intended.

She would protect him. But she could not marry him.

This conclusion brought pain, and with pain a faint, shameful grandeur. Her future opened before her as a procession of grey days through which she would walk alone, dignified, wounded, faithful to a man she had renounced. She imagined his guilt with horror; she imagined her own renunciation with a species of tragic admiration.

It did not occur to her that reality might be less noble and more ridiculous.

IV

At nine o’clock Lois went again to the ruins.

Rain had followed fire. It fell softly upon the black walls, upon twisted steel, sodden ledgers, split stone, and the pale islands of ash that floated in puddles. Smoke rose reluctantly from heaps of charred wood. The factory, stripped of roof and windows, resembled the jaw of some enormous dead animal.

Men moved through the debris. Firemen dragged hoses; policemen guarded entrances; workmen searched for embers. At the gate stood a little congregation of businessmen, each prepared to sympathise with Mr. Selby provided the insurance proved regular.

Lois saw Will before he saw her.

He was coming along the street with Jack. Both wore evening clothes beneath borrowed workmen’s coats. Both were muddy. Jack’s collar had disappeared, his cheek was grazed, and a tuft of straw clung inexplicably to his hair. Will’s hat was crushed. His right eye was purple and swollen nearly shut.

They did not resemble incendiaries. They resembled the consequences of bad judgment.

Relief struck Lois with such force that she caught the iron railing. At once relief was followed by bewilderment, and bewilderment by anger—anger partly against them, partly against herself for having constructed, through one sleepless night, an edifice of guilt, sacrifice, and lonely moral splendour, only to see it threatened by Jack’s torn trousers.

“Lo!” Jack cried. “Thank God! Tell ’em we’re respectable.”

Will stopped.

The black eye gave his face a mournful absurdity. He attempted his usual smile, but the curled lip had lost authority.

“Good morning,” he said. “You appear better preserved than the family property.”

“Where have you been?”

“In the protective custody of the Crown.”

“The Crown public-house?”

“Initially,” said Jack. “Later, His Majesty’s police.”

Lois looked from one to the other.

“You were in jail?”

“Only overnight,” Jack said quickly. “Not jail exactly. A cell. Very clean in principle, though not in fact. There’s an important distinction.”

“The factory burned.”

“Yes,” said Will.

“You were not here.”

“No.”

“Then where—precisely—were you?”

Jack drew breath with the eagerness of a man who has survived disgrace and now wishes to enjoy it as narrative.

“I’ll tell you. Selby came for me at the Crown, as arranged, only Briggs was there, and Carter, and young Hemsley, and they said one drink against the cold couldn’t imperil British industry. Well, one became another through no fault of ours—glasses being small—and when we came out there was fog enough to butter bread with.”

“Jack.”

“I’m coming to it. We set off for the works. Positively did. Took the wrong turning by Goose Gate, though, and there was this girl.”

Will looked at the wet pavement.

“What girl?” Lois asked.

“A country wench. Big arms. Twelve geese. Or eleven and a half, one being defective.”

“Lame,” Will said.

“Yes, lame. She’d brought ’em to fair and sold none. Marching home, poor devil. We offered to assist.”

“You tormented her,” Lois said.

Jack appeared injured by the crude accuracy.

“We enlivened the journey. Hemsley took the switch and tried to drill the geese like soldiers. Carter said the lame one ought to be carried, so he tucked it under Selby’s arm—”

“I did not ask for it.”

“You held it beautifully.”

“It bit me.”

“Nipped your wrist. Then the others went off in all directions. Never knew geese had so many directions in ’em. There was hissing, flapping, women screaming from doorways—and Briggs chased one into a chapel yard.”

Lois’s face remained still.

“And the girl?”

“Objected.”

“How?”

“With completeness. First she called us things I shan’t repeat before you, though several were agricultural and one anatomically impossible. Selby laughed—”

“I was drunk,” Will said.

“That explains nothing,” Lois replied. “It merely enlarges it.”

Jack hurried on.

“Then he bowed to her and asked what she’d take for the lame goose. She named a price. He offered sixpence.”

“I had no change.”

“You had sovereigns.”

“I was being funny.”

“No,” said Lois. “You were being cruel.”

Will flinched. The movement was almost invisible, but she saw it.

Jack rubbed his grazed cheek.

“Well, she didn’t think it funny either. She snatched at the bird, Selby held on, the goose set up a row fit to wake the dead, and she fetched him one—bang!—straight in the eye.”

“The girl did that?” Lois asked.

“With her fist,” said Will. “An honest instrument.”

“And then,” Jack continued, “Carter pushed her, though he swears he only encountered her accidentally. A couple of market lads came up. Briggs told them to mind their own business. They said this was their business. Hemsley removed his coat. Somebody trod on the lame goose. The goose attacked a constable. After that the law lost patience with all species concerned.”

Despite herself, Lois saw it: the fog, the scattered white birds, the country girl striking out with the accumulated rage of nine miles, twelve unsold geese, hunger, rain, and fashionable laughter; Will with a flapping bird under his arm; Jack declaiming respectability while being dragged by the collar. The vision possessed the energy of farce, but beneath it lay something sullen and ugly.

“What happened to her?”

“Released,” Jack said. “Constable said she’d had provocation. We offered to pay for the geese.”

“After you were arrested.”

“Well—yes.”

“Did she take the money?”

“No,” Will said. “She spat on it.”

The three stood in silence. Behind them a workman levered a warped iron bar from the ashes. It came free with a groan.

Jack, uneasy now, attempted lightness.

“The irony is, had we not been pinched we might’ve reached the factory and stopped the fire.”

“No,” said Will. “The irony is that a cell is the only alibi anyone will believe.”

He lifted his face towards the ruins. Shame had sobered him more thoroughly than sleep. The blackened walls, which ought to have represented his family’s catastrophe, seemed almost dignified beside him. He had prepared himself, perhaps, to be accused of arson; he had not prepared to confess to bullying a hungry girl. Great crimes lend stature even to villains. Petty cruelty merely reduces.

Jack suddenly remembered an urgent obligation elsewhere.

“I must find Father,” he said. “And possibly a solicitor. Not necessarily in that order.”

He departed through the gate.

Lois and Will remained beside the railing. People glanced at them. Rumour had noticed Will’s arrival and was adjusting itself.

“You believed I had done it,” he said.

She could have denied this. Her habits, her pride, and her love all offered arguments. She rejected them.

“Yes.”

“And yet you came.”

“Yes.”

“To see whether I had been burned with my investment?”

“To find the truth.”

He gave a short laugh.

“You have found a cheaper truth.”

She looked directly at his damaged eye.

“Everyone suspects you. Your father did not know you meant to be here. Men are saying the insurance was convenient. By noon they will say you fired the place.”

“Then I must be grateful to the Nottingham constabulary for preserving my character in a locked room.”

“Your character was not preserved.”

The words struck harder than the girl’s fist. His mouth tightened.

“No,” he said. “Apparently not.”

Rain gathered upon Lois’s lashes. She remembered the future she had imagined only hours before: the noble separation, the disciplined grief, the life moving through bleak fog. How clean it had been! How admirably arranged! The actual Will stood before her muddy, bruised, ashamed, and morally smaller than the criminal she had invented. To abandon an incendiary might have been virtue. To abandon this foolish, cruel, frightened man would be merely easy.

Love is sometimes praised for blindness. More terrible is the love that sees, condemns, and remains.

“Will,” she said, “you will find that girl.”

“If she has not murdered the lame goose by now.”

“You will find her. You will pay her the fair price for all twelve birds.”

“She refused.”

“You will ask her to choose the price.”

“And if she spits on it again?”

“You will stand there.”

His lip began to curl. Then, meeting her eyes, it subsided.

“Anything else?”

“You will apologise.”

“To the girl?”

“To the girl first. Afterwards to yourself, if you think yourself worth the ceremony.”

He looked away towards the factory. Water dripped from a broken beam into the ash—tap, tap, tap—as though the ruin counted his offences.

“And us?” he asked.

There it was: no bravado, no fashionable phrase, only the embarrassed vulnerability he had spent so much wit concealing.

Lois did not answer at once. She laid her hand upon his sleeve, not tenderly, not quite in forgiveness, but with possession and judgment strangely united.

“We shall walk,” she said.

“Where?”

“To your father first. Then to the police. Then, when this is settled, to the country road.”

“In search of twelve geese.”

“In search of one woman.”

He glanced at her hand.

“You intend to punish me for the remainder of my natural life.”

“If necessary.”

“And if I object?”

“You may begin now.”

He did not object.

They turned from the ruins and went down the wet street side by side. Behind them the factory smoked, its black machinery exposed to the grey, unastonished morning. Ahead, the fog had lifted a little, though not enough to disclose the end of the road. Will walked with his bruised face bent; Lois walked erect, severe, and close beside him.

Thus they went—not reconciled, not separated; the man submitting with bitter grace, the woman loving without mercy—while Trade, that secret enemy of factories, geese, pride, and tenderness, stirred again somewhere behind the walls, and one distant frame, obstinate among the ashes of the town, began its thin metallic beating:

Shackety-boom.

Shackety-boom.

The people of Bellwether said afterward that there had never been a more beautiful Sunday for a church quarrel.

The morning was full of those delicate noises which seem, in May, to be parts of one music. The young leaves murmured along the road; robins answered one another from the orchard trees; the brook, swollen by April rains, made a silver commotion under the bridge. Through the open windows of the white church came the organ and the choir, their voices rising over the village roofs with a sweetness that caused more than one late pedestrian to slacken his pace.

Then, during Alma Way’s solo in “O Morning Land,” another organ sounded across the common.

It was a small parlor organ, rather asthmatic in its lower notes, but it had been played for forty years by a woman who understood its infirmities. After three determined chords a high female voice entered upon “The Judgment Trumpet.” The tune did not harmonize with “O Morning Land.” It was not intended to.

Alma’s thin hands tightened upon her hymnbook. The artificial violets on her bonnet trembled as if a sudden wind had got into the choir loft. Mr. Tilton, the minister, raised his broad, distressed face above the pulpit. Every child in the church turned toward the open windows. The older people continued to look forward with an excess of Christian attention which made their awareness more conspicuous.

Across the common, in her front parlor, Miss Candace Whitcomb pumped the organ pedals with both feet and sang:

“Then shall the proud be brought down low—”

Her voice, once the clearest soprano in the county, had grown uncertain at its edges. It could still rise, but it no longer alighted securely. Sometimes it struck above the note and slid down upon it; sometimes it quivered beneath, as though the note were a shelf just beyond the reach of cold fingers. Yet there remained in it a penetrating quality which years had not softened. It came through the new leaves, across the grass and into the church like a shining needle.

Alma faltered.

The choir, out of loyalty or fear, increased its volume. Miss Whitcomb increased hers. For ten measures Bellwether heard morning land and judgment trumpet together, youth and age striving in different keys; then Alma’s voice broke into silence.

Candace finished her hymn alone.

When the congregation came out, people gathered in groups without appearing to gather. Mrs. Pike discovered something requiring prolonged adjustment in her daughter’s collar. Two deacons examined the hitching rail. The choir descended the steps quietly, fallen from their song flights to little chirps of explanation.

“I never knew anybody could sing so loud from indoors,” said Ezra Bell.

“She had both front windows up,” said his wife.

“It was warm.”

“It wa’n’t that warm.”

Alma came last. She was twenty-seven, but her slightness and her habit of yielding space to everyone made her appear younger. Her gray dress had been made twice over, and the violets on her bonnet had come from the bonnet she wore the year before. She smiled when people spoke to her, though her lips had almost no color.

Mr. Tilton detained her at the foot of the steps.

“You must not—must not take this to heart, Miss Way.”

“I don’t,” said Alma.

“She is suffering from a natural—ah—disappointment.”

“I know.”

“And it was very well sung until—until the interruption.”

Alma’s bonnet flowers moved again.

“I missed the F,” she said. “It wasn’t Miss Whitcomb’s fault I missed it.”

The minister regarded her with grateful helplessness. He was a large, slow man whose moral convictions were solidly set but not quickly moved. In moments of urgency he seemed less like a shepherd than like a block of granite which had been appointed to consider sheep.

“You behaved admirably,” he said.

Alma looked across the common.

Candace stood at one of her open windows. She was a narrow woman in black, so straight and slight that the curtain stirring behind her appeared stronger than she. Her hair was still plentiful, though entirely white, and rolled high above a face in which the eyes retained a startling darkness. She looked at the people leaving church. Then she lowered the window with a bang.

Nobody in Bellwether was ignorant of the cause of the disturbance, though opinions differed concerning its justice.

For forty years Candace Whitcomb had been leading soprano of the First Congregational Church. She had begun at sixteen, when her voice was so pure that old Dr. Marsh had stopped midway up the aisle to listen. At twenty she had been asked to sing in Worcester. Her father had forbidden it. A daughter of his, he said, did not go exhibiting herself for money. There had also been, later, a music teacher from Boston who told Candace she possessed “a real instrument”; but her mother was ill by then, and the Boston opportunity had passed like a train heard at night from a distant room.

Candace had never married. Bellwether believed she had once been disappointed, because a village will seldom permit an unmarried woman to have escaped disappointment; but no one could name the man. Music had filled the place where husband, children, and ambition might otherwise have stood. Every Sunday she crossed the common with her music rolled in one hand. At weddings her voice had promised happiness; at funerals it had carried grief upward and made it seem bearable. Generations of Bellwether children had watched the motion of her narrow back in the choir loft. People who did not like her admitted that the church would not sound the same without her.

For several years, however, it had not sounded the same with her.

She sang too loudly to conceal weakness. She selected hymns that demanded the heights she had once reached easily, and when her voice failed she blamed the organ, the weather, the arrangement, or the choir’s inability to sustain her. Mr. Noyes, the choirmaster, endured these failures with the pained loyalty of a man watching an old horse stumble. At last the music committee met.

Their decision was unanimous and their method merciful.

The committee invited Candace to the vestry on a Thursday evening, where the choir had assembled around a table decorated with apple blossoms. There was cake, coffee, and a bowl of lemonade. Mrs. Pike gave a speech about long service. Mr. Noyes gave another about sacred memories. Mr. Tilton prayed. Then they presented Candace with a photograph album containing pictures of every choir member, followed by a written tribute informing her that, after four decades of consecrated song, she had earned an honored rest.

Candace had not known that she was tired.

She stood with the album in her arms while they applauded. Across the table Alma Way had looked at her with frightened sympathy. Candace understood at once that Alma had been chosen to replace her.

“It is a surprise,” Mrs. Pike said brightly.

“So I see,” Candace replied.

“We knew you would never give up of your own accord,” said Deacon Pike, intending praise.

“No,” said Candace. “You knew that much.”

The following Sunday Alma took the leading soprano’s chair, and Candace remained at home. The first interruption had occurred during the second hymn. The next Sunday had brought “O Morning Land” and “The Judgment Trumpet.”

By noon, the entire village was discussing what Mr. Tilton ought to do. Mr. Tilton, who had already decided what he ought to do and disliked it profoundly, ate very little dinner. At two o’clock he crossed the common.

Candace admitted him before he knocked a second time.

“I saw you coming,” she said. “Walk in.”

Her parlor was neat, dim, and crowded with possessions that had survived their uses. Wax flowers stood beneath a glass dome; shells framed a picture of Niagara Falls; a red-and-green carpet covered the floor. The organ occupied the space between the windows like a dark domestic altar. On the bench beside it lay three hymnals with slips marking selected pages.

The photograph album lay on the floor beneath Candace’s feet.

Mr. Tilton noticed it after he sat down.

“I hope,” he began, “that you are feeling well.”

“Never better.”

“It is a fine day.”

“You didn’t come to talk about the weather.”

“No. No, I did not.”

Candace waited. In the church Mr. Tilton could preach for forty minutes on the duties of courage. Here, under her eyes, he found courage a more complicated doctrine.

“This morning,” he said at last, “there was an unfortunate—confusion.”

“There wa’n’t any confusion on my side. I sang the hymn I meant to sing.”

“The services can be heard quite plainly from your house.”

“I’ve heard ’em forty years.”

“And your organ can be heard—quite plainly—from the church.”

“I’ve known that house stood close to the church ever since I bought it.”

Mr. Tilton folded his hands.

“Miss Whitcomb, you must see that two hymns sung at once cannot be conducive to worship.”

“I should think the Lord could sort ’em out.”

“The congregation cannot.”

“Maybe the congregation needs practice.”

His face reddened, but his voice remained careful.

“It was distressing to Miss Way.”

Candace’s eyes sparked.

“Oh, it was distressing to Alma! Well, we mustn’t distress Alma. Alma can take my chair and my music and my solos and all the Sundays I’ve put my life into, but she mustn’t be distressed. No. We’ll bring her some lemonade. We’ll get her an album.”

“Miss Whitcomb—”

“Have you seen that album?”

“Yes. The choir hoped—”

“The choir hoped I could sit here Sundays and look at their pictures while they sang. Here’s Mr. Noyes with his mouth shut for once. Here’s Mrs. Pike in that feather she thinks makes her look tall. Here’s Alma, looking meek enough to inherit the earth and everything on it. Forty years, and what do I get? Faces. Faces pasted on cardboard. ‘Set this on your table, Candace, and when you miss the choir you can look at us.’ Look at ’em? I’ve used it for a footstool since Thursday, and it ain’t half high enough.”

“They meant kindly.”

“Kindly!” She rose, but one foot remained on the album, giving her the appearance of mounting a low barricade. “That’s the cruelty of it. If they’d come and said, ‘Candace, your voice is gone and we can’t abide another Sunday of it,’ I could have fought ’em honest. But no. Cake and apple blossoms! ‘You’ve earned a rest.’ I never asked for a rest. ‘Consecrated service.’ I’m alive yet. You don’t hang a wreath on a live woman and tell her how useful she used to be.”

Mr. Tilton glanced toward the stirring curtains.

“There are times,” he said, “when we must relinquish duties we love.”

“Have you?”

The question struck squarely. He blinked.

“That is not the point.”

“It’s exactly the point. Suppose the deacons had you over to the vestry. Suppose they gave you coffee and a picture book with the whole congregation pasted into it. Suppose Deacon Pike said, ‘You’ve preached forty years, Mr. Tilton, and lately your sermons wander and your voice cracks, so we’ve got a young man from Amherst to take your pulpit. You can sit home and remember us.’ Would you call it kindness?”

“If I could no longer discharge—”

“You’d call it mutiny.”

“I hope I should possess humility.”

“You hope so because you ain’t been asked.”

The wind moved the lilac branches outside the window, and their scent entered the room. Candace’s face, flushed by anger, seemed almost young. Mr. Tilton caught, with unexpected pain, a glimpse of the girl she must have been—the girl who had been told she possessed a real instrument, who had stayed to tend her mother, who had given the village the only greatness it had ever requested of her and now found that greatness classified as an old inconvenience.

Yet the morning service remained the morning service.

“We cannot undo what has been done,” he said. “We may regret the manner of it. Perhaps the committee—perhaps I—should have spoken more directly. But you must not interfere again.”

“Mustn’t I?”

“No.”

“Then shut the church windows.”

“In warm weather?”

“You can’t expect me to keep mine shut.”

“You deliberately chose a conflicting hymn.”

“I’m entitled to sing hymns in my own parlor.”

“At precisely the moment of Miss Way’s solo?”

“Is there a law against it?”

“There is Christian consideration.”

Candace laughed once, sharply.

“You should have brought that Thursday.”

He leaned forward with what, in a smaller man, might have been urgency.

“My dear Miss Whitcomb, this bitterness will injure you more than anyone. Alma did not seek your dismissal.”

“She took the place.”

“The committee urged her.”

“She could have said no.”

“Could you have said no at her age?”

Candace’s face changed. For an instant the question opened a door into a room she had kept locked even from herself. Then she shut it.

“At her age I didn’t creep,” she said. “I didn’t go about smiling soft so folks would hand me what belonged to somebody else.”

“Alma has had little handed to her.”

“She’s got my solos.”

“She has also,” Mr. Tilton said with greater firmness, “a widowed mother and two younger sisters to support by sewing. She receives no salary from the choir.”

Candace stepped down from the album.

“Then she’s cheap as well as meek.”

The minister stood.

“I will not listen to malice.”

“You came here to listen.”

“I came to appeal to the better nature which I know you possess.”

“You came to tell me Alma’s feelings matter and mine don’t.”

“I said nothing of the sort.”

“You didn’t need to. Nobody needs to. When a woman’s young, her feelings are tender. When she’s old, they’re temper.”

Mr. Tilton opened his mouth, but she swept on, words following one another as if forty years of disciplined breath had been saved for this protest.

“What was I to do, I’d like to know? Sit in the congregation and hear her flatten my high notes? Smile when folks turn to see how I bear it? Tell her she sang beautifully when she didn’t? Or stay home with the album? That album will solve everything. Lonely? Look at the album. Useless? Look at the album. Dead but haven’t had the decency to lie down? Put your feet on the album. There’s comfort. There’s Christian consideration bound in red leather with a brass clasp!”

“You are making yourself ridiculous,” said Mr. Tilton, and regretted it immediately.

Candace became still.

Outside, a bird whistled three descending notes. Her hands hung at her sides. They were very thin hands, corded and capable, and Mr. Tilton remembered them arranging flowers for Christmas, carrying broth to sick neighbors, fastening the choir robes of nervous children.

“I daresay,” she said. “That’s another privilege of getting old. Grief is tragedy when your face is smooth. Afterward it’s ridiculous.”

“I did not mean—”

“You meant enough. Will there be singing at afternoon service?”

“Yes.”

“Alma singing?”

“Yes.”

“Then you’d better close the windows.”

Mr. Tilton went out with the sensation of having failed morally in several directions at once.

At three o’clock the church windows were open. Deacon Pike had argued that closing them would concede too much to lawlessness, and the day had grown warmer. The congregation was larger than usual. Bellwether did not approve of scandal, but it liked to be present when scandal was rebuked.

Candace waited until Alma began “Rock of Ages.” Then she attacked “Onward, Christian Soldiers.”

The parlor organ wheezed valiantly. Candace sang faster than the church choir and reached every chorus ahead of it. The resulting collision drove solemnity from the service. A small boy laughed and was taken outside by his father, who did not return. Alma struggled through two verses, then stopped. Mr. Noyes closed the organ. Candace, hearing the church fall silent, continued alone to the final “Amen.”

This time Wilson Ford went to see her.

Wilson was Candace’s nephew, the son of her younger sister, and the only near relation she had. He was thirty-two, broad-shouldered, quick-moving, and employed at the feed store. He possessed what Bellwether called common sense, though in Wilson it generally arrived headlong and struck before it reasoned. He crossed the common so rapidly that his coat flew open.

People lingered outside the church. Alma tried to detain him.

“Don’t go angry, Wilson.”

“I ain’t angry.”

“You are.”

“I’m going to talk sense.”

“Talk it softly.”

“She’s made a circus of worship twice.”

“She’s hurt.”

“She’s hurting everybody else to prove it.”

Alma’s hand touched his sleeve and dropped away. They had been understood to care for one another for nearly four years, though no engagement had been announced. Wilson supported his mother, a complaining woman who interpreted marriage as desertion and Alma as its agent. His wages would not keep two households; Alma could not bring her mother and sisters into Mrs. Ford’s narrow house without producing calamity. Candace’s home, spacious and mortgage-free, stood across the common like the answer to a question no one dared ask aloud.

Wilson found his aunt replacing hymnals on the organ.

“You made a pretty exhibition,” he said.

“So did Alma.”

“She was trying to sing the service.”

“So was I.”

“You know what you were doing.”

“I generally do.”

“Well, you’re done doing it.”

Candace turned. “Am I?”

“Yes. This has gone far enough.”

“Who sent you? The minister or Alma?”

“Nobody sent me.”

“Then you came to protect your prospects.”

The blood rose into Wilson’s face.

“What’s that mean?”

“It means you expect this house someday, and you expect to put Alma in it.”

“I never asked you for a cent.”

“You didn’t have to. Your mother’s house is too small, and Alma won’t live with her anyhow. Sensible girl. Your mother could sour sweet cream by looking at it.”

“You leave Mother out.”

“I’d be glad to. It’s more than she ever does for anybody else.”

Wilson took a step toward the organ.

“I’m telling you plain. You play through another church service, and I’ll come over here and make kindling wood of that thing.”

Candace laid both hands upon the organ as if it were a living creature threatened.

“You touch it.”

“I mean it.”

“So do I. You lay one finger on my organ, Wilson Ford, and you won’t have this house, nor a dollar in the bank, nor so much as that album to set your feet on. I’ll leave everything to foreign missions. Then you can marry Alma in your mother’s pantry.”

“You’d hold money over me?”

“You’re holding an axe over my organ.”

“You’d throw away our chance because you can’t stand to hear somebody younger sing?”

The accuracy of it made her cruel.

“If Alma cared enough, she’d marry you poor.”

“She won’t marry me while I’ve got Mother dependent, and she’s right.”

“So everybody’s right but me.”

“You ain’t right. You’re behaving like a spiteful old woman.”

The phrase remained in the room after he said it.

Wilson’s anger faltered. He had not meant “old” to wound; it had seemed merely descriptive, like white or narrow or sixty-seven. But Candace drew herself up until she seemed taller than he was.

“Get out,” she said.

“Aunt Candace—”

“Get out before I alter my will this minute.”

“I don’t care about your will.”

“You care enough to threaten my organ.”

“I care about you making yourself hated.”

“Then you may stop caring.”

He went to the door, hesitated, and looked back. She had sat down on the organ bench. Her body appeared suddenly pliable, a blade of grass bent after the foot has passed; but her eyes forbade pity.

Wilson slammed the door.

He crossed the common without seeing Alma, though she waited near the gate. Before supper the village knew that Candace had threatened to disinherit him. By nightfall it knew she had already done so and had left her property variously to foreign missions, a home for aged singers, the Methodist church, and a cat she did not own.

That evening the wind shifted.

For three dry days a fire had been creeping through woodland beyond North Ridge, too distant to alarm Bellwether. Now smoke spread over the western sky. The sunset burned red through it, staining windows and turning the church steeple the color of old copper. People came into their yards to look. Ash fell in small gray flecks upon the young grass.

Candace sat at her organ with the lamp unlit.

Her anger had sustained her through the afternoon, but now it went out of her unevenly. In its place came exhaustion, shame, and a fierce impulse to kindle anger again before she could feel either of the others. She played the opening chords of “The Judgment Trumpet.” The organ answered weakly. When she attempted the first line, no sound emerged but a rough whisper.

She stopped.

Across the common, lamps shone in the church vestry, where Mr. Noyes was meeting with the music committee. Candace pictured Alma among them in her meek bonnet. She pictured Wilson beside her, saying that Candace was spiteful and old. She pictured Mrs. Pike explaining the entire history to anyone who would listen, omitting only that the album had been her own idea.

Candace put both feet on the pedals and forced air through the instrument. A chord swelled into the empty room. It shook the little glass dome over the wax flowers. She struck another and another, but her hands felt far away from her body.

When she stood, the walls moved.

She caught the organ edge. Her forehead was burning, though a chill ran under her dress. She thought of summoning Mrs. Bell from next door, then imagined how quickly Bellwether would hear that Candace Whitcomb had collapsed after being put out of the choir.

“I ain’t collapsed,” she said aloud.

She climbed the stairs.

In the morning Mrs. Bell noticed that Candace’s curtains had not been raised. At nine she carried over a plate of biscuits on the pretense of returning a dish. She found the front door unlocked and Candace in bed, delirious, singing fragments of hymns in a voice scarcely louder than breath.

By ten Dr. Marsh’s son had come. By eleven Alma knew. By noon the whole village had exchanged triumph for concern.

Illness altered public judgment with remarkable speed. Those who had called Candace wicked on Sunday called her high-strung on Monday. By Tuesday she had been provoked. By Wednesday the music committee’s surprise party was generally admitted to have lacked tact, though no individual remembered supporting it. Mrs. Pike put the remaining cake into a tin and hid it.

Candace’s fever rose.

Sometimes she believed herself in the choir loft and reproached Mr. Noyes for beginning too low. Sometimes she was sixteen and heard old Dr. Marsh stop in the aisle. Once she told her dead father that she would go to Worcester whether he permitted it or not. Then she begged her mother not to need her that particular winter.

Alma helped Mrs. Bell nurse her. She moved quietly through the house, airing rooms, heating water, and changing the wet cloth on Candace’s forehead. When Candace recognized her, she turned her face to the wall.

“You needn’t practice on me,” she whispered.

“I’m only bringing your medicine.”

“Same thing. Waiting for me to finish.”

“No.”

“You’ve got the chair.”

“I never wanted it that way.”

“You wanted it.”

Alma did not deny this. Her honesty, so soft in appearance, had a firm center.

“Yes,” she said. “I wanted to sing.”

Candace looked at her.

There was no triumph in Alma’s face, only fatigue and a sadness that did not ask to be admired. Candace remembered being twenty-seven and wanting to sing so badly that politeness had seemed a little sin beside the greater one of silence.

“You missed the F,” she said.

“I know.”

“It was sharp in the accompaniment, but you went flat.”

“I know that too.”

Candace shut her eyes.

On Thursday smoke from the forest fire thickened. The sun appeared as a dull red disk, and at midday the village had the color of evening. Men drove wagons toward North Ridge carrying shovels, wet sacks, and axes. Wilson went with them. For thirty hours he helped cut a break through the dry undergrowth while the fire moved among the pines with a sound like distant applause.

Alma watched from Candace’s upstairs window as sparks showed after dark above the ridge.

“She’s afire,” Candace murmured.

“The woods are.”

“The church. The choir loft.”

“No. The church is safe.”

“I set it. I sang and set it all afire.”

“You didn’t.”

“I wanted to spoil it if I couldn’t have it.”

Her hand wandered over the coverlet until Alma took it. Even in fever Candace’s fingers resisted for a moment before closing around the younger woman’s.

“I was cruel to you,” she said.

“You were hurt.”

“That ain’t an excuse.”

“It’s a reason.”

“Reasons are what folks give when excuses wear thin.”

Alma smiled faintly. “Then don’t give one.”

Candace opened her eyes. “You ain’t as meek as you look.”

“No.”

“I mistrusted that.”

The fire was brought under control on Friday morning. Rain came in the afternoon, a slow, straight rain that subdued the smoke and washed ash from the lilacs. Wilson returned blackened, hollow-eyed, and smelling of wet wool and burned wood. He went first to his mother, then to Candace’s house.

Alma met him at the door.

“How is she?”

“Worse.”

“Does she know you?”

“Sometimes.”

He looked beyond Alma into the dim hall.

“I oughtn’t have said what I did.”

“No.”

“I was mad.”

“So was she.”

“I threatened the organ.”

“I know.”

“Whole village knows, I suppose.”

“The village knows more than ever happened.”

Wilson rubbed soot from his knuckles. “Did she change the will?”

Alma’s expression caused him to flush.

“I don’t mean for me. I mean—she said she would, and if she did, that’s her right.”

“You do mean for you.”

“Well, ain’t I allowed to think of it? If she dies, and if she’s left the place away, you and me are where we were. I’m ashamed she’s sick. I’m ashamed I quarreled. But I can’t stop facts from being facts.”

Alma looked down at her narrow hands.

“I wouldn’t marry you for her house if she gave it unwilling.”

“You’d marry me if she gave it willing?”

She looked up. The hall behind them smelled of lilacs, rain, and the faint bitter medicine simmering on the stove.

“Yes,” she said.

Wilson’s headlong nature carried him one pace nearer. Then the stairs creaked, and Mrs. Bell called for Alma.

Candace wished to see the minister.

Mr. Tilton came in the rain, removing his wet coat downstairs. He entered the bedroom with the solemn caution of a man approaching both death and an old argument. Candace lay propped against white pillows. Illness had refined her face until little seemed left except eyes, bone, and will.

“Sit down,” she said.

He sat.

“I behaved wicked,” she told him.

Mr. Tilton hesitated. He had prepared mercy and was disconcerted by confession so direct.

“You were under severe strain.”

“Don’t you go excusing me. I’ve had Alma at it.”

“At what?”

“Making reasons. I wanted to hurt ’em. I wanted them all sitting in church unable to think of anything but me. That’s a poor way to lead worship.”

“Yes,” said Mr. Tilton gently. “It is.”

“I hated you.”

“I know.”

“I hated Mrs. Pike more.”

“That may be—somewhat easier to understand.”

Candace gave a breath which might have been a laugh.

“I ain’t sorry about the album.”

“One need not approve an album in order to forgive its donors.”

“I suppose not.”

Rain whispered against the leaves. The room had been arranged as carefully as Candace’s life: bottles in a straight row, folded cloths, comb and brush parallel on the bureau. On the washstand Alma had placed lilacs in a white pitcher. Their scent seemed almost visible in the damp air.

“I made singing an idol,” Candace said. “That’s what you’d call it.”

“Perhaps.”

“It was all I had.”

Mr. Tilton’s hand moved on his knee.

“Yes,” he said. “I think perhaps we failed to understand that.”

“You thought it was a duty I performed.”

“Yes.”

“It wa’n’t. It was me.”

“I understand now.”

“No, you don’t.” Her old sharpness returned, though weakly. “You can’t. But you understand that you don’t, and that’s nearer.”

He bowed his head.

After a moment Candace said, “I forgive you.”

“Thank you.”

“You must forgive me.”

“I do.”

“You ought to wait till I ask proper.”

“I have been waiting several days.”

This time she truly smiled. It altered her wasted face as sunlight alters a shut room.

They prayed together. Mr. Tilton’s voice, often ponderous from the pulpit, became simple at a bedside. He did not ask that Candace be made resigned, nor that her suffering be instructive to others. He asked only for mercy, courage, and peace. Candace’s lips moved at the amen.

Before he left she called him back.

“Tell the committee,” she said, “that Alma should keep the chair.”

“I will.”

“But Noyes takes ‘Jerusalem the Golden’ too fast.”

“I will tell him.”

“And there’s no need to sing everything loud because the windows are open.”

“No.”

“It ain’t devotion. It’s noise.”

“I shall make that clear.”

“Don’t tell Mrs. Pike I forgave the album.”

Mr. Tilton’s face became grave again.

“That detail,” he said, “may safely remain between us.”

On Saturday morning the doctor told Mrs. Bell there was little hope. By then the air was clear. The rain had passed east, leaving every branch washed and distinct. Birds flew busily through Candace’s yard, and drops fell from the flowering currant bushes whenever the wind moved them.

Candace asked for Wilson.

He came upstairs with Alma behind him. He had scrubbed the smoke from his face, but a burn crossed one wrist. At the sight of his aunt, his prepared words deserted him.

“Aunt Candace.”

“You look as if somebody tried to make kindling wood of you.”

“The fire near did.”

“Come closer.”

He knelt beside the bed. Candace examined the burn.

“You always did go at things headfirst.”

“I’m sorry.”

“So am I.”

“I didn’t mean what I said.”

“You meant it when you said it. That’s generally enough.”

Wilson lowered his head.

“I wouldn’t have touched the organ.”

“I know.”

“You don’t have to leave me anything.”

“I know that too.”

“I’ve got no claim.”

“Stop making speeches. You’re no good at ’em.”

Alma moved toward the window, giving them the privacy of her turned back. Beyond the glass, wet leaves shone against a pale sky.

Candace’s voice weakened.

“I told you I’d changed my will.”

“Yes.”

“I lied.”

Wilson looked up.

“I never changed it. Didn’t have strength to go downtown Monday, and by Tuesday I didn’t have strength to go downstairs. This house comes to you. Money too. There ain’t a fortune, but there’s enough to keep the roof and help along.”

“Aunt Candace—”

“You listen. The parlor set stays. Your mother can have the wax flowers if she wants ’em, but she ain’t moving in.”

Despite himself, Wilson made a broken sound of laughter.

“Alma,” Candace called.

Alma turned.

“You heard?”

“Yes.”

“You willing to marry this fool?”

Alma’s soft smile trembled.

“Yes.”

“He’s loud.”

“I know.”

“His mother’s worse.”

“I know.”

“You’ll have this house separate. That’s in the will. If he sells it without your signature, I’ll come back and sing under the window every night.”

Wilson gripped her hand.

“You won’t have to come back.”

“Likely I couldn’t reach the pedals.”

His face twisted. Candace looked at him with impatience and tenderness.

“Don’t cry, Wilson. It makes your nose red.”

“I’m not.”

“You always were a poor liar.”

She closed her eyes, and for a few minutes they thought she had fallen asleep. Then she said, “Alma.”

“Yes?”

“Sing something.”

Alma glanced at Wilson. “Now?”

“I ain’t asking for next Thursday.”

“What shall I sing?”

Candace considered.

“‘Abide with Me.’ Quiet. No organ.”

Alma removed her bonnet and set it on the chair. Without its trembling flowers she looked older and more resolute. She stood near the foot of the bed, where Candace could see her.

The first notes were low and nearly without vibrato. Alma’s voice had none of the brilliance Candace’s had possessed in youth. It was smaller, gentler, and warmer; it seemed made not to astonish a congregation but to enter the places grief had hollowed and remain there. Through the open window came the scent of lilacs and wet earth. A thrush answered once from the currant hedge, then was still.

Candace listened.

At first her mind moved critically, measuring breath, vowel, and tone. Alma took the second phrase with admirable steadiness. She did not force the rise. She allowed the words their meaning instead of using them as scaffolding for display. Candace, who had spent a lifetime knowing exactly what singers did wrong, felt an unwilling satisfaction.

The hymn filled the room and passed through the window over the common. A few villagers, seeing Wilson’s coat below and guessing what was occurring, stopped outside their houses. Mr. Tilton heard from the church steps. Mrs. Pike, carrying a covered bowl to Mrs. Bell, stood at Candace’s gate and began to cry.

Candace no longer thought of them.

As Alma sang, the walls of the bedroom appeared to loosen. The music gathered the distant church, the old choir loft, her mother’s sickroom, the train she had not taken, Christmas candles, wedding veils, black funeral gloves, applauding children, and all the Sundays laid one upon another like pages in a book. Nothing was restored to her, yet nothing seemed wholly lost. She saw that Alma’s song did not erase hers. A note sounded and vanished, but the air had been changed by it; perhaps a life was no different.

Her breath came shallowly.

Beyond Alma, through the window, one tall maple stood at the edge of the common. Sunlight, passing through the last smoke from North Ridge, kindled around its wet leaves. The tree seemed enclosed in a pale, transfiguring fire—not consumed, but revealed, every branch made clear by brightness.

Alma reached the final verse.

Candace’s hand relaxed in Wilson’s. The anger which had burned through her for days withdrew without leaving emptiness. She felt neither conquered nor replaced. She was only tired, and the tiredness was no insult now. It was deep water, cool and still, into which she might descend without anyone pressing her down.

Alma completed the hymn.

For a moment no one moved.

Candace opened her eyes.

“Very pretty,” she whispered.

Alma bent nearer. “What did you say?”

“I said—very pretty.”

Her gaze remained exact, alert, and faintly severe.

“Only,” she added, “you flattened the E.”

Then Candace Whitcomb smiled, and went where no choir could dismiss her.

# The Hammer of Israel

At the time we are going to speak of, about one hundred and eighty years before the birth of Christ, Judea was a small and troubled country lying between powerful kingdoms. Its people had returned long before from captivity, Jerusalem had been rebuilt, and in the Holy Temple the priests again offered sacrifice according to the Law of Moses. Yet the Jews were not free. Greek kings ruled the lands around them, and at last the throne of Syria came to Antiochus, who called himself Epiphanes, or “the illustrious,” though his deeds earned him a far less honorable name among those whom he oppressed.

Antiochus desired not merely the tribute and obedience of Judea. He wished to make its people Greeks in their speech, dress, amusements, and religion. Sad to say, some Jews were willing to forsake the customs of their fathers. They built places for Greek games, concealed the signs of their covenant, and were ashamed of the Holy Law. They thought advancement at court and the favor of a heathen king of greater value than faithfulness to God.

But Antiochus was not satisfied with willing apostasy. He determined that all should submit.

His officers entered Jerusalem, slew many of its inhabitants, plundered the city, and fortified the hill overlooking the Temple. Then the king came against the sanctuary itself. He carried away the golden altar, the candlestick, the table of shewbread, the vessels, the censers, and the precious ornaments dedicated by former generations. Upon the great altar of burnt offering he set up an idol, and swine were sacrificed where the offerings of Israel had been made. The courts were stained and deserted; weeds sprang between the stones; the songs of the Levites ceased; and the Temple, once the glory of the nation, became a place of shame.

The keeping of the Sabbath was forbidden. Circumcision was punished with death. Copies of the Holy Book were sought out, torn, and burned. Those who possessed them were slain, and mothers who obeyed the ancient command concerning their children were cruelly tortured. Faithful men and women were driven through the streets or put to death before the idols they had refused to worship.

The intention of Antiochus was plain: Israel must cease to be Israel.

In the little town of Modin, not far from Jerusalem, there lived an aged priest named Mattathias. He was descended from a noble priestly family and had five sons—John, Simon, Judas, Eleazar, and Jonathan. They were strong men, devoted to their father and to the covenant of their people.

One day the king’s officers arrived in Modin. They set up an altar and commanded the inhabitants to assemble. Since Mattathias was respected by all, the royal messenger spoke to him first.

“Thou art a ruler and an honorable and great man in this city,” he said, “and strengthened with sons and brethren. Come thou therefore first, and fulfil the king’s commandment, as all nations have done; and thou and thy house shall be numbered among the king’s friends, and shall be rewarded with silver and gold and many gifts.”

The old priest stood before his neighbors, while the altar of the false god waited for its sacrifice. Riches were offered him, and safety, and favor; refusal meant outlawry and perhaps death. But Mattathias answered in a loud voice:

“Though all the nations that are under the king’s dominion obey him, and fall away every one from the religion of his fathers, yet will I and my sons and my brethren walk in the covenant of our fathers. God forbid that we should forsake the Law and the ordinances. We will not hearken to the king’s words, to go from our religion, either on the right hand or on the left.”

Scarcely had he finished when a Jew, fearing the king more than God, stepped forward to offer sacrifice upon the forbidden altar.

The sight was more than Mattathias could endure. Filled with righteous anger, he rushed upon the apostate and struck him down beside the idol. His sons and friends fell upon the Syrian officers; the altar was pulled to pieces; and through the streets rang the cry:

“Whosoever is zealous for the Law, and maintaineth the covenant, let him follow me!”

Mattathias knew that Modin was no longer safe. Leaving houses, fields, and possessions behind, he fled with his sons into the mountains. Other faithful Jews joined them there, hiding in caves and narrow valleys, as David and his companions had once hidden from King Saul. They had little food and few weapons, but they possessed what the armies of Antiochus could not give and could not take away—a cause for which they were ready to die.

At first some of the fugitives carried obedience to the Sabbath so far that they would not defend themselves upon that day. A Syrian force discovered one band in a cave and attacked them. The Jews refused to lift a weapon, and men, women, and children were slain together.

When Mattathias heard of it, he grieved deeply; but he also saw that such conduct would destroy every faithful man in Judea.

“If we do all as our brethren have done,” he said, “and fight not for our lives and our laws, they will quickly root us out of the earth.”

It was therefore agreed that, if attacked upon the Sabbath, they might defend themselves. Faithfulness did not require them to surrender helplessly to murderers.

The little company now moved through the countryside, casting down idols, punishing traitors, rescuing the persecuted, and encouraging the fearful. Their numbers increased, while Mattathias, though old, remained their leader and father. But the hardships of the hills and the sorrows of his country weighed upon him, and before long he knew that he must die.

He called his five sons around him.

“Now hath pride and rebuke gotten strength,” he told them, “and the time of destruction and wrath of indignation. Wherefore, my sons, be ye zealous for the Law, and give your lives for the covenant of your fathers. Call to remembrance the deeds which our fathers did in their time; so shall ye receive great honor and an everlasting name.”

He reminded them of Abraham’s faith, Joseph’s endurance, Phinehas’s zeal, Joshua’s courage, Caleb’s testimony, David’s mercy, Elijah’s devotion, and the faithfulness of the three young men in the fiery furnace. Then he appointed Simon, a man of calm judgment, to be their counselor, and Judas, mighty and valiant from his youth, to be their captain.

“Let Judas be your leader,” said Mattathias, “and fight the battle of the people.”

Soon afterward the brave old priest died. His sons buried him at Modin, and all faithful Israel mourned. But they were to do something besides grieving. The charge of Mattathias remained with them, and Judas now took command.

Judas was called Maccabeus, a name commonly understood as “the Hammer,” though explanations of the name have differed. Certainly no title could have suited him better, for he was to fall like a hammer upon army after army sent against Judea.

He had at first only a small band, poorly armed and without the training of regular soldiers. Yet he knew every pass, ridge, and ravine of his own country, and his men trusted him. Before battle he prayed with them, reminded them of the covenant, and taught them that victory did not depend upon numbers.

The first Syrian commander of importance to meet him was Apollonius. Confident that the rebels could be crushed at one blow, Apollonius advanced into Judea. Judas chose his ground, attacked with sudden fury, and defeated him. Apollonius himself was slain, and Judas took his sword and carried it afterward in battle.

Then came Seron, another Syrian general, who gathered a larger army and marched by the ascent of Beth-horon, the very region where Joshua in ancient days had pursued the enemies of Israel.

When the Jews saw the multitude climbing toward them, some were afraid.

“How shall we,” they asked, “being so few, be able to fight against so great a multitude and so strong, seeing we are faint with fasting?”

Judas answered, “It is no hard matter for many to be shut up in the hands of a few; and with the God of Heaven it is all one to deliver with a great multitude or a small company. For the victory of battle standeth not in the multitude of an host, but strength cometh from Heaven.”

The words put courage into their hearts. Judas charged down upon Seron’s men, broke their ranks, slew their commander, and drove the survivors from the pass.

News of these victories reached Antiochus, and the king was enraged. He ordered a great force to be assembled under Nicanor and Gorgias, commanders who expected not only to destroy Judas but to sell the captured Jews as slaves. Merchants came to the Syrian camp with silver and chains, so certain were they that thousands of prisoners would soon be offered for sale.

Judas gathered his men at Mizpeh, where Samuel had once judged Israel. The people fasted, clothed themselves in sackcloth, spread out the books of the Law for which they were fighting, and prayed. They had no Temple in which to worship, for the sanctuary was polluted and Jerusalem held by the enemy. Therefore the open sky was their roof, and their tears fell upon the earth as they cried to Heaven.

The Syrian host lay at Emmaus. Its tents covered the ground, its shields and helmets glistened, and its campfires shone through the darkness. And the huge army of enemies was ever coming nearer!

Gorgias planned a night attack upon the Jewish camp; but Judas learned of it. Leaving his fires burning, he quietly led his men away. Gorgias reached the camp, found it deserted, and searched the surrounding hills, believing the rebels had fled.

Meanwhile Judas marched straight upon the main Syrian camp at Emmaus.

“Fear ye not their multitude,” he told his men, “neither be ye afraid of their assault. Remember how our fathers were delivered in the Red Sea when Pharaoh pursued them with an host. Let us cry unto Heaven, if perhaps the Lord will have mercy upon us.”

At dawn they attacked. The surprised Syrians were driven from their tents, and Judas’s men captured weapons, food, treasure, and armor. Then they saw Gorgias returning from the hills. Smoke rose behind the Jews from the burning camp, and the Syrians understood that their army had been beaten. They lost heart and fled.

The merchants who had come to buy Jewish slaves fled with them, leaving their money behind. Thus the price intended for the captivity of the faithful became spoil for their deliverers.

The next year Lysias, to whom Antiochus had entrusted affairs in the western provinces, sent an even greater army into Judea. It came from the south, where the open country favored the movements of regular troops. Judas met it near Beth-zur. Again his force was greatly outnumbered; again he prayed; and again the Syrians were defeated. Lysias withdrew, intending to return with still more soldiers.

Now the way to Jerusalem lay open.

When Judas and his followers entered the city, their joy was mixed with sorrow. They saw broken gates, deserted courts, bushes growing where worshippers had once assembled, and chambers blackened by fire. The altar was profaned, the sacred vessels were gone, and the hill above the Temple was still occupied by a hostile garrison.

The warriors tore their garments, cast ashes upon their heads, and wept aloud.

But they had not fought merely that they might stand among ruins and lament. Judas appointed priests who had remained faithful, and the work of purification began. The polluted altar was pulled down, and its stones were stored apart until it should be known what ought to be done with them. A new altar was built of unhewn stones. New holy vessels were fashioned, the lampstand was restored, incense was burned, curtains were hung, and the doors were repaired.

On the twenty-fifth day of the month Chislev, in the year 164 B.C., sacrifice was again offered according to the Law. Lamps shone in the Holy Place; hymns were sung; harps, cymbals, and other instruments sounded through the courts; and the people bowed in worship. For eight days they celebrated, carrying branches and giving thanks that the sanctuary had been restored.

It was appointed that this Feast of Dedication should be kept every year. In later times it was called Hanukkah, the Festival of Lights, and its lamps reminded the children of Israel that faith had not been extinguished, even in the darkest hour.

Yet the rededication of the Temple did not end the war. Antiochus died far away, but other rulers inherited his kingdom and his quarrels. Lysias returned with a formidable host, including cavalry and elephants trained for war. Upon the backs of the great beasts stood wooden towers filled with armed men, and soldiers moved beside them to protect their legs.

The sight was terrible to those who had never met such creatures in battle.

At Beth-zechariah, Judas and his brothers resisted the advancing army. Eleazar, one of the five sons of Mattathias, saw an elephant larger and more splendidly equipped than the others. Supposing that it carried the king, he determined to strike a blow that would save his people or at least inspire them.

He forced his way through the soldiers, darted beneath the elephant, and thrust upward with his weapon. The wounded beast fell, crushing Eleazar beneath its enormous weight.

Thus died a valiant son of Mattathias. His deed did not win the battle, for the Syrian army continued its advance, and the Jews were forced back toward Jerusalem. But Israel remembered that Eleazar had counted his own life as nothing when the freedom of his brethren was at stake.

The Syrians besieged the stronghold by the Temple, and food became scarce. It seemed that all Judas had gained might be lost. But disputes within the Syrian kingdom suddenly called Lysias away. Peace was offered, and the Jews were promised liberty to observe their religion.

For a little while the nation rested.

This did not, however, last long. A new king gained the Syrian throne, and with him came Alcimus, a Jew who desired to be high priest but cared more for power than for faithfulness. He was willing to obtain his office from foreign hands. Some among the people accepted him, hoping that peace could be purchased by submission; others knew that a man supported by their enemies could not safely guard the sanctuary.

Thus division, more dangerous than foreign armies, entered Israel.

Nicanor was again sent against Judas. At first he attempted deceit, speaking fair words while planning to seize the Jewish leader. Judas discovered the plot and withdrew. Then Nicanor came openly to Jerusalem and threatened that, unless Judas were delivered to him, he would burn the Temple.

The priests pointed to the offerings and pleaded that the innocent sanctuary should not suffer.

But Nicanor stretched out his hand against the holy place and boasted of what he would do after his victory.

The victory was never his. Judas met him near Adasa, and before the battle the faithful prayed that the blasphemous threat might be answered. Nicanor was slain, his army scattered, and the hand he had raised against the Temple was displayed as a warning. The day was afterward kept as one of rejoicing.

Yet every victory had cost men whom Judas could not replace, while Syria possessed new armies beyond counting. Judas sought aid from Rome, whose power was growing throughout the Mediterranean world. An agreement was made, but Roman help did not arrive in time.

King Demetrius sent Bacchides into Judea with twenty thousand foot soldiers and two thousand cavalry. Judas had only about three thousand men when the armies approached Eleasah. As the vast Syrian lines spread across the plain, fear seized many of the Jews. Some slipped away, and then others followed, until only eight hundred remained beside their leader.

Those who stayed looked upon the enemy and knew what must happen. They begged Judas to withdraw.

“We are not able,” they said, “to stand against so great a host. Let us now save our lives, and return afterward with our brethren; for we are but few.”

Judas might easily have fled into the hills. He had escaped greater armies before. He was the hope of the nation, and many would have said that prudence required him to preserve his life.

But to retire now, when eight hundred loyal men had remained because they trusted him, seemed to Judas a dishonor.

“God forbid,” he answered, “that I should do this thing, and flee away from them. If our time be come, let us die manfully for our brethren, and let us not stain our honor.”

There was no boast in the words and no hope of an easy miracle. The enemy stood before them in ordered ranks, cavalry guarding the wings and experienced soldiers filling the center. Miracles were over. Duty remained.

Judas saw that Bacchides commanded on the right and threw himself against that part of the line. The eight hundred charged with such courage that the Syrian right wing gave way, and Judas pursued it toward the hills. But the Syrian left turned and closed behind him. The faithful band was surrounded.

All day the battle raged. The little company grew smaller; one brave man after another fell; and still Judas fought with the sword of Apollonius in his hand. At last he too was struck down, and the remnant of his followers perished around him.

It was a defeat. Yet there are defeats more glorious than victories.

As the Spartans at Thermopylae had once stood in a narrow pass against the might of Persia, so Judas and his eight hundred refused to save themselves by abandoning the cause entrusted to them. Their bodies lay upon the field at Eleasah, but their courage passed into the heart of a nation.

Jonathan and Simon recovered their brother’s body and carried it to Modin. There, in the tomb of Mattathias, Judas Maccabeus was buried. Israel mourned him and cried:

“How is the valiant man fallen, that delivered Israel!”

The sorrow was bitter, for Judas had been captain, judge, comforter, and example. He had found his country bowed before idols and had raised it to resistance; he had taken frightened fugitives and made them soldiers; he had entered a polluted Temple and left its lamps burning. He had defeated generals, scattered hosts, and taught the weakest that numbers alone do not decide the justice of a cause.

But the sons of Mattathias had still something to do besides grieving.

Jonathan gathered the faithful and continued the struggle through years of danger, diplomacy, victory, and betrayal. When he was treacherously captured and killed, Simon, the wise counselor appointed long before by his father, became the leader. Patient, steadfast, and experienced, he secured the strong places of Judea, drove out the foreign garrison from Jerusalem, restored order, and at last won acknowledgment of Jewish independence.

Then the people declared that Simon should be their governor and high priest until a faithful prophet should arise. The decrees concerning him were engraved upon tablets of brass, and Judea, after long oppression, was free.

Not all the years that followed were peaceful, nor were all later rulers as noble as the men who had suffered in the mountains. Human victory is never perfect, and earthly freedom must be guarded by each new generation. But the purpose for which Mattathias had risen at Modin was accomplished. The Law survived. The covenant was kept. The Temple stood purified, and its lamps continued to shine.

Antiochus had believed that a command could destroy faith, that torture could silence Scripture, and that an idol placed upon an altar could make a people forget their God. He had armies, treasure, officers, fortresses, and elephants. Against him at first stood one old priest and five sons.

The king’s empire passed away. The light endured.

And year after year, when the lamps of Dedication were kindled, fathers told their children of Mattathias, who would not bow; of Eleazar, who gave his life beneath the war elephant; of Jonathan and Simon, who completed the work; and above all of Judas the Hammer, who chose honor before safety and faithfulness before life.

For this was the lesson of the Maccabees: a sanctuary may be plundered, sacred books may be burned, and the faithful may be hunted into caves; but so long as even a little band remains loyal-hearted and unafraid, tyranny has not conquered. The few may fall, yet their work is not lost. Their sacrifice becomes the price of freedom, their memory strengthens those who follow, and from the ruins the holy lamps are lighted again.

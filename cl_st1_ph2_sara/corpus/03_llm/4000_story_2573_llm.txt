# Sailor Jack and the Invincible Device

I joined Kelly’s Army because it was going my way, because I was hungry, and because two thousand men are harder to arrest than one.

These reasons occurred to me in the order named, though in later years I promoted hunger to first place. I was called Sailor Jack, not because I had any profound affection for the sea, but because I knew enough of ships to deceive landsmen and enough of landsmen to support myself ashore. I had drifted out of California with the great industrial tide of that season—a tide composed chiefly of men whom industry had forgotten. We were miners without mines, carpenters without lumber, railroaders without roads, and philosophers without breakfast.

General Kelly commanded us. He was a proper general, possessing a beard, a voice, and the difficult art of looking confident when two thousand empty stomachs asked what came next. The organization was military. We had companies, captains, colonels, bugles, guards, roll-calls, orders, counter-orders, and punishments. We lacked rifles, pay, uniforms, ammunition, horses, tents, and a government, but these are merely details. No army has everything.

Our purpose was to travel east and present ourselves in Washington as evidence. Of what, opinions differed. Some said we were evidence that the nation needed reform. Others said we were evidence that the railroads were tyrants. The railroads held that we were evidence in favor of stronger police forces. Personally, I was evidence that a young man can go a long way on very little, provided the very little belongs to somebody else.

We came upon Council Bluffs in force and expected transportation.

The railroad men expected us.

That was our first difficulty.

A body of unemployed citizens may capture a freight train if there is a freight train to capture. But the companies had considered the matter with a cold intelligence superior to ours. They emptied yards, switched engines away, locked tool-houses, guarded bridges, and sent their rolling stock east, west, north, south, and possibly upward. We charged a wilderness of rails. There were switches enough to carry us to the ends of the earth, but nothing on them except cinders and policemen.

Here and there an engine whistled beyond reach, insulting us from the horizon. A train would thunder past without slackening, the crews leaning from cab and caboose to inspect the national problem at leisure. We shook fists, caps, and blankets at them. They waved back. The exchange of international courtesies ended there.

Committees parleyed. Speeches were made. Promises multiplied. At last we were told that a train would be provided. Hope ran through the camp, and every man rolled his blanket and calculated the best method of securing a side-door Pullman. We marched to the appointed place.

There was a train.

It would have accommodated perhaps four hundred men if the four hundred had been thin, affectionate, and willing to stand on one another. We were two thousand, and affection had worn out during the march from camp.

The authorities suggested that the first portion embark and the rest await another train.

“Which portion is first?” somebody demanded.

Then every man became first.

The ranks dissolved. Companies flowed toward the cars. Officers shouted. The bugles blew advance, halt, assembly, and several things not found in the manual. Men climbed wheels, rods, roofs, ladders, bumpers, brake-beams, and one another. A hundred seized the same box-car door. The car gave a groan like an animal in pain.

Then word came that the train was not ours after all.

There was no first portion. There was no second train. In a few minutes there was hardly a first train, for the engine was withdrawn and the cars scattered with admirable railroad strategy. We were left in possession of the right of way, which is a valuable property unless one desires to go somewhere.

So Kelly’s Army marched.

We went into Iowa on our feet, throwing them east one after the other. Two thousand pairs of feet make a notable show in the morning and a still more notable complaint at night. The road stretched before us through fat farming country, over rolling fields newly green, between fences that seemed invented solely to prevent a hungry man from reaching a hen-house. Dust rose in fair weather. Rain turned the dust to paste. Sleet caught us in low camps, and wind prowled through our blankets as though searching for valuables.

But Iowa treated us royally.

The people came out to see the Army pass. School children waved flags. Farmers brought wagons loaded with bread, meat, potatoes, onions, coffee, milk, and pies. Bands met us at town limits. Orators welcomed us as honest workingmen and defenders of liberty, which caused several old profesh stiffs in the ranks to look embarrassed. At night there were bonfires, songs, speeches, and sometimes dancing. A town that feared us at sunrise was feeding us by noon and boasting of us by sunset.

We learned the capacities of communities with military accuracy. One village was good for bread and side-meat; another specialized in boiled eggs; a third possessed a ladies’ committee that could mobilize coffee for two thousand men inside an hour. In some places the girls pinned flowers on our coats. In others the constables counted the spoons.

The Army marched on, eating Iowa from west to east.

Do not misunderstand me, gentle reader. We did not devastate the country. We merely caused it to disappear in sections. A thousand loaves here, six hundred pounds of beef there, barrels of potatoes, tubs of beans, buckets of milk, mountains of sandwiches—these passed into us and left no archaeological remains. We were young, outdoors, and walking twenty miles a day. Hunger was not an appetite with us. It was a branch of the service.

At every halt the cooks attacked their kettles. Smoke went up, tin cups rattled, and the companies formed in lines, each man guarding his place with the gravity of a banker guarding a vault. One ate what was given, then explored for what had not been given. I acquired skill at discovering unattached pies.

The hospitality continued until we neared Des Moines. By that time our feet were blistered, our shoes were declining public office, and the Army’s enthusiasm for pedestrian reform had vanished. A man may march for justice, but after several hundred miles he begins to suspect that justice ought to provide wheels.

Des Moines received us with civic dignity, fed us, camped us, addressed us, and expected us to depart.

We did not depart.

General Kelly ordered the Army to march.

The Army declined.

The refusal was not mutinous in appearance. No swords flashed, for we had none. No barricades arose. The men simply remained seated. Two thousand tramps sat upon the ground and developed an immovable respect for principle.

A committee of public safety visited headquarters. Its members wore good coats and the expressions of men who had discovered a large, unprofitable fact.

“When will your men leave?” the chairman asked.

“When transportation is furnished,” said Kelly.

“The city cannot furnish transportation.”

“Then the men cannot leave.”

“You brought them here.”

“They walked here.”

“Let them walk out.”

Kelly looked across the camp. The Army, informed that history was being made, cheered feebly and continued sitting.

“They have voted with their feet,” said the General.

This was inaccurate. Their feet had resigned.

The committee argued. Kelly remained military. The committee threatened. Kelly became constitutional. They pointed out that the city was feeding us. Kelly agreed and praised the city. They stated that this could not continue. Kelly said that hunger could.

Then he gave them an ultimatum. If transportation were not arranged within a stated time, the Army would march into the business district and camp there.

“On the streets?” gasped the chairman.

“On the streets.”

“Two thousand men?”

“Count them.”

The committee counted mentally and departed at speed.

Meanwhile we ate.

Des Moines supplied breakfast, dinner, and supper. Do a little arithmetic. Two thousand men multiplied by three meals make six thousand meals a day. Multiply that by several days, allowing something for the men who went through twice under altered sky-pieces, and you will understand why taxpayers who had resisted the provision of a train suddenly developed an interest in navigation.

And still we ate.

We ate in sunshine and rain. We ate while committees met. We ate while ministers prayed for a peaceful solution. We ate while merchants subscribed funds to obtain that solution. We ate until civic generosity became pale about the lips.

The solution was flatboats.

The Des Moines River ran conveniently away from the city, and the city fathers, after looking at the river and then at us, perceived the hand of Providence. Lumber was bought. Carpenters were hired. Hammers rang day and night. Great rectangular scows took shape—rough boxes with shallow bottoms, built not so much to float well as to float us elsewhere.

The Army watched their construction with interest. We had become marines without passing an examination.

When the fleet was ready, provisions were loaded, companies assigned, officers posted, and orders issued concerning discipline, spacing, landings, supplies, camp routine, sanitation, scouting, straggling, singing, smoking, and the proper method of drowning under military authority. General Kelly intended the expedition to proceed as a fleet. No boat was to race ahead. No company was to stop without orders. All donations were to be gathered and distributed fairly.

The arrangements were excellent.

Company L ruined them before noon.

I belonged to Company L, and among its members were nine men who, like myself, possessed initiative. That is the word businessmen use when the enterprise succeeds. When it fails, the word is theft.

We observed that the towns downriver had been warned of the coming Army and were preparing food. We further observed that the first boat to arrive would encounter full tables, enthusiastic committees, and unexhausted women with baskets. The last boat would obtain speeches and coffee grounds.

Ten of us held a conference.

“Why should the General send an advance boat?” said the Boiler-Maker.

“He hasn’t,” I replied.

“That’s his mistake.”

Thus was strategy born.

We chose a light boat, secured poles, paddles, and one red cloth that might be mistaken at a distance for headquarters bunting. Before the main fleet got properly under way, we pulled ahead. We worked like galley slaves whose galley was nearing dinner.

At the first town we landed with official gravity.

“Advance boat of Kelly’s Army,” I announced. “Special detail.”

The citizens cheered. A band played. A minister shook our hands. The relief committee led us to tables loaded with ham, bread, cakes, pickles, cheese, beans, coffee, canned fruit, and pies so noble that memory still salutes them.

“Where is the Army?” they asked.

“Close behind,” we said, which was true in a broad geographical sense.

“And how many are in the advance?”

“Ten.”

“Only ten!”

Their compassion was tremendous.

We ate. Then we explained that the main body required strength for navigation and that certain supplies should be entrusted to us for official delivery. This produced sacks of bread, smoked meat, tins of coffee, sugar, butter, onions, cakes, and several blankets. We loaded until our advance boat became low in the water and high in patriotism.

Then we shoved off just as the first legitimate craft appeared around the bend.

At the next town we repeated the performance. We represented headquarters, scouting command, river reconnaissance, emergency commissary, and once, when pressed, the Department of Hydrographic Intelligence. The title opened every pantry.

Town after town received us first. We skimmed the cream, swallowed part of it, and stowed the rest. Behind us came Kelly’s two thousand, finding depleted tables and committees that said:

“But we gave your advance boat everything!”

Poor General Kelly!

Messages overtook us. We were ordered to fall back. We failed to understand. A police boat approached, and we drifted into a side channel. An officer stood on the bank and waved a paper. We waved our caps and increased speed.

The river was not much of a river in places. It twisted, shoaled, divided, collected trees, mislaid its channel, and changed its mind behind sand-bars. But I was Sailor Jack. I had salt water somewhere in my past, and to men who had never seen anything larger than a horse trough I was a combination of Columbus and Neptune.

“Starboard!” I would cry.

“Which side is that?”

“The other one!”

We struck snags, grounded, spun broadside, and backed through willows. Once the Boiler-Maker poled with such vigor that the pole remained in the mud while he entered the river. We hauled him out without interrupting lunch.

For several days we prospered. Then Kelly adopted mounted warfare.

Horsemen rode ahead by roads paralleling the river. They reached the towns before us and announced that ten graceless individualists were masquerading as an official advance. No provisions were to be given them. No coffee. No bread. Not even information concerning the next town.

We arrived at a landing and found a committee waiting.

“Kelly’s Army?” asked the chairman.

“Advance department,” said I.

“You are the ten rogues.”

“Sir, you wound the service.”

“We have orders.”

“Military orders do not bind civilians.”

“These bind us.”

They gave us water.

At the next town we received a lecture. At the third we received a view of locked kitchen doors. We had been coppered. Our occupation was gone. A man can live by begging when the public is uninformed, but when mounted messengers advertise him in advance it becomes impossible to make an honest living.

We held another conference.

“We’ll go back,” said the Boiler-Maker.

This was called tactical retirement.

Company L received us without flowers. Our captain had been saving language during our absence, and he spent it all. He called us deserters, thieves, pirates, hogs, traitors, and enemies of organized labor. We admitted the first four as matters of interpretation, but the last two injured our dignity.

He ordered us under guard.

We held our third conference.

A revolution followed.

It was not a bloody revolution, though the captain desired blood and several of us were willing to let him furnish it. We rejected his authority, appealed to the natural rights of man, took possession of our blankets, and withdrew from Company L. Another boatload, having disputes of its own, joined us. We organized Company M.

Why M? Because it followed L and because nobody else had thought to claim it.

General Kelly, who had learned something, ordered our two boats chained together. This was intended to prevent racing, desertion, piracy, and independent thought. A stout length of chain joined bow to bow. Guards inspected it. Officers smiled. We were fixed.

For an hour we believed so too.

Then we met a shoal.

The first boat grounded with a long scrape. The second, still floating, swung in the current. Men seized poles and cursed the chain. Then somebody shouted:

“Everybody into the rear boat!”

We obeyed. The stranded boat, relieved of twenty men, lifted from the bottom. The loaded rear boat sank deeper but held in the channel. As the first floated, we leaped into it.

“One, two—go!”

The weight shifted. The second boat rose. Current caught both. We shot over the shoal.

There was a silence.

Then Company M looked at Company M with the solemnity of inventors.

We possessed an invincible device.

At the next obstruction we practiced it. The lead boat struck.

“Back boat!”

One, two—every man jumped aft. The bow lifted.

“Front boat!”

One, two—back we ran. The stern craft cleared.

The linked boats advanced like a gigantic measuring worm. A single boat, once grounded, needed poles, ropes, profanity, and time. We needed only legs. Smash! Shift! Float! Bang! Shift! Float! Over bars, past snags, through shallows we went, one, two, one, two, sixty men stampeding from boat to boat until the decks bent and the chain clanked.

What had been devised as a restraint became our engine of superiority.

We passed boats that had started hours before us. We passed officers waist-deep in water, heaving at stranded scows. We passed cooks rescuing kettles from submerged camp equipment. We passed one boat lodged against a tree, another sitting upon stones like a hen upon eggs, and three tangled together at a bend while their captains issued contradictory commands.

Company M saluted each with courtesy.

“Can we tow you, gentlemen?”

The answers lacked gratitude.

Soon we were again at the head of the fleet. The mounted messengers might warn the towns, but they could not prevent citizens from watching the river; and citizens, once they saw our double craft coming at scandalous speed, concluded that anything so energetic must be official.

We modified our claims. We no longer called ourselves the advance boat. We were a “special navigation unit.” This was entirely true.

Kelly sent police boats after us. These were manned by loyal companies with orders to stop, board, or tie us up. But a police boat could not catch us in shallow water. It would strike a bar, and its crew would jump out to push.

Then Company M began its dance.

“One, two!”

Our lightened boat rose.

“One, two!”

The other followed.

We slipped past while the police stood to their knees in the river.

“Halt!” they shouted.

“We can’t!” we shouted back. “Current’s too strong!”

“Come ashore!”

“No control!”

“You’re paddling!”

“Trying to save ourselves!”

We paddled desperately downstream. Behind us came threats, prayers, and one boot, badly thrown.

At a narrow rapid the river divided among boulders. Two police boats waited below, having somehow got ahead by wagon assistance. Officers occupied both banks. The passage looked closed.

Our captain—Company M had elected one who understood that authority consisted in agreeing with us—asked my opinion.

“Keep moving,” I said.

This is the safest advice at a crisis, because if disaster follows nobody has time to complain.

We entered the rapid. The first boat struck.

“Rear!”

One, two—whop! The bow lifted and slewed sideways.

“Forward!”

One, two—bang! The rear boat jumped free.

A police craft pushed out to intercept us. It grounded.

The other came from the opposite bank and struck the first. Their crews shouted and swung poles. We bore down upon them broadside.

“Stop!” yelled an officer.

“Chain’s broken!” I screamed.

It had not.

“We’re helpless!”

We were paddling like fiends.

The current caught our double contraption, turned it neatly between the two police boats, and shot us through with inches to spare. One officer grabbed for our gunwale. The Boiler-Maker stepped politely on his fingers.

“A thousand pardons!” he called.

Below the rapid we looked back. The police boats were crossed among the stones, each preventing the other from floating. Their men waved fists.

Company M stood and cheered until our own craft struck another snag.

Smash!

“One, two!”

So went our river campaign.

We acquired food, glory, enemies, and a reputation for seamanship. Of these, food was the most nourishing. The country along the river fed the Army generously, but Company M generally arrived in time for the warm portion. We ate fried chicken, ham, corn bread, biscuits, potatoes, preserves, custard pies, berry pies, mince pies, and cakes with architectural frosting. We carried away sacks and baskets for “distribution.” I will say for us that some of this food reached the fleet. Ten per cent must have done so. I would not rob memory of its virtue.

The remaining ninety per cent supported initiative.

Colonel Speed joined us before Keokuk. He was a distinguished officer and, unlike some distinguished officers, knew when transportation was going some. His business required that he reach the city ahead of the main command. Kelly’s staff boats had become entangled upstream; the regular scouts were delayed; the police craft were repairing dignity and bottoms.

The Colonel looked at our chained boats.

“Can you get me there first?”

“Colonel,” said I, “we can get you there before yesterday if the river holds out.”

He embarked.

We made a ceremony of it. A blanket became a headquarters seat. One man stood lookout. Another guarded the provisions from the Colonel. At every bar we performed the manoeuvre.

“One, two!”

The Colonel leaped with us.

Rank has weight in navigation, and we used his efficiently.

Near Keokuk the channel broadened and the current improved. We paddled, poled, shouted, shifted, and drove ahead. Town whistles began to sound. Crowds gathered at the landing. They expected General Kelly and two thousand men. Instead they received Colonel Speed, Company M, two chained scows, and Sailor Jack wearing a broken straw hat.

We landed amid cheers.

The Colonel stepped ashore as though this had been the plan from the beginning. That is an officer’s privilege.

Hours later the fleet came straggling in—one boat, another, three together, five separated by bends, men wet to the waist, blankets soaked, cooks wrathful, captains hoarse. Kelly arrived and saw us comfortably fed.

Poor General Kelly!

The Des Moines River delivered us at last into the Mississippi system, where our flatboat epic began to lose cohesion. A river broad enough for real steamboats reduces amateur admirals to their proper dimensions. Currents strengthened. Towns stood farther apart. Orders altered. Some men took steamers, some remained with boats, some crossed into Illinois, some drifted toward Missouri, and some simply disappeared between supper and roll-call.

The Army had held together across plains, railroad yards, rain, hunger, committees, and civic hospitality. Success began to dissolve it.

We moved down the Mississippi in fragments. The great brown water carried driftwood, barges, steam packets, and our remaining ambitions. At night lights shone along the banks. Whistles spoke far off in the dark. The river seemed to know where it was going, which was more than could be said for us.

At Quincy I went ashore to beg.

Quincy was strange ground, and a strange town is either a gold mine or a stone wall to a stiff. I cleaned the mud from my coat, straightened my sky-piece, and selected a residential street. Begging is not a matter of asking for food. Any fool can ask. The art lies in appearing to deserve the thing before the door opens.

At the first house a woman gave me bread and cold beef. At the second I obtained pie. At the third a man demanded whether I would work.

“Gladly,” I said.

He offered to let me split wood.

I had fallen into my own trap.

There was a pile beside the shed large enough to warm a courthouse through winter. I split for twenty minutes with such conscientious violence that the man became alarmed for his axe and paid me to stop. He gave me a quarter besides a meal.

At the fourth house I was mistaken for a relative of someone in the Army and received two shirts. At the fifth a dog opposed my program. At the sixth an old lady listened to my account of industrial depression, national transportation, river hazards, and military duty.

“You are a tramp,” she said.

“Yes, ma’am,” I replied, “but temporarily under commission.”

She laughed and filled a sack.

By evening I returned loaded like a relief expedition. My companions hailed me as the finest beggar west of the Alleghenies. I accepted the honor, though the Boiler-Maker disputed it on technical grounds, saying that splitting wood was labor and therefore disqualified the quarter.

At Hannibal the ten broke apart.

There was no quarrel worth recording. That is the way road friendships usually end. Men who have shared blankets, hunger, police pursuit, and the last bite of food will separate because one freight moves north and another south. No speeches are made. A man says, “So long,” catches a ladder, and becomes a memory swinging from the side of a box-car.

Company M ceased to exist by evaporation.

Some intended to remain with Kelly. Some had relatives east. Some found work, or said they had. One chased a rumor of free transportation to St. Louis. Another met an acquaintance from California and vanished into a saloon. Our captain resigned when he discovered that he had no company to command.

The Boiler-Maker and I deserted together.

I use the military word because it sounds brave. In fact, we slipped away after dark with our blankets and no permission whatever. The river had served us; now the rails called again. A man raised on side-door Pullmans may become an admiral for a month, but the whistle of a freight train will awaken his true profession.

Five of us gathered near the yards: the Boiler-Maker, Connecticut Shorty, Big Missouri, a thin fellow called the Professor because he could read Latin inscriptions on public buildings, and myself. We intended to reach Chicago.

Our campaign survives in a pocket diary I carried that year. The entries are not literature. Hunger seldom is.

**May 22.—Hannibal. Left camp after dark. B.-M. and Shorty with me. Rain. Slept under coal chute. No supper.**

**May 23.—Up at 4:10. Missed north freight. Copper saw us by roundhouse. Walked three miles. Got bread, milk, onions. Boarded extra at 2:35. Thrown off at first stop.**

**May 24.—Caught cattle train 6 A.M. Professor sick from smell or onions. Big Mo. lost hat. Lay over in weeds all afternoon. Passenger went by fast.**

**May 25.—Crossed river. Shorty separated while dodging yard bull. Waited two hours. No Shorty. B.-M. says move on.**

**May 26.—Freight at midnight. Rode rods twenty miles. Bad. Professor quit at small town, says job waiting. No job visible.**

**May 27.—Big Mo. took westbound by mistake. Saw him waving. B.-M. laughed until he fell off water tank.**

**May 28.—Only B.-M. and me. Got meal at farmhouse. Woman asked if we were Kelly men. Said no. She fed us.**

**May 29.—Passenger blind out of division town. B.-M. failed to catch. I stayed on. Could not get off. Alone.**

That is the diary. It tells the truth more cruelly than memory does.

Connecticut Shorty I never saw again. Perhaps he reached Chicago before me; perhaps he went south; perhaps he is still looking for us in the Hannibal yards. The Professor had no employment waiting. This I know because all professors claim employment is waiting when they are tired of traveling. Big Missouri’s westbound error may have carried him to the Pacific, where he could have joined another army coming east.

As for the Boiler-Maker, I saw him standing beside the track as my passenger gathered speed. He had missed the handhold, or distrusted it. He ran a few steps, then stopped.

“Chicago!” I shouted.

“Chicago!” he shouted back.

The train curved. He disappeared.

Here’s my hand, Boiler-Maker, wherever you landed. You were a graceless individualist, an accomplished liar, a dangerous boatman, and a friend.

I rode the blind baggage until daylight. The wind beat me, coal smoke filled my eyes, and every stop threatened discovery. At one station I crawled beneath the car while trainmen walked both sides. At another I shifted to the rear platform of a sleeper and became, for fifteen splendid miles, a paying passenger in everything but fact.

Then a brakeman found me.

“Ticket?”

“Mislaid.”

“Money?”

“Invested.”

“Where are you going?”

“Chicago.”

“Not on this train.”

He put me off in virgin farming country, where the nearest house stood beyond two fences, a ditch, and a pasture occupied by a bull. Have you ever climbed barbed wire while carrying a blanket roll and two buckets of milk? I have. The buckets were acquired at the farmhouse; the bull was acquired in the pasture. I saved the milk but sacrificed dignity and part of my trousers.

Train after train carried me north and east. I rode box-cars, gondolas, brake-beams, engine tenders, and once a car loaded with agricultural machinery whose sharp edges had been designed by enemies of travel. I walked when watched, begged when empty, hid when pursued, and slept wherever horizontal space offered itself.

Three days after losing the Boiler-Maker, I entered Chicago alone beneath a tarpaulin covering lumber.

The yards seemed endless—rails, switches, engines, smoke, towers, sheds, whistles, bells, and men with lanterns moving through it all as blood moves through a body. I crawled from the car black with soot and stiff in every joint. No band played. No committee welcomed me. Nobody asked for a speech from Sailor Jack, conqueror of Iowa shoals and inventor of linked navigation.

Chicago merely swallowed me.

I had crossed half a continent by the cooperation of thousands of charitable people, hostile railroads, improvised officers, frightened municipalities, muddy rivers, and my own unblushing nerve. I possessed a blanket, two shirts from Quincy, eleven cents, and the conviction that I had helped invent a new principle of river travel.

That last possession endured longest.

Years afterward I was talking with an old traveler who had spent time in China. The subject of shallow rivers arose, and I described Company M’s chained boats. I told him how we shifted all hands from one craft to the other—one, two!—lifting the stranded hull, then reversing the weight and floating its mate. I explained the shoals, the police boats, the rapid, Colonel Speed, and our triumphant entry into Keokuk.

He listened patiently.

“Yes,” he said. “Chinese boatmen have done that for centuries.”

Thus fell our patent.

We had not invented the invincible device. We had merely rediscovered it while stealing pies on an Iowa river. But I refuse to surrender all the glory. The Chinese may have possessed the method first; Company M possessed it when urgently required. There is a difference between an idea resting respectably in the world’s knowledge and an idea dancing under sixty hoboes while two police boats stick on a bar.

One, two! Shift the weight. Float the obstruction. Keep moving.

A man can travel a long way by that method.

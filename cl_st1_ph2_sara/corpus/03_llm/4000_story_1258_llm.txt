### THE LODGE AT BRENT

For five-and-thirty years Thomas Rockett had served as gardener at Brent Hall, and during twenty-seven of them he had lived with his wife in the ivy-covered lodge beside the west gates. His right to occupy the cottage had never been expressed in terms more durable than Sir Edwin Shale’s pleasure; nevertheless, custom had given it to the family almost the sanctity of inheritance. There the Rockett children had been born, there two had died, and there the three survivors had learned to distinguish between their own obscure station and the magnificence represented by a carriage rolling up the avenue.

Thomas himself had long ceased to work. Rheumatism, contracted in the damp of successive winters, had bent his shoulders and thickened his hands; his sight, too, was failing. Yet Sir Edwin allowed him a weekly payment, modest but regular, and Thomas continued to call himself the gardener. The actual duties fell to a nephew, who came from the village each morning, while the old man attended to seed lists, pottered in the greenhouse on fine afternoons, and spoke with authority of borders he could no longer reach.

Mrs Rockett regarded these arrangements as evidence of almost supernatural goodness in the Shale family. She remembered the late baronet, Sir Edwin’s father, who had once sent broth when Thomas was ill; she remembered Lady Shale’s predecessor asking after the children during an outbreak of scarlet fever; and she had stored such incidents in a heart which compounded loyalty with gratitude and fear. That the family might at any time lose the lodge was a thought too dreadful for ordinary contemplation. When it occurred to her, she warded it off by polishing the brass plate on the gate, or by repeating that the Rocketts had always done their duty.

Of the children, May had least sympathy with this loyal subordination.

She was the second daughter, twenty-six years old, and had for three years held an appointment at a private school for girls in North London. The salary was small; the work, according to May, demanded intellectual and moral qualities which the directors neither possessed nor recognized. She taught elementary science, assisted with English literature, and gave, upon occasion, a lecture on civic responsibility. Her true interests lay far beyond the schoolroom. She belonged to a women’s debating society, had attended meetings upon dress reform, temperance, municipal housing, and the higher education of her sex, and corresponded with several persons whose names appeared from time to time in progressive newspapers.

At home these attainments were summarized by Mrs Rockett in the phrase, “May has wonderful ideas.” Thomas, less easily impressed by abstractions, would say, “The girl’s doing well in London,” which pleased May only when repeated to neighbours. In private she complained that her parents had no conception of her position.

At the end of July she returned to Brent for a fortnight’s rest. The necessity of repose had been urged upon her, she said, by a medical student of advanced opinions, who understood the nervous exhaustion produced by educational work. She arrived with a canvas travelling-case, a parcel of books, a rolled chart illustrating the human respiratory system, and instructions concerning diet which at once disturbed the domestic economy.

“Tea mustn’t be allowed to stand,” she told her mother within five minutes of entering the kitchen. “It becomes positively injurious. And I ought to have fruit at breakfast. Nothing elaborate—only fresh fruit.”

“We’ve gooseberries,” said Mrs Rockett.

“Gooseberries stewed with half their weight in sugar! I mean fruit in its natural condition. An apple, or perhaps grapes.”

“Grapes are dear just now, May.”

“My health is surely worth a few pence.”

Mrs Rockett accepted the reproach, as she accepted most things from this brilliant daughter. The next morning she walked two miles in heat to procure grapes; May ate seven, declared them sour, and advised her mother to be more careful when purchasing.

Rest, as understood by May, involved considerable activity in everyone but herself. She required a table in the little bedroom for correspondence, another chair for her clothes, hot water at unusual hours, silence while she read, and conversation when reading had fatigued her. She announced that she intended to gather first-hand information about rural labour, though the two labourers she questioned proved disappointing. One grinned at her inquiry concerning economic independence, and the other said that he had never thought about it.

The countryside itself seemed to May deficient in intellectual stimulus. She walked briskly, wearing a plain straw hat, a stiff masculine collar and a narrow tie. Her movements had the decision of one who habitually entered rooms with business to transact; her voice, when she wished to impress, rose into a clear head-note which her family associated with London and higher learning. To the villagers she was civil if they showed intelligence, corrective if they did not. She had come home, she explained, not merely for herself, but to maintain touch with realities.

Brent Hall, though it dominated those realities, had altered since May’s childhood. Sir Edwin had married late, and his wife, who brought him money as well as force of character, had introduced a stricter conception of rank. Lady Shale was neither extravagant nor amiable. She paid wages punctually, subscribed to institutions when her name would appear on a list, and dismissed servants without anger. Her judgments had the polish and finality of metal.

Her daughter Hilda, now twenty-four, resembled her in self-possession, but possessed a bodily vigour which made the mother’s stillness seem almost artificial. Hilda rode, played hockey, bicycled great distances, and shook hands with an energy painful to elderly fingers. In another sphere she might have been praised for the same independence which May considered her own peculiar distinction. But Hilda had never been obliged to insist upon independence; it attended her with money, servants, and the unexamined right to inconvenience others.

The two young women had known each other as children, though “known” was a generous word for a relation consisting chiefly of Hilda’s issuing commands and May’s resenting them. Their paths had diverged. Hilda had been educated by governesses and traveled abroad; May had passed examinations. Each, hearing occasional reports of the other, felt herself superior on grounds the other would have found unintelligible.

On the fourth day of May’s visit, Mrs Rockett received a message that Lady Shale would permit them to call on Tuesday afternoon.

“Permit us!” May exclaimed, when the matter was announced at supper. “Surely you mean she has asked us.”

Mrs Rockett looked troubled.

“It was Mrs Poole who brought word. Her ladyship said she’d be at home at four, if we wished to pay our respects.”

“I have no respects to pay.”

“Oh, May!”

“Why should I pretend? Lady Shale has never shown the smallest interest in my work. She probably thinks a woman who earns her living has failed in life.”

Thomas shifted his swollen hands upon the table.

“Your mother always calls when you’re home. It’s expected.”

“Expected by whom?”

“Come, May,” he said, with an attempt at authority. “There’s no harm in being neighbourly.”

Neighbourliness was so grotesque a description of the relation between the Hall and the lodge that May laughed. Her father reddened. She observed this and became more reasonable, for she did not wish to wound him; she wished only to cure him of pusillanimous habits developed under a feudal system.

“I’ll go for mother’s sake. But I shall behave naturally. I cannot bow and scrape merely because people happen to possess land.”

“No one asks you to scrape,” said Mrs Rockett. “Only to be polite.”

May wore her best blouse for the call, though she remarked twice that sensible people judged one by character, not clothes. As they proceeded up the avenue, Mrs Rockett grew increasingly anxious about her daughter’s hat, which seemed to her too plain, and about her own gloves, which had been mended at the thumbs. May, on the contrary, walked with chin uplifted. The grey frontage of the Hall, its long windows and shallow steps, signified to her not magnificence but accumulated injustice. She had recently heard a lecture upon land tenure.

They were admitted by a footman who recognized Mrs Rockett without appearing to do so. In the drawing room Lady Shale sat beside a table laid with silver and porcelain. Hilda stood at the window in a bicycling skirt, one hand resting upon her hip; she had just returned from a ride and looked healthily warm.

Lady Shale extended two fingers to Mrs Rockett.

“So you have come. And this is your daughter from London?”

May bowed very slightly.

“Yes. I am engaged in education.”

“Indeed.”

The word neither encouraged nor rejected information; it left May with the sensation of having placed a parcel where no one intended to pick it up.

Mrs Rockett began to speak of Thomas’s health, the weather, and the roses near the south wall. Lady Shale replied with measured sufficiency. Hilda turned from the window and inspected May with candid curiosity.

“You teach, don’t you?”

“I am at present connected with a school.”

“Children?”

“Girls from twelve to eighteen. My concern is chiefly with the senior forms.”

“That must be frightful.”

“Not if one has any belief in the future of women.”

Hilda smiled, not offensively perhaps, but with a freedom which made offence unnecessary.

“I never could endure lessons. I wanted to be out. Do you bicycle?”

May had anticipated this question, for bicycles were now the common test of energy and modernity. She desired one ardently. A machine would have enabled her to appear at meetings without dependence upon omnibuses, and several women at the debating society had lately begun to arrive in rational costume. But even a used bicycle represented many months of cautious saving.

“I have had little opportunity,” she answered. “My time is too occupied.”

“You should make time. There’s nothing like it. I rode thirty-eight miles yesterday.”

“Exercise is valuable only when it does not become a cult.”

“Is it a cult?” Hilda glanced at her mother. “I thought it was a bicycle.”

Lady Shale’s mouth moved. Mrs Rockett hastened to admire the tea service.

Hilda continued, with the genial persistence of one who has never had to consider another person’s sensitive places. “You could get quite a decent machine for twelve guineas now. Of course, the one I’m riding cost more, but that was because it had to be specially fitted.”

“Twelve guineas,” May replied, “may appear a small sum to persons who have never been obliged to earn money.”

There was silence.

Mrs Rockett bent over her cup. Lady Shale slowly replaced the tongs in the sugar basin.

Hilda’s face altered, though her tone remained brisk. “Then I suppose you can’t afford one.”

May felt the blood rush to her forehead. The statement was true, and for that reason intolerable. To be denied the machine was misfortune; to have the denial exposed before her mother, the footman, and two women of wealth was humiliation.

“I can afford whatever is necessary to my life,” she said. “A bicycle is not necessary.”

“No,” said Hilda. “Not if one prefers walking.”

The call ended soon afterward. In the avenue Mrs Rockett murmured that she wished May had not spoken about earning money.

“Why? Are wages disgraceful?”

“No, dear, but her ladyship looked—”

“I am not responsible for Lady Shale’s looks. Hilda was deliberately insolent.”

“She didn’t mean anything, I’m sure.”

“That is what privileged people always say. They wound others and call it unconsciousness. I shall not go there again.”

Mrs Rockett sighed, longing to defend May and unable to approve her. At the lodge Thomas asked how they had been received. His wife said, “Very kindly,” with nervous haste. May went upstairs to write a letter in which she described the Shales as survivals, Hilda as a muscular barbarian, and herself as having maintained the dignity of educated womanhood.

Two days later, shortly after noon, May was returning from the village when she heard the quick bell of a bicycle behind her. The road descended toward the Hall gates, where the lodge stood within its green enclosure. May knew the bell before she turned; she had seen Hilda pass that morning in a grey skirt and straw boater.

The west gate, though heavy, was commonly left open during the day. Thomas had closed it an hour earlier because cattle were being driven along the road, and had forgotten to send his nephew to reopen it. May reached the small pedestrian gate and passed through. Behind her the bicycle slowed.

“Miss Rockett!”

May continued toward the cottage.

“Miss Rockett, would you mind opening the gate?”

She stopped. Hilda had dismounted and stood with one foot on the ground, the machine leaning against her hip. There was nothing excessive in the request. To open the gate would cost half a minute. A child might have done it and thought no more of the matter.

But May saw, or believed she saw, the whole social order compressed into that moment: Hilda waiting to ride through; herself expected to lift the latch; the Hall at the end of the avenue and the lodge crouching beside the entrance. Her mother would have run at once. Her father, crippled as he was, would have struggled out. That was precisely why May could not move.

“The gate isn’t locked,” she said.

“I know it isn’t. Will you open it?”

“You are nearer.”

Hilda stared. “I have the bicycle.”

“You can lean it against the wall.”

For several seconds neither spoke. Hilda’s face, flushed from exercise, became darker.

“Do you refuse to open the gate for me?”

“I don’t acknowledge any obligation to act as gatekeeper.”

“You live in the lodge.”

“My parents live here. That does not make me your servant.”

The word, uttered in May’s public voice, carried distinctly through the open kitchen window. Mrs Rockett appeared within, pale and startled.

Hilda gave a short laugh.

“No one could mistake you for a servant, Miss Rockett. A servant would have better manners.”

May stepped back through the wicket and approached her.

“And a lady, perhaps, would open the gate for herself.”

Hilda’s hand tightened on the handlebar. For an instant the similarity between them was almost comic: two young women, erect, vigorous, modern in dress and temper, each persuaded that submission would be fatal. Then Hilda wheeled her machine to the wall, thrust the large gate open, and rode away without another word.

Mrs Rockett came hurrying from the cottage.

“May! May, what have you done?”

“I have declined to be treated as a domestic.”

“But the gate! You know we’re to attend to the gate.”

“I do not attend to it. I am not employed by Sir Edwin.”

“Your father is—or was—oh, what will Miss Shale think?”

“She will think, perhaps for the first time in her life.”

May entered the house with a sensation of victory not wholly free from sickness. She drank water, spoke contemptuously of the incident, and spent the afternoon reading without knowing what she read. At tea no one mentioned Hilda. Thomas had heard the exchange and was gloomy, but his daughter’s assurance restrained his blame. These London ideas passed his understanding; perhaps manners had changed.

At six o’clock the Hall footman arrived with a letter.

He did not enter. Mrs Rockett took the envelope at the door, saw Lady Shale’s hand, and began trembling before she had broken the seal. The communication was brief. In consequence of Miss Rockett’s gross impertinence to Miss Shale, Sir Edwin and Lady Shale could no longer permit the family to occupy the lodge. They were requested to make arrangements to leave within one month. Thomas’s weekly allowance would cease on the same date.

The old man read the letter twice. Then he sat down.

Mrs Rockett began to cry noiselessly, wiping her face with the corner of her apron. May stood beside the table, one hand upon the back of a chair.

“This is absurd,” she said. “It is an attempt at intimidation.”

Thomas looked at her.

“Intimidation?”

“She cannot turn you out for such a reason. Sir Edwin will never allow it.”

“It’s his house.”

“You have served them all your life.”

“Aye.”

“And your illness was caused by their work.”

Thomas shook his head. “No proving that.”

“There are obligations apart from law. Public opinion—”

“What public?” he asked.

The simplicity of the question checked her. She thought of the village, where Lady Shale’s displeasure had material consequences; of the newspaper, which would not concern itself with a gate; of her progressive acquaintances in London, who might pass a resolution and forget it. Public opinion seemed suddenly remote from Brent.

Mrs Rockett sank into a chair.

“Where shall we go? Thomas, where can we go?”

“We’ve a month.”

“A month! And no money coming in. Jack’s cottage has only the two rooms, and Lizzie’s husband wouldn’t—he has his own mother—”

“Don’t upset yourself,” said May sharply. “Nothing is settled.”

“It’s in writing,” Thomas replied.

She seized upon his passivity as something she could oppose. “Then you must go to Sir Edwin tomorrow. Speak to him privately. He has always been reasonable with you.”

Thomas folded the letter along its original lines. “I’ll go.”

May slept badly. At intervals she saw the gate, Hilda’s hand on the handlebar, and her mother’s face above the letter. Each time she reconstructed the incident in terms favourable to herself. She had defended a principle. She had resisted an insolent assumption. No decent person could expect an independent woman to run at the call of a landowner’s daughter. Yet with each defence came the image of her father’s bent back and the question: What public?

Thomas went to the Hall after breakfast. He wore his black coat, shiny at the elbows, and a collar which caused him discomfort. Mrs Rockett watched from the window until he disappeared behind the trees. May remained upstairs, ostensibly answering correspondence.

He returned in less than an hour.

“Well?” cried his wife.

Thomas removed his hat and placed it carefully on the dresser.

“Sir Edwin saw me.”

“And?”

“He’s sorry. Says he knows I’ve served long and faithful.”

“There!” May descended the last stair. “I knew he would be just.”

“He won’t go against her ladyship.”

The room became still.

Thomas sat near the cold hearth. “He says Miss Hilda was insulted at her own gate, and Lady Shale has made up her mind. He can’t interfere in household matters.”

“Household matters!” May repeated.

“He’ll give me a reference.”

Mrs Rockett uttered a low moan.

May walked to the window. Outside, the ivy moved gently in the warm air; through its leaves she could see the brass plate her mother polished every Friday. The cottage had always seemed to her cramped, inconvenient, and saturated with habits she had escaped. Now each defect acquired a dreadful value. The low ceiling, the worn stones, the narrow garden—all were security. Her parents, removed from this place, would not merely change their address. They would descend into dependence upon children hardly able or willing to support them. Thomas would lose the remnant of his occupation; her mother would lose the world in which deference had at least purchased peace.

“I shall write to Lady Shale,” said May.

Thomas looked up. “What will you say?”

“That her action is vindictive and out of all proportion.”

“Don’t,” whispered Mrs Rockett. “For mercy’s sake, don’t write.”

“Then what do you expect me to do?”

Neither answered. They had no language in which to make the demand forming in their thoughts. To ask May to apologize was to admit that she alone had endangered them; it was also to require a surrender they understood she would regard as degradation.

During the afternoon May went out by the road, avoiding the avenue. Pride still argued its case, but the advocate had lost fluency. She pictured herself returning to London while her parents packed their furniture. She could contribute perhaps five shillings a week; even that would mean abandoning lectures, subscriptions, and the hoped-for bicycle. Her elder brother might give something. Her sister would weep and explain that her husband objected. The family would say no word of blame. Their silence would follow May more persistently than accusation.

Before supper she made her decision.

“I am going to the Hall tomorrow morning,” she said. “I shall explain that there has been a misunderstanding.”

Mrs Rockett’s eyes filled with hope, which she tried to conceal.

“You’ll speak nicely, dear?”

“I shall speak with perfect propriety.”

“Perhaps—if you were to say you’re sorry—”

“I shall decide what is necessary when I am there.”

Thomas nodded, though he did not look reassured.

May intended to catch the twelve-fifteen train for London after the interview. Her fortnight had been reduced to nine days; she now needed, in truth, to escape and rest. She packed that evening, announcing that the school required her attention. Her parents did not question the statement.

At ten the next morning she walked toward the Hall. She had dressed with care in her dark skirt and best blouse, the stiff collar seeming tighter than usual. She approached the principal entrance, mounted the shallow steps, and rang.

The footman who opened the door listened without expression.

“I wish to see Lady Shale.”

“Her ladyship is engaged.”

“I shall wait.”

“What name?”

“You know my name.”

His eyelids shifted almost imperceptibly.

“I must ask, miss.”

“Miss Rockett. From the lodge.”

The door remained only partly open. “Persons from the estate are received at the side entrance.”

May felt the first blow physically, as if the steps had moved beneath her.

“I am calling upon Lady Shale.”

“Her ladyship is not receiving calls.”

“I have come on business.”

“Then the side entrance, miss.”

He closed the door.

For a moment May stood facing the dark wood. She could still turn away. She could write; she could refuse to participate in this calculated impropriety. But the lodge arose before her with terrible minuteness—the patched curtain, her father’s chair, the grape-stems left on her breakfast plate.

She descended and walked around the house.

At the servants’ entrance a kitchen maid admitted her after a prolonged examination. May repeated her request and was taken to a stone-floored hall, where three servants sat at a table finishing their morning meal. Conversation ceased.

“You can wait there,” said the maid, pointing to a wooden chair beside an umbrella stand.

May sat.

The servants resumed speaking in lower voices. A clock ticked above the door. From the kitchen came the clatter of pans, the hiss of water, and occasional laughter. No one asked whether she required anything. A young footman passed twice and looked at her openly the second time. The cook entered, received a whispered explanation, and said, “Oh, that one,” without lowering her voice enough.

Ten-thirty passed. Then eleven.

May’s train left at twelve-fifteen; the station was nearly two miles away. At first she calculated that she could remain until a quarter to twelve. Later she abandoned the calculation. Her hands, clasped upon her lap, had become damp inside the gloves.

The social theory of the situation was clear to her. Lady Shale intended to degrade an educated woman by classing her with petitioners and dependants. Servants, themselves oppressed by hierarchy, were being permitted the consolation of oppressing another. May could have analyzed the phenomenon brilliantly at the debating society. Here analysis did not prevent the heat rising into her face whenever the kitchen maid glanced at her.

At twenty minutes past eleven the footman appeared.

“Lady Shale will see you.”

May rose too quickly and felt dizzy.

She was led not by the direct passage but through a succession of corridors, across the main hall, and at length into the drawing room where she had taken tea. The distance seemed designed, though probably it was only customary; humiliation seldom required the trouble of invention when a house had been properly arranged.

Lady Shale sat at a writing table. Hilda stood near the fireplace, tapping one boot with a riding crop. Neither looked up when May entered.

The footman announced, “Miss Rockett, my lady.”

Lady Shale signed a letter. Hilda examined the crop handle.

May waited. The footman withdrew, closing the door with noiseless completeness.

At last Lady Shale raised her head.

“You asked to see me.”

“Yes.”

There should have been an explanation first: a calm account of differing assumptions, an appeal to proportion, perhaps a reference to Thomas’s service. May had prepared sentences in which dignity and concession were exquisitely balanced. None presented itself.

“I came about my parents,” she began.

“Your parents have been informed of our decision.”

“I know. That is why I came. They are old, and my father is not able to work.”

Lady Shale’s gaze remained coldly attentive.

“Those facts were considered.”

May’s throat tightened. “I wished to say that the incident at the gate has been—has perhaps been interpreted too seriously.”

Hilda spoke without moving. “You called me no lady.”

“I said a lady would open the gate for herself.”

“Precisely.”

“I was irritated.”

“So was I.”

The parallel, though accurate, was inadmissible because only one irritation possessed the power to evict a family.

May looked from daughter to mother. “If my manner was discourteous, I regret it.”

Lady Shale leaned back.

“If?”

The single syllable stripped the sentence of its protective qualifications. May’s heartbeat became painfully distinct.

“I mean—I regret that I was discourteous.”

“To whom are you speaking?” Lady Shale asked.

May did not understand at once. Then she understood perfectly.

“To you.”

Lady Shale’s brows lifted. “That is not the form in which persons from the estate have generally addressed me.”

Hilda turned her face aside, but not before May saw satisfaction there.

The room appeared unnaturally bright. May thought of leaving; indeed, her body made a slight movement toward the door. Pride urged that no material advantage could justify what was being demanded. But pride had no cottage to offer Thomas, no income, no bed for her mother.

“I beg your pardon—my lady.”

Her voice was scarcely audible.

Lady Shale waited.

May drew breath. “I came to apologize to Miss Shale and to your ladyship. I behaved rudely at the gate. I had no right to speak as I did.”

Hilda’s riding crop ceased tapping.

“Why did you?” she asked.

May looked at her. To answer truthfully—that the bicycle, the call, the difference in wealth, and Hilda’s unconscious command had wounded her vanity—was impossible. She sought a phrase and found only fragments.

“I thought—I have always believed that one ought not to submit merely because—because another person has a position. I did not consider my father. Or my mother.”

“No,” said Lady Shale. “You did not.”

“I am sorry.”

“For your rudeness, or for its consequences?”

May’s lips trembled. She pressed them together, furious at the bodily betrayal. She had lectured upon women’s need for self-command; she had despised tears as a refuge of the feeble. Yet tears now blurred the silver objects on the writing table.

“For both, my lady.”

“And what do you expect?”

“Nothing for myself.” Her voice broke, recovered, and broke again. “I ask that my parents may not suffer. My father has served Sir Edwin all his life. He is ill. They have nowhere else to go. If any punishment is necessary, it should fall upon me.”

Lady Shale’s face showed no change.

“You are no longer a child, Miss Rockett. We are not concerned with punishing you.”

The cruelty of this was in its perfect composure. May could have borne anger better; anger would have admitted equality of passion. Lady Shale merely corrected an error in terminology.

May turned to Hilda.

“I apologize to you. I ought to have opened the gate. I beg you to forgive me.”

Hilda studied her with frank, almost scientific interest. In their previous encounters May had been an irritation; she had now become a spectacle. Perhaps there was some discomfort beneath the young woman’s firmness, but money and maternal approval enabled her not to examine it.

“You were extraordinarily impertinent,” she said.

“Yes.”

“And at tea too.”

“Yes.”

“You seemed to think everyone meant to insult you.”

May swallowed. “I was mistaken.”

This was the hardest sentence of all, for it required not merely submission of manner but repudiation of judgment. Having uttered it, she felt suddenly weak.

Lady Shale gathered the letters before her.

“That will do.”

May remained standing.

“May I tell my parents—?”

“You may tell them nothing on our authority. Sir Edwin and I will decide what is proper.”

“But my train—”

Lady Shale glanced at the clock. “Your arrangements do not concern us.”

The dismissal was complete. May bowed—an awkward, involuntary movement deeper than any she had made during the call—and turned toward the door.

“Miss Rockett,” said Lady Shale.

She stopped.

“You may use the front entrance.”

May looked back, uncertain whether this was mercy or a final refinement. Lady Shale had already resumed her letters.

Outside the drawing room no servant waited. May crossed the hall alone, opened the great door, and descended into sunlight. The clock in the stable yard struck twelve. She could not reach the station; the train would leave without her.

At the bend of the avenue she sat upon a low stone bordering the shrubbery. Her knees trembled, and for several minutes she could not continue. Tears came then, quietly and with a violence that astonished her. She wept for humiliation, for anger, for the frightened faces at the lodge, and perhaps most bitterly because the apology had contained truth. She had not considered them. Her independence, admirable in theory, had drawn food and shelter from the very subordination she condemned.

When she reached home, Mrs Rockett ran to meet her.

“Well? Did you see her ladyship?”

“Yes.”

“What did she say?”

“She will consider the matter.”

“Did you—were you able to explain?”

“I said what was necessary.”

May passed upstairs before further questions could be asked. She washed her face, changed her collar, and announced that she would take the afternoon train. No one dared detain her. At three she left Brent with her canvas case and books, refusing Thomas’s offer to accompany her to the station.

Two days later a note came from Sir Edwin. Upon reconsideration, and in view of Thomas Rockett’s long service, the family might remain at the lodge; the weekly allowance would continue. Sir Edwin trusted there would be no recurrence of unpleasantness.

Mrs Rockett cried with relief. Thomas read the note aloud three times and declared that Sir Edwin had always been a gentleman. Neither knew whether May’s visit had influenced the decision. Mrs Rockett believed that Providence had softened Lady Shale; Thomas thought his own interview might have borne fruit after all.

In London May received the news at breakfast in the school boarding-house. Her mother’s letter overflowed with gratitude to the Shales and contained anxious hope that May had not missed her train. She folded it slowly.

That evening at the debating society she was invited to speak upon the economic dependence of women. Ordinarily she would have offered first-hand information, and the episode at Brent, modified to preserve her dignity, might have furnished an effective illustration of aristocratic tyranny. She said only that she was tired and allowed another member to speak.

Within a week her confidence had substantially returned. She corrected a colleague’s pronunciation, wrote to a journal about educational reform, and began calculating whether a bicycle might be purchased by installments. She had not become humble; so radical a transformation would have required more than one morning in a servants’ hall.

Yet she never told her parents what had passed in the drawing room. When her mother praised Sir Edwin’s kindness, May did not contradict her. When Thomas wrote that the ivy had been trimmed and the west gate newly painted, she experienced a satisfaction curiously unlike triumph. It was quiet, private, and almost painful.

For once, having done something for others, she felt no necessity to announce it.

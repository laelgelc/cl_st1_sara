### FROM THE PAPERS OF THE LATE REV. MAURICE O’DEVLIN, P.P.

My dear friend,

You ask me whether the native poetry of our peasantry possesses any quality beyond that grotesque humour which, by long exhibition upon the English stage, has come to be considered as exclusively Irish; whether our hedge-school masters, wandering harpers, rustic rhymers, and unlettered makers of songs have ever risen above whisky, faction-fighting, broken heads, unfaithful pigs, and the lamentable perplexities of the letter *h*. The inquiry is not unreasonable, because the misconception is ancient; yet I confess there is in it something which tries my patience more severely than becomes a minister of peace.

We have absurd verses—hundreds of them. What nation has not? Folly sings sooner than wisdom, and frequently much louder. But to infer from our comic songs that the Irish imagination is incapable of tenderness, dignity, or elevation, would be as just as to pronounce all Englishmen grave because one had read their tombstones.

I remember hearing, in my youth, a song composed by a poor schoolmaster upon the death of his wife. He lived beside a branch of the Shannon, in a cabin whose chimney smoked in every wind but the east; and he possessed, excepting a few books, a wooden stool, and three silver teaspoons inherited from an aunt, scarcely one of those conveniences which critics commonly regard as favourable to poetic refinement. Yet his lament began thus:—

> The moon is on the silent stream,  
> The mist is on the hill,  
> And through the white-thorn’s scattered bloom  
> The night-wind wandereth still.  
> But she who loved the moonlit wave,  
> And heard the waters flow,  
> Lies where the winter grasses bend,  
> And I am left below.  
>   
> The years go by like withered leaves  
> Upon the autumn rain;  
> The spring returns to every field,  
> But not to me again.  
> The bird may seek its vanished nest,  
> The stream its ocean find;  
> But I may call from dusk till dawn,  
> And hear but rain and wind.

There is nothing comic here, unless bereavement has become amusing in London; nor is there anything essentially rude, except perhaps that sincerity which fashionable sorrow usually corrects.

Our political verses are of another temper. They are violent, abrupt, redolent of gunpowder, old prophecies, green ribbons, and theological confidence. Their authors dispose of kings, judges, informers, and excisemen with a comprehensiveness which must surprise every constitutional lawyer. Still, even in their wildest denunciations, a passionate love of country breaks out with genuine force. I once copied from a ballad, sung at a fair under the innocent title of *The Green Linnet*, these lines:—

> May the red leaf fall and the black branch break,  
> Where the tyrant’s raven lies;  
> But God send dew for Ireland’s sake,  
> And sunlight to her skies!  
> For green was the field where her true men stood,  
> And green be their memory still;  
> They gave her the wine of their hearts’ best blood—  
> Lord rest them beside the hill!

It is unequal; but so are mountains, and no man objects to a mountain because it has not been measured with a ruler.

I grant, nevertheless, that our poets occasionally descend. One celebrated genius in this parish, being disappointed of a supper, declared:—

> There was beef in the pot, an’ a goose at the fire,  
> An’ praties enough to build Babel’s big spire;  
> But the beef it was tough, an’ the goose it was thin,  
> So I ate all the praties, an’ sorrow the sin!

This is not Homer; yet I have heard worse verses applauded because they were printed upon expensive paper.

The man, however, whom I principally desire to rescue—too late, alas!—from that oblivion which his own perverseness prepared for him, was Michael Finley, commonly called Mick the Poet. He could neither read nor write; or, to speak precisely, he knew the capital letters when they were large, black, and few, but entertained against the smaller ones a suspicion which familiarity never removed. He was a labourer by necessity, a fisherman by inclination, and a poet by constitutional misfortune. Verses came upon him while digging potatoes, mending nets, driving cattle, or lying under a hedge; and so tyrannical was the visitation that he would abandon any employment, however profitable, to follow a rhyme across three townlands.

Finley was small, broad-shouldered, red-haired, and remarkably ugly. His eyes, nevertheless, were bright and restless; and when he recited, his body appeared to enlarge, his stoop vanished, his voice deepened, and the poor labourer became successively lover, warrior, bride, priest, horse, thunderstorm, and entire wedding-company.

I frequently urged him to permit the transcription of his poems.

“Michael,” said I, “what will become of them when you are dead?”

“Why, plase your reverence,” he answered, “what becomes iv the smoke when the fire’s out?”

“That is precisely the difficulty.”

“An’ isn’t it natural for smoke to go?”

“But a song may deserve preservation.”

At this he shook his head with compassionate obstinacy.

“A song on paper,” said he, “is like Tim Doolan’s pigsty afther the surveyor measured it. There was the plan, sure enough, wid every stone in its place; but the pigs wouldn’t lie in it, an’ the rain came through the roof all the same.”

No argument could overcome this philosophy. He believed writing to be a species of enchantment by which a living song was flattened, chilled, and rendered incapable of change. If one reminded him that he altered his compositions at every recital, he claimed this as their chief excellence.

“Would ye have a boy wear the same breeches at forty that fitted him at seven?” he demanded. “Let the song grow, your reverence.”

It grew, accordingly, until no two persons knew the same version, and until Finley himself occasionally began one ballad, wandered into another, and concluded with a third. After his death, which occurred during the severe winter of 1816, his reputation declined with melancholy rapidity. For six months everybody quoted him; in two years the young people confused his songs with those of three other men; in ten, nothing remained but fragments, refrains, and the disputed assertion that he had once rhymed *orange*.

One complete production, however, I secured, though by a method which I shall neither defend nor repent. Knowing that remonstrance was useless, I concealed my nephew James in the loft of the barn where Finley had promised to recite his ballad of Phaudhrig Crohoore. James, who wrote swiftly, lay among the hay with ink, paper, and a guilty conscience, while Michael, below, delivered the piece to an audience of twelve, including myself and a blind piper. What follows has been corrected only where the transcriber’s haste made correction indispensable. I have retained the pronunciation, because to strip it away would be to hang an ancient Irish cloak upon a French tailor’s wooden figure.

### PHAUDHRIG CROHOORE

Oh, Phaudhrig Crohoore was the pride iv Glenmore,  
An’ the strength iv his arm was a proverb of yore;  
His shoulders wor broad, an’ his forehead was high,  
Wid the storm in his voice an’ the lightnin’ in his eye.

He could lift up a sack that would burden three men,  
An’ fling it, for sport, o’er the wall of the glen;  
He could breast the black flood when the Shannon rose hoarse,  
Or run from Kilcullen and tire a good horse.

But the hand that was iron, the voice that was flame,  
Grew gentle as dew at Kathleen’s sweet name;  
Kathleen O’Brien, wid her cheek like the rose  
That under the wild mountain hawthorn blows.

Her hair was as dark as a raven at night,  
Her brow like the moon when the young moon is white;  
An’ the sound of her foot on the floor, people say,  
Was lighter than rain on the blossom of May.

Phaudhrig loved Kathleen, an’ Kathleen loved him;  
But her father was proud, an’ her mother was grim.  
“A Crohoore!” cried the mother; “that beggar!—that brawler!  
We’ll marry ye first to the king’s own pig-caller!”

Now Michael O’Hanlon had houses an’ land,  
Two mills on the river, ten cows at command;  
He had guineas in bags, he had oats in the haggard,  
Though his heart it was crooked, his eye it was staggered.

O’Hanlon loved gold, an’ he hated Crohoore,  
For Phaudhrig had flung him one night from the door;  
An’ Kathleen he coveted less for her face  
Than to darken his enemy’s heart by the grace.

The bargain was settled, the wedding-day named,  
The barrels wor tapped, an’ the chimneys wor flamed;  
There were Murphys an’ Kellys, O’Briens an’ Moores,  
MacMahons from the mountain, an’ Byrnes from the moors.

There was roasting an’ boiling, an’ dancing galore,  
There was whisky behind every chest, every door;  
There was joking an’ blessing, an’ kissing an’ crying,  
Fiddles scraping, boys leaping, an’ old women trying.

The priest had come in, an’ the bridegroom stood proud,  
Wid a smile for the rich an’ a sneer for the crowd;  
While Kathleen sat pale as the mist on the river,  
An’ her fingers wor cold, an’ her lips all a-quiver.

“Cheer up!” said her mother. “The sorrow will pass.  
Take the luck that God sends ye, an’ look in the glass!  
There’s a house, there’s a dairy, there’s silver in store—  
What more could ye want?”—“Only Phaudhrig Crohoore.”

She spoke it so low that they thought it a sigh,  
But the priest turned his head, for he heard it go by;  
An’ O’Hanlon grew red, though he could not tell why,  
Like the sun in a fog on a black winter sky.

Then sudden outside rose a trample an’ shout,  
The dogs fell to barking, the boys tumbled out;  
The fiddle stopped short in the middle of “Rory,”  
An’ the door flew apart before Phaudhrig Crohoore.

Like a black cloud he came among candles an’ lace,  
Wid the rain on his coat an’ the wrath on his face;  
Not a word said the people, not one dared to stir,  
For the thunder had entered the house before him there.

He looked at O’Hanlon, he looked at the priest,  
He looked at the board and the smoke of the feast;  
Then he looked at Kathleen, an’ his voice, though it shook,  
Was as low as the wind in a cavernous nook.

“I ask no man’s pardon; I come for no fight;  
I come but to know if they do ye the right.  
By the cross on that wall, by the Heaven above,  
Are ye marrying freely the man that ye love?”

Kathleen rose slowly; she tried hard to speak;  
The tears gathered thick, but no word did they break.  
She looked at her father, whose brow had grown black,  
At her mother, whose eyes bade the poor girl turn back.

Then she looked at Crohoore, an’ the grief in her eye  
Was the grief of the earth when the last swallows fly;  
Her hand sought her bosom, her face turned snow-white,  
An’ he knew she was his before God by that sight.

“Ye’ve answered,” said Phaudhrig. “No tongue could say more.”  
“Stand back!” cried O’Hanlon. “Stand back from my door!  
She’s mine by agreement, by fortune an’ land;  
An’ I’ll prove it this minute wid stick or wid hand.”

He sprang like a wildcat, his cudgel swung round;  
The women shrieked murder, the men cleared the ground.  
But Crohoore raised no weapon, stepped only aside,  
An’ the stick smashed the bottles that stood by the bride.

Then Phaudhrig struck once—not in anger, men say,  
But as lightning strikes oak at the breaking of day.  
O’Hanlon went down, neither groaning nor crying,  
Wid his pride on the floor and the breath in him dying.

The crowd rose together like bees from a hive—  
“Seize Phaudhrig!” “Kill Phaudhrig!” “Is Michael alive?”  
But Crohoore took Kathleen as light as a flower,  
An’ leaped through the doorway in less than an hour.

(“A minute ye mean,” interrupted old Con at this point.

“An hour for the song, an’ a minute for Phaudhrig,” replied Finley, and continued.)

His horse by the gate pawed the mud into foam,  
A black mountain mare that could smell her way home;  
He set Kathleen up, sprang before her, and cried,  
“Grip fast round my heart, for there’s death at our side!”

Away went the mare through the wind an’ the rain,  
Down the stony boreen, through the mist of the plain;  
Behind them the wedding-folk shouted an’ swore,  
But the night swallowed Kathleen an’ Phaudhrig Crohoore.

They followed with lanterns, with horses, with men;  
They searched every road from the bridge to the glen.  
But the mare crossed the river where no man would dare,  
An’ the flood took the track as the wind takes a prayer.

At morning the mountains stood purple an’ still,  
The white mist was folded below on the hill;  
An’ far in a cabin where wild waters ran,  
Kathleen O’Brien woke the wife of her man.

For seven good years they had labour an’ rest,  
A child at the cradle, a hope in the breast;  
An’ Phaudhrig grew wiser, but never less bold,  
For courage, like fire, may be tempered, not cold.

Then Ninety-Eight came, an’ the country arose;  
The green flag was lifted in front of her foes.  
From cabin and forge, from the mountain and shore,  
The true men came marching—and Phaudhrig Crohoore.

“Stay here,” Kathleen whispered. “The child is but small.”  
“I would stay,” answered Phaudhrig, “if none heard the call;  
But the country that gave me my heart and my breath  
Has a claim on them both, whether living or death.”

He kissed her once softly, he kissed the child twice,  
Then strode to the ranks without turning his eyes;  
For the hardest farewell a brave heart ever knew  
Is the look that would weaken the thing it must do.

At the ford by the red field the pikemen stood fast,  
Though the guns cut them down and the horsemen rode past.  
Three times they wor broken, three times formed once more,  
An’ the front of the third time was Phaudhrig Crohoore.

His voice rose like thunder: “For Ireland! Close on!”  
An’ they charged through the smoke where the bayonets shone.  
He struck for the old, an’ he struck for the young,  
For the prayers never answered, the songs never sung.

They found him at evening beside the green flag,  
His hand on the staff and his head on a crag;  
An’ the soldiers who feared him, though victors they were,  
Stepped softly around him and left the flag there.

Oh, many die sleeping, an’ many die poor,  
But few die as bravely as Phaudhrig Crohoore;  
An’ long as the Shannon runs dark to the sea,  
Green Ireland, remember the bold an’ the free!

Such was Finley’s ballad. There are faults in it which the youngest reviewer may discover without assistance: extravagance, repetition, defective chronology, and a horse whose accomplishments approach the supernatural. Yet these blemishes belong, in some degree, to its character. The poem was not made for the lamp and the armchair, but for the crowded barn, the winter fire, the fair-day tent—for hearers who demanded that a strong man should be stronger than experience permits, that love should declare itself without metaphysics, and that justice, delayed in the courts, should descend in one sufficient blow.

As for Finley, his grave lies in the old churchyard beneath a stone on which no name can now be read. The songs he would not imprison in writing have obtained the liberty he desired: they are free—free as the smoke of his extinguished fire, and almost as impossible to recover. I sometimes condemn his obstinacy; more often I accuse myself of not having hidden two nephews in the loft.

A learned visitor, to whom I once read the manuscript, observed that Phaudhrig’s interruption of the wedding bore some resemblance to the adventure of Lochinvar. I admitted the resemblance, but reminded him that young men had carried away unwilling brides, and willing ones, before either Finley or Sir Walter made verses upon the practice. Whether the Scottish poet borrowed from tradition, whether Finley borrowed from some travelling reciter, or whether both borrowed from that common library of human passion in which love, rivalry, horses, and rash decisions have occupied the same shelf since the beginning of the world, I shall not undertake to determine.

I claim only this: that beneath the rough pronunciation, the monstrous strength, the imperfect rhyme, and the laughter which our people willingly direct against themselves, there may exist true sorrow, true tenderness, true courage, and something which, without abusing either criticism or patriotism, an honest man may call poetry.

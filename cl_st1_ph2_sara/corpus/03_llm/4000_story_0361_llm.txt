You must not interrupt me, my dear Amaral. There will be time enough afterward for objections, absolution, disgust, or a glass of water. The doctor says I have perhaps six hours; I give you four, reserving one for the priest and one for death, though either may arrive before his appointment. Take down everything. When you publish these notes—as you have promised, and as your curiosity has already promised more firmly than your lips—change my surname if you like, but not the facts. Facts are the bones of a confession. Opinions are merely the clothes in which we hope to make them presentable.

If there is an unpleasant odor about this human document, remember that it comes from a man already half in the grave. You may open the window.

My name is Procopio, and in the year 1859 I was forty-two years old, poor, patient, and capable of copying a page of Latin without understanding more than half of it. These qualities, especially the last two, recommended me to the Reverend Father Brito, who found me employment in the interior as attendant to Colonel Felisbert de Sousa. The pay was generous; the duties, according to the vicar’s letter, were light; and the colonel, though ill, possessed a robust fortune and no near relations. I mention the fortune now only because it belonged to the circumstances. At that time it no more tempted me than the Emperor’s crown, which, I assure you, has never disturbed my sleep.

I had been working in Rio de Janeiro for a dealer in devotional books, who paid me irregularly in cash and regularly in promises. When Father Brito offered me the position, I accepted with gratitude.

“Felisbert is difficult,” he said.

“Every sick man is difficult.”

“He is very difficult.”

“Then the salary is just.”

The vicar looked at me over his spectacles, perhaps measuring the strength of my Christian resignation. He had written that I possessed a tranquil disposition. Tranquillity is often another name for necessity.

The journey took nearly three days. The village lay between green hills, with a white church, a square of reddish dust, two stores, a pharmacy, and enough windows to observe the misfortunes of every inhabitant. The colonel’s house stood apart, broad and low, with dark shutters and a veranda on which no one sat. A black servant named Raimunda admitted me and crossed herself when I told her why I had come.

The colonel received me in bed. He was sixty, perhaps sixty-two, though illness had sharpened his face and loosened the skin beneath his chin. His eyes were yellow-gray, like those of a cat deciding whether to spring. On the bedside table stood medicines, a bell, a pistol, a silver watch, two prayer books, and a plate of sliced guava.

“So you are the saint from Rio,” he said.

“No, Colonel. Only Procopio.”

“We shall see.”

For the first week he treated me with an urbanity that astonished the household. He asked for his medicine rather than roaring for it, allowed me six hours of sleep, and once thanked me for adjusting the curtain. The doctor congratulated me. The barber, who knew the biography of every person within five leagues, said that the colonel had at last found a man to suit him. Even Raimunda began to walk without crossing herself.

This spring weather did not last. On the eighth day I brought his broth too hot. He called me an ass. On the ninth I brought it less hot, and he called me a tortoise, a corpse, an idiot embalmed before death. On the tenth he rang the bell fourteen times because a fly had settled on his pillow.

“Do you hear nothing, you post? Drive it away!”

I drove it away.

“Not toward me, camel! Toward the window!”

The fly escaped; my dignity did not.

You smile. Do not be ashamed; I smiled afterward myself. Misery viewed through twenty years and a good estate acquires a comic outline. At the time, however, the colonel’s temper was not amusing. His illnesses were numerous and perhaps genuine: gout, enlargement of the heart, spasms of the stomach, shortness of breath, sleeplessness, and a complaint of the liver to which he attributed every fault except his cruelty. He had also a terror of death and a hatred of those who remained healthy in his presence. I was healthy. That was my first offense.

His method was simple. At midnight he wanted tea. At half past twelve the tea had worsened his palpitations. At one he demanded that I read aloud. At two my voice irritated him. At three he slept; at ten minutes past three he woke and accused me of having closed a door loudly, coughed maliciously, moved the moon, or conspired with the clock. In the morning he reproached me for looking tired.

“You sleep too much,” he said.

“I did not sleep, Colonel.”

“Then you are naturally stupid. That is worse.”

During the day he devised exercises for my humility. He threw a spoon because I had placed it on the wrong side of the tray. He ordered me to count his pulse, then jerked his hand away and accused me of trying to steal his ring. Once, after I had spent an hour searching for his spectacles, I discovered them under his pillow.

“You put them there,” he said.

“I did not.”

“Are you contradicting me?”

“No, Colonel.”

“Then you admit it.”

To defend oneself against a tyrant is to furnish him with fresh weapons. I learned to answer little, to lower my eyes, and to imagine myself elsewhere. At first I imagined Rio: the harbor, the bookshop, the cries of street vendors, the odor of coffee. Later I imagined the colonel’s funeral. I insist that this was only an image, a refuge of the mind, not a project. Who has not buried an enemy in imagination? The difference between a decent man and an assassin is that one opens his eyes before taking up the shovel.

After a month I resolved to leave. I packed my clothes before dawn and went to the vicar. Father Mendes was a mild old man whose cassock bore the dust of the village and whose charity was most energetic when exercised through another person.

“My son,” he said, “the colonel has already dismissed six attendants.”

“I believe he murdered the seventh.”

“No, no. The seventh went to Minas.”

“Then he was the wisest.”

The vicar sighed. He spoke of forbearance, duty, the spiritual merit of serving the afflicted, and the scandal that my departure would cause. Beneath these heavenly arguments there was an earthly one: no one in the village would replace me. The colonel’s relatives lived far away and wrote seldom; the servants refused to remain in his room after dark; the doctor visited only when summoned twice.

“Stay another month,” said Father Mendes. “God will reward you.”

The colonel rewarded me first. When I returned for my trunk, he was sitting up, pale, wrapped in a woolen shawl.

“So you are deserting me.”

“I am not suited to the work.”

“You are lazy, insolent, ungrateful—and the least useless man they have sent me.”

He coughed, pressed his hand to his chest, and looked suddenly old. Then, in a voice I had not heard from him, he asked me to remain. He offered an increase in salary. He said death was near. He said he feared strangers. A tear stood in one feline eye; whether from emotion or coughing I cannot say. I remained.

For three days he was almost gentle.

On the fourth he struck me with his cane.

The blow fell across my shoulder because I had forgotten to warm his nightshirt. I seized the cane—not from anger, I believed, but to prevent a second blow. We looked at each other. His face brightened with malicious triumph.

“Ah! The saint shows his teeth.”

I released it. He struck me again, more lightly, and laughed.

From that day hatred entered me. Before then I had suffered; afterward I counted. I counted insults, bells, blows, sleepless nights. Hatred is an excellent accountant. It enters every item, adds interest, and never loses a receipt.

Yet I did not leave. The salary had become very good. There was also pride: I knew that the village admired my endurance. The doctor called me a philosopher. The notary said I had the patience of Job. Raimunda told everyone that only I understood the colonel’s attacks. We are sustained less by virtue than by the reputation of possessing it. Besides, each time I announced my departure, Felisbert softened, pleaded, or had a crisis so violent that I postponed the matter. Thus passed June, July, and part of August.

I grew thin. My hands shook. Sometimes, while shaving him, I saw the razor beside the loose skin of his throat and immediately set it down. You will say that this proves an intention. It proves only the existence of a razor and a throat. Human imagination is a disorderly servant; it lays the table for guests we have not invited.

The decisive night came in August, windless and cold. I remember every object, though I have forgotten the faces of people who loved me. That is one of memory’s indecencies.

At seven the colonel complained that his porridge was cold. I touched the bowl; it was hot enough to sting my finger.

“Cold,” he repeated.

“I will warm it.”

“You mean burn it. You know two temperatures: ice and hell.”

I brought another bowl. He tasted one spoonful and flung the plate against the wall. It broke beside my head, splashing porridge across the curtain.

“Pig!” he cried. “Did you sweeten it with mud?”

I cleaned the wall. He ordered me to read from a history of the Empire. Before I had finished the second page, he said my voice resembled a funeral drum. I lowered it. He could not hear. I raised it. I was shouting. I stopped.

“Continue, ox.”

At ten the doctor’s draught was due. He refused it. At half past ten he demanded it and accused me of withholding the medicine in order to hasten his death. At eleven he asked whether I had written to his relatives.

“No, Colonel.”

“Liar.”

“You gave me no letter.”

“You should know what I wish before I say it.”

“I am not capable of that.”

“You are capable of nothing.”

He exhausted himself and lay back. I believed he slept. I sat in the armchair with the book open on my knees. The lamp burned low; the house was silent; outside, the stars appeared pitilessly clean. I had slept perhaps six hours in three days. My head sank.

The bell rang.

I started up.

“Water,” he said.

I poured a glass.

“Fresh water.”

“This is fresh.”

“From the filter, beast.”

“It is from the filter.”

He tasted it and made a face.

“Poison.”

I turned away. Something struck me above the left eye. The water bottle fell to the floor and rolled under the chair without breaking. For one instant there was no pain, only a white flash; then warm blood ran down my temple.

The colonel laughed.

It was not a loud laugh. Had it been loud, perhaps I should have endured it. It was a small, dry chuckle, an intimate satisfaction. He called me an ignoramus, a camel, a bumpkin, a dog in trousers, a loggerhead manufactured without the log’s usefulness. The catalogue may have been longer. My blood beat in my ears.

I went toward him.

“What now?” he said. “Are you going to cry?”

I placed one hand on his shoulder. He raised his arm, perhaps to strike me, perhaps to push me away. My other hand found his throat.

I strangled him.

There, Amaral. Do not let your pen tremble; mine trembled enough for both of us. I strangled him. At first he resisted with surprising strength. His nails scratched my wrist; his heels struck the mattress; his mouth opened, but no cry emerged. The feline eyes widened. I pressed harder. I cannot tell you how long it lasted. Ten seconds? A minute? Hatred has no clock. His face darkened, then softened; his hands fell. I continued pressing after he had ceased to move.

When I released him, he was dead.

I stepped back. The room returned little by little: the lamp, the broken plate, the medicine spoon, the book lying face downward, the stain of porridge on the curtain. The clock said tick—tick—tick with an obscenity I had never perceived in clocks before. My first thought was absurd: I had forgotten to give him the draught.

I touched his chest. Nothing. I called his name in a whisper.

“Colonel?”

His open eyes accused me of stupidity rather than murder. He had always considered me incapable; now I had proved a terrible competence.

I do not know how long I remained there. I heard footsteps in the passage and nearly cried out, but they were imaginary. A face appeared at the window, pale and immense; it was the moon behind a cloud. The colonel’s mouth seemed to move. I leaned nearer. Nothing. Then I imagined a gurgling in his throat and fled to the doorway, but returned at once, fearing that absence would condemn me more surely than presence.

The marks on his neck! I saw them then: reddish impressions, already darkening, shaped by my fingers. I took a cloth, dipped it in cool water, and laid it over his throat. This was not calculation—not wholly. Panic has its own intelligence. I buttoned the nightshirt to the chin and drew the sheet upward. I put the room in order, except for the broken plate and the bottle; those belonged to his customary violence and therefore, by one of Providence’s ironies, testified in my favor.

At two in the morning I woke Raimunda.

“The colonel is worse,” I said.

She looked at my bleeding forehead.

“He struck you again?”

“He threw the bottle. Fetch the doctor.”

The doctor arrived half dressed, angry at the night, and therefore brief in his examination. He listened at the chest, touched the wrist, lifted one eyelid, and announced that the colonel had died, probably of the heart.

“Poor man,” I said.

It was an infamous phrase, but no other came.

The doctor saw me sway and made me sit down. He attributed my agitation to grief and fatigue. Raimunda wept. I tried to weep and could not, then suddenly wept so violently that they feared I would faint. Those tears, I swear, were sincere. Whether they were for him, for me, or for the gallows I cannot determine; the body has no moral vocabulary.

By dawn the house was full. The vicar prayed beside the bed. The notary discussed certificates in the passage. Neighbors came with solemn faces and alert eyes. No one unbuttoned the nightshirt. The doctor repeated that death had long been expected; the colonel’s heart was ruined, his liver ruined, his lungs compromised, his arteries old ropes. Hearing this inventory, I wondered whether I had killed a man or merely pushed a collapsing wall.

No one suspected me.

On the contrary, they embraced me.

“You cared for him like a son,” said Father Mendes.

“A martyr,” said the barber.

“He never left the colonel,” said Raimunda.

Each praise entered me like an accusation. When they spoke of my devotion, I saw my hands at his throat. When they admired my trembling, I trembled more. During the funeral I thought the coffin would open. As the earth struck the lid, three heavy clods in succession, I heard a voice within say, “Murderer.” Yet the grave remained closed, displaying in that respect more discretion than many living persons.

I departed the next morning for Rio. The vicar urged me to rest; the doctor gave me drops; the servants cried. I left with my salary, my trunk, and a small scar above the eyebrow—the colonel’s final signature.

In Rio I discovered that acquittal by others is not acquittal by oneself. I feared every knock. A policeman passing beneath my window made my stomach freeze. In the street I avoided acquaintances, convinced that they read the crime in my face. At night I woke with my fingers closed around an invisible throat. Sometimes Felisbert sat at the foot of my bed, not wounded, not angry, but smiling as he had smiled after throwing the bottle.

For seven days I ate almost nothing. I went into churches, knelt, rose, entered another church, and repeated the operation as though salvation depended upon variety. I resolved to confess to a magistrate. Then I reflected that sacramental confession was the proper first step. Then I decided that my mind was too disordered for either. Cowardice is fertile in questions of procedure.

On the eighth day a letter arrived from the village notary. Colonel Felisbert had made a will three weeks before his death. After a few small legacies to servants and Masses, he had named me his sole heir.

His fortune consisted of houses, bonds, lands, money, and cattle; altogether, more than I had ever imagined possessing. I dropped the letter. Then I picked it up and read it again. I read it standing, sitting, beside the window, under the lamp. The words did not alter.

Sole heir.

My first feeling was horror. Do not write “joy”; that came later, softly, ashamed of itself, and wearing horror’s coat. At first I saw the inheritance as blood converted into account books. I would refuse it. Naturally I would refuse it. I paced the room, composing letters of renunciation. I would give everything to hospitals, orphanages, churches, the poor. I would endow a bed for sufferers from diseases of the heart. I would found a refuge for abused servants, though that idea struck me as dangerously autobiographical.

By evening I had decided to accept temporarily.

You lift your eyebrows. Consider the situation. A refusal might arouse astonishment. Why should a poor attendant reject a fortune? People would ask questions. The will had been made before the death, yes, but malice can travel backward. To accept, therefore, was prudent; afterward I could distribute the property discreetly. In short, I accepted in order to protect the dead man’s reputation, the tranquillity of the village, and charitable institutions not yet selected. If this reasoning appears ingenious, remember that fear sharpens even a dull intellect.

I returned to the village for the inventory.

My reception was almost triumphal. The notary shook both my hands. The vicar said Providence had rewarded my patience. The doctor declared the will an act of justice. People whom I scarcely knew stopped me in the square.

“No one deserved it more.”

“You put up with what no Christian could endure.”

“He treated you shamefully.”

These remarks, intended as incense, suffocated me at first. Then they began to have another effect.

At the pharmacy, the barber said, “Felisbert broke a cane over old Bento’s back because a horse stumbled.”

“He had Bento whipped,” corrected the pharmacist.

“At his age, a cane is a whip.”

A shopkeeper recalled that the colonel had driven a tenant from his hut during the rains. A widow said he had kept money owed to her husband. Raimunda told how, years earlier, he had thrown a boy down the kitchen steps for stealing a fig. Father Mendes, though more charitable, admitted that the deceased had possessed “a difficult soul.” The doctor called him “a clinical marvel and a moral calamity.”

This chorus did not absolve me. No. I would not insult your intelligence. But it introduced circumstances. A crime in solitude is enormous; placed beside the crimes of another, it acquires proportion. I began to remember the bottle, the cane, the sleepless nights. I touched the scar above my eye. Had he not attacked me? Had I not been exhausted, bleeding, nearly delirious? It had been a struggle. The word came uninvited: struggle. He raised his hand; I restrained him; in the confusion—

But I had strangled him.

Certainly. Yet intention is the soul of murder, is it not? I had not entered the room intending to kill. I had carried no weapon. My hands, which happened to be available, acted in a moment of madness produced by months of persecution. It was, if you insist, a homicidal act; whether it made me a murderer in the full theological and juridical sense seemed less clear.

The doctor, unaware of assisting my defense, assisted it admirably.

“His heart could have stopped any night,” he told me. “Indeed, I’m surprised it lasted so long.”

“How long?”

“Days, weeks—who can say? The attack was inevitable.”

I nearly asked whether a little pressure upon the throat could be considered merely the occasion, not the cause, but prudence restrained me. Still, his words remained. The attack was inevitable. Who knows? It was even most probable that the colonel had begun dying before I touched him. His face had changed; his breath had been poor all evening. Perhaps my hands coincided with nature. Perhaps I hastened by minutes what disease had decreed for that night.

Do not stare at me so severely. I am telling you the history of my conscience, not presenting a medical thesis.

The inventory occupied two months. During the first week I renewed my plan to give away everything. During the second I reserved enough for a modest annuity; poverty, after all, does not purify everyone. During the third I decided to retain the house in Rio, since selling it quickly would cause a loss. By the end of the month I had discovered that estates are delicate organisms and should not be dismembered without study.

I gave money to the village hospital. I paid for a new roof on the church. I ordered Masses for the eternal rest of Colonel Felisbert, whose rest, if proportional to the number of Masses, ought now to be excellent. I distributed sums among the servants beyond those named in the will. These acts cost me perhaps one-tenth of the inheritance. I intended to give more later.

Later is the treasury in which we deposit our virtues.

I also erected a monument over the colonel’s grave: marble, an iron railing, and an inscription praising his fortitude in suffering and generosity in friendship. The wording was proposed by Father Mendes. I accepted it because tombs, like official documents, are not places for excessive accuracy. Beneath his name I had carved: HE RESTS AFTER LONG AFFLICTION.

This was true of both of us.

My nightmares diminished. First the colonel came nightly; then twice a week; then only when I ate heavily. His eyes lost their accusation. Sometimes in dreams he abused me as before, and I woke not terrified but irritated. Time, which cannot erase a scar, teaches us to admire its shape.

The money prospered. One property increased in value; a coffee investment yielded handsomely; bonds multiplied with the modest fecundity of respectable paper. I learned the consolations of ledgers. A column of figures has no memory. Ten thousand mil-réis do not ask how they were acquired; they merely request investment.

I married no one. You know that. Perhaps I feared telling a wife; perhaps I feared not telling her; perhaps I preferred the quiet obedience of securities. I lived decently, helped certain relations, subscribed to charitable societies, and became in time a man whose opinion was requested on public benevolence. Once I presided over a committee for the moral improvement of prisoners. You may laugh. I did.

Did remorse disappear? Not entirely. It withdrew, as an old creditor withdraws after receiving interest. On certain August nights I heard the bottle roll beneath the chair. When an ill-tempered man raised his voice, my hands grew cold. Each year I paid for another Mass. But I no longer called the event a crime. I called it the catastrophe, then the night of Felisbert’s death, then simply my years in the interior.

Language is a merciful undertaker.

Now death comes for me, without attendant, bottle, or inheritance. Yesterday the priest spoke of repentance. I repented. I believe I repented sincerely for nearly an hour. Then the steward brought the quarterly accounts, and I felt better.

You ask why I confess now. Vanity, perhaps. Fear, perhaps. A final desire to place the truth where it can neither condemn nor impoverish me. The law cannot reach a man whose candle is already blue at the wick. As for divine justice, no concealment would defeat it. Publication may injure my name, but I shall not be inside it by then. A posthumous reputation is a coat left hanging after the owner has gone; let passersby spit on the sleeves.

Keep these notes. Correct the grammar, not the conscience. If the story earns anything, spend a portion on candles for Colonel Felisbert and a portion on wine for yourself. Give my shoes to the priest; they are nearly new, and dead men have never complained of cold feet.

One final request. Do not put on my tomb that I was virtuous, patient, charitable, or repentant. Such words are expensive and rarely paid for in full. Write only my name and the dates. Or, if you require an epitaph, take this sentence, which cost me a life to compose:

Blessed are not those who mourn, for mourning passes.

Blessed are those who inherit, for they shall be consoled.

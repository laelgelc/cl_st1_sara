When Muirenn the High-Queen came to the hour of her travail, thunder walked upon the western hills, though it was the season of clear skies, and the cattle turned their heads from the wind. All night the women of the dun went softly between chamber and hearth, bearing water, linen, honey, and those herbs which ease the doors of birth; but before dawn Muirenn gave one cry, and the cry passed out over the roofs like a white bird fleeing, and thereafter she spoke no more.

The child lived.

He lay in a cradle of ashwood beside the dead queen, red as a coal and silent, with his small fists closed upon the secret he had brought into the world. The nurse covered Muirenn’s face and wept, for she had served her from girlhood; yet the fire danced upon the hearth, careless and golden, and the child slept while hail rattled against the shutters.

At midnight the door opened, though the bar was down.

There entered an old woman, tall and lean, whose cloak was sewn with grey feathers. Her feet were bare, her hair was white, and her eyes had the yellow brightness of a hawk’s eyes when it hangs above the moor.

“Whose son is this?” she asked.

“The son of Muirenn,” said the nurse, trembling.

“That is his sorrow,” said the old woman. “Whose son is this?”

“The son of Eochaid, High-King of Eri.”

“That is his burden. Whose son is this?”

“I know no other answer.”

Then the crone bent over the cradle and laid one nail upon the child’s breast.

“His heart is red with mortal blood,” she said, “and therefore he shall know pity; but pity without sight is a blind hound. I give him sight.”

She scratched her wrist, and from it fell one drop of blood, not red but grey as mist above a winter pool. It sank into the child’s mouth. Then she departed, and the door closed behind her.

The nurse had scarcely made the sign against enchantment when the door opened again, and another old woman entered. She was shorter than the first and crooked like a thorn tree; upon her shoulders were the wings of some great bird, folded and ragged with age.

“Whose son is this?” she asked.

“The son of sorrow and burden,” whispered the nurse, “and perhaps of sight.”

“His heart is red with mortal blood,” said the second, “and therefore he shall know love; but love without knowledge is a bird in a wicker cage. I give him knowledge.”

She pierced her finger with a black pin, and another drop of mist-grey blood fell between the infant’s lips. Then she departed.

And then another came, and then another and another, until seven ancient women had stood beside the cradle. One gave memory, one foresight, one understanding of the speech of beasts and waters, one knowledge of the secret weights by which truth and falsehood are balanced, and the last gave silence, saying:

“For he who knows all words and cannot keep one unspoken shall set kingdoms burning.”

At dawn the nurse was found beneath the cradle, senseless with terror, while the child smiled at the sparks rising from the hearth.

Now some said those women were queens of the Sidhe before the first ship of the Fer Bolg came to Eri; some said they were hawks that had put on human bodies; and some said they were elder than the gods whose names are uttered. But all agreed that no good fortune comes unmixed from the people beneath the hills.

The child was called Faelán, and the years passed.

When he was three years old he told the smith that iron remembered the star from which it had fallen. When he was five, he heard two ravens quarrelling above the gate and commanded that grain be sent to a widow beyond the northern ford; and when men rode there, they found that her children had eaten bark for three days. When he was seven, he judged between two herdsmen concerning a calf, saying that the beast belonged not to the owner of the cow nor to the owner of the bull, but to the little girl who had kept it alive through frost by wrapping it in her cloak. The men of law disputed this for nine nights and then declared it just.

King Eochaid rejoiced, though at times he feared to meet the boy’s eyes. The poets praised him. The druids watched him in silence. Only the nurse remembered the seven crones, and when the wind cried around the dun she laid rowan upon his threshold.

In Faelán’s ninth year she combed his black hair and found among it a single grey feather.

She plucked it out and burned it.

The next morning there were three.

Within a month a narrow crest had risen from his crown, soft and grey and bright at the edges like frost beneath moonlight. The child could not see it, though he searched his reflection in polished bronze; for the feathers lay flat when he looked, and lifted only when he turned away.

Then the nurse went weeping to the king, and the king summoned the chief poet, the keeper of genealogies, and the nine men who preserved the laws.

They sat until the lamps failed.

For an ancient law declared that no man marked by deformity, wound, or blemish might hold the sovereignty. The king must be whole, because the land was shaped after him: if he lacked an eye, crops would fail; if he lacked a hand, cattle would perish; if a blemish lay upon his flesh, a blemish would spread through Eri. Such had been the law before writing, and law does not grow young because men desire it.

“Let another inherit,” said the eldest judge.

“And cast away the wisest child born among us?” said the poet.

“Wisdom is not sovereignty.”

“Yet folly has worn the crown often enough.”

Then they devised a mercy, and every mercy hides a little knife.

A proclamation went throughout the kingdom that the hawk was sacred to the house of Eochaid, and that every free man and woman should wear grey feathers in the hair. The poets composed songs declaring that Muirenn had dreamed of a hawk before Faelán’s birth. The genealogists found seven hawk-kings among his ancestors, though none had been known the day before. Warriors bound feathers to their helmets; maidens braided them with rushes and gold; judges wore them above their brows, and children played at being birds.

Thus Faelán grew among a feathered people and did not know himself different.

Eochaid died when the boy was sixteen, and Faelán was set upon the Stone of Kings. The stone cried beneath his feet, though certain men said its cry was more like warning than welcome. Nevertheless he was crowned, and for many years no king in Eri was more renowned.

Men came across seas to hear him speak. A prince troubled by death listened to Faelán’s discourse upon the soul and went home unable to draw a sword, so that his enemies took his lands. A merchant asked how wealth might be preserved, and Faelán showed him that possession was a net woven by fear; the merchant gave away his silver, his ships, and finally his cloak, and froze beneath a hedge. A poet heard the king speak of beauty, broke his harp, and never sang again, for all songs thereafter seemed to him cracked cups offered beside an eternal fountain.

Yet others departed comforted. A mother who had lost three sons dreamed of them walking beneath green branches after Faelán spoke with her. A murderer confessed and accepted judgment. Two tribes ended a feud whose first cause had been forgotten by all save the dead.

Thus the fame of the Hawk-King passed from island to island.

But the needs of common people wear muddy shoes, and wisdom does not always admit them into its hall.

When farmers disputed a stream, Faelán spoke of the sea into which all waters mingle; the stronger farmer dammed the stream before sunset. When a woman complained that her husband beat her, the king discoursed upon the violence by which the soul wounds itself; she returned home with a bruised face and no remedy. When taxes emptied the western granaries, Faelán asked whether hunger might free men from servitude to the flesh.

Thereafter his councillors heard petitions before they reached him, and they settled boundaries, counted cattle, punished thieves, repaired bridges, and stored barley for winter. The king spoke of justice, while ordinary men performed it as best they could.

He was neither cruel nor proud. Had he understood the harm, he would have cut off his own hand to prevent it. But he saw mankind from a height, as the hawk sees villages: roofs, paths, fields, and moving specks, all clear in their order, while no crying child can be heard.

And the years passed, and loneliness grew around him like an unseen forest.

In the thirtieth year of his life there came to the dun a foreign king seeking alliance, and with him came his daughter, Ailinn. Her hair was red-gold; her skin had the pale brightness of silver; and her eyes were dark pools in which every beholder imagined some depth answering his own desire.

Faelán saw her standing among torchlight and believed that wisdom had taken mortal form.

“You are weary of lesser speech,” he said to her.

“I am weary from riding,” said Ailinn.

He smiled, supposing her answer subtle.

He walked with her upon the ramparts and spoke of the constellations, which are the thoughts of gods made visible in darkness. She listened courteously and watched young warriors casting spears below. He told her that the body was a house briefly rented by the spirit. She shivered, for his grey crest had lifted in the wind, and for an instant his shadow upon the wall resembled a bird stooping upon prey.

Yet an alliance with Faelán was desired by her father, and she dared not refuse plainly.

“Will you be my queen?” asked Faelán.

“My lord is greater than my answer.”

“Then your answer is yes.”

“My lord’s wisdom sees beyond the day.”

“Then in days beyond this one you consent.”

“My heart is a common heart.”

“That is modesty,” he said, and kissed her hand.

But Ailinn loved Cormac, a young warrior of her father’s household, whose laughter was ready, whose wisdom was small, and whose hands knew horses, shields, broken harness, and the warmth of her waist. He did not speak of eternity. He spoke of escaping before winter.

One night they met in an unused chamber above the stables. Below them horses stirred, and rain whispered upon the thatch. Neither knew that Faelán walked beyond the wall, wakeful beneath a moonless sky.

“I fear him,” said Ailinn. “He looks at me and sees not a woman but a sign written for him.”

“Come away,” said Cormac.

“His riders would find us.”

“Then I shall challenge him.”

“You would challenge thunder with a hazel wand.”

“I would rather die beneath his spear than see you caged.”

“He carries no spear. That is worse. Oh, Cormac, when his feathers rise, I think some ancient thing peers through him.”

Faelán stopped outside the shutter.

“My feathers?” he said.

Within, there was silence.

He entered. Ailinn stood white-faced, and Cormac placed himself before her, though his sword lay in the corner.

“What feathers?” asked the king.

Neither answered.

Faelán looked from one to the other, and knowledge, which had shown him the hidden courses of stars, at last turned its gaze upon the small deception nearest him. He seized Cormac’s grey plume and drew it from his hair.

There was no wound beneath it, no root, no living quill.

He plucked the feathers from Ailinn, and they too were dead ornaments. Then he passed through the dun, pulling plumes from guards, servants, judges, women at the loom, and sleeping children. Some fled from him. Some knelt. Some wept. None bore the grey crest that sprang from his own flesh.

At sunrise he commanded all the kingdom’s great ones to assemble.

Faelán entered the hall without crown or cloak. His feathers stood high upon his head, and never had he seemed so royal, nor so little a man.

“Men of law,” he said, “what is written concerning a blemished king?”

No one answered.

“Men of verse, what name is given to him who makes falsehood beautiful?”

No poet raised his eyes.

“Men who loved me, why did you teach a whole people to resemble my deformity?”

The eldest judge came forward. “Because the kingdom needed you.”

“Nay. The kingdom needed the thought of me. Meanwhile you governed roads and rivers, cattle and hunger, marriage and blood-payment. You placed me above men and then concealed mankind from me.”

“We acted from loyalty,” said the chief poet.

“Loyalty without truth is a painted shield. It pleases the eye and turns no spear.”

Then the old nurse, bent now with years, knelt before him and told of Muirenn’s death, the midnight door, and the seven women of the Sidhe. Faelán listened, and his face changed neither with anger nor wonder, but only with recognition, as when an exile hears at last the name of his homeland.

“I have violated the law,” he said, “though unknowingly. I have spoken divine things where bread and judgment were required. Wisdom belongs to the high air, but men must sow in earth. A hawk may behold the whole field, yet it cannot guide the plough.”

He turned to the assembly and named as king his steward, a broad, patient man who knew the price of oats in every province and could remember the names of servants’ children.

“Let him rule. He knows that a bridge is more useful than a perfect definition of crossing.”

Then Faelán went among the courtiers and removed the false feathers from every head. Soon the floor was grey with them, as though a flock had been slaughtered there.

He spared neither judge nor poet, princess nor warrior. Last of all he took the crown and laid it upon the heap.

To Ailinn he said, “Your heart was common, and I mistook that for smallness. A common heart is the hearth of the world.”

To Cormac he said, “Guard it better than wise men guarded mine.”

Thereafter he departed alone by the northern gate. No horse bore him, no hound followed him, and he carried neither food nor weapon. Mist lay upon the forest, and as he entered beneath the boughs, seven hawks rose without sound and vanished before him.

He was never seen again in any dun of men.

Some say he found the ancient hawk-women beside a pool where no mortal star is reflected, and that he reigns among them in a kingdom of wind and silver branches. Some say demons welcomed him, for wisdom without mercy is dear to the powers of darkness. Others say he serves the dark goddesses in the eternal forest, bringing them the dreams of kings.

Yet in years of storm, when grey birds circle above the hills, the people of Eri still remember Faelán. They remember the king who saw too far to see what lay at his feet; and they put no feathers in their children’s hair, however beautiful the feathers may be.

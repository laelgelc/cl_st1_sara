All afternoon John Hammond had been telling himself that the next ten minutes would finish it.

Ten minutes—nothing. A man could stand anything for ten minutes. He could stand the wet wind slipping under his coat collar, the odour of rope and tar and old fruit, the gulls crying out as though they too had wives aboard the *Ardent*. He could even stand that blue-coated official who had twice requested him, quite civilly but with an unforgivable firmness, to remain behind the painted line.

But the ten minutes passed, and another ten minutes began.

Hammond pulled out his watch, snapped it open, snapped it shut. Three forty-seven. No, impossible. He held it to his ear. Tick-tick-tick—the insolent little thing was alive enough. Yet the great liner out in the harbour seemed no nearer than before.

“There she is, sir,” said the porter beside the pile of luggage tickets.

“There she is!” Hammond repeated. “My good fellow, she’s been ‘there’ since two o’clock.”

The porter smiled with the patience of a man whose own wife, if he had one, could arrive or not arrive without disturbing the harbour.

Beyond the wharf sheds the water spread like a sheet of tarnished metal, rumpled here and there by a breeze that came from nowhere and went nowhere. The *Ardent*, pale above and black below, lay upon it with the absurd stillness of a painted ship. Specks clustered along her railings. They might have been passengers; they might have been smuts. A launch moved near her side, dragging a white thread behind it, and halfway up a rope ladder a tiny dark creature climbed, paused, climbed again.

Was that the pilot? Was there some difficulty? Had anything happened?

Anything! What could happen now, at the very gate of home? Ten months of distance, ten months of letters, ten months of waking in the wide bed and putting out his hand to the smooth, cold hollow beside him—and now the ship was in sight. The danger was over. Of course it was. By heaven, if a liner could cross half the world, it could manage the last mile of grey harbour.

Still, she had been due at noon.

Hammond opened his watch again. Three fifty.

A family near him had brought flowers. There were six of them, all talking at once, and the youngest boy had a paper flag which he flicked against Hammond’s sleeve until Hammond turned and looked at him. The child lowered the flag.

“Waiting for somebody?” Hammond asked.

“Our aunt,” said the boy.

“Ah! Very important person, an aunt.”

“She’s got a parrot.”

“Even more important.”

The mother laughed. Hammond laughed too, loudly and generously. He could have embraced the whole ridiculous family. Let their aunt come, parrot and all. Let the wharf fill with aunts, parrots, brass bands, customs officers—provided Janey came through them.

He moved away, tapping his furled umbrella against his boot.

Janey. He tried to see her as she would look when she first saw him. Would she lift both hands? Would her mouth tremble? Or would she give that quick, secret smile of hers, as if happiness were something she must shelter from the weather? He had imagined it a hundred times: she would be at the rail; he would call; she would lean forward, searching; then suddenly her face would change. His face. His Janey.

And after that—away. Away from the shouting people, the officials, the wretched luggage, everybody. A motor to the Royal, tea sent up, the door locked. No callers, no children until tomorrow, no sensible arrangements. They would sit before the fire, perhaps with the curtains drawn, though it would still be light, and he would look at her. Only look at her at first. There was so much to be made certain of.

The thought warmed him so sharply that he smiled at the harbour.

Ten months! And people called marriage habit. Habit? Let them try having it torn from them. Let them come home night after night to hear the clock in the passage and the maid asking whether there would be anything else. Let them find a hairpin under the dressing table and pick it up as though it were a relic from some vanished civilisation. Let them read, “Darling, we had rain today, but the hills were lovely after it,” and know nothing of the rain or the hills except that Janey had seen them without him.

Her journey had been necessary. Of course. Her sister had been ill; then there had been the convalescence, the doctor’s advice, the winter passage to avoid. He had agreed to everything. Hadn’t he written, “Stay as long as you need”? Hadn’t he sent more money than she asked for? Still, agreement was one thing and enduring it was another.

A whistle sounded across the water.

Every head lifted. The family’s paper flag sprang into life. Hammond felt his heart give one huge bound, like a dog against a chain.

“She’s moving,” somebody said.

Yes. Yes, at last.

The liner’s black flank seemed to slide across the harbour without disturbing it. Smoke unrolled from the funnels and flattened beneath the low sky. The specks at the rail grew into heads, hats, waving hands. A white handkerchief flashed, disappeared, flashed again.

Hammond stepped beyond the painted line.

“Back, please, sir.”

“I am back.”

“You’re over the line.”

“Am I? So I am.”

He retreated half a pace, laughing. The official did not laugh. What was wrong with these people? Did they imagine a man’s wife returned every Tuesday?

The ship approached slowly, cruelly slowly. Now ropes flew. Men below caught them and ran, bent almost double, like insects hauling at the leg of some enormous animal. Orders rang out. Water slapped between the hull and the stones. The *Ardent* gave a long, exhausted sigh.

Hammond searched the crowded decks.

A woman in red—no. A tall woman holding a child—no. A white veil, a black hat, an old lady with field glasses. Where was she? She had said she would be on the port side. Or had he muddled it? On the port side entering harbour—yes. Unless “port” meant the other side from the shore. Damn nautical language.

Then he saw her.

She stood a little apart beneath one of the lifeboats, in a grey travelling coat and a close dark hat. Her hands rested upon the rail. She was not waving. For one instant she looked so small against the towering white ship that fear went through him—not fear of anything that had happened, but of the mere fact that she had existed there without him, high above the water, among all those strangers.

“Janey!”

His voice cracked. He did not care.

She turned. She saw him.

Her face lit—not wildly, not with lifted hands or tears, but with a clear brightness that seemed to travel over it. She bent towards him and smiled.

“John!”

He waved his umbrella, his hat, both arms. The family beside him cheered, whether for their aunt or for Janey he could not tell. He laughed and shouted again.

“Janey! Here! I’m here!”

As if she could fail to see him! As if he had not been standing for months at the edge of everything, waiting.

The gangway took another age. A steward came down, then two officials went up, then nobody moved at all. Passengers leaned over the rail and called impossible questions to the crowd.

“Did you get my telegram?”

“Where is Mother?”

“Tell him the green case—”

Janey had disappeared.

Where had she gone? Luggage, naturally. Or some final absurdity. But Hammond’s happiness faltered. He stared at the opening from which the passengers would emerge.

One by one they came: an elderly clergyman blinking in the wind; a girl carrying a cage covered with cloth; two businessmen already arguing; a pale mother with three children tethered to her by ribbons, bags and anxious glances. They were embraced, claimed, led away. Each reunion made Janey’s absence more extraordinary.

Then she appeared at the top of the gangway.

She carried nothing but a little leather case. A ship’s doctor walked beside her, bareheaded, his cap tucked beneath his arm. He was a lean, tired-looking fellow, younger than Hammond had expected a ship’s doctor to be. He said something. Janey looked up at him gravely.

Why gravely? Why did he walk with her?

Hammond pushed forward.

“Janey!”

She came down carefully, one gloved hand on the rope rail. Halfway she saw him and smiled again, but she did not hurry. Well, she could not hurry on that infernal plank. Yet the doctor’s hand hovered at her elbow as though she might need him.

At the bottom Hammond seized her.

“My darling—my dear girl—at last!”

He kissed her cheek, her other cheek, the cool edge of her hat. For a moment her body yielded lightly to his arms. Lightly—that was the word. She seemed to weigh nothing after all his waiting.

“John,” she murmured. “Dear John.”

He held her away to look at her. “You’re thin.”

“Am I?”

“You are. And cold. Why aren’t you wearing the fur collar? I told you—no, don’t answer. You’re here. That’s all. Thank God.”

Her eyes rested on him with affection, but there was tiredness in them, or thought, or something he could not name. Behind her the doctor had stopped.

Janey turned. “John, this is Dr. Renshaw.”

“Doctor! Delighted. Most grateful to you for bringing her in safely.”

The phrase sounded foolish the moment he had spoken it. The doctor had not navigated the ship. Still, what else did one say?

Renshaw shook his hand. “Mrs. Hammond was a great help to us.”

“A help? Janey?” Hammond laughed. “I’ve no doubt she reorganised the entire vessel.”

“No, John.”

There was something in her tone—not reproof exactly. The doctor looked at her, and Hammond disliked the look because it appeared to contain a continuation of something already understood.

“We mustn’t keep you,” Janey said. “I only wanted to say goodbye.”

“I’m glad we met.” Renshaw’s voice was quiet. “Try to rest.”

“I will.”

“And remember—you did everything anyone could have done.”

A pause opened between them.

Hammond’s fingers closed more firmly about Janey’s arm.

“Come along, darling. Customs will confiscate the lot if I don’t bully them.”

Renshaw put on his cap. “Goodbye, Mrs. Hammond.”

“Goodbye.”

He went back up the gangway against the stream of descending passengers.

Hammond watched him for a second. “Cheerful fellow.”

“He’s very tired.”

“Doctors always look tired. Professional expression. Now—where are the trunks?”

There were five trunks, three cases, a hatbox, two rugs and a cane basket Janey insisted must not be turned on its side. Each piece of luggage seemed to have formed an attachment to a different official. Hammond rushed from desk to desk, signing, explaining, joking, protesting. He tipped a porter twice and another not at all, then pursued him to repair the injustice.

Janey remained near the basket. Friends from the ship stopped to speak to her. An old lady kissed her. A stout man pressed both her hands and said, “You were an angel, my dear,” before being swept away by his relations. Hammond heard it from across the room and felt a foolish little prickle.

An angel? Janey? Of course she was an angel, but what business had the stout man discovered it?

At last he marshalled everything towards the exit.

“Now then! Royal Hotel, all of it. Mind that corner. Not upside down, man—the lady has expressly forbidden it.”

“John, have you seen my letters?”

“Your letters?”

“The children’s. You said they had written.”

“Oh, those. Safe in my pocket.”

“May I have them?”

“In the motor.”

“I should like them now.”

He patted his coat, found the packet, and gave it to her. Three envelopes at once took possession of her attention: Molly’s covered with violet ink, Peter’s addressed in enormous capitals, little Anne’s bearing a mysterious brown stain.

“Darling, don’t read them here.”

“Only the first page.”

“They’ll tell you everything twice tomorrow.”

But she had already opened Molly’s. Her mouth curved as she read. Hammond felt absurdly jealous of the purple lines.

Hang the children! They had had him for ten months, had monopolised breakfast, muddied the hall and demanded stories while he was trying to write to Janey. Now they reached out from home before he had got her properly off the ship.

He softened at once. “What does Molly say?”

“That Anne cut Peter’s hair.”

“I wondered why the boy wore a cap indoors.”

Janey laughed, and at the familiar sound his heart turned over.

“That’s better,” he said. “Now you’re home.”

She slipped the letters into her case. “Not home yet.”

“No, thank goodness. We’re going to be selfish first.”

“Are we?”

“We are. Positively vicious. I’ve told them not to expect us until tomorrow afternoon. Cook has made a cake the size of a washbasin, the children have built something over the gate, and Nurse has doubtless trained them to recite. Tomorrow. Tonight you belong to me.”

He said it playfully, but once said, the words thrilled him with their truth. She belonged to him. Not to the ship, the doctor, the old lady, the children—not for this one evening.

Janey looked down while she fastened her case. “Dear John.”

The motor waited in the rain. Hammond tucked a rug round her knees and climbed in beside her. The luggage was loaded behind and above them until the vehicle resembled a travelling shop. When they started, the hatbox swayed ominously at the rear window.

“Look!” he cried. “It’s following us home like a moon.”

Janey turned and smiled. She had removed her gloves. Her hands lay loosely together upon the rug, smaller and paler than he remembered. He covered them with his own.

“Warm enough?”

“Yes.”

“Comfortable?”

“Perfectly.”

“Sure?”

“Yes, John.”

He could not stop looking at her. The city passed in wet fragments: shop lamps blurred in puddles, tram wires trembled above the streets, umbrellas bumped and separated. People hurried beneath the rain with no idea that Janey Hammond had returned. Extraordinary. The whole city ought to know.

“Tell me everything,” he said.

“Everything?”

“Start with the moment you sailed. No, start with yesterday morning. What did you have for breakfast? Where was your cabin? Did you sleep? Who did you dine with? Why were you late? What did the doctor mean about resting?”

“So many questions.”

“We have ten months to account for.”

She leaned back. “The engines slowed in the night.”

“Why?”

“There was fog.”

“Fog? They said no fog at the office.”

“It cleared before dawn.”

“Then why three hours late?”

Janey looked towards the streaming glass. “There was some confusion.”

“What sort of confusion?”

“Oh—on board.”

He waited. She did not continue.

A cart blocked the street ahead, and the motor slowed. Hammond lifted her hand to his lips. “Never mind. I don’t care if the captain lost the harbour. You’re here.”

“Yes.”

“Say you missed me.”

“Of course I missed you.”

“Dreadfully?”

“Dreadfully.”

“As much as I missed you?”

She turned her eyes to him. “How can one measure that?”

There it was—that little door she sometimes shut without seeming to touch it. He laughed to make nothing of it.

“I can. I’ve invented a scale. It begins with sleeplessness and ends with talking to one’s wife’s photograph.”

“Did you?”

“Every night.”

“What did I say?”

“Very little. You were most unsympathetic.”

This time she laughed fully, and he drew her close. Her hat brushed his chin. Beneath the faint smell of rain and travel he found the scent that was Janey’s, altered perhaps, but unmistakable. He closed his eyes. Yes. This. The months collapsed like empty boxes.

Yet when he held her tighter she became, not resistant, but still. Her stillness made him conscious of his arms.

“Tired?” he asked.

“A little.”

“You’ve changed.”

She looked startled. “Changed?”

“No, no, nonsense. I mean your hair. You’ve changed your hair.”

“It’s only flatter from the hat.”

“Of course.”

What a fool he was! In ten months anyone changed. He himself had grown grey at the temples—she had noticed that, surely? But change in Janey seemed unlawful. She must return carrying every gesture, every look, precisely as he had stored them.

At the hotel Hammond sprang out before the motor fully stopped. He directed the luggage, corrected the clerk, demanded more coal, ordered tea, then cancelled it and ordered supper, then restored the tea as well.

“Flowers,” he told the waiter. “Have you flowers?”

“There are roses in the room, sir.”

“Fresh?”

“They arrived this afternoon.”

“I sent them,” Hammond explained to Janey. “All white. You used to say white roses smelled of pepper.”

“Did I?”

“You did. At the Marshalls’ dance.”

“That was years ago.”

“Six years, four months.”

The waiter concealed a smile. Let him smile. Let the whole hotel know what sort of husband she had.

Their room was at the end of a long corridor. The porter opened the door, and warmth breathed out upon them. A fire burned in the grate. Lamps with rose-coloured shades stood beside the wide bed. White roses crowded a blue bowl upon the dressing table, and before the windows two armchairs faced each other across a small table laid for tea.

For one bright instant it was exactly as Hammond had imagined. Better. Janey stood in the middle of the carpet, taking it in. The room seemed enormous around her; the bed rose like a white country under its quilt; the wardrobe mirror held another remote Janey in grey.

“Well?” he demanded.

“It’s lovely.”

“Is it? I worried it might be too grand. Or not grand enough.”

“It’s perfect.”

The porters entered with the luggage. At once the room was invaded. Trunks thudded against walls; cases occupied chairs; the cane basket took up its station by the fireplace. Hammond hovered helplessly while the men transformed his private paradise into a railway platform.

“That trunk there—no, not beside the bed. The dressing room. Gently! It contains the crown jewels.”

Janey removed her hat before the mirror. Her hair, freed from it, lay softly against her head. Hammond saw in the glass that she closed her eyes for a moment.

“You are exhausted.”

“I shall be all right after tea.”

The final porter accepted his coin and withdrew. Hammond followed him to the door, looked into the corridor, and shut it. He turned the key.

There.

Alone.

He stood with his back to the door and gazed at Janey. The rain whispered at the windows; the fire gave a little settling cough. Down in the street a motor horn sounded, thin and far away.

Janey met his gaze in the mirror.

“Come here,” he said.

She turned.

He crossed the room and took her face between his hands. Without the hat she looked younger, almost too young to have travelled so far, to have seen cities and mountains and seas that he had not seen. Fine lines of weariness showed beneath her eyes. Her lips were parted as if she meant to speak.

“No,” he said. “Not yet.”

He kissed her.

At first the kiss was gentle because he feared her tiredness. Then all the months rushed upon him. He kissed her hair, her eyelids, the soft place below her ear. He murmured her name until it ceased to be a name and became merely the sound of wanting her.

“My darling. My own girl. Janey—Janey.”

She rested against him. Her arms went round his shoulders, but lightly again, lightly. Why lightly?

He drew back. “You are glad?”

“Glad?”

“To be with me.”

“Oh, John.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

She touched his cheek. “I’m more glad than I can say.”

“Then don’t say. Show me.”

A faint shadow passed over her face. It was gone at once, but he had seen it.

The tea arrived.

Hammond laughed too loudly. “Saved by the bell! Come in.”

A waiter wheeled in a silver service, toast, cakes, sandwiches and a covered dish of hot scones. His deliberate movements were torture. He arranged cups, straightened spoons, put a match to the spirit lamp, asked whether madam preferred China or Ceylon.

“Whichever leaves the room sooner,” Hammond said.

“John!”

The waiter’s face remained smooth. At last he bowed and departed.

Janey sat in one of the deep chairs. Almost at once the chair swallowed her. Only her head, hands and the tips of her shoes remained visible above its green velvet. Hammond piled cushions behind her.

“You look lost.”

“I feel lost.”

“Well, I’ll find you.”

He knelt beside the tea table and poured. Tea ran into the saucer.

“Look what you make me do.”

“Let me.”

“No, you’ve done enough for one day.”

Again something changed in her expression. He set down the pot.

“What is it?”

“Nothing.”

“You keep saying nothing.”

“Do I?”

“On the wharf, in the motor, now. And that doctor—what did he mean, you’d done everything?”

Janey reached for her cup. “John, must we talk about the voyage at once?”

“Certainly not. We need never mention the dreary tub again.”

He offered her a scone, broke one for himself, and began to tell her about the children. Anne had attempted to send a kitten through the post. Peter had announced his intention of becoming a burglar because burglars, according to Nurse, stayed awake after midnight. Molly had been moved up in arithmetic and down in conduct. Hammond performed their voices, even Nurse’s, and Janey laughed in the right places. But while he spoke, he watched her hands.

Once, without apparent reason, she pressed them together tightly. Once she looked past him into the fire and did not hear his question. And once the hotel clock chimed in the passage, whereupon her cup trembled against its saucer.

There was something. She was ill. That must be it.

He stopped in the middle of Peter’s latest outrage.

“Janey, did you see the doctor professionally?”

She stared. “Professionally?”

“Were you ill on board?”

“No.”

“Tell me the truth.”

“I am telling you.”

“You’ve grown thin, you’re pale, he told you to rest—”

“Everyone tells a traveller to rest.”

“And the delay?”

Her eyes dropped.

Hammond rose. The gaiety went out of him as quickly as a lamp blown upon.

“What happened?”

She placed the cup carefully on the table. “There was a passenger who became ill.”

“Ah.” Relief came first, shamefully strong. Not Janey. Someone else. “So that was it. They stopped for him?”

“They could not stop.”

“Then what did they do?”

“He had been unwell for several days. At first nobody thought it serious.”

Janey’s voice had altered. It was still quiet, but each word seemed selected before it was given to him.

“A man?” Hammond asked.

“A young man.”

“Who?”

“His name was Edward Vale.”

The name entered the room and stood there.

Hammond moved to the fireplace. “Friend of yours?”

“No. I had not met him before the voyage.”

“But you met him on board.”

“Yes.”

Naturally. People met one another on ships. They sat at tables, walked decks, complained of weather. Nothing could be more ordinary. And yet—Edward Vale. A young man.

“What was wrong with him?”

“His heart.”

“Poor devil.”

“Yes.”

Janey looked into the fire. One of the coals had fallen apart, exposing a fierce red centre that darkened even as Hammond watched.

“He travelled alone,” she continued. “He was going home to his mother. At least, he hoped to reach her.”

“Did he?”

“No.”

The rain had thickened. It rattled faintly against the glass like dry seeds.

Hammond turned. “He died?”

“This morning.”

“On the ship?”

“Yes.”

“So that caused the confusion.”

“Yes.”

He felt sorry, of course. A young fellow with a mother waiting—horrible. Some woman perhaps standing at another wharf, not knowing there would be no bright face at the rail. Hammond’s heart contracted in sympathy.

But Janey sat too still.

“Were you with him?”

She did not answer at once.

“Janey?”

“The stewardess came for me.”

“For you? Why?”

“He had asked for me.”

“Why should he ask for you?”

Her fingers tightened on the chair arm. “Because I had talked with him.”

“How much?”

“Does it matter?”

The question was soft, almost weary. That made it matter infinitely.

“How much?” Hammond repeated.

“He sometimes sat near me on deck. He was lonely. When he grew worse, Dr. Renshaw moved him to a cabin nearer his own. I brought him books. Once or twice I read aloud.”

“You read to him.”

“Yes.”

“What?”

“I don’t remember.”

She did remember. Hammond was certain of it. A book, a passage, the tone in which she had read—these existed between her and the dead man, and Hammond could not enter them.

He strode to the window, then back. The room had become absurdly spacious. Between the fireplace and Janey’s chair lay an acre of carpet patterned with vines that twisted upon themselves.

“What happened this morning?”

“John—”

“I want to know.”

“He was worse before dawn. The doctor had been with him most of the night. There was nothing more to do.”

“And he asked for you.”

“Yes.”

“Not the doctor? Not a steward?”

“He was frightened.”

“So they fetched my wife.”

The words came out harsher than he intended.

Janey looked up. “He was dying.”

“I understand that.”

“No, dear. I don’t think you do.”

Hammond gave a short laugh. “Apparently I understand nothing about it.”

She rose from the chair. Without it around her she looked smaller than ever, a pale figure before the fading fire. He had a sudden desire to wrap her in his arms, to stop the story at once. If he held her tightly enough, Edward Vale would remain no more than a name.

But he did not move.

“What did you do?” he asked.

Janey’s eyes went towards the bed and away again.

“He could not breathe lying down. We lifted him. Dr. Renshaw was called to another emergency for a few minutes—the sea was rough and an old woman had fallen. The stewardess went to bring hot water.”

“You were left alone with him?”

“For a little while.”

“How long?”

“I don’t know.”

“What did he say?”

“Very little.”

“But something.”

“He asked whether it was morning.”

“And you said?”

“I said it would be morning soon.”

The words made Hammond see it: the close ship’s cabin, the round glass dark with sea, the lamp swinging, Janey’s face bent towards a pillow. He saw it too clearly, though every detail must be wrong.

“Then he asked me to open the curtain,” she said. “There was a line of light over the water. Only a little line. He tried to look at it.”

She lifted one hand as if drawing back that curtain again.

Hammond could not bear the gesture. “And then?”

“He said his mother’s name. I think it was her name. He could scarcely speak.”

Janey’s voice grew smaller. Hammond took one step towards her.

“And then?”

“He died.”

“Yes, but how? Where were you?”

She looked at him with bewildered pain, as though the distinction he demanded had no meaning.

“He died in my arms.”

Nothing moved.

The fire gave a faint click. Outside, far below, wheels went hissing along the wet street.

Hammond stared at Janey.

“In your arms?”

“He had slipped down. I held him so he could breathe more easily.”

“You held him.”

“Yes.”

“Against you?”

Her face whitened. “John.”

“Tell me.”

“There is nothing else to tell. His hand lifted once—as if he meant to reach for something—and then it fell. He breathed out. That was all.”

That was all.

A young man’s head against her. Her arms about him. Her voice promising morning. Her body receiving the last warmth of him while the ship moved through darkness towards Hammond.

And Hammond, at that hour, had been awake in his own bed. He remembered. He had woken before dawn and listened to rain in the gutter. He had imagined Janey asleep in her cabin, coming nearer with every turn of the propellers. He had whispered, “Tomorrow,” into the darkness.

All the while she had held another man.

Dead, dying—what difference did death make to the embrace? More, perhaps. Infinitely more. There had been no demand in it, no impatience, no hunger such as Hammond’s. The stranger had asked nothing she could resent. He had surrendered wholly because he had no choice, and Janey had taken him wholly because pity opened in her a room that love had never opened for her husband.

No. Monstrous. Shameful.

The poor fellow had died.

Hammond turned away and put both hands on the mantelpiece. The roses appeared in the mirror behind it, white and swollen, their petals already loosening in the heat. Beside them stood Janey’s open travelling case. The children’s letters protruded from it. Molly, Peter, Anne—everybody was in the room. The doctor was there. The old lady, the stout man, the stewardess. And now Edward Vale lay between the armchairs with the sea-darkness around him.

“John,” Janey said.

He could not answer.

She came nearer. “It had nothing to do with us.”

Nothing to do with us.

Of course. Objectively, sensibly, decently—nothing. A human being had been frightened and she had comforted him. Any woman might have done it. Any nurse. Any stranger with kindness enough to stay.

But Janey had done it.

His Janey, whom he had wanted to keep from cold winds, rough seas, impudent porters; Janey, whose smallest fatigue tormented him; Janey, who could never quite give him the final secret part of herself. She had given that hidden tenderness freely to a man without a claim.

Had she ever held Hammond like that—simply, completely, with no self watching behind her eyes? Never. Never once. Even in happiness there had always been something light in her touch, some little reserve winged and ready.

And to the dying stranger—everything.

“Are you angry?” she whispered.

Angry? The word was absurdly small.

He looked round. She was close enough to touch. Her expression was not guilty. That was the worst of it—or the salvation of it. She looked tired, sorrowful and astonished by his pain. She truly did not know what she had done to him because, for her, she had done nothing except hold a dying man.

He saw, suddenly, the cruelty of asking her to be sorry.

“No,” he said.

His voice sounded normal.

“John, I wanted to tell you. I tried in the motor, but you were so happy.”

“I am happy.”

“You’re not.”

“My darling, a man died. Naturally you’re upset. Naturally I—” He broke off and laughed. “What an idiot I am. Come here.”

She hesitated.

That hesitation pierced him. He opened his arms.

“Come, Janey.”

She came.

He drew her against him and felt her hands rise to his shoulders. Her body was warm now from the room; her hair touched his cheek. He held her fiercely, but not so fiercely that she should know.

“There,” he murmured. “It’s over. Don’t think about it.”

“I can’t help thinking about it.”

“No, no. Of course not yet.”

“He was so young.”

“Yes.”

“And when he saw the light, he smiled.”

“Don’t.”

The word escaped.

Janey became still. “I’m sorry.”

“No—don’t be sorry. I mean don’t make yourself live it again.”

But she would live it again. He knew that. There would be mornings when a line of pale sunlight crossed the room and she would remember. There would be the smell of salt, the sway of a lamp, a page in some forgotten book. Hammond could burn the book, close the curtains, carry her a thousand miles inland; still the stranger would have his place.

The fire sank lower. Its glow left the tea things, the roses, the brass rail of the enormous bed. The trunks crouched against the wall, labelled and locked, ready at any moment to be lifted and carried elsewhere.

Janey stroked the back of his head.

It was a soothing motion—slow, tender, unconscious.

Had she soothed the dying man so?

Hammond shut his eyes. He bent his face into her breast so that she would not see it. Beneath his cheek her heart beat steadily, privately, as it had beaten beneath another cheek that morning.

She held him closer.

At last—yes, at last—her arms were wholly around him.

And in the shelter he had desired through ten hungry months, Hammond understood that the door had never closed, that no key could close it, that another life had passed through her keeping and left its shadow there.

He clasped her while the rain darkened the windows.

They had reached one another. They were together. They were alone.

And they would never be alone together again.

### THE RED ESTRAY

Among those doctrines which, having been successively embraced by philosophers, anathematized by councils, ridiculed by men of fashion, and preserved with singular fidelity by nurses and peasants, there is none more obstinate than the transmigration of the soul. The Abbé de Villars—whose authority upon any subject is perhaps valuable in exact proportion to its extravagance—assures us that the spirit, violently expelled from one habitation, may enter another before the warmth of its former passions has subsided. Herod, it is whispered, survived in a wolf; Caligula, according to an ingenious German, in a particularly vicious ape. I do not insist upon these examples. They are cited merely because the events I am about to relate have been explained by men of learning through metempsychosis, by men of religion through diabolical possession, and by men of sense through coincidence. Of the three explanations, the last is commonly the most incredible.

In the southern provinces of Hungary, where the forests retain at noon something of the solemnity of midnight, there stood, until the close of the last century, two enormous estates. Their boundaries touched for nearly forty miles; their histories, for nearly eight hundred years, were one history of insult, ambush, litigation, and blood.

The proprietors were the houses of Metzengerstein and Berlifitzing.

No man living could state with certainty how their hatred had begun. A disputed falcon, a stolen bride, a mutilated banner, and the drowning of an archbishop had each been advanced as its origin. It is sufficient that the animosity had become hereditary. Infants were taught the rival name before they learned their prayers; old men, forgetting their sons, remembered injuries committed by ancestors whose tombs had themselves become ruins. Even the armorial devices seemed to continue the quarrel: the Metzengerstein shield displayed a mailed rider trampling a fallen enemy, while that of Berlifitzing bore a crimson horse rampant beneath a crown of iron.

There existed also a prophecy—rude in expression, but therefore not less revered—which had been discovered, or invented, upon a worm-eaten missal:

_When the mortal Metzengerstein shall bestride the immortal Berlifitzing, the lesser house shall swallow the greater._

The terms had long perplexed antiquaries. The Metzengersteins possessed greater territory, wealth, and feudal influence; yet the Berlifitzings were the elder race. Thus either might, with equal inconvenience, be denominated the lesser house. As for the verbs “bestride” and “swallow,” commentators had exhausted upon them a fertility which would have honored Scripture.

At the period of this narrative the representative of the more opulent family was Frederick, Baron Metzengerstein, who, at the age of fifteen, found himself master of three castles, nine villages, a silver mine, a regiment of hereditary dependants, and revenues sufficient to render restraint unnecessary and repentance improbable.

His father died beneath the wheels of a hunting carriage. His mother followed him within a month, whether from grief or from a malady encouraged by grief was not determined. Frederick wore mourning for four days. On the fifth he ordered musicians from Vienna; on the sixth he released his hounds in the chapel; and on the seventh a peasant, who had petitioned too loudly against an unlawful tax, was discovered hanging from an oak upon the Baron’s private road.

It would be tedious to catalogue the excesses which succeeded. They were youthful only in their want of discrimination. Wine, gaming, sacrilege, nocturnal violence, and those cruelties which spring less from anger than from an experiment upon the endurance of others—these formed the occupations of his minority. Yet he was not handsome in the ordinary manner of dissolutes. His countenance was pale, narrow, and exceedingly still; his eyes, although brilliant, seemed to observe not the person before him but some degradation which that person might be made to suffer.

The nearest neighbor of this amiable youth was Count Wilhelm von Berlifitzing, a widower of seventy, parsimonious in all matters except the maintenance of his horses. He had bred them, raced them, hunted them, slept among them during epidemics, and was rumored to have ordered Masses for the soul of a favorite stallion. His hatred of the Metzengersteins equaled his equestrian fervor. He never pronounced Frederick’s title without first wiping his lips.

It was therefore unfortunate—if the word adequately describes so regular a consequence—that, upon the fourth night following Frederick’s accession, fire should have broken out in the great stables of Berlifitzing.

The conflagration was visible from the windows of Castle Metzengerstein. A coppery illumination throbbed against the western sky; bells clamored from the villages; mounted servants crossed the valley like black insects upon a sheet of flame. The Baron’s household assembled in disorder along the terraces, where expressions of horror, sympathy, and suppressed gratification varied according to rank.

Frederick did not join them.

He was seated alone in an upper gallery—a vast, ill-lighted apartment hung with tapestries representing the triumphs of his race. Whether he had chosen solitude to conceal agitation or to enjoy it cannot now be known. He reclined beneath the painted window, his chin resting upon one jeweled hand, while the distant fire cast intermittent redness over the ancient webs.

Immediately opposite him appeared the most celebrated episode of Metzengerstein history. An ancestor of the Baron, gigantic in armor, drove his lance through the throat of a Berlifitzing prince who lay beneath the hoofs of a colossal horse. The embroiderer, with that indifference to proportion which distinguishes feudal art, had rendered the horse twice the size of nature. It was of a deep and fiery chestnut; foam covered its bit; its nostrils were expanded in agony or wrath; and its enormous eyes, stitched with threads of blackened silver, looked not upon the murderer, nor upon its falling lord, but outward—directly, it seemed, upon the spectator.

The young Baron had seen the tapestry a thousand times. Now, perhaps because of the unstable light, perhaps because the faculties become treacherous when the conscience is interested, he perceived a change.

The horse’s head had moved.

Formerly bent toward the wounded Berlifitzing, it was now turned from the woven scene. The lips had withdrawn from long yellow teeth. More singularly still, the countenance of the brute had assumed an expression not merely intelligent but human—an expression of patient, deliberate, immeasurable malignity.

Frederick arose. The chair fell behind him.

At that moment a red glare poured through the windows, and upon the tapestry there sprang the enlarged shadow of the Baron himself. By an accident of posture, the shadow’s arm coincided with the uplifted weapon of his ancestor. For one instant Frederick appeared to complete the ancient assassination.

He stepped backward; the shadow expanded; the horse seemed to strain against its embroidered reins.

Then the gallery door opened.

“Your Excellency,” said a groom, breathing violently, “a strange horse has been secured in the lower court.”

Frederick turned with such abruptness that the man recoiled.

“A horse?”

“Of extraordinary size, my lord. It came from the direction of the fire. We thought it belonged to Count Berlifitzing, but his people deny it.”

The Baron cast one more glance at the hanging. The horse was motionless; yet the eyes retained, or appeared to retain, their altered direction.

“Show me,” he said.

In the courtyard stood a beast which no person present remembered to have seen before, although each servant afterward asserted that its image had been familiar to him from childhood. It was enormous—higher by a hand than the Hungarian chargers of the guard, deep in the chest, lean about the flank, and red with a redness intensified by sweat and the reflection of the burning stables. Its mane hung black and tangled like scorched grass. Smoke issued, or seemed to issue, from its convulsive nostrils.

Three grooms held it with ropes. It dragged all three across the stones.

Upon its forehead was a pale, irregular mark resembling the shape of a crown. Upon its left shoulder, distinctly branded, appeared the letters W. V. B.

“You stole it,” Frederick said to the chief groom.

The fellow crossed himself. “As God sees me, no, my lord. It sprang from the forest road. The saddle was gone; the bridle had been torn through. We sent to the Count’s people on account of the brand.”

“And?”

“They swear no such animal has stood in their stables.”

“Men will swear anything while their master’s property burns.”

A murmur arose beyond the gate. Another messenger had arrived from Berlifitzing. His face was blackened, and one sleeve of his coat had been burned away.

“Well?” asked Frederick.

The messenger bowed, but looked not at the Baron; he looked at the horse.

“Count Wilhelm is dead.”

There was a pause during which the creature ceased struggling. Its ears advanced.

“How?” Frederick inquired.

“He entered the eastern stable to free the brood mares. The roof descended.”

“Shocking!” said the Baron.

Those nearest him afterward declared that he smiled. Testimony of this kind is colored by subsequent catastrophe; nevertheless, it is certain that the horse, hearing the words, emitted a low and prolonged sound—neither neigh nor scream—which caused every torch in the courtyard to flicker.

Frederick approached it.

“My lord!” cried the groom.

The animal reared, its forefeet beating the air within an inch of the Baron’s skull. Frederick did not retreat. For several seconds boy and beast regarded one another with an intensity which excluded the surrounding world. Then the horse descended, trembling—not with submission, as some supposed, but with restrained violence.

“I will keep him,” said Frederick.

That night, when servants returned to the upper gallery, they found a portion of the tapestry torn entirely away. The missing fragment was that which had contained the gigantic horse. No shred lay upon the floor; no door had been forced; no window stood open. Only the defeated prince, the conquering Metzengerstein, and a vacancy of dark wall remained.

Reports of these circumstances circulated with the rapidity proper to domestic secrets. Within a week every cottage from the Danube to the Carpathian foothills had heard that the dead Count rode in the body of a horse. The educated rejected the conclusion but repeated the facts. A physician from Pest attributed the business to atmospheric electricity; a Jesuit discovered in it the action of an offended saint; Frederick’s chaplain, who desired neither to contradict his patron nor encourage Satan, pronounced the matter “equivocal.”

The Baron alone displayed indifference. He named the horse nothing, permitted no other person to mount it, and dismissed every inquiry with contempt. Yet his conduct soon supplied a more impressive commentary.

Previously addicted to every variety of dissipation, he abandoned them all for riding. At dawn the red horse bore him into the forest; at noon it was seen upon the southern hills; at sunset it thundered across the courtyards, drenched with foam, while the Baron dismounted with blood upon his spurs and a pallor more remarkable than fatigue. Invitations were refused. Musicians were sent away. A princess, distantly related to the Emperor, waited two days without being received. Frederick no longer gambled, feasted, hunted with company, or amused himself with the sufferings of tenants. He rode.

The occupation became more absorbing, more imperative, more impossible to interrupt. He rode beneath rain which flooded the roads; beneath heat which killed birds in the vineyards; beneath moonlight so colorless that horse and rider appeared carved from moving ash. Sometimes he was absent for thirty hours. Sometimes, after returning exhausted, he would hear the stall door shake below and descend at once, still booted, to set forth again.

The household mistook this devotion for mastery. Their error was natural. Frederick spoke to the animal with imperious brevity and punished it savagely before observers. But the old stable-master, who had served three generations and feared horses less than noblemen, maintained another opinion.

“The Baron strikes,” he said, “because the Baron is afraid.”

Afraid? Of an animal he rode alone? The proposition was ridiculed. Yet certain signs supported it. When the horse turned its head unexpectedly, Frederick’s hand flew not to the rein but to the dagger beneath his coat. When it screamed at night, candles burned until morning in his chamber. Once, upon discovering the creature at liberty in the courtyard, the Baron stood in an archway, unable either to advance or withdraw, while it approached step by measured step and pressed its nostrils against his breast.

“What did it do then?” asked a servant.

“It listened,” replied the stable-master.

“To what?”

The old man touched his own heart.

There were other peculiarities. No stable-fellow would remain beside the horse. Dogs crawled from it upon their bellies; birds became silent when it passed. It consumed no visible quantity of food, although its strength increased. Locks failed to confine it. A groom who attempted to cut the branded initials from its shoulder was kicked senseless and afterward spoke German, a language he had never learned, throughout the three hours preceding his death.

We must not exaggerate. The last incident rests upon the testimony of his grandmother, who was deaf.

Autumn descended early that year. The woods blackened; the marshes exhaled fever; mists wound among the towers of Metzengerstein and made the immense château appear suspended above the earth. Frederick grew thinner. His eyes acquired the fixed brilliance of the horse’s eyes, and servants sometimes observed in his countenance that same distortion—half terror, half hatred—which the tapestry had exhibited before its disappearance.

Still he rode.

On the anniversary of Count Wilhelm’s death, a storm gathered over the valley. All afternoon the clouds assembled without rain. They formed rank beyond rank, purple at the bases, livid at the summits, until the mountains vanished and the forests seemed enclosed beneath a vault of metal. No wind moved, yet from the stables came the incessant stamping of the red estray.

At twilight Frederick descended.

“Not tonight, my lord,” said the stable-master.

The Baron paused upon the stair. His face was ghastly; his riding cloak had been fastened awry.

“Did I ask advice?”

“No, my lord.”

“Then saddle him.”

“He is saddled.”

Frederick looked toward the old man.

“By whom?”

At that instant the stable doors burst outward. The horse sprang into the court already bridled and bearing a black saddle, although no servant acknowledged having equipped it. Its eyes blazed with a lurid, inward luminosity. The initials upon its shoulder appeared fresh and wet.

Frederick’s lips moved. Whatever command he intended was lost in thunder.

The creature knelt.

This act of apparent submission terrified the spectators more profoundly than violence. The Baron remained motionless; then, as if compelled by pride in the presence of servants—or by some coercion less easily named—he placed one foot in the stirrup and mounted.

The horse rose.

One instant it stood beneath him, colossal and rigid. Another—it had crossed the court. A third—and horse and rider were beyond the gates, rushing toward the forest as the storm broke behind them.

Hours passed.

Near midnight a watchman upon the northern tower smelled smoke. Almost simultaneously flames appeared in three unconnected wings of the château. They climbed with a rapidity which rendered human agency doubtful and supernatural agency unnecessary, for the old roofs were dry, the passages hung with arras, and the chambers stored with oils, wines, and worm-eaten furniture.

The household fled. Servants, guests, hounds, and half-clothed dependants poured across the bridge while the palace of Metzengerstein roared behind them. Towers long black against the lightning became red from within; windows burst outward; ancestral portraits shriveled in their frames. Fire ran along the battlements like troops carrying banners.

“Where is the Baron?” someone cried.

“In the forest!”

“No—listen!”

Through thunder, wind, bells, falling masonry, and the tumult of the fugitives, there arose a sound at first indistinguishable from the storm. It strengthened. It became rhythmical. It became terrible.

Hoofs.

Far down the avenue, beneath trees bent almost double, a red shape appeared and vanished between the lightning flashes. It approached with preternatural speed. Sparks flew from the roadway. The gatekeeper dragged at the chains, intending to close the iron gates, but the crowd struck him aside.

The horse came on.

Frederick was upon its back.

There could be no question now of command. The reins were wrapped repeatedly around his hands, yet his arms were extended helplessly before him. Blood streamed from his torn palms. His body had fallen backward nearly upon the animal’s haunches; his cloak snapped horizontally in the gale. His face—white, convulsed, aged by a lifetime within an hour—was turned toward the burning château.

He shouted once.

Whether the cry formed the word “Stop,” the name “Berlifitzing,” or merely that inarticulate appeal which pride permits when hope has ceased, no two witnesses agreed.

The gate stood open. The horse swerved toward it.

Men scattered. Frederick twisted the reins until the leather broke. He struck with the broken ends; he drove both spurs deep; he seized the mane and endeavored to throw himself sideways. The creature’s speed did not diminish. On the contrary, it curved its neck with an almost human exultation and bounded through the entrance.

Across the first court—through a rain of sparks—up the marble stairs.

For one suspended instant horse and rider were visible against the central doorway, black figures within a frame of intolerable fire. Then the horse reared. Frederick’s outline became identical with that of the mailed assassin in the ancestral tapestry: arm lifted, body thrown back, victim beneath him—or master above him; the distinction had lost all meaning.

The roof descended.

There followed a concussion which extinguished every lamp in the valley. The western tower folded inward; the great hall opened like a furnace; and from the multitude escaped one common cry.

Then the storm ceased.

Not gradually—not with the retreating mutter and exhausted rain customary to tempests—but at once. The winds fell. The clouds separated. A moon, enormous and red, looked down upon the ruins.

No effort was made to recover the Baron. By morning there remained of Castle Metzengerstein only steaming foundations and a portion of the eastern wall, upon which hung, untouched by flame, the mutilated tapestry. The conquering ancestor still extended his lance; the dying Berlifitzing still lay beneath him; but where the horse had been there was only darkness.

Nor were the bodies of Frederick and the estray discovered.

As dawn whitened the mountains, a column of smoke arose from the central ruin. At first it ascended narrowly, like smoke from an altar. Higher in the tranquil air it broadened, divided, and assumed an extraordinary coherence. The villagers knelt. The soldiers removed their helmets. Even the physician from Pest, who had arrived to investigate the conflagration, refrained from offering an atmospheric explanation.

Above the extinguished house, vast enough to overshadow both estates, the smoke had taken the form of a gigantic horse.

Its mane streamed though no wind moved. Its forefeet were raised above the valley. Upon its brow rested the likeness of a crown; and in its distorted, downward-bending countenance there appeared—if vapor may be said to express anything—the calm malignity of triumph.

Thus the prophecy was fulfilled, though not, perhaps, in the manner anticipated by the genealogists. The mortal Metzengerstein had indeed bestridden the immortal Berlifitzing; the lesser house had swallowed the greater—not through inheritance, conquest, or marriage, but through flame.

By noon the apparition had dispersed. Of the two families nothing remained except debts, disputed titles, a blackened valley, and the old hatred, which, being without heirs, should reasonably have perished.

Yet on certain autumn nights, the shepherds of that province still refuse to cross the foundations of the château. They say that hoofbeats sound beneath the earth; that a young man cries out once, very far below; and that from the empty place where the great doors stood there issues, slowly and without a rider, an enormous horse redder than fire.

At the northern edge of Winesburg, where the last houses gave way to fields and the road whitened under summer dust, an old man walked back and forth upon a decaying veranda.

His name was Wing Biddlebaum.

The house behind him had once been painted yellow. Now the boards were gray and split, the roof sagged above two upper windows, and mustard grew shoulder-high in the neglected field. Beyond a crooked fence, berry pickers were returning from the country. Their wagons rolled slowly toward town, wheels complaining in the ruts, while boys and women sat among empty baskets and called to one another through the dust.

Wing watched the road.

He was a small, nervous man with a bald head and a narrow body. His hands were large and restless. As he paced, they darted from his pockets, rubbed together, touched his cheeks, and disappeared again. At times they beat upon the railing like two frightened creatures demanding to be let in. Then Wing would remember himself and thrust them beneath his arms.

He hoped George Willard would come.

George, a young reporter for the Winesburg Eagle, was almost the only person who visited the old house. Others knew Wing, but they did not know him. In town he passed quickly along the street, avoiding doorways and groups of men. Children sometimes followed him and shouted his name. Farmers remembered that he could pick more strawberries in a day than any two laborers, his fingers moving above the plants like the beak of a giant woodpecker. Of the man beneath the hands they knew nothing.

With George it was different.

The young man’s presence loosened something in Wing. Words came out of him. His shoulders lifted, his eyes grew bright, and his hands forgot their shame. They flew before him, shaping thoughts in the air, striking the table, trembling above George’s head. When George went away, Wing remembered the hands and became afraid again.

The last wagon passed. A girl in a red dress waved, and Wing raised one hand before quickly lowering it. The road became empty. Far off, roofs and chimneys stood in the evening light, and from the town came the faint clatter of dishes.

“He will come,” Wing whispered.

He descended the steps and walked through the mustard to the fence. There he waited, gripping a post. A grasshopper sprang near his shoe. The old man stared at it with such attention that one might have thought he had forgotten the road, but when a figure appeared beyond the bend, he straightened at once.

It was George.

The young reporter climbed the fence instead of using the gate. He carried his coat over one shoulder, and his face was flushed from walking.

“I thought I would find you out here,” he said.

Wing laughed softly. His hands fluttered, vanished into his pockets, and fluttered out again.

“You have been in town listening,” he said. “Always listening to people. That is dangerous for a young man.”

George smiled. He was accustomed to such openings. “I listen because it is my work.”

“No, no. Your work is to see.”

Wing took George by the sleeve and led him across the field. They sat on the grass beneath a stunted apple tree. Before them the roofs of Winesburg rested in a pale haze. Behind them the old house waited with its blind windows and rotting porch.

For a time neither spoke. George pulled at blades of grass. Wing watched him with tenderness and fear.

“You want to write,” Wing said at last. “You want to understand people. But all day the voices come at you. One says, ‘Be this.’ Another says, ‘Do that.’ Your father has a voice. The men in the hotel have voices. The merchants, the preachers, the women at their windows—all have voices. Soon they will make such a noise in you that your own voice will become faint.”

As he spoke, his hands rose.

They were no longer grotesque. In the evening light they seemed delicate and eloquent. They moved like pennants stirred by a wind that touched nothing else, opening and closing as if releasing invisible birds.

“You must dream,” Wing continued. “A man must sometimes go alone into a room and shut the door. He must go into a field where no one calls his name. What lies within him is shy. It will not appear before a crowd.”

George looked toward town. “Is that why you live here?”

The hands stopped.

For an instant Wing’s face emptied. Then he smiled, but the smile was pitiful.

“I speak of you,” he said. “You are young. There is still time.”

His voice rose again, gaining strength. He spoke of boys who became clerks because their fathers were clerks, of girls who strangled their desires because old women had told them what was proper, of men who mistook the opinion of neighbors for the will of God. The words poured from him with a preacher’s rhythm. He forgot the mustard field, the town, and the long years of hiding.

“Do not let them press you into their shape,” he cried. “To dream is not idleness. The dream is the seed. Guard it. Let it grow crooked if it must, but let it be yours.”

His right hand came to rest upon George’s shoulder.

The gesture was light. Then his fingers moved toward the young man’s hair.

Wing saw what he was doing.

He sprang up with a cry. Both hands flew behind his back. His body bent forward as though expecting a blow.

“What is it?” George asked.

Wing backed away. “Nothing. Go home. It will be dark.”

“But, Wing—”

“Go!”

The old man hurried through the mustard, stumbled at the steps, and disappeared into the house. George remained beneath the tree, troubled and ashamed, although he did not know why.

To understand the terror of Wing Biddlebaum’s hands, one must leave Winesburg and travel backward twenty years to a little Pennsylvania town among wooded hills. Let us look briefly into a schoolroom there, for obscure lives sometimes require a poet, and when no poet comes, the truth must wait in silence.

Wing Biddlebaum was then called Adolph Myers.

He was a schoolteacher, young, slender, and almost beautiful. The school stood beside a creek. In spring, dogwood blossomed on the hills, and the boys came barefoot through the mud. They loved their teacher.

Adolph possessed no grand method. He did not rule by shouting or by the ruler kept upon his desk. As he walked between the benches, he touched a boy’s shoulder or smoothed the tangled hair above a bent forehead. His hands encouraged without interrupting. They awakened attention as sunlight awakens a sleeping room.

In the afternoons he sometimes led the pupils beyond the schoolyard. Under the trees they spoke of distant countries, of stars, machines, wars, poems, and the lives they might one day live. Adolph’s hands moved among them. A palm rested briefly upon a head. Fingers pressed a shoulder when a timid answer came. The boys, sensing no fear in him, forgot to fear themselves.

“Think your own thought,” he would say. “Do not borrow another man’s eyes.”

There was among the pupils a boy named Henry Bradford, tall for his age and troubled. He was neglected at home and cruel among the smaller children. At night he lay awake inventing scenes in which he was adored, punished, rescued, and destroyed. What happened in his imagination possessed for him the hardness of fact.

Adolph tried to help him. He kept Henry after school, walked with him along the creek, and spoke gently when the boy lied. Once, as Henry wept, the teacher placed both hands upon his shoulders.

The boy afterward told a story.

At first he whispered it to another pupil. Then he told his mother. Each telling added a gesture, a secret word, an ugliness that had never occurred. The mother repeated it to her husband, and by evening the invention had passed from kitchen to store, from store to stable, from stable to the dark gathering place behind the hotel.

Men nodded. They remembered the teacher’s hands.

“I have seen him pawing the boys,” one said.

“Knew there was something wrong,” said another.

A thing innocent in daylight changed its nature in their talk. A touch became a caress, kindness became corruption, and the schoolteacher’s love for his pupils became proof against him.

No one asked Adolph Myers to explain.

The school board dismissed him. That night a group of men came to his lodging. They dragged him down the stairs in his nightshirt and beat him in the road. One struck his mouth. Another kicked him after he had fallen. A rope was brought, and for a few terrible minutes the men argued over the branch of an elm.

An old farmer named Isaac Grey stopped them. He did not defend the teacher. He merely said hanging would bring trouble from the county.

They released Adolph at the edge of town.

“Get out,” a man told him. “If we see your hands near another boy, we’ll finish it.”

Blood ran from his mouth. One arm hung uselessly. He walked through the night, and by morning he was ten miles away. He did not look back.

And then began the second life.

Adolph Myers disappeared. Wing Biddlebaum came into existence. The name was taken partly from a packing crate and partly from a word overheard at a railway station. Under it he moved westward until he reached Winesburg. There he bought the ruined house outside town and went to work in the berry fields.

His shoulder healed. His lip left only a pale scar. The deeper wound did not heal.

He came to believe the men had been right in one respect: his hands were guilty. They had moved without permission. They had betrayed secrets in him that he could not name. In the darkness he sometimes held them before his face and whispered to them as to disobedient children. At other times he struck them against the wall.

During his first years in Winesburg, he tried to keep them always hidden. Yet in the berry fields they escaped. They leaped among the leaves, quick and tireless, filling basket after basket while other pickers stared. His speed brought him a little fame and gave him his new first name. The hands beat above the plants like wings. Wing, the laborers called him, and Wing he remained.

No accusation followed him. Nothing happened. That was almost worse.

Fear became a room in which he lived. Every laugh heard behind him might be the beginning of a mob. Every friendly question might open the old story. Daylight exposed him; darkness covered him. Thus the years passed, and the young teacher who had awakened dreams in boys became an old man trembling at the sight of his own tenderness.

Only George Willard came near enough to disturb the silence.

After leaving Wing beneath the apple tree, George walked slowly toward Winesburg. He tried to remember the exact movement of the hand upon his shoulder. There had been nothing offensive in it. It had seemed instead like a blessing begun and then snatched away.

At the bend he turned.

Wing stood for a moment in the doorway of the house, watching him. Even at that distance George could see the old man’s hands clasped behind his back. Then the doorway became empty.

Darkness settled over the fields. Lamps appeared in Winesburg, each making a small yellow square. The evening train came from the west with a long cry. It paused at the station, breathed heavily, and went on, leaving its smoke above the roofs like a torn black cloth.

In the old house Wing waited.

He had set two plates upon the kitchen table. There was bread, a little cheese, and a bowl of beans. From time to time he went to the window, although he knew George would not return. The darkness outside comforted him. No face could rise from the road and discover his hands.

At last he removed the second plate.

He ate standing up, taking small bites. Once a crust fell beneath the table. Wing looked down at it. His fingers twitched.

The house was silent except for the scraping of a branch against the roof. On the wall hung a cheap clock that had stopped years before, but Wing still glanced at it. Habit continued where time had failed.

He washed the plate, placed the food in a cupboard, and prepared for bed. Before extinguishing the lamp, he returned to the kitchen for the fallen crust.

He knelt beneath the table.

There, hidden by darkness and the hanging cloth, his hands became free. They searched the floor, found the crust, broke it into crumbs, gathered the crumbs, lost them, and gathered them again. His fingers moved rapidly and delicately, touching one another in a ceaseless order.

Seen from the doorway, the old man might have been a priest bowed before a humble altar. His fingers, moving through shadow, were like the fingers of a devotee counting a rosary.

No prayer passed his lips.

Still, the hands prayed.

## The Tenderfoot at Knapp’s

In the spring of 1874, when the stage road west of Fort Randall was a pair of ruts where it was not a mud-hole, Billy Knapp kept a hotel at the crossing of Red Willow Creek.

Hotel was Billy’s word. A traveller of strict habits might have called it a stable with opinions. It had one story of cottonwood logs, another of warped boards, a kitchen leaning against the rear as if tired, and a bar-room in which a man could obtain whiskey, bacon, cartridges, information, or a broken head according to the amount of money and judgment he brought with him.

The sign said:

**KNAPP’S HOTEL  
MEALS AT ALL HOURS  
NO TRUST**

The first two statements were elastic. The last was iron.

Billy had been a freighter, mule-skinner, deputy sheriff, amateur dentist and, for three regrettable weeks, owner of a newspaper. He was broad in the middle, narrow in conscience on small commercial matters, and brave enough where bravery was likely to be noticed. He possessed a red beard, an enormous voice and a pearl-handled revolver which had never killed anybody, though Billy had often improved the story of its career.

The household consisted of Charley Price, who tended bar and disliked work; Nell Mercer, who cooked and disliked Charley; and an old yellow dog that disliked everybody equally.

Nell was not handsome in the fashion then admired in Eastern parlours. She had brown arms, steady gray eyes and a mouth which looked made for deciding things. She could lift a flour sack, skin a rabbit, judge a horse’s age and tell a liar before he had finished his first sentence. Women of her sort were called tough by men who depended on them for nearly every comfort of life.

Late one afternoon a solitary traveller came down the road on a weary roan.

Billy saw him through the bar-room window and gave a disappointed grunt. The stranger did not promise trade. He was small, narrow-shouldered and dressed in a black suit which had been made for a larger and more hopeful man. His collar rose to his ears; his boots were powdered white; and beneath the rim of a wide felt hat appeared a bald head, a little pointed face and a pair of mild blue eyes.

He looked like something a professor might keep under glass after first putting a Latin label on it.

The traveller dismounted awkwardly, caught his coat on the saddle horn, apologized to the horse and entered carrying a heavy leather satchel against his breast.

“Good evening,” he said. “I beg yore pardon. Is this Mr. Knapp’s hotel?”

“No,” Billy said. “This yere’s th’ Palace of Versailles. Mister Knapp’s hotel is in Paris.”

The traveller considered this without resentment.

“I fear I have never been to Paris.”

Billy stared, then laughed so violently that the yellow dog awoke and barked.

“Set down, stranger. You’ve struck Knapp’s. What might they call you when they don’t call you late fer breakfast?”

“Alfred will answer, sir.”

“Alfred what?”

“Alfred will be sufficient, if it ain’t troubling you too much.”

Billy winked at Charley.

“Tenderfoot,” Charley said.

There were in the West men who could recognize a tenderfoot by his boots, men who recognized one by his speech, and men who called every stranger a tenderfoot until the stranger knocked them down. Charley belonged to the third and most numerous class.

Alfred paid for his lodging in advance. Then, instead of setting his satchel down, he placed it between his boots and kept both ankles pressed against it.

Billy noticed this. A hotel-keeper who failed to notice luggage soon became an employee of one who did.

“Valuables?” he asked pleasantly.

“Only some personal articles.”

“Gold tooth-brush, mebbe?”

“No, sir.”

“Patent liver pills?”

“No, sir.”

“Love letters?”

At this Alfred blushed from the points of his collar to the bald crown of his head. Billy shouted again, having found a toy that worked whenever squeezed.

Nell called them to supper.

At table Alfred sat beside his satchel and ate with grave delicacy, though the meal consisted of bacon capable of stopping a bullet and biscuits best approached with a mining tool. Two freighters, Billy, Charley and Nell shared the table. Alfred addressed Nell as “ma’am” and thanked her each time she passed anything, which caused Charley to watch him with contempt.

When Nell brought the coffee, Charley caught her apron string.

“Set yere, honey,” he said. “I got a lap ain’t doin’ nothin’.”

Nell struck his hand away with the pot lid.

“Neither’s the rest of you.”

Charley’s face darkened. He had been drinking since noon and had reached the stage where a fool mistakes humiliation for an injury.

“You’re mighty particular fer a camp cook.”

Nell turned back.

“What’s that?”

“I said a woman that’s slung hash in every bull camp from Yankton to Laramie needn’t stack herself so high in th’ blue chips.”

Billy looked at his plate. The freighters became interested in the ceiling. Frontier society was exact in some matters and conveniently blind in others.

Alfred laid down his fork.

“I beg yore pardon,” he said.

Charley looked at him. “What fer?”

“I believe you insulted the lady.”

Charley’s mouth fell open, and then he laughed.

“You believe it?”

“Yes, sir. I ain’t familiar with the customs of this particular hotel, but elsewhere a gentleman would withdraw the remark.”

“A gentleman!” Charley leaned across the table. “Listen at th’ bantam crow.”

Alfred’s hands trembled. That was plain to everyone. His small face had gone white, and he glanced toward the door as if calculating the distance to safety.

Nell saw the fear and, because she saw it, understood the courage.

Charley saw only the fear.

“What if I don’t withdraw it?”

“Then,” Alfred said unhappily, “I shall have to ask you to step outside.”

The silence that followed was almost worth the price of supper.

Charley rose. Alfred rose also, still pale, still trembling, and pushed back his chair. He had no visible weapon.

Billy decided the joke had matured far enough.

“Set down, Charley. Nell kin handle her own wars, an’ I ain’t havin’ customers mussed before they settle their bar account.”

“He’s paid,” Charley said.

“That makes him under my protection.”

Charley muttered, but sat.

Nell poured Alfred another cup of coffee. Her face did not change, though she filled his cup to the brim and Charley’s only halfway.

After supper Billy showed Alfred to the upstairs room. It contained two bunks, a washbasin and a window opening upon the rear yard. Alfred carried the satchel himself. His small trunk, brought in from the horse, proved surprisingly heavy.

“Books?” Billy asked.

“Some clothing.”

“Must wear iron drawers.”

Alfred smiled faintly.

Billy left him and went down to the bar, where the episode at supper was improved through repetition. By nine o’clock Charley had become a Goliath with blood in his eye, Alfred a consumptive sparrow, and Billy the restraining hand of Providence.

At half past ten hoofbeats came from the east.

They were too many for a stage and too orderly for cowhands.

Billy stopped talking.

The door opened and Black Hank entered with six men behind him.

Hank was not black except in reputation. His hair and beard were brown, but his eyebrows met heavily above the nose and gave his face the appearance of a storm cloud that had decided to remain. He was thick in the chest, clean in his movements and dressed without decoration. A revolver hung low at each thigh.

His band had robbed stages, freight outfits and two government pay wagons. In settled country he would have been described as a criminal. Along Red Willow he was spoken of as a power, much as men spoke of drought, smallpox or the Missouri River. One did not approve of any of them, but one calculated accordingly.

“Hullo, Billy,” Hank said.

“Hullo, Hank. You boys come fer supper?”

“No.”

“Beds?”

“No.”

“That limits th’ profitable branches of my business.”

Hank’s men spread through the room. One closed the front door. Another looked into the kitchen.

“We’re after a traveller,” Hank said. “Little feller. Black clothes. Come through yere today.”

Billy scratched his beard.

“Stage fetched two drummers Tuesday.”

“Today.”

“Feller named Swede Pete come by, but he was six foot four an’ possessed of whiskers.”

Hank rested one hand on the bar.

“This man carries a leather satchel. He’s takin’ fifty thousand dollars in government money to Spotted Tail.”

Billy’s eyes moved before he could stop them. They moved upward.

Hank saw.

So did Charley.

Upstairs, Alfred had not gone to bed. He sat on the edge of the bunk with his coat off and his ear inclined toward the floor. At the first sound of horses he opened the trunk.

The clothing inside occupied little space. Beneath it lay a buffalo coat, a coil of rawhide rope, two revolvers in soft leather, a pair of field glasses, moccasins, a plainsman’s gray shirt, a packet of maps and a knife worn thin by sharpening. These were not the possessions of a man beginning his acquaintance with the frontier.

Alfred buckled on the revolvers, put his coat over them and took up the satchel.

Nell came in without knocking.

“Black Hank’s downstairs.”

“Yes, ma’am. I gathered it might be him.”

“They know about the money.”

Alfred looked at her. “What money?”

“Don’t be foolish at me. Billy’s foolish enough fer the whole house. Kin you climb?”

“Under favorable circumstances.”

“These ain’t favorable.”

She opened the window. The roof of the kitchen sloped beneath it, and beyond the eaves stood a cottonwood, its branches brushing the shingles.

“Out there. Step on the roof, catch that limb an’ get into the tree. Don’t jump down. Ground’s soft, an’ they’ll see tracks.”

Alfred removed his boots.

Nell stared.

“Sir,” he explained, “the shingles appear noisy.”

He went through the window, sat on the sill, reached the limb and drew himself silently into the cottonwood. He moved without the least awkwardness now. In a moment he was lost among the new leaves.

Nell took his boots and satchel. “What do I do with these?”

“The boots may stay under the bed. Please throw the satchel as far as you can into the weeds behind the springhouse.”

“Throw fifty thousand dollars?”

“It ain’t in there.”

She looked at him sharply.

He smiled.

Then footsteps sounded on the stairs.

Nell flung the satchel through the window. It struck the kitchen roof, slid to the edge and dropped into a rain barrel with a splash.

They both winced.

“Beg yore pardon,” Alfred whispered.

Nell shut the window and was standing beside the bunk when Hank entered with two men.

“Where’s he?” Hank asked.

“Who?”

“The tenderfoot.”

“Gone.”

Hank saw the boots beneath the bunk. He went to the window and looked out. The yard below was moonlit mud, unmarked except for old hoofprints.

“He fly?”

“Mebbe he turned into smoke.”

Hank examined Nell. Men sometimes lied to Black Hank, but few of them did it comfortably. Nell returned his stare as if he were a pie she suspected of not being done in the middle.

Hank searched the room. His men searched the other rooms, the kitchen, loft, stable, privy and brush along the creek. One found the satchel in the rain barrel. It contained a nightshirt, shaving soap, a Bible and a small framed photograph of a woman old enough to be Alfred’s grandmother.

No money.

They took Billy into the bar-room.

Charley required little persuasion.

“He was here,” he said. “Little bald-headed runt. Guarded that bag like it was a royal flush. Billy give him the upstairs room.”

Billy turned on him.

“You low-lived, bottle-suckin’ coyote.”

“I ain’t gettin’ shot fer no tenderfoot.”

“That’s sensible,” Hank said. “Billy, where’s the messenger?”

“Vamoosed, seems like.”

“How?”

“If I knowed how folks vamoosed, I’d likely be in San Francisco with a richer woman.”

Hank’s mouth tightened.

He had a difficulty beyond the missing money. Seven armed men had entered a public house and announced their purpose. If they rode away empty-handed after being deceived by Billy Knapp and an undersized traveller, the story would cover the plains before the grasshoppers did. An outlaw chief lives partly by ammunition and partly by the belief that resistance is unprofitable. Of the two supplies, belief is harder to replace.

Hank questioned Billy for twenty minutes. Billy swore, joked, lied and offered whiskey. At last Hank glanced toward his men.

“Take him outside.”

Billy ceased smiling.

“What fer?”

“Because I can’t leave you behind to tell how you hid him.”

“I didn’t hide nobody.”

“Then you’re unlucky.”

“Rope or bullet?” Billy asked.

“Bullet. You’ve kept a fair bar.”

This was intended as a courtesy.

Two men led Billy through the kitchen and stood him against the wall beside the chopping block. The others remained in the bar-room with Hank. Charley poured himself a drink, though his hand shook badly enough to spill half of it.

Nell slipped out the rear door.

The cottonwood seemed empty.

“Alfred,” she whispered.

A voice answered just above her.

“Yes, ma’am?”

She nearly cried out. He was seated astride a branch less than ten feet away, his black coat buttoned and his stockinged feet hanging absurdly beneath him.

“They’re goin’ to kill Billy.”

Alfred said nothing.

“You kin get away,” Nell continued. “The creek bank’ll hide you. Their horses is out front. Go now.”

“Why are they killing him?”

“Because they think he helped you.”

“But he did not.”

“Black Hank don’t cut his justice that fine.”

From around the corner came Billy’s voice.

“Don’t tie th’ handkerchief too tight. I aim to look natural at th’ funeral.”

Alfred shifted on the branch.

Nell seized his wrist. “Don’t you go in there. There’s seven of ’em.”

“I counted.”

“They’ll kill you too, an’ take the money.”

“Perhaps.”

She mistook the quiet word for surrender and released her breath.

Then Alfred said, “I am greatly obliged to you.”

“What?”

“For helping me. Also for what you did before.”

“I didn’t do nothin’ before.”

“At supper. You understood.”

His face was pale in the moonlight, and his voice had become painfully earnest.

“I ain’t had much kindness from women, Miss Nell. My mother died when I was born. I was brought up by an uncle who considered affection injurious to discipline. Ladies commonly find me—well, they find other gentlemen first.”

Nell had no preparation for this confession. Somewhere beyond the corner, an outlaw complained that the firing squad was taking too long.

Alfred continued, “You have been kinder than any lady I remember.”

“Oh, hush,” she said, and, from embarrassment more than tenderness, leaned up and kissed him.

It was a quick kiss, given almost as carelessly as one might kiss the cold end of a dog’s nose. To Nell it was a method of stopping speech. To Alfred it was revelation.

He looked at her for one startled second.

Then he dropped from the branch without a sound.

Before she could catch him, he went around the hotel.

Billy stood against the kitchen wall. One outlaw held a lantern. The other, a thin man called Missouri Joe, had stepped back six paces and was choosing a place on Billy’s shirt with the care of a housewife selecting cloth.

“Hold on,” Alfred said.

Missouri Joe turned.

Alfred stood at the corner in his stockinged feet, coat open, a revolver in each hand.

Joe’s gun was half drawn when Alfred’s right-hand weapon fired.

Smash!

Joe spun sideways and fell beside the woodpile.

The lantern-bearer dropped his rifle.

“Please don’t pick that up,” Alfred said. “It might cause inconvenience.”

Inside the bar-room Hank and four men reached for their guns.

Alfred came through the kitchen doorway behind them.

“Beg yore pardon, gentlemen. Hands on the ceiling, if it ain’t too much trouble.”

One man wheeled.

Smash! Smash!

The first bullet struck the barrel of his revolver and knocked it from his hand. The second broke the chimney of the lamp above Hank’s head. Darkness fell over half the room while the remaining lamp swayed in the concussion.

“Next one won’t be decorative,” Alfred said.

Hands rose.

Hank stood motionless, fingers spread at shoulder height. He had heard of shooting like that. Nearly every frontier camp had a man who claimed to have seen it, and nearly every such man was a liar. Hank now found the genuine article less entertaining than the accounts.

“Charley,” Alfred said, “kindly collect the gentlemen’s weapons.”

Charley did not move.

Alfred looked at him.

Charley moved.

He pulled revolvers from belts and rifles from the wall and made a pile on the bar. Billy entered rubbing his wrists.

“Well, Alfred,” he said, “you shore concealed yore social gifts.”

“I’m sorry for the disturbance, Mr. Knapp.”

“Disturbance! You’ve ventilated my lamp.”

“I shall pay for it.”

“You’ll do no such thing. I been meanin’ to ventilate that lamp fer years.”

One outlaw edged toward the window.

Alfred fired without looking directly at him. A nail above the man’s hand vanished from the frame.

The outlaw froze.

“Please remain where you are.”

“I warn’t goin’ nowhere.”

“Then we are in agreement.”

Hank lowered his hands an inch.

Alfred’s left revolver cracked. The pearl button at Hank’s throat leaped away, leaving the cloth untouched.

Hank raised his hands again.

“I don’t generally permit that,” he said.

“No, sir. I don’t generally do it.”

Billy dragged Missouri Joe’s companion inside and tied him with a flour-sack cord. Joe himself was dead. The frontier did not often pause over a man who had drawn first; it had too much other business.

Alfred directed Billy to bind the remaining outlaws. His speech stayed mild, almost apologetic, but his eyes had changed. The blue had gone flat and cool, like distant ice.

When all six living men were secured, Alfred reloaded. He did it swiftly, never glancing down.

Hank watched.

“Who are you?”

“Alfred.”

“I heard that much.”

“It has usually been enough.”

“Not fer me.”

Alfred sighed, as if Hank had asked him to complete a tiresome hotel register.

Before answering, he walked to the far wall. A playing card was pinned there above a shelf. It was the ace of diamonds, greasy and bent.

“Mr. Knapp, would you object if I damaged that card?”

“I got fifty-one others.”

Alfred turned his back to it. Looking into the tarnished mirror behind the bar, he fired over his shoulder.

The red diamond disappeared.

No one spoke.

He placed a cartridge on the bar, balanced another upright upon it, walked twelve paces away and fired. The upper cartridge jumped into the air. Before it fell, his second revolver cracked and drove it sideways into the wall.

Billy drew a long breath.

“I withdraw,” he said, “every opinion I ever had of yore dimensions.”

Alfred holstered both guns.

“My name is Alfred Vane.”

Black Hank’s face altered.

“Little Vane?”

“I have been called that.”

“The scout that brought Crook’s patrol out of the Powder River country?”

Alfred looked embarrassed. “There were several others concerned.”

“The man that shot Running Wolf off a pony at four hundred yards?”

“The distance has increased considerably in the telling.”

Hank leaned back against the bar and laughed once.

“Boys,” he said to his bound companions, “we struck pyrites. This ain’t no tenderfoot. This is th’ Little Ghost.”

The name was known from the Platte to the Yellowstone. It belonged to a scout said to cross open ground without leaving sign, to shoot by reflection, to speak three Indian languages and to have carried dispatches through country where a regiment declined to travel. Most of the stories were false. Enough were true.

Billy stared at Alfred’s bald head and narrow chest.

“I’d expected more of you,” he said.

“So do most people.”

Nell came through the kitchen. Alfred saw her and at once became the man who had caught his coat on the saddle horn. He blushed, looked toward the floor and nearly backed into a chair.

Such consistency is rare in heroes and should be valued.

“The money?” Hank asked.

“Safe.”

“Wasn’t in the satchel.”

“No, sir.”

“Where is it?”

Alfred hesitated. “Since you’re tied, I suppose there’s no harm in saying. It’s wrapped in oiled cloth inside a flour tin buried beneath the manure pile behind the stable.”

Billy made a wounded sound.

“My manure pile?”

“I selected a place unlikely to be searched by gentlemen in a hurry.”

Hank shook his head.

“When’d you plant it?”

“Before supper. I came here by the creek bed, buried it, then rode back three miles and approached on the road.”

“So that’s why there weren’t no trail from a second horse,” Hank said.

Alfred nodded.

Hank looked almost pleased. A professional man can appreciate sound work even when it has been applied against him.

“What now?”

“I shall borrow your horses.”

“All of ’em?”

“And the weapons.”

“That’s horse stealin’.”

“I fear so.”

“You lawmen got flexible morals.”

“I ain’t a lawman. I’m a messenger.”

Billy and Nell brought the horses around. Alfred selected Hank’s black gelding and packed the confiscated rifles across two saddles. He recovered the government money, washed the flour tin at the pump and fitted it into a canvas sack. Billy supplied him with boots, Alfred’s own having somehow been overlooked beneath the bed.

Before dawn he was ready.

The outlaws remained tied in the bar-room, where Charley guarded them with a shotgun and more importance than he had ever possessed. Billy intended to keep them until Alfred had twelve hours’ start, then release them afoot. This was not law, but it was a practical compromise between hospitality and hanging.

At the door Alfred held out his hand to Billy.

“I regret bringing danger to your house.”

“Danger comes regular. You’re th’ first guest made it pay fer drinks.”

“I did not pay for drinks.”

“Then you owe me.”

Alfred shook hands with Nell. At least he tried. She put her arms around him and kissed him properly this time.

He stood rigid, red and speechless.

Billy watched with the reflective interest of a man who would later make three matrimonial ventures of his own and profit in wisdom from none of them.

“Miss Nell,” Alfred managed, “I—”

“You’ve got government business,” she said. “Tend to it.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

He mounted Hank’s gelding and rode west, leading the spare horses. At the crest beyond Red Willow he looked back once. Nell stood before the hotel with the dawn behind her. Billy waved his hat. Alfred lifted one hand, turned and went on toward Spotted Tail.

He delivered the fifty thousand dollars two days later, lacking neither a bill nor a cartridge.

Black Hank did not pursue him. This was partly because Hank had no horse, partly because Alfred had taken his weapons, and partly because even outlaw chiefs may distinguish perseverance from suicide. When released, Hank walked thirty miles to the next ranch and afterward told the story himself, though in his version Missouri Joe had drawn against orders and the pearl button had been one Hank never liked.

Charley left Knapp’s before summer. Billy claimed he had fired him for cowardice; Charley claimed he had resigned on account of the climate. Both statements contained enough truth for frontier history.

As for Alfred, he did not marry Nell, though Billy always said he should have. Their lives crossed only twice afterward, and neither crossing belongs here. Affection on the plains was like rain: scarce, sudden and not always destined to remain where it fell. Yet a single shower has been known to turn a traveller from one road to another, and that night Nell’s kiss turned Alfred back toward seven armed men.

The next year, when news of gold drew every idler and optimist toward the Black Hills, the government warned wagon trains not to cross. Outlaws, hostile parties and early snow had closed the northern routes. For nearly two months no outfit dared leave the Missouri.

Then Billy Knapp and Jim Buckley assembled twenty-eight wagons, eighty men and a collection of mules whose characters would have discouraged a saint. They announced that they were going through.

People laughed until they learned who rode point.

He was a small, narrow-shouldered man in an ill-fitting black coat, with a bald head, a mild face and two revolvers beneath his tails. He apologized when his horse stepped on another man’s blanket and blushed if a woman spoke to him.

Billy introduced him to the emigrants with pride.

“Gentlemen,” he said, “this yere is Alfred Vane. Don’t let him trouble you. He’s timid.”

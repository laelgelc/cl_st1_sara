Until the afternoon of our adventure, I had always supposed the shop to be an ordinary one.

It stood, or appeared to stand, in Regent Street between a dealer in photographic apparatus and a respectable establishment devoted to gloves. I had passed it perhaps twenty times, generally in haste and with my mind occupied by more reasonable things—appointments, the weather, the improper increase in cab fares—and had noticed only its narrow frontage and a modest inscription in tarnished gold:

GENUINE MAGIC  
FOR DOMESTIC AND EDUCATIONAL PURPOSES

There were, moreover, certain things in the window: a pack of cards spread in a fan without visible support, a white rabbit asleep beneath a glass shade, several bottles labelled INVISIBILITY, BEST QUALITY, a little black mask with eyes that seemed inconveniently attentive, and a papier-mâché head which put out its tongue when one was not looking directly at it.

I had never thought of entering.

Gip altered that.

He was then a little over six, a thoughtful, well-mannered boy, not given to clamour in the street. He never beat upon windows, nor asked for everything he saw, nor threw himself beneath the feet of strangers when denied a trumpet. This moderation, you know, makes a child’s wishes rather formidable when at last they are expressed.

“Dadda,” he said, taking my finger more firmly, “that is a magic shop.”

“So it announces.”

“Real magic?”

“Well—” I said. “The genuine article, apparently.”

He studied the window. The rabbit had opened one pink eye.

“There is a Vanishing Egg,” said Gip.

A large speckled egg stood upon a velvet cushion, and while we watched it disappeared. The velvet remained indented for a moment; then the egg came quietly back.

“And a Crying Baby, Very Human,” Gip continued.

That object, happily, was boxed.

“You could buy me something,” he said—not greedily, but as one states a conclusion.

“You have a birthday in three months.”

“I wasn’t asking,” said Gip, with dignity. “I was only saying you could.”

That settled it. There are occasions when parental economy becomes pettiness, and I felt that to pass on would be mean. Besides, I expected tricks—ingenious tricks, no doubt—and had an idle curiosity concerning their mechanism. So I pushed open the door.

A bell somewhere uttered one very deep note.

The interior was larger than the frontage warranted, though not at first impossibly so. It was dim, cool and crowded. There were shelves of boxes, hanging cabinets, bundles of coloured wands, mechanical birds, clocks with thirteen figures, drums, swords, trumpets, dissolving dolls, self-mending hoops and an army of little men in red trousers marching perpetually round the brim of a top hat. Glass balls lay everywhere: in bowls, under the counter, among coils of gilt rope, and even, rather hazardously, upon the floor.

At the far end stood a tall shopman.

He had been so perfectly still that I had mistaken him for a figure. He was lean and black-clad, with one shoulder higher than the other and a face of marked inequality. One eyebrow went up while the other travelled down; his left ear was larger than his right; and the point of his chin appeared to have been added as an afterthought. Yet his smile was exceedingly courteous.

“Good afternoon, sir,” he said. “And good afternoon to the Right Sort of Boy.”

Gip looked at him gravely.

“I want something magical,” he said.

“Indisputably,” said the shopman. “Otherwise it would hardly be worth your while to come here.”

“I should like,” I intervened, “some simple little trick. Not too expensive. Something suitable for a child.”

“Everything here is suitable for the child to whom it is sold,” said the shopman. “Price is a secondary phenomenon.”

“A secondary—?”

He leaned across the counter and took a glass ball out of the air.

There had been nothing in his hand before. I am quite positive on that point, because I was watching him with especial care. Nevertheless, there it was—a clear ball containing a minute revolving moon.

“Would that amuse you?” he asked Gip.

“Yes,” said Gip.

“Then catch.”

He tossed it. Gip caught it with both hands, but immediately looked perplexed.

“It’s gone.”

“Not gone,” said the shopman. “Mislaid. Try your pocket.”

Gip put his hand into the pocket of his coat and brought out the ball.

Now, naturally, there was nothing in this beyond clever prestidigitation. A confederate was unnecessary; the article might have travelled through a tube concealed beneath the counter and been introduced into Gip’s pocket by a wire, a spring or some such mechanical contrivance. I stooped to inspect the edge of the counter.

“No machinery, sir,” said the shopman, producing a second ball from my elbow.

I straightened abruptly.

He dropped that ball into his mouth. His cheek bulged; his eyes watered; and with a slight cough he brought it from the back of Gip’s neck. Then another appeared upon the tip of his nose. A fourth rolled from his sleeve. A fifth emerged from his left ear, which perhaps explained its unusual capacity.

Gip laughed. He did not often laugh aloud, and the sound disposed me to indulgence.

“Very neat,” I said. “Extremely neat. But we should prefer something he can take away.”

“Of course, sir. We have the Home Conjurer’s Cabinet, containing eighty-six deceptions and one genuine surprise; the Instantaneous Castle, requiring no foundations; the Obedient Compass, which points towards whatever is most desired; and this—”

He placed upon the counter a little cone of blue cardboard.

“What is it?” I asked.

“The Vanishing Halfpenny.”

“I know that trick.”

“Do you, sir?”

I took a halfpenny from my waistcoat pocket and put it beneath the cone. The shopman tapped once. I lifted the cone. The coin had vanished.

“Palmed,” I said.

“Certainly not by me.”

I searched my hand. It was empty.

“Perhaps your other pocket,” he suggested.

I tried it and found not the halfpenny but a small grey rat, which sat up between my fingers and washed its whiskers.

I confess I dropped it.

Before it reached the floor it became a twisted strip of paper. The shopman caught the paper, shook it open, and revealed a printed invoice headed:

ONE PERFECTLY NATURAL EXPLANATION  
Supplied to a Gentleman  
Price: Whatever He Can Believe

“That,” I said, “is an old sort of joke.”

“Very old, sir. Nearly as old as explanations.”

He crushed the invoice into a pellet. The pellet lengthened into a stick of sealing wax; the sealing wax softened into a scarlet worm; the worm tied itself in a bow and became a ribbon about a parcel which, until that instant, had not been on the counter.

Gip regarded the parcel with shining eyes.

“For me?”

“For you,” said the shopman. “Our first complimentary selection.”

“I don’t want too much given to me,” said Gip, after reflection. “Dadda may pay.”

The shopman’s smile sharpened.

“Your father will pay in the usual manner.”

I did not care for that phrase.

“How much?” I asked.

“That depends entirely upon what is purchased.”

“Then suppose we settle the price beforehand.”

“With genuine magic,” said the shopman, “nothing is beforehand.”

He turned to Gip. “Would you like to see the showroom?”

Gip looked at me.

I ought, I think, to have said no. The fellow’s tricks were becoming intrusive; the shop was badly lit; and there was in his affability something too smooth to be wholesome. But Gip’s hand was still in mine, and the showroom appeared to lie beyond a harmless curtain of green baize. Moreover, one dislikes making a scene in a shop.

“For a minute,” I said. “We are due home for tea.”

“Time,” said the shopman, “is always most accommodating to good customers.”

He lifted the curtain.

Beyond it was a long gallery filled with toys.

It was astonishingly long. Indeed, considering the two neighbouring premises and the street behind, it was preposterous; but I told myself there might be an angle in the building. The gallery bent, perhaps. Mirrors were also employed—many mirrors, narrow and tall, which multiplied the lamps into distances and reflected Gip and me in various uncomplimentary forms. In one I was squat and crimson; in another Gip had antlers; in a third the shopman alone made no reflection.

“That glass is faulty,” I remarked.

“On the contrary,” he said. “It is the only perfectly truthful one we possess.”

The toys were displayed without much regard to commercial order. A rocking-horse cropped actual grass from a tray. A regiment of blue soldiers snored in a box labelled WAKE ONLY IN CASE OF WAR. A tin railway ran vertically up the wall and vanished through the ceiling, returning with its carriages full of exceedingly small passengers. There were bottled thunderstorms, folding dragons, self-denying money-boxes, portable sunsets and a shelf of invisible dolls whose garments floated with an effect I found rather indecent.

A card above one case announced:

GOOD CHILDREN’S DEPARTMENT  
NO SUBSTITUTE MATERIALS  
BAD CHILDREN NOT ADMITTED

“An excellent regulation,” I said.

“Necessary,” replied the shopman. “The wrong sort of child damages the stock.”

As he spoke there came a thumping at a small side door. A face appeared at its grated window—a red, swollen, disagreeable face beneath a sailor hat.

“Let me in!” it bawled. “I want the dragon! I want it now!”

The shopman flicked one finger. The face became the head of a wooden demon, painted scarlet, which continued to open and shut its mouth without sound.

“Temporary treatment,” he explained. “He will improve when he has exhausted himself.”

Gip squeezed my hand, but not from fear.

“That is a bad child,” he said.

“Very,” said the shopman. “We never sell magic to that sort. They already have too much of the destructive kind.”

There was sense in the observation, though I disliked the illustration.

We passed under an arch hung with silver bells. They rang before we touched them and went on ringing after we had passed. The shopman took Gip’s free hand.

“Mind the step.”

“There is no step,” I said.

Immediately I stumbled over one.

The shopman steadied me with great politeness. When I recovered, Gip was walking between us. He held the shopman’s finger on one side and mine on the other; then, as we proceeded, a cabinet swung outward, obliging me to release him. By the time I had gone round it, Gip was entirely on the shopman’s side.

It was a trifling thing, but it annoyed me.

“What is in that case?” I asked, to reassert myself.

“Dreams, sir.”

They lay like soap bubbles in compartments lined with black velvet. Within one shone a tropical island; within another a bright nursery on Christmas morning; a third contained a flight above moonlit roofs. The fourth was clouded, but as I bent nearer I saw myself wandering through empty streets and calling Gip’s name.

I drew back.

“Not a pleasant article.”

“Not intended for you, sir.”

He covered the case.

Gip had found a box of soldiers. They were handsome little fellows in white uniforms, each with a gold sun upon his breast. Their captain saluted him.

“That was moved by a magnet,” I said.

“No,” said Gip. “He knows me.”

The captain saluted again.

The shopman clapped his hands. Instantly the soldiers leapt from the box, formed ranks upon the counter and marched, not stiffly after the fashion of clockwork toys, but with a minute and alarming suppleness. Their knees bent; their heads turned; the drummer wiped his forehead. At the word of command they wheeled, presented arms and fired a volley of little green sparks into the air.

“Careful!” I cried.

The sparks became butterflies and settled upon Gip’s hair.

He stood very still, delighted.

“They obey only their owner,” said the shopman. “There is a word to wake them and another to put them away. You must never use the waking word after bedtime, unless the house is attacked.”

“By what?” asked Gip.

“One can never tell.”

“And the word?”

The shopman stooped and whispered into his ear.

I caught only a noise like the striking of a spoon against glass. Gip nodded once, with the solemnity of a man receiving military intelligence.

“I should prefer,” I said, “not to have secrets of that sort kept from me.”

“It is not kept from you,” said the shopman. “You merely cannot hear it.”

“That is much the same thing.”

“Only to a grown person.”

He swept the soldiers into their box, wrapped it with impossible rapidity, and tied the string by snapping his fingers. The parcel joined the first, though I had not seen where the first had been placed.

“I think that will do,” I said. “We must be going.”

“But we have only begun.”

“We have seen quite enough.”

Gip looked disappointed but said nothing. This, as I have explained, was more effective than protest.

The shopman considered me with his unequal eyes.

“One final entertainment,” he said. “The Disappearing Boy. Quite harmless. Particularly admired by parents.”

“No,” I said.

“Please, dadda,” said Gip.

It was the “please” that weakened me. And, after all, I told myself, the trick would consist of a curtain, a trap, perhaps a concealed revolving panel. I should watch closely. The boy would be out of my reach for no more than a second.

“One trick,” I said. “Then we go.”

“Your judgement is impeccable.”

An assistant appeared from behind a pillar. I cannot say he entered; he was simply not there and then there. He was short and yellow-faced, in a purple coat with sleeves too long for him. His nose drooped over his mouth like soft wax.

“Bring the Cabinet of Innocent Diversion,” ordered the shopman.

The assistant seized his nose, pulled it to nearly two feet in length and used it to hook a striped cabinet from behind a screen.

This was so grossly absurd that Gip burst out laughing. Even I smiled, though unwillingly. The assistant released his nose, which recoiled with a snap and struck him between the eyes. He bowed as if this were prescribed.

The cabinet resembled a sentry box. The shopman opened its door.

“Empty, you observe.”

I inspected it. There was no back exit, no trap that I could detect, and no space beneath. Still, cabinet tricks are innumerable.

“Step inside,” he said to Gip.

Gip entered.

“Keep your hand out,” I said.

He put one small hand round the edge of the door.

The shopman closed it gently until only Gip’s fingers remained visible.

“Now,” he said, “at the count of three.”

“I don’t like this,” I said.

“One.”

“Open it.”

“Two.”

“I said open it!”

“Three.”

The fingers vanished.

I sprang forward and tore open the door.

The cabinet was empty.

For half a second I could not understand it. I thrust my hands against the walls, struck the panels, kicked the floor.

“Gip!”

No answer.

“Gip!”

The shopman stood behind me, smiling.

“Restore that boy,” I said.

“Certainly, sir. At the conclusion of the demonstration.”

“Now!”

“Your excitement is entirely natural, but injurious to the effect.”

I caught him by the collar. Or attempted to. His coat collapsed beneath my hands like black tissue paper, and from its neck rose a flock of white pigeons. They battered past my face and vanished among the rafters.

The shopman was suddenly ten yards away.

“Come, sir,” he called. “This way.”

He slipped through a blue door.

I ran after him.

Beyond the door was not the gallery but a narrow passage, crooked and dim. Shelves leaned inward on either side, loaded with masks—laughing masks, weeping masks, infant masks with ancient eyes. The floor yielded under my feet. Behind me the door slammed.

“Gip!” I shouted.

Far ahead came his voice.

“Dadda!”

I ran. The passage lengthened as I ran; the lamps withdrew; curtains brushed my face like hands. I collided with a hanging skeleton, which apologised in a hollow whisper. A row of drums began beating to the rhythm of my steps.

“Stop!” I cried.

The drums stopped.

So did the floor.

It ceased abruptly beneath my foot, and I dropped perhaps two feet into another room, landing upon a heap of stuffed serpents.

The shopman was crossing the room with Gip under one arm.

The boy did not seem frightened. Indeed, he waved.

“Dadda! I can see the roof from underneath!”

“Put him down!”

The shopman opened a door in the air. There had been no wall there, only a curtain of hanging stars, but the door swung upon brass hinges and showed a darkness beyond.

He carried Gip through.

I scrambled up and followed.

The darkness swallowed me.

There was no floor, no wall, no sense of direction—only the smell of sealing wax and the distant rustle of paper. I heard the shopman murmuring as if wrapping some enormous parcel.

Fold.

Crease.

Twist.

Then the wet slap of wax.

“Gip!”

A child laughed—not Gip, I thought, but a laugh from one of the bottled dreams.

Something brushed my cheek.

“Confound you!” I shouted. “If you harm him—”

A light flashed above me. The shopman’s face hung there without body, smiling upside down.

“We never harm the Right Sort of Boy.”

“Then give him back!”

“We give everything back, sir. Eventually. Though not always in its former wrapping.”

His face went out.

I lunged towards it and struck a curtain. The curtain wound round my arms. I tore free and saw, quite near, the assistant in the purple coat. He had Gip’s cap balanced upon his extended nose.

“Where is the boy?”

He pointed with the cap towards a red doorway.

I rushed through it.

The shopman stood at a counter exactly like the first counter, and behind him Gip sat contentedly upon a large box, holding four parcels in his lap.

“There you are, dadda,” he said.

I stopped, breathless.

“Are you all right?”

“Yes.”

“Did he frighten you?”

“No.”

“Did he take you away?”

“I was there all the time,” said Gip, and pointed to the box.

The shopman polished a glass ball with a black cloth.

“You see, sir? Entirely harmless.”

I went to Gip and put both hands on his shoulders. He was solid, warm and untroubled. I examined his face, his hair, even his ears, with a thoroughness he found faintly insulting.

“We are leaving,” I said.

“Your purchases, sir.”

“I have purchased nothing.”

“Four parcels.”

“Unsolicited.”

“Chosen with the greatest care.”

“I will not discuss it. Show us the door.”

“With pleasure.”

He indicated an archway behind me.

Through it I saw the front of the shop: the dim shelves, the original counter, and beyond the glass door the afternoon traffic of Regent Street. Never had omnibuses appeared so honest, policemen so comforting, or ordinary London smoke so full of moral worth.

I took Gip by the hand.

The shopman came close and held out the four parcels. Gip accepted them automatically.

“I shall return these,” I said.

“Returns are impossible after departure.”

“Then I shall leave them here.”

Gip tightened his arms about them.

The shopman’s smile widened, and for the first time I noticed that his teeth were exceedingly numerous.

“There will be no difficulty about payment,” he said.

“I demand your name.”

“The name is on the shop.”

“I demand the proprietor’s name.”

“Proprietors,” he replied, “are rarely the most important people in a genuine magic shop.”

I turned away.

The door seemed farther than it had been. Between us and it stood cases, curtains and rotating pillars that had certainly not been there a moment before. A stuffed crocodile descended from the ceiling and opened its mouth. Mirrors swung across the path, multiplying the shopman into a hundred black, unequal figures.

“Straight on, sir,” they said together.

I dragged Gip between two cabinets.

“Not that way.”

We turned.

A wall of clocks confronted us. Every clock pointed to a different hour, and every one began to strike.

One—three—twelve—seven—

The noise was intolerable.

“Stop it!” I shouted.

Gip spoke a single quiet word.

All the clocks ceased.

The shopman’s reflections looked at him with sudden attention.

“Excellent,” said the nearest.

I did not ask what word Gip had used.

A narrow door sprang open beside us. Beyond it was daylight.

I thrust Gip through, followed him, and felt something strike me squarely between the shoulders.

THUD!

I stumbled against a passer-by, recovered my hat and found myself standing in Regent Street.

A cabman cursed me. A lady with a small dog stared. An omnibus rolled past with thunderous normality.

Gip stood at my side, holding four brown-paper parcels.

The shop was gone.

Between the photographic dealer and the glove establishment there was no narrow frontage, no tarnished sign, no door. The two shops joined at a party wall of soot-dark brick scarcely a foot wide.

I walked up to it and touched the bricks.

They were solid.

“Dadda,” said Gip, “you’re crushing my hand.”

I released it and looked along the street. Nothing. No shopman, no assistant, no green curtain, no window containing eggs and rabbits. Only London—crowded, indifferent, practical London.

A policeman approached.

I nearly spoke to him. But what could I have said? That a retail establishment had vanished? That my son had been temporarily packed in a cabinet? That I had been assaulted by a door? One must consider how these things sound, particularly when one has no corroborating witness except a child of six carrying unexplained parcels.

So I asked the time.

“Twenty past four, sir.”

We had entered the shop shortly after four. It seemed to me we had been inside for hours.

“Thank you,” I said.

“Are we going home now?” asked Gip.

“Yes. Directly.”

He walked beside me with perfect composure.

“What happened when you disappeared?” I asked after a little while.

“I didn’t disappear.”

“You were in the cabinet, and then you were not.”

“I went through the little door.”

“There was no little door.”

“There was for me.”

“And where did it lead?”

“Into the shop.”

“We were already in the shop.”

Gip gave me a patient look.

“The other shop,” he said.

That was all I could get from him.

At home, in the sober light of the dining-room, with tea laid and the familiar clock ticking upon the mantelpiece, the affair seemed less alarming and more absurd. I locked the four parcels in a cupboard until after Gip had gone to bed. He surrendered them reluctantly but without protest.

“You won’t throw them away?” he asked.

“Not until I have examined them.”

“The soldiers don’t like being thrown away.”

“Soldiers?”

“Three boxes.”

“And the fourth?”

“He said it was a surprise.”

“Who said?”

“The gentleman.”

I did not correct this description.

When Gip was asleep, I took the parcels into my study. My wife was away visiting her sister, and I confess I was glad not to be required to explain matters immediately. I placed the poker within reach—an excessive precaution, no doubt—and opened the first parcel.

It contained a box of toy soldiers.

They were admirably made: forty infantrymen in white uniforms, an officer with a drawn sword, a drummer, two standard-bearers and a little field-piece. I examined each figure. They were wood and metal, articulated at shoulder and hip, but otherwise perfectly ordinary. There were no springs, wires or magnetic plates. None moved.

The second and third parcels contained similar regiments: cavalry in green, and artillerymen in blue. Fine toys, expensive toys, but toys.

The fourth parcel moved.

I stepped back and seized the poker.

The string untied itself—or loosened because something within had worried it. A white paw appeared through the paper. Then a head.

It was a kitten.

A remarkably clean, healthy white kitten, with yellow eyes and a black spot upon its tail, emerged from the wrapping, sneezed once, and began to purr. There was no trick about it. I took it to the kitchen and gave it milk. It drank sensibly.

The next morning Gip received his presents with quiet satisfaction.

“I knew about the kitten,” he said.

“You called it a surprise.”

“It was a surprise for you.”

We kept the animal. It grew normally, caught mice and developed no magical faculty whatever, unless one counts its ability to appear in locked rooms—which, after observation, I attributed to a defective catch on the scullery window.

The soldiers also remained ordinary, so far as I could determine.

Several times I entered Gip’s nursery unexpectedly and found them scattered upon the carpet, fallen in attitudes which suggested merely that a boy had been playing with them. Once, late in the evening, I fancied I heard a faint marching overhead. I went upstairs at once. Gip was in bed, asleep, and the soldiers stood in three neat companies upon the floor.

“You arranged them before you went to sleep,” I said next morning.

“No,” said Gip. “They arranged themselves.”

“Toy soldiers cannot arrange themselves.”

“They can when I say the word.”

“What word?”

“You wouldn’t hear it.”

This answer, I admit, irritated me.

“Try.”

Gip looked towards the ranks.

He spoke—or seemed to. His lips moved, but I heard nothing except a small click from the window, where the kitten sat watching a fly.

The soldiers did not stir.

“You see?” I said, perhaps too triumphantly.

“They won’t do it while you’re looking.”

“That is very convenient.”

“Yes,” said Gip. “They think so too.”

Months passed. The white kitten became a white cat. The soldiers lost a little paint at the elbows and knees. Gip remained healthy, obedient and disinclined to invention in every other matter. He never boasted of the shop to other children. When I questioned him, he answered simply that the captain knew his name, that the artillery could fire without damaging furniture, and that the cavalry disliked Thursdays.

I made several searches for the shop.

At different hours and in different weather I walked the length of Regent Street, examining every doorway. I questioned the photographic dealer under the pretext of requiring a camera and asked the glover, while purchasing gloves I did not need, whether the premises beside him had lately been altered.

“There have never been premises there, sir,” he said.

“Not a narrow shop? Magic goods?”

He smiled in the professionally tolerant manner of a man humouring eccentric custom.

“No, sir.”

Once, on a foggy evening, I saw across the street a window full of glass balls and a dim gold word that might have been GENUINE. I crossed between two cabs at some personal risk, but found only a tobacconist’s, where the glass balls proved to be jars of sweets. The tobacconist regarded my inquiries coldly.

Eventually I abandoned the search.

The most troublesome part, from a practical standpoint, was the bill. I expected one daily. Fine toy soldiers, three complete sets, are not cheap; nor, for that matter, are healthy kittens if supplied through unusual commercial channels. I set aside a reasonable sum. No invoice came.

At the end of six months I told myself the articles had been complimentary advertisements. At the end of a year I decided that if the shopman wished payment he knew my address—though I do not remember giving it to him. After eighteen months I spent the money on a new writing desk.

Even now, when Gip is asked about the soldiers, he says they come alive.

“Not alive like us,” he explains, “but properly alive for soldiers.”

I have never seen them move. I have never heard the secret word. The cat is entirely commonplace, except that it occasionally sits before a blank wall and purrs as if someone invisible were stroking it.

There is, therefore, no proof.

The whole thing may have been an elaborate exhibition of legerdemain, aided by concealed passages, mirrors, narcotic suggestion, expert assistants and a momentary confusion on my part. The parcels might have been attached to us in the street by confederates. Gip’s disappearance might have lasted only a second. The missing shop is more difficult, but London frontages are deceptive, and memory—particularly an agitated memory—is not a surveyor.

That is the reasonable explanation.

Still, I sometimes wake after midnight to the faintest possible sound from the nursery: a drum tapping, a trumpet no louder than a mosquito, the minute, disciplined tread of several companies crossing the floor. By the time I reach the door, all is quiet. Gip sleeps. The soldiers stand in their boxes.

And no bill has ever come.

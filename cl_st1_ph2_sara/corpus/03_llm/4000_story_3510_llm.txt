# The Pictures of Our Youth

When we were young—in the consulship of Plancus, as we may say, though Plancus himself had then been dead some little time—the world was not by any means so brightly pictured, neatly bound, gilt-edged, hot-pressed, and plentifully illustrated as it is for the happy little people who now sprawl upon our hearth-rugs. These young villains have books for breakfast, books for dinner, books to be read before bed; books where the lions are amiable, the sailors clean, the fairies respectably dressed, the robbers ultimately repentant, and even the dragons furnished with a certain domestic sweetness about the eye. Their Robinson Crusoe has two hundred pictures; their Arabian Nights is illuminated like a caliph’s palace; their histories swarm with Roman centurions, Saxon kings, British admirals, and French grenadiers, all drawn with an accuracy which makes us suspect that the artist was concealed behind the throne at the death of Cæsar, and took a lodging opposite the Bastille on the morning when it fell.

What had WE? A few prints, black as thunder, hung in passages where no lamp ever burned; a Scripture history in which the patriarchs resembled elderly brigands; an alarming portrait of an ancestor in a wig, whose varnished eyes followed us from the door to the staircase; and, in the best room, never opened except when company arrived or somebody died, a battle-piece wherein six gentlemen on horseback were killing one another with every appearance of satisfaction.

There was a pleasing picture for childhood! The wounded smiled; the horses rolled their eyes with politeness; a man whose head had apparently been removed by a cannon-ball continued to wave his sword; and in the foreground a drummer-boy, lying upon a very crimson field, looked as composed as though he had merely been sent to bed before supper. Beneath this agreeable work stood a china shepherdess, with a crook, a lamb, and no visible means of supporting either herself or her flock. On the opposite wall hung a storm at sea. A vessel was going down stern foremost, the crew were waving their arms in the dimpling water, and a benevolent moon shone upon the whole transaction.

Were these objects intended for our moral improvement? Did our grandfathers suppose that infant virtue flourished best between a shipwreck and a decapitation? We do not know. Our grandfathers were excellent men; they wore high collars, distrusted central heating, and believed a good birching to be the foundation of polite learning. We speak of them with reverence. But when we remember the pictures with which they surrounded us, the books into which they thrust us, and the schools at which they washed us—for washing formed a brief but tragical portion of the morning ceremony—we cannot deny that they had stern notions concerning juvenile pleasure.

Fancy, my dear young friend, who complain because the water in your bath has fallen from ninety-eight degrees to ninety-six, a stone trough beneath a pump in January; fancy six shivering boys in shirts, each waiting his turn for the frozen BIDENT called a towel; fancy the pump-handle creaking like an executioner’s arm, and the water descending upon your devoted skull while some elder scapegrace, already washed and therefore cheerful, remarks upon your courage! That was education in the consulship of Plancus. We learned Latin with cold fingers, Greek with empty stomachs, and fortitude because there was no help for it.

After such a morning, what solace awaited us? Perhaps an exhibition.

Exhibitions in those days were not those airy, brilliant, perfumed bazaars through which modern children skip, buying catalogues, sucking sweetmeats, and asking whether Titian belonged to the early or the late Pre-Raphaelites. Ours were held up dark staircases, behind green baize doors, in rooms smelling of damp boards, lamp-oil, oranges, and mystery. There was the Mechanical Turk, who answered questions by knocking; there was the Invisible Lady, whose invisibility did not prevent her from having a severe cold; there was the learned pig, who possessed an education considerably superior to ours; there were wax kings, wax murderers, wax victims, and a wax executioner whose uplifted axe hung for years above the same wax neck.

How we loved that executioner! How we feared the blue chamber beyond him, where an amiable assassin in a flowered waistcoat was represented inviting a lady to sit down beside the chest containing her deceased husband! The lady’s fingers quivered; the assassin’s glass eyes gleamed; a drop of red wax stood upon the husband’s brow; and a card, printed in noble capitals, requested visitors NOT TO TOUCH THE FIGURES. Touch them! We would as soon have touched the Fiend. Yet we returned again and again, paid our sixpence, crept round the curtain, and came away pale, delighted, and morally strengthened.

There was also needlework. Miss Somebody’s celebrated gallery contained heroes, landscapes, ruins, dogs, waterfalls, Madonnas, and monarchs, all wrought in worsted. The waterfalls were worsted; the clouds were worsted; the human complexion was of that rose-colored worsted which nature, happily, has denied to mankind. A warrior’s beard and his charger’s tail appeared to proceed from the same skein. Ruined abbeys rose in brown wool under skies of blue wool; sheep of white wool browsed upon grass of green wool; and the spectator, having paid his shilling, went forth with a confused impression that the entire visible universe had been knitted by an industrious aunt.

But let us not be ungrateful. Those dark rooms, those stiff prints, those crimson battles, those patriarchal robbers, formed our first museum; and affection, that excellent old varnisher, has laid a mellow coat over them. We laugh at their horrors, yet we would not willingly surrender the memory of them. Indeed, when we see Master Tommy stretched upon a velvet cushion, turning the pages of a book in which elephants dance, chimney-sweeps sing, policemen smile, and every nursery disaster ends in plum-cake, we are inclined to ask, with the gravity of Roman senators: What have THESE children done that they should be happier than we were?

Nothing. The rogues have done nothing. They have merely been born later.

And this brings us—though perhaps by a road resembling the itinerary of a drunken post-chaise—to the subject in hand: the progress of pictorial humor among us, and the arrival of that pleasant art which can enter the family room without frightening the children, offending the mother, or causing the father to conceal the page under a newspaper.

The grand old caricaturists did not enter a room: they stormed it. They came with clubs, blunderbusses, bottles, red noses, wooden legs, cloven hoofs, bursting waistcoats, and such a prodigious apparatus of kicks, cuffs, guillotines, gallowses, and bodily indignities that the quiet citizen must have felt his house invaded by a political riot. Gillray drew with a sword. Rowlandson drew with a whip, a corkscrew, and occasionally a kitchen poker. Their world is swollen, hungry, drunken, leering, magnificent; a world in which statesmen devour kingdoms as puddings, bishops sit upon sacks of gold, Frenchmen gnaw onions, Englishmen roast whole oxen, and poor Master Boney is tossed, thumped, sliced, kicked, roasted, frozen, drowned, and served up with sauce to the delighted nations.

Look at Gillray’s grandees! What calves, what bellies, what noses, what diamond buckles! A duke appears to require two chairs for the reception of his person; a minister, burdened with the taxes of the country, has stuffed a dozen of them into his pockets; a royal appetite opens like a red cavern; Britannia faints in feathers while the Opposition picks her purse. Nobody whispers in those prints. Everybody bellows. Eyes protrude, teeth gnash, fingers clutch, ribbons stream, chamber-pots descend from windows, and constitutional principles fly about in the form of brickbats.

There is genius here—ferocious, fertile, unmistakable genius. The line bites; the invention flames; a whole parliamentary debate is condensed into the turn of a mouth or the angle of a shoe. But kindness? My dear sir, kindness has gone out to call and left no address.

Rowlandson is more jovial, though his joviality sometimes resembles the hospitality of a tavern at three in the morning. He loves a coach-yard, a country inn, a racecourse, a supper, a fat parson, a lean doctor, a tipsy postboy, a red-faced landlord, a chambermaid retreating before an amorous old gentleman in top-boots. His horses gallop, his wheels whirl, his waiters spill gravy, his watchmen tumble into kennels. The guardians of the streets of Rome—in the consulship of Plancus—are overturned by young bloods, who caper away with the watchman’s rattle while that municipal warrior sits in the gutter and invokes the majesty of the law.

And what a law it was! The old watchman, with his coat like a blanket and his lantern like a coffin-window, dozed under a porch until the thief had completed his business, then woke to arrest the householder. The dandy carried a cane too delicate to strike with and a collar too high to see over; the coachman swore at six horses together; the footman displayed calves which might have sustained the national credit; the citizen ate, the sailor drank, the magistrate slept, and the caricaturist recorded all.

We need not become old-fogyfied merely because we are old. We can admire the vigor of these masters and still confess that their fun is often cruel, their gaiety coarse, and their women treated with a freedom which would make the modern tea-table rise in a body and ring the bell. They painted an age rougher in speech, rougher in manners, rougher in punishment. A boy was flogged, a debtor imprisoned, a soldier lashed, a lunatic exhibited, and a criminal hanged before breakfast, while respectable citizens attended with their wives and purchased gingerbread. Why should the caricature of such a society wear kid gloves?

Yet the gloves were coming. Humor, that wild urchin, was to be washed, combed, clothed, and taught not to make faces at the ladies. It resisted the soap, howled under the comb, kicked its instructor upon the shins, and hid a frog in the pocket of morality; but the operation was performed.

Between the savage old giants and the genial household artist stands George Cruikshank—dear, astonishing, nimble George!—with one foot in the cellar of old caricature and the other on the clean mat of the Victorian parlour. What a population issued from his ink-bottle! Misers with fingers like hooks; beadles inflated by official dignity; thieves in ragged coats gliding under lamps; sailors dancing until every board in the tavern trembles; fairies no larger than spoons; ogres with tusks and hairy wrists; children lost in forests; drunkards descending step by step into blue ruin; honest apprentices ascending with equal regularity toward a mahogany sideboard and the hand of the master’s daughter.

His line wriggles, darts, doubles, and snaps. It knows the turn of a thief’s ankle as he hears the constable behind him; the greasy nap of a bailiff’s hat; the obstinate curl of a publican’s apron; the anguish of a small boy who has swallowed medicine; the ecstasy of another small boy who has stolen jam. Give Cruikshank a cellar, and every bottle in it becomes an accomplice. Give him a garret, and the broken chair, the sloping roof, the empty grate, the mouse behind the wainscot, all confess the poverty of the tenant. Give him a crowd, and no two backs repeat themselves.

He could be terrible. Indeed, when Cruikshank undertook to show the consequences of vice, Vice must often have wished itself represented by a less conscientious gentleman. The bottle begins upon a cheerful table and ends among straw; the gin-shop shines, the family starves, the pawnbroker fattens, the children shiver, and Death, who has been waiting with professional patience in the final compartment, at length takes possession. These are sermons with a steel pen. One may question the argument, resist the preacher, or sneak away from the moral; one cannot deny the draughtsman.

But his merriment, when it runs free, is inexhaustible. Remember his harlequins bursting through doors, his giants peering over walls, his London apprentices with mouths open at a lord mayor’s procession, his little demons roasting a sinner who continues to protest against the irregularity of the proceedings. What invention! A chimney-pot becomes a character; a boot tells its owner’s history; a fork upon the table points with the solemnity of a BIDENT toward the guilty pie. He inherited grotesque force from the old men, but he had sympathy for the poor creature being made grotesque. His rogues are often hungry; his drunkards once had homes; his children are never mere furniture.

Thus comic art advanced from the street into the house. It wiped its boots. It moderated its language. It discovered the nursery upstairs, the drawing-room on the first floor, the kitchen below, and the quiet little drama proceeding in each. And presently there appeared a weekly paper—our old friend with the hooked nose and hump, a fellow descended from the puppet-show, carrying his baton as if he meant to thump the age into good humor.

Ah, *Punch*! How many Saturday evenings has that stout volume governed! The postman’s knock; the rush into the hall; the brown wrapper torn by fingers which had been expressly forbidden to tear it; the father demanding the paper, the mother asking whether there was anything improper, the elder sister pretending not to care, the schoolboy already half-way under the table with it—are these not domestic history?

One cartoon might shake ministries; another might concern a cook’s warning, a child’s question, a curate’s umbrella, or a gentleman’s unfortunate trousers. There was room in those pages for Parliament and the pantry, war and watering-places, foreign emperors and British babies. The same number might contain the Eastern Question, a joke about anchovies, a rebuke to a tyrant, and a picture of a poodle in a bonnet. What more complete image can you desire of a free people?

Among the artists who gave that paper its face, none comes before us with a warmer welcome than John Leech.

At last we have arrived! The reader, who has been dragged through waxworks, worsted abbeys, Roman wash-troughs, French wars, taverns, prisons, and moral gin-shops, may justly exclaim that the guide has taken a monstrous circuit. But was not the prospect entertaining? And can one understand the gentleness of Leech without hearing, however faintly, the roar of those old caricatural battles from which English humor emerged?

Leech does not storm the family room. He belongs there. He knows the chair by the fire, the toys upon the carpet, the bell-pull beside the mantelpiece, the album on the table, the pattern of the curtains, the attitude in which Papa reads and does not listen, the precise look with which Mamma has listened all the while. He knows the children; not ideal pink cherubs supplied by the dozen from a celestial warehouse, but actual children—round, lean, shy, greedy, bold, bewildered, affectionate, muddy, magnificent.

Watch his small boy at the seaside, standing in a bathing-dress six sizes too large, blue with cold yet declining to come out because another small boy is looking on. Observe the little girl who has dressed the cat in a cap and believes the animal grateful. Remark the solemn infant asking a visitor whether he has any little boys, and, on learning that he has none, expressing polite sorrow for his lonely condition. These children do not deliver wit as actors deliver an epilogue. They blurt the truth, and the truth explodes beneath the drawing.

Leech understands that a child’s hand is not a reduced adult hand, that its glove wrinkles differently, that its boots turn inward when it is ashamed, outward when it is defiant, and nowhere in particular when it sleeps. He knows the weight of a sleeping child upon a nurse’s shoulder, the abandoned legs, the warm cheek, the dangling toy. He knows the brave face of the boy approaching the dentist and the altered countenance of the same hero when the instrument appears.

Can you not hear the drawing?

“Now, my little man, it will not hurt.”

“Oh, I know THAT,” says the little man, clutching the chair with ten desperate fingers; “but will it hurt me?”

There is a philosophy of terror in the distinction.

And the parents! The young mother, anxious over her first baby and serenely allowing the fourth to eat a button; the father summoned from his newspaper to judge whether a wooden horse has been intentionally murdered; the aunt who brings a trumpet and leaves before it is played; the bachelor uncle, all whiskers and watch-chain, conquered by a niece who has seated herself upon his waistcoat and poured imaginary tea into his hat. How gently the vanity is touched! Nobody is flayed; nobody is branded; the little needle goes through the bubble, and we laugh as the air escapes.

Leech’s servants are a nation. Here is the superb footman, six feet of powdered contempt, opening the door to a person whom he estimates at half a crown. Here is the housemaid, with sleeves rolled above powerful red arms, receiving the attentions of the policeman at the area railings. Here is the cook, entrenched among copper vessels, flour, dripping, and authority, announcing that if master does not like the dinner master may cook it himself. Here is the nurse, ruler of the square, wheeling an infant peer and discussing the household with another nurse whose perambulator contains the heir to a railway fortune.

“Your people going out of town?”

“Not if Missus can help it. She says the country is so relaxing.”

The two babies stare at one another, already divided by politics, property, and the width of their lace caps.

Do not suppose Leech despises these people. He sees their absurdities, as he sees ours; but he grants them substance, opinion, appetite, and an independent view of the social machinery. The servant is not merely the hand that brings the tray. She has been downstairs; she knows what occurred after the bell rang; she understands the family more perfectly than the family understands itself. A turned head, a compressed mouth, a glance exchanged behind a guest’s back—and a volume of domestic history is written.

Then the dandies, the guardsmen, the young men about town! How admirably empty, how beautifully dressed! Their collars bloom, their whiskers descend, their trousers expand or contract according to the command of fashion; their little canes hover in their fingers as though too aristocratic to touch the pavement. One lounges in a club window and condemns the vulgarity of labor; another has risen at two and complains that London affords no occupation; a third, having never mounted anything more spirited than a music-stool, instructs an old sportsman upon the management of horses.

Yet even the dandy is human. He trembles before a tailor’s account; he falls in love; he attempts to make himself agreeable to a child and is asked why his hair is divided behind; he poses before the belle of the ballroom and sneezes at the decisive moment. The punishment fits the vanity, and no bones are broken.

There is a moral economy in this. The savage caricaturist would have made the dandy hideous; Leech permits him to be handsome and ridiculous together. Is not that nearer life? A fool may possess a fine profile; a bore may wear an excellent coat; a coward’s boot may be polished to perfection. Vice itself does not always advertise in horns and a tail. Nor does Leech require vice for his purposes. A mild self-importance, a social fib, a fashionable affectation, a little swagger before the ladies—these furnish game enough for his humane gun.

His sportsmen demand a gallery to themselves. We who never crossed a field except by a footpath, and regard a ditch as an argument for returning home, have nevertheless followed Leech’s hunts with intrepid pleasure. There is the scarlet-coated hero taking a hedge as though wings had been concealed beneath his coat; there is the timid gentleman approaching the same obstacle with quivering reins, rolling eyes, and prayers addressed to every available saint; there is the horse, wiser than either, turning quietly toward the gate.

How those horses live! Not wooden machines with four legs attached at geographical intervals, but hunters, cobs, hacks, ponies, screws, chargers, and unclassified demons; horses that pull, refuse, bolt, stumble, calculate, forgive, and remember. Look at the old gray carrying the little girl—every line of him says gentleness. Look at the raw-boned brute hired to the boastful stranger—his ear turned back is a whole conspiracy. In another minute that stranger will be in a pond, and the horse, standing on the bank, will contemplate him with grave republican satisfaction.

Leech knows the hunting breakfast: the steam upon the windows, the boots in the hall, the dogs under the table, the ham, the hot plate, the hurried glass, the loud man who has ridden twenty miles before anybody else has fastened a girth. He knows the field afterward: the red coats scattered over brown earth, the white breath of the horses, the bare trees, the distant church, the black thread of hounds flowing across a slope. Humor does not exclude beauty. Suddenly the joke pauses, and England lies before us—damp, spacious, wintry, beloved.

That landscape is essential. The artist who draws society must know where society goes. Leech follows it from Belgravia to Boulogne, from the nursery to the Highlands, from the club window to the village inn. He gives us wet streets shining under lamps; suburban villas with painfully new shrubs; railway platforms where shawls, trunks, umbrellas, children, and tempers are lost together; beaches populated by bathing-machines and determined families; mountains climbed by ladies in impossible skirts and gentlemen who regret their enthusiasm.

What rain he draws! Not a few decorative slashes, but British rain—steady, official, constitutional rain—which enters the boot, flattens the bonnet, extinguishes the cigar, and turns a picnic into a parliamentary grievance. The sandwiches are damp; the children sit beneath a carriage rug; Papa asserts that it will clear; Mamma looks at Papa; the hired driver, wrapped in a sack, has never expected anything else.

And what interiors! Here art becomes inventory, and inventory becomes revelation. The prosperous drawing-room has its rounded sofa, glazed chintz, crowded table, marble ornaments, framed miniatures, piano with closed music, fire-screen, fern-case, and portrait of a gentleman whose cravat alone proves the respectability of the house. The bachelor chamber has boxing-gloves, pipes, boots, bills, a fencing-mask, two engravings of dancers, and one chair upon which no visitor could possibly sit. The lodging-house parlour offers horsehair furniture, a cold grate, a glass case of shells, an engraving of a royal marriage, and a bell that rings nowhere.

Each object speaks. The too-large clock tells us the owner has lately become rich; the shabby screen says the widow remembers better days; the untouched books announce that the host desires to be thought intellectual; the toys under the sofa expose the invasion of the children despite Mamma’s assurance that they never enter the room. A historian may give us Acts of Parliament, treaties, dates, and the names of ministries; Leech gives us the umbrella-stand. Which will explain how people lived?

Clothes, too, are history. We smile at the swelling sleeve, the constricted waist, the bonnet projecting like the roof of a cab, the trousers checked like a chessboard, the whiskers cultivated into hanging gardens. But we wore them. In the consulship of Plancus we considered them elegant. That collar, which now appears to have been designed for the punishment of pride, was admired by sensible women; that bonnet, under which a lady’s face could only be seen by telescope, broke sensible hearts.

The children of the future will turn these pages and question us.

“Grandpapa, did gentlemen truly wear straps under their boots?”

“My child, they did.”

“And did ladies go walking inside those cages?”

“They called them skirts.”

“And is that hairy object beneath the gentleman’s chin a wild animal?”

“No, dear; it was his pride.”

“Were you like that, Grandpapa?”

At this point Grandpapa will cough, turn the page, and say the youth of the present day has no respect for age.

But beneath the fashion is the person. This is where Leech excels. He does not merely know that a glove was buttoned in such a way; he knows why the hand inside it hesitates. A young woman bends over a child, and tenderness appears in the curve of her back. An old gentleman listens to a tiresome story, and civilization struggles with despair in the angle of his eyebrows. A poor relation sits at the edge of a chair, occupying as little room as possible; a rich vulgarian fills the sofa, the conversation, and apparently the universe. The inner life escapes through posture.

His beauties are truly beautiful. We insist upon this because some humorists, when confronted with a lovely woman, take revenge by dislocating her mouth. Leech can draw grace without simpering, youth without sugar, elegance without turning a lady into a fashion-plate. His girls walk, ride, laugh, stoop over children, listen to nonsense, or administer small punishments to presumption. They have character in their beauty. One sees why the young gentleman stammers, drops his hat, praises the weather, and goes away believing he has made a conquest.

And his poor! Here the test of kindness becomes delicate. Sentiment may insult as readily as contempt. To put a halo over every rag is scarcely more truthful than to put a gin-bottle under it. Leech observes poverty without denying dignity or humor. The crossing-sweeper has his professional pride; the cabman his grievance; the street boy his wit; the old soldier his memories and his rheumatism. They are not arranged as moral furniture for the comfort of the prosperous spectator. They look back at us.

A ragged urchin, viewing a gentleman in an extraordinary overcoat, may possess no boots; but he possesses judgment, and exercises it. A cabman soaked through after waiting an hour does not become picturesque merely because the fare has white gloves. A servant dismissed through a misunderstanding is not consoled by the superior pattern of the drawing-room carpet. The joke sometimes ascends the social staircase and knocks at the best bedroom.

This is not revolution. Leech is no torch-bearing tribune calling the populace to tear down the railings. He loves the settled house, the family circle, the countryside, the decent habit, the old institution when the old institution behaves decently. But he cannot endure swagger, humbug, bullying, or pompous stupidity. The petty tyrant, whether he wears coronet, epaulettes, apron, or schoolmaster’s gown, is brought to the same tribunal of laughter.

Observe the official gentleman. How magnificently he explains that nothing can be done! His desk is large, his pen small, his neckcloth spotless, his ignorance complete. A widow has come thirty miles; a soldier’s pension is delayed; a citizen desires only to know which form must be filled. The official points toward another department with the serenity of a Roman augur directing a sacrifice. Behind him rise pigeonholes containing the bones of departed applications.

Leech’s mockery is mild, but mildness is not weakness. A quiet line may pierce inflated authority more neatly than Gillray’s pitchfork. The pompous man is not chopped into pieces; he is allowed to speak. That is sufficient.

Nor is Leech always laughing. Now and then the pleasant page darkens. There is a soldier leaving home; a wife with a baby watches at the gate; the regiment passes, flags stir, boys cheer, and the man turns his head. No melodrama, no theatrical tear as large as a pearl—only the turned head. Or a child stands beside an empty chair; or a worn seamstress pauses at dawn while carriages roll home from a ball. The pen that knows merriment knows sorrow, and because it has not lied to us in laughter, we trust it when it grows grave.

This, perhaps, is why parents and children can look at Leech together. The child finds the pony, the naughty boy, the dog stealing dinner, the grand gentleman tumbling into the brook. The mother sees the nursery truth, the household fatigue, the pretty dress, the foolish fashion, the glance that reveals affection. The father recognizes his club, his office, his field, his railway carriage, his own absurd attempt to appear master of circumstances. The servant, if she looks over his shoulder, may recognize rather more.

They laugh at different things, yet they laugh together. Is that a small achievement?

Formerly the caricature was often hidden. It belonged to the tavern wall, the print-shop window, the bachelor’s portfolio. Its strength was immense, but its hands were dirty and its jokes not introduced to ladies. Under artists such as Cruikshank and Leech, humor came into company. It did not cease to be humorous; it learned sympathy. It discovered that the human face could be funny without being made monstrous, that folly might be corrected without cruelty, that a woman need not be degraded to raise a laugh, and that the fall of a small boy into a puddle could represent the comic catastrophe of mankind.

We are told sometimes that gentle satire lacks force. Must every surgeon use an axe? Must every preacher shout damnation? Must every wit poison his arrow? There are vanities which harden under abuse and dissolve at a smile. Show the braggart his exact posture, the bore his listener’s exact expression, the fashionable mother her neglected child, the official his desk, the dandy his mirror—and the truth has done its business.

Leech’s truth is abundant because it is particular. The curl of the groom’s side-lock, the mud on the hunting-boot, the maid’s turned-up sleeve, the schoolboy’s inked finger, the widow’s shiny dress, the old major’s stiff knee, the baby’s fallen sock—these are not trifles. Life consists of trifles until joy or grief gathers them into a destiny. The artist preserves what official history drops.

Suppose, centuries hence, some learned Plancus desires to reconstruct Victorian England. He may read our debates and conclude that we spent life reforming tariffs. He may inspect our monuments and imagine us perpetually heroic. He may study our sermons and believe us perpetually virtuous. Then let him open Leech.

There he will find us as we were: proud of our horses, afraid of our dentists, bullied by our cooks, deceived by our children, delayed by our trains, imprisoned in our clothes, dignified in public, ridiculous at home; generous one minute, absurd the next, often pretentious, often kind. He will see our rooms and roads, our pleasures and prejudices, our umbrellas and affections. He will hear the nursery door slam, the cabman complain, the old gentleman wheeze, the young lady laugh.

And perhaps he will forgive us.

For we who remember the gloomy pictures of childhood—the smiling shipwrecks, the crimson battles, the wax murderers, the woollen skies—have watched pictorial humor pass from savagery toward fellowship. We do not deny the old masters their power. Heaven forbid! Gillray’s thunder still shakes the wall; Rowlandson’s coach still rattles; Cruikshank’s demons still dance. But in Leech the art sits beside us, knows our names, teases our foibles, loves our children, and draws the curtains when sorrow comes.

What more may we ask of a social artist? Accuracy without dryness; satire without hatred; beauty without affectation; morality without a birch-rod; laughter in which the little people may join. Can we have too much of such work? Can any nation possess too many records of its daily heart? Can any family be the worse for opening a book together and finding, among the dandies, cooks, babies, policemen, sportsmen, governesses, ponies, umbrellas, and impossible bonnets, some kindly reflection of itself?

We think not.

Let the young villains have their bright volumes, then. Let them lie upon the hearth-rug, turn the generous pages, and laugh without knowing how long humor labored before it became fit for their company. Let them pity us, if they please, for the troughs, waxworks, worsted waterfalls, and ancestral portraits of the consulship of Plancus. We shall pretend indignation; we shall call them pampered; we shall declare that cold water formed the Roman character and that no boy ever learned Latin upon a cushion.

Then we shall sit down beside them and look too.

For age may grumble at comfort, but it loves happiness; criticism may catalogue line, costume, gesture, and social type, but affection closes the catalogue. At the end there remain the things which the best humor has always served and the best art has always cherished: truth, and laughter, and beauty, and kindness.

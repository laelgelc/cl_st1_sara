## The Second Spring of Doctor Heidegger

In the western quarter of the town—where the houses, like their inhabitants, had grown old without thereby becoming venerable—there stood, some seventy years ago, a mansion whose narrow windows seemed forever contemplating a funeral. Ivy clutched the chimneys; moss softened the stone steps; and over the door hung an iron knocker shaped like a solemn human face, which, according to divers children of the neighborhood, had once bitten the hand of a tax collector. I do not affirm the circumstance. A conscientious historian must distinguish betwixt what he knows, what he suspects, and what he would gladly have invented.

The possessor of this melancholy residence was Doctor Erasmus Heidegger, a gentleman so ancient that nobody remembered him young, and so learned that nobody understood more than half his conversation. Certain persons called him physician; others, magician; while the sexton, who had dug graves for three generations of the doctor’s patients, maintained that these two professions differed chiefly in the length of their Latin words.

One autumn afternoon, when the sun shone redly through a haze and made the distant steeples look as if they stood in the smoke of the last conflagration, Doctor Heidegger invited four venerable acquaintances into his study.

They were all old; but age, which sometimes resembles a crown, sat upon their brows rather like a dunce’s cap.

The first was Mr. Medbourne, formerly a merchant of prodigious enterprise. In youth he had discovered that money might be gained by buying what one did not want, with funds one did not possess, and selling it to persons unable to pay. By this excellent principle he had risen magnificently and fallen with equal splendor. Ships had sailed under his flag to every sea, though several had existed nowhere save upon ledgers; mines had been opened in mountains which careful geographers could not find; whole cities had been laid out in swamps, and inhabited, for a season, by enthusiastic shareholders. At last, after promising golden harvests to half the town, Mr. Medbourne reaped for himself bankruptcy, disgrace, and a chamber over a cobbler’s shop.

Colonel Killigrew, the second guest, had wasted a brave constitution in wine, gaming, duels, and those divers gallantries which old men recount as victories and old women remember as injuries. His nose, once straight and martial, had become purple and argumentative; his hands trembled, though not from remorse; and his laugh, when it escaped him, sounded like a cracked drum beaten at a disbanded regiment’s parade.

The third was Mr. Gascoigne, a decayed statesman. He had spent forty years in public life without once allowing the public good to interfere with his advancement. He had been for peace when war was popular, for war when peace seemed profitable, for economy when out of office, and for generous expenditure when his friends held the purse. His speeches had been praised for containing every possible opinion with the exception of his own. Yet faction, that cannibal mother who eats her cleverest children first, had thrown him aside; and he now lived by composing anonymous letters against men whose hands he had formerly shaken.

The fourth guest was the Widow Wycherley, a lady who had preserved her pride more successfully than her reputation. In former days her beauty had caused three quarrels, two elopements, one broken engagement, and a duel in which neither gentleman was injured except in his dignity. She had been accused of vanity, coquetry, extravagance, and an excessive tenderness for admiration; but, as she herself observed, the accusations had generally proceeded from women who possessed no beauty and men who had failed to obtain her favor. Time had now whitened her hair, hollowed her cheeks, and drawn fine hieroglyphics about her mouth; nevertheless she wore a faded ribbon of rose-colored silk and glanced occasionally toward every reflecting surface.

“Dear doctor,” said the widow, settling herself in a high-backed chair, “you have summoned us with so mysterious an air that I half expect to see a ghost.”

“Nay, madam,” replied Heidegger, “four ghosts have already honored me with their company.”

Colonel Killigrew laughed until a cough seized him. Mr. Medbourne frowned, being uncertain whether the jest were at his expense; and Mr. Gascoigne smiled in the cautious manner of a politician who wishes to discover which side has made the joke before applauding it.

The study was a proper theatre for whatever comedy or catastrophe the doctor intended. Tall oaken bookcases stood along the walls, laden with black-letter volumes whose clasps had rusted shut, perhaps in mercy to mankind. A skeleton occupied a corner cupboard and appeared to grin through the glass at the living company. Above the fireplace hung a portrait of a pale young woman in bridal garments, with one white rose at her bosom. She had been, tradition tells us, Doctor Heidegger’s intended bride; but on the eve of their wedding she had swallowed a medicine of his preparing, and thereupon exchanged matrimony for immortality in oils.

There was likewise a tall mirror, dimmed by age and spotted with obscure stains. Some authorities declare that every person who looked into it beheld not his present countenance, but the face which his secret passions were preparing for him. Others say it merely needed cleaning. For mine own part, being a fiction-monger and therefore scrupulous in matters of evidence, I leave the reader to choose the more probable explanation.

On a shelf lay a huge book bound in black leather, with silver bosses and two massive clasps. Whenever this volume was opened, it was whispered, the skeleton rattled, the mirror groaned, the portrait frowned, and the iron knocker downstairs complained of toothache. Doctor Heidegger, however, did not touch the book that afternoon.

Instead, he directed the attention of his guests to a small round table in the center of the chamber. Upon it stood a vase of dark crystal, wrought with figures of serpents biting their tails. The vase contained a clear liquid which emitted a tremulous, moon-like radiance; and in this radiance the dust-motes seemed to swim like minute golden fishes.

“My respectable old friends,” began the doctor, seating himself in a carved armchair, “I have requested your presence that ye may assist me in an experiment.”

“An experiment!” cried Mr. Medbourne. “If there is capital required—”

“Or influence with the ministry,” interposed Gascoigne.

“Or courage!” said Colonel Killigrew.

“Or taste,” added the widow, arranging her ribbon.

“I require only your observation,” replied Heidegger. “And, perhaps, your self-command.”

At this they all looked disappointed.

The doctor reached toward a cabinet and took from it a little box, wherein lay a rose so utterly withered that its brown petals might have been mistaken for ashes. He held it up by the stem.

“This flower,” said he, “blossomed fifty-five years ago. It was given me by the young lady whose portrait hangs above the fireplace, on the evening before our intended marriage. Since her death I have preserved it.”

The widow raised a handkerchief to her eyes, though she peeped over its edge to observe whether the others noticed her sensibility.

“And now,” continued the doctor, “behold.”

He cast the dead rose into the luminous water.

For a few moments nothing occurred. Colonel Killigrew muttered that the flower would make a poor flavoring for punch. Then a tiny bubble clung to the shriveled calyx; another crept along the stem; and the brown petals stirred as though awakened by a whisper. Slowly they expanded. Their dullness softened into crimson; their brittle edges grew moist and tender; the stalk became green; and presently the rose floated in full bloom, so fresh that one might have believed the summer morning still trembled in its heart.

“See!” cried the widow.

Mr. Medbourne bent forward until his spectacles nearly fell into the vase. Gascoigne’s lips moved, already framing a public declaration concerning the national importance of roses. Colonel Killigrew stretched out a finger to touch the petals.

Doctor Heidegger drew the flower away.

“This water,” said he, “was sent to me by an old correspondent, lately returned from Florida. It was drawn, as he avers, from a spring which the Spaniards sought three centuries ago. Its banks are shadowed by magnolias of immense antiquity; yet the butterflies that drink of it never die, and the blossoms that fall therein rise freshly upon the current.”

“The Fountain of Youth!” whispered the widow.

“So my correspondent names it.”

“Give me a glass!” cried Killigrew.

“And me!” said Medbourne.

“And me!” said Gascoigne.

The widow said nothing, but extended both hands.

Doctor Heidegger regarded them with a melancholy amusement. “Before ye drink,” quoth he, “would it not be wise to compose a rule for your renewed lives? Ye have each had long experience of error. Surely, if youth be restored, ye will avoid the pits into which ye formerly stumbled.”

“Undoubtedly,” said Mr. Medbourne. “I shall engage in no enterprise not founded upon the soundest security.”

“I shall devote myself to the welfare of my country,” declared Gascoigne.

“I shall practice temperance,” said the colonel, staring thirstily at the vase.

“And I,” murmured the widow, “shall prize virtue above admiration.”

The skeleton appeared to shake with silent mirth. Possibly a cart passed in the street and jarred the house.

The doctor filled four crystal goblets. The water sparkled like melted diamonds, and a delicate fragrance arose from it—partly of roses, partly of rain upon warm earth, and partly of some remembered morning which none of the guests could name.

“Drink, then,” said Heidegger. “But slowly.”

They drained their goblets at a swallow.

At first they remained silent. The liquid seemed chill upon their tongues, but scarcely had it reached their hearts when a genial warmth spread through every vein. The widow’s eyes brightened. The colonel’s shoulders straightened by half an inch. Mr. Medbourne began tapping his foot beneath the table; and Gascoigne rubbed his hands as if an audience had just applauded him.

“This is excellent!” cried the colonel. “More!”

“Patience,” said Heidegger. “Youth, like strong liquor, should be taken cautiously.”

“Your caution is the prejudice of age,” retorted Gascoigne, with sudden animation. “Progress, sir, demands boldness.”

“Progress demands capital,” said Medbourne. “With exclusive rights to this water, we might command every treasury in Europe.”

The doctor poured a second measure.

Even before the four had emptied their goblets, a visible change came over them. The ashen hue of their cheeks gave place to a richer color; the deep furrows about their mouths grew shallow; their eyes, long clouded like windows in a deserted house, shone with inward lamps. The widow snatched off her cap, and beneath it her hair was no longer wholly white, but streaked with brown. Colonel Killigrew’s cough disappeared in the midst of a laugh. Gascoigne’s bent back uncurled, while Mr. Medbourne’s fingers ceased trembling and closed around the stem of his glass with a young man’s grasp.

They turned toward the ancient mirror.

Now, the mirror is said to have reflected four decrepit figures drinking with grotesque eagerness, while the guests themselves perceived faces of vigorous middle age. Whether enchantment lay in the glass or vanity in the beholders, I cannot determine; but all four exclaimed with delight.

“Twenty years gone!” cried Medbourne.

“Thirty!” shouted Killigrew.

The widow leaned close to her image, touching her cheeks, smoothing her hair, smiling first with one corner of her mouth and then the other, as though trying on expressions preserved from former seasons.

“Doctor,” said she, “you have been a cruel man to keep this secret so long.”

“I feared,” replied Heidegger, “that the world was already sufficiently supplied with youth.”

“More!” they cried together.

Once again he filled the goblets.

The third draught passed their lips like flame. Their garments, though unchanged, now hung strangely upon forms made slim and upright. Gray locks darkened to brown or black; baldness retreated, if not wholly, at least with honorable haste; wrinkles vanished; teeth seemed to multiply; and voices that had creaked or quavered rang loudly through the chamber.

Mr. Medbourne sprang from his chair, no longer a ruined old speculator but a sharp-eyed man of thirty-five, full of schemes. He seized a sheet of paper and began drawing circles, arrows, harbors, roads, and mountains.

“Attend!” he cried. “We shall purchase the entire province surrounding the fountain. We shall form the Perpetual Youth and Immortality Company, capitalized at fifty million. Shares will be issued in advance of discovery. The spring itself may be mortgaged to finance the expedition. We shall establish pipes from Florida to every civilized capital—nay, reservoirs! Public baths! Subscription plans! A premium tier for royalty!”

“Have you forgotten your former ruin?” asked Heidegger.

“Former ruin?” said Medbourne. “That was caused by timidity and insufficient borrowing.”

Meanwhile Gascoigne had mounted a footstool and begun addressing the skeleton as though it were a crowded assembly.

“Fellow citizens!” he declaimed. “At this decisive hour, when the eyes of posterity gaze backward upon the future, I stand before you neither as the advocate of age nor the partisan of youth, but as the unalterable champion of both, separately or together, according to circumstances!”

The skeleton listened with more patience than most legislatures.

“I have always maintained,” continued the statesman, striking his breast, “that principle must prevail over expediency—except when expediency is necessary to preserve principle. My opponents accuse me of inconsistency. I reply that consistency is but stagnation, and stagnation the enemy of progress, while progress—”

Here he whispered into the skull’s ear, nodded craftily, and promised it a government appointment.

Colonel Killigrew had discovered a decanter upon a sideboard. Though it contained only cordial intended for medicinal purposes, he drank to medicine, to the army, to love, to beauty, to the widow, to himself, and once more to the widow. He then drew his sword, which for reasons unknown he had worn to an afternoon experiment, and saluted the portrait above the fireplace.

“Fair lady!” he cried. “Descend from that dismal frame, and I shall teach thee to dance!”

The painted bride looked paler than before.

Widow Wycherley, who now appeared scarcely thirty, had found Doctor Heidegger’s velvet cap and placed it rakishly upon her glossy hair. She moved from one dark windowpane to another, using each as a mirror; lifted the faded rose-colored ribbon to her throat; pinched her cheeks, though they needed no assistance; and practiced a glance which, fifty years earlier, had caused a young clergyman to forget the conclusion of his sermon.

“Gentlemen,” said she, “am I indeed changed?”

“Changed?” exclaimed Killigrew. “You are Venus restored to earth!”

“A celestial emblem of our nation’s renewal,” said Gascoigne, descending from the footstool.

“A valuable partner in the company,” said Medbourne, “provided your shares are held in trust.”

The widow pouted at the merchant, smiled upon the statesman, and gave her hand to the colonel.

Doctor Heidegger sat apart, his silver head bent upon his breast. If he felt any temptation to taste the water, he gave no sign. He watched the four with the calm attention of a natural philosopher observing beetles that had fallen into a drop of honey.

Upon the window sill lay a dead butterfly, caught weeks before in a cobweb. A splash from one of the goblets had fallen upon it. Presently its folded wings quivered; its little legs released themselves from the silken threads; and it rose unsteadily into the air. Round and round the vase it fluttered, dazzlingly yellow in the supernatural radiance.

“Look!” said Heidegger. “Even that frail creature has recovered its summer.”

But nobody attended him.

The guests clamored for a fourth draught. Heidegger warned them that enough remained only for one modest cup apiece; whereupon they thrust their goblets forward with such violence that the crystal chimed like bells in alarm.

“Drink,” said the doctor, “and remember—if ye can.”

They drank.

Then youth burst upon them in its full, intolerable glory.

Mr. Medbourne became a slender young adventurer with a keen mouth, restless eyes, and the air of one who could sell the moon before ascertaining its owner. He danced around the table, scattering calculations, offering invisible shares to the furniture, and proposing that the skeleton be appointed company treasurer because dead men told no secrets.

Gascoigne, transformed into a handsome youth with flowing black hair, tore up his former speech, declared every word of it treason, and delivered another upon the opposite side. He bowed to imaginary balconies, denounced imaginary enemies, accepted imaginary office, resigned it on principle, and accepted a better one before his resignation had reached the floor.

Colonel Killigrew, broad-shouldered and ruddy, kicked aside his chair and sang a scandalous marching song. He challenged his reflection to a duel, embraced it when it declined, and attempted to climb into the portrait to kiss the painted bride. Failing in this, he turned eagerly toward the widow.

She stood in the midst of them with black hair falling about her shoulders, cheeks warm as summer fruit, and eyes bright with triumph. The years had dropped from her like a discarded cloak; but modesty, prudence, tenderness, and all the other virtues which she had promised to cultivate seemed unfortunately entangled in its folds.

“You dull philosophers!” she cried. “Why talk of companies and countries when the world was made for dancing?”

She caught Gascoigne’s hands and whirled him around the study. The statesman abandoned the nation without a qualm. Colonel Killigrew clapped the rhythm, then thrust Gascoigne aside and claimed the widow for himself. Mr. Medbourne, suddenly perceiving that beauty might be a more exciting speculation than land in Florida, seized her other hand.

“Madam,” cried he, “I loved you first!”

“You offered marriage only after calculating my income,” she replied, laughing.

“I loved you best!” shouted the colonel.

“You loved every woman best for half an hour.”

“I alone loved you honorably,” proclaimed Gascoigne.

“You sent me verses copied from a Frenchman and a letter intended for another lady.”

The three young men glared at one another.

“My dear friends,” said Doctor Heidegger, “this dispute is singularly ancient.”

No one heard him. Medbourne pulled the widow toward himself; Killigrew encircled her waist; Gascoigne seized the colonel by the collar. She screamed, laughed, protested, encouraged them all, and bestowed such impartial glances that each believed himself preferred.

The butterfly wheeled above their heads. The skeleton clattered in its cupboard. The portrait’s white rose appeared to redden, perhaps with indignation; and from the black book came a muffled thump, as if some imprisoned spirit were turning over in disgust.

Then the rivalry became battle.

Gascoigne struck Medbourne with a rolled speech. Medbourne answered with a ledger. Colonel Killigrew, scorning weapons so unwarlike, grappled both men at once. They reeled about the chamber, overturning chairs, trampling papers, and shouting threats, compliments, political slogans, military oaths, and financial terms. The widow darted among them, now pleading for peace, now accusing each of cowardice, and always contriving to remain at the center of attention.

“Mine!” cried Medbourne.

“Mine!” cried Gascoigne.

“By thunder, mine!” roared Killigrew.

Their combined weight crashed against the round table.

For one breathless instant the vase rocked upon its base. Its serpents of dark crystal seemed to writhe; the moon-like water leaped against the rim; and Doctor Heidegger half rose from his chair.

Too late.

The vase fell and shattered.

The precious liquor streamed across the floor in a widening pool, gleaming among shards of crystal. It soaked into the dusty boards, ran beneath the bookcases, and touched the roots of a dead fern in a cracked pot. The fern uncurled three green fronds, trembled in delight, and withered almost immediately.

The guests ceased struggling.

“Save it!” cried Medbourne, dropping to his knees.

They snatched up cups, spoons, scraps of paper—anything that might recover a drop. Colonel Killigrew even tried to drink from the floor; but the thirsty boards swallowed faster than he. Within moments nothing remained save a damp stain, a fragrance of vanished spring, and a few glittering fragments.

The butterfly fluttered downward. Its golden wings faded to dun; its circles grew narrow; and it fell dead beside the broken vase.

A silence entered the chamber.

Outside, the last red light withdrew from the roofs. Dusk pressed its face against the windows, and the study’s shadows lengthened until skeleton, portrait, doctor, and guests appeared all part of one dark, ancient picture.

The widow lifted a hand to her cheek.

“What is this?” she whispered.

A faint line had appeared beside her mouth. While she stared, another traced itself beneath her eye. Silver stole through her black hair like frost across midnight grass.

Colonel Killigrew staggered upright. His broad chest sank; his shoulders bowed; the ruddy color thickened into the old unhealthy purple. A cough interrupted the oath upon his lips, and his sword grew suddenly too heavy for his hand.

Gascoigne’s eloquent voice cracked in the middle of a sentence. His hair retreated from his brow; his smooth face loosened into folds; and he clutched at the table that was no longer there.

Mr. Medbourne’s fingers became knotted, his eyes watery, his back bent. The brilliant young schemer dwindled into the ruined old merchant, kneeling among scraps of worthless calculation.

“No!” they cried. “More water!”

“There is no more,” said Heidegger.

The rose, which he had placed beside him, was changing also. Its crimson darkened; its petals contracted; one by one they shriveled inward until the blossom was again the brown relic of fifty-five years.

Doctor Heidegger lifted it with a tenderness that sat strangely amid the wreckage.

“I love it better thus,” he murmured. “For it has bloomed once in truth, and once in illusion; and there are few hearts of which so much may be said.”

The four gathered before the mirror. It showed them—if common mirrors may be trusted—as they had been when they entered: gray, wrinkled, infirm, and ridiculous. The widow uttered a woful shriek and covered her face. Killigrew cursed the glass. Gascoigne accused it of belonging to the opposition. Medbourne examined the frame to discover whether it might be sold.

“Are we grown old again so soon?” groaned the widow.

“So soon?” replied Heidegger. “Ye have had youth twice. Many are exhausted by one portion.”

He looked upon them gravely, though not without kindness.

“My respectable old friends, I thank you. Had the fountain poured at mine own doorstep, I would not stoop to drink from it. Age has taken much—strength, beauty, appetite, and the flattering noise of the world—but if it has left wisdom, it has given more than it stole. Youth without innocence is merely appetite with strong limbs; experience without amendment is but a longer memory of folly.”

The skeleton nodded, or seemed to. The portrait resumed its mournful composure. The mirror held five old figures in the dusk, though one of them stood upright and unashamed.

For a little while the guests said nothing. A hopeful moralist might suppose that remorse visited them; that Medbourne perceived the madness of avarice, Gascoigne the hollowness of ambition, Killigrew the degradation of indulgence, and the widow the brief dominion of beauty. Indeed, I should gladly report such a conclusion, for readers esteem repentance, especially when it costs them nothing.

But truth—or tradition, which often wears truth’s cloak while picking its pocket—requires otherwise.

Before midnight, Mr. Medbourne had devised a plan to borrow funds for an expedition. Gascoigne had drafted a proclamation promising the fountain freely to all citizens while secretly reserving the first ten barrels for his supporters. Colonel Killigrew had found a map of Florida and marked every tavern upon the route. The Widow Wycherley had ordered a traveling cloak lined with rose-colored silk.

They resolved to depart thitherward at dawn.

“For,” said Medbourne, “the experiment failed only because the supply was limited.”

“We must drink daily,” declared Gascoigne.

“Hourly!” cried the colonel.

“Forever,” whispered the widow, gazing into the darkened mirror.

Doctor Heidegger did not attempt to dissuade them. He merely placed the withered rose back in its little box, while beyond the windows an autumn wind swept the dead leaves along the street—hurrying them southward, perhaps, toward Florida.

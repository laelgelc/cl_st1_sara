All day the rain had stitched the streets with grey thread. It fell without violence and without pity, a patient multitude of drops that polished the pavements, blackened the walls, and crept beneath the collar of the youth’s coat until the cloth lay against his neck like a cold hand.

He had come into the city at morning with fourteen cents and an idea that somewhere among the towers, warehouses, wharves, bridges, shops, hotels, and innumerable doors there must be a place for a man willing to work. By evening he possessed nine cents and no idea.

The crowds had taught him certain things. They had taught him that a soaked coat becomes a proclamation of guilt; that shoes opening at the toes are regarded as moral defects; that hunger, when publicly displayed, is a species of insult. Men had looked at him with quick irritation. Women had drawn their skirts aside. A small boy in a red cap had marched behind him for half a block, imitating his limp, while three other boys applauded.

“Say, mister, was you in th’ flood?” the child had cried.

The youth had turned once. His face, hollowed by a day without food, silenced them for an instant. Then one of the boys squealed, “Look out! He eats kids!” and they scattered into a doorway, where they jeered from safety.

He had continued down the avenue.

The city at dusk seemed to be manufacturing darkness. Smoke drooped from roofs. Under the elevated railway, iron pillars stood in two solemn files, and trains passed overhead with the grinding complaint of enormous teeth. Lamps awakened one by one in the wet air. Their reflections stretched on the pavement in yellow wounds, broken continually by wheels, hoofs, and the hurried feet of citizens.

At the corners, gongs clamored. Cable cars moved with a tranquil, terrible authority, as though conscious that flesh must yield before them. Coaches went by with lamps like the eyes of sleek animals. Behind the glass were pale faces, flowers, white gloves, and sometimes the flash of jewels at a woman’s throat. The youth watched these warm little worlds glide past him. They appeared more distant than stars.

He had spent five cents at noon upon a piece of bread and a cup of coffee which had tasted faintly of burnt wood. Since then he had made investigations. He had studied cards announcing PORTER WANTED, BOY WANTED, MAN WANTED, but each card had proved to contain some hidden clause written upon his person. His wet clothes, his unshaven chin, his damaged shoes said, Not this boy. Not this man.

At a warehouse a broad clerk had glanced down at him.

“Experience?”

“No, sir, but I can—”

“Nothing doin’.”

At a restaurant a man in a white apron had laughed. “We ain’t runnin’ no orphan asylum.”

At a stable he had been informed that he was too light. At a shop he was too dirty. At a cellar he was too late. The refusals differed in language, but together they formed a single great official voice.

Toward night he drifted into narrower streets, where the city no longer pretended to be handsome. The buildings leaned above him with faces blotched by old advertisements. Fire escapes clung to them like black parasites. In windows, women bent over sewing; children pressed round noses against glass; men in undershirts smoked with the composure of chieftains. The gutters carried cigar stumps, orange peel, grease, paper, and small nameless wreckage toward the drains.

The smell of food came upon him suddenly.

It issued from a saloon whose swinging doors opened and shut like greedy lips. Each time they parted, they released heat, tobacco smoke, sour beer, and an odor of soup. The youth halted. Through the glass he saw a row of men at the bar, their elbows planted in grave equality. Behind them, at the farther end, stood kettles and dishes beneath a sign:

FREE LUNCH.

He knew the law of such temples. A man bought a drink, and thereafter might approach the food with an air of privilege. Nine cents lay in his pocket. Five would buy beer. Four would remain between him and the morning.

He entered.

The saloon swallowed him with a puff of heated breath. At first the warmth was pain. His fingers tingled; his wet coat began to steam faintly. The long mirror behind the bar multiplied bottles into jeweled battalions. Red and green labels gleamed. Brass rails burned under the lamps. The bartender, a stout man with sleeves rolled above furry arms, moved among the glasses with the bored majesty of an official who has witnessed all human thirsts and found them alike.

“Beer,” said the youth.

The bartender drew it, shoved it forward, and swept away the nickel. The youth carried the glass toward the lunch counter. The beer was thin, dark, and bitter, but in his empty body it assumed an immediate and portentous power.

At the counter, an old man in a white jacket presided over a kettle like a priest guarding a poor altar.

“Soup?” asked the youth.

The old man looked at the beer, then at him. “Bowl’s there.”

The youth took a bowl. The server ladled into it a brown fluid in which floated a pale cube of potato and an object that might once have been acquainted with meat. He added two slices of bread.

The youth sat at a little table. He ate slowly at first, fearing that haste would betray him. Then hunger seized the spoon. The soup vanished. He returned with his bowl.

The old man’s eyes narrowed.

“Anudder?” the youth asked.

The ladle descended. “Last one.”

This bowl contained no potato, but it was hot. Heat spread through the youth’s breast and stomach, producing for a few moments the magnificent deception of abundance. He drank the beer afterward and felt almost restored to citizenship.

Near him stood a man whose appearance suggested murder committed in an alley under bad weather. He was short, powerful in the shoulders, and covered by a coat too large for him. One lapel was folded inward; the other rose near his ear. A battered hat cast a purple shadow over his eyes. His beard grew in dark, separate thickets. Across his cheek ran a scar, pale and crooked as a worm.

The man held an empty glass and stared at the youth.

“Cold night,” said the youth, because the stare demanded some payment.

“Cold?” replied the man. “Hully gee! It’s worse’n cold. It’s murder. It’s freezin’ th’ liver inside me.”

He swayed, steadied himself against the table, and examined the youth with solemn cunning.

“Yeh on th’ road?”

“I’m lookin’ for work.”

“Sure. Same t’ing. I’m a mechanic.” He struck his chest. “Good mechanic, too. Ain’t no man can say I ain’t. Trouble is, these here bosses—” He paused, overcome by the enormity of bosses. “They don’t want a man what knows his business.”

The youth nodded.

The sinister man lowered his voice. “Where yeh sleepin’?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Got money?”

“A little.”

The other looked toward the bartender, toward the free-lunch priest, then again at the youth. “I know a place. Six cents. Best place downtown. Spring mattresses, towels, hot an’ cold water, servants answer bells. Six cents.”

The youth smiled faintly.

“I ain’t kiddin’ yeh,” said the man, wounded. “Maybe they ain’t got bells. But it’s warm. That’s what counts. Warm as July. I got four cents. Lend me two an’ we both goes.”

The youth put his hand in his pocket. His four cents seemed suddenly to rattle with the loudness of chains. To give away two was absurd. To retain them was also absurd. Two cents could not protect a man from the city; yet, in that instant, the coins seemed to contain bread, light, and life.

The assassin watched him with the moist dignity of a betrayed friend.

“I’ll pay yeh in th’ mornin’,” he said. “B’ Gawd, I ain’t one t’ beat a gent. Ask anybody.”

“All right.”

The youth showed the four pennies.

The assassin’s face brightened with a childlike triumph. “There! I knowed yeh was square. Seen it soon’s yeh come in. A gent knows a gent.”

They left the saloon. Its doors closed behind them, cutting off the golden heat. The rain had thinned to a mist, but the wind ranged through the streets like a policeman clearing loiterers from a park.

The assassin walked rapidly, though not always in a straight line. He made speeches. At intervals he turned and tapped the youth upon the chest.

“I had money once. Plenty. Worked in Chicago. Worked in Buffalo. Worked in—where was that place? Well, I worked there. Boss says t’ me, ‘Bill, yeh’re th’ best man ever handled a tool.’ That’s what he says. But I’m too good-hearted. That’s me trouble. Give away everything. Shirt off me back.”

He wore no visible shirt.

At a corner they passed beneath an elevated station. Its stairways spread over the pavement like the jointed legs of a monstrous crab. Above, a train burst from the darkness, window after window blazing. For a breath the wet street was striped with light, and then the black structure trembled in thunder.

The assassin shook his fist at it cheerfully.

“Ride in them cars when I want,” he said. “I jest don’t want.”

They turned into a street where lodging houses stood shoulder to shoulder. Their windows glowed a diseased yellow. Men gathered before them in small knots, stamping their feet, coughing, arguing, waiting for some miracle involving a dime. Here poverty had lost the disguises it wore farther uptown. It sprawled openly on the sidewalks, scratched itself, cursed, begged tobacco, and spat into the gutter.

The assassin stopped before a door beneath a lamp.

“Here she is. Hotel de—hotel de somethin’. Finest in town.”

A board advertised BEDS 6 CENTS. Below it, in smaller letters, was the command PAY IN ADVANCE.

Within, a thin clerk sat behind wire netting. His face appeared to have been pressed flat by years of suspicion. The assassin pushed four cents through a hole, and the youth added eight.

“Twelve,” said the clerk.

“Count it,” said the assassin grandly. “We’re honest men.”

The clerk slid two metal checks toward them. “Upstairs.”

A narrow passage led to a stairway. As the youth climbed, a smell descended upon him. It was not one odor but a parliament of odors: wet wool, ancient boots, sour bodies, stale smoke, disinfectant, rotting wood, cheap liquor, and the damp breath of walls that had absorbed years of sleeping men. These smells did not merely hang in the air. They rushed upon the newcomer like small winged diseases and clung to his throat.

At the head of the stairs sat an attendant with a face the color of uncooked dough. He collected their checks and pointed.

The room beyond was vast and low. Rows of cots stretched into gloom beneath weak gas jets. The ceiling was supported by wooden posts, stained and greasy from the hands of generations. On every side men undressed, argued, slept, coughed, or stood in attitudes of bewilderment. Shirts hung from nails. Shoes lay like dead animals beneath the beds. From some distant point came the continuous dripping of water.

The youth halted.

The assassin spread his arms. “What’d I tell yeh? Palace.”

A man passed carrying his trousers in both hands. He was astonishingly thin. His naked back, blue in the poor light, displayed the knobs of the spine like beads beneath parchment. Another man sat upon a cot and examined his foot with grave despair. Near him, a red-haired giant snored while still wearing one boot. A small old creature wrapped himself in newspapers, carefully placing the financial pages over his chest.

The sleepers formed a strange landscape. Knees rose like hills. Arms dangled like broken branches. Heads, bald, shaggy, bandaged, black, grey, and yellow, lay upon rolled coats. Under the lamps, faces shone with sweat; beyond them, bodies dissolved into shadow. The room resembled a battlefield after some obscure defeat, yet no banners explained it and no nation would count its dead.

“Pick a cot,” said the attendant.

The youth chose one near the wall. Its leather surface was cold and slippery. When he touched it, he thought of melting snow.

The assassin had found a cot beside him. With many grunts he removed his coat and revealed a faded waistcoat over a red undershirt. He placed his boots carefully beneath his head.

“Never trust no place,” he explained. “Feller stole my shoes once. Had t’ walk barefoot all winter.”

“All winter?”

“Near all. Couple hours, anyhow. Snow on th’ ground.”

He began to unbutton his waistcoat, then suddenly stared across the aisle. A young lodger there was pulling a white nightshirt over his head.

The assassin sat rigid.

“Lookit that!” he whispered.

The nightshirt descended in clean folds to the wearer’s ankles. Amid the grime of the room it possessed the supernatural brilliance of an angel’s robe.

“Say,” called the assassin, “you expectin’ company?”

The man in white ignored him.

“Hully gee,” continued the assassin, turning to the youth. “A nightshirt! In a six-cent joint! Ain’t that style? Bet he’s a duke. Come over from Europe on business.”

The youth laughed. Several men nearby laughed also. The man in the nightshirt climbed into bed with the stiff dignity of a martyr.

The assassin lay down, crossed his hands upon his breast, and said, “Wake me at seven. Got a job.”

Within a minute he was asleep, his mouth open, his scar made innocent by repose.

The youth kept on his wet clothes. He lay upon the leather and tried to arrange his bones. Cold came through the cot as if it were a slab in a cellar. Around him the lodging house settled gradually into night.

Talk diminished to muttering. Boots dropped. The gas jets hissed. Men coughed from deep and ruined chambers within themselves. One cursed in sleep. Another ground his teeth with a sound of machinery attempting to start. The red-haired giant’s snore rolled steadily across the room and broke against the far wall.

The youth closed his eyes.

Immediately the darkness became crowded. He felt bodies everywhere, hundreds of them, packed in tiers below the streets while the city walked above. He imagined the buildings planted upon human backs. Their foundations descended through the lodging-house ceiling and rested upon ribs, shoulders, skulls. The banks, theaters, courts, churches, and offices were supported there, and from their bright windows came music, judgments, prayers, and the clink of money.

He opened his eyes.

A gas flame shook in a current of air. Its blue heart and yellow point made shadows leap upon the ceiling. A man near it had flung one arm over his face. His chest rose violently; his fingers twitched. He seemed a fish stabbed and cast upon a dock, still heaving while the sellers discussed its price.

Farther away, someone began to moan.

At first the sound was small, almost courteous. It threaded among the snores and coughs. Then it grew. The sleeper muttered words the youth could not understand. His voice climbed into terror.

“No—no—fer God’s sake—”

A pause followed, filled by the dripping water.

Then came a cry so naked that the youth raised himself upon one elbow.

“Oh, don’t! Don’t! I never done it!”

No one moved to comfort the man. A lodger turned over and swore. The red-haired giant continued his monumental snoring. The attendant, somewhere beyond the door, knocked ashes from a pipe.

The sleeper cried again.

In daylight the voice might have been ridiculous—the nightmare of a drunkard pursued by a policeman, a creditor, a dead wife, or a rat. But in that cavern of men it became immense. It was no longer one man’s protest. It seemed to issue from all the cots, from the mouths of the motionless, from the stained walls and buried floors. The youth heard in it the appeal of those who had been pushed from warm rooms, barred from workshops, chased from stations, examined by clerks, measured by policemen, mocked by boys, and finally stored here for six cents a body.

The cry accused no particular person. It accused the lamps that shone elsewhere, the locked doors, the full tables, the soft beds moving invisibly through the night behind curtains. It accused the whole magnificent city, which thundered and glittered while beneath its feet a class, a tribe, a people lay in rows and dreamed of punishment.

“Shut up, yeh fool!” yelled someone.

The dreamer subsided into sobbing.

The youth lay down again. He stared until the posts became black monuments and each cot a grave. He had entered merely to escape rain, but the room had closed over him like earth. The sleepers appeared not resting men but corpses rehearsing eternity, each with his boots beneath his head lest thieves should rob him in the resurrection.

Hours moved with intolerable slowness. The youth slept in brief falls, dropping into darkness and striking bottom almost at once. Each time he woke, some new misery had taken command of the room: coughing, cold, stench, the pressure of his own wet clothes, the anguish of his cramped legs.

At last a blade of pale light entered through a high window. It cut across the smoke and touched a wooden post. Dust moved within it.

The attendant shouted, “Up! Up, now!”

Men rose from the cots like reluctant dead. They groped for shoes, coats, hats, and missing pieces of themselves. The visionary horror of the night departed before the commonplace brutality of morning. The room was only a dirty room. The graves were cheap beds. The doomed multitude became a collection of shabby men quarrelling over a sock.

The assassin awoke with a roar.

“Who hit me?”

“Nobody,” said the youth.

“I knowed it.” He sat up and rubbed his face. “Best sleep I had in years. Feather mattress.”

They dressed, though the youth had little to do beyond standing. His clothes were still damp. The assassin struggled into his coat and discovered a piece of bread in one pocket. He regarded it with astonishment, smelled it, and ate it.

Outside, the rain had ended. The dawn was steel-colored, and the streets shone beneath it. Wagons rattled from markets. Shopkeepers raised shutters with the clank of prison doors. A woman scrubbed a step, driving dirty water into the gutter. The city, washed but not cleansed, resumed its business.

“Breakfast,” said the assassin.

“I’ve only two cents.”

The assassin appeared grieved by this evidence of poor management. He searched his pockets elaborately and produced nothing but a button.

“I got money comin’,” he said. “Feller owes me a dollar. Lives uptown. If I could jest git breakfast, I’d collect it. Lend me a couple cents more.”

“I haven’t got it.”

“Yeh said two.”

The youth looked at the pennies in his palm.

“Sure, I ain’t askin’ charity,” the assassin added hastily. “Loan. Business proposition. I pay four fer two. Hundred per cent.”

The youth gave him one penny and kept one.

The assassin frowned at the coin. “Can’t buy much.”

“No.”

After a moment he returned it. “Keep it. A gent oughta have somethin’ in his pocket. Come on. I know a place.”

They walked until they found a basement where coffee and rolls were sold for three cents. The assassin entered first, full of confidence.

“Two coffees an’ rolls,” he ordered.

The proprietor, a narrow woman with iron-grey hair, set the food before them. When she asked for payment, the assassin placed the youth’s two cents upon the counter and then began a search through his clothes that became increasingly theatrical.

“Well, I’m damned,” he said. “Had a nickel.”

“You got two cents there,” said the woman.

“My friend’s got—” He looked at the youth.

The youth produced his last penny.

The woman swept up the three cents. “One coffee, two rolls.”

She gave them a cup and two small rolls. The assassin carried the meal to a table with no sign of defeat.

“Knew she’d make trouble,” he said. “Meanest woman downtown.”

They split the coffee into two thick glasses, adding hot water from a metal pitcher. Each took a roll. It was stale, but warmth revived it. The youth held it between both hands. Steam touched his face.

For several minutes they ate in silence. The assassin dipped fragments into his coffee, carefully rescuing each one before it sank. His scar, his black beard, and his battered hat still advertised violence, but across the little table he looked merely tired.

“Where yeh from?” he asked.

The youth named a town inland.

“Know it well,” said the assassin. “Never been there, but know it. Good town?”

“It was all right.”

“Folks?”

“Some.”

“Git tired of ’em?”

The youth looked into his coffee. “There wasn’t much to stay for.”

“Same with me,” said the assassin. “Mother wanted me t’ be a preacher. I says, ‘No, ma, I got t’ see life.’ Seen it, too.”

He told of voyages on ships he had perhaps never boarded, jobs held for periods that changed with each telling, fortunes won, stolen, or nobly given away. He had been a machinist, sailor, cook, boxer, brakeman, and confidential assistant to a millionaire. In every history there was a boss who admired him, a woman who betrayed him, and an enemy whom he spared at the decisive moment.

The youth listened. In return he spoke a little of farms, freight trains, a factory that had closed, and a winter when work disappeared from his county as birds disappear before snow. Their stories, unequal in truth but equal in need, met over the weak coffee. For half an hour they were not specimens of failure. They were travelers, mechanics, men acquainted with distance and injustice. The table became a domestic island; the hot glasses were lamps of fellowship.

When the food was gone, the proprietor looked toward them repeatedly. They departed.

By then the sun had broken through the eastern clouds. It struck upper windows and made them flash like shields. Down in the streets, the cold remained blue and sharp. People emerged in increasing numbers, buttoned, brushed, gloved, fortified by breakfasts.

The two wanderers reached City Hall Park and chose a bench. The assassin leaned back, tipped his hat over his eyes, and announced that he would wait there until his debtor’s office opened.

“Wake me at nine,” he said.

“It’s nearly nine now.”

“Then wake me at ten.”

He folded his arms and slept.

The youth sat beside him. Sunlight fell through bare branches in bright, narrow spears. Pigeons strutted before the benches, fat with civic confidence. From surrounding streets came the continual roar of traffic, the clash of wheels, the cries of drivers, the bells and gongs, the pounding of construction. Above the trees rose the buildings, huge and composed, their windows holding the sky in a thousand hard blue squares.

Men hurried through the park. Their shoes shone. Their collars were white, their coats fitted closely, their faces bore the absorbed severity of those summoned by important affairs. They carried newspapers, canes, leather cases. Some glanced at watches. Some smiled at private thoughts. None looked at the youth except by accident, and each accidental glance withdrew immediately.

Women passed in dark dresses, trailing faint perfumes. A girl laughed as she crossed the path with a young man. The sound was light, careless, and complete. It seemed to belong to a climate where rain never entered through torn shoes.

The youth watched them all.

During the night he had imagined himself joined to the men in the lodging house by a common calamity. Morning had dissolved that brotherhood. Now even the sleepers who had risen with him had scattered. Each had vanished down a street to beg, search, steal, work, drink, or fall. The assassin alone remained, snoring under his hat, but his companionship was already becoming unreal, a bargain made in darkness.

The youth saw the city’s ordered procession and understood that he was outside it.

No wall enclosed the fortunate. No guard challenged him. The park was public; the streets were free; the towers rose for everyone to behold. Yet between him and the passing citizens lay a gulf more absolute than masonry. Their clothing, haste, clean hands, and unconscious expectation of shelter formed a language he could see but not speak.

The buildings appeared to contemplate him. Banks lifted granite brows. Offices stood in solemn ranks. A courthouse raised its pale dome as if justice had climbed there to escape the crowd below. Flags stirred high above the roofs, proclaiming unity to the sky.

From the streets came a metallic undertone. Wheels struck rails, harness jingled, coins rang upon counters, cash drawers snapped shut. Gradually all the sounds merged in the youth’s mind into one enormous counting. The city counted wages, fares, rents, meals, beds, minutes. It counted everything but him.

He looked down at his trousers, stained dark at the knees. One shoe had split farther during the night, exposing the grey of his sock. Mud crusted the hem of his coat. The sight filled him with sudden shame.

Yesterday he had believed these things unfortunate. Now they seemed accusations. He began to feel that the crowd was correct in avoiding him, that the clerks had properly refused him, that the boys had detected some natural comedy in his walk. Poverty, which had first appeared as weather descending upon him from without, became a substance within his skin.

A policeman crossed the park.

The youth’s heart stirred uneasily. He had done nothing. Nevertheless, he lowered his eyes. The officer passed without observing him, but the youth felt the invisibility as a judgment.

Beside him the assassin slept with his mouth open, peaceful as a battered idol. Citizens flowed around the bench in two polished streams. The sun climbed, gilding cornices, warming windows, illuminating the grand walls in which business, law, and government performed their stately ceremonies.

At their feet sat the youth.

He had entered the city asking only food, work, and a dry place to lie down. By morning it had given him a different gift: a name for himself. Outcast. The word settled upon him with the weight of an official seal.

He no longer watched the fortunate with simple hunger. His gaze had become furtive. He glanced from beneath his brow at coats, watches, boots, white cuffs, and guarded pockets. Each object seemed to suspect him. Each comfort appeared to cry that he desired to steal it.

And so, under the flags and the bright indifferent windows, while the city thundered of enterprise and morning, the youth began to look upon the world with the guilty eyes of a criminal.

# Mortazu Khan and the Bear

From the mud roofs of Sharak to the high shoulder of the Koh-i-Baba there ran, that morning, a road which no road-maker had designed. It leaped irrigation ditches, lost itself among apricot orchards, climbed naked shale, and reappeared beyond a black gorge as two pale scratches upon the mountain. Along those scratches galloped Mortazu Khan.

The sun had not yet looked over the eastern snows. Below him the valleys were full of violet darkness, wherein little villages lay like handfuls of cold ashes. Above, the peaks had begun to burn—first grey, then rose, then the fierce white of a sword-edge. Wild thyme snapped under the hoofs of Mortazu’s bay mare, and the startled marmots whistled from among blue gentians and thorn-cushions.

“Faster, daughter of the wind!” cried Mortazu, leaning over the mare’s wet neck. “There is barley for thee at Ghuzni, and praise for us both if Allah permits.”

The mare required neither promise. Her ears were flat, her breath came in red, smoking bursts, and the silver bells upon her bridle had long ceased to ring merrily. They jangled now like spoons in a beggar’s bowl.

At Ghuzni was the English hakim.

At Sharak, in a low house shaded by one ancient mulberry, Azeena, Mortazu’s wife, had lain in labour since the previous sunset. The village wise-woman had burned herbs, muttered verses, tied a blue bead to Azeena’s wrist, and ordered every man out of the courtyard. At midnight she had sent for Mortazu.

“This child,” she said, “has set his face toward Kabul and his feet toward Kandahar. I have reasoned with him, but he is obstinate.”

“He is my son,” Mortazu answered proudly.

“He will be nobody’s son unless you fetch the English hakim.”

Then pride departed from Mortazu as a bird departs from a shaken bush. He saddled in darkness. Before dawn he was ten miles upon the road.

If the passes were kind, if the mare did not founder, if the hakim was not drinking tea, pulling teeth, mending soldiers, or performing one of those mysterious operations by which Englishmen disputed with the decrees of Allah, Mortazu might return before night. Might! It was a word as narrow as a goat track and as deep on either side as death.

He knew what the doctor would say.

“Damn you, Khan! Why didn’t you come yesterday?”

“Because yesterday, Sahib, the child was not born.”

“He isn’t born now, you blockhead.”

“For that reason I have come.”

“Where’s my horse?”

“Under you, Sahib, if your honour will cease talking.”

At this imagined triumph Mortazu grinned, though his lips were cracked and his heart was heavy. Doctor Meredith had once stitched a bullet furrow in Mortazu’s thigh while abusing Mortazu, the rifle, the Russians, the weather, and Asia generally. Mortazu trusted him. A man who could curse while his fingers worked so gently possessed, in Mortazu’s opinion, a special arrangement with Providence.

The main road bent southward round a ravine. Mortazu left it and climbed west by a shepherd’s path to the plateau of Sang-i-Safed. It was a dangerous short cut in spring, when snow-water cut the turf into hidden channels, but it saved two hours.

Two hours! A kingdom of time.

The mare climbed until the valley sank away behind them. Dwarf junipers clutched at the hillside. Splintered rocks stood in ranks like wicked old men conferring together. Once a stone rolled beneath the mare’s hind foot and went bounding downward—click, crack, crash—announcing Mortazu’s haste to every creature within a league.

“Be silent, son of a loose cliff!” he shouted after it.

At last they gained the plateau, a lonely table of grass and stone, ringed by mountains whose summits crowded the sky like white-turbaned judges. A stream wandered through it, bright and useless. There were patches of snow in the hollows, primulas by the water, and, near the centre, three great firs standing apart from the lower forest.

The mare stopped.

Not checked—stopped.

Her forelegs stiffened. Her nostrils opened. A tremor ran along her body and reached Mortazu through saddle, knee and spine.

“What foolishness—?”

From behind the nearest fir rose a brown bulk.

It came up slowly at first, unfolding itself out of shadow: head low, shoulders rolling, fur ragged with the remnants of winter. Then it saw horse and rider.

“Wuff!”

The sound struck the quiet plateau like a fist on a door.

The mare wheeled so violently that Mortazu saw sky, grass, and snow exchange places. He seized the mane. The saddle slipped. A stirrup leather snapped with a dry pop, and the next instant he was on his back among the stones while the mare fled toward the southern rim, reins flying and bells screaming.

“Come back!” bellowed Mortazu. “Faithless eater of barley! Daughter of an auctioneer!”

The mare did not come back.

The bear did.

She advanced at a gallop that appeared impossible for anything so loose, hairy and badly assembled. Her paws slapped the ground. Her head swung. Little puffs of dust burst beneath her like musket smoke.

Mortazu rolled upright. His jezail was in the saddle. His short spear was in the saddle. Even his old cavalry pistol, which seldom fired except when dropped, was in the saddle.

In his girdle was a dagger.

“A generous equipment,” he said bitterly, and ran for the fir.

The trunk was thick enough to hide him. He reached it with the bear six strides behind, flung himself round, and heard her claws rake the bark where his spine had been.

Skrrraatch!

He dodged left. The bear followed. He reversed. She reversed. He slid round the trunk while she thundered after him, and for one extraordinary minute man and beast revolved about the fir as children revolve about a wedding pole.

Mortazu was the quicker. The bear was the larger. Mortazu could turn upon a heel. The bear had to collect her hindquarters, check the landslide of her own weight, and launch herself afresh. Each time she overshot, he gained half a circle. Each time his boots slipped on needles, she gained it back.

Round they went.

“May boils decorate thy nose!” panted Mortazu.

The bear lunged right.

He jerked left.

Her paw came round the trunk and hooked the tail of his choga. The cloth tore from shoulder to hem, and Mortazu escaped in his shirt, leaving a strip of wool upon her claws.

“That garment,” he cried, “was woven by my wife’s mother, O Grandmother of Fleas! Allah alone knows its value, for no merchant ever would!”

The bear bit the dangling cloth, discovered that it was not Mortazu, and sneezed. Then she charged again.

There was something ludicrous in her earnestness, but only to a spectator upon a distant peak. To Mortazu the yellow teeth, the hot carrion breath, and the claws that peeled bark in curling ribbons were altogether solemn.

Twice he attempted to break away toward the next tree. Twice she cut him off. He had the speed for twenty yards, perhaps; she had the speed for thirty. Beyond the fir lay open turf, and beyond that the stream, and beyond that nothing useful until the cliff.

Therefore—round.

The sun climbed. Shadows shortened. The snow peaks lost their blush and became hard white fire. A lammergeier sailed overhead, tilting one insolent wing as though judging a contest arranged for its entertainment.

Mortazu’s breath began to saw his chest. Sweat ran into his eyes. His right boot had split at the toe, and blood darkened the leather where a stone had cut him. The bear, too, breathed heavily, but her rage appeared inexhaustible. It replenished itself from some deep mountain cistern.

Once she reared and embraced the trunk.

Her forelegs reached round on either side. Her claws came together with Mortazu between them.

He dropped flat.

The paws clapped above his head—whump!—and showered him with bark. He crawled between the bear’s hind legs, smelt earth and musk and death, sprang up behind her and stabbed.

The dagger entered high in the haunch.

The bear screamed.

It was not the roar he expected. It was a piercing, almost human cry that ran across the plateau and returned from the mountains in thin, astonished echoes.

Mortazu snatched at the dagger, but the blade had twisted from his hand. The bear spun, faster than thought, and struck him across the shoulder.

He flew.

Ground and sky again exchanged places. He landed against a root with such force that the plateau filled with sparks. For several heartbeats he could neither move nor remember why movement was desirable.

Then the bear came limping toward him, his dagger standing in her flesh.

Mortazu gained his knees. His left arm hung numb. Blood crept warmly down his ribs from four parallel wounds.

“Enough,” he gasped. “Peace between us, Bibi. I go to Ghuzni; thou remainest here. We shall speak no more of the coat.”

“Waugh—rrr!”

“A reasonable proposal!”

She rejected it.

Mortazu rolled behind the trunk as she struck. Again the circling began, but now its comedy had leaked away. Both were wounded. Both were slowing. The bear left dark drops on the grass. Mortazu left brighter ones. His knees trembled, and his breath came in little broken whistles. Every turn showed him the sun moving westward.

Azeena!

He saw her in the dim room: black hair damp upon her temples, one hand gripping the quilt, the other clutching the blue bead. He heard her say, as she had said when he left, “Do not kill the mare.”

He had answered, “I shall ride her tenderly.”

“You ride everything as if soldiers were behind you.”

“Today death is behind me.”

“No,” Azeena had whispered. “Today death is before you. Ride through him.”

And here he was, dancing round a tree with a bear while the English hakim sat perhaps three hours away, complaining that his tea had been boiled too long.

Rage strengthened Mortazu.

“Thief of hours!” he shouted. “Clumsy aunt of wicked daughters! My wife lies dying while thou playest games!”

He snatched a fallen branch and swung it. The rotten wood burst on the bear’s muzzle. She shook her head, surprised rather than hurt, and Mortazu seized the dagger handle.

He pulled.

The blade came free in a gush of black-red blood.

The bear struck. One claw ripped his turban away; another opened the skin above his ear. Mortazu ducked, backed round the tree, and felt the warm side of the trunk against his cheek.

Then from above came a sound.

“Wheet—wheet!”

Mortazu looked up.

Among the branches, thirty feet above him, a small round face peered down. Two black eyes. A pale muzzle. Furry ears absurdly spread. The cub clung in a fork of the tree and chirped again, thinly, anxiously.

“Wheet!”

Mortazu became still.

The bear ceased her advance and looked upward too.

For one breathing-space they stood on opposite sides of the trunk, their blood mingling among the roots.

So.

That was the meaning of her attack. He had ridden between the mother and the tree; the mare’s bells had frightened the cub upward; and thereafter no power beneath heaven could persuade the bear that Mortazu was merely a hurried husband with business elsewhere.

His anger drained out of him. In its place came something heavier.

“O Bibi,” he said softly. “Thou also.”

The bear swung her head toward him. Her little eyes were furious, but behind the fury was pain, and behind the pain the same iron command that had driven Mortazu from Sharak before dawn.

The cub cried. Somewhere, far beyond the mountains, perhaps Azeena cried also.

Mortazu lowered the dagger.

“If I go,” he told the bear, “thou wilt follow. If I stay, my wife dies. If thou killest me, my child may die. If I kill thee, that little ball of impudence dies. This is a badly judged morning, and I shall complain of it in Paradise.”

The bear dropped to all fours.

There could be no peace. Blood had settled that. Wounded, she could not understand departure; afraid for the cub, she would permit none. Her shoulders rolled forward.

Mortazu wrapped the torn end of his sash round his left forearm. The arm had begun to move again, though each movement sent fire into his shoulder. He shifted the dagger to an underhand grip.

“No hatred,” he said. “Before Allah, none.”

The bear charged.

This time he did not circle.

He waited beside the trunk until the hammering paws seemed to shake the roots. He saw the foam at her lips, the blood matting her thigh, the tiny burrs caught in her breast fur. At the final instant he sprang aside and slashed.

The blade crossed her muzzle. She passed, wheeled, and came again.

Mortazu feinted left, plunged right. A paw caught his back and tore shirt and skin. He fell against the trunk. Before he could recover, the bear rose over him, shutting out the mountains.

He thrust the wrapped arm across her throat.

Her jaws closed upon it.

Pain burst white behind his eyes. The dagger dropped from his fingers.

The bear’s weight pressed him into the earth. Her breath scorched his face. He heard the cub crying overhead, heard his own bones creak, heard stones shifting beneath his heels as he pushed and pushed and gained nothing.

His free hand searched the grass.

Needles. Root. Stone.

Dagger.

His fingers closed on the hilt.

“Allah—who sees mothers and fools—judge kindly!”

He drove the blade upward beneath the bear’s ribs.

Once.

Again.

The jaws tightened until the world darkened.

A third time.

The bear gave a great coughing sigh. Her weight sagged. Mortazu twisted aside as she collapsed against the trunk, slid down it, and lay half across his legs.

For a long while nothing moved except the fir’s upper branches, nodding in the wind.

Mortazu wriggled free. He crawled several yards and sat with his back against a stone. His arm bled through the sash. His shoulder and back burned. One ear seemed full of bees. He expected the bear to rise, but she did not.

The cub descended a little.

“Wheet?”

Mortazu bowed his head.

“Poor Bibi,” he murmured. “Brave Bibi.”

There was no triumph in him. He had fought men in tribal quarrels and laughed afterward; he had seen wolves killed in winter and praised the marksman. But this dead creature had stood between danger and her young exactly as Azeena would stand, exactly as he himself had tried to stand. Only the language between them had failed.

The sun had crossed the centre of the sky.

That sight struck him harder than the bear.

“Ghuzni!”

Mortazu lurched upright. The plateau heaved beneath him. He tore strips from what remained of his shirt, packed the wounds as best he could, bound his arm and retrieved the dagger. Then he whistled for the mare.

No answer.

He whistled again, employing the special note that ordinarily brought her at a gallop from half a mile away.

A faint bell sounded near the southern ridge.

Presently the bay’s head appeared beyond a boulder. She watched him with the pious innocence of a mullah’s donkey.

Mortazu pointed at the dead bear.

“Behold what thy courage accomplished.”

The mare approached sideways, snorting. Her saddle had slipped, one rein was torn, and foam whitened her neck, but she was sound.

Above them the cub began to come down, hindquarters first, clutching the bark. It reached a lower bough, lost confidence, and hugged the trunk.

Mortazu tightened the saddle girth with one hand. He mounted, nearly fainted, dismounted, and stood breathing through his teeth.

“Leave it,” he told himself.

The cub peeped.

“Allah made bears before He made Mortazu Khan. He will arrange matters.”

The cub peeped again.

Mortazu looked west, toward Ghuzni. Every moment was a coin dropped into a bottomless well. He looked east, toward Sharak and Azeena.

Then he looked at the dead mother.

“Thou hast caused me sufficient trouble,” he said to her. “Do not now send thy child to pull at my conscience.”

The bear remained inconsiderately silent.

Mortazu climbed the fir. With one arm useless and his back bleeding, he made a spectacle that would have delighted every boy in Sharak. He gripped with knees and heels, embraced the trunk, slid, cursed bark, ancestry, gravity and cubs, then climbed again.

The cub retreated until a branch-tip bowed under its weight.

“Come, nephew,” Mortazu coaxed. “I am thy father’s oldest friend.”

The cub hissed.

“Ungrateful plum-pudding!”

He caught it by the loose skin behind its neck. The branch cracked. Man and cub descended together in a shower of needles, Mortazu scraping most of his remaining skin upon the way.

At the ground the cub bit his thumb.

“Excellent! Thy manners are inherited.”

He wrapped it in his torn choga, leaving only the head outside, and tied the bundle before him on the saddle. The cub wriggled, squealed and finally clutched the saddle pommel with all four paws.

Mortazu mounted once more.

The mare disliked the smell of bear and danced in circles. Mortazu, who had circled enough for one lifetime, spoke to her in a voice of such dreadful calm that she decided to go straight.

They crossed the plateau.

Behind them the three firs stood dark against the sky, and beneath the nearest lay the brown mother among crushed gentians. Ahead, the path dropped toward the tawny plains, the poplar lines, the domes and crowded bazar of Ghuzni. Somewhere there was Doctor Meredith, with his black bag, his outrageous tongue and his clever, merciful hands.

Mortazu pressed his heels to the mare.

“Fly, daughter! There are now two children depending upon us.”

Down they went through shale and thorn, through the narrowing gorge where the stream shouted at their hoofs. The cub bounced against Mortazu’s breast and complained. Blood from his wounded arm ran in a thin thread along the mare’s shoulder, bright in the declining sun.

“Azeena will scold me,” he said.

The cub sneezed.

“She will say, ‘I asked for a hakim. Why have you brought a bear?’”

Far away, beyond ridge after ridge, a pale square shone which might have been a wall of Ghuzni.

Mortazu bent low over the mare’s neck.

“Hurry,” he whispered.

The bells found their voice again. They rang among the stones, frail and furious, while horse, wounded man and orphaned cub raced westward beneath the enormous sky—hurrying toward the doctor, toward Azeena, toward the child not yet born, hurrying while the sun rolled down the Afghan mountains and all the long shadows ran beside them.

### The Bear of the Shibar Pass

At dawn the mountains beyond the Shibar Pass were lavender and gold, but Mortazu Khan had no eyes for their beauty.

He ran eastward with his sheepskin coat flung open and the tail of his blue turban streaming behind him. Loose stones shot from under his rawhide sandals and rattled down the slope as if the mountain mocked his haste. Below, the valleys lay blue with shadow. Above, the Hindu Kush lifted peak beyond peak into the paling sky, each summit touched by sunlight while the villages remained cold and dark.

Ghuzni was still many miles away.

“Run, son of a slow-footed camel,” Mortazu told himself. “Run, or Azeena dies while you admire the scenery.”

At the thought of her he ran harder.

Before sunrise he had left her in the women’s room of his father’s mud-walled house, her black hair wet against her face, her hands twisting the quilt. Old Bibi Gul, the village midwife, had knelt between her knees with an expression that Mortazu did not like. She had burnt herbs, muttered prayers, warmed oil, ordered water, then sent everyone out except the women. An hour later she had come to the courtyard.

“The child will not come.”

“Then persuade it.”

“I have persuaded children into the world since your beard was softer than flour. This one does not listen.”

Mortazu had stared at her.

“What must be done?”

“Fetch the English doctor from Ghuzni.”

There was no horse in the village swift enough for the broken mountain path. Mortazu’s own gray mare had gone lame the week before, and the mullah’s pony was a round-bellied philosopher who considered speed an insult. So Mortazu had buckled on his knife, stuffed bread into his sash, kissed Azeena’s burning forehead, and set out on foot.

Now, as the sun climbed, he followed a goat track through rust-colored bracken. Blue gentians stared up between stones. Dwarf juniper scratched his leggings, and resinous wind came down from the fir woods smelling of snow, earth and distant rain.

He knew every turn. Two miles to the black cliff; three more to the shepherds’ spring; then the long descent to the Ghuzni road. If Allah put wings on his heels, he could reach the doctor before noon and return by night.

If the doctor came.

If Azeena lived.

If the child—

He would not complete that thought.

“It is a son,” he said aloud. “A loud son with the appetite of ten wolves. He is waiting only to cause trouble. Therefore I must hurry and welcome him properly.”

A marmot whistled from a boulder.

“You laugh?” Mortazu called. “May your children all be born with the faces of tax collectors!”

The marmot vanished. Mortazu grinned despite himself and ran on.

The track entered a stand of mountain firs where the light became green and cold. Their trunks rose straight and red-brown from a floor of needles, moss and fallen cones. No birds sang there. Even the wind moved softly, combing the upper branches while the earth below held its breath.

Mortazu noticed the silence.

He slowed.

A patch of moss had been ripped open beside the path. In the black soil were five deep marks, then five more. The prints were broad as dinner platters. A rotten stump had been torn apart, and pale grubs writhed in the splinters.

“Bear,” he murmured.

His hand went to the hilt of his choora, the long Khyber knife tucked into his sash. It was a good blade, narrow at the point and heavy along the back, but a knife was an argument best offered to a man. Against a bear it was little more than a shining tooth.

He listened.

Nothing.

The Ghuzni road lay beyond the wood. To turn aside would cost an hour, perhaps two. Azeena had no hours to spare.

Mortazu went forward, stepping lightly.

Twenty paces farther on, a dead branch cracked behind him.

He turned.

The bear stood between two firs.

She was enormous—not black, as she had first appeared among the shadows, but blue-gray along the shoulders, with brown fur matted beneath her belly. Her small eyes shone under a pale brow. She raised her head and sniffed, lips twitching. A string of saliva hung from her jaw.

Mortazu backed one step.

“Peace, Bibi,” he said. “I am a married man on urgent business. You have your road; I have mine.”

The bear swung her head.

He moved sideways.

She moved with him.

Then she charged.

There was no warning roar, no theatrical rising upon the hind legs. One moment she stood beneath the firs; the next she came low and swift, paws drumming the earth, fur rippling over her shoulders.

Mortazu leapt behind the nearest tree.

The bear struck the trunk.

Whump!

Bark burst outward. The fir trembled from roots to crown, dropping a shower of needles. Mortazu spun around it while a paw as large as a shovel swept after him and missed his back by the width of a finger.

“Daughter of an avalanche!”

The bear wheeled. For so huge a creature she turned horribly fast, digging her claws into the moss and flinging up black soil. Mortazu went the opposite way, keeping the trunk between them.

She lunged right.

He darted left.

She reversed.

He reversed.

Round the fir they went, the bear snorting, Mortazu’s sandals sliding over needles. He heard her claws score the bark. He smelled her rank breath. Once her muzzle appeared so close around the trunk that he struck at it instinctively. The knife sliced her lip.

She recoiled with a roar.

“Wah! You see?” Mortazu shouted. “Go away, grandmother! I am made of bone and bad temper.”

Blood brightened the fur around her mouth. She licked it, puzzled. Then her ears flattened.

The second charge was more furious.

She came around the tree instead of at it, swiping as she ran. Mortazu circled, circled, the fir rough beneath his left palm, his knife held low in his right. Her paw tore the hem from his coat. He stabbed at her foreleg and felt the blade enter, but not deeply. She bellowed and flung herself backward, sitting almost like a scolded child.

For one absurd instant Mortazu wanted to laugh.

Then the bear surged up again.

He ran around the trunk.

Again she followed. Again and again and again—right, left, right—each rush crushing fern and shredding moss, each turn sending cones popping beneath her weight. The wood, lately so silent, filled with panting, snarling, scraping, the slap of Mortazu’s feet, the harsh cough of his breath.

The sun climbed between the branches.

Ghuzni waited beyond the hills.

Azeena waited farther behind him.

“A little faster, husband,” he imagined her saying. “There is no need to trouble yourself. I am only dying.”

“I am occupied!” he gasped aloud.

The bear roared.

“Yes, and this lady objects to my leaving!”

He feinted to the right, darted left and tried to break away toward the track.

The bear cut him off.

Her speed astonished him. She slid down the slope through dead needles, struck a sapling aside and appeared before him in a tumble of fur and teeth. Mortazu turned so sharply that his sandal strap snapped. He fell against the fir, ducked beneath a swinging paw and felt claws plough across his shoulder.

His coat opened like rotten cloth.

Heat ran down his back.

He stabbed upward. The knife sank into the loose flesh below the bear’s neck. She jerked away, wrenching the hilt from his hand, and the blade flew into the bracken.

Mortazu stared after it.

The bear stared at him.

“Now,” he whispered, “we are both offended.”

She rushed.

He sprang upward and caught the lowest branch of the fir. His bare foot scraped the trunk; his broken sandal dropped away. With a heave that tore fire through his wounded shoulder, he dragged himself onto the branch.

The bear reared below.

Her claws ripped through his remaining sandal and grazed his heel. Mortazu climbed higher, kicking among the boughs until he reached a fork perhaps twelve feet above the ground. There he clung, breathing in short, painful gulps.

The bear dropped to all fours and paced around the trunk.

Blood dripped from her mouth and neck. Blood ran from Mortazu’s shoulder to his elbow, gathered at his fingertips and fell in slow dark beads.

Plip.

Plip.

The bear watched each drop strike the moss.

“Go,” Mortazu told her. “You have won. I shall remain in this tree until I grow cones.”

She sat down.

Mortazu cursed her ancestry back through thirty generations. He accused her father of stealing onions, her mother of marrying a pig, her grandfathers of cheating blind beggars in the Kabul bazaar. The bear listened with her head tilted. When he paused for breath, she gave a low moan.

“A reply? Your eloquence is remarkable.”

The bear put her forepaws against the trunk and climbed.

Mortazu’s humour vanished.

She came slowly, her claws biting through the bark, her body hugging the fir. The whole tree shuddered beneath her weight. Mortazu climbed, branches lashing his face. Resin stuck to his palms. Needles entered his torn shoulder. He went higher until the trunk narrowed and swayed.

Below, the bear climbed on.

“Allah preserve me,” he muttered.

The upper branches bent in the wind. Through gaps in the foliage he saw the world spread below: ochre ridges, gray ravines, distant snow burning white against the deep blue sky. Somewhere beyond those ridges Azeena lay with her teeth clenched, fighting for one breath and then another.

The bear reached the fork where he had rested.

Mortazu climbed farther.

The trunk bowed.

For a moment man and beast hung above the mountains, both breathing hard, both stained with blood.

Then the bear’s weight betrayed her. A branch cracked beneath her hindquarters. She scrambled, bark flying, and slid downward with a startled, almost indignant grunt. She struck the lower fork, bounced, clutched at the trunk and dropped heavily to the ground.

Thud!

The earth shook.

Mortazu laughed until his shoulder punished him.

“Excellent climbing, Bibi! Shall I summon the birds to applaud?”

The bear rose, shook herself and looked up.

There was hurt in her roar—anger, certainly, but something else, a complaining note like that of a child denied a sweet. She circled the tree once, then settled again at its foot.

She would wait.

Mortazu looked at the sun.

Nearly half the morning gone.

He could not remain. Yet to descend was to place himself beneath those claws. His knife lay somewhere under the torn bracken, glinting perhaps three strides from the trunk. If he could reach it—

He tested a branch extending over the slope. It bent but held. Beyond its tip stood another fir, six feet away. Too far to jump safely, especially with one shoulder failing.

Six feet or Azeena.

Mortazu edged outward.

The branch dipped. Needles whispered around him. Below, the bear rose and followed, walking beneath him.

He crawled farther. The branch bowed like a drawn bow. The neighboring fir seemed to move closer, then farther as the wind swayed both trees.

“Bismillah.”

He stood unsteadily.

The bear gathered herself below.

Mortazu jumped.

His hands struck the second tree. One caught a branch; the other slipped. His body swung against the trunk, crushing breath from him. Pain burst through his shoulder in a white flash. For an instant he hung by one arm over the bear’s open jaws.

Then he hooked a knee across the branch and rolled onto it.

The bear rose beneath him, snapping.

“Too slow!”

Mortazu descended the far side of the tree, using the trunk as a shield. The instant his bare feet touched earth, he ran for the bracken.

Behind him came the pounding of paws.

He saw the knife—a stripe of silver beneath a fern.

He dived.

The bear’s paw struck his thigh and spun him across the ground. Stones and sky whirled together. He closed his hand around the knife as he rolled, came up on one knee and slashed blindly.

The blade opened the bear’s nose.

She recoiled with a coughing roar. Mortazu staggered toward the first fir. His left leg dragged; four parallel cuts burned across his thigh. He reached the trunk just before she charged again.

Round they went.

But now the dance had changed.

Mortazu’s bare feet were bleeding. His wounded leg answered slowly. His breath scraped like a saw in his chest. The bear, though cut in three places, seemed fashioned from rage and sinew. She rushed, braked, wheeled, rushed again. The fir became slick beneath Mortazu’s bloody hand. Torn moss gathered underfoot. Once he slipped and saved himself only by driving the knife into the bark.

Minutes stretched until they seemed longer than years.

Circling, circling.

The sun looked down through the branches, bright and indifferent.

Mortazu began to see darkness at the edges of things. He knew what would happen. His leg would fail. He would stumble. The bear would strike him down, and somewhere beyond the pass Azeena would wait for a husband who had become meat beneath a fir tree.

“No,” he whispered.

The bear charged.

Mortazu sprang aside. Her shoulder slammed the trunk, and she reeled.

He could have stabbed behind her ear. The knife was raised. The place was open.

Yet before he struck, a sound came from above.

“Wheet—wheet!”

Mortazu froze.

The bear froze also.

A small shape moved among the branches of the first fir. It was no bird. A round blue-gray face peered down between sprays of needles, ears wide, eyes black and frightened.

A cub.

Mortazu stared upward.

The cub gave another thin cry.

The mother bear lifted her muzzle. Her whole body changed. The rage went out of her stance; the great shoulders sagged. She answered with a soft, throaty murmur, and the cub crept farther into the branches.

Mortazu lowered the knife.

So. That was why she had attacked without hunger, why she had guarded the track, why she had waited beneath the tree and endured wounds rather than retreat. Her cub had climbed there at his approach. She had not been hunting him.

She had been keeping him from her child.

From Azeena came the memory of a cry in the dark room.

The child will not come.

He looked at the bear, and in her small fierce eyes he saw no monster. He saw a mother whose world was hidden in a tree.

“Ah, Bibi,” he said quietly. “Then we are two fools on the same business.”

The bear watched him.

He could not leave while she lived. If he tried to run, she would overtake him. If he climbed again, the sun would fall and Azeena might die. There was no peace to be made between them now. Too much blood lay on the moss.

Mortazu tightened his grip on the knife.

“I bear you no anger,” he said. “But my child also waits.”

He stepped away from the tree into the open.

The bear gave a warning cough.

“No more dancing.”

He spread his feet, though one leg trembled. The knife he held close to his body, point upward. His heart hammered so violently that the mountains seemed to pulse with it.

The bear came forward.

Not at once. Not wildly. She advanced three paces, testing him, blood dark along her muzzle. Mortazu stood still.

“Come, mother.”

She rose on her hind legs.

She towered above him, huge against the sunlight, forepaws lifted, claws curved and black. For a breath she seemed to contain all the strength of the mountains—the falling rock, the winter storm, the flood tearing cedar roots from a gorge.

Then she fell upon him.

Mortazu stepped inside the sweep of her paw.

The claws struck his ribs and ripped downward. At the same instant he drove the knife beneath her foreleg with both hands.

The blade went in to the hilt.

Hot blood poured over his wrists.

The bear’s weight crushed him. She roared once, a sound so vast that the firs flung it from trunk to trunk and the cliffs returned it broken. Mortazu twisted the knife deeper, seeking the heart.

There was no hatred in the thrust. Only necessity. Only sorrow. Only Azeena.

The bear shuddered.

Her jaws closed on the shoulder already torn, but without force. Her breath rushed hot across his cheek. Then she sank sideways, carrying him down beneath her.

For a while Mortazu knew nothing but fur, darkness and the iron smell of blood.

He clawed his way free.

The bear lay on the moss, one forepaw curved toward her chest. Her eyes were open. The wind stirred the fur at her neck.

Above, the cub cried.

Mortazu knelt. His hands shook. Blood streamed from his side and thigh; his coat hung in ribbons. He touched the bear’s broad head.

“May the Maker judge between us,” he whispered. “You fought well.”

The cub cried again.

Mortazu looked at the sun. Time—time was running like water through broken fingers.

He tore strips from his coat, bound his ribs and thigh, and packed moss beneath the cloth to slow the bleeding. Every knot made sparks burst before his eyes. Then he recovered his knife, wiped it on the earth and put it back into his sash.

He might have gone then.

Instead he looked up.

The cub clung to a branch, no larger than a shepherd’s dog, shivering. Without its mother it would starve, or fall to wolves, or be taken by winter. Mortazu sighed.

“Do not make me climb another tree.”

The cub answered, “Wheet!”

“Your arguments resemble your mother’s.”

He climbed slowly. Twice his wounded leg slipped. The cub backed toward the end of the branch, showing tiny teeth. Mortazu spoke to it as he would to a frightened child.

“Peace, little mountain prince. I have done you a great wrong, and I cannot mend it. Come, then. You shall bite someone else.”

He took off what remained of his turban and cast the cloth over the cub. It struggled, squealing and clawing, but he gathered it against his chest and descended.

On the ground he wrapped it securely, leaving its nose uncovered. The cub smelled of fir needles, milk and wild earth. Its heart beat against him like a trapped bird.

Mortazu glanced once more at the mother bear.

Then he turned eastward.

He could no longer run properly, but he ran nevertheless—limping, stumbling, half falling down the slope. Each stride opened fire in his wounds. Blood soaked the bandages. The cub bounced beneath his arm and complained in thin, indignant whistles.

“Hush,” Mortazu panted. “We go for a doctor.”

The trees thinned. Sunlight poured across the hillside. Far below, the road to Ghuzni shone pale beside a river, and beyond it rose the dusty roofs, minarets and crowded bazaars of the town.

“Azeena waits,” he told the cub. “And perhaps a boy waits also. You shall grow together. You shall teach him to climb trees, and he shall teach you not to attack honest travelers.”

The cub sneezed.

“Yes, yes. Your mother was brave. I shall tell him that too.”

Mortazu Khan gathered the last of his strength and hurried down toward Ghuzni, bleeding under the brilliant mountain sky, carrying one mother’s orphan to save another mother’s child.

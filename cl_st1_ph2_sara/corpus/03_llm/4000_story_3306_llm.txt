Once upon a time, in the parish of Wendron, there lived a farmer called Joby Teague, whose two eyes could never agree which road they were travelling. His right eye looked towards Helston, and his left towards Penryn; and if he stared straight at a man, that man would glance behind him to see who else Joby was speaking to.

It came about in this way.

When Joby was six months old, his father had a new fiddle, his mother had a red petticoat, and his seven aunts came over from Crowan to see which made the greater show. After supper the chairs were pushed back, and all the family joined hands and danced round the kitchen table. Round they went, and round again, and still round, until the candles leaned in their sockets from watching them.

Little Joby lay in his cradle, following them with his eyes. But one aunt was broad and slow, another thin and nimble, and his father twice went the wrong way. Joby’s eyes did their best to keep count of everybody, and at last one stopped upon Aunt Loveday while the other followed Aunt Prudence. There they remained.

His mother said they would right themselves by morning. His father said they would right themselves by Christmas. The doctor in Helston said Joby must be kept from round objects and charged half a crown. Yet morning came, and Christmas came, and many Christmases after it, and Joby’s eyes were as contrary as two goats at a gap.

When he grew up, this was troublesome, especially in farming. If he ploughed south, the furrow generally turned east; and once, meaning to harrow a field belonging to himself, he harrowed two acres of his neighbour’s barley and was thanked very coldly for it. When he threw corn to the hens, the ducks got it. When he courted Mally Rowe, he looked so steadily at her elder sister that the poor maid ordered a wedding gown before the mistake was discovered.

Still, Joby married Mally in the end, for she was a sensible woman and could tell which eye he meant.

“Joby,” said she one evening, “you must have summat done. Yesterday you put the cow in the pantry and carried the milk-pail out to pasture.”

“To be sure,” said Joby. “But the pantry door and the shippen door be much of a likeness, viewed separate.”

“You shall go to Tommy Warne.”

Now Tommy Warne lived west of Grinnidge—or so he said, though no mapmaker had ever found the place—and was accounted the cleverest conjurer between Wendron and the sea. He could discover stolen goods, charm warts, cure smoky chimneys, and tell a maid the colour of her future husband’s Sunday stockings.

To tell truth, Tommy’s fame began through a pair of pack-saddles.

A carrier had hidden them under Tommy’s hayrick, intending to fetch them after dark. Before he could do so, the saddles were missed, and their owner came to Tommy.

“Can you tell where they be?”

Tommy knew nothing of conjuring then, but he knew where the saddles were. So he shut his eyes, waved a poker, muttered, “East nor west, high nor low,” and sent the man to the hayrick.

From that day forth, Tommy was a magician. Whenever he knew anything, it was conjuring; whenever he knew nothing, it was deep conjuring; and if a cure failed, he said the moon had been standing edgeways.

Joby went to him next morning.

“Morning, Tommy,” said he. “My wife says you’m to cure my eyes.”

“I can,” said Tommy, though he had never cured so much as a sleepy ferret. “But ’tis a powerful matter.”

“I’ve brought six eggs.”

“For six eggs,” said Tommy, “I can cure one eye.”

Joby considered. “Couldn’t you divide the conjurin’ equal between ’em?”

Tommy sighed, as clever men do when asked to work cheaply.

“Very well. Tonight you must sleep atop your four-poster, not within it. Put the pillows at the foot, the blankets beneath the mattress, and the bolster crossways. Then turn every sheet three times widdershins.”

“What way is that?”

“Contrary to the sun.”

“Supposing there’s cloud?”

“Contrary to the clock, then. And mind—you mustn’t wake till you’ve come home.”

“But I shall be home already.”

“Not after the conjurin’ begins.”

Joby thanked him kindly and carried the instructions to Mally. She said they sounded foolish, but no more foolish than paying doctors, so they arranged the bed backward, bottom upward, foot to head, and all things widdershins. Then Joby climbed atop the tester, where he lay as comfortably as a loaf upon a cupboard.

He had scarcely shut his eyes when somebody called, “Joby, you land-lobster! Come down out of that!”

There stood his brother Dan, who had gone to sea twenty-three years before and had never written, chiefly because, as he now explained, the Atlantic postman was a careless fellow.

Dan wore a captain’s coat, three brass telescopes, and a hat with enough cord upon it to rig a pilchard boat.

“Where’ve you been?” asked Joby.

“Everywhere wet. Come along.”

They walked and walked, and presently came to Penryn Fair. There were lamps, striped horses, swing-boats, gingerbread queens, shooting galleries, drums, trumpets, and a steam organ playing three tunes together. Dan led Joby to a painted boat hung between two poles.

“Get in.”

“I’d rather have a turn upon the horses.”

“Horses don’t cross oceans.”

Before Joby could ask which ocean, the boat swung forward, swung back, and swung forward again. The fair fell away beneath them. The lamps became stars, the sawdust became foam, and the steam organ gave one tremendous groan and turned into a gale of wind.

The swing-boat was now a ship, with sails above, deep water below, and no Penryn anywhere.

“Where be we bound?” cried Joby.

“South-west by down-under!” shouted Captain Dan. “Keep her widdershins!”

The ship began sailing in circles, contrary to the sun. Round and round she went until Joby’s cap flew off and his right eye, which had always looked inward, swung outward instead.

“Dan!” he cried. “My squint be changed over!”

“A sailor always leaves a thing different from how he found it,” said Dan.

They sailed three days before breakfast and two Thursdays after dinner. Once they met a shark, who opened his mouth so wide that Dan mistook it for a harbour and walked halfway down its throat. Discovering his error, he came out again in a huff and complained there were no proper landing steps.

At last Joby saw a white line stretched across the blue sea.

“What’s that painted there?”

“The Equator.”

“I thought ’twas only in schoolbooks.”

“Most schoolbook things be stored somewhere.”

The ship bumped against the line and stopped. Dan leaned over with a knife and scraped it. The Equator gave a high, clear note like a wet finger circling a glass.

“Musical,” said Joby.

“All proper boundaries sing if you rub ’em.”

They tried to cross, but the ship’s keel caught upon the line. So Dan spat on his thumb and rubbed out a piece large enough for the vessel to pass through. Unfortunately, the sea began leaking through the gap.

“Best move briskly,” he said.

The ship swung widdershins once more, and Joby’s outward-looking eye turned inward. His squint was reversed completely: the eye that had watched Helston now watched Penryn, and the other changed places with it.

“That’s no cure,” said Joby. “You’ve only shifted the inconvenience.”

“You farmers be particular,” said Dan. “At sea, if a hole changes sides, we call the ship repaired.”

Just then a bell rang, though there was no bell aboard. Captain Dan clapped on his hat.

“My watch below!”

And with that he vanished, taking the ship with him.

Joby found himself sitting upon the ocean, which held him up well enough so long as he did not think about it. He called for Dan, but received no answer. He called for Mally, but she was farther off. Then he called for Tommy Warne, but thought better of it, since Tommy would likely charge another six eggs.

Presently something bright came drifting over the water. It was a great gilt weathercock, lying on its side with its claws stretched stiffly and its tail feathers tangled in seaweed.

Joby caught it by the beak.

“Leave go!” said the weathercock. “A gentleman cannot breathe through his tail.”

“Beg pardon,” said Joby, and held it by one leg instead. “Be you from Wendron Church?”

“I have stood there these hundred and forty years, in rain, hail, sermons, and repairs. Last Tuesday’s west wind blew me off.”

“Tuesday? ’Tis Thursday.”

“Not where I’ve been.”

Joby pulled away the seaweed, and the weathercock shook itself until drops flew like sovereigns in the moonlight.

“You have rendered a service,” it said ceremoniously. “Name your reward.”

“Home,” said Joby. “And straight eyes, if ’tis no trouble.”

“Home is easy. Eyes depend upon the wind.”

The weathercock grew to the size of a pony, and Joby climbed upon its back. With three flaps they rose from the sea. They flew over ships, clouds, France by mistake, and a miner coming up from below who had dug too far and was surprised to see them.

At last the weathercock cried, “Wendron ahead!”

The church tower stood beneath them, grey in the early light. But instead of landing, the bird began circling it widdershins.

Round once went Joby.

Round twice.

Round nine times, and then nine more.

His head swam, his coat-tails stood straight out, and his two eyes chased one another until neither knew which was ahead. The weathercock spun faster. Joby saw Helston, then Penryn, then Helston, then the back of his own neck—or believed he did.

“Enough!” he shouted.

“One turn more!” cried the bird.

They whirled round the steeple, and with a small click, like a latch falling into place, Joby’s eyes came straight.

The weathercock settled upon its iron spindle.

“There,” it said. “Kindly do not mention the Equator. Church people dislike foreign improvements.”

Joby looked down. Never had the world appeared so singular. One roof stayed one roof; one tree had but one trunk; and the road went where it seemed to go. Yet in the churchyard below he saw a wedding party gathering.

There was Mally in her best bonnet. Beside her stood Tommy Warne in a blue coat, smiling as if six eggs had hatched into a farm. The parson waited at the church door.

“The villain!” cried Joby. “He’ve conjured me away and means to marry my wife!”

“You have been gone some while,” observed the weathercock.

“How long?”

“Dream time or clock time?”

“Whichever gives me most hurry.”

Joby slid down the steeple to the great church clock. Its hands stood at five minutes to twelve.

“If I turn ’em back,” he reasoned, “the wedding cannot begin.”

So he seized the minute hand and dragged it widdershins. It resisted.

“Come round, you brass rascal!”

He twisted harder. From inside the clock came Mally’s voice.

“Joby! Leave go my arm!”

The tower vanished. The sea vanished. Captain Dan, the fair, the Equator, and the weathercock all went out like candles.

Joby awoke atop the four-poster, gripping Mally’s wrist and turning it contrary to the clock. She gave him such a push that he rolled from the tester into the backward-arranged blankets.

“You great dreaming turnip!” she cried. “Are you trying to screw my hand off?”

“I were stopping your wedding.”

“My what?”

“To Tommy Warne.”

“I’d sooner marry the smoky chimney he cured last month—and that still smokes.”

Mally struck a light. Then she stopped, holding the candle before Joby’s face.

“Look at me.”

“I am lookin’.”

“No, Joby. Look proper.”

He looked at her, and for the first time both eyes met upon the same object and remained there peaceably. Mally moved the candle left; both eyes followed. She moved it right; both followed again. Then she set it on the table and kissed him, though she first made certain he was looking at the correct woman.

Next morning Joby ploughed a furrow so straight that three neighbours came to measure it. Tommy Warne claimed the cure was his, of course, and said the six eggs had been unusually favourable to magic. The weathercock was found back upon Wendron tower, though nobody remembered replacing it, and from that day it always pointed a little contrary to the wind.

As for Joby’s eyes, they remained as straight as yours or mine. Yet he would never again sleep atop a four-poster, sail south of Penryn Fair, or turn anything widdershins—not even a roasting apple—for fear of meeting the Equator on the way round.

## William and the Kidnap

William stood in the hall wearing an ancient belt, two wooden pistols, a sheath-knife made from the handle of a broken clothes-brush, and an expression of stern and rather gloomy heroism.

It was Wednesday afternoon—his half-holiday—and the Outlaws were to meet at the old barn beyond Jenks’s field. Important matters were to be discussed. Ginger had discovered a hole under the hedge of Mr. Bott’s orchard. Henry had obtained three candles and part of a map which, though actually representing the water-pipes beneath his house, could easily be adapted to the purposes of buried treasure. Douglas was bringing sausages.

William himself had contributed a cake taken from the larder. He did not regard this as stealing. He regarded it as requisitioning supplies from a hostile population.

He had reached the front door when his mother called him.

“William!”

He stopped.

There are occasions when the bravest man may detect doom in a single syllable. William detected it now.

He opened the drawing-room door cautiously. His mother sat by the fire with Mrs. Butler, who was a large, cheerful woman possessing a small, cheerful baby. The small, cheerful baby sat on Mrs. Butler’s knee and hit her repeatedly with a woollen rabbit.

“Oh, there you are, dear,” said William’s mother.

William said nothing. He felt that everything depended upon saying nothing.

“Mrs. Butler has to go to the dentist.”

William continued to say nothing, but his expression became that of one who has heard the distant drums of an advancing army.

“And Nurse is away this afternoon.”

Still William said nothing.

“So I said I was sure you’d be delighted to take Baby out in his pram for an hour.”

William’s jaw fell open.

“Me?” he said at last. “Me? Me take a baby out in a pram?”

Mrs. Butler laughed.

“He simply loves babies, doesn’t he?” she said.

“No,” said William.

“William!” said his mother.

“I’m simply statin’ a fact,” said William with dignity.

He had lately heard his elder brother Robert use this phrase during an argument with their father. Robert had used it with the crushing superiority of eighteen. William had been much impressed by it and had employed it ever since in situations to which it bore increasingly little relation.

“You can easily take him round the village,” said his mother. “It’s such a beautiful afternoon.”

“I’ve gotter meeting.”

“You can have your meeting another day.”

“It’s a very important meeting.”

“I’m sure it is, dear.”

“We’re goin’ to decide whether to hold up the London train or capture Mr. Bott.”

“Then perhaps it’s just as well for Mr. Bott and the London train that you should be occupied otherwise.”

William gazed at her in speechless indignation.

The family, as represented by his mother, Mrs. Butler, and the baby, had closed round him like the Fates. The baby leaned forward and struck him on the nose with the rabbit.

“Da!” said the baby.

“He likes you,” said Mrs. Butler emotionally.

William looked at it. He had never seen anything that looked less like liking anyone. Its face was round and pink and damp. It stared at him with the cold, unwinking concentration of a judge about to pronounce sentence.

“I don’t know anythin’ about babies,” he said desperately.

“You only have to wheel him,” said his mother. “He’ll probably go to sleep.”

The baby gave a piercing yell, seized William’s belt, and pulled one of the wooden pistols to the floor.

“There!” said Mrs. Butler. “He wants to play with you.”

“He’s broke my revolver,” said William.

“He’s only a tiny baby.”

“Well, it’s broke just the same. I’m simply statin’ a fact.”

Ten minutes later William walked down the road pushing the pram.

The horrible thing demanded of him had actually come to pass.

He went with the slow and bitter tread of a martyr proceeding to execution. The wooden pistols had been removed by his mother on the ground that they might hurt Baby. His knife had been removed by Mrs. Butler on the ground that Baby might swallow it. The cake, concealed beneath his coat, remained to him, but it was small consolation.

Every person he met stopped to admire the baby.

“What a dear little thing!”

“Isn’t he good!”

“What a splendid little nursemaid!”

At the last remark William’s face assumed the expression traditionally associated with Medusa’s victims.

“I’m not a nursemaid,” he said. “I’m an outlaw.”

The speaker laughed and passed on.

The baby sat upright beneath the hood and regarded the countryside with a bland and predatory interest. It had already removed one sock and thrown it into the road. William had retrieved it. It had then removed the other sock and attempted to eat it. William had retrieved that also.

“Look here,” he said severely, stopping the pram. “You’ve gotter stop messin’ about. I’ve got important things to do. I don’t want to be wheeling you round all afternoon.”

“Dad—dad—dad—dad,” said the baby.

“I’m not your dad.”

“Dad!”

“I’m simply statin’ a fact.”

The road before him curved towards the village. To the left a lane led across the fields towards the Outlaws’ barn. William stared at it.

The Goddess of Adventure beckoned down that lane. She beckoned in the form of a muddy cart-track, a broken stile, and the distant roof of a disused barn.

William resisted her for almost half a minute.

Then an idea came to him.

It was an idea so magnificent that he stopped dead in the road. His humiliation vanished. His shoulders squared. His eyes brightened. He was no longer William Brown, unwilling nursemaid. He was Red William, Scourge of the Countryside, bearing away a kidnapped infant for ransom.

He wheeled the pram swiftly down the lane.

At the edge of the field stood an iron railing beside a cattle-pond. William tied the pram to it with a piece of string. The barn lay beyond two stiles and a ditch, and the pram could not conveniently be taken farther. He lifted out the baby.

The baby was heavier than it looked. It also appeared to contain more movable parts than any one creature could reasonably require. William held it under the arms. Its garments rose towards its neck, its legs waved freely, and it seized a handful of his hair.

“Come on,” he gasped. “You’re kidnapped.”

“Dad!” said the baby, with satisfaction.

The Outlaws were already assembled.

Henry stood beside a heap of sticks arranging the three candles. Ginger was cutting a loaf with a rusty pocket-knife. Douglas knelt by the fire, blackening the sausages. “Cooking” would have been too euphemistic a term for the process. The sausages were suspended over smoke until they became black on one side and grey on the other.

All three turned as William entered.

There was a silence.

Then Henry said, “Wotcher brought that for?”

“It’s a kidnap,” said William, placing the baby on a sack.

“A wot?”

“A kidnap. I’ve kidnapped it.”

The Outlaws looked at the baby.

The baby looked at the Outlaws. It seemed prepared to accept them as a regrettable but possibly entertaining feature of its afternoon.

“Where’d you kidnap it from?” said Ginger.

“From its mother,” said William.

“Did she see you?”

“No.”

This was not strictly accurate, but William felt that romantic necessities entitled him to ignore minor domestic details.

“What’re you goin’ to do with it?” said Douglas.

“Hold it for ransom, of course.”

“How much?”

William had not considered this.

“Hundreds of pounds,” he said.

“Who from?”

“Its mother.”

“Has she got hundreds of pounds?”

“She might have.”

Mrs. Butler’s hat and general appearance had not suggested vast wealth, but the grandeur of the enterprise demanded a wealthy victim.

“You’ve gotter send a note,” said Henry. “All kidnappers send notes.”

“I know that.”

“Have you sent one?”

“I’m goin’ to.”

“Wot’ll you say?”

William frowned.

“‘Bring five hundred pounds in gold to the blasted oak at midnight,’” he said slowly, “‘or you’ll never see your baby again.’”

“There isn’t a blasted oak,” said Ginger.

“There’s that tree struck by lightning near the pond,” said Douglas.

“That’s an elm,” said Henry.

“Well, we can blast it,” said William.

“How?”

There was another silence.

The baby, finding the discussion tedious, crawled from the sack towards the feast. The feast consisted of the cake, the loaf, six sausages, three cold potatoes, two dumplings removed by Ginger from yesterday’s stew, a bottle of paraffin for the fire, a bag of liquorice, and a dog biscuit which Douglas had brought under the impression that it was a military ration.

“You’ll have to look after it,” said Henry.

“It’s all right,” said William. “It can’t do anythin’.”

At that moment the baby reached the paraffin bottle.

The cork had been insecurely replaced after the lighting of the fire. The baby tipped the bottle over itself, watched the stream of oil with scientific interest, then sat in it.

“Here!” shouted Ginger. “It’s spilt all the paraffin!”

“It’s your kidnap,” said Henry to William.

“Well, you left the bottle there.”

“I didn’t bring the baby.”

“Nor did I,” said Douglas quickly.

“You did,” said Ginger to William.

“I know I did. I’m simply statin’ a fact.”

The baby now possessed a powerful and penetrating smell. Its clothes glistened with oil. It smiled and clapped its hands, thereby spreading the paraffin more evenly.

“You’d better wipe it,” said Henry.

William took out his handkerchief. It was not in an ideal condition for cleansing babies, having recently been used to wrap a dead frog, but it was the only instrument available. He rubbed the baby’s frock with it.

The result was disappointing. The paraffin remained, while the frock acquired broad streaks of mud.

“It’s worse,” said Ginger.

“It’ll wear off,” said William.

“Paraffin doesn’t wear off.”

“It must. Everythin’ wears off sometime.”

The baby crawled away from the handkerchief and put one of the cold potatoes into its mouth. Finding the raw skin unsatisfactory, it removed the potato, crushed it between both hands, and rubbed the fragments over its face.

“Stop it!” said William.

The baby laughed and reached for a dumpling.

William rescued the dumpling. The baby screamed.

“All right, then,” he said hastily, returning it.

The screaming ceased. The baby squeezed the dumpling. Some of it went into its hair, some down the front of its frock, and some into its left ear. The remainder it offered graciously to Henry.

“I don’t want it,” said Henry, retreating.

The baby repented of its generosity and ate it itself.

The Outlaws resumed their culinary operations at a greater distance. William cut the cake into four unequal pieces, retaining the largest on the natural ground that he had supplied it. Henry objected. Ginger supported Henry. Douglas supported William until offered a portion of Henry’s sausage, at which point his political convictions changed.

During this dispute the baby crawled silently upon the cake.

Its first action was to sit on William’s share. Its second was to insert both hands into the middle of Henry’s. Its third was to taste Ginger’s, reconsider the matter, and spit it upon Douglas’s sausage.

There followed a moment of tragic stillness.

William lifted the baby.

The cake rose with it.

“It’s spoilt the whole feast!” said Henry.

“It hasn’t spoilt the loaf,” said William.

As he spoke, the baby kicked the loaf into the fire.

The loaf smouldered quietly beside the sausages.

“It has now,” said Ginger.

A period of righteous indignation followed. Everyone accused William. William accused the paraffin bottle, the dumpling, the insecure nature of the cake, and babies in general. The baby, untroubled by legal argument, sucked its fingers.

“We’ll have to clean it,” said Douglas. “You can’t take it back lookin’ like that.”

William contemplated the captive.

It was indeed altered. The upper part was streaked with mud, oil, potato, dumpling, and cake. The lower part was blackened where it had sat on the barn floor. One cheek had acquired a patch of soot. Its hair stood erect in greasy points.

“We need water,” said William.

“There isn’t any,” said Ginger.

“There’s liquorice water,” said Douglas.

The liquorice had been brought to make a drink. It lay dissolved in a jam-jar of rainwater, producing a liquid of remarkable depth and opacity.

William examined it doubtfully.

“It’s water,” said Henry.

“It’s black.”

“That’s only the liquorice.”

“Liquorice is clean,” said Douglas. “You eat it.”

This logic appeared unassailable.

They stood the baby upright, Douglas supporting it under the arms while William dipped his handkerchief into the liquorice water and applied it to the baby’s face.

The soot spread. The potato spread. The liquorice joined both and gave the whole a rich brown finish. The baby opened its mouth to protest, received a corner of the handkerchief, tasted liquorice, and became suddenly cooperative.

“See?” said Douglas. “It likes it.”

William washed more vigorously. Brown streams ran down the baby’s neck. Its hair became black and adhesive. It clutched the handkerchief, sucked it, and rubbed it judicially across its own nose.

At the end of five minutes it no longer looked merely neglected. It looked like an infant that had spent its short life in a coal-mine and its last hour in a restaurant kitchen.

“P’raps it’ll look better when it dries,” said William.

No one answered.

At that moment there came a noise outside—a lowing, a scrambling, and an excited voice saying, “Come on, you ole thing! Garn! Get on!”

A boy named Hubert Lane appeared in the doorway holding the end of a rope. At the other end of the rope was a cow.

Hubert did not generally belong to the Outlaws. He was sometimes tolerated when he possessed useful information or food. On this occasion he possessed a cow and an expression of triumph.

“I’ve captured it,” he announced.

The cow entered the barn and looked round with large, melancholy eyes.

“Where from?” said William.

“Farmer Jenks’s field.”

“That’s not capturin’,” said Henry. “That’s stealin’.”

“It’s just the same as cattle-rustlers,” said Hubert. “I read about ’em. They drive off cattle an’ hide ’em till there’s a reward.”

“You can’t hide a cow here,” said Ginger.

“Why not?”

“Because it’s a cow.”

Hubert looked at him coldly.

“I’m simply statin’ a fact,” said Ginger, with an irritating glance at William.

The cow advanced into the barn. Hubert, who had imagined himself controlling it, discovered that he was merely attached to it. It paused beside the baby.

The baby wore a small knitted hat tied beneath its chin. The hat had once been white. It was now mottled brown and black, with an ornamental smear of yellow potato near the crown.

The cow sniffed it.

The baby stared up in grave interest.

The cow took the hat into its mouth.

“Hi!” shouted William.

The baby leaned backwards. The ribbons tightened under its chin. William sprang forward and pulled at the hat while Hubert pulled at the cow. Henry, Ginger, and Douglas offered advice.

“Hit it!”

“Don’t hit it! It’ll run!”

“Untie the baby!”

“Untie the cow!”

“Pull harder!”

The hat came free of the baby but not of the cow. The cow chewed twice and swallowed it.

There was silence.

“It’s ate it,” said Douglas.

“I can see it’s ate it,” said William bitterly.

“It’s Henry’s cow,” said Hubert.

“It’s your cow,” said Henry.

“No, it isn’t. I only captured it.”

“Well, I didn’t.”

“It wasn’t my hat,” said Ginger.

They all looked at William.

“It’s your kidnap,” said Henry.

Before William could reply, the cow turned. Its tail swept across the baby’s face with a solid, deliberate thump.

The baby sat down abruptly.

There was a second of astonished silence, then it opened its mouth and screamed.

The scream filled the barn, rose to the rafters, startled pigeons from the roof, and caused the cow to move forward upon the fire. The sticks scattered. A sausage disappeared beneath one hoof. The jam-jar of liquorice water overturned. Smoke rose around the cow’s legs.

The cow lowed. The baby screamed. Hubert shouted. The Outlaws rushed in four directions, each chosen without reference to any of the others.

“Get it out!”

“Get the baby!”

“Get the sausages!”

“Never mind the sausages!”

“It’s standin’ on mine!”

The cow backed into the sack, trod upon the dog biscuit, swung its tail into Douglas’s face, and dragged Hubert through the remains of the feast. Hubert let go of the rope. The cow, freed from the last trace of human influence, walked peacefully out of the barn and began to eat grass.

The baby stopped screaming as suddenly as it had begun.

“Dad,” it said to Douglas.

Douglas, who was on his hands and knees with cake upon one elbow, brightened.

“It called me Dad,” he said.

“It calls everyone Dad,” said William.

“It didn’t call Henry Dad.”

“I don’t want it to,” said Henry.

“Dad—dad—dad,” said the baby to Henry.

Douglas laughed.

Henry suggested that they begin their games.

The original programme had included ambushing a stagecoach, defending the barn against a siege, and discovering treasure. As no stagecoach was available, Ginger represented it by walking past the barn carrying an old sack. William leapt from behind the door and shouted, “Stand and deliver!”

At that instant the baby crawled between his feet. William fell over it, Ginger escaped, and the stagecoach was declared victorious.

They next defended the barn against Hubert, who had returned after tying the cow to a tree. The baby, placed behind a box for safety, discovered a candle and ate half of it. It then crawled through the defenders’ position, pulled down the barricade, and allowed Hubert to enter without opposition.

For treasure-hunting Henry produced his map. William buried an old tobacco-tin containing two buttons, a marble, and a note saying TREASURE in red pencil. While the others searched, the baby found the tobacco-tin, removed it from its hiding-place, and dropped it through a crack in the floor.

Henry accused William of never having buried it. William accused Henry of looking in the wrong place. Ginger accused everyone of wasting time. Douglas gave the baby a piece of dog biscuit which had survived beneath the cow.

The baby bit it, grew thoughtful, and put it into William’s pocket.

At length the church clock struck five.

William froze.

Mrs. Butler had said an hour. He had been absent nearly three.

“I’ve gotter go,” he said.

“What about the ransom?” asked Henry.

“There isn’t time.”

“You can’t kidnap someone an’ then take ’em back without gettin’ ransom.”

“I’ll get it another day.”

“You can’t kidnap the same baby twice,” said Ginger. “They’d be watchin’.”

William lifted the baby. Its clothes were still damp with paraffin and liquorice. Its face had dried into a dark, variegated mask. One shoe was missing. Its hat was inside a cow. It smelled of oil, smoke, sausage, and infancy.

William’s heart sank, but hope still survived in the form of the pram. With the hood raised and the rug arranged judiciously, much could be concealed.

They crossed the field.

The pram was not tied to the railing.

For a moment William stared at the empty space. Then he saw it fifty yards away, lying on its side in a ditch. Three village boys were running off across the field.

William handed the baby to Douglas and raced after them, followed by the Outlaws. The pursuit was brief but expressive. The village boys escaped through a hedge. The Outlaws returned to inspect the wreckage.

The pram had plainly enjoyed a crowded and adventurous afternoon. One wheel leaned outward. The handle was bent. The hood was torn. Mud covered the sides. A parcel of stinging-nettles occupied the interior. Someone had drawn a face upon the cover in chalk.

William stood before it like Marius among the ruins of Carthage, though with less command of classical allusion and more mud upon his knees.

“It wasn’t my fault,” he said.

“You left it here,” said Ginger.

“I tied it up.”

“Not very well.”

“I’m simply statin’ a fact.”

“We can bend the wheel back,” said Henry.

They could not.

They bent it farther out, then slightly inward, then into a position in which it revolved only when kicked. The handle resisted all treatment. The chalk face spread under William’s handkerchief, which was already so saturated with liquorice, oil, and soot that it could only redistribute disaster.

“We’ll put the baby in,” said Douglas.

They removed the nettles and arranged the rug. The baby was lowered into the pram. It smiled up at them from amid the wreckage, brown-faced, hatless, greasy-haired, and perfectly content.

“Pull the hood up,” said William.

The hood came halfway and stuck. Henry pushed while Ginger pulled. There was a tearing sound.

“Leave it!” cried William.

They left it. The hood now slanted over the baby like the roof of a collapsing shed. It concealed the worst of the child, though not the smell.

William took the handle. The pram moved reluctantly. The damaged wheel traced a series of protesting curves.

“You’re comin’ with me,” he said to the others.

“No, we’re not,” said Henry.

“You helped.”

“It’s your kidnap.”

“It’s Hubert’s cow,” said Douglas.

“The cow didn’t break the pram,” said Ginger.

“The baby spoilt our feast,” said Henry. “You ought to pay us back.”

William stared at them.

One by one they slipped away—Henry towards the lane, Ginger over the stile, Douglas after the cow in the hope that it might call him Dad again. William was left alone with the pram.

He began the journey home.

It was a journey of almost unexampled suffering. The wheel jammed every few yards. The pram pulled towards the ditch. The baby threw out the rug, then one shoe, then the dog biscuit from William’s pocket, which it had somehow recovered. William restored each article with diminishing patience.

As he neared the village, people turned to look at him. The paraffin announced their approach before they arrived. William avoided the main road and entered his own garden by the side gate.

Mrs. Butler stood at the front door with William’s mother.

“There he is!” she cried. “My little darling! And he’s been out all this time! William, you dear, good boy—you must have given him such a lovely afternoon.”

William said nothing.

He brought the pram to a halt.

Mrs. Butler bent over it, smiling, and lifted the hood.

Then she saw the baby.

Once upon a time there was a village cat called Ribby, who lived in a white cottage with green shutters and a brass knocker shaped like a fish.

Ribby was an excellent housekeeper. Her hearth was swept every morning, her plates stood in shining rows, and her best tablecloth was kept folded between sheets of blue paper.

One Tuesday Ribby wrote a note to Duchess, the little black-and-tan dog who lived beside the grocer’s shop.

“My dear Duchess,” wrote Ribby, “if you are not otherwise engaged, will you come to tea on Friday at four o’clock? I am baking a mouse-and-bacon pie, with pastry leaves upon the top. Your affectionate friend, Ribby.”

She sprinkled sand upon the writing, shook it off, and sent the note by a young rabbit who delivered parcels.

Duchess received it while she was polishing her collar-bells.

“How very kind!” she said aloud.

But when she read the words mouse-and-bacon, her ears drooped.

“A mouse pie! Oh dear! I cannot abide mouse. It has whiskers, and a tail, and tiny claws. Yet I shall have to eat it, because it is a party.”

Duchess sat upon the rug and considered. Then she remembered that she had a veal-and-ham pie of her own, prepared for Saturday dinner.

“I shall exchange the pies,” she whispered. “Ribby will never know. Both dishes have pink rims, and both pies have pastry leaves. It is the simplest plan in the world!”

Now Ribby spent Thursday making preparations. She purchased one pound of lump sugar, half a pound of tea, a jar of orange marmalade, two ounces of bacon, and three small mice from the market stall.

She rolled the pastry very thin. She lined a deep pie-dish, arranged the filling neatly, added pepper and a little gravy, and covered it with a handsome crust. In the centre she set a tin funnel to let out the steam.

Ribby’s kitchen range had two ovens. The upper oven was fierce and quickly browned muffins; the lower oven baked more slowly and evenly.

Ribby placed the mouse-and-bacon pie in the lower oven.

“There,” she said, shutting the door. “It will do very nicely.”

On Friday morning she scrubbed the front step, dusted the parlour, and brought out two flowered cups. Then she put on her shopping bonnet and went to buy cream.

Duchess had been watching from behind the grocer’s curtains.

The moment Ribby turned the corner, Duchess hurried into her own kitchen. Her veal-and-ham pie was waiting beneath a clean cloth.

Before covering it, Duchess had placed a small china patty-pan upside down in the middle. This was intended to support the crust. It was a pretty patty-pan, white with three blue forget-me-nots.

Duchess put the pie into a covered basket and ran across the village.

Ribby’s back door was not locked. In those days, respectable animals left their doors upon the latch, except at night or when fish was being delivered.

Duchess slipped inside.

“Now—where is the mouse pie?”

She opened the pantry. There were jars of currant jelly, a bowl of cream, six eggs, and a plate of little cakes; but no pie.

She looked beneath the kitchen table. She looked in the cupboard. She even looked behind the coal-scuttle, although a pie could scarcely have got there by itself.

Then Duchess noticed the oven.

“Of course!”

She opened the upper door. It was empty.

Duchess did not know there was a lower oven. The fire was warm, the kitchen was quiet, and she felt in a dreadful hurry.

“Ribby must have forgotten to put her pie in,” she said. “Perhaps it is upstairs. I cannot search the bedrooms—that would be impolite.”

So she placed her own veal-and-ham pie in the upper oven, shut the door, folded the cloth into her basket, and hurried home.

Five minutes later Ribby returned with the cream.

She stirred the fire, peeped into the lower oven, and found her pie progressing beautifully. Then she noticed that the upper oven door was warm.

“How odd,” said Ribby. “I do not remember putting anything there.”

But just then the kettle boiled over, and she forgot to investigate.

At half past three Ribby changed her apron. She wore a lavender gown, a white muslin kerchief, and a cap with two narrow ribbons. She arranged marmalade, muffins, sugar, and a vase of sweet-peas upon the table.

At precisely four o’clock there came a tap-tap-tap at the front door.

Duchess stood outside in a yellow bonnet and her best spotted shawl.

“How do you do, my dear Ribby?”

“I hope I see you well, my dear Duchess?”

“Very well indeed, I thank you kindly.”

They went into the parlour and sat opposite one another. Ribby poured the tea. Duchess balanced a lump of sugar upon her nose, tossed it neatly, and caught it in her mouth.

“What excellent sugar!” she said.

“I am glad you approve, my dear.”

They spoke about the weather, the price of coal, and a family of squirrels who had taken a furnished attic without providing references.

All the while Duchess thought about the pie.

Surely Ribby would fetch it from the upper oven. Surely it would be veal and ham. Surely everything had happened exactly as planned.

Presently Ribby rose.

“I believe the pie must be ready.”

Duchess gave a little sigh of relief.

Ribby went to the kitchen, opened the lower oven, and brought out the mouse-and-bacon pie. Its crust was golden and crisp; the pastry leaves shone with butter.

She did not open the upper oven.

“What a lovely pie!” cried Duchess, when Ribby carried it in.

“I hope you will enjoy it.”

“Oh, I am certain I shall. I am especially fond of veal and ham—I mean to say mouse and bacon.”

Ribby cut a generous slice.

Duchess inspected it anxiously. The pieces of bacon looked exactly like ham beneath the brown gravy. She could not distinguish anything resembling a whisker.

“This must be my pie,” she thought. “How clever I have been!”

She ate the whole slice.

“May I offer you another helping?” asked Ribby.

“If you please, my dear.”

Duchess ate a second slice, and then half of a third. It was remarkably savoury.

“I had no notion mouse—I mean veal—could be so delicate,” she said.

Ribby looked puzzled, but she was too polite to ask what Duchess meant.

Suddenly Duchess dropped her fork.

“The patty-pan!”

“I beg your pardon, my dear?”

“The little china patty-pan with three blue flowers! I baked it inside the pie to hold up the crust. Where is it?”

Ribby blinked.

“There was no patty-pan in my pie.”

“But there must have been! I have eaten three pieces, and I have not seen it. Oh, Ribby—I have swallowed it!”

Duchess clutched her shawl. Her eyes became round.

“I shall break! I shall rattle when I walk! Perhaps the flowers are poisonous. Fetch a doctor—quickly!”

Ribby attempted to calm her.

“Perhaps it might be wise to look beneath your plate.”

They looked beneath the plate, beneath the table, and inside the teapot. Ribby searched the pie-dish with a spoon.

There was no patty-pan.

“I shall die!” cried Duchess.

Ribby took her bonnet from the peg.

“Remain quietly here, my dear. I will fetch Dr. Maggotty.”

Dr. Maggotty was a magpie who lived in a tall elm beyond the blacksmith’s forge. He wore a long black coat and carried a silver-headed cane. Ribby ran through the village to find him, which caused some comment, because cats in lavender gowns do not usually run at tea-time.

Duchess was left alone.

For one minute she sat perfectly still. Then a warm smell drifted from the kitchen—a rich, meaty smell, not at all like burnt coal or muffins.

Duchess pricked up her ears.

She crept to the range and opened the upper oven.

There was her veal-and-ham pie!

The crust had risen splendidly. In the centre was a small crack, through which she could see the edge of the china patty-pan with its three blue forget-me-nots.

Duchess stared at it.

“Then what have I eaten?”

She looked toward the parlour, where Ribby’s half-finished pie stood upon the table.

“Mouse!” whispered Duchess.

She felt uncommonly silly. She had eaten three portions of the very pie she had plotted to avoid, and had enjoyed them besides.

Worse still, Ribby would soon return with Dr. Maggotty. There would be questions. There might be a prescription. Duchess’s secret would certainly come out.

She seized a towel, lifted the hot veal-and-ham pie from the oven, and carried it through the back door. At the end of Ribby’s garden stood a laurel bush beside the ash-bin.

Duchess pushed the pie beneath the leaves and covered it with an old flowerpot.

Then she hurried back to the parlour and arranged herself upon the sofa.

Ribby returned with Dr. Maggotty.

The doctor listened to Duchess’s chest, examined her tongue, and tapped her gently with his cane.

“Do you feel any china sensation?” he inquired.

“No—not now,” said Duchess.

“Any blue flowers before the eyes?”

“No, doctor.”

“Then I believe the danger has passed. The object, if object there was, has proceeded without difficulty.”

Duchess thanked him repeatedly. She declined another cup of tea and left at once, walking very carefully until she was out of sight.

Ribby watched from the doorway.

“A most peculiar visit,” she said.

At dusk Duchess returned with her basket to recover the hidden pie.

But voices came from behind the laurel bush.

Dr. Maggotty was there with two jackdaws. They had overturned the flowerpot, broken the crust, and were eating veal and ham as fast as they could.

“A remarkably successful case,” said Dr. Maggotty, wiping his beak.

Duchess did not wait to argue. She fled home with her empty basket and locked the door.

The next morning Ribby went into the garden to scatter crumbs.

Beneath the laurel she discovered a shattered pink-rimmed pie-dish, several flakes of pastry, and one little china patty-pan decorated with three blue forget-me-nots.

Ribby turned it over in her paw.

“How did this come here? I have never owned such a thing.”

She examined the broken dish. It was exactly like her own, except that her own stood safely washed upon the dresser.

Ribby never solved the mystery.

But when she next polished the flowered cups and considered giving another tea-party, she shook her head.

“No,” she said. “I shall not invite Duchess. I think I shall ask Cousin Tabitha instead. She may criticize the curtains, but at least she understands an oven.”

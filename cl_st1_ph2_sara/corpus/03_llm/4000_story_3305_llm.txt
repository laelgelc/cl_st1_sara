### The Vacancy at Blackthorn House

I have slept in hedges, hayricks, church porches, empty lime-kilns, and once—though the circumstances would not bear a respectable explanation—in the family vault of a Hampshire baronet; yet I do not remember that I was ever more sincerely grateful for the sight of a roof than on the night when I first came upon Blackthorn House. I say *first*, though I remained beneath that roof for less than six hours, and have never willingly been within twenty miles of it since.

It was close upon midnight, in November of the year 17—, when I lost my road upon the moor between Wexcombe and the northern turnpike. The rain did not fall so much as travel sideways, being driven over the heather in long grey volleys; the wind caught my coat-tails and cracked them behind me like pistol shots, and every rut in the track had become a brown brook, running wherever it pleased. My mare, Molly—an honest chestnut with less imagination than most Christians—had gone lame an hour earlier. I had therefore led her until she twice stumbled on the stones, after which I abandoned her in the lee of a ruined sheepfold, with my cloak over her back and such consolations as a wet and profane man may offer a horse.

My own position was the worse because I had urgent reasons for avoiding the turnpike. Two Bow Street runners had been enquiring after a gentleman of my height, complexion and uncommon elegance of dress, in connexion with the removal of certain negotiable papers from the mail near Andover. Their description of me was unjust in several particulars, but accurate enough in the principal ones to make public roads distasteful.

Thus I went over the open moor.

The darkness there was not the familiar darkness of a lane or wood, where a man has at least a bank to touch or branches to curse. It was immeasurable. The heath ran away on all sides into blackness; the clouds appeared to have settled upon the earth; and, when the wind slackened for a moment, the silence had the muffled quality of a room in which someone is listening. Twice I fancied I heard steps behind me, but they proved to be the water in my boots. Once I heard a sigh close at my shoulder.

I turned with my pistol drawn.

There was nothing: only the wet furze bending, and a pale scrap of wrack driven low across the ground.

I was then twenty-seven, which is an age when a man fears neither God nor the gallows so much as he ought, yet I confess that the sigh put speed into me. Ten minutes later—or twenty, for time upon a moor in a storm is measured chiefly by discomfort—I saw a light.

It shone above me and somewhat to the left: a small square of amber, vanishing and reappearing through the rain. I judged it to be an upper window, perhaps half a mile away, and altered my course towards it. The ground rose steeply. I crossed a ditch, tore my breeches on a thorn, and found at last a wall higher than my head. By following it I came to an iron gate, chained and padlocked, with stone pillars surmounted by things that might have been urns or owls. There was no lodge.

A gentleman in my profession is not discouraged by a locked gate. I climbed the wall where an elder-tree had loosened the stones, dropped into a plantation, and made my way between dripping yews until the house rose before me.

It was larger than I had expected—a square, old mansion of dark stone, with two wings running forward to embrace a paved courtlage. The roofs were crowded with chimneys; the rainwater gargoyles poured like ale-spouts; and all the windows were blind except that one upper casement whose candle had guided me. In its warmth the rain became fine gold threads, and for a moment I felt towards that little light the affection a prodigal might feel towards the lamps of home.

Then the candle moved away.

The window went black.

I stood under a cedar, considering matters. A sleeping house is like a sleeping man: it has a posture, a breathing, small accustomed noises. Blackthorn House possessed none of these. No shutter tapped, no servant crossed behind a curtain, no dog barked at my approach. Even the storm appeared to draw back from its walls.

But I was wet, hungry, cold, and more for the bucket than the manger. Moreover, if a householder chooses to display a light at midnight, he must accept the consequences. I crossed the court and tried the front door.

It opened beneath my hand.

Now, an open door at such an hour might have warned a wiser man. It did warn me; but warning and obedience are cousins who seldom visit one another. I entered, closed the door against the wind, and found myself in a broad hall, where a lamp burned upon a table.

The change from the moor was so complete that I might have stepped into another season. There was warmth from a banked fire, the smell of beeswax and old leather, a Turkey carpet underfoot, and dark family portraits upon the walls. Water streamed from me in a circle. I removed my hat, shook it, and listened.

Nothing.

At the foot of the staircase stood a mastiff.

He was an enormous brute, brindled about the muzzle, with a brass-studded collar and jaws capable of settling an argument instanter. He lay stretched on the black-and-white flags, his head upon his paws and his eyes half open. I had failed to see him until I was within six paces, and I do not deny that his appearance reduced my heartbeat by several years.

“Good fellow,” I whispered.

He did not stir.

I edged towards the staircase. Still he remained motionless.

It occurred to me that the animal was stuffed. Country gentlemen preserve all manner of deceased favourites—dogs, foxes, badgers, maiden aunts in oil—and this one had the glassy, ill-tempered stare common to the taxidermist’s art. To test the hypothesis, I tossed my wet glove so that it landed near his forepaw.

He neither moved nor blinked.

My confidence returned. “A formidable guardian,” I told him, “though deficient in initiative.”

Beyond the hall was a passage furnished with pegs. Upon these hung several coats: a black clerical coat, a weathered riding cloak, two sober greatcoats, and a servant’s brown livery with silver buttons. A pair of muddy boots stood beneath them. One coat was wet about the shoulders, as if recently worn; another had a torn buttonhole. I noticed these matters because clothes have always interested me. A man’s coat tells his occupation, purse, vanities and recent company more faithfully than his face, which has generally been taught to lie.

A narrow light showed beneath a door at the passage end.

I had by then formed no settled plan. Had the house been empty, I might have relieved it of portable valuables; had the owner proved charitable, I might have requested shelter under some harmless fiction; and had he proved neither absent nor charitable, I retained the argument in my right-hand pocket. This flexibility has often been miscalled indecision by persons who have never practised a liberal profession.

I went softly to the door and listened. Paper rustled within. A glass was set down. Someone sighed—not the wandering breath I had heard upon the moor, but a deliberate, weary exhalation.

I opened the door.

A clergyman sat behind a writing desk.

He was a man of perhaps sixty, though candlelight is apt to confer age where none exists and conceal it where it does. His hair was white and brushed back from a broad forehead; his face long, pale and clean-shaven; his clothes black, precise and of excellent cloth. A pair of silver spectacles lay upon the desk beside an open ledger. At his elbow stood a decanter, two bottles of wine and a single glass.

He looked at me without starting.

That, more than a shout or a snatched bell-rope, disturbed me. I was dripping upon his carpet, masked to the cheekbones, with one hand still in my pocket, yet he regarded me as though I were a parishioner who had arrived early for advice.

“Good evening,” he said.

“Good evening, sir.”

“You have neglected to knock.”

“The door was open.”

“So it was.”

He waited. I had always thought silence a weapon of my own, but he used it with the authority of a magistrate. At last I took out my pistol—not flourishing it, which is vulgar, but laying it across my palm where its significance could not be mistaken.

“You perceive,” I said, “that I possess the stronger card.”

He glanced at it. “A Queen Anne pistol, double-barrelled, badly exposed to rain. Yes, Mr—?”

“Foot. Gabriel Foot.”

“An unexpected candour.”

“I have never been ashamed of my name.”

“Only of your face, apparently.”

I laughed. I could not help it. “Sir, you are a cool hand.”

“And you are wet. Pray shut the door.”

I did so. He indicated a chair on the opposite side of the desk.

“If you mean to rob me,” he continued, “you may be seated while we determine what is worth taking. If you mean to murder me, I should prefer to finish my brandy first.”

“Your priorities do you credit.”

“They have been acquired by experience.”

I sat, though I kept the pistol before me. The fire in the grate was low but generous. Steam began to rise from my sleeves. The clergyman poured brandy into the second glass, which had been hidden behind the decanter, and pushed it across.

“You will forgive me,” I said, “if I decline.”

“You entered a strange house at midnight with a firearm, yet hesitate over a glass?”

“A firearm is honest about its purpose.”

“So is brandy, in sufficient quantity.”

He drank. I smelt mine. It was old, strong and innocent of almond. I tasted it, and felt life return to my body from the throat downwards.

“Better,” he said.

“Much.”

“Now, Mr Foot, what do you require?”

“Money, principally.”

“A common complaint.”

“Jewels, plate, watches, small securities—I am not dogmatic.”

“I fear you will be disappointed. The plate is locked in a safe whose key is not in my possession. There are no jewels in the house. My watch”—he drew out a heavy gold repeater—“was my father’s and may fetch twelve guineas from a receiver, though I value it more highly. You may have eight guineas from my purse, if poverty rather than ambition has brought you here.”

“Do you offer charity?”

“I offer economy. Eight guineas are cheaper than a funeral.”

“You assume I would accept eight.”

“I assume that, if you were a successful housebreaker, you would not arrive on foot, drenched through, at the one house in the county where the silver is inaccessible.”

This was severe but not wholly inaccurate. I drank again.

“You have overlooked your cellar,” I said. “That brandy alone might console me.”

“You are welcome to it.”

“And the wine?”

His eyes sharpened a little. “Ah. Do you know wine?”

“I have been expelled from Oxford, entertained by a duke, and employed for six weeks by a Bristol vintner. Between the three, I acquired an education.”

“Expelled from Oxford?”

“Sent down for a disagreement concerning ownership.”

“Of books?”

“Of a horse.”

He leaned back. The action seemed natural, yet I noticed that his left hand closed briefly upon the desk. It was a curious hand for a clergyman: smooth on the back, the nails carefully trimmed, but with a flattened thumb and small scars across the fingers such as are got by polishing plate or handling kitchen knives. I saw the marks, considered them, and forgot them. Brandy is a benevolent editor.

“You interest me, Mr Foot,” he said. “Do you read Latin?”

“Better than I speak it.”

“Greek?”

“Worse than I pretend.”

“French?”

“With women, fluently. With men, sufficiently.”

“And you know wine?”

“Within reason.”

“Reason has a wide circumference after midnight.”

He rose, took one of the bottles from the side table, and poured a little into a clean glass. He moved with a slight stiffness in the right leg, and kept his face turned from me while he did so. When he returned, he placed the glass at my hand.

“What is that?”

I examined the colour against the candle. “Madeira.”

“Is it?”

“No.”

His eyebrows rose.

“It wishes to be Madeira,” I continued, “and may deceive a guest who has dined too heartily. But it is Marsala—dry, respectable, and somewhat offended by the disguise.”

He tasted it himself.

“Excellent.”

“The wine?”

“The answer.”

“Was I under examination?”

“You are now.”

“For what office?”

“Butler.”

There are offers that a man meets with laughter, and others so absurd that laughter cannot at first encompass them. I stared at him. He remained grave.

“Your butler,” I repeated.

“My former butler left my service this evening. The place is vacant.”

“Was his departure sudden?”

“Extremely.”

“Did he give notice?”

“He gave me cause.”

“And you propose to replace him with the armed stranger who entered your study after midnight?”

“You have presented qualifications.”

“For burglary.”

“The professions overlap. A butler must know silver, locks, cellars, clothing, habits and the quiet movement of objects from one room to another. He must maintain discretion, preserve his composure under questioning, and distinguish a gentleman from an impostor. You have shown ability in most of these.”

“I failed at the open door.”

“Every candidate has deficiencies.”

I replenished my brandy without invitation. “You neglected honesty.”

“Honesty is useful in a servant, but less rare than intelligence and more easily supervised.”

“Sir, either you are mad or I am.”

“The alternatives are not mutually exclusive.”

He spoke with the driest exactness, yet there was nothing playful in his eyes. They were pale grey and watchful. I remember them more clearly than I remember his other features; indeed, I have sometimes wondered whether the white hair, clerical bands and long pious countenance impressed themselves upon me because he intended that they should, while the eyes alone belonged wholly to the man.

“What became of your former servant?” I asked.

“Parkinson?”

“If that was his name.”

“It was the name he used. He had been with me seven years. An accomplished fellow in his way, but presumptuous.”

“In what particular?”

“In every particular, eventually. At first he imitated my handwriting as a parlour trick. Then my voice. Later he adopted my phrases, copied the cut of my clothes, read the books I read and practised my manner before mirrors. Servants imitate their masters naturally, just as provincial actors imitate Mr Garrick; but Parkinson possessed unusual diligence. He could counterfeit my cough, my gait, even certain idiosyncrasies of the hands.”

The clergyman folded his own hands upon the desk.

“An alarming talent,” I said.

“Talent is seldom alarming until joined to resentment.”

“He resented you?”

“He resented the fact that resemblance does not confer equality. A servant may put on his master’s coat; he remains the man who brushed it.”

“Unless the room is dark.”

“Quite so.”

“And tonight?”

“I dismissed him.”

“For imitation?”

“For theft.”

“Ah.”

“He had been taking money. Small sums first, then larger ones. When confronted, he attempted a defence so elaborate that it became an accusation. He maintained that years of service entitled him to some share in the estate. He had forgotten the distinction between proximity and possession.”

“As many landlords do.”

The clergyman’s mouth almost smiled.

“We quarrelled,” he went on. “There were threats. He left by the front door shortly before the storm reached us.”

“And left the door open?”

“So it appears.”

“Where are the other servants?”

“Dismissed for the night to attend a wedding in the village. They will return tomorrow.”

“All of them?”

“The cook has a niece who is being married. Rural households become democracies on such occasions.”

This explained the silence, though not entirely. “And you remained here alone after being threatened by a thief?”

“I had business.”

“With your ledger?”

“With my affairs.”

He glanced at the papers before him. One sheet bore a dark smear near the corner. Ink, I thought; though it was too brown for fresh ink and too red for old.

“Why should I wish to be your butler?” I asked.

“Because the county is being searched for you.”

The room seemed suddenly cooler.

He raised one hand. “No alarm. Your face has been described in the newspapers. The mail robbery near Andover, was it not?”

“I never read newspapers. They encourage melancholy.”

“You cannot ride the roads safely. You require shelter, clothing, a respectable identity and time in which public interest may decay. I require a servant who is not entangled in local gossip and who will ask few questions. The arrangement has symmetry.”

“It has the symmetry of a trap.”

“Most employment does.”

“What wages?”

“Forty guineas yearly, board, livery and perquisites.”

“Fifty.”

“Forty-five.”

“And the right to leave without a character.”

“Given your present character, that seems fair.”

I considered him over the rim of my glass. At twenty-seven I had the vanity to suppose that an adventure could not be dangerous if I understood that it was odd. Here was warmth, food, concealment and a private cellar; outside were rain, darkness and possible constables. I had accepted less promising invitations from prettier faces.

“I cannot promise permanence,” I said.

“I should distrust a man who did.”

“Nor can I promise to abandon my former calling altogether.”

“Only while you are in my service.”

“That is tyranny.”

“It is domestic discipline.”

“Then I accept.”

We shook hands across the desk. His palm was dry and cold.

“Very good, Foot.”

“Very good, sir. How shall I address you?”

For the smallest fraction of time he hesitated.

“Reverend Doctor Erasmus Vale.”

“Then, Doctor Vale, I am your butler.”

“Until one of us thinks better of it.”

He rang a handbell, apparently forgetting that there were no servants to answer it. The bright little note passed through the house and died. We both listened.

“Habit,” he said.

“An inconvenient master.”

“The most powerful.”

He showed me the pantry and cut me bread, cold beef and cheese with his own hands. This inversion of our new relations did not trouble either of us. I ate in the study while he sorted papers, placing some in the fire and others in a leather case. He worked quickly but without haste. Once I observed him hold a page close to the candle, comparing a signature at its foot with another upon a folded document. When he saw me looking, he covered both.

“Professional curiosity?” he enquired.

“Professional admiration.”

“Moderate it.”

By the time I had eaten, I had taken four brandies—perhaps five; memory is a conscientious witness in matters of murder, but an arrant mumper concerning drink. Doctor Vale opened a drawer and gave me a key.

“The room above the stable passage is prepared. Parkinson’s room.”

“Has he taken his belongings?”

“Only what he could carry.”

“Any danger that he may return?”

“None.”

The certainty of that answer should have sobered me.

We went into the hall. The mastiff lay where I had left him, vast and unmoving. The doctor stopped beside it.

“You have met Brutus?”

“We exchanged opinions.”

“He is not always amiable.”

“Nor am I.”

“Then perhaps you understand one another.”

He took a candle from the hall table and led me upstairs. His gait was peculiar: a measured stiffness for three steps, then a freer fourth, as though the lameness required recollection. At the landing, portraits crowded the walls. One showed a younger Doctor Vale in hunting dress beside the very mastiff below, though the painted animal was less grey about the muzzle. The face in the portrait was broad at the jaw and florid in complexion. My host, by contrast, appeared narrow and pale.

“An unkind likeness,” I remarked.

“Painted during an illness.”

“Yours or the artist’s?”

“Both, I believe.”

He conducted me along a passage and indicated a door at its end.

“My own chamber. If you require anything, you may knock. Do not enter.”

“A butler’s first lesson?”

“His last, in Parkinson’s case.”

My room was small but comfortable, containing a truckle-bed, washstand, press and a crucifix over the mantel. A brown livery coat hung upon the press door. A razor, comb and little pot of black moustache-wax lay neatly upon the washstand, though Doctor Vale had described Parkinson as already gone. Beside them stood a basin of pinkish water.

“The previous occupant departed in haste,” I said.

“He cut himself shaving.”

“Deeply?”

“He was clumsy at the end.”

Again there was that almost-smile.

He gave me the candle. “Sleep, Foot. We will discuss your duties at breakfast.”

“And my past?”

“We have discussed enough of both our pasts for one night.”

He closed the door.

I removed my wet clothes, rubbed myself down with a coarse towel and got into bed. The sheets were cold but dry. Rain rattled against the panes; the chimney muttered; somewhere a board creaked with the slow deliberation of a footstep. I had my pistol under the pillow and brandy warming my blood, yet sleep would not come.

This sleeplessness was unusual. I have slumbered soundly while officers searched the room beneath me, and once while a landlord attempted to smoke me out of his attic. But Blackthorn House resisted sleep. Its silence was too complete between noises, and the noises, when they came, were too particular. A faint scraping moved along the wall. A door closed at a distance. Once I heard a soft thud overhead, although mine was the upper floor.

I considered leaving. The sensible Gabriel Foot—who has frequently offered excellent advice after the event—urged me to dress, descend and regain the moor. The younger Gabriel replied that it was raining, that the bed was tolerable, and that forty-five guineas were not found under every wet bush.

Then there was the doctor himself. I did not trust him; but distrust was familiar company and had never prevented business. His offer might be eccentric, yet eccentricity in the rich is merely character with an income. As to Parkinson, a thief who mimicked his master was precisely the kind of servant a lonely scholar might employ for seven years and dismiss upon a stormy night.

Thus I reasoned, carefully and foolishly, until the candle burned down.

At some hour before dawn, the rain ceased.

The silence that followed woke me, if I had slept at all. In it I heard a key turn below, very softly; then the front door opened. There was a pause. A draught moved through the house, stirring some loose paper in the passage. The door shut again.

Someone had gone out.

I sat up. The window showed a thin, colourless light. Dressing hurriedly, I looked into the corridor. All was still. Doctor Vale’s door stood closed at the far end.

“Sir?” I called.

No answer.

I went downstairs. The hall lamp had gone out, but dawn entered by the fanlight, laying a grey bar across the flags. The mastiff lay within it.

At once I saw that something was altered. His forelegs were no longer neatly extended; one had slipped outward. A dark pool showed beneath his collar.

I approached.

He was not stuffed.

His throat was gashed from ear to ear.

The blood had dried black in the night, but was wet beneath his neck where the warmth of the hall had preserved it. My glove still lay beside his paw. I touched his flank. It retained the faintest heat.

The dog had been alive when I entered—or had been dead only moments—and his terrible stillness had not been the taxidermist’s art. It had been death.

I straightened and looked about the hall. The servant’s brown coat was gone from its peg. So was the black clerical greatcoat. The muddy boots remained, but a lighter pair had disappeared.

“Doctor Vale!”

My voice struck the walls and came back smaller.

I ran to the study. The desk drawers stood open. Papers littered the carpet. The leather case was gone, as were the gold watch and decanters. In the grate, charred scraps shifted among the ashes. I drew one out. It was part of a household account, signed in the bold hand I had seen the night before: *Erasmus Vale*.

Beneath the desk lay a scrap of white hair.

I picked it up. It was attached to fine netting.

A wig.

I confess that my mind then moved more quickly than my courage. The pale face, the remembered limp, the clerical coats in the passage, the scars upon his hands, the mismatch between man and portrait, the razor and dark wax in the servant’s room—all these stood suddenly in new relation, like cards thrown at random which prove, when turned, to be a losing hand.

Yet one fact remained unknown.

Where was the true Doctor Vale?

I went upstairs.

The door at the end of the passage was locked. I knocked once, then set my shoulder against it. The wood held. On the second attempt the old frame split by the latch, and I stumbled into a bedroom made dim by heavy curtains.

The air smelt of lavender, candle grease and blood.

An elderly man lay in the bed. He was broad-jawed and heavy, with scant white hair and a florid face—the face from the portrait. One arm hung over the bedside, the fingers nearly touching the carpet. His nightshirt was dark across the breast. A pillow lay on the floor.

I went closer, though there was no need.

He had been stabbed twice through the heart.

For a moment I heard nothing but my own breathing. Then a rook called outside, and another answered from the cedar.

The murdered man’s right hand was closed. I forced the fingers apart and found a silver livery button pressed into the palm. On it was engraved a small blackthorn branch.

Parkinson’s button.

I stood beside the bed while the whole contrivance assembled itself in my understanding.

Parkinson had murdered the dog to silence it, perhaps because Brutus knew him well enough to approach and hate him well enough to bark. He had gone upstairs in his master’s clothes—or taken them after the killing—entered the chamber and stabbed Doctor Vale. Then, with wig, paint, clerical bands and seven years’ practice, he had assumed the dead man’s person. He had burned papers, gathered money and waited for the storm to cover his departure.

My arrival had disturbed him.

No: it had assisted him.

A stranger walked into the house—a stranger already wanted by the law, armed, masked and wet from the moor. Parkinson had learned my name, my history and my necessity. He had fed me, filled me with brandy, appointed me to his own vacant office and placed me in his room. Then he had left before dawn dressed, I did not doubt, as a servant travelling on his master’s business.

When the household returned, what would they find? Their master stabbed in bed. His dog’s throat cut. Plate, papers and money missing. A notorious highwayman sleeping in the former butler’s chamber, wearing, perhaps, the former butler’s livery, and prepared to explain that the dead man had hired him after an interview concerning Marsala.

I had been indiscreet in taking service with a murdered master.

That sentence, though mild in form, conveyed the fate of my neck with sufficient exactitude. I searched the room quickly, not from avarice—the time for professional enterprise had passed—but for anything that might prove Parkinson’s deception. There was little. A handkerchief marked E.V.; a broken spectacle lens; blood on the carpet; the dead man. Truth, as usual, was abundant and evidence scarce.

From below came a sound.

Wheels.

I crossed to the window and pulled aside the curtain. A dogcart had entered the court. In it sat a stout woman in a bonnet, a red-haired maid and a narrow young man holding the reins. Returning servants, doubtless, merry from the wedding and ready for breakfast.

The stout woman looked up.

She saw me at the window beside her master’s bed.

Her mouth opened.

I did not remain to hear the scream.

There are occasions when dignity must yield to architecture. I ran from the chamber, along the passage and into my room, snatched up my coat, pistol and boots, then opened the casement. The stable roof sloped below. I climbed out as voices rose in the hall.

“Door’s broken!”

“Brutus!”

“Oh, merciful Heaven—”

I slid down wet slates, caught the gutter, swung and dropped into a heap of mouldy straw behind the stables. My left ankle turned under me, but held. Someone shouted from an upper window. I scrambled over the yard wall, tore the same breeches upon a different thorn, and reached the plantation as a bell began to ring furiously in the house.

The moor beyond was pale beneath a clearing sky. Rainwater shone in every hollow; mist streamed low over the heather; and, far to the east, a strip of cold yellow daylight had opened under the clouds. The storm, which had seemed my greatest enemy at midnight, was gone. I had never missed it more. In daylight every moving man may be seen for a mile.

Behind me, a gun fired.

The ball clipped a twig above my shoulder.

I ran.

I ran without road or plan, over sodden furze, through a sheep mire and down into a gully where black water reached my knees. The bell at Blackthorn House continued to sound, diminishing as I went. Once I looked back and saw the mansion standing dark among its cedars, the upper windows kindling one by one in the dawn. It no longer resembled a refuge. It resembled what it was: a trap whose jaws had closed one instant too late.

By noon I found Molly where I had left her. The ungrateful creature received me with a bite at my sleeve, but her lameness had eased, and we travelled south by field paths. Before evening I had exchanged my coat with a charcoal burner, my horse with a dissenting minister, and my name with nobody at all.

Parkinson was never taken, to my knowledge. A man answering his description later appeared in Ghent as secretary to an English antiquary, and another in Lisbon as the Reverend Septimus Crane; but imitation is a broad kingdom, and its citizens are difficult to number. Doctor Vale was buried, Brutus with him, while the newspapers gave Gabriel Foot the credit for both murders and added several ornaments of their own invention.

I bore the injustice with my customary patience—that is, from a safe distance.

Still, I profited by the affair. I learned never to trust an open door, a motionless dog, a clergyman who pours his own brandy, or an employer who seems too eager to overlook defects in one’s character. Above all, when offered a respectable post by a stranger after midnight, I now enquire why the vacancy has occurred.

It is a precaution which has kept me out of domestic service ever since.

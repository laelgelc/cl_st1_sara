You know, in China the Emperor is Chinese, and all his people are Chinese too; at least they were in the days of this story, which happened so long ago that even the oldest court historian had to stand on a ladder to read the first page of it.

The Emperor’s palace was the most splendid palace in the world. It was made of porcelain, very costly and very delicate, so that one had to walk carefully and speak politely, lest a wall should crack from surprise. Golden dragons curled about the roofs; lanterns of painted silk hung beneath the eaves; and in the great hall stood a throne carved from a single piece of pearl, or so the keeper of the throne always said.

The gardens stretched farther than anyone knew. Even the chief gardener had never seen the end of them. There were blue lakes with white bridges, orange trees bearing fruit beside almond trees in blossom, and flowers so rare that little silver bells had been tied to their stems. Whenever a visitor passed, the bells rang, as if to say, “Pray look down! Here is something remarkable!”

Beyond the gardens lay a deep wood, and beyond the wood the blue sea. Great ships sailed beneath the branches, for some of the trees leaned so far over the water that their leaves could hear the sailors singing.

In one of these trees lived a little gray nightingale.

She was not handsome. Her feathers did not flash like emeralds; her tail had no golden eyes; and no silver bell announced her presence. But when evening came and the moon rested upon the sea, she sang so beautifully that fishermen paused with their nets in their hands.

“Ah,” they said, “that is lovely!”

Then they remembered the fish and went on working; but on the next evening, when the nightingale sang again, they forgot the fish once more.

Travelers came from every country to see the imperial city. They admired the porcelain palace, the gardens, the bridges, and the flowers with bells; yet when they heard the nightingale, they said, “This is best of all.”

And they wrote it in books.

One day the Emperor sat in his library reading a large foreign volume bound in green leather. It described all the wonders of his empire.

“The porcelain palace,” he read, “is beyond comparison. The imperial gardens exceed imagination. But the nightingale is the greatest wonder in all China.”

“The nightingale?” said the Emperor. “I know nothing of any nightingale.”

He rang a golden handbell. At once there entered the First Lord-in-Waiting, who was so grand that when anyone below his rank spoke to him, he answered only, “Pooh!” and considered that sufficient.

“There is said to be a most remarkable bird in our empire,” said the Emperor. “It is called the nightingale, and foreigners declare it the finest thing I possess. Why have I never been informed?”

“I have never heard the creature mentioned,” said the lord-in-waiting. “It has certainly not been presented at court.”

“I command it to sing before me this evening,” said the Emperor. “The whole world knows what I possess, while I do not know it myself!”

“Pooh!” said the lord-in-waiting; but he bowed so low that three decorations struck the floor. Then he hurried through the palace, asking every nobleman and official if he knew the nightingale.

No one did.

“It must be a newly invented title,” said the Keeper of Imperial Umbrellas.

“Perhaps it is a kind of jewel,” suggested the Third Assistant Examiner of Ceremonial Fans.

“Possibly a large green bird with a scarlet beard,” said the court painter, who had never seen one but was ready to paint it.

At last the lord-in-waiting returned.

“Your Imperial Majesty,” he said, “the bird appears not to exist. Foreign authors are often imaginative.”

“But the book was sent me by the Emperor of Japan,” replied the Emperor, “and therefore it must be true. The nightingale shall be here tonight. If it is not, every person at court shall be tapped upon the stomach after supper.”

This was a terrible punishment, especially after supper.

The whole court rushed about crying, “The nightingale! The nightingale!” Staircases trembled beneath embroidered slippers. Messengers questioned gardeners, soldiers, scholars, lamp-lighters, boatmen, cooks, and finally a poor little girl who worked in the imperial kitchen.

She had heard the commotion while carrying a basket of scraps to her sick mother, who lived near the sea.

“Oh, I know the nightingale,” she said. “How beautifully she sings! Every evening, when I take food home, I sit in the wood and listen. Her song makes tears come into my eyes, and then it is as if my mother kissed me.”

“Kitchen child!” cried the lord-in-waiting, “if you can lead us to this bird, I shall obtain for you the high distinction of watching the Emperor dine. You may stand behind the door.”

The little girl thought that a very great honor. So she led the lord-in-waiting, the music-master, twelve cavaliers, six ladies with very long sleeves, and several necessary officials into the garden.

As they walked, a cow lowed in a meadow.

“Moo-oo!”

“There she is!” whispered one of the ladies. “What strength! Such a voice from so small a creature!”

“That is a cow,” said the kitchen girl.

“Of course,” said the lady. “I was merely testing you.”

They went farther. In a marsh the frogs began to croak.

“Croak! Croak! Croak!”

“Charming!” said the palace chaplain. “It resembles the ringing of little temple bells beneath water.”

“Those are frogs,” said the girl.

“Yes, yes,” said the chaplain. “But now I think I hear the bird.”

Then, from a shadowy branch above them, came a clear, living stream of music. It rose and fell; it trembled like moonlight on water; it seemed now near, now far away, and every leaf in the wood stood listening.

“There she is,” said the kitchen girl, pointing to a small gray bird.

“That?” exclaimed the lord-in-waiting. “She has a very modest appearance. No doubt she has turned pale at seeing so many distinguished persons.”

“Little nightingale,” called the girl, “our gracious Emperor wishes you to sing before him.”

“With pleasure,” said the nightingale, and she sang again.

“Like glass bells!” cried the lord-in-waiting. “Observe the activity of her throat. It is remarkable that we have never heard her before. She will make a great success at court.”

“Shall I sing once more for the Emperor here?” asked the bird, supposing he might be among the jeweled gentlemen.

“His Imperial Majesty is not in the forest,” said the lord-in-waiting. “His Imperial Majesty never visits places without floors. You are commanded to appear at the palace this evening.”

The nightingale looked through the trees toward the open sea.

“My song sounds best among green branches,” she said. “Still, I will gladly come if it pleases the Emperor.”

That evening the palace shone with ten thousand lamps. Every porcelain corridor had been polished; every silver bell in the garden had been made to ring; and the courtiers wore their finest robes. The kitchen girl was permitted to stand behind the great hall door, where she could see one corner of the throne and the backs of several important heads.

A golden perch had been placed beside the Emperor. The little gray nightingale settled upon it.

The music-master raised his hand, though no one had asked him to do so, and the hall became silent.

Then the nightingale sang.

She sang of the deep wood where the stars shone between black branches. She sang of the sea rolling gently beneath the moon. She sang of the fisherman, tired and poor, who forgot his weariness when her voice reached his boat. And although there were no words in the song, each listener heard something different within it.

The kitchen girl heard her mother breathing peacefully. A soldier remembered his childhood garden. An old minister remembered a promise he had once made and never kept.

The Emperor heard all the quiet things that imperial drums had drowned.

A tear gathered in his eye and rolled down his cheek.

The nightingale saw it, and her song grew lovelier still.

“Wonderful!” cried the whole court when she had finished.

The Emperor was deeply moved. He offered the bird his golden slipper to wear about her neck.

“I thank Your Majesty,” said the nightingale, “but I have seen tears in the Emperor’s eyes. That is reward enough. An emperor’s tears have a special brightness.”

Then she sang again.

The ladies tried to imitate her by holding water in their mouths and making little bubbling noises. They thought this made them nightingales also, but nobody wept.

The nightingale remained at court. She had a cage of gold and permission to fly out twice by day and once by night. Twelve servants attended her, each holding a silk ribbon tied about one of her legs. As she had only two legs, the other ribbons were attached wherever the Master of Birdly Arrangements considered proper. There was no pleasure in such an excursion.

The citizens gathered outside the palace whenever she sang. They listened and nodded, and some said, “Ah!” because they truly felt something, while others said “Ah!” because the first group had said it.

The kitchen girl was appointed Assistant Imperial Nightingale-Bringer-in-Chief. This gave her no more food and much longer words to remember, but she was allowed to visit her mother every Sunday.

So a year passed.

Then one day a large parcel arrived for the Emperor. Upon it was written:

THE NIGHTINGALE

The court expected feathers, but inside was a mechanical bird, made to resemble the living one. It was covered with diamonds, rubies, and sapphires. Its beak was gold, its feet were silver, and around its neck hung a ribbon bearing the words:

THE NIGHTINGALE OF THE EMPEROR OF JAPAN IS POOR BESIDE THAT OF THE EMPEROR OF CHINA.

“Most courteous!” said everyone.

The mechanical bird was placed upon a cushion. When a little key in its side had been turned, it lifted its tail, glittered magnificently, and sang a waltz.

Its notes were bright and exact. They came in the proper order and lasted the proper time. While it sang, bands of gold moved beneath its wings, and precious stones flashed red, blue, and green.

“Superb!” cried the court.

The Imperial Music-Master demanded that the two birds sing together.

“Music! music!” said the Emperor.

The living nightingale began as she always did, freely and according to what she felt. But the jeweled bird sang only its waltz. It kept perfect time; the nightingale followed the wind.

They did not agree at all.

“The natural bird lacks discipline,” said the music-master. “The artificial bird has been trained according to scientific principles.”

The real nightingale sang high and low, lingering on one note and passing swiftly over another, while the machine clicked steadily through its appointed tune.

At last the nightingale flew through an open window, over the palace roof, and away to the green wood.

“What is the meaning of this?” cried the Emperor.

“Most ungrateful!” said the courtiers.

“Pooh!” said the lord-in-waiting, and everyone knew the bird’s position was hopeless.

Fortunately, they said, the better nightingale remained.

The mechanical bird sang its waltz thirty-three times. The court would gladly have heard it a thirty-fourth, but the music-master said there ought to be supper.

The Emperor banished the living bird from his dominions, though no one could explain exactly how a banished bird was to be prevented from flying over the frontier. The jeweled bird was given the title of Chief Imperial Bedside Singer, First Class, and placed upon a velvet cushion at the Emperor’s left hand, for that was the side on which the heart was supposed to be.

The music-master wrote twenty-five volumes about the machine. The books were very long, very learned, and filled with the hardest words. Everyone declared that they had read and understood them, because those who did not were liable to be tapped upon the stomach and called stupid.

The people learned the mechanical bird’s song. Children sang it in the streets.

“Zi-zi-zi! Cluck-cluck-cluck! Tra-la-la!”

Even the fishmongers whistled it. If a person sang any other tune, his neighbors looked at him suspiciously.

“See,” said the music-master, “the artificial bird is superior to the living one. With the natural bird, one never knows what is coming. With this bird, all is certain. One can open it, study it, and explain precisely where the waltz begins, how it continues, and where it ends.”

“That is true,” said the courtiers.

They were repeating this one evening when the machine made a sound from within:

“Crack!”

Then something spun loose.

“Whirr-r-r!”

The music stopped.

The Emperor sent for his clockmaker. After much examination, the clockmaker said the machinery had worn thin.

“It must be used sparingly,” he declared. “Some of the wheels cannot be replaced, and the small silver pin that controls the golden drum has become fatigued.”

The bird was therefore permitted to sing only once a year.

On that occasion the music-master made a speech before turning the key. He said the song was finer than ever, having benefited from silence. The court agreed, for agreement was the cheapest thing at court and always in fashion.

Five years passed.

The Emperor grew gravely ill.

At first the physicians said it was a royal chill, which required royal medicine. Then they said it was an imperial fever. At last they gave it a name so long that everyone knew there was no hope.

The Emperor lay in his magnificent bed beneath curtains of heavy silk. His face was pale, and his breath came faintly. The palace corridors were muffled with carpets so that no footstep should disturb him.

The courtiers had already gone to salute the emperor who was expected to come next. The attendants whispered together and divided the vacant offices among themselves. The servants went out to drink tea and discuss the funeral procession.

No one remained with the sick man.

Through the open window came the moonlight. It shone upon the jeweled bird, which stood silent on its velvet cushion. It shone also upon the Emperor’s face.

The Emperor opened his eyes and saw Death sitting on his chest.

Death wore the imperial crown. In one hand he held the Emperor’s golden sword; in the other, the great banner of the realm. His eyes were dark and patient.

Around the bed peered strange faces from the folds of the curtains. Some were beautiful and gentle; others were twisted and terrible.

They were the Emperor’s deeds.

“Do you remember me?” whispered one.

“And me?” asked another.

“I was the hungry farmer whose grain you took for the palace horses.”

“I was the prisoner you pardoned.”

“I was the widow whose petition your ministers hid.”

“I was the child who received medicine from your physician.”

Good deeds and evil deeds crowded about him, speaking one after another.

The Emperor turned his head.

“Music!” he cried. “Music! Beat the great drum, so that I need not hear them!”

But no drum sounded.

“Little jeweled bird,” he gasped, “sing! Sing! I gave you gold and precious stones. I hung my slipper around—no, that was the other one—but I gave you the highest place beside my bed. Sing!”

The artificial nightingale remained silent.

There was no one to wind it.

Death bent nearer, and the faces continued whispering.

“Hark, hark!” said Death suddenly.

Outside the window, in the darkness, a bird had begun to sing.

It was the living nightingale.

She had heard from the wind that the Emperor was dying. She had flown from the forest and settled upon a branch beside the window, and now her song entered the chamber like cool air.

She sang of the quiet churchyard beneath white roses. She sang of fresh grass shining with dew, and of the evening bell that calls the weary home. She sang of sleep without fear and memory without bitterness.

The dreadful faces grew still.

Death listened.

“Sing on, little nightingale,” he said. “Sing on.”

“Will you give me the Emperor’s golden sword?” asked the bird.

Death surrendered the sword for a song.

The nightingale sang of the sea at dawn, when gray water becomes silver and the poor fisherman lifts his eyes toward the light.

“More,” said Death.

“Will you give me the banner?”

Death gave up the banner.

Then the nightingale sang of the forest in spring: of roots drinking rain, buds opening in secret, and small wings testing the warm air.

“More,” whispered Death. “Little bird, sing more.”

“Will you give me the crown?”

Death placed the crown upon the Emperor’s pillow.

The nightingale sang then of a mother bending over her sleeping child, of forgiveness arriving late but not too late, and of tears falling from the eyes of one who has at last heard the truth.

Death rose from the Emperor’s chest.

He became a white mist, drifted toward the window, and vanished into the night.

The faces disappeared.

The room was quiet, but it was no longer the quiet of death. The Emperor breathed deeply. Warmth returned to his hands, and a faint color came into his cheeks.

“Thank you,” he said. “Faithful little bird, I drove you from my empire, yet you have driven Death from my bed. How shall I reward you? I will place the golden slipper around your neck. I will give you a title with twelve lines in it. You shall live beside me always.”

“I desire neither slipper nor title,” said the nightingale. “I have already received my reward. I saw tears in your eyes when first I sang to you, and tonight I saw them again. Keep those tears; they are jewels no goldsmith can make.”

“Then remain with me,” said the Emperor. “Sing whenever you please. I will break the mechanical bird into a thousand pieces.”

“Do not break it,” replied the nightingale. “It did what it could. Keep it kindly. But I cannot live in the palace.”

“Why not?”

“Because my song belongs also to the fisherman, the woodcutter, the poor mother, and the child who walks alone. I must fly where the green branches grow and where the sea carries news from distant shores.”

The Emperor was sorrowful, but illness had made him quieter, and the song had made him wise enough to listen.

“Then must I never hear you again?”

“I will come,” said the nightingale. “In the evening I shall sit upon the branch outside your window and sing to you. I will sing of those who are happy and of those who suffer. I will tell you of the good that is hidden and the evil that is concealed. A little bird flies far. I shall visit the fisherman’s hut and the farmer’s field, the school, the prison, the sickroom, and all the places that lie far from your porcelain walls.”

“That would be worth more than twenty-five learned volumes,” said the Emperor.

“But one thing you must promise.”

“Anything.”

“You must not tell anyone that a little bird brings you the truth. Then I shall sing more freely.”

The Emperor promised.

The nightingale looked at the jeweled machine upon its cushion.

“Let it stand there,” she said. “It is beautiful in its fashion, though it cannot sing unless someone turns the key. Beauty is not wicked because it is made of jewels; it merely becomes foolish when people demand that jewels should have a heart.”

Then she sang one short, tender phrase and flew out into the paling sky.

The Emperor slept.

When morning came, sunlight entered the chamber. The Emperor awoke strong and rested. He rose, put on his robe, set the crown upon his head, and fastened the golden sword at his side.

Outside, the servants approached on tiptoe. They had come to look upon their dead master and to begin, with proper sadness, their service to the next one.

The First Lord-in-Waiting opened the door. Behind him stood the physicians, ministers, cavaliers, ladies, attendants, and the music-master, who had prepared a speech concerning the solemn suitability of the mechanical bird for funeral occasions.

They peered into the room.

The Emperor stood at the window, alive and upright.

“Good morning,” he said.

No one answered.

And that, you know, was perhaps the first completely honest silence ever heard at court.

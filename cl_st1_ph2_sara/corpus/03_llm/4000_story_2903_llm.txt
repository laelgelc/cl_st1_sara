### On the Tales of Gogol

There are writers whom we admire because they raise nature above herself, and others because they surprise her in undress. Gogol belongs, as far as I can judge, to the latter and less ceremonious family. He does not conduct mankind into a hall illuminated for tragedy, nor into a garden arranged for love; he catches it in an inn-yard, disputing about a wheel, hiding a bribe beneath official papers, or examining with melancholy solicitude the vacancy left by a front tooth. Nothing escapes him—not the greasy collar, the submissive cough, the importance assumed by a provincial secretary when he sharpens his pen. His observation is so exact that one suspects him of having served alternately as clerk, landlord, postmaster, police-agent, and kitchen-maid; yet this universality of experience is perhaps no more mysterious than the eye of a great painter, which finds in a gesture the biography of a man.

His first merit, therefore, is life. His people enter before they are announced; one hears their boots in the passage. A novelist of inferior faculty informs us that his magistrate is vain, his landowner avaricious, his widow credulous; Gogol gives the magistrate a new uniform, the landowner a dispute over three dead serfs, and the widow a belief that her departed husband has returned in the person of a piebald dog. The definition is complete. A single absurdity, rightly chosen, may contain more truth than twenty pages of psychology; and Gogol, when he chooses rightly, possesses the fine pencil of his language with astonishing command.

But selection is more difficult than observation. Nature offers details with the prodigality of a bankrupt giving promises, and the artist must be severe precisely where nature is inexhaustible. Here Gogol’s fidelity becomes indiscretion. Having seen the stain upon a waistcoat, he cannot resist telling us its colour, origin, antiquity, and probable destination; he adds the fly which settles upon it, the hand which brushes away the fly, and the opinion of a bystander concerning the hand. The picture remains truthful, but truth, when made to stand too long for her portrait, grows stiff. Teniers knew that a tavern contains a thousand objects and painted perhaps fifty; a lesser Dutchman paints nine hundred and congratulates himself upon omitting the broom. Gogol is sometimes that lesser Dutchman, though at his best he is very much more than Teniers.

The same want of measure may be observed in his irony. Satire is a weapon whose fitness depends not merely upon its sharpness but upon the adversary against whom it is drawn. To employ a sabre against a fool is barbarous; to tickle a murderer with a feather is impertinent. Gogol’s irony is often too cruel for human weakness and too sportive for human corruption. He pursues a ridiculous man as a hunter pursues a wolf, through swamp and forest, firing after him when the animal is already wounded; but when he encounters a scoundrel, he is sometimes so delighted by the fellow’s inventiveness that indignation yields to curiosity. This is the temptation of every comic genius. Aristophanes could forgive a knave who supplied a good chorus; Rabelais, I should be inclined to believe, would have pardoned the Devil himself if that personage had eaten with sufficient imagination.

For my own part, I confess to an improper liking for energetic rogues. A bandit who scales a convent wall during a thunderstorm interests me more than a virtuous deputy who enters by the door at noon. Literature has profited by this weakness in respectable readers ever since Homer made piracy conversational; Walter Scott understood it, and our newspapers understand it still better. Yet energy is not morality, and picturesqueness is no absolution. Gogol occasionally forgets the distinction. His swindlers shine, while honest men give out a poor provincial light, like candles purchased by the municipality. He does not see men or things in a bright light. Even innocence, when it appears, looks as though it had been badly washed.

This sombre predilection explains the abundance of grotesque forms in his gallery: swollen officials, stupid colonels, litigious neighbours, husbands terrified by their wives, wives who govern estates and husbands with equal economy, petitioners who bow themselves almost double, and police officers who regard the law as a private domestic animal. Callot would have recognized these beings; Balzac would have asked for their incomes. Gogol knows the value of a bent nose or a missing button, and no author has better understood that the body sometimes confesses what the mind attempts to conceal. But grotesqueness, perpetually insisted upon, becomes a uniform; after the tenth monster we begin to suspect the whole province of having been exposed to bad water.

His construction is less certain than his characterization. He starts admirably, proceeds capriciously, and concludes when continuation has become inconvenient. Events do not always arise from one another; they collect, like travellers detained at a post-house, because the horses have not arrived. The dénouement may descend by accident, revelation, arrest, fever, or supernatural intervention; any staircase will serve, provided it leads out of the building. Sterne could make digression itself the road, but Gogol often mistakes a roadside amusement for progress. We forgive him because the inn is entertaining, the host a thief, the soup extraordinary, and one of the travellers has lost his passport; nevertheless, we have not reached our destination.

I hasten to add that probability in narrative is not the same thing as occurrence in life. The most improbable incidents happen daily, especially in countries possessing an elaborate administration; but art cannot exhibit the certificate signed by witnesses. It must persuade. Gogol’s descriptions persuade us absolutely, while his plots sometimes ask for credit. Every room is real; the door leading from one room to another may open upon impossibility. This paradox is particularly apparent in his historical tales, where the furniture, costume, weapons, and feasts have substance, but the passions are occasionally dressed for the theatre.

Take *Taras Bulba*. The Cossack world is presented with a vigour which might have satisfied a chronicler and alarmed a tax-collector. We smell the horses, powder, leather, smoke, and brandy; we see the vast plain, the rude fellowship, the sudden movement of armed men. There are pages which advance like cavalry. Yet the principal figures belong partly to epic, partly to caricature, and partly to melodrama. A father executes a son with the promptitude of Brutus, lovers recognize one another amid famine, cities burn to furnish a background, and death speaks in capital letters. Had the whole been composed in the grand manner, we might submit; had it remained grotesque, we might laugh with confidence. But the passage from buffoonery to pathos is a bridge too lightly built for such heavy emotions.

Pushkin, with his exquisite instinct for proportion, could suggest a whole historical tempest by causing a door to open upon snow. Gogol requires drums, torches, executions, and a population shouting in chorus. Walter Scott also loved a crowd, but he knew how to place an ordinary man among extraordinary events; Gogol too often places an extraordinary man among extraordinary events, and the result is multiplication without increase. Still, the historical tales contain magnificent fragments. Their failure is not poverty but excess; they resemble a banquet at which every dish is seasoned for soldiers.

In the fantastic tale, on the other hand, Gogol’s realism becomes an admirable accomplice of terror. The supernatural is most effective when it does not announce itself. A ghost who enters with blue fire, clanking chains, and a written explanation of his death is merely an actor demanding applause; but let the familiar chair stand a little nearer the bed than it stood yesterday, and philosophy itself may prefer to sleep elsewhere. Gogol understands this in *Viy*. The world at first is coarse, daylighted, almost comic: students are hungry, roads dusty, peasants slow, and supper more important than metaphysics. Then, by gradations too delicate to mark, the natural order loosens. A witch rides through the night; a dead girl lies in a church; a chalk circle becomes the frontier of reason.

The success of the tale lies in the solidity of its earth. The marvellous grows out of reality as a poisonous flower grows out of common soil. We believe the terror because we have believed the hunger. Gogol does not ask us to exchange the visible world for a fantastic one; he merely discovers that the fantastic world has been standing behind the visible, listening through a crack. Read such a story at noon, in a railway carriage, and you may criticize its machinery; read it alone, while wind shakes the casements and a branch taps the glass with the obstinacy of a creditor, and you will postpone criticism until morning. I make this confession without shame. Courage in literature is generally assisted by good lighting.

Other supernatural inventions are less fortunate when allegory becomes too evident or extravagance too deliberate. A nose which abandons its owner and assumes official rank begins with the finest possible insolence; society is exposed by anatomy, and hierarchy acquires a countenance—or rather deprives another man of one. Yet even here Gogol presses his advantage. The first impossibility delights, the fifth requires bookkeeping. Hoffmann, when sober enough to arrange his phantoms, knew that mystery diminishes under explanation; Gogol occasionally examines the marvellous as though it were contraband seized at a customs-house.

His psychological stories cause me greater embarrassment. I have no taste for madness as a literary spectacle. This is, perhaps, a personal infirmity; physicians and novelists appear to find insanity inexhaustible, and medico-legal studies multiply with the confidence of mushrooms after rain. But a mind deprived of its laws cannot easily sustain artistic development. Where every association is possible, none is significant. The madman may believe himself a king, converse with dogs, receive letters from the moon, and prosecute his pillow for treason; surprise soon expires because contradiction has become the rule.

In *Memoirs of a Madman*, there are, undoubtedly, touches of painful truth. The petty clerk’s humiliation, his hunger for rank, his ridiculous love, and his gradual retreat from an intolerable world are observed with terrible precision. At first we laugh: the dogs correspond, social ambition acquires canine confidants, and bureaucracy enters the kennel without appearing out of place. But the laughter turns against us. The poor creature’s delusion ceases to be grotesque and becomes suffering; then Gogol, uncertain whether to pity or exhibit him, increases the anguish until the story resembles a public punishment.

I do not deny its power. I question its justice. The final cry for tenderness is affecting, but it seems obtained by mechanical pressure, as a musician may produce emotion by striking the loudest chord and holding his foot upon the pedal. Madness is not in itself tragic; it becomes tragic through the remnants of reason which resist it, through remembered affection, through the spectator’s sense of a destroyed order. Gogol gives us these remnants, then buries them beneath theatrical misery. A loaded gun upon a table may terrify; ten guns suggest a shop.

Domestic life suits him better, though one might not have expected it. The satirist, accustomed to hunting deformity, is rarely patient with quiet goodness. He detects vanity in hospitality, selfishness in marriage, calculation in tears; if a widow mourns, he examines the will. Human types become his game, and innocence offers poor sport because it does not run. Yet Gogol, in one simple tale of an old-fashioned household, lays aside the whip, the mask, and nearly all the apparatus by which he commonly commands attention. The result, in my opinion, is among the most perfect things he wrote.

Nothing could be less adventurous than the existence of the elderly pair. Their estate is small, their habits immemorial, their conversation composed largely of meals. Cupboards, preserves, pastries, mushrooms, pickled fruits, soft chairs, drowsy servants, and a cat make up their kingdom. The husband is mild, the wife practical and tender; she directs him with the authority that long affection renders almost invisible. They have repeated the same jokes until repetition itself has become a caress. A critic enamoured of events would say that nothing happens. He would be wrong. Time happens.

Every detail contributes to the general effect. The food is not mere decoration, as in some of Gogol’s more gluttonous pages; it is the language in which care expresses itself. “Will you eat?” means “Are you well? Do you still belong to me? May I continue to protect you?” The warm room, the familiar dishes, the doors complaining upon their hinges—all form the material body of a shared life. Love in youth speaks extravagantly because it has no past; love in age may ask only whether the other has taken broth. There is more fidelity in that question than in many oaths pronounced beneath balconies.

Then the wife dies. Gogol does not improve the event with unnecessary thunder. The household continues, as households indecently do; dinner is served, servants whisper, dust gathers, and the widower appears at first almost unchanged. Indeed, he seems to recover a certain careless tranquillity. He eats what is placed before him, smiles vaguely, and forgets what he has been saying. A superficial observer might conclude that habit, rather than love, had joined them, and that habit has merely changed its coat.

But habit was the form his love had taken. Deprived of her governing presence, he does not break like a tragic hero; he loosens. His clothes become neglected, his meals disordered, his thoughts uncertain. He looks toward the place where she sat, not with eloquent despair, but with the bewilderment of a child who has misplaced the world. The decline is gradual, almost comic in its incidents, and therefore profoundly natural. Gogol, who elsewhere piles agony upon agony, here understands that sorrow works most surely through trifles. A forgotten napkin may be more fatal than a dagger.

At length the old man follows her. There is no elaborate reconciliation in heaven, no spectral hand extended through clouds, no orchestra of angels—only the extinction of a life whose meaning had departed before its breath. We then perceive that his apparent insensibility was not absence of grief but grief too complete for display. The tree struck at the root may remain green for a little while; its leaves do not yet know what has happened.

This tale reconciles me to many of Gogol’s faults, because it reveals that beneath the satirist’s severity there existed a tenderness of which he was perhaps distrustful. Here his observation selects instead of accumulates, his irony softens without disappearing, his grotesque details become human, and his loose manner finds unity in feeling. He neither flatters the old couple nor transforms them into saints. They are limited, indolent, provincial, and devoted; their virtues are inseparable from their absurdities, as the fragrance of an old room is inseparable from its faded curtains.

In short, Gogol’s genius is a lantern carried through unclean streets: it illuminates sharply, exaggerates shadows, and seldom persuades us that morning is near. He is strongest when fact and fantasy approach one another quietly, weakest when history puts on melodramatic armour or madness is compelled to perform for our pity. His plots wander; his details encroach; his irony sometimes wounds what it should merely touch. Yet he creates men whom we recognize after one gesture and rooms we remember after many years.

And in that modest household, where an old woman arranges preserves and an old man waits without knowing that he waits, the lantern gives at last a gentle light. Satire falls silent; grotesqueness becomes tenderness; and death, stripped of theatrical properties, proves the depth of a love which life had hidden beneath dinners, repeated jokes, and the daily opening of a familiar door.

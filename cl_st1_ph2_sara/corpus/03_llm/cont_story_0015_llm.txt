The truck was supposed to arrive at four, which meant it came at five-thirty, after we had spent ninety minutes preparing ourselves spiritually for disaster.

My religion at seventeen was Precise Calm. It had one commandment: do not let anybody see the exact size of the thing sitting on your chest.

Precise Calm allowed you to say, “No problem,” when the electric company sent a red envelope. It allowed you to make macaroni for your little sister and call it Italian Night. It allowed you to stand behind the home improvement store in a reflective orange vest, watching rain turn the loading dock black, while your mother’s medicine waited at the pharmacy and your father had been in Ghana for fourteen weeks.

He had said two.

“Trailer’s here,” Reese announced.

He was smoking under the NO SMOKING sign. Reese believed signs were advice from people who had not met him.

Cato rolled his shoulders. “Hercules.”

He said this whenever something required lifting, enduring, or lying to yourself.

“Hercules,” I agreed.

The three of us were the evening unload crew, also known as the Specialists, though only to ourselves. Cato was Weapons and Heavy Artillery. Reese was Intelligence, because he was forty-six and knew which managers were sleeping together. I was Reconnaissance, because I was skinny enough to climb over the merchandise and report what was trying to kill us.

The trailer backed toward the dock with the shrill beeping of a hospital machine. Its rear doors opened. Inside were refrigerators, grills, toilets, lawn mowers, boxed cabinets, and several hundred cartons positioned according to a system invented by men who hated gravity.

Cato peered into the darkness.

“Behold,” he said. “The beast.”

The trailer’s mouth stood open. Its silver guts waited.

We went in.

* * *

When my father told stories, his hands stopped belonging to him.

His right hand became Anansi: small, twitchy, quick, the fingers bent into legs. His left hand became whichever fool had chosen that night to underestimate Anansi. Often it was Lion.

Lion had my father’s deep voice, enlarged. Lion was all stomach and command.

“Bring me food,” Lion would roar, and my little sister, Abena, would scream happily even though she knew Lion was coming. She always knew. Knowing never saved her from delight.

Anansi spoke fast, sometimes in English, sometimes in Twi, sometimes in a language my father claimed was spoken only by spiders and troublesome children.

One night Lion found three rabbit children playing beneath a silk-cotton tree.

“Three?” I asked.

“Three,” my father said.

“Why didn’t their mother watch them?”

My father frowned at me. I was ten and already irritating stories with logistics.

“The mother had gone to gather cassava.”

“She left all three?”

“You want me to tell it, or you want to tell it?”

Abena slapped my arm. “Let Daddy tell.”

My father’s left hand opened wide. Lion’s mouth.

The rabbit children ran. Lion was faster. One, two, three: he swallowed them whole.

Abena gasped.

My father smiled because he had made the world dangerous, and because only he knew how to make it safe again.

* * *

The first thing inside the trailer was a tower of paint cans leaning toward Cato.

I said, “Left.”

He moved right.

“Your other left.”

The cans trembled. Cato put both palms against them and held.

“Hercules,” he whispered.

Cato had run the two hundred meters in twenty-one-point-something when he was a junior. His picture still lived in the trophy case at our school, although he himself had graduated three years before and now lived mainly in receiving. In the photograph, he was crossing a finish line with his mouth open, all tendon and triumph. At work he wore a black brace around his left knee and moved carefully until something began to fall. Then his body remembered being fast.

I slid the pallet jack under the paint. Reese guided the forks.

“Easy, Professor,” he told me.

He called me Professor because I had been accepted to colleges, a fact he repeated more faithfully than I did.

The pallet shifted. Three cans rolled loose. Cato caught one against his chest, trapped one with his boot, and let the third fall.

It hit the trailer floor without bursting.

We stared at it.

“That,” Reese said, “is why the Lord invented underpaid youth.”

My religion had no official relationship with the Lord, but I accepted the miracle.

We dragged the pallet out. Rain misted through the dock opening and cooled the sweat on my neck. My phone buzzed in my pocket. I hoped it was my father, then hated myself for hoping, then hoped harder.

It was Abena.

MOM SAYS GET MILK.

A second message arrived.

AND NOT THE WEIRD KIND.

The weird kind was powdered.

I wrote: OK.

There was food in our refrigerator because of me. Milk, when available, was also because of me. This thought sometimes made me stand taller. Sometimes it made me want to lie down in traffic.

Precise Calm had no position on which.

* * *

My father left on a Sunday morning in February.

He wore a blue shirt and the cologne my mother liked, the one that entered a room before he did and remained after he left. His suitcase stood by the door. It was the large brown one, which worried me. A two-week trip should not require a suitcase that could hold a smaller person.

“Your grandmother has business with the land,” he said.

“What land?”

“Our land.”

We had never visited this land. It appeared in conversations the way kingdoms appear in fairy tales: vast, disputed, requiring money.

My mother sat at the kitchen table with both hands around a mug. Her wrists looked too thin. The treatment made her nauseous in the mornings and tired in the afternoons, but she still dressed for work three days a week at the daycare, where other people paid her to watch their children.

“Two weeks,” my father said.

He said it to all of us, but he looked at me.

“Help your mother.”

I was eating cereal. The spoon stopped halfway to my mouth.

It is possible to receive an order and an abandonment in the same sentence.

“Okay,” I said.

He squeezed Abena until she complained, kissed my mother’s forehead, and put a hand on the back of my neck. He did not hug me. We had reached the age when fathers and sons exchanged brief, coded pressures, as if affection were information that might be intercepted.

“You are the man here,” he said.

My mother’s eyes moved toward him. Only for a second.

He took the suitcase outside.

Two weeks later, my mother made jollof because he was coming home. She seasoned the chicken and told Abena to clean the living room. I kept checking the street through the blinds.

At eleven, my mother put the food away.

“He missed his connection,” she said.

She said it calmly. Calm was her inheritance to me.

The next morning, his phone went straight to voicemail.

* * *

Lion slept after his meal, his belly round beneath the silk-cotton tree.

Inside him, the three rabbit children cried.

“Can’t they just climb out?” Abena asked.

“Through where?” I said.

She considered this and hid her face in a cushion.

Anansi came walking along. My father’s fingers traveled over his knee, light and quick.

He heard the rabbit children singing from Lion’s stomach:

Anansi, Anansi, clever and small,  
Lion has eaten us, fur, feet and all.

“That doesn’t rhyme,” I said.

“It rhymes in Twi.”

“You said it in English.”

My father gave me a look. In that look I was not merely wrong; I was exhausting centuries.

Anansi climbed onto Lion’s nose and shouted, “Wake up, great king!”

Lion opened one eye.

“Who disturbs me?”

“Only your servant, who has come to warn you.”

“Warn me of what?”

“A terrible sickness. It begins with the swallowing of rabbits.”

Lion sat up.

My father sat up, too. Abena clutched my arm. His stories used our bodies as furniture.

“I have swallowed no rabbits,” Lion said.

From his stomach came three small voices: “Liar!”

Lion slapped both paws over his belly.

Anansi pretended not to hear.

* * *

By April, the mortgage company had called enough times that I recognized the number.

My mother stopped answering. I started.

I became a courteous middle-aged man on the telephone.

“Yes, I understand.”

“No, sir, she is not available.”

“Yes, we are aware of the amount.”

“When can you expect payment?” the man asked.

Behind me, Abena was trying to microwave a fork.

“Soon,” I said.

Soon was a useful word. My father had gone to Ghana soon ago. He would return soon. We would pay soon. My mother would feel better soon. Soon had no legal definition and required no down payment.

I hung up and confiscated the fork.

“You can’t put metal in there.”

“I wasn’t going to turn it on.”

“Then why put it in?”

She shrugged. At twelve, Abena regarded causes and consequences as unfriendly rumors.

There had been one call from my father, nine days after he was supposed to return. The connection crackled as if he were speaking from inside a frying pan.

“The matter is complicated,” he said.

“What matter?”

“The land. There are documents.”

Mom was asleep on the couch, one arm over her eyes.

“When are you coming back?”

“Soon.”

“We need—”

The line broke.

I waited for him to call again. He did not.

After that came a message saying his phone had been stolen. Then an email from an address I did not recognize: I am handling things. Be patient. Tell your mother not to worry.

I did not tell her that part. My mother was already performing not worrying full-time.

At school, acceptance letters arrived.

The state university gave me a blue folder with a photograph of students lying on grass, as if college were an organized program of outdoor reclining. A private college two hours away offered aid but not enough. Another school offered enough but was six hours away, which made the amount seem like an accusation.

My guidance counselor called me into her office.

“You haven’t deposited anywhere.”

“I’m comparing packages.”

“The deadline is Friday.”

“I like to be thorough.”

Precise Calm sounded a lot like intelligence if you wore a collared shirt.

She pushed her glasses up. “Is something happening at home?”

“No.”

A small word. Strong enough to hold up a roof, if nobody touched it.

* * *

Cato got pinned by a washing machine on a Thursday.

It happened near the back of the trailer. We had unloaded vanities, lumber, garden chemicals, and a mountain of ceiling fans. The deeper we went, the hotter it became. The trailer smelled of cardboard, dust, metal, and the trapped breath of factories.

The washing machines were stacked two high.

“Bad geometry,” I said.

Reese wiped his face with his vest. “Everything in this building is bad geometry.”

We loosened the straps. The top machine shifted.

There are moments when an object becomes an animal. You see intention in its movement. That washing machine wanted Cato.

He shoved me backward.

The machine tipped, struck his shoulder, and drove him against the wall. His knee folded.

He made a sound I had never heard from him.

Reese and I pushed. The machine weighed more than the combined value of our lives according to payroll, but we moved it enough for Cato to slide free.

He sat on the trailer floor, breathing through his teeth.

“You good?” I asked.

It was a stupid question, but workplaces provide only three responses to injury: You good? Walk it off. Don’t tell management.

Cato looked at his knee.

“Hercules,” he said faintly.

The manager wanted him to finish the shift after ice.

Reese became very still.

Now, Reese had said things about immigrants, women, vegetarians, people from California, people who drove electric cars, and one particular Polish family who had purchased the wrong mulch. Morally, he was a house where several rooms had caught fire. But when the manager suggested Cato could return to work, Reese stepped close enough that the manager looked at his shoes.

“He’s going to urgent care,” Reese said.

“We’re short tonight.”

“You can be shorter.”

Reese drove him.

I stayed because I needed the hours.

For the rest of the shift, I unloaded alone with a temporary worker named Kyle who kept disappearing to vape. I moved toilets and doors. I dragged a pallet of tile that resisted like an anchor. By closing, my arms shook.

At the bus stop, I imagined the washing machine falling on me instead. I imagined not moving fast enough. I imagined my mother getting a call and having to choose whether to wake Abena before going to the hospital.

Then I imagined finding a baby behind a row of refrigerators.

This was a regular fantasy.

The baby’s name was Cristie, because that was printed on a torn shipping label I had once found inside a trailer. She would be wrapped in bubble plastic, unharmed and cheerful. The Specialists would raise her in secret. Cato would teach her to run correctly, once his knee healed. Reese would teach her every word she was not allowed to say at school. I would read to her in the break room and explain algebra.

We would keep diapers behind the insulation and formula in the manager’s refrigerator. During overnight inventory, Cristie would sleep in a display crib in Seasonal. Customers would wonder why three exhausted men kept buying tiny socks.

In later versions of the fantasy, we took turns bringing her home. She learned to call all of us Dad.

This was funny until it wasn’t.

The bus came. I climbed aboard and paid with money I had earned carrying machines that could crush me. Heroic music did not play.

* * *

Lion narrowed his eyes at Anansi.

“What sickness?”

“A royal sickness,” Anansi said. “Too grand for goats. Too powerful for antelope. It afflicts only kings who swallow three rabbit children before sunset.”

Lion’s ears flattened.

“What is the cure?”

Anansi paced across my father’s arm.

“The cure is difficult.”

“I am Lion. Nothing is difficult.”

“You must open your mouth.”

Lion opened it.

“Wider.”

Lion opened wider.

“Wider still.”

My father opened his own mouth so wide Abena squealed. His gold tooth flashed. He had broken the original tooth playing football when he was young, though he claimed different opponents depending on the audience: a boy from Accra, a soldier, a goat.

Anansi peered into Lion’s throat.

“Very serious,” he said. “I see rabbit fever.”

The three children shouted from below, “We are not fever!”

“Hush,” Anansi whispered. “Do you want saving or do you want accuracy?”

I laughed hardest at that line. My father noticed.

Being noticed by him was like having sunlight choose you.

* * *

Cato returned three days later with a new brace and doctor’s orders forbidding lifting.

The manager assigned him to inventory, which meant he walked around with a scanner and lied about not lifting.

We ate lunch on overturned buckets behind Receiving. Reese had a meatball sub. Cato had chips. I had crackers from the vending machine because I had used lunch money for milk.

Cato pointed at my crackers. “That’s not food.”

“It has a nutrition label.”

“So does dog food.”

“Luxury dog food is expensive.”

Reese chewed and looked at me. “You decide on college?”

“Working on it.”

“Work faster.”

“I may take a year.”

Cato stopped eating.

Reese said, “No.”

I laughed. “Thank you, admissions committee.”

“I’m serious.”

“So am I.”

“You got accepted.”

“I also got bills.”

Reese waved one thick hand. “Everybody got bills.”

“Not everybody’s are mine.”

The words came out sharper than Precise Calm permitted.

Cato leaned forward, forearms on his knees. “You take a year, this place eats it. Then another. Soon you’re thirty and arguing with Gary about overtime.”

From inside the building came the beep of a forklift reversing.

“I’m not staying forever.”

“I said that,” Cato replied.

He did not sound tragic. That made it worse.

Reese wrapped the rest of his sandwich and shoved it toward me.

“I don’t want that.”

“Didn’t ask.”

He lit a cigarette.

After a while he said, “Your old man still overseas?”

“Yes.”

“When’s he back?”

“Soon.”

Reese blew smoke toward the dumpsters. “Right.”

That right contained everything I did not let people say.

I ate the sandwich.

* * *

My father had disappeared before, but only in smaller units.

An evening. A weekend. Once, five days.

He would come home carrying explanations and sometimes gifts. A wooden bracelet for Mom. A football jersey for me. A doll for Abena whose eyelids did not close at the same time.

My mother never shouted when he returned. Her calm was more frightening than shouting. She would stand at the sink while he spoke about helping a friend or fixing a problem or needing space to think.

“Space,” she once said, washing the same plate for several minutes. “You have found much of it.”

Later he took us for ice cream. He let Abena order two scoops and told an Anansi story in the car. By the time we came home, we were laughing. My mother was not.

I used to think this meant she did not understand forgiveness.

Now I understood she was tired of being made into an audience.

Still, my father could cook eggs with peppers the exact way I liked them. He attended every one of my middle-school award ceremonies and whistled when my name was called. He taught me to change a tire, to greet elders properly, to never allow someone to insult Ghanaian football in my presence unless I knew how many people were with them.

He made Abena and me stars in his stories.

We were hunters, queens, children born carrying golden drums. We crossed the Togo Mountains in one night. We answered riddles that defeated chiefs. Whenever danger came, he leaned close and asked, “And who can save them now?”

We shouted our own names.

This was the trouble. He had taught us to expect rescue in his voice.

* * *

At the end of April, my mother fainted in the kitchen.

She did not fall dramatically. She placed one hand on the counter, missed the chair, and folded down as if obeying careful instructions.

Abena screamed.

I called the ambulance. I found Mom’s insurance card. I packed her medications in a plastic grocery bag. In the emergency room, I answered questions while Abena sat with her knees tucked beneath her chin.

“Spouse?” the nurse asked.

“Out of the country.”

“Can he be reached?”

“No.”

My mother opened her eyes.

“He is in Ghana,” she whispered, as if clarifying made it respectable.

They kept her overnight for dehydration and adjusted two prescriptions. The copay erased most of that week’s paycheck.

On the bus home the next morning, Abena slept against my shoulder. Her hair smelled like hospital air and coconut oil. I looked at our reflections in the window: one child pretending to be unconscious, one pretending not to be a child.

At home, the mailbox held a foreclosure warning.

I put it beneath my mattress.

There was food in the fridge because of me.

My mother was home because I had called.

My sister was safe because I had watched her.

My father: elsewhere.

I felt powerful. I felt robbed. Mostly I needed sleep.

* * *

Anansi asked Lion to lie on his back.

“To cure rabbit fever,” he said, “you must remain completely still.”

Lion lay down.

“Now I need a vine.”

“What for?”

“To pull out the sickness.”

Anansi tied one end of the vine to a tree. He carried the other end across Lion’s teeth and told him to bite.

“Hard,” Anansi said.

Lion bit.

Anansi pulled the vine tight, forcing Lion’s mouth to remain open.

My father spread his fingers. His left hand became a cage of teeth. His right hand crept toward it.

“Then Anansi entered Lion’s mouth,” he said quietly.

Abena said, “No.”

“Anansi is brave,” my father said.

“Anansi is stupid,” I said.

“Sometimes these are cousins.”

He made Anansi descend past the tongue, down the throat, into the enormous dark of Lion.

Inside Lion’s stomach sat the three rabbit children, wet, frightened, and offended.

“You took long enough,” said the oldest.

“Do you want saving,” Anansi asked, “or do you want to complain?”

“Both,” said the middle one.

“That is reasonable,” Anansi said.

* * *

Graduation was one week away when the truck did not come.

No truck meant we were assigned to the sales floor, a place of unnatural brightness where customers asked questions such as, “Where are your nails?” while standing beneath a sign that said NAILS.

I was straightening paint samples when Reese appeared.

“Professor.”

He jerked his head toward the end of the aisle.

A man stood there in a blue button-down shirt. He was thinner than I remembered, or maybe I had built him larger during his absence. He had more gray in his beard. Beside his shoes sat the brown suitcase.

My father.

For one second my mind rejected him as a badly placed object. He belonged at the airport, at our front door, in an argument with my mother. Not between Interior Satin and Weather-Resistant Exterior.

He smiled.

It was not a big smile. He had enough sense for that.

“Kojo,” he said.

Cato came around the corner carrying a scanner. He saw my face, then saw the man. Cato’s expression changed.

My father walked toward me.

Three and a half months late.

He put his hand on my shoulder.

His hand was warm. Familiar pressure. The old code.

“My son.”

I had prepared speeches.

On buses, in trailers, during chemistry, while lying awake listening to my mother cough, I had addressed him with the disciplined fury of a lawyer in the final scene of a movie. I told him about the mortgage company and the ambulance. I held up pharmacy receipts. I made him look at Abena’s face when the phone rang. I explained what soon meant when rent was due.

In some versions I punched him.

In others I refused to speak.

In the most satisfying version, I walked past him carrying an enormous refrigerator by myself.

Now he stood in front of me, thinner and older, smelling of travel and that same cologne.

“Dad,” I said.

That was all.

His fingers tightened on my shoulder.

“I came directly here,” he said. “Your mother told me you were working.”

Of course he had spoken to her first. The idea hurt and relieved me simultaneously.

“You have grown,” he said.

I had not. Not in inches.

Behind him, Reese pretended to study caulk. Cato did not pretend anything.

My father looked at my vest, my work gloves, the dust on my pants. Pride entered his face as if he had discovered something he had planted.

“You are helping the family.”

There was food in the refrigerator because of me.

Mom’s prescriptions were paid because of me.

Abena’s field-trip form had twenty dollars clipped to it because of me.

I wanted to tell him he did not get to be proud. I wanted him to be proud. I wanted him to apologize in the paint aisle until the store closed and the lights went out and we were left among the gallons of color with nowhere to hide.

Instead I said, “Yeah.”

His hand remained on my shoulder. Affectionate. Insufficient.

* * *

Anansi could not lead the rabbit children back through Lion’s throat because the passage was too narrow.

“What will they do?” Abena asked.

“You will see,” my father said.

Anansi searched Lion’s stomach. There he found everything Lion had swallowed without thinking: bones, bells, half a drum, a hunter’s sandal, two cooking pots, a spearhead, seven stones, and a gourd filled with palm oil that somehow had not spilled.

“Why not?” I asked.

“Magic gourd.”

“Convenient.”

“All magic is convenient.”

Anansi poured the oil along the walls of Lion’s stomach until everything became slick.

Then he instructed the rabbit children to climb into one cooking pot. Anansi climbed into the other.

“Now,” he shouted, “we make Lion angry.”

They banged the pots against Lion’s ribs.

Boom. Boom. Boom.

My father drummed his fists against his chest.

Lion roared. The vine held his jaws open. The rabbits banged harder. Anansi jabbed the inside of Lion’s stomach with the spearhead.

Lion coughed.

The first pot shot upward and out of his mouth, carrying the three rabbit children into the grass.

Lion coughed again.

Anansi flew out in the second pot and struck the silk-cotton tree.

Lion snapped the vine and leaped to his feet.

“Anansi!”

But Anansi had climbed high into the branches. The rabbit children were already running home.

“You have cheated me!” Lion shouted.

“No,” Anansi called down. “Your appetite cheated you. I only introduced you.”

My father loved that ending. His teeth showed. His hands returned to being hands.

“And Lion?” Abena asked.

“Lion went hungry.”

“What about the rabbits?”

“They reached their mother.”

“Did she punish them?”

“First she held them,” my father said. “Then she punished them.”

I asked, “What happened to Anansi?”

My father leaned back on the couch.

“Anansi went home.”

* * *

At the store, my father glanced toward Reese and Cato.

“These are your friends?”

“Coworkers.”

Cato stepped forward first. He held out his hand.

“Cato.”

My father shook it.

“Thank you for looking after my son.”

Cato looked at me, then back at him. “He looks after us.”

Reese came next, wiping his palm on his jeans before offering it.

“Reese.”

“Pleasure.”

Reese nodded once. His face revealed nothing. For perhaps the first time in his life, he kept every opinion indoors.

My father said, “I have heard he will attend university.”

The aisle seemed to narrow.

“I’ve been accepted,” I said.

“You will go,” my father replied.

He said it with his old authority, as if my future had been waiting for his signature. Anger moved through me, clean and hot. Also gratitude, which was worse.

“How?” I asked.

It was the first true thing I had said.

His eyes shifted. Only slightly.

“We will discuss it at home.”

That meant there were no answers suitable for paint.

A customer entered the aisle, pushing a cart containing one tiny brush. He looked at our group and reversed out.

My father removed his hand from my shoulder. The place where it had rested felt colder.

“There were problems,” he said. “With the family land. Your uncle—”

I nearly laughed. The family land. The mythical kingdom. Acres of complicated soil powerful enough to erase phone calls.

He stopped.

Perhaps my face had spoken.

“I should have called more,” he said.

This was not enough. It was something.

Cato’s scanner beeped. Reese studied the ceiling.

My father looked tired. Not story tired, when he leaned back and asked Abena to bring water. Truly tired. There were hollows beneath his eyes, and one cuff of his shirt had torn. I noticed these details against my will.

Precise Calm instructed me to inventory facts:

He had left.

He had returned.

The bills remained.

I loved him.

I was angry.

No fact canceled another.

My manager appeared at the far end of the aisle. He saw us and made the expression managers make when human life interferes with scheduling.

“You need something?” he called.

“My father,” I said.

The manager waited, perhaps expecting the rest of the sentence.

My father: what?

Back from Ghana. Gone for months. Teller of stories. Owner of land. Eater of time. A man with a torn cuff and an inadequate hand.

“My father is here,” I finished.

The manager looked at the suitcase.

“You can take five.”

Five minutes for resurrection.

We walked toward the garden-center doors. Outside, the rain had stopped, leaving the parking lot washed and shining. My father’s car was parked crookedly near the contractor entrance. Our old car. I had imagined it being repossessed, stolen, abandoned at the airport. There it was.

He picked up the suitcase.

“I brought things for you and Abena,” he said.

I wondered what object could survive the distance between us. A shirt. A book. A wooden spider. An explanation folded small enough to fit between socks.

“You graduate next week,” he said.

“Saturday.”

“I will be there.”

I looked at him.

He understood. His mouth tightened.

“I will be there,” he repeated, quieter.

Through the glass doors, I could see Cato and Reese pretending not to watch. Cato raised one fist near his chest.

Hercules.

My father took out his keys.

“Do you need a ride home?”

The bus would come in forty minutes. If it came. My shift was not over, but Reese would cover me. Cato would tell the manager there had been a family emergency, which was true three months late.

I could have said no.

I could have made him wait. I could have chosen the bus, the long route, the hard plastic seat, the life I had managed without him. For months I had been carrying boxes out of dark places. I had learned weight, leverage, how to brace my feet, how to keep my hands clear when a load began to fall.

But I was tired.

I wanted to see my mother’s face when he walked through the door. I wanted to hear Abena shout. I wanted, against evidence and dignity, the end of the story.

My father opened the passenger door.

“Yes,” I said. “I do.”

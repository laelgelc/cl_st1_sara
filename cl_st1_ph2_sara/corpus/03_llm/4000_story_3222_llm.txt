The white monument to St. Alban stands on the ridge above Blackwater, where the wooded hills draw apart and the river, dark and creeping, turns toward the sea.

From the terrace of Sir Henry Marquis’s house we could see it between two ancient oaks. The last light of evening touched the shaft, so that it shone against the purple distance like a needle of ivory. Below us the lawn descended in quiet green steps; beyond it the river moved under the willows; and somewhere in the house a clock struck six with that solemn, unhurried note which belongs to old houses and settled lives.

Sir Henry put down his cup.

“You are looking at St. Alban’s monument,” he said.

I admitted it. Indeed, I had been regarding the thing for several minutes.

“It is a noble position,” I said. “One understands why they chose it.”

“One understands why they chose the inscription,” replied Sir Henry, “but not, perhaps, why they chose the man.”

He was an imposing figure even in repose. Age had whitened his hair without diminishing the breadth of his shoulders or the extraordinary authority of his face. He sat upright in the wicker chair, one hand resting upon its arm, the other beside the silver tea service. Nothing in his manner suggested emotion; yet I had learned that when his fingers became quite still, his mind had entered upon some grave country.

The inscription was known to every schoolboy:

DON’T THREATEN, FIRE IF YOU LIKE!

Beneath it stood the name of St. Alban and the date of the celebrated encounter in the Channel.

“You mean the circumstances of his death?” I asked. “But that mystery was never cleared up.”

Sir Henry looked at me.

“There was no mystery.”

“He was found shot in his library.”

“Yes.”

“The windows were open, the pistol lay beneath the curtain, and there were footprints on the gravel.”

“Yes,” said Sir Henry. “The windows were opened afterward, the pistol was pushed away with a walking stick, and the footprints belonged to his brother-in-law.”

I stared at him.

“Then you know who killed him?”

“Nobody killed him.”

The white needle on the ridge seemed suddenly less remote.

“St. Alban killed himself,” Sir Henry continued. “His family altered the room. They meant well. Families commonly mean well when they begin to manufacture evidence.”

“But the police—”

“The police discovered enough to suspect the truth and not enough to compel its publication. The Home Office desired no scandal. England had need of St. Alban as he appeared to be, not as he was. There had been speeches, recruiting posters, school prizes, a destroyer named for him, and half the pulpits in the kingdom had preached upon his words. One does not easily indict a stained-glass window.”

“My word!” I said. “Then the whole thing was arranged?”

“The death, yes. The legend, no. Legends are not arranged. They arise when an accident satisfies a national desire.”

He turned his head toward the memorial.

“Every visible impression of the thing was wrong. The murder was a suicide. The German chivalry was calculated cruelty. The heroic defiance was the speech of a man scarcely conscious of what he said. And St. Alban’s glory—his immense, resounding glory—was the outward shape of an inward ruin.”

For a moment there was no sound but the faint stirring of leaves over the terrace.

“I cannot connect these things,” I said.

“Naturally. They were kept disconnected. You know the public story?”

“Only as everyone knows it.”

“Then tell it.”

I repeated the familiar account. During the war St. Alban, returning wounded from France in a hospital transport, had been stopped in the Channel by a German submarine. Its commander, Captain Plutonburg, boarded the vessel, terrorized the wounded, and finally levelled a pistol at St. Alban. The Englishman, pale from loss of blood but perfectly composed, had replied, “Don’t threaten, fire if you like!” Overawed by this magnificent courage, Plutonburg had lowered his weapon and departed without sinking the transport.

Sir Henry listened with his eyes half closed.

“That,” he said, “is the scene in bronze. It has simplicity, balance and a phrase. Herodotus could have used it. Rugby could teach it. The English people could understand it. A wounded man, a Hun with a pistol, and six words of defiance. It was perfect.”

“And untrue?”

“Not in its facts. That is the devil of it. The submarine stopped the transport. Plutonburg came aboard. He drew a pistol. St. Alban spoke those words. Plutonburg went away. Every fact was true, and the truth was false.”

He rose, crossed the terrace, and stood with his back to me, looking down toward the river.

“Do you remember,” he said, “the first assault on the Somme?”

“No Englishman is likely to forget it.”

“The scale of the preparation was beyond anything the world had then seen. Roads had been laid, railways extended, dumps established, guns massed, divisions moved by night, and plans multiplied through that prodigious machine which modern war calls secrecy because ten thousand clerks are warned not to talk. Yet the Germans knew. They knew the sectors, the concentrations, the probable hour, the arrangement of supports, even certain changes made late in the programme. When our men went forward, the enemy was waiting.”

“There were many explanations.”

“There are always many explanations for a catastrophe. Bad wire. Bad shells. Deep dugouts. Weather. Staff work. The anger of Chemosh, the jealousy of Beelzebub, the gods of the seas and the stupidity of generals. Every explanation contained something. None contained the map.”

“The map?”

Sir Henry returned to his chair.

“Now we come to the first of the unjointed segments. St. Alban had devoted the better part of two years to German espionage.”

“I thought he was attached to the diplomatic service.”

“That was convenient. In reality he worked with us. He had money, languages, courage, position and the invaluable appearance of a man whom no practical concern could greatly disturb. He could dine with an ambassador, play cards with a neutral shipowner, and spend the following morning in a dockside café questioning a waiter. He broke the Hollmann circle in Liverpool. He traced the wireless station at Haversham. He exposed three passport routes through Switzerland. He understood espionage, or believed he did, as a surgeon understands an infection.”

Sir Henry’s mouth hardened.

“That belief was important. A man may endure misfortune. He may even endure disgrace. What he cannot always endure is to become the instrument of the evil he has built his identity upon defeating.”

“You mean that St. Alban betrayed the Somme plans?”

“I mean that he carried them to Plutonburg.”

The statement was so monstrous that I found no immediate reply.

Sir Henry poured himself a little cold tea and did not drink it.

“In May,” he continued, “St. Alban went to France because there had been indications of a leak near one of the planning departments. An agent in Paris, a woman using the name Madame Lorée, had obtained access to officers through a private convalescent home. Nothing could be proved. St. Alban moved between Paris, Amiens and the headquarters behind the line. He reported that the danger had been removed.”

“Had it?”

“No. The danger had seen him.”

The sun had fallen behind the ridge, but the monument still held a colourless gleam.

“On his return journey,” said Sir Henry, “St. Alban stopped near Abbeville to inspect papers belonging to a suspected courier. There was an air raid. A window broke, and a splinter of glass cut his left arm. It was a trifling wound—long, shallow and bloody enough to seem more serious than it was. He went to a dressing station. A nurse cleaned the cut and bandaged it.”

“The agent?”

“Not the woman herself. One of her people. A decent-looking creature in white, with tired eyes and capable hands. It is astonishing what villainy may pass under a clean cuff.”

“But how could a nurse—”

“Wait.”

Sir Henry raised one finger.

“St. Alban continued to Boulogne. He felt feverish. That was partly fatigue, partly a drug put upon the dressing. He was placed aboard the hospital transport Inverly, bound for Southampton with wounded officers and men. The sea was choppy. A wet wind came out of the west, and the vessel rolled badly among the short grey ridges. St. Alban lay in a deck chair under blankets, his left arm in a sling. Witnesses afterward described him as pale, distracted and curiously slow in his replies.”

“The drug?”

“The drug—and more than the drug, as you shall see.”

The footman came through the long window and removed the tea things. Sir Henry waited until the man had gone. Then he drew his chair closer, as though the pleasant emptiness of the terrace might overhear us.

“At a quarter past three,” he said, “the lookout observed what he took for a spar. It disappeared, returned, and lengthened into the low black whaleback of a submarine. The Inverly could neither run nor fight. She stopped. The submarine came alongside, and Plutonburg boarded her with six men.”

I knew the famous photographs of the German commander: the heavy jaw, the cropped skull, the scar descending from the ear toward the mouth. But as Sir Henry spoke, the living man emerged from those blurred newspaper images.

“Plutonburg,” he said, “was not the theatrical savage invented afterward. He did not shout. He did not threaten the nurses. He had the manners of a provincial banker and the face of an iron idol dug up from the altar of Attila. His eyes were small, pale and patient. When he smiled, the scar pulled one side of his mouth upward. It was not a smile to reassure a Christian.

“He asked for the captain’s papers. He inspected the wireless room. Then, without hesitation, he crossed the crowded deck to the precise place where St. Alban lay.”

“Then he knew St. Alban was aboard?”

“He had received a signal from Paris before the Inverly sailed.”

Sir Henry paused.

“Consider the tableau. The wounded men crowded along the rails. The nurses in their white caps. The grey water rising and dropping beyond them. The submarine grinding against the transport’s side like some long sea beast. And in the centre St. Alban, with his livid face and bandaged arm, looking up at a man who had come through thirty miles of dangerous water expressly to see him.

“Plutonburg saluted.

“‘You are injured, Sir St. Alban?’

“St. Alban said that it was nothing.

“‘Nevertheless,’ replied Plutonburg, ‘a wound must be properly dressed.’

“He called to the ship’s surgeon. The surgeon came forward, but Plutonburg waved him back. He produced a knife, cut the outer folds of the bandage, and began to unwind them.”

“Before everybody?”

“Before everybody. That was essential.”

Sir Henry leaned toward me.

“The bandage had a double lining. Between two layers of prepared linen lay a sheet of silk, thin as a cigarette paper and printed by a photographic process. On it was a detailed map of the Allied positions for the opening attack—gun batteries, ammunition depots, assembly trenches, routes of advance and the disposition of reserves.”

I looked again at the white shaft on the hill.

“The bandage,” I said.

“The bandage,” repeated Sir Henry. “That trivial white rag joined Paris to the Somme, St. Alban to Plutonburg, private agony to public disaster. All the armies of England and France moved behind it. The decisive secret of the coming battle was wrapped round the arm of the man charged with preventing its betrayal.”

“But St. Alban could not have known.”

“He did not. That made no difference to him.”

“It made every difference!”

“To the law, certainly. To his own conscience, none.”

Sir Henry’s voice remained level, but his fingers closed upon the arm of his chair.

“Plutonburg held the silk open. The wind fluttered it between his hands. Then he looked at St. Alban and grinned.

“St. Alban had spent two years hunting the German intelligence service. He knew at once what the markings meant. He knew their value. He knew that the woman at the dressing station had selected him because his position, his honour and his very vigilance made him the safest courier in Europe. Had they hidden the map in the coat of a doubtful man, he might have been searched. Had they entrusted it to an agent, the agent might have been followed. But who would examine the surgical dressing of Sir St. Alban?”

“Satanic!” I said.

“Yes. And simple. The devil’s best devices commonly are.”

“But why did Plutonburg reveal it on deck? Why not remove the bandage privately?”

“Because the map was only half his purpose.”

Sir Henry rose again. The evening had deepened, and his tall figure stood black against the western sky.

“He wished to destroy St. Alban. Not merely to defeat him, not merely to secure the plans, but to crush the man’s soul under the knowledge of what he had done. Plutonburg understood the English temperament imperfectly, yet in this instance he judged St. Alban with precision. He believed St. Alban would conceal the affair. If he confessed, he must admit that the enemy’s greatest intelligence success had been carried under his own sleeve. If he remained silent, he would live knowing that the failure on the Somme was partly fastened about his arm.”

“And so Plutonburg spared the ship?”

“It was all grim calculation!”

The words struck across the quiet terrace.

“He could have sunk the Inverly. He did not. Dead men cannot suffer humiliation. He could have taken St. Alban prisoner. He did not. Prison would have removed him from the consequences. He left him alive, free and honoured, because honour itself was to be the instrument.”

Sir Henry resumed his seat.

“St. Alban sat there in a mental blur. The drug had slowed his body; the revelation stunned his mind. One witness said he kept looking from the strip of silk to the torn bandage as though the two things could not belong to the same world. Another remembered that he rubbed the fingers of his right hand against his sleeve. He was not thinking of death. He was scarcely thinking at all.

“Plutonburg folded the map and put it inside his coat. Then he drew his pistol.”

“Why?”

“For the scene. Perhaps also to test him. The pistol was held loosely, but its muzzle pointed at St. Alban’s breast.

“‘It would be better for you,’ Plutonburg said, ‘if I fired.’

“St. Alban looked up.

“‘Don’t threaten,’ he said. ‘Fire if you like.’”

Sir Henry repeated the words without emphasis, and for that reason they sounded more terrible than they had ever sounded from a platform.

“Everyone heard them. The captain heard them. The surgeon heard them. The wounded men heard them. One young subaltern began to cheer until a nurse stopped him.

“Plutonburg stared at St. Alban. Then that crooked grin came over his face. He replaced the pistol, bowed, and returned to the submarine.”

“And they thought—”

“They thought courage had conquered barbarism. What else could they think? They had not seen the map clearly. Most supposed that Plutonburg had removed some private paper concealed for safety in the bandage. The surgeon believed the German had been searching for money or codes. Only St. Alban and Plutonburg understood.

“The submarine moved away without firing. At sunset a destroyer found the Inverly. Before midnight the story of St. Alban’s words had passed by wireless to the Admiralty. Within forty-eight hours it was in every newspaper in England.”

“And St. Alban said nothing?”

“Not to the newspapers.”

Sir Henry’s eyes turned toward the ridge.

“Plutonburg’s calculation failed at one point. He thought shame would keep St. Alban silent. He mistook him there. St. Alban reached Southampton in collapse. The moment he could stand, he demanded to see an intelligence officer. He told the whole story. He named the dressing station, described the false nurse, reconstructed his journey and submitted to every question we could devise. He did not spare himself by one syllable.”

“Then the authorities knew before the attack?”

“They knew that the map was gone. But military operations are not a carriage one turns about in a lane. The preparations were colossal, the political commitments fixed, the machinery already moving. Changes were made. Batteries shifted. Certain units were redirected. But to redesign the entire offensive would have advertised our knowledge and cost months that France did not possess.”

Sir Henry spoke slowly.

“The attack went forward. The Germans were ready.”

I thought of the familiar figures, those rows of numbers printed in histories with the neatness of accounts. Beneath Sir Henry’s words they became men waiting in chalk trenches while dawn gathered over the wire.

“Did St. Alban know the result?” I asked.

“He read every casualty list.”

The answer was simple and ghastly.

“England meanwhile made him a hero. His photograph appeared in shop windows. Children repeated his words. The King sent an inquiry. Committees requested speeches. A popular actress recited a poem about him under the tragic muse in gilt cardboard. Each tribute was a fresh turn of the screw.”

“Could not the Government stop it?”

“On what ground? We could not publish the truth while the war continued. Nor could we publicly discourage a tale so useful to morale without creating suspicion. England had been shaken. Here was a man, wounded and unarmed, who had faced a German pistol and said, ‘Don’t threaten, fire if you like!’ The words travelled because the nation needed them.”

“But he really had shown courage.”

“Perhaps. Courage is not always conscious. A drowning man may strike at a rope; a stunned man may speak with the voice of his deepest habit. St. Alban did not calculate the phrase. It came out of him. It may have been the old school, the old pride, Rugby and the hunting field and a thousand years of men pretending not to be afraid. Or it may have been merely impatience with Plutonburg’s melodrama. We shall never know.”

Sir Henry picked up a fallen oak leaf from the table and broke its stem between his fingers.

“The public sees a moment,” he said. “A man lives before and after it.”

“What happened afterward?”

“At first he continued his work. That was a form of punishment. He sought Madame Lorée with an energy which alarmed even his colleagues. Two members of the Paris group were arrested; the false nurse vanished. Plutonburg’s submarine was later mined off the Danish coast, though Plutonburg himself survived and commanded another boat.”

“He survived the war?”

“Yes.”

“And St. Alban?”

“He survived it by less than a year.”

Sir Henry’s face had become almost indistinguishable in the dusk, but his voice came with undiminished precision.

“I saw him three weeks before his death. He came to my room at Whitehall late in the afternoon. He had grown thin, and there was a nervous movement in his right hand—the hand that had tugged at his sleeve on the deck of the Inverly. He asked whether the final report on the Somme leak had been completed.

“I told him that it had.

“‘And the conclusion?’

“‘That the enemy obtained essential information from the map concealed in your dressing.’

“He stood by the window. For a long time he said nothing.

“Then he asked, ‘How many?’

“I pretended not to understand.

“‘How many men,’ he said, ‘went down because of it?’

“I told him no calculation could separate that cause from all the others.

“‘How many?’

“I gave him no figure. There was no honest figure to give. But he had formed one of his own, and private arithmetic is seldom merciful.”

Sir Henry let the broken leaf fall.

“I reminded him that he had been drugged and deceived. I told him that no court, military or moral, could condemn a man for carrying what he did not know he carried.

“He said, ‘You would acquit the hand, Marquis. What about the mind that was so certain it could not be deceived?’

“That was the wound. Not the cut on his arm. Not even the deaths. His confidence in himself had been absolute. The Germans had used that confidence as one uses a sealed diplomatic bag.”

“And he shot himself?”

“One wet morning in October. In his library, beneath a portrait of his father.”

“Then the open window and the footprints—”

“Family piety. His sister found him. Her husband had served in the police before the war and possessed the dangerous half-knowledge of an amateur dramatist. They feared the disgrace of suicide. More than that, they feared what people would ask if St. Alban, the national emblem of cheerful courage, had deliberately destroyed himself. So the brother-in-law opened the French window, carried the pistol outside, then changed his mind and pushed it beneath the curtain. He walked twice across the gravel in an old pair of boots belonging to a dismissed gardener. There was even a torn thread on the latch. A half-penny novelette could not have improved it.”

“And you exposed them?”

“I explained that they had better tell the police the truth. Then the Government explained that the truth had better remain unpublished.”

“Was that just?”

Sir Henry looked toward the monument.

“Justice is a word men use when they wish the dead to behave conveniently.”

The moon had risen behind us. Its light now caught the upper edge of the distant shaft, cold and hard above the dark woods.

“You disapprove of the monument?” I asked.

“No.”

His answer surprised me more than anything he had said.

“But it commemorates a falsehood.”

“It commemorates words he truly spoke.”

“In circumstances wholly misunderstood.”

“All monuments are erected to misunderstandings. Alfred’s White Horse, if we could recover every fact of it, would become a matter of wet boots, bad provisions and conflicting memoranda. History cannot cast complexity in bronze. It chooses a gesture.”

“But St. Alban would have hated it.”

“Yes.”

“And Plutonburg’s cruelty created it.”

“Yes.”

Sir Henry’s tone deepened.

“That is the final irony. Plutonburg meant to leave St. Alban alive under an intolerable shame. He arranged the deck like a stage: the wounded audience, the pistol, the recovered plans, the contemptuous departure. He wished St. Alban to remember the grin. Instead, England remembered the words.

“Plutonburg thought he was fashioning a private hell. He fashioned a public hero.

“He believed that by sparing the transport he had condemned St. Alban to silence. St. Alban confessed.

“He believed the confession, if made, must destroy the man’s name. The confession was sealed, while the legend ran through the world.

“He took the map. He helped wreck the opening of the offensive. He broke St. Alban’s spirit. And yet, in the memory of the English people, he remains forever the armed barbarian who recoiled before an unarmed Englishman.”

Sir Henry extended his hand toward the white shaft.

“There is Plutonburg’s defeat.”

I could not accept it altogether.

“And St. Alban’s victory?”

Sir Henry was silent for so long that I thought he would not answer.

“At a price,” he said finally, “which no decent chap would willingly pay.”

We sat without speaking. Lights appeared one by one in the valley. The dark, creeping river received them and drew them out into trembling lines. Behind the woods the monument remained visible, not as stone now, but as a narrow pallor suspended between earth and sky.

I thought of the deck of the Inverly: the choppy sea, the white caps of the nurses, the long black whaleback of the submarine, Plutonburg’s iron face, and the strip of silk fluttering in his hands. I saw St. Alban in the centre of them all, staring at the torn bandage while his mind moved in a blur through the ruin of everything he had believed himself to be.

Then the pistol came up.

Don’t threaten, fire if you like.

The phrase had been carved on the ridge, printed in schoolbooks, spoken over graves and shouted in recruiting halls. Yet behind it, invisible and inseparable, lay the bandage, the map and the grin.

“Will the truth ever be known?” I asked.

“It is known,” said Sir Henry.

“I mean publicly.”

“Probably not. The reports may be opened by some future generation that has ceased to care. A scholar will compare dates. A reviewer will call the affair controversial. There will be a footnote.”

“And the monument?”

“The monument will remain.”

He rose and walked to the edge of the terrace. I followed him. Below us England lay profoundly at peace: oak, meadow, water and distant roofs under the moon.

Sir Henry stood bareheaded before that tranquil country.

“The facts belong to St. Alban,” he said. “The legend belongs to England. Men suppose that truth destroys legend. It seldom does. The fact creeps through documents; the legend stands on a hill.”

Far off, the white memorial shone above the river.

And even after all I had heard, the words upon it seemed to ring across the darkness with the perfect, terrible accent of courage:

DON’T THREATEN, FIRE IF YOU LIKE!

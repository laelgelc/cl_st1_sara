The café had altered its name, but not its smell. Beneath the fresh gilt lettering on the window there remained the same mingling of coffee, wet wool, extinguished matches and orange peel warming on a saucer. Vera noticed this only after she had sat down. It seemed then less a smell than a hand laid lightly over her mouth.

Outside, the afternoon was colourless. Umbrellas passed in the glass like black fish, and the wheels of cabs stitched and unstitched the shining street. She had ordered tea because one must order something. Her muff lay on the chair beside her, her gloves across it, and her veil, damp at the edges, clung disagreeably to her cheek.

She was looking for a pin in her handbag when a voice said:

“Vera?”

Even before she raised her head she knew the careful astonishment of it—the two syllables separated, as though her name were a small object he had found in a drawer.

He stood beside the table, smiling.

Six years had sharpened him. The old softness at his jaw had gone; his moustache was clipped more closely; there were pale lines at the corners of his eyes. His coat was admirably cut. He held a silver cigarette case in one hand, and the sight of it, turning between his fingers, brought back not tenderness but irritation. He had always clicked things. Cases, pencil tops, the clasp of her bag. Click, click—while one spoke.

“How extraordinary,” he said. “May I?”

“Of course.”

She moved the muff, though there were other empty tables. He sat opposite her, placed his hat on the chair, then rescued it at once from a drop of rain falling from his sleeve.

“You haven’t changed.”

It was absurd; she had changed entirely. Her hair was different, her face thinner, her mouth less ready to forgive. But she said, “Nor have you,” and instantly remembered how he used to draw his chair too near the table and knock his knees against it. He did so now.

They laughed.

That laugh—quick, private, almost guilty—was worse than anything. For one moment the café disappeared, and they were in the old garden outside Richmond, hiding behind the currant bushes from an aunt who believed they were looking for a lost brooch. There had been wasps among the fallen pears. One had crawled into his cuff, and he had leapt about, slapping his arm and crying, “Kill it! For heaven’s sake, kill it!” She had been ashamed of him for a week.

He was saying, “I thought it was you from the street. That particular way you bend your head. As if you were listening to something under the table.”

“Was I?”

“You always did.”

The waitress came. He ordered coffee, then added, “With cream. But separately, please. Quite separately.” He watched until she had written it down.

Vera remembered a supper at which he had sent back caviare because the portion seemed too small. How loudly he had said, “At that price!” How the people beside them had looked. She had pretended to examine the chandelier.

“And you?” he asked. “Where have you hidden yourself all these years?”

“Nowhere. I live here.”

“Still?”

“Still.”

The word sounded shabby. He opened the cigarette case and offered it. She shook her head. He took one himself but did not light it.

“I’ve been away,” he said.

“Yes?”

“Everywhere we used to talk about.”

His face changed as he spoke; warmth moved across it suddenly, not quite a smile, but something brighter—as if a lamp had been turned up behind his eyes.

“Spain first. Then Morocco. Greece. Russia for nearly a year. The Crimea, the Caucasus. Last spring I crossed into Persia. Do you remember how we used to plan it all?”

Of course she remembered. Their journeys had been made on Sunday afternoons with an atlas spread over her mother’s dining table. He had traced routes with a blunt blue pencil while she cut oranges, dropping crescents of peel into a dish. They would go south always, toward heat, bells, white roads and water. They would own nothing that could not be packed in one leather case. At night they would sleep on decks beneath astonishing stars.

But what came first to her now was the sting of orange oil in a crack beside her thumbnail, and his annoyance when a drop had marked the map.

“You’ve forgotten,” he said gently.

“No. Not altogether.”

“Not altogether! Vera, you had the whole world in your head. I merely followed.”

His coffee arrived with a little jug of cream and two sugar lumps wrapped in blue paper. He shifted the jug to the edge of the tray, away from the cup, then leaned forward.

“In Granada,” he said, “there was a courtyard where the water ran all night. You couldn’t see it from my room, but you heard it slipping over the tiles. And there were orange trees. That scent—my dear, it was you. The whole place was you.”

Her first thought was that she had never smelt like an orange tree. Then the courtyard formed within her: white arches, glazed tiles green as deep water, a strip of night overhead. The fountain spoke tirelessly to itself. Somewhere a woman loosened her hair at a window. Vera sat quite still in the café, yet felt a warm stone beneath her hand.

He went on. He had learned to ride in the Atlas foothills. He had sailed from Odessa in a freighter carrying grain, with a captain who slept in his clothes and a cook who kept a yellow bird in the galley. He had watched dawn rise over the Black Sea.

“The sea there is not black,” he said. “It is velvet—blue velvet bruised by light. At evening the fishermen sang. Only one line, over and over. I never knew what it meant.”

He hummed three low notes.

A spoon struck a cup at the next table. The paper daffodils in the brass vase trembled, too bright and too stiff, while beneath them the imagined water widened. Vera saw the blunt prow turning through it, saw ropes shining with spray, heard the unseen boatman’s voice travel over the dark. The song entered her like remembered grief, though she had never heard it.

“How fortunate you are,” she said.

He looked almost hurt. “Fortunate? You made me go.”

“I?”

“You don’t know?”

He lit the cigarette at last. The flame enclosed his lowered face, intimate and boyish. “After we parted, I could not endure the old life. Rooms, calls, the same people saying the same things. You had shown me what freedom might be. Even when you refused it, I—well, I carried the idea for both of us.”

She disliked the phrase “when you refused it.” It made her sound cowardly. Had she refused? There had been practicalities: her mother’s illness, the uncertainty of his money, his horrible scene in the railway refreshment room when he accused her of loving safety more than him. She had been twenty-six and exhausted. Surely one could not call exhaustion cowardice.

Yet the strange beast that had slept in her for years lifted its head.

She had thought it dead. She had fed it small consolations: concerts, books, a fortnight by the sea, dinners at her cousin’s where the talk moved politely from servants to influenza. At night it had lain curled beneath her ribs. Now it stretched, pressing its warm flanks against her heart. Morocco. Persia. A grain ship under stars.

“And you travelled alone?” she asked.

“Almost always.”

“No one—”

“No one who mattered.”

He said it without looking at her, and she felt the blood move into her face. How easy it was for him to make an empty place and invite her to stand in it.

He spoke of a road above Tiflis, where rain had washed the hills until each leaf shone separately; of a monastery whose bells began before sunrise; of sleeping one winter night in a post-house while wolves cried beyond the shutter. He told her that in a market in Fez he had seen a length of red silk and thought, Vera would understand that red. Not crimson, not scarlet—the colour of a pomegranate opened in the sun.

“Do you remember your red scarf?” he asked.

She did. He had disliked it.

“You called it vulgar.”

“Did I? Impossible.”

“You said it made me look like an organ-grinder’s wife.”

He laughed delightedly. “How monstrous of me! No, I remember it differently. You wore it that day by the river, and I thought I had never seen anything so alive.”

There had been no river, surely. Or had there? She remembered a cold platform, smoke, the scarf caught in the clasp of her case, his impatience while she pulled it free. Yet as he watched her, waiting, the platform faded. Water appeared between bare willow trees. A red scarf fluttered; his arm was around her; their two figures, reflected, leaned toward each other until the current broke them.

His memories were lovelier than hers. That did not make them false. Perhaps pettiness had preserved the wrong things in her: the wasp, the caviare, the clicking case. Perhaps the truth of happiness was always lost at the time beneath buttons, bills, damp shoes, badly chosen words. He had rescued it. He had carried their past from country to country while she had left hers in a drawer with old receipts.

“Do you remember the storm?” he asked.

“What storm?”

“At your aunt’s house. The electricity failed, and we sat on the stairs.”

“No. We had candles.”

“Yes, candles everywhere. But we chose the dark.”

They had sat on the stairs because her aunt was arguing with the maid in the dining room and they dared not pass. He had whispered foolish verses into Vera’s sleeve. His breath smelt faintly of anchovy. Still, now came the pressure of his shoulder, the black landing, rain beating the fanlight. Their silence had seemed to float above the household like a sealed room.

“I wanted to protect you,” he said. “That was the pain of it. You seemed so defenceless, though you pretended not to be. I wanted some fabulous conveyance—a winged carpet, an enchanted boat—anything that could lift you clear of all that suffocation.”

He made a sweeping movement, and the cuff of his coat brushed her gloves to the floor.

“Oh—your glove.”

He stooped, found one, and held it between both hands. For a second he did not return it. The soft grey fingers hung over his palm.

Vera had the absurd sensation that it was herself he held: empty, yielding, shaped by someone absent. If his hand entered it, it would become alive. She wanted him to smooth the wrist, to remember the exact warmth of her skin. Instead he inspected a faint stain near the thumb.

“These pale colours are so impractical.”

He gave it back.

“Yes,” she said.

The beast within her did not retreat. It settled down, almost contentedly, and purred.

How lonely she had been! The admission came without drama, like a door opening on a room long known to be empty. Friends had married; her mother had died; cousins required cheerfulness in exchange for invitations. Men had liked her cautiously, admiring her voice, her taste in books, the way she poured tea. None had recognized the secret country in her. None had said: You understand that red.

And here he was, transformed and yet familiar, carrying all the proof of what they had been. Perhaps life did return what one had thrown away. Not unchanged—that would be crude—but burnished by distance, made worthy through suffering.

He crushed out his cigarette.

“You must have been very happy,” she said.

“Happy?” He considered. “No. Interested, absorbed, alive sometimes. But happiness—”

He looked directly at her. The café sounds drew back. Even the rain seemed suspended in thin silver threads beyond the window.

“Vera,” he said, “there were moments when I thought, if only she were here.”

Her hand tightened around the glove.

He continued more softly. “On the Black Sea particularly. Or that night in Persia, on a roof above the town. All the lamps going out one by one. I thought of our talks—how no one else ever followed me as you did.”

She could not answer. If she spoke, hope would show itself nakedly, and hope at her age was not graceful. Yet why should grace matter? Had grace taken her anywhere? Six years of proper rooms, proper departures, proper sleep.

She imagined saying, Take me now. Not on a magic carpet; the evening train would do. They would buy toothbrushes at the station. He would complain about the porter, and she would forgive him. In a month they would be on some foreign terrace where grapes hung overhead and unknown bells scattered sound across the roofs. She would wear the red scarf. They would laugh at the lost years.

“I, too—” she began.

He raised one finger. “But do you know what I understand now?”

The interruption scarcely hurt; it was his old manner. He had always preferred to complete the thought for both of them.

“I understand what we were doing,” he said. “At last.”

He sat back, pleased by the seriousness of his tone. “I met a doctor in Vienna—a remarkable fellow, really. We talked a great deal about attachment, projection, the manufacture of sympathy. It became perfectly clear.”

“Clear?”

“Our relationship. We believed we understood each other because each of us was perpetually looking for ourselves in the other. You encouraged my dramas; I encouraged yours. Two mirrors facing. Infinite reflections, no real room.”

Vera stared at him.

He smiled—not unkindly, which was worse. “It was mutual egotism. Almost a condition. Beautiful in its way, perhaps, but unhealthy. We were in love with being understood.”

The Black Sea folded soundlessly and vanished. The courtyard tiles went out. Morocco, Persia, the mountain road—all were extinguished as quickly as coloured pictures when a book is slammed shut.

“Of course,” he continued, “one sees these things only after one has outgrown them. At the time the emotions appear tremendous. Sacred, even! But one mustn’t mistake intensity for truth.”

Outgrown. The word touched her with the small cold point of a needle.

She saw suddenly that he had not opened an empty place for her. He had arranged an exhibition. The red silk, the fountain, the fishermen’s song—objects behind glass, each labelled with his development. She had inspired him as weather might inspire him, or a city, or an illness successfully recovered from.

The strange beast gave one astonished movement. Then it crouched, wounded and ashamed.

He reached for the cream jug. “The curious thing is, I bear you no resentment. None. I feel gratitude.”

“Gratitude,” Vera repeated.

“Exactly. We served a purpose for one another.”

On the table, a strip of orange peel had twisted as it dried. Its white inner skin showed like something exposed. She noticed the minute pores, the oily shine, the torn end darkening in the heat. How unbearable that objects persisted so calmly.

He was speaking again, explaining release, maturity, the freedom of seeing oneself without illusion. His voice moved smoothly over her. Perhaps he believed every word. Perhaps this, too, was a story polished by repetition in hotel lounges and railway carriages. Perhaps tomorrow he would remember her as profoundly moved, nobly silent.

Vera drew down her veil.

“You’re not going?” he asked.

She buttoned her collar. One button slipped twice before entering its hole.

“But your tea—Vera, have I said something?”

She put on one glove and then the other. The stained thumb disappeared.

“Surely you’re not offended. We’re beyond that, aren’t we?”

She rose. The chair scraped the floor with a violence that made two women glance round. He half rose also, one hand on the table, bewilderment spreading across his face.

“Vera?”

She could have told him. One sentence might have cut him: You have not changed. Or she might have laughed, which would have been finer. Instead she picked up her muff and walked out without a word.

The rain met her like a crowd. She crossed the pavement blindly, passed under streaming awnings and between umbrellas smelling of metal and damp silk. Behind the veil her face felt remote, a face glimpsed through the window of a departing carriage.

In the café he remained standing until the door had closed. Then he looked at the people who had watched her go, as if one of them might explain.

“Extraordinary,” he murmured.

The waitress brought the bill. He read it, frowned, and tapped the paper with one clean fingernail.

“One moment. You’ve charged me for cream.”

“Yes, sir.”

“But I didn’t use it.”

“It was served, sir.”

“That is hardly the point. It is untouched. You can see for yourself.”

The little white jug stood at the edge of the tray, filled neatly to its blue rim. He pushed it toward her.

“I really cannot be expected to pay for something I haven’t had.”

## The Garden Lodge

There were women who said that Caroline Noble had been born without the faculty of regret. They said it with the peculiar resentment which the unfortunate reserve for those who have not only escaped misfortune but have declined to be improved by it. Caroline had health, money, an attentive husband, two agreeable children safely established elsewhere, and a house on the northern coast which appeared, in May and June, to have been arranged by Providence for the humiliation of her friends. Its grey terraces descended through orchards white with blossom; its lawns, running between banks of azalea and laurel, ended above a strip of blue water; and the salt wind, after passing over the roses, arrived at the open windows with the mingled freshness of sea and garden. Yet Caroline was believed to take all these benefits as she took her breakfast tray—promptly, temperately, and without emotion.

Her composure had long been an affront to less fortunate women. She did not complain of her servants, although they remained with her; she did not discuss Howard’s shortcomings, although no husband could have been without them; she wore excellent clothes but never described their purchase; and she had passed the dangerous middle years without becoming religious, philanthropic, literary, or unwell. When friends came to Briarcliff burdened with broken engagements, disappointing sons, alarming doctors, or the discovery that life had omitted something promised in girlhood, Caroline listened, offered practical assistance, and refused to confuse sympathy with participation. There was this to be said for her: the assistance was generally useful. But advice, however sound, is seldom forgiven when it comes from a woman whose carpets are neither faded nor unpaid for.

That spring there had been an additional provocation. Raymond d’Esquerre, the celebrated tenor, had spent nearly a month at Briarcliff preparing a new rôle, and Caroline, who had studied music in her youth, had played for him every morning in the old lodge at the lower end of the garden. To have sheltered d’Esquerre beneath one’s roof would have unsettled most women; to have made music with him daily and remained unchanged seemed almost indecent. At least three of Caroline’s friends would have betrayed their husbands in imagination for considerably less.

“Didn’t he make the place impossible after he went?” Edith Fane asked, looking down from the terrace toward the trees that hid the lodge. “I should think every room would echo.”

“My dear Edith, rooms don’t echo unless they are badly furnished,” Caroline replied.

Edith laughed, but not altogether pleasantly. “You know what I mean. Surely you missed him?”

“We all missed him. He was an agreeable guest.”

“Agreeable! One might call the Adriatic damp.”

Caroline lifted her parasol against a flight of apple petals. “Raymond would be delighted by your comparison. He collects superlatives; they are part of his professional equipment.”

Such replies established her reputation. After d’Esquerre had gone, carrying with him the gratitude of Howard, the adoration of the servants, three boxes of music, and a confusion of scarves, gloves, and silver-backed brushes which his valet had afterwards to recover by post, Caroline resumed the ordinary administration of her household. She inspected the kitchen garden, disputed an estimate for repairing the west roof, answered eighteen letters, and gave instructions that the singer’s rooms were to be aired before they were closed. Her friends decided that she had remained untouched.

They did not know—how should they?—that every evening, after Howard had gone to the billiard room or become absorbed in the financial pages, Caroline walked down through the orchard and stood outside the locked lodge, never entering it, merely resting her hand upon the door as if to assure herself that the building was still there.

Her practicality, which looked to others like a defect of temperature, had been painfully acquired. Caroline’s childhood had been spent among people who held practicality to be a vulgar concession, rather as bathing was considered by certain saints. Her father, Giles Merrick, had painted pictures no one bought and composed sonnets no one printed; her mother, who had once possessed a pretty contralto voice, moved through successive phases of enthusiasm for Swedenborg, vegetarianism, Byzantine embroidery, and the education of the soul through color. They lived in rooms where the wallpaper peeled behind portfolios of unfinished designs, where the piano was always out of tune, where unpaid tradesmen entered through the same door as poets, sculptors, spiritual healers, and young men who had discovered a new theory of beauty but had not discovered how to pay for cigarettes.

Giles spoke continually of the Masters. The Masters absolved him from completing portraits, meeting commissions, mending chairs, or remembering rent day. When there was no coal, he explained Michelangelo; when the laundress kept back the linen, he read aloud from Blake. He could discourse magnificently upon the desecration of art while eating the herring Caroline had bought with money earned by giving scales to a brewer’s daughter. His contempt for commerce increased in proportion to his dependence upon it.

Caroline’s elder brother Julian inherited the rhetoric without even the intermittent industry. At twenty he was a poet; at twenty-five a dramatist; at thirty, when his tragedy on Icarus had been refused by every manager in London, he became the victim of an age incapable of greatness. He wore soiled gloves and quoted Heine, borrowed money from women he despised, and made of every failure a distinction. Later there were chloral, absences, scenes in cheap hotels, and letters in which self-commiseration achieved almost lyric beauty. Caroline paid his bills until the day he died. She also paid for his funeral, choosing the least pretentious coffin because she knew he would have preferred the most expensive.

Poverty itself had not embittered her. It was the genuflection before poverty, the insistence that disorder was spiritual, that hunger nourished genius, that a torn carpet was evidence of superiority to those who could afford a whole one. She had seen her mother shivering beneath a shawl while Giles explained that the true artist must not submit to bourgeois necessity; she had seen Julian take the last sovereign from a drawer and spend it upon absinthe for men who praised him. By seventeen Caroline knew what the worshippers of beauty too often forgot: butcher, landlord, and laundress were not symbols but persons; bills were postponed, never annihilated; and somebody—usually the least inspired member of a household—must stand between the dreamers and the consequences of their dreams.

She made herself that person.

Music, in those years, was not romance but employment. Caroline had a clean, strong technique and a memory that saved the cost of scores. She accompanied singing lessons, played at dancing classes, coached ambitious girls whose mothers believed them destined for opera, and once performed three evenings a week in a restaurant where the diners spoke so loudly that she could scarcely hear herself. The music passed through her fingers while her mind calculated rent and coal, medicine and tram fare. Schumann paid for Julian’s chloral; Schubert bought her mother’s winter cloak; a simplified Wagner selection settled the grocer’s account. If, now and then, some phrase opened beneath her like a sudden depth of blue water, she drew back. She had no leisure for depths.

At twenty-seven she married Howard Noble, who was twelve years older, prosperous, fastidious, and incapable of regarding insolvency as picturesque. He loved her intelligence, her cool beauty, and her evident preference for facts. Howard’s money came from ships, warehouses, and investments so unpoetical that Giles had denounced the marriage as the sale of a soul. Caroline, who had heard him pawn her mother’s silver-backed brush that same afternoon, did not answer.

Her marriage was successful. She gave Howard loyalty, order, hospitality, and a companionship free of emotional levies; he gave her security without humiliation and affection without theatrical demands. If he sometimes wished that Caroline would be foolish enough to need him, he did not press the wish. Their children grew up without fearing a knock at the door. Their servants were paid. Their guests were well fed. The rooms at Briarcliff, spacious and quiet, contained no masterpieces, but neither did they contain a chair whose broken leg was propped upon a volume of poetry.

And then Raymond d’Esquerre came.

He was not handsome in the usual sense. His face, seen at breakfast, could be heavy; his eyes had pouches beneath them, and the lower part of his jaw suggested an obstinate peasant ancestry of which he was proud only on suitable occasions. But upon the stage, or even at a piano in a dim room, these imperfections became instruments. His large head took the light nobly; fatigue deepened his expression; the peasant jaw became the resolution of a knight or martyr. He had the malleability of all great performers, yet beneath it there remained something watchful and inaccessible, something which permitted each observer to imagine a private understanding.

Women supplied what he withheld. In the theatre they came to him burdened with the unexpended portions of their lives: young girls guarded into vacuity, wives whose husbands had forgotten to look at them, widows relieved of duty but not restored to youth, dressmakers, duchesses, governesses, shop assistants, women worn by children, women preserved by idleness, women who had chosen wrongly and women who had never been permitted to choose. They sat beneath the gilded balconies as within a temple; and when his voice rose, carrying some prince’s despair or outlaw’s devotion into the heated air, each received from it the form of consolation she required. For three hours he lent them not his love—which would have been impossible and, in most cases, inconvenient—but the sensation of possessing a soul upon which love might have acted magnificently. It was a mystic bread broken among thousands, a eucharist of sentiment; and they went home nourished by what had never occurred.

Caroline understood this mechanism and had thought herself protected by understanding it. During d’Esquerre’s first week at Briarcliff she watched him with an almost professional amusement. She noted the pauses by which he made commonplace speech appear significant, the gracious concentration he bestowed upon whoever addressed him, the sudden melancholy with which he looked over the sea when conversation flagged. She knew that genius, like beauty, develops habits; that a celebrated man learns unconsciously to provide the attitude expected of him. She had grown up among less successful practitioners of the same art.

Yet d’Esquerre’s discipline differed from their indulgence. He arrived at the lodge at ten each morning, worked until two, repeated difficult passages without petulance, accepted criticism, and conserved his voice with an austerity Caroline respected. He was extravagant in effect, not in preparation. The fires by which his audiences were consumed were measured, fed, and tended. Here, at least, was an artist who paid his servants.

The lodge had once been a gardener’s cottage. It stood beyond the orchard in a hollow protected from the sea wind by cypresses and ilex, and Howard had allowed it to decay because he intended, every summer, to replace it with a larger pavilion. Caroline had caused one room to be cleared for the rehearsals. A piano was brought down; a faded carpet covered the uneven boards; two chairs, a table, and a brass lamp completed the furnishing. Through the open windows came the smell of box and damp earth, the hiss of the sea beyond the cliff, and sometimes the drowsy knocking of a woodpecker.

There d’Esquerre worked upon the part of a king who renounces his crown for a woman and discovers, after renunciation, that she has loved chiefly the crown. The opera was absurd in its events and formidable in its music. Kingdoms dissolved in brass; forests whispered through strings; desire, betrayal, sacrifice, and death moved upon harmonies too spacious for human conduct. Caroline played the orchestral score at the piano, reducing tempests to ten fingers, while d’Esquerre stood beside her and summoned out of the bare room towers, armies, moonlit battlements, a woman’s white arms, a king’s last despair.

At first she thought only of tempo. Then, gradually, she began to anticipate the moments when his voice would enter; not with the accompanist’s calculation, but with an attention that seemed to involve her whole body. Before a certain phrase in the second act he always drew one slow breath. Caroline came to feel that breath before she heard it. When he leaned above the score, his sleeve sometimes touched her shoulder. She did not move away, because moving would have acknowledged the contact.

He spoke to her seriously, which was his most effective form of homage.

“You do not merely accompany,” he told her one morning. “You know what the music is concealing.”

“Music conceals very little from a competent analyst.”

“It conceals everything. That is why we endure it.”

She laughed. “You mean that is why audiences endure themselves.”

He looked at her with an attention so complete that the dusty lodge, the cracked panes, the salt-dimmed shrubs outside appeared to withdraw.

“Perhaps,” he said, “you have endured yourself too well.”

It was a theatrical sentence. Caroline knew it to be one. She knew that he had probably said analogous things to women in Vienna, Paris, Buenos Aires; that the precision with which it seemed to find her was part of his gift; that a man who lives by awakening emotion acquires an instinct for the locked chamber in every listener. She knew all this.

Knowledge was not enough.

On his last day the weather had been extraordinarily clear. The apple trees were losing their blossom, and petals drifted past the lodge windows in slow white companies. They rehearsed the final scene twice. On the second repetition d’Esquerre sang with an inwardness he had not given it before; his great voice, instead of expanding against the room, seemed to withdraw into some inaccessible distance, taking the room, the garden, and Caroline’s measured life after it. When the last chord ended, neither of them spoke.

Then he closed the score.

“You have been very generous,” he said.

“I have enjoyed the work.”

“No. You have permitted yourself to enjoy the work. It is not quite the same.”

She rose from the piano. “Raymond, must everything become a diagnosis?”

“Only what refuses to become a confession.”

He smiled, and the line became light enough to be forgiven. A few moments later they were walking back to the house, discussing trains and luggage. At luncheon he praised Howard’s wine; at the station he kissed Caroline’s hand; by evening he was gone.

The household contracted about his absence. Chairs recovered their ordinary dimensions. The piano in the drawing room became furniture again. Howard remarked, with affectionate relief, that famous men required a shocking quantity of hot water. Caroline agreed.

A week passed before she unlocked the lodge.

She went at twilight, telling no one. The air was warm, and the orchard had exchanged its bridal whiteness for a thick, serious green. When she opened the door, the room received her with the dry smell of wood, dust, and extinguished music. The piano remained; Howard had neglected to have it moved. On the stand lay a pencilled copy of the score, and beside it, caught under the brass lamp, one of d’Esquerre’s handkerchiefs, forgotten by the valet’s inventory. Caroline looked at the square of linen with disgust. It was too obvious, too like the relic of a boarding-school infatuation. She put it into a drawer.

Then she sat at the piano and played eight measures from the second act.

The sound in the closed room was startling. It seemed not produced but released, as though the walls had stored every chord and now gave it back enlarged by silence. Caroline stopped, locked the piano, and returned to the house.

After that she went often. She did not always play. Sometimes she sat by the window while dusk gathered under the cypresses; sometimes she read the annotations in the score, recognizing the quick slant of d’Esquerre’s pencil; once she spoke aloud a phrase he had used, and was so ashamed that she left at once. She told herself that solitude had become pleasant after the strain of guests, that the walk benefited her, that she wished to supervise the building before Howard demolished it. Her explanations multiplied with the visits.

What did she want? Not an affair. The thought, when stated in that crude form, offended her intelligence. She did not wish d’Esquerre to return and compromise her, to write letters, to make demands, to become one more magnificent dependent. She had no desire to see his brushes on her dressing table or to hear him complain of drafts at breakfast. The real man, with his valet, his throat sprays, his vanity, his professional economies, was not the object of her longing.

But if not the man, what then?

The part of him that had entered the music; the part of her that had answered it. The impossible king, the renounced crown, the forest sounding beyond the walls; the conviction—absurd, intoxicating—that somewhere behind necessity there existed a life in which feeling was not an error to be corrected but a sovereign law. She had made an idol, and she knew she had made it. Yet what is an idol but a form given to the worshipper’s own hunger? D’Esquerre had supplied the face and voice; the adoration was hers.

This knowledge was the most humiliating discovery of all.

Late in June Howard announced at breakfast that he had obtained estimates for the summer house.

“We ought to pull down that dreary lodge in August,” he said. “The foundations are damp, and it blocks the view from the lower walk. Lennox could begin while we’re in Scotland.”

“August is a bad month for building,” Caroline replied.

“He says not.”

“Builders always say not.”

Howard looked at her over his coffee. “You have developed a curious tenderness for the place.”

“Nonsense. It is merely wasteful to destroy a serviceable structure.”

“My dear Caroline, it has not been serviceable since d’Esquerre left.”

She folded the newspaper. “Then let it stand until next year.”

Howard laughed. “Very well. I’m charmed to discover you can be sentimental about something.”

The word angered her disproportionately. She spent the morning examining household accounts, and at luncheon corrected the butler for bringing claret too warm. By evening the sky had become overcast, though no rain fell. Heat lay over the garden with a weight unusual on the coast; the sea was smooth and metallic, the leaves motionless, the roses exhaling a sweetness almost corrupt. Along the horizon clouds massed in purple shelves, and now and then a pale flicker passed within them, too distant for thunder.

Howard went to a neighbor’s house for cards. Caroline pleaded a headache and remained behind. At ten she dismissed her maid, waited until the upper corridors were quiet, then put on a dark cloak and walked to the lodge.

The garden seemed to hold its breath. Gravel grated beneath her shoes with an alarming distinctness. The roses along the lower path, invisible except when heat-lightning opened the sky, brushed her sleeves like warm hands. Far below, the sea moved without sound.

Inside the lodge the air was stifling. Caroline opened both windows and lit the brass lamp. Its circle of light fell upon the piano, the faded carpet, the empty chair in which d’Esquerre had rested between passages. Beyond that circle the corners of the room were obscure; they seemed deeper than the small building allowed, recesses from which the ordinary laws of size and time had withdrawn.

She took out the score.

At first she played quietly. The familiar reductions marched beneath her hands—horns diminished to octaves, strings compressed into tremolos, the whole elaborate machinery of passion made manageable by notation. She kept the tempo exact. Even alone she could not play carelessly.

Then she reached the king’s entrance.

Here d’Esquerre had stood behind her. Here he had drawn breath. Caroline heard that breath now, not as memory but as physical presence, and her hands faltered. She began again. The phrase rose from the piano incomplete, requiring the voice that had once possessed it. She supplied the vocal line inwardly; at once he was there—not the fatigued guest who had mislaid his handkerchief, but the figure created in the lodge, the king divested of kingdom, the lover before whom sacrifice became splendor.

Outside, lightning whitened the windows. For an instant the garden appeared in violent detail: every leaf rigid, every branch arrested, the wet-looking path empty beneath a sky without depth. Darkness closed again.

Caroline played on.

The room enlarged about her. The cracked plaster became a high vault; the cypresses beyond the windows were the black forest of the opera; the sea was an army moving in the night. She knew this was fantasy. She named it fantasy, hysteria, belated self-indulgence, the revenge of exhausted nerves. She supplied every accusation in advance. Her mother had lived by such transformations, covering cold rooms with imaginary tapestries, turning debt into martyrdom, weakness into spiritual distinction. Julian had populated taverns with gods and called his drunkenness Dionysian. Was she, Caroline, to join them at last? Was this the inherited poison, dormant beneath solvency and discipline, now flowering ridiculously in middle age?

She struck a wrong chord and stopped.

Silence rushed upon her. From the clouds came a low, delayed thunder.

“No,” she said aloud.

The word had no authority in the room.

She rose and went to the window. The heat pressed against her face. Somewhere in the dark shrubbery a bird gave a short, disturbed cry. She thought of Briarcliff behind her—its ordered rooms, locked doors, fresh linen, sound roof, Howard’s mild affection, the whole secure architecture she had built between herself and chaos. She had wanted that life. She still wanted it. Security was not contemptible because artists despised it; order was not sterile because disorder called itself freedom. She knew what hunger was, what unpaid bills did to tenderness, how ideals grew rancid in rooms where no one accepted responsibility.

She knew, she knew, she knew.

It was not enough.

The sentence formed with terrible simplicity. All she had defended was valid, and it was not enough. A life might be just, useful, loyal, beautifully administered—and still leave some creature within it unfed. She had believed that creature dead because she had kept it silent. But it had not died. It had crouched in the dungeon depths of her nature, blind and patient, taking what crumbs of music fell through the stones; and now, at the accidental passing of a voice, it had risen with the strength of all her self-deprivations.

She saw d’Esquerre as he had looked after the final chord, his head bent, his face altered by an emotion which may have been artistic concentration, fatigue, vanity, or kindness. What did it matter what it had been in him? In her it had been real. That was the horror. She could dismiss his intention; she could not dismiss her response. The genie had not entered from without. It had been sealed in her own vessel.

A gust struck the lodge. The lamp flame bent; loose pages lifted and scattered across the floor. Then the storm broke.

Rain fell with a sudden violence that obliterated the garden. Thunder crashed almost upon the lightning, and the windows slammed inward. Caroline struggled to close them, drenched at once, her hair loosened by the wind. The lamp went out. In the darkness she struck against the piano and cried out—not from pain, but because the contact released in her the remembered vibration of his voice.

She sank upon the bench.

There, while rain hammered the roof and water ran beneath the ill-fitting sill, she abandoned herself to what she had refused. Not to a plan, not to adultery, not even to the image of a future; merely to longing itself, naked of its extenuations. She wanted to be seen as d’Esquerre had seemed to see her; she wanted the years of competence stripped away, the careful woman dissolved; she wanted to go back to the moment before she had learned that desire was costly, before music had become coal and medicine, before beauty had acquired the smell of stale cigarettes and boiled mutton. She wanted the crown, the forest, the impossible choice; she wanted to suffer magnificently instead of succeeding sensibly. For one hour she wanted every dangerous thing.

She wept with a violence unknown even in grief. She had not wept when Julian died; there had been arrangements to make. She had not wept at her mother’s burial; Giles had forgotten the money for the carriage. She had not wept when her children left home, because departure was natural and tears burdened the departing. Those restrained waters seemed now to have joined the storm. She wept for no single loss and for all losses: for the girl playing scales in the restaurant while diners laughed; for the woman who had confused invulnerability with freedom; for the years given willingly and therefore irrecoverably; for the absurd singer who had opened a door without knowing what stood behind it.

At some hour after midnight the storm moved out to sea. Thunder receded, muttering among the eastern clouds. Rain softened upon the leaves. Caroline remained beside the piano, her cheek against its closed lid, while the room slowly cooled.

She imagined writing to d’Esquerre. The thought came with almost comic distinctness: the paper, the salutation, the impossible first sentence. What could she say? You have awakened me? He had received such letters by the hundred. Women in hotels, schoolrooms, palaces, suburban villas had written them after performances; they had thanked him for revealing their souls, for saving their lives, for making life unendurable. His secretary perhaps sorted them into heaps. Caroline saw her confession lying among mauve envelopes and perfumed stationery, one more tribute to the successful operation of his art.

She laughed then, a low and bitter sound.

No, she would not give him that. What had happened belonged to her. He had been the torch, perhaps; but the chamber, the stored air, the combustible things crowded within it—these were hers. Even if he returned, he could not inhabit the kingdom she had made for him. The real man would diminish it merely by entering.

Toward dawn she relit the lamp. The room was pitifully ordinary. Plaster had fallen near the window; rain spotted the score; her cloak lay in a wet heap, and muddy water had stained the carpet. In the grey light the lodge showed its age and shabbiness without romance. The enchanted chamber was again a neglected gardener’s cottage.

Caroline found d’Esquerre’s handkerchief in the drawer. She held it a moment, then laid it on the table. To burn it would have been melodrama; to keep it, sentimentality. She left it where a servant might find it and think nothing.

When she stepped outside, the eastern sky was pale above the sea. The garden, battered and washed, gave off the sharp odors of wet earth, box, and broken roses. Petals lay plastered upon the path. Birds had begun tentatively, each note separate in the drenched stillness. The house stood above the orchard, its windows blank, its chimneys substantial against the growing light.

She walked toward it slowly. Her wet skirts impeded her; her face felt swollen, her body old. Dawn did not refute the night. That, she understood, was another childish expectation: that daylight would expose passion as illusion and restore the former self untouched. The ordinary world returned, but it returned around knowledge. She could lock the room; she could not be again the woman who had never entered it.

At seven her maid found her already dressed.

“You look tired, madam,” the girl said.

“The storm kept me awake.”

“It was dreadful near three.”

“Yes. Bring the blue morning dress, please. And ask them to serve breakfast on the east terrace.”

By half past eight the sea had recovered its innocent brilliance. Sunlight shone upon the drenched lawns, and gardeners were already gathering fallen branches. Howard came down cheerful from his cards and kissed Caroline’s forehead.

“Head better?”

“Quite.”

He unfolded his napkin. “You missed a remarkable storm.”

“So I heard.”

“Lennox says weather like that proves the lodge foundations are rotten. I expect the place is half flooded.” He buttered his toast, then glanced at her with the tentative mischief of a husband returning to an old joke. “But perhaps we must preserve it now. A shrine to your one sentimental attachment.”

Caroline poured the coffee. Her hand did not tremble.

“No,” she said. “You were right. It should come down.”

Howard looked surprised. “This summer?”

“As soon as Lennox can begin. The ground ought to be drained before the new foundations are laid. Have him enlarge the terrace on the seaward side; otherwise the afternoon shadow will make the rooms damp.”

“That is my practical Caroline.” He smiled. “For a moment I hoped d’Esquerre had made you a little bit foolish.”

She looked beyond him toward the orchard. From the terrace the lodge could not be seen; only the tops of the cypresses marked the hollow where it stood. In full sunlight they appeared dense and commonplace, no black forest, no guardians of a forbidden tower. Presently men would enter with crowbars. The roof would be stripped, the walls broken, the piano wheeled back up the path. Dust would rise where music had been; bricks would be carted away; by autumn a neat foundation would occupy the place.

Caroline laughed, and Howard, satisfied, returned to his breakfast.

Her laugh was perfectly natural. It contained no appeal, no confession, nothing that could disturb the morning. Yet beneath it, sealed from Howard, from d’Esquerre, from her friends and even from the future woman she would again become, the night remained: the lightning, the scattered music, the kingdom renounced and desired, the imprisoned creature lifting its face at last.

“Not Raymond,” she said, and reached for the letters beside her plate. “The rain did.”

## The Long Way Home

Tony Rossi woke when the sleep-compartment folded itself into the wall.

For a moment he lay tangled in the warm sheet, blinking at the reddish-yellow light beyond the quartz window. The sun of Pas-udet looked bigger than an Earth sun was supposed to look. It hung low over the dust plain, blurred by copper-colored clouds.

The weather dial beside the window showed a green bar.

“Outside day,” Tony said.

The compartment did not answer. It had no voice circuit. Eep, waiting in the corner, answered instead.

“Outside conditions are acceptable for a Terran child wearing pressure seals.”

Eep stood one meter high on six black legs. His round body was white plastic, scratched where Tony had driven him through thornbrush. Two tool-arms folded against his sides. His head was only a glass vision-band with a blue light moving behind it.

“You always say that,” Tony told him.

“Conditions are often acceptable.”

Tony got dressed. He pulled on dust trousers, stamped his feet into seal-boots, and fastened the throat ring of his light pressure jacket. The morning air outside the city envelope would not kill a man, but it was thin and full of grit. Terrans could breathe it for an hour without equipment. After that their lungs hurt.

Pas-udeti breathed it all day.

They were built for it.

Tony hurried into the main room. His model spaceport covered half the table. Three tin cruisers stood on magnetic ramps, and a little converted freighter waited beside a painted fuel tower. He reached for the freighter, meaning to move it out of the military lane.

His father slapped his hand away.

“Not now.”

Joe Rossi sat before the news-screen in his gray service shirt. He had not shaved. A cup of coffee rested beside his fist, untouched.

On the screen, a star map showed Orion Sector. Red points burned around a white curve. Letters crawled under them too quickly for Tony to read.

Leah Rossi stood by the food unit. “Eat your breakfast, Tony.”

“What happened?”

“Nothing you need to worry about.”

“Then why’s Dad home?”

Joe looked at the screen, not at Tony. “Because all civil engineering crews are on standby.”

“Standby for what?”

“For eating breakfast,” Leah said. “Sit down.”

Tony sat. The food unit had made him egg-protein, toast and a cup of blue fruit juice. He did not like the juice. Pas juice from Karnet tasted better, though his mother said nobody knew what the natives put in it.

The screen changed to a view of ships in darkness. One ship rolled slowly, with white fire leaking from its middle. Another was only a bright cloud. A man’s voice spoke in a careful, flat tone.

“—confirmation from Fleet Command remains incomplete. Surviving units of the Seventh Warfleet are withdrawing toward the Rigel supply bases. Civil authorities urge restraint in all protectorate regions.”

Tony stopped chewing.

“Did we lose a battle?”

Joe’s fist moved. The coffee cup jumped.

“We didn’t lose anything.”

“The man said they’re withdrawing.”

“He said surviving units,” Leah whispered.

Joe turned on her. “I heard him.”

Tony looked from one to the other. “Who were they fighting?”

Nobody answered.

He knew there was a war somewhere. There had always been wars somewhere. At school the edutapes showed green arrows moving from Earth across the spiral arm. Sometimes the arrows met black arrows. Then little battle symbols flashed until the black arrows went away.

Earth had started expanding six hundred years ago. That was longer than anyone could imagine. Terran ships had crossed the dead systems, opened supply bases and brought law to inhabited planets. When they found hostile species, they fought. When they found friendly species, they traded.

The Pas-udeti were friendly. Mostly.

Tony had lived among them all his life.

He pointed at the screen. “Was it the Pas fleet?”

Joe swung around. “What Pas fleet?”

“The one at Orion.”

“They haven’t got a fleet. They’ve got freight tubs with guns welded on.”

“But did the freight tubs win?”

“Tony,” Leah said.

Joe shoved back his chair. “They hit our depot while the Seventh was dispersed. They used merchant signals. They packed freighters with fusion bombs and rammed the screen. That isn’t winning a battle. It’s a trick.”

The announcer said, “Reports indicate the Orion supply complex has fallen.”

Joe crossed the room and shut off the screen.

The silence seemed louder than the announcer.

Tony picked up his toast. “Mek says his oldest brother works on a freighter.”

Joe stared at him.

“Mek who?”

“Mek-vir. You know. He lives by the north pumping yard.”

“One of the beetle kids?”

“Don’t call them beetles.”

The words came out before Tony thought. His father’s face hardened.

Leah set down a plate. “Joe, please.”

“They are beetles,” Joe said. “Black shells, too many joints and a stink you can smell through a pressure hood.”

“They’re Pas-udeti.”

“On their own time. Not at my breakfast table.”

Tony looked down at his food. Mek had never sat at their breakfast table. No Pas-udeti had. The Terran settlement and Karnet were only six kilometers apart, but natives entered New Ashton only to repair drains, deliver goods or clean public buildings. Tony had once asked why. His mother said the atmospheric envelope was adjusted for Terran comfort.

Mek could wear a pressure shell. Tony wore one when he went the other way.

“I’m going to Karnet,” Tony said.

“No,” Leah answered quickly.

“I told Mek I’d help with his launch pit.”

“No.”

“But it’s an outside day.”

Joe went to the quartz window. Beyond the reinforced houses, dust rolled in low sheets across the plain. The city envelope made the dust turn aside in long curling waves.

“Let him go,” Joe said.

Leah stared at him. “After that announcement?”

“They won’t know yet.”

“The whole planet will know.”

“What are they going to do? Attack a ten-year-old?”

Tony did not like the way his mother looked at him.

Joe rubbed his jaw. “The district patrols are active. He takes his EEP and the north tram. He comes back before dust-shift.”

“Joe—”

“The boy grew up here. Half Karnet knows him.”

“That’s what worries me.”

Eep clicked into the room. “Tony’s outdoor equipment has been checked.”

Tony grinned, because that settled it. Eep was an Emergency Escort and Protection machine. Nobody argued with an EEP about equipment.

His mother knelt to fasten his jacket again, though he had already fastened it.

“Straight to Mek’s house,” she said. “No market. No freight yard. If Eep tells you to come home, you come home.”

“Sure.”

“And don’t say ‘sure’ that way.”

“Fine.”

Joe laughed once, without humor. “He’s becoming a native.”

Tony smiled uncertainly. Nobody else did.

He put on his hood and went out.

The settlement streets were nearly empty. Usually the morning shift crowded the walkways—miners in yellow skinsuits, hydroponics workers, children going to the education hall. Today men stood in doorways listening to pocket receivers. Two patrol cars waited beside the administration dome. Their guns were uncovered.

Tony slowed to look.

“Maintain pace,” Eep said.

“What are the patrols for?”

“Civil disturbance is possible.”

“Here?”

“Here is included in civil.”

Eep always gave answers like that.

They crossed Envelope Gate Three. Air hissed around Tony’s boots, and a pressure light winked from green to amber. The outer door opened.

Dust struck his hood with a dry whisper.

Beyond the gate, the Terran road ran straight toward Karnet. It was a smooth metal strip, laid over the older native highway. On either side stretched red flats broken by black rock and patches of gray thorn. The sun painted everything the color of old blood.

The north tram waited at the platform. It was an ancient Pas vehicle, low and long, with wheels instead of induction plates. Its engine coughed smoke.

The driver was old even for a Pas-udeti. His shell had faded from black to brown, and one of the small side arms below his shoulders was missing. A metal cap covered the joint.

He looked at Tony, then at Eep.

“New Ashton child,” he said in the local dialect.

Tony answered in the same language. “Karnet north square.”

The driver’s four mouth-parts moved against one another. Pas faces did not show expressions the way Terran faces did, but Tony could usually read them.

Today he could not.

“No fare,” the driver said.

“I have fare.”

“No fare today.”

Tony held out a coin. The driver did not take it.

“Is the tram free?”

“For you.”

“Why?”

The old Pas looked toward New Ashton. “Because Terrans have paid enough coin. Because Pas-udeti have paid enough blood. Choose whichever answer pleases you.”

Tony understood every word, but not the sentence.

“I only want to go to the north square.”

“Then board.”

Tony sat on a hard bench. Eep locked his feet to the floor rings. Three Pas passengers occupied the rear compartment. They had been talking when Tony entered. Now they were silent.

The tram lurched forward.

Karnet rose from the plain like something dug up rather than built. Its houses were squat structures of native stone, half underground, their doors covered by pressure curtains. Pipes crossed overhead. Old combustion cars rattled between open drains. Dust smoked from every wheel.

Tony liked Karnet better than New Ashton. New Ashton was clean, square and all the same. Karnet had tunnels and markets and roofs a boy could climb. It smelled of hot metal, spice smoke and the oily odor of Pas bodies. Tony scarcely noticed that odor unless his father mentioned it.

They passed the memorial wall near the western reservoir.

Thousands of Pas names had been cut into the stone.

Tony knew the school explanation. The names belonged to victims of the Unification Campaign, when rival Pas nations had fought each other until Terran forces restored order. Mek’s grandfather said they were the names of people killed resisting Earth.

That was probably the same thing seen from the other side.

The tram radio crackled. The old driver struck it with one claw. A voice broke through, loud and excited.

“—Orion is ours. The Seventh Terran Warfleet has abandoned the sector. Our ships hold the gates from Bellatrix to the Three Fires. Orion is ours.”

One of the rear passengers made a sharp clicking cry.

Another answered, “Ours!”

The old driver turned up the receiver.

“The protectorate council calls upon all district assemblies to resume sovereign authority. Planetary defense units are ordered to secure Terran military stores and communications stations. Citizens are warned not to damage water equipment, pressure plants or food systems.”

The tram slowed.

Tony looked through the dusty window. People were coming out of the houses. Pas workers left factories. Shopkeepers pulled down metal shutters. Everywhere radios called the same words.

Orion is ours.

Sovereign authority.

Secure Terran stores.

A young Pas at the back saw Tony watching. “Your warfleet ran,” he said in Terran.

“It withdrew to Rigel,” Tony replied.

The passenger clicked his mouth-parts. The others clicked too. It took Tony a moment to understand that they were laughing.

The tram stopped before the north square.

“Come, Tony,” Eep said.

Tony got down. Before the doors shut, the old driver leaned toward him.

“Go back to your people, small one.”

“But I’m meeting Mek.”

“Go back.”

“Why?”

The driver glanced at the gathering crowd.

“Because today everyone remembers.”

The tram rolled away.

Tony watched it until smoke hid its rear wheels.

“What does that mean?” he asked.

“Insufficient information,” Eep said.

They crossed the square.

A group of Pas-udeti stood around the public telescreen. The screen showed a blurred picture of Orion Station. The white Terran sunburst had been pulled from the command tower. In its place hung a black banner marked with three silver circles.

Tony had seen that design in a museum.

It was the Pas Confederacy symbol, illegal since the occupation.

Nobody in the square paid attention to Tony. That was worse than if they had stared. He kept close to Eep and entered a narrow street between shell-brick houses.

A Pas woman waited beneath an awning. She wore a copper chain around her head and a padded telepath’s harness over her chest. Tony recognized her. Her name was Seli-amat, and people said she could hear emotions at fifty paces.

Terrans said Pas telepathy was exaggerated. Tony knew better. Once Seli-amat had told him he was hiding a stolen sweet under his tongue.

She lifted one long hand.

“Stop.”

Tony stopped.

“You are afraid,” she said.

“No, I’m not.”

“You were not afraid when you left the white city. Now you are.”

“I’m just going to Mek’s.”

“His family will not welcome you.”

“I go there all the time.”

“Yesterday is not today.”

Tony shifted his weight. Dust whispered around his boots. “Because of Orion?”

“Yes.”

“My father says it was a trick.”

“All victories are tricks to the defeated.”

“He says Earth still has more fleets.”

“Earth has many fleets. Earth has many worlds. Earth has many names for land belonging to others.”

“It’s not called land when it’s a planet.”

The woman’s head tilted. Tony felt foolish, though he was sure he was technically right.

“You speak our words,” she said. “You eat our food. You know the songs children sing in the reservoir tunnels. That will not protect you.”

“Protect me from what?”

“Memory.”

Eep moved between them. “The child is under civil protection.”

Seli-amat looked at the machine’s smooth white body. “Civil protection is another Terran name.”

“For what?” Tony asked.

“For force.”

A distant crash sounded from the square. Then a cheer.

Tony turned.

Pas youths were pulling down the blue-and-white sign from the District Tax Office. It struck the pavement. They jumped on it until the thin metal bent.

“I didn’t put up that sign,” Tony said.

Seli-amat’s mouth-parts tightened. “Go home, Tony Rossi.”

He wanted to obey. His mother had said to listen to Eep, though, and Eep had not told him to go home.

Besides, Mek’s house was only two streets away.

Tony walked on.

Mek’s family lived behind a machine shop. The front yard was filled with engine shells, pressure pumps and stripped vehicle parts. Usually Mek and the others played there. They built forts from scrap or pretended the broken engines were starships.

Today six Pas children stood around the launch pit.

Mek was the tallest. His shell was glossy black except at the joints, where new growth showed pale gray. Beside him stood Vel, Iri, little Sott and the twins from the fuel depot. They had known Tony since he was five.

Tony raised a hand.

“I brought Eep. He can cut the new exhaust opening.”

Nobody answered.

The launch pit was a hole lined with metal plates. Yesterday it had been a model spaceport for the Pas Confederacy. Tony’s tin cruiser had played the Terran patrol ship. They had planned a battle in which everybody switched sides twice and then fought the ice monsters of Procyon.

Mek held something behind his back.

“What have you got?” Tony asked.

Mek showed him.

It was Tony’s tin cruiser.

The toy had been flattened. Its magnetic wings were bent, and the blue sunburst on its side had been scratched away.

Tony stared at it. “You broke my ship.”

Mek tossed it at his feet.

“It crashed at Orion.”

The twins clicked with amusement.

“That was my best one.”

“Terrans have many ships,” Vel said. “Your father can get another.”

“My father doesn’t get me ships. I bought that with school credits.”

“Take it home.”

Tony picked it up. One jagged wing had cut his glove, but not deeply enough to break the pressure seal.

“What’s wrong with you?”

Mek’s eyes—four dull red beads beneath his brow ridge—watched him without blinking.

“Go back to New Ashton.”

“But we were going to finish the port.”

“It is not a Terran port now.”

“It wasn’t before. It was Confederacy.”

“You said the Confederacy ships needed Terran engines.”

“They did. Otherwise they couldn’t reach orbit.”

“They reached Orion.”

“Those were converted freighters.”

Mek stepped closer. “Converted freighters beat your warfleet.”

“Only because they used a trick.”

The Pas children clicked angrily.

Tony heard his father’s voice in his own words and wished he had not said them.

“We can still play,” he added. “I’ll be the Confederacy.”

“No,” Mek said.

“Then I’ll be the ice monsters.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Mek’s smaller arms twitched against his chest. “Because this is not a game.”

“It was yesterday.”

“Yesterday we had no ships.”

“You don’t have ships. Grown-ups do.”

Mek struck Tony across the front of his hood.

It was not a hard blow, but Tony staggered. Eep’s tool-arms unfolded at once.

“Threat detected,” the machine said. “Stand clear.”

The Pas children backed away.

Tony touched the scratch on his hood. “Why did you do that?”

Mek’s voice came low and careful.

“My father died at the south mine.”

“That was an accident.”

“The Terran guards shot him.”

Tony knew there had been trouble at the mine two years ago. Joe had called it a wage riot. Seven Pas workers had died.

“Your mother said he was crushed.”

“My mother told you that.”

“Why would she lie?”

“Because your father worked for the district. Because you came to our house. Because we had no ships.”

There it was again.

Ships.

Warships, freighters, supply ships. Tony felt as if everyone had agreed on the meaning of something while he slept.

“I never hurt your father.”

“You live in his world.”

“It’s my world too. I was born here.”

Mek bent and scooped up a handful of red dust. “Were your people born from this?”

“Nobody is born from dust.”

“We were.”

He threw the dust against Tony’s jacket.

Eep seized Mek’s wrist.

The machine moved so quickly that Tony barely saw it. One moment Mek stood before him; the next, his arm was locked straight and Eep’s steel clamp held it at the joint.

Mek screamed.

“Let him go!” Tony shouted.

“Assault has occurred.”

“You’ll break his arm!”

“Further assault must be prevented.”

The other children attacked.

Vel struck Eep with a metal bar. One twin jumped onto the machine’s back. Iri clawed at the vision-band. Eep swung a tool-arm and threw Vel into a pile of engine parts.

Tony grabbed Eep’s body. “Stop! They’re my friends!”

A stone hit his hood.

More Pas had entered the yard.

Older youths crowded the gate. Behind them came workers from the square, shopkeepers, women in dust cloaks. Someone must have heard Mek scream. Someone saw the white Terran machine holding a Pas child.

“Guard robot!” a voice cried.

“It is killing him!”

“Pull it apart!”

Eep released Mek and pushed Tony behind its body.

“Emergency withdrawal,” it said. “Remain within one meter.”

The crowd surged into the yard.

A pipe rang against Eep’s shell. A claw tore off one of his tool-arms. Sparks snapped from the joint. Eep sprayed a white cloud of crowd-control gas, but the thin wind carried it away.

Tony coughed inside his hood.

“Mek!” he shouted. “Tell them! Eep didn’t—”

He could not see Mek.

The crowd pressed tighter. Black shells, leathery faces, claws and bars. Mouth-parts clicked. Voices rose in both languages.

“Occupiers!”

“White-grub!”

“Terran!”

“Break the machine!”

Eep’s remaining arm locked around Tony’s waist. The robot began climbing over the scrap heap toward the rear wall. Pas hands caught his legs. One leg came off with a shriek of metal.

Eep lurched but did not fall.

“Mob conditions exceed civil tolerance,” he said.

His voice remained calm. That made everything worse.

Someone hit Tony’s shoulder. Another hand grabbed his boot. He kicked wildly and felt the boot strike a hard shell.

“Get away, beetles!” he screamed. “Get away!”

The word stopped him.

Beetles.

His father’s word.

Mek’s face appeared between two adults. Tony saw him hear it. The four red eyes did not change, but something in them closed.

Then the crowd swallowed him again.

Eep reached the rear wall. His feet clamped to the stone, and he lifted Tony toward the top.

A chain looped around the robot’s body.

Twenty Pas pulled.

Eep fell backward. Tony struck the ground. His hood flashed red where the pressure lining tore.

“Seal failure,” Eep announced. “Respiratory danger.”

Tony tasted dust.

It was dry and bitter. He tried to breathe and could not get enough air.

Eep crawled over him. The robot had only three legs now. His white shell was split open, exposing cables and silver control boxes. Stones hammered him. Bars chopped into plastic. Claws pulled at the cables.

Still he covered Tony.

“Emergency signal transmitted,” Eep said.

His voice skipped.

“Signal—transmitted. Remain—remain calm.”

The vision-band shattered.

Its blue light went out.

Hands pulled the robot away.

Tony clung to the cracked shell. “Don’t! He’s mine! Don’t break him!”

A Pas youth raised an engine block above his head.

The block came down.

Eep’s body flattened with a heavy crunch.

Tony screamed.

The crowd dragged pieces in every direction. A leg spun across the yard. The remaining tool-arm disappeared under stamping feet. Someone held up the memory core, a bright metal cylinder, and dashed it against the wall.

Eep had helped Tony learn to walk. He had found him once in a dust storm. He remembered every school mark Tony had ever earned and every lie Tony had ever told.

The cylinder split.

The mob roared.

Tony curled beside the scrap heap, trying to cover his torn hood with both hands. The air would not come. Shapes blurred in the red light.

A sharp blast cut through the noise.

Then another.

The wall behind Tony exploded inward.

A Terran patrol skimmer hovered beyond it, its induction field hurling dust in every direction. Two armored men fired shock rifles over the crowd. Blue flashes cracked against shells. Pas-udeti fell twitching.

The rest scattered.

A trooper jumped from the skimmer. He wore a sealed helmet with no face behind its dark plate. For one terrible instant Tony thought he was another machine.

Then the man grabbed him.

“Terran child located!”

Tony fought him. “Eep! Get Eep!”

“Move!”

“My EEP’s there!”

The trooper lifted Tony under one arm and ran. A stone struck the man’s armor. Another bounced off the skimmer.

Mek stood at the broken gate.

He did not throw anything. He only watched.

Tony tried to call his name, but dust filled his mouth. The trooper shoved him into the skimmer’s rear compartment. A medic pulled off his ruined hood and clamped a breathing mask over his face.

The skimmer rose.

Below, the yard became small. Pas crowded the streets. Black banners hung from roofs. Smoke climbed over the square.

Tony saw the pieces of Eep scattered on the red ground.

Then the skimmer turned toward New Ashton.

The city envelope shimmered ahead like a glass wall. Beyond it, Terran houses stood in neat white rows.

Home, Tony thought.

But the word did not fit as easily as it had that morning.

The skimmer entered through an emergency lock. Sirens sounded throughout the settlement. People ran between buildings carrying cases, rifles and pressure gear.

The medic removed Tony’s mask.

“You took some dust. Nothing serious.”

“My robot—”

“Forget the robot.”

“It was an EEP.”

“Kid, Karnet is in revolt. We’re losing the outer pumps. Forget the damned robot.”

Tony’s mother reached him before the skimmer settled completely. She pulled him down and held him so tightly his damaged shoulder hurt.

“I told you,” she said. “I told him not to let you go.”

“I’m all right.”

“You’re bleeding.”

“It’s just my glove.”

Joe stood behind her with a service pistol strapped over his shirt. “Who did it?”

Tony looked at him.

“Who hit you? Which one?”

“I don’t know.”

“Was it the beetle boy? Mek?”

Tony remembered himself screaming the word.

“Don’t call him that,” he said, but there was no strength in it now.

A blast shook the envelope.

Everyone looked up.

A section of the transparent pressure field above the south quarter flashed white. Something burning curved through it and struck a warehouse. The roof opened. Flame rolled out.

Joe drew his pistol as if he could shoot the fire.

“Inside,” he said.

They ran home.

The news-screen was working again. Every channel showed fighting. Pas defense units had seized two communication towers and the northern fusion plant. Terran troops still held the starport, the administration dome and New Ashton.

Across the planet, settlements were calling for military assistance.

No assistance was coming.

The Orion route was closed. The Seventh Warfleet had lost eleven capital ships, nineteen cruisers and most of its carriers. Rigel was under attack. Pas forces from six occupied systems had declared for the Confederacy.

Tony sat wrapped in a thermal blanket while his mother cleaned the cut in his hand.

On the screen, converted freighters moved against a Terran battle line. They looked clumsy—thick-bodied cargo ships with gun turrets bolted along their sides. One vanished under a burst of light. Two more kept coming.

“Freight tubs,” Joe muttered.

A freighter struck a cruiser.

The screen filled with white fire.

Joe turned away.

Outside, pulse guns hammered from the direction of the envelope gate.

“Why are they doing it?” Tony asked.

Neither parent answered.

“They know us. The Pas at the water plant know you, Mom. Kesh brings our food packs. Dad works with Pas crews. Why are they attacking everybody?”

Leah pressed a bandage over his palm.

“People are frightened.”

“They weren’t frightened. They were happy.”

Joe opened a cabinet and took out a rifle. “They think the warfleet defeat means they can take the planet.”

“Can they?”

Joe checked the rifle’s charge.

“Not while I’m alive.”

Tony watched him. “But it is their planet.”

Leah’s hand stopped.

Joe looked slowly around.

“What did you say?”

“The old driver said we paid for it with coin and they paid with blood. Seli-amat said Earth has names for land belonging to others.”

“Native slogans.”

“Mek said his father was shot at the south mine.”

“His father threw a cutting torch at a guard.”

“Was he crushed?”

Joe said nothing.

Tony pulled his bandaged hand away from his mother.

“At school they said the Pas nations were fighting each other and Earth restored order.”

“They were fighting,” Joe said. “They’d been fighting for eighty years.”

“So Earth took the planet?”

“We established a protectorate.”

“That’s a name.”

“For what?”

Tony looked toward the window. Smoke drifted over the clean white roofs. Beyond the envelope, red dust moved across the ground where Karnet had stood for thousands of years.

“For taking it.”

His father’s face became dark.

“You’re ten years old.”

“I know.”

“You don’t know anything about frontier history. Before Earth came, they had combustion engines and chemical rockets. Half the population starved every dry cycle. Their governments butchered one another. We brought pressure fields, medicine, fusion power—”

“And mines?”

“Yes, mines. Civilization needs mines.”

“And Terran houses?”

“For Terran workers.”

“And Terran guards?”

“To stop them cutting our throats!”

A warning buzzer sounded. The house lights dimmed, then brightened on emergency current.

Leah went to the window. “The south generator is gone.”

Joe touched the receiver at his belt. Voices crackled through it.

“—all reserve personnel report to Sector Four. Native armor observed on the Karnet road. Repeat, native armor—”

Tony pictured the old tram rattling through dust. He pictured black banners, flattened ships and Eep’s blue light going out.

“They only liked me because of our warships,” he said.

Leah turned from the window. “That isn’t true.”

“Mek did.”

“He was your friend.”

“He said his mother lied because we had ships.”

“He’s a child. He’s repeating what adults say.”

“So am I.”

Joe flinched.

Tony had not meant it as an accusation. It was simply true. Tricks. Freight tubs. Beetles. The words had been ready inside him when he became afraid.

The receiver barked again. Joe lifted it.

“Rossi here.”

“Report to Gate Three,” a voice ordered. “Bring pressure gear. Evacuation priority is being set for dependents.”

“Evacuation?”

“Fleet shuttle from the survey ship Argo. It can take sixty. Women and children first.”

“What about the rest?”

“Hold until relief arrives.”

Joe laughed once.

“What relief?”

The receiver went silent.

Leah began packing. Food concentrates, water tubes, medicine, heat sheets. Her hands shook, but she moved quickly.

Tony remained by the table.

His model spaceport was still there. The cruisers stood on their ramps. The little converted freighter waited beside the fuel tower.

He picked up the freighter.

“Did Earth steal all the worlds?”

Joe was pulling on his pressure jacket. “Not now.”

“You said Earth brought civilization.”

“It did.”

“But did it steal them?”

Joe sealed the jacket ring with unnecessary force. “Some were empty. Some were bought. Some asked for protection.”

“And this one?”

Leah stopped packing.

Joe looked at Tony for a long time.

“When the first Terran expedition came here,” he said, “the Pas-udeti were divided into nine governments. Two asked for our help against the others. We built a base. Then we built another. We took control of the orbital stations to keep the peace.”

“And then?”

“The governments that objected were defeated.”

“By the Terran fleet.”

“Yes.”

“Then it was stolen.”

Joe’s jaw worked. “History isn’t a schoolyard, Tony. Worlds don’t belong to whoever touched them first. They belong to whoever can hold them.”

A pulse blast struck nearby.

The quartz window bowed inward and burst.

The inner safety film held most of the fragments, but the room filled with glittering chips. Leah threw herself over Tony. Joe dropped beside the wall with his rifle raised.

Cold, thin air rushed through the broken frame. Automatic shutters slammed down.

For a few seconds nobody moved.

Then Tony realized he was still holding the toy freighter.

The white cruisers had fallen from their ramps.

They lay upside down on the floor.

At Gate Three, hundreds of Terrans crowded around the emergency lock. Men shouted names from evacuation lists. Children cried inside pressure hoods. The wounded lay on cargo pallets while medical robots moved among them.

The survey ship Argo hung above the road on blue landing jets.

It was not a warship. It was narrow and silver, with scientific sensors along its nose. Its crew had painted over the survey markings and mounted two light guns beneath the hull.

Beyond the envelope, Pas vehicles approached through the dust.

They were low armored tractors, quarry haulers and fuel carriers with plates welded over their engines. Converted freighters on the ground.

Terran guns fired from the gate towers.

One vehicle burst apart. The others kept coming.

“Freight tubs,” Tony whispered.

Joe heard him.

“Yes.”

The boarding officer called, “Leah Rossi! Anthony Rossi!”

Leah pulled Tony toward the ramp.

Tony resisted. “Dad’s coming.”

Joe shook his head.

“I’m assigned to the gate.”

“No,” Leah said.

“I can’t board.”

“You can. Throw away the rifle.”

“There are four hundred people here and sixty seats.”

“Throw it away!”

The ground shook. A gate tower leaned sideways, trailing sparks. Terran troops ran from beneath it.

Joe gripped Leah’s shoulders.

“Get him to the inner systems.”

“Joe—”

“Earth if you can.”

Tony stared at his father. “Aren’t we coming back?”

Joe looked past him toward the red plain.

Pas banners moved in the dust. The black cloth and three silver circles were almost invisible against the darkening sky.

“Maybe,” he said.

“You said we’d hold the planet.”

“We’ll fight for it.”

“Will we win?”

His father’s hands fell from Leah’s shoulders.

All morning Joe had been angry. At the announcer, at the Pas, at the freighters, at Tony. Now the anger drained from him. He looked suddenly older.

“Not this time,” he said.

Leah made a small sound.

Joe glanced at the sky, as if he could see beyond it to Orion, Rigel and all the stars on Tony’s edutapes.

“We’ve been moving outward for six hundred years,” he said. “Every generation farther from Earth. Bases, colonies, protectorates. We thought the arrows only went one way.”

Tony remembered the green arrows spreading across the spiral arm.

“What happens now?”

“Now they turn around.”

“All of them?”

“Not today. Not in a year. Maybe not in a hundred years.” Joe tightened the strap of Tony’s hood. “We’ll defend every base. Every moon. Every rotten piece of rock. We’ll call retreats strategic withdrawals and lost planets temporary reverses. We’ll build new fleets. They’ll build fleets to break them.”

“Until Earth?”

“If it goes that far.”

The boarding officer shouted their names again.

Tony looked toward Karnet. Smoke covered the city, but he knew its streets—the reservoir tunnels, the machine shop, the tram line and the memorial wall.

“I was born here,” he said.

“I know.”

“It’s home.”

Joe nodded. “That’s the trouble. It was their home first. Then we made it ours. Now both sides will kill to decide which word matters.”

Another blast struck the envelope. A blue-white crack spread over the field above them.

The boarding officer seized Tony’s arm.

“On the ship!”

Leah kissed Joe. Then she dragged Tony up the ramp.

He twisted around for one last look. His father stood among the soldiers and engineers beneath the failing gate. Behind them lay New Ashton, its reinforced roofs burning. Ahead came the Pas vehicles, slow and ugly and impossible to stop.

The Argo’s hatch closed.

Jets thundered. The deck pushed against Tony’s boots.

Through a small quartz port he watched the settlement drop away. The envelope flickered over it, pale as a soap bubble. Karnet spread beyond the Terran road, older, larger and dark beneath the dust. For the first time Tony saw that New Ashton had been built inside Karnet’s outer wall. Terran streets covered Pas foundations. The starport cut through an ancient burial field. The white city had not been placed beside the native one.

It had been placed on top of it.

The Argo climbed.

Atmosphere reddened around the hull, then thinned to black. Stars appeared.

Somewhere among them, ships were fighting at Rigel. Beyond Rigel were other bases, other settlements, other children who had been born on worlds their fathers called home.

Tony opened his hand.

He still held the little converted freighter. Its paint was chipped, but it had not broken.

Below, the city envelope flashed once and went dark.

The Argo turned toward the inner systems.

On the navigation screen, a thin course line reached from Pas-udet toward Earth. It was only a line of light, but Tony thought of his school map and all the arrows reversing, one after another, across the centuries.

Behind him lay a stolen home.

Ahead lay the long retreat.

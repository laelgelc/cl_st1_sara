## The Apparatus of Impartial Correction

The public hall of the Provincial Assembly had not been so full since the agricultural exhibition, when a two-headed calf was shown beside a new Belgian threshing machine and received, according to the local newspaper, considerably more attention than the machine.

By seven o’clock every chair was occupied. Schoolmasters stood along the walls; merchants’ wives, rustling in bright dresses, filled the first three rows; officers displayed polished boots and expressions of official endurance. On the right sat representatives of the municipal council, the district court, the prison committee, the temperance society, and the Society for the Encouragement of Useful Knowledge. On the left, beneath a portrait of the Sovereign, sat His Excellency the governor, who was deaf in the left ear and almost deaf in the right. On his knees slept a yellow pug-dog named Hector.

The air was hot and smelled of wax, wool, dust, and perfume. A single fly buzzed against the upper pane with such persistence that several persons looked at it reproachfully.

Upon the stage stood a table, a lectern, a glass of water, and an object of considerable dimensions concealed beneath green baize. From under the cloth protruded two nickel-plated rods, one brass pedal, and something resembling the end of a black leather bench.

At precisely twelve minutes past seven, State Councillor and pedagogue Arkady Arkadyevich Pruzhin mounted the platform.

He was a broad, smooth-faced man of forty-eight, with carefully arranged whiskers, a white waistcoat, and the tranquil forehead of one accustomed to improving others. For twenty-three years he had served the cause of popular education: first as a teacher of penmanship, then as inspector of district schools, and finally as author of several works, among which *The Child as a Future Administrative Unit* had received honourable mention from a committee in Tambov.

He bowed to the governor, to the ladies, to the officers, and separately to the portrait.

“Most respected audience,” he began, “ladies and gentlemen, fathers and mothers, representatives of enlightenment, administration, law, industry, domestic virtue, and our beloved fatherland!”

Hector opened one eye and sneezed.

Arkady Arkadyevich paused until the audience had understood that this was accidental.

“The question which brings us together tonight is ancient, painful, and, I venture to say, unavoidable. How shall we discipline the young? How shall we reform the fallen? How shall we awaken the idle, restrain the violent, admonish the careless, and direct the morally wavering citizen toward the straight and radiant highway of duty?”

The governor cupped a hand to his ear.

“What’s he saying?” he asked his adjutant.

“Discipline, Your Excellency.”

“Quite right. Twenty-five strokes.”

Hector barked once.

A schoolmistress in the third row frowned, but Arkady Arkadyevich, who had heard the recommendation, inclined his head as though acknowledging support from the highest administrative quarter.

“For centuries,” he continued, “these great tasks have been entrusted to the human hand. The father raises his hand against the son; the schoolmaster against the pupil; the sergeant against the recruit; the overseer against the negligent workman. And what follows? Anger! Partiality! Excess! Insufficiency! One child receives three strokes because he has broken a window; another receives seven because the educator’s soup was cold. One soldier is punished weakly because the sergeant likes his singing; another severely because the sergeant has lost at cards. Can this be called justice?”

“No!” cried someone near the door.

“Precisely. It cannot. Conventional corporal punishment, ladies and gentlemen, is barbarous, unscientific, unhygienic, emotionally compromised, administratively inconvenient, and statistically useless.”

There was applause, especially from the schoolmistresses. One young barrister whispered that the speaker was evidently a progressive.

Arkady Arkadyevich drank water.

“And yet,” he said, raising one finger, “must we, because the hand is imperfect, reject the principle embodied by the hand?”

The applause ceased.

“Shall we uproot from Russian soil an institution sanctified by household custom, military experience, monastic practice, proverbial wisdom, and the recollections of our own childhood? No, ladies and gentlemen! A thousand times no! The birch rod, properly understood, is not an instrument of cruelty. It is a national pedagogical symbol. It is, if I may so express myself, the severe but loving comma which Providence places in the unfinished sentence of human misconduct.”

The young barrister stopped smiling.

“We must not abolish correction. We must purify it. We must take it from the trembling, irritable, subjective hand and place it in the calm embrace of mechanism. Steam has transformed transport. Electricity has transformed communication. The calculating machine has transformed accounts. Why, then, should exact science hesitate before the final fortress of disorder—the human posterior?”

A merchant coughed violently into his beard. Several ladies lowered their eyes. The governor, having caught only the last word, nodded.

“Fifty,” he said.

Hector barked twice.

Arkady Arkadyevich turned toward the covered object.

“For eleven years, at the sacrifice of rest, fortune, family tranquillity, and two fingernails, I have laboured upon the solution. Tonight I have the honour to present the first Russian Automatic Apparatus for Impartial Correction, Model P-3, with combined moral, statistical, restraining, and revisionary functions.”

He seized the baize and drew it away.

It is difficult to describe the impression produced upon the public.

The machine resembled at once a dentist’s chair, a railway buffer, a sewing apparatus, and an instrument discovered in the cellar of a medieval castle. Its principal body consisted of an iron frame painted a cheerful blue. In the middle was a sloping leather platform furnished with straps for the wrists, ankles, waist, shoulders, and, for reasons not immediately apparent, the chin. Above the platform rose a steel arm terminating in a bundle of interchangeable rods. On one side were three dials marked **AGE**, **CONSTITUTION**, and **DEGREE OF MORAL OBDURACY**. On the other stood a glass-covered counter. Brass pipes descended into a locked box. A nickel receptacle, shaped like a church collection plate, projected from the front.

The audience was silent. The fly continued its work at the window.

Arkady Arkadyevich laid one hand affectionately upon the frame.

“Do not be alarmed by its appearance. Power without elegance is Asiatic; elegance without power is French. Here, I trust, we have achieved a Russian harmony of both.”

He indicated the dials.

“The operation is simplicity itself. The offender is placed here, face downward, though for certain administrative purposes an alternative position may be selected. The restraining bands close automatically. The operator then sets age, physical condition, and moral category: first degree, inattention; second degree, disobedience; third degree, falsehood; fourth degree, theft; fifth degree, repeated theft with insolence; sixth degree, nihilistic tendency, mockery of superiors, or refusal to confess.”

“And lateness?” called a factory owner.

“Second degree for the first offence, third for repetition, fourth if accompanied by excuses.”

“Excellent!”

“The motive force is obtained from a tempered spring. No steam boiler is required. There is no smoke, no coal, no licensed engineer. One winding provides eight thousand corrective movements, though I recommend inspection after every six thousand, especially in damp weather.”

He removed a birch bundle from the steel arm and replaced it with a narrow leather strap.

“For schools, the birch attachment. For prisons and military establishments, leather. For factories, according to the collective agreement, either may be used. A velvet-covered attachment is under consideration for institutes attended by young ladies of noble birth.”

The schoolmistresses conferred in undertones.

“But who,” asked the young barrister, “decides the number?”

Arkady Arkadyevich smiled. It was the smile of a man who has waited eleven years for that question.

“Nobody.”

He allowed the word to travel through the hall.

“Therein lies the moral grandeur of the invention. Men decide badly. Mechanism decides exactly. Observe the receptacle. Into it is placed a specially issued correction coupon. Each coupon possesses a calibrated weight. One gram represents one stroke. Five grams, five strokes; ten, ten; twenty-five, twenty-five. The coupon bears the offender’s name, offence, date, institution, and signature of the responsible authority. As it falls, a balance descends, the weight is registered, the straps close, the apparatus begins, and the counter records the execution of justice.”

He produced several round brass discs.

“For small institutions, where printed coupons would burden the budget, ordinary coin may be employed. A one-kopeck piece, three strokes; two kopecks, seven; five kopecks, twenty; silver denominations according to a scale provided in Appendix B. Any round object of sufficient weight activates the mechanism.”

“Could a button do it?” asked a boy from the gallery.

“If metal, yes. If bone, probably not, though experiments continue.”

The boy looked respectfully at his coat.

“To prevent sentimental interference,” Arkady Arkadyevich went on, “the apparatus cannot be stopped during operation. This is essential. How often does weakness enter at the seventh stroke and ruin the benefit intended by ten! How often do tears, promises, maternal shrieks, influential relatives, or sudden appeals to Christian mercy interrupt an educational process at its most fruitful stage! Here such abuses are impossible. Once begun, correction proceeds to its mathematically appointed conclusion.”

A thin priest raised his eyebrows.

“In emergencies?” he asked.

“There can be no emergency in exactitude, Father.”

“But if the apparatus malfunctions?”

“It cannot.”

“But if—”

“For the satisfaction of persons whose imagination outruns their reason, two special interruption keys have nevertheless been provided. They must be inserted simultaneously into locks situated on opposite sides. The keys are dissimilar, numbered, and entrusted to separate responsible persons. Thus neither caprice nor panic can obstruct the law.”

He called his attendant, a red-haired former soldier named Spiridon, who came forward carrying two enormous keys on blue ribbons.

“Key A remains with the institution’s director. Key B with the physician, chaplain, senior clerk, or other individual enjoying confidence. Spiridon, show them.”

Spiridon held up the keys.

“Don’t lose them,” said Arkady Arkadyevich.

“Never, Arkady Arkadyevich. I keep them as I keep my own soul.”

“Your soul is not an approved storage location.”

The audience laughed. Spiridon laughed also, after looking around to determine whether he might.

A leather dummy was brought from behind the curtain. It wore a schoolboy’s jacket on its upper half and military trousers on its lower, thereby representing the widest practical range of the invention.

“Let us suppose,” said the lecturer, “that this citizen has broken a window, smoked behind the chapel, stolen six pears, contradicted his mother, failed to salute an officer, and written an anonymous letter to the newspaper. A complex case. Twenty strokes.”

He placed a twenty-gram coupon into the receptacle.

There came a metallic click.

The platform opened like a jaw. Two padded arms seized the dummy and drew it flat. The straps snapped shut. A warning bell sounded with surprising delicacy.

Then:

Whack!

Several ladies started.

Whack! Whack! Whack!

The steel arm rose and fell with sober celerity. The counter displayed 1, 2, 3, 4. At ten strokes a small brass hand moved from a white section marked **RESISTANCE** into a pale blue section marked **REFLECTION**. At eighteen it entered **REPENTANCE**. At twenty the machine stopped, released the straps, rang twice, and stamped a paper certificate.

Spiridon carried the certificate to the governor.

“What’s this?” asked His Excellency.

“Successful correction.”

“Not enough. Give him a hundred.”

Hector, disturbed by the demonstration, barked furiously at the dummy.

The applause lasted nearly a minute.

Arkady Arkadyevich’s cheeks glowed. He walked from dial to dial, explaining the self-cleaning bearings, the winter lubrication, the adjustable velocity, the sanitary leather, the duplicate record cylinder, and the optional drawer in which confiscated sweets, cards, tobacco, political pamphlets, and slingshots might be stored.

He then advanced to the broader consequences.

“Imagine, ladies and gentlemen, not one apparatus, but ten thousand! In every school, punctuality. In every office, diligence. In every regiment, obedience. In every factory, productivity. In every prison, remorse. In every home, peace!”

A mother in the second row whispered that the thing would never fit beside the samovar.

“Consider the economy. At present, punishment consumes the valuable time of teachers, warders, non-commissioned officers, foremen, fathers, guardians, and elder brothers. It causes fatigue of the arm, derangement of the nerves, quarrels, complaints, inquiries, medical certificates, and occasionally lawsuits. The Automatic Corrector eliminates all these.”

“Except medical certificates,” murmured the doctor.

“Crime will disappear because punishment will be certain. Idleness will disappear because correction will be immediate. Budget deficits will disappear because the apparatus, when not occupied with offenders, may stamp envelopes, crack nuts, and, with a special attachment, beat carpets. Even family discord will diminish. The husband need no longer punish in anger; the wife need no longer reproach in tears. Both will refer the matter to the domestic tariff.”

This proposal produced mixed expressions among the married portion of the hall.

“A husband coming home after midnight—fifteen grams. A wife exceeding the household allowance—ten. A child lying—five. A cook spoiling the roast—three, unless intentional. Thus emotion gives way to procedure, procedure to order, and order to prosperity.”

The governor leaned toward his adjutant.

“Is he proposing to flog cooks?”

“Among others, Your Excellency.”

“Two hundred. Cooks are incorrigible.”

Hector yawned.

“And our fatherland,” Arkady Arkadyevich cried, now reaching the loftiest portion of his address, “will stand before Europe not as a backward pupil ashamed of its ancestral rod, but as a mature teacher who has fitted that rod with bearings, indicators, safety valves, and a numbered government seal!”

Applause broke out in several parts of the hall. The factory owner shouted “Bravo!” A police captain demanded information about wholesale prices. The headmaster of the classical gymnasium calculated aloud that two machines would suffice if the lower forms were processed before luncheon.

Arkady Arkadyevich bowed. His triumph was complete.

“One final detail,” he said. “Certain critics have objected that coupons may be falsified. To them I answer with this.”

From his waistcoat pocket he drew a large gold chronometer, presented to him by grateful colleagues on the twentieth anniversary of his service. It was a heavy, splendid object, bearing on its lid a double-headed eagle surrounded by laurel leaves and the inscription: **TO THE UNTIRING SOWER OF REASON**.

“The sensitivity of the balance may be verified by any certified weight. This chronometer, for example, weighs exactly two hundred and twelve grams. Were it round enough to enter the receptacle—and it is—its descent would command, under the present ratio, two thousand one hundred and twenty strokes.”

He held it over the nickel plate.

The hall leaned forward.

“Observe, however, that activation requires actual release. So long as the object remains in my rational control—”

At that instant Hector barked.

Arkady Arkadyevich turned his head. The chronometer slipped between his fingers.

It struck the rim, spun once with a golden flash, and dropped into the receptacle.

Click.

For perhaps half a second nobody understood what had happened.

Then the apparatus opened.

Arkady Arkadyevich attempted to step back, but the lower gripping arm caught him behind the knees. He sat abruptly, waved both hands, and vanished forward onto the leather platform. The wrist straps snapped shut. The ankle clamps closed over his boots. A broad belt fastened itself around his waist, crushing his white waistcoat. Finally the chin strap descended and pressed his face sideways against the cushion.

The warning bell rang.

“No,” said Arkady Arkadyevich.

Whack!

His whole body jumped.

Whack!

“Stop!”

Whack!

The counter showed 3.

Spiridon rushed toward the machine.

“The keys!” cried the lecturer. “Keys, idiot!”

“Immediately, Arkady Arkadyevich!”

Whack! Whack! Whack!

The polished public orator disappeared with astonishing speed. In his place lay a red-faced man kicking inside new trousers and using words not listed in any pedagogical manual.

“Turn it off! Break it! Murderers! Donkeys!”

“Do not excite yourself,” said the doctor, coming onto the stage.

“Whack!”

“You lie there and say that!”

The counter reached 17.

Spiridon searched his coat, his trousers, and his boots. He looked under the table, inside the lectern, beneath the dummy, and finally in his cap.

“Where are the keys?” shouted the police captain.

“I had them in my hands!”

“That is not where they are now!”

“Perhaps I put them down.”

“Where?”

“If I knew where, they wouldn’t be lost.”

Whack! Whack! Whack!

“Twenty-five,” announced the governor, watching the counter. “That ought to be sufficient.”

“It’s already thirty-two, Your Excellency.”

“Then stop.”

“It cannot be stopped.”

“Fifty more for insubordination.”

Hector barked encouragement.

The audience rose. Ladies climbed onto chairs; officers climbed onto the stage. Someone shouted for water. Someone else shouted that water must never be poured on machinery. The headmaster demanded everyone remain calm and was knocked against the dummy by the factory owner.

“Key A!” screamed Arkady Arkadyevich. “Blue ribbon! Key B! Other blue ribbon! Find them!”

“They were both blue?” asked the police captain.

“For aesthetic uniformity—ah!—curse aesthetics!”

The counter showed 74, 75, 76.

The doctor pulled at the waist strap. It tightened automatically.

“Self-adjusting,” groaned the inventor. “I made it self-adjusting.”

The young barrister tried to lift the steel arm. It struck his hat into the audience.

Spiridon crawled under the machine and seized a rod.

“Don’t touch the compensator!” cried Arkady Arkadyevich.

“What’s the compensator?”

“The thing you’re touching!”

A spring released with a bang, and the rate increased.

Whack-whack! Whack-whack! Whack-whack!

The counter passed 100.

It should be said to Spiridon’s credit that his devotion never weakened. His judgment, however, remained unequal to his devotion.

“Cut off the trousers!” ordered the doctor.

“Not the trousers!” Spiridon protested. “They’re new. Thirty-two roubles, from Moscow.”

“Cut them!”

“Cut the straps first!”

“The straps are steel-lined.”

“Then cut around them carefully.”

Arkady Arkadyevich raised his head as far as the chin restraint allowed.

“Spiridon,” he said in a dreadful voice, “if I survive, I will personally put you through this machine with the church bell.”

Whack!

The governor’s adjutant drew his sword and struck the main spring housing. The blade broke. Its upper half flew across the stage, knocked a plaster laurel from the portrait frame, and landed in the lap of the chairwoman of the temperance society.

The machine continued.

237, 238, 239.

The police captain ordered the exits closed, though no one had attempted escape. The factory owner offered five hundred roubles to anyone who stopped the mechanism. Three engineering students began turning different screws. One screw released black oil over the governor’s uniform. Another opened the drawer of confiscated objects, scattering tobacco, nuts, and sample slingshots across the floor. The third caused the certificate printer to issue a paper every ten strokes:

**CORRECTION IN PROGRESS.**

**CORRECTION IN PROGRESS.**

**CORRECTION IN PROGRESS.**

At 318 the priest attempted to comfort the victim.

“Endure, Arkady Arkadyevich. Suffering purifies.”

“It is easy to purify others!” came the reply.

At 401 the electric lamps flickered, though the machine was not connected to them.

At 460 Spiridon found Key A in the pocket of the schoolboy dummy.

“Here!”

“Where is B?”

Silence followed.

The former soldier turned pale.

“I may have placed it somewhere safe.”

“Where is safe?”

“My overcoat.”

“Where is your overcoat?”

“At the railway station.”

The machine maintained mathematical precision.

Whack. 501.

Whack. 502.

Whack. 503.

The first enthusiasm of the public had by now disappeared. The schoolmistresses looked ill. The factory owner no longer asked about wholesale prices. Several mothers silently removed metal buttons from their children’s coats. The headmaster of the gymnasium, who had intended to request two machines, slipped his prospectus beneath a chair.

At 600, the municipal engineer arrived. He walked around the apparatus, tapped it with a pencil, and declared that it had been incorrectly installed.

“It isn’t installed!” cried the barrister. “It’s portable!”

“That explains the instability.”

“Can you stop it?”

“Not without plans.”

“The plans are in my desk,” gasped Arkady Arkadyevich.

“Where is the desk?”

“In my house.”

“Then we require a written requisition for access.”

The police captain threatened to requisition the engineer personally. This accelerated matters but did not improve them.

At 713 the doctor announced that the victim possessed an unusually strong constitution.

“Set the dial to weak!” pleaded Arkady Arkadyevich.

The doctor turned the **CONSTITUTION** indicator. It resisted, then sprang from **ROBUST** to **ATHLETIC**.

The blows became heavier.

The doctor stepped away.

At 800, the audience began counting involuntarily. At first only a clerk near the wall murmured the numbers, but soon others joined him, perhaps because numerical participation gave them the illusion of assistance.

“Eight hundred and eleven… eight hundred and twelve… eight hundred and thirteen…”

“Stop counting!” shrieked Arkady Arkadyevich.

They stopped for six strokes, then resumed in whispers.

The governor had at last understood the seriousness of the situation.

“Use artillery,” he ordered.

“The hall would be damaged, Your Excellency.”

“What sort of provincial hall cannot withstand one shell?”

The adjutant proposed the fire brigade as a compromise.

A messenger was sent.

At 936, Key B was discovered. It hung from Spiridon’s neck beneath his shirt, where he had placed it for absolute security. He wept with joy. Key A was inserted into the left lock, Key B into the right.

“Turn!” commanded the police captain.

They turned.

Nothing happened.

“Opposite directions!” cried Arkady Arkadyevich.

They turned opposite directions.

A small panel opened and displayed the words:

**INTERRUPTION REQUIRES AUTHORISATION SEAL.**

“Where is the seal?”

Arkady Arkadyevich closed his eyes.

“In my desk.”

The engineer nodded. “As I anticipated.”

At 1,000 strokes, the machine rang ceremonially, stamped a larger certificate, and shifted the indicator from **REPENTANCE** to **MORAL RENEWAL**.

Arkady Arkadyevich no longer insulted anyone. He asked only, in a quiet and astonished voice, whether it was still going.

It was.

The fire brigade arrived at 1,127 with helmets, axes, hooks, two hoses, and a red wagon drawn by exhausted horses. Its chief, a small man with a magnificent black beard, surveyed the scene.

“Fire?”

“No.”

“Smoke?”

“No.”

“Then why were we called?”

“To destroy a pedagogical instrument.”

The chief considered this unusual but not beyond municipal experience.

The firemen first attempted water. A jet struck the apparatus, ricocheted from its casing, drenched the first six rows, and knocked the governor backward over his chair. Hector escaped into the orchestra enclosure and barked there with increased authority.

The machine, washed and shining, continued.

1,204. 1,205. 1,206.

Axes were brought down upon the outer housing. Blue enamel flew. One fireman lost his helmet; another struck the counter, which spun to 9,999, returned to 1,231, and proceeded accurately from there.

“Careful with the counter!” cried the municipal clerk. “It is required for the report.”

The chief ordered crowbars. The iron frame bent but held. The floorboards cracked. The platform tilted, carrying Arkady Arkadyevich with it, while the corrective arm adjusted its angle and continued its duty.

1,390.

1,391.

1,392.

The lecturer’s commemorative chronometer could be heard rattling inside the balance chamber at each stroke, untiringly sowing reason.

At 1,500, the chief lost patience.

“Break everything,” he said.

Six firemen attacked at once. They smashed the spring box, tore away the certificate printer, severed two rods, split the blue casing, and drove a hook through the principal gear. The apparatus hesitated.

Whack.

1,647.

A gear screamed.

Whack.

1,648.

The steel arm rose slowly, as though reconsidering its entire philosophy.

Whack.

1,649.

Everyone held his breath.

The arm descended one last time.

Whack.

1,650.

Then the machine gave a deep metallic sigh. Springs burst from its side. The counter fell off. The restraining straps opened, the leather platform collapsed, and Arkady Arkadyevich slid gently onto the wet floor among brass coupons, broken rods, official certificates, and fragments of the inscription **IMPARTIAL CORRECTION**.

Thus perished the apparatus, having completed one thousand six hundred and fifty strokes out of the two thousand one hundred and twenty assigned to it by mechanical justice.

The idea perished somewhat sooner.

Arkady Arkadyevich remained in bed for three months. The provincial newspaper reported that his indisposition had resulted from “an unforeseen technical misunderstanding during a highly successful scientific demonstration.” The governor recommended him for a medal, then forgot the matter. Spiridon returned the trousers to the Moscow tailor, who declined all responsibility.

In autumn Arkady Arkadyevich resumed teaching.

Those who had known him before observed a remarkable change. He no longer spoke of the child as an administrative unit. He ceased writing regulations. When a pupil came late, he asked whether the road was muddy. When a boy broke a window, he first examined the putty. He forgave noise, clumsiness, ink stains, forgotten books, and even whispering during geography. His voice became low; his step cautious; his expression gentle and permanently melancholy.

The pupils loved him.

Sometimes, during arithmetic, when a child recited numbers in a steady sequence—forty-seven, forty-eight, forty-nine—Arkady Arkadyevich would grow pale and ask him to begin again from another place.

He never rebuilt the apparatus, never mentioned mechanical justice, and would not carry coins.

As for the golden chronometer, it was recovered from the wreckage, flattened almost beyond recognition. Arkady Arkadyevich kept it in a drawer without having it repaired. Beneath the crushed double-headed eagle, the final words of the inscription could still be read:

**THE UNTIRING SOWER OF REASON.**

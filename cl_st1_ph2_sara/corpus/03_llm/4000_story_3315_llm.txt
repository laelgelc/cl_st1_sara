“Why do I keep that queer green satin gown, my dear? Because it is the most beautiful gown I ever wore.”

Yes, I know what it looks like now. Hold it farther from the window, if you please; the sun is not kind to ancient satin. The sleeves are absurdly tight, the waist would scarcely encircle a modern schoolgirl, and that little train—well, no sensible woman would sweep a floor with her skirts nowadays. Yet you must not laugh too heartily. Your grandfather first saw me in that gown.

There! I have let the secret out before the story is half begun. Pour me another cup of tea, and bring your mending nearer. If you prick your finger at the interesting places, I shall not be responsible.

I was seventeen that autumn and staying with Miss Persis Lane, who had been my mother’s dearest friend. She lived in a small gray house beside the Le Baron estate, with a quince hedge in front and such spotless muslin curtains that sparrows seemed ashamed to flutter dust upon them. Miss Persis was tiny, brisk, and incurably hospitable. She wore black bombazine, smelled faintly of lavender, and could make a festival out of a dropped stitch.

Madam Le Baron lived in the great house beyond the elms. She was not, of course, truly a madam in any foreign sense; the title had belonged by courtesy to the oldest Le Baron widow for generations. She was eighty then, erect as a candle, with white hair rolled high and eyes that had lost none of their dark brightness. The village stood a little in awe of her. Miss Persis did not. They had played together as children, though one had been the judge’s daughter and the other the mantua-maker’s.

One October afternoon I sat at Miss Persis’s parlour window, shortening a cuff and thinking, as girls do, that nothing of consequence could ever happen in such a quiet place. The maples were red, the kettle purred, and Miss Persis was counting the threads in a linen hem.

Then the side door burst open and Jessop, Madam Le Baron’s old coachman, appeared without ceremony.

Jessop ordinarily moved with the solemnity of a hearse. That day he behaved like a jack-in-the-box.

“Miss Persis!” he cried. “Madam’s compliments—and would the young lady come at once?”

Miss Persis dropped her spectacles.

“What has happened? Is Madam ill?”

“No, ma’am. Company.”

“Company?”

“Miss Euphemia arrived from Boston an hour ago, with six young ladies and five young gentlemen. Their coach lost a wheel beyond Brackett’s turn, and the whole party is to remain until to-morrow. Madam says there are thirteen at table, counting herself, and Miss Le Baron declares thirteen unlucky. Madam requests that Miss”—he bowed at me—“will make fourteen.”

“The Sultan is on the front door-step,” said Miss Persis, “and wishes an extra spoon! Of course she will go.”

I did not say of course. I sat with my cuff in one hand and my needle in the other, feeling that consequence had arrived too suddenly.

Madam’s grandniece, Effie Gay Le Baron, was the most fashionable girl of my acquaintance—if one can call a person an acquaintance who has twice looked through one at church. She had been educated in Boston, wore bonnets from New York, and was said to dance with officers. Her companions would be equally splendid. I possessed one brown merino for Sundays and a blue sprigged muslin suitable for tea with the minister. An evening toilet? I had never needed one.

“I cannot go,” I said.

“Nonsense,” said Miss Persis. “Madam has asked you.”

“I have nothing to wear.”

“You have your muslin.”

“It comes to my ankles.”

“So do your ankles, I presume.”

At seventeen this did not seem amusing. I explained—with some warmth—that one could not dine among fashionable people in a washed muslin with darned lace at the throat.

Jessop coughed behind his hand.

“Madam anticipated the difficulty, miss. She says you are to come as you are, and she will attend to everything.”

That sounded more alarming than comforting; but Miss Persis had already wrapped me in my cloak and tied my bonnet strings. She kissed my cheek, whispered, “Remember your manners, child, and do not imagine the world is looking only at you,” and sent me across the lawn under Jessop’s escort.

The Le Baron house seemed to me then as grand as a palace. We entered through a hall paved in black and white marble, cool even in autumn and dim beneath family portraits. There were carved chests against the walls, tall silver candlesticks, and a smell compounded of beeswax, sandalwood, and generations of rose leaves. From the drawing-room came laughter, piano notes, and the rustle of silk—so much silk that it sounded like wind in dry grass.

Jessop conducted me upstairs, where Madam Le Baron waited in her chamber.

She sat beside a fire, wearing dove-coloured silk and an Indian shawl. Her maid, Martha, stood near an open cedar chest. Across the bed lay a quantity of green satin.

“My dear,” said Madam, holding out her hand, “you are very good to rescue us from our unfortunate number.”

I murmured that I was honoured. This was strictly true and wholly incomplete.

“I have taken a liberty,” she continued. “Persis tells me you are nearly the size I was at seventeen. Martha has brought down a gown of my youth. It has been carefully kept, and I think no alteration will be required beyond letting out a seam.”

She lifted the satin.

My dear, you have seen that gown, faded and flattened by more than a hundred years. You cannot know what it was then. The colour was not apple green, nor emerald, nor olive, but a clear, cool shade like sunlight through young beech leaves. Silver leaves were embroidered along the bodice; the skirt opened over a petticoat of pale brocade; the sleeves ended at the elbow in three little falls of lace.

It was exquisite—and fifty years out of fashion.

Was this a jest? For one dreadful instant I thought Effie Gay had planned it. I imagined myself entering the drawing-room dressed like a portrait, while eleven elegant strangers whispered behind their fans. I imagined the young gentlemen bowing like pump-handles in mock admiration. I saw every ribbon, every ridiculous silver leaf, every inch of that betraying train.

Madam watched me. Her face was courteous and tranquil, but there was eagerness beneath the tranquillity, and something more tender than eagerness. The gown was not merely an old garment to her. It was a door, and she had opened it upon her youth.

“How charming it is,” I managed to say.

“You do not think it too antiquated?”

There are questions that test one’s character more severely than questions of war or fortune. At least they do when one is seventeen.

I could have said that my muslin would serve. I could have pleaded a headache. Madam would never have insisted; indeed, she would have apologised for embarrassing me. Yet I knew she had meant kindness. She had remembered that I owned no fine dress and had offered the loveliest thing her memory contained.

Then I recalled Miss Persis’s words: Do not imagine the world is looking only at you.

My mother, too, had often told me that happiness depended upon maintaining a sense of proportion. At seventeen I considered this the least inspiring of her counsels. Proportion! What had proportion to do with a green satin gown and possible social destruction? But suddenly I set my wounded vanity on one side and Madam’s pleasure on the other. Vanity made a great noise; kindness made none. Yet kindness weighed more.

“Too antiquated?” I said. “No. I should be proud to wear it.”

Madam’s fingers tightened over mine.

“Martha,” she said, “we shall dress her exactly as I was dressed for the Governor’s ball.”

That was how my martyrdom became a pageant.

Martha brushed my hair upward over a cushion, leaving soft curls before my ears. She powdered it lightly—only lightly, though I sneezed three times—and fastened it with a high tortoise-shell comb. The satin fitted after one seam had been eased. There were slippers with silver buckles, a gauze scarf, and a fan painted with shepherdesses who had never encountered a real sheep.

When all was complete, Madam led me to the long mirror.

A perfect guy? Well, perhaps—and yet—

I did not look like myself. I looked taller, paler, and oddly stately. The gown demanded a straight back and quiet hands. One could not fidget in that satin. Each time I turned, the silver embroidery caught the candlelight as frost catches moonlight.

Madam was not looking at me, however. She was looking beyond me at the portrait over the mantel.

I had noticed it before: a girl in green satin standing beside a marble pillar, one hand holding a closed fan. Her face had Madam’s dark eyes but none of Madam’s age. A strand of green stones lay about her throat.

Martha drew a quick breath.

“She is the very image, ma’am.”

“No,” said Madam softly. “She is prettier than I was.”

Old ladies are permitted certain tender inaccuracies.

Madam crossed to a cabinet and unlocked a small drawer. From it she took a narrow morocco case. Inside lay a necklace of chrysoprase—oval green stones, translucent and cool, clasped with seed pearls.

“I had intended to leave these sleeping,” she said. “But they belong with the gown.”

I drew back. “Oh, I could not!”

“You can, if you stand still.”

She fastened them herself. Her hands trembled once against my neck.

“There,” she said. “Now the picture is complete.”

When I turned, tears shone in her eyes. That ended my rebellion. Let all Boston laugh! For the sake of that look I would have worn a helmet, a ruff, and Queen Elizabeth’s farthingale.

We went down together.

The drawing-room doors stood open. Within were Effie Gay and her friends, bright as a box of French bonbons. There were pink silks, blue gauzes, yellow ribbons, white shoulders, black coats, shining boots, and enough curls to stuff a sofa. Effie Gay herself wore crimson velvet and a gold chain wound twice about her waist.

Conversation ceased.

Then one young lady tittered.

Another whispered, not softly enough, “It is Madam’s grandmother!”

One of the gentlemen turned abruptly toward the window, but I saw his shoulders shake. Effie Gay stared, flushed, and came forward.

“My dear Aunt,” she said, “what a delightful masquerade!”

Her tone made the word less delightful than it appeared.

Madam’s hand rested on my arm.

“This is Miss Clara Bell,” she said. “She has been so obliging as to wear my gown, because I desired to see it living once more.”

There was a small silence. Even thoughtless people sometimes recognise dignity when it is placed before them.

Effie Gay recovered first. She made me a very proper courtesy, though merriment still danced in her eyes.

“How romantic,” she said. “We shall all be jealous.”

No one was jealous.

We went in to dinner, and I learned that humiliation does not kill, however earnestly one may expect it to. I had been placed beside a fair-haired youth named Mr. Pritchard, who asked whether my fan concealed a dagger. Across from me, two girls examined my sleeves whenever they thought I was not looking. A gentleman at the lower end requested to know if minuets were to follow dessert.

Cry? No, I did not cry. Tears would have spotted the satin.

Instead, I remembered the portrait. I kept my back straight, moved my hands quietly, and answered civil questions with civil answers. When Mr. Pritchard asked whether I had come by sedan chair, I said no, but that my attendants had been obliged to stable the six white horses. Madam laughed aloud. Even Effie Gay smiled with some good nature.

There was one young man who did not laugh.

He sat near the head of the table and had grave gray eyes, a broad brow, and a habit of listening before he spoke. His name was Henry Arden. I had noticed him when I entered because he had moved a chair out of Madam’s path without making a display of the service.

During dessert Effie Gay asked him what he thought of “our ancestral apparition.”

“I think,” he said, “that Miss Bell wears the handsomest gown at the table.”

This was so plainly uttered that nobody could decide whether to tease him. I dared not look up for several minutes; but seventeen-year-old girls can see remarkably well while contemplating their plates.

After dinner there was music and dancing. The ladies wished me to dance a quadrille, partly, I suspect, to see what the train would do. It did what trains always do: entangled itself about my ankles and endangered everyone else. Yet I survived. Mr. Arden danced with me and did not once refer to my costume. He asked about books, Miss Persis, and whether I preferred the sea in winter or summer. These questions seemed evidence of extraordinary intellect.

The evening, which I had expected to last a century, ended before midnight. The girls’ ridicule softened when it failed to distress me. One even begged to examine the embroidery and declared it finer than anything in Boston. Effie Gay kissed Madam good-night and told me I had been “a famous sport.” She had no mother, poor child, and had been taught to value brilliance before kindness. One should remember such things. They do not excuse all faults, but they put them—there is that tiresome word again—in proportion.

Madam sent me home wrapped in a fur-lined cloak, with Jessop carrying the gown’s train over one arm. At Miss Persis’s door I removed the chrysoprase necklace.

“I shall never forget your goodness,” Madam said as she took it. “Nor how you gave an old woman back one evening of her girlhood.”

I had thought myself the receiver of the favour; it appeared that she did not. Perhaps the best kindnesses are those in which neither person can determine who has given more.

Miss Persis was waiting in the kitchen with hot chocolate and both candles burning.

“Well?” she demanded.

“I have been laughed at by Boston.”

“And are you mortally wounded?”

“Not mortally.”

“Did you spill anything on the satin?”

“No.”

“Then Providence has been attentive. Drink your chocolate.”

Of course I told her everything, including Mr. Arden’s remark. I pretended that it had made no impression upon me. Miss Persis pretended to believe me. We were both accomplished women in our fashion.

A year later Madam Le Baron died quietly in that great dim chamber. By her will she left me the green satin gown, the slippers, fan, comb, scarf, and chrysoprase necklace. In one of the gown’s deep pockets Martha found twelve old gold pieces wrapped in paper. Upon the paper Madam had written, in her fine pointed hand:

“For my young gentlewoman, who knew how to make courtesy more becoming than fashion.”

The gold helped furnish my first home. The chrysoprase necklace was worn at my wedding. The fan hangs above your mother’s writing desk, and the tortoise-shell comb is in the sandalwood box you were forbidden to rummage through last Tuesday.

Yes, my dear, I knew you had done it. Old women are not blind merely because young people are hopeful.

And the gown remained folded in lavender until your mother asked the very question you asked to-day: why keep such an outlandish thing?

Because, as I told you, it is the most beautiful gown I ever wore. It taught me that ridicule is noisy but seldom dangerous; that vanity, though natural, is a poor counsellor; and that an old woman’s happiness may be worth more than a young woman’s momentary triumph.

But that is not the whole reason.

When Henry Arden asked leave to call upon Miss Persis the following week, he told me he had admired the gown from the first. I accused him of admiring a curiosity.

“No,” he said, “I admired the girl who wore it after she had discovered that someone else’s pleasure mattered more than her own.”

A very polished speech, you think? So did I. Your grandfather could be polished when necessity pressed him.

He later confessed that he had first noticed me before dinner, standing beneath the portrait while Madam fastened the green stones around my neck. The door had been ajar; he had come to tell her that the guests were assembled and had paused when he saw her face.

“I knew then,” he said, “that any girl who could make age look so happy would make a home happy too.”

Now you understand why the satin is not merely satin, and why I shall not have it cut into cushions, however beautifully they would suit the spare room.

Fold it gently, child. Put the tissue between the sleeves, and lay the necklace case on top. Some evening, perhaps, you may wear the chrysoprase yourself—though not, I think, the gown. Courage may be inherited, but waists have altered.

And ring for more hot water, if you please. Your tea is cold, your mending is untouched, and you have been sitting for an hour with a green satin sleeve across your knees—exactly as your grandfather found me, more than fifty years ago.

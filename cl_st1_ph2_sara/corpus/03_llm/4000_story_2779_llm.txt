I had often heard people call Bernard Lenoir mad because he preferred his boat to his house, and the company of reeds, mud banks, and towpaths to that of men. He was not mad. He was merely one of those persons in whom a familiar passion has grown so strong that it colors every object and gives to ordinary things an almost secret meaning.

He owned a narrow sailing boat, scarcely large enough for two men to sleep in, and spent all his free days upon the Seine. He knew every bend between Saint-Cloud and Mantes, every island covered with poplars, every shallow where the keel grated, every old willow whose roots drank from the current like black fingers.

One evening, while we were dining beside the open window of an inn at Argenteuil, I asked him whether he had ever been afraid on the river.

He put down his glass.

“Afraid?” he said. “Yes. Once. And I have never understood exactly what frightened me. Yet nothing in my life has equaled the terror of that night.”

Then, after a silence, he told me the following story.

“You know how I love the river,” he began. “But you cannot know it as I do. To you it is water moving between two banks, a road without dust, a pleasant mirror for the sky. To me it is a living country, a country with caprices, sicknesses, tempers, and treacheries.

“The sea is terrible, but honest. It bellows, it strikes, it overturns ships, it breaks men’s ribs and spits their bodies upon the sand. One sees its anger coming. It shouts its threats aloud. But the river is silent and perfidious. It slides without haste; it smiles under the sun; it caresses the grass and carries flowers upon its breast, while in its depths there are holes of black mud, drowned branches, twisted chains, dead animals, and dead people rolling slowly among the weeds.

“Ah, the drowned people! Every boatman thinks of them, though he does not speak of them. The river takes them gently. It closes over them without a splash, washes their faces, pulls their clothes, turns them beneath its lilies, and keeps them until it chooses to give them back.

“It was in early autumn, perhaps fifteen years ago. I had passed the afternoon sailing alone. The weather was magnificent, one of those still, clear days when the sky appears higher than usual and every distant tree seems sharply cut from blue glass. Toward sunset the wind fell, and I took up the oars.

“I had no reason to return. My boat contained bread, cold meat, tobacco, a flask of rum, and two blankets. So, when I reached a quiet part of the river, bordered on one side by reeds and on the other by low meadows, I decided to remain there until morning.

“The channel was perhaps two hundred meters wide. No house could be seen. Far behind the bank rose a line of poplars, already dark, though the uppermost leaves still caught the sunset. I let the boat drift toward midstream, dropped my anchor, paid out several meters of chain, and felt it take hold at once.

“Then I made everything ready. I lowered the little sail, arranged the ropes, placed one blanket beneath me, and lit my pipe.

“It was delightful.

“The western sky remained red for a long time, and this red light lay stretched across the river like a sheet of copper. A few insects skimmed the surface. From the reeds came the occasional dry rustle of some small creature settling itself for the night. Then the colors faded; the banks lost their shapes; the water became dark and polished.

“I smoked, lying on my back, with my hands beneath my head.

“The silence did not surprise me at first. Indeed, I enjoyed it. There are silences that soothe you because they seem filled with minute, peaceful sounds—the fall of dew, the passage of air through leaves, the soft breathing of the earth. But little by little I became aware that this silence was different. It was complete, heavy, watchful.

“The frogs had stopped croaking. No bird called. The reeds did not stir. Even the water appeared no longer to flow.

“I sat up.

“The boat moved slightly under me, with that almost imperceptible balancing motion which ordinarily gives me the pleasant sensation of being rocked. Yet, when I looked over the side, I could see no ripple. The river was black, smooth, and thick, like oil.

“Then, quite near the bow, something made a small sound.

“Plop!

“It was nothing, no doubt—a fish touching the surface. Nevertheless, I leaned forward and stared. I could see only my own face, pale and vague, floating in the darkness.

“I laughed aloud. The sound of my laugh seemed foolish, almost indecent, and it traveled far over the water. I heard it weaken in the distance, then die. Afterward the silence became more profound.

“I relit my pipe, which had gone out, but an uneasiness had entered me. It was not fear yet. It was the sensation that I was no longer alone.

“You understand how absurd that was. The river is full of life. Fish pass under you, water rats swim between the grasses, birds sleep in the banks, and perhaps a poacher crawls nearby with his net. But these are known presences. What troubled me was the idea of another presence, something without a name, concealed beneath that black surface and aware of me.

“Twice I thought the boat gave a slow turn against the chain, as if the current had suddenly changed direction. Then I heard a faint rubbing along the hull.

“I stood up.

“‘Enough,’ I said. ‘You are behaving like a child.’

“I decided to move farther downstream, where I knew there was a village. I seized the chain and pulled.

“The anchor resisted.

“I pulled again, more sharply. It did not rise.

“That often happens. An anchor catches beneath a root or wedges itself between stones. There was nothing remarkable in it. I passed the chain around a cleat, rocked the boat, released it, and hauled with both hands. The links came up taut, dripping and cold, then stopped with a violent jerk.

“The anchor was firmly caught.

“I tried from the stern, then from the bow. I rowed several strokes upstream to change the angle and pulled once more. Nothing. The boat swung back to its former position as soon as I let go.

“I began to feel horribly uncomfortable.

“At that same moment a white vapor appeared above the water.

“It came so low that at first I mistook it for a paler strip of current. It spread rapidly, however, sliding out from the banks in long, thin folds. Within five minutes the reeds had vanished. Within ten, the world had disappeared.

“I was enclosed in fog.

“It was not the gray, transparent mist through which trees and houses loom like shadows. This was dense, cold, almost solid. It pressed against the boat on every side, reducing the universe to three meters of wet whiteness and a small patch of black water.

“I could no longer tell where the banks lay. There was no sky, no river, no distance. The lantern at my feet seemed wrapped in cotton. Drops formed on my hair and ran down my cheeks.

“Again I hauled at the chain.

“I pulled until my shoulders burned. I braced one foot against the mast and strained with all my strength. The anchor did not yield.

“Then I became angry. Anger was a relief. I cursed the chain, the anchor, the river, and my own stupidity. I shook the iron links with both hands as if they belonged to an enemy I could frighten.

“From beneath the boat came three knocks.

“Knock. Knock. Knock.

“I released the chain.

“They were slow, distinct blows, delivered against the planking under my feet.

“I remained bent forward, listening. My heart had begun to beat so violently that each pulse seemed to fill my chest.

“Nothing followed.

“A piece of driftwood, I thought. Some submerged branch had been brought against the hull by the current. That explanation was certain, reasonable, sufficient. But another part of me—the cowardly, involuntary creature who lives inside every man—did not accept it.

“There were two of us in that boat: one who knew, and one who feared. The first said, ‘Wood.’ The other whispered, ‘A hand.’

“I poured myself a glass of rum and drank it at once.

“The warmth revived me. I ate a little bread, smoked another pipe, and forced myself to consider the facts. Fog is common in autumn. Anchors catch. Fish leap. Branches strike boats. A man alone mistakes ordinary noises because he cannot see their causes. Everything had an explanation.

“While I reasoned thus, something breathed in the fog.

“It was far away at first, a long, faint sigh. Then the sound approached, swelling and diminishing, as though an enormous creature were swimming around me just beyond sight.

“I held my breath.

“The noise continued.

“At last I understood: reeds rustling under a wandering current of air. Yet no air reached me. The fog hung motionless, and the flame in my lantern did not tremble.

“Soon other sounds began—distant splashes, a metallic croak, the brief yelp of a dog upon one bank or the other. Because I could judge no direction, each sound seemed to circle the boat. Once an unseen bird passed overhead with a rapid beating of wings, and I ducked so suddenly that I struck my knee.

“Then came another knock from below.

“Only one.

“I drank again.

“An hour passed, perhaps two. Time had ceased to move normally. At moments I believed dawn must be near; at others I felt I had been there for only a few minutes. I repeatedly took up the chain and repeatedly put it down. I wanted to pull, but I could not make myself do so. I was afraid of feeling something move at the other end.

“That is the strangest part. An anchor is merely iron. Yet I came to imagine the chain descending through the water into another world, a world of mud and blind creatures, where it had been seized and was being held. I pictured long weeds winding around it; pale fish nosing it; swollen faces lifting from the bottom to stare upward. Each link appeared to connect my hand with all the hidden corruption of the river.

“Suddenly the fog above me grew luminous.

“I looked up. A round, diffused glow had formed overhead, and through it the moon emerged, enormous and white. The vapor thinned in the center of the channel, drawing slowly aside, until the sky opened like a black vault filled with stars.

“The fog did not disappear. It retreated toward the banks and stood there in two dazzling walls, smooth, high, and radiant under the moon. Between them the river shone with such brilliance that I could see every rope in my boat, every grain of wood, every drop on the chain. The water was a moving road of silver.

“It was the most astonishing sight I have ever beheld.

“On either side, those motionless white ramparts concealed the earth; above me stretched the infinite night; beneath me flowed a river of light. I felt suddenly delivered. The fear fell away as quickly as a garment. Nothing could be dreadful in such beauty.

“I laughed again, but this time my laugh sounded natural. I drank a little rum, not from terror but from pleasure, wrapped myself in the blankets, and watched the moon.

“I must eventually have fallen asleep.

“When I awoke, everything was black. The moon had vanished. A cold wind whipped the surface, short waves struck the boat, and rain fell heavily. The anchor chain creaked at the bow while the hull jerked and swung.

“For a moment I did not remember where I was. Then the whole night returned to me, and I seized the chain with renewed fury. The rough weather had restored my courage. Wind and rain are honest things. They announce themselves. They strike your face, and you strike back.

“I hauled, rocked the boat, rowed in a circle, hauled again. The anchor remained fixed.

“I waited for daylight.

“It came slowly, a dirty yellow light spreading under low clouds. The banks returned—flat meadows, dripping reeds, poplars bent in the wind. They looked ordinary and miserable, and I loved them for it.

“At about seven o’clock I saw two fishermen approaching in a broad, heavy skiff. I shouted. They came alongside, laughing when I explained that I had been held prisoner all night by my anchor.

“One of them, a thickset fellow with red whiskers, climbed into my boat. Together we pulled. The other man rowed upstream to loosen the grip. For several minutes nothing happened. Then the chain gave a little.

“‘There!’ cried the red-whiskered man. ‘It’s coming.’

“We hauled slowly. The weight was enormous. Black bubbles rose beside the bow, and the water clouded with mud. First the anchor appeared, one fluke buried in a mass of cloth. Then gray hair spread over the surface.

“None of us spoke.

“We pulled once more and brought up the corpse of an old woman, a large stone tied around her neck.”

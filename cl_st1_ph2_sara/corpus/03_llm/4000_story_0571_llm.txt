### Bitter Root Billings, Arbitrator

There are three ways of asking a man how he acquired fame. You may question him directly, which is crude; you may wait until he volunteers the information, which is tedious; or you may leave the evidence of his glory lying where he can see that you have noticed it, which is diplomacy.

On a September evening at the Lazy K ranch I adopted the third method.

Bitter Root Billings had returned from Chicago two weeks before with a new hat, a scar across his chin, and a packet of newspaper clippings folded inside a pink ribbon. He carried these clippings everywhere, but pretended not to. If he sat down, they fell from his pocket. If he removed his coat, they slid mysteriously onto the nearest table. If nobody remarked upon them, he picked them up, sighed over the vanity of human distinction, and dropped them somewhere else.

That evening they reposed on the porch between the tobacco jar and my elbow.

I unfolded the top clipping.

The headline said:

**WESTERN COWBOY BREAKS GREAT RIVER STRIKE**

Below it, in smaller type:

**LABOR LEADER COMPELLED TO ARBITRATE UNDER EXTRAORDINARY CONDITIONS**

I read these lines aloud.

Billings continued cleaning his fingernails with a match.

“Newspapers,” he observed, “is powerful careless about details.”

“What details did they miss?” I asked.

“All the important ones.”

“How did you happen to break a strike?”

“I didn’t break it. I merely bent it till it got reasonable.”

Old Man Ricker leaned back against the porch post.

“With what?”

“Rawhide.”

There was a silence. Billings inspected the match, found it unequal to further labor, and threw it at a chicken.

“You might as well tell it,” said Ricker.

“I ain’t forcing no autobiography onto a peaceful community.”

“You’ve dropped those clippings into my coffee twice,” said I.

“That,” Billings replied, “was the movements of Providence. If Providence desires me to explain how I become a compulsory arbitrator, I ain’t the man to obstruct religion.”

He put his boots on the porch rail and began.

“You boys remember when me and Shorty drove them three carloads of Lazy K beef to Chicago. Shorty quits me at the stockyards because he’s got a married aunt somewheres in Indiana, and any man that deliberately visits married relations is outside my jurisdiction. So I collects the cattle money, wires it home, and starts in to examine the municipal economies of Chicago.

“I had heard considerable about that town. Folks claimed it was active, but they understated the case. Chicago don’t merely move; it kicks. It’s a city with its tail caught in a gate. The buildings is all trying to climb over one another, the street cars is ringing for help, and everybody you meet has got business six blocks ahead and is mad because you occupy the intervening geography.

“I hadn’t been loose there two hours before I experiences thirst. Now, Montana thirst is a dignified institution. A man takes it into a saloon, sets it down, and reasons with it. Chicago thirst is more nervous. It runs ahead and orders before you arrive.

“I enters an establishment down by the river where the floor had been washed during the administration of President Hayes, and there I observes a large red-headed man absorbing beer like a human blotter. He was six feet three by the tape, with shoulders constructed on the suspension-bridge principle and hands like two hams wearing freckles.

“‘Stranger,’ says he, ‘you look Western.’

“‘You look thirsty,’ says I.

“‘I am Murdock,’ says he.

“‘That ain’t no cure,’ says I, ‘but have another.’

“That pleased him. We fraternized.

“Murdock tells me he is a tugboat fireman by profession, but at present he is resting from labor on account of Oily Heegan’s strike. He explains that all the river men—firemen, deck hands, engineers, bridge tenders, dock rollers, rope carriers, and miscellaneous amphibians—has gone out, and the shipping of Chicago is tied in a hard knot.

“‘What are they striking for?’ I asks.

“‘Principle,’ says Murdock.

“‘What principle?’

“‘The principle that we want more money.’

“‘That’s one I’ve always favored,’ says I. ‘Who’s Heegan?’

“Murdock takes a drink and points solemn toward the ceiling.

“‘Oily Heegan,’ says he, ‘is Labor.’

“‘All of it?’

“‘Practically. When Oily raises his hand, ten thousand men quit work. When he lowers it, they remain quit till he raises it again.’

“‘Sounds like his signal system is defective.’

“‘He has the river closed,’ says Murdock. ‘Not a tug can turn a wheel. Not a vessel can shift a hawser. Not a pound of freight can move, unless Heegan says so.’

“I asks what caused this epidemic.

“Murdock says there’s a shipping magnate named Badrich who owns tugs, warehouses, elevators, and about half the river except the water. One morning Badrich eats four fried eggs, some sausage, half a pie, and a composition known as buckwheat cakes. This gives him an internal revolution. A committee of firemen calls on him that same morning for higher wages, and Badrich, confusing industrial economics with his breakfast, throws an inkstand at ’em.

“The committee goes to Oily Heegan. Heegan makes a speech declaring that capital has dipped its hands in the heart’s blood of labor. The papers print it. Badrich replies that labor ought to wash its neck and go to work. The papers print that. Then everybody quits, and the river begins to smell worse than usual from standing still.

“‘How long has this been going on?’ I asks.

“‘Three weeks.’

“‘Why don’t they get together and settle it?’

“‘They tried. Badrich called Heegan a walrus. Heegan called Badrich a parasite.’

“‘What’s a parasite?’

“‘Something that lives on something else.’

“‘Then,’ says I, ‘they’ve both diagnosed each other accurate. Have another drink.’

“Murdock and me become intimate. He knows a little place farther west where the liquor is cheaper, and after that he remembers another where it is purer, and then a third where a man can get something to eat. I follers him because I am a stranger and because hospitality is the cement of civilization.

“Along about midnight, Murdock introduces me to a thin party called Pidge, who has eyes like two nail holes in a cellar door. Pidge shakes my hand without enthusiasm and asks whether cowboys truly carry large sums of money in their boots.

“‘Only when their pockets is full,’ says I.

“Murdock laughs powerful at that. Then he buys a bottle of something green which tastes like a brass doorknob dissolved in kerosene.

“The next portion of my career is disconnected.

“I remember explaining the cattle business to a lamp-post. I remember Murdock telling me that Oily Heegan could be president if he wasn’t too honest. I remember Pidge leaning against me in a friendly manner while his hands conducted independent explorations. Then I remember waking in an alley at daylight with my head enlarged to the dimensions of a county and my money absent.

“They had took eighty-six dollars, my silver watch, my coat, and one boot.”

“Why one boot?” asked Ricker.

“Pidge had a narrow mind,” said Billings. “He probably didn’t believe in pairs.

“I comes out of that alley wearing one boot and an expression of constitutional grief. At the mouth of it I finds Murdock and Pidge talking to a policeman.

“‘There he is!’ yells Murdock. ‘That’s the man assaulted us.’

“I considered this ungrateful. I advances to revise his memory, but the policeman puts a club against my digestion and advises me to preserve the peace.

“‘They robbed me,’ says I.

“‘He’s drunk,’ says Pidge.

“‘He tried to hold us up,’ says Murdock.

“Just then another man comes along. He is short and broad and oily-looking, with black hair plastered down over his forehead and a mouth made for public speaking. Murdock says, ‘Mr. Heegan, didn’t you see this desperado attack us?’

“Heegan looks me over. He had never seen me before, but that don’t impede him.

“‘I did,’ says he. ‘It was a brutal and unprovoked assault upon two honest workingmen.’

“‘You’re Oily Heegan?’ I asks.

“‘I have that honor.’

“‘Then come close enough so I can honor you.’

“The policeman prevents the ceremony.

“They takes me to court. Chicago justice is rapid because they have so much of it to distribute. The judge asks Murdock what happened. Murdock says I attacked him with a deadly weapon. The deadly weapon turns out to be my missing boot, which Pidge produces from under his coat.

“‘That your boot?’ asks the judge.

“‘It resembles my late footwear,’ says I.

“‘Why did you strike this man with it?’

“‘I didn’t.’

“‘Mr. Heegan saw you.’

“Oily rises. He says he witnessed me rush from the alley with murderous intent; that Murdock and Pidge defended themselves with admirable moderation; and that law-abiding citizens must be protected from imported frontier turbulence.

“He makes a fine speech. He packs philanthropy, civic virtue, organized labor, and my depravity into three minutes. When he finishes, the judge looks at me as if I had personally burned down the national Capitol.

“‘Ten dollars and costs,’ says he.

“‘What for?’

“‘Disorderly conduct.’

“‘I ain’t got ten dollars. These statesmen stole it.’

“‘Then you may work out the fine.’

“‘Where?’

“‘Bridewell.’

“‘How long?’

“‘Twenty-six days.’

“‘Judge,’ says I, ‘ain’t there no discount for innocence?’

“‘Next case,’ says he.

“So I become a municipal employee for twenty-six days, breaking rocks in return for the privilege of having been robbed. This is what Eastern folks calls jurisprudence. In Montana we conduct law with less machinery. If a man steals your money, you retrieve it; if he disputes your title, you submit the matter to a jury consisting of whichever hand reaches the gun first. It ain’t a perfect system, but it saves court costs.

“At the Bridewell they gives me a striped suit designed by an enemy of the human shape and puts me alongside a philosopher named Kelly. Kelly had been sentenced for sleeping in a freight car.

“‘The trouble with society,’ says Kelly, ‘is maldistribution.’

“‘The trouble with me,’ says I, ‘is ten dollars and costs.’

“Every morning I hits a rock. Every evening I thinks of Murdock. At noon I concentrates on Oily Heegan. By the end of twenty-six days I had reduced several tons of limestone to a democratic condition, where no piece was bigger than another, and had developed a personal policy toward labor leaders.

“When they releases me, the keeper returns my hat, my belt, and my remaining boot. I walks into Chicago looking like half of a respectable citizen. My finances consists of nine cents, a brass button, and a prejudice.

“I buys a newspaper. There on the front page is Oily Heegan announcing that the strike will continue till capitalism is brought to its knees. On another page is an advertisement:

“‘MEN WANTED—RIVER WORK—HIGH WAGES—DANGEROUS CONDITIONS—APPLY BADRICH TRANSPORTATION COMPANY.’

“I reads that advertisement three times.

“High wages appealed to my purse. Dangerous conditions appealed to my temperament. Badrich appealed to me because Heegan hated him.

“I went.”

Billings paused to roll a cigarette.

“Did you know anything about tugboats?” I asked.

“No,” he said, “but I knew a heap about intentions, and intentions is the steering gear of achievement.”

He lit the cigarette and continued.

“Badrich’s office is on the river, behind barricades of lumber and private policemen. The windows is busted and the front door has enough dents to resemble a waffle iron. Inside, I finds Badrich walking up and down.

“He is a little man with white side-whiskers and a stomach that enters a room shortly before the balance of him. Every time somebody hollers in the street, he jumps, grabs a paperweight, and looks disappointed when no committee comes through the door.

“‘You answered the advertisement?’ he snaps.

“‘I did.’

“‘River man?’

“‘No.’

“‘Engineer?’

“‘No.’

“‘Fireman?’

“‘No.’

“‘Deck hand?’

“‘Not yet.’

“‘Then what are you?’

“‘Available.’

“He stops walking.

“‘Can you fight?’

“‘That depends on whether the opposition can run.’

“‘Can you shoot?’

“‘Frequently.’

“‘Can you obey orders?’

“‘If they coincide with my judgment.’

“Badrich says I am exactly the sort of lunatic he needs.

“He explains that the steamship *Duchess* is lying above the river bends with a valuable cargo, and unless she gets down to the lower docks by evening his company loses many thousands of dollars. A small tug named the *Resolute* is ready. The engineer is Badrich’s nephew, a pale young man called Simms. The pilot is an old captain named Dacey, who hates Heegan worse than Badrich does. But they need a fireman and somebody to help aboard the *Duchess*.

“‘I’ll fire,’ says Badrich.

“‘You?’ says Simms, looking alarmed.

“‘Yes, me! I fired a tug when I was nineteen.’

“‘That was forty years ago.’

“‘Coal hasn’t changed.’

“‘Neither has your temper,’ says Captain Dacey, ‘and that’s what worries me.’

“We go down to the *Resolute*. She is a black, snub-nosed tug about the size of a bunkhouse and twice as dirty. Steam leaks out of her in thoughtful little sighs. The river around her is crowded with idle boats, while men on the docks watch us with the expression of wolves observing a lame calf.

“I had acquired two boots from Badrich’s storeman, though they disagreed in color, and I felt restored to military efficiency. I also found a coil of three-quarter-inch manila rope. It wasn’t rawhide, but it had moral possibilities, so I takes it aboard.

“‘What’s that for?’ asks Simms.

“‘Emergency diplomacy,’ says I.

“We cast off.

“The news spreads before us. Every whistle on the river begins yelling. Men run along the docks, and from windows and warehouses comes a roar like ten thousand cattle discovering a gate open. The strike pickets throw lumps of coal. One hits Badrich between the shoulders.

“He turns around and shakes his shovel.

“‘Assassins!’ he cries.

“‘Keep firing,’ says Dacey. ‘We ain’t paying you to make speeches.’

“That touched Badrich’s pride. He jumps into the fire room and starts shoveling. In five minutes the smokestack is coughing black clouds and the *Resolute* is charging upstream like a tarred pig escaping a barbecue.

“We reaches the *Duchess*. She is a long lake vessel with two watchmen aboard. They take one look at the crowd gathering on shore, climb down the far side, and depart in a rowboat.

“‘There goes our crew,’ says Simms.

“‘Crew?’ says I. ‘I thought they was scenery.’

“Captain Dacey puts me on the *Duchess* to handle the towline and wheel. He tells me to keep her following the tug.

“‘How?’

“‘Use the rudder.’

“‘Which way?’

“‘Opposite the way you don’t want to go.’

“That was the first nautical instruction I received which sounded practical.

“We get the line fast, and the *Resolute* starts downstream with the *Duchess* behind. I stands in the wheelhouse, making experiments. A ship’s wheel has too many handles. A sensible wheel ought to indicate which handle is currently in authority, but this one kept offering alternatives. I turns it left and the vessel’s nose goes right. I turns it right and she thinks about it, then goes left. Navigation is largely a science of discovering that water has opinions.

“At the first bridge we meets organized hostility.

“The bridge is black with strikers. The docks on both sides is packed. Women is leaning from windows, boys is climbing poles, and policemen is standing where they can observe without becoming prejudiced by participation.

“When the *Resolute* comes under, the sky commences to fall in sections.

“Coal, bricks, bottles, potatoes, iron bolts, dead fish, and household utensils descends upon us. A cabbage hits Captain Dacey and bursts. A tomato catches Badrich in the face just as he comes up for air. With the coal dust, sweat, and tomato juice running down his whiskers, he looks like an expensive ice-cream drink.

“He grabs a rifle.

“‘Put that down!’ yells Dacey. ‘You’ll kill somebody.’

“‘That is the germ of my idea!’

“I leans from the *Duchess* wheelhouse and shoots the handle off a man’s shovel. That causes discussion on the bridge. Some ducks, some runs, and one fellow shakes his fist but remains behind a stone pillar while doing it.

“Then a bottle comes through the wheelhouse window and removes my hat.

“I picks up a coupling pin and throws it. I ain’t claiming it struck the same man who threw the bottle, because at that distance justice gets dispersed, but it discouraged the neighborhood.

“We passes the bridge.

“‘How many more?’ I yells.

“‘Six!’ Dacey yells back.

“I had never realized Chicago was so overbridged. Whenever the city found a place where a man might move without being assaulted from above, it built a bridge there.

“At the second crossing they drops a mattress. It lands over the tug’s smokestack, and for ten seconds the *Resolute* disappears inside her own smoke. Badrich climbs up with a boat hook and fights the mattress. The mattress wins the first round, wrapping him complete, but Simms pulls him out by the legs. Badrich comes free so mad he forgets he is old and shovels coal with the velocity of a steam derrick.

“At the third bridge somebody lowers a cable. It catches the *Duchess* forward mast. The towline tightens, the tug heels over, and every loose object aboard travels west. I takes an axe, runs forward, and chops the cable while bricks patter around me.

“One brick hits my shoulder.

“‘Quit that!’ I hollers.

“A voice from above replies with an opinion of my ancestry.

“‘I’ll discuss that when I get closer!’ says I.

“The cable parts, and we jump ahead. The crowd cheers because it thinks we are sinking. Then it boos because we ain’t. Public sentiment is unstable.

“Between bridges I begins to understand the wheel. The *Duchess* quits wandering from bank to bank like a drunken steer and follows tolerable straight. I am feeling proud when two of Badrich’s hired watchmen crawl from a hatch. They had concealed themselves below until they supposed the danger was over.

“‘Take the wheel,’ I says.

“‘Not us,’ says one. ‘We didn’t sign for this.’

“‘You signed to work the vessel.’

“‘We’re quitting.’

“They head for a boat hanging over the side.

“Now, when a man quits a ranch during a blizzard, leaving the herd to scatter, he is not exercising personal liberty. He is declaring war on everybody who remains. I explains this doctrine, but they lower the boat.

“I assists their decision by cutting the falls on one end. The boat tips upright, spills both men into the river, and hangs there like a picture on a wall.

“‘You can swim ashore,’ I tells ’em. ‘There’s your union headquarters.’

“They swim with enthusiasm.

“But while I’m attending to this personnel matter, the *Duchess* sheers sideways. Her bow heads for a stone pier. The towline screams, the *Resolute* swings, and Captain Dacey waves both arms like a windmill afflicted with religion.

“I runs back, grabs the wheel, and turns it whichever way appears least fatal. Nothing happens.

“I turns it the other.

“The *Duchess* misses the pier by the width of Badrich’s whiskers. Her side scrapes stone with a noise that files the teeth. Men on the bridge howl. A rain of vegetables descends, indicating that we are passing the wholesale market district.

“Badrich gets a pumpkin in the chest and sits down abruptly.

“‘Are you dead?’ I hollers.

“‘No!’

“‘Then shovel!’

“He looks at me in astonishment, but he shovels. Danger had improved him. In his office he was a capitalist; in the fire room he was merely a little old man with a grievance and a scoop, which is a more useful animal.

“We rounds a bend where the river narrows between warehouses. Here the mob has collected thickest. They have wagons across the docks, men on roofs, boats in the water, and a brass band playing something patriotic in the wrong key.

“And on a platform beside the river stands Oily Heegan.

“I knew him at once. His hair was still pasted down, his mouth was still working, and one hand was raised over the crowd. Every time he points, a shower of rocks comes at us. He looked like a bandmaster conducting an orchestra of paving material.

“My twenty-six days rises before me.

“I could see every rock I had busted. I could taste every plate of prison stew. Somewhere inside my memory the judge says, ‘Ten dollars and costs.’

“I leaves the wheel.”

“You left the wheel again?” asked Ricker.

“Only for the higher branches of justice,” said Billings.

“The *Duchess* begins wandering, but this was no time for minor navigation. I grabs my coil of rope and makes a loop. Manila ain’t rawhide. It’s limp and unreliable and has no more Western spirit than boiled spaghetti. But I talks to it.

“‘You got one chance,’ says I. ‘Don’t disgrace the range.’

“I measures the distance. Oily is maybe forty feet off as the river bends, standing seven feet above the water. Between us men is throwing coal, the tug is bucking, the vessel is swinging, and Captain Dacey is using language that wrinkled the paint.

“I swings the loop.

“The first circle catches a flying cabbage and takes it overboard.

“The second strikes a sign reading LABOR WILL TRIUMPH.

“The third settles over Oily Heegan from his raised hand to his prosperous waist.

“I jerks.

“Oily leaves labor.”

Billings paused and smiled into the dusk.

“He comes off that platform like a hooked black bass, arms waving and legs conducting elections. For half a second he is horizontal above the river. Then he hits.

“The crowd stops making noise.

“It is astonishing how quiet ten thousand people can become when their spokesman unexpectedly changes elements.

“Oily goes under. I takes a turn around a cleat. The rope tightens, and he comes up behind the *Duchess*, coughing river and waving both hands.

“‘Help!’ he yells.

“‘Arbitrate!’ says I.

“‘I’ll have you murdered!’

“I lets the rope slip.

“He goes under again.

“‘Ten dollars and costs!’ I reminds him.

“When he comes up the second time, he has lost his hat, his oratory, and most of his labor policy. He revolves slowly in the wake, now face up, now face down, resembling a toadfish attached to a clothesline.

“The crowd begins running along shore. Some yell to cut the rope. Others throw things, but they don’t throw accurate because they’re afraid of hitting Heegan. For the first time that day he becomes useful as protection.

“I hauls him toward the vessel. He is heavier than he looks, partly from water and partly from importance. At the stern I gets a boat hook under his belt and brings him aboard.

“He lies on deck breathing like a punctured bellows.

“‘You assaulted me,’ he gasps.

“‘No,’ says I. ‘I invited you to a conference.’

“‘I’ll prosecute!’

“‘Ten dollars and costs?’

“He stares.

“Then he remembers me.

“‘You!’ says he.

“‘Me,’ says I. ‘The imported frontier turbulence.’

“Just then the *Duchess* heads for another dock. I runs to the wheel and straightens her. Oily crawls toward the rail.

“I ropes his ankles.

“‘What are you doing?’

“‘Preserving the continuity of negotiations.’

“I drags him forward, crosses the towline onto the tug, and brings him to the fire-room door. Badrich is almost finished. His eyes is white in a black face, his whiskers is baked stiff, and he can barely lift the shovel.

“When he sees Heegan, he revives like a dry plant in rain.

“‘Heegan!’ he shrieks.

“‘Badrich!’ says Oily.

“They start exchanging public statements.

“‘Silence,’ says I. ‘This ain’t a convention. It’s arbitration.’

“I puts the shovel in Heegan’s hands.

“‘Fire.’

“‘I am president of the River Workers’ Federation!’

“‘Then you understand the work.’

“‘I won’t!’

“I takes the rope around his middle, leads the loose end over a beam, and draws it tight enough to improve his posture.

“‘You got a choice,’ says I. ‘You can shovel standing up, or you can discuss hydrodynamics behind the boat again.’

“Oily looks at the river.

“He shovels.

“Badrich sits on a coal pile and begins laughing. At first it’s merely a chuckle, but it grows till he rocks back and forth, pointing at Heegan.

“‘The leader of labor!’ he says. ‘Working!’

“‘Don’t strain yourself,’ says I. ‘You ain’t permanently cured.’

“We comes to the fourth bridge with Heegan at the furnace. The crowd sees him through the fire-room door. He is black with coal and red with heat, shoveling while I stands behind with the rope.

“A howl goes up.

“‘Release him!’

“‘He’s conferring!’ I hollers.

“They shower us with bricks.

“I gives the rope a twitch.

“‘More steam,’ says I.

“‘I’ll die.’

“‘That’ll weaken your argument.’

“He shovels faster.

“The gauge climbs. The stack throws sparks. The *Resolute* jumps forward so hard the towline hums. We passes under the bridge before half the ammunition arrives, and most of it falls into the river behind us.

“At the fifth bridge, Oily tries politics.

“‘My men will never forgive this,’ he says.

“‘They ain’t forgiving it now.’

“‘You cannot settle an industrial dispute by violence.’

“‘That’s curious. You started one that way.’

“‘There are principles involved.’

“‘Put another scoop on the principles.’

“He does.

“At the sixth bridge the crowd has rolled barrels into the channel and stretched ropes between barges. Captain Dacey threads the tug through a gap no wider than a corral gate. I steer the *Duchess* after him. A barrel catches under our bow and explodes in splinters. A barge swings out, but the vessel shoulders it aside. Men leap into the water. Whistles scream, steam boils, boards crack, and the brass band, which has followed us on a wagon, becomes separated from its music.

“Oily’s men see him firing and hesitate. They cannot decide whether to rescue him, obey him, or pretend they never voted for him. That pause saves us.

“We crashes through the ropes.

“Then the lower harbor opens ahead.

“There are police launches there, and newspaper boats, and Badrich’s office force, and enough spectators to populate Wyoming. When they see the *Resolute* towing the *Duchess*, every whistle in the harbor lets go.

“It was a magnificent noise. It had no tune, but neither has victory.

“We brings the *Duchess* to her dock.

“Badrich climbs out of the fire room and falls into a chair. Simms shakes my hand. Captain Dacey says I am the worst pilot he ever saw and the best deck hand, which I accepted as a balanced testimonial.

“Oily collapses on the coal.

“‘Release me,’ he whispers.

“‘Not yet. We got to go back.’

“His eyes opens.

“‘Back?’

“‘Ain’t no use proving a tug can go downstream if it can’t return. That would be one-sided navigation.’

“Badrich says he will pay me five hundred dollars if I bring the *Resolute* back.

“‘And my eighty-six dollars?’

“‘What eighty-six dollars?’

“‘Incidental justice.’

“‘Done.’

“We casts off from the *Duchess* and heads upriver.

“This time Captain Dacey puts me at the tug’s wheel. Simms tends the engine. Badrich rests. Oily fires under continuing persuasion.

“The crowd on the bridges has changed. A mob is courageous when it thinks you’re running away. When you turn around and come back, it gets philosophical.

“They still yell, but they don’t throw so much. Also, Heegan is visible at the furnace door. Every time we pass a bridge, I makes him stand where they can see him.

“‘Wave,’ says I.

“‘Never.’

“I tightens the rope.

“He waves.

“His followers looks puzzled. Some wave back.

“By the time we reaches Badrich’s dock, thousands are waiting. Union men fills the street and warehouses. Murdock and Pidge are near the front. I recognizes Murdock by his red hair and Pidge by the fact that he is wearing my coat.

“We ties up.

“I leads Oily ashore. He is wet below, scorched above, black all over, and so tired his knees negotiate separately. The crowd growls.

“Murdock steps forward.

“‘What have you done to our president?’

“‘Improved his knowledge of working conditions.’

“‘Turn him loose.’

“‘Presently.’

“I climbs onto a coil of hawser and addresses the citizens. I ain’t much on speeches, but the circumstances had already supplied the grammar.

“‘Gentlemen,’ says I, ‘your leader has been in conference with capital, navigation, combustion, and me. He has arbitrated.’

“Oily starts to object, but I lifts the rope.

“He changes his mind.

“‘He has discovered,’ I continues, ‘that tugs can run, ships can move, and coal goes into a furnace better when somebody shovels it. He has also discovered that the Chicago River is damp. On these facts, the strike is over.’

“The crowd mutters.

“Murdock yells, ‘That’s a lie!’

“I points at my coat.

“‘Take that off.’

“He turns to run.

“I had the loop ready. After Oily, Murdock was easy. I catches him around the knees and brings him down. Pidge darts through the crowd, but Captain Dacey trips him with a boat hook. In three minutes I recover my coat, my silver watch, forty-one dollars of my money, and one boot.

“‘Where’s the rest?’ I asks.

“‘Spent,’ says Murdock.

“‘Then you can work it out.’

“Oily groans.

“The union men begin laughing.

“That ended it.

“A crowd can remain angry at an enemy, but it can’t preserve a revolution while laughing at its own officers. One man asks Heegan whether he recommends river work for the complexion. Another wants to know if arbitration is paid by the hour. Somebody brings Oily a shovel and the whole waterfront roars.

“Badrich, seeing which way sentiment is moving, climbs onto a barrel.

“He announces that any man returning to work by morning will receive the old wage plus ten cents a day.

“Oily raises his head.

“‘We demanded twenty-five!’

“Badrich says, ‘Twelve.’

“‘Twenty.’

“‘Fifteen.’

“‘Done,’ says Oily.

“‘There,’ says I. ‘Compulsory arbitration.’

“And that,” Billings concluded, “is how I restored the arteries of commerce, recovered one boot, and collected ten dollars and costs.”

The porch remained quiet for a moment.

Then Ricker said, “You expect us to believe all that?”

“No,” said Billings. “Belief is a private vice.”

He handed me the remaining clippings.

The first described the successful movement of the *Duchess*. The second reported the sudden end of the strike. The third contained a blurred photograph of a blackened, exhausted Oily Heegan standing beside Bitter Root Billings, who held a rope in one hand and Heegan’s hat in the other.

The final clipping was an editorial. Its concluding paragraph read:

> Industrial history records many forms of mediation, but Chicago yesterday witnessed one without precedent. A citizen of Montana, applying the direct methods of the range to a complicated labor emergency, induced President Heegan to enter immediate personal conference with the practical conditions of river transportation. The result was an agreement, a restored commerce, and peace upon the waterfront. Mr. Billings may lack the language of diplomacy, but his grasp of compulsory arbitration appears complete.

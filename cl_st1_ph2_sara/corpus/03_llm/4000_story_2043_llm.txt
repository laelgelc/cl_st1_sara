### The Lilac Miniature

There are seasons when work, having been neglected with impunity for a reasonable time, assumes the character of an importunate creditor. It waits upon the stair; it peers through the breakfast-room window; it accompanies one to the club, where it sits invisible between the coffee and the evening paper; and, if one flies from it to the theatre, it may even be perceived, in moments when the actors are least diverting, occupying a dark corner of the box.

Such a season overtook me in June, eighteen—. I was engaged upon a book which I had begun with confidence, continued with misgiving, and abandoned, for the seventh or eighth time, with despair. In London I could do nought. The cabs rattled beneath my windows; the organs played; my friends called to tell me how admirably I must be getting on; and every post brought either an invitation which I ought to decline or a letter demanding copy which I had promised and had not written.

I determined, therefore, to seek solitude.

One is apt, while living among bells, wheels, voices and engagements, to regard solitude as a mild and obliging servant, who will close the door upon interruption, place pens and paper in convenient order, and retire until wanted. I had yet to learn that solitude is a master and a teacher, and sometimes a very severe one; that it will indeed remove the world from a man, but may leave him in company which is less easily dismissed.

A friend recommended to me a village on the Yorkshire Wolds. It had, he declared, no doctor, no circulating library, no resident clergyman save one who was deaf, and no railway within nine miles. These particulars decided me. I sent my heavier luggage in advance, reserved for myself a bag, a rug and a parcel of manuscripts, and arrived, upon a bright afternoon, at a small station whose porter appeared astonished that any passenger should voluntarily descend there.

The carrier who undertook the remaining stage was a broad, weather-coloured man named Baines, and his conveyance was a high, yellow cart which smelt of rope, meal and damp straw. He placed my bag among a number of parcels, a crate containing two indignant fowls, and a tin bath destined, I understood, for the schoolmistress.

“Tha’ll be for Mrs. Dacre’s?” he asked.

I said that I believed so.

“Aye. Widow Dacre. White cottage by t’ beck. Quiet there.”

“That is precisely what I require.”

He looked at me with a composure which implied that I should presently require something else.

“Quiet’s what London folk allus wants,” he said, gathering up the reins; “till they gets it.”

We climbed for nearly two hours. The little industrial town at which I had changed trains lay behind us under a canopy of smoke, its chimneys standing like black fingers raised in protest against the serene sky. Before us the road mounted between hedges powdered with dog-roses. Then the hedges fell away, and the immense, smooth country opened—green slopes, pale roads, fields of young corn shaken by the wind, and here and there a dark plantation drawn across the brightness as though by a single stroke of the brush.

The hills seemed first to recede from us, then to advance, and at length to swallow horse, cart, carrier and myself into their broad silence. Only the wheels spoke, and the harness answered. Two brooks ran for a while on opposite sides of the road, flashing beneath alders and chattering independently among stones; but lower down they joined hands under a little bridge and went on together. I remember that I watched their union with the idle sentimentality which landscape excites in men who are escaping from unpaid obligations. It seemed pleasant that two fresh streams, born apart upon the hills, should find companionship before they passed through smoke-darkened towns and at last heard the sea calling them to the extinction of all separate voices.

“That’s Blackwater Beck now,” said Baines. “Runs by Mrs. Dacre’s.”

“And the other?”

“Were Linton Syke till they met.”

“Then it loses its name?”

He considered this.

“Mebbe. But it keeps t’ water.”

The village appeared at dusk: a grey church without a tower, a smithy, an inn with a blue sign, and a dozen cottages set irregularly beside the beck. Mrs. Dacre’s stood a little apart. It was low and white, with a roof softened by moss and a garden so full of June roses that the walls seemed to blush through them. Behind it the Wold rose steeply, carrying a narrow footpath towards the open sky.

Mrs. Dacre herself received me. She was a small, upright widow of seventy or thereabouts, dressed in black silk which rustled with an air of permanent propriety. Her face had the smooth pallor of old ivory, and her eyes were still bright, though they had acquired the watchfulness of one who has lived long among things she does not discuss.

“This is my daughter, Miss Dacre,” she said.

A woman who had been standing in the shadow near the stair came forward.

My first impression of Miss Dacre was uncharitable, and since the correction of it is the substance of this narrative, honesty compels me to record it. She was perhaps forty-eight, perhaps fifty-two; women who have ceased to expect admiration often cease also to declare their age by any intelligible sign. She was thin, stooping and colourless. Her brown hair, threaded with grey, was arranged in a fashion twenty years too young for her, and a faded blue ribbon at her throat suggested not vanity precisely, but a piteous fidelity to the habits of better days. Her features were regular without beauty; her hands, however, were unexpectedly delicate.

She murmured that she hoped I should be comfortable, and immediately withdrew.

“A nervous disposition,” Mrs. Dacre observed. “She does not see many strangers.”

I answered, with the amiability of a traveller who has discovered that his rooms are clean, that strangers were commonly not worth seeing.

My sitting-room looked upon the garden and the beck. It contained a horsehair sofa, an oval table, two uncompromising chairs, a desk placed before the window, and a tall bookcase of black wood whose glazed doors had not, to all appearance, been opened since the Reform Bill. There were roses on the mantelpiece, sweet grass in a blue bowl, and a small brass lamp which threw a mellow circle upon the ceiling. I congratulated myself. Here, surely, the fugitive book must surrender.

The next morning I rose early, walked before breakfast, returned full of excellent resolutions, arranged my papers, sharpened three pencils, and wrote a date. By eleven o’clock I had altered the date, examined the map, and composed half a letter to my publisher explaining that the air of the Wolds, though admirable for the lungs, produced an unusual torpor of the faculties.

At noon I opened the bookcase.

The books were of that miscellaneous character which marks rather the accidents than the tastes of a household: sermons, annuals, a history of the Jews, several volumes of Scott, a French grammar, collections of verse bound in calf, and novels whose heroines had long since ceased to suffer except from mildew. Dust lay softly upon them, preserving every vacancy left by a withdrawn volume.

I pulled out a heavy anthology. Something slipped from behind it and fell face upward at my feet.

It was a miniature.

The frame, of tarnished silver, was oval and no larger than the palm of my hand. The painting represented a young woman, scarcely more than twenty, in a gown of lilac silk. Her dark hair was parted simply and drawn back from a low, clear forehead. The mouth was tender, yet not weak; the eyes were grey, or perhaps hazel—the pigment had faded—and possessed that direct, grave sweetness which portrait-painters often attempt and seldom seize. One hand rested upon the edge of a chair. It was a beautiful hand, narrow, flexible and alive.

The face disturbed me by its familiarity.

I do not mean that I recognised it. Recognition is a definite event; this was rather the sensation of remembering what had never occurred. I had seen those eyes, I thought, in some gallery, or theatre, or forgotten house; perhaps in a dream. The more I searched my memory, the more importunately the face receded.

I set the miniature upon the desk and returned to my chapter.

During that day I wrote six pages, which was six more than I had written in London; but the young woman shared the labour. When my attention wandered, her gaze received it. When I sought a word, she appeared to withhold it. Once, irritated by the absurdity, I turned the miniature face downward; five minutes later I restored it, since to condemn even painted eyes to darkness seemed a punishment disproportionate to their offence.

At dinner I asked Mrs. Dacre whether the books in my room belonged to her.

“Some do,” she replied. “Some were left.”

“By former lodgers?”

“By one former lodger.”

Miss Dacre, who sat opposite me and ate very little, let her spoon fall against her plate.

“An admirer of poetry, apparently,” I said.

Mrs. Dacre looked at her daughter and then at me.

“She had books. She came here ill, poor thing, and died before the summer was out. There was no family to claim what remained.”

“Was it long ago?”

“A good many years.”

That answer, delivered in the tone which terminates a subject, checked my loquacity. Miss Dacre did not raise her eyes again.

The following afternoon rain enclosed the cottage. Mist descended from the Wold, the beck grew brown and noisy, and the roses beat their wet heads against the panes. I worked until dusk with some success. Then, having gone to the shelf for a volume of verse, I found in it a short poem marked heavily in pencil.

It was not a distinguished poem. A maiden released her lover because she loved him too well to burden his happiness; the lover blessed her nobility; the maiden died; and heaven, in the final stanza, repaired with characteristic ease what earth had ruined beyond remedy. Beside the last lines a woman had written:

*We speak of sacrifice as if the heart were dead when the lips consent. It lives. That is the cruelty.*

The hand was fine and sloping. Beneath the words were the initials “M. D.”

I carried the book to the window. The rain had ceased, but a vapour still hung over the garden, and the failing light turned the wet grass almost black. As I looked out, I perceived a woman standing beyond the path beneath the old pear-tree.

She wore lilac.

Her face was raised towards my window. Even at that distance I saw its pallor and the heavy sadness of the eyes. One hand gathered the folds of her gown; the other hung beside her. I remember, with an exactness which no subsequent reasoning has weakened, the pale gleam of the fingers.

I moved nearer the glass. At that moment the vapour drifted across the tree. The figure wavered and disappeared.

I went immediately into the garden.

There was no one there.

Now I am not a believer in ghosts. This statement, which in ordinary society should be as unnecessary as a denial of cannibalism, becomes essential when one relates an experience of this nature. I did not then, and do not now, accept the theory that the deceased retain an interest in the windows of furnished lodgings. I had been looking at a miniature; I had read a sentimental poem; the light was uncertain; a lilac bush grew beside the pear-tree. The materials of illusion were complete. Nevertheless, I slept badly.

On the next day I removed the remaining books from the lower shelf. Behind them lay a narrow drawer which had been painted to resemble the backboard. Its brass handle came away at my touch, but I contrived to open it with a knife.

Inside were letters tied with ribbon, a diary covered in faded green leather, two dried sprigs of heather and a pressed rose so brown and fragile that it crumbled when the air reached it.

I ought not to have read the diary. No argument can acquit me. The dead possess few rights we are willing to respect, but privacy should be among them; and at that time I believed the writer dead. Yet curiosity, when allied to literary habit, wears the grave expression of scholarship. I told myself that I would merely identify the owner. I read one page, then another, and by lamplight that evening read all.

The earliest entries belonged to a girl. One knew it not only from the date, which was thirty-one years old, but from the breathless abundance of the sentences. She had walked with Christopher. Christopher had praised the colour of the heather. Christopher had said that no hands in the world were so perfectly shaped as hers. She had prayed that she might be worthy of him, kissed the page where she had written his name, and described his genius in terms which would have embarrassed Raphael.

I smiled. We writers, who manufacture emotion by the quire, are severe upon the unpractised rhapsodies of the heart.

Christopher, it appeared, had been her childhood companion. He was poor, ambitious and determined to be a painter. Their families had expected them to marry, though no formal engagement had been permitted, since prudence—an elderly deity much worshipped by the parents of young lovers—required him first to establish himself.

There followed the account of a summer day upon the Wold.

*He painted me under the ash-tree in my lilac gown. He said I must not smile, because my smile altered all my face and he had not skill enough to follow it. Then he made me laugh by pretending rage, and afterwards he kissed my hands, first the right and then the left, saying that when he was famous people would know them in the portrait and envy him. I asked what they should envy. He said, “That these will keep my heart safe while I am away.”*

I looked involuntarily at the miniature upon my desk.

The next entry recorded his departure for London. His letters, enclosed among the pages, began with ardour. He was lonely; he was hopeful; he saw her face in every crowd; he worked for her; he would conquer for her. Then commissions came. He met patrons. He travelled to Paris. His handwriting became firmer as his assurances became shorter.

The diary faithfully registered each diminution.

*No letter to-day, but he is busy.*

*Only four lines. He says Lady Harborough has sat three times and has a wonderful head. I am glad, because her portrait may make his name.*

*He has not written for nineteen days. I will not count again.*

*Mother says men cannot bear to be watched by expectation. I did not know that waiting in silence could be called watching.*

There was no vulgar betrayal, no sudden cruelty. Neglect accomplished its work by grains. Christopher still remembered her birthday, though a week late; still called her “dearest,” though no longer “my own”; still spoke of the future, but as a country which changing circumstances might render inconvenient to visit.

A new name entered the letters: Eleanor Vane. She was the daughter of an influential man, handsome, cultivated, accustomed to the world into which Christopher had climbed. At first he mentioned her often, then ceased to mention her at all.

The diary did not.

*I dreamt of Eleanor Vane. She had no face, but she wore diamonds, and when she put out her hand Christopher went away with her. I woke biting the sheet that I might not cry aloud. How hateful I am becoming! If he knew my thoughts, he would despise me. I despise myself, but that does not make the thoughts cease.*

Presently came a letter longer than its predecessors. Christopher wrote of gratitude, affection and honesty. He had struggled against a change which he could neither defend nor deny. He dared not ask release; yet without it three lives might be made miserable.

The entry following this letter was written with unnatural neatness.

*I have answered him. I told him that he is free; that I have long known our childish affection could not survive separation; that I care for him only as a friend and rejoice in his happiness. I am calm. I thank God that I have been able to act rightly. Some day he will know that I loved him well enough not to stand in his path.*

Two pages had been torn out.

On the next remaining page the writing plunged across the paper as though the pen had been driven by a hand no longer governed by shame.

*It is false—false from first to last. I am not calm. I do not rejoice. I love him, and I have given him away because they said goodness required it. What is this goodness which demands that one woman shall tear out her heart to furnish another with a triumph? I want him back. I want the old poor Christopher who needed me. I hate his success, his fine friends, his Eleanor. I hate her beauty which I have never seen, her voice which I have never heard, the hand that will lie in his. I wrote that I did not care. Suppose he believes me? Suppose one true word would have brought him home, and I sent him instead that smooth, dead lie?*

Below this, in smaller characters:

*I have knelt to pray that they may be happy. I could only pray that she might die.*

The last sentence had been crossed out, but not so completely that it could not be read.

I closed the diary. Outside, the beck hurried through the darkness, full from the rain, and the leaves whispered against the wall. I was ashamed of the smile with which I had greeted those first foolish pages. The story was conventional, certainly. Every circulating library contained its dozen variations; every poet had rhymed “part” with “heart” over its grave. But human beings are not protected from suffering by the circumstance that others have suffered before them. A knife does not grow blunt because it has an ancient pattern.

There remained one final letter. It announced Christopher’s marriage.

The signature arrested me.

“Christopher Marr.”

I knew him.

Every person with even a modest acquaintance among artists knew Sir Christopher Marr: portrait-painter, Royal Academician, possessor of a red ribbon from France and a house in Belgrave Square. I had dined with him many times. He was then a man of sixty, broad-shouldered, handsome and grey, with a manner at once brilliant and weary. His portraits of women were famous for the hands. Critics said that Marr painted hands as if each possessed a secret life separate from the face.

I had met Lady Marr also.

She was still beautiful in the hard, finished fashion of a jewel. Her table was excellent, her conversation correct, her rooms magnificent and without warmth. She and her husband addressed one another with perfect courtesy. No anger disturbed that household, no tenderness compromised it. One felt there the peculiar chill of a house in which two people have long ceased to quarrel because neither expects to be heard.

I recalled an evening when Marr, after dinner, had stood before the fire while the other men discussed ambition.

“Success,” he had said, “is an admirable ladder. The mistake is to suppose there must be something at the top.”

Lady Marr’s voice had come from the doorway.

“There is usually another invitation, Christopher.”

He had laughed. I had thought them witty.

For several days I attempted to learn from Mrs. Dacre the identity of the woman who had died in the cottage. My questions were examples of that indirect fencing in which the questioner imagines himself subtle because the person questioned is too polite to call him impertinent.

“Your former lodger,” I said one evening, “must have been young.”

“Not very.”

“She had known London?”

“Perhaps.”

“Her initials may have been M. D.”

Mrs. Dacre folded her napkin.

“Many people have those initials.”

Miss Dacre rose so suddenly that her chair struck the wall.

“I beg your pardon,” she murmured, and left the room.

Her mother watched the closed door.

“My daughter has suffered from her nerves,” she said. “You must not heed it.”

I did heed it. Indeed, I began to observe Miss Dacre with an attention that was almost cruel. She avoided my sitting-room. If she passed its open door and saw the miniature upon the desk, a faint colour came into her cheeks. Once I caught her standing in the garden beneath the pear-tree. She wore a grey dress, not lilac, and the afternoon was brilliantly clear; yet for one instant the stooping figure straightened, and I saw—not with my eyes alone, perhaps—the outline of the girl who had watched my window through the mist.

Still I clung to my first conclusion. The diary belonged to the dead lodger. Miss Dacre had known her story and shrank from its revival. This explanation was rational, charitable and wrong.

The truth came on the eve of my departure.

My book had advanced sufficiently for me to return to London without confessing total defeat, and my publisher, whose faith in rural inspiration was less robust than mine, had begun to telegraph. I replaced the letters, diary and poor dead flowers in the drawer. The miniature alone remained upon my desk.

Miss Dacre entered to bring fresh roses. Finding me there, she hesitated.

“I beg your pardon. I thought you were walking.”

“I have just returned.”

She set down the bowl. Her hands trembled slightly among the flowers. They were no longer young hands; the veins showed blue beneath the skin, and the fingers were thin. But their shape was unmistakable.

“Miss Dacre,” I said, “there is something I ought to restore to you—or to your mother. I found it in the bookcase.”

I gave her the miniature.

She did not take it at once. She looked at the painted face, and a change passed over her own—not youth returning, for time does not surrender so cheaply, but recognition, tenderness and a pain grown quiet through long use.

“Where did you find this?” she asked.

“Behind the books.”

“I thought it had been lost.”

“You know the lady?”

She raised her eyes. For the first time they met mine directly, and I understood why the painted eyes had seemed familiar.

“It is myself.”

I believe I repeated the words.

“You?”

“Yes.” She touched the tarnished rim with one finger. “A friend painted it, long ago.”

A friend.

The colour came into her face then—not the colour of youth, but its last faithful messenger—and I saw that I had been blind in the elaborate fashion peculiar to observant men. I had divided one life into two women: the beautiful girl, whom I had pitied because she was dead, and the faded woman, whom I had dismissed because she lived. Art had preserved the bloom, grief had preserved the memory, and time, doing its ordinary work, had disguised them both from me.

I thought of Sir Christopher’s great house, of Lady Marr’s diamonds, of the famous portraits with their eloquent hands. I thought also of the smooth entry of renunciation and the wild confession that followed it. Miss Dacre stood before me quietly, awaiting some commonplace courtesy.

No apology could have been offered without revealing an intrusion; no sympathy could have been expressed without insolence. I had entered the most secret chamber of her life and possessed no right even to let her know that I had found the door.

So I merely said, “I see the likeness now.”

She looked down at the miniature.

“Do you?” she answered.

Beyond the open window the two brooks, joined beneath the bridge, moved together towards the town and the sea. The evening light lay lilac upon the Wold. When I turned again, Miss Dacre had taken the roses and the little portrait in her beautiful, altered hands.

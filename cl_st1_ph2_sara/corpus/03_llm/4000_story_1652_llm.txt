One dollar and eighty-seven cents.

There it lay upon the kitchen table: two quarters, seven dimes, three nickels, and forty-two pennies—an assembly of coinage so humble that no treasurer would have troubled to count it twice. Della counted it six times.

One dollar and eighty-seven cents.

And tomorrow was Christmas.

Now, dear friends, before you accuse Arithmetic of cruelty, take a look at the home in which it had performed this particular act of oppression. The furnished flat cost eight dollars a week. It contained a couch that protested whenever sat upon, a frying pan with a hereditary attachment to onions, and a looking-glass so narrow that a person could observe herself only by installments. The front bell declined to ring, while the letter box received no letters. Both had retired from public service without surrendering their positions.

On the door was a card bearing the name:

MR. JAMES DILLINGHAM YOUNG

When Jim had earned thirty dollars a week, “Dillingham” had spread itself there with considerable dignity. Now that his wages had fallen to twenty, the middle name seemed overburdened by its responsibilities and appeared ready to shrink into a mere D. But when Jim came upstairs each evening, thin and tired and needing his supper, Della called him “Jim,” and that settled all questions of rank.

Della swept the money into her palm, dropped it on the table, and sat down upon the rebellious couch.

There followed sobs, sniffles, and silence.

This is not remarkable. Life, as you know, is largely composed of tears, laughter, and unpaid bills, with the bills enjoying the most regular attendance.

Della had saved for months. She had disputed with grocers, challenged butchers, and scrutinized vegetable men as if each potato were a suspect in a criminal proceeding. A penny here, two cents there—these had been rescued through parsimony, vigilance, and the serving of chops thin enough to read through. Yet all her strategy had produced one dollar and eighty-seven cents.

She had wanted to buy Jim something fine. Something rare. Something that whispered—not shouted, for genuine quality never needs to shout—that its owner was the best husband in the city, possibly in the nation, and not entirely without standing among the better husbands of antiquity.

Instead she had one dollar and eighty-seven cents.

Della went to the window. Outside, a gray cat moved along a gray fence beneath a gray sky. It was an economical landscape, requiring only one color.

She turned suddenly toward the looking-glass.

Now there were two treasures in that household. This statement disregards Jim and Della themselves, who were treasures to one another, but narrators must occasionally speak in terms understood by insurance companies.

One treasure was Jim’s gold watch. It had belonged to his father and to his grandfather before him. Solomon might have owned seven hundred clocks, yet had Jim passed him in the street and drawn out that watch, Solomon would have tugged at his royal sleeves from envy.

The other treasure was Della’s hair.

She pulled out the pins.

Down came the hair—brown, shining, and abundant. It fell below her knees in a rippling curtain, a cascade at sunset, a royal cloak woven by no loom and taxable by no government. Had the Queen of Sheba lived across the air shaft, Della would have hung her hair out to dry merely to make the lady feel commonplace.

For one moment Della stood motionless. Then two tears slipped downward and vanished into the worn carpet.

She twisted the hair up again, seized her old brown jacket, put on her hat, and rushed out.

Her skirts flew. Her heart flew faster. Time, that solemn old clerk, was forced to trot behind her carrying the paperwork.

A sign on the second floor of a narrow building read:

MADAME SOFRONIE  
HAIR GOODS OF EVERY DESCRIPTION

Della climbed the stairs and entered.

Madame Sofronie was large, pale, and chilly. Nothing about her suggested the arts of southern France except the sign.

“Will you buy my hair?” Della asked.

“I buy hair,” said Madame. “Take off yer hat. Let’s see it.”

The brown torrent fell.

“Twenty dollars,” said Madame, lifting it with practiced fingers.

“Give it to me quick,” said Della.

There are moments when reflection is merely cowardice wearing spectacles. Within twenty minutes—it seemed to Della both an age and the snap of a finger—the splendid hair was gone, and she was hurrying through the streets with twenty-one dollars and eighty-seven cents in her purse.

Then began the search.

She inspected watch fobs with meretricious ornamentation, chains burdened by eagles, shields, horseshoes, cupids, and various metallic vegetables. She rejected one chain that seemed fit for a gambler, another for an undertaker, and a third for a gentleman who had mistaken noise for elegance.

At last she found it.

It was a simple platinum watch chain, finely made, without ornament. Its value lay in its material and workmanship, just as Jim’s value lay in—well, in Jim. The chain was quiet, honest, and strong. Twenty-one dollars was its price.

Della paid and hurried home with eighty-seven cents.

When she arrived, enthusiasm gave way to prudence. Prudence, arriving late as usual, pointed toward the mirror.

“Oh,” said Della.

Her head was covered in short brown curls. They clustered close about her face, giving her the appearance of a mischievous schoolboy who had unexpectedly become responsible for a household.

She heated the curling irons and labored. The smell of scorched hair floated through the flat, joining the resident perfumes of coffee, dust, and yesterday’s onions. For forty minutes she pinched and twisted. Then she studied the result.

“If Jim doesn’t kill me,” she said, “he’ll say I look like a chorus girl. But what could I do with one dollar and eighty-seven cents?”

She prepared supper. The chops went into the pan. Coffee waited on the stove. The platinum chain rested in her hand, cool and beautiful.

At seven o’clock she heard Jim’s step below.

Della sat near the door and clasped her hands. She had a habit of saying little prayers about ordinary things, and now she whispered:

“Please, God, make him think I’m still pretty.”

The door opened.

Jim stepped in and closed it behind him. He was only twenty-two, but responsibility had laid a serious hand upon his shoulders. He needed a new overcoat. He had no gloves. His face was thin from work and winter.

He looked at Della.

Then he stopped.

A dog scenting a bird in high grass could not have become more still. His eyes remained fixed upon her, and the expression in them was not anger, nor horror, nor surprise alone. It was something Della could not read, which is a terrifying thing in a husband’s face when supper is already on the stove.

“Jim, darling,” she cried, “don’t look at me like that! I cut my hair off and sold it because I couldn’t have lived through Christmas without giving you something. It’ll grow again. You don’t mind, do you? Say you don’t. My hair grows awfully fast.”

Jim looked around the room, as though the missing hair might be hiding behind the couch.

“You cut off your hair?” he asked.

“Yes, and sold it. But I’m still me, Jim. Ain’t I?”

“Your hair is gone?”

“You needn’t look for it,” said Della rapidly. “It’s sold, I tell you—sold and gone. It’s Christmas Eve, sweetheart. Be good to me, because I did it for you. Maybe the hairs on my head could be counted, though it would keep a bookkeeper busy, but nobody could count how much I love you. Shall I put on the chops?”

Jim seemed to awaken.

He crossed the room and gathered Della into his arms.

Let us inspect some unimportant object elsewhere for ten seconds. The rent was eight dollars a week. The room was shabby. Jim’s overcoat was thin. None of these facts had changed. Yet an accountant who attempted to value what stood in that embrace would have broken his pencil and gone home.

Jim drew a package from his coat pocket and placed it on the table.

“Don’t misunderstand me, Dell,” he said. “There isn’t any haircut that could make me love you less. But open that package, and you’ll know why I looked funny.”

Della tore at the paper.

First came a cry of delight. Then came a softer sound, and tears.

Inside lay the combs.

They were the combs Della had admired for months in a Broadway window: a beautiful set, carved of tortoiseshell, with jeweled rims glowing faintly in the lamplight. They were precisely the color for her magnificent brown hair. She had known they were expensive. She had gazed at them without hope of ownership, performing that ancient feminine ceremony by which desire presses its nose to the glass and pretends not to mind.

Now they were hers.

But the hair that should have worn them was gone.

Della gathered the combs to her breast. After a while she looked up through her tears and smiled.

“My hair grows fast, Jim.”

Then, with a leap of remembrance, she sprang from her chair.

“Oh! Oh!”

Jim had not yet seen his gift.

She held out the platinum chain upon her palm. The lamplight ran along it in a pale, noble gleam. Della’s face shone more brightly.

“Isn’t it dandy, Jim? I hunted all over town for it. You’ll have to look at the time a hundred times a day now. Give me your watch. I want to see how it looks.”

Jim did not move.

Instead, he sat upon the couch, put his hands behind his head, and smiled.

“Dell,” he said slowly, “let’s put our Christmas presents away for a while. They’re too fine to use just now.”

Della stared.

“I sold the watch,” Jim continued, “to get the money to buy your combs. And I reckon you’d better put the chops on.”

There it is.

No trumpet sounded. No angel leaned through the ceiling. The coffee threatened to boil over, and one of the chops was already approaching a state of carbonization. Upon the table lay a watch chain with no watch and jeweled combs with no long hair to hold them.

A practical person might call this foolishness.

Practical persons are useful citizens. They remember umbrellas, compare prices, and refuse warranties of doubtful scope. But when they judge love, they often apply the wrong arithmetic.

Long ago, wise men came from the East bearing gifts for a child. They were learned men, and their gifts were wise. From them comes the custom of Christmas giving, or so we are told. Had two gifts been alike, they would perhaps have exchanged one at the palace.

But here I have related the uneventful chronicle of two foolish young people in an eight-dollar flat who sacrificed, each for the other, the finest treasure of the house. Their gifts could not be used. Their planning had defeated itself. Their wisdom wore the cap and bells of folly.

Yet among all who give gifts, these two were wise.

Of all who give and receive, they were the wisest.

They are the true gift-bringers.

And now, dear friends, we may leave them to their chops.

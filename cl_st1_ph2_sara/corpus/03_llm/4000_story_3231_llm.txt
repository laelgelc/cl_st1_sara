### THE RESPONSIBLE AGENT

The terrace of the Villa Miraflores hung above Cannes like the quarter-deck of a vessel arrested in a blue and motionless sea. Below it, the roofs descended in warm confusion toward the harbor; beyond them, yachts and fishing craft lay upon the Mediterranean, each with its shadow so perfectly repeated that one could not easily have said which vessel belonged to the world and which to its reflection.

At a small table, under an awning striped in faded crimson, three men remained after luncheon.

One was Sir Henry Marquis, an Englishman whose mild expression had enabled him to dispute with nearly every eminent person in Europe without being denied a second invitation. The second was Mr. Justice Caleb Vane, of an American appellate court, a large, deliberate man with iron-gray hair and that attentive immobility which judges sometimes retain outside the courtroom, as though every conversation might presently be incorporated into a bill of exceptions.

The third, and by far the most remarkable, was Count Giulio di Serravalle.

He had passed his ninety-second year. His body seemed not so much bent by age as folded into an attitude which centuries of ceremony had imposed upon it. The skin of his face was yellow and delicate, like old parchment held before a lamp; his hands were narrow, transparent, and covered with rings whose stones had outlived dynasties. One fancied that he had been old when Garibaldi was young, that he had looked from a balcony upon the last carriage of the Bourbons, and that Rome, having mislaid some minor senator of the pagan world, had found him again in evening clothes upon the Riviera.

Yet his eyes were not old. They were black, exact, malicious, and full of a patience which was itself a form of superiority.

He selected a cigarette from a silver box and tapped it twice against the lid.

“Justice,” he said, “is the most provincial of the virtues.”

The American judge smiled.

“That is a severe judgment, Count.”

“Pardon me, monsieur; it is not a judgment. It is merely an observation. A judgment requires jurisdiction, evidence, and—if one is fortunate—a tribunal incapable of understanding either.”

Sir Henry laughed.

“You have begun badly, Giulio. Our guest is himself a tribunal.”

“For that reason I speak with confidence. No one knows the defects of a palace so well as the man obliged to inhabit it.”

Justice Vane turned his coffee cup slowly between his fingers.

“You mean, I suppose, that legal systems differ.”

“I mean something more offensive. Men agree readily that murder, theft, treachery, and cruelty are evil; but the moment they undertake to prevent these things by rules, their agreement ends. The Englishman puts on a wig, the Frenchman sends for a dossier, the Italian sends for his cousin, and the American sends for twelve citizens who would rather be attending to their shops.”

“That,” said Vane, “is cosmopolitan satire, not argument.”

The Count inclined his head.

“Then permit me an argument. Let us imagine Corporal Flint.”

“Who is Corporal Flint?” asked Sir Henry.

“No one, which makes him admirably suited to philosophy. He is a British soldier in a barbarous country. He has been captured by men who intend to amuse themselves before killing him. They fasten his arms to two young saplings, bend the trees toward one another, and propose, after certain preliminary attentions with knives and heated iron, to release the trunks in opposite directions.”

Sir Henry’s pleasant face became grave.

“A disagreeable invention.”

“I apologize. The ancients would have considered it commonplace. Now another British prisoner, hidden near by and unable to rescue Flint, possesses a rifle. Flint sees him. He cries out: ‘Shoot me!’ The second soldier obeys. Corporal Flint dies instantly and escapes an hour of torture followed by inevitable death.”

The Count lit his cigarette. A thin line of smoke rose between his fingers.

“Who killed Corporal Flint?”

“The man with the rifle,” said Vane.

“Legally?”

“If he intentionally fired the fatal shot, yes.”

“Morally?”

“That depends upon the circumstances. He might be praised.”

“Yet the barbarians created the necessity, selected the victim, prepared his death, and would certainly have accomplished it. Are they innocent of homicide because a merciful bullet arrived first?”

“Certainly not.”

“Then two causes may stand behind one death?”

“Of course.”

“And the intervention of a human will does not always relieve the earlier wrongdoer?”

“Not always.”

“Excellent. We have advanced very far.”

Sir Henry leaned back.

“I know that tone, Giulio. You are leading us toward a pit.”

“A pit, Sir Henry, is merely a conclusion observed too late.”

Justice Vane said, “The principle is plain enough. An intervening act breaks the chain of causation only when it is independent, voluntary, and not a natural consequence of the first act.”

The Count’s eyebrows rose.

“You have made it beautiful already. That is an American gift. You build a capitol over an abyss and inscribe a maxim upon the dome.”

“You object to the maxim?”

“On the contrary, monsieur, I admire it profoundly. I admire all maxims. They are the medals which law awards itself after the battle.”

For a moment no one spoke. From the promenade below came the remote complaint of a motor horn. The Count watched the smoke of his cigarette drift beyond the balustrade.

“I knew a case,” he continued, “in which the maxim descended from its dome and walked among men. The result was—how shall I say?—instructive. It occurred in one of your American coal districts, Justice Vane, many years ago, when I still possessed estates sufficient to make the troubles of my relations impersonal.”

The judge settled himself in his chair.

“In Pennsylvania?”

“Near enough that you may imagine Pennsylvania without doing injustice to geography. There were mountains, wooden houses stained with smoke, a railway that carried coal by night and immigrants by day, and a town gathered at the mouth of the mines as refuse gathers at the mouth of a furnace. The population had come from Calabria, Croatia, Wales, Bohemia, Hungary, Ireland, and those disputed valleys where every man speaks three languages and denies the nationality of his neighbor in all of them.”

“A difficult community to police,” said Sir Henry.

“Impossible. Therefore it was policed.”

The Count smiled faintly.

“The officer chiefly responsible was Captain Abel Trent. I never saw him; I depend upon descriptions, photographs, sworn testimony, and one letter written by a priest who disliked him. These sources agree sufficiently. Trent was a huge man, without imagination and consequently without fear. He had served in the cavalry, had broken strikes, arrested gamblers, faced mobs, and acquired the inconvenient reputation of being incorruptible. He was not amiable. Justice, when embodied in a man of six feet four inches, rarely is.”

“Was he the chief of police?” asked Vane.

“He was captain of the mine constabulary, deputy sheriff when required, and Providence whenever no magistrate could be found. His methods were abrupt. If a man drew a knife, Trent struck him with a chair. If ten men resisted arrest, he arrested eleven. There were complaints, naturally; but the district had reached that stage of civilization in which respectable persons condemn violence at breakfast and send secretly for a violent man before dinner.”

Sir Henry nodded.

“I have known districts of that sort in several empires.”

“The criminal in the affair was one Paolo Brizzi. There can be no delicacy in describing him. He was a thief, an informer, a blackmailer, and, when courage could be supplied by darkness or companionship, an assassin. He had a narrow forehead, excellent teeth, and the sentimental vanity common to scoundrels who sing well. Women excused him because he wept over songs of Naples. Men employed him because he accepted risks for small sums.”

“And he was Italian?” said Vane.

“Nominally. Italy has produced Dante, Caesar, Saint Francis, and my grandmother. It must not be made responsible for Paolo Brizzi.”

The Count removed a fragment of ash from his sleeve.

“Brizzi had helped to rob the office of a coal company. During the robbery an elderly watchman was beaten nearly to death. One of the thieves was arrested and talked. A warrant issued for Brizzi, who hid in a boardinghouse above a saloon.

“Captain Trent went there alone.

“This detail was established by twenty witnesses and disputed by no one. He entered by the front door, climbed the stairs, and found Brizzi in a room with two women, a gramophone, and a revolver lying upon the washstand. Brizzi reached for the weapon. Trent seized his wrist, threw him across the bed, took the revolver, and marched him downstairs by the collar.

“The street was crowded. It was Sunday afternoon. Men sat before the saloon; children played beside the drainage ditch; women leaned from windows. Everyone saw Trent and his prisoner.

“Now among those who saw them was my relative.”

The Count paused.

Neither of the others spoke immediately.

“Your relative?” Sir Henry said at last.

“A remote one. Blood, monsieur, is a fluid of astonishing legal tenacity. My great-grandfather had a daughter by a second marriage; her grandson’s son married the sister of one Salvatore Bellandi. It is a relationship which the Church can calculate, a lawyer can exploit, and a gentleman would prefer to forget.”

“Bellandi was Brizzi’s accomplice?”

“He had been. Salvatore Bellandi was small, vain, indolent, and violent—the sort of creature who believes himself formidable because decent men step aside to avoid touching him. He wore a red neckcloth and carried a pistol too large for his hand. When Trent passed with Brizzi, Bellandi rose from a chair outside the saloon and followed.”

“Did Trent observe him?”

“He observed everyone. But he had Brizzi’s revolver in one hand and Brizzi’s arm in the other. Moreover, Bellandi did nothing at first. He merely walked twenty or thirty paces behind them.

“The road to the jail crossed the lower end of the town, ran beside the railway, and entered a narrow cut known as Mercer’s Ravine. There were houses along the first part of the road and laborers everywhere. Thus there were witnesses—too many witnesses, one might suppose, for any man intending murder.

“Bellandi called out in Italian.

“He asked Brizzi whether he should shoot the officer there in the street or wait until they reached the ravine.”

Justice Vane lifted his head sharply.

“In the hearing of witnesses?”

“In the hearing of at least seven persons who understood him and perhaps thirty who did not. Brizzi answered without turning. ‘Not here. At the cut.’”

“That alone proves premeditation,” Sir Henry said.

“Observe, monsieur, how obediently the pieces arrange themselves. Bellandi followed. Twice more he asked whether the moment had arrived. Once Brizzi told him to keep back. A woman at a pump heard it. A railway worker heard it. A boy carrying bread heard it and repeated the words to his mother before the shots were fired.

“They entered Mercer’s Ravine.”

The old man laid his cigarette upon the edge of the ashtray, where it continued to burn.

“The cut was perhaps seventy yards long, with steep banks of shale on either side. At its entrance stood a signal shed; at the farther end, the road curved toward the jail. Trent and Brizzi were halfway through when Bellandi drew his pistol.

“He fired twice into Trent’s back.”

The sentence fell with no adornment.

“One bullet entered below the left shoulder and passed through the lung. The other severed a great vessel near the heart. Trent released the prisoner, staggered against the bank, and turned. Bellandi ran. Brizzi ran with him.

“But Captain Trent did not fall immediately.

“A signalman saw him remain upright, with one hand against the rock and the other pressed to his chest. Blood came through his coat and from his mouth. He tried to call after the fugitives, but could produce only a whisper. Then, with the deliberation of a man performing a familiar duty, he took Brizzi’s confiscated revolver from his belt.

“He examined it.

“He placed the muzzle against his right temple.

“And he fired.”

The Count’s cigarette had gone out. He did not relight it.

Below the terrace, the Mediterranean remained intensely, offensively blue.

“Was he conscious of what he did?” asked Justice Vane.

“Entirely.”

“How can that be known?”

“Because three men had entered the ravine from the signal shed after Bellandi’s shots. They were within fifteen yards. One called to Trent not to fire. Trent looked at him and said—very distinctly—‘They have finished me. I will not wait for it.’ Then he shot himself.”

Sir Henry drew a breath.

“A Roman conclusion.”

“Or a military one. Saul fell upon his sword. Brutus demanded the office of a friend. Ajax preferred his own hand to the leisure of his enemies. We admire such resolutions in the Iliad and condemn them in a coroner’s report. Trent had seen men die of wounds in the mines. He knew the red suffocation of a pierced lung, the thirst, the terror, the useless hands of companions. He believed death certain and refused its delay.”

“Was death certain?” Vane asked.

“Beyond every reasonable doubt. The first physician arrived in eleven minutes. At the trial he testified that either of Bellandi’s bullets would have caused death; the second must have done so within a very short time. A surgeon from the city agreed. The defense summoned another, who agreed reluctantly and charged twice the fee. There was no medical dispute.”

“But the bullet through the temple caused the immediate death.”

“Precisely.”

The Count regarded the judge.

“Tell me, monsieur: who murdered Captain Abel Trent?”

“Bellandi.”

“Not Trent himself?”

“Trent killed himself in the physical sense. Bellandi was legally responsible for the death if his act produced the condition under which Trent acted.”

“Even though Trent’s decision was conscious?”

“Consciousness alone is not enough to break causation.”

“You are certain?”

“I should want the record, naturally; but upon the facts as you give them, yes.”

The Count bowed.

“No advocate could ask a more candid tribunal.”

“They caught Bellandi?” Sir Henry asked.

“Before dawn. He hid in a coke oven which, to his surprise, was scheduled to be lighted at six. Brizzi escaped across the state line and was later killed in a quarrel concerning cards. Bellandi’s pistol was found in the ravine. Its chambers corresponded to the two shots. The bullets taken from Trent’s body matched the weapon. Powder stains, footprints, witnesses—the prosecution had an embarrassment of riches.”

“And Bellandi confessed?”

“First to a priest, then to a woman, then to a cellmate, and finally, with improvements, to the sheriff. The confession to the priest was privileged; the confession to the woman she repeated to everyone in town; the confession to the cellmate was challenged because the cellmate hoped for leniency in another matter; and the confession to the sheriff was alleged to have been made under fear. None was necessary.”

“Were the Italian statements admissible?” asked Vane.

“Ah!”

The Count chose another cigarette but held it unlighted between his fingers.

“There arose the expected comedy. Seven witnesses had heard Bellandi ask Brizzi whether he should shoot Trent immediately or in the ravine. Two were unable to speak English. One spoke English but became so frightened by the judge that he forgot Italian. One understood Bellandi’s dialect but insisted upon translating every phrase with an explanatory account of his village. The official interpreter came from the north and declared that no civilized man could understand a Calabrian. The Calabrian witnesses replied that no civilized man would employ the interpreter.

“For three days the court investigated the Italian language.”

Sir Henry smiled.

“And the result?”

“The words were admitted. A schoolmaster translated them. The defense objected that he had learned Italian from books. A grocer corrected him from experience. The court eventually concluded that ‘Shall I shoot now?’ meant, with reasonable certainty, ‘Shall I shoot now?’ Thus jurisprudence survived.”

Justice Vane permitted himself a laugh.

“I have seen worse contests over interpretation.”

“Undoubtedly. The law distrusts hearsay because a statement changes as it passes from mouth to mouth; yet when a witness speaks through an interpreter, the law creates hearsay in the courtroom and calls it procedure.”

“That is not quite fair.”

“I should be ashamed to be quite fair in conversation.”

The Count allowed Sir Henry to light his cigarette.

“The trial was otherwise complete. The signalman described the shots. The men in the ravine described Trent’s last words and act. The physicians described the mortal wounds. Bellandi was identified by every person who had followed the procession from the saloon. His threats were proved. His agreement with Brizzi was proved. His flight was proved. His weapon was proved. His confessions, except the priest’s, were proved.

“The judge charged the jury upon malice aforethought, conspiracy, reasonable doubt, and murder in the first degree. He told them that if Bellandi deliberately shot Trent with intent to kill, and if those wounds caused or materially contributed to Trent’s death, the prisoner was guilty notwithstanding the self-inflicted wound.

“The jury retired at six minutes after four.

“At twenty-two minutes after four they returned.”

“Guilty,” Sir Henry said.

“Of murder in the first degree.”

“And the sentence?”

“To be hanged.”

There was a small sound from the harbor, perhaps a chain running over a capstan. The three men listened until it ceased.

Justice Vane said, “So far there is no paradox.”

“No, monsieur. So far there is only murder.”

The Count leaned forward. In this position the ancient folds of his face tightened, and for an instant the intelligence behind them seemed to consume age altogether.

“Bellandi’s counsel appealed.”

“Naturally.”

“He assigned thirty-one errors. The interpreter had been improperly selected. The sheriff’s confession was involuntary. The cellmate was unworthy of belief. A juror had once purchased onions from the dead man’s brother. The judge had used the phrase ‘natural and probable consequence’ where counsel preferred ‘direct and necessary result.’ A newspaper had printed Bellandi’s portrait beneath the word ASSASSIN, which the defense considered prejudicial, although the portrait itself was perhaps the greater injury.

“All these objections failed.”

“Then what succeeded?” asked Sir Henry.

“The thirty-second.”

Justice Vane was no longer smiling.

“The causal instruction?”

“Yes.”

The Count exhaled smoke slowly.

“The appellate court began with a proposition so reasonable that no gentleman could object to it. To constitute homicide, the unlawful act of the accused must be the legal cause of death. If an independent responsible agency intervenes and itself causes death, the original wrongdoer cannot be convicted of homicide merely because he furnished the occasion.”

“That is orthodox,” said Vane.

“Beautifully orthodox. The court then observed that Captain Trent, after receiving the wounds, remained conscious, understood his situation, formed an intention, selected a weapon, placed it against his head, and voluntarily fired. He was not moved by reflex. He was not delirious. Bellandi did not compel his hand. Trent was, in the language of the opinion, ‘a free, conscious, and responsible moral agent.’”

Sir Henry’s hand descended to the arm of his chair.

“They held the suicide an intervening cause?”

“They held it the immediate and independent cause of death.”

“But the wounds were mortal.”

“Admitted.”

“He would have died in minutes.”

“Admitted.”

“He killed himself solely because Bellandi had murdered him all but the final instant.”

“Admitted with elegance.”

“And Bellandi escaped?”

“The conviction for murder was reversed.”

Justice Vane frowned.

“That need not mean acquittal. He might be retried for assault with intent to kill.”

“He was. But assault with intent to kill was not murder in the first degree, and the statute of that jurisdiction presented certain complications because the indictment had charged the completed offense. There were further proceedings, further arguments, and a compromise. Bellandi served some years and emerged alive, which was the principal object of his counsel.”

The Englishman rose and walked to the balustrade.

“That is intolerable.”

The Count watched him.

“Which part, Sir Henry? The shooting, the suicide, the doctrine, or the survival?”

“The conclusion. A man cannot make himself innocent of murder merely because his victim declines to endure the full operation of the wounds.”

“Yet Captain Trent was a responsible agent.”

“Responsible, yes; independent, no.”

“Ah. Now we arrive at the adjective upon which a man’s neck depends.”

Justice Vane said, “I am not persuaded the court was right. A victim’s attempt to escape pain, danger, or death is ordinarily foreseeable. If a man leaps from a burning room and is killed by the fall, the incendiary cannot answer that gravity was the responsible agent.”

“An admirable example. But suppose the room is on the first floor and the victim, finding a pistol on the table, shoots himself rather than risk a scorched hand?”

“Then the act may be disproportionate and independent.”

“Suppose the flames have already reached his clothing?”

“Then less so.”

“Suppose he will certainly die after an hour?”

“The chain is stronger.”

“After ten minutes?”

“Stronger still.”

“After ten seconds?”

“Bellandi is plainly the cause.”

The Count spread his hands.

“Nevertheless, at some point between the scorched hand and the final ten seconds, law must draw a line invisible to morals and fatal to somebody. That line may be called proximate cause, remoteness, volition, foreseeability, novus actus interveniens, or any other Latin incantation by which educated men conceal the tremor of the pencil. But the line must be drawn.”

“Not there,” said Vane.

“Perhaps not. Yet observe your difficulty. You first said that the rifleman killed Corporal Flint, though mercifully, and that the torturers also bore responsibility. In Flint’s case the second will was external; in Trent’s it was his own. Is a man’s will more independent because it inhabits the injured body? Does the law say: ‘If your friend releases you from torture, your torturers killed you; if you release yourself, they did not’?”

“The cases differ.”

“All cases differ. Otherwise lawyers would starve.”

“Flint commands another person to act. Trent acts upon himself.”

“And this distinction satisfies justice?”

“It may satisfy causation.”

The Count smiled with exquisite courtesy.

“Exactly, monsieur. Justice is a lady; causation is her accountant. They do not always dine together.”

Sir Henry returned to the table.

“What became of Bellandi?”

“He returned to Italy, discovered socialism, abandoned it when required to work, married twice without completing the formality of becoming a widower, and cultivated the infirmities appropriate to a man without occupation. He is now seventy-three and resides near Bari.”

“And your family connection?”

“Unfortunately established.”

“But why should that concern you?”

For the first time, the Count’s composure showed something like personal pain. It was not grief; it was the expression of a proprietor remembering taxation.

“My family possesses an ancient charitable trust,” he said. “One of my ancestors, alarmed by death after a life that had given him excellent reason to be alarmed, provided that indigent members of several collateral branches should receive an allowance from the head of the house. Bellandi’s wife descended from one of those branches. Upon her death, he demonstrated—through documents of a quality that would have astonished the court which tried him—that he was entitled to continue the payment.”

“You could contest it,” said Justice Vane.

“I did.”

“And lost?”

“In three tribunals.”

The Count sighed and brushed the ash from his cigarette.

“My estates, once considerable, have suffered from war, currency, governments, tenants, nephews, and vines afflicted by a disease of republican principles. Nevertheless, on the first day of every quarter, my steward remits money to Salvatore Bellandi.”

Sir Henry stared at him; then laughter overcame his indignation.

“So that is your interest in criminal causation!”

“My interest is jurisprudential. My irritation is quarterly.”

Justice Vane shook his head.

“You have made the case too perfect, Count. I should like to see the report.”

“I anticipated professional suspicion.”

From within his coat he produced a folded sheet, thin and yellow at the creases. He put on spectacles of a severe, antiquated pattern and read:

“‘The State against Bellandi, Reports of the Supreme Court, volume eighty-six, page four hundred and eleven: Where death proceeds directly from the voluntary act of the deceased, he being at the time conscious, rational, and under no physical compulsion, such act constitutes an intervening responsible agency, though the condition inducing it was occasioned by the unlawful violence of the accused.’”

He removed the spectacles.

“There, monsieur, is the sentence. It has cost me, with interest and changes in exchange, a sum I hesitate to calculate.”

Vane took the paper and examined it.

“The opinion is old.”

“So am I. Age does not necessarily invalidate an absurdity.”

“It may have been limited by later cases.”

“I hope so. But later cases do not hang Bellandi, nor do they refund my allowance.”

The sun had moved beyond the striped awning. One edge of the table shone fiercely; the other remained in cool shadow. Far below, a white yacht began to turn toward open water, disturbing for a little while the perfect image beneath it.

Sir Henry said, “Then your proposition is that justice varies because legal systems classify causes differently?”

“My proposition is more modest and more dangerous. Every legal system must convert life into categories, and life resents the operation. The categories are indispensable; without them we have vengeance, caprice, and the strong man’s appetite. Yet once established, they proceed with the serene cruelty of geometry. A murderer may be saved by a definition, an innocent man ruined by an exception, and both results announced in the name of principle.”

“You would not abolish principle,” said Vane.

“Certainly not. I would abolish confidence.”

The judge looked again at the yellow sheet.

“There is still something to be said for the court. If free will is never permitted to interrupt consequence, responsibility becomes limitless.”

“Precisely. And if it interrupts too readily, responsibility disappears. Between those abysses sits the judge, with precedent on one side, public outrage on the other, and counsel assuring him that the path is perfectly clear.”

The Count rose. The movement was laborious, but once upright he seemed to recover the ceremonial stature of some ambassador accredited by a kingdom no longer on the map.

He bowed first to Justice Vane and then to Sir Henry.

“Pardon me, messieurs. I must write to my steward. It is the final day of the quarter.”

“And Bellandi’s allowance is due?” Sir Henry asked.

“It was due yesterday.”

The Count looked toward the tranquil sea, where the yacht’s divided image had begun slowly to unite.

“Justice,” he said, “may occasionally be delayed. My relative never is.”

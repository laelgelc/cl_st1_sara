# THE SALVAGE OF THE NEPTUNE

The hurricane of September 17, 1893, which swept northward from the Bahama Islands, crossed the Gulf Stream, and spent its last force upon the Banks of Newfoundland, was responsible for the abandonment of no fewer than fourteen vessels between the parallels of 27° and 38° north latitude. Nine of these were schooners or brigs carrying lumber from ports in Georgia and Florida; and, although several were afterwards recovered, the remainder continued for months to be reported by passing steamers as dangerous obstructions in the tracks of coastwise commerce.

A lumber-laden vessel possesses certain advantages when overtaken by disaster. Her cargo, if properly stowed, may keep her afloat after the seams have opened and the pumps have ceased to be of use; but that same quality makes her, when abandoned, a particularly troublesome ocean waif. She will drift with the wind and current, frequently showing no light and carrying only a few feet of black, weed-covered hull above the water. A ship loaded with iron or grain goes down and is no longer a menace. A ship full of pine boards may remain afloat for a year.

The schooner Mary D. Cole, abandoned in March, 1888, was sighted thirty-two times before she stranded on the Irish coast. The Norwegian bark Frithiof crossed the Atlantic without a living person aboard her and was found within eighty miles of the Azores, although she had been deserted off Cape Hatteras. In each case the wreck’s progress was plotted with much interest by hydrographical officers, and warnings were distributed to shipmasters. But warnings did not always prevent collisions, for a derelict which had travelled ten miles during daylight might travel twenty more before midnight.

The September hurricane provided an unusually profitable harvest for those sea-going tugs whose owners were willing to risk coal, tackle, and men in search of salvage. Sound yellow pine was then selling in Boston at from eighteen to twenty-four dollars per thousand feet, and a brig carrying half a million feet might therefore have ten thousand dollars’ worth of cargo in her hold and on her deck, even though the vessel herself had lost her masts and was fit for little more than the breaker’s yard. The law allowed a liberal award to the men who brought such property safely into port. There were consequently several tug captains who studied the reports of abandoned vessels with as much care as a merchant studies the prices current.

Among these was Captain Ezra James, master of the Philadelphia tug Atlas.

The Atlas was an iron vessel of 146 feet over all, with a beam of twenty-eight feet, a compound engine of 700 indicated horse-power, and coal bunkers capable of holding 128 tons. She was not new, nor did she possess the graceful proportions of the passenger steamers which passed her in the Delaware; but she had a powerful towing-winch, a stout oak fender around her bow, and a reputation for remaining at sea in weather which sent lighter tugs running for the nearest inlet. Captain James had commanded her for seven years.

On the morning of September 23 he entered the office of Harlan & Pope, her owners, carrying a copy of a shipping paper folded beneath his arm.

“There is money drifting about here,” he said, spreading the paper upon Mr. Pope’s desk, “if we can find it before somebody else does.”

His forefinger rested upon a paragraph which stated that the brig Neptune, of St. John, New Brunswick, had been abandoned on September 18 in latitude 31° 40′ north, longitude 74° 10′ west. Her crew had been taken off by the British steamship Cerdic after the brig had lost her foremast, maintopmast, bulwarks, boats, and deck-house. She carried 487,000 feet of pitch-pine lumber from Darien, Georgia, to Boston.

“She was badly knocked about,” Mr. Pope observed.

“That makes no difference if her bottom is under her.”

“Five days have passed.”

“Five days since the Cerdic took off the men. The hurricane drove north-east, and the Stream will have carried the Neptune north. I allow fourteen miles a day for current, seven for wind, and something for the irregular set along the western edge. She ought to be between latitude thirty-four and thirty-five by this time.”

Mr. Pope regarded the chart which the captain had brought with him.

“How long to reach her?”

“Thirty-six hours, if she has behaved reasonably.”

A derelict seldom behaves reasonably; nevertheless, the owners agreed to the venture. Coal was ordered at once, together with provisions for twenty days, a new eleven-inch Manila hawser, two smaller towing-lines, iron shackles, spare blocks, and a coil of wire rope. Before nightfall the Atlas lay deep at her berth, steam was being raised, and the crew had been increased by four men.

Albert Shaw went with her. He was a young clerk in the counting-room of Harlan & Pope, and had never been farther to sea than Cape May on an excursion boat; but he had begged permission to accompany the tug, partly because he wished to represent the owners should the Neptune be found, and partly because the prospect of hunting an abandoned vessel appealed strongly to an imagination not yet disciplined by experience.

Captain James looked him over on the wharf.

“Have you sea-boots?”

Albert displayed a pair so new that their varnished tops shone in the lantern-light.

“Oilskins?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Can you cook?”

“No, sir.”

“Then I don’t know what use you’ll be. Come aboard.”

At eleven o’clock that night the Atlas swung out into the Delaware. Her whistle gave two hoarse blasts, the deck-hands hauled in the breast-line, and Philadelphia, with its lamps stretched along the black river, began to fall astern. There was a cool wind from the west, and Albert remained on deck until the tug had passed Chester. By that time the vibration of the engines, the smell of hot oil, and the short movement of the vessel in the lower river had reduced his enthusiasm, and he went below without mentioning the matter to anyone.

At breakfast the cook, who was called Old Martin though he was not more than fifty, observed that landsmen always brought bad weather.

“There wasn’t a cloud showing when we left the wharf,” said Albert.

“That proves it,” Martin replied. “The weather has to come farther to find you.”

The Atlas passed the capes shortly after noon and took a course south-east by east. Captain James had plotted upon his chart the Neptune’s probable movement for each day since her abandonment. The Gulf Stream at the reported position ran north-east at a speed varying from one to three knots, but its motion was neither uniform nor confined within fixed boundaries. Eddies sometimes carried floating objects westward for many miles, and a strong north-east wind might hold a wreck nearly stationary against the current. The captain therefore drew not one line but three, representing what he called the slow, fair, and quick drifts.

Albert stood beside him in the pilot-house.

“If the brig is somewhere between those lines,” he said, “we shall have a strip nearly a hundred miles wide to search.”

“Seventy-three at the widest point.”

“And she may not be inside it.”

“She may be under us, for that matter,” answered Captain James. “But a man has to put his first guess somewhere. The Cerdic gave a good position, the wind is known, and the Stream has been running strong. I’ll find her.”

This was spoken without boasting. Captain James regarded navigation as an affair of observed facts corrected by experience, and he had learned that confidence saved time. At four o’clock he changed course three points to the southward, intending to cross the calculated line of drift and then work back across it in long diagonals.

All that afternoon the lookouts watched the empty sea. A haze lay upon the eastern horizon, and here and there were streaks of floating grass which indicated the current. Once a dark object caused the Atlas to alter course; but it proved to be a cask, barnacled and half full of water. At sunset the taffrail log showed 174 miles from Cape Henlopen.

The night was clear. An additional lookout was placed on the foredeck, while Albert, who had recovered from his first discomfort, was permitted to stand with the mate in the pilot-house. The searchlight was turned from side to side, its white beam travelling across the low swells; but a waterlogged hull is difficult to distinguish at night, and the captain reduced speed until daybreak.

At half-past six on the following morning a sailor named Sharkey, who was aloft with a glass, called down that he saw wreckage two points on the port bow.

“What kind?” Captain James shouted.

“A spar, sir. Looks to be the heel of a topmast.”

The Atlas steamed towards it. Ten minutes later they passed a broken mast about forty feet long, with several fathoms of rigging trailing below it.

“Could that belong to the Neptune?” Albert asked.

“It could belong to fifty vessels. But the growth on it is new, and that iron band has not rusted. Keep your eyes open.”

At seven-fifteen they sighted three pine boards floating together; at seven-forty, a portion of a painted deck-house; and shortly after eight the lookout reported a black mark on the horizon. Captain James took the glass himself. For nearly a minute he said nothing.

“There she is.”

The words brought every man who could leave his duty to the forward deck.

At first Albert saw only a dark interruption in the bright line where sea met sky. Then the Atlas rose upon a swell, and the object resolved itself into the low hull of a vessel, with a single ragged mast leaning aft. A few ropes hung from it, dipping into the water and rising again with each roll. No flag flew from the masthead; no smoke or movement appeared anywhere aboard.

The captain examined his watch and then the chart.

“Two miles west of the fair-drift line,” he said. “She has made about twenty-three miles a day.”

There was satisfaction in his voice, for the Neptune had been found within forty-six hours of leaving Philadelphia and less than six miles from the point at which his calculation had placed her.

As the Atlas approached, the extent of the hurricane’s work became visible. The brig’s foremast had broken six feet above the deck and gone over the starboard side, tearing away the fore-braces and a considerable part of the bulwarks. The main topmast was missing; the lower mast remained, but its standing rigging was slack and one shroud had parted. The deck-house had been crushed as though by a descending weight, both boats were gone, the wheel was broken, and loose lumber projected through the forward rail.

Nevertheless, the hull rode high enough to show four feet of her copper-streaked side amidships.

“Fine lumber, that,” said Sharkey, eyeing the yellow ends of the boards.

“If it stays inside her, it is,” replied the mate.

The Atlas circled the derelict twice. Captain James wished to make certain that no hidden spar or wire rigging lay where it could foul the tug’s propeller. He then brought the Atlas within fifty yards to leeward, and a heaving-line was thrown across the Neptune’s afterdeck. It missed. The second line lodged over the remains of the taffrail.

Joe Miller and Sharkey went aboard by means of the tug’s small boat. They carried axes, lanterns, a compass, provisions, and a stout line with which the end of the towing-hawser could be hauled across. Albert watched them climb the sloping deck and disappear behind the broken cabin. The brig rolled heavily, though the sea was moderate, and each time she went down by the bow a sheet of green water passed across the forward hatch.

After half an hour Sharkey signalled that it was safe to come aboard. Captain James, the mate, and Albert followed.

The Neptune’s deck was a scene of disorder, but not of complete destruction. Several hundred feet of the deck-load had been washed away, leaving the chain lashings loose and twisted; elsewhere the boards remained in place. The forward hatch-cover was stove in at one corner, through which water poured when the brig plunged. A tangled mass of rope, sailcloth, and splintered wood lay to starboard where the foremast had fallen. The smell was compounded of wet pine, tar, salt water, and the sour odour of long-closed cabins.

They entered the after-house. Broken glass covered the floor. A mouldy mattress, torn from a bunk, floated in six inches of water; charts and papers had become a grey pulp in the captain’s room; rusty knives, a coffee-pot, and two tin plates moved to and fro with the roll of the vessel. One clock had stopped at eighteen minutes after three, presumably at the hour when the sea entered the cabin. The lazarette contained spare rope, paint, and a barrel of potatoes which had burst open.

Albert picked up a small framed photograph of a woman and two children, but Captain James told him to leave it.

“This is not a curiosity shop,” he said. “Everything aboard belongs to the owners until a court says otherwise.”

Soundings showed nine feet of water in the main hold. Since the brig drew more than sixteen feet when loaded, the water had risen well above the floor timbers; but the lumber prevented her from sinking, and there was no reason to suppose that her condition would immediately become worse. The pumps were choked and one had been torn from its bed. Miller cleared the after pump while Sharkey rigged a temporary wheel by fastening a spare tiller-bar to the rudder-head.

“Can she be towed?” Albert asked.

“Anything afloat can be towed in good weather,” Captain James replied. “The question is whether the weather will stay good.”

The barometer aboard the Atlas had fallen one-tenth of an inch since midnight, and long streaks of cirrus cloud were appearing in the south-west. The signs did not indicate immediate danger, but Captain James intended to waste no time. Boston lay about 410 miles to the north-east. Norfolk was nearer, though towing against the current towards that port would be difficult, and the lumber had been consigned to Boston. He therefore decided to proceed there.

The eleven-inch hawser was passed aboard and secured around the Neptune’s mainmast and through the after bitts, which were strengthened with a chain led around the deck-load. Seventy fathoms were paid out. Such a long scope allowed the tug and tow to rise independently upon the sea, thus reducing the sudden strains which would occur if both were held close together. Miller and Sharkey volunteered to remain aboard the brig. Their duty was to keep the wreck before the wind, watch the lashings, and show a lantern if assistance was needed.

“You’ll have no steam pump and no dry bunk,” said the captain.

“We’ll have the cook’s coffee in a bottle,” Sharkey answered.

Old Martin filled two large flasks, added a tin of meat and a loaf of bread, and sent word that the Neptune’s galley was not to be blamed if the meal proved cold.

At one-fifteen in the afternoon the Atlas moved ahead slowly. The hawser rose dripping from the sea, straightened, and came taut. For a moment the Neptune did not move. Then her bow swung through two points, water boiled along her battered side, and the wreck began to follow.

The engine-room telegraph rang for half speed.

They made four knots during the afternoon. This was not rapid travelling, but a waterlogged brig cannot be hurried, and at four knots Boston was less than five days away. The sky remained clear until sunset. Miller and Sharkey could be seen working aft, where they had stretched a tarpaulin over part of the ruined cabin. When darkness came they hoisted a red lantern to the stump of the mainmast. From the Atlas it resembled a will-o’-the-wisp, rising, falling, and occasionally disappearing behind the swells.

Albert stood near the towing-winch.

“What would happen if the hawser parted?” he asked the mate.

“We would put another aboard.”

“And if it parted in a storm?”

“We would do whatever we could.”

The answer did not satisfy him, though it was the only one possible.

The wind freshened after midnight and came around to the east. By four o’clock the Atlas was pitching into a short, steep sea which reduced her speed to three knots. The barometer had fallen to 29.55 inches. Captain James altered course towards the north-west, hoping to ease the tow and work out of the strongest part of the Gulf Stream.

At breakfast no dishes were placed upon the table, for they would not have remained there. Martin served coffee in tin mugs and bread cut into thick pieces. Albert, holding his mug with both hands, heard a heavy thud somewhere aft.

“What’s up now?” called the cook.

“Only the hawser taking the strain,” said an oiler.

“Only!” Martin repeated. “That rope costs more than my year’s wages.”

By noon the wind had increased to a moderate gale. Rain swept the deck in hard, slanting lines, and the sea had acquired the dull leaden colour which generally accompanies bad weather on the Atlantic coast. The Atlas rose by the bow, hesitated with half her propeller clear, and plunged into the next hollow. Each plunge sent a shudder through the towing-winch.

The Neptune behaved badly. Without sufficient sail to steady her, she yawed from side to side, sometimes sheering thirty degrees from the tug’s course before her makeshift steering-gear brought her back. Miller and Sharkey could be seen near the stern, one handling the tiller and the other examining the chafing gear where the hawser passed aboard.

Captain James reduced speed.

At two o’clock a sea carried away the Neptune’s remaining port bulwark. Several boards floated free. One passed within twenty feet of the Atlas, standing almost upright in the water before it fell flat with a splash.

“Every one of those is a dollar or two out of our pockets,” said the mate.

“At present,” Captain James replied, “I am more interested in keeping the men.”

The gale continued to increase. By dusk the wind was blowing from east-north-east with a force of forty-five miles an hour, directly against the tug’s intended course. The Atlas could make little headway; yet if she turned and ran before the sea, the Neptune might overtake her, foul the hawser, or swing broadside upon the tug. Captain James therefore kept the engines at slow speed and held the wreck’s bow as nearly into the sea as possible.

At six-thirty Miller showed two lanterns placed vertically. This was the agreed signal that the steering-gear had failed.

The Atlas edged back until she lay within sixty yards of the brig. Under the glare of the searchlight Sharkey could be seen fastening a tackle to the rudder-head, while Miller waved from the lee side of the cabin. The tug’s mate shouted through a megaphone, but the wind carried away his words. Miller pointed towards the stern, made a circular motion with one arm, and then held up both hands.

“He says they have lost the tiller-bar,” Captain James interpreted. “They’re rigging tackles.”

Ten minutes later one lantern was lowered. The brig steadied, and the Atlas again moved ahead.

It was now fully dark. The rain, striking the pilot-house windows, made it impossible to see the tow except when the searchlight found her. The red masthead lantern appeared and vanished in a wild and irregular fashion. Once it seemed almost level with the sea; a moment afterwards it rose high against the black sky.

At nine o’clock the wind shifted two points to the northward, and the seas became confused. The towing-winch groaned at every strain. Men stood ready with axes in case the hawser had to be cut to save the tug, although no captain engaged in salvage would willingly sever the line while there remained another choice.

Captain James had the towing-bitts examined. He ordered twenty fathoms more hawser paid out, but the drum had scarcely begun to turn when the Neptune sheered suddenly to port. The line leaped from the water, rigid as an iron bar. There was a deep report—not a sharp crack, but a heavy, fibrous explosion.

The hawser had parted.

For several seconds no one spoke. The severed end recoiled across the stern grating, struck the rail, and dropped into the sea.

“Searchlight on the tow!” shouted the captain.

The beam swept through rain and flying spray. It found nothing. The Atlas had already moved ahead, while the Neptune, freed from restraint, had fallen away into the darkness.

“Hard a-starboard! Stop her! Full speed astern!”

The tug turned until the sea struck her broadside. A mass of water came over the port rail, smashed a ventilator, and flooded the galley. Albert, who had left the pilot-house, was thrown against the chart-room door and would have gone overboard had the mate not caught his oilskin.

“Below with you!” the mate ordered.

“But the men—”

“Below!”

The Atlas attempted to circle towards the point where the wreck had last been seen. Her searchlight showed only breaking crests. Twice Captain James thought he saw the Neptune’s lantern, but each time it proved to be white water caught for an instant in the beam.

To continue manoeuvring across the sea was to risk broaching to or having the engine disabled when the propeller raced. The captain held on for twenty minutes, then reluctantly brought the Atlas head to wind and hove to. A rocket was fired, followed by another. No answering light appeared.

The Neptune and the two men were gone.

There was no sleep aboard the tug that night. Captain James remained in the pilot-house, maintaining only enough steam to keep the Atlas’s bow against the gale. The crew knew that Miller and Sharkey had provisions, lanterns, and some shelter; but they also knew that the brig’s after cabin was partly flooded, her steering-gear was makeshift, and the broken hawser might foul her rudder. If the deck-load shifted, no amount of buoyant lumber would keep the wreck upright.

Albert asked whether a boat could have been lowered.

“It would not live five minutes,” said Captain James.

“Then we can do nothing?”

“We can keep ourselves afloat until daylight. After that we can search.”

The gale moderated shortly before dawn. At six o’clock visibility had improved to three miles, and the Atlas began running widening circles around the position at which the hawser had parted. Captain James calculated that during the night the Neptune should have drifted west-south-west from twelve to eighteen miles, but the change of wind introduced uncertainty, and the brig might have lain broadside to the sea or turned stern-first.

Fragments of lumber were plentiful. The tug stopped beside every large collection, because loose boards might indicate that the Neptune had capsized. They found a hatch-cover, two battens, and a broken oar, but none could be positively identified as belonging to the brig.

At noon the Atlas reached the western edge of the calculated area without seeing her. She then steamed north for twenty miles and searched eastward. The lookout remained continuously at the masthead. Albert took his turn with the glass, although his eyes soon ached from watching the grey horizon.

The weather cleared on the second day. This improved visibility but provided no trace of the wreck. Captain James sent up the Atlas’s wireless aerial, but the tug’s set was of limited range, and only two shore stations and a southbound steamer could be reached. A despatch describing the Neptune and requesting passing ships to keep watch was transmitted.

On the third day a tramp steamer reported having seen wreckage seventy miles east of Cape Charles. The Atlas proceeded to the position, losing nearly twelve hours, and found the water empty except for a patch of seaweed and an upturned boat which had plainly been afloat for months.

On the fourth day they spoke the schooner Annie Bell. Her captain had seen no derelict, though he had passed through the district during the night. On the fifth, a fog shut down, reducing visibility to less than a quarter of a mile. The Atlas steamed slowly, blowing her whistle every two minutes. The sound seemed to Albert like the cry of a blind creature wandering through an immeasurable room.

Captain James scarcely left the pilot-house. His first calculation had found the Neptune with remarkable precision; now every calculation failed. He drew drift-lines in blue and red pencil, corrected them for each alteration of wind, and compared them with the taffrail log. The brig, he said, could not have gone beyond a certain boundary; but when that boundary had been searched, he enlarged it.

“She has played me a mean trick,” he remarked on the sixth evening.

Albert understood that he referred to the brig, not the sea. Sailors frequently speak of vessels as though they possess intention, and after six days the Neptune did indeed appear a stubborn and mischievous wanderer, hiding herself while men spent coal and strength in pursuit.

The crew’s mood became grave. Miller was a quiet Philadelphian with a wife and infant son; Sharkey, whose proper name was Daniel Sharpe, had sailed in tugs since boyhood and was considered capable of meeting almost any ordinary danger. The danger aboard the Neptune, however, had ceased to be ordinary.

On the seventh day the Atlas searched southward. On the eighth she ran north-east nearly to the track of the New York steamers. Two ships altered course to speak her, but neither had news. On the ninth the chief engineer reported only twenty-seven tons of coal remaining.

“We can stay three days more if the weather holds,” he said, “but we shall not have enough to get home against a head wind.”

Captain James reduced the allowance to the galley and ordered all unnecessary steam shut off. The searchlight was no longer used by day, and speed was lowered to six knots.

On the morning of the tenth day a black squall came out of the west. The Atlas was then 190 miles from the capes. Her bunkers contained less than twenty tons, and the barometer was falling. Captain James stood over the chart for a long time.

“We are going home,” he said at last.

No one disputed the decision. A tug which exhausted her coal at sea would herself require assistance, and it was not sound seamanship to sacrifice twelve men because two might still be saved. Nevertheless, the order was bitter to Captain James. The Atlas had found her prize, secured it, and held it for more than a day; yet she would return without wreck, men, or salvage money, and with a coal bill exceeding nine hundred dollars.

The homeward passage occupied forty-one hours. Head seas delayed them during the first day, and the engineer burned pieces of broken dunnage with the coal. At Cape Henlopen they received newspapers from a pilot-boat, but none contained mention of the Neptune. Albert searched every shipping column.

The Atlas reached Philadelphia shortly after three o’clock in the afternoon of October 6. Her decks were salt-stained, one ventilator was missing, and the frayed end of the towing-hawser still hung over the stern. A small group waited on the wharf. Albert recognised Mr. Pope, but beside him stood two men in clean shore clothes.

They were Joe Miller and Sharkey.

For once Captain James’s composure deserted him. He stared from the pilot-house window, thrust his head out, and shouted:

“How in thunder did you get here?”

“By train from Boston,” Sharkey called back.

The crew crowded to the rail, cheering and asking questions. Miller laughed, while Sharkey displayed a newspaper parcel which he declared contained gifts for Old Martin. Before the lines had been made fast, both men were aboard.

Their explanation was simple, and its simplicity added to the irony of the Atlas’s ten-day search.

When the hawser parted, the Neptune fell off before the gale and drove westward. Miller and Sharkey had seen the tug’s searchlight twice, but they could not keep their lanterns burning in the rain, and their rockets were in the flooded cabin. They spent the night steering by tackles, expecting either to capsize or to be swept ashore.

At eight o’clock the next morning, less than twelve hours after the accident, the fruit steamer Santa Lucia, bound from Jamaica to Boston with bananas, sighted the brig. The sea was still too high to lower a boat safely, but the steamer stood by until eleven, when her second officer and four men went aboard.

The broken end of the Atlas’s hawser was still attached to the Neptune. Nearly sixty fathoms of it had trailed astern during the night without fouling the rudder. The Santa Lucia recovered the end, bent on a wire towing-pendant, and proceeded north at five knots. Three days later she brought the Neptune into Boston Harbor.

“And while you were towing comfortably to Boston,” said Captain James, “we were burning up the Atlantic looking for you.”

“Comfortably is not the word I should use,” Miller replied. “The cabin had two feet of water in it, and Sharkey ate the last of the bread.”

“It was going mouldy,” said Sharkey.

Old Martin pushed through the crowd.

“What have you got in that parcel?”

“Bananas.”

“Throw them overboard. They’ve made trouble enough.”

That evening the owners provided a dinner for the Atlas’s crew, and Captain James invited everyone to splice the main brace—a nautical expression which, in its original meaning, referred to repairing an important piece of rigging, but which had long been used for the serving of an extra allowance of spirits.

The commercial and legal questions remained to be settled. The owners of the Santa Lucia claimed salvage upon both vessel and cargo, contending that their steamer had found the Neptune adrift, placed men aboard, and towed her into port. Harlan & Pope replied that the brig had not been abandoned by the Atlas. Miller and Sharkey, members of the tug’s crew, had remained aboard throughout the gale, and the original hawser, though broken, was still secured to the wreck when the Santa Lucia arrived.

The distinction was important. Maritime possession does not depend solely upon an unbroken rope. Had Miller and Sharkey left the Neptune, the Santa Lucia might have claimed that she found an abandoned derelict; but because the two men remained in charge and intended to restore the tow as soon as possible, the brig continued legally in the Atlas’s possession. The fruit steamer had rendered valuable and perhaps indispensable assistance, but she had not made an independent capture.

The matter was referred to the United States District Court in Boston. After hearing the testimony of both crews, the court fixed the gross salvage award at $18,400. One-quarter, or $4,600, was given to the Santa Lucia and her people. The remaining $13,800 went to the owners, master, and crew of the Atlas, in proportions determined by their risk and service. Miller and Sharkey received additional shares because they had remained aboard the wreck during the gale.

The pine cargo, after allowance for 61,000 feet lost overboard or damaged by salt water, sold for $8,970. The Neptune herself was not worth repairing as a sea-going brig. Her broken spars were removed, her upper works cut down, and her hull strengthened sufficiently for service in smooth water. She was afterwards sold for $2,100 and converted into a harbor lighter, carrying coal and stone about Boston.

Thus the hurricane’s aimless wanderer ended her voyages within sight of wharves and warehouses. The Atlas cleared a little more than nine thousand dollars after paying for coal, provisions, tackle, repairs, and legal expenses—not a bad haul for a fortnight’s work, though less than Captain James had expected when he first placed his finger upon the shipping report.

As for Albert Shaw, he returned to the counting-room with a less romantic but more accurate understanding of salvage work. He had learned that finding a wreck might be easier than keeping it, that an eleven-inch hawser could part like thread, and that the soundest calculation was always subject to wind, current, and chance. Captain James had found the Neptune almost exactly where he said she would be; yet fortune, which takes no account of dead reckoning, had permitted a fruit steamer to finish the task.

Whenever the Santa Lucia afterwards entered Philadelphia with her yellow cargo, Old Martin declared that he could smell trouble before she rounded the bend. Captain James merely smiled.

He had received his share of the award, his men were alive, and the Neptune, after all her tricks, had been brought safely into port.

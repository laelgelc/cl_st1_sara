## HAPPY PETER

There are several ways of making a living in the West, and nearly as many ways of failing to make one. A man may raise cattle, provided the winter does not bury them; he may raise wheat, provided the sky remembers to rain; he may keep a saloon, provided other men continue to fail at cattle and wheat; or he may hunt gold, silver, copper, lead, and those rarer metals which nobody wants until somebody has found them. He may also survive by trusting no one, sleeping lightly, bargaining closely, and keeping his hand near his revolver. Another way of not surviving—perhaps the surest—is to die.

Happy Peter chose mining.

His name was not Happy, of course. His mother had christened him Peter Larrabee in a white meetinghouse in Vermont, where rain tapped the windows and the minister took so long over the rite that the baby fell asleep. The adjective came later, bestowed upon him by men who had never seen Vermont, never met his mother, and knew nothing of the promise which ruled his life.

They called him Happy because he bore ill luck without complaint.

A mule kicked him and broke two ribs. Peter said the animal had been frightened. A partner deserted him in Nevada with the provisions, the blankets, and the only sound burro. Peter said the man had probably received bad news from home. A shaft caved in and buried six months of labour. Peter climbed out, coughed the dust from his lungs, and began another shaft ten yards farther up the hill. He was cheated once, twice, three times of properties which made other men rich; yet, when the third cheat drove past him in a carriage behind matched bays, Peter lifted his battered hat.

“Howdy, Mr. Blount,” he called.

Blount looked the other way.

“There goes Happy Peter,” said the camp, and laughed with affection.

He was a little man, though not so little as he appeared, for his shoulders sloped and his head had the forward inclination of one accustomed to carrying packs uphill. His beard was sandy, his eyes pale blue, his boots habitually broken, and his hands—those hands which could distinguish a promising specimen by its weight in darkness—were broad, scarred, and shy. A curious word for hands, did I say? Yet shy they were. They hovered at the brim of his hat when strangers addressed him; they twisted together when a bargain was discussed; they retreated into his pockets when there was nothing honest for them to do.

Peter possessed the prospector’s eye.

That is a gift not to be confused with knowledge, though knowledge may polish it; nor with luck, though luck sometimes wears its coat. A mining engineer sees what ought to be in the mountain, having read books and taken measurements. A prospector sees what might be there, having walked until his feet bleed. One reasons from facts to conclusions; the other from a stain on the rock, a twist in the country, a handful of float, a gleam no broader than a pin. The engineer proves. The prospector believes. And now and then belief, which is frequently foolish in churches and always dangerous in business, becomes wisdom under the ground.

Peter could follow a lead where cleverer men lost it. He could look across a barren slope, scratched by sage and scorched by noon, and say, “Seems like she runs thar.” Afterward, when the shaft was sunk, the crosscut driven, the assays returned, and the wise men had finished explaining why the ore must necessarily be where it was, there it would be—precisely “thar.”

But he could not sell a mine.

The first mine was in Colorado. Peter had been in the country four years then, and Martha Bell had been waiting in Vermont four years.

Martha was not beautiful in the manner that causes men to turn in the road. She had brown hair, steady gray eyes, capable arms, and a way of listening which made foolish speech ashamed of itself. Her father owned thirty acres above the White River, an old farmhouse with lilacs under the windows, and a barn whose roof sagged like the back of a tired horse. Peter had worked there as a hired man. On Sunday afternoons he and Martha walked to a covered bridge where the water talked among stones.

When he determined to go West, he did not ask her to marry him. A hired man with twenty-seven dollars, two shirts, and no land may love honourably, but he cannot marry honourably—or so Peter believed.

“I’ll come back when I’ve made enough,” he told her.

“How much is enough?” Martha asked.

Peter had not considered this. “Enough for a place. An’ stock. An’ so you won’t have to—”

“To what?”

He looked at her hands, reddened by soap and cold water.

“To be forever washin’ and scrubbin’ and mendin’.”

Martha smiled a little. “Women do those things even in rich houses.”

“Not forever,” Peter insisted. “Not washin’, scrubbin’, mendin’; mendin’, scrubbin’, washin’. I want you to have things easy.”

“I don’t mind hard things, Peter.”

He mistook this simple truth for courage in parting, as men often mistake women when women speak clearly.

“You’ll wait?”

“I’ll wait.”

Thus the bargain was made. There were no witnesses except the river, which was moving east and could not be summoned later.

In Colorado, Peter found a lode that widened from eight inches at the surface to four feet in the lower working. He showed specimens to a man named Gault, who owned a general store, two stamp mills, half the town lots, and opinions upon every subject. Gault said the rock was “promisin’,” but that development required capital. Peter had no capital. Gault had capital. They formed a company, though Peter never quite understood that he had exchanged nine-tenths of his property for a promise to spend money upon it.

Gault spent seven hundred dollars, stopped work, declared the mine worthless, and offered Peter fifty dollars for his remaining interest.

“I wouldn’t like to sell if she’s worthless,” said Peter. “It don’t seem fair to you.”

Gault assured him that he was accustomed to risks.

Peter accepted the fifty dollars, and within eighteen months the mine had paid one hundred and forty thousand.

The second mine was in Arizona. It was copper, lying under a greenish blow-out in hills so dry that the lizards seemed to carry canteens. Peter located it, worked it through two summers, and sold half to a promoter named Scales. Scales wore white linen in the desert, possessed three watches and no visible conscience, and spoke of millions as another man might speak of beans.

He persuaded Peter that the claim had been improperly recorded.

“There’s a defect in the description,” he said. “A fatal defect. Anybody can jump her.”

Peter went pale. “Couldn’t we fix it?”

“We might. Courts cost money.”

“I ain’t got much.”

“That’s the trouble.”

Scales offered to assume the risk if Peter would sign several papers. Peter signed. The defect, had one existed, was cured by those papers; so also was Peter’s ownership. He emerged with four hundred dollars, a new hat, and gratitude toward Scales for having rescued him from litigation.

The copper property was afterward sold to an eastern syndicate for three hundred thousand dollars.

The third was silver in Montana. By then Peter had learned, as he thought, to distrust promoters. He would take no partner. He would sign no paper. He would sell only for cash. He guarded the location notice, recorded the claim himself, and kept every receipt in an oilskin pouch buttoned beneath his shirt.

But men who live by taking advantage of others are not discouraged because the door is locked; they merely inquire whether there is a window.

A banker named Blount loaned Peter money for machinery. The note was plain enough, but the mortgage was not, and Peter, who read every line, understood no more at the bottom than at the top. When a vein faulted, he missed an interest payment. Blount foreclosed. Two weeks later the new superintendent crosscut twelve feet through a horse of barren porphyry and struck the vein again.

That mine purchased the matched bays.

After each disaster Peter wrote to Martha. He did not tell her that he had been cheated, for that would have made him appear foolish; he wrote instead that the prospect had “not turned out according to expectation,” and that he was moving on to better country. Martha answered in a round hand which grew, with the years, a little sharper and less regular.

The farm was much the same. Her father’s rheumatism troubled him. The south pasture fence needed replacing. The McIntyre girl had married. The pear tree had borne heavily. Snow stood to the lower window sash. Spring had come early. Her father had died. She was keeping the place with the help of a boy from Sharon. The lilacs were blooming.

Always, at the end: I am waiting.

Peter kept her photograph in the oilskin pouch that had once guarded the Montana receipts. It showed a woman of twenty-three seated stiffly beside a table on which an artificial rose had been placed for purposes of beauty. The picture faded; the cardboard bent; the face remained young.

Years went by.

Years pass strangely in the mining country. In settled places they are divided by harvests, school terms, births, weddings, funerals, and the repainting of the front porch. In the hills one winter resembles another; one camp resembles the camp abandoned three months before; breakfast is bacon, dinner is bacon, supper is bacon, and a man wakes one morning to discover that ten years have elapsed while he was sharpening drills.

Peter went north into Wyoming.

He entered a region where the mountains rose in long gray backs above forests of pine, and the wind moved forever along the heights, combing the grass one way, then another. There were elk in the high meadows, trout under the cut banks, and, far below, a little town consisting of a store, a stable, two saloons, a blacksmith shop, and fourteen opinions as to where the next strike would be made.

The country had been prospected. That is to say, men had followed the creeks, examined the obvious outcrops, sunk shallow holes in convenient places, become discouraged, and pronounced it barren. Peter, who was not clever enough to accept this verdict, climbed where the others had not climbed.

He found colour in a wash below a ridge called Ragged Top. It was not much—three bright points in the pan—but the gravel told him a story. He followed the wash upward, testing tributaries, rejecting one, choosing another, until the colour disappeared under a fan of slide rock. Above the slide stood a wall of iron-stained quartz.

“Seems like,” said Peter, “she ought to be here.”

He staked four claims along the ridge: the Martha, the Homeward, the Green Mountain, and the Last Chance. Sentimental names are common in mining districts and generally signify nothing. A man who calls a claim after his wife may desert both within the month. In Peter’s case the names signified everything.

He built a cabin beside a spring. It had one room, a stone chimney, a bunk, a table, a cracked mirror, and shelves made from powder boxes. Through summer heat and autumn frost he worked. He stripped the ledge, sank on the Martha, and drove a tunnel into the Homeward. The vein pinched, widened, split, and vanished. He found it again. He followed stringers through granite hard enough to turn a drill. He worked alone because wages required money and partnership required trust.

Drilling, blasting, mucking; mucking, blasting, drilling.

In winter the snow roofed his cabin and made a white desert of the ridge. The pines bent, groaned, shook themselves free, and stood up darkly against the storm. Peter mended tools, sorted specimens, cooked beans, and wrote to Martha by the yellow light of a kerosene lamp.

In spring the snows withdrew reluctantly, pausing in hollows and under northward cliffs. Water shouted in the gulches. Tiny flowers looked out among the rocks with that imprudent confidence peculiar to flowers and prospectors. Peter resumed work.

The first assays were poor.

The second were better.

The third caused the assayer in town to test his chemicals.

Peter distrusted them. He took samples across the range to another office, dividing the ore himself and sealing each sack. The returns agreed. He cut channels at measured intervals, drove deeper, and sampled again.

Silver, lead, and gold; gold, lead, and silver. Five feet of ore, then six; five, six; five, six—and richer with depth.

Still he said nothing.

The West admires a loud success but relies upon a silent man to discover it. Peter kept silent for nearly two years, proving the vein on the Martha and the Homeward, then tracing it across the Green Mountain and Last Chance. He surveyed the lines. He checked his monuments. He reread the notices until the words floated before him. He paid an attorney in Cheyenne to examine the title, and when the attorney declared it sound, Peter asked him to examine it again.

At last there could be no doubt. The four claims were not merely good; they were immensely rich. Even allowing for pinches, faults, water, bad management, dishonest smelter returns, falling prices, rising freight, labour troubles, litigation, and all the other agencies by which Providence moderates a miner’s joy, they represented a fortune.

Peter sat alone in the tunnel with the assay sheets on his knee.

The candle flame leaned in a draught. Water ticked from the roof. The fresh face of the vein shone dully where his last blast had torn it open.

He did not shout. He did not dance. He folded the papers and said, “Well, Martha, I reckon that’s enough.”

That evening he took her photograph from the pouch and set it against the sugar bowl. The kettle purred on the stove; beans simmered; outside, the pines sang in a wind travelling eastward.

“I’ll sell ’em,” Peter told the young woman in the picture. “I won’t hold out for too much. Enough for the farm, an’ some hired help, an’ maybe a bathroom. Folks have bathrooms right in houses now. I seen one down Cheyenne.”

Martha, aged twenty-three, looked calmly past him.

Peter smiled. “You waited a tolerable spell.”

He went to the mirror to trim his beard.

The mirror was a poor one. It had lost silver at the edges, warped a man’s mouth, enlarged one eye, and gave the cabin behind him the uneasy appearance of a place under water. Peter leaned close, scissors lifted.

There, above his temple, was a patch of gray hair.

He lowered the scissors.

The discovery of gray hair is not usually dramatic. A man sees it, plucks it, deplores it, or pretends not to see. But Peter had been absent from Vermont twenty-six years. The gray hair was not one hair, nor ten. It lay in both temples. It threaded his beard. It shone in his eyebrows when he turned toward the lamp.

He stared at the mirror; then at the photograph; then again at the mirror.

The woman in the picture was twenty-three.

“How old—” he began.

He calculated. He lost the number and calculated again.

Martha was forty-nine.

It is easy to say that forty-nine is not old. We say it cheerfully when we are forty-nine, gallantly when another is forty-nine, and truthfully enough in a world where people live to eighty. Yet Peter did not see a healthy woman of forty-nine. He saw twenty-six years of washing, scrubbing, mending; mending, scrubbing, washing. He saw snow against the Vermont windows, spring mud in the yard, apples gathered, fences repaired, sickness endured alone. He saw birthdays on which she had listened for wheels in the road. He saw her father buried, neighbours married, children born and grown, while a young woman in a photograph remained young because Peter had commanded her to wait.

He put both hands on the table.

“I got to get home.”

Until that hour patience had been his strength. Now it became impossible. Yesterday he could have spent another year proving the Last Chance below the fault; tonight a week seemed theft. He must sell, collect the money, and go. Not next summer. Not when the railroad improved its rates. Now.

He went to town the following morning.

By noon the assayer knew. By one o’clock the storekeeper knew. At two, Peter showed specimens in the back room of the hotel to a mine broker, a newspaper correspondent, two ranchers, a drummer selling pumps, and a man who said nothing but examined the rock with a glass. At three the news reached both saloons. By sunset it had acquired three zeros, a free-gold streak four feet wide, and a personal endorsement from a senator who had never heard of it.

Peter’s caution did not wholly desert him. He wanted publicity because publicity brought buyers; yet publicity also brought men like Gault, Scales, and Blount. He told himself that this time the title was sound, the ore proved, the lines surveyed, the monuments set. He possessed maps, assays, receipts, opinions, certificates. Q.E.D. No one could cheat him.

Nevertheless, before leaving town he visited the recorder’s office.

“You looked at these descriptions last month,” said the recorder.

“I know. I was wonderin’ if you’d look again.”

“They’re regular.”

“Sure about the quarter section?”

“Sure.”

“An’ the Martha end line?”

“It closes.”

“An’ the notices was filed in time?”

The recorder pushed the papers toward him. “Peter, those claims are tied tighter’n a widow’s purse.”

Peter laughed apologetically. “I reckon I’m foolish.”

“You always was,” said the recorder, not unkindly.

The rush began next day.

Men came on horseback, in buckboards, on foot. They climbed the ridge, swarmed into the workings, chipped specimens, estimated widths, disputed values, and staked every patch of ground within five miles. Tents sprang up beside the spring. Axes rang. Campfires stained the twilight. Someone opened a saloon under canvas and named it the Gray Eagle, though there was no eagle and little gray about the liquor.

Peter had desired buyers. He had obtained a population.

At first he was pleased. He guided visitors through the tunnel, holding the lamp while they examined the face.

“Right thar she widens,” he would say. “I followed the footwall till—well, seems like I follered it nigh onto forty feet. This sample run two hundred an’ sixteen. That one was less. I wouldn’t want to mislead nobody.”

Experts arrived wearing field clothes too new for the field. They measured, sampled, whispered, and asked questions with deliberate carelessness. Promoters slapped Peter on the back. Lawyers requested copies of documents. Reporters described him as a grizzled pioneer, a hermit millionaire, a simple child of nature, and “Old Pete Larrabee,” though he had never been called Pete and had not yet thought himself old.

Offers came.

One syndicate proposed cash and stock. Peter refused the stock.

Another offered a large sum payable in instalments. Peter refused the instalments.

A third desired a ninety-day option.

“Ninety days!” Peter exclaimed. “I can’t wait ninety days.”

The agent smiled. “These transactions require time.”

“I give twenty-six years to this transaction.”

The agent supposed he meant the mine and concluded that age had confused him.

Every conversation ended with papers. Papers to be examined, papers to be drawn, papers to be signed. Peter read them until his eyes burned. Words ordinary in isolation became sinister in company: whereas, aforesaid, appurtenances, assigns, exceptions, reservations. He hired a lawyer named Judge Pennock, who had never been a judge but had once run for the office and retained the title as consolation.

“Don’t sign anything without me,” said Pennock.

“I won’t.”

“And don’t show anxiety. They’ll lower the price.”

“I ain’t anxious about price.”

“You’re in a strong position. Make them wait.”

Wait!

The word struck Peter like an insult.

At night he lay in his bunk and listened to the new camp. Voices drifted through the trees; horses stamped; a hammer rang long after dark. His claim notices stood outside under the stars. His corner monuments stood on the ridge. Men passed them continually.

Suppose one moved a monument?

Suppose the survey was wrong?

Suppose there had been an earlier location, forgotten and unrecorded?

Suppose the attorney in Cheyenne had conspired with Pennock?

Suppose Gault had heard? Scales? Blount?

He rose, dressed, and went out with a lantern.

The monuments were undisturbed.

He returned to bed.

A coyote cried across the valley. The camp answered with laughter. Peter sat upright. Was that laughter about him?

He went out again.

There is no terror so clamant as the terror one cannot prove unreasonable. Peter had been cheated three times. Therefore men cheated. Each fraud had begun with friendliness, advice, a legal phrase, a defect nobody mentioned until the proper moment. Therefore friendliness concealed fraud, advice concealed fraud, and defects waited in darkness like snakes under stones. His fears were not fantasies created from nothing. They were facts distorted by repetition and sleeplessness.

On the third night someone stole a pick from beside his cabin.

It was found next morning near the spring, where a visiting prospector had borrowed and forgotten it. But for four hours Peter searched the tunnels, convinced that the thief had taken specimens to salt another claim—or had exchanged his rich ore for barren rock—or had widened some crack which could be presented as an older working.

“You’re run down,” Pennock told him. “Come to town. Sleep at the hotel.”

“An’ leave them here?”

“Leave who?”

Peter looked toward the tents. “Them.”

He began patrolling the boundaries.

At dusk he walked from monument to monument: Martha northeast, Martha southeast, Homeward southwest, Green Mountain north centre, Last Chance end line. He touched each pile of stone. He checked the stakes. He read the notices. Then, and not until then, he ate. Then, and not until then, he lay down. Then, and not until then, he remembered a phrase in one description and rose to check it again.

Sleep came in scraps. Ten minutes, twenty; an hour, then none. The candle burned beside his bunk. Coffee replaced food. His beard grew ragged. His pale eyes acquired a fixed brightness that caused men to lower their voices when he approached.

Happy Peter was no longer happy, the camp said.

This was considered ingratitude.

Had he not made the biggest strike in Wyoming? Were buyers not offering more money than any sensible man could spend? Was not his luck the envy of every prospector who had worn out boots on barren hills? Men who had known him in adversity now resented his anxiety in success. Suffering is forgiven when it remains unsuccessful; fortune is expected to smile.

Spring moved into summer. The meadows turned gold beneath the sun. Resin scented the air. At noon the pines stood motionless, holding their shadows close; toward evening a breeze came down from the snowfields and laid invisible hands upon a man’s head. The nights were deep and serene. Stars burned above the ridge with such multitude and order that human documents, claim lines, mortgages, and deeds appeared briefly ridiculous.

But not to Peter.

A telegram came from Martha.

The reporter had sent her news of the discovery, and she had replied through the town office:

SO GLAD STOP COME HOME SOON STOP I AM WAITING MARTHA

Peter read it five times.

Come home soon.

He placed it beside the photograph and packed his trunk that night. Two shirts, extra boots, the oilskin pouch, Martha’s letters tied with cord. In the morning he unpacked. He could not leave before the sale. He packed again after supper. At midnight he unpacked to search for the original location certificates, which he had imagined missing.

They were in the pouch.

“Soon,” he whispered. “Soon as it’s safe.”

But safety retreated before him. If Pennock approved a buyer, perhaps Pennock had been bought. If an offer was high, there must be some hidden advantage to the purchaser. If low, it was an insult. If a man asked detailed questions, he planned a contest. If he asked none, he knew something already.

Men ceased making serious offers.

“Larrabee’s crazy,” they said.

“Holdin’ for a million.”

“Scared of his own shadow.”

“Somebody’ll jump him yet, foolin’ the way he does.”

That last sentence reached Peter.

He bought a revolver.

He had owned weapons before, as every man did, but he disliked carrying them. Now he wore the revolver low on his hip. Its weight reassured him for half an hour and burdened him thereafter. When strangers entered the tunnel, his right hand hovered near it—the shy hand, the gentle hand, the hand that knew ore by touch.

Pennock came up from town with a firm cash offer.

“It is less than the property may ultimately be worth,” the lawyer said, “but it is fair. They will deposit the purchase money in the bank. Title examination should take ten days.”

“Ten?”

“Possibly fourteen.”

Peter paced the cabin. “Why so long?”

“There are four claims, overlapping rights, water arrangements—”

“Overlapping?”

“Not overlapping claims. I mean related matters.”

“You said overlapping.”

“A manner of speech.”

Peter stopped. “What ain’t you tellin’ me?”

Pennock looked at him carefully. “Nothing.”

“Did they find a defect?”

“No.”

“You’d say if they did?”

“Of course.”

“Would you?”

The lawyer gathered his papers. “Get some sleep, Peter.”

“I can’t sleep till this is done.”

“And it cannot be done while you’re in this condition.”

So the sale waited upon Peter’s calm, and Peter’s calm waited upon the sale.

The end came on a clear morning in August.

A dozen men stood near the Martha discovery shaft, examining specimens from a fresh blast. Among them was Charlie Rusk, a young prospector from Idaho, red-haired, loud, and gifted with that unfortunate humour which mistakes another man’s pain for an invitation.

Rusk knew something of mining law, less than he believed and more than was good for conversation. He had heard that Peter guarded his boundaries by night. He had heard of the repeated visits to the recorder. He had seen Peter check the notices before breakfast. To Rusk, as to the camp, this was comedy.

Peter emerged from the cabin carrying a coffee cup. He had not slept. All night he had compared the typed copies of his claim descriptions with the originals, word by word, letter by letter. The morning light hurt his eyes.

Rusk lifted a location notice from a stone where Peter had placed it during the comparison.

“Say, Pete,” he called, “you got ‘northwesterly’ here, an’ your record says ‘northwest.’”

Peter set down the cup.

“What?”

“Right here. Northwest in one, northwesterly in t’other.”

The men glanced at Rusk. One grinned. Another said, “Quit your foolin’, Charlie.”

But Rusk had found his audience.

“Fatal defect,” he declared solemnly. “Whole location’s void.”

Peter crossed the clearing and took the paper. His hands shook so violently that it rattled.

“It don’t make no difference,” he said.

“Oh, don’t it? Description’s got to correspond.”

“The lawyer examined it.”

“Lawyers!” Rusk winked at the others. “They ain’t goin’ to tell a man his mine don’t belong to him, long as he’s payin’ fees.”

Peter looked from face to face. He saw the grin, the lowered eyes, the pretended seriousness. To a rested mind these would have exposed the joke. To Peter they proved conspiracy.

“You knowed this?” he asked Pennock, who had just ridden into camp and was tying his horse.

“Knew what?”

“This word.”

Pennock took the paper. “It’s immaterial.”

“That’s what he says it ain’t.”

Rusk laughed. “Immaterial till somebody relocates. I reckon I’ll jump her.”

“You won’t,” said Peter.

The men became quiet.

Rusk should have stopped. There are moments when even a fool hears warning in the air. Yet his companions were watching, and he had committed himself to being amusing.

“Why not?” he demanded. “Ground’s open. I’ll put up my notice right now. ‘I, Charles Rusk, do hereby locate—’”

“This is mine.”

“Not with that wording, she ain’t.”

“I found it. I worked it. Twenty-six years—”

“Twenty-six years don’t mend a bad location.”

Peter’s face had gone gray.

Pennock stepped forward. “Rusk, that’s enough. There is no defect. Peter, listen to me. The variation has no legal significance.”

But Peter no longer heard distinct voices. He heard Gault explaining capital. He heard Scales explaining the fatal defect. He heard Blount explaining the mortgage. He heard the river in Vermont moving east, Martha saying I’ll wait, the hammer in the camp after dark, the laughter beyond the pines. He saw gray hair in the mirror. He saw a woman of forty-nine standing at a farmhouse window while strangers moved his monuments and lawyers spoke of ten days, fourteen days, ninety days, more waiting, always waiting.

Rusk pulled a carpenter’s pencil from his pocket and bent theatrically over a scrap of paper.

“I’ll just write me a notice.”

Peter, without a word, shot the speaker through the heart.

The sound struck the ridge and travelled outward, from wall to wall, diminishing among the peaks.

Rusk fell across the stones. The pencil remained between his fingers.

For a moment no one moved. The pines whispered overhead; a jay screamed in the brush; far below, the new camp continued hammering at a roof.

Peter looked at the revolver as if it had appeared in his hand by sorcery.

“He was takin’ it,” he said.

Pennock removed the weapon gently.

“No, Peter.”

“He said he’d jump her.”

“He was joking.”

Peter stared at Rusk. The young man’s red hair stirred in the breeze.

“Jokin’?”

They took Peter to town. He went quietly, asking only that someone guard the claims until he returned. During the ride he turned repeatedly in the saddle to look back at Ragged Top. At the jail he gave the sheriff Martha’s photograph and telegram for safekeeping.

The trial occurred in November.

By then newspapers had transformed the story. Peter was “the Wyoming Mine Maniac,” “a miser crazed by sudden wealth,” and “the hermit gunman of Ragged Top.” Rusk was a popular young mining man slain during a friendly discussion. The four claims were appraised at a figure large enough to establish motive and repeated often enough to become fact.

The courtroom was warm and crowded. Snow pressed against the windows. Peter sat beside Pennock in a borrowed black coat. Without his revolver he seemed again the small, harmless man the camps had called Happy, though his hair was whiter than it had been in August.

Witness after witness described the morning.

Rusk had made no threatening movement.

Rusk carried no weapon in his hand.

Rusk had spoken jokingly.

Peter had fired without warning.

The prosecution asked whether there had been a quarrel. No. Whether Rusk had attacked him. No. Whether any legal defect existed. No. Whether the claims had been endangered. No.

Fact by fact, answer by answer, the case became simple.

Pennock attempted to explain the years behind the moment. He told of Gault, Scales, Blount; of the three properties lost; of the twenty-six years; of the woman waiting in Vermont; of insomnia, fear, obsession, and collapse. The judge permitted some of it and excluded more.

“We are trying an act,” the prosecutor said, “not a lifetime.”

That is the law’s necessity. A lifetime is cumbersome evidence. It cannot be labelled, passed to the jury, and entered as Exhibit A. It contains too many irrelevant winters, too many private hopes, too many nights in which nothing visible occurs. The law asks: What happened at nine o’clock on the morning of August seventeenth? It cannot easily ask what had been happening since a Sunday afternoon beside a Vermont river.

A physician testified that Peter knew right from wrong.

Peter himself took the stand. He twisted his hands and looked toward the windows.

“Did Charles Rusk threaten to kill you?” asked the prosecutor.

“No, sir.”

“Did he strike you?”

“No, sir.”

“Did he attempt to seize your papers?”

“No, sir.”

“Yet you shot him.”

“He said he was goin’ to take the Martha.”

“Was he armed?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did you see a weapon?”

“No, sir.”

“What did you see?”

Peter was silent a long time.

How could he answer? He had seen a storekeeper’s back room in Colorado, white linen in an Arizona desert, matched bays passing on a Montana road. He had seen claim stakes falling in darkness, papers changing their words, gray spreading through a beard. He had seen twenty-six years close like the walls of a shaft. But none of these things had stood before him in the clearing. There had been only a red-haired young man, a scrap of paper, and a carpenter’s pencil.

“I seen him takin’ it,” Peter said.

The prosecutor faced the jury.

“You heard the witnesses. You heard the defendant. There was no assault, no danger, no dispute even—only a jest. The victim was unarmed. The defendant fired deliberately and without warning. Whatever strange ideas he may have entertained, the facts admit of no confusion.”

The jury retired.

Peter leaned toward Pennock. “If they let me go, can the sale still be made?”

Pennock looked away.

“Miss Bell’ll be wonderin’,” Peter added.

Outside, snow fell over the town, softening the stable roofs, whitening the road, covering the ruts of wagons and the tracks of men. Far away in Vermont, snow would also be falling upon the lilacs, the sagging barn, the covered bridge, and a farmhouse window where a woman had waited so long that waiting had ceased to be an act and become the shape of her life.

The jury returned before supper.

Peter Larrabee was found guilty of the most brutal and unprovoked murder in the history of the county.

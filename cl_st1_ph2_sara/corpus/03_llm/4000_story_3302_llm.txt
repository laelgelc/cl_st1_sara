The hut stood on a shelf of black rock above the frozen inlet, eight paces long by five broad, with walls of double planking packed between with sailcloth, moss, and snow. Its roof had been made from the quarter-deck of the lost *Mercy Bell*, and retained, in places, the green paint and brass screw-holes of that more fortunate elevation. A square trap in the roof admitted stores and, when the weather allowed, air. A second trap, cut low in the eastern wall, served as a door. Both were fitted with latches fashioned by the carpenter from the ship’s broken skylight.

The lower latch was of brass. Long Ede polished it every Saturday.

There were six hammocks, three to a side, and a seventh hanging empty near the fireplace. That had been Bill Ash’s. Bill had died in October, while the sea was still moving beyond the inlet and before they understood that no boat could reach them until summer. They had sewn him in canvas, weighted him with furnace-bars, and lowered him through a fault in the young ice. The fault closed that night. His hammock remained because the Gaffer disliked changes which had not been ordered.

“Room ain’t victuals,” he had said when Cooney proposed cutting it down. “Nor yet fuel. Let it bide.”

So it had bided.

The six living men were Amos Pike, master of the *Mercy Bell* and therefore the Gaffer; Long Ede, an Essex harpooner whose height made the roof seem lower whenever he stood; Martin Cooney of Waterford, boat-steerer and occasional optimist; Jem Lashman, a sailmaker from Wapping; Peter Rusk, called the Snipe because of his nose and his habit of standing on one leg while thinking; and Duncan Veitch, an Orkneyman who had served as cook until scurvy loosened his teeth.

Their stores, when reduced to figures, appeared almost comfortable: forty-three tins of preserved beef; eighteen of mutton; ship’s biscuit enough for eleven weeks at full ration and perhaps twenty at the Gaffer’s; two sacks of pea-flour; nine pounds of coffee; three of tea; salt, lime-juice, candles, tobacco, medicines, powder, shot, and four jars of something labelled DAMSON, though no man had yet found a damson in them. There was also blubber, if they could kill seals, and wreck-wood enough for the stove until March, unless February proved immoderate.

But lists do not take account of darkness.

By the middle of November the sun had gone. At noon there remained a violet thinning of the south, like the memory of daylight seen through closed eyelids; then that also failed, and the stars wheeled continually above the hut. Moonlight came and went. Snow travelled over the rock with a dry whisper. The inlet tightened, cracked, boomed, and settled. Sometimes the sound of the ice resembled cannon heard from beyond a hill; sometimes a groan beneath the floor made each man look up, though no one spoke.

Inside there were heat, smoke, six breaths, wet mittens, rancid grease, and the sourness of men who could no longer wash. The stove glowed red on mild nights and dull crimson on cruel ones. Above shoulder-height the smoke made a blue ceiling. Below it the men played cards, mended clothes, quarrelled, read, slept, woke, and listened.

Their books numbered five. The Gaffer owned *Paradise Lost*, a navigational almanac six years out of date, and a swollen volume called *Letters of a Turkish Spy*. Cooney had a song-book without its cover. Long Ede possessed a Bible, small enough for a coat pocket and printed in type so fine that the Snipe said it had been made to save sinners by blinding them.

At first Ede read aloud on Sundays. He preferred the histories, which gave the men fighting, storms, voyages, murders, and kings who seldom survived their errors. But as the dark deepened he turned, for reasons he could not explain, to the Song of Solomon.

“Take us the foxes,” he read one evening, bending the little book towards the lamp, “the little foxes, that spoil the vines: for our vines have tender grapes.”

Cooney, sorting a hand of greasy cards, looked round.

“What vines?”

“Vines,” said Ede.

“Here?”

“It don’t say here.”

“Then why trouble us with ’em?”

“Because it’s Scripture.”

“That ain’t an answer.”

“It is commonly allowed to be,” said the Gaffer, without lifting his eyes from the Turkish Spy.

They played on. Yet the phrase remained. The little foxes. It fitted the faint scratchings that came from beneath the floor; it fitted the pale Arctic foxes that sometimes watched the refuse heap; it fitted, too, the small thoughts which entered a man’s head and spoiled what little peace had survived there.

In December the foxes began their proper work.

Lashman first declared that somebody was stealing his biscuit. The Gaffer weighed each ration in his presence and showed that no theft was possible. Lashman then alleged that the scales were false. Cooney laughed, and Lashman threw a pannikin at him. It struck the wall, scattering coffee grounds over Bill’s empty hammock.

“Pick ’em out,” said the Gaffer.

Lashman stared.

“You fouled that berth. Pick ’em out.”

“He’s dead.”

“The berth ain’t.”

Lashman removed the grounds one by one.

After that he complained continually: of his gums, his knees, the smoke, the Gaffer’s arithmetic, the stove’s appetite, Cooney’s singing, Rusk’s nose, and Veitch’s habit of saying “Aye” when he meant neither yes nor no. His body diminished while his grievances grew. His fingers became glossy at the joints. Bruises spread over his shins without cause.

Veitch was worse in body and better in temper. He lost two teeth, wrapped them in paper, and put them in his sea-chest in case, as he said, they might be wanted. The Snipe developed a cough that came in careful groups of three. Cooney, strongest of them, would sit opening and closing his hands as if uncertain whether he meant to pray or strike somebody. The Gaffer remained calm, which the others sometimes resented more than fear.

Only Long Ede continued useful in the old fashion. He cut fuel, adjusted the ventilator, cleaned the guns, inspected the roof lashings, and kept the brass latch bright. His reliability was so established that when he began to doubt himself, he concealed the doubt as he might have concealed damage below the waterline.

He had begun hearing running water.

Not ice. Not the drip beside the stove. This water ran over stones in sunlight. He heard it when the hut was quietest, usually near three in the morning, and with it there came the smell of wet earth, crushed mint, and flowers warming against a wall. Once he woke certain that hawthorn branches were brushing the roof.

He sat up. The air was thick with smoke. Cooney snored below him. Beyond the stove, Bill’s hammock hung empty and still.

“The little foxes,” Ede whispered.

Then he bit his knuckle until the scent of spring departed.

January arrived without announcement. The almanac said it had arrived; the sky showed no preference. They marked the days by notches on a beam, though two notches had been disputed and one erased. On Sundays the Gaffer held prayers. His theology was severe, practical, and not entirely hospitable.

“We are preserved,” he told them, “according to Providence, discipline, and the proper reduction of rations. Providence comes first, being above discipline in station, though I’ll observe it don’t commonly act against an empty flour-bin.”

“Amen,” said Veitch.

“You wait till I’ve done.”

“Aye.”

The prayers steadied them, if only because they required clean faces and silence. Each man stood by his hammock. Bill’s place remained empty. The Gaffer read a psalm, Ede read a chapter, and Cooney—who claimed no particular friendship with Heaven—sang whatever tune he could remember. Once he fitted “O God, Our Help in Ages Past” to the melody of a comic song concerning a red-haired widow. The Gaffer endured half a verse.

“Different tune.”

“Sure, He’ll know the words.”

“Different tune.”

By late January they no longer laughed. The dark had become an element inside the hut, entering through seams, settling in the corners, and waiting behind a man’s eyes when he shut them. Their speech shortened. Their thoughts lengthened. A dropped spoon could provoke an oath; a sigh could be interpreted as an insult. They guarded their possessions—needles, tobacco crumbs, scraps of pencil—with the zeal of merchants. Yet they also tended one another with a rough delicacy. Cooney rubbed Veitch’s legs. Lashman, complaining all the while, patched the Snipe’s mittens. The Gaffer divided the last spoonful of damson among them and pretended not to notice that his own share had been transferred to Ede.

It was during the night of February the second that the latch lifted.

All six were awake, though only Ede admitted it afterward.

The stove had burned low. A layer of ash slipped inward, so softly that in the surrounding silence they could almost hear each grey flake settle. The lamp was out. Moonlight entered by the roof trap and lay across the floor in a narrow bar.

Then came a small metallic sound.

Tick.

Ede opened his eyes.

The brass latch stood at the far end of the hut, six paces away. He could not see it from his hammock, because Cooney’s boots hung between; but he knew the sound. He had filed the catch himself. He knew how it clicked when lifted a quarter-inch, how it scraped if a mitten touched it sideways, and how it dropped with a double note because the door had warped in the frost.

Tick.

Cooney’s snoring ceased.

No one moved.

The latch rose slowly. Its shadow, cast upward by the embers, lengthened on the wall.

It stopped.

“Wind,” said Lashman from the dark.

“There’s no wind,” the Snipe whispered.

“Then snow.”

“Snow don’t lift brass.”

“Shut your mouth.”

They listened.

The hut seemed to contract about them. Breath tightened. Somewhere beneath the floor a grain of ice cracked with microscopic violence.

The latch lifted another inch.

Cooney sat up. “Who’s there?”

The latch dropped.

Click-click.

Nobody answered.

The Gaffer swung from his hammock and landed in his stockings. “Lamp.”

Veitch struck a match. It failed. The second flared, showing six faces turned towards the door and, beyond them, the polished brass rectangle of the latch. The lamp caught. Smoke rose.

“Gun,” said the Gaffer.

Ede had it down before the word was finished. They waited while Cooney dragged on his boots and the Snipe took the axe. Lashman stayed in his hammock, muttering that it was an animal, only an animal, and that opening the door would freeze them all for the sake of a fox.

“The little foxes,” Ede said.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

The Gaffer examined the door. Frost feathered its edges, but the latch was properly seated.

“A bear might nose it,” Cooney said.

“A bear knocks,” said Veitch. “A bear doesna pick locks.”

“Maybe a man,” Lashman whispered.

No one rebuked him.

A man was less reasonable than a bear. No expedition lay within three hundred miles. Their own boats were splinters beneath the snow. Bill lay under five feet of ice. Yet each of them looked, for one instant, towards the empty hammock.

The Gaffer noticed and scowled.

“Ede, roof. Take a line.”

There were two ways to inspect the door from outside. One was to open it. The other was to climb through the roof trap, cross the sloping planks, and descend by the snowbank against the western wall. The second lost less heat.

Ede dressed. Two shirts, jerseys, sealskin trousers, stockings, boots, scarf, cap, mittens. Cooney tied the line round his waist.

“Give three pulls if aught’s there,” Cooney said.

“If there’s aught, I’ll likely give more.”

“Don’t be smart.”

“I ain’t.”

They lifted the roof trap. Cold poured down, pure and enormous. The lamp flame flattened. Ede climbed into the moonlight, and the trap shut beneath him.

For several seconds he crouched on the roof, blinking.

The world outside was so bright that it seemed to possess its own transparent day. Moonlight glazed the inlet; the pressure ridges stood in long blue shadows; every nail-head in the roof glittered with frost. The air entered his lungs like broken glass, yet after the smoke below it tasted clean.

He listened.

Nothing.

No wind. No animal. No footfall. The silence was not absence but weight, laid equally over sea, rock, hut, and man. Far to the north an aurora trembled, pale as breath upon a mirror.

Ede crossed the roof. Halfway to the western edge he saw a mark in the frost.

He bent over it.

Five toes. The ball of a foot. A narrow heel.

A bare human footprint.

It was filled with blood.

He remained crouched, one mitten against the planking. The line at his waist trembled as Cooney shifted below. Ede looked ahead.

Another print lay near the chimney, and another beyond it. They came from the north edge of the roof, where no ladder stood and the wall dropped twelve feet to rock. The prints crossed behind the chimney, passed the trap, and went down the western slope.

Bare feet. Right, left, right.

Each print held a dark red smear beneath a skin of freezing brightness. Blood had run from the heel of one and made three beads like berries.

Ede’s first thought was not terror but calculation. The foot was smaller than Cooney’s, larger than the Snipe’s. The stride was short. Whoever had crossed the roof had walked slowly.

His second thought was of Bill.

His third was that the men below must never see.

Lashman would scream. Cooney would rush outside. Veitch, weakened and devout, might kneel in the snow until he froze. The Gaffer would ask for measurements and explanations, but his eyes would remember what his mouth denied. Six men could endure hunger, cold, and darkness. Six men, perhaps, could not endure arithmetic amended by a bloody foot.

Ede crawled forward and swept his mitten over the first print.

The frost broke. The blood smeared into the grain of the plank. He scraped snow from beside the chimney and pressed it down. He covered the second, then the third. The line tugged once.

He gave one pull in reply.

At the roof edge the tracks descended the snowbank. Ede followed. There were seven visible prints before the drift met the door. The last stood directly below the latch.

Beyond it, none.

He stared at that final mark. The toes pointed towards the hut.

Something had come down from the roof. Something with bleeding, naked feet. It had crossed the snow to the door, lifted the latch, and—

And what?

Failed to enter? Entered? Returned?

Ede turned towards the moonlit inlet. For one moment the white distance altered. Snow became flowering grass. The pressure ridge was a hedge thick with briar. Water ran over brown stones; bees worked among purple bells; warm earth breathed beneath his boots. A figure stood by the imagined brook, not facing him, and its hair shone in sunlight.

“Take us the foxes,” Ede said.

The figure turned.

The vision collapsed.

He was kneeling in snow with one mitten off. His bare hand pressed into the bloody footprint. The cold had already gone beyond pain.

He snatched up snow and scrubbed. Red faded to pink, pink to grey. He worked at the mark until the surface was trampled past recognition. Then he began covering the others, crawling backwards up the bank, but his limbs had become distant machinery and would not obey together.

The line pulled twice. Then three times.

“I’m coming,” he called, though his voice made no sound that he could hear.

He reached the roof and dragged himself to the trap. His mitten lay somewhere below. His right hand was white. The running water had returned, louder now, and with it the smell of roses, apples, spices, and sun-warmed hay. He thought the entire frozen world was only a door, and somebody on the other side had raised the latch.

The trap would not open.

He beat upon it with his fist. Or believed he did.

Then he was at the lower door. He had no memory of descending. The brass latch shone before his face. He lifted it.

Warmth touched him.

That was all.

Inside, they heard the latch rise for the second time.

Cooney seized the axe. The Snipe took the gun. The Gaffer held up one hand.

“Listen.”

Something struck the door softly.

“Ede?” Cooney called.

No answer.

They opened it. Long Ede fell inward on his knees and then sideways, bringing a slab of snow with him. His scarf was frozen to his mouth. One hand was bare, and there was blood under the nails.

For an hour they worked over him. They stripped him, rubbed his limbs with snow, wrapped him in blankets, and forced warm coffee between his teeth. The Gaffer forbade them to place him near the stove, though Lashman protested that he would die cold.

“He’ll die quicker hot,” said the Gaffer. “Cooney, keep rubbing. Rusk, shut that ventilator a thumb. Veitch, coffee. Lashman—”

“What?”

“Be quiet.”

Ede’s heart fluttered and stopped and fluttered again. At last he groaned.

“Praise God,” Veitch said.

“Presently,” said the Gaffer. “Let him breathe first.”

Ede opened his eyes. The six men—or what he saw as six men—leaned over him. Behind them stood another.

This seventh was indistinct because of the smoke. Ede could make out no face, only a tall, quiet shape near Bill’s hammock, with bare feet below the hem of some pale garment. The others did not look towards it.

He counted.

The Gaffer. Cooney. Lashman. Veitch. The Snipe.

Five.

He himself made six.

And the figure by the hammock made seven.

“Seven,” Ede whispered.

“What’s that, boy?” asked the Gaffer.

“Seven of us.”

“Six.”

Ede counted again. “Seven.”

Cooney glanced over his shoulder. The empty hammock moved slightly in the heat from the stove.

“He’s wandered,” Lashman said. “I told you. He’s took it in the head.”

“Shut up,” said Cooney.

The Gaffer leaned close. “Ede, hear me. There are six men in this hut.”

“Aye.”

“Name ’em.”

Ede named them. When he had finished, he added, “And him.”

“Who?”

Ede looked towards Bill’s berth.

The seventh figure was gone.

He began to laugh—not loudly, but with such exhaustion that Cooney gripped his shoulder.

“Don’t,” Cooney said. “Don’t do that, mate.”

“The little foxes,” Ede murmured. “Mind the little foxes.”

They put him into the Gaffer’s hammock, being nearest the stove, and the Gaffer slept on the floor. No one asked what Ede had seen outside until morning, and by morning he had determined to say nothing.

“Bear,” he told them.

“Tracks?” asked the Gaffer.

“Snowed over.”

“There was no snow,” said the Snipe.

“Drifted, then.”

“No wind,” Lashman said.

Ede looked at him. “Bear.”

The Gaffer studied Ede’s raw hand and said no more.

That Sunday they prayed. It was Veitch’s proposal, though the Gaffer accepted it as if it had been entered beforehand in the ship’s articles. They stood in their accustomed places. Ede, still weak, sat wrapped in blankets. The lamp burned poorly.

The Gaffer read: “Whoso dwelleth under the defence of the Most High shall abide under the shadow of the Almighty.”

Ede looked round and counted.

One, the Gaffer.

Two, Cooney.

Three, Lashman.

Four, Veitch.

Five, the Snipe.

Six, himself.

Seven—

Someone stood by Bill’s hammock with head bowed.

Ede shut his eyes. The Gaffer’s voice continued, firm and dry. When Ede looked again, the space was empty.

Yet during the prayer that followed, another voice entered among theirs.

It did not rise above them. It was not even possible to say that it had a separate sound. Rather, their six voices seemed suddenly supported, as a weak roof may be supported by an unseen beam. Cooney heard it and ceased fidgeting. Veitch opened his eyes. The Snipe’s cough stopped halfway to his lips. Lashman, who had complained of every earthly and several heavenly arrangements, began to weep without noise.

The Gaffer prayed on.

Afterward no one spoke of the seventh voice. Men who have survived at sea possess a useful understanding of what must not be entered in a log.

From that morning the hut changed.

The cold did not lessen. Their stores did not multiply. No angel repaired the ventilator or put flesh upon Veitch’s gums. Nevertheless, the pressure within them eased. Cooney sang while cutting fuel and, when corrected for his choice of song, chose another without offence. Lashman still complained, but now complained chiefly of the inconvenience of dying when so much work remained. The Snipe began carving a little boat from wreck-wood. Veitch’s appetite returned.

Long Ede recovered slowly. His hand blistered, blackened at two fingertips, and healed. He no longer smelled flowers in the night. Once or twice he heard running water, but it seemed farther away.

He continued to count.

At meals: seven bowls in his first glance, six in his second.

At cards: seven shadows cast by six bodies.

At prayers: seven persons, though the seventh never retained a face.

He did not know whether this comforted him. Madness was not made wholesome merely by standing reverently. Yet he had seen blood freeze in the shape of human feet. He had felt a presence at the threshold. Either the world contained more things than the Gaffer admitted, or Long Ede’s mind had cracked neatly enough to let hope through.

In March the paraffin failed. They burned seal oil and endured the smell. In April the pea-flour developed worms, which the Gaffer classified as additional nourishment. The men disputed this but ate it. The south grew gradually pale at noon, though clouds often concealed the change.

Ede had left his outer sleeping-bag on the roof the night of the latch. It had frozen into the snow beside the chimney and could not be moved without tearing. As the weather softened by degrees, he thought of it more often.

“Fetch her down,” he told Cooney one morning. “Put her in Bill’s berth.”

Cooney looked up from the cards. “Why there?”

“Dry place.”

“There’s other places.”

“Put her there.”

The Gaffer lowered the Turkish Spy. “Any reason, Ede?”

“A reason ain’t always an explanation.”

“That’s theology,” said the Gaffer, “and bad theology at that.”

But he nodded to Cooney.

Cooney climbed through the roof trap, taking a knife to free the bag. For several minutes they heard him scraping. Then silence.

The Snipe looked up. “He’s stopped.”

“Cooney!” called Lashman.

No answer.

The Gaffer rose.

A sound came from above: not alarm, not pain, but a short, broken cry such as a man makes when struck by good news beyond his capacity.

The trap flew open.

Cooney’s face appeared, streaming with tears.

“The sun,” he said.

Nobody moved.

“The sun, ye fools! The blessed sun!”

They climbed out as they were—without coats properly fastened, without mittens, Veitch with one boot unlaced. The Gaffer protested until his own head cleared the trap, and then he too was silent.

On the southern horizon, between two banks of cloud, lay a narrow blade of fire.

It was no larger than the brass latch and could have warmed nothing. Yet its light touched the pressure ridges, the chimney smoke, the black rocks, and six ruined faces lifted towards it. Colour returned to the world. The snow was no longer merely white but rose, gold, blue, and violet. The smoke cast a shadow. Their own bodies cast shadows.

Long Ede counted them.

One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six.

Then, beside the western wall, a seventh shadow stretched briefly over the snow.

A cloud crossed the sun. When the light returned, there were six.

Ede said nothing.

They stood until the Gaffer ordered them below for fear of frostbite. Cooney carried the stiff sleeping-bag and laid it in Bill’s hammock. That night no one suggested removing it.

With the returning sun came labour. They hunted seals, cleared the roof, repaired the signal mast, and dug out what remained of the boat. The improvement in their health seemed out of proportion to their food, though the seal meat checked the scurvy and the light did what medicine could not. Veitch’s gums hardened. The Snipe’s cough diminished. Lashman became so energetic in his complaints that the Gaffer declared him out of danger.

By May they could walk miles over the inlet. By June water shone between the floes, and birds returned, crying over the black leads. Their cries sounded indecently loud after the winter.

On the fourteenth of June the Snipe saw smoke.

Three whalers entered the outer water under shortened sail, feeling their way among rotten ice. The first boat that reached the shore found six men standing beneath a ragged ensign, shaved, washed, and arranged by rank because the Gaffer would not be rescued untidily.

They were taken aboard the *North Star*. The surgeon examined them twice and said he had expected corpses. The fleet’s captains asked about the wreck, the stores, the winter, the death of Bill Ash, and the condition of the ice. All these things the Gaffer reported with precision.

He did not report the latch.

Weeks later, when the hut was already a dark mark behind them and green water ran along the ship’s sides, Ede found the Gaffer alone by the taffrail. The evening smelled of tar, salt, and distant land. Gulls followed the wake.

“You never asked what I saw,” Ede said.

The Gaffer folded his arms. “I asked. You said a bear.”

“It weren’t.”

“No.”

Ede described the footprints: the naked toes, the blood, the path over the roof, the final mark before the door. He confessed to wiping them away. Then he spoke of the seventh figure and the extra voice at prayer.

The Gaffer heard him without interruption.

“You think me mad?” Ede asked.

“I think all men mad in particulars. Some conceal it by agreeing with their neighbours.”

“There were seven of us after that night.”

“There were six.”

“I counted seven.”

“You’d been frozen.”

“Cooney heard the voice.”

“Cooney hears tunes where none exist.”

“The others mended from that day.”

“They had seal meat.”

“We had none for a fortnight after.”

The Gaffer looked astern. The wake shone white beneath the lowering sun.

“What d’you make of it, then?” Ede persisted.

The Gaffer took time to answer. He had never liked being hurried into metaphysics.

“I make this much,” he said. “Something lifted the latch. You went out. Something brought you back to the door, for you’d no business finding it in your state. Afterward the men improved. These are facts, or near enough.”

“And the feet?”

“May be fact.”

“The seventh?”

“May be fever.”

“Providence?”

“May employ fever.”

Ede considered this. “I reckon we were presairved.”

“So do I.”

“All six?”

The Gaffer’s mouth shifted slightly.

“Well,” he said, “perhaps you were preserved, Ede. The rest of us may have come through incidentally.”

Long Ede laughed. The Gaffer allowed himself half a smile, then turned serious again as the sun entered a bank of cloud.

Far behind them, in a hut already filling with summer damp, seven hammocks stirred in the air from the open roof trap. One held a weather-stiffened sleeping-bag. The polished brass latch on the lower door remained dropped in its catch.

Outside, upon the last patch of shadowed snow, the meltwater gathered briefly in the shape of a naked foot, brightened, and ran away.

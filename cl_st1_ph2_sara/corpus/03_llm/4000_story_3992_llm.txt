# Constable Plimmer’s Peaceful Beat

There are districts of London which shout for the policeman as naturally as a baby shouts for its bottle. Whitechapel, for example, has always understood that the constable is not merely an ornament in blue, but a vital ingredient of civic life. It receives him with bottles, boots, cries of “Murder!” and other little attentions calculated to prevent his profession from becoming monotonous.

Battersea, on the other hand, particularly that portion of Battersea where the tall residential flats stand shoulder to shoulder along broad, respectable roads, regards the policeman as part of the landscape. He is there, like the pillar-box and the plane tree, and no one thinks of making special use of him. The cliff-dwellers in these towering blocks may assault and batter pianos, steal nothing but one another’s ideas, and murder nobody except Chopin and Beethoven. But they seldom commit Crime in its coarser forms.

To Police Constable Edward Plimmer, transferred thither after five active years in the East End, the peace of the neighbourhood was at first delightful.

He had come from a beat where Life was a series of blunt instruments. On his first evening there, a chair had descended upon him from an upper window; on his second, he had removed three intoxicated sailors from a fried-fish shop; and on his third, a lady in a pink hat had bitten him in the wrist. It had been stimulating, but wearing.

Battersea soothed him. Its cats slept on area steps. Its children played games which, though noisy, were not indictable. Its citizens passed him without attempting to deprive him of his helmet. He began to feel that civilization, while possibly overrated, had its points.

After a month, he became restless.

Nature, having manufactured Edward Plimmer in a large, solid mould, had plainly intended him for action. She had given him shoulders broad enough to inconvenience passers-by, feet of exceptional authority, a red face, and a nose which had been broken in the execution of his duty and had never forgiven the world. In repose he resembled a gorilla trying to remember where it had put something. In motion he suggested a wardrobe on its way downstairs.

Such a man cannot live indefinitely upon cats and pillar-boxes.

He longed for the old heroic evenings: the whistle in the night, the patter of fleeing feet, the crash of glass, and the subsequent pleasure of writing the whole affair down in language beginning, “At approximately nine-forty-five p.m., while proceeding in a northerly direction—”

But no whistle came.

Instead came Alf Brooks.

And with Alf Brooks came Jealousy.

* * *

Every morning at half-past seven, before Art in the flats had risen to assault its piano, a bright green milk-float entered Merivale Road. Upon it stood Alf Brooks, guiding his horse between the cliffs of brick with the careless grace of Apollo steering the chariot of the sun.

There were differences, of course. Apollo, so far as our information goes, did not wear a pink tie, nor did he perfume his hair with something which could be detected at a hundred yards in a following wind. Nor is there any reason to suppose that the divine chariot rattled with quart cans and was decorated with the name of the Battersea Hygienic Dairy Company. Nevertheless, the effect was regal.

Alf was a handsome young man, and knew it. He had curly brown hair, blue eyes, a neat moustache, white teeth, a narrow waist, and that gift of conversation which its possessors call the gift of the gab and its victims call something else. Cooks, housemaids, nursemaids, and young married women leaning from windows all agreed that he was a proper caution.

He had a word for everybody.

For Mrs. Larkins at Number Six he had sympathy concerning the baby’s teeth. For the housemaid at Number Eight he had a remark about the weather which, though meteorologically unsound, made her giggle. For the cook at Number Twelve he had songs.

The cook at Number Twelve was Ellen Brown.

At half-past seven precisely Ellen would appear at the basement entrance in a print dress and white apron, with a curl or two escaping from beneath her cap. She was a compact, bright-eyed girl, with a firm chin and that air of having already won three arguments before breakfast which belongs to the best type of London cook.

Alf would leap from his float.

“Morning, Nell.”

“Morning.”

“Sun’s come out special to see you.”

“Then it’s got precious little to do.”

“What sort of a night did you have?”

“Same as other nights. Went to sleep and woke up.”

“There’s romance for you!”

“Don’t you go dropping that can on my clean step.”

This exchange, repeated with slight variations, was their balcony scene. Romeo had milk on his boots; Juliet smelled faintly of bacon; the lark was represented by a horse coughing into its nosebag. Yet Love, who has never been particular about properties, hovered near.

Constable Plimmer knew this because he had the misfortune to patrol Merivale Road at half-past seven.

He would approach with the measured tread of the Law. He would see Alf leaning elegantly against the railings and Ellen looking up at him. He would hear laughter. Then something would occur inside his chest like the eruption of a volcano which has been officially ordered not to erupt while on duty.

He would pass them rigidly.

“Morning, constable,” Alf would say.

Plimmer would grunt.

“Beautiful weather,” Alf would continue.

Plimmer would regard the sky as if contemplating its arrest.

Ellen would hide a smile.

This was the wound. Alf’s charm he might have endured. Ellen’s amusement made the iron enter his soul and turn round twice.

For Edward Plimmer loved Ellen Brown.

He had loved her for four months, three weeks, and a number of days which varied according to whether one counted the Sunday when she had told him his face would frighten a burglar out of his senses. Plimmer counted it. Indeed, he counted everything: every word she addressed to him, every glance, every occasion on which she asked him the time and did not immediately walk away.

He had first encountered her while she was carrying a basket of eggs. A boy on a bicycle had nearly collided with her; Plimmer had seized the bicycle, the boy, and most of the basket in one sweep. Two eggs had broken over his tunic.

Ellen had laughed.

Romance is born in stranger fluids than egg.

From that hour he was lost.

His courtship, however, lacked polish. Alf could speak for ten minutes without saying anything and leave a woman delighted. Plimmer, when full of profound emotion, became silent and kicked the pavement.

On the rare evenings when Ellen stood at the area gate after supper, he would loom beside her and attempt conversation.

“Been cooking?” he would ask.

“What do you think I’ve been doing?”

“Dunno.”

“Standing on my head in the coal-cellar, likely.”

“Ah.”

A pause would follow. Plimmer would kick the pavement.

“Alf says he may take me to Richmond Sunday,” Ellen would remark.

Plimmer would kick it again.

“You’ll wear that pavement out,” said Ellen once.

He looked down at the scuffed stone.

“Let it,” he said.

This was not the way of the troubadour. No nightingale, listening from a neighbouring tree, would have mistaken it for song. But it represented strong feeling.

One evening Ellen, who enjoyed testing human machinery to see where it would squeak, said, “Alf’s got lovely eyes, hasn’t he?”

Plimmer made no reply.

“And lovely teeth.”

Silence.

“And lovely hair.”

A large boot struck the pavement.

“You’re jealous,” said Ellen.

Plimmer’s face deepened from its normal crimson to a hue associated with sunset over industrial regions.

“Yes,” he said.

The directness of the answer startled her. She had expected denial, bluster, perhaps a remark upon milkmen as a class. She looked at him more closely.

For an instant she became aware that beneath the helmet, the broken nose, the enormous tunic, and the official boots there was a man being hurt. Then Alf came round the corner, whistling, and the instant passed.

“Cheer-o, Nell!”

Ellen brightened.

Plimmer went away and arrested a dog which had no collar. It was the only outlet offered to him.

* * *

If Merivale Mansions had been managed according to the principles laid down in advertisements, life within Number Twelve would have been one long murmurous hymn of domestic contentment. The drawing-room curtains were artistic; the hall possessed a brass gong; and the dining-room wall bore an engraving of a stag looking nobly across a Highland glen.

But beneath these cultural advantages there existed Mrs. Winfield.

Mrs. Winfield was a thin, sharp-faced woman with a talent for suspicion. She suspected tradesmen of overcharging, servants of pilfering, her husband of hiding behind the newspaper, and the cat of having designs upon the cream. She had never actually caught the cat, but this did not weaken her case. To Mrs. Winfield, absence of evidence merely proved cunning.

Mr. Winfield was bald, mild, and rich enough to afford invisibility. He spent the greater part of his life behind newspapers, emerging at meals with the nervous expression of a rabbit which has heard a twig snap. When his wife said, “George!” he replied, “Yes, my dear,” without first inquiring into the charge against him.

Ellen had been in their service six months. She was a good cook, which had so far protected her against Mrs. Winfield’s temperament. A badly made soufflé may be dismissed with calmness; a perfect soufflé gives its maker a constitutional safeguard.

But even constitutions may be suspended in time of crisis.

The crisis began with three sovereigns.

Mrs. Winfield kept them in a small leather purse in the second drawer of her escritoire. She knew they were there because she had counted them after luncheon on Tuesday, again before dinner, and once more before going to bed. On Wednesday morning they were gone.

At first she suspected Mr. Winfield.

“George, have you taken money from my purse?”

“No, my dear.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes, my dear.”

“Then who has?”

Mr. Winfield considered the question. It seemed to him that this was a moment when a newspaper might be of assistance. He opened one.

“George!”

“Yes, my dear.”

“I am asking you.”

“So you are, my dear.”

“Don’t be maddening.”

“No, my dear.”

Mrs. Winfield rang the bell.

The housemaid came. She was examined and dismissed trembling. The parlourmaid followed. She wept, which Mrs. Winfield considered practically a confession, but no money was found in her box.

Ellen entered last, wiping flour from her arms.

“You rang?”

Mrs. Winfield fixed her with the gaze before which strong men had admitted to muddy boots.

“Three sovereigns have disappeared from my escritoire.”

“Have they?”

“You know nothing about them?”

“No.”

“You were dusting this room yesterday.”

“I dust it every day.”

“The drawer was not locked.”

“Then it ought to have been.”

Mrs. Winfield stiffened.

This was the wrong answer. Innocence, in her view, should be pale, respectful, and preferably in tears. Ellen was pink, floury, and annoyed.

“What do you mean by that?”

“I mean if I’d three sovereigns I didn’t want to lose, I’d lock them up.”

“You are impertinent.”

“I’m truthful.”

“That remains to be seen.”

Ellen’s chin moved forward by half an inch. Historians familiar with the early stages of international conflict will recognize the gesture.

“You aren’t accusing me, are you?”

“I am asking questions.”

“You’re as good as accusing me.”

“Kindly do not raise your voice.”

“I’ll raise it if I choose.”

“Ellen!”

At this point Mr. Winfield’s newspaper trembled. The cat, entering in search of peace, perceived the atmosphere and withdrew.

Then Mrs. Winfield’s eye fell upon the brooch at Ellen’s throat.

It was a small gold brooch set with a blue stone—not a great jewel, but one which Mrs. Winfield knew well.

“Where did you get that?”

Ellen put up a hand.

“This?”

“Yes, that. It is mine.”

“I borrowed it.”

“You stole it!”

“I never!”

“You took it without permission.”

“I meant to put it back.”

“When?”

“Tonight.”

Mrs. Winfield drew herself up. Virtue stood before the criminal classes.

“George, send for the police.”

The newspaper lowered several inches.

“My dear—”

“Send for the police.”

“Perhaps Ellen—”

“The police, George.”

Mr. Winfield looked at Ellen. Ellen looked at Mr. Winfield. In another age he might have smuggled her through a secret passage and provided a swift horse at the postern gate. In this age he went to the telephone.

* * *

Constable Plimmer was standing at the corner of Merivale Road when the message reached him.

The morning was warm. A child dragged a wooden horse by one leg. A woman at an upper window sang inaccurately about roses. Somewhere behind the flats a dog barked with the tireless conviction of one who believes that the very foundations of society depend upon it.

Plimmer had been thinking of Ellen.

He frequently tried not to think of her and found the effort gave his thoughts a useful sense of direction. On this occasion he was recalling the way she had smiled at Alf that morning. He brooded.

Then a boy came running from Number Twelve.

“Pleeceman! You’re wanted.”

The words electrified him. Here at last was Action.

“What for?”

“Robbery.”

Plimmer’s professional spirit rose magnificently. Battersea had justified itself. Crime, after lurking coyly behind lace curtains, had come into the open. He adjusted his belt and strode towards Number Twelve.

The boy ran beside him.

“Who’s been robbed?” Plimmer asked.

“The lady.”

“Who done it?”

“The cook, she says.”

Plimmer stopped.

The child continued for three paces, discovered himself alone, and looked back.

“What cook?”

“Miss Brown.”

There are moments when the human Brain, though celebrated by philosophers, behaves poorly. Plimmer’s Brain refused the information. It would admit each word separately, but rejected the sentence.

“Miss Brown?”

“Yes. Took money. And a brooch.”

The policeman stood motionless. Then Duty, a stern abstraction wearing the face of an inspector, took him by the collar.

He went on.

In the drawing-room he found Mrs. Winfield upright beside the escritoire, Mr. Winfield behind his newspaper, the housemaid in tears, and Ellen standing by the window. She had removed the brooch and placed it on the table. Her face was flushed, but she held her head high.

Mrs. Winfield began at once.

“Officer, this girl has stolen three sovereigns and has been found wearing my brooch.”

“I didn’t steal the money,” said Ellen.

“Silence!”

“Don’t you tell me to be silent.”

“Ellen!”

“I only borrowed the brooch. I was going to wear it Sunday.”

“To Richmond?” asked Plimmer before he could stop himself.

Ellen’s eyes flashed.

“That’s my business.”

The words struck home. Alf. Of course. She had borrowed it to make herself pretty for Alf.

The volcano began operations.

Mrs. Winfield spoke in a hard, triumphant voice. She gave facts, suspicions, times, drawers, sovereigns, and moral conclusions. Plimmer heard them through a mist. He looked at Ellen, and Ellen looked at the street.

“Have you searched her box?” he asked.

“I have. The money was not there.”

“Then you’ve no proof she took it.”

“The brooch is proof of her character.”

“I said I borrowed it,” Ellen snapped. “And I did.”

Mrs. Winfield turned upon Plimmer.

“Surely you intend to do your duty?”

There are phrases which poison the air. “Do your duty” is one. It has sent soldiers up hills, husbands to dinner parties, and policemen into emotional catastrophes.

Plimmer became wooden.

“I’ll have to take her to the station.”

The housemaid sniffed. Mr. Winfield’s newspaper drooped. Ellen went white for a moment, then recovered herself.

“All right,” she said. “I’ll come.”

“You should put on your hat,” said Mrs. Winfield.

Ellen looked at her.

“I know how to go outdoors.”

She left the room.

Plimmer stood staring at the brooch. It was a foolish little thing, scarcely worth the price of one decent impulse; but it had done its work. He hated the blue stone. He hated Richmond. He hated Alf Brooks’s teeth.

Mr. Winfield coughed behind him.

“Constable.”

Plimmer turned.

The bald head appeared cautiously above the newspaper.

“I ought perhaps to mention that my wife may possibly have—well—mislaid the money.”

“George!”

“I merely said possibly, my dear.”

“You said nothing of the sort.”

“I thought it, my dear.”

“Then kindly stop thinking.”

The bald head disappeared.

Ellen returned in her coat and hat. She did not look at anyone.

“I’m ready.”

Plimmer led the way out.

* * *

It is curious how efficiently a blue uniform can conceal a man’s emotions. To the casual observer, Constable Plimmer proceeding beside Ellen Brown was merely the Law escorting a young woman to the station. His face was blank, his step measured, his arms official.

Inside, civil war raged.

Duty said: “Proceed.”

Love said: “Release her.”

Jealousy said: “She borrowed the brooch for Alf.”

Common Sense said: “Shut up, all of you.”

Ellen walked fast, looking straight ahead.

The morning continued offensively pleasant. Sunshine lay upon the pavement. A cat washed itself in a doorway, indifferent to human tragedy. Two children bounced a ball against a wall. The woman at the window had changed from roses to mother, and was doing neither justice.

For fifty yards neither spoke.

Then Plimmer said, “Did you take that money?”

“No.”

“You took the brooch.”

“I borrowed it.”

“Without asking.”

“Yes.”

“What for?”

“I wanted to wear it.”

“At Richmond?”

She turned sharply.

“What’s that got to do with you?”

“Nothing.”

“You’re glad this has happened.”

Plimmer stared at her.

“Glad?”

“You hate Alf. You’d like me put away. Then I couldn’t go out with him.”

The charge was monstrous, unjust, and so precisely aimed at his secret shame that he flinched. A part of him had indeed thought bitterly of the ruined outing, of Alf waiting in his pink tie while Ellen sat behind locked doors. It had been only a flash, rejected at once, but Plimmer knew it had existed.

“I’m not glad,” he said.

“You look it.”

“This is how I look.”

“Well, you can’t help that, I suppose.”

Under ordinary circumstances the remark would have caused him to kick the nearest pavement into gravel. Today he merely walked on.

At the end of the road came a jangling of cans.

Alf Brooks turned the corner.

He was driving back towards the dairy, his morning round completed. His pink tie glowed; his curls shone; his horse moved at a meditative pace. Alf himself was whistling a song about a girl he had left in Devon, though he had never been to Devon and had no intention of leaving any girl unless circumstances required it.

He saw Plimmer first and grinned.

“Morning again, constable.”

Then he saw Ellen.

The grin remained, but lost structural integrity.

Ellen stopped.

“Alf!”

In that instant Alf Brooks understood everything. The policeman. Ellen walking beside him. No basket, no apron, no banter. Trouble.

Now, Alf had always considered himself loyal. He had often said so. Loyalty, like courage and generosity, was one of those qualities which he possessed most fully when no demand was made upon it. He would have stood by a friend through thick and thin, provided the thickness remained moderate and the thinness did not involve policemen.

His mind worked rapidly.

If he acknowledged Ellen, questions might follow. The dairy might hear of it. Customers might talk. A man in his position had to think of his position. It was not cowardice. It was Prudence, Respectability, and Business.

“Alf,” Ellen said again.

He looked at her with polite surprise.

“Beg pardon?”

“It’s me.”

Alf raised his cap slightly, as one gentleman might salute a stranger who had mistaken him for another gentleman.

“Sorry, miss. Don’t think I’ve had the pleasure.”

The world paused.

Even the horse seemed ashamed.

Ellen’s face emptied. The fighting light went from her eyes. For one dreadful second she looked younger, almost like a child who has put out a hand and found nobody there.

“You know me,” she whispered.

Alf glanced at Plimmer.

“You’ve made a mistake, miss.”

Then he shook the reins.

“Come up.”

The milk-float rattled past.

Alf kept his eyes ahead. He felt uncomfortable, but told himself that it was the only sensible thing. A chap couldn’t be too careful. Once Ellen had explained matters, he might perhaps send a message. Not a written message. Written messages could be produced. Something verbal and friendly. He was not hard-hearted.

Behind him, Ellen stood without moving.

Plimmer watched the green float diminish along the road. He had imagined this moment in a hundred jealous dreams: Alf exposed, Alf defeated, Alf removed from Ellen’s affections. In those dreams Plimmer had experienced triumph.

There was no triumph now.

Only pity—and an anger colder than anything Alf’s smiles had ever roused.

Ellen made a small sound. It was not quite a sob. She would have despised herself for sobbing in public.

Plimmer spoke gruffly.

“Come on.”

She walked beside him.

The children had ceased bouncing their ball. The dog had stopped barking. Even the singer withdrew from mother in the middle of a note. For a few moments the two of them seemed curiously alone in the sunlit street.

Then Plimmer stopped.

“Hop it,” he said.

Ellen looked up.

“What?”

“Hop it.”

“I don’t understand.”

“Clear off. Down that turning. There’s a railway arch. You can be through it before anyone knows.”

She stared at him. His face had resumed its wooden expression, but the wood was cracking.

“You mean run away?”

“Yes.”

“But you’re taking me to the station.”

“Not if you hop it.”

“What’ll happen to you?”

“Nothing.”

“That’s a lie.”

Plimmer said nothing.

“They’ll ask where I am.”

“I’ll say you got away.”

“And then?”

“Then they’ll deal with me.”

“How?”

He kicked the pavement.

Ellen seized his sleeve.

“How?”

“Dismissal, likely.”

“And prison?”

“Maybe.”

The word fell between them.

Ellen released his sleeve. She looked down the turning. It was narrow, empty, inviting. Beyond the railway arch lay streets, crowds, trains, escape. She had a little money of her own. She might get to an aunt in Croydon. She might disappear before Mrs. Winfield could sharpen another accusation.

Freedom stood at her elbow.

So did Plimmer.

“Go on,” he said. “Quick.”

“Why?”

“You know why.”

“No, I don’t.”

“Yes, you do.”

“Say it.”

He glared at the pavement as if it had put him in this position.

“You’ve always been down on me,” Ellen said. “You’re forever scowling and stamping about. This morning you could hardly look at me. And now you’re asking me to run and get you locked up. Why?”

Plimmer’s voice came out thickly.

“Because I don’t want them putting you in prison.”

“They might put you there instead.”

“That’s different.”

“How?”

“It is.”

She studied him.

He was not handsome. No process of spiritual illumination could straighten the broken nose or make the large red face classical. He remained a massive, awkward policeman with a helmet too small for his feelings. Yet, where Alf’s beauty had seemed a bright painted thing, Plimmer’s ugliness now had the grave solidity of a wall built to stand between her and harm.

“Go on,” he repeated.

“No.”

His head jerked up.

“What?”

“I’m not going.”

“Don’t be a fool.”

“I’m not letting you do it.”

“It’s nothing to do with you.”

“It’s everything to do with me if you lose your place and go to prison.”

“I can look after myself.”

“So can I.”

“They may send you away for months.”

“Then they send me.”

“You don’t know what it’s like.”

“Neither do you.”

“I’ve seen women come out.”

“And I’ve seen men.”

Plimmer breathed heavily. Argument was not his art. He felt like a bear required to thread a needle.

“Ellen,” he said.

It was the first time he had used her name without “Miss” before it. She noticed.

“Hop it,” he said, but now the words were almost a plea.

She shook her head.

“No.”

“For Heaven’s sake.”

“No.”

“You’d rather go to prison?”

“I’d rather go myself than send you.”

He looked at her helplessly.

She looked back.

Somewhere far off a cab horn sounded. The ordinary world was beginning again, but it seemed to them that the street still held its breath.

“Why would you care?” Plimmer asked at last.

Ellen frowned, as if the question were badly put.

“I’m not made of stone.”

“You like Alf.”

At the name, pain crossed her face. Then anger came to its assistance.

“I did.”

Plimmer’s heart gave one enormous beat.

“Did?”

“I don’t want to talk about him.”

“He’s a swine.”

“He’s worse. He’s a coward.”

“Yes.”

“And I thought—” She stopped. “Never mind what I thought.”

They resumed walking, slowly now.

The police station lay three streets away. Its blue lamp could not yet be seen, but Plimmer felt its presence as a sailor feels the coast towards which a current bears him.

“You didn’t take the money?” he said.

“I told you I didn’t.”

“I believe you.”

“Thank you.”

“The brooch was foolish.”

“I know.”

“You shouldn’t have done it.”

“I know.”

“Especially for him.”

“I know!”

Playfulness, after this, was at a discount.

They crossed the road. A cart passed, the driver calling to his horse. On the opposite pavement an old gentleman inspected them with mild curiosity and then decided that the private affairs of youth were less interesting than the price of fish.

Plimmer cleared his throat.

“If they send you down,” he said, “I’ll be there when you come out.”

Ellen’s step faltered.

“What for?”

“To meet you.”

“You won’t want to.”

“Yes.”

“It might be six months.”

“I’ll wait.”

“A year, maybe.”

“I’ll wait.”

She looked at his profile. It was not a profile designed for medals, except perhaps medals awarded for stopping runaway vehicles with the face. But there was no doubt in it.

“Suppose I look awful when I come out?”

“You look all right now.”

This was Plimmer’s lyric moment. Shakespeare, Petrarch, and the author of the song about Devon may have expressed admiration more richly, but none could have meant it more.

Ellen’s mouth trembled.

“You’ve got a funny way of talking to a girl.”

“I don’t know how.”

“No,” she said. “You don’t.”

They walked another twenty yards.

“Is it true,” she asked, “what I said that night?”

“What?”

“That you were jealous.”

“Yes.”

“You didn’t have to say yes so quick.”

“It’s true.”

“Because of Alf?”

“Yes.”

“And you—” She hesitated. “You care for me?”

Plimmer’s great hands closed.

“Yes.”

The word came as simply as before. Yet it contained all the kicked pavements, all the silent mornings, all the pain of seeing her smile at another man, and all the future he had never dared to imagine.

Ellen lowered her eyes.

“You’re not much to look at,” she said.

“No.”

“You needn’t agree quite so hearty.”

“I know what I look like.”

“And you’re awkward.”

“Yes.”

“And bad-tempered.”

“Sometimes.”

“Most times.”

He accepted this.

“And your feet,” she continued, “are something shocking.”

Plimmer looked down at them. They moved beside her with the grave majesty of twin barges.

“Can’t help them.”

“No.”

They reached the corner from which the blue lamp of the station became visible.

Plimmer’s face set. The station represented the return of Authority. Once they passed through its doors, he would no longer be Eddie Plimmer, lover and potential criminal. He would become Police Constable 425B, delivering a prisoner.

Ellen saw the change. The wooden mask had descended again.

She touched his arm.

“Don’t look like that.”

“Like what?”

“Like a door.”

He tried to alter his expression and failed.

“Listen,” she said. “I don’t know what’ll happen in there.”

“I’ll speak up for you.”

“You mustn’t get into trouble.”

“I don’t care.”

“Well, I do.”

He turned to her.

The blue lamp waited. A pigeon strutted beneath it with the complacency of a creature whose record was clean.

Ellen spoke quickly.

“I was a fool over Alf.”

Plimmer said nothing.

“I thought because he was smart and knew how to talk, he was—well, better than other people.”

“He isn’t.”

“I know that now.”

Plimmer’s face remained stern, but hope had entered him and was making itself thoroughly at home.

“You offering to let me go,” she continued, “that was foolish too.”

“Yes.”

“But different foolish.”

He considered this.

“I’d do it again.”

“I know.”

“And if they lock you up—”

“They won’t, if I can help it.”

“You’ll let me come when you get out?”

She smiled faintly.

“Maybe.”

“Promise?”

“I’m the prisoner. I don’t have to promise the policeman anything.”

He kicked the pavement once more, though gently.

Ellen looked at the station, then back at him.

“They call you Edward, don’t they?”

“Yes.”

“But what do your friends call you?”

“Don’t have many.”

“What would they call you if you did?”

He pondered.

“Ed. Sometimes Ted.”

“Not Eddie?”

His ears reddened.

“Might.”

She nodded, as though settling a matter of administrative importance.

“Well, then,” she said, “when I come out—if I have to go in—I might call you Eddie.”

Plimmer stopped breathing.

Ellen walked on two steps, then looked back.

“Aren’t you coming?”

He came.

Together they went beneath the blue lamp and through the station door: Ellen to meet whatever punishment the Law and Mrs. Winfield could contrive between them, Plimmer to state the facts with a force which would make the sergeant’s ink tremble.

Behind them Battersea resumed its morning. The dog barked. The children’s ball struck the wall. The woman at the upper window returned to roses. Far away, the green milk-float rattled towards the dairy, bearing Alf Brooks and his spotless reputation.

But on the pavement outside the station lay a fresh white mark where Constable Plimmer’s boot had struck the stone.

It was the last time that day he kicked it from jealousy.

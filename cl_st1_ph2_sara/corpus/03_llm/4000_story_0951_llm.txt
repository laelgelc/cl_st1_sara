## THE MAY KING

William was bored.

Boredom in William was not a passive condition. It was not the pale, drooping boredom of people who sit at windows and sigh. It was an active, dangerous force. When William was bored, doors slammed, ornaments fell, dogs fled, and adults began suddenly to inquire who had been meddling with things.

On this particular afternoon the schoolroom offered no legitimate outlet for his energies. Miss Milton was explaining the May Day celebrations.

“The election of the May Queen,” she said, smiling sentimentally over the rows of desks, “is an ancient and beautiful custom.”

William scowled.

He disliked ancient customs. They generally involved facts, dates, clean collars and standing still. He disliked beautiful customs even more. They generally involved girls.

“The child chosen,” Miss Milton continued, “should represent sweetness, grace and dignity.”

Every girl in the room sat suddenly upright and represented sweetness, grace and dignity.

William looked round in disgust.

Evangeline Fish represented them most conspicuously. She had pale-gold ringlets, large blue eyes and a blue sash. She generally wore blue. Her mother said it brought out the colour of her eyes. William considered that the colour of her eyes had been brought out quite far enough already.

Evangeline lowered her lashes.

“I shouldn’t like to be Queen,” she said in a squeaky little voice that could be heard in every corner of the room. “I’d be too shy.”

This was Evangeline’s opening move in the electoral campaign.

The election was to be conducted by ballot. Miss Milton distributed small squares of paper and instructed each child to write the name of the girl whom he or she considered most suitable.

“You must vote according to your conscience,” she said.

William had no high opinion of consciences. His own generally advised him to do things that grown-ups afterwards condemned. Still, he accepted the paper.

Evangeline turned round and smiled at him.

“You’ll write my name, won’t you, William?”

“No,” said William.

Her smile became sweeter.

“I know you will.”

“I won’t.”

“You promised you’d write my name.”

“I never did.”

“You did yesterday.”

William frowned. He remembered yesterday. Evangeline had offered him a bull’s-eye if he would write her name on the voting paper. William, wishing to obtain the bull’s-eye without entering into political controversy, had agreed to write it.

He took up his pencil.

Evangeline watched him.

Slowly and laboriously he wrote EVANGELINE FISH across the paper. Then, with true political address, he drew a thick line through it and wrote beneath:

BETTINE FRANKLIN.

“You said you’d write my name,” he whispered.

“You’ve crossed it out!”

“I never said I wouldn’t cross it out.”

Evangeline made a noise expressive of outrage, betrayal and the unexpected loss of public office.

Miss Milton looked up.

“Is anything wrong, Evangeline?”

“No, Miss Milton,” said Evangeline, with gentle fortitude. “It doesn’t matter.”

This was the second move in her campaign.

The papers were folded and collected. Miss Milton counted them, recounting twice because William’s paper contained so much pencil and because one elector had voted for the school cat.

At last she raised her hand.

“Evangeline Fish has been chosen May Queen by a large majority.”

Evangeline looked astonished. She had been preparing the astonishment for three days.

“Oh!” she squeaked. “Me?”

The girls clapped. Some of the boys clapped. William did not clap.

Bettine Franklin did. She was a small, dark child who sat at the end of the back row and was generally forgotten by everyone except the person collecting exercise books. Her hair was straight, her dress was brown, and her expression was one of shy expectation, as though life had promised her something but had not yet remembered what it was.

Evangeline stood while Miss Milton spoke about honour, responsibility and May blossoms. Evangeline’s ringlets trembled modestly. She glanced round to see whether everyone was looking at them.

William regarded her with growing dislike.

It was not merely that she had won. William could forgive victory if it were obtained by force, cunning or superior aim with a catapult. Evangeline had obtained it by smiles, ringlets, whispering, canvassing and the distribution of bull’s-eyes. She had won, in fact, by methods which William considered underhand.

Then came the blow.

“The Queen will have four attendants,” said Miss Milton, “and four pages. William Brown, Ginger, Douglas and Henry will be the pages.”

William stared at her.

“I’m not bein’ a page.”

“You are to have the honour of attending the May Queen.”

“I don’t want the honour.”

“You will wear a white blouse, green sash and a wreath of leaves.”

“I won’t.”

“And you will walk behind Evangeline carrying a garland.”

“I’ll be ill that day.”

Miss Milton smiled the smile with which adults acknowledge a child’s declaration of war.

“I’m sure you won’t, William.”

William looked at Evangeline. Evangeline smiled at him.

“I’ll try not to walk too fast for you,” she said kindly.

At that moment Evangeline’s fate was sealed. Nothing happened at once. Nothing ever did. William’s plans required time, material and an injustice large enough to sustain them. But when anything jarred upon William’s nerves so violently, something always happened in the end.

At recreation he found Bettine sitting alone beneath the chestnut tree.

“’Ullo,” he said.

“Hello, William.”

“Why did you vote for Evangeline?”

Bettine looked alarmed.

“Everyone did.”

“I didn’t.”

“Oh.”

“I voted for you.”

Her small face brightened.

“Did you really?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

William had not considered this. He kicked a stone.

“Dunno. You’re better’n her.”

“I’m not pretty.”

“You’re all right.”

This was not lavish praise, but Bettine received it as though a poet had addressed her beneath a balcony.

“I’d like to be Queen,” she admitted. “Not for always. Just for a little while. I’d like to wear a crown and sit up there where everyone could see me. But I wouldn’t be vain.”

“I know.”

“And I’d choose you for my page.”

“I wouldn’t be anyone’s page.”

“You could be King, then.”

William looked at her.

There was no May King in Miss Milton’s programme. This omission had already struck him as a grave defect. To William, a Queen without a King was not a ceremony but merely a girl showing off.

“Why isn’t there a May King?” he demanded later of Miss Milton.

“Because there isn’t,” said Miss Milton, who had been correcting arithmetic for forty minutes.

“There ought to be.”

“It is the custom to have a May Queen.”

“Well, it’s a silly custom.”

“William!”

“Kings are more important than Queens.”

“That is quite enough.”

“An’ I’m not bein’ a page.”

Miss Milton gave him extra arithmetic. Thus do governments answer constitutional criticism.

During the following week the May Day arrangements advanced. Garlands appeared, ribbons were measured, and Miss Milton rehearsed the children in a dance around a maypole erected on the village green. Evangeline attended every rehearsal wearing blue and issuing suggestions.

“I think the pages should walk more slowly,” she said.

“I think the Queen should shut up,” said William.

“William!”

“I only said—”

“I heard what you said.”

Evangeline practised ascending the platform. She practised smiling at the audience. She practised bowing to the Vicar’s wife. Once she tripped over her robe and blamed the nearest page, who happened to be William.

“You pushed me.”

“I never touched you.”

“You looked as if you were going to.”

Evangeline’s mother was making her a dress of white silk with forget-me-nots sewn round the hem. Reports of it circulated through the school like dispatches from a royal household.

Meanwhile Bettine remained at the back of everything. She was not an attendant because her dress was unsuitable, and she was not in the first dancing circle because she was small. She watched Evangeline’s rehearsals with admiration unspoilt by envy.

William had begun to share his treasures with her. He gave her a green marble, a dead beetle in a matchbox, half an apple and a centipede which escaped before she could take it home. Bettine accepted each gift gravely. In return she gave him a pencil, two bits of string and unlimited admiration.

This friendship caused some surprise in William’s family.

“William seems to be mellowing,” said Mrs Brown.

“Mellowing?” said Robert gloomily. “No one mellows. People pretend to mellow, and then they ruin your life.”

Robert had lately suffered an emotional disaster. A girl called Celia had danced twice with a man in a grey suit, and Robert’s faith in women, happiness and civilization had collapsed.

“You don’t understand,” he told his mother. “When one has built one’s entire existence upon a single perfect trust, and then sees it shattered—utterly shattered—before one’s eyes—”

“You’ve dropped marmalade on the tablecloth,” said Mrs Brown.

Robert stared into the distance.

“What does marmalade matter when life itself—?”

“It matters to me. Please wipe it up.”

Robert wiped it up with the air of a man signing away his ancestral estates.

Ethel, William’s elder sister, was preparing for a fairy play at the Women’s Institute. She had a silver wand, gauze wings, a rose-coloured tunic and a starry head-dress. These objects lay in her room under strict prohibition.

William had examined them all.

He had also found in the box-room an old black jacket belonging to Robert, a pair of scarlet curtains, a cardboard shield and a quantity of tinsel left from Christmas. At the time he had no settled purpose. William often collected information before events supplied it with meaning.

The chief difficulty was money.

William’s ordinary capital consisted of three halfpence, a foreign coin, two buttons and a tram ticket. He could sometimes negotiate with Ginger, but Ginger’s resources were little better. Douglas had sixpence, but intended to buy a whistle. Henry had nothing except a white mouse and would not sell it.

Then Uncle Frederick came to tea.

Uncle Frederick was stout, prosperous and fond of children from a safe distance. He arrived in a motor-car, pinched Ethel’s cheek, asked Robert whether he was breaking hearts yet, and slapped William on the shoulder with enough heartiness to cause permanent resentment.

“Well, young scamp,” he said, “and what do you want to be when you grow up?”

“Nothin’,” said William.

“Ha! Ha! A gentleman of leisure, eh?”

William did not know what a gentleman of leisure was, but suspected it of being insulting.

At tea Uncle Frederick talked of business, taxation, golf and the decline of modern manners. Jumble, William’s mongrel, sat beneath the table and received in rapid succession a piece of cake, two sardines, a lump of cold tongue and a sugared almond. The contributions came from different people, each believing himself alone in generosity. Jumble accepted them all with the fatal politeness of his kind.

After tea Uncle Frederick prepared to leave.

“Here, my boy,” he said. “Buy yourself something useful.”

He gave William five shillings.

Five shillings!

William stared at it. Never before had wealth in such solid and splendid form entered his possession. Five shillings meant sweets, fireworks, catapult elastic, a mouth-organ and still perhaps enough left for another mouth-organ when the first was confiscated.

Mrs Brown intervened.

“You must put it in your money-box, dear.”

William closed his hand over it.

“I’ll put it somewhere safe.”

His pocket was somewhere, and it was as safe as any place whose contents included a penknife without a handle, two acorns, a bent nail and a piece of treacle paper.

That evening Jumble was ill.

He lay beneath the kitchen table looking reproachfully at mankind. His sufferings were various and audible.

“I can’t think what he’s eaten,” said Mrs Brown.

William could. William had given him the sugared almond.

The family discussed Jumble’s digestion, Robert’s ruined life and Ethel’s fairy costume. William sat silent. He had suddenly remembered something.

That afternoon, after rehearsal, he had seen Evangeline enter the baker’s shop with a shilling her mother had given her for ribbon. She had emerged ten minutes later without ribbon, but eating a cream bun. She had then eaten a jam tart, two sponge fingers and a macaroon while walking home. She had eaten steadily, secretly and with remarkable concentration.

The next day he tested his discovery.

“Want a bit of cake?” he said to her at recreation.

“What sort?”

“Currant.”

“I don’t mind.”

She ate it.

“Thought you didn’t like currants.”

“I don’t, really,” said Evangeline, licking a crumb from her finger.

On the following day he offered her a stale ginger biscuit which he had found in his pocket.

She ate that too.

William considered the matter. Evangeline was vain, deceitful and squeaky, but these qualities were merely irritating. Her appetite was strategic.

On the morning of May Day, William went alone to the baker’s shop.

He bought two cream buns, four jam tarts, six sponge cakes, three macaroons, a currant cake, a bag of meringues, two iced buns, half a pound of biscuits and a small cake bearing the word WELCOME in pink sugar. The baker, accustomed to small purchasers of one halfpenny sweet, regarded him with respect.

William carried the parcel home by a circuitous route and concealed it in the woodshed behind the coal scuttle.

He then inspected the other materials of his campaign.

Ethel and Mrs Brown had gone early to help decorate the tea marquee. Robert had gone for a walk in order to recover, if possible, from love. The housemaid was upstairs. William entered Ethel’s room.

He took the rose-coloured tunic, the gauze wings and the silver wand. After reflection he left the starry head-dress because it would not fit Bettine and substituted a narrow band of tinsel fastened to cardboard points. It was a crown of simple but effective construction.

From Robert’s room he took the old black jacket. From the box-room he took the scarlet curtains and the cardboard shield. From the kitchen he took a burnt cork.

The burnt cork was important.

At half-past one the village green was bright with flags, white dresses, straw hats and adults congratulating one another upon the weather. The maypole stood in the centre with coloured ribbons hanging from it. A flower-covered platform had been built beside the tea marquee. On this Evangeline was to be crowned at two o’clock.

Evangeline arrived at one-thirty in white silk, forget-me-nots and ringlets. Her mother walked beside her carrying a cloak and looking as if she had personally arranged the month of May.

“You look quite angelic, darling,” said Miss Milton.

Evangeline lowered her eyes.

“I feel so frightened,” she squeaked.

She was not frightened. She had spent the morning practising a royal wave before the looking-glass.

William, wearing his ordinary clothes and a forbidding expression, approached her.

“Got something to show you.”

“I’ve no time. I’m the Queen.”

“It’s cakes.”

Evangeline hesitated.

“What cakes?”

“Lots.”

“I’ve had my lunch.”

“All right,” said William, turning away.

“Where are they?”

“In our woodshed.”

“Why?”

“’Cause I put ’em there.”

“I oughtn’t to go. Mummy said I mustn’t get my dress dirty.”

“They’re wrapped up.”

“What sort are they?”

William began the catalogue. At the second cream bun Evangeline glanced towards her mother. At the first macaroon she took one step after him. At the WELCOME cake she surrendered.

“I’ll only look,” she said.

“Please yourself.”

They slipped from the green by the lane and entered the Browns’ garden through a gap in the hedge. William opened the woodshed.

The cakes lay upon an upturned packing-case. Seen together they possessed a grandeur beyond their price. The meringues shone. The jam tarts glowed. WELCOME smiled pinkly upon the world.

Evangeline drew in her breath.

“They’re for the celebration,” said William. “But they won’t miss a few.”

“Are they really?”

“Yes.”

This was untrue, but William’s scheme had reached a point at which truth was no longer practical.

Evangeline sat on a box. She took a cream bun.

“You won’t tell?”

“No.”

William shut the door, leaving it unlatched.

He did not lock her in. This was partly because the lock was broken and partly because he understood Evangeline. No prison constructed by man could have held her more securely than those cakes.

At a quarter to two William found Bettine behind the tea marquee.

She wore her brown dress and was carrying a tray of paper napkins for Miss Milton.

“Come on,” he said.

“Where?”

“You’re goin’ to be Queen.”

Bettine stared at him.

“I’m not.”

“You are.”

“But Evangeline—”

“She’s busy.”

“What with?”

“Government business.”

He led Bettine through the hedge and up to Ethel’s room. There he explained the plan with the brevity of a commander whose troops need confidence rather than details.

Bettine turned pale.

“Miss Milton’ll be angry.”

“She’s always angry.”

“My mother’ll be angry.”

“She won’t till afterwards.”

This distinction reassured her slightly.

William pulled the fairy tunic over her brown dress. It hung crookedly, one wing pointing upwards and the other down. He fastened the scarlet curtain round her shoulders and placed the tinsel crown upon her dark hair.

Bettine looked at herself in the glass.

For one moment she said nothing.

“Oh, William,” she whispered. “I do look like a Queen.”

“You look better’n Evangeline.”

This was William’s sincere opinion. Bettine had none of Evangeline’s finish, but she had ceased to be shy. Her eyes shone, her cheeks were flushed, and the bent crown had altered her entire relation to the world.

William then dressed himself.

Robert’s black jacket reached nearly to his knees. He tied the second scarlet curtain round his waist, hung the cardboard shield from his neck and made a crown from a strip of gold paper. Last, with burnt cork, he drew a heavy moustache across his upper lip.

The effect was not conventional royalty. It suggested a pirate who had recently inherited a Balkan principality. William was satisfied.

“A King ought to have a sword,” he said.

He took the poker.

They returned by the hedge.

At five minutes past two Miss Milton was becoming anxious.

“Where is Evangeline?”

No one knew.

“Where is William?”

No one knew this either, but less anxiety was felt.

Evangeline’s mother searched the marquee. Miss Milton searched behind the platform. The Vicar’s wife looked at her watch. The musicians, under the impression that everything was ready, raised their instruments.

Then William and Bettine appeared.

They came through the crowd hand in hand. Bettine’s gauze wings trembled behind her. Her scarlet cloak dragged over the grass. William marched beside her, moustached, crowned, shielded and carrying the poker upright.

The audience became silent.

Adults are slow to believe in disaster when bunting is present. For several seconds everyone assumed that this extraordinary entrance formed part of the entertainment.

“How original,” murmured the Vicar’s wife.

“Some sort of allegory,” said the Vicar.

“I expect he represents Spring,” said a lady in mauve.

William mounted the flower-covered platform. He helped Bettine up after him and seated her upon Evangeline’s throne.

Miss Milton recovered the use of her limbs.

“William!”

At that instant the band began.

The children, drilled for weeks to move when the music started, moved. The attendants formed their circle. The pages took up the garland. Ginger, seeing William upon the platform in a moustache, nearly dropped his end, but discipline prevailed. The dancers caught the ribbons and began to weave them round the pole.

Miss Milton could not stop the dance without crossing the ring, upsetting the children and publicly admitting that she had no idea what was happening. She stood at the edge of the green, smiling with desperate brightness.

William raised the poker in salute.

The audience applauded.

Encouraged, he bowed. The cardboard shield struck his knees. His gold-paper crown slipped over one eye. He pushed it back and assumed an expression of ferocious majesty.

Bettine sat very still.

She had never before been watched by so many people. Usually, if people looked in her direction, it was because someone more important stood behind her. Now the music circled her, the ribbons flashed round the pole, and a hundred faces smiled up at her. She lifted one hand and waved.

The audience applauded again.

“She’s charming,” said the Vicar’s wife.

“Such a natural little thing,” said the lady in mauve.

“Far sweeter than the fair child who was to have done it,” whispered someone who had not been introduced to Evangeline’s mother.

Bettine heard the applause. Her crown was crooked, her wing was tearing, and William’s scarlet drapery had begun to come undone; but she was Queen.

William looked at her and grinned beneath his moustache.

“Like it?” he whispered.

“Oh, yes.”

The dancers completed their final turn and bowed. William stepped forward.

He had intended to make a speech, but had prepared only its opening.

“Ladies an’ gentlemen,” he said loudly, “this is the May Queen, an’ I’m the May King, ’cause there ought to be one.”

There was a pause.

Then a man at the back laughed. Others joined him. The applause became tremendous. The audience believed the speech to be a carefully designed piece of childish comedy.

Miss Milton did not.

“Come down at once,” she said through a fixed smile.

William, seeing no advantage in remaining after the triumph, helped Bettine from the throne. They descended amid cheers.

Bettine’s mother reached them first. She stared at the tunic, the wings and the tinsel crown.

“Bettine! What have you done?”

Bettine looked up at her, still radiant.

“I’ve been Queen.”

This was undeniable. Even her mother could not immediately discover an answer to it.

Then Ethel arrived.

“My costume!”

Robert arrived behind her.

“My jacket!”

Mrs Brown arrived behind Robert.

“William!”

Miss Milton came from the other direction.

“Where is Evangeline?”

Four accusations, each serious in itself, converged upon William. He stood with his crown over one eye, his moustache smeared across his cheek and the poker in his hand.

He considered retreat.

At that moment Evangeline’s mother screamed from the edge of the green.

“Evangeline!”

Everyone turned.

Evangeline was approaching slowly through the hedge. Her white silk dress bore jam upon the front, cream upon one sleeve and crumbs in every fold. One forget-me-not hung from a ringlet. She carried the remains of the WELCOME cake in both hands and was eating it as she came.

She had eaten the cream buns, the jam tarts, the sponge cakes, the macaroons, the meringues, the iced buns, the biscuits and most of the currant cake. She had heard the distant music. She had heard the applause. But there had always been one more cake.

“What have you been doing?” cried her mother.

Evangeline stopped.

She looked at the empty throne, at Bettine’s crown, at William’s moustache and at the crowd staring at her.

Then she looked down at the cake.

There was one sugared pink letter left upon it.

Evangeline ate it.

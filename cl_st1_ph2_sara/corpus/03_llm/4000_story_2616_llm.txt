### THE LAST TIDE

I have read De Quincey with that mingled admiration and distrust which his honeyed confessions must awaken in every temperate mind; I have wandered, though always as a spectator, among the perfumed corruptions of Baudelaire; and I am not unacquainted with those Oriental legends wherein hashish, lotus, or some subtler nepenthe opens to the entranced intelligence palaces more enduring than dynasties, gardens inaccessible to waking feet, and cities whose brazen gates confront the very moon. Yet I had never believed—until the deplorable incident which I am about to relate—that any drug, whether administered by vice, curiosity, or accident, could unseal actual gulfs beyond the familiar phantasms of sleep. I believed the visions of opium to be but grotesque rearrangements of remembered things: fragments of temples, faces, sunsets, loves, and terrors, shaken together in the fevered crucible of the brain.

I do not believe so now.

The occurrence befell during the blackest month of the plague, when bells tolled without cessation, funeral carts went heavily through the rain, and men crossed the street to avoid the breath of their oldest friends. I had laboured for six weeks among the afflicted, not as a physician—for I possessed no such honourable qualification—but as secretary to the municipal relief committee, and had thereby exchanged sleep, regular nourishment, and tranquillity for the spectacle of every conceivable degradation of the human frame. At last I sickened. Whether the pestilence itself had seized me, or whether exhaustion merely mimicked its signs, my attendants could not determine; but for nine days I lay between stupor and frenzy, tormented by a pain which appeared less in my skull than behind it, as though some invisible smith were hammering upon the rearward wall of consciousness.

On the ninth night the pain became insupportable. The physician ordered a small quantity of laudanum, but the exhausted nurse, confusing two bottles in the half-light, gave me an amount which, as I afterwards learned, might readily have destroyed a stronger man. I remember the bitterness upon my tongue, a lamp burning behind green glass, and the rain’s thin fingers at the window. Then came a prodigious heaviness, followed not by sleep, but by the sensation of being drawn backward through innumerable chambers, each darker and more remote than the last. Throughout that descent the hammering continued.

Pound—pound—pound.

I sought to call out, but my voice had gone before me into darkness.

Then I opened my eyes.

I lay upon a low couch in a chamber of such strange and sumptuous fashion that for several minutes I could not decide whether it belonged to antiquity, futurity, or some civilization wholly divided from mankind. The walls were panelled in lustrous woods of amber, violet, and black; the ceiling, rising like the interior of a shell, was encrusted with mother-of-pearl; and the floor consisted of translucent green slabs beneath which tiny golden fish seemed to move through fathomless water. Curtains, embroidered with birds of no earthly species, stirred before tall apertures where windows should have been, though no wind entered. A perfume compounded of sandalwood, wet roses, cinnamon, and something faintly cadaverous oppressed the air.

At first I imagined myself in some tropical sanatorium, carried thither during delirium; yet this rational hypothesis dissolved when I saw upon a table beside me a lamp fashioned from the skull of an unknown horned creature, within whose crystal brain a blue flame burned without oil or wick. Nor could I account for the carvings which covered the furniture: men with wings folded inside their bodies; flowers whose petals were eyes; crowned serpents ascending stairways toward clustered stars.

The pounding persisted, measured and stupendous. It shook the vessels upon the table and sent small iridescent powders drifting from the rafters.

I rose with difficulty. My body was clothed in a loose robe of yellow silk, and though I retained the memory of illness, I felt neither fever nor pain. Beyond the nearest curtain lay a veranda encircling the cottage. I passed through, and beheld a spectacle whose beauty was so intimately conjoined with menace that even now I cannot recall it without trembling.

The house perched at the extremity of a narrow promontory, perhaps two hundred feet above the sea. Behind it rose a land of luxuriant, almost insolent verdure; before it, on three sides, the ocean stretched beneath a sky of livid copper. Yet this was no ocean known to geography. Its waters were black at the horizon, purple in the middle distance, and a poisonous milk-white near the cliffs; while across that unnatural gradation ran long veins of scarlet foam. There was no wind, though the waves advanced in mountainous files, deliberate and erect, each lifting a glassy brow higher than the cottage before bowing itself against the land.

Pound—pound—pound.

With every impact the promontory shuddered. Great pieces of its flank fell soundlessly for an instant, then struck the boiling water far below with detonations like artillery. I watched a grove of delicate silver trees, growing upon a neighbouring spur, tremble, incline, and disappear entire as the cliff beneath them collapsed. The sea ate and ate. It did not seem to erode the shore by blind persistence, but to bite it knowingly, maliciously, with a hideous appetite increased by each mouthful.

I turned toward the cottage, intending to seek its inhabitants, but perceived with horror that a fissure had opened beneath the veranda. It widened whilst I looked. The painted balustrade sagged outward, and one corner of the fantastic dwelling hung already above the abyss. From within came a faint chiming, as of small bells disturbed in an empty temple; then the whole seaward chamber broke away and plunged downward, its bright curtains streaming like the garments of falling women.

I fled inland.

A path, paved with blue stones and overgrown by blossoms, wound from the promontory into the forest. I followed it at a speed which should have been impossible after my sickness, whilst behind me the ocean kept up its gigantic percussion. The vegetation through which I passed defied all botanical classification. Ferns taller than cathedral towers unrolled fronds of translucent crimson; orchids as large as infants breathed warm vapour from spotted throats; and pale vines, hanging in festoons from unseen branches, contracted at my approach like sleeping reptiles disturbed. Once a many-jointed thing, half lizard and half lily, detached itself from a trunk and walked solemnly into the undergrowth upon its roots.

The atmosphere grew hotter, but the sky darkened. Birds—or beings which used the sky as birds do—moved high above the canopy, displaying triangular wings that flashed alternately gold and sable. Their cries resembled distant laughter uttered backward. Yet beneath every rustle, cry, and dripping leaf sounded the pounding sea, impossibly close though I had travelled many miles.

The path descended into a valley where dusk lay like violet water. At its centre stood a palm of such colossal magnitude that the mountains themselves appeared but excrescences about its roots. Its trunk, smooth and pale as ivory, rose beyond sight before dividing into vast emerald fans that supported clouds upon their leaves. Around its base flourished a garden of amber lilies, blue pomegranates, and low shrubs bearing luminous fruit. There the air was cool, the pounding diminished, and for the first time I heard music.

It consisted initially of one clear note, sustained at the threshold of audibility; then other tones assembled about it, not in any harmony devised by men, but in ascending circles, each more delicate and comprehensive than the one beneath. I thought of Pythagoras and the celestial intervals; of the songs which Orpheus heard beyond death; of all those myths by which our fathers confessed that the universe may possess an order inaudible to ordinary flesh.

Beneath the palm stood a child.

Whether male or female I could not discern. The little figure wore a garment white as sunlit snow, fastened at the shoulder by a clasp of chalcedony, and its bare feet rested upon the lilies without bending them. Its hair, falling in golden curls, possessed the radiance of a lamp, whilst its eyes were of that profound blue sometimes visible at the zenith after rain. No earthly childhood had ever contained such beauty; yet there was in the face an immemorial gravity, as if what smiled upon me had watched the first beasts crawl from the warm primordial sea.

“You have come late,” said the child.

The words were spoken softly, but I heard them above the forest and the dreadful ocean.

“Where am I?” I demanded. “What country is this? Why does the sea destroy it?”

“You stand upon the final garden of the world.”

I sought to reject this declaration as the incoherence of delirium. “The world has many shores.”

“It had many.”

At this the child extended one radiant hand, and from behind the enormous roots emerged a company of beings whose aspect I can describe only through comparisons necessarily inadequate. They were shaped like men and women in the general disposition of limb and countenance, but taller, slenderer, and invested with an unearthly lucency. Some wore robes of green and silver; some were naked save for coronals of leaves or stars; and some appeared to be clothed in their own streaming hair. Their faces united the vigour of youth with the serenity of extreme age. I thought of Apollo, Astarte, Balder, and the beneficent deities of forgotten islands; yet these were not copies of gods imagined by mankind. Rather, our gods seemed broken memories of them.

One of the women came forward. Her skin had the faint translucence of amber held before flame, and her black eyes contained innumerable points of light.

“We go to Teloe,” she said.

The name awakened in me a nostalgia more powerful than memory. “What is Teloe?”

“The country above change. There no leaf withers, no face grows old, no lover becomes indifferent, and no desire survives its fulfilment. The rivers are sweet, the nights fragrant, and every dawn restores the pleasures of the day before without diminishing their novelty.”

“And these seas?”

“They cannot rise to Teloe.”

A crashing greater than any previous concussion rolled across the valley. The earth swayed; the luminous fruit fell from the shrubs and burst upon the ground in showers of sparks. Far behind us the forest canopy subsided, ridge after ridge, as though an enormous weight were pressing it downward. Above the sinking trees appeared the white, curving summit of a wave.

“Take me with you!” I cried.

The child smiled and placed its hand in mine. At that touch my body became light. The godlike company lifted their arms, the music expanded, and we rose.

No wings bore us. The garden simply receded, whilst the colossal palm remained beside us for a little while, its enormous leaves passing downward through ranks of cloud. Then the entire valley lay revealed: an oval cup of purple jungle enclosed by black mountains and hemmed on every side by the advancing sea. The cottage had vanished. The promontory was a ragged stump upon which waves fought like pale beasts.

We ascended through aether coloured rose, saffron, and opal. About us gathered multitudes of luminous singers—children, women, young men with brows like tranquil emperors—whose bodies seemed now substantial, now composed of flame. Their music entered not merely the ear but every faculty; I tasted it as honey, saw it as revolving geometries, and felt it move through my thoughts like a cool wind through grass. The plague, my illness, my former griefs, and even the habitual burden of personality loosened and fell from me. Above, beyond strata of golden vapour, shone an archipelago of green islands suspended in a silver firmament.

“Teloe,” whispered the amber woman.

The child tightened its fingers about mine.

“Whatever you hear,” it said, “do not look below.”

We rose higher. The earthly sky darkened beneath us, whilst the celestial country grew clearer overhead. I could distinguish terraces, domes, blossomy hills, and slender cascades descending endlessly into light. A fragrance like roses after thunder surrounded us. I believed myself saved.

Then, beneath the harmony, I heard it.

Pound—pound—pound.

The sound was faint, yet no music could conceal it. It travelled upward through leagues of air, the old detestable rhythm which had pursued me from the sickroom to the cottage, from the cottage to the forest, and from the forest into heaven. The singers increased their song. Cymbals of crystal rang; voices bright as morning gathered into majestic concord. Still the pounding continued.

“Do not look,” said the child.

There exists in terror a curiosity stronger than obedience, stronger perhaps than the instinct of life. I looked.

The globe of Earth lay below, enormous yet complete, turning in a gulf of blackness. At first it retained its familiar divisions of blue ocean and brown-green land, though every sea was convulsed beneath cyclonic systems broader than kingdoms. Then I perceived that the waters were climbing the continents.

Along the coasts of Asia, Europe, Africa, and the Americas advanced one continuous white circumference. The seas crossed beaches, drowned harbours, and rushed into river mouths; they traversed fields and forests, divided about hills, reunited across plains. Cities shone briefly beneath the encroaching foam. I saw towers, bridges, factories, monuments, and innumerable roofs crowded with specks which I knew to be men. Bells rang in a thousand steeples. Fires reddened the tempest. Great vessels were carried over sea walls and hurled through streets, whilst multitudes fled toward elevations already surrounded.

The ocean ate and ate.

England diminished to a cluster of dark ridges. The Mediterranean overspread Italy, leaving the Apennines as momentary islands before swallowing them. The Atlantic poured through the valleys of North America; the Pacific crossed China in a gleaming, murderous sheet. Desert sands vanished under black tides. Snowy mountains protruded alone, crowded to their summits by the remnants of nations; then waves, rearing higher than those mountains, folded over them.

I tried to turn away, but the sight had acquired a magnetic sovereignty over my will. The luminous beings rose without me—or I fell from them; I cannot determine which. The child’s hand slipped from mine. The music receded, whilst the planetary roaring grew.

Soon no continent remained. Earth was a sphere of water wrapped in cloud, ever turning, ever darkening. Yet even then the catastrophe had scarcely commenced; for at a point in the southern ocean there appeared a black line, narrow at first as a scratch upon glass. It lengthened from pole to pole.

The globe cracked.

The fissure widened and widened. Into it the universal sea began to drain, slowly at first, then with a velocity beyond imagination. Currents thousands of miles broad curved toward the abyss. The clouds themselves were dragged downward in spirals, and the naked ocean-bed emerged behind the retreating waters.

What was disclosed there has poisoned all my subsequent dreams.

Upon plains which had lain beneath miles of sea stood drowned cities older than humanity. Their avenues extended in geometrical immensities; their pyramids were hollow and many-windowed; their temples bore domes shaped like crouching brains. Colossal statues, green with submarine decay, represented beings with finned hands, triple faces, and eyes arranged in vertical rows. In the Atlantic deeps appeared a continent of red stone, traversed by canals and bordered with terraces upon which forests of bone-white trees still stood. Under the Indian Ocean, mountain chains rose around black citadels whose battlements had been fashioned before the moon attained its present orbit. Everywhere the withdrawing sea unveiled roads, walls, amphitheatres, machines of unheard-of design, and the remains of prodigious creatures which had ruled Earth during epochs omitted from every geology.

Still the waters fell. The familiar continents were but recent scabs upon an immemorial planet, and beneath them, layer after layer, emerged the ruins of successive worlds. I saw buried suns carved upon basalt; gates large enough to admit migrating nations; towers whose foundations penetrated the glowing interior of the globe. Some cities seemed newly abandoned. Lights moved in their windows.

Then the sea reached the fissure in its entirety.

No language can reproduce that sound. It was not a roar, nor thunder, nor the united shriek of all living beings, though it contained and surpassed each of these. The planet bellowed like a wounded god. Water struck the incandescent deeps, and steam erupted from the wound in a white column wider than Europe.

It engulfed me.

The radiant singers vanished. Teloe vanished. I was alone amid boiling vapours, turning helplessly above a world in dissolution. Through rents in the steam I saw the crust buckle. Continents, newly stripped of water, slid toward the chasm; ancient cities toppled in files; mountains leaned together and broke. The fissure glowed first crimson, then yellow, then an intolerable white. Around the planet ran branching wounds, dividing hemisphere from hemisphere.

“It is the end,” said a voice, perhaps my own.

Earth swelled.

For one suspended instant it became perfectly silent. The ruined globe hung beneath me, webbed with fire, an enormous dark fruit ripened to corruption. The moon stood nearby, pale and indifferent. Beyond it burned the sun, and beyond the sun the humorous stars.

Then Earth exploded.

Fragments of oceans, mountains, sepulchres, palaces, beasts, forests, and human habitations rushed outward in a sphere of flame. The moon was struck and spun away, shedding silver dust. A burning continent passed so near that I discerned upon it the broken palm of the final garden. Afterward came darkness filled with expanding embers.

I beheld—though by what faculty I cannot say—the other planets proceeding in their appointed courses. Mars turned his scarred face toward the vacant orbit; Venus shone through a veil of cloud; gigantic Jupiter, attended by his moons, rolled onward like a king searching the night for a lost child. They circled and circled, seeking their vanished sister. But Earth was gone, and the cold stars looked down without grief.

The pounding ceased.

I awoke in my own bed at noon.

The physician sat beside me, whilst the nurse wept near the window. They told me that for fourteen hours I had lain without perceptible motion, my pulse at times scarcely discoverable; then, shortly before waking, I had cried out so violently that those in the adjoining rooms believed death itself had entered the house. The plague had spared me. Within a month I could walk, and before the year ended my health was outwardly restored.

Outwardly—yes.

But on nights when rain touches the panes, I smell sandalwood and cadaverous flowers. In the groaning of ships, the turning of mills, the distant passage of trains, I hear that measured percussion. Sometimes, at sunset, clouds assume the semblance of green islands suspended in a silver firmament, and I seem to discern a radiant child who beckons but no longer smiles.

Physicians assure me that the whole vision arose from fever, exhaustion, and an accidental excess of opium. I have accepted their explanation in conversation, for reason requires such civil hypocrisies. Yet I know that beneath our oceans lie roads no living cartographer has traced; I know that the sea remembers every kingdom it has consumed; and I know that somewhere beyond the mutable heavens waits Teloe, inaccessible now because I looked back.

As for opium, no persuasion, agony, or sentence of approaching death could induce me to taste it again. For I dread not the dream itself, but the possibility that it was no dream—and that the pounding, which even now sometimes trembles beneath the quiet operations of the world, is merely the sea beginning to remember its appointed hour.

# The Price of a Morning

“Henry Spear?” repeated Arnold Thorndike.

His secretary, who had learned to repeat nothing unless repetition was demanded, glanced at the letter in his hand.

“Yes, sir. He was employed here four years ago. Stenographer in the railroad department.”

“What does he want?”

“A character reference.”

“What has he done?”

“Stolen eight hundred dollars.”

Thorndike looked up.

The daily audience in the private office of Arnold Thorndike had begun at nine precisely. At nine-three a western railroad president had been soothed, at nine-seven a cable concerning a Balkan loan had been answered, at nine-eleven a museum director had been told that Mr. Thorndike would not pay seventy thousand dollars for a doubtful Tintoretto merely because another millionaire desired it, and at nine-fourteen Henry Spear, charged with theft, entered by letter into the affairs of the Wisest Man in Wall Street.

Thorndike held out his hand.

The secretary gave him the letter. It was written by an attorney whose name conveyed nothing, and it stated, with the confidence peculiar to those who ask favors from powerful men, that Henry Spear had pleaded guilty, that sentence would be passed Friday morning, and that any assurance as to his previous good character might have weight with the court.

“Eight hundred dollars,” said Thorndike. “From whom?”

“His present employers. Leavitt Brothers, wholesale leather.”

“And they are prosecuting?”

“They recovered the money. Apparently they are not vindictive.”

“What did he do with it?”

“Lost most of it gambling after he had been drinking.”

“Then he stole it while sober and lost it while drunk.”

The secretary permitted himself a thin smile.

“The attorney puts the drunkenness first.”

“They usually do. What do we know of him?”

The secretary consulted a memorandum.

“Entered our service at nineteen. Remained two years and eight months. Accurate, punctual, obliging. Left for a better salary. No complaint against him.”

“Then say so.”

“I have drafted something.”

He read:

“‘In reply to your inquiry concerning Henry Spear, I beg to state that during his employment with this company his services were satisfactory, and that nothing reflecting upon his honesty came to our attention.’”

Thorndike nodded.

“That is exact.”

“It is not enthusiastic.”

“Enthusiasm,” replied Thorndike, “is not exact.”

He took up a cable. Henry Spear ceased to exist.

This disappearance was neither cruel nor unusual. Hundreds of men entered Thorndike’s office every year by door, telephone, cable, memorandum, petition, introduction, and rumor. Those who represented large interests remained longest; those who represented small interests remained exactly as long as their business required. It was not Thorndike’s fault that the world had arranged itself in varying denominations.

At fifty-two he directed banks, influenced railroads, lent money to governments, embarrassed governments by refusing to lend it, and possessed a collection of paintings which persons who had never seen them discussed with reverence. His friends declared that he wasted no time. His enemies declared that he wasted no mercy. Both statements were exaggerated, but each contained that fragment of truth without which a public reputation cannot flourish.

The letter concerning Spear was signed and dispatched.

On Friday morning Thorndike breakfasted at eight in the glass room of his house overlooking the park. The day was the first candid day of spring. The trees, which for a week had been making guarded promises, had suddenly trusted the weather. The air was clean, the sky young, and along the bridle path horses and riders moved with a brightness that made even exercise appear agreeable.

Thorndike was reading two newspapers and refusing grape-fruit when his sister entered.

“You’re dining with me Sunday,” she said.

“If I am in town.”

“You said that last Sunday.”

“I was not in town.”

“You were in the library.”

“That is frequently farther away.”

She removed one of his newspapers.

“Arnold, do you remember the young man who used to bring flowers to Mother?”

Thorndike looked annoyed, chiefly because he did remember.

“What young man?”

“The stenographer. Spear.”

The name, after its two days of extinction, returned.

“Henry Spear?”

“I think that was it. Mother was very fond of him.”

“He is being sentenced this morning for theft.”

His sister sat down.

“Sentenced?”

“He took money from his employer.”

“Oh.” She considered this without the businesslike ease with which Thorndike had considered it. “What will happen to him?”

“I don’t know.”

“Can’t you do anything?”

“I have written a letter stating the facts.”

“Was it a nice letter?”

“It was a truthful one.”

“That means it was horrid.”

Thorndike reclaimed his newspaper.

His sister, who had never been intimidated by organizations possessing less authority than Providence, continued:

“He used to bring Mother violets. Not florist’s violets—those tight, purple, expensive things—but little wild ones. He picked them himself.”

“I remember.”

“He came all the way from somewhere in New Jersey. He said the flowers grew near the station.”

Thorndike remembered more than he desired. His mother, then invalided in the great bedroom at the back of the house, had waited for those modest bunches with an eagerness she had never shown toward the orchids sent by directors and ambassadors. Spear had been a shy, long-limbed boy with an ill-fitting coat and a singular gravity toward old ladies. Once, when Mrs. Thorndike’s nurse had forbidden visitors, he had left the flowers below with a note apologizing because the stems were short.

“Mother said,” his sister added, “‘That boy has a gentle heart.’”

“Mother,” said Thorndike, “discovered gentle hearts very readily.”

“Yes. That was one of her successes.”

She went out.

Thorndike read six lines concerning copper, three concerning a tariff, and none of either. In the park the sunlight moved over the young grass. A delivery boy passed the house carrying a flat basket of flowers, and the sight irritated him by its obvious irrelevance.

He rang.

His secretary, who breakfasted early so that the financial world might breakfast late, answered from the office.

“What time is Spear sentenced?”

There was a pause.

“I’ll find out.”

“Find out now.”

Within four minutes the answer came. Sentence would be passed at ten-thirty in a criminal court downtown.

“What engagement have I at ten-thirty?”

“The Alton reorganization committee.”

“At eleven?”

“The French bankers.”

“At twelve?”

“Mr. March regarding the western merger.”

“Put the committee at eleven, the Frenchmen at twelve, and March at one.”

“The Frenchmen sail at two.”

“Then they had better be concise.”

“May I ask—”

“No.”

Thorndike hung up, finished his coffee, and ordered his motor.

He had no intention of spending the morning in court. His plan, as he conceived it, possessed the simplicity of all plans made by men accustomed to having other persons remove the complications. He would drive downtown, see the district attorney, say a few words concerning Spear, receive the grateful assurances of that official, and proceed to his office. Twenty minutes might be lost. At the current valuation of his time the act would cost perhaps a thousand dollars.

This calculation pleased him. A thousand dollars was a respectable sum to give away before ten-thirty, especially when given in minutes.

The car carried him south through the awakening city. On the rear seat lay a portfolio containing papers upon which the ownership of miles of railroad depended. Beside it was a catalogue from a London dealer who offered a portrait alleged to be by Gainsborough. Thorndike read neither. The spring air entered through the lowered window and brought with it smells of wet pavement, horses, gasoline, river damp, and flowers displayed outside shops.

At the courthouse the motor halted before broad steps darkened by traffic and weather. Men lounged against stone pillars; women climbed slowly, holding children or parcels; policemen directed a stream of anxious humanity through doors which appeared to swallow hope more readily than they returned it.

Thorndike’s chauffeur opened the door.

“Wait,” said Thorndike.

“For how long, sir?”

“Twenty minutes.”

The chauffeur looked toward the courthouse and, being experienced in the more stubborn facts of life, said nothing.

Thorndike mounted the steps and passed from sunlight into the gloom of an echoing rotunda. At once the atmosphere offended him. It was damp, acrid, overheated, and populous. The floor was tracked with mud. Voices rose toward a dirty dome and fell back in fragments: names, oaths, questions, pleas, the laughter of court attendants, the crying of a child. Around him moved policemen, bondsmen, attorneys, witnesses, prisoners’ relatives, collarless men with the gray faces of bad nights, overdressed young women, exhausted mothers, sharp-eyed idlers, and boys who stared at every uniform as though rehearsing a future acquaintance.

Thorndike looked for an information desk. There was none where an information desk should have been.

He stopped a uniformed officer.

“I wish to see the district attorney.”

“Which one?”

“The district attorney.”

“Got business with the chief?”

“I have.”

“Third floor. Ask outside Part Six.”

The officer turned away before Thorndike could thank him, or correct him, or explain who he was. It was Thorndike’s first intimation that the building contained persons indifferent to explanation.

He entered an elevator crowded beyond what he considered its legal capacity. A woman carrying a covered cake pressed against his right arm; a laborer smelling of rain and tobacco occupied his left; behind him a man whispered repeatedly, “Don’t say nothing till Mike comes.” Thorndike drew his coat over his watch chain and discovered that the laborer was standing upon his shoe.

“Third!” shouted the elevator attendant.

Thorndike emerged into a corridor lined with benches. Here the air was hotter and the waiting more visible. Men waited with hats between their knees. Women waited with fixed eyes. Lawyers waited while pretending not to. Every door bore numbers and initials whose significance was evidently known to everyone except the great man.

“Part Six?” he asked.

A clerk jerked his thumb.

At Part Six an attendant sat behind a rail reading a sporting page.

“I wish to see the district attorney,” said Thorndike.

“Name?”

“Arnold Thorndike.”

“Case?”

“It concerns Henry Spear, who is to be sentenced this morning.”

“You counsel?”

“No.”

“Witness?”

“Not exactly.”

“Relative?”

“Certainly not.”

The attendant looked up for the first time.

“Then what are you?”

Thorndike had never been required to define himself in so narrow a space.

“I am here to speak concerning the prisoner’s character.”

“See Mr. Andrews.”

“And where is Mr. Andrews?”

“In his office if he’s there. End of the corridor, turn left, room forty-eight.”

Thorndike proceeded to room forty-eight. The door stood open. Inside, two young men dictated, a policeman telephoned, a woman sobbed, and a red-haired assistant district attorney in shirtsleeves ate a sandwich while examining a file.

“Mr. Andrews?”

“That’s me.”

“My name is Arnold Thorndike.”

“Glad to know you. Shut the door if you can find it.”

Thorndike remained standing. Andrews bit into the sandwich, signed a paper, and told the policeman:

“No, tell Cassidy I said bring the fellow in. If Cassidy says he can’t, tell him I SAID he can. If he still says he can’t, tell him to come explain the laws of nature to me.”

The policeman departed.

Andrews brushed crumbs from a chair.

“Sit down, Mr.—what was it?”

“Thorndike.”

“Sure. I know. Banks.”

“Among other things.”

“I’ve got cousins in banks. They don’t own ’em.”

Thorndike sat, though sitting had not formed part of his plan.

“I came about Henry Spear.”

Andrews found the file under his elbow.

“Pleaded guilty to grand larceny. Took eight hundred from Leavitt Brothers. First offense. Twenty-four years old. Lives with his mother. Father dead. Got drunk, played cards, woke up famous.”

“His record while employed by me was excellent.”

“So your letter says.”

“You have seen it?”

“I can read when business is dull.”

Thorndike ignored this.

“I knew his family circumstances slightly. My mother took an interest in him. I felt that a personal word from me might be of service.”

“It might.”

“Then perhaps you will convey it to the judge.”

“You can convey it yourself.”

“I had not intended to appear in court.”

Andrews looked genuinely surprised.

“What did you intend to appear in?”

“My engagements—”

“Yes, I’ve heard of ’em. Everybody’s got engagements. Spear’s got one with the State for from one to ten years.”

Thorndike’s expression cooled.

“I came here voluntarily, Mr. Andrews.”

“That’s what makes it nice.”

“I supposed the matter could be handled without unnecessary delay.”

“There isn’t any necessary delay in this building. It’s all unnecessary. That’s our specialty.”

A telephone rang. Andrews seized it.

“Yes? No. NO. If the complainant won’t identify him, you haven’t got a case; you’ve got a social call. Let him go.”

He hung up and turned again to Thorndike.

“Spear’s on the calendar in Part Four. Judge Bannerman. They begin sentences at eleven.”

“I was told ten-thirty.”

“That’s when everybody’s told to come.”

“It is now ten-forty.”

“Then you’re doing fine.”

“How long will it take?”

Andrews laughed—not rudely, but with the expansive delight of one to whom the question offered rare nourishment.

“You finance railroads, don’t you?”

“Yes.”

“How long does it take a train to arrive?”

“That depends upon the train.”

“NOW you understand courts.”

Thorndike rose.

“I cannot remain indefinitely.”

“Nobody can. That’s why we die.”

“Will you tell the judge I was here?”

“I’ll tell him. But if you want to help Spear, stay where he can see you.”

“I do not perceive the importance of that.”

“Spear might.”

Andrews pulled on his coat, swept three files into his arms, and led Thorndike into the corridor. The financier followed because the alternative required a definite surrender, and Arnold Thorndike did not surrender indefinitely. He surrendered only from minute to minute.

At a turn near the stairs Andrews was captured by a clerk. Thorndike waited while they argued over bail. He consulted his watch. Ten-forty-seven. His committee would already be collecting in the paneled room above his office. Men whose time was also expensive would be asking where he was. The absence of Arnold Thorndike from an appointed chair was not an absence; it was an event.

He beckoned to a messenger boy.

“Telephone this number. Tell Mr. Larkin I shall be delayed until eleven-fifteen.”

The boy examined the card.

“Telephone’s downstairs.”

“Then go downstairs.”

“Can’t leave this floor.”

“Why not?”

“I work here.”

Thorndike stared at him. The boy stared back with the invincible calm of public employment.

Andrews returned.

“Come on.”

They descended a flight of stairs, crossed a bridge-like passage, passed through two courtrooms, and entered a chamber filled with people. Andrews pointed to a bench.

“Sit there.”

“I prefer to stand.”

“Not when the judge comes in.”

“Where is Spear?”

“Downstairs. They’ll bring him.”

“When?”

“When they do.”

Andrews vanished through a gate.

Thorndike stood beside the bench. It was narrow, hard, and already occupied by a thin woman in black, an elderly man with a carpenter’s hands, and a broad young fellow who smelled powerfully of hair tonic. None moved. Thorndike sat upon six inches of government property and placed his hat between his knees.

The courtroom possessed none of the solemn grandeur which architecture had promised. Its walls were stained, its windows dusty, its benches scarred by generations of suspense. Lawyers crossed and recrossed the enclosure; clerks arranged papers; an officer called names which were answered from remote corners by frightened voices. Behind Thorndike a baby began to cry. Its mother whispered, “Hush, honey, Papa’ll come,” in a tone suggesting that Papa’s return depended upon the baby’s silence.

At ten-fifty-five Thorndike sent a court attendant to telephone his secretary. The attendant accepted the coin and the message. At eleven-ten he returned.

“Your office says shall they hold the committee?”

“Tell them yes.”

The man remained.

“Well?”

“Another nickel.”

Thorndike gave it to him.

At eleven-twenty the judge had not appeared. Thorndike’s indignation, having exhausted the court, turned upon Andrews. He saw the assistant district attorney laughing with a lawyer near the rail.

“Mr. Andrews!”

Andrews approached.

“I have now been here nearly an hour.”

“You’re getting acclimated.”

“I must leave.”

“All right.”

The ease of the permission annoyed him.

“You said my presence might assist Spear.”

“It might.”

“But you do not regard it as essential?”

“Nothing’s essential except the judge, and we’re not dead sure about him.”

“You are trifling with me.”

“No, sir. I’m busy. If you stay, Spear’ll know somebody outside his own little crowd thought enough of him to stay. If you go, he won’t. The judge may notice it, or he may not. You wanted facts. Those are the facts.”

Thorndike reached for his hat.

At that moment a side door opened and a group of prisoners, escorted by officers, entered the enclosure.

They came in without chains, which made their captivity more apparent. Some attempted bravado; some stared at the floor; one smiled toward the benches with desperate animation. Henry Spear walked third.

Thorndike recognized him slowly. The shy boy had become a pale young man with a bruised look about the mouth. His clothes were neat but wrinkled, his hair carefully brushed, his shoulders held with the unnatural straightness of one who fears that any relaxation will be mistaken for collapse.

Spear glanced toward the benches. His eyes passed over the crowd, returned, and found Thorndike.

Hope transformed him.

It did not make him cheerful; it made him younger. For an instant he was again the long-limbed stenographer standing in a hallway with wild violets wrapped in damp newspaper. He half moved forward, checked himself at an officer’s hand, and bowed.

Thorndike’s fingers loosened from his hat.

Then someone touched his sleeve.

“Mr. Thorndike?”

The thin woman beside him had risen. She wore black gloves mended at the fingers, and beneath the edge of her veil her face was white with fatigue.

“I’m Mrs. Spear. Henry’s mother.”

Thorndike bowed.

“My son saw you,” she said. “I can’t tell you what it means to him that you came.”

He could have told her that he had not yet done anything, that he might leave before the case was called, that his presence was accidental in duration and uncertain in effect. These statements would have been exact.

Instead he said, “I am glad he saw me.”

Mrs. Spear smiled, and the smile, though small, placed an obligation upon him more securely than any contract he had signed that year.

They sat.

At eleven-thirty-two Judge Bannerman entered. Everyone rose. Thorndike rose because a court officer commanded him to do so. The judge, a heavy, silver-haired man with tired eyes, seated himself and immediately began consulting papers, apparently unconscious that Arnold Thorndike had been standing for him.

The calendar opened with the case of a boy who had broken into a pharmacy. His mother, whose covered cake Thorndike recognized from the elevator, was called to the rail.

“He ain’t bad, Judge,” she said. “He wanted money to go West.”

The judge asked why.

“He seen pictures.”

There was laughter. The mother did not laugh.

The boy had taken watches as well as money. He had been convicted before. The judge spoke sharply, then less sharply, and finally sent him to a reformatory. The mother held out the cake as he was led away.

“They won’t let him take it,” whispered Mrs. Spear.

Thorndike watched an officer shake his head. The woman remained at the rail with the cake in her hands until a clerk guided her aside.

Next came a gaunt man who had failed to support his children. His wife demanded punishment, then wept when punishment was promised. A foreman from a machine shop offered the man work if he were released. The judge suspended sentence.

“Don’t make a liar out of your foreman,” he said.

The foreman answered, “He won’t, Your Honor,” with such confidence that the gaunt man looked startled by the character suddenly assigned to him.

A young woman in a red hat was sentenced for shoplifting. She protested that the stores set things out to tempt poor girls and then acted surprised when temptation worked. Andrews replied that the Ten Commandments did not contain a department-store exception. Even the judge smiled. The girl did not.

An old man had forged a pension check. A waiter had struck a customer with a bottle. A clerk had taken money to pay a doctor and then lied about it. With each case there appeared from the benches some wife, sister, employer, neighbor, priest, union delegate, landlord, or improbable friend carrying a fragment of the accused person’s former life.

At first Thorndike viewed them as types: the broken laborer, the shrill wife, the sly youth, the sentimental employer. Then, under the pressure of names and details, the types refused to remain compressed. The broken laborer had a daughter with scarlet fever. The shrill wife earned twelve dollars a week pressing coats. The sly youth had surrendered because his younger brother had been arrested in his place. The sentimental employer had kept a job open for seven months.

Thorndike forgot to look at his watch for almost eleven minutes.

When he did, it was twelve-fifteen.

The French bankers were at his office. The western committee had either met without him—a form of insubordination—or had not met at all—a form of paralysis. The cable regarding the Balkan loan might require immediate action. March, who represented three hundred miles of strategic railroad, would be arriving at one. Somewhere clerks were waiting, telephones ringing, ticker tapes lengthening, and secretaries using phrases such as “unavoidably detained” with diminishing conviction.

Thorndike felt for the familiar alarm produced by neglected business.

It was present, but remote.

A dark-haired boy of nineteen stood before the judge. He had stolen tools from the garage where he worked. His employer would not take him back. His father had put him out. No one rose from the benches.

“Anybody here for this defendant?” asked the clerk.

Silence.

The boy turned his head slightly. He did not expect anyone, but the human body, Thorndike observed, may look toward hope after the mind has abandoned it.

“What will happen to him?” Thorndike whispered.

Mrs. Spear answered, “I don’t know.”

Andrews recommended a short sentence. The judge agreed.

As the boy was led away, Thorndike leaned forward.

“Mr. Andrews.”

The assistant district attorney looked over.

“That garage boy—what is his name?”

“Dolan.”

“Has anyone investigated whether work might be found for him?”

Andrews stared, then came nearer.

“Want to give him a job?”

“I asked whether anyone had investigated.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because there are forty Dolans a week and one of me.”

Thorndike frowned as if this were a defective arrangement he might later reorganize.

“Where is he being sent?”

“Workhouse. Three months.”

“Could the sentence be reconsidered if employment were available?”

“Maybe. You opening a branch office up the river?”

“I may know someone who can use him.”

Andrews’s expression changed. His irreverence remained, but something respectful entered behind it.

“Give me a name before court adjourns,” he said. “I’ll see what can be done.”

The next defendant was called.

By one o’clock Thorndike had obtained from Mrs. Spear the name of the garage boy’s employer, from Andrews the address of a lodging house where another defendant’s children were staying, and from a probation officer a confused explanation of why certain men could be released if someone would guarantee employment. He had also lent his pencil to a woman who did not return it and moved farther along the bench to make room for a priest.

No one in the courtroom now seemed aware that he was the Wisest Man in Wall Street. He was the gentleman with Mr. Andrews, the man who had a pencil, the person sitting beside Mrs. Spear. His public identity, which usually preceded him like a brass band, had shrunk to these local dimensions.

To his surprise, he did not resent it.

At one-ten the clerk called:

“People against Henry Spear.”

Mrs. Spear’s hand closed upon the edge of the bench.

Spear stepped before the rail with his attorney, a small, earnest man whose coat had been made for a broader advocate. Andrews rose for the prosecution. Thorndike sat very still.

The attorney spoke first. He admitted the theft without decoration. Spear had been drinking for several days; he had taken a customer’s payment from his employers’ desk; he had lost part of it in a card room; on recovering sobriety he had confessed, surrendered what remained, and helped his family raise the balance. He had pleaded guilty. He had never before been arrested.

Then Mr. Leavitt, the employer, came forward.

“Henry did wrong,” he said. “I’m not excusing it. But he worked for us three years, and before this he was honest. My brother and I don’t want him sent away if there’s another way. We’ll take him back—provided he quits liquor.”

Judge Bannerman looked over his spectacles.

“You would reemploy a man who stole from you?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Why?”

Mr. Leavitt shifted his weight.

“Because he told us where the money went before we knew it was gone. Because his mother paid what she couldn’t afford. And because if every man’s worst day was the only day counted, there wouldn’t be many of us working.”

The judge turned to Spear.

“Is drink your difficulty?”

“Yes, Your Honor.”

“Can you leave it alone?”

Spear swallowed.

“I have to.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No, sir. I mean—yes, sir. I can.”

His attorney produced letters. There was one from a clergyman, one from a former schoolmaster, one from a superintendent in Thorndike’s railroad department, and the exact, unenthusiastic statement from Thorndike’s office. The judge read them.

“Is Mr. Arnold Thorndike in court?”

A murmur moved along the benches.

Thorndike rose.

“I am, Your Honor.”

Spear turned. Mrs. Spear drew a breath.

The judge regarded Thorndike with public deliberation.

“Do you wish to address the court?”

This was the moment for which Thorndike had supposedly sacrificed his morning. He had imagined himself standing with a few decisive sentences prepared, the machinery of mercy responding to the pressure of his influence. But now Mr. Leavitt had said what mattered, the schoolmaster had remembered what mattered, the mother had paid what mattered, and Spear’s own white, ashamed face said more than a financier’s polished endorsement could add.

Thorndike answered:

“I came because Henry Spear was once in my employ and was well regarded there. Others who know him better have spoken more fully. I should merely like the court to understand that I am willing to assist him in making a new beginning.”

The judge inclined his head.

“That is an important assurance.”

Andrews recommended probation. He did so briefly, almost casually, but Thorndike noticed that he laid particular emphasis upon the employers’ confidence and the presence of men prepared to help.

Judge Bannerman addressed Spear.

“You have been more fortunate in your friends than your conduct deserves. That is not a reason for punishing you; it is a reason for expecting more of you. Your employers, whom you wronged, ask mercy. Your former associates give you a good name. Your mother has stood beside you. And a citizen whose affairs are known to be numerous and important has thought it worth his time to appear personally in your behalf.”

Thorndike felt every face in the courtroom turn toward him. The sensation, ordinarily agreeable, now caused him embarrassment.

The judge continued:

“Men of large responsibility often believe civic duty can be discharged by subscription. Money is useful. Presence is sometimes more useful. Mr. Thorndike’s attendance here is an example of generous citizenship, and the court is pleased to acknowledge it.”

There was a faint stir of approval. Andrews, catching Thorndike’s eye, winked.

Thorndike had subscribed to hospitals, libraries, relief funds, universities, missions, orchestras, and committees for causes whose names required two lines of type. At dinners he had heard himself praised for generosity in terms more elaborate than the judge’s. Yet no testimonial had ever given him the same warm and rather boyish satisfaction as this wholly excessive tribute for sitting three hours upon a hard bench.

The judge suspended sentence and released Spear on probation, upon condition that he abstain absolutely from intoxicating drink, report regularly, and remain employed.

“If you take one glass,” said Judge Bannerman, “you may find the prison door at the bottom of it. Do you understand?”

“Yes, Your Honor.”

“Then don’t look back. Next case.”

The machinery moved on.

Mrs. Spear did not. She sat with both hands pressed to her face.

Thorndike bent toward her.

“It is all right,” he said.

She nodded, but tears escaped between her fingers.

“It is all right,” he repeated, with the confidence of a man accustomed to making statements become facts.

Spear was detained a few minutes for papers. When he came through the gate, his mother embraced him. He submitted awkwardly, conscious of the courtroom, then held her with sudden force.

Thorndike turned away. Andrews appeared at his shoulder.

“Well,” said Andrews, “cost you a morning.”

Thorndike cleared his throat.

“I had intended to speak more.”

“You did fine.”

“I said almost nothing.”

“That’s why.”

Spear approached. He could not at first find words. Thorndike rescued him.

“Your employers are taking you back?”

“Yes, sir.”

“You will accept?”

“Yes, sir. If they still mean it.”

“They mean it.”

“I’ll never touch liquor again.”

“Do not say ‘never’ to me. Say it to yourself tomorrow evening, and the evening after that.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And if the position with Leavitt Brothers proves unsuitable, come to my office. Not today. My office appears to have enough trouble today. Come next week.”

Spear’s face altered again, not with the violent hope Thorndike had first seen, but with steadier courage.

“Mr. Thorndike, I don’t know how to thank you.”

“By not requiring me to return here on your account.”

Spear almost smiled.

His mother took Thorndike’s hand in both of hers.

“Your mother knew,” she said.

“Knew what?”

“What kind of man you were.”

This was praise beyond the judge’s and much less deserved. Thorndike attempted to withdraw behind dignity, but Mrs. Spear’s grip prevented the maneuver.

“My mother,” he said, “was an optimistic woman.”

“She was right about Henry.”

“Then Henry must prove her right about both of us.”

Outside the courtroom the corridor remained crowded, but it no longer presented an undifferentiated mass. The woman with the cake sat upon a windowsill, waiting to learn where she might send it. The foreman was lecturing his reclaimed workman. The wife who had demanded punishment was arguing with a clerk about visiting hours. Each anxious figure carried a private case more complicated than the file which represented it.

Thorndike stopped beside Andrews.

“The Dolan boy,” he said. “I have a machine-shop superintendent in Jersey City. Telephone Larkin at my office. Use my name. If the boy is competent, there may be work.”

Andrews studied him.

“You collecting prisoners?”

“I am attempting to complete one matter before beginning another.”

“That’s bad court technique. Nothing’s ever completed here.”

“Then we shall begin.”

They found a telephone in Andrews’s office. Thorndike called Wall Street.

His secretary answered with the controlled desperation of a man defending civilization alone.

“Where are you?”

“At the courthouse.”

“I know that. The committee has adjourned. The French bankers waited until twelve-forty. March has been here twenty minutes. There are cables from Paris and Buenos Aires. Mr. Field says the market—”

“Tell March I shall not see him today.”

There was silence.

“Not today?”

“No.”

“Are you ill?”

“Not yet.”

“What about the Paris cable?”

“You answer it.”

“I?”

“You know my position.”

“I know three of your positions.”

“Choose the wisest.”

“Mr. Thorndike—”

“And send someone to the courthouse with my car. Mrs. Spear and her son are to be driven home.”

“The car has been waiting there since ten-twenty.”

Thorndike had forgotten it.

“Excellent. Then no one need send it.”

“What are you going to do?”

Thorndike looked through the dirty office window. Beyond the roofs a strip of spring sky shone with unreasonable brightness. Andrews had put on his hat and was gesturing toward the door.

“I am going to luncheon.”

“With whom?”

“With Mr. Andrews.”

The secretary knew when disaster had exceeded the value of protest.

“Shall I expect you this afternoon?”

“No.”

This second silence was longer.

“Have you been sentenced too?”

“Possibly. Good day.”

He hung up.

Andrews laughed.

“You treat him rough.”

“He treats me efficiently. It amounts to the same thing.”

They descended the stairs together. On the way Andrews was stopped five times—by a detective, a bondsman, a probation officer, a woman seeking her husband, and the attorney in the red-hat case. Thorndike waited without consulting his watch.

At the rotunda Spear and his mother stood uncertainly near the entrance. Thorndike gave the chauffeur their address and saw them into the motor.

“But how will you go?” protested Mrs. Spear.

“I shall walk.”

“Walk?” repeated the chauffeur.

The word traveled among them like news of a financial panic.

“Yes,” said Thorndike. “People do.”

Spear leaned from the car.

“I’ll come next week, sir.”

“Come sober, come early, and bring no flowers. We have enough decoration.”

The motor moved away.

Thorndike and Andrews emerged into sunlight. The city, which in the morning had appeared to Thorndike as an organization imperfectly managed by others, now seemed crowded with persons on errands of their own. Office boys hurried past; trucks thundered over the stones; women bargained at flower stalls; an old man slept on a bench with his face turned toward the sun. From somewhere near the river came the hoarse call of a steamer.

“Where do you lunch?” asked Andrews.

“At the club.”

“They’d throw me out.”

“They would first ask whether you were with me.”

“That might make ’em throw us both out.”

Thorndike considered this prospect with unexpected pleasure.

“Where do you lunch?”

“There’s a place around the corner. Soup’s honest and the waiters aren’t.”

They walked toward it.

At the corner Andrews asked, “What was so special about Spear?”

Thorndike watched a flower seller arrange small bunches of violets in a tray.

“He used to bring flowers to my mother.”

“That all?”

“No,” said Thorndike. “Apparently it was not all.”

At luncheon Andrews told him of probation, overcrowded prisons, boys held because no one would offer work, men released because some obscure employer trusted them, and women who crossed three states to tell a judge that a defendant had once been kind. His facts were disorderly, his language irreverent, and his conclusions frequently contradicted one another; but Thorndike listened with an attention he had denied that week to two ambassadors and a senator.

After luncheon they returned to the courthouse to inquire about Dolan. At three o’clock Thorndike spoke by telephone with the machine-shop superintendent. At three-thirty he met a probation officer. At four he promised nothing, suggested much, and discovered six things which money could accomplish and four which required a man to appear in person.

When at last he and Andrews came out again, the long spring light lay across the courthouse steps.

Andrews stretched.

“Well, Mr. Banks, what did it cost you?”

Thorndike understood the question. Somewhere downtown his secretary was measuring the wreckage: postponed conferences, offended Frenchmen, unanswered cables, a fluctuating market, a merger delayed, hours squandered at the highest prevailing rate. In the morning Thorndike had valued twenty minutes at a thousand dollars. Since then he had lost nearly a day.

He looked back at the dark entrance through which tired people still passed. He thought of Spear’s face when hope found it, of Mrs. Spear’s mended gloves, of the covered cake, of the garage boy turning toward an empty bench, and of his mother holding short-stemmed violets as though they had been brought from a kingdom.

Then the Wisest Man in Wall Street smiled.

“Whatever it cost,” he said, “it was worth it.”

# The Ash of Castringham

Those who have had the good fortune to travel through the eastern counties of England at leisure—not in a motor-car, which confounds observation with arrival, but by such slower means as permit a church tower, a line of elms, or the unexpected gleam of a moat to declare itself gradually—will remember a particular class of country house, neither very ancient nor altogether modern, which belongs peculiarly to that region.

Its front, as the stranger approaches, may offer a pillared portico, a pediment, urns, and a coat of arms in stone; but a turn of the drive betrays gables behind the parapet, or a mullioned window imprisoned in eighteenth-century brickwork. The architect of Queen Anne’s or the first George’s reign has done his best to persuade the house that it is Italian; yet the older English building remains beneath, patient and inconvenient, with passages at unexpected levels, stairs too narrow for the dignity of the façade, and chambers whose windows look not upon the prospect intended for them, but into some court, garden, or thicket belonging to an earlier disposition of the ground.

I confess to a weakness for such houses. Their libraries contain books which have not been bought by the yard; their portraits, though often indifferent as paintings, preserve faces which the family has had cause to remember; and their gardens are apt to include one or two trees which no surveyor would willingly measure and no gardener willingly condemn. The reader will therefore excuse me if I describe Castringham Hall somewhat more particularly than is necessary to the principal matter. Indeed, I am not sure that the principal matter can be understood without the description.

Castringham lies in the northern part of Suffolk, within a few miles of the Norfolk border. The Hall stands low, upon ground sloping gently toward a little river, and is approached through an avenue which was planted, according to the estate books, in 1712. The present front dates from 1736 and is of pale brick, with stone dressings. Behind it remains a considerable portion of a house of Henry the Eighth’s time, and perhaps something earlier still. At the western end is a square projection containing, on the first floor, the room formerly called the Great West Chamber. Its principal window, though altered, looks into a small enclosed garden; and within twenty feet of that window there stood, until the middle of the eighteenth century, an ash of extraordinary age and dimensions.

No picture of the tree survives which can be trusted. A painting in the Hall, once supposed to represent it, shows an oak of the conventional landscape-painter’s sort, with cattle reposing beneath; while a small engraving in a county history of 1821 substitutes something resembling a Lombardy poplar. We are better served by a memorandum of Mr William Crome, rector of Castringham from 1739 to 1768, who measured the stump after the tree’s destruction. He gives its circumference at the ground as thirty-one feet and nine inches, adding that “the Heart thereof was wholly gone, tho’ the outward Timber continued of a surprising Toughness.”

It is with this ash, and with the room overlooking it, that my narrative is concerned.

The Fells had been seated at Castringham since the reign of Elizabeth. They were not a brilliant family; but they supplied the county with magistrates, deputy lieutenants, one member of Parliament, and a dean whose sermons enjoyed the rare distinction of a second edition. Sir Matthew Fell, who succeeded to the estate in 1677, was a man of strict habits, respectable learning, and considerable local authority. His portrait shows a long, pale face, a narrow mouth, and grey eyes which suggest neither credulity nor indulgence. This is worth observing because the earliest testimony in the affair rests chiefly upon his word.

In the spring of 1690 there occurred in Castringham parish a series of petty misfortunes such as, in less uneasy times, would probably have been attributed to weather, disease, or negligence. Several calves died; the rector’s wife miscarried; milk turned sour in two cottages; and a child, having fallen into a pond, was rescued only after being thought drowned. Suspicion fixed itself upon a poor woman named Anne Mothersole, a widow of uncertain age, who lived in a cottage on the waste.

The name occurs frequently in that part of the county. There is no proof that she came of any family other than the ordinary labouring class, though later accounts made her the daughter of a wandering scholar, an Irishwoman, and even a foreigner from the Low Countries. She was said to possess remedies for ague and burns, to gather plants by moonlight, and to have foretold the death of a farmer’s wife. Such accomplishments were dangerous in 1690, particularly when joined to poverty, an irritable temper, and the dislike of one’s neighbours.

The depositions taken before Sir Matthew and preserved among the Castringham papers contain the usual matter of witchcraft prosecutions: a spectral child, a black dog, a pain in the shoulder, a cake which would not rise. Most of it is foolish, some malicious, and none relevant to my account, except a statement made by Sir Matthew himself.

I reproduce it with its spelling partially retained. The original is in a cramped legal hand, and a blot has rendered three or four words uncertain.

> “The Examination of Sr Matthew Fell, Knt., touching Anne Mothersole, Widdow, taken the xiiijth Day of Aprill, 1690.
>
> “This Examinant saith, That being abroad somewhat after Eleven of the Clock at Night, the Moon shining very cleare, he did perceive a Person close under the great Ash by the West End of his House; and conceiving the same to be one of his Servants, did call aloud, but received noe Answer.
>
> “That going neerer he saw it to be the said Mothersole, who had in her Hand a Hook or crooked Knife, and was cutting small Twigs from the Tree.
>
> “That he required her to declare her Business, whereupon she looked upon him very spitefully and put somewhat into her Bosom. He then advanced toward her, intending to apprehend her, but at that Instant there came a Hare from between the Roots of the Tree, and ran toward the old Church Way.
>
> “That he saw noe Person afterward in that Place, though the Ground was open and the Wall too high for any Woman to pass over.
>
> “That on a second Night he caused Watch to be made, and saw again a Hare, being of uncommon Greatness, which was struck by his Keeper with a Staff; and on the Morrow the said Mothersole was found lame upon her right Leg.
>
> “That this hath happened three several Times, or appeared so to happen, but whether by Witchcraft, Poyson, meer Thought, or some Horrid and Artfull Designe, this Examinant will not presume to determine.”

The last caution has led one modern writer to acquit Sir Matthew of all responsibility for what followed. That is charitable, though perhaps not entirely just. He was the principal magistrate; he signed the commitment; and at the assize he repeated his evidence. Yet he also urged the jury, according to a contemporary letter, to “separate what was seen from what was imagined,” which was more than most persons in his position would have done.

Mrs Mothersole was convicted.

The judge’s charge has not survived, nor can I discover that the trial attracted attention beyond the neighbourhood. She was hanged at Bury in June of that year. A chapbook account, printed many years later and plainly embellished, describes a tempest, the extinction of the scaffold lamps, and a flock of birds circling above the gallows. The weather register kept by a gentleman at Thetford records a calm and cloudless day. We may dismiss the tempest and, I think, the birds.

One sentence, however, appears in two independent sources. As Mrs Mothersole was brought from the court, she turned toward Sir Matthew and said, in a voice which several persons heard distinctly:

“There will be guests at the Hall.”

The remark was remembered because of its oddity. If she intended a curse, it lacked the violence expected on such occasions. If she meant no more than that trouble would visit the Fell family, events soon supplied an interpretation.

Sir Matthew returned to Castringham and resumed his usual life. For some weeks nothing happened. The ash was watched, and no one approached it. Twigs found in Mrs Mothersole’s cottage were burned; her few possessions were sold; and her body was buried, not in consecrated ground, but beyond the north wall of Castringham churchyard, close to the foundations of an old charnel-house.

Then, on the night of 18 August, Sir Matthew dined with his chaplain and steward in the small parlour. The chaplain, Mr Poynter, afterwards wrote an account to his brother. It is preserved in the muniment room at Ely.

They had been discussing, of all things, the repair of the church leads. At about ten o’clock the steward left. Sir Matthew and Mr Poynter remained over a bottle of claret, the former unusually silent. At last he asked whether any creature known in England climbed trees at night.

Mr Poynter suggested a cat, a marten, or an owl.

“No,” said Sir Matthew. “It had no feathers. And it went upward as a spider goes upon a wall.”

“Was it a spider?”

“If it was, it was larger than any Christian man should look upon.”

He then described what he had seen from his chamber window on the previous night. The moon had been behind the tree, so that the trunk and branches were black against a luminous sky. Something had emerged from the grass, crossed the roots, and climbed the ash. At first he took it for a dog. When it reached the trunk, however, its body spread close against the bark, while “a confusion of legs”—these were his words—moved rapidly about it. It vanished into a hollow where one of the greater boughs had been torn away.

Mr Poynter laughed, though not heartily, and spoke of moonlight and leaves. Sir Matthew agreed that they might account for much.

That night he retired to the Great West Chamber. His valet, Robert Bush, attended him at eleven and afterward slept in a smaller room across the passage. Bush stated that he was awakened sometime before dawn by a sound of tapping or scratching. It was not loud. He compared it to thorn branches drawn repeatedly across glass. The sound continued for several minutes, ceased, and began again. He rose, opened his door, and listened. Hearing nothing in the passage, he returned to bed.

At seven the household found that Sir Matthew had not rung his bell. Bush knocked, then entered.

The casement beside the bed stood open. There had been no rain, yet the floor beneath it was marked by irregular dark stains. The curtains were partly torn from their rings. Sir Matthew lay upon the bed, dead and blackened as if by suffocation, though his face was composed and there was no swelling of the tongue. Upon his chest, immediately above the heart, were two small punctures separated by perhaps half an inch.

The county surgeon found no weapon. The coroner’s jury returned a verdict of visitation by God, with an additional note that the deceased might have suffered “a sudden corruption of the Blood.” The chaplain insisted that the chamber be searched for an adder, but none was discovered.

It is tempting to connect the death at once with Mrs Mothersole’s words and Sir Matthew’s many-legged creature. We must remember, however, that both the chaplain’s letter and the valet’s deposition were copied at least once before reaching us. The reader must judge for himself.

After Sir Matthew’s death the West Chamber was shut. His son, Sir Thomas Fell, disliked Castringham and spent most of his time in London. During his infrequent visits he used apartments on the south side. The ash remained untouched.

The parish books and estate accounts supply, over the next sixty years, a melancholy sequence of lesser incidents. They might have no significance if considered separately. Taken together, they are less easily dismissed.

In September 1698, two sheep left overnight in the West Garden were found dead beneath the ash. The shepherd supposed dogs had worried them, but the fleeces were not torn. In 1704 a young heifer died in the same place. Its hide, according to an apothecary’s bill, was “discoloured throughout the Forepart.” In 1711 three fowls disappeared from a locked coop beside the garden wall; feathers were found among the roots. A spaniel belonging to Lady Fell ran barking toward the tree after sunset in 1719 and did not return. The gardener declared that he heard it yelp “from within the Wood,” though the trunk showed no opening at ground level.

There were also stories among the servants. One concerned a hand seen upon the outside of the West Chamber window; another, a woman in a brown dress passing through the walled garden at dawn. Children were forbidden to gather ash keys there. The older gardeners would not prune the western branches, and one of them, an Irishman named O’Hagan, resigned when ordered to do so.

His words are preserved in a household memorandum:

> “Ye may loppe the Ewe, and ye may rive the Thorne,
>  
> But he that hews the Ash shall wish himself unborn.”

This has been quoted as an ancient Irish charm. I can find no other example of it and suspect Mr O’Hagan composed it for the occasion. It served its purpose, for the branch remained.

Sir Thomas died childless in 1728, and the estate passed to his nephew Richard Fell, then aged twenty-four. Richard was later created a baronet upon the accession of George II, though he had little interest in politics. His chief enthusiasms were building, drainage, horse-breeding, and the exposure of what he called “country errors.” He removed a reputed holy well, opened a barrow to obtain gravel, and broke up a carved stone long used by village women as a cure for toothache. The eighteenth century produced many men of his sort: active, useful, often generous, and possessed of that peculiar intolerance which mistakes every inherited caution for ignorance.

The present front of Castringham is his work. He concealed the old gables, enlarged the dining-room, moved the principal staircase, and supplied the entrance with a portico which, though admired in its day, looks somewhat surprised to find itself in Suffolk. He did not, however, disturb the West Chamber. His mother, who had known Sir Matthew, requested that it remain locked during her lifetime, and Richard, for all his impatience, was an affectionate son.

His alterations extended to the church. In 1738 he proposed adding a north aisle and constructing a family vault beneath it. The work required the removal of several burials lying outside the old wall, among them the grave attributed to Anne Mothersole.

The rector, Mr William Crome, protested—not upon supernatural grounds, but because he doubted the legality of interfering with a felon’s burial. Sir Richard obtained the necessary faculty and directed the work to proceed.

On 14 March the labourers uncovered a narrow oak coffin at a depth of four feet. The wood was dark and unusually hard. Its lid bore no plate, but the position agreed with an entry in the sexton’s book for 1690: “A.M., from Bury, without ye Wall.”

Mr Crome attended the opening. His diary records what followed:

> “The Coffin being raised, we found the lower Part much consumed, the upper black as with Fire. Upon taking away the Lid there was neither Bone, Dust, Hair, nor any Remain of a humane Body, but only a Quantity of dry Matter like burnt Leaves, and in one Corner a small Bundle of Ash Twigs bound with Thread.
>
> “Sir R. said some Villain had stoln the Corpse for the Surgeons. I answered that Surgeons seldom take the Trouble to replace and fasten the Lid. He did not relish the Observation.
>
> “The Men would by no Means touch the Twigs until Sir R. himself cast them into the Lime.”

The coffin was burned. The grave disappeared beneath the church aisle, and the matter was publicly forgotten. Privately, it revived all the old tales. An elderly woman informed Mr Crome that Mrs Mothersole had never been in the coffin; another asserted that she had seen the widow, unchanged in age, gathering berries near the ash in 1720. Neither story deserves serious attention, but both show the condition of local feeling.

Sir Richard’s mother died in 1753. By then the rebuilding of the Hall was complete, and the old West Chamber formed an awkward but handsome room at the end of a new gallery. Its carved oak panelling had been painted a pale green; the chimney-piece was replaced by one of veined marble; but the window remained, since altering it would have spoiled the symmetry of the outer wall.

In the summer of 1754 Sir Richard announced his intention of occupying it.

The decision was discussed at dinner in the presence of the rector; Mr Crome’s son Henry, then a fellow of St John’s College, Cambridge; Dr Pratt, the family physician; and the Bishop of Killaloe, an old university acquaintance of the rector who happened to be staying at Castringham. The bishop was a cultivated and agreeable man, with a taste for old books, ghost stories, and excellent port, though I do not know in what order.

“You will find the room damp,” said Dr Pratt.

“Then we shall discover whether damp or devils killed my grandfather,” replied Sir Richard.

The bishop looked toward the window. “And the tree? I am told it has a reputation.”

“It has the reputation of ruining the west wall, darkening three chambers, and frightening every gardener I employ. Those are offences enough. I mean to cut it down in October.”

Mr Crome laid down his glass.

“You may remember O’Hagan’s warning.”

“I remember that O’Hagan was paid wages for refusing work.”

“You have also opened a grave,” said Henry Crome, “and found it empty.”

“My dear Harry, if every stolen corpse were to preserve every inconvenient tree in England, the surgeons would have depopulated our parks.”

The bishop smiled. “Superstition is often absurd in the particular, Sir Richard, but occasionally prudent in the general. Men who fear a haunted tree do not sleep beneath falling branches.”

“I shall neither fear it nor sleep beneath it. I shall sleep beside it, behind a sound wall and a glazed window.”

He did so that night.

No disturbance occurred. Nor did anything happen during the following week. Sir Richard made a point, perhaps too often, of declaring the chamber dry, quiet, and comfortable. He ordered a writing table placed before the window and spent his mornings there examining accounts.

On 3 September he wrote to a cousin:

> “My new old Room answers perfectly. The celebrated Ash scratches the Glass when the Wind is West, which explains one Chapter of the Family Romance. As to Grandfather’s Death, Pratt inclines to an Apoplexy. The Marks may have been from the clasps of his Shirt, or from some officious Blood-letting after Death. Thus do two Buttons and a Branch make a Witch.”

The explanation of the scratching is plausible. Several boughs came within a yard of the casement; one, according to Henry Crome, nearly touched it. Yet the weather on the night of 6 September was still.

The household retired early. Sir Richard had spent the evening reading in the library with Henry Crome, who was preparing an edition of a minor Latin chronicler. Before parting, they amused themselves with the Sortes Biblicae—a practice which consists in opening the Scriptures at random and accepting the first passage seen as advice or prophecy. The practice has seldom improved either scholarship or religion, but has entertained many respectable persons.

Henry opened at Amos: “I saw the Lord standing upon the altar: and he said, Smite the lintel of the door.”

Sir Richard protested that this was architectural criticism and demanded another trial. His own finger fell upon a verse from Isaiah: “Their houses shall be full of doleful creatures.”

“That is better,” said Henry. “You are promised company.”

“Guests at the Hall,” replied Sir Richard, laughing.

The phrase, carelessly spoken, silenced them both. It was the last conversation Henry Crome had with him.

At some hour after midnight Henry awoke in a room along the gallery. He had been dreaming of a large clock whose pendulum scraped the inside of its case. On waking he found that the noise continued: scratch—pause—scratch, with a faint tremor following each contact.

He rose, lit a candle, and opened his door.

The gallery was dark and cold. At its farther end a narrow line of light showed beneath Sir Richard’s door. Henry walked toward it. As he approached, the scratching ceased.

He knocked.

Sir Richard answered immediately, but with irritation rather than alarm.

“Who is there?”

“Crome. I heard something at your window.”

“A branch. Go back to bed.”

“There is no wind.”

“Then the branch is diligent without assistance.”

Henry hesitated. He heard Sir Richard cross the floor and throw up the sash.

“There!” called the baronet. “Nothing. Unless your witch has become an owl.”

A moment later came a soft thud, as of something dropping upon the floor.

“What was that?” Henry asked.

“Nothing that concerns—”

The sentence ended abruptly.

The candle in Henry’s hand guttered. From within the room came a rapid succession of light impacts: not footsteps, exactly, but the patter of many small things striking wood and fabric. Sir Richard uttered a short cry.

Henry tried the door. It had been locked on the inside.

He called loudly. No answer came. The movements continued, passing from the window toward the bed. Something brushed against the inner face of the door. Then there was silence.

By now servants had appeared with lamps. The butler brought a poker, and two footmen threw themselves against the door. The lock held. A third blow split the frame, and they entered.

What they believed themselves to see in the first uncertain light was afterward much disputed.

The window stood wide. Between it and the bed several low, dark shapes moved with extraordinary speed. One servant described them as monkeys; another as bundles of cloth pulled along the carpet by strings. Henry Crome was more precise, though his precision does not make the image easier to accept. He said that each shape possessed a rounded body, “hairy or rough,” and that about it numerous jointed limbs rose and fell. One was upon the bed. Another clung to the curtain. Their heads—or what seemed heads—turned toward the lamps.

For perhaps a second the room presented this spectacle. Then the shapes scattered. One passed behind the bed; two reached the sill and went out; another ascended the curtain with a dry rustling. The footman nearest the window struck at it with the poker. He hit the frame and broke a pane.

Henry afterward wondered whether smoke from the lamps, moving curtains, and terror had created the whole appearance. The reader is free to adopt that conclusion. He must then explain the tracks upon the sill and the condition of Sir Richard’s body.

The baronet lay across the bed, dead and black in his nightclothes. Two punctures marked the skin above his heart.

Dr Pratt arrived before sunrise. He found no pulse, no wound beyond the paired marks, and no evidence of poison that the medicine of his day could recognize. The face and chest were darkly discoloured; the hands remained pale. A black fluid had stained the linen beneath the punctures.

The death was recorded as apoplexy.

No one slept in the West Chamber again.

Sir Richard’s heir was a boy, and the estate passed temporarily into the management of trustees. They had no wish to spend money on demolishing a tree which had stood for centuries and was, despite its hollowness, still vigorous. The ash remained.

Henry Crome, however, did not forget what he had seen. He visited the Hall repeatedly during the autumn, examining the window, branches, and ground. On the sill he found several coarse hairs, brown at the tips and black at the roots. Dr Pratt believed them animal, but could not name the species. Henry sent two to a naturalist in London, who replied that they resembled bristles from a foreign spider, though they were “of Dimensions almost preposterous.”

This letter survives. The hairs do not.

The decisive events took place the following spring.

Mr Crome kept at the rectory a large grey cat named Toby, celebrated in the parish for his appetite and ferocity. The animal accompanied Henry to the Hall on 21 April 1755 and followed him into the West Garden. There it became immediately interested in the ash.

Near the base, partly concealed behind ivy and a swelling of the roots, was a narrow fissure. It had probably opened during the winter frost. Toby sniffed it, drew back, and then approached again with his body low and tail enlarged. From within came a faint clicking noise.

Henry called the gardener, John Baines, who inserted a stick. The stick met no obstruction for several feet. When withdrawn, its end was wet and gave off a disagreeable, sweet odour.

The cat could not be persuaded to leave. It watched the opening for nearly an hour, uttering from time to time the peculiar low cry by which cats announce prey beyond their reach. At last Henry carried it away.

That evening Toby escaped from the rectory.

Shortly after midnight a groom crossing the Hall yard heard a cat screaming in the West Garden. He fetched Baines, and together they went with a lantern. Toby stood before the fissure, striking at something within. As the men approached, the cat thrust its head and forequarters into the opening.

There was a violent movement. Toby screamed once and was dragged forward.

Baines caught its hind legs. For a moment he felt a pull from within the tree so powerful that he was forced against the trunk. The groom seized him by the coat. The cat’s body convulsed; its claws tore strips from the bark.

Then the resistance ceased.

They drew Toby out. The animal was dead. Its head and shoulders were blackened, and two drops of dark blood stood upon its neck.

Baines refused to remain in the garden. The groom, who had more courage or less imagination, held the lantern near the fissure. He saw, several feet inside, a surface which shone like an eye and then withdrew.

By nine the next morning a party had assembled: Henry Crome, his father, Dr Pratt, the steward, Baines, two labourers, and Mr Hawkins, a neighbouring justice. The bishop, unfortunately for both him and us, had returned to Ireland.

Their first intention was to enlarge the opening and smoke out whatever occupied the tree. Henry wished to proceed cautiously. Baines, mindful of Toby, proposed gunpowder. The rector objected to gunpowder within so short a distance of his son and recommended sulphur.

While this discussion continued, one labourer climbed a ladder and inspected the upper hollow into which Sir Matthew’s creature had vanished sixty-five years earlier. He lowered a weighted cord and found that the cavity extended almost to the ground. When he drew the cord up, it was coated in a sticky grey substance resembling cobweb, but far stronger. Entangled in it were feathers, fragments of wool, and a small bone.

At this point no one spoke of rats.

A bundle of lighted straw and sulphur was pushed into the lower fissure. Smoke issued from the upper hollow and from three cracks among the roots, showing that the interior of the ash was largely empty. For several minutes nothing else happened.

Then a heavy object moved within.

The trunk gave a series of dull knocks. The ladder shook. From the upper opening protruded two long, jointed limbs, each covered in coarse dark hair. They felt about the bark, withdrew, and reappeared at another angle.

The labourer on the ladder dropped his lantern.

It struck a shelf of rotten wood inside the cavity. Oil spread over the dry fibres; flame ran upward with a speed no one had expected. The man leapt clear, breaking his ankle, while fire burst from the fissures and caught the ivy.

What follows is drawn from the independent statements of Henry Crome, Mr Hawkins, and the groom. Their agreement is close.

The ash begins to burn from within. Smoke pours through the broken crown, yellow at first, then black. The bark splits with reports like pistol-shots. Something scrambles behind it—one thing, then many.

A creature emerges from the lower fissure.

It is of the spider kind, but larger across than a dinner plate, with a swollen body and eight legs thick as a man’s finger. Hair covers it in ridges. Its colour is black, except beneath the joints, where the skin shows a livid red. It pauses in the sunlight as if blinded.

Baines strikes it with a spade. The body bursts under the blow, releasing dark fluid and a smell so powerful that he drops the tool and vomits.

Another follows. Then three more.

They come from the roots, from cracks in the trunk, from the hollow bough above. Some are no larger than a hand; others approach the first in size. They move toward shadow—under stones, into ivy, beneath the skirts of coats. Men shout and stamp. Dr Pratt beats one from the rector’s sleeve. A labourer runs across the garden with a creature clinging to his back until Henry knocks it away with a pole.

The fire climbs higher.

Now the great ones appear.

One forces itself from a split six feet above the ground and drops upon the grass with a soft, substantial impact. Its legs, extended, cover nearly three feet. The body is grey with age or ash; two polished clusters upon the front catch the daylight. It rears when Hawkins approaches and strikes at his stick with forelimbs that end in hooked points. Behind the mouth are two curved fangs.

Hawkins fires a pistol. The shot tears away part of the body, but the creature continues to move until a gardener’s mattock divides it.

A second enormous spider descends the outer trunk, keeping to the side away from the flames. For one dreadful instant its limbs surround a knot in the bark like the fingers of a hand. Then heat reaches it. It releases its hold, falls into the burning ivy, and contracts slowly, the legs folding inward while the hairs flame blue.

From all over the tree comes a dry, ceaseless rustling.

The creatures stream out by dozens.

Some escape over the wall, though searchers afterward claim to have destroyed most of them. Others retreat into the ground beneath the roots. The men pour oil into every visible hole and set it alight. Smoke emerges twenty yards away beside the church path, proving the existence of passages under the garden.

The ash, hollow to its crown, is beyond saving. By noon the principal limbs have fallen. One smashes the West Chamber window. Another tears away part of the garden wall. Toward evening the trunk collapses into itself in a column of sparks.

Only then does the rector remember Mrs Mothersole’s words.

The guests had come to the Hall long before. They had merely not been received.

During the next two days the men dug beneath the burned stump. They found a network of cavities, some natural, others perhaps enlarged by generations of occupation. The walls were lined with the tough grey web already observed in the trunk. In these chambers lay the remains of hares, sheep, dogs, birds, and smaller animals; buckles, bones, scraps of cloth, and a corroded spur were also recovered. One cavity extended toward the Hall and ended beneath the West Chamber wall. Marks in the masonry suggested that creatures had repeatedly climbed to the window by way of the ash’s western bough.

The deepest passage ran toward the churchyard.

At a depth of eight feet the labourers exposed a chamber between two great roots. It had been closed by fallen earth and thick layers of web. Within it, seated rather than lying, was the body of a woman.

The preservation was remarkable. The soil was dry, and the web had formed a close wrapping about the corpse. The dress, though decayed, was of late seventeenth-century pattern: brown wool, a linen coif, and a bodice fastened with pewter hooks. The neck vertebrae were displaced in a manner consistent with hanging. One leg showed an old injury below the knee.

About the wrists and breast were tied bundles of ash twigs.

Dr Pratt estimated that the woman had been dead for roughly fifty years, perhaps more. Mr Crome believed the body to be that of Anne Mothersole. No one present proposed another identity.

How it came beneath the tree cannot now be established. It may be supposed that friends removed the corpse from its coffin before burial and concealed it there. Against this is the apparent soundness of the coffin when opened, and the difficulty of carrying a body into a cavity then believed inaccessible. It may also be supposed—if one is prepared to suppose a great deal—that the coffin was never occupied. Yet the burial register states otherwise.

Nor is it clear what relation existed between the dead woman and the creatures. Were they foreign animals kept and fed by her, enlarged through some accident of climate or breeding? Had she discovered them in the ash and used their venom to support her reputation? Did the hare appearances arise merely because hares lived among the roots and emerged when she fled by some concealed way? These explanations are possible, though they leave several points untouched.

A country tradition offers a simpler and less acceptable answer: that Mrs Mothersole, gathering twigs beneath the ash, found there the ancient things which became her familiars; that she sent them against Sir Matthew; and that after her execution her body was brought back to their nest, whether by human hands or by others.

I do not recommend the tradition. I only record it.

The corpse was buried in consecrated ground in May 1755, without a name upon the stone. Mr Crome entered the service in his register and added the text, “The earth shall disclose her blood, and shall no more cover her slain.” The bishop, on receiving an account of the discovery, replied with another verse: “The spider taketh hold with her hands, and is in kings’ palaces.”

The West Chamber was repaired, though it continued unoccupied for many years. When the Hall was restored in 1842, workmen found behind the panelling a number of small, blackened bodies resembling spiders, and one shed skin of exceptional size. These were thrown away before the owner could examine them. Such is the ordinary fate of evidence in old houses.

The ash itself is gone. A circular hollow remains in the lawn where the roots were burned out, and the grass there is said to grow poorly. I visited Castringham in October some years ago and was shown the place by the present representative of the family. He treated the story with amusement, but admitted that no one had succeeded in planting another tree upon the spot.

We went afterward to the library, where I examined Sir Matthew’s deposition, Henry Crome’s papers, and the naturalist’s letter concerning the hairs. At dusk, as rain began against the windows, my host produced from a drawer a small glass case.

Inside lay a curved black object, pointed at one end and hollow at the other. It was rather more than two inches long.

“A thorn?” I asked.

“So my grandfather called it,” he said. “It was found in the frame of the West Chamber window.”

I thought it resembled neither a thorn nor any part of a British plant. I did not touch it.

That night I was given a room on the south side of the Hall. The rain continued, and a branch of ivy tapped intermittently against the pane. I knew very well what it was. Nevertheless, before going to bed, I fastened the shutters.

This may have been superstition.

It was also prudent.

For ten years Stuart McGregor was afraid of a dead man.

Not remorseful, be it understood. Remorse is a pale ecclesiastical emotion, nourished by candles, confessional boxes, and the soft admonitions of old priests. McGregor had spoken with priests. He had listened, dry-lipped and impatient, while Father O’Donnell urged repentance, restitution, prayer. He had even knelt once beneath the painted saints of a little church on West Twenty-Eighth Street, where the winter rain ticked against the windows like fingernails.

But repentance? No.

“I’m not sorry,” he had told the priest. “That’s the ugly part of it. I’d do it again. What I am is scared.”

“Of judgment?”

“Of Hutchison.”

Father O’Donnell had looked at him with gentle uncertainty.

“But Hutchison is dead.”

“That’s why I’m scared.”

Always Hutchison.

In the shriek of an elevated train rounding a black curve above Third Avenue, McGregor heard Hutchison’s last laugh. In the furtive rubbing of branches against his bedroom window, he heard the rasp of African reeds. In the steam pipes he heard drums—first a mutter, then a pulse, then the deliberate basso proclamation of death. Even the stars above New York sometimes took on the phosphorous green of equatorial insects and leered down through the smoke.

Always there was the eagle.

It had been tattooed across Hutchison’s broad back, from shoulder blade to shoulder blade: a blue-black bird with crimson claws, an ochre beak, and one sardonic ruby eye. The tattooist, some gin-soaked wizard of the Singapore waterfront, had given the creature a curious distortion. When Hutchison moved his right shoulder the skin puckered, the eyelid dropped, and the eagle appeared to wink.

It had winked at McGregor beneath the Bakoto moon.

It winked still.

Ten years had placed flesh on McGregor’s bones and silver in his hair. He lived well, dressed heavily, drank little, and collected African objects with the meticulous appetite of a man arranging the instruments of his own execution. His apartment was crowded with masks, carved stools, iron knives, beadwork, ivory trumpets, leopard teeth, antelope horns, and squat wooden gods whose polished bellies reflected the firelight. Visitors considered the collection picturesque. McGregor knew better. Each object was a sentry posted against the past, or perhaps an accomplice waiting to admit it.

At night the masks leaned from the walls.

The drums listened.

And from the dark glass of cabinets came the glimmer of a single ruby eye.

He remembered Africa most vividly in hot weather. On such nights New York ceased to be a city and became a displaced jungle of brick and iron. The asphalt sweated. Fire escapes resembled black vines. Motor horns cried like tormented beasts. The tunnels exhaled damp, subterranean breath, and the buildings retained the heat like boiler pipes in a factory. Then the years between dissolved. The curtains of the present drew apart, and McGregor saw again the Bakoto country beneath a moon that squinted like a diseased eye.

He and Farragut Hutchison had gone inland for ivory, though ivory had been the respectable name they gave their appetite. They were adventurers in the old, gaudy, disreputable sense—men with rifles, cards, lies, and a constitutional belief that whatever was weakly guarded belonged to the bold.

Hutchison was the larger man, red-bearded, loose-jointed, profane, and magnificently cheerful. He could repair a rifle with copper wire, cure a fever with quinine and curses, and sing seventeen verses of a Liverpool dock song without repeating the indecencies. McGregor was quieter. He watched. He calculated. Where Hutchison broke a door, McGregor found the hidden hinge.

They had been six months without civilized companionship when they reached the Bakoto village.

Six months!

Six months of blackish-green water, purple mud, red ants, blue flies, ochre dust, and vegetation that crept, clung, strangled, sweated. Six months of feverish dawns and crimson evenings; of crocodiles sliding from banks with ridiculous, inadequate splashes; of monkeys hurling insults from the treetops; of scorpions ticking beneath blankets—tchk-tchk-tchk—as though counting the seconds left to a man’s life.

Six months without a familiar face.

Without decent liquor.

Without cards played for anything more consequential than buttons, cartridges, or imaginary estates in Scotland.

When at last they entered the Bakoto village, they drank palm spirit with the recklessness of the damned. The stuff was warm, milky, and ferocious. It produced in Hutchison a desire to sing and in McGregor an impulse toward metaphysical mockery.

The fetish stood in a thatched enclosure near the chief’s house.

McGregor remembered the door: red wood, studded with brass nails, its threshold dusted with white powder. He remembered Hutchison raising the lamp. He remembered their laughter.

Inside squatted a figure scarcely three feet high, carved from dense black timber and crusted with sacrificial substances—feathers, clay, beads, dried blood, honey turned brown with age. Its belly was swollen; its arms were too long; copper eyes bulged from a face of solemn obscenity. Around its neck hung a chain of rough gold nuggets, and in its lap rested a little pouch sewn with seed pearls.

“Well,” Hutchison had said, breathing gin and palm spirit, “there’s your heathen banker.”

McGregor bowed elaborately.

“Good evening, sir. We’ve come to inspect the accounts.”

They took the gold. They took the pearls. Hutchison crowned the image with his filthy hat. McGregor poured liquor over its head, where the liquid trickled through feathers and clotted honey like amber corruption. Then, in a final flourish of drunken contempt, they dragged the fetish from its stool and rolled it upon the floor.

Something rattled inside it.

Both men stopped laughing.

From beyond the enclosure came a soft stirring, no louder than leaves turning in sleep.

“Rats,” said Hutchison.

Then the jungle spewed warriors.

They seemed not to enter but to materialize: tall, silent men with spears, knives, and shields painted in red and blue geometries. There was no shout, no theatrical rush, only a tightening ring of bodies and the tiny oily glints of spearheads. One instant the enclosure was empty. The next it had become a cage.

Hutchison reached for his revolver.

A spear touched his throat.

“Easy,” McGregor whispered.

The Bakoto chief arrived after dawn.

He was an old man with a narrow, composed face, draped in pale cloth and necklaces of polished bone. To McGregor’s disordered imagination he resembled Nero, a Manchu emperor, and an elderly professor of jurisprudence. He examined the fallen fetish, the empty chain, the spilled liquor, and Hutchison’s hat. Then he seated himself upon a carved stool.

“You were welcomed,” he said in the trade language.

His voice was grave and ceremoniously polite.

Hutchison spat dust from his mouth. “Looks that way.”

“You entered the sacred house.”

“No argument there.”

“You robbed the guardian of our fathers.”

McGregor said nothing.

“You made filth upon holiness,” continued the chief. “Therefore one of you must repair what has been broken.”

“How?” McGregor asked.

The chief’s eyes moved toward him.

For the first time, the old man smiled.

The explanation was given without passion. That made it ghastly. There would be a post in the clearing. There would be honey. There would be small knives, red ants, smoke, drums, and time. Especially time. The sacrifice must not die quickly, for an abrupt death would be discourteous to the violated guardian.

Hutchison’s ruddy face turned gray beneath its sweat.

“And the other man?” asked McGregor.

“The other shall go free, carrying the memory.”

“How do you decide?”

“We do not decide. You do.”

The chief inclined his head with hideous civility.

“You have shared the offense. Therefore you shall choose the payment.”

They were left beneath guard in a hut that smelled of dust, old smoke, and fear. Outside, the village moved with dreadful normality. Women pounded grain. Children laughed. Somewhere a goat bleated. From the forest sounded the shrill treble of insects and, beneath it, the slow experimental tapping of a drum.

Hutchison sat with his back against the wall.

The eagle spread its wings across him.

“Well, Mac,” he said, “which of us volunteers?”

McGregor watched a beetle crawl through the dust.

Neither spoke for a long while.

At last McGregor said, “We’ll play for it.”

“Play what?”

“One hand of poker. High hand walks.”

Hutchison stared, then laughed once.

“Cards? Here?”

“I’ve got a pack in my coat.”

The cards had escaped the search because the Bakoto guards had considered them meaningless painted paper. McGregor drew them out. They were warped by damp, stained with travel, softened at the corners by six months of use.

Hutchison rubbed his beard.

“One hand,” he said. “No draw?”

“One hand.”

“Cut?”

“Cut.”

The chief, when summoned, approved. Perhaps he recognized in the proposal some alien ritual equivalent to his own. A mat was laid between the prisoners. Warriors gathered at the door. The old man watched with the serene interest of a judge observing a curious but valid point of law.

McGregor shuffled.

He remembered every sound: the whisper of pasteboard; the buzzing of a fly; the crackle of a cooking fire outside; Hutchison’s breath moving harshly through his nose. He remembered the chief’s bracelets clicking together. He remembered his own hands, steady as machinery.

For during the night, while Hutchison had dozed and muttered, McGregor had prepared the deck.

There are men who cheat from greed and men who cheat for amusement. McGregor cheated from the pure, lucid concentration of terror. Four aces had been nested where his fingers could command them. The fifth card did not matter. Life and death lay arranged beneath his thumb.

Hutchison cut.

McGregor completed the cut.

He dealt.

Five cards to Hutchison.

Five to himself.

“Turn them,” he said.

Hutchison spread his hand: two queens, two tens, and a seven.

“A respectable funeral,” Hutchison muttered.

McGregor laid down four aces.

Silence.

The chief leaned forward. Even without understanding poker, he perceived the finality in Hutchison’s face.

Then Hutchison looked at the cards. He looked at McGregor’s hands. He looked into McGregor’s eyes.

Recognition came slowly.

That was the worst of it—not accusation, not rage, but the gradual extinction of comradeship.

“You cold-decked me,” he said.

McGregor’s throat clicked.

“No.”

“You did.”

“No.”

Hutchison stood. The guards lowered their spears.

For one instant McGregor believed the big man would spring across the mat and tear his throat out. Instead Hutchison turned his back.

The eagle’s blue-black wings shifted.

Its ruby eye closed.

Opened.

A wink.

“All right, Mac,” Hutchison said. “You win. But don’t get cocky. It’s a long road home.”

They took him into the clearing at sunset.

McGregor never afterward admitted how much he had seen. He claimed that the chief sent him away before the ritual began; then, in fever or drink, he would speak of honey glowing upon flesh, of knives that flashed like little red tongues, of smoke crawling low over the ground. He recalled the drums beginning slowly—boom... boom... boom—while a shriller instrument answered from across the river. He recalled ants moving in a black ribbon. He recalled Hutchison cursing the Bakotos, McGregor, Africa, poker, and the entire architecture of creation.

Most clearly he remembered Hutchison at the post, his back turned.

The eagle winked whenever the muscles jerked.

Again.

Again.

Again.

The chief released McGregor at dawn with his rifle, a little food, and the cards. The stolen gold and pearls had been restored to the fetish.

“You are free,” the old man said.

McGregor looked toward the clearing.

The drums had ceased.

“Is he dead?”

“He has paid.”

“Dead?”

The chief’s composed face revealed nothing.

McGregor stepped closer.

“Tell me he’s dead.”

“You wished to live,” said the chief. “Live.”

Then McGregor went out alone beneath the dripping trees.

The jungle closed behind him like a curtain.

For ten years he lived.

That was his punishment, though he would have laughed at the word. He made money in rubber, lost it in shipping, made it again in machinery. He crossed the Atlantic. He settled in New York. He acquired broad rooms, deep chairs, servants, a cellar, and a reputation for knowing African antiquities.

But uncertainty stalked him.

Had Hutchison died? Had the Bakotos prolonged the sacrifice for hours, days, weeks? Had he escaped? Had the chief released him also, by some malignant balancing of justice? Was he scarred, mutilated, and patient? Was he following McGregor from port to port, waiting until fear had ripened?

A dead Hutchison was terrible.

A living Hutchison was worse.

Then the two possibilities began to mingle. McGregor dreamed of a corpse paddling across the Atlantic in a canoe of human bone. He dreamed of Hutchison beneath the Hudson, red beard waving among blind fish, one hand pressed against the roof of a subway tunnel. He dreamed of the eagle peeling itself from rotted skin and flying over Manhattan, its wings blotting out electric signs.

“He’ll get me,” McGregor would whisper.

No one had been mentioned.

No one needed to be.

Father O’Donnell visited him twice a month, partly from duty and partly from pity. The priest was small, gray, and possessed of hands that looked incapable of violence. McGregor told him the story in fragments, never twice alike, yet always returning to the cards.

“Why four aces?” Father O’Donnell asked once.

“To make sure.”

“You might have dealt yourself a modest winning hand.”

“I wanted certainty.”

“And did certainty save you?”

McGregor glanced at the masks upon the wall.

“No.”

The priest urged him to confess the fraud fully, not merely narrate it as an exploit. McGregor grew angry.

“What do you want me to say? That I killed him? I didn’t tie him to the post.”

“You chose him through deceit.”

“I chose not to die.”

“And now?”

“Now I want proof.”

“Of forgiveness?”

“Of death!”

The proof arrived in August.

The day had been monstrous. A copper sun hung above the city; roofs quivered; horses stood in the streets with foam drying upon their bits. The air was thick enough to chew. By evening a purple storm massed beyond the Hudson but would not break. Thunder muttered without rain.

McGregor sat alone in his collection room wearing a silk dressing gown. The windows were open, yet no wind entered. The carved figures sweated oil. A necklace of leopard claws gave off a faint carrion odor. From below came the staccato clamor of traffic, the cry of a newspaper boy, the clang of a streetcar—urban noises distorted by heat until they resembled the grotesque orchestration of the Bakoto forest.

Boom.

McGregor raised his head.

Boom.

Not a drum. A servant knocking.

A crate had arrived from a dealer named Ezra Pike, who had recently returned from the Congo with objects obtained from missionaries, merchants, soldiers, thieves, and men who answered inconvenient questions with smiles.

The crate was long and narrow.

A note lay beneath the lid.

My dear McGregor,

Here is the signal drum I mentioned. I believe it comes from the Bakoto region and is unquestionably of unusual construction. The skin appears to be human, though I leave the final judgment to your superior knowledge. Observe especially the decoration. It may interest you.

Pike

McGregor read the note twice.

The room contracted.

“Open it,” he told the servant.

The nails squealed as they came free. Each shriek traveled along McGregor’s nerves. The lid rose. A smell escaped—not strong, but dry and ancient: dust, spice, leather, and the sweetish ghost of corruption.

Within lay a drum.

It was perhaps three feet long, pinched at the center, with a body of black wood carved into interlocking faces. Tiny teeth had been set around the rims. A cord of faded red fiber laced the drumhead in place.

The skin was white.

Not ivory-white, not the clean pallor of linen, but a ghastly yellow-white mottled with brown, like parchment kept in a tomb. Across it sprawled a faded tattoo.

Blue-black wings.

Crimson claws.

An ochre beak.

One ruby eye.

The servant heard McGregor laugh.

It began as a gasp, rose into a shout, and broke into peals of wild, almost boyish merriment. He seized the drum, thrust it toward the lamp, turned it one way and another.

“Hutchison!” he cried. “By God—Hutchison!”

The eagle’s body had been distorted by stretching, yet there could be no doubt. There was the crooked left talon. There the little scar interrupting one wing. There the ruby eye, half closed by a crease in the hide.

Proof!

The proof he had demanded through ten years of fear.

Hutchison had died in the Bakoto village. His skin had been taken, cured, stretched, and fitted to a signal drum. The dead man had not escaped. He had not crossed oceans. He had not crouched in alleys or breathed outside bedroom doors. He was leather. He was an artifact. He was dead beyond dispute, dead beyond revenge, dead beyond all those midnight possibilities which had multiplied like red ants in McGregor’s skull.

“Bring champagne!” McGregor shouted. “Bring every bottle in the damned house!”

The servant fled.

McGregor remained alone with the drum.

Outside, lightning flickered behind the clouds, turning the buildings phosphorous green. The storm still withheld its rain. Heat pressed into the room like a living bulk. Somewhere among the carved objects a beetle clicked.

McGregor placed the drum upright upon his knees.

“Well, Farragut,” he said, “you took the long road home after all.”

He stroked the tattoo.

The hide was warm from the air, almost flesh-warm.

A faint sound came from inside the drum.

Tchk.

McGregor froze.

Tchk-tchk.

He bent his head. Silence.

“Wood contracting,” he said aloud.

The room listened.

He laughed again, but less loudly. Then triumph swelled in him, obliterating caution. He clasped the drum against his chest as a man might embrace a recovered brother.

Something moved within.

There came a dry, muscular whisper; a swift sliding coil; a black, shining head shot through a split beneath the lacing.

McGregor saw a narrow mouth, scales green as putrescent bronze, and two eyes like polished beads.

The snake struck him below the jaw.

Once.

Twice.

He hurled the drum away. It rolled across the carpet with a hollow booming, while the reptile spilled from it in glossy loops. Long dormant in the cool darkness of crate and ship’s hold, it had revived in the infernal New York heat.

McGregor staggered backward.

His hand flew to his throat. Two points of blood appeared above his collar. They were absurdly small. Already fire raced downward beneath his skin; already the room tilted, lengthened, darkened.

The drum struck a cabinet.

Boom.

A spear fell from the wall.

Boom.

McGregor tried to shout for the servant, but his tongue had thickened. The masks leaned nearer. The carved gods bulged and grinned. Through the open windows came thunder, traffic, animal cries, laughter, curses, the basso and treble of two continents hideously combined.

The snake vanished beneath a chair.

McGregor dropped to his knees beside the drum.

The tattoo faced him.

As poison tightened his muscles, the years opened. He saw the hut. The cards. Four aces. Hutchison turning away. He heard again the ceremonious voice of the chief:

You wished to live. Live.

“Not—sorry,” McGregor rasped.

His breath came in fragments.

“Never was. But you—you said—you’d—”

The drum rocked gently.

Perhaps a floorboard shifted. Perhaps the snake moved beneath it. Perhaps McGregor’s failing sight altered the folds of that stretched and ghastly skin.

The eagle’s ruby eye closed.

Opened.

It winked.

McGregor gave a choking laugh, half terror and half admiration.

“Greek,” he whispered. “Damned Greek.”

Then the thunder broke, the rain descended upon New York with the roar of a tropical river, and the signal drum answered from the floor—boom, boom, boom—as though carrying to some immeasurable distance the news that Farragut Hutchison had won the final hand.

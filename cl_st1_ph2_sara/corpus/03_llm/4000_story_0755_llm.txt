# A Bale of Goat-Feathers

I am going to tell the truth about myself.

This announcement should put you on your guard, for whenever a man begins by saying he is going to tell the truth about himself, he has already selected the truth, brushed its coat, altered its trousers, and taught it to bow when his name is mentioned. Nevertheless, I shall do my best. I have always done my best, except when anything else offered itself.

I shall be fifty years old next Thursday at twenty minutes past three, unless some committee postpones it. Fifty is a disconcerting age. At forty-nine a man may still describe himself as a young fellow whose powers have not quite ripened. At fifty, people begin asking him what he has done with the powers after they ripened, and he has to admit that he left them on a trolley car while going to a meeting about Municipal Shade Trees.

I have had success. I do not deny it. Denial would be affectation, and affectation is a vice I have never had time for, owing to my numerous other vices. Twenty-two years ago I wrote a humorous story called “The Mayor’s Umbrella.” It was printed in a reputable magazine between an advertisement for liver tablets and a poem about twilight. People laughed at it. Editors wrote me cordial letters. A conductor on the New York Central recognized me, or said he did, and allowed me to remain in the smoking car after my ticket had expired. For six months I was regarded as a young author of astonishing promise.

That promise is now twenty-two years old and has begun to shave.

“The Mayor’s Umbrella” is still mentioned. Occasionally a man comes up to me at a banquet and says:

“By George, you’re the fellow who wrote ‘The Governor’s Rubbers’!”

I tell him I am, because life is short.

What I should have done after that story was to sit down and write another story. Then another. Then fifty others. By this time my collected works would occupy a shelf, my photograph would be printed in the Sunday supplements with my chin resting thoughtfully on my hand, and parties of schoolgirls would come from Indiana to steal sacred blades of grass from my lawn. The town might have placed a bronze tablet on my birthplace, even though I was born in a hospital that has since become a garage.

Instead, I gathered goat-feathers.

You do not know what a goat-feather is? That proves you have been leading a more useful life than mine.

A goat-feather is anything a man does which is not the thing he ought to be doing. It may be large or small, public or private, gilt-edged or moth-eaten. It may wear a silk hat and call itself a Civic Responsibility, or it may crouch behind the wastebasket under the name of Sharpening Pencils. It is still a goat-feather.

Goats, properly constructed, have no feathers. That is the point. A man may spend all afternoon collecting them and at sundown possess nothing but an aggravated goat.

I have accumulated, according to a conservative estimate made on the back of a dinner invitation, eleven tons, four hundred and sixty pounds, and three handfuls of goat-feathers. Had they any commercial value, I should be the Woolworth of goat-feathers. Had they insulating properties, my house would be warm. Had they nourishment, my creditors could eat them. But they are useless. I have merely packed my years with them until the useful minutes stick out at the corners like socks in an overfilled valise.

At the very moment when “The Mayor’s Umbrella” began to make my name known, I was elected secretary of the East Side Improvement Association. I did not live on the East Side, and I had never noticed that it required improvement, but a friend told me the work would take only one evening a month.

Nothing in this world takes only one evening a month except the full moon, and even that gets talked about beforehand.

The East Side Improvement Association met every Tuesday. It also held special meetings on Thursdays to decide what had been left undecided on Tuesday. On Friday the executive committee met to determine why nothing had been decided on Thursday, and on Monday I wrote minutes proving that all the meetings had been extraordinarily decisive.

During the first year we improved the East Side by passing a resolution.

It was a magnificent resolution. It had five whereases, seven semicolons, and a concluding clause so powerful that, if it had ever escaped from the secretary’s desk, it would have paved six streets and planted elms along both sides. I spent ninety-three hours composing it. In ninety-three hours I could have written three humorous stories, or one serious novel in which nobody is happy until the last chapter and then only because the book is over.

The resolution was a goat-feather weighing eight pounds.

After that came the Society for Supplying Window Boxes to Overworked Bookkeepers, the League Against Unsightly Hitching Posts, the Committee on Public Drinking Fountains, the Association for the Encouragement of More Cheerful Railroad Stations, and the Brotherhood of Men Who Believe Something Ought to Be Done About Smoke.

I was secretary of three, treasurer of two, honorary vice-president of four, and the only member who could be depended upon to bring a corkscrew.

Each office was presented to me as an honor. Honors are goat-feathers with ribbons on them.

Now, I am not saying that all outside service is foolish. During the war I worked for the Liberty Loan, the Red Cross, and two relief committees whose names were longer than their subscription lists. I made speeches in towns where the railway station consisted of a bench, a lantern, and a man who had not heard the news since 1898. I came home hoarse, hungry, and proud. Those hours were not goat-feathers. They were eagle-feathers, and I would gather them again.

An eagle-feather is time given to a need greater than your own.

Unfortunately, after a man has worn one eagle-feather, he begins to think every feather makes him an eagle. I performed patriotic service for fourteen months and afterward continued accepting committee work for seven years, three months, and nine luncheons. I marched out to save civilization and remained to select the color of the new clubhouse curtains.

That is how the goat gets you.

He does not come ringing the doorbell and say, “Good morning; I have called to ruin your career.” He comes with a letter on engraved paper:

“Dear Sir: In view of your well-known public spirit—”

When a letter begins by referring to your well-known public spirit, lock the desk, conceal the ink, and leave town by freight train.

My public spirit is not well known. It is merely unable to say no.

The telephone has helped. Alexander Graham Bell may have been a worthy man, but he put a bell in every home and arranged that anyone in the county could ring it while I was writing the best sentence of my life.

I have received, by careful count, 87,492 telephone calls. Of these, twelve concerned matters of genuine importance. One informed me that my aunt was ill. One told me my house was on fire. The remaining ten were wrong numbers, but they became important because the callers apologized briefly and hung up.

The other 87,480 calls were from persons who wished me to speak at dinners, contribute to funds, recommend nephews, identify quotations, settle wagers, explain jokes, buy tickets, lend manuscripts, sign petitions, attend receptions, or say whether I thought Abraham Lincoln would have favored daylight saving.

A woman once telephoned me at eight in the morning to ask for five hundred dollars for a Home for Aged Canaries.

“Madam,” I said, “I haven’t five hundred dollars.”

“Surely you can give two hundred and fifty.”

“I haven’t two hundred and fifty.”

“Then perhaps you could write us a humorous sketch to be sold at our bazaar.”

“About what?”

“About aged canaries.”

I spent the morning inventing a canary ninety-six years old who had forgotten how to sing but could whistle campaign speeches. The woman never called again. The Home for Aged Canaries may not exist. The sketch certainly does not. But the morning is gone.

One morning weighs approximately three ounces when it is ahead of you. When it is behind you and wasted, it weighs forty-seven pounds and sits on your chest at night.

Visitors have been even more expensive. A telephone call may be strangled at the wire; a visitor has a hat and must be treated as a human being until he leaves. My office has been visited by inventors, reformers, poets, actors, beggars, bores, insurance agents, and men who merely happened to be passing and remained until they were no longer passing anything.

One inventor brought me a machine for buttering corn by electricity. He required only twelve thousand dollars to place it on the market. He demonstrated it on my desk. The corn turned at nine hundred revolutions per minute, the butter struck the ceiling, and my typewriter wore a yellow overcoat for six weeks.

I did not invest. But I listened for two hours, wrote him a letter of introduction, and afterward spent another hour wondering whether electrically buttered corn might be the coming industry. That afternoon produced four fine goat-feathers and a small ear of corn behind the radiator.

Nor can I blame all my waste upon other people. Other people have tempted me, but I have met temptation more than halfway, dragged out a chair for it, and asked whether it took cream.

Once I began a story at nine in the morning. The first paragraph was excellent. I say this without vanity, because I have forgotten what it was about. The second paragraph was going to be better.

Before beginning it, however, I noticed that the letter R on my typewriter was sticking.

A disciplined writer would have struck the R harder.

I removed the typewriter ribbon. Then I removed the cover, the carriage, fourteen screws, and a small spring which sprang. By noon the typewriter looked like a hardware store after an earthquake. I cleaned every part with alcohol, oil, soap, powdered pumice, and a toothbrush belonging to my youngest son.

At two o’clock I discovered that the R had stuck because a crumb of toast lay beneath it. At four I had reassembled the machine, except for three screws which the manufacturer had evidently included from motives of generosity. At five the typewriter worked perfectly.

The story never did.

That day I did not merely gather a goat-feather. I plucked the goat, upholstered a sofa with him, and sat down upon it.

On another occasion I left my desk to look for a ruler. The ruler was in a drawer containing a design I had once made for a folding camp stool. The design reminded me that my daughter wanted a doll’s house. By midnight I had built the front wall of a Georgian residence, with eight windows, two chimneys, and electric wiring.

I worked on that house for eleven days.

It contained running water, miniature Venetian blinds, a dumbwaiter, a cellar furnace, and a burglar alarm which frightened the doll into a permanent horizontal position. I sent to Boston for wallpaper and to Philadelphia for hinges. The final structure cost $63.40, not counting my time, and weighed so much that my daughter used it as a seat while playing with the cardboard box in which the hinges had arrived.

Between paragraph one and paragraph two of my story, therefore, stood an eleven-room dwelling.

Do you see what I mean by goat-feathers? They grow into architecture.

There are men who never do this. I distrust them, naturally, but I envy them. They select one road and follow it. If the road bends, they bend. If it climbs, they climb. If a brass band goes by on another road, they do not chase it to discover where it is going.

I have always been the express train that leaves Chicago for New York, turns aside at Cleveland to investigate an attractive silo, proceeds south to hear a lecture on Egyptian beetles, spends August repairing a canoe in Tennessee, and eventually arrives in New York by way of Vancouver with a crate of peaches and no passengers.

Then I wonder why the other trains get there first.

Writers who began behind me have passed me. Men whose early stories I read with kind condescension now have twenty books, country houses, foreign editions, and secretaries who inform me that they are too busy to contribute to the Society for More Cheerful Railroad Stations. They had no more talent than I had. Some of them had less, which makes their conduct almost insulting.

But they stayed at their desks.

While I was addressing the Annual Dinner of the Consolidated Retail Awning Dealers, they wrote a chapter. While I was deciding whether the Civic Fountain should be granite or concrete, they wrote a chapter. While I was building a doll’s dumbwaiter, removing toast from my typewriter, and advising an inventor to reduce the velocity of corn, they wrote chapters.

Chapters are what books are made of.

Goat-feathers are not.

A man may possess genius enough to illuminate a city, but if he turns it upon twenty things at once he will not warm a sandwich. The sun itself, scattered over the earth, makes people comfortable; concentrated through a cheap magnifying glass, it sets the curtains on fire. Concentration is the trick.

This is not a new truth. It was old when Noah was interrupted in building the ark by a delegation advocating a municipal aquarium. But every man believes himself exempt. He thinks he can be a lawyer in the morning, novelist in the afternoon, reformer after dinner, inventor on Sunday, and a devoted husband whenever his wife locates him.

He cannot. He may be making a goat of himself.

The plumber who spends half his week making violins may remain an indifferent plumber and produce violins which leak. The farmer who practices tightrope walking while milking does not improve agriculture, acrobatics, or the cow. The surgeon who stops in the middle of an operation to compose a sonnet may eventually achieve a rhyme for “spleen,” but he will lose the applause of the patient.

A man’s central work must be central.

Yet how eagerly we move it to the edge! We say, “I shall begin the book as soon as I have answered these letters.” There are forty letters. Answer them, and forty-two replies arrive. We say, “I shall begin after I put my affairs in order.” Affairs have no order. They breed in drawers. We say, “I shall begin when the house is quiet.” A quiet house means the children have gone to college, the dog is dead, the furnace has stopped, and one’s hearing has failed.

Begin in the noise.

The world has never arranged a perfectly convenient hour for any worthwhile work. It sends telegrams. It knocks. It develops a leak in the kitchen. It remembers that the church roof needs shingles and that you once made a humorous speech in 1911. If you wait until nobody wants you, you may get six uninterrupted weeks at the cemetery.

This is the part of my confession at which I ought to become solemn. I am nearly fifty. The ledger is open. On one page is the talent I believe I had; on the other is the use I made of it. I say “believe” because a man naturally values his unused talent more highly than his used talent. Unused talent is perfect. It has never misspelled a word, bored a reader, or been rejected by an editor.

Mine, being almost entirely unused, is magnificent.

I could have been one of the great humorists of my generation. There is no way to disprove it now. I could have written six immortal books, three nearly immortal books, and one book which schoolchildren would be forced to read until they hated my name. I could have been translated into seventeen languages, including two in which my jokes might have improved.

Instead, I am principally the author of a story which strangers misname.

It is not poverty that troubles me, though poverty does telephone regularly. It is not the absence of praise; I have praised myself sufficiently to cover temporary shortages. It is the knowledge that days have gone where days always go and have taken my unwritten pages with them.

At twenty-five, a wasted week is amusing. At thirty-five, it is unfortunate. At fifty, it joins a procession carrying banners.

There are nights when I see the procession clearly: the Tuesday meetings, the unanswered stories, the ceremonious luncheons, the petitions, the inventions, the lost mornings, the sharpened pencils, the typewriter screws, the doll’s Georgian residence. They march past my bed in pairs. Each wears a goat-feather in its cap. The goat himself follows at the end, grown enormous, with my collected minutes hanging from his horns.

Then I leap up and resolve to reform.

My resolutions are powerful documents. They resemble the East Side Improvement resolution and have produced almost as much pavement.

I resolve that tomorrow I shall work from eight until one. I shall refuse telephone calls. I shall admit no visitors. I shall join no societies. I shall build nothing smaller than a barn or larger than a sentence. I shall write two thousand words before lunch and strike the R as hard as necessary.

At eight-ten the telephone rings.

“Are you the author of ‘The Mayor’s Umbrella’?” asks a timid voice.

“Yes,” I say, already pleased.

“Our association is planning a dinner—”

“No!”

“We hoped you might serve as honorary chairman.”

“No!”

“It would require no work whatever.”

This is the fatal phrase.

“What association?” I ask.

“The National Committee for the Preservation of Historic Weathervanes.”

I glance through the window. On the roof opposite, a tin rooster turns nobly in the wind. I have never considered historic weathervanes. The subject possesses sudden grandeur. Who preserves them? How old must a weathervane be before it becomes historic? Are there neglected weathervanes standing in lonely fields, pointing forever east because no one oils them?

“I could attend one meeting,” I say.

The goat has entered the room.

Therefore I am writing this warning to you, and especially to the young man or woman who has done one good thing and thinks that proves there is plenty of time for the second. There is not plenty of time. There is exactly the amount you defend.

Choose your work. Give it the first hour, not the exhausted scraps. Shut the door. Disconnect the telephone if you are brave; if not, tell the caller you have gone to preserve a weathervane. Let committees find other chairmen. Let the ruler remain lost. Let the R stick. Let the doll live in a shoebox. Do not polish the engine while the train is supposed to be traveling.

Above all, learn the difference between an eagle-feather and a goat-feather. Serve when service matters. Help when help is needed. A life narrowed entirely to self is merely another sort of failure. But do not call every invitation duty, every curiosity opportunity, or every interruption fate. Vanity often puts on the coat of generosity, and idleness wears a surprising number of official badges.

Ask of each new demand: Is this mine to do? Is it worth the hour it asks? Does it advance the central purpose of my life, or does it merely have an engraved letterhead?

If it is a goat-feather, drop it.

Do not save it for later. Goat-feathers multiply in pockets. One committee breeds three subcommittees. One harmless speech produces six banquets. One camp-stool design becomes an electrified doll’s mansion with plumbing. Before long you will be wrapped from head to foot in useless plumage, looking less like a successful man than a llama decorated for a lodge parade.

I know.

I have worn the costume.

And now, having set down this noble counsel, I feel greatly improved. Three thousand words of repentance ought to cancel at least eleven years of misconduct. Tomorrow I shall begin the new book. Nothing will stop me. I shall rise early, refuse all invitations, ignore the telephone, and concentrate with the terrible singleness of an eagle descending upon its prey.

There is only one small matter to settle first.

The Historic Weathervane people have asked me to design their membership certificate. It will take no work whatever. I have already sketched a border of clouds, lightning rods, and patriotic roosters. With a little gold ink it should be very handsome.

After that I shall write the book.

Unless, of course, I decide to construct a working model of the rooster.

The heat of the Sunday had lingered after sunset, caught between the brick fronts and shuttered windows, and all along Dame Street the lamps shone in the mild blue dusk like beads newly polished. Under them the people moved in streams: girls in light blouses, shopmen released from counters, soldiers walking with their sticks, clerks who had put carnations in their buttonholes. Faces appeared, brightened and vanished. The shapes of the crowd altered continually, dividing at corners and closing again, but above the shuffle of feet and the rolling of trams there hung one steady murmur, as if the city, unable to rest, were talking in its sleep.

Two young men came down from George’s Street.

The taller of them, Corley, walked with his chin raised and his eyes fixed ahead. His body had thickened early. The close-set shoulders, the large head shining through cropped black hair, and the slow swing of his arms gave him the air of a man conducting a procession arranged in his honour. A low-crowned bowler sat on his head with an exactness which made hat and skull appear parts of one construction. His blue serge suit was brushed, though glossy at the seams, and a pale handkerchief showed from his breast pocket.

His companion, Lenehan, kept half a pace behind. He was small and loose in the joints. Wisps of grey hair escaped beneath a soft brown hat set at a youthful angle, and his face, whenever it settled after laughter, showed a yellow, worn-out sadness. He wore pointed tan shoes, a raincoat too light for dignity and too warm for the evening, and trousers creased with great care. Every few moments he bent towards Corley, listening eagerly, then drew back to enjoy some remark with a thin wheezing laugh.

“So she says to me,” Corley was saying, “‘You’ll not forget me when you’re away?’ Away! Do you mind that, man? Me away!”

Lenehan laid two fingers on Corley’s sleeve.

“Ah, the irresistible exile! The broken hearts of Dublin packed in your portmanteau!”

Corley did not smile. He allowed the compliment to pass into him.

“She’s dead gone, you know. Properly gone. I can tell. There’s ways.”

“Ways!” Lenehan cried softly. “There speaks experience. There speaks the gay deceiver.”

They stopped to let a tram grind round the corner. Its windows passed one after another, each displaying a suspended interior: a woman with a parcel, two red-faced men, a child asleep against a sleeve. When it had gone, Corley stepped into the roadway without looking and Lenehan followed quickly.

“What did she give you last?” Lenehan asked.

Corley’s lips moved in a private smile.

“Cigars.”

“No!”

“A box of them. The old fellow she works for keeps them for visitors. Real good ones too. None of your cabbage leaves.”

Lenehan whistled. “A box! Upon my word, you’re living like a duke.”

“And before that there was the pair of gloves.”

“I saw the gloves.”

“And she paid for the jarvey out to Dollymount.”

“Did she now?”

“Every penny.”

Lenehan glanced sideways at his friend with admiration touched by uneasiness. Corley’s face remained forward, heavy and composed. The recital was not a confession but an inventory.

They entered College Green. The sky above the bank had faded from blue to a thin greenish light, and the columns stood pale against it. Outside a public house several men blocked the pavement, their voices bursting suddenly above the traffic and dropping again. Corley shouldered through them. Lenehan slipped after him, smiling apologies.

“And what class is she?” he asked. “I mean—is she a decent sort?”

“A slavey.”

“I know that. But is she—?”

“She’s all right.”

“Quiet?”

Corley gave a short laugh.

“She was quiet enough till I took her out.”

Lenehan’s mouth opened in appreciation. “You wicked dog.”

“I brought her down by the canal the second night. She wouldn’t at first. ‘Oh, Mr Corley,’ says she, ‘I never went walking with a gentleman before.’”

“Gentleman!” Lenehan repeated reverently.

“That’s what she said.”

“And did you correct the unfortunate girl?”

Corley turned his head. For an instant his broad face showed suspicion, but Lenehan’s expression was loyal and solemn. Corley looked ahead again.

“She’s not bad looking,” he said. “A bit soft about the mouth. Fine hair. She has money put by too.”

“Ah.”

“Seven pounds in the bank, she told me.”

“Seven sovereigns! The heiress of Fitzwilliam Square.”

“You may laugh. Seven pounds is seven pounds.”

“It is indeed,” Lenehan said quickly. “No man respects capital more than I do.”

They passed beneath the windows of the club. A carriage waited at the kerb; the horse lowered its head with the patience of an old servant. Corley slowed, drew a cigarette from a silver-coloured case and tapped it against his thumb. Lenehan watched the case.

“One of hers?” he asked.

Corley lit the cigarette before answering.

“Got it off another one.”

“Of course. A memorial from a former conquest.”

“She was married.”

Lenehan clasped his hands. “Corley, man, you take the biscuit. You don’t merely take it—you walk away with the bakery.”

His laugh came out in dry, failing gusts. A young couple passing them looked round. Lenehan recovered himself and brushed his moustache with one finger, as though the laughter had issued from someone else.

At the corner of Grafton Street they paused. The evening crowd moved slowly before the bright windows. Hats, flowers, walking sticks and pale summer dresses floated in the glass among pyramids of gloves, silver brushes and folded silks. From farther down the street came the weak notes of a harp.

Corley glanced at a clock.

“She’ll be there in ten minutes.”

“And tonight is the night?”

“That’s right.”

“You think she’ll manage it?”

“She said she would.”

“What exactly did you ask her to—?”

Corley drew at his cigarette. “That’s my business.”

“Certainly. Entirely. I am the soul of discretion.”

“She’s to see what she can do, that’s all.”

“A delicate mission.”

“She knows the old woman keeps it upstairs.”

Lenehan’s eyes sharpened. “Money?”

“I didn’t say money.”

“No, no.”

“And don’t be hanging around us either. She might get shy if she sees you.”

Lenehan’s face fell. “You’ll not leave me stranded for the night?”

“You can meet me after.”

“Where?”

Corley considered, enjoying the small authority of the decision. “The corner by Merrion Street. Half ten.”

“Half ten sharp?”

“Near enough.”

“And if the enterprise is crowned with success?”

“You’ll know.”

Lenehan rubbed his hands. “Excellent. A capital arrangement. Corley, you were born for diplomacy.”

They walked on towards the harp. Its player sat on a low stool beside a chemist’s window, his hat placed open near his feet. The instrument leaned against him, tall, brown and dulled by weather. One of its carved edges had been repaired with a strip of darker wood. The old man’s fingers moved through an air so frail that the street noises tore it continually; yet it gathered itself after each tram bell and each shout and began again. The harp seemed weary of the tune, weary of the bent man, weary of exposing its bare strings to every idle glance. Still it spoke, patient and reproachful.

For several steps neither man talked.

Then Lenehan began to tap the melody on the front of his coat.

“Mournful stuff,” Corley said.

“It has the grand old sorrow in it.”

“It would give you the hump.”

Corley threw away his cigarette.

Near Stephen’s Green he stopped beside the railings and inspected the people coming towards them. The last light lay in the leaves, and the enclosed lawns had the dim, composed look of property seen through a locked gate. Nurses pushed perambulators homeward. Two students went by arm in arm, disputing loudly. A woman selling flowers arranged her remaining blossoms beneath a lamp.

“There she is,” Corley said.

Lenehan looked too quickly.

“Where?”

“Don’t stare, man.”

A girl stood across the road near the corner. She wore a dark skirt and a straw hat with a band of red ribbon. Her blouse, tightly buttoned, strained a little at the shoulders. One hand held a small purse; with the other she touched the hat whenever a breeze came round the green. Though she pretended to examine the passing trams, her gaze travelled repeatedly towards the place where Corley stood.

“That her?” Lenehan whispered.

Corley adjusted his cuffs.

“She’s not bad,” Lenehan said. “Not bad at all. There’s health there.”

“I told you.”

“A pleasing figure. Domestic, but with possibilities.”

“Clear off now.”

Lenehan lingered. “Half ten?”

“Yes.”

“You won’t fail me?”

Corley turned his large head slowly.

“Clear off.”

Lenehan laughed, raised his hat and crossed the road. At the opposite pavement he turned as if interested in a shop window. In the glass he could see Corley approach the girl. Corley did not lift his hat. He planted himself before her, spoke, and after a moment she looked up at him with a broad, shy smile. He took her elbow and guided her away.

Lenehan watched them pass along the railings. Corley’s bulk leaned slightly towards the girl; her red ribbon bobbed below his shoulder. There was possession in the set of his arm and triumph in his heavy stride. He looked neither right nor left.

When they had disappeared, Lenehan remained before the darkened window. A row of walking canes, silver-topped and elegant, pointed down at him from their stand. He surveyed his reflection among them: soft hat, pinched cheeks, pale coat, bright shoes. From a little distance the whole arrangement might pass. It had passed often enough.

He set off down Grafton Street.

At first he walked briskly, humming the harpist’s tune and swinging his stick, though he carried no stick. He nodded to a bookmaker’s clerk whom he knew by sight. The man stared past him. Outside a tobacco shop Lenehan paused to read the racing results, tracing the names with his lips. He had backed nothing. In the window, cigars lay in polished boxes under small flags, and a gilt figure of a sailor offered cigarettes to the street.

He turned into Duke Street, came out again, and drifted towards Nassau Street. He had two hours to kill. Two hours could be pleasant with a shilling; with sixpence they could be managed; with three coppers they became a district one must cross on foot.

He put his hand into his trouser pocket and brought out his money.

Twopence.

He searched the other pockets, though he knew their contents: a tram ticket, a broken pencil, a collar stud and a pawn ticket already useless. From the lining of his waistcoat he rescued a third penny.

Threepence.

The sight of the coins made his hunger immediate. It rose from his stomach with a clean, hollow insistence. He remembered that he had eaten nothing since morning except half a roll taken from a plate in a public house while the barman’s back was turned.

He returned the money carefully to his pocket.

There were people he might meet. Toner perhaps, if Toner had been paid; or little Hanley, who never refused a drink when someone else proposed the round first. But Sunday was bad. Men belonged to their families on Sunday, or pretended to. They vanished behind curtains, into parlours, beside cold beef and pickles. Even the unmarried ones had sisters who poured tea for them.

At the bottom of Dawson Street he saw two acquaintances approaching. He prepared his face, giving it an expression of hurried prosperity, but they crossed to the other pavement. Had they seen him? One of them laughed at something. Lenehan looked into the window of a stationer’s until they had gone.

It was no matter. They were fellows with no breeding.

He wandered northward. The warm clarity of evening had thinned. Clouds had spread across the sky, and the lamps now cast definite circles on the pavement. A tram passed, bright and full, carrying people towards rooms, suppers, beds. Its iron noise dwindled between the houses.

The harp’s air returned to him. He tapped it with his forefinger against his thigh: three notes, a pause, three notes descending. The old fellow had known how to play it. Or perhaps any tune sounded affecting when a man was hungry.

Near Trinity College a shower began, not rain exactly but a floating dampness which showed itself in the lamplight. Lenehan turned up his collar. He walked beneath the trees, choosing the dry patches, then through Fleet Street, where the smell of beer came from open doors. Public houses drew him by their warmth and brass and noise. At each entrance he slowed, hoping to discover a familiar profile.

No one called him.

He pictured Corley seated snugly somewhere with the girl, receiving drinks paid for from her purse, speaking low, promising whatever was required. Corley never went hungry. There was always a girl, a cousin, a simple fellow newly paid. He had the genius of attaching himself to the full pocket.

Lenehan smiled despite himself. Corley was a character. A magnificent scoundrel. There were men who had education, men who had positions, and men who had Corley’s forehead. One had to admire success.

Yet the girl’s face came back to him: the quick smile, the trusting upward look. A servant girl. She would rise early, carry coal, polish grates, answer bells, and at night come out in her best blouse to meet Corley. Perhaps she believed he meant marriage. Corley would have hinted at it. He could hint heavily enough.

Lenehan dismissed the thought. Women knew the game. They were no saints either. Besides, what concern was it of his?

His hunger pinched again.

In a narrow eating-house off Capel Street he stopped before a steamed window. Painted on the glass, in flaking white letters, were the words HOT DINNERS. Inside, men sat at small marble tables beneath a gas jet. A girl in a dirty apron carried plates from a hatch.

Lenehan entered.

The warmth smelled of boiled vegetables, damp coats and vinegar. He chose a table near the wall and sat with his hat on his knees. The waitress approached, wiping her hands.

“What have you for threepence?” he asked.

“Peas and a bottle of ginger beer.”

“No bread?”

“Not for threepence.”

“Bring the banquet.”

She looked at him without expression and went away.

A labourer at the next table ate bacon, pressing pieces of bread into the grease. Lenehan avoided looking, but the scrape of the knife drew his eyes back. When his own plate arrived, the peas were pale and collapsed, gathered in a shallow pool of water. He shook salt over them, then shook more. The ginger beer fizzed when opened, and for a moment its sharp sweetness pleased him.

He ate slowly at first, preserving the appearance of choice. Then he bent closer and spooned the peas rapidly into his mouth. One escaped onto the table. He pursued it with the edge of the spoon, caught it and ate it too.

When the plate was bare, he raised the bottle and drained it. The meal had quieted his stomach without satisfying it. He sat back, reluctant to return to the streets.

Thirty-one years of age. Was that old? Not old, if a man had prospects. There were fellows who began at forty. But what was he beginning? Every year had left some mark on him and brought nothing in exchange. He had worn out friends, situations, stories and shoes. He had been messenger, canvasser, racing tipster, collector of small accounts. For three months he had written paragraphs for a sporting paper until the editor discovered that several horses praised by him had been dead for years.

Still, he had intelligence. Everyone said so. A tongue, a manner, a knowledge of the world. Give him one proper chance and he could hold a respectable place. Fifty pounds a year to begin with. Even forty, if regular.

With regular money a man might take two rooms in a quiet street. There would be a fire in winter and clean curtains. A small table set for supper. A wife—not a beauty, no need for that—but a warm, sensible girl with a little money and no grand relations. She would call him by his Christian name and complain if he stayed out late. On Sundays they would walk together, and people seeing them would know that he belonged somewhere.

The vision comforted him until he imagined the girl’s relations asking what position he held.

He looked at his reflection in the spotted mirror behind the counter. Under the gas, his face appeared older than it had in the shop window. The skin hung beneath his eyes; his moustache was thin; the damp had made his grey hair cling in separate threads to his forehead. He put on his hat and tilted it.

A rough night or two, that was all. A fellow recovered.

The waitress stood waiting for payment. Lenehan arranged his three pennies in a row, pushed them towards her and rose with a slight bow.

“You may convey my compliments to the chef.”

She swept the coins into her palm.

Outside, the shower had ceased. The stones shone black under the lamps. He consulted a clock over a jeweller’s door: five minutes past ten.

He was late.

A tremor of excitement passed through him. Perhaps Corley had already reached the corner. Perhaps the thing had failed and he had gone home. Worse, perhaps it had succeeded and Corley had gone drinking without him.

Lenehan began to hurry southward. His pointed shoes slipped on the wet pavement. He crossed before a tram, heard the driver curse him, and raised one hand in courtly apology. The city which had seemed so wide during his idleness now opposed him with crossings, slow walkers, carts and sudden knots of people.

At College Green he checked the clock again.

Twenty past ten.

He broke into a trot along Nassau Street, then slowed before reaching the appointed place, unwilling to arrive breathless. He wiped his forehead with the corner of his handkerchief and composed his mouth.

The corner was empty.

He walked past it, turned, and walked back. No Corley. He examined every approaching figure. A stout man in a bowler appeared at the far lamp; Lenehan’s heart lifted, then fell when the man entered a doorway.

Half ten sounded from a distant clock, each stroke travelling heavily through the damp air.

Corley might have altered the place. Had he said Merrion Street or the square? Lenehan repeated the conversation. The corner by Merrion Street. Half ten. Near enough.

Near enough could mean eleven to Corley.

He began another circuit, keeping the corner in sight. His mind moved among possibilities. The girl had lost courage. The housekeeper had discovered her. Corley had demanded too much. Or Corley had obtained the money and decided that one companion was easier to entertain than two.

That would be like him.

Lenehan’s admiration cooled. He recalled drinks he had paid for in better days, introductions he had given, lies he had told on Corley’s behalf. There should be honour among friends, even if there were none elsewhere.

At ten minutes to eleven he saw them.

They were coming slowly along the opposite side, Corley and the girl. Lenehan stepped back into the shadow of a porch. The girl walked close beside Corley, speaking earnestly. Her face looked pale under the red ribbon. Corley had one hand in his coat pocket. He answered without turning his head.

They passed the appointed corner.

Lenehan followed.

He kept thirty yards behind, crossing when they crossed, pausing when they paused. They went through quieter streets where the tall houses stood with extinguished windows and polished knockers. Here the city’s murmur had sunk to a distant breathing. Their footsteps reached him distinctly: Corley’s slow tread, the girl’s quicker tap beside it.

At the railings of a square they stopped. The girl faced Corley. She seemed to plead with him. He bent his head, said something, and pointed down the street. She shook her head.

Lenehan pressed himself behind a tree.

At last they moved on. The shower began again, fine as dust. The girl took a handkerchief from her purse and covered her hat. Corley walked uncovered, his bowler shining with drops.

They turned into a narrow, respectable street of red-brick houses. Most of the windows were dark, but in one basement a servant was fastening shutters. Halfway down, the girl halted before a house with white steps and an iron boot-scraper. She spoke rapidly. Corley put both hands in his pockets and waited.

She mounted the steps, glanced once along the street and let herself in.

The door closed.

Lenehan watched from the corner. Corley stood below the lamp, motionless. One minute passed, then two. Lenehan could hear rain ticking on leaves. A cab entered the street, its horse weary and the driver bowed beneath a cape. Corley turned his back as it went by.

What if she did not return?

A light appeared behind an upper blind. It moved, disappeared, then showed in another room. Lenehan imagined the interior: the dark hall, the stairs, the sleeping employers, the drawer opened by a trembling hand. He felt suddenly afraid for the girl. If she were caught, Corley would walk away. He himself would walk away too. What else could they do?

The door opened.

The girl came out without her handkerchief. She descended quickly and went to Corley. For a moment their bodies hid their hands. Corley said something. She shook her head, then caught his sleeve. He removed her fingers one by one.

She went back up the steps.

At the door she looked over her shoulder. Corley had already turned away.

The door shut upon her.

Corley approached the corner with his usual deliberate gait. Lenehan sprang from the shadow.

“Well?”

Corley continued walking.

“Did she do it?”

No answer.

Lenehan fell into step beside him. He searched the heavy face, but Corley looked straight ahead.

“Was it all right, man? Did she bring it?”

Corley’s lips tightened with importance.

“For God’s sake,” Lenehan said. “Did you pull it off?”

They reached the next lamp. Corley stopped beneath it. The rain had laid a dark gloss on his shoulders and dotted the brim of his hat. He drew his right hand slowly from his pocket and held it closed before Lenehan’s face.

Then, with grave ceremony, he opened his fingers.

In the hollow of his palm a small gold coin caught the lamplight and shone.

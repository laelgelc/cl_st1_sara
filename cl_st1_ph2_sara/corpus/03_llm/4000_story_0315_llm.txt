The Light in the Window

Old Ellen Gray died as she had lived for many years—poor, intemperate, and forsaken. Yet when the news passed from door to door, harsh judgment was silenced for a little while; for death touches the hidden spring of our common humanity, and even those who had turned coldly from the living woman spoke gently of the dead.

“She had a hard life,” said one.

“And made it harder,” answered another.

“But she is beyond hunger and blame now,” murmured a third; and this was true.

The miserable hovel in which Ellen had passed her last days stood at the far edge of the village, where the road dipped toward a marshy hollow. Rain had loosened the stones of its chimney, the roof sagged, and through a broken window the wind entered at pleasure. There, amid scant furniture and rags that could scarcely be called bedding, lay the mother in her grave-clothes; and there, shrinking together in fear and hunger, were her three children.

“What is to be done with them?” This was the question soon asked by every tongue.

The two older children, Robert and Annie, were strong enough to be useful. Robert, who was twelve, could drive cattle, carry wood, and work in the fields. Annie, two years younger, had learned to wash dishes, tend a baby, and use a needle after a poor fashion. Their grief did not make them less serviceable; and so, before the earth had closed over their mother, homes were found for both.

Farmer Bell took Robert.

“He will earn his bread,” said the farmer, as though the boy’s hunger were an account already balanced.

Mrs. Ellis agreed to receive Annie.

“I am doing it from charity,” she declared; but she added, almost in the same breath, that the girl must be obedient, rise early, and make herself useful.

There remained Maggie, the youngest.

Two years before, Maggie had fallen from a loft while searching for a hen’s nest. Her spine had been injured, and from that day she had never walked. She was now eight years old, though her thin face and large, patient eyes made her appear at once younger in body and older in sorrow. She could sit when supported by pillows, and move her wasted hands; but her lower limbs were powerless.

No one offered to take Maggie.

“Who wants a bed-ridden child?” said a rough man who stood outside the hut after the burial. “She’d be nothing but a burden.”

“The poorhouse is the place for her,” replied Mrs. Ellis. “She’ll have food there, and a bed too, which is more than she has had here.”

“Yes, the poorhouse,” repeated several voices, relieved that a public duty had been discovered to excuse private mercy.

It was agreed that someone should notify the overseer on the following morning. Meanwhile the villagers departed, each having business that could not be delayed. Robert was led away toward Farmer Bell’s broad acres; Annie went crying after Mrs. Ellis; and Maggie remained upon a heap of straw in the little back room of the hut.

The afternoon declined. No fire burned upon the hearth. No food stood within reach of the helpless child. Through cracks in the wall the air entered cold and damp, stirring the loose ends of a ragged quilt drawn over her wan and wasted form.

She was alone!

Those who had talked so freely of duty had forgotten the interval between promise and performance. Their hearts were not wholly wicked; few human hearts are. But pity that does not move the hands is a flower without fruit, and good intentions make a poor covering for a shivering child.

One man had not gone home.

Joe Thompson, the village wheelwright, had remained near the grave after the others dispersed. He was a broad-shouldered, rough-handed man, slow in speech and shy of displaying feeling. Many supposed him dull because he did not talk much; but there are deep wells that make little sound.

Joe had watched the children separated. He had seen Robert look back until a turn in the road hid the hovel. He had heard Annie calling for Maggie as Mrs. Ellis hurried her away. All through the burial service he had noticed the youngest child’s white face at the broken window, for no one had thought to carry her to her mother’s grave.

At last he entered the hut.

“Maggie,” he called softly.

There was no answer.

He went into the back room. The child lay with her face turned toward the wall. At the sound of his step she looked around quickly, and terror flashed into her eyes.

“Don’t be frightened,” said Joe. “It’s only me.”

“Yes, sir,” whispered Maggie.

“Have you had anything to eat?”

“No, sir.”

“Since morning?”

She shook her head.

“Why didn’t you call somebody?”

“I did call once. Nobody heard me.”

The wheelwright turned away and looked through the broken window. His great hands opened and closed. He was angry—but not with the child, nor even with any single person. His anger rose against that cowardly selfishness which belongs to all and therefore seems to belong to none.

“They’re going to send me to the poorhouse, aren’t they?” Maggie asked.

“So they say.”

“Will they let Annie come to see me?”

“I don’t know, child.”

“Maybe Robert can come sometimes.”

“Maybe.”

She was silent for a moment. Then she said, with painful effort, “I’ll try not to be troublesome.”

The words entered Joe’s heart like an accusation.

He bent over the bed. “Put your arms around my neck, Maggie.”

Her eyes widened. “Are you taking me to the poorhouse?”

“Not tonight.”

“Where, then?”

“Home with me.”

He wrapped the quilt closely about her, slid one arm beneath her shoulders and the other under her knees, and lifted her as carefully as though she had been made of glass. She was very light—far too light for a child of eight. Yet never had Joe Thompson carried a burden that seemed so heavy with human responsibility.

Maggie laid her head against his coat. After a few steps she whispered, “You won’t drop me?”

“No, dear.”

It was the first time he had called her dear, and his voice trembled.

Now Joe had a wife, Jane, who was not known in the village for softness of temper. She was a clean, industrious woman and neglected no task that she acknowledged as her own; but disappointment had soured her. For fifteen years she had lived in the little brown house beside the wheelwright’s shop. No child’s feet had pattered across its floors, no young voice had called her mother, no tender dependence had drawn from her the affection that lay buried beneath habits of complaint.

Love, denied an outlet, does not always remain sweet. It may turn bitter, as clear water turns bitter when shut away from light and air.

Joe knew his wife’s temper, and as he approached the house his courage failed a little. He had faced an unruly horse and once stood alone against three drunken men; but the thought of Jane’s sharp eyes made him pause at the gate.

Maggie felt the hesitation.

“Am I too heavy?” she asked.

“No,” said Joe, and went in.

Mrs. Thompson was preparing supper. When she heard the door open, she came from the kitchen wiping her hands upon her apron. At sight of the bundle in her husband’s arms, she stopped.

“What have you there?”

Joe drew back the quilt, revealing Maggie’s pale face.

Jane’s own face hardened. “You haven’t brought that child here!”

“I couldn’t leave her alone.”

“You could have taken her to the poorhouse.”

“The overseer won’t be back until tomorrow.”

“Then somebody else might have kept her.”

“Everybody else had gone.”

“That is no reason why you should bring her here. We have troubles enough without taking another person’s burden on our shoulders.”

During these words Maggie did not speak. She only looked from husband to wife with the startled watchfulness of one accustomed to learn her fate from the tones of others.

Joe felt the child’s arms tighten faintly around his neck.

“Jane,” he said, more firmly, “she has had no food since morning.”

Mrs. Thompson glanced at the small white face and then away.

“That doesn’t make us responsible.”

“No,” answered Joe; “not unless we choose to be.”

The reply disturbed her more than argument would have done.

“Well, what do you expect?” she demanded. “Is she to lie on the floor?”

“There is the little chamber.”

“The little chamber!” Jane repeated. That room had stood unused for years. Once, long ago, she had placed a narrow bed there and sewn curtains for its window; but the hoped-for guest had never come. Afterward she shut the door.

“Only for tonight,” said Joe. “Tomorrow I’ll speak to the overseer.”

Mrs. Thompson’s lips remained tightly closed; but she did not forbid him. Taking silence for permission, Joe carried Maggie upstairs.

Dust lay upon the chamber furniture, but the bed was dry and soft. Joe placed the child on it, supporting her while he arranged the pillows. His large fingers were awkward at the work, yet tenderness taught them caution.

“Are you comfortable?” he asked.

“Yes, sir. It is a beautiful bed.”

Beautiful! It was only a plain bedstead, with faded curtains and a patchwork covering. But to Maggie, after straw, rags, and cold boards, it seemed fit for a queen.

Joe descended. Jane was setting two plates upon the table.

“She’ll need something warm,” he said.

“I suppose I know that,” she answered sharply.

She poured milk into a small pan and set it over the fire. Then she cut bread into a bowl, added a little butter, and stirred the whole with more force than was necessary. Yet she was preparing food for Maggie; and action in the service of pity is often the door through which pity enters.

“Take this up,” she said.

Joe looked at her. “You’d better go, Jane. She may need lifting.”

For an instant refusal rose to her lips. Then, bowl in hand, she mounted the stairs.

The lamp burned low in the chamber. Maggie lay exactly as Joe had left her, afraid to move even her hands lest she disarrange something and give offense. When Mrs. Thompson approached, the child watched her anxiously.

“Can you sit up?” Jane asked.

“Not without somebody holding me.”

Mrs. Thompson set down the bowl. With one arm she raised Maggie, and with the other placed a pillow behind her back. In doing so her cheek touched the child’s forehead.

“How thin you are!” she exclaimed involuntarily.

“Yes, ma’am.”

“Does it hurt when you’re moved?”

“Sometimes. But I don’t mind much.”

Jane took a spoonful of bread and milk.

“Open your mouth.”

Maggie obeyed. She ate slowly, and after each spoonful a faint warmth seemed to return to her face. Mrs. Thompson watched her. The child’s hunger was evident, but she did not snatch at the food or complain that it came too late.

“Is it good?” Jane asked.

“Oh, yes, ma’am. It is very good.”

“Didn’t your mother give you milk?”

“Not often. She couldn’t get it.”

There was no reproach in the answer—only a gentle loyalty to one who had failed in many duties. The dead mother had little claim upon praise; but the child’s love covered her faults as grass soon covers an uncared-for grave.

When the bowl was empty, Maggie said, “Thank you, ma’am.”

Something long silent stirred in Jane Thompson’s bosom. The helpless body resting against her arm was no longer an unwelcome object discussed by the village, but a living child—warm, breathing, grateful; a child who needed her.

“Do you want anything else?” she asked, and her voice had lost its edge.

“No, ma’am.”

“Are you sure?”

“Yes, ma’am. I never had such a nice supper.”

Mrs. Thompson laid her down and drew the covering beneath her chin. At the door she looked back. The lamp shone upon Maggie’s face, giving to its whiteness a tender, almost spiritual beauty.

“Poor thing!” Jane murmured.

Joe, who had come quietly to the foot of the stairs, heard the words.

He went outside after supper on pretense of putting away a tool. Darkness had fallen, and the village lay still beneath a cloudy sky. From the upper chamber a square of yellow light shone across the yard. Presently Jane’s shadow passed over the curtain, paused, and bent toward the bed.

Joe’s eyes grew dim.

There are moments when Heaven seems to come very near—not with thunder, nor with the voices of angels, but through some common act of mercy performed in a humble room.

When Jane came down, Joe sat by the stove.

“She’s asleep,” she said.

“Did she suffer much?”

“She says her back aches nearly all the time. Nobody has attended to her properly.”

“That’s bad.”

“It’s shameful,” Jane replied, forgetting that only an hour before she had wished the child elsewhere. “A little care might make her stronger.”

Joe wisely said nothing.

After a while he opened the Bible that lay upon the shelf. He turned the pages slowly, though he knew the passage he sought.

“Jane,” he said, “do you remember where it tells how the Lord took little children in His arms and blessed them?”

“I remember.”

“He didn’t ask whether they were strong enough to work.”

Mrs. Thompson’s hands stopped over her sewing.

“You needn’t preach to me, Joe.”

“I’m not preaching. I was thinking, that’s all.”

“Well, think quietly.”

But no further sharpness came. Before going to bed, Jane climbed the stairs twice to see whether Maggie slept comfortably.

In the morning Joe found a bright fire in the kitchen and broth simmering in a pot.

“That for Maggie?” he asked.

“She can’t live on bread and milk alone,” Jane answered. “And don’t be in haste about the overseer. The roads are wet, and moving her today might bring on a fever.”

“So it might,” said Joe.

The next day Maggie was no stronger. On the third, Jane said her appetite had only begun to improve. On the fourth, the child smiled when Mrs. Thompson entered the room, and put out one thin hand.

Jane took it.

“Were you wanting something?”

“Only you,” Maggie said.

Only you! No one had ever spoken those words to Jane Thompson. She sat upon the bed, and the child drew her hand against her cheek.

From that hour the poorhouse was seldom mentioned. When Joe once observed that the overseer had returned, Jane replied that Maggie was not fit to be moved. A week later she said it would be cruel to send her among strangers just as she was learning to trust them. Then winter approached, and surely no helpless child should be carried away in bitter weather.

Thus mercy found reasons where selfishness had formerly found excuses.

Days lengthened into weeks, and weeks into months. The little chamber changed. Clean curtains hung at the window; a vase sometimes held flowers; a low shelf supported Maggie’s few treasures. Joe fashioned a wheeled chair in his shop, carving its arms with clumsy ornaments. When spring came, he carried her downstairs and placed her by the open door, where sunshine warmed her face.

Jane changed also.

The villagers noticed that her voice was gentler and her brow less clouded. She no longer watched for faults with hungry eyes. A smile, rare at first, came often. The tenderness that had slumbered in her heart, shut away so long that even she had forgotten it, awoke under the touch of a child’s hand.

And Maggie? She remained frail and helpless; yet she gave more than she received. Her patience rebuked irritation, her gratitude sweetened labor, and her affection filled every silent room. Joe hurried home from the shop to hear her call his name. Jane’s hardest tasks became light when Maggie praised her or laid a loving cheek against her hand.

People had called the child a burden. So she was—but a precious burden, binding two lonely hearts to duty, to one another, and to God. The poorhouse never received her. No formal promise was made, no grand declaration spoken. She simply remained, until the thought of her removal became as impossible as the removal of the hearth from the house.

Before Maggie came, the Thompsons possessed food, shelter, and honest work, yet their home was poor in the things that make life rich. Afterward there was sacrifice, anxiety, and ceaseless care; but there was also the honey of affection, the precious wine of gratitude, and the daily bread of loving service.

The villagers had left an angel in a ruined hut because they saw only a sick, motherless child. Joe Thompson carried that angel home in his strong arms; and she, in her weakness, brought with her the one blessing neither labor nor money had been able to buy.

Night still fell around the brown house, and winter winds still shook its doors. But in the little chamber a lamp was burning; and from that quiet flame the sunshine of love spread through every room.

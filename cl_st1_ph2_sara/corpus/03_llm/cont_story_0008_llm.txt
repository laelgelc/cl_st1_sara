Dear Robbie,

Your grandmother says the deer have eaten the last of the tulips, including the red ones she planted around the mailbox after I told her—correctly, though this fact has not improved my standing—that deer do not generally care for tulips. They care for these. Yesterday morning there were three of them in the yard, a doe and two nearly grown fawns, all chewing with the solemn concentration of people reading legal documents. I waved my blue mug at them. They looked at me as if I were the trespasser.

This is not the answer to your message. I know that. Your grandmother says I begin with the weather or the animals whenever I am avoiding something, which would mean I have avoided most things for fifty-two years. She is in the kitchen now, pretending not to listen to the scratch of this pen. The blue mug, by the way, is the one you made in sixth grade, with the handle too small for a human finger. I still use it. That may count as loyalty under the old definition.

I read what you wrote about J. twice, then burned the second page in the grill, as you asked. I burned the first as well, though you did not ask, and stirred the ashes with one of the long metal skewers. I am not sure this was necessary. I am not sure anything is necessary until, suddenly, it is.

From what I understand—and I understand only what you told me—J. was called in to answer a “simple question” about G., specifically whether G. had proper paperwork and whether M. knew that he did not. J. declined to provide the requested info. After that they kept her. Is that right? You say “detained,” which is their word and perhaps the only safe word, but a person who cannot leave a room is, in my old-fashioned view, being held prisoner. Of course words are now among the rights and privileges we are expected to use responsibly.

I remember J. from the lake, if it is the same J.: narrow face, green scarf, better at backing the trailer than either you or your father (do not tell him I wrote that, although he knows). She climbed into the freezing water after your grandmother’s hat and came out laughing, holding the ridiculous thing above her head like a rescued infant. A brave girl. Or reckless. At my age the distinction grows muddy.

You ask what you should do.

My first answer, and the answer you are not going to like, is nothing.

Not nothing forever. Not nothing in your heart, if that phrase means anything. But nothing now that can be traced, repeated, recorded, quoted, or transformed into what the district liaison calls “evidence of coordinated disaffection.” Do not go to the building. Do not contact M. Do not ask your friends to ask their friends where G. went. Do not send J. a message through one of the systems that claims to erase messages, because the only thing those systems reliably erase is common sense. If someone contacts you, be polite. Say you have no relevant knowledge. Do not become sarcastic. You have inherited your grandmother’s eyebrows, and sarcasm is visible on your face before it reaches your mouth.

I can hear you objecting. If everyone does nothing, you will say, then this is how they win.

Yes. Probably. It may also be how you stay alive long enough to see them lose.

You are twenty-six, Robbie. At twenty-six you believe survival is the coward’s consolation prize. At seventy-nine it looks more like the whole contest. This is not because old people become wise. Mostly we become attached—to the chair that does not hurt our backs, to the pills arranged by weekday, to the woman humming badly in the kitchen, to the cardinal that appears at the feeder at four-fifteen and attacks its own reflection. Attachment makes poor revolutionaries of us. It may make poor citizens too. I am trying to be honest.

The courts will not help J., or not in the manner you mean. The local judge who might once have signed a release order resigned after his daughter’s university appointment was reviewed. His replacement has given six talks on Civic Gratitude and owns, unless I am misinformed, a roofing company with three federal contracts. Appeals go to the regional panel. Two members of that panel were pardoned before they were appointed, which used to be considered peculiar. Now it is called proof that the Leader believes in redemption.

A lawyer can file something. A lawyer can always file something; forms are the last birds still migrating on schedule. But the lawyers who file too many wrong things lose their access credentials, and then their office leases, and then sometimes their brothers-in-law discover tax problems from twelve years ago. Nothing is officially connected. Everything is coincidental. It is astonishing how efficiently coincidence can govern a nation.

Last winter I was stopped outside the pharmacy because one of my rear lights was out. The officer could not have been older than nineteen. His face had the soft unfinished look you had when you were ten, but below that face was an adult’s heavy body and so much equipment that when he leaned toward my window he clanked. I remember his bare forearm. Pale hairs, one red pimple near the wrist, a black strap cutting into the skin. He asked why I had been in a restricted retail zone after six.

I said I was collecting your grandmother’s antibiotics.

He said, “That isn’t what I asked, sir.”

There are moments when the body recognizes a country before the mind does. My mouth went dry. My hands, which are unreliable when holding a spoon, became perfectly still on the steering wheel. I apologized. I thanked him for his service. I showed him the bottle. He made me wait while he spoke to someone I could not hear, then gave me a warning for defective equipment and another for “evasive demeanor.”

When I got home, your grandmother said I looked ill. I told her the pharmacy line had been long.

This is the sort of courage I am offering you.

—

You asked how it happened, too, though perhaps you meant that as an accusation. Fair enough. Young people are entitled to accuse the old; it is one of the few traditions not yet requiring a permit.

The textbook answer, if they permit textbooks again, will have dates and arrows. First this emergency, then that election, then the ruling that allowed the next thing, and there will be a shaded box titled THE COLLAPSE OF INSTITUTIONAL NORMS. Students will look at the diagram and wonder why everyone did not stand up at the red arrow.

But lived history does not have red arrows. It has a dentist appointment at eleven and a leak under the sink.

There was an election whose totals did not make sense, but there had been totals before that did not make sense, depending on which channel you watched. There were lawsuits. There were experts. One expert said the process was sound; another said it had been compromised beyond repair; a third shouted until the host thanked all three for a valuable discussion. We went to bed.

Then came the pardons. Some of the pardoned men were crooks in the plain old sense—envelopes, contracts, girlfriends on payroll—and I remember thinking it was vulgar but temporary, like a drunk guest taking a dump on your rug. You would throw out the rug, open the windows, and never invite the fellow again. Except the fellow bought the house. Then he declared the rug a national symbol and made everyone salute it.

Forgive the image. Your grandmother says it is disgusting. It is also accurate.

Judges were called enemies. Reporters published their home addresses. Election workers found photographs of their children pasted beneath messages about treason. The ministries—agencies, we still called them then—issued statements saying intimidation had no place in public life, while the people doing the intimidating were invited to dinner. A prosecutor was dismissed on a Tuesday and charged on Friday. Someone drove a truck through the windows of an opposition office. No one important approved of this, naturally, but many important people found it understandable.

And we continued.

We had your parents over for salmon. Your grandmother knitted that gray scarf you hate. I replaced the boards on the back steps, badly the first time and less badly the second. We finished a thousand-piece puzzle of Venice, though neither of us has been to Venice and, barring a dramatic improvement in my left knee and the Department of Foreign Travel’s opinion of me, neither of us will go. The puzzle had six hundred pieces of sky, all nearly the same blue. We complained about them for two weeks.

Outside, the president ordered the arrest of three inspectors who had investigated his friends.

Inside, I found the corner of a cloud.

I do not mean that ordinary life was worthless. That is an easy thing for fiery young men to say, usually while some woman washes their socks. The salmon mattered. The scarf mattered. Your mother’s laugh mattered, and the fact that you called from college pretending you did not need money when you plainly did. Birds still flew. People still fell in love, paid bills, got cavities, brought casseroles after funerals. These things are not distractions from life. They are life.

But they can also be used as sandbags against reality.

I told myself the next election would correct matters. When that election was “secured” by the Emergency Verification Act, I told myself the courts would object. When the courts were enlarged, I told myself the states would resist. When governors were removed for administrative disloyalty, I told myself the civil service was too large to conquer. When the loyalty attestations came, I signed mine because my pension depended on it and because, I reasoned, loyalty to one’s country was not loyalty to one person. A clever distinction. The form did not ask for my distinction.

Your grandmother refused at first. You should know that. She sat at this table with the pen in her hand, lips pressed tight, and said, “This is how they count us.”

I said, “It’s only paperwork.”

There are sentences a person would remove from his life if life were a document. That is one of mine.

In the end she signed because my cardiac coverage had been attached to her account after the consolidation, and I had a procedure scheduled. We made what I called an intelligent accommodation. This is a phrase cowards use when “surrender” seems impolite.

What should we have done? Marched? We attended two marches. At the first, there were families, union people, veterans, a brass band, and a prince of a guy selling lemonade for ten dollars. At the second, drones photographed everyone. Three months later our neighbor’s teaching license was suspended because he appeared in a crowd image beside a sign he had not been carrying.

Should we have written? I wrote letters. Actual letters, with stamps. One representative sent a form reply thanking me for my perspective; the other was arrested before my letter arrived. Should I have refused the loyalty form and lost the pension? Perhaps. Then your grandmother’s surgery would have been postponed, and you will say that thousands suffered because people like me placed our surgeries above their freedom. You will not be wrong. You will also, I hope, never have to look at someone you love and decide that history needs her pain.

There is the trap. They did not ask us to choose between courage and cowardice in bright clean terms. They asked us to choose between a principle and a prescription, a stranger and a daughter, public risk and private duty. Then they asked again. And again. Each concession made the next one more sensible.

Your father says I am too hard on myself. He says no single person could have stopped it. True enough. No raindrop is responsible for the flood, as the raindrop’s attorney will be pleased to establish.

Still, I keep thinking of the puzzles.

After Venice we did a harbor with seals. Then a cottage under snow. Then, God help us, a fairy castle with purple towers, which your grandmother bought because it was half price. Night after night we searched for tiny bits of an invented kingdom while the real one outside was being taken apart. A border piece here, a window there. We were very patient.

Last week I dreamed we were back at this table ten years ago. The room was exactly right: the old curtains with lemons on them, the scratch near the sugar bowl, rain against the west window. On the television, the Leader was telling a crowd that judges would soon remember who appointed them.

In the dream I understood. Not later. Then.

I turned to your grandmother and said, “This is the moment.”

She understood too. We did not discuss the mortgage or the operation or whether anyone else would join us. We stood up. I do not know what we intended to do—call people, drive somewhere, tear down a fence. Dreams are generous about logistics. But I felt young, not in the knees or teeth but in the soul, if an old agnostic may borrow the word. I felt that action was still connected to consequence.

Then I woke.

It was four in the morning. Your grandmother was asleep with one hand under her cheek. The security light had come on in the yard, and for a few seconds I thought men were approaching the house. It was only a raccoon at the feeder.

Only a raccoon. Do you see? Even now relief defeats revelation.

—

So we return to J.

You said she refused to answer because answering would harm G. and M. That is a simple fact, or once would have been. She chose the danger to herself rather than transferring it to someone more vulnerable. I have spent six pages explaining why you should not imitate her, and I find I respect her more with every page.

Perhaps this is because I had imagined she was merely a friend.

Forgive me. “Merely” is the wrong word. Friendship has saved more people than romance and produced fewer terrible songs. But I remember the lake now. The way you watched her when she climbed out of the water, the way you handed her your coat and then stood shivering as if weather were a rumor. Your grandmother noticed too. Grandmothers notice love before the people suffering from it do.

Was she your lover? Is she still? You do not have to answer in writing.

If she is, my advice becomes both more urgent and less useful. Love makes caution sound obscene. It also makes traps effective. They may expect you to come. They may be waiting for precisely the noble gesture—the visit, the call, the attempt to find a clerk willing to accept money or kindness. A regime like this depends upon love; it threatens one person in order to move another. Hatred is noisy, but love supplies the address.

You must consider your parents. Your sister. Us, though we are old and should perhaps not weigh heavily in the calculation. If you act, they may question your father at work. They may review your mother’s clinic license. Your sister’s travel permission could vanish. The family association rules are vague because vagueness is the point. Punishment spreads until everyone learns to police everyone else.

I hate myself a little for writing that. Not enough, apparently, to stop.

There is money.

Your grandmother does not know I am telling you this, although she probably knows about the money; there are no secrets in a long marriage, only subjects temporarily left undisturbed. After the currency conversion I kept some of the old notes, and there is also a foreign account opened before such accounts became evidence of “exit intent.” It is not a fortune. Twenty-three thousand, perhaps less after fees. The access numbers are in the basement workshop, inside the red toolbox, beneath the tray of drill bits. There is a false bottom. Yes, your ridiculous grandfather built a false bottom. It is not very good. Anyone searching properly will find it in thirty seconds, so I hope they send an idiot.

I am not advising you to use this money.

I am telling you it exists.

Those are different statements, legally and morally, although perhaps not to the people who matter. It might pay a lawyer, a driver, documents, a debt. It might do nothing except attract another set of eyes. It might endanger J. further. You need facts, and facts are the one commodity no one can safely obtain.

Do not rush because you feel guilty. Guilt enjoys disguising itself as courage. Do not delay merely because I am frightened. Fear has been disguising itself as wisdom for most of my life.

What do you intend to do, Robbie?

Tell me, but not through the usual channels. Better yet, come here if you can do so without creating a pattern. Bring laundry. Young men may visit grandparents for laundry, especially young men who still believe shirts become clean through proximity to a washing machine. We will sit at the kitchen table. Your grandmother will make soup and pretend she cannot hear us. I may tell you again to do nothing. I may be right.

Or I may open the red toolbox.

I wish I could give you the advice a grandfather is supposed to give: be brave, do right, fear nothing. That is advice from stories in which the brave are shot only in places that heal, the right path is marked, and fear belongs to villains. In our country now, decent people disappear over “simple questions,” and a boy with a child’s face can ruin an old man because his equipment clanks and the old man wants to get home.

Survive, I want to say. Keep your mind intact. Remember the names they erase. Wait. Countries recover. Bad seasons end. Even frightened people may outlive the things that frightened them.

But waiting is what I did.

So come if you can. Be careful. Burn this. And if you have already decided—if there is some plan beneath your question, some door you mean to knock on or border you mean to cross—do not mistake my hesitation for refusal.

I am afraid for you.

I am proud of J.

I may be proud of you too, though I would prefer, selfish old fart that I am, to have you safe enough that pride is unnecessary.

With all my love,

Grandpa

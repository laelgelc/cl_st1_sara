### The Roof Garden

When a woman decides to wash her hair, the established machinery of the universe may as well cease revolving until the ceremony is accomplished.

Wars may be declared, governments may totter, publishers may discover a new adjective for “thrilling,” and the young man in the third-floor front may call twice with tickets for a matinee; but once the idea has seized her, there are towels to be found, soap to be sliced, hairpins to be extracted from inaccessible corners, and hot water to be persuaded from plumbing designed by an enemy of civilization. Nothing else matters.

Mary Louise Moss decided to wash her hair at three minutes past eleven on a Tuesday morning.

Before that moment she had been trying to make Reginald Vane kiss Edythe on a moonlit veranda.

Reginald had been approaching the kiss since nine o’clock. He had crossed the veranda, paused by a wistaria vine, looked into Edythe’s violet eyes, taken both her hands, released one of them for no reason, and said, “Beloved—”

There he had remained.

Mary Louise reread the passage. Reginald’s eyes possessed a steady flame. His mouth was stern but tender. His profile might have brought a sculptor to tears. He owned motorcars, estates, polo ponies, and a sorrow concealed from the world.

He was, in short, admirably equipped for existence except for being alive.

“You wooden thing,” said Mary Louise.

Reginald did not resent this. Wooden things seldom do.

She rose from the table, pushed back the dark mass of hair which had fallen about her face, and walked to the single window of her apartment. The word “apartment” was an act of courtesy toward two rooms, a bath resembling an upright coffin, and a kitchenette in which a determined woman might make coffee if she first removed the bread box.

Outside were brick walls, fire escapes, windows, chimneys, clotheslines, a white cat asleep upon an ash barrel, and twelve square inches of sky.

New York was magnificent. Everybody said so. It possessed towers, theatres, orchestras, publishers, electric signs, and more successful authors than could comfortably dine at one table. It also possessed Mary Louise Moss, though as yet it had shown no consciousness of the distinction.

Six months earlier she had resigned her position in the school at Maple Junction, Michigan, and come east with nine hundred and forty dollars, three finished stories, seven unfinished ones, a letter to the cousin of a woman whose brother had once met an editor, and a conviction that talent was all one needed in New York.

The conviction had cost eight hundred and twelve dollars.

The letter had cost two cents and produced no result.

The stories had been returned with such promptness that Mary Louise sometimes suspected the postman of reading them in the hall and rejecting them on his own authority.

She had visited editorial offices with polished shoes and a brave hat. She had sat among stenographers, office boys, discarded umbrellas, successful-looking women in furs, unsuccessful-looking men with brief cases, and young persons who spoke mysteriously of “features.” She had been told that her work was charming but unavailable, promising but unsuitable, clever but not quite, fresh but familiar, and sincerely regretted.

One editor had advised her to gain experience.

Mary Louise had desired to ask him where experience was sold and whether it could be charged.

Now only one hundred and twenty-eight dollars remained between her and Maple Junction. Maple Junction meant the school bell, thirty-one children with thirty-one colds in winter, Miss Trumbull’s boarding house, fried liver on Thursdays, and the annual debate on whether the new drinking fountain encouraged extravagance.

It also meant lilacs beside the kitchen door, grass under bare feet, wind moving across cornfields, and her mother’s old back yard where sheets swelled on the line like white ships sailing among hollyhocks.

Mary Louise looked again at the twelve square inches of sky.

“I shall wash my hair,” she announced.

It was not surrender. It was strategy. Napoleon crossed the Alps; Caesar crossed the Rubicon; Mary Louise crossed the room and turned on the hot-water faucet.

The water was hot.

Miracles are often quiet.

Twenty minutes later she emerged in a blue kimono, carrying two damp towels, a comb, a brush, and enough wet hair to upholster a sofa. In Maple Junction she would have gone into the back yard and sat on the kitchen step. The sun would have dried her hair while bees investigated the clover and Mrs. Bixby next door called over the fence to inquire whether Mary Louise had heard that the Methodist minister’s wife had ordered a red hat.

But there are no kitchen steps on the sixth floor.

She opened her door and looked into the dim hallway. At its far end Charlie Gaffney, the janitor, was engaged in persuading a radiator to feel ashamed of itself.

“Charlie,” she called.

Charlie raised a red and friendly face.

“Say, Miss Moss, if it’s that heat again, I’m tellin’ you, that radiator’s got a disposition. You got to coax it. Bangin’ only makes it stubborn.”

“I don’t want heat. I want sunshine.”

Charlie looked at the wet towels and then at her hair. Understanding dawned slowly, struggled with disbelief, and sat down exhausted.

“You washed it.”

“I did.”

“Right in the middle of Tuesday?”

“Tuesday seemed helpless to prevent me.”

Charlie scratched his head.

“What I need,” said Mary Louise, “is a back yard.”

“In this neighborhood?”

“A little grass would help.”

“You ain’t askin’ much, are you? Maybe a cow, too?”

“I could do without the cow. I want somewhere sunny where I can sit and dry my hair.”

Charlie considered the building. He knew its pipes, locks, tenants, weaknesses, mortgages, noises, and moral failings. After a moment he jerked his thumb upward.

“There’s the roof.”

“May I go there?”

“Well, the door’s generally locked.”

“Why?”

“To keep people from goin’ on the roof.”

This logic was so complete that Mary Louise bowed before it.

Charlie lowered his voice.

“The boss uses it sometimes.”

“What boss?”

“The guy that owns the buildin’. He’s got the top floor.”

“I thought nobody lived above me.”

“He does, but he don’t like it known. Queer bird. Writes all night, sleeps all day, don’t want bells rung, don’t want questions asked, don’t want nobody tellin’ him what the water pipes is doin’.”

“A literary man?”

“If that means he throws paper all over and forgets his rent receipts, yeah.”

“Charlie, lend me the key.”

Charlie studied her. It was an unusual picture even for New York: a determined young woman in a blue kimono, her damp hair falling below her waist, demanding entry to a forbidden roof in the interests of capillary freedom.

“All right,” he said. “Only if you see him, don’t blame me.”

“I shall blame the architecture.”

She turned toward her apartment, then stopped.

“One more thing. Do you know where I can get parsley?”

“Parsley!”

His voice contained all the emotion of a man who has seen civilization finally abandon restraint.

“Fresh parsley,” said Mary Louise. “A good bunch of it.”

“What for?”

“To smell.”

Charlie stared at her.

“You wimmin,” he said at last, “is the reason N’ York never gets dull.”

There was a vegetable stand on the corner, and five minutes later Mary Louise had acquired a bunch of parsley large enough to decorate the funeral of a popular statesman. Armed with towels, brush, comb, manuscript paper, pencil, and the parsley, she followed Charlie up a narrow flight of stairs.

He unlocked the roof door.

“There you are. Fifth Avenoo.”

Mary Louise stepped out.

It was not Fifth Avenue. Neither, judged strictly, was it a back yard. The roof contained a water tank, three chimneys, two broken chairs, a crate, a soap box, several tin cans, and an object which might once have been an umbrella but had lost confidence. Beyond these rose the city, stern and crowded, its roofs climbing one behind another toward distant towers.

But the sun was there.

So was the wind.

Mary Louise lifted her face. The wind caught her loosened hair and sent it streaming behind her. For the first time in weeks there was nothing close above her, nothing directly before her, no wall, no ceiling, no editor’s secretary saying that Mr. Somebody was in conference.

She chose the soap box, spread a towel over it, placed the parsley beside her, and sat down.

The parsley was important. Closing her eyes, she could almost persuade herself that she smelled the Moss back yard after rain: damp earth, tomato vines, mint by the pump, sheets drying in the sun, the sharp green fragrance from her mother’s garden border. Smell is memory’s dishonest cousin. Give it one crushed leaf and it will manufacture a whole county.

Mary Louise combed her hair and forgot Reginald Vane.

She remembered instead Mrs. Bixby leaning over the fence with flour on her nose; old Mr. Cramer carrying his canary outdoors because he believed the bird enjoyed politics; the Moss family wash flapping on Monday morning; her father shelling peas under the apple tree; her mother calling, “Louise, fetch me the blue bowl”; small Tommy Pike solemnly burying a potato bug with Christian rites.

She laughed.

A voice behind her said, “What are you doing?”

Mary Louise turned.

A man stood near the chimney.

He was tall and lean, with a long, brown face, dark hair which had plainly separated from a comb some time ago, and gray eyes containing annoyance enlivened by curiosity. He wore trousers, a white shirt open at the throat, an old velvet coat, and slippers. A pipe was in one hand; several sheets of manuscript were in the other.

His costume suggested that a gentleman had begun dressing for dinner, changed his mind, and taken to piracy.

“I’m drying my hair,” said Mary Louise.

“Why?”

“Because it is wet.”

He looked at the hair as though this explanation, while scientifically sound, offended him personally.

“On my roof?”

“Is it yours? I thought it belonged to the building.”

“The building is mine.”

“That was careless of you.”

His eyebrows rose.

Mary Louise resumed combing.

He came nearer.

“Are you the janitor’s daughter?”

“Are you the scrub-lady’s son?”

“No.”

“Then we have both escaped a natural mistake.”

The man looked at her, looked at the parsley, and looked at her again.

“What is that?”

“Parsley.”

“I can see that it is parsley. Why is it here?”

“To smell.”

He glanced about the roof as if searching for assistance.

“You brought parsley six stories up to smell it?”

“Seven. You have forgotten the stairs from my floor.”

“Women do not ordinarily sit on roofs in blue robes smelling parsley.”

“Women do very little ordinarily. Men simply fail to hear about the rest.”

The corner of his mouth moved, but he suppressed it. He sat upon the broken chair, crossed one long leg over the other, and filled his pipe.

“Do you always answer questions with insults?”

“Only rude questions.”

“And what do you call a rude question?”

“One asked by a collarless stranger before an introduction.”

He touched his open throat.

“My collar interfered with thought.”

“Then by all means protect thought. It has enemies enough.”

This time he laughed.

The laugh improved him. Mary Louise did not wish him improved and therefore bent industriously over a tangle in her hair.

“You are not a New Yorker,” he said.

“I am at present.”

“You were imported.”

“From Michigan.”

“What did you do there?”

“I lived.”

“And here?”

“I attempt to.”

“Profession?”

Mary Louise hesitated. For six months she had answered that question proudly. During the last two weeks pride had developed a slight cough.

“I write.”

The man groaned.

“You needn’t,” she said. “Other people do enough of that.”

“Poetry?”

“No.”

“Social reform?”

“No.”

“Confessions of a chorus girl written by a minister’s daughter?”

“Not yet.”

“Then love stories.”

Mary Louise’s chin lifted.

“Occasionally.”

“Unpublished love stories.”

She pulled the comb through her hair with unnecessary force.

“Some editors,” she said, “are unable to recognize merit unless it has been recognized by several other editors first.”

“Ah. A struggling genius.”

“I did not say genius.”

“You implied it with your eyebrows.”

“My eyebrows are private.”

“Nothing is private on a roof.”

She gathered her hair over one shoulder and regarded him coldly.

“You appear to know a great deal about writers.”

“I have met hundreds.”

“Poor things.”

“And I recognize the symptoms. Ink on the second finger. Stamped envelopes on the table. An expression of injured nobility. Inability to answer a simple question simply.”

Mary Louise held up her hand and examined the faint stain on her finger.

“That proves nothing. I might keep accounts.”

“You don’t. People who keep accounts do not buy parsley for emotional purposes.”

This was unjust, principally because it was true.

He tapped the manuscript against his knee.

“What are you writing now?”

“A story.”

“About?”

“A man and a woman.”

“Revolutionary.”

“They love each other.”

“Impossible!”

“They have difficulties.”

“Naturally. Otherwise the story would be four lines long.”

Mary Louise tried not to smile.

“The man,” she said, “is wealthy, reserved, powerful, and misunderstood. The woman is beautiful and proud.”

“Have you ever met a wealthy, reserved, powerful, misunderstood man?”

“No.”

“A beautiful and proud woman?”

“Several. They were selling face cream.”

“Where does your story happen?”

“In a country house on Long Island.”

“Have you been in one?”

“I have passed one on a train.”

“Near enough for literature, perhaps. What does your hero do?”

“At present,” said Mary Louise bitterly, “he stands by a wistaria vine.”

“And then?”

“That is the trouble.”

“Can’t get him away from it?”

“I can’t make him kiss her.”

“Perhaps he has intelligence.”

“He has everything except circulation.”

The man leaned back and laughed so heartily that his pipe went out.

Mary Louise laughed too, unwillingly at first and then with relief. Reginald Vane, who had been a tragedy at eleven o’clock, became at noon a stuffed gentleman trapped in vegetation.

The stranger wiped his eyes.

“Why write about him?”

“Because magazines publish love stories.”

“Magazines publish lively stories. Love is only one way of making trouble.”

“I have sent them other kinds.”

“What kinds?”

Mary Louise named three. In the first, a New York shopgirl married the son of the proprietor. In the second, an ambitious actress gave up the stage for a young doctor. In the third, a lonely millionaire discovered happiness through the orphaned child of his former partner.

The stranger’s face became progressively mournful.

“Where did you get those people?”

“I made them up.”

“That is evident.”

“You are very encouraging.”

“I am trying to save your life. What did you teach in Michigan?”

Mary Louise stared.

“How did you know I taught?”

“You say ‘very’ when you mean ‘extremely,’ and you corrected my count of the stairs.”

“English and history.”

“How long?”

“Four years.”

“Why did you leave?”

“To come here and write.”

“Why?”

“Because I wanted to write.”

“That answer almost lives. Try again.”

She frowned toward the city.

Far below, a wagon rattled over the street. Smoke drifted from a chimney and was torn thin by the wind.

“I was tired,” she said slowly, “of correcting other people’s sentences when I had sentences of my own. I was tired of telling children that Columbus had courage and then behaving as though I had none. I had saved money. I thought if I came to New York—”

“You would be discovered?”

“I thought I would work.”

“And haven’t you?”

“I have worked every day.”

“At being somebody else.”

Mary Louise turned sharply.

He pointed with his dead pipe toward the parsley.

“Five minutes ago you described a back yard to yourself.”

“I said nothing.”

“Your face did. You sat there grinning at that vegetable like a homesick rabbit.”

“I was remembering.”

“What?”

She told him.

At first she did so resentfully, offering only the kitchen step and the clothesline. Then one memory pulled another after it. She told him about Mrs. Bixby’s floury nose, Mr. Cramer’s political canary, Tommy Pike’s potato-bug funeral, her mother’s blue bowl, the minister’s wife and the red hat. She described Monday wash days when every yard in Maple Junction flew domestic banners; winter mornings when the school stove smoked and thirty-one children denied touching it; spring evenings when lilac scent entered every open window and made even algebra seem temporary.

The stranger did not interrupt.

Encouraged by this unprecedented editorial behavior, Mary Louise went on. She spoke of the children she had taught: solemn Annie Bell, who wrote an essay proving that George Washington was a Democrat because he opposed kings; Freddie Moore, who believed punctuation was an attempt to discourage boys; Sarah Lee, who came to school hungry and pretended not to be; and the day the superintendent visited while a mouse crossed the room and history gave way to nature study.

She told him how Maple Junction had seemed intolerably small until New York showed her that loneliness required a large population.

“That,” said the man, “is a sentence.”

Mary Louise stopped.

“What is?”

“Loneliness requiring a large population.”

“I didn’t mean it for one.”

“Your best sentences usually arrive when you aren’t strangling them.”

He rose and paced to the parapet. The wind flapped the papers in his hand.

“Write the school,” he said.

“School stories are sentimental.”

“Then don’t be sentimental.”

“Editors don’t want Michigan.”

“Editors don’t care whether a story comes from Michigan or the moon, if it arrives alive. Write the woman with flour on her nose. Write the political canary. Write the hungry child without putting a halo on her and killing her in the last paragraph. Write about washing your hair because you are too homesick for a back yard to endure a Tuesday indoors.”

Mary Louise looked at the parsley.

“That would be ridiculous.”

“Good. Ridicule is a pulse.”

He began walking again, long strides carrying him from chimney to water tank and back.

“Stop borrowing millionaires. There are too many in fiction and not enough in circulation. Stop sending shopgirls into country houses you have seen from railway windows. Stop manufacturing emotions because you think editors order them by the gross. You know a place. You know people. You know what it costs a woman to leave a safe job with nine hundred dollars and discover that ambition eats three meals a day.”

Mary Louise’s eyes widened.

“How did you know it was nine hundred dollars?”

“I didn’t.”

“It was nine hundred and forty.”

“Then your extravagance is worse than I feared.”

She gathered the towel around her shoulders.

“I have one hundred and twenty-eight left.”

“Exactly?”

“One hundred and twenty-eight dollars and thirty-five cents.”

He nodded gravely.

“That thirty-five cents may yet found a dynasty.”

“It may buy supper.”

“More practical.”

For a moment neither spoke. The city spread below them, bright, hard, enormous. Mary Louise had loved it on arrival. She loved it still, though lately she had loved it as one loves a handsome brute that has eaten one’s hat.

“I may have to go back,” she said.

“To Michigan?”

“The school board has kept my place open until next month. They haven’t found anyone willing to teach Freddie Moore.”

“And will you go?”

“No.”

The answer came before prudence could stop it.

The stranger smiled.

“That is also a sentence.”

“I may have to,” she amended. “But I shall not go until I have spent the last thirty-five cents. Perhaps not then. I could owe somebody.”

“Excellent. Credit is the backbone of the arts.”

He returned to the broken chair and wrote something on the margin of his manuscript.

“Make a story of what you told me. Twelve pages. Not twenty-eight. No wealthy hero. No violet eyes. No wistaria unless you have actually smelled it.”

“And when I have written it?”

“Send it to Cecil Reeves.”

Mary Louise gave a short laugh.

“Why not send it to the President?”

“The President does not edit the *Metropolitan Monthly*.”

“Cecil Reeves does not see unknown writers.”

“He sees their manuscripts.”

“His secretary sees the envelopes. His reader sees the first page. His office boy sees the return postage. Mr. Reeves, I am told, sits behind a door of solid gold and rejects celebrated novelists by telephone.”

“Exaggerated. The door is oak.”

“You speak with authority.”

“I read trade gossip.”

“Cecil Reeves has already rejected two of my stories.”

“Then he possesses judgment.”

Mary Louise rose so quickly that the parsley fell from her lap.

“You are the rudest man I have ever met.”

“That may be why you are listening.”

“I am not listening.”

“Write the story.”

“I shall write precisely what I please.”

“Write that, then.”

She snatched up the parsley.

The stranger watched her, gray eyes amused.

“Your hair is almost dry,” he said.

“Thank you. I had feared this interview would continue until winter.”

“You looked rather like a Lorelei when I came out.”

“A Lorelei lures men.”

“So I understand.”

“You did not look lured. You looked bilious.”

“Another cherished illusion destroyed.”

“And kindly put on a collar before advising any more women about their careers.”

“Collars are fatal to sincerity.”

“Then risk insincerity.”

She marched to the roof door.

“Miss Moss.”

She turned.

He had somehow learned her name. Doubtless Charlie had told him; yet the manner in which he said it made the windy roof seem briefly quieter.

“You will not go back to Michigan,” he said.

“How can you know?”

“Because Maple Junction is behind you, and because you are angry. Anger, properly revised, is a useful literary fuel.”

“You have left out hunger.”

“No. That is the editor.”

She ought not to have smiled.

She did.

Then she descended the stairs, carrying her towels, brush, comb, manuscript paper, and battered parsley. Behind her the roof door closed.

Charlie was waiting on the sixth-floor landing with a wrench in one hand.

“Well?” he said.

“Well what?”

“Get enough sun?”

“Yes.”

“See anybody?”

“Yes.”

Charlie’s expression became cautious.

“He didn’t throw you off?”

“He tried to throw me back to Michigan.”

“Huh?”

“He is an offensive, inquisitive, collarless, conceited person who believes that because he owns a roof he also owns literary judgment.”

Charlie blinked.

“He told me to write a story about home and send it to Cecil Reeves.”

Charlie sat down on the top step.

Mary Louise paused.

“What is the matter?”

He looked up at her.

“Lady,” he said, “who d’you think you been talkin’ to?”

“The owner of the building. A hermit. A literary man of some sort.”

Charlie put the wrench across his knees.

“That’s Cecil Reeves.”

Mary Louise did not move.

A towel slipped from her arm and fell softly upon the stair.

“Cecil—”

“Reeves. Writes books, runs that big magazine, owns this dump, and don’t speak to nobody if he can help it. Been up there three years. I seen him talk longer to you than he talks to me in a month.”

Mary Louise sat down beside Charlie.

Below them, the radiator began suddenly to clang with triumphant violence.

Charlie listened.

“See?” he said. “You got to coax ’em.”

Mary Louise looked at the parsley in her hand. It was wilted, crushed, and wholly unfit for fashionable decoration. It smelled of home.

After a moment she rose.

“Charlie, if anyone asks for me, I am working.”

“Anybody likely to ask?”

“No,” she said, and then, remembering a collarless man’s certainty, amended it. “Not yet.”

She went into her apartment, closed the door, swept Reginald Vane, Edythe, the moonlit veranda, and the wistaria vine into a drawer, and put a clean sheet of paper on the table.

For one minute she sat motionless.

Then she wrote:

“When a woman decides to wash her hair, the established machinery of the universe may as well cease revolving.”

The sentence looked back at her with bright, living eyes.

Mary Louise dipped her pen again.

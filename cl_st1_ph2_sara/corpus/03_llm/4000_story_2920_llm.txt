## Root and Branch

When Ellis Duncan entered his uncle’s counting-room at Dalrymple Mill, Timothy Duncan did not ask him to sit down.

The boy noticed this, but gave no sign of resentment. He stood beside the high desk, cap in hand, waiting while his uncle finished adding a column of figures. Ellis was fourteen, though his height and broad shoulders made him appear older. His brown coat was worn at the elbows, but neatly brushed, and his shoes, though twice mended, were clean.

At last Timothy laid down his pen.

“Well, Ellis, what is it?”

“I came to ask if you could give me work, Uncle Timothy.”

“Work!” repeated Timothy. “What sort of work?”

“Any honest kind. I can write a fair hand, keep simple accounts, drive a team, tend stock, or do field work.”

“Ye have a good list ready.”

“I have been looking for a place these ten days.”

“And found none?”

“No, sir.”

Timothy leaned back and examined his nephew. He was a prosperous man, with a square chin, iron-gray hair, and eyes which appeared to put a cash value on everything they saw. He was called hard by some of his neighbours and prudent by others. It must be admitted that he was both. He spent no money foolishly, exacted every debt, and expected a full day’s labour for a full day’s pay; yet he never cheated a man of a penny. Genuine thrift is not miserliness, though it may sometimes wear an equally severe face.

“Why should I hire ye?” he asked.

“Because I need work, and I mean to do it faithfully.”

“Your father often said as much.”

Ellis flushed.

“My father is dead,” he answered quietly.

“Aye. Dead three weeks, and left your mother without a dollar. That is the harvest of idleness.”

The boy’s fingers tightened around his cap.

“I know he did not provide for us as he ought,” said Ellis, after a pause. “But I did not come here to hear him spoken against.”

“Then ye came to the wrong man. Joel Duncan was my brother, but he was shiftless from the day he could walk. He borrowed when he should have earned, promised when he should have performed, and blamed ill luck for what was only laziness.”

Ellis’s face grew hotter, yet he controlled himself.

“Perhaps that is true. But it is not my fault.”

“Maybe not. Still, a bad tree is apt to send up bad shoots.”

“You think I am like him?”

“I think blood tells.”

“Then you will not employ me?”

“No. I have no place for a boy I cannot depend upon.”

Ellis put on his cap.

“You have never tried me, Uncle Timothy.”

“I have tried Duncans before.”

“You have not tried me,” repeated Ellis.

He left without another word.

Timothy watched him cross the mill yard, walking erect though his disappointment was heavy enough to bend stronger backs.

“Proud,” muttered Timothy. “Joel was proud, too, when pride cost nothing.”

But he did not return immediately to his figures.

Ellis’s father had not been a wicked man. He had been pleasant, generous with what he did not need, and always ready to help a neighbour so long as the help required conversation rather than labour. He could tell a story, sing a song, or mend a fishing-line better than any man in Westdale. Unfortunately, stories, songs, and fishing-lines do not pay rent.

When Joel Duncan died after a short illness, there remained in the house two chairs, a pine table, three cracked plates, several unpaid bills, and his widow, who had long been delicate. Ellis also remained, and he was worth more than all the rest, though Westdale had not yet discovered it.

Mrs. Duncan earned a little by sewing, but her eyes were weak and her cough troublesome. Thus the duty of supporting the household fell upon Ellis. He did not complain. He had attended the village school faithfully, read every book he could borrow, and during vacations had learned the common tasks of farm and stable. He was both able and willing, but his father’s reputation went before him.

At Gorman’s store the proprietor shook his head.

“I reckon I don’t need another clerk.”

At the livery stable Hiram Bell said, “Ye look strong enough, but I can’t have a lad forgetting to feed the horses.”

“I shouldn’t forget.”

“Your father forgot most things.”

At the sawmill the foreman told him to come again in autumn. At two farms he was refused without explanation, which was kinder but no more useful.

On the eleventh day Ellis heard that Thomas Fillmore wanted a piece of rough pasture cleared.

Fillmore owned forty acres west of Dalrymple, besides two houses in the village and money at interest. He was not poor, though his shrewd, nutcracker face always suggested that poverty was standing behind him with a club. He disliked paying wages and had therefore failed to find a man willing to undertake the pasture.

The field contained elderberry bushes that had been cut, burned, grazed, cursed, and neglected for twenty years. They did not seem to mind either moon or mowing. Their roots were thick, twisted, and deep, and every spring they sent up new shoots with the cheerful persistence of weeds that know the landowner is stingy.

Ellis found Fillmore repairing a gate.

“You want them elders grubbed out?” he asked.

“Root and branch,” replied Fillmore. “Every last one.”

“What will you pay?”

Fillmore named a sum so small that Ellis thought he had misunderstood.

“For the whole pasture?”

“For the whole pasture. Take it or leave it.”

“How many acres?”

“Nigh on six.”

Ellis looked through the gate. The elderberries stood in dark, stubborn companies, some taller than his head.

“How long may I have?”

“Till the first of September.”

“I’ll do it.”

Fillmore’s eyebrows rose.

“Ye will?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Mind, I pay when the work’s done. If ye quit halfway, ye get nothing.”

“That is hard terms.”

“It’s my terms.”

Ellis looked again at the field. Then he thought of his mother bending over her sewing beside the window.

“I accept them.”

Fillmore wrote the agreement on the back of an old seed bill.

“There,” he said. “If ye clear it clean, I’ll pay. If ye don’t, I won’t.”

The next morning Ellis began.

He had an axe, a mattock, a spade, and a length of chain borrowed from Hiram Bell. First he cut the stems close to the ground. Then he dug around the roots, chopping through the lesser fibres and prying at the great central knots. Some came out after an hour. Others required half a day. One seemed to Ellis to reach directly beneath Dalrymple Mill and take hold of its foundation.

At noon he ate bread and cheese beneath an elm. At sunset he went home with blistered hands and aching shoulders.

“Well?” asked his mother.

“It is hard work.”

“I feared it would be.”

“But it is work,” Ellis added, smiling.

The blisters broke, healed, and hardened. His back ceased to ache so sharply. Every evening a larger brown space showed behind him, while the green enemy before him appeared as thick as ever.

People travelling the Westdale road saw him at labour. Some praised him; some laughed.

“Fillmore has caught another one,” said Hiram Bell. “That boy’ll never finish.”

“He’s Joel Duncan’s son,” replied the blacksmith. “He’ll tire of it.”

Ellis heard such remarks. Sometimes he flushed, but he kept his temper and swung the mattock.

Once Timothy drove past in his buggy. He saw Ellis knee-deep in a trench, his shirt dark with sweat.

“Still at it?” he called.

“Yes, sir.”

“How long have ye been working?”

“Three weeks yesterday.”

Timothy glanced over the cleared ground.

“Fillmore paying by the day?”

“No, sir. By the job.”

“Then he has the best of the bargain.”

“I knew the terms.”

Timothy clicked to his horse and drove on.

He said nothing more, but three days later he passed again, though the pasture was not on the shortest road to the mill.

July was hot. The earth baked hard, and the roots seemed to grip it more fiercely. Ellis rose at five, milked Mrs. Crane’s cow in exchange for milk, ate his breakfast, and walked two miles to the pasture. He worked until dusk except on Sundays, when he took his mother to church and read aloud to her in the afternoon.

By the middle of August, four acres were cleared.

One morning Jacob Green stopped his wagon at the gate. Green owned the largest dairy farm in the township and paid fair wages.

“Ellis,” he called, “come here a minute.”

Ellis laid down his axe.

“I’ve been watching ye. I need a hand till November. Ten dollars a month and board.”

Ellis stared at him. It was more money than he had expected to earn in years.

“When do you want me?”

“Tomorrow.”

“I can’t come tomorrow.”

“Why not?”

“I have to finish this pasture.”

Green laughed.

“Lemme understand. Fillmore is paying ye next to nothing to grub them elders, and ye refuse ten dollars a month?”

“I promised to finish.”

“Give him back what he has paid.”

“He has paid nothing.”

“Great snakes! Then quit. Ye owe him nothing.”

“I gave my word.”

“A promise ought to have sense in it.”

“It had sense when I made it. I knew the work and the price.”

Green looked at the boy more carefully.

“How much remains?”

“About two acres.”

“You won’t finish before September.”

“I mean to.”

“My offer won’t wait.”

“I am sorry.”

“Think it over till morning.”

“I have thought it over.”

Jacob Green gathered up his reins.

“Well, ye ain’t your father,” he said bluntly, and drove away.

Ellis stood a moment watching the wagon. Ten dollars a month would have bought food, medicine, and fuel. The temptation was severe, and no sensible person could have blamed a hungry boy for feeling it. But to feel temptation is not to yield to it. Ellis picked up his axe.

Behind a clump of sumacs on the road stood Timothy Duncan.

He had heard the conversation. He had come, as he told himself, to examine a stone wall belonging to the mill property. The wall was half a mile distant, but a man may deceive himself more easily than his neighbours.

Timothy watched Ellis strike twice at a root and wipe his forehead.

“Foolish bargain,” he murmured. “But he stands by it.”

That evening Timothy called at Thomas Fillmore’s house.

“How is Ellis doing?”

Fillmore pursed his lips.

“Fair.”

“Fair! He has cleared four acres.”

“Ground’s softer on that side.”

“Will he finish?”

“If he keeps at it.”

“What if he doesn’t?”

“Then I save my money.”

Timothy’s eyes hardened.

“You set harsh terms.”

“He accepted ’em.”

“That does not make them generous.”

“Since when did you object to a close bargain, Timothy?”

“A close bargain is one thing. Skinning a boy is another.”

Fillmore gave a dry chuckle.

“Blood tells, after all.”

Timothy went away without replying.

The final two weeks tested Ellis more cruelly than the first two months. The remaining bushes grew in stony ground. His left palm split open. Rain filled the trenches, and then two days of sun made the wet soil heavy as clay. Yet each morning he returned.

His mother begged him to rest.

“You will make yourself ill.”

“I have six days left.”

“Mr. Fillmore might extend the time.”

“He might, but he did not promise to.”

On the fourth day before September, Timothy again appeared at the pasture. Ellis was levering at the largest root in the field.

“That one looks tough,” said Timothy.

“It is.”

“Jacob Green hired Silas Ward.”

“I heard so.”

“Sorry ye lost the place?”

“Yes, sir.”

Timothy seemed surprised by the answer.

“Then why didn’t ye take it?”

“Because I promised Mr. Fillmore.”

“Suppose no one had known?”

“I should have known.”

Timothy walked to the edge of the cleared ground. The uprooted elders lay in heaps, twisted and ugly. The clean earth stretched behind them, ready for grass.

“Your father would have quit,” he said.

Ellis straightened. He was tired, sore, and less able than usual to govern his feelings.

“My father is not here to defend himself.”

“He needs a powerful defence.”

“He had faults. I know them better than you do, because Mother and I suffered from them. But he loved us. He was kind to me, and when he was dying he asked me to take care of Mother. I mean to do it. You may judge me by what I do, Uncle Timothy, but you shall not come here and insult him.”

For several seconds Timothy said nothing.

Ellis’s face was flushed, but his eyes did not fall.

At last Timothy nodded.

“Get on with your root,” he said, and walked away.

On the evening of August thirty-first, Thomas Fillmore inspected the pasture.

Ellis followed him with the mattock over one shoulder. Every bush had been cut, every root dug out, and every hole filled. Fillmore walked slowly, hoping, perhaps, to discover one surviving sprout worth the price of the contract. He found none.

“Well,” he said reluctantly, “ye finished.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Clean, too.”

“That was the agreement.”

Fillmore counted out the money. Then, after a struggle which did him credit, he added two dollars.

“What is that for?” asked Ellis.

“Don’t ask too many questions. Take it.”

Ellis took it.

“You’ve earned it,” said Fillmore. “Though I don’t want this getting about. Folks will think I’m growing careless.”

The following morning Ellis went to Dalrymple to buy flour. As he passed the mill, Timothy called from the office door.

“Come in.”

Ellis entered. This time his uncle pointed to a chair.

“Sit down.”

“I would rather stand, sir.”

“Please yourself. Did Fillmore pay?”

“Yes.”

“All of it?”

“And two dollars besides.”

“Then the world is nearing its end.”

Ellis smiled in spite of himself.

Timothy opened a ledger, closed it again, and cleared his throat.

“I was wrong about ye.”

Ellis waited.

“I judged ye by Joel. That was unjust. A man may inherit a name without inheriting the faults attached to it.”

“Yes, sir.”

“I need a reliable lad here at the mill. Ye would begin with errands, weighing sacks, and keeping tally. If ye prove able, there will be better work. Wages are eight dollars a month at first.”

“That is more than Mr. Green offered, counting board.”

“I know what he offered.”

Ellis looked up quickly, but Timothy’s face remained grave.

“There is another matter,” continued his uncle. “The cottage beyond my orchard is empty. Your mother can live there. No rent. She may take her meals at my house when she chooses, though my housekeeper will likely fuss over her whether she chooses or not.”

Ellis could not speak at once. Timothy turned toward the window.

“Well?” he demanded.

“I accept—on one condition.”

Timothy wheeled around.

“Condition! Ye are bold for a new hand.”

“You must never speak against Father to me again.”

Timothy frowned. Then his expression changed.

“I spoke the truth about his conduct.”

“Perhaps. But he is dead, and he was my father.”

Timothy paced once across the room.

“Very well. I’ll say no more against Joel.”

“Then I will work for you.”

“Mind, I expect ye at seven sharp.”

“I shall be here.”

“No shirking.”

“No, sir.”

“And if a task is begun, it must be finished.”

Ellis met his uncle’s eyes.

“You know it will be.”

Timothy’s mouth twitched at one corner.

“Aye,” he said. “I reckon I do.”

Mrs. Duncan moved to the orchard cottage before the week was out. The air there was better, and with freedom from worry her health slowly improved. Ellis proved as faithful at the mill as he had been in the pasture. He was not perfect, for no honest boy is; but he was prompt, teachable, and dependable, which are qualities close enough to perfection for ordinary business.

One winter evening, after Ellis had balanced a difficult account, Timothy looked over his shoulder.

“Ye have a good head.”

“Thank you, Uncle.”

“And steady habits.”

Ellis smiled.

“I hope so.”

Timothy tapped the ledger.

“Keep on as ye have begun, and there may come a time when I shall think of ye less as a hired lad and more as—well, as a son.”

He spoke roughly and immediately began sharpening a pencil that did not require it.

Ellis understood.

Outside, snow covered the Fillmore pasture. Beneath it no elder roots remained. They had been taken out slowly, painfully, and completely—root and branch. So, too, had an old prejudice been torn from Timothy Duncan’s heart; and if that was the harder labour, it must be admitted that Ellis had performed both tasks well.

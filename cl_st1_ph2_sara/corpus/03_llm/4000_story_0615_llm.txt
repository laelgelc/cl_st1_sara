# The Clock on the Mantelpiece

Every Friday evening, with a punctuality which neither war, weather, municipal elections, nor the annual inconvenience of the Bursley Wakes had yet impaired, Richard Morfe took supper with his sister Mary.

They lived in the same house.

This circumstance may seem to deprive the arrangement of some of its grandeur; but only to those who have not observed the ceremonial habits of the unmarried in the Five Towns. Richard and Mary breakfasted together daily, and dined together on Sundays, and encountered each other at various points on the staircase, and had even been known to exchange observations concerning the coal-scuttle on a Tuesday afternoon. But Friday evening was reserved. Friday evening was theirs. On Friday evening they belonged to one another with an exclusiveness which married couples, distracted by children and bills and visits, could scarcely hope to attain.

At half-past seven Mary entered the drawing-room, where Richard was already seated by the fire, and said:

“Well?”

“Well,” Richard replied.

Then Mary took the chair at the left of the hearth, Richard occupied the chair at the right, and they read. At nine they had coffee and thin biscuits. At ten Richard wound the clock. At a quarter-past ten they discussed whether it looked like frost. At half-past ten Mary retired. Nothing was compulsory; nevertheless, everything occurred.

The custom had existed for eleven years, having begun after the death of old Mrs. Morfe, who had ruled both children and the house from a padded couch, and who in her last illness had repeatedly urged them to look after each other. They had obeyed her with that completeness which occasionally renders obedience indistinguishable from revenge.

Richard was forty-two and looked forty-four. Mary was thirty-eight and looked thirty-seven—an advantage which she neither advertised nor forgot. Richard was cashier at the Crown and Provincial Bank in Hanbridge, a post that demanded discretion, a severe collar, and a daily mastery of sums which would have reduced many otherwise worthy men to tears. He was tall, dry, deliberate, and slightly bald at the temples. His moustache, short and sandy, had the air of having been approved by a committee. He had never married. The town explained this by saying that Mary would not permit it.

Mary had never married. The town explained this by saying that Richard required too much looking after.

Both explanations were false, and both were universally accepted.

They inhabited a double-fronted red-brick house in Wycliffe Avenue, Bursley, with encaustic tiles in the hall, a bathroom added at great expense in 1908, and a narrow conservatory in which nothing flourished except a fern and the reputation of the house. The drawing-room contained a piano, six chairs, two water-colours of Welsh scenery, and a black marble clock with green veins, presented to their father by the employees of Morfe & Sons upon his retirement from the manufacture of decorated earthenware. The inscription declared that the clock testified to thirty-nine years of mutual esteem. It had kept excellent time ever since, perhaps because it had so little else to do.

On the Friday with which we are concerned Richard read the *Musical Herald*, while Mary read a novel from the subscription library. Richard had not turned a page for seven minutes. Mary had turned three pages and understood none of them. Yet the room presented a picture of perfect composure.

The cause of Richard’s abstraction was a lady.

The cause of Mary’s abstraction was a gentleman.

And each, regarding the other over the edge of a book, felt a compassionate certainty that no such cause could possibly disturb that other’s mind.

At twenty minutes to nine the front-door bell rang.

Both started.

Richard looked at Mary.

Mary looked at Richard.

“A caller!” said Mary, as though announcing an invasion by sea.

“Who can it be at this hour?” Richard demanded.

The hour was not late. Indeed, throughout the Five Towns a great deal of social activity was only then loosening its collar. But callers on Friday evening were rare, and callers without notice were barbarous. Mary closed her novel upon one finger. Richard folded the *Musical Herald* in half. Each prepared to forgive the offender, if necessary, without enthusiasm.

There followed voices in the hall: first the cautious murmur of Annie, the maid; then a woman’s clearer answer; then Annie’s return from consultation with the dark powers governing the drawing-room.

“Miss Harracles,” she announced.

And Eva Harracles entered.

Now Eva Harracles was thirty-one, though a certain generosity of manner made her seem younger, and a certain decision in the eyes made her seem older whenever decision was required. She had brown hair, a pleasant, broad brow, a neat figure, and a smile which had not been trained to apologize for itself. She taught singing at the Bursley High School for Girls on three afternoons a week and accepted private pupils in Hanbridge, Turnhill, and such outlying mining villages as possessed pianos and ambition. Her father had once managed the Clough Pit at Knype. Her mother, widowed and invalided, lived with her at Moorthorne, beyond the northern tram terminus.

Eva sang contralto in the Bursley Orpheus Glee and Madrigal Society.

Richard conducted it.

I ought perhaps to explain that the Bursley Orpheus Glee and Madrigal Society was not a mere choir. It was an institution, a cause, and at moments a military power. It had performed in the Town Hall before the Mayor, had carried away the second prize at the North Staffordshire Musical Festival, and had once rendered Pearsall’s “Lay a Garland” so exquisitely that a rate-collector in the third row had been seen to wipe his spectacles. Its members quarrelled continuously over phrasing, attendance, solo parts, and the temperature of the rehearsal room; but against any rival organization they stood welded in homicidal brotherhood.

The chief rival was the Hanbridge Philharmonic Union, conducted by Mr. Josiah Broome, a solicitor’s clerk with long hair and foreign ideas about tempo.

Eva came forward quickly.

“I do hope you’ll forgive me, Miss Morfe. And you too, Mr. Morfe. I know Friday is—well, I know you don’t usually—”

“Not at all,” said Richard, rising with such abruptness that the *Musical Herald* fell to the carpet.

“Of course not,” said Mary.

Both lied politely.

Eva removed her gloves but not her hat. This indicated urgency.

“I’ve only called because I thought Mr. Morfe ought to know at once. It may be nothing. In fact, I’m sure it is nothing. Still, if it should be true—”

“What?” Mary asked.

Mary had the gift of reducing a cloud of language to a drop of information.

“It’s about Mr. Loggerheads.”

Richard’s expression altered.

“Simon?”

“Yes. They’re saying he means to leave us.”

“Leave the Orpheus?”

“And join the Hanbridge Philharmonic.”

Richard stared at her.

There are treacheries which a man may conceive in the abstract—embezzlement, desertion, even the secret watering of milk—but which, when brought home to him in a familiar name, shake the moral universe. Simon Loggerheads was the first tenor of the Orpheus. He could take the upper A without redness of countenance. He had sung under Richard for seven years. Richard himself had coached him through the tenor solo in Sullivan’s “The Long Day Closes,” correcting him patiently, bar by bar, until the man had ceased to sound like a chapel harmonium in distress.

“Impossible!” Richard said.

“I knew you’d say so.”

“Who told you?”

“Miss Plover heard it from her cousin in Hanbridge.”

“Which Miss Plover?”

“Agnes.”

Richard’s face expressed relief mixed with contempt.

“Agnes Plover would hear the Last Trump a fortnight before the Day of Judgment and get the key wrong.”

“Still,” Eva said, “her cousin’s engaged to Broome’s assistant secretary.”

“That proves nothing.”

“I said it proved nothing.”

“Loggerheads is a loyal man.”

“Exactly.”

“He has repeatedly expressed his opinion of Broome’s interpretation of Elgar.”

“He has.”

“And we begin the festival work on Monday.”

“We do.”

“He couldn’t.”

“No.”

Mary glanced from one to the other. Their agreement, though absolute, appeared to require many statements. She knew enough of choirs to understand that the rumor was serious; she also knew enough of Richard to understand that it had already been dismissed. What interested her was Eva’s arrival at the house on a Friday night to dismiss it in person.

“Did you come all the way from Moorthorne to tell Richard this?” she asked.

Eva coloured slightly.

“I had a late pupil in Bursley.”

“Ah.”

“And as I was passing near Wycliffe Avenue—”

“The High Street is not very near Wycliffe Avenue,” said Mary.

“No. But I thought—”

“You thought the matter urgent,” Richard intervened.

“Yes. For the Society.”

“For the Society,” Mary repeated.

There was nothing improper in her tone. Its propriety was crushing.

Richard picked up the *Musical Herald*, set it on the table, and then moved it two inches, as though its position had become crucial to the defence of Eva’s character. Eva spoke of Monday’s rehearsal. Richard replied. Mary contributed three syllables in four minutes. Soon the rumor had been examined, repudiated, revived, qualified, and condemned to death. In the course of this process Simon Loggerheads was described as steadfast, sensible, musically conscientious, vain, susceptible to flattery, incapable of treason, and precisely the sort of man whom Broome might attempt to steal.

“And if he did go,” Richard concluded, “we should manage without him.”

This was the supreme fiction of the evening. Without Simon, the tenors of the Orpheus consisted of a dentist who sang sharp, two young men who sang only when accompanied by the entire bass section, and Mr. Berridge, whose attendance depended upon the health of a collie dog.

The clock chimed nine.

Eva looked up.

“Nine! I must go.”

“You’ve plenty of time,” Richard said. “The Moorthorne tram leaves the Duck Bank at twenty-five past.”

“It connects with the last car at Longshaw at five to ten.”

“Easily.”

“I oughtn’t to have stayed.”

“You’ve been here ten minutes.”

“Twelve,” said Mary.

Eva put on her gloves, then somehow remained. Richard asked whether her mother’s bronchitis had improved. Eva said it had, though the east wind was troublesome. Mary reopened her novel. The conversation wandered to the new soprano from Turnhill, whose upper register was admired by the committee and distrusted by every soprano already in the choir. It proceeded thence to the heating apparatus at the rehearsal rooms. Richard, ordinarily concise, discovered remarkable resources on the subject of heating apparatus.

Presently Eva glanced at the mantelpiece.

“Is that clock right?”

“Certainly,” said Richard.

“It says ten past nine.”

“Then it’s ten past nine.”

Mary read.

A few minutes later Eva looked again.

“How odd!”

“What?”

“It still says ten past.”

Richard drew out his watch.

“By Jove!”

Mary lowered her book.

“My watch says twenty-nine minutes past nine,” Richard continued. “The clock’s stopped.”

“Stopped?” Mary echoed.

“It has.”

The fact lay before them, astounding and indecent. The presentation clock had stopped. It had maintained its duty through frost, thunder, the General Strike, and the installation of electric light; and now, on a Friday evening, it had abandoned time itself.

Eva sprang up.

“I shall miss the Duck Bank car!”

“If you hurry—” Richard began.

“I can’t reach it in six minutes.”

“There’s the thirty-seven from St Luke’s Square,” said Mary.

“That doesn’t connect at Longshaw,” Eva replied. “Not on Fridays.”

Richard knew this to be true. On Fridays the thirty-seven terminated at Bleakridge depot, for reasons which the Tramways Committee had explained in a document of nine pages and which no passenger had ever understood.

“There’s the train,” he said.

“The last train leaves Hanbridge at nine-forty. I couldn’t get there.”

“A cab?”

Eva laughed. “To Moorthorne?”

The extravagance required would have financed three private singing lessons.

“You can stay here,” Mary said.

Both Richard and Eva looked at her.

“Of course,” Mary continued. “Annie can prepare the spare room.”

Eva protested. Her mother would worry; a neighbour was sitting with her only until ten; no message could reach her; and in any case she could not dream of imposing.

Mary listened and then performed an action so unexpected that Richard afterwards wondered whether he had imagined it.

She urged him to escort Eva home.

“You might walk with her as far as Longshaw,” she said. “There are sometimes cabs by the Crown Hotel. Or you could see if the Bleakridge conductor knows of a connection.”

“But—” Richard began.

“You can’t let her go alone.”

“No, naturally.”

“And if there’s no cab, you can walk on to Moorthorne. It’s scarcely three miles from Longshaw.”

“Three and a quarter,” said Eva.

“Then Richard had better start.”

Richard stared at his sister. Mary’s face was composed, almost benevolent.

“My dear girl,” he said, “it will make me very late.”

“I shall survive.”

“You’ll be alone.”

“I have been alone before.”

“On Friday?”

Mary’s lips tightened.

“Even on Friday.”

Thus authorized and commanded, Richard fetched his coat and hat. Eva renewed her apologies. Mary rejected them. Annie opened the front door and admitted a draught of such vigour that the hall gas flickered.

“Don’t wait up,” Richard told Mary.

“I probably shall.”

“And don’t forget to wind the clock.”

“I won’t.”

Richard and Eva passed through the portals. The door shut.

Mary stood motionless for three seconds. Then she went into the drawing-room, took the black marble clock in both hands, and pushed the minute hand forward to the correct time.

The clock immediately resumed ticking.

It had not stopped of itself.

Outside, Richard and Eva walked to St Luke’s Square at a pace dignified enough for respectability and rapid enough for romance. The night was cold, with a faint smoky mist descending upon the roofs. Shop windows cast yellow rectangles across the pavement; a public-house door opened and released noise, warmth, and two potters; from the direction of Hanbridge came the clang of a tram negotiating the incline.

Neither spoke until they had turned from Wycliffe Avenue.

Then Richard said:

“That was rather fine of Mary.”

“Very.”

“She can be extraordinarily considerate.”

“Yes.”

“People misunderstand her.”

“I’m sure they do.”

“She likes you.”

Eva considered this.

“Does she?”

“Oh, yes.”

This was not merely untrue; it was one of those heroic assertions by which men attempt to influence fate. Eva slipped her hand through Richard’s arm. He glanced about. The avenue was empty except for a boy delivering a parcel and a woman drawing a blind. Nevertheless, he removed Eva’s hand.

“Not here,” he murmured.

“You’re frightened of Mary.”

“I am not frightened of Mary.”

“You are.”

“I merely don’t wish her to hear from some busybody—before we tell her properly.”

Eva smiled. “When shall we tell her properly?”

“Soon.”

“You said that three weeks ago.”

“This evening would have been impossible.”

“I didn’t expect to come this evening.”

“No?”

“No. The story about Simon was only an excuse.”

Richard halted beneath a municipal lamp.

“An excuse?”

“I wanted to see you.”

“But the rumor?”

“Agnes did tell me. It may even be true. Though I don’t believe it.”

“Then you came chiefly—”

“To see you.”

“My darling!”

He said it severely, like a conductor reproving an inattentive alto. But he took her hand, and in the smoky shadow between two lamps the bank cashier and the singing teacher, whose combined age was seventy-three, behaved with the imprudence of persons not yet trusted with latchkeys.

They had been engaged for eighteen days.

The engagement had arisen after a rehearsal of Stanford’s “The Blue Bird.” Richard had accompanied Eva to the tram, had complained that the contraltos were flat in the second verse, and had suddenly asked whether she could contemplate sharing her life with him. Eva, who had contemplated it for approximately two years, said she thought she could. The whole transaction occupied less than four minutes, but its concealment had already demanded hours of diplomacy.

“I mean to tell Mary tomorrow morning,” Richard said, when they resumed walking.

“At breakfast?”

“At breakfast.”

“Will she be shocked?”

“Utterly.”

“Will she mind?”

Richard hesitated.

“She may feel the change.”

“Richard, you’re forty-two.”

“I’m aware of my age.”

“She can hardly have expected you never to marry.”

“My dear Eva, Mary has never expected me to do anything she hasn’t arranged beforehand.”

“Then perhaps she arranged the clock.”

He laughed.

“Mary? Stop Father’s clock? She regards it as sacred.”

They reached St Luke’s Square just as the thirty-seven tram rattled in, magnificently illuminated and nearly empty. Richard made inquiries of the conductor, who declared that there was no connection from Bleakridge, had never been a connection from Bleakridge, and would not personally trust any printed notice suggesting otherwise. They boarded nevertheless.

During the journey Eva mentioned Simon Loggerheads again.

“Agnes says he’s been seen in Wycliffe Avenue several evenings lately.”

“Has he?”

“Near your house.”

Richard frowned. “He knows people in this district, I daresay.”

“She says he calls on Miss Morfe.”

“Mary?”

“Yes.”

Richard gave a short laugh.

“You needn’t laugh.”

“My dear Eva, Simon comes occasionally about subscriptions. Mary is treasurer of the Ladies’ Cottage Hospital Fund.”

“Agnes thinks he admires her.”

“Agnes’s tongue is a scorpion fed upon imagination.”

“Mary is very handsome.”

“Certainly. In her way.”

“And Simon is a bachelor.”

“So is the archdeacon. You aren’t going to engage Mary to every unmarried man in North Staffordshire.”

“I only said—”

“Besides, Mary would have told me.”

Eva looked out of the window to conceal a smile. Richard’s confidence in his sister’s revelations was sublime, especially from a man who had hidden an engagement from her for eighteen days.

At Bleakridge they found no cab. They therefore walked through Longshaw, past the dark pottery works, across the canal bridge, and up the road toward Moorthorne. The way was long, but it possessed advantages. Richard twice forgot that the weather was cold. Eva, whose mother would indeed be anxious, nevertheless failed to regret the stopped clock.

Meanwhile Mary waited in the drawing-room.

At five minutes to ten the bell rang again.

She rose before Annie could come from the kitchen.

“Mr. Loggerheads, miss,” said Annie, opening the door.

Simon Loggerheads entered with a soft felt hat in his hand and an expression of apprehensive importance. He was forty-six, substantial in body, pink in complexion, and accurately dressed. By profession he was confidential correspondence clerk to the Bursley and District Building Society, a title of which he was justly proud. He had a high, pale forehead and a small golden moustache. When embarrassed he said “Oh!” This habit, though inadequate to the higher flights of conversation, had carried him through many drawing-rooms.

“Mary!”

“Simon!”

They gazed at each other. Then Mary seized his arm and drew him into the room.

“He’s gone,” she said.

“Gone?”

“With Eva Harracles.”

“Oh!”

“He won’t be back for hours.”

“Oh!”

“And you are late.”

“I couldn’t get away. Old Hawkes wanted the Knype mortgage letters copied before Monday, and then I missed the nine-twenty from Hanbridge.”

“You might have telephoned.”

“To say what?”

“That you were delayed.”

“Richard might have answered.”

Mary had no reply to this. The telephone stood on a bracket in the hall and was an instrument without moral discrimination.

Simon placed his hat on the piano and approached her. His apprehensive importance softened.

“Well?” he said.

“Well?”

“Here we are.”

“Yes.”

“And alone.”

“Yes.”

He kissed her.

The kiss was not notable for technical brilliance, but it was earnest, prolonged, and repeated. Simon then knocked his hat off the piano, and Mary, in bending to retrieve it, laughed. Richard had not heard her laugh in that fashion since 1906, when a cousin fell backward into a hamper at a picnic.

Simon beamed.

“What a lark!” he said.

This phrase was not ideally suited to a gentleman of forty-six embracing the treasurer of the Ladies’ Cottage Hospital Fund beneath a water-colour of Snowdon; but joy is not always felicitous in its vocabulary.

They too were secretly engaged.

Their engagement was six weeks old and had followed a concert committee meeting at which Mary accused Simon of failing to obtain two promised advertisements for the programme. He defended himself badly, accompanied her home in rain, lent her an umbrella which belonged to the Building Society’s manager, and proposed marriage outside the Bursley Conservative Club. Mary accepted before he had completed the sentence.

They had intended to tell Richard that very evening.

This intention had been formed with great care. Simon was to call at nine-fifteen; Mary was to lead the conversation toward the loneliness of advancing years; Simon was then to request a few private words with Richard; and the announcement was to be made in the manner of an honourable negotiation between men. Mary herself had drafted the substance of Simon’s speech. Unfortunately, Eva Harracles had arrived, the clock had stopped, and the honourable negotiation had been postponed.

“Did he suspect anything?” Simon asked.

“Richard? Nothing.”

“You’re sure?”

“Perfectly.”

“He looked at me rather queerly on Wednesday.”

“He always looks queerly at tenors who miss an entry.”

“I didn’t miss it. Berridge came in early.”

“Then why should Richard suspect?”

“I don’t know.”

“Exactly.”

Simon sat beside Mary on the sofa. Their hands met with the furtive solemnity of conspirators exchanging state papers.

“How did you get rid of him?” he asked.

“I didn’t.”

“But you said—”

“Eva got rid of him. Or, rather, I sent him with Eva.”

“Why?”

“She missed her last connection.”

“At ten o’clock?”

“She thought it was nine-ten when it was really half-past nine.”

Simon looked at the mantel clock, which now indicated two minutes past ten and ticked with brazen innocence.

“Was the clock slow?”

“It had stopped.”

“Your father’s clock?”

“Yes.”

“Never!”

Mary’s mouth curved.

“I stopped it.”

Simon blinked.

“You?”

“Yes.”

“On purpose?”

“Of course on purpose. Do you imagine I stop clocks accidentally?”

“But why?”

“To make certain Richard would go out.”

“Oh!”

“I knew Eva had to return to Moorthorne. I knew that if she missed the proper car Richard could hardly let her travel alone. And I knew you were coming.”

“Oh!”

“I stopped it when Annie went upstairs for the coffee tray.”

Simon regarded her with admiration not unmixed with alarm. Until that moment he had imagined himself the bolder party because he had once pressed her hand in the Hanbridge covered market. He now perceived that he was engaged to a woman capable of laying violent hands upon chronology.

“That was clever,” he said.

“It was necessary.”

“And Richard never guessed?”

“Richard believes the clock incapable of stopping, and me incapable of stopping it. The first belief made him astonished; the second will keep him ignorant.”

Simon squeezed her hand.

“You’re a wonderful woman, Mary.”

“No, I’m not.”

“You are.”

“I merely wanted half an hour with you.”

At this confession Mary looked away. Simon’s pink face became pinker. He leaned toward her, and the black marble clock, restored to honourable service after its brief criminal career, ticked discreetly over their heads.

Presently Simon said:

“Do you think there’s anything between Richard and Miss Harracles?”

Mary turned sharply.

“Certainly not.”

“I’ve heard talk.”

“From whom?”

“Well—Plover.”

“Agnes?”

“Her cousin told Hawkes’s niece that Richard has taken Miss Harracles home after rehearsal three times.”

“He conducts the choir. He takes many people home.”

“Not to Moorthorne.”

“He may walk with her to the tram.”

“And last month they were seen having tea at the Hanbridge Café Royal.”

“Choir business.”

“On a Thursday?”

“Music does not cease on Thursdays.”

Simon meditated upon this institutional truth.

“Somebody said they were engaged.”

Mary gave a dry laugh.

“Richard? Engaged to Eva Harracles?”

“She’s a fine-looking woman.”

“I have eyes.”

“And she sings well.”

“I have ears.”

“You don’t like her.”

“I neither like nor dislike her. But Richard would have told me.”

“Perhaps he’s nervous.”

“Of me?”

Simon prudently avoided an answer.

“My dear Simon,” Mary continued, “Richard is settled. He has his bank, his choir, his books, and this house. He is not a marrying man.”

Simon, himself forty-six and until six weeks earlier considered by all Bursley a permanently finished bachelor, said:

“No. I suppose not.”

“Besides, Eva is independent.”

“So are you.”

“That is different.”

“How?”

“It simply is.”

Simon accepted this. A man engaged to Mary Morfe learned very quickly that some propositions were not to be investigated but saluted.

“And I don’t care for the idea of Loggerheads going to Hanbridge,” Richard was saying at almost the same moment, two miles away on the Moorthorne Road.

Eva laughed. “You’re still thinking about the choir?”

“A conductor must.”

“Even when he’s with his future wife?”

“Especially then. A conductor who neglects his tenors is soon without any.”

“You said Simon was loyal.”

“He is. But vanity is a dangerous solvent.”

“Perhaps Mary could persuade him to stay.”

“Mary?”

“If he calls on her.”

Richard made an impatient sound.

“You’ve got that absurd story in your head.”

“I only think they would suit one another.”

“My sister and Simon Loggerheads!”

“Why not?”

“Mary would terrify him.”

“Does she terrify you?”

“Certainly not.”

“Of course not.”

“She and I understand one another perfectly.”

Eva’s smile was visible even in the darkness.

“What will she say tomorrow?”

“First she will stare. Then she will ask how long this has been going on. Then she will say she had already noticed it.”

“Though she hasn’t.”

“Though she hasn’t. After that she will become practical about rooms.”

“Rooms?”

“The spare room must be turned into a dressing-room. Unless we take another house.”

Eva stopped walking.

“Richard, we are taking another house.”

“Are we?”

“We are.”

He reflected. The thought of leaving Wycliffe Avenue had hitherto existed only as a pale possibility kept at a great distance from the furniture. Now it advanced upon him with rooms, rent, curtains, and a front door of its own.

“Yes,” he said bravely. “Naturally.”

At the Morfe house Simon had reached an equally formidable portion of the future.

“Would Richard remain here?” he asked.

“Why shouldn’t he?”

“Well, when we’re married—”

“This is my house as much as his.”

“Exactly.”

“And there is plenty of room.”

“Plenty.”

“You don’t object to living here?”

“Object? My dear girl!”

This was his first consideration of the proposal.

“It would save expense,” Mary said. “And Richard is at the bank all day. In the evenings he has rehearsals and committees.”

“And Fridays?”

Mary hesitated.

“Friday would have to be reconsidered.”

Simon nodded gravely. Institutions cannot be abolished without transitional arrangements.

“Still,” he said, “Richard may prefer to make other plans.”

“What other plans?”

“I don’t know.”

“He has none.”

“How will you tell him?”

“At breakfast.”

“Tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

“Wouldn’t you rather I came round?”

“No. If you are present, he will feel obliged to behave ceremoniously. Alone with me, he can recover from the shock.”

“Will it be a shock?”

“An enormous shock.”

“He may have guessed.”

“Simon!”

“Only a little.”

“He has guessed nothing.”

“I’m jolly glad.”

Mary surveyed him.

“You are afraid of Richard.”

“I’m not afraid of him.”

“You are.”

“I respect him.”

“You fear his manner.”

“His manner can be dry.”

“So can mine.”

Simon smiled with the courage of a man who knew he would soon be kissed again.

“Yours is different, old girl.”

Mary ought to have objected to “old girl.” She had objected to it the first time, the second time, and the third. On this occasion she merely adjusted his tie, which needed no adjustment.

At half-past ten Simon rose.

“You must go,” she said.

“I suppose so.”

“Richard may return.”

“I should like to meet him and have it over.”

“No. Not tonight. Everything has been disturbed.”

“I could tell him straight out.”

“You would say ‘Oh!’ six times and knock something down.”

“I wouldn’t.”

“You knocked your hat down.”

“That was different.”

“It was a hat. Richard is more formidable than a hat.”

Simon sighed, but submitted. In the hall Mary helped him into his overcoat, not because he required assistance but because lovers extract tenderness from garments when direct expression proves alarming.

At the door he said:

“Breakfast, then?”

“Breakfast.”

“And you’ll send me a note?”

“I’ll telephone at the office.”

“Not before ten. Hawkes is always about before ten.”

“One minute past.”

He kissed her once more and descended the steps.

As Simon reached the corner of Wycliffe Avenue, Richard turned into it from the opposite end.

The two men saw each other beneath a lamp.

“Hullo, Morfe!” Simon called.

“Hullo, Loggerheads!”

They stopped.

“Out late,” Richard observed.

“Yes. A little business.”

“So have I.”

“Oh!”

There was a pause. Simon felt the engagement ring in his waistcoat pocket—Mary did not yet wear it publicly—and Richard felt in his own pocket the small envelope containing particulars of a house in Porthill which Eva had suggested they inspect. Each man possessed news capable, as he believed, of annihilating the other’s composure. Each decided that the pavement, the smoky air, and eleven o’clock at night were unsuitable to the majesty of disclosure.

“Choir on Monday,” Richard said.

“I shall be there.”

“Good.”

“You didn’t think I was leaving, did you?”

Richard stared.

“Leaving?”

“For Hanbridge.”

“Certainly not.”

“Oh! Somebody’s been talking.”

“Pay no attention.”

“I don’t.”

They shook hands with unusual warmth, each privately congratulating himself on his command of secrecy, and proceeded in opposite directions.

Mary had returned to the drawing-room. She arranged the chairs precisely as they had stood before Eva’s arrival, placed Richard’s *Musical Herald* beside his chair, and took up her novel. She read the same page for the fourth time.

At five minutes to eleven Richard entered.

“You’re back,” she said.

“As you see.”

“Did Miss Harracles reach home safely?”

“Yes. There was no cab. I walked all the way.”

“That was good of you.”

“Not at all.”

“Her mother?”

“Anxious, but quite well.”

There was a pause.

“Simon Loggerheads passed me at the corner,” Richard said.

“Did he?”

“Yes. What was he doing here?”

“How should I know?”

“He seemed to be coming from this direction.”

“Many people come from this direction. It contains several hundred houses.”

“True.”

Richard sat down. Mary read. Richard examined the fire. Each was intensely conscious of tomorrow’s breakfast.

After a minute Mary asked:

“Is Simon leaving the Orpheus?”

“No. The rumor was nonsense.”

“I thought it would be.”

“Eva merely felt bound to tell me.”

“Very conscientious.”

“She is.”

Mary turned a page.

“Do you suppose,” Richard said with elaborate carelessness, “that Simon ever calls here about the Hospital Fund?”

“Occasionally.”

“Ah.”

“Why?”

“No reason.”

Mary’s eyes remained upon the novel.

“Do you suppose Eva often misses her tram after rehearsal?” she asked.

“Never.”

“Ah.”

“Why?”

“No reason.”

The clock ticked. It had become the only honest creature in the room, and even its honesty was recent.

At eleven Richard rose.

“I’m tired.”

“So am I.”

“We’ll talk at breakfast.”

“Yes,” said Mary. “I have something to tell you.”

Richard smiled.

“Curious. So have I.”

“Business?”

“Not precisely.”

“Choir?”

“No.”

Mary looked at him. Richard looked at Mary. For one instant each experienced a faint disturbance, a tiny ripple upon the profound lake of certainty. Then certainty restored itself. Mary supposed that Richard wished to discuss alterations at the bank, or possibly a new accompanist. Richard supposed that Mary intended to mention Annie’s wages or the replacement of the dining-room curtains.

“Eight o’clock?” he said.

“As usual.”

“Good night, Mary.”

“Good night, Richard.”

He paused at the door.

“By Jove, it’s odd about that clock.”

“Very odd.”

“First time it has ever stopped.”

“Yes.”

“You did wind it?”

“Yes.”

Richard listened to its steady beat.

“Seems perfectly right now.”

“It does.”

He went upstairs.

Mary remained alone for a moment. Then she approached the mantelpiece and laid one hand upon the black marble case.

“You behaved beautifully,” she told the clock.

This compliment was unjust, for the clock had done nothing; but instruments of conspiracy are often awarded the glory due to the conspirator.

At eight o’clock the next morning Richard and Mary would sit opposite one another at the breakfast table. Annie would bring kidneys. The coffee would be poured. Richard would clear his throat. Mary would say, “Before you begin, I have something to tell you.” Richard would insist that she speak first; Mary would insist that he speak first; and beneath these courtesies two middle-aged hearts would wait in triumph, each certain of exploding a bomb beneath the other’s ordered existence.

What happened when the explosions occurred—what Mary said of Eva, what Richard said of Simon, who first exclaimed, who denied astonishment, who demanded dates, and how many cups of coffee grew cold—you can picture for yourself.

It is enough to record that throughout the night, under one respectable roof in Wycliffe Avenue, the brother slept with the confidence of a man who knew his sister completely, and the sister slept with the confidence of a woman who had never been deceived in her estimate of her brother.

And between their rooms, on the landing below, the presentation clock ticked on with impartial regularity, knowing better.

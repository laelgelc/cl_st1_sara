### The Picture in the Horse-Car

I had stopped at Minver’s to look at a little painting which he had lately cleaned, or, as he more cautiously expressed it, “rescued from the consequences of somebody else’s cleaning.” It stood upon an easel near the window, and represented a yellowish Italian villa with a long, low terrace, a black-green file of cypresses, and a hill withdrawing into that violet atmosphere which seems natural in pictures and improbable everywhere else.

Minver stepped back, narrowing his eyes at it.

“It isn’t Blakey at his greatest,” he said, “but it’s Blakey before he knew he had a manner. That counts for something.”

His brother Joe, who was stretched in the easiest chair and was contributing nothing to the criticism but tobacco smoke, said, “It counts for a good deal more than the picture.”

Minver turned on him. “You’ve always hated it.”

“I’ve never hated it. I’ve had better reasons for feeling strongly about it.”

“You mean you stole it once.”

“I didn’t steal it.”

“You lost it, then.”

“I didn’t lose it, either.”

“You did everything but keep it,” said Minver.

I asked whether the picture belonged to Joe, and Minver laughed.

“Belonged? It was presented to him under circumstances calculated to make the gift sacred; and within three hours he had got rid of it so completely that all the horse-car companies in Boston couldn’t find it.”

Joe took his cigar from his mouth and regarded the ash with a judicial air. “If you’re going to tell it, tell it right.”

“I can’t tell it right. Nobody can. You’ve told it so often that the facts are worn through.”

“That’s why I have to tell it,” Joe answered. “You fellows always leave out the interesting part.”

“The interesting part,” said Minver to me, “is where he went off his nut.”

Joe ignored him with the patience of one who has long accepted interruption as a brother’s substitute for admiration.

“You see,” he began, “Blakey gave me that picture when I was engaged. It was a mighty nice thing for him to do, because he hadn’t any pictures to spare in those days, and though this one was small, he thought well of it. He’d painted it in Florence—or outside Florence; I never knew whether the place was legally in the city or merely looked down on it—and it was the villa where I first met Annie.”

“You told Blakey that,” Minver put in. “The villa never confessed it.”

“It happened to be true. Annie was making one of those bread-and-butter visits that girls used to make before they discovered Europe was a place to be educated in. She was staying with the Masons, who had rented the villa for the spring, and I went there with Blakey. Annie came through a doorway with a basket of roses, and—well, that’s neither here nor there.”

“It was very much there at the time,” said Minver.

Joe smiled. “Blakey knew about it, anyway, and when he heard we were to be married he hunted up the sketch, put it into a gilt frame, and brought it round to me. He said, ‘Here’s the scene of the disaster.’ I told him it was first-rate, which it was, and that Annie would value it beyond anything we were likely to get unless somebody gave us a house. Then I started out to take it to her.

“The family were living in Brookline. I was in rooms on Pinckney Street, and I had an errand downtown before I went out. I wrapped the picture myself—brown paper, with stout string crossing both ways—and carried it under my arm. It was about eleven in the morning, the day before Washington’s Birthday. I remember that because the holiday made all the later arrangements uncertain, and every man I spoke to warned me that the offices might close early.”

Minver looked at me. “Observe the date. It proves everything.”

“It proves I remember the date,” said Joe. “I went down Charles Street, took a car across to Scollay Square, attended to my errand in State Street, and came back by way of Tremont. On that first car I put the picture on the seat beside me. That’s important.”

“You usually say it twice,” Minver suggested.

“I’m going to say it as often as it’s needed. I put it on my right, between me and the side of the car, and kept my hand on it nearly all the way. The car was crowded after we reached Bowdoin Square, and a stout woman with parcels sat opposite me. Every time we lurched, she looked at the picture as if I were trying to slide it under her feet. So I held it steady. When I got off, I took it with me. There isn’t any question about that part.”

“There wasn’t any question about any part,” said Minver, “until the picture arrived from somewhere else.”

Joe waved this aside. “I was to take the Brookline car later, but first I wanted some flowers for Annie. There was a florist’s near the corner—Dunham’s; the old place with the green wooden tubs outside and a window always misted as if the plants breathed more heavily than ordinary people. I went in with the picture under my arm.

“The shopman knew me by sight, and he knew where the flowers were going, too. In those days a young fellow couldn’t buy six roses without the whole neighborhood taking an interest in his intentions. I told him I wanted something cheerful and not too formal. He offered me carnations. I said carnations were friendly, and my feelings were not. Then he showed me roses at a price which implied a serious attachment, and I took them.”

“Eight,” said Minver.

“A dozen.”

“It was eight when you told this last winter.”

“The number has nothing to do with it.”

“It has everything to do with the moral character of the witness.”

Joe appealed to me. “You see what I’ve had to contend with for thirty years. Well, while the man tied up the flowers, I set the picture down somewhere. I didn’t remember setting it down. That’s the point. I paid him, took the flowers, and went out to the car.

“I boarded at the corner. It was one of those old cars with the narrow aisle and the little stove in the middle, though I may be mixing the stove with another car—”

“Your scrupulousness does you honor,” said Minver.

“—and I sat on the left-hand side, facing forward. There were not more than six people aboard. I put the picture beside me, just as I had on the earlier car, laid my right hand on it, and held the flowers in my left. At least, that is what I remembered afterward. I remembered it as distinctly as I remember sitting here.”

Joe dropped his hand upon the arm of the chair.

“I can feel the edge of the frame through the paper now. The string made a little ridge under my palm. At Dartmouth Street, or thereabouts, Harvey Tisdale got in. You didn’t know Tisdale? He was a cheerful, freckled fellow in the insurance business, always hurrying and always willing to miss an appointment if he met anybody he knew. He sat opposite me and began asking about the wedding. He wanted to know whether I was frightened. I told him I was only frightened Annie might come to her senses before Thursday. Then we talked about a dinner somebody was giving me, and all the while I kept my hand on the picture.

“Tisdale got off at Massachusetts Avenue. I remember his standing on the rear platform, grinning in through the glass and pointing at the flowers. I nodded to him. Then I moved the flowers from my left hand to my right—or I thought I did—and when the conductor called my stop, I rose, went out, and walked up to the house.

“Annie met me in the hall. I gave her the flowers. She looked behind me and said, ‘Did you bring anything else?’”

Minver groaned. “She never said that.”

“She may not have said it in those words. She looked as if she expected something else.”

“She looked at the flowers,” said Minver. “Most people do when flowers are handed to them.”

Joe went on without haste. “It came over me all at once. I had left the picture in the car.

“I ran out of the house before I had told her what was wrong. There was no car in sight in either direction. I thought I might catch the one I had left if I cut across to the stables, but after running half a block I realized it would be miles ahead. So I went back to the nearest waiting room and found the starter.

“He was a comfortable man with gray side-whiskers, and he took the view that no article had ever been permanently lost upon a Boston horse-car. Umbrellas, he admitted, might change hands; but a framed picture wrapped in brown paper was not an object a man could carry off inadvertently.

“‘Don’t worry a mite,’ he said. ‘The conductor’ll turn it in at the end of the run.’

“I described the car, the time, the place where I sat, and the people in it. I told him about Tisdale. I told him I had kept my hand on the picture until Tisdale left. The starter wrote everything down on a pad, though I noticed that he stopped writing before I stopped talking.

“He said the car ought to reach the outer station at twelve-forty. He telephoned—or sent word; there were telephones, but not enough of them to be where you wanted one—and told them to inspect it. I waited. Annie’s mother sent a message by the maid asking whether I was coming back to luncheon. I said I was detained on business.

“At one o’clock the report came: no picture.”

“Curtain,” said Minver.

“That was only the first act. The starter said it might have been picked up by the conductor and carried in his coat until he could turn it over to the lost-property clerk. Or perhaps the car had been changed. Cars were always being changed. The horse might go lame, a trace might break, a wheel might object to public service—there were twenty reasons why the particular car I had ridden in might not be the car they inspected.

“He gave me a note to the superintendent, and I went to the main office. The superintendent was a brisk little man who treated lost articles as if they were merely delayed passengers. He said, ‘A picture? You’re lucky it wasn’t a pocketbook. We’ll have it by evening.’

“He asked whether my name was on it. I said no. He asked what it represented. Now, I didn’t want to tell him.”

“Here,” Minver said, lifting one finger, “we approach the really discreditable feature.”

“I was ashamed of having forgotten it,” Joe continued. “You understand? If I said it was the villa where I first met Annie, and that Blakey had painted it and given it to us as a wedding present, every man in the office would feel that I couldn’t care much for Annie if I could walk away from the thing within an hour of receiving it.”

“An intelligent conclusion,” said Minver.

“So I told him it was a little landscape—a field, some trees, and a white cow.”

“You added the cow,” Minver said, “to make yourself easier to respect.”

“I wanted something easy to recognize.”

“You turned Tuscany into agricultural New England.”

Joe laughed. “Well, the superintendent had a circular sent to the stables. I went from there to the terminal where the conductors reported. One conductor remembered seeing me. At least he remembered a man with flowers. He thought the man had carried a flat parcel, too, but when I pressed him he said he couldn’t be positive. Another man said a boy had found a paper-wrapped object in a car that morning, but it proved to be a patent ironing board.

“I returned to Brookline at half-past two. Annie was waiting for me in the parlor with the flowers in a vase. She asked what the business was that had driven me from the house like a criminal escaping arrest.

“I said, ‘Annie, I’ve done a stupid thing.’

“She said, ‘That isn’t news, Joe. What particular thing?’

“Then I told her Blakey had given us a painting and I had left it in the car.”

“Not all,” said Minver.

“No. I said it was one of his Italian sketches.”

“You said it was a study of clouds.”

“I didn’t want her to know it was the villa.”

“Because your crime would then have become emotional.”

“Exactly. She might forgive negligence, but how could I make her believe I loved her if I had forgotten the place where I fell in love with her? You can call that nonsense now, but when a man is four days from being married, his mind isn’t governed by the ordinary statutes.”

I said that this seemed reasonable enough, and Minver accused me of encouraging him.

“Annie took it better than I did,” Joe resumed. “She said it would certainly be found. Everybody said that. Her father came in and said horse-car employees were the most honest body of men in the Commonwealth. Her mother said no one would want a picture without knowing what it was. The maid said her cousin had once left a basket of laundry on a car and received it back before supper, with one handkerchief more than she had sent. Every instance of recovery made me feel worse, because my picture alone remained missing.

“At four I went downtown again. No news. At five I found Tisdale at his office. I asked him whether he remembered the parcel beside me.

“‘Perfectly,’ he said.

“‘I had my hand on it?’

“‘The whole time.’

“‘When you got off?’

“‘You were holding it then.’

“That settled the fact in my mind. I could see the car, the stove, the dirty snow streaking the windows, Tisdale’s freckled face, the flowers in my left hand, and my right hand resting on the picture. I asked whether anyone had taken his seat when he left. He thought a thin man in a derby had moved across the aisle. Within ten minutes I had created a distinct suspicion of that thin man.”

“You wanted him arrested,” said Minver.

“I wanted him identified. There’s a difference. The office promised to question the conductor again. They advised me to call the next morning.

“The next morning was the holiday. I was up before seven and at the stable before eight. The night clerk had heard nothing. I examined three cars myself, though I knew it was absurd; I looked under seats, behind the stove, even on the platform roof of one car, because a stableman said boys sometimes hid things there. At nine the superintendent arrived, in a silk hat and a temper, on his way to some civic ceremony. He told me, still quite confidently, that the picture would come back. At ten the conductor was produced. He remembered the flowers but not the picture.

“I described putting it beside me.

“He shook his head.

“I described Tisdale getting in.

“He remembered Tisdale.

“I described holding the frame with my right hand while Tisdale talked.

“He said, ‘If you say so, sir.’

“That ‘if’ offended me. By then I was more certain of the picture than of Tisdale.

“I spent the afternoon visiting lost-property rooms. You’d be surprised what people abandoned in public conveyances. There were umbrellas enough to roof a village, bundles of clothing, carpetbags, canes, birdcages, a plaster bust of Daniel Webster, and one shoe of a size that suggested the owner could hardly have gone far without it. But no Blakey.

“By evening I had reached the stage where the loss seemed a judgment. I had concealed the subject from Annie; I had invented the cow for the superintendent; I had suspected a thin man who perhaps never existed; and all the time the villa was probably hanging in a pawnshop, where some dealer was saying Blakey’s early work lacked finish.

“Annie was first-rate through it all. She said, ‘Joe, I’d rather lose the picture than have you make yourself ill over it.’

“That touched me, and made me feel still more unworthy. I said, ‘If you knew what the picture was, you wouldn’t say so.’

“Then I had to tell her. I told her it was the villa where we met.

“She looked at me for a moment and began to laugh.

“That was not the reception I expected. I asked what she found amusing.

“She said, ‘Oh, you poor goose! Is that why you’ve been looking as if you’d lost *me*?’

“‘It amounts to the same thing,’ I said.

“‘Not unless I was wrapped in brown paper and left on a horse-car.’

“She said she cared for the picture because Blakey had given it, and because I cared for it, but the villa itself had never been sacred to her. She had been having a miserable time there, she said, until I came, and even afterward the plumbing remained bad. Women have a way of letting realism into a man’s best recollections.”

At this point the door opened and Mrs. Minver came in, fresh from the street, bringing with her that impression of victorious motion which some women retain even while taking off their gloves.

“What recollections are being spoiled now?” she asked.

“The villa,” said Minver.

“Oh, Joe’s lost picture!” She sat down. “Has he reached the florist?”

“Not yet,” Joe said, with reproach. “You’ve given it away.”

“My dear, there never was any suspense after the third time.”

“There’s always suspense if a thing’s told properly.”

Minver said, “He has just made you laugh in the parlor.”

“I did laugh,” Mrs. Minver admitted, “but not until later. At the time I was much too anxious about him. He looked perfectly wild.”

Joe accepted this correction gratefully. “Well, the next morning, about ten, a delivery boy came to my rooms carrying the picture. Dunham’s label was tied to the string. The shopman had found it behind a stand of palms where I’d leaned it while choosing the flowers. He knew it was mine, but I’d said I was going directly to Brookline, so he supposed I meant to call for it on the way back. Then came the holiday, and the shop was closed. As soon as he opened again, he sent it to me.

“I stood there staring at it. My first feeling was joy, naturally. My second was something like indignation. The picture had no right to be there. I had carried it out of the florist’s. I had taken it into the horse-car. I had put it beside me. I had kept my hand on it while Tisdale talked. I had left it when I got off. I remembered every particular.”

“You remembered what hadn’t happened,” Mrs. Minver said.

“Yes; but I didn’t merely suppose it. I *saw* it. Then, all at once, I understood—or thought I did. On the earlier car, before I went into the florist’s, I really had placed the picture beside me and kept my hand on it. When I afterward discovered it missing, my mind took that true recollection and moved it forward to the second ride. It supplied the flowers, brought in Tisdale, changed the side of the car, and made one complete scene out of pieces that had never occurred together.”

“And Tisdale confirmed it,” I said.

“Tisdale confirmed anything put to him with sufficient confidence. When I showed him the picture and told him where it had been, he said, ‘Of course! Now I remember you had only the flowers.’”

Minver laughed. “A valuable witness.”

“But that wasn’t all,” Mrs. Minver said. “Tell him what I called it.”

Joe looked indulgently toward her. “Annie had a theory.”

“I have it still,” she declared. “He was so afraid of losing the picture, because of what it meant and because he wanted to bring it safely to me, that he imagined in advance the very thing he dreaded. Only he imagined it backward, after the danger was over.”

“She called it an inverted presentiment,” said Joe.

“It was an inverted presentiment. His mind knew he had left the picture somewhere before he knew where.”

Minver tapped the ash from his cigar. “His mind knew nothing. It had mislaid the facts and was too proud to admit it.”

Mrs. Minver turned to me. “You see how jealous he is because Joe had one really curious psychological experience.”

“Curious!” Minver cried. “A man leaves a parcel in a shop, invents a cow, persecutes a street railway, accuses a citizen in a derby, and nearly breaks off his marriage because he can’t distinguish one horse-car from another; and you decorate the performance with a scientific name.”

“I didn’t nearly break off the marriage,” Joe objected.

“You were only testing its elasticity.”

Mrs. Minver looked at the little villa, which had kept its mild Tuscan sunshine through all their years of dispute.

“I’ve always been fond of it,” she said, “though Joe told me at first it was a cloud study.”

“I corrected that.”

“After the florist corrected you.”

Joe rose and went to the easel. “Anyway, the picture came back.”

“It never went,” said Minver.

Joe considered this, smiling.

“That’s true. I was the only thing that got lost.”

Minver nodded. “And you came back, too. Temporarily.”

Mrs. Minver protested that this was silly, though she looked pleased with Joe and with the picture and even with Minver’s brutality. I ventured that “inverted presentiment” was not a bad phrase for the mind’s habit of turning fear into recollection.

Minver gave me the cold regard reserved for an accomplice in sentimentality.

“You may call it what you like,” he said. “Joe was off his nut.”

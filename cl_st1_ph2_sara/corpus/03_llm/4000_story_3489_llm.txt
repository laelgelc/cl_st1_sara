The House by the River

When Kiran recovered from the fever which had nearly carried her beyond the reach of medicines, prayers, physicians, and her husband’s anxious temper, everyone agreed that she must not immediately return to the crowded ancestral house.

Everyone, in this instance, meant Sharat, the doctor, Sharat’s mother, two elderly aunts, and Kiran herself whenever she wished to be reasonable. Whenever she did not, it meant nobody at all.

“You are still weak,” Sharat told her.

“I am strong enough to hear that twenty times a day.”

“The doctor forbids excitement.”

“Then send the doctor away. He excites me.”

Nevertheless, she allowed herself to be taken to a lonely garden-house beside the Ganges, where the air was pure, the rooms large, and amusement scarce. Sharat watched over her with the devotion of a jailer guarding a royal prisoner. She must not bathe too long, walk in the sun, sit in the evening mist, eat sour mangoes, laugh excessively, or remain awake after ten.

Kiran submitted to everything except submission.

The garden-house stood among mango, jackfruit, and champak trees. Beyond the bathing steps the river spread broad and pale, carrying fishing boats, rafts of bamboo, fragments of waterweed, and the unrecorded business of many lives toward the sea. In the morning, Kiran sat beneath the veranda awning cutting betel leaves, merely to have something to do with her fingers. At noon, even the birds were silent. At night, the river sighed against the bank like a sleeper troubled by an old dream.

Recovery is an excellent thing; but to recover in solitude, under the guardianship of a loving husband, may sometimes appear merely a more comfortable form of illness. Kiran had been born for company. Her laughter required witnesses, her jokes victims, her tenderness someone upon whom to spend itself. Sharat loved her profoundly, but he could not become an audience, a playmate, a child, and a household festival all at once.

Fate, which is often blamed for bringing trouble, deserves some credit for occasionally bringing diversion.

One evening, the summer heat broke beneath a violent storm. Clouds gathered over the river as if a black army had been summoned from every quarter of the sky. The trees bent, leaves flew across the garden, doors banged in their frames, and rain rushed upon the earth in silver-grey sheets. From the veranda, Sharat’s servants saw a small country boat overturn near the farther shoal.

There was shouting, then darkness, then only the angry water.

An hour later, a drenched boy appeared at the garden gate.

He had swum ashore with nothing but a wet cloth round his body and a sacred thread across his chest. His long hair clung to his forehead; mud streaked his knees; his thin ribs rose and fell like the slats of a broken fan. He belonged, he said, to a travelling theatrical troupe. Their boat had sunk. Of the others he knew nothing.

His name was Nilkanta.

Kiran received him as though the storm had delivered a toy specially ordered for her convalescence. She had him bathed, wrapped in one of Sharat’s dry cloths, seated in the kitchen veranda, and fed with hot rice, milk, bananas, fried fish, and sweets until even gratitude could swallow no more.

“Enough, sister,” he murmured.

“Nonsense. A boy rescued from the Ganges must eat for all those who were not rescued.”

Sharat objected that this arithmetic might prove fatal, but Kiran pushed another sweet onto Nilkanta’s plate.

Nobody knew his age. He might have been fourteen, seventeen, or any uncomfortable point between the two. Years of poor food had kept his body small, while years of wandering had sharpened his face. On the stage he had played women, divine messengers, young princes, faithful queens, and once, he proudly reported, a demon’s daughter who died to save a holy man. Off the stage he was simply an orphaned Brahmin boy accustomed to eatings and beatings, and ready to accept either without much surprise.

Kiran commanded him to perform.

At first he hesitated. Then, with a strip of red cloth tied around his head and a brass plate for a shield, he became a warrior. His narrow chest expanded. His eyes flashed. He challenged invisible armies, embraced invisible friends, and died upon the floor in such a prolonged and musical fashion that Kiran laughed until Sharat warned her she would bring back the fever.

Nilkanta sprang up at once.

“Shall I die again, sister?”

“You have died enough for one evening,” said Sharat.

Thus the castaway entered their household.

His temporary refuge soon acquired the permanence natural to things nobody has had the courage to end. Kiran gave him clothes, encouraged his songs, and taught him card games, though he cheated so openly that instruction was unnecessary. At mealtimes she supervised his plate. In the afternoons she made him recite parts from plays, sometimes dressing him in a shawl and bangles, sometimes painting a moustache upon his lip. He submitted to everything for the sake of her laughter.

The servants disliked him. He smoked Sharat’s hookah whenever he found it unattended, broke the gardener’s sickle, slept upon freshly spread beds with muddy feet, and assembled from the neighbouring village a band of boys whose industry was chiefly devoted to ensuring that no mango in the orchard reached maturity.

A new umbrella belonging to the head servant was found floating down the river. Nilkanta expressed grief at this misfortune and later used the bamboo handle as a fishing rod.

“Did you take it?” Sharat demanded.

“Why should I take a broken umbrella?”

“It was not broken.”

“Then perhaps it broke itself before floating away.”

Reasoning with Nilkanta was like trying to tie water into a bundle. Sharat scolded him; the servants cuffed him when Kiran was absent; Kiran defended him when she was present. Under this system he flourished.

He acquired a mongrel dog, yellow, scarred, and possessed of four muddy paws with which it expressed affection upon every clean garment. The creature followed him everywhere and slept outside his door. When it invaded Kiran’s room and ruined a newly washed coverlet, Sharat threatened to have it driven away.

Nilkanta stood silent, but his eyes filled at once.

Kiran said, “It is only a coverlet.”

“It has become a map of the whole district,” said Sharat.

“Then we have gained an education in geography.”

Between husband and wife, justice is often required to wait outside while affection settles the case. The dog remained.

For a time Nilkanta was happy. Happiness, with boys, does not always wear a quiet face. It may shout, climb trees, steal tobacco, upset ink, and run barefoot through another person’s lunch. His devotion to Kiran was concealed beneath mischief because he did not yet know it was devotion. She was to him warmth after rain, food after hunger, applause after long humiliation. Her room, with its scent of betel and jasmine oil, was richer than any palace he had entered beneath painted canvas skies.

Yet while he lived in her affection, another change came upon him silently.

He began to grow.

Not much in height, perhaps, nor sufficiently in wisdom; but childhood loosened its hold upon him. He became ashamed when Kiran asked him to dress as a girl. Formerly he had jingled bracelets and mimicked the coyness of a princess without concern. Now his dark face flushed, and he found excuses.

“My throat is bad today.”

“Your throat was good enough to quarrel with the cook.”

“That was speaking, not singing.”

He ceased appearing bare-bodied before her, tied his cloth with greater care, and combed his long hair in secret. If Kiran praised his eyes or pinched his cheek, an unknown discomfort burned in him—half sweetness, half shame.

He was either a man too early or a boy too late. The household saw only that he had grown idle and sulky.

Sometimes he took a book from Sharat’s room and sat beneath the champak tree, holding it open before him. He could scarcely read. When a boat passed, he bent over the page with intense dignity, lest the passengers mistake him for an ignorant strolling player. The printed letters remained black insects marching toward destinations he could not follow.

But songs required no letters.

In the evening, when the river turned copper beneath the departing sun, he sang the verses of his troupe. One song told of a king’s daughter who had found a wounded stranger in the forest and saved him from death. As Nilkanta sang, the words ceased to belong to the cracked drum, smoky lamps, painted beards, and hungry actors of his old life. The river widened into the boundary of a marvellous kingdom. The champak leaves trembled with secret messages. White birds crossed the sky like loosened necklaces. Somewhere beyond the darkening bank, a princess waited beside a golden lamp.

And Kiran, bright with health again, her bracelets chiming as she walked, became mingled with that impossible country.

To call this love would perhaps be to give a name too precise for what even Nilkanta did not understand. The heart often receives its wound before the mind has learned the weapon.

Then Satish arrived.

He was Sharat’s younger brother, recently released from college for the holidays, lively, handsome, and furnished with the easy confidence of one who has always belonged wherever he stood. He and Kiran had been companions from childhood. Within an hour of his arrival, peace fled the garden-house.

Kiran stole his key and hid it beneath a flowerpot. Satish mixed pepper with her betel. She wrote “monkey” upon his back in sandal paste while he slept. He locked her sewing basket and claimed to have thrown away the key. Their quarrels rang from room to room, rapid, merry, and entirely without consequence.

Sharat smiled. His wife was herself again.

Nilkanta watched.

When Kiran called, he came more slowly than before. When she asked him to sing, Satish interrupted with an imitation of theatrical wailing, and everyone laughed.

“Go on, Nilkanta,” Kiran said.

“I have forgotten.”

“You sang it yesterday.”

“My throat is bad.”

Satish clutched his neck and croaked, “My throat is bad! Alas, the royal swan has swallowed a frog!”

Kiran laughed, not cruelly, not even carelessly by her own measure. But privileged affection seldom knows its own weight. A touch that is nothing to the giver may be a crown to the receiver; a laugh tossed aside may strike like a stone.

Nilkanta went away.

From that day, Satish’s soap vanished before his bath and reappeared in the ashes. His favourite slippers were discovered on separate floors. A new shirt acquired a mysterious slit. When he went swimming, his fine muslin garment drifted from the steps and was rescued only after a boatman had pursued it halfway across the river.

“It is that actor boy,” Satish declared.

Nilkanta, questioned, looked offended.

“Why should I touch your cloth?”

“You were on the steps.”

“The river was also there. Ask the river.”

His persecution was generally arranged so that Fate might bear the blame.

But jealousy, forbidden to strike upward, flows downward. Nilkanta grew tyrannical with the village boys who had once been his companions. He beat the smallest for failing to fetch tobacco. He kicked his dog when it came joyfully against his legs, then later embraced it and fed it half his supper. He shook unripe mangoes from the trees merely to see them fall.

At meals he sometimes refused food.

“Nilkanta has not eaten,” the cook told Kiran.

“He will eat when he is hungry,” she replied, for at that moment Satish had wagered that she could not thread a needle while looking into a mirror.

Nilkanta lay upon his mat in the darkness, waiting.

He imagined her footsteps. She would come carrying a lamp and say, “What is this? My Nilu has eaten nothing?” She would scold the cook, sit beside him, and feed him with her own hand.

No footsteps came.

At last Mother Sleep, kinder than human expectations, laid her soft palm upon his burning eyes. The next morning he stole cold rice from the kitchen and ate it behind the cowshed.

So the holidays advanced, carrying them toward departure. Kiran had fully recovered, and letters from home became insistent. The river-house must be closed. Boxes were aired, clothes folded, jars sealed, bedding beaten, and servants sent running in all directions.

Nothing reveals a person’s place in a household so clearly as preparations to leave it. Satish’s books were packed, Sharat’s hookah wrapped, Kiran’s bottles nested in cotton. Nobody packed anything for Nilkanta.

Sharat said, “We cannot take him home. Mother will never allow it.”

Kiran knew this was true. The ancestral house already contained relations, dependants, servants, widows, children, cows, creditors, and opinions in sufficient number. A wandering player with a dog would overflow even its generous confusion.

She called Nilkanta into her room.

He came with his hair uncombed and stood by the door.

“Nilu,” she said gently, “we are going home in two days.”

“I know.”

“You must also think of where you will go.”

He stared at the floor.

“Perhaps your troupe—”

“They drowned.”

“Not all, surely. You might find another. You sing well. Or Sharat can arrange—”

His face crumpled. Tears fell before he could turn away.

Kiran’s own eyes softened. She drew him near. “Oh, foolish boy, do you think I am driving you away?”

Satish, who had entered without announcement, laughed. “Then take him home, sister. Give him your room and let brother sleep with the cows.”

Nilkanta tore himself free and fled.

“What have I said?” Satish asked.

Kiran rounded on him. “You have a stone in place of a heart.”

“A stone does not steal soap.”

“He did not steal your soap.”

“He steals everything else.”

“Shame on you!”

Satish retreated, grinning, but Nilkanta had heard enough. Kiran’s kindness, instead of healing him, had exposed his helplessness before his enemy. Tears in solitude may belong to sorrow; tears witnessed by a rival become humiliation.

That evening Satish brought out his treasured inkstand while writing a letter.

It was an absurdly splendid object: a silver-coloured boat resting upon a black base, with a little metal bird perched at the prow and two glass wells fitted amidships. Satish had bought it in Calcutta and polished it daily. Kiran often teased him by tapping the bird’s beak and chanting, “Sail away, little captain, sail away!”

The next morning the inkstand was gone.

Satish searched his table, trunk, bedding, and shelves. He questioned the servants. His voice grew louder with each denial.

At last he cried, “Nilkanta!”

The boy appeared in the doorway.

“You came into my room yesterday.”

Nilkanta said nothing.

“You took my inkstand.”

His face went dark, not with guilt alone but with the fury of being named.

“I did not.”

“You did. Who else would?”

“I did not!”

Satish seized his arm. Nilkanta sprang like a trapped animal, teeth clenched, fingers curved. Had Sharat not stepped between them, accusation might have been answered with blood.

“Search his box,” Satish said.

“No,” said Kiran.

Everyone turned toward her.

Satish laughed incredulously. “Why not?”

“Because I say he did not take it.”

“You cannot know that.”

“I know him.”

Nilkanta looked at her. For one instant the whole world, which had been slipping beneath his feet, became firm again.

Satish persisted. “If it is not there, no harm is done.”

“There is harm in searching,” Kiran replied. “You have called him a thief. Is that no harm?”

Sharat tried reason. “Kiran, the thing is valuable. It may be best to settle the matter.”

“It is settled. His box will not be touched.”

Her eyes flashed with such authority that even Sharat yielded. Satish muttered, “Very well. Let the pet actor rob us all,” and left the room.

Kiran waited until the others had gone. Then she said softly, “Nilu, if by some foolish chance you took it only for a joke, give it to me. I shall put it back, and nobody will know.”

The light went out of his face.

He had not taken it for greed. The previous night, passing Satish’s room, he had seen the ridiculous silver boat gleaming beneath the lamp. Rage had risen in him—the rage of all the lost songs, missed meals, careless laughter, and the approaching abandonment. He had seized it intending to fling it into the Ganges, there to let Satish’s pride sail toward the sea.

But footsteps had sounded in the passage. He had hidden the inkstand beneath his cloth and carried it to his room. Unable to reach the river unseen, he had thrust it into his wooden box under a heap of costumes.

To him this was revenge. Theft was taking for profit, hiding for possession, selling for money. He wanted nothing from the object except its destruction. Between the deed and the name he placed a difference vast enough to shelter his honour.

Now Kiran, too, suspected him.

He could have confessed anger. He could not confess theft.

With a strangled cry he ran from her room.

Kiran wiped her eyes and scolded Satish for the remainder of the afternoon. Perhaps she defended Nilkanta more fiercely because a small doubt had entered her own heart; when affection feels doubt, it often shouts in order not to hear itself.

On the last night, after the household had gone to bed, she prepared a farewell bundle. Two new garments, a pair of shoes, a packet of sweets, and a ten-rupee note—these she carried to Nilkanta’s room. She meant to place them secretly in his box, so that he should discover, after they had left, one final proof that she had not forgotten him.

The room was empty. His dog slept outside, twitching in a dream.

Kiran lifted the lid of the battered wooden box.

A smell of dust, damp cloth, oil, and boyhood rose from it. At the top lay a spinning top without its string, a cracked comb, shells, bits of coloured glass, a blunt knife, reels of thread, a broken toy pistol, theatrical anklets, a painted crown crushed flat, and rags which had once represented royal garments. These objects possessed no value, and therefore contained an entire life.

She removed them one by one.

Beneath the costumes, the metal bird of Satish’s inkstand flashed in the lamplight.

Kiran remained motionless.

The little boat, so comic upon Satish’s polished desk, looked monstrous there among Nilkanta’s poor treasures. It seemed to accuse him, accuse her, accuse the whole careless arrangement by which one person may play with another’s heart and never know that a game has begun.

Behind the half-open door stood Nilkanta.

He had returned barefoot and seen the lamp in his room. From the darkness he watched Kiran holding the inkstand.

If she had turned, if she had called him, if she had struck him, perhaps the knot within him would have broken. He might have cried out: “I am not a thief. I meant to throw it away. I hated him. I hated you for loving him. I am not a thief—not a thief!”

But pride is often strongest where power is least. He stood without speech.

Kiran understood something, though not all. She saw again his tears, his fierce denial, his thin body at the garden gate on the night of the storm. With trembling hands she returned the inkstand to the bottom of the box. Over it she placed the costumes, the shells, the broken toys, the cracked comb. On top she laid the new clothes, shoes, sweets, and money.

Then she closed the lid and went away.

Nilkanta remained in the darkness. He had seen her discover his guilt and conceal it. Yet mercy did not comfort him. Mercy confirmed that there was guilt to pardon. She would never again think of him as the boy who sang beneath the champak tree, the prince rescued from the storm, the beloved nuisance whose plate she filled with sweets. In her secret mind he had become what Satish had called him.

A thief.

The word entered him and bled and bled.

Before dawn, Nilkanta disappeared.

At first they thought he was hiding in the orchard to frighten them. Sharat sent servants to the village, the bathing steps, the road, and the ferry. Kiran herself searched beneath the champak tree and called until her voice grew hoarse.

“Nilu! Nilu!”

Only the leaves answered.

His dog raced from place to place, sniffing footprints, whining at the river, returning to Kiran, then rushing away again as though some new hope had struck it. No boatman remembered carrying the boy across. No troupe had been seen on the road. The river, questioned by many eyes, flowed on with its ancient indifference.

Satish suggested informing the police.

Kiran turned upon him. “No.”

“They may find him.”

“They will search his things.”

Satish fell silent.

That afternoon Kiran went alone to Nilkanta’s room. She opened the box, took out the inkstand, and wrapped it in her shawl. At the bathing steps she stood for some time looking over the broad water.

Then the silver boat flew from her hand.

It flashed once in the sun and vanished. Rings widened upon the surface, touched one another, and were lost among the river’s innumerable ripples.

The next morning the family departed. Boxes were loaded, doors locked, servants counted, and farewells exchanged with the caretaker. Sharat helped Kiran into the boat and arranged cushions behind her back, still treating her health as a fragile article entrusted to him by Providence.

As the boat moved from the bank, Kiran looked toward the trees. Perhaps she expected a thin figure to spring from behind them at the last moment, laughing at the success of his trick.

No one appeared.

Only Nilkanta’s dog ran along the bank. Muddy, hungry, and bewildered, it followed until reeds barred the way. Then it stood with its forepaws in the water, whining and whining after the departing boat.

The distance widened. The garden-house sank behind the trees. The river took the oars’ disturbance into itself and smoothed it away.

But the dog remained upon the lonely bank, calling for the master whom neither house, road, nor river would return.

### The Shadow of a Little Cloud

There are mornings when the sun shines broadly upon roof and garden, yet, within the walls of a dwelling, a cloud has arisen which no outward brightness can disperse. Whence came it? Perhaps from bereavement, sickness, or some heavy disappointment. These are clouds whose shadows we may pardon, even while we seek to rise above them. But sometimes—shall we confess it, gentle reader?—the obscurity proceeds from a cause so slight that pride is ashamed to name it, though temper has not been ashamed to spread its consequences through many hearts.

Such a cloud rested upon the brow of Mrs. Abercrombie one pleasant morning in May.

At breakfast, the children, who usually came about their mother with the gladness of birds around a fountain, perceived at once that something was wrong. Children possess, in these matters, a sensitiveness which older persons do not always sufficiently regard. The faintest jar in the domestic atmosphere reaches them; the smallest vibration of anger passes from heart to heart, until all are disturbed.

“Mary, how awkwardly you have placed the cups!” said Mrs. Abercrombie.

Mary moved them, though they stood where they had stood every morning for a month.

“And Henry, do not strike your spoon against your plate. I cannot endure that noise.”

Henry ceased; but in his anxiety to be quiet he overturned the salt.

“There! I knew you would do something!” exclaimed his mother.

The child’s eyes filled with tears. Mr. Abercrombie, who had entered the room cheerful, looked first at the salt and then at his wife.

“It is of no consequence, my dear,” said he mildly.

“Of no consequence to you, perhaps,” replied Mrs. Abercrombie; “but I must attend to everything.”

This was unjust, and Mr. Abercrombie felt it to be so. He was a man of unusual sensitiveness. A gentle word touched him deeply; an unkind one wounded him in proportion. Had his wife been suffering from any known trouble, his tenderness would have borne much without complaint; but there was in her displeasure an undefined sharpness which pressed upon him the more painfully because he could not discover its cause.

He folded his newspaper, though he had scarcely read a line.

“Thomas,” said he to the servant, “is the coffee always to be brought in cold?”

“It is not cold, sir,” answered Thomas, surprised.

“Do not contradict me.”

Now, the coffee was not cold. But the little cloud had already crossed the table.

Mary accused Henry of pushing her chair. Henry denied it loudly. Jane laughed at both. Their mother commanded silence; their father frowned; Thomas carried away the dishes with an injured air, and the baby, perceiving that harmony had departed, began to cry with all the strength of innocence.

How rapidly does discord multiply! A cheerful mother would have smiled at the salt, soothed the baby, and made the whole occurrence pass like a ripple on summer water. But irritability, once admitted, finds food everywhere. It interprets accident as offence, hesitation as disobedience, and even affection as intrusion.

After breakfast Mr. Abercrombie rose.

“I shall probably be late this evening,” said he.

“You are often late,” replied his wife.

There was nothing violent in the words; yet their coldness followed him into the hall, entered the carriage with him, and went with him to his store.

Mrs. Abercrombie remained at home; but peace did not remain with her.

Before ten o’clock a quarrel arose in the nursery. A painted box belonging to Jane had been broken, and the fragments were discovered beneath a chair. Jane declared that Henry had done it. Henry, indignant at the charge, declared that Mary had done it. Mary burst into tears and denied touching the box.

Their mother entered while all were speaking together.

“What disgraceful conduct!” she exclaimed. “Henry and Mary, go at once to the little chamber and remain there until dinner.”

“But, mamma—” began Mary.

“Not a word!”

“I didn’t break it,” said Henry.

“Your denial only makes your fault worse.”

The two children were sent away, wounded less by confinement than by the conviction that their mother would not hear them. Scarcely had the door closed when little Jane, terrified by the result of her accusation, confessed that she herself had broken the box while trying to reach a shelf, and that her cousin Charles had hidden the pieces for her.

Mrs. Abercrombie’s cheek flushed. She had punished the innocent without inquiry. Here was an opportunity for immediate justice and humility; but pride, when joined with a disturbed temper, often resists the very act which would bring relief.

She released Henry and Mary, it is true, and told them that a mistake had been made; yet the acknowledgment was so constrained that it healed little. Then, turning upon Jane and Charles, she visited their offence with a severity far beyond its desert. Jane was deprived of a promised walk for a week; Charles was sent home in disgrace; and both received such a lecture upon falsehood that the trembling culprits might have been supposed guilty of some deep and deliberate wickedness.

When the room was at last silent, Mrs. Abercrombie sat down alone.

There is a silence which brings peace, and another which compels us to hear the accusations of conscience. Hers was of the latter kind. She saw Mary’s grieved face, Henry’s indignant tears, and Jane’s frightened look. She remembered her husband’s quiet attempt to soothe her, and the unkind answer she had made him.

“What has happened to me?” she asked aloud.

Nothing had happened—nothing worthy of the name. And now, fair reader, we must disclose the mighty grievance whose shadow had fallen upon husband, children, servant, nursery, and breakfast-table.

A new pair of gaiters did not fit.

They had been sent home the evening before. Mrs. Abercrombie had ordered them for a visit she expected to make that afternoon; and when she tried them on in the morning, they pressed one foot painfully and wrinkled around the other in a manner wholly destructive, as she believed, to elegance. The maker had blundered; the visit must be postponed; and vanity, disappointment, and physical discomfort united to cloud her brow.

A pair of gaiters! We smile, perhaps. But have we never permitted a very little thing—an ill-cooked meal, a delayed letter, a misplaced ribbon, an unseasonable shower—to disturb the happiness of those who were innocent of it? The cause may be trivial; the pain it communicates is not therefore unreal.

Mrs. Abercrombie possessed both warmth of heart and strength of principle, though neither had guarded her in the first moment of trial. Reflection now did what impulse had failed to do. She went first to Henry and Mary, kissed them, and frankly confessed her injustice.

“I was wrong,” she said. “Will you forgive me?”

The astonished children needed no entreaty. Their arms were around her in a moment.

She then called Jane, moderated her punishment, and spoke with tenderness upon the falsehood without excusing it. Jane wept upon her shoulder and promised amendment. Charles received a message of forgiveness; the baby was brought in; and by noon the sunshine had begun to return. Not wholly were the shadows expelled, for wounded feelings do not always close the instant repentance touches them; but love, like warmth after frost, was quietly restoring what harshness had contracted.

Meanwhile the cloud had travelled farther than Mrs. Abercrombie knew.

Mr. Abercrombie entered his place of business with a compressed lip and an impatient step. The clerks perceived his mood and bent more closely over their work. He was not ordinarily an unjust employer. He demanded diligence, but he could also appreciate fidelity; and among those in his service none had been more faithful than Edward Wilson, a young man of nineteen.

At nine o’clock Edward’s desk was vacant.

Mr. Abercrombie looked at the clock.

“Not come yet?” he asked.

“No, sir,” replied the chief clerk.

At five minutes past nine he looked again.

“Not come yet?”

“No, sir.”

At ten minutes past nine Edward entered, pale, breathless, and agitated.

“You are late,” said Mr. Abercrombie.

“Yes, sir. I am very sorry. My mother—”

“I want no excuses.”

“But, sir, if you would only permit me—”

“I have permitted irregularity long enough.”

This was not true. Edward had been late but once before, and then only by a few minutes.

“My mother was taken worse in the night,” continued the youth. “I had to wait until the neighbour—”

“Edward Wilson,” interrupted Mr. Abercrombie, “you may go to the cashier and receive what is due to you. Your services will no longer be required.”

The blood left Edward’s face.

“Dismissed, sir?”

“Dismissed. I cannot argue the matter.”

The young man stood for an instant as if the words had struck him physically. Then his self-respect came to his aid. He bowed, turned away, and passed into the adjoining room.

Almost immediately Mr. Abercrombie regretted what he had done. Conscience whispered that the boy had always been attentive; justice demanded that his explanation should be heard. He even took one step toward the door. But pride arrested him.

“If I recall him now,” he thought, “my authority will be weakened.”

Ah, how many cruel acts have been maintained under that false name—authority! True authority loses nothing by justice, and dignity is never so dignified as when it repairs an error. Yet Mr. Abercrombie returned to his desk and let the injured boy depart.

Before noon another trial presented itself. A man named Perkins, who had for some years dealt honestly with the firm, called to ask the renewal of a small note.

“I have met with unexpected losses,” he said. “If you will allow me thirty days, I shall be able to pay the whole. Without the extension I may be forced to close my shop.”

“You knew when the note was due,” replied Mr. Abercrombie.

“I did; and until yesterday I believed myself prepared. One of my customers has failed, and the money I depended upon is lost.”

“That is not my affair.”

“No, sir; but I have never asked such a favour before.”

“You ask it now, and I refuse it.”

Perkins looked troubled.

“The refusal may ruin me.”

“Business cannot be governed by sentiment.”

The man slowly folded the note he had brought.

“Very well, Mr. Abercrombie. I had hoped my punctuality through all these years would count for something.”

He withdrew.

Again regret followed harshness, swift as an echo follows sound. The accommodation requested was slight, the security sufficient, and the man worthy. In another state of mind Mr. Abercrombie would have granted the favour without hesitation. But the shadow upon his feelings had altered the colour of every object. He saw presumption where there was only distress, and carelessness where there was misfortune.

The day wore on heavily. He made errors in two accounts, spoke sharply to three clerks, and returned home with a heart more burdened than when he left it. Evil temper punishes its possessor even while it wounds others.

At home he found a different atmosphere. Mrs. Abercrombie met him with a smile, not bright enough to be careless, but tender enough to confess regret.

“I am glad you have come,” she said. “I fear I sent you away unhappy this morning.”

Mr. Abercrombie’s heart was touched; nevertheless, remembering Edward and Perkins, he answered only, “The day has not been a pleasant one.”

At dinner the children were affectionate and subdued. Mary offered her father a flower she had saved for him; Henry placed his chair; Jane climbed upon his knee after the meal. Slowly the domestic bonds resumed their accustomed pressure—not the pressure that wounds, but that gentle constraint which keeps the heart from wandering into selfishness.

In the evening Mrs. Abercrombie spoke of a visit she had received.

“Mrs. Wilson was here to-day.”

Mr. Abercrombie started.

“Edward Wilson’s mother?”

“Yes. I had not known how ill she was. She has been confined to her bed for several weeks.”

“Did she say anything of Edward?”

“She spoke of little else. He is her only support. He prepares her food before he leaves in the morning, gives her medicine, and pays the rent from his wages. Last night she became suddenly worse, and he could not leave her until a neighbour came.”

Mr. Abercrombie turned toward the window.

“She seemed anxious,” continued his wife. “Edward came home this morning and told her he had lost his place. She fears they will be turned from their rooms.”

“Did he complain of me?” he asked.

“No. She said he blamed himself for not finding some way to arrive earlier. But he told her that you would not hear his explanation.”

A long silence followed.

Mrs. Abercrombie, perceiving his agitation, laid her hand upon his arm.

“I, too, judged hastily to-day,” she said. “I punished Henry and Mary for what they had not done, and then punished Jane too severely for what she had done. The cloud was in my own mind, yet I thought everything around me dark.”

“What cloud?” asked her husband gently.

She hesitated, then smiled through tears.

“My new gaiters did not fit.”

Mr. Abercrombie looked at her in astonishment. The absurd disproportion between cause and consequence might have provoked laughter had not his own conduct stood before him with terrible solemnity.

“The gaiters disturbed you,” said he slowly; “you disturbed me; and I—Heaven forgive me!—have perhaps deprived a sick woman of food and shelter.”

That night he slept little. Edward’s pale face appeared before him; then the invalid mother, listening for her son’s step; then Perkins, with ruin made possible by a refusal which had cost the speaker nothing and might cost the petitioner everything. He saw how pride had fastened what temper began. The original cloud had been slight as vapour, but its shadow had fallen far.

Morning came clear and golden. Mrs. Abercrombie rose with a cheerful spirit—not because the gaiters fitted, for they remained obstinately as ill-shaped as before, but because she had resolved that no outward trifle should govern her inward duty. Her voice brought harmony to the breakfast-table. The children laughed without fear; Thomas poured coffee without trembling; and Mr. Abercrombie, feeling the sunshine enter his own heart, kissed his wife before departing.

His first act at the store was to send for Edward.

The young man came, anxious and reserved.

“Edward,” said Mr. Abercrombie, “I acted unjustly yesterday. I should have heard you. If you are willing to return, your place is open.”

Edward’s face brightened.

“Thank you, sir!”

“And your wages shall be increased. Your care of your mother leaves you little margin for necessity. Until she recovers, you may come half an hour later.”

The youth attempted to speak, but gratitude overcame him.

Mr. Abercrombie next wrote to Perkins, granting the extension and expressing regret for his needless severity. Thus far the shadows were lifted. Yet we may believe that Edward never wholly forgot the hour in which he carried dismissal home to his sick mother, nor that Perkins ceased at once to feel the wound of undeserved mistrust. Repentance may arrest an injury; it cannot always recall every consequence.

Let us, then, guard the atmosphere of our homes and hearts. Never, never do we know how far an impatient word may travel, upon whose weakness its pressure may fall, or in what distant life its darkness may appear. A cloud no larger than a pair of ill-fitting gaiters may pass from brow to brow until children weep, the poor tremble, and the innocent suffer.

But sunshine, too, is contagious. A tender word, a humble confession, a just act promptly done—these also travel outward, bearing warmth and restoration. Since, then, we must cast either light or shadow upon those around us, shall we not strive, with watchfulness and self-command, to make our daily influence bright?

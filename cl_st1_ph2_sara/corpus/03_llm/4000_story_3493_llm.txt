### THE PALACE AT BARICH

The train from Ajmere, which should have conveyed us from the junction at eleven minutes after ten, had not arrived at midnight; nor did the station-master, a melancholy Eurasian in carpet slippers, entertain any definite hope of its appearance before dawn. A goods train had, he said, “gone wrong” somewhere beyond the black hills. In what manner a train might go morally or physically wrong he did not explain.

My kinsman, Haran, and I disposed ourselves upon two wooden benches beneath a smoky lamp. Haran had lately become a theosophist. He carried in his valise three woollen shirts, a toothbrush, and a translation of the Upanishads annotated by a lady in Boston who remembered seven previous births. He had spent the whole evening proving to me that matter is an illusion; yet when he discovered a scorpion beneath his rug he sprang upon the bench with an agility altogether inconsistent with his philosophy.

It was then that the stranger entered.

He wore a long English overcoat above white paijamas, patent-leather shoes, and a red fez. His beard was trimmed with precision, his eyes were luminous and restless, and his whole person suggested a man accustomed to addressing assemblies which had not invited him. He saluted us gravely, rebuked the station-master in Persian for the inadequacy of the lamp, quoted Shakespeare upon the infirmity of human arrangements, and seated himself between us.

Within ten minutes he had touched upon the Vedas, the fiscal policy of the Government, the decline of Arabic astronomy, the secret diplomacy of Russia, the dramatic unities, and the superiority of Hafiz to Goethe. His information was boundless. Where it ended, conjecture took up the discourse with equal confidence.

I confess that I listened with some amusement. Haran listened with reverence. Whenever the stranger lowered his voice, my kinsman bent forward as if a Mahatma were dictating from Tibet.

At length some observation of mine concerning the economic value of cotton caused the stranger to smile.

“Cotton,” he said, “has an economic value by day. At night it is a contemptible vegetable fibre. I once collected duties upon cotton, and I know.”

He looked towards the rails, which shone faintly beneath the stars, and after a pause added:

“It was in Barich that I first learned the difference between what is useful and what is true.”

Haran clasped his hands about his knee.

“You had an occult experience?” he asked.

The stranger regarded him with favour.

“My dear saheb, all experience is occult until one has suffered it. Afterwards, it is merely incredible.”

The station bell gave a feeble stroke and relapsed into silence. Somewhere in the darkness a jackal cried. The stranger drew his overcoat closer about him and began.

---

At the age of twenty-nine, he told us, I was appointed collector of cotton duties at Barich, on a salary of four hundred and fifty rupees per mensem, with travelling allowance. I mention the salary because it represented, at that period, my precise value to the Government of India. Whether the estimate was too high or too low, subsequent events may show.

Barich lies below the Susta hills, in a valley where the air is steeped in the fragrance of wild basil, fennel, mint, and flowering shrubs whose names no botanist has troubled to ascertain. Through the valley flows the Shusta, transparent over pale stones during the dry months and violent as an insulted queen during the rains. Above its right bank, on a terrace of rock, stands a palace of white marble.

Two hundred and fifty years ago Shah Mahmud, ruler of that district, built it for pleasure. He brought architects from Isfahan, dancers from Shiraz, singers from Delhi, gardeners from Kashmir, and, according to popular report, three hundred and sixty-five women, in order that no day in the year should suffer repetition. The court has vanished; the musicians are dust; the ruler’s descendants now petition the Government for pensions; but the palace remains.

It stands apart from the village. The broad steps descend to the river, and behind it rise the dark hills. Its pillared halls, arched corridors, baths, courts, alcoves, terraces, and subterranean chambers are entirely deserted. In the central hall there are three rows of columns and a fountain long since dry. Coloured stones still form flowers beneath the dust. Above certain doors one may read Persian verses celebrating love, wine, spring, and immortality—with the usual disregard for experience.

When I first arrived, Karim Khan, the old clerk of the cotton office, received me with many salaams. He was a thin, parchment-coloured man with white whiskers, mild eyes, and the habit of lowering his voice whenever common sense required that he raise it.

“Where has residence been arranged?” I asked.

“In the dak bungalow, huzoor.”

“I shall live in the marble palace.”

He looked at me and then at the floor.

“The palace is large.”

“So am I,” said I, being then moderately pleased with my own wit.

“It is not suitable after sunset.”

“Why?”

Karim Khan moved his lips, but at that moment the head chaprasi entered with an account relating to eleven maunds of cotton. When the matter had been settled, the old clerk repeated that the palace was not suitable.

“Snakes?” I asked.

“No.”

“Thieves?”

“No, huzoor.”

“Damp?”

He shook his head.

“Then it will suit me admirably.”

He sighed. “If the saheb must stay, let him remain there only by day.”

This naturally determined me to remain there by night. A man of twenty-nine, invested with authority over cotton and possessing a loaded revolver, does not submit his lodging to the superstitions of an elderly clerk.

I moved in that afternoon.

My household consisted of a cook, a bearer, two chaprasis, and a brown dog of doubtful ancestry. They arranged my camp bed and boxes in a small chamber overlooking the river; but when twilight approached, the cook announced that his grandmother was ill, the bearer discovered an obligation to attend a cousin’s marriage, and the chaprasis remembered official duties in the village. The dog alone had no relative to serve as an excuse. Yet at sunset even he crept whining beneath my dog-cart and refused to follow me indoors.

Thus I dined alone.

The first evening was perfectly tranquil. I sat on the river steps in a cane chair, smoking a cigar and reading the weekly newspaper, though its leading article on municipal drainage failed to compete with the sunset.

The Shusta had dwindled to a clear and narrow stream, exposing broad shelves of sand. Across the water the forest climbed the hill, every leaf motionless. The western sky was gold; then copper; then a pale green, transparent as a grape. No bird called. No wind stirred. The fragrances of the hills gathered invisibly, as though the whole valley had filled with incense for a worship whose priests and deity alike were absent.

The sun sank.

At once the solitude became enormous.

It seemed to me that some vast curtain had fallen behind the hills, shutting away the familiar world of offices, market dues, quarterly reports, railway timetables, and gentlemen wearing sola hats. The palace at my back grew suddenly attentive.

I heard a footstep upon the lowest stair.

I turned. Nobody was there.

After an interval came another, then another, then a quick multitude of light feet descending past me. I rose. The steps were empty from terrace to river. Nevertheless, a troop of women appeared to hurry down, laughing in whispers, jostling one another with jewelled arms, their loose garments fluttering, their anklets ringing beneath the silence.

They passed me.

I saw nobody.

Then came a splash in the river, another, and many together. The still water seemed struck by invisible bodies. I heard the playful cries of bathers, the clapping of hands, the quick breath of girls startled by cold water; I heard bracelets chiming, droplets falling from lifted hair, a voice singing half a line and breaking into laughter.

Yet the river remained smooth. Not a ripple crossed its evening light.

I stood motionless, gripping the back of my chair. Had there been a moon I might have distrusted my eyes; but it was my ears that betrayed me, and presently not my ears alone. A wind blew from the water—though the trees did not move—and it touched my face with the cool fragrance of rose-water and wet hair.

What should a collector of cotton duties do when invisible ladies of the seventeenth century bathe before him?

I retreated to the house.

In the great hall the darkness lay between the columns like folded velvet. My footsteps flew upwards to the arches and returned in hollow pursuit. I called for my servant, forgetting that filial devotion and matrimony had removed my entire establishment. The echo answered in a voice larger than mine.

I lit a kerosene lamp, opened a tin of biscuits, and laughed.

The explanation was simple: an empty stomach, aromatic hills, too much silence, and perhaps a touch of malaria. Having eaten six biscuits and swallowed quinine, I went to bed armed with the weekly newspaper.

Nothing more occurred that night.

Or, rather, I remember nothing more.

At dawn I awoke with the conviction that someone had stood beside my bed for hours, watching me.

---

By daylight the palace lost much of its authority. Its stains were merely stains, its shadows obeyed the sun, and its rooms revealed dust, bats, lizards, and a deplorable want of furniture. I went to the cotton market in my dog-cart and spent the morning assessing bales, hearing complaints, signing permits, and correcting the arithmetic of my subordinates. Never had Government service seemed so wholesome.

Karim Khan watched me over the rims of his spectacles.

“Did the huzoor sleep well?”

“Excellently.”

“In the palace?”

“Where else?”

He bowed, but his old face was troubled.

That evening I returned rather later than necessary. The market detained me, then a puncture in one wheel detained me, then I stopped beside the road to admire a geological formation for which I had never previously felt interest. Nevertheless, when I saw the palace whitening above the dusk, my reluctance vanished. It seemed not a house but a woman who had expected me, was offended by my delay, and would not confess it.

I hurried in.

No lamps were lit. A bar of sunset entered through the western arches and lay red upon the marble floor. As I crossed the first hall, a sound moved ahead of me—a soft rustling, a retreating footfall, the faint music of ornaments worn beneath silk.

“Who is there?” I called.

The sound paused.

Then, from beyond a curtain which did not exist, someone laughed.

I followed.

From room to room, from corridor to corridor, from one pillared court into another, the unseen guide drew me onward. A perfume floated before me, now distinct, now lost—the sweetness of attar of roses mingled with henna, smoke, and the faint bitterness of some Eastern wine. Once I felt a veil brush my cheek. Once a warm breath touched my ear. Once I heard a whispered Persian word which I understood, though I had never learned it.

Come.

The palace changed about me. I cannot say that I saw the transformation as I see you beneath this station lamp. Sight was perhaps the least of it. I felt the dry fountains leap into silver; I felt carpets unroll across the naked floors; I felt the blank walls glow with blue tiles and golden flowers. Silence became crowded with latent music.

Beyond a pierced marble screen, women tuned the sitar and the rebab. Somewhere a narghileh bubbled with lazy intervals. I heard the measured beat of a small drum, the murmur of men disputing a verse, the fall of dice upon a tray, and, far away, the nahabat sounding from a gate over which no tower now stood.

I entered a long chamber.

Two negro slaves in embroidered jackets appeared to stand at the doorway with curved swords. Under a canopy, upon a low couch, reclined a person whose face I could not see. Her small feet rested upon a cushion of dark green velvet. They were bare, save for golden slippers loose upon the toes; the nails were coloured crimson, and about one ankle was a chain set with tiny bells.

A blue curtain swelled in an unfelt wind.

I knew that if I lifted it, if I passed the slaves, if I looked once upon the face beneath the canopy, the riddle of my existence would be solved.

I advanced.

One of the swords flashed.

A tremendous crash shook the chamber.

I opened my eyes and found myself sitting upon the floor of the empty hall. My kerosene lamp had fallen from my hand and broken. Its flame crawled towards a heap of papers. I extinguished it with my coat, thereby saving Government property at the expense of a garment which had cost me eighty rupees in Calcutta.

That incident should have restored my reason. Instead, it annoyed me merely because I had not seen her face.

She had already begun to possess me.

---

From that night forward my life was divided.

By day I was Srijut—well, the name is of no consequence—collector of cotton duties, Barich subdivision, salary four hundred and fifty rupees per mensem. I wore an English coat, rode in a dog-cart, signed documents, reprimanded chaprasis, ate eggs, and conversed intelligently on rainfall.

By night I was another man.

At sunset the official personality dropped from me like an ill-fitting disguise. I bathed, perfumed my hair, and dressed myself in loose paijamas, a flowered silk coat, and a red cap which I purchased from an Armenian merchant. I placed attar upon my handkerchief. I ordered my cook to prepare pilau, spiced meat, pistachios, and sherbet, though the fellow refused to remain while I ate them. I abandoned cigars for a narghileh.

You may laugh. I also laughed—in the morning.

At ten o’clock, reading my own memoranda, I would blush to remember that the collector of cotton duties had spent half the night walking under vacant arches in Turkish slippers. I would resolve to send the clothes away, give the attar to Karim Khan, and sleep thenceforth in the dak bungalow.

But evening came.

The hills exhaled their perfume. Shadows climbed the marble stairs. One by one the offices of the world were closed; reason extinguished its lamp; time loosened the fastenings of its robe. Then the palace called, not in words but through every stone.

I went.

Night after night that vanished court revived around me. I wandered amid chambers where fountains scattered coolness through air heavy with saffron and rose. Lamps burned in niches of coloured glass. Wine shone like imprisoned sunset in crystal cups. Slender hands drew curtains aside and vanished. Eyes, darkened with antimony, watched me through carved screens. I heard Persian ghazals sung in a voice so low that longing itself seemed to have found breath:

O traveller, drink before the garden dies;  
The moon is but a wound in parted skies.

Whether those lines were truly sung, or whether I composed them, I cannot tell. In the cotton office I had never composed anything more lyrical than a notice concerning fraudulent weights.

And always, among that multitudinous invisible life, I sought one woman.

Sometimes I glimpsed her reflection where no mirror hung: an oval face, pale beneath a fold of saffron gauze; black hair braided with pearls; proud lips; eyes in which fear kept watch behind weariness. Sometimes only her hand appeared upon the rail of a gallery, white and jewelled, then withdrew as I approached. Sometimes I followed the perfume of her garments to a door that would not open. Sometimes, awakening at midnight, I found the hollow of my pillow wet with tears that were not mine.

Who was she? From what rose garden of Persia had she been stolen? What caravan had borne her, veiled and guarded, through dust, mountain snow, and robber-haunted passes? Had she been purchased in a bazaar where grave men examined beauty as my inspectors examined cotton? Had she entered the palace in triumph, with drums at the gate and gold scattered before her litter? Or secretly, by a river stair, while the moon hid behind clouds?

I fashioned histories for her, and each history ended in blood.

Perhaps the Badshah loved her, and another woman mixed poison with her sherbet. Perhaps she loved a young soldier whose severed head was shown to her at dawn. Perhaps she conspired, betrayed, waited, and was strangled behind the blue curtain while the musicians played more loudly in the outer hall.

What sorrow had sealed those eyes? What command had silenced that mouth? What iron door, what jealous knife, what velvet cord?

I would fain have cried, “Tell me! Though thou wast dead before my fathers’ fathers were born, tell me! I have come.”

Then the palace would answer with laughter from a hundred rooms.

---

There was one interruption to my enchantment, and that was Meher Ali.

Each morning at sunrise he circled the palace. He was a tall, gaunt madman with grey hair streaming upon his shoulders, a torn cloak flapping from his limbs, and eyes fixed upon things behind the visible world. Round and round the outer walls he ran, striking his forehead with his palm and crying:

“Stand back! Stand back! All is false! All is false!”

At first I threw him a coin. He did not see it.

Later I ordered the chaprasis to remove him, but no one would touch him. Karim Khan said quietly, “Let him circle, huzoor.”

“Why?”

“It is his road.”

A very inconvenient road, I thought, since it passed each morning beneath my bedroom window.

Yet his cry entered my dreams. When the hidden musicians played, their melody would suddenly divide, and through it came that cracked voice from beyond the walls: All is false! When a perfumed veil brushed my hand, I heard him. When the Persian woman’s anklets trembled beyond a door, I heard him. When the fountains sprang beneath the moon, I heard him.

All is false!

But which world did he condemn? The palace of delight, or the cotton office?

That question began to trouble me.

One afternoon I sat before a table heaped with accounts. An agent from Bombay was explaining the quality of a consignment in elaborate detail. His lips moved; figures passed beneath my eyes; the punkah creaked overhead. Suddenly the room, the agent, the bales, the inkpot, my English coat, and my own brown hand holding a pen became thin and absurd, as though painted upon paper.

Behind them I heard water falling in marble basins.

A woman whispered my name.

I rose so violently that the Bombay agent fled, leaving his account unfinished. I drove to the palace while the sun was still high and waited for darkness as a lover waits beneath a forbidden balcony.

Thus the dead grew substantial, and the living became a rumour.

---

On the fourth or fifth morning—I had ceased to count accurately—I found a rent in the silk coat I wore at night. Upon the sleeve were three dark stains like the marks of fingers dipped in blood.

That shook me.

I summoned my servants, packed my boxes, and moved to the dak bungalow. Karim Khan received the news without surprise. The cook recovered his grandmother; the bearer’s cousin concluded his marriage; even the dog consented to sleep beneath my bed.

For two nights I remained away.

The bungalow was small, square, whitewashed, and rational. Its furniture consisted of a table, two chairs, an iron bedstead, a cracked basin, and a portrait of the Queen. No invisible women bathed outside; no dead courtier sang; no sword flashed above the threshold. Lizards hunted moths upon the wall with commendable devotion to fact.

I slept badly.

On the first night I dreamed of the palace. On the second I did not dream at all, which was worse. I lay awake and felt an anguish not my own moving through the darkness. She was searching for me. Room after room she crossed, lifting curtains, calling softly, finding nothing. Her bare feet bled upon the marble. Her lamp failed. The doors closed one by one.

At two in the morning I rose, dressed, and walked out.

The moon had not yet risen. Clouds travelled low over the hills, and the river muttered in its stones. I told myself that I intended only to walk as far as the market road. My feet chose the road to the palace.

When I reached the gate, every window was dark.

I entered.

That night the house received me with such joy that I knew I had committed myself beyond retreat. Doors opened in succession before me. A gust of warm fragrance rushed along the corridors. Laughter broke overhead; bells answered beneath the floor; the dry fountain gave one silver leap and fell silent.

I ran, calling to her.

She appeared at the end of the eastern gallery.

For the first time I saw her wholly, though only for an instant. She wore a dress of deep orange embroidered with gold. A veil hung from a jewelled cap and streamed behind her. Her face was turned towards me. Beautiful? Yes—but beauty is too harmless a word. She was desire sharpened by terror; pride wounded but not conquered; youth imprisoned in a fate older than itself.

She raised both hands.

Whether she summoned me or warned me away I could not tell.

Then a door slammed between us.

I flung myself against it. There was no door—only a blank marble wall cold beneath my palms.

From within came a low, suffocated sobbing.

I beat upon the stone until my knuckles bled.

---

My work now fell into disorder. Reports remained unfinished. Revenues were entered twice or not at all. Cotton passed the barrier with duties suitable to silk, while a load of melons narrowly escaped assessment by weight and staple. Karim Khan corrected what he could in silence.

I wrote an application for leave, tore it up, wrote another, and carried it in my pocket for three days. Had Government transferred me then, I might have escaped; but to request transfer seemed treachery. She had waited two hundred years. Should I plead pressure of departmental business?

Moreover, I had conceived a theory.

Modern science, I told myself, acknowledges that matter retains impressions. A wire preserves a voice; a plate receives an image; a magnet remembers the north. Why, then, should not marble preserve passion? For generations those chambers had enclosed ambition, jealousy, music, perfume, fear, and unsatisfied love. Their walls had drunk tears, wine, blood, and the breath of sleepers. Perhaps certain conditions of heat, solitude, and nervous receptivity released those impressions, as warmth releases scent from a sealed vessel.

This explanation delighted me because it permitted me to be haunted scientifically.

Yet in my heart I knew that the palace did not merely remember.

It hungered.

It had tasted my wonder; now it desired my identity. Every night it took something from me: first curiosity, then sleep, then duty, then shame, then fear. The stones were not a record but a living digestion. They softened my present, dissolved my name, absorbed my will, and fed me in return with dreams.

I was becoming one more memory in the house.

Still I went.

Would a moth be comforted by an entomological account of flame?

---

Then came the storm.

All that day the heat had pressed upon Barich like a hand. The hills stood black against a copper sky. No bird flew. At sunset a wind sprang from the north and raced along the dry riverbed, lifting dust in pale columns. The forest writhed awake. Branches struck one another; leaves showed their silver undersides; the palace windows darkened as if rank upon rank of watchers had gathered there.

My servants begged me to remain in the office compound. I laughed and rode out.

Before I reached the palace the rain descended. It fell not in drops but in cords, in sheets, in whole rivers overturned from the sky. Lightning opened the hills from summit to root; thunder rolled among the corridors before I entered them.

Within, the palace was alive with panic.

No music greeted me. No fountains played. Doors banged in the wind, although some had been sealed for centuries. Invisible feet fled over the stairs. Women cried behind the walls. Somewhere glass shattered; somewhere a heavy body fell; somewhere a voice shouted an order and was cut short.

I called to her.

A flash filled the great hall. For an instant I saw the columns red—not with reflected lightning, but as if blood ran down them.

Then darkness.

From beneath the floor came her voice.

Not speech. A moan—low at first, then rising, breaking, gathering into such misery that the storm itself seemed but its outer garment. She called from a dungeon, from a grave, from the sealed chamber of time. She called to me, or to God, or to one who had betrayed her. The stones carried her anguish from wall to wall until the whole palace sobbed.

I ran blindly. My hands struck pillars, screens, locked doors. I descended one stair, then another, following the voice into the foundations. The air grew cold. Water streamed beneath my feet. In a flash of lightning that seemed to enter through the earth, I saw a narrow passage and at its end a black iron door.

Behind it she cried.

I seized the ring. It would not move.

“Who art thou?” I shouted. “What dost thou demand of me? Blood? Life? Release?”

Her answer came in a whisper against the iron:

“Remember.”

The word may have been thunder. It may have been wind. It may have been spoken within my own skull.

I dragged at the ring until I fell exhausted. Then, above the tempest, from outside the palace, arose Meher Ali’s cry:

“Stand back! Stand back! All is false! All is false!”

Nearer it came, circling the walls.

“All is false!”

The iron door vanished beneath my hands. I was kneeling before a blank stone recess. The passage behind me had become an ordinary cellar half-filled with rain.

I fled upstairs.

At the main gate Meher Ali passed through the storm, his torn cloak spread like black wings, his face uplifted to the lightning. He did not look at me. Round and round he went, as he had gone in summer stillness, in winter fog, beneath stars, beneath sun, beneath the laughter and tears of generations.

“All is false! All is false!”

I followed him as far as the road and fell unconscious in the mud.

---

When I awoke, I was in the office bungalow. Karim Khan sat beside me counting his beads.

For several days I had fever. During that time I spoke, so they afterwards told me, in Persian, though my ordinary knowledge of that language was limited to polite greetings and quotations inaccurately remembered. I called for a woman named Gulrukh. I demanded that the guards open the lower gate. I accused a certain prince of murder. Once I sang a ghazal in a woman’s voice.

On the fifth morning the fever left me.

Karim Khan brought broth. I drank it and asked, “Who is Meher Ali?”

The old man’s hand trembled.

“You know.”

“I know nothing. Tell me.”

He shut the door and sat upon the floor.

“Meher Ali was not always mad,” he said. “He was a young man of good family, educated, strong, and proud. He came to Barich many years ago as keeper of the revenue records. Like you, he chose the palace. Like you, he laughed at warnings. He stayed one night, two nights, three—”

“And then?”

“He remained six years.”

“In the palace?”

“In its shadow. When they found him, he remembered neither father nor mother, neither prayer nor name. Only the cry remained.”

“Why does he circle it?”

“Perhaps to keep others away. Perhaps to keep himself from entering.”

The rain tapped softly at the shutters. After the violence of the storm, that little sound filled me with dread.

“Karim Khan,” I said, “what is in the palace?”

He looked towards the hills, though the closed wall stood between.

“Hunger.”

I laughed, but my laughter was weak.

“Stones do not hunger.”

“Then stay a fourth night and teach them.”

He leaned nearer.

“Huzoor, men think that only living mouths consume. They do not know the appetite of places. A house where many desires have ripened and none been fulfilled; where women have waited behind screens, where brothers have poisoned brothers, where the old have clung to power and the young to love; where music has hidden cries, perfume has covered blood, and every door has heard a secret—such a house does not die when its people die. It becomes one desire made of many desires.”

I remembered the corridors opening before me.

Karim Khan continued:

“The marble drank all. The fountains washed all. The rooms held all. Century after century the palace has lain empty, but emptiness is not peace. Its stones are famished. Whoever remains there for three consecutive nights is drawn into its dream. It takes his memory and gives him its own; it takes his blood and gives him perfume; it takes his daylight and gives him lamps. At last he cannot leave. If his body escapes, his mind continues to walk from room to room.”

“Yet Meher Ali escaped.”

“His madness escaped. The rest is there.”

I rose from the bed.

“Then I too am lost.”

“Not yet.”

“You said three nights.”

“Three nights open the gate. But there is a way—difficult, almost impossible.”

“What way?”

Karim Khan was silent for so long that I gripped his shoulder.

“Tell me!”

He said, “The hunger began with one woman. Until her history is known, none can understand the release.”

“Gulrukh?”

At that name he covered his face.

So she had a name.

The room darkened about me, though it was broad morning. I smelled wet henna and roses. Somewhere, very far away, an anklet rang.

“Who was she?” I whispered.

Karim Khan lowered his hands.

“She came from Persia in the reign of Shah Mahmud. Her father—”

---

At that instant the station bell clanged with frantic energy. The station-master emerged from his office shouting, “Train coming! Train coming!” as though he had personally summoned it from another world.

A whistle cried beyond the curve. Red and green lamps moved through the darkness. The stranger stopped, glanced down the line, and sprang to his feet.

“My train,” he said.

Haran caught his sleeve.

“But Gulrukh! What happened to her? What was the way of escape?”

The stranger smiled while gathering his valise.

“My dear saheb, the railway administration respects neither love nor metaphysics.”

“Did you escape?”

He placed the red fez more firmly upon his head.

“Am I here?”

“That proves nothing,” said I.

He turned his bright eyes upon me.

“Precisely.”

The train rolled in with a roar, scattering smoke, sparks, sleep, and speculation. Our stranger opened the door of a second-class carriage, saluted us with grave courtesy, and disappeared among merchants, bundles, brass vessels, and one loudly protesting goat. A moment later the train drew away. His face appeared at a window, pale beneath the fez; then steam covered it, and the red tail-lamp dwindled between the rails.

For some time Haran and I remained silent.

At last he said, “An extraordinary narrative.”

“An extraordinary liar.”

My kinsman looked at me with pain.

“Can you not perceive that the palace was an astral reservoir?”

“I perceive that our friend had read several books and missed no opportunity to misuse them.”

“The persistence of emotional vibration in mineral substance,” Haran continued, “is accepted by advanced schools of occult research.”

“Is it accepted by the railway timetable?”

“You heard how exactly his account accords with the doctrine of psychic impregnation.”

“I heard how exactly it accords with the requirements of a delayed train and two captive listeners.”

Haran rose.

“There are more things in heaven and earth—”

“Yes,” I interrupted, “and our stranger has mentioned most of them.”

He would not forgive this. I, being younger and no wiser, would not withdraw it. The discussion proceeded from haunted marble to reincarnation, from reincarnation to the character of Western science, from Western science to Haran’s intellectual capacity, and from that subject—on which I expressed myself with regrettable freedom—to mine.

When our own train arrived at dawn, we entered separate carriages.

We had travelled together as friends for eleven years. We never travelled together again.

As for the palace at Barich, I subsequently made inquiries. One official informed me that no such palace existed. Another said that it existed but was used as a warehouse. A third remembered that a collector had once gone mad there; a fourth maintained that the madman was a clerk; a fifth declared that the whole tale was a Persian legend attached to several houses in the district.

Karim Khan could not be traced. Of Gulrukh there was no record. Meher Ali, if he had ever circled those walls, had long since ceased.

Yet sometimes, when a train is delayed at night and the rails run away into an empty country, I remember the stranger’s last answer.

Am I here?

That proves nothing.

Then the lamp above me seems to burn with the fragrance of attar; the station walls widen into marble halls; beyond the clatter of wheels I hear women laughing as they descend to an invisible river; and from very far off, whether in warning or derision I cannot tell, a madman’s voice goes round and round the darkness:

“Stand back! Stand back! All is false! All is false!”

When the Revolutionary War had ended, and the thirteen colonies had become thirteen independent States, there remained to be disposed of a vaster prize than many of the victors had understood while they were fighting for it. Beyond Pennsylvania and Virginia, beyond the narrow farms, towns, and church-spires of the seaboard, lay the immense country north of the Ohio and east of the Mississippi—a land of deep forests, broad rivers, rich black soil, and ancient hunting paths. Virginia, New York, Connecticut, and Massachusetts possessed, or pretended to possess, portions of it by charter; land companies coveted it; soldiers looked to it for the payment of their services; and thousands of poor families regarded it as the place where a man might obtain with his axe what he could never purchase with money.

The claims of the States were at length surrendered to the Union, and in 1787 Congress established the Northwest Territory. From it were afterward formed Ohio, Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, Wisconsin, and part of Minnesota. The celebrated ordinance declared that slavery should never exist there. This was a noble decree, and we may honor the statesmen who made it; but history is not a picture to be painted wholly in softer lights. The territory was declared free for men of one race while much of its soil was taken, by doubtful bargain or naked force, from men of another.

It is true that treaties had been signed. It is true that certain chiefs placed marks upon papers at Fort Stanwix, Fort McIntosh, and Fort Finney; but it is also true that many tribes denied the authority of those chiefs to sell a common inheritance. The Wyandots, Delawares, Shawnees, Miamis, Ottawas, and other nations maintained that the Ohio River was the boundary between themselves and the whites. They had not forgotten that British officers had once encouraged them to defend it; nor could they comprehend how peace between Britain and America gave Americans a rightful title to forests which neither nation had owned.

Yet the settlers came. They crossed the Ohio in flatboats, drove cattle up the muddy banks, girdled trees, planted corn, and built cabins and blockhouses. Some held warrants; some had purchased shares from land companies; some carried papers whose seals were more imposing than their titles; and many had no authority whatever except a rifle, an axe, and the determination to remain. Once the backwoodsman planted his foot upon the western bank, he seldom turned it eastward. Behind the first cabin rose a second, behind the second a little stockade, and presently there were roads, ferries, taverns, courts, and claims that the wilderness had always waited for the plough.

To the native owners this movement did not resemble peaceful cultivation. It resembled an invasion. Cabins were burned, horses stolen, travellers killed, and lonely families carried away. In return, white parties attacked villages, destroyed corn, and sometimes murdered Indians who had come under assurances of friendship. Blood demanded blood; a slain hunter was answered by a slain settler, and that death by the burning of a town. Officially there were treaties. Actually there was war.

In 1790 General Josiah Harmar marched from Fort Washington, where Cincinnati now stands, against the Miami villages. His army was composed largely of militia, brave enough as individuals, but ill-trained, disorderly, and impatient of restraint. The towns near the Maumee were burned; yet the warriors withdrew only to watch their enemies from the woods. When Harmar began his retreat, detachments were sent back and were drawn into ambuscades. Rifles flashed from behind trees; officers fell; companies lost formation; and the expedition returned humiliated. The confederacy had learned that an American uniform was no charm against defeat.

The government then placed a larger force under Major-General Arthur St. Clair, governor of the Territory and a veteran of the Revolution. He was brave, honorable, and already broken in health. His army was badly supplied; enlistments expired; desertions were frequent; packhorses strayed or were stolen; and the militia, who could shoot squirrels from tall trees, had little knowledge of standing in line beneath a sudden attack. Still the expedition moved northward in the autumn of 1791, cutting a road and leaving forts behind it.

On the evening of November 3, the army encamped beside a small branch of the Wabash. The regulars formed two lines, with the artillery between; the militia lay in advance across the stream. There were women with the camp, teamsters, servants, laundresses, and children. Snow or frost whitened the ground. Fires shone among the trees, kettles swung, sentries paced, and tired men wrapped themselves in blankets without knowing that Little Turtle and the confederated warriors were gathering around them.

Before sunrise the militia were called to parade. Then came a yell from the forest, followed by a crashing volley. The militia broke almost at once, rushing through the stream and into the regular lines. Warriors moved swiftly from tree to tree, surrounding the camp and directing their deadliest fire upon the officers and artillerymen. Horses plunged among the wounded; wagons blocked the paths; women screamed; drums beat commands which few could hear and fewer obey.

St. Clair, weak with illness, had several horses shot beneath him. Eight bullets passed through his clothing. He rode or was helped from point to point, ordering charges in the hope of clearing the woods. The regulars advanced with bayonets, and the Indians yielded before them, but only for a moment; when the soldiers returned to their lines, the circle closed again. The artillery was taken and retaken. Gunners who attempted to load were shot down until the pieces stood among heaps of dead men and horses.

The battle lasted for hours. At length there was no battle-line, but only a crowded camp filled with smoke, blood, broken muskets, overturned wagons, and men firing wherever they saw movement among the trees. Nearly half the force had been killed, and many more were wounded. A final charge opened the road toward Fort Jefferson, and the survivors fled. It was an easy slaughter.

Among those who afterward told of the defeat was a soldier named Fowler. At the beginning of the action he took shelter behind a tree and fired carefully, for every bullet had value. A comrade, passing him in haste, thrust several cartridges into his hand.

“Count them,” the man said.

Fowler looked at the storm of lead clipping twigs above them.

“There will be time enough to count them when this business is done,” he replied.

“No,” answered the other; “count them now, for if you live I shall want them again.”

Whether the lender ever reclaimed his bullets is doubtful. Such grim gayety belongs to battle, where men jest because the truth is too terrible to be spoken.

William Kennan, a young and powerful soldier, escaped by fleetness of foot. He ran with the foremost until he came to a great tree which had been torn up by the roots and lay across the path. The trunk and tangled earth formed an obstacle over which exhausted men crawled with difficulty. Kennan leaped upon it and looked back. The pursuing warriors were close; but on the farther side he saw General Madison, wounded and unable to climb.

To return appeared certain death. Kennan returned nevertheless.

He caught Madison, lifted him, and dragged or carried him over the fallen tree. Then, while bullets struck the bark and the cries of pursuit drew nearer, he helped the wounded officer onward. Courage is often described as the advance of a gay line beneath waving colors; here it was one breathless man refusing to save himself because another could not run.

Kennan afterward became separated from the main body. Three warriors pursued him, believing that his strength must soon fail. Coming to a place where the wind had thrown down several trees, he turned aside and hid beneath their branches. One pursuer passed close enough for Kennan to hear his breathing; another paused, examining the snow or frozen leaves; but the fugitive remained still until they moved on. Hunger, cold, and exhaustion attended him through the forest, yet he finally reached safety.

There were brave men of another race on that field whose names were less carefully preserved. A Chickasaw warrior serving with the Americans took position behind a fallen log. He loaded and fired with such steadiness that those near him marvelled. At last a ball shattered his arm. Unable to hold his rifle in the usual manner, he rested it upon the log, loaded as best he could, and continued firing. When retreat became unavoidable, he would not quit his place until friends seized him. He had come from a people often at war with the northern confederacy, and he fought with the stern pride of his nation.

The retreat soon became more dreadful than the battle. Men threw away rifles, blankets, knapsacks, and even coats. The wounded begged not to be abandoned. Some were carried until those supporting them could carry no longer; others crawled toward the road and were trampled beneath frightened horses. The victorious warriors entered the camp and killed nearly all who remained. The tomahawk made no distinction between the uniform of an officer and the rags of a teamster.

A woman fled with an infant in her arms. The snow clung to her shoes, the road was crowded, and behind her rose the cries of the dying. For miles she struggled onward, though every step diminished her chance of escape. At last, in the blind terror which war calls into the human heart, she laid the baby beside the road and ran on.

We may refuse to judge her, for we did not hear what she heard or feel pursuit at our backs. Perhaps she believed some compassionate person would lift the child; perhaps she had ceased to believe anything. The little bundle remained upon the snow while an army fled past it. There is more of war’s truth in that abandoned child than in many monuments of generals on horseback.

The news of St. Clair’s defeat filled the country with grief and indignation. More than six hundred were dead; the frontier lay exposed; and the young government seemed unable to protect the territory it had opened to settlement. Congress investigated. Men blamed St. Clair, the quartermasters, the contractors, the militia, and the War Department. St. Clair demanded inquiry and defended his conduct with dignity. He had committed errors, but cowardice was not among them. The chief error had been greater than one general: a nation had imagined that disputed land could be occupied without paying the price of conquest.

President Washington next entrusted the task to General Anthony Wayne, whose daring in the Revolution had earned him the name of “Mad Anthony.” Yet in this campaign he displayed not madness but caution. He trained his Legion, enforced discipline, gathered supplies, and refused to hurry into the forest merely because impatient citizens demanded action. As he advanced, he constructed forts—Fort Greenville, Fort Recovery, and Fort Defiance—each a stockaded link binding his army to the settlements behind it.

At Fort Recovery, built upon the ground of St. Clair’s disaster, the confederated warriors attacked in 1794, but found disciplined soldiers behind works and could not repeat their former triumph. Wayne then moved toward the Maumee. His very roads and forts announced that, whether justly or unjustly, the United States intended to remain.

Near Fort Defiance and along the rapids of the Maumee, the confederacy prepared to meet him. On August 20, 1794, the final encounter took place amid trees blown down by a storm, from which the battle received the name Fallen Timbers. The Indians expected the Americans to become confused among the trunks and tangled branches. Wayne instead ordered a rapid advance with bayonets, supported by mounted troops. The warriors were driven from their positions before they could settle into prolonged woodland fighting.

They retreated toward the British Fort Miami, hoping for protection; but the gates were closed. Britain did not choose to begin another war with the United States for allies whom it had encouraged but would not now receive. Within a short time villages and fields were destroyed, and the confederacy’s military resistance was broken. The Treaty of Greenville followed in 1795, surrendering a great portion of Ohio to American settlement. Peace, or what the conqueror calls peace, endured for about sixteen years.

The wilderness changed swiftly. Surveyors stretched their chains across the hunting grounds; towns arose where armies had camped; mills sounded beside the streams; and roads covered the paths along which wounded men had once staggered southward. The settler pointed to orchards, schools, and cultivated acres as proofs of rightful possession. Yet fruitful fields do not make a fraudulent bargain honest, nor does the building of a courthouse answer those who were driven from the land.

When Wayne’s soldiers came upon the field of St. Clair’s defeat, they found the dead still scattered there. The forest had hidden but had not consumed them. Beneath leaves, among weeds, beside rusted camp furniture and fragments of leather, lay human bones. Soldiers gathered skulls by the hundreds and buried them. Some had been cleft; some were pierced by bullets; others bore the marks of the tomahawk. No roll-call could name them all.

Thus ended the proud expedition which had marched northward with flags, drums, commissions, and orders from the government. Its last memorial was not a song of glory but a pit filled with skulls.

We may praise St. Clair’s fortitude, Kennan’s loyalty, Wayne’s discipline, and the courage of the native warriors defending the country of their fathers. We may also honor the ordinance that barred slavery from the Northwest. But we must own the darker inheritance with the brighter one. The cabins and cities of Ohio rose upon contested soil; lawful possession followed victory, while rightful possession remained a question which victory could not settle.

War has often been painted as a stern angel carrying the banner of a nation. On the Wabash it showed another countenance: a shattered gun crew, a wounded man dragged over a tree, stiff fingers clutching an empty rifle, a child left crying in the snow, and hundreds of skulls whitening beneath the leaves. Such is the hideous face of the pitiless demon when the drums are silent.

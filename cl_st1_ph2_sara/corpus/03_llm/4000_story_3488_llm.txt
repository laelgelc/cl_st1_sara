In the first five years of her existence my daughter Mini had acquired many accomplishments, but none so remarkable as her command of conversation. Other children must pause to breathe, to eat, or at least to consider whether they possess anything further to say; Mini regarded all such pauses as reprehensible concessions to silence. From the hour when she awoke until sleep overtook her, she pursued speech as a bird pursues the open sky.

One morning, while I was endeavouring to carry the hero of my seventeenth chapter across a moonlit river, Mini entered my study.

“Father, why does the gatekeeper call a crow a cow?”

I explained that the gatekeeper came from another province and had his own opinions concerning our language. Before I had concluded, she passed to another matter.

“Bhola says there is an elephant in the clouds, and when it pours water from its trunk it rains. Is Bhola a liar?”

“Bhola,” I replied, “is a philosopher.”

She considered this.

“Mother says you talk nonsense.”

Having delivered this domestic judgment, she seated herself beside my writing table and began beating her knees with both hands, chanting a rhyme whose meaning, if it possessed one, was concealed from mankind.

At that moment a cry rose from the street below. It was deep, musical, and prolonged—the call of a travelling merchant. Mini sprang to the window.

“O Cabuliwallah! Cabuliwallah!”

The man looked up.

He was tall and broad, with a dark, weather-beaten face, a loose turban, and garments which seemed to carry upon them the dust of countries beyond my knowledge. Across one shoulder hung a capacious bag; in his hand he bore small boxes of grapes. His appearance, framed by our narrow Calcutta lane, suggested mountains, armed caravans, and passes where a misstep might send a traveller down a precipice.

When he smiled and turned towards our door, Mini’s enthusiasm vanished. She fled behind her mother, persuaded that the great bag contained not raisins and almonds but an assortment of stolen children, packed away without demur.

The merchant came into the courtyard and saluted me. I bought a little fruit, partly from civility and partly because he had already interrupted my hero’s escape and I could see no advantage in preserving the remainder of the morning. We spoke of Kabul, of the British frontier, and of those remote northern roads which the newspapers alternately opened and closed according to the requirements of politics.

Meanwhile Mini watched from the doorway, one eye visible beyond the fold of her mother’s sari.

“Come,” I called. “There is nothing to fear.”

She advanced with considerable hesitation. The stranger took out a handful of raisins and offered them to her.

Mini retreated at once and clung to my knee.

This was their first meeting.

A few days later I found her sitting upon a low bench near the door, talking with the Cabuliwallah as though they were old companions who had been separated by an unfortunate misunderstanding. Rahmun—for that was his name—sat at her feet, listening with grave attention.

“You have an elephant in your bag,” Mini declared.

Rahmun slapped the side of his empty sack. “A very large elephant, little one.”

“How can it fit?”

“I fold it.”

“The trunk too?”

“The trunk twice.”

Mini laughed until she nearly fell from the bench. Rahmun displayed his great teeth and laughed with her.

Then he asked, “Little girl, when are you going to your father-in-law’s house?”

In our society a child might be promised in marriage before she understood the meaning of the promise, yet the expression itself remained an inexhaustible source of mirth. Mini, who knew only that a father-in-law’s house was some mysterious destination used by adults to embarrass children, replied promptly:

“You go to your father-in-law’s house!”

Rahmun shook his fist with mock ferocity. “I shall beat my father-in-law!”

Again they laughed, the small Bengali child and the enormous man from the mountains, while I stood at the window above them and neglected literature.

Their friendship soon became an established fact. Rahmun appeared every morning when his rounds brought him near our lane. There were always nuts in his pocket for Mini, and frequently grapes or dried apricots, although I repeatedly instructed him not to purchase her favour by gifts.

One day I discovered an eight-anna bit in Mini’s hand.

“Where did you get this?”

“The Cabuliwallah gave it to me.”

“Why did you take money from him?”

“I did not take it. He gave it.”

This distinction, though logically sound, failed to satisfy Mini’s mother. She summoned Rahmun and demanded an explanation. The poor man protested that I had paid him for fruit and that he had merely returned the coin to the child as a token of affection.

“A token!” cried my wife. “Today it is a coin; tomorrow he will carry her away in that bag.”

Rahmun, not understanding the whole of this speech, smiled respectfully. His smile increased her alarm.

My wife possessed a tender heart, but the world, as she conceived it, was organised expressly for the production of impending disaster. If a servant coughed, it was consumption; if a mosquito entered the room, malaria had established its headquarters among us; if Mini played in the courtyard, she might be bitten by a snake, seized by a thief, attacked by a tiger, or poisoned by a caterpillar. That no tiger had been observed in our neighbourhood for several years did not reduce the danger. Tigers, she said, were secretive.

“Can you swear,” she asked me that evening, “that no Afghan has ever stolen a child?”

I could swear to very few propositions concerning the entire Afghan nation.

“Rahmun has a daughter of his own,” I said.

“That is what he tells you.”

“And why should he not?”

“Because wicked men tell lies.”

This terminated the discussion, for my wife had secured an argument of universal application.

Nevertheless, I allowed Rahmun’s visits to continue. Beneath his rough garments and bargaining voice I had begun to perceive something that neither accent nor nationality could obscure. When Mini chattered, his attention was not the indulgence of a customer to a child; it was the attention of one listening across a great distance to another voice.

Once, when Mini had been called indoors, Rahmun drew from his tunic a small, folded piece of paper.

“My daughter,” he said.

There was no portrait upon it. He opened the paper and showed me the black impression of a tiny hand. The child’s palm had been inked and pressed upon the sheet; each finger stood there in clumsy perfection.

“How old is she?” I asked.

“When I left, she was like your little one.”

“How long have you been away?”

He looked towards the strip of sky above the courtyard. “Too long.”

Each winter he came down from his country with raisins, grapes, shawls, and small goods; each spring he gathered his debts and returned by the mountain roads. Trade was his necessity, but return was his fixed star. Whenever autumn sharpened the air and the sunlight assumed that clear golden colour which makes even our stained walls appear briefly innocent, my mind travelled with him. I saw defiles between stone cliffs, lines of camels advancing beneath a pale sky, men with long guns upon their shoulders, lonely fires at night, and beyond them a mud-walled house where a child waited—or had ceased to wait.

I, who seldom crossed the river and regarded a journey to the western districts as a formidable enterprise, took pleasure in these expeditions of the imagination. Rahmun’s arrival opened the walls of my study. Yet while I journeyed in this fashion, my tea remained beside me, my slippers beneath my chair, and Mini’s voice within call. It is easy to admire exile when one is at home.

The year of which I now speak was nearing its end when the accident occurred.

One cold morning the lane rang with angry voices. Looking from my window, I saw two policemen leading Rahmun away. His turban was disordered, his clothes were stained, and his hands were bound. Behind them came a crowd of boys, delighted by any procession which promised scandal.

I hurried downstairs.

Rahmun had sold a shawl to a neighbour on credit. When he demanded payment, the man denied the purchase and insulted him. Anger, that swift counsellor of men who carry both pride and knives, completed the matter. Rahmun had stabbed him. The wound was not fatal, but it was grave.

As he saw me, Rahmun began loudly to explain. He invoked Heaven, the honour of his tribe, the villainy of debtors, the unquestionable identity of the shawl, and the general decay of truth in Bengal. The policemen pulled him forward.

At that moment Mini came running from the house.

“O Cabuliwallah! Cabuliwallah!”

Rahmun’s face changed. For an instant the street, the crowd, and the fetters disappeared from his eyes.

Mini noticed that he had no bag.

“Where is your elephant?”

Rahmun looked down at his bound hands. “The elephant has escaped.”

She laughed; then, remembering their other joke, asked, “Are you going to your father-in-law’s house?”

“Yes, little one,” he said. “I am going there.”

He raised his hands as far as the chain permitted and added, “But how shall I beat my father-in-law now?”

The crowd laughed. Mini laughed also. I did not.

The case went before the court. Rahmun’s rage, though provoked, could not excuse the knife, and he received a sentence of several years’ imprisonment. For a little while Mini asked where he had gone. I said that he was away. Children accept distance without demanding its geography.

Another fruit seller began to pass through our lane. Mini called to him once, but he did not know about the elephant. His replies were dull, and she abandoned him.

Time passed away.

At first Mini bestowed her friendship upon a groom in a neighbouring house; then upon several girls of her own age. Her endless questions altered their subjects. Dolls succeeded elephants; lessons succeeded dolls; later still, songs, dresses, glances, and whispered confidences occupied that swift intelligence. She no longer entered my study whenever she pleased. Sometimes I heard laughter from the inner apartments and realised with unreasonable surprise that she possessed a life in which I was not the chief person.

This is the ordinary theft committed by time. It takes nothing in one dramatic seizure. It removes a word today, a habit tomorrow, a small hand from yours the day after, until you wake among familiar walls and find that the child who filled them has disappeared without leaving the house.

Eight years went by.

On an autumn morning our home awoke before sunrise. Mini’s wedding day had come.

From the inner rooms arose a ceaseless tumult of footsteps, instructions, disputes, laughter, and the clatter of vessels. Bamboo poles stood in the courtyard for the wedding canopy. Servants climbed ladders to fasten lamps along the roof. Musicians in the entrance hall tuned their instruments, and the first notes of Bhairavi trembled through the cool air with a sweetness which, under the circumstances, I considered almost malicious.

Sunlight streamed into my study. It shone upon accounts, lists, garlands, brass plates, and the anxious face of a father discovering that ceremonial splendour has an appetite more formidable than any Afghan’s bag.

Mini was within, surrounded by women, undergoing those elaborate preparations by which a daughter is made beautiful for her departure. I had scarcely seen her that morning. Whenever I attempted to enter, somebody drove me away as a useless male.

I was examining the cost of the illuminations when a man came to the door and saluted.

At first I did not recognise him. He had no bag, his hair was cut short, and the vigorous ease of his former bearing had gone. His face seemed smaller, as landscapes seem diminished after a flood.

Then he smiled.

“Rahmun!”

“I was released yesterday,” he said.

The word released brought back the narrow lane, the blood upon his clothes, the hands in irons. On any other day I might have welcomed him warmly, but the household was in confusion, and an ex-prisoner—however old the acquaintance—did not appear an auspicious addition to a wedding morning.

“There is a great ceremony here today,” I said. “I am extremely occupied. Come another time.”

He stood for a moment without reply. Then he turned towards the gate.

“Sir,” he asked, “may I not see the little one?”

He believed, I understood, that she remained exactly as he had left her: a five-year-old child who would run crying, “O Cabuliwallah!” and demand news of the folded elephant. The prison walls had kept out more than freedom. They had kept out time.

“She is being married today,” I said.

He stared at me.

“Married?”

“Yes. Today she goes to her husband’s house.”

The old joke, stripped of childhood, stood between us. Rahmun lowered his eyes.

“I shall go,” he said.

He reached the doorway, hesitated, and returned. From inside his loose garment he produced a small packet of grapes and a few almonds wrapped in paper.

“I brought these for the little one. Give them to her.”

I took out money.

He drew back, almost offended.

“No, sir. You are kind, but these are not merchandise. I have a daughter in my country. When I saw your child, I remembered mine. I did not come to trade.”

Then he unfolded a worn scrap of paper.

The ink had faded, the creases had nearly divided it, and years of concealment against his breast had made it soft as cloth; nevertheless, the small black hand remained. Five fingers reached across the page from a vanished morning in Afghanistan.

Rahmun spread it upon my table with great care.

I looked at that hand, and the accounts before me became obscure. The musicians continued below; women called from room to room; the wedding canopy stirred in the autumn breeze. Yet all at once these distinctions which we construct with such industry—Bengali and Afghan, householder and wanderer, respectable man and convicted criminal—fell away.

He also was a father.

I had Mini within the house, though she was passing from me. He had possessed nothing of his daughter for years except this print of her hand. While I had watched my child grow, protested against her noise, paid her tutors, chosen her ornaments, and sighed over wedding expenses, his child had been growing beyond mountains, perhaps forgetting the sound of his voice. What right had I to guard my happiness from his sorrow merely because the day was busy?

I sent for Mini.

There was considerable opposition from the inner apartments. My wife declared that it was improper, unlucky, impossible. A bride could not be summoned before a foreign fruit seller who had lately emerged from jail. For once, however, I did not surrender to the combined authority of custom and apprehension.

Mini came at last.

She wore the red silk of a bride. Sandal paste adorned her brow; ornaments shone at her neck and wrists; a veil fell over her hair. The child who had once rushed barefoot through my papers now walked slowly, her eyes lowered, conscious of every gaze.

Rahmun rose.

For some moments he said nothing. His smile appeared, failed, and returned with painful uncertainty.

“Little one,” he began, “are you going to your father-in-law’s house?”

The words had once produced instant laughter. Mini now understood them too well. Her face grew crimson; she turned aside and drew the veil closer.

I remembered the tiny girl upon the bench, her knees beating time, her questions tumbling over one another. I could see—. But no; the bride stood before us, silent.

Rahmun sat down upon the floor.

His eyes remained fixed upon Mini, yet he was no longer seeing her. Through her changed face he had perceived another transformation: a little girl in Kabul grown tall; a voice altered; a stranger perhaps waiting where his daughter had been. The eight years descended upon him at once.

Mini withdrew.

Rahmun took up the paper with the handprint, folded it, and placed it against his breast. No complaint passed his lips. That restraint was more terrible than lamentation.

“How soon can you return home?” I asked.

“If I have the fare, I can begin at once.”

I opened the cash box and gave him a sum sufficient for the journey. He looked from the notes to my face.

“Go,” I said. “Go to your daughter. Let your meeting bring happiness to my child’s wedding day.”

He touched the money to his forehead. Then, with a blessing upon Mini and upon our house, he departed through the decorated gate.

The gift required certain economies. The electric illumination planned for the outer wall was reduced; the hired military band was dismissed; some silver favours vanished from the list. My wife and several relatives regarded these omissions as a calamity and did not hesitate to inform me that the wedding had been permanently disfigured.

Perhaps it was. The courtyard was less brilliant than intended, and no trumpets announced the bridegroom’s arrival.

Yet to me the lamps shone sufficiently. Beyond their light I saw a solitary man travelling northward—past the smoky limits of Calcutta, across rivers and plains, towards stone passes beneath the autumn sky. In his garment rested the black impression of a child’s hand. Ahead of him, somewhere beyond the mountains, waited the living hand itself, no longer small.

That thought illuminated our house more deeply than all the lamps I had sacrificed. And while the music rose and my daughter prepared to leave me, I understood that the sorrow of separation, which appears to divide one heart from another, is sometimes the very road by which they meet.

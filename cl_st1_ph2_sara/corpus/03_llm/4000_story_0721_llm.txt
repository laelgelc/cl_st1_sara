## THE KIT-BAG

“You have earned your holiday, Johnson,” said Arthur Wilbraham, folding the evening paper across his knee. “Indeed, if I possessed your conscience, I should take six weeks instead of three.”

Johnson smiled and glanced at the sleet that ticked sharply against the windows of the barrister’s chambers.

“My conscience has nothing to do with it, sir. My purse has.”

“Then go for three, and be thankful. Sunshine on snow is a better tonic than London fog, and you look as though you had swallowed the whole Turk case and found it indigestible.”

At the name, the secretary’s smile faded.

The long trial had ended that afternoon. John Turk, charged with a murder whose peculiar brutality had shocked even those accustomed to the darker revelations of the criminal courts, had been acquitted upon the ground of insanity. Wilbraham, who had conducted the defence with his usual cold precision, had obtained the verdict against an array of facts which, to the ordinary mind, appeared less to prove madness than an extraordinary and deliberate wickedness.

That Turk had murdered the man was beyond question. That he had afterwards divided the body and conveyed the portions away in a large canvas kit-bag was equally certain. The entire issue had rested upon whether he knew the nature and quality of his act; and Wilbraham, aided by specialists whose evidence had filled the court with a vocabulary of morbid impulses and diseased inhibitions, had persuaded the jury that he did not.

Johnson had sat behind his employer throughout the trial, arranging papers, taking notes, and avoiding, whenever possible, the prisoner’s face.

It was that face which troubled him now.

“I suppose,” he said slowly, “there was really no doubt about the insanity?”

Wilbraham raised his eyebrows.

“There is always doubt. That is why the law employs us.”

“But you believe it?”

“My personal belief has no place in the matter.”

“Still—”

“My dear Johnson,” interrupted the barrister, not impatiently, but with the weariness of one who had repeated an explanation too often, “a lawyer is not a priest. He neither absolves nor condemns. He presents facts and tests evidence. The jury accepted the medical testimony. Turk is not responsible in the legal sense, and there the affair ends.”

Johnson turned towards the window. Beyond the streaming panes, the lamps of the court shone dimly in the wet dusk. Cabs moved past with glistening roofs, and the cries of boys selling the later editions rose thinly above the traffic.

“There was something in his eyes,” he murmured.

Wilbraham looked at him more closely.

“You observed him too much. These pictures, once admitted to the mind, have a trick of returning when least wanted. That is precisely why I advise the mountains. Go and look at wholesome things—snowfields, pine woods, rosy-cheeked girls. Skate, dance, eat too much, and forget John Turk.”

“I mean to,” Johnson said, with an attempt at cheerfulness. “I leave to-morrow evening.”

“All packed?”

“Not yet. That reminds me. I have no proper bag. My portmanteau is too large, and the small valise won’t hold half my things.”

“There are two or three kit-bags at my house. Send for one.”

“That is very good of you.”

“I shall tell Richards to put one out. Send your landlady’s boy round this evening.”

They spoke for another ten minutes about routes, trains, and the probable condition of the ice at Mürren. The healthy details did Johnson good. He saw in imagination the vast white slopes under a blue sky, the little rink alive with coloured caps and flying skirts, the red lamps of the hotel after dusk, and heard already the scrape of skates and the quick music of a waltz. When at last he buttoned his coat and went down into the raw December night, he carried the pleasant vision with him.

Yet, as he crossed the crowded Strand, another face moved beneath it like something seen under thin ice—a broad, dead-white face, with low black hair upon the forehead and eyes that seemed not merely cruel but inhabited by cruelty.

At ten minutes before eight Johnson reached the gaunt Bloomsbury house where he occupied two rooms on the third floor. Once it had sheltered a single prosperous family; now it was divided among lodgers, whose names appeared on small cards above a disorderly collection of bell-handles. The basement and ground floor belonged to Mrs Monks, the landlady. A commercial traveller occupied the first floor, but was in Manchester; the second was taken by an elderly violin teacher, who had gone to spend Christmas with a married daughter at Croydon. Above Johnson were two narrow attic rooms, empty except for discarded furniture.

Thus, though the streets outside were populous and noisy, the upper part of the house had that peculiar emptiness which is more oppressive than solitude in the country. A country house stands among fields and trees and admits its isolation honestly. A London house is one of a multitude; walls press against it, windows overlook it, wheels and footsteps pass continually at its door—and yet within, behind the shut doors and darkened landings, a man may be as completely alone as in a desert.

Mrs Monks met him in the hall.

“The bag’s come, sir,” she said. “A great ugly thing. My George carried it up, though it near pulled ’is arm off.”

“Pulled his arm off? It ought to be empty.”

“So it is, sir. But there’s bags as carries light and bags as don’t. This one’s stiff and awkward like.”

She lowered her voice, glancing towards the kitchen stairs.

“And I don’t say as it’s clean.”

“Oh, that won’t matter. It has only to last the journey.”

“You’ll find it outside your sitting-room. And there’s a note for you on the table.”

Johnson thanked her and climbed the stairs.

The gas on the first landing was turned low; on the second it had gone out altogether. He struck a match, but a draught descending from the attics extinguished it before he had taken three steps. He knew the staircase well, however, and continued in darkness, one hand upon the banister.

Halfway up, he stopped.

Above him, upon the third-floor landing, something moved softly across the boards.

It was not the creak of timber nor the tapping of a loose window-catch. It was a tread—slow, guarded, unmistakably human. There came one footstep, then another, followed by a slight dragging sound, as though a heavy object were being drawn a few inches and set down.

“Who’s there?” Johnson called.

His voice travelled upwards and lost itself in the hollow spaces of the house.

No one replied.

After a pause he ascended rapidly, reached the landing, and found it empty. The narrow passage led in one direction to his rooms and in the other to the attic stair. At the farther end a window showed a blurred patch of grey night; the curtain, stirred by a current of air, swayed out and sank back again.

Outside his sitting-room door stood the kit-bag.

Even in that poor light it appeared unusually large. It was made of coarse brown canvas, much rubbed and darkened by use, with leather straps and brass rings. Its mouth had been folded down, so that the upper part sagged in a shapeless, almost huddled fashion. Johnson, whose nerves had not yet recovered from the trial, thought for one absurd instant that someone was crouching there with his head bent upon his knees.

Then the curtain moved again, throwing a new shadow, and the illusion vanished.

“Pure nerves,” he said aloud.

He unlocked his door, switched on the electric light, and dragged the bag inside. It resisted clumsily at the threshold, one corner catching against the frame, and when he gave a stronger pull it came with a sudden lurch that sent him staggering backwards.

The room, with its familiar books, red curtains, writing-table and brightly burning fire, restored him at once. Mrs Monks had set out his supper under a metal cover. Wilbraham’s note lay beside it.

*Dear Johnson,* it ran, *Richards has sent the larger bag, as you are bound to take twice what you need. Do not spend your holiday reading depositions. Yours, A. W.*

Johnson laughed. The sound was unnecessarily loud, but it was a genuine laugh nevertheless. He ate with an excellent appetite, drank two glasses of beer, and read the sporting news. Only when the clock struck nine did his glance return to the kit-bag.

It stood where he had left it, close to the door.

There was nothing remarkable about it now, except its ugliness. Yet it was certainly dirtier than any bag Wilbraham was likely to have used for ordinary travel. The canvas showed several stains, the leather had been scratched and cracked, and the brass rings were dulled almost black. One side bulged outward, although the bag was empty—or supposed to be empty—and its folds cast little shadows which altered whenever the fire rose or sank.

Johnson pushed back his chair.

“Now for the packing.”

He carried the bag into the bedroom, where clothes were spread upon the bed in orderly piles. There was pleasure in the business at first. Thick boots, sweaters, woollen shirts and skating things belonged to the other world—the clean, vigorous world that waited for him beyond the Channel. He hummed as he packed. From time to time he stopped to imagine the journey: dawn over the French fields, coffee at a station buffet, the first pale line of mountains, and then the great ascent into glittering air.

The wind increased. It roared intermittently among the black roofs, drove volleys of sleet against the glass, and made the sash rattle in its frame. The fire in the adjoining room fluttered and sent a faint smell of smoke through the doorway.

Johnson had packed one layer when he noticed a dark mark inside the bag.

It lay at the bottom near a seam, partly hidden by a pair of folded trousers. He removed them and bent closer. The stain was broad and irregular, brown at the edges and nearly black in the centre. It might have been oil, wine, mud—anything. Old canvas gathers discolorations as old men gather scars, and ordinarily he would not have given it a second thought.

But he thought of the exhibit in court.

The bag in which Turk had packed the body had been produced on the second day. Johnson remembered the constable lifting it upon the table; remembered, too, the murmur that had passed through the crowded court when one of the medical witnesses pointed to certain stains on the inner surface.

He straightened himself quickly.

The resemblance meant nothing. All kit-bags looked much alike, and this one had come from Wilbraham’s house. It was natural that a bag should have stains. To connect it with the exhibit was grotesque.

He put the trousers back and continued packing.

A minute later he heard the tread again.

It sounded now in the passage immediately outside his rooms: one soft step, a pause, another step, and then that faint dragging shuffle.

Johnson ceased humming.

The noise was too deliberate for the wind, yet he told himself that the old house was full of loose boards and ill-fitting doors. Pressure of air could produce curious effects. A board lifted in one place might settle in another; a curtain ring could scrape upon a rod; sleet sliding from a sill—

The handle of the sitting-room door turned slightly and returned.

He stood motionless, holding a woollen shirt between his hands.

There are impressions which reach the mind complete, without passing through any visible process of reasoning. Johnson knew suddenly that someone stood outside. Not a caller who had climbed openly and was preparing to knock, nor Mrs Monks bringing coal, but someone who had come very quietly up the dark stairs and was now listening.

His first emotion was not fear, but indignation.

He dropped the shirt, crossed the sitting-room in three strides and flung open the door.

The passage was empty.

At the far end, the white curtain lifted and streamed inward. Below, the staircase curved into darkness. From the street came the muffled roll of an omnibus and the cries of men in the wind.

Johnson stepped out.

“Mrs Monks!”

A door opened in the basement far below.

“Yes, sir?”

“Has anyone come in?”

“No, sir. Not a soul.”

“Is George upstairs?”

“George ’as been gone this hour.”

“Very well. It was nothing.”

He shut the door and locked it.

The act of turning the key annoyed him. He was not a coward. His face, with its fine skin, fair moustache and rather delicate features, had sometimes caused strangers to mistake gentleness for weakness; but Johnson possessed real pluck, and in situations of actual danger had more than once shown an admirable coolness. Yet courage before a visible danger and courage before an invisible suggestion are separate qualities. A man may face a charging animal and still shrink from a closed cupboard in a room where he believes himself alone.

Fear, moreover, seldom enters full-grown. It forms film by film, as ice forms upon still water. A sound that cannot be placed, a movement noticed at the edge of vision, an object not quite where memory declares it to have been—each leaves its thin deposit; and while reason assures us that the surface is still open, some deeper sense knows that the water is freezing.

Johnson went back to the bedroom.

The kit-bag was no longer beside the bed.

It stood some two feet nearer the door.

He stared at it.

For several seconds his mind refused the fact and searched among hurried explanations. He had dragged it aside with his foot. The weight of the clothes had altered its balance. The boards sloped. The bag had settled and slid. Yet he remembered distinctly that its leather handle had rested against the bedstead, whereas now there was a clear strip of carpet between them.

He seized the handle, lifted the bag, and set it firmly against the wall.

“There!” he said. “Let us see you move from that.”

The words were spoken as a challenge, and the instant they were uttered he regretted them. They gave personality to the thing. The bag was no longer merely an object concerning which his memory might be at fault; it had become, by the foolish form of his address, something capable of hearing and obedience—or disobedience.

He laughed and resumed his work.

The laugh failed in the middle.

From behind him came a long, quiet sigh.

It was the sigh of a tired man—deeply drawn, slowly released, and so near that Johnson felt, or imagined that he felt, the faint passage of breath across his neck.

He turned with a bound.

No one was there.

The bedroom was brilliantly lighted. The white counterpane, the open drawers, the little mirror upon the chest, and every harmless detail of the room stood revealed under the electric globe. The curtains were still. The door into the sitting-room remained open, and beyond it the fire burned low in the grate.

The bag rested against the wall.

Johnson went first to the sitting-room, then to the passage, and finally, taking a candle, up the narrow stairs to the attics. Both rooms were empty. In one, a broken chair leaned against a heap of rolled carpets; in the other, dust lay thick and undisturbed beneath the window. The roof creaked overhead, and the gale made a sound in the chimney like a distant, desolate voice, but there was no person in either room, nor any place where one might reasonably hide.

He returned, locking every door behind him.

Ten o’clock struck from a neighbouring church as he re-entered the bedroom.

At that precise moment the electric light went out.

Darkness fell, sudden and absolute.

Johnson’s breath caught. He remained by the door with one hand still upon the switch. The fire in the next room gave no more than a dull red glow, insufficient to illuminate the bedroom but enough to show the doorway as a deeper oblong in the blackness.

Then came the tread.

It began outside his rooms.

Soft—stealthy—measured.

A step crossed the landing. Another entered the sitting-room. There followed the dragging shuffle, the slight complaint of a board under weight, and then silence.

Johnson pressed the switch repeatedly. Nothing happened.

He could hear his own heart, the sleet upon the window, and beneath both, so faintly that it might have been the movement of blood within his ears, a sound of breathing.

“Who is there?” he demanded.

His voice was firm, but the darkness seemed to swallow it before it travelled six feet.

No answer came.

He remembered the candle in his hand and fumbled for matches. The first broke. The second slipped from his fingers. The third struck, flared blue, and then burned steadily.

For one instant the tiny flame showed the bedroom empty.

The bag was gone.

The match scorched his fingers and went out.

He backed against the door, fumbling for another, while terror, no longer an accumulation of doubts but a definite force, took hold of him. He found the box, shook it, and heard the small sticks rattle. Before he could draw one out, the electric light returned.

The room sprang into merciless brightness.

The kit-bag stood in the centre of the floor.

Its mouth, which he had left folded down, was now partly open. The packed clothes had been displaced and thrust upward, giving the sides a swollen, straining appearance. The brass rings faced him like dull eyes. Between them a fold of canvas sagged into a pale, coarse imitation of a cheek, while the dark opening above had the look of hair lying low across a forehead.

Turk’s face!

The resemblance lasted scarcely a second. Johnson stepped sideways and the folds changed. It was a bag again—canvas, leather, brass, and nothing more.

But the inner stain was now exposed.

Under the white light its colour could no longer be mistaken for ordinary dirt. It was old and dark, yet in places the brown deepened towards a hideous rusty red. A second smear ran upward along the seam, as though some soaked substance had pressed there before being removed.

Johnson felt cold about the heart.

He had seen that stain before.

Not one like it. That very stain.

He saw again the crowded courtroom; the yellow winter light falling from the high windows; the faces turned towards the table; the officer’s gloved hands holding the sides of the exhibit apart. He heard the medical witness explaining in a dry voice how blood, when absorbed by coarse fabric, spread unevenly among the fibres. He saw Turk in the dock, motionless, with his broad white face and low black fringe, looking not at the witness, not at the jury, but at the bag.

This was the murder bag.

“No,” Johnson said aloud. “That is impossible.”

He knelt and examined one of the leather straps. Near the buckle was a small triangular cut. He remembered it. Counsel for the prosecution had asked whether the cut might have been caused by a knife. The witness had replied that it might, though he could not swear to it.

Johnson rose unsteadily.

How could the thing be here? The exhibits ought to remain in official custody. Yet the trial was over. They might have been returned. Wilbraham might temporarily have received them with other papers. Richards, sent carelessly to select a kit-bag, might have taken the wrong one from a cupboard or lumber-room.

It was an error. A disgusting, outrageous error—but an error that explained everything important. The stain had recalled the trial; the trial had excited his nerves; the wind and the empty house had done the rest. As for the bag’s movement, he had been mistaken. As for the sigh—

He would not consider the sigh.

There remained only one sensible course. He would empty the bag, fasten it, place it outside, and inform Wilbraham in the morning.

The thought steadied him. Action often breaks the spell that speculation strengthens.

He approached the bag and pulled out the clothes. They came away untidily. A sweater had become wedged at the bottom, and as he tugged at it the bag collapsed sideways with a heavy flop. Something within gave a soft, sliding thud.

Johnson snatched his hand away.

The bag was empty.

He could see the whole interior. Nothing lay there but stains and shadows.

He took up the sweater again.

The canvas under his hand was warm.

Not warm from the fire, for the bedroom had no fire, and the bag had stood against the cold wall. It had the close, unpleasant warmth of something recently inhabited.

Johnson recoiled, wiping his palm upon his coat.

At once the bag righted itself.

There was no gradual settling, no natural fall of the canvas. It gave a single violent lurch, rolled from its side, and stood upright.

For perhaps five seconds Johnson and the bag confronted each other.

Then it moved towards him.

A contraction ran through its sides. One corner thrust forward, the other dragged after it, and with a shuffle—precisely the dragging sound he had heard upon the stairs—it advanced several inches across the carpet.

Johnson’s self-control broke.

He rushed into the sitting-room and slammed the bedroom door. The key was on the other side. He dragged a chair beneath the handle, then stood gripping its back, ashamed of what he had done and yet unable to go near the door again.

Nothing followed.

The wind roared; the windows shook; somewhere in the house a loose door banged once with a report like a pistol shot. After that, silence descended, enormous and listening.

Johnson looked at the clock.

It was seven minutes past ten.

“I shall go downstairs,” he thought. “I shall sleep in the kitchen, if necessary. Mrs Monks may laugh, but I shall not remain here.”

He crossed towards the outer door.

Before he reached it, three slow knocks sounded from the bedroom.

Not on the door—from within the room.

Knock.

A pause.

Knock.

Another pause, longer than the first.

Knock.

Johnson stopped. Every impulse urged him to escape, yet a contrary force held him. It was partly pride, partly the paralysis of fear, and partly that dreadful human curiosity which makes a man look over the edge from which he most fears to fall.

The handle of the bedroom door began to turn.

The chair resisted it. For a moment the handle pressed downward without sound. Then the chair moved. Its front legs slid slowly over the carpet.

Johnson watched.

An inch.

Two inches.

The door opened a narrow black line.

He could have run then. The outer door was behind him, unlocked; the staircase beyond was clear. But darkness showed through that widening crack, and darkness, strangely enough, appeared merciful. Whatever waited inside was still concealed. To turn his back would be to imagine it coming after him. To look was dreadful; not to look had become impossible.

The chair slid aside.

The bedroom door swung open.

At first nothing appeared. The electric light within had failed again, though that in the sitting-room still burned. Its glow entered a little way and lay across the carpet in a yellow strip. Beyond it the room was black.

Then the kit-bag came through.

It did not roll. It advanced with small, spasmodic thrusts, pausing between them as if gathering strength. The leather handle nodded; the brass rings clicked softly together. At the threshold it caught, struggled, and with a sudden heave fell forward into the sitting-room.

Its mouth opened.

A human sigh issued from it.

Johnson’s flesh crawled from head to foot. He pressed himself against the wall, unable even to cry out.

The bag swelled slowly, its sides stretching as though something inside were rising from a cramped position. The canvas lifted. The open mouth widened. For an instant there was only darkness within; then a hand appeared upon the rim.

It was a large, colourless hand.

The fingers closed over the canvas. Another hand followed. Between them rose a forehead, fringed low with black hair; then eyes, deep-set and evil; then the whole broad, white face of John Turk.

No mist obscured it. No merciful uncertainty softened its outlines. It was clear as life under the electric light, yet more terrible than life because the flesh had the fixed pallor of death. The eyes alone moved. They passed from Johnson to the bag, and from the bag back to Johnson.

The apparition lifted itself higher.

The shoulders were visible now, clothed in the dark suit Turk had worn upon the last day of the trial. One side of the collar was turned inward. A thin stain marked the chin. The figure seemed both to emerge from the bag and to stand behind it, violating perspective as easily as it violated reason.

Johnson tried to speak. His tongue moved without sound.

Turk’s lips parted.

“It’s my bag. And I want it.”

The words were quiet, almost conversational.

The simplicity of them completed the horror. Had the thing shrieked, threatened, or uttered some message from the dead, Johnson’s mind might still have resisted; but the plain possessiveness of the demand, the brutal claim to that stained and dreadful object, belonged wholly to the man he had watched in court.

The figure reached forward.

The light went out.

Something brushed Johnson’s face—cold hair, or canvas, or the passage of a dead hand. He heard the bag scrape across the boards, heard a deep sigh beside his ear, and then the whole room seemed to swing downward beneath him.

He knew no more.

Morning entered greyly through the windows. The storm had passed, leaving the roofs wet and the gutters full. Milk-carts rattled in the street, and a pale band of ordinary daylight lay across the sitting-room floor.

Johnson opened his eyes.

He was on the sofa, with a cushion beneath his head. Mrs Monks stood over him holding a cup of tea, while a square-built man in a dark overcoat waited near the door.

“Drink this, sir,” said Mrs Monks. “You gave me a proper turn, lying there white as paper. If Richards ’adn’t come when he did—”

“Richards?” Johnson whispered.

Wilbraham’s servant stepped forward.

“Beg pardon, sir. Mr Wilbraham sent me directly we found out.”

Johnson sat up. His eyes travelled unwillingly towards the bedroom.

The kit-bag stood near the outer door, strapped and folded. In daylight it looked merely old, filthy and ill-used. Nevertheless Johnson shrank from it.

“Take it away,” he said.

“That’s what I’ve come for, sir.”

“You sent me the wrong bag.”

“Yes, sir. A most unfortunate mistake. They’d brought several things from chambers after the case, and this bag was put temporary in the hall cupboard. The master told me to send you the large brown kit-bag. There was another behind it, a clean one. I took the first without looking close.”

“Then it is—?”

Richards glanced at Mrs Monks and lowered his voice.

“It’s the bag used in the Turk case, sir.”

Mrs Monks gave a small exclamation.

Johnson set down the cup because his hand shook so violently that the china rattled against the saucer.

“At what time did you discover the mistake?”

“Early this morning, sir. When I saw the proper bag still in the cupboard.”

“And Turk?” Johnson asked.

Richards hesitated.

“There’s bad news concerning him, sir.”

“What news?”

“He’s dead.”

The room seemed suddenly very still.

“Dead?”

“Yes, sir. Poisoned himself. Directly after he was released, so they say. The police telephoned Mr Wilbraham’s chambers this morning, there being a letter addressed to the master among his papers.”

Johnson leaned forward.

“When did he die?”

“About ten o’clock last night, sir.”

Mrs Monks crossed herself hurriedly.

Richards continued in the practical, unemotional voice of a man repeating facts whose strangeness had not yet penetrated him.

“The letter was mostly thanks to the master for getting him off. But there was one queer instruction in it. He said he’d made arrangements concerning his burial, and that this particular bag was to be used.”

“Used?” Johnson’s voice was scarcely audible.

“Yes, sir. He asked to be buried in it.”

Johnson looked towards the stained canvas object by the door.

For a moment no one moved.

Then one of the dulled brass rings, though no hand touched it and no draught stirred in the room, turned slowly against the other with a faint metallic click.

# The Last Army

The cruiser came down through the copper clouds without lights.

It had crossed three hundred million miles under false registration, hiding among the wreckage of fleets, drifting when patrols drew near, burning its engines only when darkness and distance offered safety. Once it had been the *Imperial Vengeance*, flagship of the Inner Planets and terror of the Martian lanes. Now its armored flanks were scorched, its forward batteries were dead, and a merchant’s name had been painted hastily across its bow.

On the bridge, Captain Vorst stood rigid before the man in the gray traveling cloak.

“The beach is empty, Number One.”

“Call me Mr. Varon.”

The captain swallowed.

“Yes, Number One.”

Varon turned from the black viewplate. Venus rolled beneath them, green and white and blue, too peaceful to belong to the same solar system that had spent seven years destroying itself. Beyond the glass, the planet’s clouds boiled soundlessly. Far behind them, beyond those clouds and the black gulf, the war was over.

All was lost.

The words had arrived four days earlier in a message compressed to seven seconds.

Earth occupied. Luna fallen. Directorate dissolved. Galactic Guard entering system. All is lost.

Varon had ordered the messenger shot.

The news had remained true.

“What of the Guard?” he asked.

“No sign of pursuit.”

“They will pursue.”

“Perhaps they believe you dead.”

“They know better. My enemies have always known better.”

Captain Vorst’s face revealed nothing. He had served Varon for twenty-two years, through the purges on Ganymede, the Uranian revolt, the coronation beneath the black banners, and the final days when blood and steel had fallen from the sky. He had watched cities become furnaces. He had watched fleets blossom into brief white flowers in space. He had watched men go smiling into death because Number One had promised that history was watching.

Now history was elsewhere.

The cruiser settled above a white crescent of sand between the jungle and a violet sea. No landing jets flared. Repulsors hummed, and palm-like trees bent away from the invisible pressure.

Varon descended through the belly hatch carrying a small valise.

Without his uniform he seemed diminished. The broad shoulders were still there, the hard wedge of jaw, the pale eyes under heavy brows. But the gray cloak concealed the body armor, and the civilian cap covered the iron-white hair known to billions from statues, coins, broadcast screens and compulsory portraits.

At the edge of the beach stood an abandoned radar shack, a low metal box furred with green corrosion. Its dish pointed uselessly toward the sky.

“You will return in sixty days,” Varon said.

Captain Vorst looked past him toward the jungle.

“Yes, Number One.”

“By then you will have reached the outer colonies. The loyalists must be rallied. The Guard cannot hold nine planets and all the moons. Tell them I live.”

“I will tell them.”

“Tell them the defeat was temporary.”

“Yes.”

“Tell them the Directorate endures wherever one loyal heart still beats.”

“Yes, Number One.”

Varon extended his hand. Vorst took it, then bent and pressed his forehead to the dictator’s knuckles.

“For Race and Destiny,” said the captain.

“For Race and Destiny.”

Varon stepped back.

The hatch closed. Sand rose in a white spiral, stinging his cheeks. The cruiser climbed silently, a dark blade against the blue. For one instant it hung above the clouds. Then it vanished.

Varon remained on the beach with his valise at his feet.

The surf whispered.

Somewhere in the green interior, a marigee screamed.

He was alone.

* * *

The shack contained a cot, a table, two metal chairs, a rusted water condenser and the dead radar equipment. Fungi had spread across one wall in pale, fleshy shelves. Small blue beetles nested in the communications console, and something with six legs and a feathery tail had died under the cot.

Varon spent his first day cleaning.

He worked with the severity he had once demanded of ministers and generals. The carcass went into the jungle. The fungi he burned. He drove the beetles from the console, scrubbed the floor with sand, inventoried his supplies and arranged them according to category and size.

From the valise he removed civilian clothing, medical tablets, concentrated food, a compact needle gun with twelve cartridges, and a sealed packet containing his black Directorate uniform.

He did not open the packet.

He placed it under the cot.

By nightfall, the shack had acquired discipline.

Outside, Venus shouted.

The sea boomed in the darkness. Marigees screamed from the treetops like women discovering knives in their beds. Baroons gibbered and laughed in the low branches. Winged things beat against the walls. The leaves rubbed together with the whisper of conspirators.

Varon lay awake.

He had slept during bombardments. He had slept while the rebel fleet entered Earth orbit, while ministers waited outside his chamber with casualty lists, while traitors were questioned in the rooms beneath the Black Palace. He had trained himself to sleep because Number One could not afford exhaustion.

But the jungle would not obey.

Toward dawn he heard drums.

They came from very far away: a soft, repeated pounding, then silence, then pounding again.

Boom. Boom. Boom.

Like artillery beyond a horizon.

Like a heart.

He sat up and reached for the needle gun.

The sound ceased.

In the morning, he found three brown insects feeding on a crumb beside the table. They were each as long as the last joint of his thumb, with black jaws and narrow, armored bodies.

He crushed them under his boot.

* * *

On the ninth day, Varon saw the cruiser return.

It came low over the sea, its hull restored, its banners flying. Captain Vorst stood in the open hatch, laughing, while behind him ranked the marshals, admirals and ministers of the Directorate.

Varon ran onto the sand.

The cruiser became a cloud.

He stood beneath the bright sky until his breathing slowed.

“A trick of the light,” he said.

The sea made no reply.

He began keeping a calendar, cutting marks into the wall with his knife. Each dawn he stood before the marks and counted.

Nine days.

Ten.

Eleven.

The regularity pleased him. Time, at least, still submitted.

He established a schedule. Wake at six. Exercise. Bathe. Inspect the perimeter. Breakfast. Political reflection. Maintenance. Noon meal. Weapons drill. Rest. Evening patrol. Supper. Sleep.

Political reflection occupied two hours.

At first he composed speeches for his return.

“My people, betrayed but unconquered—”

No. Too defensive.

“Soldiers of the Inner Worlds, the hour of vengeance—”

No. Premature.

“Citizens, you have suffered beneath the alien yoke—”

Better.

He paced between the shack and the sea, one hand tucked into his cloak, giving shape to the future. The occupation would produce famine. The Guard would grow arrogant. The lesser races would turn upon one another, as lesser races always did. Then Captain Vorst would come with the loyal colonies. Varon would return not as a fugitive but as a savior.

He could see the avenue to the Black Palace, lined with kneeling millions.

The palace itself no longer existed. The last orbital bombardment had broken its towers and melted the Square of Destiny into a lake of glass.

He revised the vision.

A greater palace would be built.

During maintenance he repaired the condenser, patched the roof and cleared vegetation from the shack. During weapons drill he fired at driftwood effigies.

The needle gun made a dry whisper.

Tchk.

A sliver of metal crossed the beach too quickly to be seen. Driftwood jumped. Varon imagined a traitor’s face.

Tchk.

Admiral Lorne, who had surrendered the Lunar Fleet.

Tchk.

Minister Cael, who had fled with the treasury.

Tchk.

The crowds who had torn down his statues.

Tchk. Tchk. Tchk.

At dusk he collected the needles when he could find them. Ammunition was limited. Discipline must be maintained.

On the fifteenth day he found a line of the brown insects crossing the floor.

Kifs. That was what the Venus surveys had called them.

The line entered through a crack beneath the eastern wall, crossed seven feet of open floor and disappeared into a split in the water condenser. Each kif carried a white fragment in its jaws. They moved with perfect spacing. No kif turned aside. No kif hesitated.

Varon watched for several minutes.

Then he poured boiling water into the crack.

The line convulsed. Steam rose. Kifs twisted on their backs, legs clawing at the air, but others continued across their bodies. The line bent around the dead and re-formed.

Varon frowned.

He fetched the fuel can.

* * *

He burned the nest beneath the shack.

Smoke poured from cracks in the sand, and thousands of kifs boiled up from below. They came in shining brown streams. Workers carried pale eggs from one hole to another. Soldiers, larger and black-jawed, advanced toward Varon’s boots.

He burned them too.

Their bodies made tiny popping sounds.

The smoke was oily and bitter. It clung to his clothes, his skin, his hair. When the fuel was gone, the sand around the shack had been baked black.

The next morning, a new line crossed his table.

He killed every kif with the edge of a tin plate. Then he sealed the wall cracks with resin.

At noon, he found them inside the food cabinet.

That evening, they came through the roof.

“Inferior organisms,” he told them.

They did not listen.

He studied the survey manual from his emergency kit. The section on Venusian fauna described the kif as a seasonal colonial scavenger.

During the equatorial dry period, the manual said, separate kif colonies combined into migratory aggregations. These aggregations might contain billions of individuals and travel up to sixty kilometers in search of feeding grounds. Small animals, crops and unprotected stores were at risk.

Billions.

Varon read the passage twice.

He imagined the colonies combining—not through negotiation, not through weakness or compromise, but in complete obedience to instinct. Each individual vanished into the purpose of the whole. No doubts. No elections. No opposition. No mercy.

Theirs the total state.

He shut the manual.

They were vermin, of course.

Still, there was something admirable in the system.

* * *

The days became hot.

Clouds retreated from the coast, leaving a blue sky that seemed too tall and empty. The violet sea drew back, exposing reefs like black bones. Leaves curled at their edges. Animals gathered around shrinking pools in the jungle, where they screamed and fought beneath the heavy sun.

The drums grew louder.

Boom. Boom.

Boom.

Varon sometimes heard them at noon now. When he entered the jungle, they stopped. When he returned to the shack, they began again.

He searched for the drummers.

He found tracks in the mud: three-toed footprints, roughly humanoid, but broad and bare. Venusian natives lived in the interior. The old survey parties had described them as blue-skinned, tribal, peaceful and intellectually undeveloped.

Varon was not concerned.

If discovered, he would dominate them. Primitive races recognized authority instinctively. A straight back, a hard voice, the willingness to punish—these were a language older than words.

Nevertheless, he erased his own tracks.

On the twenty-eighth day, red birds appeared in the trees around the shack.

They watched him with round yellow eyes.

There were six at first, then twelve, then perhaps fifty. They hopped along the branches and repeated a two-note cry.

Lost! Lost!

Varon threw stones.

The birds fluttered away, only to settle farther down the beach.

Lost! Lost! All lost!

“They are birds,” he said.

Lost!

“They are producing meaningless sounds.”

All is lost!

He fired the needle gun. A bird fell from the branch, red wings beating against white sand.

The others rose shrieking into the sky.

Red wings. Red cries. Red noises whirling above him.

Varon ran beneath them, firing until the needle gun clicked empty.

When silence returned, five birds lay dead on the beach.

He had used an entire cartridge.

That night he dreamed of the final council.

The marshals sat around the long basalt table beneath the Black Palace. Their uniforms were immaculate, but their faces had been burned away. In place of eyes and mouths, each had only a smooth expanse of meat.

Varon struck the table.

“Earth will not surrender!”

The faceless marshals nodded.

“Earth will fight in every street!”

They nodded.

“Every child will carry a charge! Every old man will poison a well! There are no civilians in a war for survival!”

They nodded.

Far above, through six hundred feet of armor and stone, the orbital bombs began to fall.

The table jumped.

Dust descended.

One marshal lifted a hand and pulled away his featureless face as though it were a mask. Underneath was the head of a brown insect.

“Number One,” it said.

The others removed their faces.

“Number One. Number One. Number One.”

Their jaws clicked.

Varon woke outside the shack with the unloaded gun in his hand.

The drums were beating.

Boom. Boom. Boom.

His heart answered.

* * *

Captain Vorst did not return on the sixtieth day.

Varon stood on the beach from dawn until the first stars appeared.

At intervals he checked the marks on the wall.

Sixty. There could be no mistake.

He had watched the sky all the previous day as well, because a commander should allow for initiative. He remained the following morning, because navigational difficulties might have caused delay.

On the sixty-third day he stopped watching.

“The outer colonies required more time,” he said.

He opened the packet beneath the cot.

The black uniform came out without a wrinkle. Silver lightning flashed on the collar. Medals covered the breast, each a small memory of triumph: the Martian Pacification, the Pallas Campaign, the Siege of Triton, the Cleansing of Europa. Above them hung the gold solar disk of Number One.

He put on the trousers and jacket. They were looser than before.

He polished his boots. He trimmed his hair with knife and mirror. He stood at attention before the dead radar screen.

“Citizens,” he said.

His reflected face hovered in the black glass.

“Citizens, reports of defeat have been exaggerated by enemies of the state. The war enters a new and more glorious phase.”

The reflection’s cheeks were hollow. Salt had bleached the hair at the temples. A sore reddened the lower lip.

“Our losses were intentional. Strategic contractions. Necessary sacrifices in the design of ultimate victory.”

The eyes were correct. The eyes still possessed force.

“The true state is not territory. It is not buildings, fleets or planets. The true state resides in the will of its leader.”

He raised his right arm.

The reflection did the same.

“One Leader. One Race. One Destiny.”

Outside, the red birds cried.

Lost! Lost!

Varon went out and shot two of them.

After that he wore the uniform every day.

* * *

The kifs multiplied.

They invaded the condenser, the cot, the food tins. They fell from his clothing when he undressed. He woke with them exploring his beard and once found a soldier kif biting the soft flesh between two toes.

He developed methods.

A moat of oil around the shack stopped them for a day. Ash stopped them for an hour. Poison powder killed thousands, until rain dissolved it. He dug out nests, crushed queens, burned eggs in pits.

Each victory revealed another column approaching.

He began making maps.

The eastern colony had three principal entrances. The northern colony had at least six. A larger nest occupied the dunes beyond the shack, while scouts from the jungle suggested reserves of enormous strength.

Colored lines spread across the radar wall.

Varon planned campaigns. He assigned names to operations.

IRON HEEL.

BROWN DEATH.

FINAL PURITY.

He spoke aloud while he worked, explaining his strategy to absent generals.

“The error of the eastern offensive was insufficient encirclement. You see, Marshal? Extermination must be complete. A surviving worker becomes a future colony.”

The empty chair listened.

“Compassion is treason.”

The chair agreed.

Sometimes Marshal Venn occupied it, though Venn had died above Mercury two years before. Sometimes Captain Vorst sat there, pale and evasive, trying to explain why sixty days had become seventy, and seventy had become eighty-five.

Varon interrogated him.

“Were you intercepted?”

Silence.

“Did the Guard seize the ship?”

Silence.

“Or did you betray me?”

At this Captain Vorst usually vanished.

Varon came to believe this an admission.

His thoughts sharpened. He slept less. The weakness and uncertainty of the first days receded, and he felt himself improving. The fact that he identified this improvement by the increased number of conspiracies he perceived did not trouble him.

The trees were signaling to one another.

The baroons mocked his speeches.

The sea erased his footprints to assist pursuers.

At night, ships crossed the sky. He saw their engine flares between the stars and heard rockets muttering above the clouds.

He prepared ambushes around the shack. He slept in the jungle. He returned unexpectedly to catch the conspirators unprepared.

Always there were kifs.

Kifs on the walls.

Kifs in the sand.

Kifs carrying away the bodies of their dead.

They never abandoned their own. Even corpses served the colony, eaten and transformed into strength. It was perfect economy. Perfect loyalty.

Varon crouched beside a column one evening and watched them pass.

“You understand,” he whispered. “Don’t you?”

The kifs flowed around a pebble.

“The individual is nothing.”

Brown bodies. Black jaws.

“The whole is everything.”

One kif climbed onto his boot.

He let it reach his knee before crushing it between finger and thumb.

“I made you,” he said.

Then he laughed, because that was absurd.

For a few moments he could not stop laughing.

* * *

The migration began with silence.

One dawn the marigees did not scream. The baroons did not gibber. The red birds were gone. The jungle held its breath beneath a yellow sky.

Varon noticed the absence while shaving.

He stepped outside.

The surf moved heavily, but from inland came no cry, no wingbeat, no cracking branch. He felt watched by the entire green wall.

Then a leaf stirred.

Another.

A dry whisper passed through the undergrowth, so faint that he mistook it for wind.

The air was still.

Varon fetched his gun.

The whisper thickened.

It was the sound of paper rubbed against paper, of rain falling far away, of innumerable small feet moving over dead leaves.

The kifs emerged at noon.

First came scattered scouts, then thin columns threading between the roots. The columns joined. Brown streams poured from the jungle and spread across the sand.

Varon climbed onto the shack roof.

From there he saw the earth moving.

A dark band filled the spaces between the trees. It stretched north and south beyond sight, advancing toward the coast in a ragged crescent. Kifs climbed over stones, over fallen timber, over one another. Small reptiles fled before them. A crippled marigee flapped along the ground until the brown line reached it.

The bird screamed.

The mass covered its red feathers.

The scream rose, broke, ceased.

Drums began somewhere beyond the swarm.

Boom.

Boom.

The migration manual returned to him.

Billions of individuals.

Varon lifted the needle gun, then lowered it.

The weapon was ridiculous.

He had poisoned nests. He had burned colonies. He had imagined that he was conducting a war.

This was the enemy’s main army.

“The vanguard,” he whispered.

A detached part of his mind appreciated the coordination. No marshal ordered them. No radio transmitted commands. Yet the entire front moved as one organism, widening around obstacles, closing gaps, consuming weakness.

His ideal perfected in miniature.

His ideal without banners, speeches, monuments or Number One.

Hatred entered him then—pure, hot and almost joyful.

“No,” he said.

The swarm advanced.

“There must be a leader.”

The swarm advanced.

“Where is your leader?”

No answer. Only the dry, growing rustle.

A state without a leader was an obscenity. It contradicted every law of destiny. The lesser body must submit to the greater, and at the summit of the structure there must be one will, one voice, one man.

Without Number One, order became mockery.

Varon poured the remaining fuel around the shack and set it alight.

Flame raced in a crooked circle. Kifs entering it curled and blackened. Thousands behind them diverted, forming two streams that passed around the fire.

He fired into the streams.

Tchk. Tchk. Tchk.

Each needle killed perhaps two.

The jungle floor was brown now. The sand was brown. The earth itself crawled.

Varon fired until the cartridge emptied. He changed it. His hands trembled.

“All forces hold position!”

Kifs reached the base of the shack.

“Hold position!”

They climbed the walls.

He stamped on them. They came over the roof edge, seething around his boots.

His boots were brown with kifs.

One bit through his trousers.

Then another.

Then ten.

Their jaws entered his flesh like hot wire.

Varon screamed and jumped from the roof.

He struck the sand, rolled, surged upright. Kifs clung to his uniform. He slapped at them, tore them away, crushed them in his hands. More poured across the beach.

He ran toward the sea, but the brown front had already cut him off. It thickened around his ankles.

He turned inland.

That way lay the greater mass.

Another bite stabbed his calf. Another fastened onto his wrist.

He ran north along the narrow strip between surf and jungle, stumbling, kicking, shrieking commands. The swarm flowed beside him. It seemed not to pursue him, and that was worse. He was irrelevant to it. It would have swallowed him without anger and continued on.

“Retreat in order!” he gasped.

There was no order.

He tore off his jacket. Medals scattered across the sand. Kifs writhed in the lining. He threw it behind him.

He ran in trousers and undershirt, carrying the gun.

White sand. Blue sky. Violet water. Brown death.

Falling, rising, falling again.

A root caught his foot. His face struck earth. Kifs closed over his hands before he could rise.

He screamed.

He had heard men scream in interrogation cells and dismissed the sound as weakness. He had heard cities scream through open military channels. He had heard his own soldiers call his name as their ships burned.

None had screamed as loudly as Number One screamed then.

He lurched up and plunged into the jungle.

Branches whipped his face. Mud swallowed his boots. The dry rustling followed on every side, accompanied now by drums.

Boom. Boom. Boom.

His heartbeat.

Boom. Boom.

The guns above Earth.

Boom.

He did not know how long he ran. Fever entered the bites. Green light became red, then black, then green again. Once he saw the Black Palace between the trees and rushed toward it, only to collide with a mossy trunk. Once his mother called to him from a pool of orange water. Once Captain Vorst walked beside him, politely asking whether the strategic contraction was proceeding well.

At last the jungle opened.

Beyond a low fence stood round huts roofed with silver leaves. Blue-skinned figures turned toward him. Children cried out. A drum stopped beneath the hand of an old man.

Varon raised the needle gun.

“I claim this territory,” he said.

Then the village tipped sideways.

The last thing he saw was a woman’s blue face above him, her eyes wide not with submission but concern.

* * *

He woke under white cloth.

Cool paste covered his legs and arms. The air smelled of crushed leaves, smoke and flowers.

A blue-skinned girl leaned over him with a wooden cup.

“Drink,” she said.

Varon stared.

Her face was almost human, though the eyes were golden and the nostrils lay flat against the cheeks. Black hair hung in three braids. She wore a simple green garment and a necklace of polished shells.

“Where am I?”

“With the People of the Hollow Trees.”

He tried to rise. Pain drove him back.

“You have sickness from the kif mouths,” she said. “You must be still.”

“Where is my weapon?”

The girl pointed to a shelf. The needle gun lay there beside his boots.

Fools.

“How many days?”

“Three sleeps.”

“Who commands here?”

She considered the word.

“Old Taval speaks when speaking is needed.”

“Bring him.”

“You must drink.”

“Bring him!”

The girl smiled as if he were a difficult child. She held the cup to his lips.

Varon drank because he was thirsty.

Old Taval came in the evening.

He was tall and narrow, his blue skin faded almost gray. White cords were woven into his hair. He carried no weapon, only a carved staff. Two children followed him into the hut and sat beside the door.

“You crossed the marching path,” Taval said. “This is unwise.”

“I require transportation.”

“We have river boats.”

“Space transportation.”

Taval’s golden eyes brightened.

“Ah. You are a sky person. The teacher from Earth was a sky person.”

“What teacher?”

“He came when I was small. He stayed seven wet seasons. He spoke of the sky worlds and the Maker of All. He taught us signs for words.” Taval touched the shell necklace at his throat. “He said kindness is the law above laws.”

“A missionary.”

“Yes. Mish-nary.”

Varon looked around the hut. Its walls were woven reeds. Bundles of herbs hung from the rafters. The technology was primitive beyond contempt.

Yet they possessed boats.

“Your village will provide food, carriers and a guide to the nearest settlement.”

“If you are well enough to travel.”

“I will decide when I am well enough.”

Taval inclined his head.

“You are troubled in the mind from kif poison.”

“I am Chancellor Arvek Varon, First Protector of Humanity, Supreme Commander of the Solar Directorate and hereditary guardian of the Inner Worlds.”

The old man waited.

“Do you understand?”

“I understand the sounds.”

“You will address me as Number One.”

“Is that your name?”

“It is my position.”

Taval nodded slowly.

“Then rest, Position Number One.”

The children laughed.

Varon’s hand clenched beneath the cloth.

Taval silenced them with a glance, but the insult had entered the room and could not be removed.

They thought him powerless.

They had carried him, washed him, fed him, treated his wounds. In their simplicity, they mistook kindness for equality. They did not yet understand that service was submission and submission, once offered, could not be withdrawn.

Varon smiled.

“The girl may continue attending me.”

“Her name is Sela.”

“She may continue.”

Old Taval studied his face for a long moment.

Then he left.

* * *

For twelve days the villagers nursed him.

They brought broth, fruit and cool water. They changed the paste on his wounds. Children gathered outside the hut to hear him speak of other worlds.

Varon told them of Earth’s towers, the canals of Mars, the domes beneath Europa’s ice. He did not tell them of the towers falling, the canals clogged with bodies, or the domes opened to vacuum on his orders.

He told them he had ruled the stars.

“Why?” Sela asked.

“Because I alone possessed the will.”

“Did the people ask you?”

“People do not know what to ask. That is why they require a leader.”

“Old Taval says a speaker must listen before he speaks.”

“Old Taval is a savage.”

Sela frowned.

“No. He is old.”

She returned to grinding medicine.

Varon studied the village through the doorway. There were perhaps eighty inhabitants. They shared food from common ovens. They worked without visible overseers. Disputes were taken to a circle beneath a broad yellow tree, where everyone spoke and no one saluted.

Disorder masquerading as peace.

There were no prisons.

There were no soldiers.

Their taboos forbade killing people, even enemies. They ate fish and certain jungle animals, but they would not kill a speaking being. The missionary had strengthened this custom with tales of universal love.

Weakness elevated into law.

Varon’s strength returned. He walked first with a stick, then alone. The villagers greeted him cheerfully. They gave him fruit. They asked after his pain. Their open faces disgusted him.

At night the distant drums continued.

The kif migration had turned west, Taval explained. It would pass the village at the next moon, following an ancient trail between two ridges. The villagers had already cleared their gardens from the route and raised bridges over the expected columns.

“They come every dry season,” said Sela. “We move aside.”

“You permit them to occupy your land?”

“They do not know it is ours.”

“You should destroy them.”

“Why?”

“They threaten you.”

“We move aside.”

Varon looked at her with pity.

Her people had not grasped the first principle of history: that anything not mastered would eventually become master.

On the thirteenth morning he returned to the beach.

He said he wished to find his supplies. Taval sent a young hunter to guide him, but Varon ordered the man to wait at the edge of the jungle.

The radar shack was a black shell. Kifs had stripped everything edible. The sand was scattered with medals.

Varon found his jacket half buried near the water. One sleeve had burned, but the solar disk remained. He brushed away the sand and carried it into the shack.

The sealed valise contained a second needle gun cartridge, spare insignia and his dress sash.

He dressed before the dead radar screen.

The uniform was scorched. His face was thin. Purple scars marked his arms. Yet when he fixed the gold disk over his heart, the transformation was complete.

Number One stood again.

He loaded the weapon.

From beneath the console came a solitary kif. It paused between his boots, antennae moving.

Varon stared down at it.

“You failed,” he said.

He crushed it.

* * *

The village gathered when he returned.

His black uniform drew them from huts and gardens. Children pointed at the medals. Women stopped weaving. Old Taval rose from beneath the yellow tree.

Varon marched into the central clearing.

The hunter followed at a distance, troubled by the weapon in his hand.

Sela came from the healing hut.

“You found your clothes.”

“These are not clothes.”

Varon climbed onto the flat stone used during village debates. He looked down upon the blue faces.

The old force filled him. For the first time since the cruiser had vanished, there was an audience before him. Human or alien, civilized or savage, an audience was the raw material of power.

“People of Venus,” he began, “you have been selected for a great honor.”

They listened politely.

“You live without law, without destiny, without order. You hide from vermin. You permit age and weakness to speak with the same voice as strength. This ends today.”

Sela looked toward Taval.

“By right of conquest and by the eternal supremacy of the Solar Directorate, I assume command of this territory and all who live upon it.”

A child laughed.

Varon drew the needle gun and fired into the air.

Tchk.

A leaf jumped from the yellow tree.

The child’s mother pulled him close.

“That is the first law,” Varon said. “When Number One speaks, there will be silence.”

Taval approached the stone.

“Friend Arvek, you are still sick.”

“I am not your friend.”

“You have fever in the black part of the mind.”

“You will kneel.”

“We kneel to plant and to drink. We do not kneel to people.”

Varon pointed the gun at his chest.

“I said kneel.”

Taval looked at the weapon. Then he looked at Varon.

“No.”

The answer was soft.

It struck harder than a shout.

Varon saw other faces behind Taval—the ministers in the bunker, Admiral Lorne on the screen, the crowds surging through the Square of Destiny. No. That one word had destroyed fleets. That one word had opened the gates to chaos. That one word was the seed of treason, and treason, if not crushed at once, multiplied like kifs.

“Last warning.”

“You must put down the sky weapon.”

Varon fired.

The needle entered Taval below the throat. The old man made a small surprised sound. His staff fell. He remained standing for an instant, hands open, then folded onto the earth.

No one moved.

Varon raised his arm in salute.

“This is the fate of all traitors.”

Sela ran to Taval.

A young man rushed from the crowd. Varon shot him in the face. Another came from the left. Tchk. He fell. A woman screamed and threw a basket. Varon fired at the scream.

Then the village moved.

Not as a mob. Not as soldiers. As people saving one another.

A woven mat struck Varon’s arm. The next needle went into the ground. Two men seized him from behind. He drove an elbow into one blue face and kicked another in the knee. Sela clung to his gun wrist.

“Release me!” he roared.

Hands closed everywhere.

He pulled the trigger again. Someone cried out.

A stone struck his temple.

The sky flashed white.

He fell beneath them.

Even then they did not kill him. They tore away the weapon. They bound his wrists. They carried him into the storage hut while he shouted titles, laws and death sentences.

The door closed.

Darkness returned.

Alone.

Again alone.

* * *

Old Taval died before sunset.

So did the young man shot in the face. The woman survived until midnight. A fourth villager, wounded in the belly, died at dawn.

The People of the Hollow Trees sat beneath the yellow tree and debated what must be done.

Varon heard their voices through the storage-hut wall.

“He killed Taval.”

“He killed Roon and Mother Yala.”

“He will kill again.”

“We cannot kill a speaking being.”

“He is not a being. He is an evil god.”

“The missionary said there are no evil gods.”

“Then the missionary did not meet him.”

“We may carry him to the river and send him away.”

“He will return.”

“Break his hands so he cannot use the sky weapon.”

“To break is harm.”

“Bind him forever.”

“Who will feed him?”

“He must be sick.”

“Sickness can be pitied and still be dangerous.”

Their voices rose and fell.

Varon smiled in the darkness.

They lacked the will to execute him. Their taboo protected him. In time their grief would cool. He would divide them, reward some, frighten others. Four deaths had already created factions. In a month he would rule the village.

In a year he would rule the valley.

Then Captain Vorst would return.

Or perhaps Vorst was dead. No matter. The Venusians could be organized. There would be levies, mines, workshops. Primitive materials at first, then industry. A transmitter. A ship.

The Directorate would rise from the jungle.

Number One could begin with eighty subjects. Once he had begun with fewer.

Outside, the argument quieted.

Sela spoke.

Her voice was low, and Varon moved closer to the wall.

“Taval taught that the hand must not make death.”

“Yes,” someone said.

“The hand must not cut, strike, poison or drown.”

“Yes.”

“If a baroon enters the red-vine swamp, the swamp kills it. We do not.”

Silence followed.

Then the hunter said, “The kifs come tomorrow.”

There was more silence.

Varon stopped smiling.

Sela continued.

“The marching path passes the old lightning tree.”

Someone began to weep.

“We would bind him only,” she said. “Binding does not kill. The kifs choose where they walk. Their mouths choose what they bite.”

“The missionary’s god will know.”

“The missionary’s god knows what this man has done.”

A long debate followed, quieter than before.

Varon kicked the door.

“You will release me!”

The voices stopped.

“I am protected by interplanetary law! The Galactic Guard will burn this village! Release me and I may show mercy!”

No one answered.

He strained against the cords around his wrists.

“You ignorant animals!”

Still nothing.

Then footsteps approached.

The door opened.

* * *

They came at dawn.

Six men and four women entered the hut. Their faces were marked with white mourning clay. Sela carried a strip of leather and a wooden bowl.

Varon stood with his back to the wall.

“You have reached a wise decision,” he said. “Untie me. Those principally responsible for the rebellion will be punished, but the remainder—”

The men seized him.

He fought. He kicked one in the stomach and bit another’s hand, but his wrists were already bound, and they had learned caution. A loop went around his ankles. Another tightened above his knees.

Sela approached with the leather strip.

Varon jerked his head away.

“What is that?”

“For your mouth.”

“I order you—”

They forced the leather between his teeth and tied it behind his neck.

His words became animal sounds.

They carried him through the village.

No children watched. The dead lay beneath woven covers near the yellow tree. Taval’s carved staff rested across his body.

Varon twisted toward him.

For a moment, the old blue face became Marshal Venn’s, then Captain Vorst’s, then his own father’s. Each dead man opened his eyes.

All is lost, they said.

Varon tried to answer through the gag.

The villagers carried him into the jungle.

Morning light slanted through silver leaves. Moisture glittered on red flowers. A pair of marigees watched silently from a branch.

The path climbed between low ridges. Signs of the coming migration appeared everywhere. Small animals moved west. Kif scouts crossed roots and stones. Bridges of woven reeds had been laid over narrow sections of trail, allowing villagers to pass above the swarm.

The drums began.

Not village drums.

His heart.

Boom.

Boom.

Varon struggled harder.

The carriers said nothing.

They reached a dead tree on a patch of open ground. Lightning had split its trunk years ago, leaving a black spear of wood above the main trail. The earth around it was bare and hard, marked by countless tiny grooves.

The kif road.

Varon bucked against the hands holding him. A wild sound pushed past the leather.

Sela looked into his eyes.

“You understand now.”

He shook his head violently.

“We cannot kill you,” she said. “Killing is forbidden.”

He tried to speak. He tried to promise wealth, rank, planets. He tried to explain that he was Number One, that armies had moved at his gesture, that this was impossible.

Only muffled grunts emerged.

They stood him against the tree.

Ropes passed around his chest, waist, thighs and ankles. The villagers worked carefully, avoiding his wounds. They tied his arms behind the trunk and knotted the cords beyond reach of his fingers.

The hunter tested each knot.

Varon glared at him with such hatred that the man stepped back.

Sela did not.

“Taval said kindness is the law above laws,” she said. “We were kind to you. You thought kindness meant we belonged to you.”

Varon’s eyes burned.

“I do not know if this is right,” she continued. “I know only that you must not return.”

She placed the wooden bowl upside down over his head to shield him from the sun, then changed her mind and removed it.

“No,” she whispered. “You should see.”

They left.

Sela was the last to go. At the edge of the clearing, she looked back once.

Then green leaves closed behind her.

Varon was alone.

* * *

At first he raged.

He pulled until the cords cut his wrists. He hammered the back of his head against the dead trunk. He bent his knees, twisted his hips and tried to slide downward.

The ropes held.

He screamed into the gag until saliva soaked the leather.

Birds watched.

The sun climbed.

His rage consumed itself and left terror.

He listened.

The jungle had fallen quiet again. No baroons. No marigees. No wings among the branches.

From very far away came a dry whisper.

Varon shut his eyes.

It was rain.

There were no clouds.

It was wind.

The leaves did not move.

It was static from the radar shack. Rockets above Venus. Applause from the Square of Destiny. Millions saluting beneath black banners.

The whisper grew.

He opened his eyes.

A single kif crossed the path.

Then another.

Scouts.

One paused near his boot, antennae testing the air. It turned and vanished beneath a root.

Varon began pulling at the ropes again.

Pain cleared his mind.

For months madness had softened the world, changing memories into visions and sounds into conspiracies. Now the world became precise. He saw every fiber of cord. Every crack in the black trunk. Every grain of yellow dust on his boots.

He knew Captain Vorst had never intended to return.

Perhaps the cruiser had been intercepted. Perhaps the crew had mutinied. Perhaps Vorst had simply obeyed the last useful order and then painted away the ship’s old name forever.

He knew the Directorate was dead.

Earth had fallen. The Black Palace was glass. His fleets were metal vapor and drifting tombs. The crowds no longer saluted. Children born tomorrow would learn his name as a curse.

All was lost.

The words possessed no drama now. They were merely true.

The rustling became louder.

A brown line appeared at the far end of the trail. It came around a bend in the roots, two feet wide, moving steadily. Behind it came another line, and another. They merged at the edge of the clearing.

Varon stared.

The vanguard.

The kifs crossed the open earth in perfect order. Workers in the center. Soldiers along the edges. No leader among them. No Number One.

Yet they marched.

His breath sobbed through his nose.

He remembered his speeches about the individual vanishing into the state. He remembered cities sacrificed because maps required straight borders. He remembered regiments ordered to hold ground after ammunition was gone. He remembered telling mothers that sons were seeds from which empire grew.

The kif column widened.

Theirs the complete submergence of the individual to the whole.

He had admired them.

A sound like surf filled the jungle. Not thousands now. Millions. Billions. Brown bodies poured over the ridges, across fallen logs, beneath leaves. The ground darkened.

One army remained in the solar system, and it had come for him.

Varon’s eyes widened.

He began to laugh.

The gag turned it into a wet, choking noise, but still he laughed. His body shook against the cords. Tears ran down his cheeks.

How small the soldiers were.

How perfect.

How loyal.

The first kif reached his boot.

It climbed the polished leather, pausing at the gold trim. A second followed. Then ten. Then a hundred.

Varon laughed harder.

The sound of the marching billions rose around him, falling, rising, falling again—a rustle, a whisper, a roar. It became the applause of his vanished crowds, the crackle of burning cities, the murmur of dead generals in their graves.

Number One.

Number One.

Number One.

His boots turned brown.

The army climbed.

## The Natural Increase Department

When Mr. Horace Morehouse entered the Westcote office of the Continental Rapid Express Company, he carried a small wooden crate under one arm and the calm expression of a man who expected to be obeyed.

From the crate came two faint whistles.

Mike Flannery, the agent, stood behind the counter adding a column of figures. He added it twice, because the first result seemed too large, and obtained two different totals, both equally discouraging. He therefore laid down his pencil with relief when Morehouse approached.

“Two guinea pigs,” said Morehouse. “They arrived this morning. I am here to pay the charge.”

“Sixty cints,” said Flannery.

“Fifty.”

“Sixty.”

Morehouse placed his gloves upon the counter. He did this carefully, as a gentleman does when preparing to become unreasonable.

“The domestic-pet rate,” he said, “is twenty-five cents per animal. Two animals, therefore, cost fifty cents.”

“Pigs,” said Flannery, tapping the rate book. “Thirty cints aich. Two pigs, sixty cints.”

“They are guinea pigs.”

“I heard ye.”

“They are not pigs.”

Flannery looked at the crate. One of the animals thrust its nose between the slats, considered him without enthusiasm, and retired.

“Thin what are they?” he asked.

“They are guinea pigs.”

“Exactly. Pigs is pigs.”

Mr. Morehouse drew a long breath. “Do you charge a butterfly at the dairy rate because it contains butter?”

“Niver had wan shipped.”

“Would you classify a horse chestnut as a horse?”

“Is ut alive?”

“No!”

“Thin ut would go merchandise.”

Morehouse stared at him. It is one of the inconveniences of sarcasm that it occasionally meets a man against whom it is useless.

“These creatures,” he said, leaning across the counter, “are small South American rodents. They are household pets. They do not become livestock merely because some ignorant person centuries ago gave them a ridiculous name.”

“Maybe so,” said Flannery. “But I’m not paid to correct cinturies. I’m paid to collect rates. The book says pigs, thirty cints. Ye say these is pigs. So ’tis sixty.”

“I say they are called pigs.”

“An’ the company don’t charge for what a thing is called by mistake. It charges for what’s written on the box.”

There was enough confusion in this statement to satisfy Flannery completely.

Mr. Morehouse took up his gloves.

“I refuse to pay.”

“Thin I cannot deliver thim.”

“You will hear from me.”

“I’ll be here.”

Morehouse departed, leaving the crate on the counter and the office door trembling behind him.

Flannery looked down at the two animals.

“Ye’ve cost the company tin cints already,” he told them, “an’ that’s before lunch.”

That evening Mr. Morehouse wrote to the president of the Continental Rapid Express Company. He wrote seven pages, although the essential portion of his complaint could have been placed upon a postage stamp. In business correspondence, however, indignation expands naturally to fill the paper supplied.

He described Flannery as ignorant, obstinate, insolent, unfit for public contact, and probably unable to distinguish an aardvark from an umbrella. He explained the zoological position of the guinea pig, the geographical position of Guinea, and the moral position of a corporation that attempted to extort ten cents from an honest citizen.

The letter reached the president’s office on Tuesday. The president’s secretary read the first paragraph, underlined “rate dispute,” and forwarded it to the Assistant General Manager. The Assistant General Manager’s clerk stamped it, numbered it C.R.E. 41827-P, and sent it to the General Rate Department. The General Rate Department sent it to the Domestic Animals Classification Bureau. The Domestic Animals Classification Bureau observed that the question involved livestock and referred it to the Agricultural Tariffs Division.

All this was done with the care that characterizes a corporation when it is systematically conducting a matter away from everyone authorized to decide it.

Meanwhile, Flannery borrowed a cage from a poultry dealer and purchased lettuce.

At the end of the first week he wrote:

> Westcote Office  
> To General Superintendent  
> Sir: The two guinea pigs refused by H. Morehouse is still here and wan of thim has become foive. This is not clear to me unless three was concealed in transit, which I do not accuse anny wan of but report same. They ate sixteen cints’ worth of cabbage. Please advise disposition.  
> M. Flannery, Agent.

The superintendent’s office replied:

> We are in receipt of your communication concerning shipment C.R.E. 41827-P. Pending determination of classification, you are instructed to retain shipment in good condition. Under no circumstances dispose of company property or property held in dispute.

Flannery read the letter twice.

“Company property, is ut?” he said. “Thin the company has a family.”

By the following Monday the family numbered eleven.

Flannery placed them in two cages. By Thursday he needed three cages and a box that had formerly contained soap. He paid a boy named Timmy Groll ten cents a day to gather grass from the vacant lot behind the office. Timmy’s mother, learning that he was employed by an express company, began telling the neighbors that her son had entered transportation.

Mr. Morehouse called once to ask whether the company had surrendered.

“Not yit,” said Flannery.

“And the animals?”

“Healthy.”

“How many?”

“Twinty-three at breakfast. I’ve not counted since.”

Morehouse’s face changed.

“Twenty-three!”

“Do ye want to pay the sixty cints now?”

“I will pay fifty.”

“Thin they remain.”

“Let them remain,” said Morehouse. “The principle is worth more than the inconvenience.”

A principle is generally worth more than an inconvenience when the inconvenience belongs to someone else.

At home that night, Morehouse mentioned the increase. His wife asked why he did not simply pay the extra dime.

“Because,” said Morehouse, “there are moments when a man must resist tyranny.”

His wife looked at the new hat she had purchased that afternoon and said nothing. His twelve-year-old son Edwin, however, avoided his father’s eye. Edwin had once owned two guinea pigs and knew something of their habits. He felt that he ought to speak, but guilt commonly makes a boy silent where innocence would make him noisy.

At the office, Flannery wrote again:

> Sir: In answer to yours I am retaining them in good condition, better than meself. They now number forty-eight, not counting wan that keeps moving during the count. Food to date $2.14 and cages $1.80. I request authority to deliver the lot for fifty cints or sell same or drown same humanely, though drowning so manny would require municipal cooperation.  
> M. Flannery.

His letter went to the superintendent, who sent it to Claims because expenses were mentioned. Claims returned it because no loss had occurred. The superintendent then sent it to Legal, since drowning had been proposed. Legal advised that no property should be destroyed while title remained uncertain. The question of title was referred to the Ownership and Custody Section.

The Ownership and Custody Section found that Morehouse had not accepted delivery, but had not legally abandoned the shipment. Therefore the guinea pigs belonged neither wholly to Morehouse nor wholly to the company. They occupied a useful middle ground in which both parties had obligations and neither had authority.

The Agricultural Tariffs Division, considering the original question, prepared a memorandum:

> The term “pig,” as employed in current livestock schedules, ordinarily denotes immature swine. However, where a qualifying geographical or descriptive term precedes the word, ambiguity may exist. Examples: guinea pig, sea pig, ground pig, and pig iron. The last is presumed not to be alive.

This memorandum was sent to the Metal Products Bureau for confirmation regarding pig iron.

The Metal Products Bureau confirmed that pig iron was not alive and added that it was charged by weight.

Three weeks later Flannery had one hundred and sixty-four guinea pigs.

They occupied the rear storeroom, the space beneath the counter, two chicken coops, eight orange crates, and a cupboard that had once held waybills. Their voices blended into a continuous shrill conversation. Every time a customer entered, the entire colony whistled in expectation of food.

A lady shipping a vase to her sister heard the noise and asked what it was.

“Official correspondence,” said Flannery.

The guinea pigs consumed lettuce, cabbage, hay, carrots, turnip tops, stale bread, and part of a broom. Flannery discovered that they disliked onions, respected nothing made of cardboard, and could escape from openings apparently narrower than their whiskers. He ceased trying to count them individually and estimated them by the crate.

His next report was a telegram:

> GUINEA PIGS NOW APPROXIMATELY THREE HUNDRED STOP REQUIRE IMMEDIATE RULING STOP THEY HAVE EATEN PACKING STRAW RESERVED FOR GLASSWARE AND ARE LOOKING AT RATE BOOK STOP FLANNERY.

Headquarters replied:

> DO NOT PERMIT DAMAGE TO COMPANY RECORDS STOP CLASSIFICATION UNDER ACTIVE CONSIDERATION STOP.

Flannery moved the rate book to the top shelf.

The classification question had by then reached Dr. Ambrose H. Peabody, consulting zoologist to the company. Dr. Peabody was a careful man and could not give an opinion without inspecting the authorities. He spent twelve days consulting six volumes, writing to a university professor, and preparing a paper entitled “The Taxonomic Status of *Cavia porcellus* in Relation to Commercial Nomenclature.”

His conclusion was definite.

The guinea pig, he stated, was not a pig. It was a rodent of the family Caviidae. It was not native to Guinea. Its relationship to the domestic hog was negligible. Properly speaking, it was neither guinean nor porcine.

The General Rate Department found this conclusion unsettling. If names were not dependable, the rate book might contain other concealed errors. The prairie dog might not be a dog. The catfish might not be a cat. A sea horse might be neither marine freight nor equine livestock. The possibility opened an abyss beneath the entire schedule.

A committee was appointed.

The committee consisted of one representative each from Rates, Legal, Livestock, Domestic Pets, Claims, Auditing, and Public Relations. Its first meeting was postponed because the representative from Livestock attended in the wrong building. At the second meeting, the representative from Domestic Pets asked whether guinea pigs could properly be regarded as pets if the consignee refused to take them home. Legal replied that intention to pet might be sufficient. Auditing requested documentary evidence of such intention.

Mr. Morehouse’s original letter was examined. It contained the phrase “harmless household pets,” which the committee regarded as persuasive but not conclusive.

The matter was tabled pending a definition of “household.”

At Westcote, summer arrived.

Flannery rented the empty stable next door. He hired Timmy full-time after school and added two more boys on Saturdays. The boys carried vegetables in and bedding out. Flannery purchased wire netting by the roll. He learned to construct a serviceable guinea-pig cage in twelve minutes, then in nine, and finally abandoned separate cages in favor of fenced districts.

He named these districts North Guinea, South Guinea, and the Republic of Morehouse.

The numbers rose from five hundred to eight hundred, from eight hundred to thirteen hundred, and from thirteen hundred to a quantity Flannery described as “all the numbers there are, with more behind thim.”

His monthly expense statement reached $187.42.

Auditing returned it because he had entered “vegetables” under “livestock maintenance,” while the company’s account book required vegetables to be divided among “feed,” “packing material,” and “miscellaneous perishables.”

Flannery replied:

> Sir: The cabbage is feed before they eat ut, packing material while they sit on ut, and miscellaneous perishables after. Put ut where ye like but send the money.

Auditing requested a more professional tone.

Flannery sent a revised statement headed “Nutritional Supplies for Temporarily Undetermined Property.” It was paid at once.

Morehouse continued to inquire, though less often. His visits had become cautious because the sound from the stable disturbed him.

“I presume,” he said one afternoon, “that headquarters has at last admitted its mistake.”

“They’re approachin’ the admission from sivin directions,” said Flannery.

“How many animals are there?”

“Would ye loike the number before or after ye ask the question?”

“Before.”

“Two thousand, maybe.”

“And after?”

Flannery listened to the many small whistles behind the wall.

“Two thousand an’ an even dozen.”

Morehouse swallowed. “That is not my responsibility.”

“I’m not sayin’ ut is.”

“I ordered two.”

“An’ two ye’ll receive, if I can identify thim. Describe their faces.”

“Don’t be absurd.”

“I’ve been studyin’ their faces for months, an’ I’m no nearer the truth.”

Morehouse left angrily, but not with his former magnificence. A man may defend a principle against two guinea pigs and appear heroic. Against two thousand, he begins to seem personally involved.

The committee finally defined a household as “premises occupied or intended for occupation by one or more persons in a domestic capacity.” It then ruled, four votes to three, that guinea pigs might qualify as household pets when shipped in quantities consistent with domestic use.

This introduced the question of quantity.

Were two guinea pigs consistent with domestic use? Certainly. Were two thousand? Certainly not. But the shipment had originally consisted of two, while the increase had occurred during company custody. Claims argued that the later animals were incidental consequences of detention. Rates argued that every animal tendered for delivery must be charged. Legal asked whether animals born in transit had technically been shipped at all.

Dr. Peabody was consulted again.

He answered that they had been born, not shipped.

The committee adopted this distinction with gratitude.

At last, nine months after Mr. Morehouse had refused to pay sixty cents, the General Rate Department issued its ruling:

> Re: C.R.E. 41827-P. Guinea pigs are hereby classified as domestic pets for purposes of tariff schedule 14-B. Charges on original shipment of two animals shall be fifty cents. Natural increase occurring while shipment remained in carrier custody shall be delivered without additional charge. Agent is instructed to collect amount due and complete delivery immediately.

The order reached Westcote at ten in the morning.

Flannery read it, removed his coat, rolled up his sleeves, and called the three boys.

“Lads,” he said, “the company has finished thinkin’. Now we must act fast before the pigs do.”

They prepared forty-three crates, twelve cages, six hampers, two ventilated trunks, and a delivery wagon borrowed from the grocer. At noon they had packed seven hundred animals. At one they had packed twelve hundred and discovered that the unpacked number appeared undiminished. At two Flannery hired a second wagon. At three he hired every basket in the fruit market. At four he borrowed the hotel laundry carts.

Guinea pigs were carried with gloved hands, with nets, with bushel baskets, with a grain scoop, and, in one emergency, with the clean coal shovel. They flowed from one enclosure into another like furry water. The boys shouted counts until all counts lost meaning.

By sunset the procession stood ready: two wagons, four carts, the laundry van, and Timmy pushing a wheelbarrow containing a particularly independent group.

Flannery placed the company’s order in his pocket and marched to the Morehouse residence.

The house was empty.

A sign on the gate read:

FOR RENT. APPLY WALCOTT & SONS.

Flannery stared at it. Then he rang the bell, because nine months with the Continental Rapid Express Company had taught him not to accept visible evidence without official confirmation.

No one answered.

A neighbor leaned over the fence.

“If you’re looking for the Morehouses, they moved to Cleveland three weeks ago.”

“Cleveland,” repeated Flannery.

“Yes. Mr. Morehouse took a position there.”

“Did he leave a fifty-cint piece?”

The neighbor had not heard of one.

Flannery walked back to the road. Behind him, the vehicles squeaked, the boys waited, and several thousand guinea pigs commented sharply on the delay.

At eight that evening headquarters received a telegram:

> MOREHOUSE REMOVED TO CLEVELAND ADDRESS UNKNOWN STOP HAVE PREPARED ENTIRE NATURAL INCREASE FOR DELIVERY BUT CONSIGNEE ABSENT STOP KINDLY ADVISE BEFORE MORNING AS PRESENT PACKING ARRANGEMENT IS SOCIAL RATHER THAN PERMANENT STOP FLANNERY.

The telegram traveled through Night Operations, Delivery Exceptions, and Unlocated Consignees. At eleven it reached an assistant superintendent, who had authority to issue emergency instructions but no desire to own the emergency. He telephoned his superintendent at home. The superintendent telephoned the General Manager, who was attending a dinner. The General Manager, between the soup and the fish, said the animals must be removed from Westcote and sent to the central office for disposition.

Thus, at midnight, Flannery received the order:

> FORWARD ENTIRE SHIPMENT INCLUDING INCREASE TO CENTRAL WAREHOUSE BY FIRST AVAILABLE SERVICE. MARK FOR ATTENTION GENERAL RATE DEPARTMENT.

Flannery smiled slowly.

“Attention General Rate Department,” he said. “They’ll get attention.”

The first available service consisted of two express cars attached to the 6:10 freight. They were insufficient. Flannery loaded them anyway and sent a telegram stating that the first installment was on the way.

The second installment left at noon in three cars. The third left the following morning. Guinea pigs arrived at the central warehouse in crates, cages, barrels with wire tops, hampers, poultry coops, and wooden boxes perforated according to Flannery’s increasingly liberal ideas of ventilation.

The warehouse superintendent telephoned the General Rate Department.

“What shall I do with them?”

“Hold pending instructions.”

“There are fifteen hundred here now.”

“Maintain in good condition.”

“Another train is unloading.”

“Then maintain those also.”

By evening the warehouse floor was alive from wall to wall. Clerks climbed onto desks. Porters built barricades from ledgers. A junior rate examiner opened a closet and found that sixty guinea pigs had reached it before him. The animals ate two bundles of blank claim forms and half of Circular 22-D, concerning the proper treatment of perishable shipments.

The next morning five more cars arrived.

The warehouse superintendent telegraphed Westcote:

> CEASE FORWARDING. CAPACITY EXCEEDED.

Flannery answered:

> UNABLE CEASE. YOUR ORDER ENTIRE SHIPMENT. THREE CARS ALREADY DEPARTED FOUR MORE LOADING. CAPACITY HERE ALSO EXCEEDED SINCE FEBRUARY.

Headquarters attempted to distribute the animals among branch warehouses, but every transfer required papers, and every paper required an exact quantity. No exact quantity survived long enough to be written down. A count made at nine was obsolete by lunch. A count made at lunch was inaccurate before the ink dried.

Public Relations proposed donating the animals to schools. Legal warned that schools might return them. Sales suggested offering one free with every prepaid shipment. Auditing asked which department would absorb the fifty-cent original charge.

At last the General Manager gave an order free from all technical ornament:

“Get rid of them.”

They were sent to pet shops, laboratories, zoological gardens, orphanages, county fairs, and anyone who answered a telephone without first asking why the company was calling. For two weeks the Continental Rapid Express Company carried guinea pigs free in every direction. Its own messengers delivered them by the pair, the dozen, and the hamper. Employees took them home. Employees’ children brought some back.

For months afterward, isolated guinea pigs were discovered in the central warehouse behind cabinets and beneath platforms, living comfortably upon rate schedules.

At Westcote, Flannery loaded the final cage into the final car. It contained one elderly-looking animal, though Flannery knew enough by then not to trust appearances.

He nailed the label in place:

GENERAL RATE DEPARTMENT  
WITH CARE

Then he wiped his forehead and looked at the empty stable, the chewed woodwork, the mountains of discarded wire, and the cabbage leaves blowing across the platform.

Timmy stood beside him.

“Mr. Flannery,” the boy asked, “what if somebody ships more pigs?”

Flannery put on his coat.

“From this day,” he said, “there’ll be no pigs in this office.”

“What if they’re real pigs?”

“Pets.”

“Cows?”

“Pets.”

“Horses?”

“Pets.”

“Lions?”

“Domestic pets, twinty-foive cints.”

“Tigers?”

“Household pets, an’ I’ll swear to their affectionate nature before anny committee in the land.”

The freight train began to move. From the last car came a chorus of whistles, fading gradually down the line.

Flannery watched until the train disappeared.

“Pigs is pigs, maybe,” he said, “but rates is trouble.”

Then his face brightened.

“Still,” he added, “there’s wan mercy in the world.”

“What’s that?” asked Timmy.

“They wasn’t elephants.”

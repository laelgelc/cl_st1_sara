In those portions of Texas where, in the early eighties, the district court sat at long intervals and justice traveled on a tired horse, men were sometimes obliged to appeal from the statutes of the State to a tribunal of more immediate jurisdiction. Judge Colt commonly presided there, with Judge Parker’s double-barreled associate upon the woolsack, and while the decisions of that court were not always sound law, they were seldom misunderstood. The case of Gray against Sheriff Ninde was one of the fortunate causes in which the judges were brought into court, but no opinion was delivered.

Gray was a ranchman of the old school, and had accumulated nearly two hundred head of horses, counting mares, colts, and a number of serviceable saddle animals. His earlier history is somewhat obscured by the fact that every man who knew him told it differently; but he had come through hard times, paid what he owed when he could, and was accounted honest by neighbors whose opinion was worth more than a certificate under seal. He was not quarrelsome. Indeed, he had reached an age when a man prefers counting colts to exchanging bullets, though this preference, as will appear, did not amount to cowardice.

Some years before, Gray had been involved in debt. A judgment had been rendered against him, and he maintained that it had afterward been satisfied. Whether the payment had been improperly entered, whether papers had been lost, or whether some enterprising person discovered that a dead judgment might be revived and made to walk, cannot now be stated with certainty. The records were not models of clerical virtue. At any rate, an execution was issued, and Sheriff Ninde levied upon Gray’s horse herd.

Ninde was a sheriff with a reputation to maintain, which is sometimes more troublesome than a county. One newspaper had described him and a deputy as “human bloodhounds,” an expression that pleased them greatly and alarmed nobody who had seen either man after supper. Ninde carried two revolvers, wore his hat at a determined angle, and had acquired the habit of looking over his shoulder as if expecting an assassin. He was not destitute of courage, but had accepted so many compliments upon it that he no longer considered it necessary to examine the article closely.

When Gray learned of the levy, he protested that the judgment had been paid and that the horses could not lawfully be taken. The sheriff replied that the execution was regular upon its face and that he proposed to serve it. This was law enough for Ninde and too much law for Gray.

Gray consulted friends, and perhaps counsel, though upon this point the accounts differ. His next move had at least the appearance of legal intelligence. Texas process in a civil case could not run into the Indian Territory. Therefore, before the sheriff could gather the herd, Gray and several of his sons and neighbors rounded up the horses and started north.

Moving nearly two hundred head was not a matter of tying a bundle behind the saddle. The mares had colts; some of the young stock had never been driven beyond the home range; and a horse herd possesses, in large measure, the reasoning powers of a political convention. It travels well when it has nowhere to go and scatters when speed is important. Nevertheless, Gray’s men kept the bunch together, crossed the Red River, and pushed into the Territory.

Gray had a brother living near the Kansas line. The country about his place offered grass, water, and friends, and there the herd was held. It was not hidden, for this sized bunch could no more be hidden than a courthouse, but it was beyond Ninde’s civil jurisdiction. Young Gray and several others camped with the horses, night-herding them and keeping close watch upon strangers.

The sheriff, in the mean time, had difficulties of his own. His bondsmen had guaranteed his official conduct, and he feared that failure to produce the property would be charged against him. Worse than that, men were beginning to say that Gray had beaten him. A sheriff may survive a doubtful execution more readily than a joke.

Ninde decided to recover the horses. A sensible officer might have returned to court, stated that the property had been carried outside the State, and let the lawyers open the ball. Ninde either relied on his reputation as a bad man, or probably did not know any better. He recruited a party of frontier heelers, men who wore their cartridge belts conspicuously and had been in the neighborhood of several killings. Some were guards and trail hands of real experience; others were ornamental human cattle, fierce in the saloons and obedient wherever shooting seemed likely.

The party rode north, crossed the river, and entered a country in which Ninde possessed no more civil authority than a drummer selling patent churns. He did not announce this fact to his posse. The men were informed that they were assisting an officer, which was true in the social but not the legal sense.

They approached Gray’s camp at night. The herders had watched for ordinary horse thieves, but they did not expect a Texas sheriff accompanied by enough six-shooters to furnish a militia company. Ninde’s men surrounded the camp, woke young Gray and the others, and ordered them to stand away from their arms.

Young Gray asked whether Ninde had process good in the Territory.

“I’ve got all the process I need,” said Ninde, touching his pistol.

“That may be enough to take the horses,” the young man answered, “but it won’t make the taking lawful.”

Several of the posse rattled their revolvers and made remarks about gore. These demonstrations were unnecessary, since the herders were plainly outnumbered. Young Gray advised his companions not to resist. He also advised Ninde that if he took the herd he would be pursued, and that the elder Gray would not surrender his property merely because a number of armed men had stolen it under color of office.

Ninde took Gray’s horses. Unfortunately for him, the herd had been running with about sixty head belonging to local settlers. In the darkness, and perhaps from haste, his men drove off the entire bunch. Thus the sheriff, who had entered the Territory to execute a Texas judgment upon one man’s property, departed with the property of half a dozen men against whom no judgment existed anywhere.

This was doing the Sherlock act backward. Instead of narrowing the number of interested parties, Ninde had increased it.

As soon as the raiders were clear, young Gray sent word to his father and to the owners of the missing sixty head. Horses were saddled all over that settlement. Gray’s brother came in, as did nephews, neighbors, and men whose brands had been swept away with the herd. Some wished to start at once, but Gray insisted upon knowing how many men they had, what arms they carried, and where Ninde was likely to stop. He was angry, but anger had not yet deprived him of judgment.

By daylight the rescue party was on the trail. They did not let much grass grow under their feet. The big herd left a sign that a blind man might have followed, and Ninde’s company, burdened by loose stock and confident that its show of weapons had settled the matter, made no remarkable speed.

The pursuers learned from a traveler that the sheriff intended to hold the horses at a corral some miles ahead, sort out Gray’s brand, and turn the remaining animals loose. This plan, if carried out, would have corrected one blunder while leaving the principal one untouched. It also gave Gray precisely what he needed—a fixed place at which to find the sheriff.

The corral stood near a small trading point, with open ground on three sides and a line of scrub timber on the fourth. Ninde’s men had crowded the herd inside and were separating the animals by brand. They had not completed the work when a lookout saw riders coming.

The hired gunmen became active. They fastened cartridge belts, examined cylinders, and took positions where they could be plainly seen. A few talked loudly about what would happen if Gray attempted to interfere. Ninde walked into the open and ordered his men to stand firm. Yet it was noticed that he placed himself where the corral fence offered some protection, a practical arrangement not entirely consistent with the newspaper picture of a human bloodhound.

Gray’s party halted within easy speaking distance. They were fewer than Ninde expected, but there was no confusion among them. The local owners demanded their sixty horses. Ninde said he would return those after the sorting. Gray demanded all of his own.

“I hold them under execution,” said the sheriff.

“You hold them in the Indian Territory,” Gray answered.

“I took them, and I’ll take them back to Texas.”

“No,” said Gray, “you won’t.”

There followed some argument concerning jurisdiction, debt, bondsmen, and resistance to an officer. It was not learned, and neither side changed its mind. The men behind Ninde fingered their revolvers. Gray’s relatives spread out slightly, not in parade formation, but in the natural way of men who do not wish one bullet to account for two of them.

Gray then dismounted and took a double-barreled shotgun from across his saddle. It was loaded with buckshot. He broke it, looked at the shells, closed it carefully, and started toward Ninde.

He did not carry the gun leveled. It rested in his hands as if he expected to flush a partridge from the weeds. This quiet manner was more convincing than the pistol-rattling of the sheriff’s heelers. Gray’s face had gone pale, and those who knew him understood that the pallor did not indicate a change of purpose.

Ninde called to him to stop.

Gray continued until no man present could doubt what a charge of buckshot would do at that distance. Then he halted.

“Ninde,” he said, “that judgment was paid. You know these horses were taken where you had no authority, and you know part of them never belonged to me. I’ve worked too long for what I have to let you carry it off because you wear a badge.”

“I’m acting as sheriff,” Ninde replied.

“You’re acting as a horse thief.”

At this, one of Ninde’s men drew his revolver halfway. Gray’s brother covered him, and the weapon went back into its holster. Things looked bad for the sheriff.

Ninde said that if Gray advanced another step he would be killed.

“That may be,” Gray answered. “But if you don’t turn those horses loose, I’ll kill you first. I’ll send your soul glimmering into eternity, and take my chance of following it.”

No one laughed at the expression. Gray spoke without raising his voice, and the muzzle of the shotgun had risen only a few inches. Ninde looked at him for some time. He afterward claimed that he yielded to prevent useless bloodshed, and this may have been true. He also saw that Gray had already accepted the possibility of death and was prepared to shoot. Reputation is a poor shield against a man who has reached that conclusion.

The sheriff glanced toward his posse. Those human bloodhounds had ceased baying. They were brave enough to assist in taking sleeping herders by surprise, but the present business offered no certainty as to who would be alive when the smoke cleared. Moreover, several had begun to suspect that killing men in the Indian Territory while stealing local horses might require explanations to authorities less friendly than a Texas court.

“Open the gate,” Ninde said.

For a moment no one moved. Then he repeated the order. The bars were pulled aside, and the horses came out in a confused stream, mares calling to colts and the riders turning the bunch away from the corral. The local owners cut out their sixty head. Gray’s men gathered the remainder, counting by brands as well as circumstances allowed.

Gray stood where he was until the herd was clear. Then he lowered the shotgun. His hands began to shake so violently that he could scarcely break the barrels. He sat down upon the ground, white and weak, and for several minutes could not speak.

There were men present who had mistaken loudness for courage all their lives, and this affected them more than the threat had done. Gray had not approached Ninde because killing was easy for him. He had approached knowing exactly what it meant, and his body paid the reckoning after his purpose no longer had to sustain it.

No blood was shed. This was fortunate, for had one nervous man opened the ball, a dozen might have fallen over a civil judgment whose validity was already doubtful.

Gray’s party returned the herd to the range near his brother’s place and kept a stronger guard. Ninde did not again attempt to take it. Indeed, he found reasons for remaining away from Texas for a time, while the more decorative members of his posse returned separately and gave accounts in which they had each restrained all the others from massacre.

The sheriff’s bondsmen, being practical men with money at stake, investigated the original proceedings. They found irregularities enough to make another expedition unattractive and a lawsuit dangerous. The alleged satisfaction of the judgment looked better under examination than it had when Ninde set out to recover his reputation with armed assistance. After some correspondence and a considerable decline in official enthusiasm, the matter was dropped.

Thus Gray kept his horses. The law had first taken his property without establishing justice, and armed men afterward restored justice without establishing a very respectable law. Yet the final result was less lawless than it appeared. Ninde yielded because he knew Gray would shoot; but the bondsmen abandoned the case because, when they finally examined it, Gray appeared to have been right.

Judge Colt and the double-barreled member of the court therefore adjourned without firing an opinion. On the frontier, that was sometimes the highest evidence of good judgment.

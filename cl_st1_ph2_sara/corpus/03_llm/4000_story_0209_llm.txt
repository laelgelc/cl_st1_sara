# Rosy and the Three Little Friends

Once upon a time there lived, in a small cottage at the edge of a wood, a little girl named Rosy. She was called Rosy because her cheeks had once been as pink as wild roses, though in those days they were often pale, for there was not always enough bread in the cupboard.

Rosy lived with her mother, who had long been ill. Her father, Tom, had gone away many years before to seek gold in a country beyond the sea, beyond a great desert, and beyond some mountains so high that their snowy tops seemed to touch the moon. At first letters had come from him, but by and by they ceased, and no one knew whether he had grown rich, or remained poor, or lost his way in that strange country.

Rosy worked hard. She swept the cottage, gathered sticks, fetched water, and made broth when there was anything with which to make it. But one cold evening her mother called her to the bedside and took her hand.

“My dear child,” she said, “I fear I must leave you. When I am gone, seek your father. He loved us well, and if he lives, he will be waiting for some news of home.”

Rosy tried not to cry, but two large tears rolled down her cheeks.

“How shall I cross the sea and the desert and the mountains?” she asked.

“Be brave, be kind, and never pass suffering by,” said her mother. “Kindness is a little seed, Rosy, but no one can tell how large a tree may grow from it.”

That night her mother died very peacefully.

The neighbors were good to Rosy, and wished her to remain with them, but she remembered the last words she had heard. So she packed a loaf, a small cheese, and her mother’s blue handkerchief into a bundle. Then she put on her warm red cloak and started out to find her father.

She walked through the wood while the dew still shone on the leaves, and though she was lonely, she said to herself, “I am going to Father, and every step brings me nearer.”

Toward noon she came to a little stream. On the muddy bank something silver was jumping and twisting in the sun. It was a tiny fish which had been left behind when the water fell.

“Oh, poor little creature!” cried Rosy. “You will die here.”

She laid down her bundle, took the slippery fish gently in both hands, and put it into the deepest pool. For a moment the fish vanished. Then its bright head popped above the water.

“Thank you, good little Rosy,” it said.

Rosy was surprised, for she had never before heard a fish speak, but she answered politely, “You are welcome.”

“If you should ever need me,” said the fish, “stand beside water and call:

“Silver fin and silver scale,  
Come and help me without fail!”

“But you are such a tiny fish,” said Rosy. “How could you help me?”

The fish whisked its tail and splashed a drop upon her nose.

“Tiny friends may know great friends,” it said. “Wait and see!”

Then it darted away like a silver needle.

Rosy trudged on and on. That evening, as she passed a field, she heard a faint squeaking beneath a hedge. There lay a brown mouse with one little paw caught under a sharp stone.

“Keep still,” said Rosy. “I will soon set you free.”

The stone was heavy, and Rosy had to push with all her strength, but at last it rolled aside. She tore a strip from the blue handkerchief and bound the mouse’s injured paw. Then she gave it three crumbs of cheese.

The mouse sat up and brushed its whiskers.

“You are a capable person,” it said briskly. “I like capable persons. If you are ever in trouble, tap three times upon the ground and call:

“Quick little feet beneath the stone,  
Come, for Rosy is alone!”

Rosy smiled. “You are kind, but you are even smaller than the fish. What could you do against a great danger?”

The mouse twitched its ears.

“Small doors sometimes open into large houses,” it said. “Wait and see!”

Then it limped into the hedge, carrying the last crumb of cheese.

The next day Rosy entered a dark part of the wood. Between two thorn branches she saw a spider’s web, and in the middle of it a fly struggled and buzzed.

“Help! Help!” cried the fly. “This net is strong, and the spider is coming!”

Rosy saw a fat spider creeping from beneath a leaf. She did not wish to harm it, for spiders must eat too; so she broke the threads around the fly, lifted it free upon a twig, and set the twig far away. Then she dropped a raisin near the spider, which seemed content with this exchange.

The fly rubbed its wings, then its head, then all six legs, for it was an energetic little fellow.

“A narrow escape!” it buzzed. “A very narrow escape indeed! Rosy, if ever you need help, wave something blue in the air and sing:

“Fly that hums and fly that sings,  
Bring me help on shining wings!”

Rosy looked at its thin legs and gauzy wings.

“I fear the wind might carry you away,” she said.

“Perhaps,” replied the fly, “but the wind goes to many places and hears many things. Wait and see!”

Away it flew, buzzing so loudly that one might have thought it was as large as a goose.

For several days Rosy walked through green country. She slept in barns or beneath haystacks, and kind people sometimes gave her milk. At last she climbed a hill and saw the sea before her.

It stretched farther than she could see, blue near the shore and dark as ink at the edge of the sky. Ships with white sails moved upon it like little toys. Rosy’s courage almost failed.

“I cannot swim across,” she said, “and I have no money for a ship. What shall I do? What shall I do?”

Then she remembered the tiny fish.

She stood with her feet in the foam and called:

“Silver fin and silver scale,  
Come and help me without fail!”

At once a bright spot appeared in the water. The little fish swam into the shallows.

“Here I am!” it cried. “Why do you look so sorrowful?”

“I must cross the sea to find my father.”

“That is easily managed.”

“Easily!” said Rosy. “It would take you a year to carry me, even if I could sit upon your back.”

The fish laughed, making three bubbles.

“I told you that tiny friends may know great friends.”

It turned toward the open sea and gave a sharp whistle. The water began to roll and heave. A black shape rose from the deep, larger than a cottage and nearly as large as an island. It was a whale.

“Good afternoon,” said the whale in a voice like distant thunder. “Is this the passenger?”

“Yes,” said the fish. “Carry her safely across the sea, Cousin Whale, for she saved my life.”

“With pleasure,” replied the whale.

He lowered his broad back beside a rock, and Rosy climbed upon it. The little fish leaped into a hollow near his head where a cupful of water had gathered.

“Hold fast!” called the whale.

Off they went, his tail steering like the rudder of a great ship. At first Rosy shut her eyes, but presently she grew brave enough to look around.

Never had she seen such wonders. Dolphins sprang from the waves in silver curves, and porpoises rolled like black wheels. A nautilus sailed by in its pearly boat. Far below, forests of red coral swayed, and pale shells lay upon the sand. Once a troop of mermaids rose from the green water, combing their long hair and singing so sweetly that Rosy forgot her weariness.

At night the moon made a shining road across the sea. The whale swam gently, and the fish sang a little watery lullaby:

“Sleep while the quiet billows roll,  
The moon keeps watch from pole to pole;  
No storm shall harm, no wave shall sweep  
The kindly child who rests in sleep.”

Rosy lay between two smooth ridges on the whale’s back, wrapped in her red cloak, and slept until morning.

On the second afternoon they reached the opposite shore.

The whale bent his head, and Rosy stepped onto dry land.

“Thank you,” she said, patting his great black side. “I was foolish to think a little fish could do nothing.”

“Most people judge by size,” said the whale. “The sea knows better.”

The tiny fish winked at her.

“Wait and see,” it said once more, and the two disappeared beneath the waves.

Beyond the shore lay a desert. Its sand was yellow and hot, and the wind drove it in thin streams over the ground. Rosy walked until her shoes burned and her throat ached. There was no tree, no house, and no patch of shade, only sand, sand, sand.

By noon she could go no farther. She sank beside a stone and looked across the trembling plain.

“I shall never cross this dreadful place,” she whispered.

Then she remembered the mouse. She tapped three times upon the hard ground and called:

“Quick little feet beneath the stone,  
Come, for Rosy is alone!”

A tiny hole opened beside the stone, and out popped the brown mouse. Its paw was quite well, though it still wore the blue bandage.

“You look overheated,” it said. “This desert is no place for a person without four quick feet.”

“Even four mouse feet would be tired before we crossed it,” said Rosy.

“Mouse feet, perhaps,” replied the mouse, “but I did not say whose four feet.”

It stood on its hind legs and gave a shrill squeak. From behind a ridge of sand came a deep roar, and over the ridge strode a splendid yellow lion. His mane shone like a ring of bronze.

Rosy stepped backward, but the lion bowed.

“Do not be afraid, dear child,” he said in a noble, genial voice. “Any friend of the mouse is a friend of mine.”

The lion crouched like a great cat before the fire, and Rosy climbed onto his back. The mouse sat between his ears.

“Ready?” asked the lion.

“Ready!” cried the mouse.

The lion bounded away. His paws scarcely seemed to touch the sand. Up one dune and down another he raced, while Rosy’s red cloak streamed behind her.

They passed a caravan of merchants, who stared in astonishment.

“Look!” cried one. “A red monkey riding a lion!”

“I am not a monkey!” Rosy called, but they were already far away, and the mouse laughed until it nearly fell between the lion’s ears.

When night came, the lion dug a hollow in the warm sand and curled around Rosy to keep off the cold wind. In the morning he carried her onward, and before sunset the desert ended in a pleasant green country watered by streams.

“There you are,” said the lion. “The mountains are only a day’s walk ahead.”

Rosy hugged his shaggy neck.

“You have carried me as gently as my mother once carried me.”

The lion’s golden eyes grew soft.

“Then I am well repaid,” he said.

The mouse jumped down.

“Do not forget,” it said briskly, “small doors, large houses!”

Then it and the lion went back across the desert.

The next day Rosy reached the foot of the mountains. They rose before her, gray and purple, with white snow on their peaks. She found a narrow path and climbed until evening, but in the night part of the path fell away with a terrible crash. In front of her stood a wall of rock, and behind her yawned a deep chasm.

“I cannot go forward, and I cannot go back,” cried Rosy. “Must I fail when I am so near?”

Then she remembered the fly. From her bundle she took the torn blue handkerchief, waved it, and sang:

“Fly that hums and fly that sings,  
Bring me help on shining wings!”

There came a buzz beside her ear.

“Present! Ready! Waiting!” cried the fly. “Dear me, this is a high place. You certainly choose large troubles.”

“I must reach the other side of the mountains,” said Rosy.

“And so you shall.”

“But your wings could not carry one of my fingers.”

“My wings need only carry a message,” said the fly. “A message weighs nothing.”

It flew up, up, up until it looked no larger than a speck. Presently a shadow swept over Rosy. Down came an enormous eagle with gray feathers, a hooked beak, and claws as strong as iron.

“Climb on,” he said rather roughly, though not unkindly. “I have been told you rescue flies.”

Rosy seated herself between his wings and held fast.

The eagle sprang from the ledge.

Up, up, up they flew, past black rocks and white waterfalls, past clouds that felt cool upon Rosy’s face, and over peaks where no flower grew. The villages below looked like handfuls of pebbles, and rivers were no wider than silver threads.

Then they descended into a long valley. Rosy saw tents, wooden huts, smoking fires, and men moving among piles of stone.

“That is the gold camp,” said the eagle. “Your father’s hut stands by the three pine trees.”

He set her down gently and flew away, while the fly buzzed after him.

Rosy ran toward the pines. On the door of a poor little hut someone had cut the name TOM.

She pushed it open, but no one was there. A pick stood in one corner, a rough coat hung on a nail, and on a shelf lay a faded ribbon which had once belonged to her mother.

“Father!” Rosy cried. “Father, I have found you!”

But only the wind answered.

At that moment a storm broke over the mountains. Rain struck the roof like thrown pebbles, and thunder shook the valley. Rosy ran outside. The miners were hurrying from every direction.

“The river is rising!” shouted one.

“The lower mine has fallen in!” shouted another.

“Men are trapped below!”

“Who? Who is trapped?”

They called out several names, and the last was Tom.

Rosy’s heart seemed to stop.

“My father is there!” she cried. “Oh, some one save him!”

The rain poured down, and the river rushed brown and wild toward the mine entrance. The miners tried to heap stones against it, but the water swept the stones away.

Then a silver head appeared in a flooded ditch.

“Rosy!” called the little fish. “I followed the streams from the sea. What is wrong?”

“The mine will flood, and my father is inside!”

The fish gave a piercing whistle.

From the riverbanks came a troop of beavers—ten, twenty, then fifty of them. They cut branches with their sharp teeth, rolled logs into the water, and slapped mud into every crack with their flat tails, which worked like little shovels.

“Faster, cousins!” cried the fish. “Build high and build strong!”

The beavers worked so quickly that the miners could scarcely believe their eyes. Soon a stout dam turned the river aside, and the water no longer poured into the mine.

But the entrance was still blocked by fallen earth.

“We cannot dig through in time,” said a miner. “The men below will have no air.”

Tap, tap, tap came a sound under Rosy’s feet. The brown mouse poked its head from a crack.

“This is a matter for underground workers,” it said.

It squeaked a rapid command, and the earth began to stir. Hundreds of moles appeared, black and velvety, with broad pink paws. They dug from every side, making narrow passages through the loose soil.

“An air tunnel here!” ordered the mouse. “A rescue passage there! Do not loosen that stone! Support this beam!”

The miners followed its directions. They worked with picks and spades, while the moles worked beneath them. By and by a cool breath came through one of the tunnels.

“Air!” shouted a miner. “The trapped men can breathe!”

Then there came faint knocking from below.

“They are alive! They are alive!”

Every one cheered and dug harder. At last the fallen earth broke apart, and one by one the trapped miners crawled out, muddy and weary.

The last man had a brown beard streaked with gray. He carried an enormous lump of gold against his chest, but when he saw Rosy, he dropped it as though it were merely a stone.

“My Rosy!” he cried.

“Father!”

They ran into each other’s arms, and for some time neither could say anything at all.

At last Tom held his daughter away and looked at her face.

“How did you cross the sea, the desert, and the mountains?”

“With the help of a fish, a mouse, and a fly,” said Rosy.

Tom looked puzzled, but the miners laughed and pointed to the beavers and moles.

“You had better believe her,” said one. “Small friends have done large work today.”

Tom picked up the lump of gold. It was so heavy that he could scarcely lift it.

“I found this just before the roof fell,” he said. “It may be worth a fortune, but you, my dear child, are better to me than a million pounds of gold.”

Some of the miners were honest and rejoiced in Tom’s good luck, but others stared at the gold with greedy eyes. The fly, who had been sitting unnoticed upon Rosy’s hood, buzzed into her ear.

“Danger! Envy! Whispering behind the tool shed!” it said. “You and your father must leave at once.”

“But how?” asked Rosy. “The mountain paths are broken.”

“Messages weigh nothing,” said the fly.

It darted away and soon returned with two great eagles. One was the gray eagle who had carried Rosy; the other was larger and darker.

“Quickly,” said the gray eagle. “Night is coming.”

Tom tied the gold in his coat. He climbed upon the dark eagle, while Rosy mounted her old friend. The greedy miners came running from behind the huts just as the eagles spread their wings.

“Stop them!” the men shouted.

But no one could stop an eagle in the sky.

Up rose Rosy and her father, over the mine, over the roaring river, and over the snowy peaks. Far below, the beavers slapped their tails in farewell, the moles waved their pink paws, and the little fish leaped from the water like a flash of silver. The mouse stood upon a rock, looking very pleased with the efficiency of everything, while the fly flew in circles and buzzed, “Faster! Higher! Away!”

The eagles carried them beyond the mountains to a distant city beside a broad, peaceful river. There Tom sold the gold, and it brought enough money for a comfortable house with a garden.

Yet they did not become proud. They gave help to widows, food to hungry children, and warm beds to travelers. In the garden Tom made a clear pond for fish, a little stone house for mice, and a sunny corner where flies might safely hum. Near the gate stood three carvings—a whale, a lion, and an eagle—so that Rosy would never forget the great friends who had answered the calls of the small ones.

Rosy and her father lived happily together for many years, which was better than all the gold in all the mountains.

And if anyone laughed when Rosy said that no creature was too small to be useful, she would smile and answer:

“Kindness may look tiny when it begins. But wait and see.”

### The Voyage to Sandpeep Island

I was born within sound of the sea, and I think I learned the speech of waves before I had wholly mastered that of men. Rivermouth stood where the broad brown river, after winding through farms and salt meadows, lost itself in the Atlantic; and every second house contained either a sailor, the widow of a sailor, or a boy who intended to become one. We knew the tides as inland children know the hours of school. We could distinguish, without looking from our beds, the long roll of the surf on the outer beach from the slap of the river against the wharves. The odor of tar, wet rope, drying nets, and codfish was the native perfume of the place.

To us boys the sea was not merely a great sheet of water. It was a road—perhaps to China, perhaps to the Spanish Main, perhaps to nowhere in particular, which was even better. Every bark that spread her topsails beyond the lighthouse carried a portion of our souls with her. We had all discovered America several times before we were twelve, though we had never been farther from Rivermouth than Sandpeep Island.

Sandpeep was a low, green-backed island lying four miles down the harbor, with a fringe of white beach, a deserted fisherman’s hut, and three ragged pines twisted eastward by the gales. At high water it looked like a little country complete in itself; at low tide black reefs and glistening flats appeared about it, as if the island had suddenly put out claws. It was famous for clams, blueberries, and a spring of icy water hidden under a bank of bayberry. It was also famous among us because old Captain Nutter had once found there a brass button which he declared had belonged to a French admiral. I never heard how he established the ownership.

The expedition of which I am about to tell grew out of our purchase of the Dolphin, a twelve-foot wherry with a patched sail, two sculls, a blunt cutwater, and a disposition—though we did not know it then—to turn over whenever left to herself.

The Dolphin cost eighteen dollars. Charley Marden, Ned Ropes, Binny Wallace, and I subscribed four dollars and a half apiece. For nearly three months we deprived ourselves of gingerbread, marbles, fireworks, and such other necessities of life as boys commonly squander their fortunes upon. Charley even sold a white rabbit, though he afterwards maintained that the rabbit had run away and that the purchaser had merely paid a reward in advance.

When the Dolphin became ours, she was painted green outside and a painful yellow within. On her stern we inscribed her name in letters of varying size. The D was Charley’s, the O mine, the L Ned’s, and the remaining letters Binny’s; this accounts for the circumstance that the word began like the title of a royal decree and ended in a delicate feminine hand.

Our parents did not regard the enterprise with the enthusiasm we felt. The harbor had swallowed too many Rivermouth lads for boats to be looked upon as innocent playthings. After a council, the elders permitted us to use the Dolphin under certain restrictions. We were not to go beyond the black buoy. We were not to take her out in rough weather. Above all, Charley, Ned, and I were forbidden to raise the sail. Binny alone might do so, because he had spent two summers with an uncle who owned a fishing-smack and was supposed to possess nautical judgment.

This arrangement offended me deeply. I considered myself a born mariner, though my chief experience consisted in having fallen off a raft in three feet of water. Charley said the prohibition was “bosh,” which did not prevent him from obeying it whenever there was a possibility of discovery. Ned, who was of a practical turn, observed that rowing made stronger arms. Binny merely smiled.

That smile of his comes back to me across all these years. He was the youngest of us, a slight, fair-haired boy, with grave blue eyes and a gentleness which did not make him less manly. Boys have an instinctive contempt for softness, but no one ever thought Binny soft. He was brave without noise, generous without knowing it, and so truthful that we sometimes found him inconvenient. Quarrels died in his presence, not because he preached—nothing would sooner have caused us to pitch him off the wharf—but because angry words sounded foolish when Binny looked at you.

One Thursday in September, Master Grimshaw dismissed the Latin class until Monday while he journeyed to Boston concerning an inheritance. The inheritance proved, I believe, to be a mahogany clock; but to us it was four days of liberty, and therefore scarcely less valuable than the Indies.

“Sandpeep!” cried Charley, before the schoolhouse door had ceased to bang behind us.

“Tomorrow,” said Ned.

“With chowder,” I added.

“And lemons,” said Binny, who had a curious belief that nothing eaten out of doors was complete without lemons.

The plan expanded as plans will. We were to start at six in the morning, fish on the way, land at the southern cove, cook dinner, explore the island, bathe, and return before sunset. Charley proposed that we spend the night in the fisherman’s hut and sail home at dawn like castaways rescued from a desert island. We rejected this, chiefly because none of us had obtained permission to be cast away.

Our stores, when assembled, would have provisioned a small garrison: potatoes, onions, salt pork, crackers, a loaf of bread, pepper, butter in a jar, four hard-boiled eggs, a pie, six apples, a coffee-pot without a lid, and a tin pail for the chowder. Charley contributed three cigars which he had abstracted—or, as he expressed it, borrowed indefinitely—from his elder brother. Binny brought the lemons in a blue handkerchief.

My mother inspected the cargo with misgiving.

“You will keep within the harbor, Tom?”

“Yes, mother.”

“And not touch the sail?”

“Not unless Binny orders it.”

This was an artful answer, since Binny never ordered anyone to do anything.

My mother laid her hand on my shoulder and looked toward the east, where morning had come up pale and pearly over the roofs.

“There may be weather before night,” she said.

The sky was as smooth as a robin’s egg, and the smoke from the chimneys rose straight into the clear air. I laughed, kissed her, and ran down to the landing. If death lurked anywhere on that shining morning, he hid himself well.

We pushed off at half past six. The river was nearly at flood, and the Dolphin slipped along under our sculls with a pleasant chuckle at her bow. Binny sat in the stern and steered; Ned and I rowed; Charley, who had appointed himself quartermaster, guarded the provisions and criticized our stroke.

“Pull together,” he said. “You row like a pair of undertakers.”

“You will need an undertaker if you don’t keep quiet,” Ned replied.

We passed the wharves, where sleepy men in red shirts were rolling barrels; the shipyard, full of pale ribs and skeleton keels; the old fort, grass-grown and harmless; and finally the lighthouse, whose white tower shone against the morning like a bone. Beyond it the harbor widened. Gulls floated over us with slow wings, uttering now and then a thin, petulant cry. A school of mackerel broke the surface to starboard, flashing like handfuls of silver money.

At the black buoy we rested on our oars. Sandpeep lay two miles away, greener and more mysterious than ever. Outside the harbor the sea breathed gently, long blue swells lifting and falling under the sun.

“We ought to sail,” said Charley.

“There isn’t wind enough,” Binny answered.

This was true. The sail hung loose when we tried it, and presently we lowered it and continued with the sculls. Thus we technically obeyed our parents, though no one had authorized the experiment.

Halfway to the island we fished. Ned caught a small cod, I drew up two cunners and a mass of seaweed, and Charley hooked the seat of his trousers. Binny caught nothing, but unfastened Charley from the line and rebaited all our hooks. By nine o’clock we had fish enough for the chowder, and Sandpeep’s little cove opened before us, smooth as a pond between two black ledges.

We ran the Dolphin upon the sand, carried out the provisions, and drew the boat above the wash of the tide. Binny made the painter fast to a root exposed beneath the bank.

“Put a second turn on it,” said Ned.

Binny did so.

It is strange what small pictures the mind preserves while larger scenes fade away. I can still see his brown hand passing the wet rope around that gray root; the sun shone on his hair, and a sandpiper ran behind him, flirting its tail. For years afterwards I dreamed of that knot. In the dream I always reached forward to tighten it, and always awoke before my fingers touched the rope.

But there was no shadow on us then. We became Crusoes in a moment. Charley discovered the fisherman’s hut, though it had been visible from the boat; Ned found the spring; I took possession of the three pine-trees in the name of the United States; and Binny, less ambitious, cleaned the fish.

The hut contained a broken stool, a rusted stove, two lobster-pots, and a smell which no language can adequately describe. On one wall somebody had drawn a ship under full sail, with a prodigious flag at the masthead. Charley said it was probably the work of a pirate awaiting execution.

“More likely a fisherman waiting for the tide,” said Binny.

There was also an inscription, cut with a knife: SAMUEL HOBBS, 1821. We spoke the name softly. Samuel Hobbs might have been alive in Portsmouth at that very minute, but the date made him seem as remote as Julius Caesar.

We built our fire outside, between stones, and set the chowder-pot over it. Everybody cooked. This, I may say, is the only successful method of producing a boy’s chowder. Ned sliced the pork; Binny peeled potatoes; I attended to the fish; and Charley put in whatever he thought we had forgotten. The result contained sand, scales, several pine-needles, and, I suspect, part of a cork; nevertheless, no banquet of my later life has equaled it.

We sat in a semicircle with tin cups between our knees. The sea glittered through the bayberry bushes; the air smelled of smoke and salt; little waves ran up the beach, whispered together, and withdrew.

“If the King of France offered me a dinner,” said Ned, “I should refuse it.”

“He hasn’t asked you,” Charley observed.

“He might.”

“There isn’t any King of France,” said Binny.

“That makes it safer to refuse him,” said I.

After the chowder came the pie, which had suffered in transportation and resembled a geological formation. We divided it with a bait-knife. Then Charley produced the cigars.

Smoking was an important part of all adult adventure, as we understood it. Columbus smoked; Robinson Crusoe smoked; pirates smoked continually, except during battles and burials. We therefore lighted the cigars with solemnity.

Mine went out. Ned’s burned down one side. Binny took two puffs, turned pale, and laid his cigar aside, saying he did not care for it. Charley persisted the longest, out of principle; but shortly afterwards he wandered behind the hut and remained there until the color returned to his face. We agreed that the cigars had been injured by dampness.

The day passed in that prodigal fashion peculiar to childhood, when an hour is a kingdom and noon seems separated from night by half a lifetime. We hunted for Samuel Hobbs’s treasure, gathered shells, chased crabs among the pools, and pelted one another with the soft red berries of the sumac. We found no treasure, but Charley unearthed the hoop of a barrel and declared it Spanish armor.

At two o’clock we went bathing from the eastern beach. The water was warm near shore and startlingly cold beyond the shoal. Binny was the best swimmer. He floated on his back with his fair hair streaming and his face turned tranquilly to the sky, while the rest of us splashed, shouted, and performed such feats as nearly drowned us.

A bank of cloud lay then along the southwest, low and dark. None of us noticed it until Ned, coming out of the water, shivered and said the sun had gone in.

The change was slight at first. The bright surface of the sea dulled as if a breath had passed over metal. The gulls, which had been wheeling high, came inland and settled noisily on the beach. Across the harbor the lighthouse lost its whiteness and became gray. There was still no wind.

“We had better start,” Binny said.

We dressed and gathered our belongings. The tide had risen, narrowing the strip of sand. The Dolphin floated at the end of her painter, rising gently to each swell. Some of the stores—our coats, the empty baskets, and the remaining loaf—had been placed in her after dinner.

“Where are the lemons?” Charley asked.

Binny looked about.

“In the boat, under my jacket.”

“Leave them,” said Ned.

“My mother lent me the handkerchief,” Binny replied. “I’ll fetch it.”

He ran down the beach. We remained by the dead fire, stamping sand into our shoes and disputing who should row first. I was tying the coffee-pot to a basket when Ned cried out.

The Dolphin had drifted several yards from shore.

At first I thought Binny was pushing her around the point. Then I saw him standing in the stern, with the blue handkerchief in his hand. The painter trailed in the water. The root to which it had been tied had torn from the rain-softened bank and hung at the end of the rope like a crooked arm.

“Jump over!” screamed Charley.

Binny sprang toward the bow, but his movement sent the boat farther away. The water between us was already deep. He looked for the sculls.

They lay on the beach at our feet.

For one instant no one moved. The whole disaster was contained in that little space of water: Binny in the boat, we on the sand, and the useless sculls shining beside us.

“Throw the painter!” Ned shouted.

Binny hauled it in, coiled it, and flung. It fell short. He tried again. The rope struck the water halfway to shore.

“Swim!” I cried. “Swim, Binny!”

He pulled off his shoes. At that moment the first gust came across the island. It flattened the bayberry bushes, lifted sand in a pale sheet, and drove the Dolphin broadside from the cove. The boat leaped as if a hand had seized it.

Binny caught the gunwale. His face, a minute before merely puzzled, became white.

We ran along the beach. Ned tore off his coat and plunged into the water, but the bottom dropped sharply; a breaker struck him in the chest and hurled him back. I went in after him. The cold took my breath. We fought forward together, swimming perhaps twenty yards, perhaps forty—I cannot tell. The Dolphin moved faster than we did. The wind had found her now and played with her cruelly, spinning her half around, then sweeping her seaward.

“Go back!” Binny called.

His voice came thinly over the water.

“Jump!” I shouted. “We’ll get you!”

He shook his head. Whether he feared to leave the boat, or knew that we could never all reach shore, I do not know. Another wave broke over us. Ned disappeared; I caught his arm and dragged him upward. When we turned again, the Dolphin was beyond the point.

We struggled back to the beach and raced over the rocks, barefooted, bleeding, scarcely conscious of pain. From the western ridge we could see far across the harbor. The storm advanced like a black wall. Beneath it the sea had changed from gray to an evil green, streaked everywhere with white. The Dolphin was a small dark shape climbing and dropping among the waves.

Binny had raised a hand. Or perhaps it was only the loose end of the painter. We waved our shirts and screamed until our throats tore.

“Lie down in the bottom!” Ned roared.

“Binny!”

“Binny!”

The wind flung our voices back into our faces.

The squall struck.

I had never seen rain descend in that manner. It did not fall; it swept horizontally, a solid flood full of stinging salt. Sea and sky vanished together. For one second, through a rent in the rain, I saw the Dolphin high on the shoulder of a wave, black against a livid strip of horizon. Binny clung near the stern. His fair head showed with extraordinary distinctness, surrounded by spray like the nimbus about a little saint.

Then the boat slipped down the farther side.

We never saw it upright again.

“Binny!” Charley cried.

There was no answer except the wind.

We remained on the ridge, searching the confusion of water until our eyes ached. Every black wave was the Dolphin; every glimmer of foam was Binny’s face. The sea rose higher. The reefs disappeared. Great white-maned horses charged upon the island and broke at its feet. Thunder rolled overhead—not in separate peals, but continuously, as though an enormous wagon were passing across the roof of the world.

“He may drift to the lighthouse,” Ned said.

“He may have found a scull under the seat,” Charley said.

“There was no scull,” I answered.

“He may use a board.”

“There was no board.”

“Then keep your mouth shut!” Charley screamed, and struck me.

A moment later he put his arms around me and sobbed.

We had no shelter except the fisherman’s hut. By dusk the tide surrounded half of it, but the floor remained above water. We stumbled inside, soaked, shivering, and exhausted. The rusted stove would not draw, and our matches were wet. Ned found some dry grass under the stool; I had one match in the lining of my cap; by a miracle it burned. Soon a poor little fire flickered in the stove, smoking abominably yet saving us, perhaps, from death.

We had eaten everything except a handful of crackers which Charley found in his pocket. They were pulpy with seawater. We divided them.

Night descended like a curtain dropped before a tragedy whose ending the audience already knows. The chinks of the hut whitened with driven spray. Rain drummed on the roof; wind entered under the eaves and ran about the walls like an animal seeking us. At intervals a breaker struck the outer ledge with such violence that the floor trembled.

We spoke of Binny at first in hopeful phrases.

“The Dolphin has air-tanks.”

“She might wash ashore on the Point.”

“A fishing schooner could pick him up.”

“His uncle taught him what to do in a squall.”

Presently we ceased. Each hope, once uttered, sounded thinner than before. We knew the harbor. We knew how the ebb ran beyond the black buoy, straight toward the outer shoals where the waves broke in thirty feet of water. We had heard fishermen tell of strong men lost there within sight of land. Yet Binny was not a strong man. He was a little boy, eleven years old.

I sat with my back against the wall and saw his face each time I closed my eyes—calm at first, then frightened, then lifted above the green gulf with spray around his hair. I saw his hand passing the painter about the root. I saw the knot. Why had I not examined it? Why had we left the sculls ashore? Why had I urged the voyage when my mother spoke of weather? A child’s conscience is often as merciless as a judge and as unreasonable as a mob. I accused myself of everything, even of eating the last apple at dinner, as if Binny might have been saved by an apple.

Toward midnight the fire died. We huddled together under an old net which smelled of fish and mildew. Charley muttered in his sleep.

“Pull together,” he said. “You row like undertakers.”

Then he awoke with a cry.

Once we heard, above the storm, a thin wailing sound.

“That’s him!” I shouted.

We rushed out. The darkness met us with a cold, clammy touch. Nothing could be seen beyond the white rush of the nearest breakers.

“Binny!” we called.

Again came the cry, piercing and desolate.

A gull, driven inland by the storm, passed over the hut.

We went back without speaking.

How long that night was! Watches have since measured darker hours for me—hours of illness, failure, and death—but none has equaled it. Childhood does not know how to divide misery by experience. It has no yesterdays of sorrow with which to compare today, no knowledge that the sun will rise in spite of us. To those three drenched boys the world had ended. There was only the hut, the blackness, the roaring sea, and Binny forever falling beyond the ridge of a wave.

Near dawn the wind weakened. Rain continued to drip through the roof, but between the gusts we heard the separate beat of the surf. A gray light gathered at the doorway. The objects in the hut returned one by one—the stove, the broken stool, the lobster-pots, and Samuel Hobbs’s ship sailing eternally upon the wall.

Ned, who had been watching outside, suddenly shouted.

A sail moved in the harbor.

Then another appeared behind it, and a third—a yawl from the lighthouse, two fishing-boats, and presently the harbor-master’s launch, its black funnel smoking. Men stood in the bows, searching the water. The yawl turned toward us.

We ran down to the cove, waving and stumbling. As the boat grounded, a tall man sprang into the shallows before anyone could stop him.

It was Mr. Wallace.

I shall never forget his face. He had always seemed old to us because there were threads of gray in his beard; that morning he became old indeed.

“Where is my boy?” he asked.

No one answered.

He looked from one to another of us. His eyes settled upon me.

“Tom—where is Binny?”

“The boat broke loose,” I said. “He was in her. We tried—we swam—but the storm—”

My voice failed.

Mr. Wallace turned toward the open sea. He did not cry out. He merely took off his hat.

The fishermen searched all that day and the next. They followed the coast northward, dragged the channels, questioned incoming vessels, and watched the beaches. No trace was found. Hope, that obstinate weed, grew even in such barren ground. A schooner might have picked him up and continued to Portland. The Dolphin might have drifted behind Gull Rock. Binny might be lying ill in some farmhouse along the coast.

I was carried home in a blanket. My mother met me at the door and held me so tightly that I could not breathe. That night fever took possession of me. For more than a week I wandered in a world of green waves and slipping ropes. I rowed endlessly toward a boat I could never reach. Sometimes Binny sat beside my bed with the blue handkerchief around his neck; sometimes he called from under the floor.

On the ninth day I awoke in my own room and knew the pattern of the wallpaper. My mother was by the window.

“Have they found him?” I asked.

She came to the bed and pressed my head against her breast.

“No, Tom.”

Three days later the Dolphin was discovered overturned among the rocks at Saltmarsh Point, twelve miles north of Rivermouth. Her mast was broken; the air-tank had been stove in; the painter was still fast to the bow, with the dead root at its end. One of Binny’s shoes lay under the stern-seat. The blue handkerchief was gone.

They did not tell me this until I was strong enough to leave my room.

When I returned to school, Master Grimshaw made no reference to the expedition. He placed his hand on my shoulder as I entered, which was more terrible than any words could have been. Binny’s desk stood empty near the window. In its lid were his slate-pencil, a bit of twine, two chestnuts, and a folded paper addressed to me.

The paper contained a drawing of the Dolphin under sail. Beneath it he had written: TOM AT THE HELM WHEN WE ARE ALLOWED.

I could not read the sentence aloud.

Winter approached. The harbor took on its iron color; the boats were hauled up; the sand froze in the streets. Men ceased searching, though Mr. Wallace continued to walk the northern beaches after every tide.

Late in November, a coast-guardsman found Binny on a strip of sand beyond Saltmarsh Point.

He was brought home at dusk. The church bell tolled slowly while a cold rain fell upon Rivermouth. We boys did not see him. I am thankful for that. Death could not be permitted to alter the face I remembered above the green wave, young and fair and shining in its circle of spray.

They buried him on the hill overlooking the harbor. His stone was small, with a carved anchor at the top and the words:

**BENJAMIN WALLACE  
AGED 11 YEARS  
LOST AT SEA**

Charley, Ned, and I stood together at the grave. We had grown older in a night, yet we looked absurdly small in our black jackets. Mr. Wallace placed the blue handkerchief beneath the flowers. It had been found tangled in the Dolphin’s rigging after the boat was turned over.

The years moved on, as years have the hard-hearted habit of doing. Charley became a lawyer and lost his hair; Ned went west and grew rich in lumber; I left Rivermouth, returned, left it again, and learned to call many strange places home. The Dolphin rotted behind Mr. Ropes’s barn. The fisherman’s hut on Sandpeep was carried away in a winter gale. Even the black buoy has been replaced by a red iron one which rings mournfully whenever the tide turns.

But Binny has not changed.

We who survived became tall, loud, occupied men, with ledgers, appointments, disappointments, and gray hairs. We learned to conceal tenderness, to compromise with truth, to laugh when we were weary, and to pass old griefs in the street without appearing to know them. Binny escaped all that. He remains eleven years old, with clear eyes and a grave smile, holding a blue handkerchief in one hand while the enchanted sea opens behind him.

Sometimes, on September mornings, I walk to the old landing. The river shines as it shone that day; the measured dip of unseen sculls comes through the mist; a gull utters one lonely cry above the wharves. Then for an instant the vanished world returns entire—the yellow inside of the Dolphin, Charley’s crushed pie, smoke among the bayberries, warm sand under bare feet, and Binny floating on his back with his face tranquilly turned toward heaven.

Afterwards the wind changes. I hear the surf on the outer shoals, and childhood slips from me again like a boat whose painter has broken.

Ah, Binny, we called until the storm took our voices. We have been calling ever since, though the world has not heard us.

Poor little Binny Wallace!

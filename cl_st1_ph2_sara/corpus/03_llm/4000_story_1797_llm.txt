# The Long Trail Home

In the years before the American Revolution, Kentucky was neither an empty wilderness nor the undisputed possession of any single people. No great Indian nation had its permanent seat there, yet the tribes north of the Ohio regarded its forests as a common hunting ground, where Shawnee, Wyandot, Mingo, and others pursued the buffalo and deer from season to season. The white hunters who crossed the mountains found a land of extraordinary richness: rivers winding beneath giant sycamores, meadows where the cane grew above a horseman’s head, and salt licks trampled by innumerable beasts. To them it appeared a natural paradise awaiting the ax and plow; to the tribes it was an inheritance which strangers had no right to seize.

Thus every cabin raised south of the Ohio became a challenge, and every fresh path through the woods seemed to announce the coming of a multitude. The pioneers complained that the Indians attacked without warning; the Indians answered that the warning was already written in the felled trees, the trampled corn, and the smoke of the settlements. Violence followed violence, and it is certain that cruelty was not confined to either side.

Among the first and most famous of the hunters was Daniel Boone, a man whose name has become almost another word for the wilderness. He was not a loud man, nor one given to boasting. He spoke slowly, endured hardship without complaint, and found more pleasance beneath the open boughs than in any crowded town.

In 1769 Boone entered Kentucky with a small company and remained there through dangers that would have driven most men homeward. He was captured by Indians, escaped, lost companions, and for a time dwelt almost alone in that immense forest, with no roof save a bark shelter and no neighbor whom he could reach in a day’s march. Yet solitude did not terrify him. He watched the movement of game, studied the watercourses, and learned every secret by which a traveler might pass unseen.

When settlements began in earnest, Boone helped to mark the Wilderness Road through Cumberland Gap, and in 1775 he established Boonesborough on the Kentucky River. The fort was no imposing structure. It consisted chiefly of log cabins and palisades, but behind those rough timbers women baked bread, children played, rifles hung ready beside the doors, and a new commonwealth took root. Boone brought his family thither, and the presence of women and little ones declared more plainly than any proclamation that the pioneers intended to remain.

Then came the Revolutionary War. The British posts in the Northwest supplied and encouraged war parties against the Kentucky settlements, while the tribes, needing little persuasion, saw in the conflict an opportunity to sweep away the advancing border. Boonesborough, Harrodsburg, and the smaller stations were repeatedly threatened, and no man who stepped beyond the stockade could be sure that unseen eyes were not following him.

During the winter of 1777–78 the people of Boonesborough needed salt, without which their meat could not be preserved. Boone therefore led a party to the Blue Licks, where the precious mineral could be obtained. The work was slow, and the men, scattered about the place, were poorly prepared for a sudden attack.

In February, Boone was hunting apart from the others when warriors under the Shawnee chief Blackfish surprised and captured him. He might perhaps have attempted resistance, but he understood that such resistance would bring destruction upon his companions. By concealing the weakness of the salt-makers and speaking with his usual calmness, he persuaded them to surrender upon a promise that their lives should be spared.

Some blamed him afterward, as men safe behind walls have often blamed those compelled to decide in the presence of death; but Boone knew that one discharge of rifles would have ended in slaughter. His judgment saved the party.

The prisoners were taken across the Ohio and marched to Old Chillicothe. There Boone became the object of particular attention. Blackfish admired his skill and composure, and Boone was adopted into an Indian family according to a custom by which captives sometimes replaced relatives lost in war. His hair was plucked after the tribal fashion, he was washed and painted, and he received a new name. He joined in hunts, shared the food of the town, and bore himself so prudently that his captors began to trust him.

He did not attempt to outshoot them, though few could equal him with the rifle. If he brought down enough game to prove useful, he was careful not to excite envy by needless display. He appeared contented, and because he was treated with kindness, his apparent contentment did not seem strange.

The British at Detroit desired to ransom him, but Blackfish refused. Boone had become too valuable. Here was a tribute more honorable than any medal: those who had captured the great woodsman would not exchange him even for British goods.

Yet beneath Boone’s quiet countenance there was continual anxiety. His wife and children were at Boonesborough, and he knew they could not learn whether he was living or dead. Still he waited. A premature attempt to escape would not only cost him his life but might bring suspicion upon the other captives.

At length he discovered that a great attack upon Boonesborough was being prepared. Warriors assembled, arms were made ready, and the talk in the town left no doubt of their purpose. Boone saw that he must now venture everything. Watching his opportunity, he slipped away and turned southward through the woods.

Between him and Boonesborough lay about one hundred and sixty miles of wilderness. Behind him were pursuers who knew the country almost as well as he did. He carried little food, dared not fire his gun except in extremity, and crossed streams where the water would hide his trail. He traveled by night and by day, rested only when his strength failed, and ate such roots, berries, and scraps of game as could be obtained without delay. On the fifth day he reached the Kentucky River and entered the fort.

His appearance caused amazement, for many had long believed him dead. Yet the joy of his return was shadowed by bitter news. His wife, Rebecca, despairing of ever seeing him again, had taken the children back to North Carolina.

Boone listened in silence.

“They thought you dead,” someone told him.

“I reckon they had cause,” he answered.

That was nearly all. Such men did not commonly uncover their hearts before a company. He had crossed a savage wilderness to save his home, and he had found the home empty; but there was no time for grief. The fort must be strengthened, the men drilled, and the enemy’s attack prepared for. Afterward he went for his family and brought them once more into Kentucky.

Boone lived through many other adventures, hunted and fought, lost lands through defective titles, and saw towns rise where he had once followed buffalo traces. Civilization, which he had helped to bring over the mountains, pressed upon him until the woods seemed no longer wide enough. In his later years he moved westward to Missouri, still seeking that solitude which forever retreated before the settler’s ax.

Among Boone’s companions was another borderer whose adventures were even more terrible. This was Simon Kenton, known for a time as Simon Butler.

Kenton had fled from Virginia while still a young man because, after a violent struggle, he believed that he had killed an opponent. Since a charge of murder might follow him, he assumed the name of Butler and went into the western country. He was tall, powerful, swift of foot, and so perfectly at home in the woods that a bent leaf or a broken twig often told him what another man could not have discovered from a written page. He became a scout, hunter, and Indian fighter, and on one occasion saved Boone’s life.

In 1778 Kenton and two companions crossed the Ohio upon a reconnaissance. Near an Indian town they found a number of horses. Horse-stealing was then regarded upon the border as an achievement when performed against an enemy, though each side considered it an outrage when practiced by the other. Kenton and his friends secured several animals and began their return.

They reached the Ohio, but the horses would not take readily to the water. The men shouted, struck them, drove them back, and tried again, while precious minutes slipped away. Kenton could have escaped by abandoning the animals, yet he could not bear to lose so rich a prize. This stubbornness proved nearly fatal.

The pursuers came upon them suddenly. One companion was killed; another escaped; and Kenton, after a desperate effort to break through, was seized and bound. The warriors knew him, or at least knew his reputation, and they were delighted to have captured a man whose rifle had so often opposed them.

“You have been stealing horses,” one said.

“Yes, sir,” answered Kenton.

The reply was neither a plea nor a denial. He knew that excuses would avail him nothing.

His captors stripped him, tied his hands, placed him upon an unbroken horse, and fastened his feet beneath the animal’s body. Thus began a journey in which every mile brought some new torment. Branches lashed his face; his cramped limbs swelled; and when the party halted, he was thrown to the ground like a bundle. The warriors beat him with sticks and gun barrels, yet took care that he should not die too soon.

At the first town the inhabitants rushed out to see the celebrated prisoner. A gantlet was prepared. Two lines of men, women, and boys stood facing one another, armed with clubs, switches, and whatever else might inflict pain. Kenton was placed at one end and told to run toward the council house.

He sprang forward. Blows rained upon his head and shoulders; clubs struck his ribs; hands clutched at him, and twice he was nearly thrown. Yet his speed saved him. He dashed between the lines, tore himself from those who seized him, and reached the appointed shelter bleeding but upright.

Many men would have died in one such trial. Kenton was forced to run the gantlet again at other towns, for every village demanded its share in the punishment of so notable an enemy. Sometimes he escaped the heaviest blows by speed; sometimes he was felled and beaten until his captors feared they had killed him. When he recovered, the journey continued.

At one point he was condemned to be burned. The stake was set, wood gathered, and the council had apparently decided his fate. Kenton, who had faced rifles without shrinking, now saw before him a death deliberately prolonged. We are not told all that passed through his mind; but courage does not consist in feeling no fear, and it is enough that he neither begged nor disgraced himself.

Before the sentence could be carried out, some disagreement arose, and his death was postponed. He was moved to another town, where the same question was debated again.

During the march Kenton once broke loose. His guards had grown careless, and with a sudden wrench he freed himself and plunged into the forest. For a few glorious minutes he was again a woodsman among trees, choosing his own direction and hearing the cries grow faint behind him.

Then, as he crossed an open space, he encountered another party of warriors returning from a foray. They spread out, cut off his flight, and seized him. His brief taste of freedom only increased their anger, and he was beaten until he could scarcely stand.

He was taken to Wapatomica, where the council once more sentenced him to death. Among those present was Simon Girty, a white man who had cast his lot with the tribes and whom the settlers therefore called a renegade. Girty had known Kenton years before under his assumed name of Butler, but the bruised and swollen captive was scarcely recognizable.

Girty looked at him closely.

“Who are you?”

“Simon Butler.”

At the sound of the voice, or perhaps at some remembered expression, Girty started.

“Is it you?” he cried.

“It is what is left of me.”

The two had once been friends, and Girty now exerted all his influence to save the prisoner. He spoke in council, reminding the chiefs of his services and asking that Kenton’s life be granted to him. For a time friendship prevailed. The stake was abandoned, Kenton’s bonds were loosened, and Girty gave him food and clothing.

But the respite did not last. A war party returned after suffering defeat at the hands of the whites, and the sight of Kenton revived its fury. The warriors demanded a victim in payment for their dead. His former sentence was renewed, and he was ordered to be carried toward Sandusky, where the execution was to take place.

At another council the Mingo chief Logan interceded for him. Logan was a man of great dignity, whose family had been slain by whites in time of peace. His famous lament, declaring that no drop of his blood ran in any living creature, ought to be remembered wherever the sorrowful history of the border is told. Though he had cause to hate the settlers, he did not believe that vengeance upon a helpless captive was bravery.

His plea gained Kenton another delay, but not freedom. The prisoner was sent onward beneath guard, weak from wounds, scarred by the gantlets, and uncertain each morning whether he would see another sunset. Again the stake was prepared; again the crowd gathered; and again death appeared certain.

At Sandusky, however, a British agent appeared and demanded custody of him. The British authorities preferred prisoners who might be questioned or exchanged to ashes at an Indian stake. After a heated dispute the warriors yielded, and Kenton was taken northward to Detroit.

His captivity there was lenient when compared with what he had endured. He received food, his wounds healed, and he was allowed some liberty within the settlement, though always under watch. To a man who had been beaten through village after village, Detroit might almost have seemed a place of rest; but no prison is pleasant to one who can remember the boundless woods.

Kenton soon began to plan an escape. Two fellow prisoners agreed to accompany him. They secured a small quantity of food and such equipment as could be hidden, watched the habits of the guards, and waited for a favorable night.

When the opportunity came, they slipped away from Detroit and entered the wilderness. Before them stretched a journey of several hundred miles. Behind them lay the British post and the certainty of punishment if they were retaken. They avoided known trails, traveled through swamps, crossed rivers upon logs or crude rafts, and slept without fire whenever pursuit seemed near.

Their food was soon exhausted. A little game was obtained, but powder had to be spared, and hunger became their daily companion. Rain soaked their clothing; thorns tore their feet; insects tormented them in the lowlands. Sometimes they lost direction beneath a clouded sky and wandered half a day before Kenton recovered the proper course. Yet his old skill returned with his strength. Moss, water, slope, and wind became his guides.

For nearly a month the three men struggled southward. Then at last they came within sight of the falls of the Ohio and the settlement at Louisville. They entered gaunt, ragged, and scarcely recognizable, but free.

Most men, after such an ordeal, would have avoided the frontier forever. Kenton did not. He returned to scouting and fighting, and for years his knowledge of the country remained of great value to the settlements. The contest continued until 1794, when General Anthony Wayne defeated the confederated tribes at Fallen Timbers, breaking organized resistance in the Ohio country.

Thereafter Kenton settled in Ohio. By one of those strange turns in which history seems almost to jest with men, he lived in later years upon or near the very ground where he had once expected to be burned. Fields spread over the place; cabins appeared; roads crossed the old traces; and children played where armed crowds had formerly assembled to witness his death.

His last years were not without poverty and disappointment, for the nation has too often forgotten its border defenders after their usefulness was past. Yet he lived to see his services honored, and visitors came to hear him speak of Boone, Girty, Logan, and the terrible road to Sandusky. He died peacefully at the age of eighty-one.

Boone and Kenton belonged to the same age, though they met its perils in different fashion. Boone was silent, patient, and ever drawn toward the farther forest; Kenton was impetuous, enduring, and repeatedly cast by his own daring into the hands of death. Both were children of the border, where courage was measured not by words but by the miles a man could travel hungry, the blows he could survive, and the calmness with which he could look upon an enemy.

Yet we ought also to remember the cause beneath their adventures. The wilderness through which they moved was not vacant. Every new station displaced a hunting path; every road foretold farms and towns; and the tribes fought not merely from love of war but because they saw their country passing from them. The pioneers, in turn, believed that they carried homes and civilization westward, and defended their wives and children with desperate resolution. Thus brave men stood opposed to brave men, and each called justice by a different name.

The old hunting grounds have long since become commonwealths. The cane has vanished, the buffalo traces are roads, and steamboats and bridges have conquered rivers that Boone crossed upon logs or by swimming. But the names remain. Boone is remembered as the solitary hunter forever moving toward the sunset; Kenton as the type of the frontier fighter, beaten but not broken, condemned but not destroyed. One sought peace in the wilderness and found conflict following him; the other lived amid conflict and, after innumerable escapes, found peace at last.

Let us call him Max Z., though the name proves nothing. There have been many Maxes, and the alphabet has buried more hearts than history can catalogue. Better still, let us call him Mr. N+1, because before him stood Mr. N, who likewise considered himself the first genuine lover, and after him will come Mr. N+2, who will make the same discovery with improved vocabulary. Max had no special peculiarities. He was young, ardent, poor enough to borrow money without shame and rich enough to despise it, and he possessed that magnificent confidence by which a man mistakes the temperature of his own blood for a new law of the universe.

He loved a woman.

Naturally, he believed that no one had loved before him. Adam may have experienced a rural attachment to Eve, poets may have made certain experiments in rhyme, and respectable citizens may have perpetuated their affections in marriage registers, family portraits, newspaper scandals, and children; but these were preliminary sketches. Max’s love was the completed work. It was as strong as death, as pure as an unwritten page, and as unprecedented as sunrise appears to a man who has overslept.

He also had a friend.

This friend was trusted with all that could be spoken and much that ought not to have been spoken: the beloved’s smallest phrases, the sacred angle of her head, the theological significance of her glove, Max’s plans for eternity, and the exact sum he needed until Thursday. Max believed in friendship no less absolutely than in love. Indeed, the two beliefs supported each other like two drunkards walking home, each seeming upright because the other leaned against him.

On Monday—or perhaps Tuesday; calendars are accurate only concerning unimportant events—Max entered a familiar room without knocking. There he saw his beloved kissing his friend.

The kiss was not long. Such things seldom require the time they destroy. Her hand rested on the friend’s shoulder; the friend’s eyes were closed; and on her face Max saw erased, in one careless stroke, the entire inscription he had read there. He waited for an explanation, not because any explanation could help him, but because the condemned man is entitled to hear the sentence. The woman cried. The friend spoke rapidly. Both assured him that matters were not what they appeared to be, which was true in the sense that they appeared simple and were, in reality, commonplace.

Max went away.

He conducted, that evening, a complete liquidation of his spiritual property. Love was declared fraudulent, friendship insolvent, memory a hostile creditor, and the future a vacant office whose rent he refused to pay. Having settled these affairs, he decided to die.

His method need not detain us. Methods are the clerks of despair: rope, bullet, water, poison—each stamps the same document in a different ink. Max selected one with sufficient solemnity and insufficient competence. He awoke later among physicians, policemen, and offended relatives. Medical experts established through pulse, breath, reflex, pupil, and several other psycho-physiological acts that he was alive. Max disputed their conclusion. They answered that a living man’s denial of life was itself evidence for the prosecution.

Thus began the first intermission.

For weeks he ate because food was placed before him, slept because fatigue bullied him, and answered questions because silence attracted attention. His soul was dead. That is to say, he continued to remember, reason, suffer, and resent, but none of these activities seemed to him sufficient proof of a soul. The earth, meanwhile, turned light-mindedly around the sun, exposing first one hemisphere and then the other, while the moon followed with the pale devotion of a neglected admirer. No star stopped. No government resigned. The newspapers found more recent tragedies.

Then, one morning, Max noticed a laborer sharing bread with a child who was not his own. On another day he heard that strangers had entered a burning house to rescue an old woman. Later he read of a physician remaining in a plague-stricken town after wealthier citizens had escaped. These incidents were small, badly advertised, and mixed with the usual thefts, murders, speeches, and market reports. Yet something in Max stirred.

His faith in one woman had died, and his faith in one friend had been buried beside it; but mankind, being conveniently vast and mostly unknown to him, remained available for worship.

Max revived.

He now understood—or believed he understood, which is the more active condition—that personal happiness was a childish toy. Let others marry, quarrel over lampshades, kiss in doorways, produce children, and seek immortality in birth records. He renounced the private paradise that had expelled him and devoted himself to the public heaven. Human beings suffered, he reasoned, because they were irrational, ignorant, and governed by evil wills. Therefore they required truth, instruction, discipline, and, if necessary, a firm hand applied in a loving manner.

The logic was faultless until one included human beings.

At first Max spoke quietly. He taught patience to the angry, courage to the frightened, and justice to those who profited from injustice. The poor listened because he had nothing to sell them. The young followed because he spoke of tomorrow as though he had inspected it personally. Even the comfortable admired him for several weeks, believing his doctrines were intended for their neighbors.

He became famous.

His words traveled farther than his intentions. One group printed them in gold; another burned them in public squares. Admirers called him the conscience of the age. Opponents called him a diseased ambition wearing the mask of pity. Scholars proved that his ideas had all been expressed previously by Greeks, prophets, heretics, and three obscure Germans. Max replied that hunger was also ancient, but dinner remained useful.

When persuasion failed, his followers demanded action. Max resisted them, then led them; condemned violence, then justified it; carried an olive branch in one hand and, when circumstances became historically urgent, a sword in the other. At times he walked unarmed into hostile crowds and subdued them by courage. At other times fires burned behind him, prisons filled before him, and widows cursed his name without knowing whether he had caused or attempted to prevent their sorrow.

He was praised, condemned, arrested, released, exiled, recalled, crowned with flowers, pelted with stones, and examined by committees. He lived in palaces without owning a bed and in cellars where kings sent messengers. Once a judge sentenced him for disturbing the peace; once a general decorated him for restoring it; both referred to the same afternoon.

Years passed—seven, seventeen, or perhaps only the prolonged Wednesday of public life. Max grew thin. His hair whitened, though his convictions retained their original color. He still believed that wisdom, if spoken clearly enough and supported by adequate organization, could establish peace on earth. He saw mankind as a sick giant: feverish, powerful, confused, yet curable. He did not notice that every physician who approached the giant was eventually mistaken for an insect and slapped.

Then came the panic.

A rumor ran through the cities that destruction was near. No one knew who had begun it. Some said Max’s enemies had whispered it; others said his disciples had shouted it; a third party demonstrated statistically that nothing had been said at all. Nevertheless doors were barred, warehouses emptied, soldiers summoned, and peaceful citizens armed themselves against other peaceful citizens who had armed themselves first. It was like a crowded theatre where someone imagines a cry of fire: the audience tramples the exits, overturns the lamps, and then identifies the man who begged them not to run as the incendiary.

Max stood before the crowd and pleaded for reason.

They heard command.

He begged them to lay down weapons.

They heard threat.

He warned them that fear itself would bring the ruin they feared.

They heard confession.

Soon the red flower of evil opened everywhere—on walls, on snow, on children’s clothing—and each person insisted that another had planted it. Houses burned. Bridges fell. The earth continued its coquettish revolutions, showing the moon first this battlefield, then that cemetery, as though asking which ornament suited her better.

At last the people united. Not in peace, of course, nor in wisdom, nor in any of the difficult virtues Max had preached, but in hatred of Max. He was declared responsible for the panic because he had predicted it, responsible for the violence because he had opposed it, and responsible for human misery because he had promised that misery could end. His oldest followers testified against him with special eloquence; betrayal, when practiced publicly, becomes civic duty.

Max did not defend himself well. He looked at the faces before him—faces he had trusted in the plural because trusting one face had once proved dangerous—and saw the old kiss repeated on a continental scale. Humanity, his last beloved, had embraced his enemies while resting one hand upon his shoulder.

His soul died again.

This second death differed from the first. Then he had lost personal love and preserved mankind; now he lost mankind and had no larger abstraction to inherit. He could not appeal to the stars, which were remote; to God, concerning whom committees disagreed; or to history, which accepts every crime provided it has occurred. The officials observed his silence and concluded that it showed neither repentance nor insanity, but an aggravated form of thought.

They sentenced him to life in solitary confinement.

The cell had been designed by enlightened experts. It was narrow enough to prevent walking, low enough to discourage standing, and furnished with a bench arranged so that no adult body could rest upon it without negotiating separately with the spine, knees, neck, and feet. A white lamp burned day and night behind thick glass. Darkness, the authorities explained, encourages unhealthy fantasies; therefore Max was to receive light without interruption until death.

No window opened outward. Food arrived through a slot. Waste departed through another. The walls were smooth, white, washable, and indifferent. No voice addressed him. Even the guard’s steps were softened by a corridor constructed according to the latest humane principles.

At first Max counted hours. When hours lost their boundaries, he counted meals. When meals varied, he counted breaths, heartbeats, cracks that were not cracks, and the slight tremor of light upon the wall. He tried to sleep with his sleeve over his eyes, but the light entered through cloth and blood, painting the lids a reddish white. He dreamed of night as thirsty men dream of water.

His body changed. Muscles weakened, joints stiffened, and his feet, unable either to walk or lie comfortably, swelled like neglected objects belonging to someone else. Physicians visited at intervals. They measured him, tapped him, questioned him, and reported that his adaptation was remarkable. One asked whether he wished to die. Max did not answer. The physician recorded “indifference,” because science must write something in every box.

Why did he not die?

He could have refused food. He could have broken his head against the wall, though the wall had been padded precisely against such philosophical objections. He could have surrendered the mind first and let the body follow like an obedient servant. Men have died for love, honor, boredom, embarrassment, unpaid debts, and opinions about the shape of heaven. Max had lost love, honor, freedom, society, darkness, movement, usefulness, and even the elementary pleasure of turning his face away from the day. Surely the account was complete.

Yet he ate.

He bent and straightened his fingers to preserve their motion. He repeated words so that language would not dissolve. He reconstructed streets, voices, clouds, and the beloved’s face, though he no longer hated her. He remembered his friend, his judges, his disciples, the crowds. Hatred required too much furniture, and his inner room had become bare.

Outside, years accumulated. Governments changed names. Children learned distorted lessons about the monster Max and the saint Max, sometimes from the same textbook. Monuments were proposed, postponed, erected, and pulled down. The woman he had loved died; the friend followed; generations kissed, betrayed, forgave, preached, panicked, imprisoned, and called themselves unprecedented.

Inside, the lamp remained.

It illuminated everything and explained nothing. Under its merciless whiteness Max sat surrounded by an inward darkness no instrument could penetrate. He no longer believed in unique love. He no longer believed in perfect friendship. He no longer believed that wisdom could command mankind into happiness. Perhaps he did not believe in himself. All the great possessions of his spirit had been confiscated, broken, or returned stamped “undeliverable.”

But he was a man.

And each time the slot opened, he reached for the bread. Each time sleep released him, he opened his eyes. Each time his heart prepared another small, monotonous stroke, he permitted it.

Why?

He hoped.

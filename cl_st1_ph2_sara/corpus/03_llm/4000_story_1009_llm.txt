# THE KING OF THE PASTERS

If I had an enemy—and I trust I have none, except a gentleman in Camden Town who borrowed my umbrella in a thunderstorm and has ever since regarded me with the estrangement natural to injured innocence—I would not seek to punish him by legal proceedings, private detectives, anonymous letters, or any of those clumsy instruments by which revenge commonly blunts its own edge. I would hand him over to the advertising walls of London.

If he had committed a secret fraud, and, walking forth in the morning with his hat slightly over his eyes, were to behold upon the opposite house the enormous words, WHERE IS THE MONEY? I should leave him there.

If, recovering himself, he turned into a quieter street, and discovered at the corner, in letters of a blood-red complexion, THE GUILTY MAN TREMBLES! I should not interfere.

If he entered an omnibus, and found above the conductor’s head, A STARTLING DISCLOSURE—READ THE CONFESSION! I should consider that the moral machinery was in excellent order.

If he alighted upon a bridge, and saw, pasted upon a parapet, beneath a representation of a pale female pointing with both hands, HE KNOWS THE REASON! I should acknowledge the justice of Providence.

If he took refuge in a railway carriage, and, lifting his eyes from the floor, encountered SECRET KEYS! THE LOCK FORCED! NO ESCAPE! I should admit the uselessness of human flight.

If, at last, he hurried into the country, and there, on a peaceful barn, among haystacks, cows, and the innocent perfume of clover, beheld, in black capitals six feet high, HAVE YOU TOLD YOUR WIFE? I should feel that London had stretched out an arm after him, and that the arm was long enough.

There is no accusation so particular as one which accuses nobody in particular. A charge addressed by name may be answered, disproved, or knocked down; but a vague question, printed in purple, and repeated upon eleven successive walls, enters the conscience like a cold draught under a door. What does THE DAY APPROACHES mean? Whose day? For what? At whose expense? Why does REMEMBER THURSDAY appear at the end of one’s street on Monday, increase in size on Tuesday, turn yellow on Wednesday, and vanish on Friday without furnishing any explanation? Why is a gentleman in a Roman helmet perpetually warning me to BEWARE OF IMITATIONS? Which of us is the imitation? Am I genuine? Is he? And if neither of us be genuine, what becomes of society?

I have sometimes fancied that the hand which wrote upon the wall at Belshazzar’s feast belonged to the earliest branch of the bill-posting profession; that the interpretation was supplied by a rival contractor; and that the royal dissatisfaction which followed arose less from the message than from its having been posted without permission upon palace property. Certain it is that walls have spoken ever since, and that London, being nearly all wall, is the most talkative city in creation.

While these reflections occupied me one damp afternoon, I came upon a building in the neighbourhood of Whitechapel which appeared to have been posted out of existence.

It had formerly been a warehouse. I collected this fact from a crooked iron crane projecting from the upper story, and from three letters—A, R, and E—which still clung over a doorway, though whether they had once formed WAREHOUSE, HARDWARE, PREPARED, or BEWARE, I could not determine. The building had no longer any separate brick, beam, shutter, or sill. All had passed into paper.

The lower part was swathed in old theatrical announcements: ladies in white muslin fled eternally from gentlemen with daggers; ships foundered every evening at seven; lost heirs returned at eight; villains repented at nine; and the whole company appeared again on Saturday afternoon at reduced prices. Above these were patent medicines, their virtues turned inward by rain; auction sales of estates which had perhaps since become continents; lectures on Memory, now illegible; exhibitions of Two-Headed Persons, of whom only half a head remained; and portraits of candidates for Parliament, each candidate having been pasted over the mouth by a cheaper candidate, and over the eyes by soap.

The building bulged as if afflicted with a paper dropsy. Its corners were rounded by paste. Its wounds were poulticed with announcements. Long strips hung from it like neglected bandages; damp bills curled out from the surface like shavings from an ancient cheese; and wherever a rent exposed the interior, there appeared, not brick, but more paper—blue beneath yellow, red beneath blue, black beneath red—descending through commercial ages to a geological depth at which I expected to find the first proclamation of the Norman Conquest, with WILLIAM THE CONQUEROR, NO CONNECTION WITH ANY OTHER HOUSE, printed below it.

A scrap at the summit, surviving wind, weather, and at least three changes of government, waved with the melancholy distinction of a last flag upon a wreck. Its remaining message was simply TRY—

What I was to try had perished. I might have tried patience, cod-liver oil, elastic boots, a new system of bookkeeping, a Turkish bath, matrimony, or the sixpenny dinner at a neighbouring chop-house. The building made no further recommendation. Yet the solitary TRY affected me. There was courage in it. In the midst of ruin, dissolution, mildew, municipal regulations, and total ignorance of the object, it still advised exertion.

I was preparing to record this moral in my pocket-book when a disturbance arose in the street.

First came two boys running backward, with that miraculous confidence in Providence which belongs exclusively to boys running backward in crowded thoroughfares. Then came a dog, barking as if officially retained for the occasion. Then a cabman, whose horse had been brought to a philosophical pause; then a costermonger, whose barrow formed a temporary barricade; then three policemen, all looking in different directions; and then a procession of advertising vans.

They were tall, square, painted vehicles, roofed and panelled with printed calamity. One announced:

> APPALLING MYSTERY IN ISLINGTON!  
> THE CARPET SPEAKS!

Another proclaimed:

> FRIGHTFUL COLLISION!  
> NINE HUNDRED PERSONS ALARMED!  
> FULL PARTICULARS!

A third, in funeral black, bore:

> THE DUKE’S LAST WORD!  
> EXTRAORDINARY SCENE AT BREAKFAST!

A fourth demanded:

> WHO IS THE WOMAN IN GREEN?

A fifth replied to nobody:

> SHE HAS BEEN FOUND!

Thus they passed, these fatal cars, these rolling temples of suspense, these wheeled Ministers of Fate, carrying terror at three miles an hour and obstructing the ordinary traffic in order that the public might learn what had obstructed somebody else. The drivers sat high and unmoved, like executioners who had heard every appeal. Behind them, printers’ boys distributed little slips; shopmen came to doors; maidservants leaned from windows; and a stout gentleman, attempting to read three vans at once, encountered a lamp-post with an emphasis which supplied a minor paragraph of his own.

In the last vehicle I perceived a human figure reclining behind the driver, where a narrow platform had been contrived among ropes, brushes, paste-pots, and folded bills. He wore a brown coat with large buttons, a scarlet comforter, and a hat which had clearly disputed many questions with many walls. His face shone; his head was close and compact; his eye was bright; his wink was moist; his speech, when he addressed the driver, was quick; and his whole person had a ready air, as if he could paste an announcement upon the moon, provided somebody held the ladder.

He smoked a short pipe, and held in one hand a small metal vessel. The ease of his attitude amid the surrounding horrors impressed me profoundly. Nine hundred persons might be alarmed, the carpet might speak, the Duke might utter his final word over the muffins, and the Woman in Green might be found every half-mile from Whitechapel to Hyde Park Corner; but this man remained tranquil.

I followed.

It is not my habit to follow strange vehicles. I state this because the practice, if generally attributed to me, might occasion inconvenience. But there are moments when a private individual is selected by Destiny as its unpaid investigator, and I felt that this was one of them. Moreover, the van travelled slowly, being repeatedly stopped by carts, omnibuses, drays, perambulators, and the police, so that I kept pace with it without any display of athletic capacity.

At Aldgate it halted altogether.

The reclining person looked down at me.

“You’ve kep’ along of us pretty steady,” he said.

“I have ventured,” I replied, “to accompany your progress at a respectful distance.”

“Want a lift?”

I accepted. He shifted a bundle of THE MURDERER’S BOOT DISCOVERED, made room beside the paste-pot, and extended a hand of such adhesive character that our introduction was not immediately dissolved.

“Collector?” he asked suspiciously.

“In a humble way.”

“Taxes?”

“No.”

“Water?”

“Certainly not.”

“Gas?”

“Never.”

“Paving rate?”

“I hope I have not the appearance.”

“Drainage? Sewers? Poor rate? Church rate? Queen’s taxes? Income?”

“None of these. I collect impressions.”

He looked relieved, though doubtful.

“Queer things to collect,” said he. “Where d’ye keep ’em?”

“In memory.”

“Ah,” he returned, after considering the answer. “No rent.”

I explained that I was interested in the bill-posting art; that his appearance suggested extensive authority in that profession; and that, if he would permit me, I should be proud to inscribe his name upon the tablets of my recollection.

“Name’s Tapp,” he said. “Solomon Tapp. But in the trade they calls me the King of the Bill-Stickers.”

I removed my hat.

“Your Majesty!”

“Don’t,” said the King. “You’ll lose it under them wheels.”

His Majesty offered me the metal vessel. It contained rum-and-water, warm, sweet, and of a potency which I then attributed to royal custom. I drank to the prosperity of his dominions. He drank to mine, though I possessed none, except a bedroom on the second floor and a disputed umbrella.

“Thirty-two year,” said the King, filling his pipe. “Boy and man, thirty-two year on the walls. There an’t a better bit of dead timber between Bow and Brentford than I don’t know the value of; nor a blank gable from Kennington to Kentish Town that I couldn’t price in the dark.”

“You speak,” I said, “like a sovereign acquainted with every province of his realm.”

“Province!” said he. “I know every crack. That’s more.”

The driver cracked his whip; the van moved; the Appalling Mystery in Islington rolled behind us; and I entered upon my audience.

The King began with the infancy of his art.

“Bills was little once,” said he. “Very little. A man could carry a day’s work under his arm and not know it. ‘Lost, a Mare.’ ‘Found, a Watch.’ ‘To Be Sold, a Quantity of Tallow.’ ‘Run Away, a ’Prentice.’ That was about the size of ’em. No pictures, no colours, no women flying out of winders, no lions, no ships, no gentlemen with their insides painted red to show where the medicine goes. Plain words. If a man lost a dog, he said dog. He didn’t say ASTONISHING DISAPPEARANCE OF A DISTINGUISHED MEMBER OF SOCIETY.”

Here the van stopped so suddenly that I was thrown against THE DUKE’S LAST WORD, which proved softer than might have been expected.

“Then come the lotteries,” continued His Majesty, when I had resumed my seat. “That made the business. State Lotteries. Fortunes for everybody; blanks for most. ‘Twenty Thousand Pounds for a Shilling!’—that sort. The walls was all gold palaces, coaches-and-six, bags of money, happy families, and old gentlemen fainting because their tickets had come up. Ah! them was posters. You could see a number across the river in a fog.”

“The State,” I observed, “appears to have been the father of modern advertising.”

“And abolished its own child,” said the King. “Just like a father.”

He informed me that in those prosperous times companies of stickers moved through London like military bodies. There were advance men to select the ground, carriers to bring the bills, paste men, ladder men, brush men, guards, and occasional negotiators with householders, constables, watchmen, and persons of a pugilistic disposition. The bills increased from crown to double crown, from double crown to quad, from quad to dimensions at which ordinary arithmetic withdrew in alarm. A proclamation which had once occupied a board presently covered a house; what covered a house next year required two houses and a stable; and there were rumours of an advertiser who intended to take the north side of the Thames entire, but failed through a difference with his paper-maker.

“With size,” said His Majesty, “come implements. In the little-bill time a chap had a brush and a pot. Now you wants a ladder, a long brush, a short brush, a dabber, a roller, a scraper, a line, a hook, a bucket, a barrow, and judgment. Judgment most. Paste’ll stick paper, but it won’t stick sense into a fool.”

“What,” I asked, “is a dabber?”

“This,” said the King, lifting an implement resembling a flat, padded club. “For making the joints lay. Some young ’uns uses their sleeves. That’s why you knows ’em at tea-time.”

“And a tramper?”

“A man as goes the rounds on foot, looking for places, patching damages, and seeing rivals don’t get liberties. Good tramper’ll smell a blank wall through two streets and a brewery.”

“And black-wash?”

“Covering up what’s underneath when it’s too strong to paste over. You black-wash a board, then post fresh. Same as Parliament.”

I made a note of this constitutional analogy.

The King divided the profession into branches. There were the theatre stickers, who worked late and considered themselves artists; the auction stickers, who travelled rapidly and dealt in red flags; the election stickers, active for three weeks and political for none; the shop-bill men, steady but despised; the country trampers, who carried paste in bladders and slept where they could; the railway men, who cultivated bridges, arches, and approaches; the fly-posters, who descended upon forbidden surfaces by night; the posting contractors, who owned stations; and the board men, who let spaces by the week, month, quarter, or degree of public impatience.

“Every branch,” said he, “looks down on every other branch. That keeps a trade respectable.”

His Majesty’s own apprenticeship had begun at the age of eleven, under an uncle who possessed three ladders, a donkey, and a right—fiercely disputed—to a wooden fence near Sadler’s Wells. His first duty was to sit upon the paste-tub while it was conveyed through the streets.

“Why sit upon it?” I asked.

“To keep the lid down.”

“A position of trust.”

“More trust than comfort. Paste was warm in winter and warmer in summer. Once the donkey bolted, tub upset, and I was stuck to a butcher’s shutter for twenty minutes. Uncle posted over me, being pressed for time. Left my boots showing. Crowd thought it was a new tragedy.”

From this adhesive foundation he had risen by perseverance, combat, sobriety at selected intervals, and a capacity for estimating walls. He had carried bills; mixed paste; held ladders in high winds; negotiated narrow parapets; posted over wells, under arches, beside canals, behind slaughter-houses, around circuses, and upon the temporary fronts of buildings which vanished before payment could be obtained.

“Paste,” he said, “is flour, water, boiling, and experience. Too thin, the bill slips. Too thick, it humps. Too cold, it sulks. Too hot, it scalds your mate. In frost you salt it; in wet you pray over it; in summer you keep the boys out, for they’ll dab one another till there’s murder.”

I inquired whether there were rules concerning the occupation of walls.

“Rules!” cried the King. “Hundreds. Written nowhere and broke everywhere. If a station’s yours, it’s yours till another man proves it isn’t. If you posts a clean board regular, decent firms leaves you. If you neglects it, they covers you. If a wall falls down, rights falls with it. If it’s built up again, there’s opinions. If a landlord says no bills, that generally means whose bills? If he paints NO BILLS, you don’t cover the words—not unless it’s a very pressing job.”

The territories of the principal companies, he explained, were mapped not by parish, ward, or borough, but by habitual possession. One firm held nearly every available surface from the Elephant and Castle to the Obelisk, except a cooper’s yard and the back of a dissenting chapel. Another commanded the approaches to the western theatres. A third ruled Clerkenwell, but dared not cross a certain pump. The City was divided by lanes, courts, markets, tavern signs, and ancient grudges. A hoarding at Holborn might belong to one contractor at the eastern end and another at the western, the middle being neutral ground where bills of public charity were permitted, though generally covered before the charity could benefit.

“Bethnal Green,” said the King, counting on his fingers, “was Snobbs’s. Stepney was old Crawler’s, except brewery property. Borough was the United Metropolitan, till the split. Lambeth was mixed. Paddington was mostly railway. Chelsea had too many painted walls. Westminster was politics and punishment. Covent Garden was theatres, pills, and lost dogs. Wapping was ships and sales. Islington was everything.”

“And who maintained these boundaries?”

“We did.”

“By arbitration?”

“Sometimes.”

“And at other times?”

“Ladders.”

He related the celebrated Battle of Seven Dials.

A new company, calling itself the Imperial Universal Posting Association, Limited—though its empire consisted of two handcarts and a room over a fried-fish shop—had attempted to seize a valuable hoarding long held by the King’s employers. They arrived at two in the morning with six men, twelve pots, twenty-four sheets of a foreign opera, and one constable who had misunderstood the nature of the engagement. The King’s party, warned by a pie-man, advanced from St. Giles’s with ladders.

“We come round one corner,” said His Majesty, “and they come round another. Both stops. Our foreman says, ‘That’s our board.’ Their foreman says, ‘Prove it.’ Our foreman says, ‘We will.’ Then they puts up a ladder; we pulls it down. We puts up two; they shoves ’em sideways. Their paste-boy empties a bucket over our carrier. Our carrier, being hot-tempered, puts him head-first into a roll of OTELLO. Constable says keep the peace. Somebody pastes the constable to the hoarding. Then it begins.”

“What begins?”

“The discussion.”

The discussion lasted nineteen minutes, employed ladders as quarterstaves, brushes as maces, paste as artillery, and bills as bandages. When reinforcements arrived, the constable had upon his back the complete title of an opera, so that he appeared officially connected with the performance. Seven men were arrested, three escaped through a public-house, one remained stuck in a doorway, and the contested hoarding was accidentally torn down by all parties, thus terminating the dispute on equitable principles.

The magistrate fined each combatant five shillings and warned them against obstructing the streets.

“Which,” said the King, “was fair enough, only the streets had obstructed us first.”

There had been other wars. The Whitechapel Pasting Affair arose from an auction bill being placed half an inch over a medicine bill. The Camberwell Brush Riot began with an alleged trespass upon a stable door and ended when the horse kicked down the door. The Great Northern Junction Case occupied two courts, fourteen witnesses, and a surveyor, though the wall in question was demolished before judgment. At Hammersmith, two rival crews posted over each other for six continuous hours, until the accumulation projected so far into the road that the parish authorities removed it as a dangerous building.

“Competition,” I remarked, “has enlarged the profession.”

“Competition,” replied His Majesty, “has enlarged the bills and shortened the wages.”

Wages, it appeared, varied by branch, season, skill, weather, altitude, and the conscience of the employer. A steady man might receive so much a week; a tramper so much a day; a country hand so much a mile; a ladder man an extra allowance for danger, unless danger was considered included; and a fly-poster payment by the hundred, provided he was not caught, in which case his time before the magistrate was his own.

“Boys gets little,” said the King, “but they eats paste.”

“Surely not?”

“Not purposeful. Sandwich in one pocket, brush in another—things mixes. I’ve knowed a boy take a bite out of THE BEAUTIFUL COUNTESS and never notice till he come to the word ‘continued.’”

The van turned through Leadenhall Street. Around us rose warehouses, banks, taverns, offices, churches, alleys, and scaffolds; and upon every permitted, unpermitted, temporary, permanent, visible, half-visible, upright, sloping, wooden, brick, stone, tin, or canvas surface, commerce cried aloud. PROFESSOR BRONCHIO restored the lungs. MADAME CELESTINA read the future. MOSES AND SONS clothed the present. DR. GRUBB removed the consequences of the past. A lecture asked, ARE WE CIVILISED? A circus replied with SIX PERFORMING BEARS. A religious tract announced THE END OF ALL THINGS beside a notice that the premises would reopen on Monday.

I pointed these out to the King as proofs of the greatness of his empire.

He shook his head.

“Not what it was.”

“Surely, Sire, your dominion surrounds us.”

“Quantity,” said he. “Not quality. Look at that.”

He indicated a poster containing seventeen lines, three alphabets, two portraits, a list of prices, four addresses, and a testimonial from a deceased clergyman.

“Bad,” he said. “Crowded. A bill should strike. Three lines—four at most. Name. Thing. Place. There you are. A man walking don’t want a family history. Give him:

> BLOOD!  
> AT THE SURREY!  
> TO-NIGHT!

“He’ll remember it. Or:

> LOST HEIRESS!  
> FOUND IN A WELL!  
> BOXES THREE SHILLINGS!

“That’s business. But the new chaps puts in everything: author, actors, plot, opinions of the press, where to get the omnibus, and a small map of Europe. Before you’ve read half, you’re in another parish.”

“Yet,” I said, “the modern advertiser may fear ambiguity.”

“Ambiguity’s money,” replied the King. “Tell ’em all, they stays home. Tell ’em nothing, they comes to ask.”

This maxim, though delivered between a paste-pot and a bundle of patent-corset announcements, appeared to me worthy of Machiavelli.

The decline of the old art, His Majesty continued, had several causes. First, the abolition of the State Lotteries deprived walls of their most lavish patron. Then came new regulations, improved streets, respectable squares, glazed tiles, iron railings, plate glass, police supervision, and a general conspiracy among architects to erect buildings on which paste would not take. Old markets disappeared; narrow courts were opened; wooden fences gave way to stone; vacant lots became stations; and landlords, once satisfied with a gallon of ale for the side of a house, discovered the principle of rent.

“London’s getting smooth,” the King complained. “That’s the ruin of us. Give me an old wall—soft brick, cracked mortar, a bit of damp, top leaning forward. That wall loves paper. New walls rejects it. Stucco’s proud. Granite’s heartless. Iron’s no feelings. Glass is insult.”

There were also official notices: STICK NO BILLS, POST NO BILLS, BILL-STICKERS WILL BE PROSECUTED, OFFENDERS WILL BE GIVEN INTO CUSTODY. These, being themselves bills, were in His Majesty’s view traitors to the profession.

“And councils,” he continued, “makes regulations. Gentlemen gets up and says posters disfigures the metropolis. Same gentleman stands for election next month and wants his name on every pigsty from here to Greenwich. ‘TAPP FOR PURITY!’ ‘TAPP FOR ECONOMY!’ ‘TAPP, THE PEOPLE’S FRIEND!’—ten thousand of each, and don’t cover the other side’s bills till they’re dry, because it tears.”

The King had once served such a candidate, an alderman of stern civic principles who publicly denounced visual pollution and privately ordered his portrait placed at the entrance to every gin-shop in the ward. The portrait represented him with one hand inside his coat and the other resting upon a book marked REFORM. Rain enlarged his nose, frost split his principles, and a rival poster supplied him with the body of a dancing woman. Nevertheless he was elected.

“Posters didn’t do it,” said the King. “He bought suppers.”

As he spoke, His Majesty produced from beneath the seat a long folded document, greasy at the edges and magnificently tied with string. This he called the Royal Scroll. It contained, in his own handwriting and several inks, a record of memorable stations, vanished walls, distinguished campaigns, rates of pay, recipes for paste, names of dishonest contractors, and deaths in the profession.

He read portions aloud.

“‘Old Brick Gable, Blue Anchor Yard. Forty foot by twenty. Good dry station. Took twelve doubles. Lost by rebuilding, 1847.’

“‘Fence opposite Minor Theatre. Fine for reds. Owner partial to rum. Destroyed by gale.’

“‘Dead wall behind Fish Market. Shared with Pounce and Company after dispute, each alternate week, except Christmas.’

“‘Bill Jenkins, fell off ladder through looking at balloon. Broke arm. Balloon not worth seeing.’

“‘Samuel Nib, pasted by mistake over area door while cook inside. Settled with household for eighteenpence.’

“‘Thomas Waddle, called Long Tom, best top-sheet man south of river. Could post a six-sheet in wind enough to take your teeth out. Died respectable, leaving two brushes.’”

The King paused over Long Tom’s entry, and drank.

“To departed merit,” I said.

“To Tom,” said he.

We honoured the deceased.

The Royal Scroll also contained a table of celebrated advertisers: an anatomist who displayed transparent men; a dentist whose teeth stretched from Charing Cross to the Bank by instalments; a tailor who represented all rival tailors as scarecrows; a lecturer who exposed everything except his accounts; a soap-boiler who washed half the babies in England upon paper; a conjurer who vanished nightly but never from the walls; and a physician whose portrait, after twenty years of universal exposure, had become more familiar to London than the faces of many reigning sovereigns.

“Best customers,” said the King, “is them as believes repeating. One bill tells a man. Ten reminds him. A hundred worries him. A thousand makes him think he’s the only man in London that hasn’t bought it.”

This brought us back to my original reflections upon persecution by poster.

I asked whether His Majesty had ever known an announcement produce distress in a guilty person.

“Known it?” said he. “There was a cashier absconded—years ago. Paper put out, ‘WHERE IS BIDDLE?’ We had forty thousand. Biddle wasn’t accused in the bill; newspaper wanted him for information. But he sees his name at King’s Cross, sees it at Euston, sees it in Tottenham Court Road, sees it on a van at Oxford Circus, sees it outside a theatre, sees it on the back of a cart taking furniture to his own lodgings. Gives himself up before dinner.”

“Conscience!”

“Bills,” said the King. “Conscience wouldn’t have had the stations.”

The afternoon darkened. Lamps shone through mist; wheels ground in mud; cries rose from markets; bells sounded from steeples; and the procession continued with funereal perseverance. Wherever we passed, faces turned upward to read of calamities which had occurred elsewhere. A man who would not have crossed the street to prevent a quarrel pushed through traffic to learn the details of a duel. A woman carrying a sick child stopped beneath FRIGHTFUL SUFFERINGS OF A FAMILY! A pickpocket, I observed, was deeply interested in THE STOLEN DIAMONDS RECOVERED. London fed upon its printed alarms and went about its business.

The rum-and-water also continued. So did His Majesty’s pipe, which he courteously passed to me from time to time. The tobacco was dark, compact, and curled like a piece of paper removed from the hair of a chambermaid of severe habits. I am not ordinarily affected by tobacco; still less by rum-and-water; least of all by both together in a moving van beside a warm paste-pot under a roof of wet bills. It must therefore have been some other agency which caused the houses to bow as we passed and the lamps to perform slow circles round the King’s head.

His Majesty was describing technical improvements.

“There’s the telescopic brush,” he said, extending an imaginary handle toward the sky. “Reaches a top sheet without shifting ladder. There’s the double-action roller. There’s prepared paste—rubbish. There’s iron posting-frame. There’s the portable platform. There’s—”

At this point the Woman in Green leaned forward from the next van and looked at me with painful intelligence.

“There’s also,” I said, “a certain undulation in the metropolis.”

“Road’s rough.”

“It is not the road alone. St. Paul’s has just inclined to the left.”

“Always did, from this side.”

The King offered me further refreshment, which I accepted from motives of loyalty. It tasted strongly of monarchy.

He continued his lament over modern practitioners. They did not trim edges; they left wrinkles; they mismatched sheets; they allowed heads to join wrong bodies; they pasted Hamlet’s legs under a racehorse and put a duchess’s bonnet upon a murderer. They had no pride in joints. They could not carry ladders through traffic. They mixed paste by guess and spoiled walls by ignorance. Worst of all, they shouted at one another while working.

“Old hand don’t shout,” said His Majesty. “He looks. Mate understands. One look means ladder. Two means brush. Wink means copper. Cough means landlord. Whistle means run.”

“What,” I asked, “means this?” I made an involuntary movement which nearly placed me in the paste-tub.

“That,” said the King, catching my collar, “means you’d better get down.”

I protested that I was in excellent health, but the words issued from my mouth in the wrong order. The Appalling Mystery in Islington had multiplied into three mysteries; the Duke appeared to have spoken his last word in Greek; and the carpet, no longer content with speaking, rolled itself round my faculties.

The van stopped near a public-house.

With the assistance of His Majesty and the driver, I descended. The pavement rose hospitably to meet me, but they prevented the introduction and placed me upon a doorstep. The King regarded me with benevolence.

“Fresh air,” he prescribed.

“I have been poisoned,” I said.

“Likely.”

“By the paste.”

“Very likely.”

“Or the printer’s ink.”

“More likely.”

“Possibly by some mineral colouring in the Woman in Green.”

“Green’s dangerous,” said the King gravely.

“But not,” I added, “by the tobacco.”

“Never.”

“Nor by the rum-and-water.”

“Couldn’t be.”

Having thus established the cause upon a scientific basis, I thanked him for my audience and requested permission to pay my respects again.

“Look out for the vans,” said he. “Ask for King Solomon Tapp.”

The procession moved. The driver raised his whip. The King resumed his seat among his implements, lifted the metal vessel in farewell, and passed away beneath:

> TERRIBLE POISONING CASE!  
> THE PUBLIC WARNED!

I remained upon the doorstep.

How long I sat there I cannot state with precision. A charitable old woman gave me water; a boy proposed fetching a policeman; a second boy recommended rolling me in the gutter; and a third, of a speculative turn, examined my pockets. Eventually the houses resumed their upright position, the lamps ceased revolving, and St. Paul’s returned, as nearly as I could judge, to its customary place.

The vans had vanished.

In later months I searched for the King. Whenever an advertising procession blocked the Strand, I investigated it. Whenever a fatal car rolled through Fleet Street with a murder on one side and a miracle on the other, I looked behind the driver. I questioned paste-men at hoardings, carriers in alleys, ladder-men outside theatres, and fly-posters who declined conversation on the ground that a constable was coming. Some had heard of Solomon Tapp; some maintained that he had retired; one said he was dead; another said there had never been such a person, but offered to introduce me to the Emperor of Sandwich Boards.

I examined many vans. In one I found a sleeping boy, in another a broken ladder, in a third a dog and six hundred announcements of a French dancer. But I never again encountered the shining face, the tight head, the bright eye, the moist wink, the quick speech, and the ready air of the King of the Bill-Stickers.

Sometimes, when the fog is low and the streets are loud, I fancy I see his court pass at the end of a thoroughfare: the tall vans, the grave drivers, the flying handbills, the boys, the dog, the obstructed cabmen, the astonished public, and, reclining behind the last announcement, a compact figure raising a royal cup. I hurry after it. But an omnibus intervenes, a crowd closes, the procession turns a corner, and I arrive only in time to read upon a wall:

> TOO LATE!  
> HE HAS DISAPPEARED!

On such occasions I do not pursue him farther.

For if Solomon Tapp still reigns, he reigns over a kingdom more extensive than maps acknowledge: a kingdom of blank walls and ruined fences, of temporary boards and forgotten gables, of paste-pots, ladders, brush-marks, wet paper, old rivalries, vanished theatres, abolished lotteries, demolished markets, and names which survive their owners by clinging obstinately to brick. His decrees peel, but they do not wholly perish. One age is pasted over another; one wonder covers the last; the lost heiress conceals the patent pill; the election candidate hides the lost heiress; the circus lion devours the candidate; and beneath them all, protected by damp and darkness, there may still remain some tiny ancestral proclamation concerning a runaway apprentice, a strayed mare, or a quantity of tallow to be sold.

As for my indisposition, I have considered it carefully and can assign no reasonable cause except arsenic in the green pigment, lead in the red, copper in the blue, fermentation in the paste, corruption in the paper, infection in the warehouse, poisonous exhalations from printer’s ink, or the deleterious influence of sensational headlines upon a cultivated nervous system.

The tobacco was innocent.

The rum-and-water was above suspicion.

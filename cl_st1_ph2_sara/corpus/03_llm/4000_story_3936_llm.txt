# The Prince Above the City

On a tall column in the largest square of the city stood the statue of the Golden Prince.

He was covered from crown to slippers with leaves of fine gold. In the hilt of his sword burned a ruby as red as a coal in a king’s fireplace, and his eyes were two blue sapphires brought long ago from an island where the sea was said to sing. By day he flashed in the sun; by night he shone under the lamps, and people were pleased to think that so much beauty belonged to them.

“He is as handsome as a weather-vane,” said one of the Town Councillors, who was anxious to show that he possessed taste. Then, remembering that weather-vanes were useful, he added, “Though of course not quite so necessary.”

“When you are discontented, look at the Golden Prince,” mothers told their children. “He never complains.”

“I am glad there is someone perfectly happy in the world,” sighed a disappointed man.

The Mathematical Master at the school, however, did not approve when the children said the statue looked like an angel.

“You have never seen an angel,” he observed.

“We have, in dreams,” they answered.

The Mathematical Master frowned. He considered dreams untidy things, since they could not be measured with a ruler.

One autumn night a young Swallow flew into the city. His companions had left six weeks before for Egypt, but he had remained behind because he was in love with a Reed.

He had first met her beside the river in early spring. She was slender, green, and graceful, and when the wind touched her she bowed so charmingly that the Swallow believed she was bowing to him.

“You have a beautiful figure,” he had said, circling above the water, “and I am celebrated for the excellence of my wings. We should make a remarkable pair.”

The Reed had rustled softly.

The Swallow took this for consent.

All summer he had courted her. He skimmed the river to show her how low he could fly, caught gnats for her benefit, and told her stories of tiled roofs, church towers, and the private gardens of dukes. But as autumn approached, he began to find her conversation limited.

“She is affectionate,” he told himself, “but too domestic. She never travels, and she has far too many relations. Wherever one looks, there are reeds.”

When the north wind came, the Reed bent nearly to the water.

“You see,” said the Swallow, “she has no strength of character.”

At last he asked if she would go to Egypt with him. The Reed only shook her head. Her roots held her firmly in the mud.

“You have been amusing yourself with me,” cried the Swallow. “I prefer someone with broader views.”

He flew away.

All day he travelled, and at nightfall he reached the city.

“Where shall I lodge?” he wondered. “The authorities ought to provide accommodation for birds of distinction.”

Then he saw the statue on its high column.

“I shall sleep there,” he said. “It has a fine position and plenty of fresh air. Gold is also extremely respectable.”

He settled between the Prince’s feet and tucked his head beneath his wing.

“A golden bedroom!” he exclaimed. “How fortunate I am.”

Just then a large drop of water fell on him.

He looked up. The sky was clear, and the stars were bright.

“How extraordinary,” he said. “Not a cloud anywhere, and yet it rains. The climate of northern Europe is greatly exaggerated in guidebooks.”

A second drop fell.

“What is the use of a statue if it cannot keep off the weather?” he complained. “I must find a chimney-pot.”

He spread his wings, but before he could fly away a third drop struck his breast. Looking up again, he saw that the Golden Prince’s eyes were filled with tears. They ran down his gilded cheeks and trembled on his lips.

The moonlight made his face so sorrowful that the Swallow felt ashamed of his irritation.

“Who are you?” he asked.

“I am the Golden Prince.”

“Then why do you weep? You have soaked me quite thoroughly.”

“When I was alive,” said the Prince, “I lived in the Palace of Bright Windows. Sorrow was not admitted there. The gates were guarded, the walls were high, and the gardens were arranged so that no ugly thing could be seen from the terraces.

“In the morning I played with companions dressed in silk. At noon I ate from silver plates. In the evening there were dances, and the musicians wore white gloves so that even their hands should not offend us. Beyond the walls lay the city, but I never asked what happened there. Everyone called me the Happy Prince, and I believed them.

“Now I stand above the roofs, and I can see all the grief that the palace walls concealed. My heart is made of lead, yet it has learned to ache.”

“Lead is generally considered a practical metal,” thought the Swallow, “but the Prince speaks beautifully.”

“Far away,” continued the statue, “in a narrow street behind the market, there is a poor house. Through an open window I can see a woman seated at a table. Her face is thin, and her hands are rough and red from labour. She is sewing silver flowers on a satin gown for one of the Queen’s attendants, who will wear it at tomorrow’s banquet.

“In the corner lies her little son. He is ill with fever and asks for oranges. His mother has nothing to give him but water. Swallow, take the ruby from my sword and carry it to her.”

“I am expected in Egypt,” said the Swallow. “My friends are flying above the Nile. They will soon rest among the lotus flowers and discuss my delay in a most unfavourable manner.”

“The child is thirsty,” said the Prince, “and his mother is exhausted.”

“I do not care for children,” replied the Swallow. “Last summer two boys threw stones at me by the river. They did not hit me, naturally. Swallows are far too quick. Still, the intention was disrespectful.”

The Prince said nothing, but his tears fell silently.

The Swallow looked at the small ruby glowing in the sword, and then at the sorrowful face above him.

“It is very cold here,” he said. “Nevertheless, I will stay for one night and be your messenger.”

“Thank you, kind Swallow,” said the Prince.

The bird pulled the ruby from the sword and flew over the sleeping city.

He passed the cathedral tower, where stone saints stood in rows and looked down with empty eyes. He passed the palace, where music poured from tall windows. On a balcony a young lady complained to her companion that the dressmaker was terribly slow.

“If my gown is not ready,” she said, “I shall be miserable.”

At last the Swallow reached the poor house. The sick child tossed beneath a patched blanket, while his mother had fallen asleep over her work. The bird flew in, laid the ruby beside her thimble, and brushed the child’s forehead with his wings.

“How cool I feel,” murmured the boy. “Perhaps I am getting better.”

He sank into a peaceful sleep.

The Swallow returned to the Prince.

“It is curious,” he said. “The air is freezing, yet I feel warm.”

“That is because you have done a merciful thing,” answered the Prince.

The Swallow did not reply. He disliked explanations of his feelings, especially when they were correct.

At dawn he flew down to the river and bathed.

“A remarkable example of the northern swallow!” cried the Professor of Ornithology, who was crossing the bridge.

That afternoon the Professor wrote a long letter to the newspaper. It contained seven Latin names, three references to the direction of the wind, and one theory about civic drainage. Everybody admired it, though nobody understood it.

When night came, the Swallow returned to the statue.

“Have you any messages for Egypt?” he asked. “I leave immediately. By tomorrow evening I may see the pyramids turning purple under the sunset.”

“Swallow,” said the Prince, “stay with me one night more.”

“I have already stayed one night.”

“Across the city, in a room beneath the roof, I see a young playwright. He leans over a desk covered with papers. He is trying to finish a comedy for the theatre, but the grate is empty and hunger has made him faint. His lips are red as a pomegranate, and his hair is dark as ebony, yet his face is as pale as paper.”

“Perhaps his comedy is not amusing,” said the Swallow. “Playwrights often suffer from a high opinion of their sadness.”

“He has eaten nothing since yesterday,” said the Prince. “Take him one of my eyes.”

“Your eye?” cried the Swallow. “But it is a sapphire of great value.”

“It came from an eastern island where miners work by torchlight beneath the mountains. Take it to him. He can sell it and buy food and firewood.”

The Swallow was troubled.

“If I take your eye, you will see only half the city.”

“I have seen enough to know that he is hungry.”

The Swallow pecked out one sapphire and carried it through the dark.

The playwright’s window was locked, but there was a hole in the roof. The Swallow slipped through and dropped the jewel among the papers.

The young man lifted his head.

“At last the theatre manager has recognized my genius!” he cried when he saw the sapphire. “This is clearly a gift from an admirer.”

It is pleasant to be admired, even when one is mistaken about the admirer.

The Swallow flew back to the square.

“Now I must go,” he said. “In Egypt the crocodiles lie in the mud and watch the desert. Green palm trees bend over the water. Merchants lead camels loaded with amber, carpets, and blue-glazed jars. At night the stars are enormous, and the moon looks like a silver shield.”

“Stay one night more,” said the Prince.

“It grows colder every hour.”

“In the square below,” said the Prince, “there is a little match-girl. Her matches have fallen into the gutter and are spoiled. If she returns without money, her father will beat her. She has no shoes, and her feet are purple with cold.”

“I will stay one night more,” said the Swallow, “but I cannot take your other eye. Then you would be blind.”

“Take it,” said the Prince.

“I cannot.”

“Take it.”

So the Swallow removed the second sapphire and flew down to the match-girl. He dropped the jewel into her hand.

“What a lovely piece of blue glass!” she cried.

She ran home laughing.

Then the Swallow returned to the statue and perched upon the Prince’s shoulder.

“You are blind now,” he said. “Therefore I shall remain with you.”

“No,” answered the Prince. “You must go to Egypt.”

“I shall remain with you,” repeated the Swallow, and he slept at the Prince’s feet.

The next morning he sat on the Prince’s shoulder and described the countries he had visited.

He told of red flamingos standing in the shallows like flowers on long stems; of lions coming at dusk to drink from a lake smooth as jade; of temples carved with kings who had been dust for three thousand years; of yellow snakes sleeping beneath broad leaves; of caravans crossing the desert while bells rang at the camels’ throats; of merchants weighing cinnamon in brass scales; and of small dark children who floated on palm logs and laughed at the monkeys in the trees.

“Dear Swallow,” said the Prince, “you tell me of many wonders, but misery is the greatest mystery of all. Fly over my city and tell me what you see.”

The Swallow flew away.

He saw the rich dining in bright rooms while beggars waited beneath their windows. He saw ladies wrapped in scarlet cloaks stepping from carriages, and old women in thin shawls searching the gutters for coal. He saw merchants arranging fruit in shining pyramids while a hungry child stared at a pear until the merchant drove him away.

In a lane behind the courthouse, two little boys lay beneath an archway with their arms around each other.

“We are hungry,” said one.

“We are cold,” said the other.

A watchman ordered them away because sleeping beneath an archway injured the dignity of the building.

The Swallow flew over factories where pale girls bent above machines. He saw a man dismissed because illness had made him slow, and another employed because desperation had made him cheap. He saw prisoners behind barred windows and debtors hiding from the knock at the door. In the market, he heard a baker explain that bread was expensive because hunger had increased the demand.

At sunset he returned to the Prince and told him everything.

“I am covered with fine gold,” said the Prince. “Peel it away, leaf after leaf, and give it to the poor.”

“But without your gold you will no longer be splendid.”

“The living believe splendour will comfort them,” said the Prince. “Give them bread instead.”

So the Swallow pulled off a leaf of gold and carried it to the mother of five children who had no fire. He pulled off another and gave it to the old woman in the shawl. He carried gold to the boys beneath the arch, to the sick labourer, to a washerwoman whose fingers bled, and to a soldier’s widow who had pawned her blankets.

Leaf after leaf disappeared.

The children bought bread and cried, “We are hungry no longer!”

The old woman lit a fire.

The washerwoman rested her hands.

The boys beneath the arch danced in the street, and the watchman threatened to arrest them for excessive happiness.

Soon the Prince was grey and dull. The rain stained him, and the soot settled on his face. He no longer flashed in the sun. Yet those who had received his gold thought him more beautiful than before, although they did not know why.

The Mayor noticed the change while walking through the square with the Councillors.

“Dear me!” he said. “How shabby the Golden Prince has become.”

“Extremely shabby,” agreed the Councillors.

“The ruby is gone, his eyes are missing, and he is no longer golden,” continued the Mayor. “In fact, he is little better than a beggar.”

“Little better,” echoed the Councillors, who preferred agreement to thought.

“There is also a dead leaf at his feet,” said one of them, pointing to the Swallow. “Birds should not be permitted to die near public monuments.”

The Swallow was not dead, but he was too tired to object.

The winter deepened. Snow lay over the roofs like white linen, and icicles hung from the eaves like crystal knives. The fountains stopped singing. Men stamped their feet in the streets, and children breathed upon the shop windows to make little circles through which they could look at food.

The Swallow grew colder every day, but he would not leave the Prince.

He found crumbs outside the bakery when he could. Sometimes there were none, for the poor had become careful even with crumbs. He beat his wings to keep warm and told himself stories of Egypt.

He imagined the broad Nile under the sun, the lotus opening its blue heart, and his companions darting above the reeds. He imagined the old stone king in the desert staring at the stars. He imagined warm winds smelling of myrrh and dates.

But when the Prince asked, “Are you still there, dear Swallow?” he always answered, “I am here.”

One evening the Swallow knew that his strength was nearly gone.

He climbed slowly to the Prince’s shoulder.

“Goodbye,” he whispered.

“Are you going to Egypt at last?” asked the Prince.

“No. I am going to the place where all journeys end. May I kiss your hand?”

“I am glad you are going to Egypt,” said the Prince, “for you have stayed too long in the cold. Kiss me on the lips, because I love you.”

The Swallow kissed the Prince.

Then he fell at the statue’s feet and lay still.

At that moment a sharp crack sounded inside the statue. The leaden heart had broken into two pieces.

The frost was exceptionally severe that year, which may have had something to do with it.

Early the next morning the Mayor came into the square with the Councillors.

“Look!” he cried. “The statue is positively hideous.”

“Positively hideous,” they answered.

“The bird at his feet is dead,” said the Councillor responsible for Public Cleanliness. “We must issue a notice forbidding birds to die in civic spaces.”

A clerk wrote the suggestion down. Clerks write down many things, and this is why offices require such large cupboards.

The Mayor ordered the statue removed.

“Since he is no longer beautiful, he is no longer useful,” he explained.

The Councillors applauded this principle. Each secretly hoped that a new statue would be erected in the square.

“It should represent the Mayor,” said the Mayor.

“A statue of a Councillor would symbolize responsible government,” said each Councillor at once.

They quarrelled all the way to the Town Hall.

Workmen pulled down the Prince with ropes. His grey body struck the stones with a heavy sound, and the citizens scarcely looked up. People are often too busy to notice the fall of what they once admired.

The statue was taken to a furnace to be melted.

“What a peculiar thing!” said the foreman. “This broken heart will not melt.”

“It is therefore defective,” said the supervisor. “Throw it away.”

The two halves of the leaden heart were tossed onto a heap of rubbish. Beside them lay the body of the Swallow, swept from the square that morning.

That evening God said to one of His angels, “Bring Me the two most precious things in the city.”

The angel flew down.

He passed the palace, where candles glittered in crystal chandeliers. He passed the bank, where gold slept behind iron doors. He passed the museum, where a crown lay beneath glass and a guard yawned beside it. He passed the Mayor’s house, in which the Mayor was composing a speech about economy while eating a supper of twelve courses.

At last the angel came to the rubbish heap. There he found the broken leaden heart and the little dead Swallow.

He carried them to Heaven.

“You have chosen rightly,” said God. “For in My garden this bird shall sing forever, and in My golden city the Prince shall praise Me.”

Then the Swallow opened his wings, and winter fell from them like dust. The Prince could see again, though his eyes were no longer sapphires, and his heart was whole, though it was no longer made of lead.

Below them, the earthly city continued to admire whatever glittered.

But in Paradise, where the value of things is better understood, the Prince and the Swallow lived forever.

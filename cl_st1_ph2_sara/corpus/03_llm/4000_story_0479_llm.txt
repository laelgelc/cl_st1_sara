I had put away five tutors before Father got John Twigg.

That is what I called it—putting them away. Father called it disgracing the Dale name, and Aunt Emily called it breaking the hearts of worthy young men, though not one of them looked heartbroken when he went down the drive. Mostly they looked relieved.

The first tutor was an old fellow with eye-glasses who sniffed when he talked. I put a tree-toad in his water pitcher. The second was young and wore patent-leather shoes even in the country. I got him into the marsh after what I said was a wounded duck, and he lost one shoe and his dignity. There were three more after that. One preached, one begged, and one tried to thrash me. He stayed just two days.

I was sixteen then, and I didn’t care whether I ever got into college or not. I said colleges were places where fellows learned Latin and came home with loud neckties. Father said I was ignorant, idle, vicious, ungrateful, and no gentleman.

He generally said those things after dinner, when he had been drinking his port.

I don’t mean Father was a drunkard. He wasn’t. He never did anything that could be called common. At least, that was what he said. If he lost his temper and threw a glass into the fireplace, that was nervous strain. If I slammed a door, that was low breeding.

Well, one hot afternoon in June, Father sent Peters to find me. I was down at the stable rubbing Molly’s foreleg where she had nicked it on a stone.

“Your father wishes you in the library, Master Raymond,” Peters said.

“Tell him I’m dead.”

“I fancy he would desire to see the remains, sir.”

Peters was like that. He had been with us since before I was born and never smiled where anybody could catch him at it.

I washed the worst of the dirt off my hands and went in. Father stood by the mantel, stiff and white around the mouth. A stranger sat near the window with one ankle across his knee.

He was not a large man, exactly, but he had broad shoulders and looked as if his clothes belonged on him. That was uncommon among tutors. His suit was gray and rather old, and his shoes were ordinary brown ones, fit for walking. He had a square, sunburned face, a short mustache, and eyes that seemed lazy until you noticed they had missed nothing.

“Raymond,” Father said, “this is Mr. John Twigg.”

I looked him over.

“What kind of name is Twigg?” I said.

“The one I was given,” he said.

He did not seem hurt. He didn’t even look surprised.

Father’s nostrils pinched up.

“Mr. Twigg has consented to undertake what several others have failed to accomplish.”

“That isn’t much recommendation.”

“No,” said Twigg. “It isn’t.”

I looked at him again.

Father went on. “You have one month, Twigg. I have been perfectly candid as to the boy’s disposition.”

“Very candid,” said Twigg.

“If, at the end of that time, you have established control and made definite progress toward preparing him for college, the engagement may continue. You are to have complete authority.”

“Complete?” I said.

Father did not look at me. “Complete.”

“Suppose I don’t recognize it?”

“Then Mr. Twigg may employ whatever measures he considers proper.”

“Can he hang me?”

“If it seems useful,” said Twigg, “I shall consider it.”

Father frowned, because he never knew what to do with a joke unless he had made it.

“The boy must learn obedience,” he said. “He must learn honor, self-command, and the conduct expected of a gentleman.”

Twigg’s eyes moved from Father to me and back again.

“That seems a considerable curriculum for one month,” he said.

“You accept it?”

“I accept the month.”

Then Father told me to shake hands.

I meant to give Twigg my dead-fish grip, because that used to make grown people think you were frightened of them. But he took my hand before I got it arranged. He didn’t squeeze and show off. His hand just closed firm and dry, and somehow I felt he could have cracked my knuckles if he had wanted to.

“What sports?” he asked.

“What?”

“What do you do when you are not dismissing tutors?”

“I ride. Fish. Shoot. Play some golf.”

“Any good?”

“At which?”

“Any of them.”

It was an insulting question, and I started to tell him so. Then I saw one corner of his mouth move.

“I can beat you at all four,” I said.

“Excellent. We shall not lack subjects for examination.”

“I don’t take examinations.”

“Neither do I, when I know I shall fail.”

He picked up a battered briar pipe from the table. Father looked at him in a puzzled way, as if he had ordered a tutor and somebody had delivered the wrong parcel.

I went out hating Twigg. Or I thought I did. Anyhow, I made up my mind he would be gone before the month was half over.

The next morning he came to the schoolroom at nine. I came at twenty minutes past, carrying my fishing rod.

Twigg was sitting by the window reading a book. There were three other books on the table, besides paper, pencils, and an algebra.

“You are late,” he said.

“I’m going fishing.”

“So I inferred from the rod.”

“Then you needn’t have waited.”

“I didn’t wait. I read.”

I leaned the rod in the corner and looked at the books. “I won’t do Latin.”

“Very well.”

“And I won’t do algebra.”

“Very well.”

“Or history.”

“Very well.”

That took most of the wind out of it.

“What am I to do?” I asked.

“Go fishing, apparently.”

I grabbed up my rod.

“One thing,” he said.

I turned at the door.

“Your father has given me authority over your time from nine until one. Since you decline to study, you may spend those hours here doing nothing.”

“I thought you said I could fish.”

“After one.”

“You said go fishing.”

“I described your intention. I did not approve it.”

“I’m going anyway.”

He shut his book and marked the place with a slip of paper.

“If you go,” he said, “I shall follow you.”

“You can’t stop me.”

“Perhaps not.”

“And what’ll you do when you catch me?”

“Fish, if there is time left after I bring you back.”

That sounded so silly I laughed and went out.

He followed.

I went across the lawn fast. He came at an ordinary walk. I cut through the rose garden, jumped the low wall, and ran down through the birches. I knew all the paths, and he didn’t know any. In five minutes I could not see him.

I reached Black Pond, put my rod together, and had just opened the bait tin when Twigg stepped from behind the boathouse.

“You run fairly well,” he said.

“How’d you get here?”

“By the lane.”

The lane was shorter. I had not thought of that.

He held out his hand for the rod.

“What for?”

“We are returning.”

“Take it if you can.”

“All right.”

He took off his coat, folded it, and laid it on the landing.

That bothered me. The tutor who had tried to thrash me had rolled up his sleeves and made a speech. Twigg only stood there in his shirt sleeves, waiting. His arms were brown, and they did not look like a tutor’s arms.

“Were you a prize fighter?” I asked.

“No.”

“You look like one.”

“I rowed.”

“For a college?”

“Yes.”

“Which?”

He named it. It was one of the colleges Father was always talking about when he said I should never get into any.

“Did your crew win?”

“Twice.”

I gave him the rod.

I did not give it because I was afraid. I just didn’t see any sense in fighting over a fishing rod when he probably knew some dirty rowing hold that would break my arm.

We went back to the schoolroom. He put the rod behind his chair and took up his book.

“Now what?” I said.

“Whatever you please, provided you remain here.”

I sat down and whistled. He read.

I kicked the table leg. He read.

I made paper pellets and flipped them at a bust of Cicero. One hit Cicero on the nose and bounced into Twigg’s lap. He picked it up and put it in his pocket.

At ten I got out a cigarette. Father didn’t know I smoked, though he had found out several times.

“Does smoke annoy you?” I asked.

“Some smoke does.”

I lit the cigarette and blew it toward him.

Twigg laid down his book, filled his briar pipe, and struck a match. The tobacco he used must have been compounded of burning rope, old boots, and the floor of a stable. Within a minute the room was blue.

I coughed.

“Good tobacco?” I asked.

“Very.”

“It stinks.”

“That is a matter of education.”

I stood it for perhaps four minutes. Then I threw my cigarette out the window.

He smoked on.

“You can stop now.”

“I haven’t finished.”

“You only started to bother me.”

“That was one consideration.”

I went out into the passage for air. He caught me by the back of the coat without rising and swung me around. He did it so neatly that I was sitting in my chair before I had decided whether to resist.

“Complete authority,” he reminded me.

“You’re a bully.”

“Possibly.”

“I hate you.”

“That is inconvenient, but not fatal.”

At half past ten I opened the algebra.

“Will doing one problem get you to stop smoking?” I asked.

“No. But it may get the problem done.”

I did six.

At one o’clock he knocked out his pipe, gave me my rod, and said, “Now we can fish.”

“We?”

“If you prefer to go alone, say so.”

I nearly did. Then I thought it would be a fine chance to get him into the mud, or upset him from the punt, or at least find out whether he could cast.

“All right,” I said. “Come on, Sissy.”

He put on his coat.

We fished Black Pond until nearly supper. I had always used worms there, though old Caleb at the stable said the big bass would rise to flies. Twigg looked at my tackle and asked if he might try it. His first cast laid the fly under the willow branches as light as thistledown.

“You’ve fished before,” I said.

“Now and then.”

He hooked a two-pound bass ten minutes later. He played it without fuss and let me net it. Then he took the hook out and slipped the fish back.

“What’d you do that for?”

“We have no need of him.”

“He was a fine fish.”

“Yes.”

“Then why not keep him?”

“Because he is more useful in the pond.”

I said that was darned foolishness, but later, when I caught one hardly half as big, I let it go too. I told Twigg it was because the cook would ruin it.

After that the days got into a sort of order, though I never admitted there was one. We worked mornings, unless I refused. When I refused, I stayed in the schoolroom anyhow, which was worse than working because Twigg could sit still forever.

Once I climbed from the window and went to the stables. He did not chase me. He found Father and had Molly, Duke, and my bay gelding Rufus turned out in the north pasture. Then he locked the saddle room.

I came back at eleven.

“Ready for Caesar?” he asked.

“You had no right to touch my horses.”

“They are your father’s horses.”

“Rufus is mine.”

“Then I advise you to earn access to him.”

“You—you just wait. I’ll get even.”

“Please do it after translating the first paragraph.”

He had a way of making revenge seem like part of the lesson.

In the afternoons we did what I liked, and that was where he was crafty. If he had made the whole day lessons I could have gone on hating him easily. But he could ride, and he could shoot, and he knew things that were not in schoolbooks—or, if they were, nobody had ever told me.

The first time we rode together I gave him old Emperor. Emperor was a tall black horse with a Roman nose and a disposition like Father’s. He had thrown the patent-leather tutor into a hedge.

“Quiet animal?” Twigg asked.

“Perfectly,” I said.

Ben, the stable boy, looked at me and then at the ground.

Twigg ran his hand down Emperor’s neck and felt the saddle girth.

“He dislikes the curb,” he said.

“How do you know?”

“He told me.”

“Did he say anything about being a dangerous brute?”

“No. He called himself misunderstood.”

I snorted. Twigg mounted, and Emperor went straight up on his hind legs.

Twigg bent forward and spoke to him—not loudly. The horse came down, crow-hopped twice, and tried to bolt for the drive. Twigg turned him in a narrow circle, gave him his head, and cantered across the paddock. By the time I got on Rufus, Twigg and Emperor were waiting at the gate, both looking pleased with themselves.

We rode twelve miles that day. On the way back we found a tree down across the lower road. I knew Rufus could jump it, but I waited for Twigg to go around because Emperor was old and heavy.

Twigg looked at the trunk.

“Too much for him?” I asked.

“Probably.”

“Afraid?”

“Yes.”

That surprised me so much I stared.

“You admit it?”

“Certainly. Fear is useful information.”

He dismounted, led Emperor to the bank where the trunk lay lowest, and let him inspect it. Then he mounted and took him over at a quiet pace.

Rufus followed. He clipped the bark with one hind hoof and nearly unseated me.

“You took off too soon,” Twigg said.

“I know that.”

“I thought you might.”

“I suppose you think you’re a better rider than me.”

“Than I.”

“There! That’s what makes everybody hate tutors.”

He laughed. I had not heard him laugh before. It was a good laugh, not the thin sort that meant you were being improved.

Two days later I went into his bedroom to see if I could find out what he had written Father.

I had reasons. Tutors always wrote letters about me. They said I was deficient in application, devoid of moral sense, or suffering from an excess of animal spirits. That last fellow had used all three in one page. I knew because I had steamed the envelope.

Twigg’s room was at the west end of the second floor. It was plain, with his books lined up on the mantel and two fishing rods in cases by the wardrobe. His brown bag stood under the bed. On the table was a black notebook.

I opened it.

June 4. Entered upon my duties at Greylands. R. D., aged sixteen, presents an appearance at once healthy and sullen. His hostility is defensive and has been strengthened by repeated contests with adults who sought submission before establishing either confidence or respect.

I did not know exactly what “defensive” meant there, but it sounded cowardly.

I read on.

The boy is woefully ignorant, though by no means deficient in intelligence. His manners are lamentable, his speech intentionally coarse, and his propensity for falsehood chiefly theatrical. He lies less to gain advantage than to provoke disapproval, which he is certain of receiving in any event.

That was a lie itself. I had gained plenty of advantages.

His father attributes the condition to bad blood inherited from the mother’s family, indulgence by servants, and the boy’s own perversity. I suspect a simpler history: early neglect, alternating severity and indifference, and the absence of any sustained affection not made conditional upon performance.

I stopped there.

My mother had died when I was six. I hardly remembered her, except she wore something soft and blue once and let me sleep with a sick puppy in my bed. Father never spoke about her unless he was blaming her family for me.

There was more.

Mr. Dale speaks frequently of gentlemanly conduct, yet addresses his son chiefly through insult and predicts failure before effort begins. This is an ungentlemanly action, even when performed with excellent grammar. The boy appears morally calloused. I doubt the callus is deep.

I shut the book.

I was pretty mad. Not because of what he said about me. I didn’t care a hang about that. But it was sneaking to write about Father when Father paid him.

Then I heard a step in the hall. I shoved the notebook back and got behind the curtain.

Twigg entered, took a clean collar from the drawer, and went out again.

I waited, though there was no need. I was just being careful. Then I slipped away.

At lessons next morning, he gave me Caesar and said nothing about his room. I watched him for signs. There weren’t any.

After lunch we played golf. I was better at golf than most fellows my age because I hit hard and did not bother much about rules. Twigg said bothering about rules was the chief distinction between golf and assault.

He beat me by three holes.

He did not hit far, but the ball went where he intended. Mine went into long grass, a bunker, and once through a greenhouse pane.

“Bad luck,” I said.

“Bad direction.”

“The wind took it.”

“There was no wind.”

“You’re a liar.”

Twigg looked at me, and suddenly I remembered the notebook—my “propensity for falsehood chiefly theatrical.” My ears got hot.

“Take that back,” he said.

I thought he might hit me. He didn’t move toward me, but there was something different in his voice.

“I won’t.”

“Then the game ends here.”

“You’re afraid I’ll beat you.”

“You may say what you like about my golf. You may not call me a liar.”

“Why not?”

“Because it is untrue, and because a gentleman does not use an insult he is unwilling to answer for.”

“There’s Father’s gentleman stuff.”

“No,” Twigg said. “It is mine.”

He put his club into the bag and walked toward the house.

I stood by the damaged turf and dug my heel into it. I wanted to let him go. I hoped he would go. You bet I did.

But I didn’t.

“Twigg!”

He stopped.

“I take it back.”

He waited.

“I said I take it back, didn’t I?”

“Yes.”

“Well, come on. It’s your shot.”

He came back. He did not grin or make me apologize a second time. That was one of the things about him. When a fight was finished, he did not keep kicking the remains.

That evening I went to his room again.

I only wanted to see if he had written about the golf. I was not spying regularly or anything like that.

There was a new entry.

June 9. R. D. has read this diary, as I intended he should when I left it conspicuously upon the table. If he reads further, I trust he will have the decency to replace the blue ribbon between these pages at the proper point. Yesterday he replaced it at June 2, thereby advancing my record backward in time.

I dropped the book as if it had snapped at me.

Then I picked it up, because it had fallen open, and read the rest.

It is tempting to rebuke a boy for spying. It may be more useful to permit him to discover that a man can know his worst action without despising him. His curiosity may also accomplish what assigned reading has not.

Underneath, in different ink, he had added:

Note: If R. D. is now reading this, the trout at Mill Creek will be rising tomorrow at five-thirty. Attendance is conditional upon completion of the Latin exercise.

I put the blue ribbon exactly where it had been, which took some figuring, and went back to my room.

The next morning I had the Latin done by five-twenty.

I did not tell him I had read the note. I came downstairs in fishing boots and found him by the door with a basket.

“Going somewhere?” I asked.

“Mill Creek.”

“I might come.”

“If you wish.”

“Latin’s on your desk.”

“So I observed.”

He never said another word about the diary. Neither did I.

Mill Creek ran cold under alders, and the trout were rising in the gray light just as he said. We worked upstream, taking turns at the best pools. At the bend above the mill I hooked a trout bigger than any I had ever caught there.

He ran under a root. I pulled hard.

“Give him line,” Twigg said.

“He’ll snag me.”

“He will break you if you don’t.”

I gave line, and the fish came out. Then he went lickety-cut downstream. I splashed after him, slipped, filled one boot, and yelled at Twigg to net him. Twigg waded waist-deep without taking off his coat and got the net under the fish at the tail of the pool.

It was a beauty, all red spots and gold sides.

“Two pounds,” I said.

“One and three quarters.”

“Two.”

“One and seven eighths, in honor of the capture.”

I carried it back to the house. I was going to show Father, but he had gone to town. So Twigg and I ate it for breakfast.

I suppose that was about the time I began working without having to be driven every minute. I still raised Cain when I felt like it. I put a tack on Twigg’s chair once, but he saw it and moved the chair around for me.

“You placed it,” he said. “You should have the benefit.”

I removed it.

We made a bargain about lessons. If I did three solid hours in the morning, he would give me the last hour before luncheon for something I wanted to read. Not trash, he said. I said all books were trash except adventure books, and he said that simplified selection.

He gave me a story about a shipwreck and another about men hunting treasure in Africa. When I finished those, he slipped in Plutarch.

“What’s this?” I asked.

“Adventure stories.”

“It’s ancient history.”

“Much the same thing, except that the dead are less likely to sue.”

I started with Alexander because there was a horse in the first pages. Then I read about Caesar, and that made the Latin seem slightly less idiotic. Not sensible, you understand. Just less idiotic.

At supper one evening Father asked Twigg how I was progressing.

“Fairly,” Twigg said.

Father raised his eyebrows. “Indeed?”

“I have learned a good deal.”

Father frowned. “I referred to Raymond.”

“So did I.”

I laughed before I could stop myself.

Father turned on me. “You find your condition amusing?”

“No, sir.”

“Then do not bray at the table.”

I pushed back my chair.

“Sit down,” Twigg said.

Father looked at him. “I shall manage my own son at dinner.”

“You placed him under my authority.”

“That did not empower you to correct me.”

“No,” Twigg said. “But it obliges me not to allow him to escape correction by quarreling with you.”

That was so tangled I had to sit down to think about it.

Father said, “The boy lacks breeding.”

“I believe he lacks practice.”

“You are inclined to excuse him.”

“Not at all. I am inclined to require things he may possibly perform.”

Father’s face went red. For a second I hoped there would be a real row. Then Twigg asked me, in the same tone as always, to pass the potatoes.

Afterward I said, “You were scared of Father.”

“Yes.”

“You didn’t look scared.”

“Looking scared is seldom useful.”

“Why’d you take his side?”

“I didn’t.”

“You said I brayed.”

“I said you were escaping correction.”

“That’s the same.”

“No. Your father insulted you. You answered with bad manners. Both facts may exist at once.”

I kicked a pebble down the terrace.

“He hates me,” I said.

Twigg was filling his pipe. “I think your father dislikes what he has come to expect from you.”

“That means he hates me.”

“No. It means you have both made a habit, and habits are tiresome things to break.”

He struck the match. I watched the flame jump into the briar.

“You put that stuff about him in the diary for me to read.”

“Yes.”

“You knew I’d snoop.”

“I hoped your character would remain consistent.”

I tried not to grin.

The bad part came in the third week.

There were three fellows I used to meet in the village—Sam Vickers, Joe Pell, and a big fellow named Rudge who was nearly twenty. Father called them low companions, mostly because their fathers worked. That made me stick to them more.

They smoked, played cards in the loft over Vickers’s livery stable, and had a bottle sometimes. I had told them about my tutors and what I did to them. If I quit coming around because of Twigg, they would say he had tamed me.

One Wednesday, Joe sent word that Rudge had borrowed a repeating rifle and we were going to shoot crows at dusk. Twigg and I were to ride after lessons.

“I can’t ride today,” I said.

“Why not?”

“Headache.”

He looked at me. “We can walk.”

“I want to sleep.”

“Very well.”

He went out alone.

As soon as he was past the south gate, I took my bicycle and went to the village.

We didn’t shoot any crows. Rudge had the rifle, but he also had a bottle, and after a while they began shooting at weather vanes and tin signs. I knew it was rotten. I said so too. At least, I said we ought to go farther from town.

Rudge called me Schoolboy Dale and asked whether my nurse objected to noise.

I took the rifle away from him and said I could hit the bell on the abandoned quarry shed. It was over a hundred yards off. Joe bet me a dollar I couldn’t.

I fired. The bell clanged.

Then a man yelled from the shed.

We ran.

I ran with the rest at first. At the road I stopped.

“Somebody was in there,” I said.

“Then he ought to have rung before we fired,” Rudge said.

“We’ve got to see.”

“You go, Schoolboy.”

They mounted their bicycles and rode off.

I went back. An old quarryman named Eb Nash was behind the shed, holding his shoulder. The bullet had hit the bell frame, knocked off a splinter of iron, and driven it into him. There was blood all over his shirt, though the wound was not as bad as it looked.

My insides felt hollow.

“Who fired?” Nash asked.

“I dunno.”

He stared at the rifle in my hand.

Then I heard hoofbeats. Twigg came along the quarry road on Emperor.

He took in Nash, the blood, the rifle, and me.

“What happened?”

“I—I shot at the bell.”

“Anyone else injured?”

“No.”

Twigg dismounted. He examined Nash’s shoulder, tore a strip from his handkerchief, and bound it. Then he put Nash on Emperor and told me to walk beside him.

“What’re you going to do?” I asked.

“Take Mr. Nash to Doctor Henry.”

“I mean after.”

“Afterward we shall decide.”

He did not say one thing on the way to the doctor. That was worse than a sermon. I kept waiting for him to call me a coward or a criminal. I had already called myself both, though not out loud.

Nash needed three stitches. The doctor said the fragment might have struck his neck if it had flown two inches higher.

Outside, Twigg held out his hand.

“The rifle.”

I gave it to him.

“Whose?”

“Rudge’s. He borrowed it.”

“Who was present?”

I told him.

“Who fired?”

“I did.”

“Were you drinking?”

“No. I had one swallow. It was rotten.”

“That is not an answer.”

“Yes.”

He tied the rifle to Emperor’s saddle.

“You broke your agreement.”

“What agreement?”

“That your mornings belonged to study and your afternoons to recreation we undertook together.”

“I never promised not to see anybody else.”

“No. You allowed me to rely on a false statement about a headache.”

I wanted him to shout. I wished he would hit me.

“Well, tell Father,” I said.

“I shall.”

“Go ahead.”

“And you will tell him first.”

“I won’t.”

“Then I shall tell him you injured a man and lacked the courage to confess it.”

“I don’t lack courage!”

“Prove it.”

We walked home. I thought of running away, but that seemed exactly what he expected me to think of, so I didn’t.

Father was in the library. I stood by the desk and told him. My voice sounded queer, like somebody else was using it.

When I finished, Father looked at Twigg.

“This is your control?”

“No,” Twigg said. “This is Raymond’s account.”

“He might have killed a man!”

“Yes.”

“With village riffraff, drinking and shooting like a poacher!”

“Yes.”

Father strode toward me. “You young blackguard—”

Twigg stepped between us.

“Raymond has confessed and will make restitution. He will also accept punishment. There is no need to strike him while he is trying to stand.”

Father stopped.

“He deserves a horsewhipping.”

“Perhaps. But not in anger.”

They looked at each other. Twigg wasn’t as tall as Father, but he seemed to take up more room.

At last Father said, “Deal with him, then. That is what I pay you for.”

He walked out.

I stood there feeling worse than if Father had thrashed me.

Twigg said, “You will sell your shotgun and pay Nash’s doctor’s bill and his lost wages.”

“My Greener?”

“Yes.”

“Father gave me that.”

“Then the sacrifice is partly his. You may add the money in your savings box.”

“There’s twelve dollars.”

“A beginning.”

“And what else?”

“No horse, fishing, shooting, or golf for ten days. You will work all day except for exercise.”

“Ten days! That’s nearly the rest of your month.”

“Yes.”

“You planned that.”

“I did not plan for you to shoot Mr. Nash.”

I said something then that I won’t put down. It was about him and his punishment and his college.

He listened.

“Finished?” he asked.

“No!”

But I was. I slammed out and went upstairs.

At supper he did not mention it. The next morning the extra books were on the table.

For two days I hated him worse than at the beginning. I did the work because there was nothing else to do, but I did it hard, jabbing holes through the paper and slamming dictionaries.

On the third day Eb Nash sent me a note. It said:

Mr. Dale, sir, the doctor says I am all right. I thank you for coming back. Many would not have. E. Nash.

I read it twice and put it away before Twigg came in.

The Greener brought forty dollars. It was worth twice that, but I didn’t argue. Much.

About then Twigg began talking of college, though never as if it were medicine I had to swallow.

He told me about boat races, winter evenings, fellows who had walked two miles through snow to put a cow in a professor’s chapel, and another fellow who studied so little his father made him work in a glue factory for a year.

“Did he go back?” I asked.

“With enthusiasm.”

“Could I make your college?”

“Not this year.”

“I mean ever.”

“Yes.”

He said it so simply I thought he had misunderstood.

“Father says I’m too far behind.”

“You are.”

“Then how could I?”

“By advancing.”

“You always say fool things like that.”

“They are easy to remember.”

“What would I have to do?”

“Work.”

“How much?”

“A great deal.”

“Would I have to know Greek?”

“Yes.”

“Then I won’t go.”

But that night I got the Greek grammar from the bottom shelf and looked through it. It was even worse than Latin. I left it on the table by accident, and the next morning Twigg put a marker in the first lesson.

The month went fast after that. I did not notice it going. That is, I knew the date, but I did not count the days as I had at first.

On the final Saturday, the ten-day punishment was over. Twigg and I rode before breakfast. Emperor had become almost respectable, and Rufus was full of ginger from idleness. We raced from the oak pasture to the stone bridge. Rufus won by half a length.

“I beat you!” I yelled.

“You did.”

“Fair?”

“Quite fair.”

“You didn’t hold back?”

“No.”

I felt better over that than I had over anything for a long time. It sounds silly, but there it is.

When we returned, Peters said Father wished to see Twigg in the library. I went upstairs to change. Passing Twigg’s room, I saw his door open.

His bag was on the bed.

It was packed.

I stood there, and something seemed to come up under my ribs.

He was only packing because the month was over. I knew that. Father would engage him for the year. Of course he would. Twigg had lasted, and I was studying, and Father liked results, especially when he could buy them.

I went to the schoolroom and waited.

Twigg came in after twenty minutes.

“What did Father say?” I asked.

“He offered me the continued position.”

“I knew he would.”

I opened the algebra. “This next one’s a brute.”

“I declined.”

I looked up. “What?”

“I declined.”

“Why?”

“Our agreement was that I should have one month to establish complete control and make you ready to proceed toward college.”

“Well?”

“I have not established complete control.”

“That’s rot.”

“You deceived me, broke your word, associated with companions you knew to be unworthy, drank, and nearly killed a man.”

“That was once.”

“Once is sufficient.”

“You punished me.”

“Punishment does not erase an action.”

I got up so fast the chair fell backward.

“So you’re quitting because I wasn’t perfect in one month?”

“No. I am leaving because I cannot honestly report complete success.”

“Tell Father whatever you want. He won’t know.”

“I shall know.”

I walked to the window. Outside, the lawn looked too bright. Somebody had replaced the glass in the greenhouse where my golf ball went through it. Beyond the trees I could see the strip of Black Pond.

“You wanted to leave all along,” I said.

“No.”

“You did. You think I’m morally calloused and lamentable and all that diary stuff.”

“I think you are sixteen.”

“That’s worse, I suppose.”

“Frequently.”

I kicked the fallen chair. It skidded across the rug and gouged the polished floor.

“There,” I said. “Put that in your diary.”

“I may.”

“I don’t care.”

“I know.”

“I’m glad you’re going.”

“I’m sorry to hear it.”

He bent and set the chair upright. Then he went to the shelves and began taking down his books—his own books, the ones about the sea and Africa and the rowing fellow who became a judge.

“You can leave those,” I said.

“I may need them.”

“You’ve read them.”

“I sometimes read a book twice.”

“Well, I haven’t finished the sea one.”

“You may keep that.”

“I don’t want your old book.”

He put it on the table anyway.

I stared out the window and dug the toe of my shoe into the turf at the edge of the rug. I had a lump in my throat, which was ridiculous. The room smelled faintly of his horrible pipe. There was his ink mark on the blotter and the little nick my knife had made in the desk while I was supposed to be translating Caesar.

“Where’ll you go?” I asked.

“I have been offered a position at a school in Connecticut.”

“When?”

“Next week.”

“You’ll take it?”

“Probably.”

“You’ll like a pack of sissies better.”

“I have not met them.”

“They’ll hate you.”

“That risk attends education.”

He tied the books with a cord.

I thought of mornings in the schoolroom with another patent-leather fool, and fishing Black Pond alone, and Emperor going back to being a brute because nobody understood what he said. I thought of Twigg telling some Connecticut boy how to give a trout line, and it made me mad.

“Suppose—” I said.

He waited.

“Suppose you didn’t have to tell Father it was complete.”

“I would still have to know what the arrangement was.”

“Change the arrangement.”

“Into what?”

“I dunno.”

“I cannot stay to repeat this month indefinitely, Raymond.”

“I didn’t ask you to.”

“No.”

He picked up the bundle.

“Wait.”

He stopped by the door.

My face felt hot. I could have cursed him, and that would have made everything easy. Instead I stood there looking at the torn place in the turf.

“What would I have to do?” I asked.

“To accomplish what?”

“You know darned well.”

“I prefer you to say it.”

That was like him. He would make a fellow haul his own fish in, even if it got away.

“To have you stay.”

He set the books down.

“The terms would be severe.”

“How severe?”

“Four hours’ study every weekday, increasing to six when preparation requires it. My decision final regarding lessons and recreation. No visits to Vickers, Pell, or Rudge unless I approve them.”

“All right.”

“No cigarettes.”

“I don’t smoke much.”

“No cigarettes.”

“All right.”

“No liquor.”

“That one swallow—”

“No liquor.”

“All right.”

“You will obey without making every instruction a contest.”

“I can’t promise never to argue.”

“I should be alarmed if you did. You may argue after obeying.”

“That’s no use.”

“It improves the argument.”

He was trying not to smile. I could see it.

“And,” he said, “we require a rule regarding profanity.”

“What rule?”

“Each oath costs you ten cents.”

“I haven’t got much money left.”

“Then your language should become economical.”

“What happens to the money?”

“Mr. Nash receives it.”

“That’s low.”

“It is practical.”

I shoved my hands into my pockets.

“Is that all?”

“No. If you give your word, you keep it. If you fail, you admit the failure without lies. And if I punish you, you accept it without appealing to your father.”

“Father wouldn’t help me.”

“That is not the point.”

I went over all of it. Six hours’ study sounded like prison. No cigarettes was worse than I let on. Giving Twigg final say was the worst of all.

He picked up the books again.

“All right,” I said.

“Is that an answer to the conditions or an expression of relief at my departure?”

“You know.”

“I want to hear it.”

I hated him then. I certainly did. No fellow ought to be made to say things that another fellow already knows.

I swallowed, but the lump stayed there.

“I accept your darned conditions.”

“Ten cents.”

“You haven’t stayed yet.”

“Then you owe it to yourself.”

I nearly threw the ink bottle at him.

He waited.

“You—you just stay here,” I said. “I’ll work. I’ll quit the cigarettes and those fellows. I’ll take the punishments. I’ll—well, I’ll do it. Only don’t go off to Connecticut.”

“Why not?”

“Because—because I want to get into your college, don’t I? And you said I could. And Emperor needs riding. And I haven’t beaten you at shooting yet.”

“Those are substantial reasons.”

“They’re the only reasons.”

“Of course.”

He held out his hand.

This time I did not give him the dead-fish grip.

We shook.

Then he opened the door and called Peters to take his bag back out of the carriage.

I found out later that he had never accepted the Connecticut position and that the carriage had not been ordered. He said he had packed because an honest man must be ready to carry out a decision.

I said he had bluffed me.

He said perhaps.

That afternoon we went to Black Pond. He sat in the stern of the punt, broad shoulders bent a little, his old briar pipe between his teeth, while I worked the oars. The smoke was just as bad as ever.

“You know,” I said, “there ought to be a rule about that pipe.”

“There is.”

“What?”

“The owner decides when to smoke it.”

“That’s not fair.”

“Argue after obeying.”

“I’m not obeying anything.”

“You are rowing.”

I thought that over and pulled harder.

We reached the willow bank, and a bass struck near the reeds. Twigg handed me the rod.

“You take him,” he said.

“Why?”

“You are nearer.”

That was not the reason, but I took it.

The bass ran deep, then broke water in a flash. I gave him line before Twigg had to tell me. After a while I brought him alongside, and Twigg slipped the net beneath him.

He was bigger than the first one—three pounds if he was an ounce.

We looked at him shining in the net.

“Keep him?” I asked.

Twigg shook his head.

I loosened the hook and let the fish slide back. He rested a second in the shadow of the boat, then vanished with one sweep of his tail.

“More useful in the pond,” I said.

“Precisely.”

“Don’t get proud. I knew that.”

“Of course.”

And I suppose that was the beginning of my education, though it was the end of the month.

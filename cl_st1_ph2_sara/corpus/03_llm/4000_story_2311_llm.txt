Beauvale lies in a hollow of the north country, where three wooded ridges stoop together above a little river, and the river, finding no honest road between them, turns twice under the hill before escaping eastward among osier beds. There is now neither beauty nor vale enough to justify the name. The collieries have gnawed the farther slopes; a railway runs upon an embankment through what was once the abbey meadow; and the village, which belonged formerly to Saint Mary’s house, consists chiefly of a public-house, a blacksmith’s, twelve cottages of grey stone, and a church restored beyond recognition in 1874.

The abbey stands—or rather reclines—half a mile beyond the church. Its nave is a pasture, its cloister an orchard, and cattle rub themselves against the bases of the piers. Only the east wall retains grandeur. It rises from nettles and fallen masonry, weighted at the summit by ivy, and pierced by the vast vacancy of its window. In April, when I first saw it, blue hyacinths shone like water beneath the oaks; their colour seemed to have drained from the absent glass and settled upon the earth.

The vicar of Beauvale, Mr Colbran, had invited me to dine after discovering that I possessed an unprofitable interest in monastic records. He was a little man, lame in one leg, with a face drawn sharply awry, as though Nature, having intended benevolence, had been interrupted by a satirist. His parish disliked him. This was not because he neglected the poor, for he gave them more than he could afford; nor because he preached too long, for his sermons rarely exceeded twelve minutes; nor, I think, because he disbelieved more than a vicar ought. They disliked him because he knew precisely when they lied and generally forgave them before they had finished.

His housekeeper gave us mutton, pickled walnuts, a cheese of astonishing severity, and port which was much too good for the vicarage. During dinner Colbran spoke of drains, a disputed footpath, Roman pottery, and the bishop’s latest appeal. Only when we had drawn our chairs toward the fire did he mention the abbey window.

“You admired our great emptiness,” he said.

“The east window?”

“What remains of it. There are five lights, if one may call holes lights, and tracery of about 1430. The original glazing survived, according to the accounts, for only thirty-seven years. An expensive brevity. The Devil broke it.”

He said this in the mild sing-song tone with which he announced matters particularly open to doubt.

“The Devil?”

“A provincial devil. The great ones would have entered by the west door.”

He limped to a cabinet and took from it a flat volume bound in cracked brown calf. The leaves within were of vellum, but the writing was a seventeenth-century hand, copying an older record. He handled it with the tenderness he denied to most living institutions.

“This belonged to the abbey. It was found in a malt-house at Mansfield, being used to press hops. Listen.”

He translated from the Latin as he read:

“In the winter after the glazing of the great window, upon the Vigil of the Purification, while the brethren sang at nocturn, there appeared outside the eastern wall a spirit of fire. Its face was black, its limbs naked and bleeding, and it clung upon the stones as a bat cleaveth to a tower. Brother Anselm cried out that the Enemy was come. Then the spirit struck the image of blessed Saint Michael, and cast it down, and brake the middle light, so that the fair glass flew inward and outward with great thunder. Thereupon it fell from sight. And when, at dawn, the brethren went forth with cross and holy water, they found upon the snow many pieces red as new blood, but no body, nor print of cloven foot.”

Colbran closed the book.

“There is a marginal addition. Brother Anselm, who had seen the fiend most plainly, was thereafter troubled with wind.”

“That may explain something.”

“It explains Brother Anselm. The window needs another explanation.”

“A falling branch?”

“There were no trees near the east end. A stone from the vault? It fell both outward and inward. Lightning? In a snowstorm, perhaps, though lightning does not usually climb before striking. No. I prefer the Devil.”

He filled my glass, then his own, though less generously.

“Or rather,” he continued, “I prefer the man whom they made into one.”

He leaned back, and the firelight caught the crooked planes of his face. Outside, rain whispered against the windows; but in the stillness of the room I seemed to hear another weather, older and more savage—the dry hiss of snow among trees, and ice whooping upon a frozen river.

“A serf, perhaps,” said Colbran. “Young. Ignorant. Hurt. We know that the abbot’s neighbour, Sir Ralph de Mornay, lost two barns by fire that winter, and that one of his bondmen fled after killing a horse. Put those scraps together. History does no more, though it charges by the volume.”

He looked into the fire.

“And suppose the fellow could speak.”

* * *

The horse came at me with his mouth open.

I had known Red Sorrel since he was foaled, and had slept beside him in harvest weather, my head against his warm flank. But that day there was devilry in him. His eyes showed white; the bit was red with his blood; and Hobb had beaten him about the ears till he screamed like a woman.

“Take him,” said Hobb.

“I dare not.”

“Take him, dog.”

I caught the rein. The horse struck me down. When I rose he came again, flinging his forefeet, and I saw the iron above my face. There was a fork beside the wall. I took it. I remember the jar in my hands, the shaft bending, and the great beast kneeling as if at prayer. Then he rolled over, blowing blood upon the straw.

Sir Ralph said the horse was worth twenty of me.

They bound me to the ash in the court. My mother came out of the bakehouse with flour upon her arms, but Hobb thrust her back. Sir Ralph sat beneath the pentice and watched. He did not rage. Had he raged I might have borne it better. He ate an apple while they flogged me, cutting away each bruised part with his little white teeth.

At first I cried out. Afterward I bit the tree. The bark broke between my teeth, and its taste was sour and green. They beat till the thong was clogged and would no longer whistle. Then they cast water over me and beat again.

“Twenty of him,” Sir Ralph said.

Whether he meant twenty blows or twenty lives I knew not. The sun went across the court. Chickens scratched near my feet. My mother knelt within the bakehouse door, holding her apron to her mouth, and I watched her because there was naught else in the world I loved, and because I thought that, if I looked away, she would vanish.

At dark they loosed me. I fell, for my legs had forgotten their work.

“Crawl home,” Hobb said. “On Monday thou shalt be hanged.”

Why Monday, I could not think. It was Thursday. Perhaps Sir Ralph would not hang men on the Blessed Virgin’s day, or perhaps the rope-maker came on Monday. Such thoughts troubled me as I lay in our hut. My mother washed my back with warm water and salt, weeping without sound. I heard the children breathe in the straw. Every breath was a little life, and mine had only three nights remaining.

Toward midnight I rose.

My mother woke and caught my wrist.

“Lie down.”

“I thirst.”

“There is water.”

“I thirst,” I said again.

She knew. Her hand tightened, then opened.

I crept to the door. Before I passed out she said my name, very low. I dared not turn. Had I seen her face I should have stayed and been hanged on Monday.

The frost had hardened the dung in the yard. Clouds went low above the roofs, and the moon appeared and was hidden, appeared and was hidden, as though she fled behind black hedges. I went first to the stable. Red Sorrel lay where he had fallen, covered with sacking. I touched his mane. It was stiff with frozen sweat.

“Forgive,” I told him.

There was a lanthorn in the tack room, and a coal glowed within it. I carried that coal to the hay.

Fire begins like a small beast. It crouches, licks, hides itself; then finds courage. A yellow tongue went along the manger. Another climbed the post. All at once the loft opened red, and the roof-thatch answered with a roar.

I stood watching. The heat pressed my face, and the wounds upon my back drew tight. Horses kicked at their stalls. One broke loose and came through the smoke with its mane afire. It ran across the court like a king crowned in burning gold.

Then men shouted.

I fled.

The wood received me, but gave no shelter. Bramble tore my legs; low boughs struck my head; roots rose beneath the snow to trip me. Behind, the sky grew red above Sir Ralph’s hall. The fire seemed to run from tree to tree after me, though when I turned there was only darkness and the red breathing of the clouds.

I went north because the moss grew thick upon that side of the trunks. So my father had taught me. Yet in frost all trees wore white, and I knew not north from death.

By dawn I could go no farther. I pressed myself beneath a holly, where the leaves stabbed but the snow was thin. My back burned, my hands were without feeling, and hunger turned within me like a millstone grinding air. All day I heard men. Sometimes they were near, sometimes far; once a hound gave tongue, and my body stiffened till I was no more than the frozen earth beneath me.

They passed.

At dusk I ate holly berries. I vomited them, then ate them again.

Night after night I went through the forest. By day I hid in ditches, in hollow trees, beneath fern dead and brown as old hair. I drank snow. I chewed bark. Once I found a turnip frozen in a field and sucked it till my gums bled. The cold entered me by the mouth and remained behind my heart.

On the third night—or the fifth, for nights had grown many and days were no more than grey pauses—I heard water beating under ice.

A mill stood there. The wheel was still, but the stream fought it and fought beneath its frozen boards. From one shutter came a thread of light.

I crept to the door and lay against it. Warmth breathed through the wood: warmth and meal and beasts and man. I feared man, yet I feared the forest more. I scratched once. No answer. I scratched again.

The door opened.

She held a rushlight above her head. Her hair was red—not bright as flame, but deep and dusky, like beech leaves after rain. She had a narrow face and great eyes darkened by the light behind them.

“What art thou?”

I could not answer.

She lowered the rushlight and saw the blood upon me.

“Jesu.”

“I am no thief,” I said.

“Nay. Thieves stand upright.”

Then I fell across her threshold.

Her name was Martha. She dragged me into the byre and covered me with sacks. Cows turned their slow heads, breathing sweetness. I lay between their bodies like a nut within its shell, and for a while there was neither frost nor Sir Ralph nor Monday, but only dark warmth, the chewing of beasts, and her hands about my face.

She brought milk in a wooden bowl. I drank too greedily, and she drew it away.

“Softly.”

“More.”

“Softly, fool.”

I caught her wrist. It was thin, but I could feel her blood moving beneath the skin.

“More.”

“If thou diest of it, wilt thou blame me?”

“I shall bless thee.”

She laughed once. Then she gave me all.

In the morning her father came to the byre. I heard his heavy tread and burrowed beneath the straw. He thrust hay into the racks, cursed a cow for kicking, and stood so near that meal dust fell from his clothes upon my lips. Martha entered behind him.

“Men have come from Mornay,” he said. “A bondman burned the stables.”

“What bondman?”

“A black-haired knave. Scarred now, they say. He slew the red horse.”

“The horse that savaged two grooms?”

“Sir Ralph’s horse may savage whom he pleases.”

“What will they do with the man?”

“Hang him if they are merciful.”

“And if not?”

Her father spat into the gutter.

“They ask through every township. There is silver for him.”

“Much?”

He looked at her. “Enough.”

When he had gone she uncovered me.

“They will kill thee,” she said.

“No.”

“They will.”

“No.”

I knew she spoke truth, but truth is sometimes a door one can keep shut by setting one word against it.

She washed my wounds. The water became pink, then red, then pink again. When the cloth entered the deep cuts I bit my arm. She told me not to make a sound. I made none. Sweat ran from my forehead though the byre was cold.

“Why didst thou burn it?” she asked.

“They beat me.”

“For the horse?”

“For living.”

She sat back upon her heels. A strand of hair had fallen across her mouth, and she blew at it impatiently.

“My father says bondmen are born to service.”

“My mother bore me, not service.”

“Who owns thy mother?”

I shut my eyes.

For two days she hid me. She brought oatcakes, onions, curds and milk. Once she brought honey upon her finger. I licked it, and she snatched her hand away as if I had bitten her. Thereafter she would not look at me for a while, but her cheeks were red.

At night we whispered. Her father slept above the mill; every creak of the beams seemed his waking. Martha told me that her mother was dead, that she hated the mill because flour whitened all things alike, even a young face, and that beyond the northern ridge lived men under no lord.

“Wolfsheads,” I said.

“Free men.”

“Thieves.”

“Wouldst thou not steal bread?”

“I have stolen none.”

“Thou wilt.”

She was right in this also.

The next afternoon riders entered the yard.

I heard bridles, the stamping of hooves, men striking their gloves against their thighs. Through a crack I saw Hobb. Snow shone upon his shoulders. His nose was red with cold, but I knew the stoop of him, and my wounds knew it.

Martha came swiftly into the byre.

“Behind the wheel-pit,” she whispered.

I crawled where she pointed. There was a space beneath the flooring, close above the black water. I lowered myself into it. Ice touched my belly. The stream sucked at my knees.

Hobb entered with another man.

They turned the hay. They thrust a spear into the straw where I had slept. The point came through the boards above my cheek.

“A wench lives here,” said Hobb.

“The miller’s daughter.”

“Search her bed.”

The other laughed.

I had no weapon. My fingers closed upon a stone beneath the water. Had they touched her, I would have risen, though the spear waited. But the miller shouted from the yard that no man entered his daughter’s room, whether for Sir Ralph or the king.

At last they left.

Martha drew me up. I shook so that my teeth struck one another.

“Tonight,” she said. “Thou must go tonight.”

“Come with me.”

She stared.

I had not thought the words before they came. Yet once spoken they stood between us, more solid than the mill.

“Come,” I said.

“Into the snow?”

“To the free men.”

“Thieves.”

“Free men.”

She put both hands to her hair and twisted it back. “My father—”

“Would sell me.”

“He has not.”

“He will.”

She struck me. Not hard; yet I saw in her face that she wished she had struck harder.

“Go alone.”

“I cannot.”

“Then be hanged.”

“I cannot go alone,” I said.

This was not because I knew no path. I had gone alone through forest and frost and fire. But in two days she had given me milk, and the touch of her wrist had awakened in me a hunger worse than the belly’s. The thought of leaving her made the world as empty as it had been beneath the holly.

She turned away.

At night she returned wearing a woollen cloak. She carried a sack.

“Bread,” she said. “Cheese. Flint. A little salt.”

“Martha—”

“Say nothing.”

“Thy father?”

“Say nothing.”

She gave me a knife. It was the miller’s broad knife, sharpened thin by years of cutting rope and leather.

“Bring me a knife, I thought,” she said. “If I bring him a knife, he can go. Then I thought, Bring me food. Then, Bring me my cloak. Fool, fool, fool.”

She began to weep angrily.

I put my arms about her. For a moment she resisted, straight and hard; then she came against me with such force that the pain opened across my back. I felt warmth soaking through my shirt. She felt it also and drew away, her fingers dark.

“Blood.”

“It is naught.”

“It is thy life.”

“There is more.”

We went by the millstream, walking in the water so our tracks should not show. Ice broke about our ankles. The cold bit upward into the bones.

Snow began before we reached the trees.

At first it fell softly, lighting upon Martha’s hair and lashes. Then the wind arose. The larchwood hissed. Snow crossed sideways, quick as thrown sand, hiding the path behind us and the world before. Trees reeled out of whiteness and were gone. Darkness pursued from trunk to trunk.

Martha held my belt. Twice she fell. The second time she did not rise until I dragged her up.

“Leave me,” she said.

“No.”

“I cannot.”

“No.”

We had no longer north or south. The moon fled above torn clouds. The forest cried in many voices: bough against bough, fox, owl, wind imprisoned in hollow trunks. Once we heard bells. They sounded beneath the earth, then above us, then nowhere.

“Faery,” Martha whispered.

“Church bells.”

“No church rings at such an hour.”

“Then faery.”

We went toward them.

The trees ended suddenly. Before us stood a great black wall, and in the wall burned heaven.

I had seen horn lanthorns, rushlights, candles upon Our Lady’s altar. I had seen sunrise lying red upon flooded fields. But this was light made solid: high as trees, divided by stone branches, blue and gold and green, with red in the midst of it deep as a wound. Shapes lived therein—kings, winged beasts, women crowned with stars. They neither stirred nor breathed, yet their colour poured upon the snow.

Martha fell to her knees.

“Saints,” she said.

Behind the shining shapes moved other beings, pale and many-faced. They swayed together. Their voices came through the wall, rising and falling like bees within a summer roof.

I was afraid, but wonder was stronger.

“See,” I said. “They have caught the dawn.”

“Come away.”

“They have bound it in stone.”

“Come away.”

The red light touched her hair. It shone there so that she seemed kin to the fiery people in the wall, only more living and more beautiful because she trembled.

“I shall bring thee some.”

“What?”

“Of the light.”

She seized my hand. “Dare not.”

But I desired it. I had nothing to give her. The bread was hers, the knife was hers, the cloak about us hers. My body belonged to Sir Ralph, my soul to God, and Monday waited somewhere behind the snow. Yet there was the red light, burning freely, and I thought: I will tear down a piece of heaven. I will give her that.

The wall was rough with ledges. I put my foot upon a carved face and climbed.

My hands had little feeling. Snow packed between stone and skin. Once I slipped, tearing my knee, but caught an iron bar. The singing within ceased.

The many pale faces turned toward me.

Then they cried out.

I saw them clearly through the coloured brightness: men with shaven heads, their mouths round, their eyes white. To each body the glass gave many heads—a blue head, a gold one, a red one floating above the shoulders. They raised their arms. One flung water. Another held a cross before him.

“Devils!” I shouted to Martha. “There are devils within!”

“Come down!”

A stone man stood beside me in armour, wings spread from his back. His spear was lifted against a beast beneath his feet. I caught the spear to raise myself higher. The stone moved.

There came a cracking like ice breaking upon the river.

I struck the shining wall with the knife-hilt. Once. Twice. The red light shivered but held. I struck again.

Heaven burst.

Glass flew about me, bright leaves of blue and crimson. The pale beings fled, tumbling over one another. Wind rushed through the wound I had made and flung their lamps into darkness. The stone winged man bowed outward. I clung to him. For one instant saint and sinner hung together above the snow.

Then we fell.

I remember the weightlessness, Martha’s cry, and red shards turning beside me like blood that had forgotten to fall.

The snow received us. The stone man struck near enough to cover me with white. I lay without breath. Something cut my palm.

Martha dug me out.

“Alive?” she said. “Alive?”

I could not speak, so I touched her face.

Bells began above us, wild and hammering. From behind the broken light came wailing, and a chant shouted fast as men could form the words.

Martha dragged me up. In my hand remained a piece of the red brightness.

Away from the wall it seemed darker, yet a coal lived in it. I held it between us. The storm-light entered one edge and came forth upon her cheek like warm blood.

“For thee,” I said.

She stared at it.

“Magic.”

“A bloodstone.”

“What blood?”

“Mine.”

She wrapped it in a fold of cloth and put it against her breast. Then we fled while the bells cast their iron voices after us.

At dawn we reached deeper woodland. The storm had passed, leaving the trees bent under snow. Day made the bloodstone black and rough, no better than a broken potsherd. Martha took it out and frowned.

“The magic is gone.”

“Night will wake it.”

“How knowest thou?”

“I know.”

We found a hollow beneath the roots of a fallen oak. I cut dead twigs from under the snow while Martha struck flint. The first sparks died. We struck again and again, our hands clumsy, our breath clouding the hollow. At last a shred of bark smouldered. I bent above it, giving it my breath until flame came—a frail yellow creature, crouching, licking, finding courage.

We fed it.

Heat entered our fingers with pain. We ate bread made wet by snow, and cheese hard as wood. Never had feast tasted so sweet. Martha laughed with her mouth full. I laughed because she did. The forest stood white and grave around us, but beneath the root there was fire, and bread, and the round enclosure of her arms.

We slept pressed together. Her head lay beneath my chin. Her hair smelled of smoke and meal. I thought of my mother, but grief was distant, across a field I could not cross. I thought of Sir Ralph’s apple teeth; of Red Sorrel crowned in fire; of the coloured wall and the many-faced devils. All sank away.

When I woke, evening had entered the wood.

The fire was nearly dead. Martha still slept. I took out the bloodstone.

Night had awakened it. The ember’s last light entered the glass and spread there, red and secret, as though my heart had been taken from my breast and made hard enough never to perish. I held it above Martha. Colour lay upon her closed eyes, her lips, her throat.

She woke and saw.

“Thy life,” she murmured.

“My life.”

She closed my fingers around it and pressed my fist to her breast.

Then, far off, a wolf cried.

Another answered from beyond the ridge.

Martha sat upright. The forest listened with us. A third cry rose, nearer than the first, thin at the beginning and deepening until it seemed to run beneath the snow.

The fire gave one small flame and went out.

* * *

Colbran stopped.

Rain still whispered at the vicarage windows. The port stood between us, darkly reflecting the lamp.

“Well?” I said.

“Well what?”

“What happened?”

He spread his hands. “They found the outlaws. The outlaws were all amiable social reformers and received them with rustic delicacy. Our young incendiary became a prosperous yeoman; Martha bore twelve red-haired children; and the bloodstone was set in silver and remains to this day in the possession of their numerous and entirely respectable descendants.”

“The wolves?”

“Were singing madrigals.”

“You do not believe that.”

“My dear fellow, belief is a luxury of those who possess evidence. We have a burned stable, a broken window, a frightened monk with indigestion, and some red glass dug up in the abbey meadow. Between those facts there is room enough for happiness.”

“And for death.”

“Much more room,” he said softly.

The fire settled. For a moment his twisted face lost its mockery and seemed merely tired.

“They were together,” he added. “That is something. They had warmth. They had the stone. Let us say they lived happily ever after.”

Outside, along the wooded ridge, the wind rose and passed from tree to tree. I remembered the white hollow, the dead fire, and the answering voices drawing nearer through the dark.

“No,” I said.

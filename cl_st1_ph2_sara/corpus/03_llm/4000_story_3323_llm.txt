### The Nest-Egg

After wandering for the better part of a July morning along a creek which, according to the solemn testimony of three men at the village store, contained trout enough to provision a regiment, but which, according to my own hooks, flies, lines, patience, and increasingly profane experience, contained nothing more animated than minnows, water-spiders, and one aged turtle of a peculiarly contemptuous expression, I came at last—hot, hungry, and anathematizing the whole finny creation—upon a house that seemed to have grown where it stood without ever consulting carpenter, compass, or public opinion.

It was long where it ought to have been broad, low at one end and unexpectedly aspiring at the other; it had a porch that began with confidence, reconsidered the matter, and stopped midway; and one green shutter, hanging by a single hinge, made a continual bow to the breeze. Yet there was no desolation about it. Not even a growling dog. The yard descended in a velvet-like slope to the road; hollyhocks stood in companies along the fence; hens promenaded under the currant bushes with that busy and consequential air which belongs equally to barnyard fowls and minor officials; and from an open kitchen door came the fragrant evidence of coffee, bread, and something savory in a skillet.

All was open—gracious-looking—pastoral.

A little beyond the house, under a shed whose roof had ripened to the color of old silver, an elderly man sat shaving a cedar peg with his pocketknife. He was spare, brown, and bent, though not infirm; his chin was furnished with a white beard of uncertain boundary, while his eyes retained so much youth that they appeared to have been borrowed from a boy and set, by some benevolent mistake, in that weathered face.

“Good morning,” I said.

He surveyed my fishing basket, which was light enough to have floated away on a sigh.

“Fishin’?”

“I have been engaged in what is locally described by that name.”

“Git any?”

“I have brought away a profound respect for their intelligence.”

He laughed, shut his knife, and slapped one knee.

“Well-sir, you struck the wrong branch. They ain’t no fish there—leastways, not this time o’ year. Folks’ll tell you anything at the store. Set there all day whittlin’ soap-boxes and stockin’ the cricks with lies. You look het up.”

I admitted that I was thirsty.

“Worter’s what you want. Come right along. Spring-house is back here. Marthy!” he called toward the kitchen. “Stranger here wants a drink!”

A woman’s voice answered, “Take him the gourd, father,” with such composed authority that he immediately began searching for it.

The spring-house stood beneath two sycamores, half hidden by fern and jewelweed. Inside, where the air was cool enough to seem a separate climate, clear water ran through a stone trough among brown crocks of milk and yellow bowls of cream. The old man could not find the gourd; he tried a tin cup, discovered a family of spiders in it, apologized to them and to me, and finally proffered a small blue bowl.

“Drink hearty,” he said. “Worter won’t hurt nobody, ’less they git it in their boots.”

I drank once for thirst, once for pleasure, and a third time because he stood over me with the hospitable anxiety of a physician administering a revivifying cordial. The water had the chill of underground darkness and a faint sweetness from the stone. When I had finished, he removed a saucer from one of the crocks.

“Now try some cream.”

I protested feebly.

“Sho! You’re holler as a stovepipe. Here, Marthy sets this un fer churnin’, but she won’t begrudge ye.”

Thus urged, I drank cream of a thickness and plenitude unknown to city commerce. It descended like liquid velvet and left upon my mustache an incriminating crescent which I attempted to remove without attracting notice.

“Good?” he asked.

“It would reconcile a man to failure.”

“Marthy’s cows,” he said proudly. “She knows cattle. Knows ’em better’n most men knows their own relations.”

At that moment Marthy herself appeared in the doorway.

She was not young, although her father’s manner of calling her had somehow prepared me for a girl. She may have been forty; perhaps a little more. Tall, broad-shouldered, and sun-browned, she carried a basket of newly gathered beans against one hip. Her sleeves were rolled above the elbow, showing strong bare arms; her dark hair, touched here and there with gray, was parted smoothly and gathered at the back without ornament. There was health in her, and quiet command, and something in the level frankness of her gaze which approached the heroic—not the heroine of balcony, moonlight, and impracticable garments, but the older and rarer figure who could lift a flour barrel, soothe a sick child, face a winter night, and ask no audience.

“You’ve near drowned the gentleman, father,” she said.

“Drowned! He ain’t had but three bowls o’ worter and a mite o’ cream.”

“He’ll stay to dinner, then.”

I began the customary refusal, which she received as one receives the clucking of a hen—an expected noise without binding significance.

“We’re eating in half an hour,” she said. “You can wash at the bench. Father, don’t keep him standing in the damp.”

As she turned, a little oval locket slipped from inside her dress and swung against the basket. She caught it quickly and put it away. The act was trivial; yet I remember it, as one remembers the single low note beneath a cheerful tune.

The old man took me about the place while dinner advanced toward us in fragrant stages. He showed me six cows, naming the character and moral deficiencies of each; a red bull whom he praised from a safe side of the fence; the orchard; the smokehouse; and a rooster of rubicund magnificence, who tilted his head while my host sang two lines of an ancient camp song, and seemed to listen with critical intelligence.

“Marthy runs it,” the old man said, waving toward barns, fields, house, and the general abundance. “I make out to help. Leastways, I don’t hinder more’n I kin help. She’s my oldest. Three gals we had—Marthy, Annie, and Luce. Luce is out West, married to a feller in dry goods; Annie lives over beyond Bell’s Ridge. But Marthy—she’s the nest-egg. Always was.”

He sat on the lower rail of the orchard fence and resumed his cedar peg. I perched beside him, for I confess—though perhaps confession is needless—that curiosity had begun to overcome even hunger.

“Never married?” I asked.

“Marthy? No, sir. Had chances, I reckon. Must of. Woman like her! But she never took to the men-folks, some-way-another. Too sensible, maybe. Then she knowed her pap needed her.”

He shaved the peg thoughtfully.

“Though onc’t,” he continued, “I mistrusted they was a young army chap hangin’ round.”

The knife paused. A bright shaving curled away and dropped upon his boot.

“This was years ago—purt’ nigh twenty, must be. Marthy’d been visitin’ her Aunt Salome, down by the barracks, and she come home one Saturday with Lieutenant Morris. Fine-built feller. Straight as a ramrod; boots shined fit to signal with; talked polite and easy. You know that kind?”

I said I had known several whose boots exceeded them in brilliance.

“Well-sir, Morris wasn’t no fool. I liked him right off, which made it worse. Stayed to supper, stayed Sunday, stayed Monday, and after that he’d ride over whenever he could git leave. Marthy’d fetch out the best preserves fer him. He’d help her carry things, and they’d walk down by the spring. I says to myself, ‘There it is. He’s after Marthy.’”

The old man’s face took on a retrospective anxiety, comic at first, but softened by affection.

“Now, I didn’t want it. Ain’t ashamed to say so. Annie was prettier—little, yeller-haired thing, all curls and dimples—and Luce could chatter a bird out of a tree; but Marthy was mine. Her mother’d been poorly fer years, and Marthy held us together. She knowed where everything was, what everything cost, which cow’d freshen, when the roof leaked, how much medicine to give, and who needed comfort without their askin’. Take Marthy out, and this place’d been like a wagon with the kingbolt gone.

“So I watched that lieutenant. Blame-don, I watched him close. If him and Marthy went to the gate, I’d need the mail. If they set in the parlor, I’d remember the clock wanted windin’. If he praised her biscuits, I’d tell him Annie could sing. I wasn’t mean to him, you understand—never mean. Jest wanted to turn his attention a little.”

“Did Marthy perceive your strategy?”

“Marthy? No. Leastways, she never let on. She’d look at me sometimes, queer and quiet, but she never said nothin’. That was her way.”

He tested the sharpened peg against his thumb.

“Then her mother died. Marthy done everything. Washed her, dressed her, set up two nights, comforted the little gals, and kep’ me from goin’ clean to pieces. Morris come to the buryin’. Stood back under the maple in his uniform. Afterward, when the folks was gone, I seen him talkin’ to Marthy by the lilacs. She had her hand on the gate, and he bent close, solemn-like. Well-sir, my heart jest dropped. ‘He’s asked her,’ says I. ‘I’ll lose her now.’”

From the house came the bright clatter of plates. Somewhere in the meadow a cowbell sounded and stopped. The old man smiled.

“But I’d got it wrong.”

He had driven to town some months afterward, he told me, to purchase hinges, coffee, and a new piano which Annie, through a campaign of sighs, hints, and catalogues, had at last persuaded him was indispensable to civilized existence. He returned at dusk, inflated with news and half inclined to postpone its announcement for the luxury of being entreated.

“Marthy was at the gate,” he said. “Standin’ jest so, with both hands atop of it. She’d been waitin’, I reckon. Soon as I come up, she says, quick-like:

“‘Did it come, father?’

“‘What?’ says I, though I knowed well enough.

“‘The new piano?’

“‘Yep,’ says I, ‘the new peanner’s come. But that ain’t it.’

“She looked up the road past me. ‘Did you get the coffee?’

“‘Got the coffee, but that ain’t it.’

“‘Strawberries for supper?’

“‘Two boxes. But that ain’t it.’

“Then she kind o’ laughed. ‘Well, what is it? Has somebody come?’

“I says, ‘Somebody’s comin’ Sunday.’

“She changed—jest a little. Couldn’t say how. Her face got still, and she says, ‘Somebody coming to see me?’”

Here the old man chuckled and shook his head at the ancient misunderstanding.

“See how she took it? Thought it was Aunt Salome, likely, or one of them school friends. I says, ‘No, not exactly to see you. Lieutenant Morris is comin’. He met me in town and told me the whole thing. Him and Annie’s goin’ to be married!’”

The knife slipped from his hand then and landed in the grass. He bent after it, so that for a moment his face was hidden.

“Marthy jest looked at me,” he went on. “White—white as milk. Then down she went, right there by the gate. Fainted dead away. Scared me out of seven years’ growth, I tell ye. I hollered fer Annie, fetched worter, fanned her with my hat. We got her in. Doctor said she’d been doin’ too much since her mother died, and the good news upset her. Marthy always did feel things deep fer her sisters.”

He found the knife and carefully wiped the blade on his trousers.

“Annie married Morris that fall. Pretty weddin’. Marthy made the cake and fixed Annie’s veil. Wouldn’t let nobody else do it. Cried some, too, but women always cries at weddin’s. Morris done well. Captain now—or major, maybe. Got two boys. They come here summers, and Marthy thinks the world of ’em.”

He looked toward the kitchen, where her shadow passed across the open door.

“Yes, sir. She’s the nest-egg. Others flew off, but she stayed. Never had no foolishness in her.”

I made no reply. There are moments when even an educated and sociable outsider may improve conversation by withdrawing from it. The old man, serenely unconscious of the story he had told, whittled another shaving and began to explain how lightning had once entered the north chimney, displaced three bricks, frightened Annie’s cat, and spared a jar of pickled peaches by what he considered direct intervention of Providence.

Dinner rescued me.

It was spread in the cool kitchen beside two open windows: fried chicken, potatoes creamed with parsley, green beans, corn bread, rolls of a tenderness almost theological, butter like molten gold, comb honey, sliced tomatoes, and coffee whose aroma restored my confidence in earthly arrangements. The pullet before us may have been, for all I knew, a near relative of the gay patrician rooster in the yard; but hunger is notoriously indifferent to genealogy, and I accepted a second portion.

Marthy moved about the table with quiet efficiency, filling cups before they were empty and resisting thanks as if gratitude were an unnecessary tax on hospitality. Her father talked without interval—of crops, veterans, calves, politics, and my deplorable choice of fishing water. Once he called her “Nest-egg.”

She glanced at him and smiled.

It was a good smile, warm and patient, but I noticed that her hand went briefly to the locket beneath her dress.

After dinner she cut bread and wrapped chicken for my return journey, though I represented, with perfect truth, that I had eaten enough to sustain me through a siege. At the yard gate she directed me to a better stream beyond the ridge.

“You may find trout there,” she said.

“And if I do not?”

“Then you’ll at least have had a pleasant walk.”

This philosophy, so superior to my morning’s, rebuked me gently. I thanked her, thanked her father, and went down the road. At the bend I looked back. The old man had returned to his shed; Marthy stood upon the porch, one strong arm lifted to draw in a curtain before the sun reached the room. Behind her were the irregular house, the open kitchen, the shining pans, the long years of labor; before her, only the familiar yard and road. Then she went inside, and the house seemed to gather itself about her.

I caught no fish that afternoon. I must be excused when I confess that I scarcely tried.

I am always thinking, when summer roads grow white under the noon and sycamore leaves turn their pale sides to the wind, of that cool spring-house and the blue bowl brimming with water.

I am always thinking of crocks in the running trough; of coffee and rolls; of honey luminous as amber; of the rooster listening gravely to a foolish song; of an old man sharpening a cedar peg while, with cheerful innocence, he laid bare the wound he had never seen.

And most of all I am thinking of Marthy—upright at the gate, white for one revealing instant; then risen again to make the wedding cake, arrange the veil, tend the father, keep the farm, welcome the stranger, and conceal in a little oval locket whatever part of her life no one had thought to ask about.

The nest-egg, the old man called her.

The one kept safely at home, while all the others flew.

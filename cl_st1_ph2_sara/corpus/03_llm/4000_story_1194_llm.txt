## The Bell at Angeline’s

The meeting-house bell began tolling while the dew was still heavy on the grass, and the sun, not yet clear of the low eastern woods, struck through the apple branches in long, pale bars. Every spider’s web on the currant bushes was a little wheel of silver. In the Lambert yard three persons stood listening: Oliver Lambert, with one suspender unbuttoned and his gray head bent; his wife, Hannah, holding the dish-cloth she had brought out with her; and their granddaughter Ada, who had come barefoot from the kitchen.

“Fifty-eight,” said Oliver, when the bell stopped.

“Fifty-nine,” said his wife.

“I counted,” said Oliver. “I guess I can count a bell yit.”

“So can I. ’Twas fifty-nine.”

Ada looked across the wet fields toward the meeting-house steeple. The last tone seemed still to be moving in the morning air, though it had ceased; it went away over the meadows like a dark bird which one could follow after it had become too small to see.

“Who is it?” she asked.

Neither answered her at once. Oliver went to the chopping-block, took up the axe, and set it down again.

“If ’twas fifty-eight, it’s Mis’ Patterson,” he said.

“Mis’ Patterson’s sixty-one, if she’s a day,” said Hannah. “Besides, she was settin’ up in bed yesterday and askin’ for fried pork. It’s Angeline Laurence.”

Oliver’s mouth took an obstinate downward turn.

“Angeline ain’t fifty-nine.”

“She is, too.”

“She was two years younger’n Edward.”

“She was one year older. I ought to know. I went to school with her.”

“You went one term.”

“I went long enough to know her age.”

Oliver limped toward the woodshed, as if the matter might be settled by his withdrawing from it.

“Well, I ain’t goin’ to be beat by a meetin’-house bell,” he said. “They make mistakes tollin’.”

Hannah watched him with the severe patience of a woman accustomed to seeing truth resisted on grounds of personal honor. Then she turned to Ada.

“It’s Angeline,” she said. “She’s been failin’ ever since March, though she wouldn’t own to it. She was one that never owned to much.”

Ada had seen Angeline Laurence only at church and occasionally in the village store. She remembered a tall, slight woman in a black bonnet, with a face pale and delicately worn. Angeline’s eyes had always seemed fixed upon something beyond the people to whom she spoke. To Ada, who was seventeen and had a natural reverence for concealed grief, that face had resembled the faces of saints in the illustrated Bible, although no saint in the Bible wore steel-bowed spectacles or carried a paper of cream-of-tartar from the store.

“Was she all alone?” Ada asked.

“Lizy Mears was with her last night. She’s got cousins over to North Pembroke, but they ain’t been near her more’n twice in ten years. She wa’n’t one to complain.”

Oliver’s axe began to fall upon the wood in short, angry blows.

“Your gran’ther knows well enough she’s fifty-nine,” Hannah continued. “He’s thinkin’ about the property. He’s heard she left the Laurence place to the missionary society, and he says they’ll turn it into a home for heathen children.”

“I never said no such thing,” Oliver called from the shed.

“You said near enough to it.”

“I said there’d be fools enough ready to take it.”

Hannah went back to her kitchen. She mopped the table with unsteady vigor, although it was already clean. Ada followed and began putting away the breakfast dishes. After a while her grandmother spoke again.

“Angeline was goin’ to marry your father once.”

Ada turned with a saucer in her hand.

“My father?”

“Yes. Edward.”

This was how Ada first heard of it. Her father had been dead eleven years, and her mother nearly as long. She remembered him only as a tall man who whistled while shaving and once carried her through a snowdrift. His youth had never seemed to belong to him. She had supposed that he had come into the world already married to her mother, already concerned with mortgages and hay and the price of boots.

“He was engaged to Angeline,” Hannah said. “That was before he went West and met your mother. They had a quarrel at the church sociable. There was a spelling match, and Edward spelled down everybody but Angeline. Then she gave him ‘separate,’ and he put an ‘e’ where the second ‘a’ belongs. She laughed. Everybody laughed. He said she’d prompted the schoolmaster, and she said he was a poor loser. He walked home with Lottie Fernald that night jest to plague her.”

“And Angeline?”

“She walked home alone.”

Hannah lifted the kettle from the stove, though the water was not boiling.

“Edward expected she’d send for him. She expected he’d come. Neither one did. He went out West the next spring. Men can generally get farther away from a quarrel than women can. He married your mother, and there wa’n’t a word to be said against her. She was a good woman. Still—”

“Still what?”

“Angeline never got over it.”

The statement dropped into the warm kitchen with the simplicity of a stone into deep water. Ada looked toward the window. Beyond it, the currant bushes shone, and the white webs had begun to disappear as the sun dried them.

Thirty years. Angeline had gone to church, made preserves, washed her curtains, swept her front steps, and watched winters sink into spring for thirty years, loving a man who belonged elsewhere. Ada imagined that hidden love enclosed in the severe old house like a lamp burning in an upstairs room.

Oliver came in with an armful of wood.

“Your grandmother’s fillin’ your head with notions,” he said.

“Wa’n’t Angeline engaged to Father?”

“A boy-and-girl business.”

“Didn’t she love him?”

Oliver dropped the wood into the box.

“She had plenty of time to stop.”

“But did she?”

“I don’t know as it’s anybody’s concern now she’s dead.”

He shut the wood-box lid with unnecessary force. Ada saw that he knew the answer.

The funeral was held two days later. Rain had beaten the lilacs beside Angeline’s porch, and their purple heads hung low under the drops. The parlor smelled of wet wool, camphor, and the white lilies arranged around the coffin. Ada stood beside her grandmother and looked at Angeline’s quiet face. Death had removed the reserve which had guarded it. She seemed not old, nor even greatly tired, but only beyond the reach of explanation.

A week afterward Squire Bellows drove into the Lambert yard.

Angeline Laurence had left her house, land, furniture, linen, dishes, clothes, books, and the sum of twelve hundred dollars to Ada Lambert, daughter of Edward Lambert.

Oliver read the copy of the will three times.

“There must be some mistake,” he said.

“There ain’t,” said Hannah.

“I don’t see why she didn’t leave it to Edward’s parents, if she was set on the family.”

“Because she wa’n’t in love with Edward’s parents.”

Oliver looked injured.

“She could have remembered us some.”

“She remembered Ada.”

Ada sat by the window with her hands folded. The inheritance embarrassed and awed her. It seemed less like property than a confidence. Angeline’s love, which had been hidden for thirty years, had been opened after death before the whole village, like the skeleton of an old beauty taken from a locked room. People discussed it at the store and after prayer meeting. Some said it was romantic; more said it was peculiar; two of Angeline’s North Pembroke cousins said it was unsoundness of mind.

Ada went through the house with her grandmother. The rooms were scrupulously ordered. In the upper chamber hung Angeline’s dresses: brown alpaca, gray merino, and the black silk she had worn on Sundays. Her folded handkerchiefs had narrow hems stitched with a minuteness which was almost painful. There were twelve linen sheets, bundles of lavender, blue-edged china, pewter spoons, and a mahogany sewing-table. In a drawer lay a faded ribbon and a small tintype of Edward at twenty-three.

Hannah looked at it and sighed.

“He was handsome enough then,” she said. “Though he always had that same look as if the world had miscounted against him.”

Ada put the tintype back.

Nothing was sold. Oliver urged that the hayfield might as well go, and there was no sense keeping clothes which would be eaten by moths; but Ada would not consent.

“She gave them to me,” she said. “I’m going to keep them.”

“You can’t keep every rag because a dead woman wore it.”

“I can try.”

Hannah sided with Ada, though less from romance than from respect for good linen. Once a month they opened the house. They aired the rooms, beat the rugs, and dusted the dark furniture. Ada learned where Angeline had kept the starch, where the roof leaked over the back stairs, and which window swelled in wet weather. The garden grew neglected, but she tied up the roses and cut the dead stalks in autumn. Each year the old apple tree leaned farther over the well, its branches gray and beseeching.

Five years passed. Oliver died in February after insisting for three days that his illness was “jest a cold.” Hannah survived him one summer. She failed quietly while the garden beans were coming in, and in September Ada followed her grandmother’s coffin past the meeting-house where the bell tolled seventy-four times without anyone disputing the count.

Afterward Ada moved into Angeline’s house.

She was twenty-two, with brown hair, candid gray eyes, and a face which had not yet learned the village habit of withholding approval. Her friends thought it lonesome for her at the edge of the north road. Ellen Page, who lived near the mill, urged her to take a room in the village.

“You’ll hear every board creak up there,” Ellen said. “And that Noble man lives next door.”

“His house is a quarter of a mile away.”

“That’s next door in the country.”

“What’s wrong with him?”

Ellen lowered her voice.

“Nothin’ that anybody can prove. He’s queer. Love-cracked, they call him.”

Ada disliked the phrase. It made private sorrow sound like a defective jug.

Sylvester Noble’s house could be seen from the west chamber, beyond an abandoned orchard and a stone wall. It was a small, weather-darkened place with a workshop behind it. He lived alone and came seldom to the village except for flour, lamp oil, and books arriving by post. He was perhaps thirty-eight. His beard was dark, his shoulders slightly stooped, and his eyes, when he lifted them, were of an unexpectedly clear blue.

Ada first spoke with him on an October afternoon. She was trying to mend the garden gate, which dragged on one hinge. She had raised it upon a stone and was striking the hinge with a hammer when a shadow fell across the path.

“You’ll split the post,” he said.

She started.

“I guess it’s split already.”

“Not past saving.”

He removed his hat. His hair had been flattened by it and stood up boyishly when the hat came away.

“If you don’t object, Miss Lambert, I could set that hinge.”

“I don’t object.”

He went home for tools and returned. He worked without waste of motion, driving a new pin and planing the swollen edge. Ada held the gate steady. There was an awkward gentleness in his manner, as if he feared even his assistance might be unwelcome.

“You’re staying here through the winter?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“It drifts bad along this road.”

“I’ve seen snow before.”

“I expect you have.” He examined the gate. “Still, a woman alone might need help.”

Ada smiled a little. “I can ring the meeting-house bell.”

“It’s a mile off.”

He did not smile. The next day he came with a small brass handbell mounted on a black wooden handle.

“My mother used this when she was sick,” he said. “I thought you might keep it. If you ring from the back porch, I can hear at my place, unless the wind’s wrong.”

Ada took the bell. It was polished, but there was verdigris in the carved lines around its rim.

“I couldn’t call you every time a hinge comes loose.”

“No. But if you were frightened, or ill, or needed anything.”

“Wouldn’t it trouble you?”

He looked toward the orchard.

“I’m not troubled by being needed.”

She placed the bell on her bureau beside Angeline’s lavender box. In the lamplight it shone warmly, a small domestic moon. She sometimes touched it when she passed, though she did not ring.

Winter came early. Snow filled the north road and heaped itself against the stone walls until the walls seemed to sleep under long white bodies. Sylvester broke a path to Ada’s pump after every storm. He brought dry kindling when hers smoked, reset the kitchen stove, and once climbed the roof in sleet to stop water coming through the back chamber.

“You’ll fall,” Ada called from below.

“I’ve fallen before.”

“That ain’t a reason to do it again.”

He looked down, smiling then, and the smile altered his whole face. The villagers’ name for him seemed more foolish than ever.

He never stayed without a reason. If Ada offered tea, he accepted with gratitude, but he sat on the edge of the chair and left as soon as his cup was empty. He spoke more readily of birds, stones, and plants than of people. He had cases of moths in his workshop, notebooks filled with drawings of ferns, and jars which Ada preferred not to inspect closely.

In January her pump froze. Sylvester carried water from his well twice a day until the thaw.

“You’re spoilin’ me,” she told him.

“No.”

“I’ll forget how to do for myself.”

“You won’t forget.”

“Folks say you don’t care much for company.”

“I don’t care for a great deal of it.”

“Is that different?”

“To me.”

He stood by the kitchen window, watching snow sift from the old apple branches. Ada thought some grief must have made him solitary. Perhaps the woman he had loved had died; perhaps she had married another. Angeline’s story had prepared in Ada a tender chamber for all defeated affection, and she admitted Sylvester there before she knew what claim he had upon it.

By March she listened for his step. When the door opened and the cold entered before him, she felt the room become less empty. She began to notice the distinction of his hands, which were brown and scarred but fine at the fingers, and the patience with which he repaired anything breakable. Once he mended the catch of Angeline’s workbox, though the brass was thin and the wood had split.

“Most men would say it wa’n’t worth doing,” Ada said.

“Most things are worth doing if somebody cares whether they’re done.”

She looked down quickly at the red thread in her lap.

In spring they worked in the garden. Sylvester pruned the roses and braced the old apple tree. Ada planted peas and sweet alyssum. The house seemed to wake. The open windows filled with the smell of wet earth; birds disputed under the eaves; pale green entered the neglected orchard like light through old glass.

Ellen Page came one afternoon in May and found Sylvester kneeling beside the currant bushes.

“He’s there considerable, ain’t he?” she said after he had gone.

“He’s my nearest neighbor.”

“That don’t always mean a man has to weed your onions.”

“He wasn’t weeding onions.”

“Currants, then. Weeds is weeds.”

Ada folded a dish towel.

“I guess Mr. Noble can be civil without the whole village measuring it.”

Ellen’s expression sharpened with the concern of a friend who has brought warning and intends that it shall not return unused.

“You don’t know about him.”

“I know what I’ve seen.”

“You ain’t heard about his brother?”

“No.”

Ellen drew her chair nearer.

The story came circumstantially. Sylvester and his younger brother, Martin, had inherited their father’s farm and money. Martin had been engaged to a girl named Ruth Warden. According to Ellen, Sylvester had discovered some flaw in the will, taken the greater share, and then won Ruth away from his brother. Martin and Ruth had gone to Vermont, poor and brokenhearted. Sylvester, ashamed or disappointed, had withdrawn from everybody.

“My Aunt Caroline knew the Wardens,” Ellen said. “She heard the whole thing. That’s why folks call him love-cracked. He got what he wanted, and it didn’t do him no good.”

Ada’s face had grown pale.

“Are you sure?”

“As sure as anybody can be about what happened fifteen years ago. I wouldn’t repeat it except I saw how things was goin’. You haven’t got father nor mother to tell you.”

Ada went to the west window after Ellen left. Sylvester’s roof showed above the blossoming orchard. All at once his solitude looked different. What she had regarded as sorrow became punishment; his generosity became a possible disguise. She remembered every silence and fitted guilt inside it.

Yet she did not ask him. Angeline and Edward had lost thirty years because neither would cross the road between pride and love, but an old warning is often clearest after one has repeated it.

Sylvester came that evening. He was leaving the next morning for the Green Hills, as he did every June. He had a cabin there, twelve miles away, where he studied plants and reptiles.

“Reptiles?” Ada said, though she knew this already.

“Mostly snakes.”

“You kill them?”

“Rattlers, near the farms. Not the harmless kinds.”

He looked at her, troubled by her coolness.

“I’ll be gone three or four weeks. I wanted to see if the back window catches secure.”

“It’s secure enough.”

“And whether you had kerosene.”

“I can buy kerosene.”

“Yes.” He turned his hat in his hands. “If you needed anything, Mr. Green’s boy comes along Fridays.”

“I don’t expect to need anything.”

He was silent. The room, which had held so many easy pauses between them, now seemed crowded with all Ada refused to say.

“Have I offended you, Miss Lambert?”

She wanted him to deny the story without being charged. She wanted innocence to speak by some miracle of its own.

“I don’t know as you’ve any duty to me,” she said.

His face changed slightly.

“I thought perhaps I had made one.”

“No. I wouldn’t want you to feel bound.”

“I didn’t say bound.”

“Well, it’s best not to misunderstand.”

He laid the brass bell upon the table. Ada had brought it down that morning, intending somehow to return it.

“You won’t need that, then,” he said.

“No. I guess not.”

His hand rested for a second beside it. Then he bowed with a formality more painful than anger and went out.

Ada carried the bell upstairs and set it at the back of her bureau behind Angeline’s lavender box, where she could not see it from the bed.

For two weeks she heard nothing of Sylvester. She told herself she was relieved. She went to church, planted beans, and helped Ellen’s mother put up strawberries. The roses flowered extravagantly along the porch, their pink faces open to every passerby.

Then Ellen came hurrying up the path one hot afternoon.

“I’ve got to tell you,” she said before she entered. “I’ve got to, if you never speak to me again.”

Ada’s hands became cold.

“Has something happened?”

“No—leastways I don’t know. It’s what I told you. I told it wrong.”

Ellen had met her Aunt Caroline at a funeral in West Pembroke. When she repeated the Noble story, her aunt corrected her.

It was Martin who had taken Ruth from Sylvester.

Their father’s will had left nearly everything to Sylvester, the elder son. After Martin and Ruth fled together, Sylvester had sold the best land and given them the money. He kept only the small house and workshop. Martin had wished people to believe the division required by law; Sylvester had never contradicted him.

“He gave up the girl and the property,” Ellen said. “Aunt Caroline says Ruth wrote him for help after Martin took sick, and he sent it every year till she died. I got the names crossed. Or Aunt Caroline told it crooked the first time. I don’t see how I— Ada, say something.”

Ada was holding the edge of the table.

“You were sure.”

“I thought I was.”

“You said you were as sure as anybody could be.”

“I know it. I feel terrible. But he’ll come back, and you can tell him.”

Ada looked toward the west window.

“He may not want to hear me.”

“Oh, men don’t hold things the way women do.”

“My father did.”

Ellen began to cry, partly from remorse and partly from the injustice of having truth prove less manageable than rumor.

Ada did not reproach her again. She had believed because the story had been brought to her, but also because there had been a small pride in her which preferred judging Sylvester to risking trust. Ellen had opened the door; Ada herself had gone through it.

That night she brought the brass bell from behind the lavender box. A pale line of dust marked where it had stood. She polished it with flannel until its rim caught the lamplight, then placed it in front of the box. The act comforted her for only a moment.

Three weeks passed. Then four.

Sylvester did not return.

His house remained closed. Rain darkened the roof and beat the orchard blossoms into the grass. Ada watched his chimney morning and evening. Once, at sunset, she thought she saw smoke, but it was mist rising behind the trees.

She asked Mr. Green’s boy if he had heard anything.

“No’m. Mr. Noble most generally comes back ’fore July.”

At five weeks she went to the post office.

“He’s got mail,” the postmistress said. “Two papers and a letter from Vermont. Queer he don’t send for it.”

“Maybe he means to stay longer.”

“Maybe. He ain’t regular except when he is.”

The phrase followed Ada home.

At six weeks she obtained directions to the cabin from Amos Green, who had hauled lumber into the hills.

“You ain’t thinkin’ of goin’ up there?”

“I might send word.”

“There ain’t anybody to send. Cabin’s beyond Hawley pasture, then up Snake Brook. Path forks by a split boulder. You take the left. Right goes to an old charcoal pit. Rough country for a woman alone.”

“Is it rougher for a woman than a man?”

Amos considered this.

“Rocks don’t know the difference, I expect. But there’s rattlers.”

Ada packed bread, cheese, matches, a flask of tea, and strips of clean linen. She did not explain to Ellen, who would either have tried to stop her or insisted on going. Before dawn the next morning she put on stout shoes and Angeline’s old brown shawl. At the last moment she slipped Sylvester’s bell into her bag.

The road to the Green Hills ran north between meadows dim with mist. The sun rose as she walked, touching the upper branches first and then spreading over the fields. Grasshoppers sprang from the dusty wheel tracks. Farmhouses became fewer. By eight o’clock she had passed Hawley pasture and entered the woods.

The path climbed beside a stream. Ferns crowded it, brushing wetly against her skirt. The hills, which from home looked smooth and blue, were made here of steep earth, roots, ledges, and shadow. Ada began to understand how distance can conceal the nature of a place, as gossip conceals the nature of a life.

At the split boulder she stopped. Moss covered both sides. One path was plain; the other had almost disappeared beneath mountain laurel. Amos had said left, but which direction had he faced when he said it? She stood listening. Nothing moved except the brook and a woodpecker striking somewhere far off.

She took the fainter path.

By noon she knew it was wrong. It descended into a hollow blackened by old fires. Round pits sagged among the trees, and charcoal fragments crumbled under her shoes. She sat on a stump and ate a little bread, though she had no appetite. The quiet seemed to gather around her. She thought of Angeline waiting thirty years in ordered rooms because one foolish quarrel had never been mended. Love might endure; that was not the same as saying it would return in time.

Ada went back to the boulder and chose the other path.

The afternoon grew hot. Once a long green snake slid across the trail, and she stood rigid until it vanished. Later she saw a small brown coil on a sunlit rock. She could not tell whether it was a root or a rattler and made a wide circuit through the brush.

Toward four o’clock she smelled wood smoke.

The cabin stood in a clearing under hemlocks, lower and darker than she had imagined. One shutter hung open. A stack of firewood had fallen beside the door. There was no sound.

“Sylvester!” she called.

Her voice went strangely among the trees.

She crossed the clearing and knocked. The door yielded under her hand.

At first she saw only a table, a cold stove, and shelves crowded with bottles and specimens. Then she saw him on a bunk against the wall.

Sylvester opened his eyes.

“Ada?”

He spoke as though her name belonged to a fever.

She went to him. His face was hollow and flushed, his beard untrimmed. One leg was wrapped from ankle to knee in soiled cloth.

“What happened?”

“Rattler. Not a bad strike. I cut it. Thought I’d done enough.”

“You’ve been here alone?”

“Couldn’t walk far. Fever came on.”

“How long?”

He tried to reckon and failed.

Ada set down her bag. The cabin smelled of sickness, dried plants, and something bitter simmered long ago. A pail beside the bunk was empty.

She brought water from the spring, made tea, opened the shutter, and washed the wound. The bite itself had begun to heal, but the leg was swollen and infected where he had cut it with his knife.

“You made it worse,” she said.

“I often do.”

He watched her with an expression she could not bear.

“I thought you wouldn’t care to see me again.”

“I was wrong.”

“About needing me?”

“About everything.”

He closed his eyes.

“You oughtn’t be here. It’s near dark.”

“I’m here.”

“There’s snakes.”

“I noticed.”

His mouth moved with the shadow of a smile.

Ada fed him tea by spoonfuls. Then she gathered wood and kindled the stove. Her hands were steady while there was work. She spread her shawl over him when the mountain air cooled, and sat on a stool beside the bunk.

At dusk he became restless. He spoke once of Ruth, asking someone to take her safely down the road. Later he said Martin’s name with no anger, only weariness. Ada heard in those broken phrases the remains of a loyalty which had survived betrayal without requiring witness.

Night entered the clearing. The open shutter became a square of darkness. Ada lit the lamp, and its small flame laid gold upon the bottles and the edges of leaves pinned over the table. Moths struck the screenless window.

Sylvester woke near midnight.

“Why did you come?”

“You didn’t.”

“Didn’t what?”

“Come home.”

He looked at her for a long moment.

“I thought I’d wait till I could pass your house without looking.”

Ada bowed her head. On the floor beside her bag the brass bell showed a narrow curve of light.

“I heard a story about you,” she said. “I believed it. Then I heard the truth.”

“Stories don’t often trouble with the truth.”

“I should have.”

“Why?”

“Because I knew you.”

“You knew how I fixed gates.”

“I knew more than that.”

The lamp made his eyes very blue in his wasted face.

“I sent you away,” Ada said. “I wanted you to know I was sorry, but I was afraid you’d come back before I could say it. Then I was afraid you wouldn’t.”

He turned his face slightly toward the wall.

“Ada, fever makes a man hear what he wants.”

“Then I’ll say it again when you’re well.”

He did not answer. After a while his hand moved across the blanket, uncertainly, and she placed hers beneath it.

At dawn she rang the bell.

She stood outside the cabin and rang until the clear sound passed through the hemlocks and returned from the ledges. It was not loud, yet in that enclosed wilderness it seemed to travel with a purpose larger than itself. She rang, waited, and rang again.

Near seven o’clock a man answered from the lower path. It was a shepherd searching for two lost ewes. He stared at Ada, then at the cabin, and went at once to fetch help.

By afternoon Sylvester was carried down the hill on a litter made of poles and blankets. Ada walked beside him. At the split boulder he opened his eyes.

“Which path did you take?” he whispered.

“Both.”

“That’s like you?”

“No. It’s like people.”

He smiled and slept again.

Sylvester was ill for another month at his own house. Ada and Ellen took turns nursing him, though Ellen never entered his room without looking guilty. She confessed the whole matter on the second day.

“I don’t ask you to forgive me,” she said. “I wouldn’t, in your place.”

Sylvester regarded her gravely.

“You told Miss Lambert the correction?”

“Yes.”

“Then I guess you did what you could.”

“But I’d done what I couldn’t undo.”

“Most of us have.”

Ellen cried after she left, and afterward defended Sylvester so vigorously through the village that people began to suspect she had always done so.

Autumn came early that year. The maples flamed along the north road, and the old apple tree, despite its props, bore a dozen small red apples. Sylvester could walk as far as Ada’s porch with a stick. The first time he came, she had set the brass bell upon the table between them.

“I owe you this,” she said.

“I gave it to you.”

“You took it back.”

“I left it. That ain’t the same.”

She turned the bell by its black handle.

“If I keep it, what am I to ring for?”

He looked across the garden. Dead flower stalks leaned in the clear light; the currant leaves were spotted and thin.

“Anything you need.”

“And if the wind’s wrong?”

“I could stay nearer.”

Ada’s hand became still upon the bell.

“How near?”

He pointed with his stick toward the west field, but his eyes remained on her face.

“There’s two houses. Seems a waste to heat both all winter.”

“That sounds practical.”

“I’ve been tryin’ to make it sound so.”

She smiled. He did also, though with the shyness which had always dignified his happiness by making it difficult.

They were married in November, quietly, before the first snow. Some said Ada had inherited Angeline Laurence’s house and her romantic notions together. Others said Sylvester had done very well for a man considered love-cracked. Ellen maintained that she had known from the beginning how it would end, though no one, including Ellen, believed her.

Ada wore no veil. She wore Angeline’s black silk, altered at the waist, and a small piece of the faded ribbon from the drawer. Before leaving for the meeting-house, she stood alone a moment in the upper chamber. The dresses hung in their dim row; lavender scented the linen; Edward’s young face remained shut inside the drawer.

The room no longer seemed guarded by grief. Angeline’s long affection had not been fulfilled, yet neither had it vanished uselessly. It had passed into the keeping of a girl who had nearly repeated the old loss and had, at last, gone out to meet it.

Below, Sylvester called that they would be late.

“I’m comin’,” Ada answered.

She took the brass bell from the bureau and carried it downstairs.

Years afterward it stood upon the kitchen shelf at Angeline’s house. Its handle became worn smooth. It summoned Sylvester from the orchard, called children in from snow, and once brought a neighbor running when Ada fell beside the stove. On still evenings its note could be heard beyond the stone wall and across the field where Sylvester’s empty house slowly weathered among the trees.

People who heard it seldom thought of death, though bells had first taught Ada to count the years of the dead. This one counted nothing. It merely called, and because someone listened, it was answered.

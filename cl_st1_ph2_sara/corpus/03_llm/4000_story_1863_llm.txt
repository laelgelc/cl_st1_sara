### THE STOUT GENTLEMAN

*“What man is this, whose tread disturbs the night?”*  
—An inquiry which, though never put by Hamlet, might very properly have been.

It was on a Sunday afternoon, in the ancient and respectable town of Derby, that I found myself imprisoned by rain and a fever.

Of the fever I shall say little. It was one of those disobliging visitations which neither confines a man honestly to his bed nor permits him to go peaceably about his affairs. It lodged behind my eyes, beat a small drum at my temples, dried my tongue, chilled my shoulders, and filled my mind with that fretful activity which mistakes idleness for meditation. As for the rain, there was no saying little of it. It was everywhere.

Rain—rain—rain!

It came down upon the roofs in gray sheets; it drummed upon the coach-house; it gurgled in the gutters; it hissed in the yard; it descended from the eaves in solid ropes, as if heaven had lowered its buckets and forgotten to draw them up again. The clouds were slovenly, ragged, spongy fellows, lounging upon the chimney-pots. The whole town appeared steeped in melancholy.

I had arrived the preceding evening by coach, wrapped in a damp upper Benjamin, and had intended to proceed in the morning; but the fever, the Sabbath, and the weather formed a conspiracy against me. The roads were reported half drowned; no chaise could be had without a price fit for the ransom of a minor prince; and my host assured me, with a solemn satisfaction peculiar to innkeepers, that I should “never do it” if I ventured out.

Thus I remained at the inn.

It was a large, rambling establishment, built apparently at several periods and repented of at none. Passages took sudden turns and lost their resolution; staircases ascended with great confidence and arrived at insignificant landings; doors stood where no reasonable person expected doors, while rooms which ought to have possessed entrances seemed to have been sealed up in remote ages. The house enclosed a stable-yard paved with rounded stones, greasy with rain and littered with wet straw.

From the window of the travellers’ room I surveyed this little world until I knew every creature belonging to it. There was a carroty-headed hostler, who passed continually between stable and taproom, always carrying a bucket, though I never saw him either fill or empty one. There was a wall-eyed horse that thrust its long spectral head from a loose-box and regarded the weather with a look of personal injury. A miserable, crestfallen cock stood on one leg beneath a tilted cart, accompanied by two draggled hens, who appeared to reproach him for the climate. Three ducks, boon companions and confirmed topers, drank round a puddle with such perseverance that I expected every moment to hear them call for another bowl.

Now and then a commercial traveller crossed the yard with his coat over his head, splashing valiantly through the channels. These men, the knights-errant of modern traffic, wander from town to town, encountering not giants and enchanted castles, but vinegar-faced customers, bad dinners, and hard beds; bearing no shields except sample cases, and seeking no distressed damsels except unpaid accounts. On ordinary days their society is various and enlivening. But this was Sunday. The bar was quiet; the market was shut; the travellers had dispersed after breakfast to visit relations, sweethearts, or dissenting chapels; and I remained alone.

The travellers’ room offered few resources. On the table lay a stained county newspaper, six days old, which I read from the title to the printer’s name, including an advertisement for a lost spaniel and a long report of a meeting about turnpikes. There were also two odd volumes of sermons, a racing calendar for the year before last, and a pamphlet proving that the French nation must inevitably perish before Christmas. The clock ticked with a loud, pompous deliberation, as though each second were being entered in a ledger. The fire smoked. The window rattled. My candle, although it was scarcely three in the afternoon, drooped over its wick like a sleepy old gentleman at church.

I ordered breakfast, having eaten none.

The waiter brought me eggs overdone to the hardness of musket-balls, toast which had acquired the toughness of saddle-leather, and butter whose antiquity was not merely to be tasted but contemplated. I rang. The waiter came.

“What is this?”

“Butter, sir.”

“I am aware of its profession. I ask its history.”

He stared at me, removed it, and returned with another piece of precisely the same family, perhaps a younger brother.

It was while I sat trifling with this repast that a bell rang in an upper part of the house.

Not a modest, accidental tinkle, but a long, decided peal—the summons of one accustomed to being obeyed.

The waiter started.

“Number Thirteen,” said he, and hurried away.

Presently he returned, carrying a tray upstairs. Upon it I observed a substantial breakfast: a covered dish, a loaf, a tankard, and a pot of coffee large enough for a small committee.

“Who is in Number Thirteen?” I asked.

“The Stout Gentleman, sir.”

“The what?”

“The stout gentleman as came late last night.”

This answer, instead of satisfying me, opened a region of conjecture.

A stout gentleman! Here was at once figure, rank, appetite, and mystery. I pictured a prosperous elderly man, broad in the waistcoat, full in the cheek, rubicund about the nose; one who had dined well for thirty years and intended to dine better for thirty more. He wore, in my imagination, a blue coat with brass buttons, a watch-chain heavy enough to moor a boat, and a white neckcloth tied with civic dignity. He was perhaps an alderman. Yes—an alderman from London, travelling north on municipal business, with opinions on turtle soup, poor-rates, and the decline of public manners.

The bell rang again.

The waiter flew upstairs and presently descended with a plate in his hand and indignation in his face.

“What is amiss?”

“Eggs not done to his liking.”

“Ah!” thought I. “A man of consequence. Exact at table; impatient of neglect; accustomed to command.”

A second plate went up. In a few moments another peal shook the passages.

“Now what?”

“Coffee cold.”

The coffee was replaced. Then came a third complaint: the toast was damp. A fourth: there was no mustard. A fifth concerned the newspaper.

“He wants a London paper,” said the waiter.

“There is none.”

“So I told him.”

“And what did he say?”

“Said an inn without a London paper wasn’t fit for Christians.”

This shook my aldermanic theory. A true alderman would have carried the London intelligence within himself. Perhaps the stranger was a politician—a fierce parliamentary man, travelling from borough to borough; stout not merely in body, but in principle. He might be a Radical orator, nourished upon grievance, who required his newspaper as another man requires salt. I now clothed him in a brown surtout, gave him shaggy eyebrows and a loud voice, and imagined him thumping the breakfast table while denouncing ministers, bishops, taxes, boroughmongers, and lukewarm coffee.

Heavy footsteps crossed the room above me.

Thump—thump—thump.

They paused. Then returned.

Thump—thump—thump.

The ceiling creaked under their authority. The Stout Gentleman was pacing his chamber.

This agreed perfectly with a political character. He was composing a speech! I could almost hear its periods forming beneath his tread. At the eastern wall he abolished sinecures; at the western he reformed Parliament; at the fireplace he paused to overthrow the aristocracy; then turned and reconstructed the constitution before reaching the bed.

The rain continued.

Patter, patter, patter upon the glass; rush and gurgle in the spout; drop—drop—drop from a leak in the corner into a blue basin placed there by the chambermaid. The gray light grew grayer. Smoke descended the chimney and spread itself about the room with the insolent familiarity of an old acquaintance.

The chambermaid came in to trim the fire. She was a thin, red-armed girl in a slammerkin gown, with a cap forever sliding from one side of her head to the other.

“Does the gentleman in Number Thirteen stay long?” I asked carelessly.

She tossed her head.

“Long enough, I should say.”

“He is difficult to please?”

“He’s no call to speak to a person as if they was a black beetle.”

“What has he done?”

“Asked whether I called that a clean towel.”

“And was it?”

She looked offended by the irrelevance of the question.

“Clean enough for any gentleman as behaves like one. Then he says the sheets was damp. Damp! In this weather!”

There was an unanswerable grandeur in her argument.

“And what kind of gentleman is he?”

“Stout,” said she.

“Yes; but elderly?”

“I never noticed.”

“Dark or fair?”

“Couldn’t say.”

“Tall?”

“Maybe.”

“Does he wear spectacles?”

She regarded me with growing suspicion.

“I don’t pry into gentlemen’s faces.”

With this rebuke—which came admirably from one who had been listening at his keyhole—she departed.

Her resentment furnished new evidence. The stranger was not a polished man. My respectable alderman faded; my parliamentary reformer lost dignity. In their place arose a coarse, purse-proud fellow, perhaps lately enriched by contracts—a retired brewer, a dealer in tallow, a grazier of prodigious cattle. I gave him square-toed boots, a plum-coloured waistcoat, thick hands, and a large seal-ring. His complexion became mottled. His speech took on a provincial breadth. He called servants “you there,” slapped his pockets for silver, and believed civility to be included in the bill.

At four o’clock another bell rang, and this time the landlady appeared.

She was a portly widow with a countenance constructed for public occasions. Her manner ordinarily combined the majesty of an empress with the vigilance of a housekeeper who suspects spoons. She listened to the waiter’s message, drew herself up, adjusted her cap-ribbons, and marched upstairs like a commander proceeding against an enemy’s citadel.

The house became still.

Presently a murmur arose overhead—first low, then animated. A deep voice boomed through the floor, but the words were indistinguishable. The landlady’s sharper tones answered. The deep voice rolled again. There followed a silence, a movement of furniture, and one emphatic stamp.

Then the landlady descended.

Her face was flushed, but her victory uncertain.

“Well?” said the waiter.

“He says,” replied she, “that the room smells of chimney.”

“So it does,” I observed.

She turned upon me.

“He says also that the bed-curtains are dusty, the bell-rope is greasy, the boots scratched his boots, and the bill will be looked into.”

“What did you tell him?” asked the waiter.

“I told him this house has been kept by respectable people for fifty-three years and has entertained noblemen.”

This appeared to me a curious reply to a greasy bell-rope, but inns, like nations, often answer present complaints by appeals to former glory.

“And what did he say then?”

The landlady compressed her lips.

“He laughed.”

This laugh demolished my tradesman. No mere tallow merchant would laugh at fifty-three years of respectability and noble guests. There was something fearless, perhaps aristocratic, in such conduct. Could the unknown be a peer travelling incognito? Stout peers are by no means rare. Indeed, rank, port wine, and stoutness have long maintained a secret alliance. I rebuilt him at once. His plum-coloured waistcoat became one of quilted silk; his seal-ring multiplied into ancestral signets; his coarse hands grew white, though still substantial. He was an eccentric nobleman disgusted with court, journeying unattended to escape ceremony. Perhaps he had fought duels in his youth, broken hearts in middle age, and now amused himself by alarming provincial landladies.

But why room Number Thirteen? A nobleman would demand the best apartment.

Unless—here romance seized me—unless concealment were essential.

He might be flying from enemies. He might be engaged in an affair of state. The brown travelling-coat hanging outside his chamber—if indeed it were his—might cover decorations, stars, perhaps even a ribbon. Who knew but that some illustrious personage, whose portrait hung in shop windows and whose health was drunk at public dinners, sat at that moment above my head, listening to the rain and eating bad toast?

At five, the Stout Gentleman ordered dinner in his room.

This privacy confirmed everything.

The order was not one of vulgar abundance, but of judicious appetite: a roast fowl, ham, potatoes, a small Stilton cheese, a bottle of port, and afterwards brandy and water. The waiter repeated the items in the passage, while I received each as a document of state. The fowl indicated breeding; ham, old English solidity; Stilton, discernment; port, loyalty; brandy and water, experience of the world.

“Does he dine alone?” I asked.

“Yes, sir.”

“Has he received letters?”

“No, sir.”

“Visitors?”

“No, sir.”

“Does he travel with servants?”

“Not as I know.”

“Much luggage?”

“One trunk, one carpet-bag, two coats, and an umbrella.”

The umbrella troubled me. It was too domestic for conspiracy, too prudent for a fugitive, too commonplace for royalty. Yet the greatest men carry umbrellas—or have them carried—and I refused to surrender my theory for so trifling an objection.

The dinner went up. During the next hour the house was haunted by savoury smells. The roast fowl, in passing, cast a reproachful fragrance over my invalid meal; the Stilton followed, grave, blue-veined, and potent; then came the port, glowing darkly in its decanter like a bottled sunset.

I heard the stranger’s chair move. A cork popped. There was silence. Another cork popped—or perhaps the stopper of the brandy decanter. The heavy feet crossed the floor more briskly than before.

Then singing began.

It was a deep, uncertain voice, strong in patriotism and weak in tune. The singer rolled through a song concerning England, oak trees, honest hearts, roaring cannon, lovely women, and the confusion of all foreign powers. At every chorus he struck the table, and at one particularly triumphant passage something fell over.

This altered the portrait once more.

A secret statesman would not bellow national songs through an inn floor. A fugitive prince would be more cautious. An aristocrat might sing badly, but seldom alone, and never before finishing the port. I now conceived him as an old military man—a major on half-pay, hearty, irascible, scarred, and loyal; one who had served abroad, hated the French, distrusted reforms, adored the navy, and drank the king’s health whether invited or not. His complaints were no longer insolence but habits of command. His heavy tread was the measured pace of the parade. His treatment of the chambermaid arose from a natural inability to distinguish servants from recruits.

I gave him gray whiskers, a bald crown, a red face, and a blue coat tightly buttoned over a martial stomach. There was perhaps a wooden leg.

No—the footsteps were too equal.

Unless he had two wooden legs.

The fever was increasing.

Evening closed early. The windows became black mirrors in which the travellers’ room, with its smoky fire and yellow candles, seemed doubled into another apartment inhabited by a pale, restless copy of myself. The yard vanished, except when a lantern passed and drew from the darkness a wet wheel, a glistening flank, or the white, astonished face of the wall-eyed horse. The ducks had retired, probably to a nightcap. The miserable cock remained under the cart, meditating suicide.

Sunday quiet settled upon the inn. The taproom murmurs died away. Doors shut; bolts slid; voices receded through passages. Somewhere a servant laughed and was instantly hushed. The clock ticked louder.

Tick—tick—tick.

Above me, the Stout Gentleman continued at intervals to move.

Thump—thump—thump.

Who could he be?

This question, which in health might have entertained me for five minutes, had now become the axis upon which my mind revolved. I attempted to read the sermons; the Stout Gentleman appeared between every two lines. I tried the turnpike report; he became chairman of the meeting. I looked at the fire; his face formed among the coals, swelled, reddened, and collapsed. Every sound belonged to him. The rattling casement was his umbrella; the chimney his breathing; the clock his watch; the wind his political opinions.

At ten, the waiter brought me hot water and recommended bed.

“What time does the gentleman in Thirteen leave?”

“Couldn’t say, sir.”

“Has he ordered a coach?”

“Not yet.”

“Did you see him dine?”

“I carried in the dishes.”

“Then you have seen his face.”

The waiter scratched his head.

“Well, yes, sir.”

“What is he like?”

“Why—stout.”

I could have thrown the hot water at him.

“Is he old?”

“Not particular.”

“Young?”

“Not as you might say young.”

“Gentlemanly?”

“Quite the gentleman when suited.”

“Military?”

“Couldn’t undertake to say.”

“Red-faced?”

“His back was to the candle.”

“Then how do you know he is stout?”

The waiter brightened, having at last reached firm ground.

“Because he fills the chair, sir.”

He withdrew, leaving me richer by this geometrical fact.

I went to my room, which lay across a narrow passage and two doors from Number Thirteen. The corridor was dimly lighted by a single candle placed in a wall bracket. It burned with a long, blue-edged flame and occasionally gave a nervous jump, as though startled at its own situation. Coats hung from pegs along the wall; swollen by shadow, they resembled headless men assembled for some ghastly council. At the far end stood a tall clock, ticking in a wooden case like a creature imprisoned upright.

I undressed, put on my nightcap, and lay down.

Sleep would not come.

The rain whispered against the panes. Water dropped somewhere with maddening regularity. A horse stamped in the stable. The boards creaked. Once I heard a prolonged sigh outside my door and sat upright, only to discover that it was the wind working under the threshold.

Then the heavy footsteps sounded.

Thump—thump—thump.

They came from Number Thirteen, crossed the room, paused, returned. A chair scraped. The bell rang once—violently. A servant shuffled along the passage. There was low talk, then silence.

I looked at my watch. Half past eleven.

My curiosity had reached that degree at which a man ceases to call it curiosity and names it duty. I felt that I owed it to myself, to society, perhaps to the constitution, to behold the Stout Gentleman. Should I return home without having seen him, I knew that his unseen face would pursue me through life. I should imagine him in crowds, in theatres, at public meetings. Every broad back would awaken hope; every stout waistcoat end in disappointment. I might grow old in conjecture.

At a quarter to twelve I arose and dressed.

The undertaking was simple: I would walk down the passage, pretend to seek the stairs, and, if the door of Thirteen happened to open, glance in. Nothing could be more innocent. Yet as I buttoned my coat, the business assumed the desperation of romance. I was a spy entering an enemy’s camp, a lover approaching a forbidden balcony, a knight advancing upon an enchanted chamber.

I opened my door.

The corridor was empty.

The candle had burned low, and a little lake of grease had formed around it. The coats watched me. The clock ticked.

I stepped out.

At that instant a board groaned under my foot with such treachery that I nearly retreated. Recovering, I proceeded on tiptoe. Number Eleven—Number Twelve—then Number Thirteen.

The door stood ajar.

My heart beat absurdly. I listened.

No breathing. No movement.

I knocked softly.

No answer.

I knocked again and pushed the door.

The room was deserted.

A candle guttered upon the table. The remains of supper stood in melancholy disorder: a plate with crumbs, the wounded Stilton, an empty decanter, a glass with a little brandy and water, and a crumpled newspaper which must have been procured from some coffee-room. The bed had not been disturbed. The trunk and carpet-bag were gone.

I entered one step farther. The fire gave a faint red blink, like an old eye preparing to close. Two chairs stood apart as if they had quarrelled. On the hearth lay the patriotic song, printed on a cheap broadside.

The Stout Gentleman had vanished.

I hurried into the passage. At its end was a back staircase. Had he descended while I dressed? Had he detected my interest and escaped? Had mysterious agents removed him? My fever instantly restored the affair to politics. I saw secret dispatches, midnight carriages, cloaked emissaries, perhaps assassination.

Then, near the stairhead, I noticed a pair of boots.

They were large, square, wax-topped boots, freshly blacked and standing side by side with a composure offensive to my excitement. They might be his—or they might belong to any stout gentleman in England. Boots, the servant so called because boots were his chief companions, had evidently placed them there for the morning.

I examined them as a naturalist examines an unknown species. They were broad in the foot, stout in the calf, and worn slightly at the outer heel. From this I inferred decision of character, a tendency to turn out the toes, moderate wealth, and perhaps gout. But boots are guarded witnesses. They disclose occupation readily enough—clay for the farmer, spurs for the horseman, dust for the pedestrian—but concerning the soul they preserve a leathern silence.

The night porter, appearing below with a lantern, demanded what I wanted.

“Nothing,” said I, with the dignity of a man discovered in his stockings beside another person’s boots.

I returned to bed and slept badly.

In the morning the rain had slackened to a fine gray drizzle. The yard awakened in steam and splashing. Hostlers shouted; chains rattled; horses coughed; coachmen in many-caped coats stamped their feet and drank hasty measures at the bar. The mail coach was to leave at eight.

I was at breakfast when the chambermaid entered.

“The stout gentleman’s going by the mail,” she said.

I sprang up.

“Where is he?”

“Paying his bill.”

“Which door?”

“Coach entrance.”

I rushed to the window. The coach stood below, shining with wet, its lamps pale in the morning. Passengers were mounting; parcels were thrust into the boot; the guard adjusted his coat; the carroty-headed hostler tugged at a strap with his whole body.

A broad figure came from beneath the archway.

But another coach passed at that instant and hid him. I shifted from one side of the window to the other. A vinegar-faced woman with three children obstructed the view. The guard cried, “Now, sir!” The broad figure placed one foot upon the step.

I saw only his back.

A brown coat of ample skirts; broad shoulders; a glimpse of drab breeches, remarkably full and respectable; one wax-topped boot rising; then the whole man disappeared into the coach.

“All right!”

The horn sounded. The horses sprang forward. Wheels hissed over wet stones. The mail rolled beneath the arch and was gone.

I remained at the window, staring at the empty yard.

Thus departed the Stout Gentleman—alderman, radical, brewer, nobleman, fugitive, major, and possible prince—leaving me, after twelve hours of investigation, in possession of no certain knowledge except that he had broad drab breeches and mounted a coach with his back toward me.

Even now, when I travel, a large pair of wax-topped boots will sometimes revive the whole adventure. I look eagerly at their owner; but he is always too thin, too civil, too young, too old, too plainly known, or otherwise unequal to the magnificent uncertainty of Number Thirteen.

The real man, whoever he was, could scarcely have matched him.

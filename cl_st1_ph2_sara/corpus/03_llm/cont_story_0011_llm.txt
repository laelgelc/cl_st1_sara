We called Gloria Finch “the Finch,” which shows you what kind of town we were.

Her last name was not Finch. It was Bell. She did not know about the nickname until years later, and by then she only said, “Well, birds are nice,” which made some of us feel worse and others feel vindicated, because that was exactly the sort of thing Gloria said.

She had a way of entering a room in three quick movements—head first, shoulders second, the rest of her catching up—and a way of pecking at her lunch with the tines of a plastic fork. Her hair was the color of old straw. Her nose came to a determined point. Behind her large brown glasses, her eyes made nervous trips from face to face, as though checking whether everyone had been fed.

“Cold one,” somebody would say.

“It is cold,” Gloria would answer.

Or someone would look out the window of Harlow’s Home and Feed and say, “Coming down hard.”

“Raining cats and dogs,” Gloria would say.

She did not seem empty, exactly. Empty things echo. Gloria absorbed whatever was said to her and returned it in a smaller, smoother form, like a machine that took in rocks and gave back driveway gravel.

Harlow’s belonged to Mrs. Harlow, Randy’s mother, though most of us still called it Harlow’s after Randy’s father, who had been dead eleven years and had never, even when alive, spent as many hours in the place as his wife. They sold paint, chicken wire, work gloves, birdseed, rat poison, ceramic flowerpots, keys cut while you waited, and some groceries for people who did not care to drive out to the highway.

Gloria worked the register and stocked the shelves. Randy handled deliveries, lumber orders, and anything requiring a ladder. His mother handled everything else, including the truth.

Mrs. Harlow believed in honesty the way other people believe in baptism. She wore her dead husband’s heavy black glasses, though the prescription gave her headaches, because she said modern frames made a woman look “frivolous.” Her face was tanned and folded. Her voice sounded as though it had been stored overnight in a whiskey barrel.

“People appreciate candor,” she said.

People did not, but they appreciated knowing where she was.

Randy was thirty-two then, broad through the chest, soft through the middle, with blond hair he combed straight back while it was wet. On Friday evenings he washed his red truck beside the store, even in winter, letting gray rivers run across the parking lot. He laughed at his own dirty jokes and ate snack cakes two at a time. He had a peculiar voice, rough and bright, like an orange rolled over broken glass.

Gloria loved him.

We did not know at first. We knew she watched him. But Gloria watched everybody, and when Randy lifted fifty-pound sacks of seed she watched in the same wide-eyed manner she used when Mrs. Harlow replaced the receipt paper.

Then Gloria began changing.

She started with opinions. Opinions were what interesting women had, she had decided, perhaps from television.

“Too much rain,” said Leon Mears one morning.

“We need it,” Gloria replied firmly.

Leon looked through the front windows at a parking lot under six inches of water.

“Not that much,” he said.

“No,” Gloria agreed. “Not that much.”

She tried politics for two days, saying taxes were terrible until Mrs. Harlow asked which tax, whereupon Gloria said, “All of them, I imagine,” and dropped a box of nails.

She attempted humor. When Randy said he was starving, she told him not to eat the hardware. When he laughed, surprised more than amused, Gloria repeated the joke to four customers, changing hardware to merchandise, supplies, and, finally, galvanized roofing screws.

Next came femininity. This was how we understood it at the time, though what we meant by femininity was that Gloria burned a crescent into her forehead with a curling iron, purchased a pink blouse with pearl-colored buttons, and began wearing enough perfume to defeat the smell of fertilizer.

She laughed differently too. Her old laugh was a single startled bark. The new one climbed a staircase: ha, ha-ha, ha-ha-ha. Sometimes Randy had stopped speaking before she reached the top.

“She’s fixing to make a fool of herself,” Mrs. Harlow told us.

She said this to customers while Gloria stood ten feet away pricing mousetraps.

“What for?” one of us asked, though we knew.

Mrs. Harlow leaned on the counter with the theatrical patience of a doctor explaining a fatal condition.

“My son.”

Gloria’s pricing gun clicked faster.

Mrs. Harlow turned toward her. “You hear me, Gloria?”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“And?”

“It’s good to hear clearly.”

This was such a strange answer that Mrs. Harlow had no immediate knife for it.

Randy, for his part, had hardly noticed. A person who has been admired all his life notices admiration as much as weather; Randy had not been admired all his life, but he had been fed, defended, corrected, employed, and loudly discussed, which can create the same dullness in the receiving muscles.

He learned from Denny Pike, who learned from his wife, who had seen Gloria buy a men’s shaving lotion because the clerk said women found it attractive on men.

“The Finch has got it bad for you,” Denny said.

Randy frowned. “Who?”

“Gloria.”

Randy looked through the loading-bay door. Gloria was sweeping. She moved the broom in brisk little strokes and wore the pink blouse beneath her brown apron.

“You think?”

That was the beginning.

Once the fact of her affection was placed before him, Randy could not stop looking at it. It was like a new sign across the road, ugly perhaps, or unnecessary, but impossible not to read. He began testing her.

“Best truck Ford ever made,” he said within hearing of Gloria.

“It’s a good truck,” she said.

He had spent the previous year hearing that truck called a rust bucket, a gas hog, and a public cry for help.

At lunch he held up his ham sandwich.

“Made it myself.”

“It looks delicious.”

“It is,” he said, after biting it. “It’s a damn good sandwich.”

“It’s a good sandwich,” Gloria said.

Now, we had heard these responses and judged them proof that Gloria contained about a teaspoon of personality. Randy heard them and judged her sensible. More than sensible: loyal. Here at last was a woman who understood quality without needing to hammer a man over the head with her understanding.

His mother noticed the change before Gloria did.

“Don’t go getting ideas,” she told him.

“What ideas?”

“That girl would agree with a fence post.”

“So? A fence post doesn’t have any complaints.”

“She’s got the sex appeal of a damp envelope.”

This angered him, possibly because it was cruel and possibly because, until that moment, he had not considered Gloria in terms of sex appeal. Opposition can be a match applied to material a man had assumed was stone.

“She’s nicer than most people.”

“She’d have to know most people to be nicer than them.”

“No skin off your ass who I talk to.”

“It becomes my ass when you talk to her in my store.”

Randy left, slamming the office door. Mrs. Harlow smiled. She believed she had won, because the last loud sound in an argument generally belonged to the loser.

That afternoon Randy left a chocolate mint beside Gloria’s register.

“For me?” she asked.

“Unless the cash register eats candy.”

Gloria laughed her staircase laugh, but halfway up it collapsed into her old bark. Randy smiled. He liked the bark better.

Imagine you are Gloria. Imagine that childhood has been a series of light but painful little blows: girls exchanging looks when you sit beside them, boys asking you to dances as a joke, a teacher saying you are “pleasant” because she cannot think of anything else, your own aunt advising you to develop useful skills. Imagine you have spent thirty-four years being tolerated in public and forgotten in private. Then a man puts a chocolate mint beside your hand and says it is for you.

A cheap mint. Green foil. Two bites if you are careful.

You might carry it home. You might place the wrapper in the little drawer where you keep stamps, loose buttons, and your father’s expired driver’s license. You might lie awake with your heart behaving foolishly, extravagantly, as if someone had hung the moon when all he had done was spend twelve cents.

The next gift was a cream-filled snack cake. Then a key ring shaped like Texas, though neither of them had been to Texas. Randy began leaving notes beneath the register tray.

YOU LOOK NICE TODAY.

Gloria read that one until nice grew strange and detached from language.

I LIKE YOUR PERFUME.

She wore twice as much the following day, and Randy, watery-eyed, did not complain.

His notes grew longer.

WHEN YOU SAY I CAN FIX THINGS IT MAKES ME FEEL LIKE I CAN.

And later:

MOM SAYS I’M NO GOOD WITH MONEY BUT YOU DON’T. YOU SEE THE BEST IN ME.

Mrs. Harlow discovered a note because Gloria, startled by a customer, dropped it among the coupons.

She read it aloud.

“‘You see the best in me.’ Well, that doesn’t take long.”

Randy snatched the paper from her.

“You had no right.”

“I own the counter.”

“You don’t own what’s on it.”

“I own everything on it until it’s sold.”

“Gloria isn’t for sale.”

Nobody had suggested that she was. Nevertheless, he said it with such force that Gloria turned pink from throat to hairline.

We expected the whole thing to end there. This is important. We expected Gloria’s heart to be broken because that was the shape of the story we recognized. A plain woman reaches too high; a foolish man enjoys being worshiped; the sharp mother cuts the string; down comes everybody. Off to the races and then into the ditch.

Instead, Randy kissed her in the stockroom.

There were bags of birdseed stacked behind Gloria and a shipment of plungers at Randy’s elbow. The fluorescent light flickered. Romance does not always wait for the staging it deserves.

He said, “Can I?”

Gloria said, “You can.”

Afterward he asked, “How was that?”

“Not bad.”

Randy laughed.

“Not bad at all,” she added, and because he knew her by then, he understood this as a strongly self-certain lightning bolt of constant opining.

They began going out. They ate at the Wagon Wheel, where Gloria ordered what Randy ordered until Randy, wanting to display generosity, ordered what she ordered, and the two of them spent several minutes saying, “No, you first.”

They drove to the reservoir and watched the water. Randy told stories he had told before. Gloria laughed. She pointed at a horse and said, “A horse.” She rolled down the window and said, “Some fresh air.” She was no more dazzling than she had been. But she glowed. There is no other accurate word, and we objected to it when someone first used it, because glow seemed sentimental and Gloria was standing under the blue-white pharmacy lights at the time. Still, she glowed.

And imagine you are Randy. Imagine that most praise you have received came with an invoice attached. Your mother calls you strong when furniture needs moving, smart when she wants the bookkeeping finished, handsome when a divorced woman enters the store. Imagine a woman praises you whether you are useful or not, whether she is nearby or not. She tells other people you are patient, that you know engines, that you can make anybody laugh. Soon you become more patient. You learn more about engines. Your jokes, being listened to with love, lose some of their mean edges.

Perhaps admiration is not always a mirror. Perhaps sometimes it is a lamp.

Randy began taking pride in the store. He reorganized the lumber racks and found a cheaper supplier for pet food. He stopped arriving twenty minutes late. He gave an elderly customer free delivery and refused to let his mother add a fee afterward.

Gloria stopped trying so hard. The curling iron went back beneath the sink. The pink blouse remained, but she wore it because Randy liked it. Her perfume settled to a survivable level. She expressed two or three authentic opinions, one of which was that canned peas were “mushy in a bad way,” and Randy treated this statement with the seriousness of a Supreme Court decision.

They married in October.

The ceremony was held at the little brick church whose tower had no bell. A storm in 1968 had removed it, and fundraising efforts had since purchased a freezer, carpeting, hymnals, and an electronic keyboard, but never another bell. Mrs. Harlow sat in the front pew wearing a navy dress and her dead husband’s glasses.

She had opposed the wedding until the week before. Then something shifted. No one knew exactly what. Gloria claimed Mrs. Harlow had seen Randy asleep at the break-room table with Gloria’s cardigan tucked under his cheek. Mrs. Harlow claimed she simply preferred a lawful marriage to “all that mooning around.” I imagine the truth was less tidy. Love can wear down a hostile person through repetition, the way footsteps wear a path nobody planned to make.

At the reception Mrs. Harlow stood to speak.

We braced ourselves.

“My son,” she began, “has never known when he had a good thing.”

Randy lowered his eyes.

“But this time,” she said, “he does.”

That was all. Gloria cried so hard she hiccupped. Mrs. Harlow shoved a napkin at her and said, “Don’t drown in public.”

We predicted six months. Some gave them a year. Denny Pike said Randy would tire of hearing his own opinions returned to him. His wife said Gloria would finally wake up. Mrs. Harlow said anyone betting on her family’s misery could do it somewhere besides her store, thank you very much.

The marriage thrived.

We were almost irritated by it.

Randy washed Gloria’s car on Fridays beside his truck. Gloria brought him lemonade and announced that it was hot out. He agreed. They held hands at the Wagon Wheel. He drew cartoons on her lunch sack: a square-jawed man lifting a truck, a woman in big honker glasses shooting stars from her eyes, two stick figures under a moon labeled US.

Gloria praised him whether he was nearby or not. Randy sang her praises whether she was nearby or not. Each became the other’s publicity department, defense attorney, witness, and home crowd.

Mrs. Harlow became worse in a different direction. Having accepted Gloria, she did so with total occupation. She called her “my girl.” She showed customers photographs they had not requested. When Gloria developed pneumonia, Mrs. Harlow guarded her house as though germs might return with reinforcements. She made soup, insulted the doctor, and told Randy he was holding the thermometer wrong.

Years passed. Gloria grew rounder. Randy’s hair thinned. Harlow’s expanded into the vacant laundromat next door. The bell-less church tower was repaired but remained bell-less, having by then become part of what we liked about it.

Once, at a town supper, a woman visiting from elsewhere asked how Gloria and Randy had met.

Gloria said, “At work.”

Randy said, “She thought I hung the moon.”

Gloria looked at him. “You do.”

We laughed. Not cruelly, I hope, though who can be sure about a whole town’s laughter? Randy laughed too. Then he kissed the point of her nose, right there beside the potato salad.

We had called her empty because she used common words. We, who said time would tell, love is blind, there’s no accounting for taste, and every dog has its day. We had called her predictable because she agreed with people. We, who agreed among ourselves that she was ridiculous.

The Finch, we called her.

A small, nervous thing.

But watch a sparrow sometime. Watch closely instead of naming it and moving on. See how fiercely it occupies its inch of branch, how alertly it receives the weather, how it carries one dry thread at a time and makes, from scraps nobody values, a place where something living can endure.

Their marriage may not end well. No marriage has promised to. Sickness, boredom, money, grief—these wait without needing an invitation. Randy may still fail Gloria, or Gloria may disappoint him, or time may do what time does to the best-kept store in town.

But it has not ended badly yet.

As of this morning, Gloria was behind the register in her brown apron, smaller than she used to be and brighter somehow. Randy came in from washing the truck and set a chocolate mint beside her hand.

“For me?” she asked.

“For who else?”

She smiled up at him.

“It’s a good mint,” she said.

And Randy, who had learned how much could be meant by ordinary words, said, “The best.”

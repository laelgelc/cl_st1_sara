### The Door in Belgrave Square

Ten years—though in the wild countries where Arthur Price had spent them, years had not the same ceremonious importance that they possessed in London. There they passed openly, under immense skies, and announced themselves by rains, droughts, failures of crops, the rising of brown rivers and the bleaching of cattle bones beside them. Here they had gone underground, as it were, returning now to confront him in stone, brass and polished glass.

Ten years; and the house in Belgrave Square appeared not to have forgiven him one of them.

It stood behind a little enclosure of black railings, immense, pale and self-possessed, staring over the clipped shrubs with all its blank windows. The afternoon was already smoky, though the lamps had not been lit, and motor-omnibuses groaned at the farther end of the square like beasts imperfectly subdued. Arthur, pausing at the gate, felt the Old Country close about him with a pressure more formidable than any wilderness.

Why in the world had he come?

He could still turn back. No one had seen him; no one expected him—except that there had been the telegram, of course, sent the previous day from Liverpool:

**ARTHUR PRICE RETURNED AFTER TEN YEARS ABROAD BEGS PERMISSION CALL THURSDAY HALF-PAST FOUR.**

It was not precisely eloquent, but telegraph clerks were not confidants, and he had believed that she would understand. She had always understood things which more brilliant persons overlooked.

Lady Vane. Hester.

He had learned casually, in a Melbourne club, that Sir Julian Vane was dead; and at that news the whole fabric of his exile—its dust, hardships, prosperity, masculine companionship and hard-earned indifference—had opened suddenly, revealing at its centre the one memory he had never conquered. He had not rejoiced at another man’s death. Certainly not. Yet from that hour England had called him with a voice which sounded perilously like hope.

He was rich now, or what ten years earlier he would have called rich. The irrigation company had prospered beyond prophecy; the acres bought for almost nothing had become valuable; men who would once have dismissed him as a chicken-hearted clerk now waited respectfully for his judgment viva voce. He had faced bush fires, litigation, fever, armed strikers and one memorable night when the river rose six feet before dawn. But all that courage, practical and serviceable in its place, deserted him before a white door in Belgrave Square.

The door seemed to know it. Its brass knob projected like a small bronze fist insolently offered for his inspection.

Ten years before he had entered by that door as tutor and occasional secretary to young Hugh Vane, Sir Julian’s son by his first marriage. He had been useful, unobjectionable and poor. He had sat at the lower end of tables among cousins, aides-de-camp, flunkeys, politicians, old ladies with diamonds in their hair, and young men who spoke of India, Newmarket and the Treasury as though these had been rooms in their own houses. Through them all Lady Vane had moved with her quiet amber eyes and pale, kind smile, bringing to Arthur some suggestion of sunlight seen through autumn leaves.

He had worshipped her.

To call it love had seemed an impertinence. She was Sir Julian’s wife, though a lonely one; she was five years older than Arthur; she belonged to that serene upper air in which the awkward little timid men of the world could scarcely breathe. Yet she had talked to him beside fires, in conservatories, during long wet afternoons in the country. Once, when his mother died and he possessed no money for the journey north, she had placed a banknote beneath his coffee-cup without a word. Once she had said, “You will go far away, Mr. Price, unless somebody gives you a reason to remain.”

He had gone far away.

He mounted the steps and rang.

No sound followed. He rang again, with greater determination, and immediately regretted the violence. The house continued to stare over his head. Perhaps they had moved. Perhaps Hester was abroad. Perhaps she had received his telegram and chosen this discreet method of refusing him.

He rang a third time.

At last the door opened noiselessly—not slowly, not mysteriously, but with the dreadful smoothness of doors attended by perfectly trained servants. A butler stood within, tall, grey and polished, his expression devoid alike of welcome and objection.

“Mr. Price?”

Arthur’s heart leapt at the recognition.

“Yes.”

“Her ladyship is expecting you, sir.”

There are moments when a life of doubt may be overthrown by six ordinary words. She had remembered him. She had received his telegram, had perhaps carried it about with her, had said, “Mr. Price is coming at half-past four.” His name had once more been spoken in that house.

The butler relieved him of hat and coat and conducted him across a hall so silent that the London traffic seemed to fall away behind them. Arthur recognised the black-and-white marble, the dark portraits, the staircase curving upward beneath a painted ceiling. Yet the place was changed in ways too delicate to define. A cabinet had disappeared; there were lilies where Hester had preferred roses; a Chinese screen stood before the library door. Such alterations affected him absurdly. They were small encroachments made by time in his absence.

“This way, sir.”

He was shown into the morning-room and left alone.

Tea had been laid near the fire, but only one cup waited upon the tray. Arthur noticed this and then rebuked himself. Another cup could be brought. One could not expect every domestic arrangement to tremble in anticipation of Arthur Price.

The room, at least, was nearly as he remembered it. There was the little French clock, the lacquered desk, the faded tapestry in which a lady hunted a white stag through a forest. The fire burned low, casting no cheerful crackle, and the thick carpet muffled even his own uneasy movements. He sat; he rose; he examined a book without seeing its title. What would she think of him, now?

He rehearsed their meeting.

“My dear Lady Vane, it is extraordinary to find you unchanged.”

That was foolish.

“I have often wished to explain why I left so abruptly.”

Worse. It sounded like a demand for confession.

“I heard of your loss. Pray accept—”

No! Not on first seeing her. Sir Julian had been dead three years, and condolences, like invitations, became grotesque when delayed.

He crossed to the window. Outside, the square had darkened; a cold mist rested among the trees, and the windows opposite shone faintly, suspended in gloom. Ten years. He saw himself as he had been: narrow-shouldered, badly dressed, miserably conscious of the shine upon his cuffs. He had believed poverty the locked door between them. Had he possessed money, ease, position—had he been free to speak—

But she had not been free.

That fact, simple and honourable, had governed everything. Or had it? Sir Julian had lived for clubs, committees and other women whose names travelled even as far as the schoolroom. Hester’s marriage had been a stately solitude. Yet Arthur had never spoken, never offered so much as one warm word upon which memory could rest. He had hidden behind delicacy because delicacy resembled courage at a distance.

Faint footsteps sounded in the passage.

He turned. The handle moved, and the door opened without any servant announcing her.

Lady Vane came in.

For one instant Arthur could neither advance nor speak. All the phrases constructed during ten years vanished like writing breathed upon a glass. She wore grey—soft, luminous grey—with a narrow line of lace at her throat, and her fair hair had the same warm shadow in it that he remembered. Her eyes were amber; her smile trembled a little.

She had not aged.

The knowledge touched him, but without alarming him. Some women changed hardly at all; memory had exaggerated the interval; the shaded room was kind. These explanations moved beneath his happiness like small leaves beneath clear water.

“Arthur,” she said.

No one in that house had ever called him Arthur.

He went towards her, then stopped, overcome by a reverence stronger than embarrassment. “Lady Vane—”

“Hester,” she said gently. “Surely, after ten years.”

“Ten years,” he repeated.

“You made them very long.”

There was no reproach in her voice, and therefore the words pierced him the more deeply. She seated herself on the small sofa beside the fire and extended her hand. It was cool and light; he scarcely knew whether he had touched it before she withdrew it into the folds of her gown.

“You received my telegram?”

“I knew that you would come.”

“I should have come before.”

“Yes.”

The simple assent left him defenceless. He sat beside her, though not close, and looked at her with the hunger of exile. She appeared exactly as on the evening when he had last seen her: the same delicate line from temple to chin, the same inward radiance, the same grace which had never seemed a product of rank or fashion but of some gentleness native to her blood.

“I thought of you,” he said. “In places where there was nothing that could have reminded me—in the dust, in the heat, among rough men—I thought of you.”

“I know.”

“You could not know.”

She smiled. “Where else should I have been?”

He mistook her, naturally; for all men who have waited long mistake mercy for encouragement and encouragement for revelation.

“Here,” he said, looking about the room.

“Here,” she answered, touching her breast, “and here.” Her hand moved lightly towards him.

It was then that his old diffidence broke—not nobly, perhaps, but completely. Money, years and Sir Julian’s death seemed to have removed those barriers which his imagination had fortified until they appeared eternal.

“Hester,” he said, “I loved you. I loved you then. I left because I loved you, and because I was poor and ridiculous and afraid. I believed I had no right even to let you see. If there is anything in me now worth offering—”

“There was always something worth offering.”

He caught her hands. Or thought he caught them; for they were so cold that his own burning fingers could not be certain of them.

“Then let me offer it now.”

Her expression changed. The sadness which had lain faintly behind her welcome came forward and darkened it.

“No, Arthur.”

He released her and shrank back, instantly the poor secretary again. “Forgive me. I have presumed upon your kindness. Ten years have taught me nothing.”

“They have taught you to speak.”

“Too late.”

“Yes,” she whispered. “Too late.”

The fire seemed to sink. Beyond the curtains, the London evening pressed close against the panes.

“But you are free,” he said, and hated himself for saying it. “I heard that Sir Julian—”

“I am free.”

“Then why—?”

“You are not free as I am free. Not yet.”

He stared at her. The sentence, uttered without emphasis, appeared to open some vast darkness beyond the little room, but his mind refused to look into it.

“I do not understand.”

“Dear Arthur, you never understood the easiest thing.”

“That you were unhappy?”

“That I loved you.”

He rose abruptly. “No.”

“I waited for you to know.”

“No,” he repeated, as though denial could restore ten years to their proper places.

“You were so careful of me—so honourable, so silent. I gave you what signs I dared. You turned each one into kindness, pity, friendship. And then you went away.”

“I had nothing.”

“You had yourself.”

“A wretched gift!”

“It was the one I wanted.”

He walked to the mantelpiece and gripped its edge. The room moved gently around him: tapestry, desk, silver tray, low fire. Could truth be so simple? Had there been no locked door except the one his own feeble temperament had erected? All the wilderness of his exile seemed suddenly contained within his silence.

“If you had only spoken,” she said.

He turned. She had risen and stood near the sofa, very pale, very sweet, already seeming at a greater distance than the width of the room allowed.

“I am speaking now.”

“And I have heard you.”

“Then do not send me away.”

“I did not send you away before.”

He went towards her. “Hester!”

She lifted both hands—not to repel him, but as one might bless a child who cannot accompany one farther.

The door opened.

The butler entered carrying a silver salver. He looked directly at Arthur and nowhere else.

“I beg your pardon, sir. Her ladyship asks whether you would be so good as to bring your papers upstairs. She has another engagement at six.”

Arthur remained motionless.

The butler glanced at the tea. “I trust you have found everything you require, Mr. Price.”

“Her ladyship?” Arthur said.

“Yes, sir.”

The sofa was empty.

He was caught.

His eyes searched the room—the hearth, the window, the narrow space beside the lacquered desk. There was no second door. The curtains hung still. He saw, with hideous clarity, that only one cushion on the sofa bore the shallow impression of a body, and that impression was where he himself had sat.

He rose from a sofa that held no one but himself.

“Is sir unwell?”

“Lady Vane was here.”

For the first time the butler’s composure altered, though only in the slight tightening of the mouth.

“Her ladyship is in the blue drawing-room, sir.”

“No. Lady Hester Vane.”

The man’s face became professionally blank.

Arthur heard his own voice from a great distance. “Sir Julian’s wife.”

“I regret, sir—her late ladyship died six years ago.”

Six years.

The cold which had touched Hester’s hands entered his blood. The whole earth seemed to darken, while the commonplace room remained offensively visible: the unpoured tea, the teaspoon, a coal settling in the grate.

“After a short illness,” the butler added. “Sir Julian survived her by three years.”

Arthur found himself staring at the man’s white gloves.

“Then whom—who is expecting me?”

“Lady Vane, sir. Mr. Hugh Vane’s wife. Mr. Price, the surveyor, was to bring the papers concerning the country property. We understood your telegram to refer to the appointment. There appears to have been a misunderstanding.”

“Yes,” Arthur said. “A misunderstanding.”

The phrase had a ghastly adequacy.

He murmured something of indisposition, of a mistake, of returning another day. The butler did not oppose him. Indeed, the ceremonial machinery of the house appeared to assist his escape: the door opened, his coat arrived, his hat was placed in his hand.

As he crossed the hall, a young woman descended the staircase, pausing beneath the painted ceiling. She was dark, beautiful and faintly impatient; one hand rested upon the balustrade, and diamonds flashed at her throat.

“Is that Mr. Price?” she called.

Arthur looked up. Behind her stood Hugh Vane, older, heavier, wholly unrecognising.

The young Lady Vane smiled with courteous inquiry.

But Arthur had seen, beyond them where the upper corridor fell into shadow, the suggestion of a grey dress passing noiselessly through a closed door.

He went out into the square.

The white door shut behind him with scarcely a sound. London roared at the farther end of the mist; lamps burned; men hurried homeward beneath black hats; the living world continued, punctual and preoccupied.

Arthur stood upon the pavement, prosperous at last, ten years too late, and understood that the dead had opened to him the door which life had never closed.

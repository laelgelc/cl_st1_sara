## THE PRICE OF A HEART

“Why do you love Edward Allen?”

The question was asked in a quiet voice; yet Fanny Lovering started as if some sudden hand had drawn aside the curtain from a secret chamber of her heart.

“Why do I love him, Aunt Mary?” she repeated, while a warm colour o’ermantled her fair cheek. “Because—because he is Edward.”

Mrs. Waring looked tenderly upon her beautiful niece. The evening sunshine entered through the western windows and lay in golden bars across the carpet. Beyond, the sky was clear, save where a few light clouds rested upon the horizon. Fanny, seated with her embroidery in her lap, seemed an image of innocent happiness. But the aunt’s experienced eye saw, afar off, a cloud no larger than a man’s hand.

“That is no reason, dear child,” she replied.

“Oh, Aunt Mary! What reason can love give? Edward is kind to me; he is attentive, generous, and cheerful. Everybody says he is rising in the world. Father calls him one of our most enterprising young men.”

“All this may be true; yet you have not answered me. Why do you love him?”

Fanny’s lips parted in a smile, though there was now some embarrassment in her countenance.

“I love his frank manners, his fine spirits, his manly appearance—”

“His manners, spirits, and appearance!” Mrs. Waring shook her head. “These are pleasant adornments, Fanny; but they are not foundations upon which a woman may safely build the happiness of her whole life. Beauty fades; gayety may become flippancy; attentiveness in a lover may give place to indifference in a husband. What do you know of Edward Allen’s principles?”

“My dear aunt! Surely, surely you do not suspect Edward of anything dishonourable?”

“I accuse him of nothing. I only ask whether you have looked beneath the surface. Is he truthful? Is he just? Does he regard the rights of others when interest tempts him to forget them? Has he that inward goodness which will constrain him to act well when no approving eye is upon him?”

Fanny’s needle fell upon the floor.

“You frighten me,” she said, with a slight quiver in her voice. “Has anyone spoken against him?”

“No one.”

“Then why raise these dreadful doubts?”

“Because you are about to place your earthly happiness in his keeping. The maiden who enters marriage gives more than her hand. She gives the peace of her daily life, the confidence of her heart, the future of her children. Shall she ask no surer security than a graceful person, a prosperous business, and flattering words?”

“You have never liked Edward,” said Fanny, wounded by the grave earnestness of her aunt.

Mrs. Waring took the young girl’s hand.

“I have tried to esteem him—for your sake. But when he speaks of gain, his eye kindles more warmly than when he speaks of goodness. He judges men by their purses and houses. His conversation is full of worldly success; and sometimes, beneath his agreeable manner, I perceive a flippant commonplace of thought that troubles me. I may be wrong. God grant that I am! But affection, dear child, is not moral wisdom.”

Fanny withdrew her hand and turned toward the window. Tears gathered beneath her lashes.

“Edward loves me,” she murmured. “I cannot doubt that.”

“Perhaps he loves you as deeply as he is capable of loving. But what is the quality of that love? Does he value your truth, your gentleness, your pure principles? Or are you to him merely the prettiest girl in his circle, the daughter of a respectable family, an ornament for his handsome house?”

“No! no! You wrong him!”

The interview ended painfully. Fanny soon left the room; and Mrs. Waring remained by the darkening window, looking out upon the last bright streaks of day. The light faded; the little clouds in the west spread into a dusky veil; and one by one the stars came forth, calm and steadfast above the gathering shadows.

“Truth is like those stars,” she said within herself. “It is often unseen in the glare of day; but when darkness comes, it is our only guide.”

Edward Allen was the proprietor of a fashionable dry-goods establishment in the busiest street of the town. He was not yet thirty, and already men spoke admiringly of his success. His windows displayed rich silks, ribbons, shawls, and laces; his clerks were active; his customers numerous. He dressed with taste, gave pleasant entertainments, drove a fine horse, and knew how to make every evidence of prosperity tell upon the public mind.

Mr. Lovering regarded him with unmixed approval.

“He will be a rich man,” he said. “Fanny might travel far and fare worse.”

Mrs. Waring, who had been left a widow while still young, knew that wealth might soften many outward hardships; but she knew also that it could not warm a cold heart, ennoble a selfish purpose, or sweeten the daily intercourse of two souls divided in principle. Her uneasiness increased. Yet she desired proof, not prejudice.

A few days after her conversation with Fanny, she had occasion to purchase silk for a dress. Ordinarily she dealt at another store, and Allen had met her only once, briefly, in a dimly lighted parlour. Wearing a plain bonnet and veil, she entered his establishment, confident that he would not recognize her.

Allen himself approached. His manner was courteous, but the courtesy was that of a tradesman measuring the probable value of a customer.

“I wish a serviceable silk,” said Mrs. Waring, “not too costly.”

Several pieces were displayed. She examined them with an air of uncertainty and selected one of moderate quality.

“What is the price of this?”

Allen glanced at her dress, her unfashionable gloves, and the small, worn purse in her hand.

“A dollar thirteen a yard,” he answered.

“Is it durable?”

“Very. Indeed, madam, it is one of the best articles in the store at the price. You could not purchase the same quality elsewhere for less than a dollar and a quarter.”

Mrs. Waring turned the silk slowly between her fingers.

“I am not much acquainted with such goods,” she said. “You are sure it will wear well?”

“My word upon it.”

She asked the number of yards required. Allen measured the silk; but while doing so, a clerk came near and whispered that a lady at the opposite counter wished to speak with him. The lady was wealthy and well known. Allen’s manner changed instantly; his smile became warmer, his step more eager.

“Charge this lady a dollar thirty,” he said carelessly to the clerk, supposing his customer too far away to hear.

Mrs. Waring heard every word.

The clerk hesitated.

“I think you named a dollar thirteen, sir.”

Allen’s brow contracted.

“A dollar thirty, I said.”

He then crossed the store.

When the bill was presented, Mrs. Waring looked at it with assumed perplexity.

“Thirty cents? I thought the gentleman said thirteen.”

“You must have misunderstood him, madam,” replied the clerk, with visible discomfort.

“Perhaps I did. I am not used to buying silk.”

She paid the money and left the store.

The difference was small—a few dollars only. But dishonesty is not measured by the amount it steals. The hand that unjustly takes one coin has broken the same law which guards a fortune. Mrs. Waring walked home with a sad heart; for, though her judgment had been confirmed, she thought of the loving, confiding child whose bright hopes must now be darkened.

That evening Allen called at the Loverings’. Fanny received him with her accustomed affection, though her aunt’s questions had left a shadow upon her mind. Mr. Lovering was present, reading the newspaper; Mrs. Waring sat sewing near the lamp.

After some conversation, Fanny noticed the folded silk upon a chair.

“What have you there, Aunt Mary?”

“A dress I purchased this morning.”

Fanny took up the silk.

“It is a pretty shade. What did you pay for it?”

“A dollar thirty a yard.”

Allen, who had been leaning with easy grace against the mantel, suddenly changed countenance. The alteration was slight, but Mrs. Waring observed it.

“A dollar thirty!” exclaimed Fanny. “Why, Aunt Mary, you have been badly cheated. I saw silk of the same quality yesterday for a dollar thirteen.”

“Did you?” Mrs. Waring looked directly at Allen.

He stooped to pick up a book from the table, though no book had fallen.

“Where did you buy it?” asked Fanny.

“At a store in Market Street.”

“There are many stores in Market Street,” Allen said quickly.

The voice was constrained. Fanny turned toward him.

“Do you think it worth a dollar thirty, Edward?”

“I cannot judge without examining it. Prices vary. Goods that appear alike may differ materially.”

He took the silk, but his hand was not steady.

“I trusted the seller,” said Mrs. Waring. “He assured me upon his word that the article was excellent. At first he named a dollar thirteen; afterward he directed his clerk to charge me a dollar thirty.”

Allen laid the silk abruptly upon the chair.

“Possibly you misunderstood him.”

“So the clerk told me.”

“And very probably you did. Ladies are often mistaken in hearing prices across a counter.”

“Very probably,” repeated Mrs. Waring. “Yet I stood close to him. I heard both statements distinctly.”

A deep red mounted to Allen’s forehead.

“It is scarcely fair to condemn a tradesman because of an error which may have been accidental.”

“I have not said it was accidental.”

“Nor have you proved the contrary.”

The sharpness of his tone startled Fanny. She looked from her lover to her aunt. Mrs. Waring’s face was pale but composed.

“You appear strangely interested in defending an unknown tradesman, Mr. Allen.”

He made no answer.

A silence fell upon the room—one of those dreadful silences in which truth, though yet unspoken, seems to stand visibly among us. Mr. Lovering lowered his newspaper. Fanny’s lips grew white.

“Aunt Mary,” she whispered, “where did you buy the silk?”

Mrs. Waring named Allen’s store.

The young man sprang to his feet.

“So this was a trap!”

“It was a test,” said Mrs. Waring, rising also. “A test which an honest man could not have feared.”

“You entered my store disguised!”

“I wore no disguise beyond a plain bonnet and veil. You did not recognize me; therefore you believed me ignorant and friendless. You named one price, charged another, and supported the wrong by a falsehood.”

“Pooh! pooh!” cried Mr. Lovering, recovering from his surprise. “Aunt Mary makes too much of a business transaction. Merchants must secure profits where they can. Perhaps the silk was marked incorrectly.”

“It was not,” said Mrs. Waring.

Allen turned toward Fanny.

“Surely you will not permit this absurd affair to come between us?”

But Fanny shrank from him. Tears filled her eyes; her frame trembled.

“Did you say a dollar thirteen?”

“I may have done so carelessly.”

“And did you afterward order your clerk to charge a dollar thirty?”

“There were circumstances—”

“Edward! Did you?”

He bit his lip.

“Yes; but the matter is of no importance. The difference was trifling.”

“Trifling!” Mrs. Waring’s voice rang clear through the room. “The money was trifling; the principle was not. Had the gain been a thousand dollars, the act could not have been more dishonest. Had it been a single penny, it would still have revealed a willingness to profit by another’s ignorance.”

Allen’s eyes flashed.

“You have chosen to put the worst construction upon an ordinary practice of trade.”

“Then ordinary trade stands in need of extraordinary reformation. Custom cannot make a lie truthful nor theft honourable.”

“Strong words, madam!”

“True words, sir.”

Mr. Lovering rose impatiently.

“Come, come! Let us be reasonable. Edward has been a little sharp, perhaps. Business is business. A man who means to prosper cannot always be too nice.”

“Brother,” said Mrs. Waring, turning upon him with sorrowful dignity, “do you know what you are saying? Is there one honesty for the shop and another for the home? May a man deceive a customer in the morning and become trustworthy when he enters his parlour at night? Principle is not a garment to be worn on Sunday and laid aside on Monday. The man who wrongs a stranger for profit will wrong a friend when interest becomes stronger than affection.”

“But Edward would never wrong Fanny.”

“Why not? Because he loves her? Selfish men love so long as love gratifies self. Marriage brings sacrifices, trials, opposing wishes, sickness, diminished beauty, and sometimes poverty. What security has a wife except the fixed honour of her husband? If he possesses no law within himself—if gain is dearer to him than truth—upon what shall she rest when the bright weather is past and the storm comes?”

Fanny uttered a low sob and covered her face.

Allen approached her.

“Fanny, you know me better than your aunt does. Will you judge my whole character by this miserable trifle?”

She looked up. Love pleaded strongly within her; but the man before her seemed changed. No longer did she see only his handsome countenance and graceful bearing. Behind them stood the unrecognized customer, the altered price, the deliberate command, the ready falsehood.

“Oh, Edward,” she said, “why did you not at once confess that you had done wrong?”

“Because I do not admit the right of anyone to question my business methods.”

Her last hope trembled and went out.

“If you had said, ‘I was tempted, and I am ashamed,’ I could have forgiven you. But you defend it!”

“A man cannot submit his affairs to the judgment of every timid girl and meddling relative.”

Fanny recoiled as if struck.

Mrs. Waring placed an arm around her. Allen saw that he had gone too far, but pride prevented retraction.

“I shall leave you,” he said coldly. “When Fanny is prepared to treat me with confidence, she knows where I may be found.”

He bowed and withdrew.

The door closed. Fanny stood listening to his departing steps; then, with a cry of anguish, she sank upon her aunt’s breast.

Mr. Lovering walked the room in agitation.

“This is unfortunate—very unfortunate! A marriage broken off for a few yards of silk!”

“Not for silk,” answered Mrs. Waring; “for want of honour.”

“It is easy for those who have never conducted business to preach perfection.”

“Honesty is not perfection; it is duty. If the world calls cunning wisdom and falsehood enterprise, so much the worse for the world. Would you give your daughter to a man whose prosperity is built upon the ignorance of others?”

“He is prosperous, nevertheless.”

“And if prosperity is purchased by meanness, it becomes only a gilded sign hung over a corrupt heart.”

During the days that followed, Fanny moved through the house like one upon whom a great sorrow had fallen. Every sound of wheels made her start; every knock brought a sudden flush to her cheek. She believed Edward would return—humbled, penitent, and eager to repair his wrong.

But he did not come.

Pride and interest had been wounded more deeply than his affection. He told himself that no man of spirit could submit to Mrs. Waring’s interference; yet beneath his anger lurked the consciousness that he had been exposed. The eye of innocence, once full of trust, had looked upon him with horror, and he could not forgive the witness whose purity condemned him.

A week passed. Then came a brief letter.

He wrote that recent events had destroyed the confidence necessary to happiness; that Fanny appeared to prefer the opinions of her relatives to his honour; and that he therefore released her from the engagement.

Released her! The word seemed cruelly chosen, as if her faithful heart had been a bond from which he desired freedom.

Fanny read the letter once, and then again. Her face became deathly pale.

“What shall I answer?” she asked.

“The truth,” said Mrs. Waring gently. “Say that you accept his decision.”

“Only that?”

“Only that.”

With trembling fingers, Fanny wrote the few words. When they were sent, she went to her room and wept until tears brought exhaustion.

Let none speak lightly of such sorrow. Though the object loved may be unworthy, love itself may have been pure; and when its earthly image is broken, the true heart bleeds over the fragments. Fanny had not merely lost a lover. She had lost the future her imagination had adorned—the home, the companionship, the long years of mutual tenderness. The path before her, lately bright beneath a summer sky, seemed to end suddenly at a precipice.

Yet she had not fallen. A merciful hand had stayed her feet upon the brink.

Three months later, society was surprised by the announcement of Edward Allen’s marriage to Miss Clarissa Bolden, the only daughter of a wealthy speculator. The lady was older than Fanny, imperious in temper, vain of her possessions, and little fitted either to give or receive domestic affection. But she brought Allen a large fortune and valuable commercial connections.

When Fanny heard the news, she could not speak. She laid her head upon Mrs. Waring’s shoulder, while tear after tear stole down her cheeks.

“He never loved me,” she said at last.

“He loved you less than money,” replied her aunt. “And now, dear child, can you not see the danger from which you have escaped?”

“Yes—though my heart is slow to learn what my judgment knows.”

“That is often so. The clouds may pass from the sky while their shadows yet linger upon the earth. Be patient. Pain borne with truth does not embitter; it purifies.”

Mr. Lovering himself was silenced. Allen’s haste and the wealth of his bride supplied an argument which even worldly prudence could understand.

“I confess,” he said, “the affair has an ugly appearance.”

“Character always appears at last,” answered Mrs. Waring. “For a time it may hide beneath polished manners, handsome features, and agreeable speech; but temptation draws the veil aside. We need not see a tree in every season to know its nature. One sound fruit may promise goodness; one rotten fruit may reveal disease at the root.”

Time, that quiet minister of Providence, performed its healing office. Fanny’s gayety did not return at once; but thought took the place of blind confidence. She reviewed her former conversations with Allen and perceived much that love had concealed. She remembered his contempt for unsuccessful men, his admiration of wealth without inquiry into its source, his impatience of self-denial, his habit of making every question terminate in personal advantage. The silk had not created his character; it had only made it visible.

Nearly two years afterward, Fanny became the wife of Henry Morton, a man whose worth was not proclaimed by showy accomplishments. His means were moderate, his manners quiet, and his advancement slow; but truth governed his smallest actions. He was considerate toward inferiors, faithful in promises, and incapable of taking an unjust advantage. He loved Fanny not merely because her face was fair, but because her heart was gentle and her principles pure. In his affection there was reverence; in hers, confidence.

Their home was not splendid, yet peace dwelt there. If trials came, they met them side by side. If one erred, confession restored harmony. Neither sought happiness by sacrificing the other; and thus, by the beautiful law of unselfish love, each received more than was demanded.

Allen, meanwhile, grew rich. His stores increased; his house was enlarged; his entertainments became more costly. Men called him fortunate. But those admitted behind the glittering exterior saw no domestic joy. Clarissa valued her husband’s talents chiefly because they multiplied her fortune; he endured her pride because her wealth ministered to his ambition. They disputed in private and smiled in public. Their union had been a bargain, and each rigidly exacted the price.

Sometimes Fanny met them in society. Allen’s eye would rest for a moment upon her serene countenance and then turn away. Perhaps, in such moments, he contrasted the quiet light of her home with the cold splendour of his own. But if regret visited him, it came too late. Wealth had answered many wishes, but it could not purchase tenderness, confidence, or peace.

And Fanny, looking upon the man who might have been her husband, would press closer to Henry’s side. Then, remembering the little deceit which had revealed a great moral danger, she silently thanked God for the sorrow that had saved her.

For no truth is more worthy to be written upon the heart of youth than this: love without esteem is a flower without root; admiration without knowledge is a light that may lead toward a precipice; and marriage, if it is to withstand the clouds and storms of life, must rest not upon beauty, wealth, manner, or desire, but upon proved goodness, steadfast principle, and honour that will not sell itself—though the price be only a few cents upon a yard of silk.

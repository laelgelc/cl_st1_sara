## THE MARK UPON THE GOLD

It was on the evening of the Russian opera that Sir Henry Marquis spoke of inspiration in the detection of crime.

We had dined early in his rooms above St. James’s Street. Outside, a wet November fog pressed against the windows, and the lamps below stood in it like pale moons drowned in yellow water. A fire of sea-coal burned on the hearth; the silver coffee service reflected its red points of light, and Sir Henry, in immaculate evening dress, sat with his long fingers joined beneath his chin.

The carriage had been ordered for ten. It lacked twenty minutes of the hour when I asked him whether the popular conception of a detective’s intuition had any foundation in fact.

He looked at me through the blue smoke of his cigarette.

“Intuition,” he said, “is a word by which men excuse themselves from examining the machinery of thought. When they see the result and cannot perceive the process, they cry out upon inspiration.”

“Then there is no inspiration?”

“I did not say that.”

He rose, crossed to an old walnut cabinet, and took from it a little book bound in cracked calf. The covers had warped; the brass clasp was green with age.

“There are moments,” he continued, “when the mind takes fire before reason has laid the fuel. A fact, trivial and even grotesque, flashes suddenly into its appointed place; and one sees, not the beginning of a road, but the whole country through which it runs.”

He returned to the hearth.

“This volume,” he said, “is a copy of a diary kept by an old justice in the Virginia mountains. The man was neither trained detective nor metaphysician. He was a county magistrate, a farmer, and, upon occasion, a patriarch out of the Hebrew Scriptures. Yet he understood that evil, however carefully clothed in legality, commonly leaves some corner of its garment in the door.”

He opened the book.

“The entry is imperfect. It was written by the justice’s son, and the continual phrase ‘my father’ gives the narrative, I think, a dignity which no proper name could improve.”

The clock on the mantel struck the quarter.

“We shall miss the first act,” I said.

Sir Henry turned a page.

“The first act,” he replied, “is usually an error of the composer. Listen.”

And he read.

My father encountered the girl at the lower gate of the Courthouse road, where the locust trees begin.

It was late in September, in the year when the frost came before the corn had hardened. The afternoon lay white with dust. No wind moved in the dead grass, and the mountains, beyond the hollows, had the color of iron cooled after the forge. My father was returning from the circuit on his great sorrel horse when he saw a woman sitting upon a stone beside the road.

She wore no bonnet. Her hair had fallen loose, and one sleeve of her black dress was torn at the shoulder. A small leather case lay in the dust near her feet.

My father drew rein.

“Whose daughter are you?” he said.

In that country it was the natural question. A man might be poor, abandoned, or unknown; but if his blood belonged anywhere, the mention of it established him before the world.

The girl lifted her face.

She was perhaps nineteen. Her eyes were dry; nevertheless, grief had marked her more terribly than tears could have done. Her features possessed that fine, grave character which survives in certain old families after their lands, their money, and even their names have gone down under the hammer.

“I am David Morrow’s daughter,” she said.

My father got down from his horse.

“Then you are Judith.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And why do I find David Morrow’s daughter upon the public road?”

She looked toward the black line of the locust trees. Behind them, two miles away, stood the house called Morrow Hall, although for many years Hambleton Dillworth had lived in it.

“My uncle has put me out.”

My father did not speak for a moment. He was a very large man, not stout, but broad through the shoulders, and when he stood still there was in him something of a wall or a great rock rooted in the earth. Timid persons found courage in his shadow; guilty persons commonly found another thing.

“Hambleton Dillworth is your guardian,” he said.

“He was.”

“Guardianship does not cease because the guardian opens a door.”

“He says it has ceased because there is nothing left to guard.”

My father looked at the leather case.

“Is that all you have?”

“All that is mine.”

“No,” he said. “There is also your blood, and the law, and God Almighty.”

At this the girl began to tremble.

He tied her case behind his saddle and placed her on the horse. Then, walking beside it, he led her to our house.

My mother was dead, and the place was kept by old Milly and her two sons. Milly received Judith without a question. Before sunset the girl had eaten a little food and fallen asleep in the south chamber, with a lamp burning beside her bed.

When my father came downstairs, I found him in the library.

He had taken off his riding coat. His gray hair stood up over his forehead, as it did when some matter troubled him. He was reading a thin packet of papers tied with a faded green ribbon.

“Hambleton Dillworth’s accounts?” I said.

“The copies deposited with the clerk.”

“They were approved by the court.”

“They were.”

“Then they are legal.”

My father laid his hand upon the papers.

“Cain’s speech to the Lord was also in proper form,” he said.

At eight o’clock he ordered the horses.

We rode under a moon scarcely risen. The road to Morrow Hall ran through abandoned fields where the broom-sedge stood waist high and pale as the hair of dead men. An owl passed us without a sound. Farther on, the chimney of a burned cabin lifted above the sumac; and our shadows, travelling beside the road, were distorted by the crooked fence until they seemed like black riders urging us forward.

Morrow Hall stood in a hollow.

Once it had been a place of consequence. The old approach remained, with its double row of oaks; but half of the trees were dead, the stone posts leaned outward, and the iron gate hung by one hinge. The house itself was long and low, built before the Revolution, with a central portion of gray stone and two wings of brick. Ivy covered the eastern wall. The orchard had gone wild, and the fruit, unpicked, lay black beneath the trees.

Only one room showed a light.

My father struck the brass knocker.

The door was opened by Dillworth himself.

He was a little man with one shoulder higher than the other. His head seemed pressed forward between them, and his arms were of unusual length. His face had the smooth gray color of plaster; his eyes were bright, restless, and set near together. At sight of my father, his hand tightened upon the door.

“Justice Pendleton,” he said.

“Hambleton.”

“You have come late.”

“Evil keeps late hours.”

Dillworth smiled. The smile travelled over his mouth but did not touch any other part of his face.

“You retain the magistrate’s privilege of speaking in maxims. Come in.”

He led us into the west drawing-room.

Four candles burned upon the rosewood table. Their light stopped at the circle of chairs, so that Dillworth, when he seated himself beyond the table, appeared isolated in a small island of yellow flame. Behind him hung the portraits of the Morrow family—soldiers, judges, and grave women in silk. One picture, that of David Morrow’s father, had been moved recently. A clean rectangle showed upon the faded paper about its frame.

I observed this because my father observed it.

Dillworth folded his hands.

“You have brought me news?”

“I have brought you a question.”

“One?”

“Perhaps.”

My father remained standing.

“Why did you drive Judith Morrow from this house?”

“Drive is an emotional word. She departed after I explained her circumstances.”

“She was found on the road with a torn dress.”

“She refused to listen. Young women, when disappointed, commonly sacrifice both reason and clothing.”

“What disappointment?”

Dillworth leaned back.

“The discovery that she is penniless.”

My father drew a chair forward and sat down. I stood near the door.

“She inherited money,” he said.

“Eight thousand dollars.”

“And land.”

“A fractional interest in certain mountain tracts.”

“You were guardian of both.”

“By appointment of the county court.”

“What became of the money?”

Dillworth’s eyes moved toward the papers in my father’s hand.

“You have examined my accounts.”

“I have.”

“Then you know.”

“I know what is written.”

“What is written was sworn to.”

“Perjury also requires an oath.”

Dillworth’s fingers separated and came together again.

“This is offensive.”

“Murder is more so.”

For the first time his mask altered. It was no more than a tightening about the nostrils; but the candle flames seemed to leap, and the four shadows behind him stirred like things awakened.

“You use an extraordinary word, Justice Pendleton.”

“I shall use others. But we will begin with the money. You invested six thousand dollars of your ward’s funds in the purchase of the Bellweather tract.”

“I did.”

“Under order of court.”

“Exactly.”

“Upon the report of commissioners in chancery.”

“Yes.”

“The title failed.”

“It did.”

“The heirs of Abner Bellweather possessed no transferable title, because their ancestor had conveyed the tract forty years earlier.”

“That was the judgment.”

“The purchase money was lost.”

“Unhappily.”

“And you retain no liability because you acted under a judicial decree.”

Dillworth permitted himself another smile.

“You have stated the law.”

My father’s voice remained quiet.

“I have stated the trap.”

“I did not make the law.”

“You selected the investment.”

“As guardian, I exercised discretion.”

“You employed the attorney.”

“He was competent.”

“You procured the commissioners.”

“They were appointed by the court.”

“You urged the sale.”

“I believed it advantageous.”

“You knew that the title was worthless.”

Dillworth sprang up.

“That is false!”

The word rang among the portraits.

My father did not move.

“Sit down, Hambleton.”

The little man remained erect for an instant, his long arms hanging beside him. Then he sat. He seemed, in that moment, less like a gentleman behind a table than a spider returning unwillingly to the center of its web.

My father took a paper from the packet.

“The Bellweather deed to Nathan Cole was recorded in Greenbrier County. You examined it twelve years ago while defending the boundary suit of Cole against Pritchard.”

“I have examined many deeds.”

“You cited this one in your brief.”

“I may have done so.”

“You knew.”

“I had forgotten.”

“You remembered everything that served you and forgot only that which would save your ward.”

Dillworth shrugged.

“If negligence is alleged, let an action be brought. Caveat emptor applies to judicial purchasers as to other men. I acted within the decree, my accounts were approved, the term for exception has passed, and the matter is res judicata.”

He spoke the Latin with satisfaction, as a priest might speak the words that transformed bread upon an altar.

My father looked at him.

“You have built your refuge carefully,” he said. “Every stone is legal. Yet the house smells of the Pit.”

Dillworth’s mouth opened and closed.

“The remaining two thousand dollars?” my father continued.

“Expended for her maintenance and education.”

“In this house?”

“Yes.”

“Where she served as unpaid companion to your sister until that lady’s death; where she wore her mother’s altered dresses; where her schoolmaster received thirty dollars in six years.”

“Economy is not a crime.”

“No. But economy, like charity, may cover one.”

My father put down the account.

“There remain the lands.”

Dillworth’s eyes narrowed.

“A useless inheritance.”

“Three hundred acres along the north branch.”

“Rock and laurel.”

“Also timber.”

“Inaccessible.”

“Also a vein of coal.”

“Unproved.”

“And for these useless acres an unknown claimant has brought suit under an ancient tax title.”

“A most unfortunate affair.”

“You appear often to meet misfortune when another person owns property.”

Dillworth made a gesture of weariness.

“The suit is regular. The plaintiff, one Caleb Wren, has produced a tax deed dated 1819. If the court sustains it, Miss Morrow’s title will fail. I did not create the claim.”

“You employ Wren’s counsel.”

“I do not.”

“You paid him.”

“I did not.”

“You furnished money to the plaintiff.”

“That is absurd.”

My father put his hand into his waistcoat and drew out a gold coin.

It was a five-dollar piece. Across the eagle, and deep within the milling of the edge, ran a dark blue stain.

He laid it within the candles’ light.

Dillworth did not touch it.

“Have you seen this coin?” my father asked.

“No.”

“Look closely.”

“I can see it.”

“The mark is strange.”

“It is dirt.”

“It is indigo.”

Dillworth’s left hand moved from the table to his knee.

My father continued:

“Last Tuesday Caleb Wren paid his lawyer with five gold pieces. The lawyer gave one of them to the clerk in satisfaction of a fee. The clerk gave it to the innkeeper. This morning the innkeeper paid it to me in change. The coin has travelled; but the mark has travelled with it.”

“What does that establish?”

“Nothing—alone.”

“Then why display it?”

“Because the remaining coins bore the same stain. Because Wren had been without ten cents a week earlier. Because the gold came to him on Monday night. And because, Hambleton, the gold in your iron box is stained with indigo.”

Dillworth’s face seemed to grow flatter, as though an invisible hand had pressed it.

“My money is my concern.”

“Certainly. How did the stain come there?”

“I do not know.”

“You do.”

“I have no obligation to explain the condition of my property.”

“Then I shall explain it.”

My father leaned forward.

“Your grandfather imported indigo before the war of 1812. The business failed. A small quantity of gold, perhaps three hundred dollars, was hidden in an indigo chest when his creditors came. He died without recovering it. The chest remained in the loft above the old counting-room. Last winter, when the roof fell, the box was broken. You found the coins embedded in the rotten blue cake.”

Dillworth laughed, but there was no mirth in the sound.

“This is a nursery tale.”

“You told it yourself, twenty-two years ago.”

The laugh ceased.

My father pointed to the portraits.

“You told it in this room, to David Morrow.”

At the name, something passed over Dillworth’s face—something naked and instantaneous. It was gone before one could define it; but my father saw it.

So did I.

He went on.

“David was your cousin.”

“And my friend.”

“He owned this house.”

“I lived here at his invitation.”

“He was older than you, careless with money, generous, and given to wandering over his lands. You managed certain business for him.”

“I assisted him.”

“You desired the estate.”

Dillworth’s voice sharpened.

“I had expectations under his will.”

“You drafted that will.”

“At his request.”

“The will gave you this house if David died without issue.”

“He had no child then.”

“But he married.”

“Yes.”

“And Judith was born.”

“Yes.”

“The child destroyed your expectation.”

Dillworth spread his hands.

“This is melodrama. Men do not murder infants because wills become inoperative.”

“No,” my father said. “Sometimes they wait.”

The candle nearest Dillworth guttered. Wax ran down its side in a red line.

My father resumed:

“David’s wife died. Two years later David vanished. You became guardian of his daughter and tenant of his house. You have consumed her money, attacked her land, and this day thrust her out.”

“David abandoned her!”

Dillworth spoke too quickly.

My father’s head lifted.

“That is what you said.”

“It is the truth.”

“What else did you tell her?”

Dillworth hesitated.

“I told her what decency had prevented me from telling her before.”

“Say it.”

“That her father is alive.”

“Where?”

“I do not know.”

“You told her he fled.”

“He did flee.”

“Why?”

Dillworth rose again, but this time my father did not order him down. The little man walked to the fireplace and stood with his crooked back toward us.

“He was a thief,” he said.

The sentence was soft, almost reverent. It was Cain’s speech prepared through twenty years.

“What did he steal?” my father asked.

“Gold.”

“What gold?”

“The hidden money of which you spoke.”

“Stained with indigo?”

“Yes.”

“How much?”

“I never knew.”

“And a horse?”

“My black mare disappeared the same night.”

“David stole gold and rode away?”

“Yes.”

“Why did you conceal this from his daughter?”

“She was a child.”

“Why conceal it from the county?”

“For the honor of the family.”

“You caused search to be made.”

“I feared he had met with accident.”

“You represented that he had ridden toward the western mountains.”

“His tracks led that way.”

“Did they?”

“They did.”

“You found signs of a struggle in this room.”

“I had been assaulted.”

“By David?”

“Yes.”

“He struck you?”

“With the brass poker.”

“Where?”

“Across the shoulder. I was insensible.”

“Who discovered you?”

“My servant, at dawn.”

“The iron box stood open?”

“Yes.”

“The gold had vanished?”

“It had vanished.”

“The mare had vanished?”

“Yes.”

“And David?”

Dillworth turned from the fire.

“He vanished.”

The word remained in the room.

Vanished. Gold, horse, man. Three disappearances, tied together by the neat cord of a story. Outside, an owl cried in the orchard, and from some remote chamber came the soft fall of loosened plaster.

My father regarded Dillworth for a long time.

“Twenty years,” he said.

“What do you mean?”

“For twenty years you have repeated the word until you believe it has a power. But men do not vanish.”

“Many have.”

“They go somewhere.”

Dillworth’s eyes flickered toward the eastern wall.

It was no more than a glance. Yet it fell upon the portrait which had been moved.

My father rose.

“Why did you shift that picture?”

Dillworth’s hand went behind him and gripped the mantel.

“I altered the arrangement.”

“There is no arrangement. That portrait alone has been moved.”

“The cord was unsafe.”

“It hung for seventy years in the other place.”

“I desired it here.”

“No,” my father said. “You desired something behind it to be covered.”

He crossed the room.

Dillworth came away from the mantel with an extraordinary movement—sideways, swift, and low. His hand darted beneath his coat.

My father was swifter.

He caught the little man’s wrist, turned it, and a pistol fell upon the carpet. I picked it up.

It was an old percussion weapon, short in the barrel and heavy in the stock.

My father released him.

Dillworth staggered back against the table. Two candles went over. One extinguished itself in the wax; the other rolled upon the carpet, and I crushed the flame beneath my boot.

The two remaining lights lengthened every object in the room. My father’s shadow reached across the floor and rose over Dillworth to the ceiling.

“Give me the pistol,” my father said.

I did.

He examined the lock and the mouth of the barrel. Then he placed it in his pocket.

“Loaded?” Dillworth whispered.

“Yes.”

“I carry it for protection.”

“From whom?”

There was no answer.

My father took a candle and lifted the portrait from its hook.

Behind it, in the plaster, appeared a small, ragged hole. About the edges was a dark discoloration.

He touched it with the point of his knife.

“A bullet entered here,” he said.

Dillworth’s lips drew back from his teeth.

“Old houses have many marks.”

“This one is not old.”

“It may have been made by any person.”

“It was made on the night David vanished.”

“You cannot know that.”

“I know the room was papered the preceding summer. I know no one has occupied it since your sister’s death except yourself. And I know why you moved the portrait yesterday.”

Dillworth’s voice became almost inaudible.

“Why?”

“Because damp loosened the plaster, and the hole, which you had filled, opened again.”

The moon had risen over the eastern wing. Its light entered through the tall windows and lay upon the floor, white and rectangular, dividing the darkness like a grave opened among black earth.

My father set down the candle.

“You told the county that David struck you with the poker.”

“He did.”

“No. You struck yourself.”

“That is madness.”

“You bruised your shoulder against the edge of the mantel, opened the iron box, scattered papers upon the floor, and told your servant a thief had assaulted you.”

“You have no proof.”

“You forgot the pistol.”

“What pistol?”

“The one in my pocket.”

Dillworth was silent.

“It belonged to David,” my father said.

“No. It is mine.”

“You have carried it since his death. His initials are scratched beneath the grip.”

“He gave it to me.”

“He did not. I saw it in his hand the week before he vanished.”

“You cannot establish—”

“The phrase will not help you.”

My father’s voice had changed. Until then he had spoken as a magistrate, patient before evasions, exact in particulars. Now another authority came into it, older than the county court and more terrible than statute.

“David entered this room after midnight,” he said. “He had found the broken indigo chest in the loft. He brought down a handful of stained gold and called you from your bed. He was laughing, perhaps. To him it was a curious discovery—a little family treasure out of a tale. To you it was more.”

Dillworth did not move.

“It proved that the gold existed. You had known the legend. You had searched for it and failed. You asked where the remainder lay. David would not tell you at once; or perhaps he mocked your eagerness. You quarrelled.”

“No.”

“You seized his pistol.”

“No.”

“He turned toward the bell rope.”

“No!”

“You fired.”

The word struck him like a blow.

Dillworth caught at the table. The last candles trembled between us.

My father pointed toward the uncovered hole.

“The ball passed through David’s body and entered that wall.”

“There was no blood!” Dillworth cried.

Then he stopped.

No man spoke.

The old house seemed to draw one vast breath about us.

My father said, very softly:

“I did not mention blood.”

Dillworth’s face became the color of ashes after rain.

My father continued.

“There was blood. You washed the carpet. But water warped the rosewood boards, and the carpet was replaced. I saw the boards last spring when the county assessors came. At the time I did not understand.”

He paused.

“God sent this tragic thing to meet me on the road today. The girl’s face opened the door in my mind. The coin entered. The mark upon the gold illuminated all that was behind it.”

Dillworth stood crouched, his head thrust forward.

“You accuse,” he said, “but accusation is not proof. Where is David? Where is the body? Where is the horse? Produce them. Bring your indictment. Summon your jury.”

“My father,” I said, for I saw suddenly where the danger lay, “the servant swore there were hoofprints on the mountain road.”

My father did not look at me.

“There were hoofprints,” he said.

Dillworth’s eyes shone with a feverish hope.

“You see!”

“They went west from the stable.”

“Exactly.”

“But the ground was hard on the road. The only clear impressions were near the spring.”

“The mare was shod. Any smith—”

“The prints showed a broken tip on the left hind shoe.”

Dillworth swallowed.

“The black mare had such a shoe,” my father said. “The smith remembered it.”

“Then David rode her.”

“The prints passed the spring twice.”

A silence followed.

“Once going west,” my father continued, “and once returning east. You led the mare out to make a trail. You returned along the creek where stone concealed the prints.”

Dillworth’s hope went out.

“What became of the mare?” my father asked.

“I have told you.”

“You told me she vanished.”

“She did.”

“Where is David?”

“Gone.”

“Where is the mare?”

“Gone.”

“Men do not vanish. Horses do not vanish. They go somewhere.”

My father turned toward the window.

Beyond the glass, the wild orchard descended to a field of dead grass. At its lower edge stood the ruin of the old dye-house. Only a chimney and one corner remained. Near it, hidden beneath briars, lay a cistern built when the indigo works had operated.

The cistern had not been used within living memory.

My father looked back.

Dillworth followed his gaze. His knees appeared to fail. He caught the chair and sank into it.

“The cistern,” my father said.

The little man made no answer.

“It is deep.”

No answer.

“Forty feet, perhaps.”

Dillworth covered his face.

“You put David’s body upon the black mare,” my father continued. “You tied the wrists to the saddle and the feet beneath the animal. You led her through the orchard to the old dye-house. There you pushed both into the cistern.”

“No.”

“The mare resisted.”

“No.”

“You struck her with the pistol.”

“No!”

“You were forced to fire again.”

Dillworth lowered his hands.

“How,” he whispered, “could you know that?”

“Because the pistol has been discharged twice since it was cleaned.”

“That cannot be seen after twenty years.”

“Not by the eye.”

“Then—”

“By the confession of the man who denies it.”

Dillworth sprang from the chair and rushed toward the door.

I barred the way.

He wheeled, and for a moment he moved in the candlelight like a trapped animal, his long arms raised, his gray face twitching. Then he turned upon my father.

“You cannot do this! You are a justice. You are bound by law.”

My father’s expression did not change.

“Bound by law,” he repeated.

“Yes. Bound! There must be a warrant. There must be evidence lawfully obtained. There must be an inquest, an indictment, a trial. Your suspicions are nothing. Your tales are nothing. Even if bones lie in that pit, who will say whose bones they are? Who will say how they came there? The servant is dead. The child knew nothing. The time has passed. The law cannot touch me.”

He straightened as he spoke. Every legal phrase restored something to him. It was as if he climbed, stone by stone, back into his refuge.

My father listened.

When Dillworth had finished, he took the pistol from his pocket and laid it on the table.

“You are right,” he said.

Dillworth stared.

“The law may fail.”

“It must fail.”

“It may.”

“You admit it!”

“I admit that forms decay; witnesses die; records burn; and a clever man may build a wall of years between his crime and the courthouse.”

Dillworth’s hands began to tremble with relief.

“Then this interview is ended.”

“No.”

My father removed from his coat a folded parchment and placed it beside the pistol.

“What is that?” Dillworth said.

“A deed.”

“To what?”

“Morrow Hall, the north-branch lands, the lower farms, and every parcel which came to you through your dealings with David Morrow’s estate.”

Dillworth gave a short laugh.

“You cannot compel me to sign it.”

“I can.”

“By what authority?”

My father looked toward the white window and the dark orchard beyond it.

“By the authority that existed before a clerk wrote in a book.”

“You threaten me?”

“I judge you.”

“You have no jurisdiction!”

“Not in the court you mean.”

He opened the deed and dipped a pen into the inkhorn.

“You will convey these lands to Judith Morrow.”

“Never.”

“You will sign.”

“I shall call my servants.”

“You have none in the house tonight. You sent the boy to the village and the old woman to her daughter.”

Dillworth’s jaw fell.

My father had known. How he knew, I cannot say. Perhaps he had seen the absence of smoke from the kitchen, perhaps the single horse in the stable, perhaps something else. With him observation and inspiration often met so closely that no man could perceive the line between them.

Dillworth glanced at the pistol.

My father’s hand rested beside it.

“Do not,” he said.

The little man drew back.

“This is robbery!”

“No. Restitution.”

“Coercion invalidates a deed.”

“If you remain to plead it.”

“What do you mean?”

My father pushed the parchment across the table.

“The mountain road divides above the mill. The south branch crosses into Kentucky. A horse is saddled in your stable. You will sign, and I will give you until dawn.”

“To do what?”

“To go.”

Dillworth looked from my father to me.

“And if I refuse?”

“Then we shall walk to the old cistern.”

The two candles burned lower. One flame touched a pool of wax and shrank to a blue point.

Dillworth licked his lips.

“For what purpose?”

“To join you to your victim.”

The little man recoiled as though the mouth of the cistern had opened at his feet.

“You would murder me!”

My father’s eyes were terrible.

“No. I would execute the sentence which you have carried within you for twenty years.”

“You speak like a madman.”

“Perhaps.”

“I shall denounce you.”

“If you remain.”

“I shall appeal to the governor.”

“If you remain.”

“I shall—”

“If you remain,” my father said, “the cistern will be opened at sunrise. David’s bones will come up. The mare’s bit will come up. The saddle buckles will come up. The two bullets may come up. And whether the law hangs you or does not hang you, I shall take you to the edge and require you to look down.”

Dillworth stared at him.

“No,” he whispered.

“Yes.”

“I cannot.”

“You required David to look.”

“No!”

“He saw the pit before he entered it.”

“He was dead!”

Again the house was silent.

Dillworth stood frozen. His own words seemed to hang before him like the countenance of the slain.

My father moved the pen nearer.

“Sign.”

Dillworth signed.

His name sprawled across the parchment, uncertain at first, then heavy where his hand had pressed the nib into the paper. My father summoned me to witness it. I signed. Dillworth affixed his seal.

“It is done,” he said.

“Not yet. The acknowledgment.”

“You are yourself a justice.”

“I am.”

Dillworth’s mouth twisted at the irony; but he acknowledged the instrument, and my father certified it in due form.

Thus the law, which had been made the servant of fraud, was compelled at the last to carry restitution upon its back.

My father folded the deed.

“The gold,” he said.

Dillworth did not pretend to misunderstand. He went to a cabinet, unlocked it, and brought out an iron box. Within lay perhaps fifty coins, all stained in the crevices with the old indigo.

My father took them.

“These belong to David’s daughter.”

“They were mine.”

“They marked you.”

He put the coins in his coat.

“Now go.”

Dillworth looked around the room.

The portraits regarded him from the walls. David’s father, restored to his ancient place when my father rehung the picture, seemed to look directly down upon him. The moon filled the room with a cold judicial light.

“May I take clothing?”

“What you carry.”

“Money?”

“You have a horse.”

“Food?”

“The Evil One fed you here twenty years. Let him provide upon the road.”

Dillworth’s face convulsed.

“You speak of God and commit violence.”

“I have not spoken of mercy.”

“Is there none?”

My father’s expression altered then, but not toward softness. It became immeasurably sad.

“There was mercy,” he said. “It sat on a stone beside the Courthouse road, without a bonnet, asking why her father had abandoned her.”

Dillworth bent his head.

For one instant I thought he would confess fully, perhaps kneel, perhaps accept the public judgment he had evaded. But repentance did not enter him. Fear alone entered, and fear is not repentance.

He went out.

We heard him cross the hall, fumble with the outer bolt, and descend the steps. After a little while came the sound of a horse brought from the stable. The gate creaked. Hoofs struck the hard road, slowly at first, then rapidly.

My father extinguished the candles.

We stood by the window.

Dillworth passed through the lower field and reached the road where it climbed from the hollow. The moon threw his shadow before him—a long, broken thing that ran over the stones like some black herald hastening toward the mountains.

At the ridge he turned once.

The house lay pale behind us, with its ruined orchard and its blind eastern windows. He could not have seen our faces, yet my father stood there, immense and motionless, like the angel stationed before a forbidden gate.

Dillworth rode on.

By dawn he had crossed the county line. By the following night he had crossed the mountains. No certain word of him ever returned.

Men said afterward that he reached the Mississippi, that he died in a gambling room at Natchez, that he took ship for Havana, that he was seen years later in a Mexican mining camp. Perhaps one tale was true. Perhaps none was. He had used the word vanished too long, and in the end it became his own epitaph.

At sunrise we opened the cistern.

The workmen removed stones, briars, and a rotted covering of oak planks. A foul cold air came upward. Far below, among black water and fallen masonry, lay the bones of a horse and a man.

The man’s wrists were encircled by remnants of leather.

A corroded saddle clasp bore the initials D. M.

Two bullets were recovered. One was found beneath the ribs of the skeleton. The other lay within the skull of the mare.

They fitted the bore of the pistol.

Judith received the deed that morning. My father did not tell her all at once. He told her first that David Morrow had not fled, had not stolen, and had not abandoned his child. That was the resurrection she required. The rest came later, when grief could no longer destroy her.

She remained in our house until the following spring. Then she returned to Morrow Hall.

The coins marked with indigo paid for the repair of the roof, the restoration of the east wing, and the clearing of the orchard. The north-branch coal lands were afterward leased at a sum sufficient to preserve the estate. Judith married a man worthy of her, and the old house, which had sheltered avarice and murder, heard again the voices of children.

But the cistern was never filled.

My father ordered a wall built around it and an iron cross set above the stones. He would not permit the place to be hidden.

“Why leave it?” I once asked him.

“Because the earth remembers,” he said.

“And the law?”

“The law is the memory men write down.”

He looked toward the black mouth within the wall.

“But there is another memory,” he added, “which no man keeps and no man can destroy.”

Sir Henry closed the diary.

The fire had sunk low. Beyond the windows the fog pressed against London, impenetrable and vast, and somewhere below us a motor horn sounded with a melancholy persistence.

For a moment neither of us spoke.

“Your justice,” I said finally, “relied upon threats not recognized in any civilized code.”

Sir Henry fastened the green clasp of the book.

“He lived in a place where civilization was sometimes an entry in a docket and sometimes a man standing between a child and the dark.”

“But was it intuition that solved the crime?”

“The intuition came first. The proof followed.”

“The stained coin?”

“The coin was the spark.”

“And the picture, the tracks, the weapon, the cistern?”

“Fuel already laid.”

He returned the diary to the walnut cabinet.

The clock struck ten. Our carriage had been waiting below for several minutes.

“We have certainly missed the first act,” I said.

Sir Henry took up his opera cloak.

“No matter,” he replied. “We have heard an overture in which every theme was resolved.”

As we descended the stairs, I looked back into the unlighted room. The fire threw one final shape upon the wall—a vast black shadow preceding us toward the door.

Then the coal fell inward, and it vanished.

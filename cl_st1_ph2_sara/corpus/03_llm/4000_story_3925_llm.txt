### The Fourth of July Account

I first saw Jimmy Powers on the Fourth of July in a lumber town whose principal street ran directly toward the river, as though even the surveyor had understood that all things there must eventually come to water.

The town had been built for business rather than permanence. Its stores were broad-fronted and shallow; its hotels possessed verandas, brass spittoons, and no silence; its sidewalks rose and fell over stumps which the founders had found easier to plank around than to remove. Behind everything—hotel, blacksmith shop, church, saloon, warehouse—stood the dark ranks of pine. Before everything flowed the tawny river, carrying bark, sawdust, single wandering logs, and the smell of the upper country.

I had come from the East with a letter to a timber buyer and the belief that the Fourth would be celebrated by speeches, flags, and perhaps a brass band. There were flags, certainly. They hung from windows, from hitching posts, from the antlers over Murphy’s bar, and from the smoke-blackened funnel of the little stern-wheel tug. There was also a band, consisting of a cornet, two drums, and a German with a horn of heroic dimensions. But the true celebration belonged to the rivermen.

They had come down from camps and drives in red shirts, blue shirts, Mackinaw jackets, patched trousers, and narrow felt hats tilted at improbable angles. Most wore the calked, or “cork,” boots of their profession—heavy soles studded with sharp steel points, useful for gripping wet timber and murderously destructive to floors. Wherever they entered, proprietors laid down boards; wherever boards had not been laid, the men left punctures as neat as though a large mechanical insect had crossed the room.

Their talk, too, had points.

“Who’s th’ dude?” one asked of my acquaintance, without lowering his voice.

“He buys pine with a lead pencil,” came the answer.

“Then he’s dangerous. Keep him off’n th’ timber.”

This produced general laughter, and a small, light-built young fellow slapped me on the shoulder.

“Don’t mind ’em, bub,” he said. “They ain’t used to a man can spell his name.”

He was Jimmy Powers.

At first sight there seemed nothing formidable about him. He stood perhaps five feet seven, with a thin triangular face, a freckled nose, gray eyes that changed continually from laziness to mischief, and arms disproportionately long for his body. Those arms hung loose from sloping shoulders and ended in broad, muscular hands. He looked like a boy whom growth had forgotten in some particulars and exaggerated in others. When he walked on land he slouched. When he stepped upon a floating log, as I afterward saw, every joint took intelligence.

The town had arranged horse races, a greased-pole contest, wrestling, and a public reading of the Declaration. The rivermen tolerated these preliminaries because the log-birling match was set for four o’clock.

I had never heard of birling. Jimmy explained it while we leaned against a boom pile and watched the committee select a log.

“Two men git on her,” he said. “They start her rollin’. Each fellow runs to keep top-side. One changes, other changes. One speeds, other checks. Purty soon somebody’s wet.”

“It is a test of balance?”

“It’s a test of stayin’ out th’ river.”

The chosen timber was a smooth cedar about fourteen feet long and sixteen inches through, stripped of bark and water-soaked until it floated low. The committee poled it inside a square boom before the sawmill, where the current was easy and the spectators could crowd upon three sides. Boys swarmed over the lumber piles. Women in white waists occupied wagons along the bank. The band, after a final collision among its instruments, ceased altogether.

Then a murmur passed through the crowd.

Roaring Dick Darrell had arrived.

He came down the street with six men behind him—not because he required an escort, I think, but because his motion drew lesser motion after it. He was under medium height, broad through the ribs, narrow at the hips, and so compactly built that he seemed less a man than a knot tied in rawhide. His black hair grew low on his forehead. Heavy eyebrows met above a short nose, forming a dark quadrilateral frame about eyes unexpectedly pale. His arms were long, though not so long as Jimmy’s, and his fingers curled inward even when relaxed.

He did not roar at that moment. The nickname referred not to the loudness of his customary voice but to the quality of his anger. Ordinary lumber profanity may be continuous without being significant; it oils conversation, fills pauses, praises breakfast, condemns weather, and assists a man with his boots. Darrell’s anger was different. When he chose to curse, each word was selected and driven.

“That’s Dickey,” Jimmy told me.

“The champion?”

“Champion from here to where th’ river quits. He’s hell and repeat.”

Darrell stripped to undershirt and trousers. He retained his calked boots. So did his opponents. The steel points bit the cedar with a faint, rapid sound—spat, spat, spat—as the log turned.

The first challenger was a large, deliberate Irishman from a lower-river crew. He mounted from a plank, planted his feet wide, and grinned at the crowd. Darrell stepped aboard from the opposite side.

“Ready?”

“Roll her!”

Their feet began walking. The cedar revolved slowly, black water shining upon its surface. Each man moved with short backward steps, arms spread, head steady. Darrell increased the pace. The Irishman followed. Darrell checked. The Irishman checked. For half a minute they seemed parts of one machine, opposed but coupled, their boots striking in alternating rhythm.

Spat-spat! Spat-spat!

Suddenly Darrell reversed. The log stopped, hesitated, and spun the other way. The Irishman’s right foot crossed his left. His arms flew upward. He sat down in the river with a crash that splashed the judges.

“Clean birled!” shouted somebody.

The next man lasted less. A third fought Darrell for nearly four minutes, employing a cunning series of checks and rushes; but Darrell rode every change with knees bent and pale eyes fixed, then drove the log to such speed that the water rose from it in shining sheets. His opponent ran, stumbled, recovered, ran again—slap! slap! slap!—and finally sprang for the boom, missed it, and vanished.

Darrell laughed then, not loudly, and looked about.

“Any more?”

Jimmy took off his hat.

“I guess.”

The crowd approved. The two were known rivals, though Jimmy had not yet won the town championship. He pulled off his red overshirt and gave it to me with his hat.

“Hold them, bub. Don’t sell ’em unless I stay drowned.”

He was very slight beside Darrell. Yet when he hopped to the cedar, he landed as lightly as a squirrel upon a branch. The log rolled under him; his feet answered; it stopped. He did not glance down.

Darrell looked at him across ten feet of wet timber.

“You’re due fer a bath,” he said.

“Been savin’ it.”

They began.

I had thought the earlier contests rapid. This was another thing. The cedar snapped beneath their feet as if alive and anxious to be rid of them. They ran forward, backward, checked, doubled, and checked again. Sometimes Jimmy’s long arms circled like the loose spokes of a wheel; sometimes Darrell crouched until his hands nearly touched the revolving surface. The steel points rang against a buried knot—clink, clink, click!—and pale scars multiplied in the wood.

Neither looked at the log. Each watched the other’s hips and shoulders, reading intention before it reached the feet.

“Now, Jimmy!”

“Give him th’ watch-chain!”

“Walk him, Dick!”

“Shake him loose!”

The shouts blurred into one continuous sound.

Darrell forced a furious downstream roll. Jimmy answered. Faster—faster. Their feet became indistinguishable; only their bodies remained poised above the flying cedar. Then Jimmy stamped twice, checked the rotation dead, and reversed it.

Darrell lurched.

The crowd gave one great inhalation.

But the black-haired man recovered with an ugly, catlike twist. For an instant they stood almost breast to breast, each having run toward the center. Then they separated, and the log flew again.

I saw the foul clearly because I was watching their feet.

Darrell permitted the timber to roll half a revolution beneath him. As Jimmy’s left boot came forward, Darrell drove his own foot across the narrow interval and stamped. One of his steel calks entered the leather over Jimmy’s instep. Jimmy jerked with pain. The trapped foot could not lift; the log continued turning; his free leg was swept from under him.

He struck the water sideways.

A roar went up. Some laughed; some cheered; a few shouted accusation. Darrell stepped to the boom and ashore.

Jimmy surfaced at once. His hair was plastered to his forehead and blood darkened the top of his boot. He swam to the plank, refusing three hands until I caught his wrist, whereupon he allowed himself to be pulled up.

“That man spiked you,” I said.

“Sure Mike.”

“You must tell the judges.”

“What fer?”

“He cheated.”

Jimmy sat down and worked off the boot. Two punctures showed through the leather; one had cut deeply into the flesh.

“Should’ve kept my hoof out from under his,” he remarked.

“But the match ought to be declared yours.”

He looked at me with tolerant surprise.

“Bub, if a man can stick his corks in me, I ain’t protected myself. That’s my trouble.”

Darrell passed us, carrying the silver-plated cup and a purse collected by the saloons. He did not look down.

Jimmy looked up.

“Dickey!”

Darrell stopped.

“I’ll git you,” Jimmy said pleasantly. “Maybe not to-day. But I’ll git you square.”

Darrell’s pale eyes narrowed.

“Come when you’re able.”

“That’s when I’m comin’.”

No more was said. The band resumed with a patriotic air in three independent tempos, and the town returned to celebration.

I left next morning. During the following months, when business letters came from the north country, I occasionally asked after Jimmy Powers. One correspondent reported that he was driving on the Pine; another that he had joined a crew under Darrell; a third that the two had not yet killed each other, though this was thought temporary. Their quarrel, which to me had the dramatic proportions of sworn feud, appeared to interest the lumbermen chiefly as preparation for another contest.

The following spring I again travelled north, this time to inspect a tract beyond the upper forks. The snow had gone suddenly. Every creek ran brown and full, swamps shone between the roots of the tamarack, and the river had risen into the alders. The winter’s cut was coming down.

A log drive, seen first at a distance, resembles a moving field. Thousands upon thousands of trunks cover the river from bank to bank, all journeying in the same general direction yet each rolling, nosing, crowding, and sheering according to its private madness. Men in calked boots move over them with peavies—stout poles furnished with steel spikes and swinging hooks—turning a crosswise log, freeing a stranded tier, hustling laggards from the shallows. The labour appears casual until one notices that every step is made upon something round, wet, heavy, and in motion.

At a bend below Split-Rock Rapids the drive had jammed.

The head of it rested against a ledge and two half-submerged boulders. More logs had descended upon those first arrested timbers; more upon those; and still more, until the river for nearly a mile was roofed with pine. Trunks lay straight, slanting, crossed, up-ended, and thrust beneath one another. Here the surface rose into a mound twenty feet high; there a tree projected from the confusion like a lance; elsewhere the logs were packed so smoothly that one might mistake them for a rough brown road. Beneath, the imprisoned river pressed and snarled.

The jam crew had been working since dawn. Their shanty stood among the spruces above the bend, but dinner had been brought to them in pails because the rear boss would not call them off.

The rear boss was Roaring Dick Darrell.

I found Jimmy Powers on the shore, driving the point of his peavey into a log that had stranded above the main mass.

“Hello, bub,” he said, as though we had parted the day before. “Come to buy th’ jam?”

He had filled out a little, but his triangular face remained boyish and his arms still swung almost to his knees. His wool shirt was open at the throat. His trousers were tucked into scarred cork boots. A strip of red flannel bound one wrist.

“Are you working under Darrell?” I asked.

“Looks that way.”

“And how do you agree?”

“We don’t.”

“Has anything happened since July?”

“Nothin’ useful.”

From the jam came a hard voice.

“Powers! Quit entertainin’ company and fetch that line!”

Jimmy’s expression did not change.

“Sweet-tempered as a bear with bees,” he observed. “That’s him.”

He shouldered a coil of rope and sprang from the bank. I followed as far as the edge, where a gray-bearded man advised me not to go farther unless I wished to make the remainder of my journey underneath the logs.

From there I watched.

Darrell stood near the center of the jam on a ridge of tilted timber. Against that enormous disorder he appeared minute, yet his authority carried over it. He pointed with his peavey, dispatched two men to the left wing, three to the sluice side, and sent Jimmy with the rope across a broken place where muddy water boiled between upright trunks.

There is in a great jam usually some key-log, or small combination of key-logs, which sustains the lock. Remove it, and the whole accumulation may move. The difficulty lies in discovering the key among ten thousand plausible candidates; the greater difficulty lies in releasing it without remaining where the river can collect payment.

Darrell possessed a diabolical eye for such matters. He walked the jam, sounded with his peavey, examined the pressure lines, and watched the seep and spurt of water. At times he lay flat to stare into a crevice. At times he stood motionless while the timber beneath him groaned and adjusted itself by inches. Then he would leap up and run to another point, shouting orders.

All morning the crew picked at the structure. They broke out a side pocket. They rolled loose logs into the current. They dragged a great pine backward with tackle fixed to a stump. The jam answered each operation with deep reports—boom! boom!—like artillery underground, but did not release.

At noon a man near me said, “She’s gatherin’.”

The phrase meant that pressure was increasing. Fresh timber continued to arrive from above, floating slowly at first, then crowding, climbing, and piling upon the rear. The whole mile of wood seemed motionless, but it was not. It tightened. It settled. Small openings closed. Logs that had lain loosely together touched and began to grind.

Darrell sent the men back.

“Off that center! All of you! Powers, take ’em shore!”

The crew obeyed without question. They came over the jam at a trot, leaping from trunk to trunk. Darrell remained behind beside a great hemlock which slanted downward into the lock.

Jimmy reached the bank and turned.

“Dick!” he shouted.

Darrell waved him away and set his peavey hook.

The point entered. The handle rose. Darrell threw his weight upon it. Once—twice. The hemlock shifted six inches.

Nothing happened.

Then everything happened.

There came first a muffled cracking, not loud but universal, as though every stick in the jam had broken a separate bone. The center sagged. A pine reared upright, stood for one grotesque second, and plunged. Water burst through in a yellow fountain.

“Run!” somebody screamed.

Darrell ran.

The ridge collapsed behind him. Logs rolled from under his feet; others shot upward before him. He skipped, bounded, whirled, recovered—his peavey gone, his arms spread. The entire surface awakened. What had been a field became a stampede.

The jam divided along the main current. The forward mass started downstream, slowly at first, then with the heavy acceleration of a freight train. The rear mass pressed after it. Between them opened a lane of brown water fifty feet wide.

Darrell had been caught upon three loose logs in that lane.

He ran one trunk to its end and sprang to another. It rolled. He ran backward, checked, leaped again. A log struck its neighbour with a cannon report. One dove beneath his feet. He dropped to his hands, clung, rose. The moving masses on either side towered above him like millstones drawing together.

No man on shore spoke.

It was plain even to me that he could not reach either side. The forward jam was sweeping past too rapidly; the rear was advancing. Loose timbers in the gap revolved end over end, struck, rebounded, and were sucked under. Darrell stood upon a short cedar, riding it with the perfect balance I remembered from the Fourth of July. But this was no polished contest log in quiet water. It pitched, plunged, and rammed. One end sank under another trunk; the other flew up and threw him to his knees.

Jimmy Powers stepped from the bank.

The gray-bearded man seized at him and missed.

“Stay out! He’s done!”

Jimmy was already running.

He went over the rear mass with his peavey held crosswise. The logs beneath him moved in waves. Sometimes he took three quick steps upon one trunk; sometimes only one; sometimes he struck the steel point ahead and swung around it when the footing rolled. He did not choose a path in any fashion that my eye could follow. His body chose. Left—right—forward—across. A squirrel might have gone so over tossing branches; a circus rider, perhaps, over galloping horses; yet neither squirrel nor rider ever travelled upon a road that could crush him between its pieces.

“Come back, Jimmy!” the men yelled.

He paid no attention.

At the edge of the gap he halted for the smallest fraction of time. A pine revolved below, its broken branches sweeping through the water. Jimmy waited until one branch passed, planted his peavey, and vaulted.

He landed on the pine.

It rolled violently. He ran with it—spat, spat, spat!—then leaped to a cross-log, from that to a pitching balsam, and from the balsam to Darrell’s cedar.

The two men came together shoulder to shoulder.

Darrell’s left leg was useless. Whether it had been struck or pinched, I could not then tell. He tried to rise and fell. Jimmy caught him.

The rear jam advanced.

There was no possibility of returning by the route Jimmy had used; it had already vanished. Logs cannoned into the gap and stood on end. The open water narrowed to twenty feet, then fifteen.

Jimmy hauled Darrell upright and shouted in his face. Darrell shook his head. Jimmy shouted again. Then, with a movement astonishing in so slight a man, he ducked beneath Darrell’s arm, caught him around the thighs, and heaved him across his shoulders.

The cedar rolled.

Jimmy ran.

Burdened by the heavier man, he moved not with his former lightness but with a terrible, deliberate exactness. Each foot went down hard. Each set of calks held or did not hold. He stepped to one log, rode it half around, stepped higher to another, and paused as two trunks clashed before him.

Clank! Boom! Grind!

The timbers separated.

He jumped.

A space of muddy water appeared beneath him. He struck the slanting end of a pine, slipped to one knee, and drove his free hand into a splintered knot. Darrell’s body rolled across his shoulders. For an instant both hung there.

Then Jimmy rose.

The men on shore had spread along the bank. A rope sailed out, fell short, and disappeared between the logs. Another struck Jimmy’s arm, but he could not seize it.

“Three more!” shouted the gray-bearded man. “Git over three more!”

Jimmy stepped once, twice, and again.

The third log was part of the shore mass. Four men sprang upon it, caught Darrell, caught Jimmy, and dragged both toward the alders.

Behind them the gap closed.

The two halves of the drive met with a detonation that shook the bank. Logs leaped into the air, crossed like jackstraws, toppled, plunged, and were buried. The cedar on which Darrell had stood disappeared entirely. A moment later the whole jam swept around the bend, booming, grinding, and shouldering the river white.

Jimmy lay face downward in the mud.

Darrell lay beside him, conscious but silent. His left trouser leg was ripped from knee to boot, and blood ran through the wool. Presently he raised himself upon one elbow and looked at Jimmy.

He appeared to search for words.

“Powers,” he said at last.

Jimmy rolled over.

“Shut up,” he answered. “You’re heavy.”

That afternoon Harry Thorpe rode into camp.

Even I had heard of Thorpe. In the north country his name carried the authority of a government and something of the glamour of a ballad. He had broken drives thought impossible, operated camps beyond the last settlements, fought fire across forty miles of slash, and brought out timber when rival firms had abandoned horses, supplies, and hope. Men spoke of working for him as soldiers might speak of having served under a famous general.

He had witnessed the closing minutes of the break from the opposite bluff. He listened to Darrell’s account, examined the injured leg, and then found Jimmy behind the cook shanty washing mud from his cork boots.

“Powers,” he said, “when you’re through here, report to my Camp Three.”

Jimmy looked up.

“What fer?”

“I need a river man.”

“Dickey’s got river men.”

“I’m not hiring Dickey’s. I’m hiring you.”

Jimmy considered this, rubbing pine gum from a calk with the point of his knife.

“Wages?”

Thorpe named them.

Jimmy whistled softly.

“Grub any good?”

“No.”

“Guess I can stand it.”

Thorpe’s mouth moved at one corner. “Report Monday.”

That was all. From any other man the offer might have been employment. From Harry Thorpe it was a decoration.

Near sunset I sat with Jimmy on the shanty steps. The river had fallen into a broad, innocent glitter between the trees. Darrell lay inside with his leg splinted. Now and again we heard him swear, not with his customary careless fluency, but in short, concentrated bursts when someone touched the wound.

I had been much moved by what I had seen, and, being then younger and more certain that noble actions should be provided with noble explanations, I attempted to express myself.

“Jimmy,” I said, “you did a very great thing to-day.”

He was sharpening his knife on the sole of his boot.

“Thorpe job ain’t bad.”

“I don’t mean the job. Darrell had cheated you, insulted you, and treated you as an enemy. You hated him.”

“Do yet.”

“And nevertheless, when he was helpless, you forgot the injury. You saw only another man in danger.”

Jimmy tested the blade on his thumb.

I continued, with the unfortunate eloquence of an outsider who has discovered common humanity in a place where the inhabitants have long managed it without a name.

“You risked your life because, beneath rivalry and resentment, there is a fellowship stronger than either. No private quarrel could survive that moment. You saved him because he was a man.”

Jimmy stared at me. Then his gray eyes narrowed with amusement.

“Bub,” he said, “you don’t know nothin’.”

“Then why did you save him?”

He folded the knife, slipped it into his pocket, and nodded toward the shanty where Roaring Dick Darrell lay cursing his splinted leg.

“Fourther July’s comin’,” Jimmy said. “I ain’t goin’ to beat no dead man.”

### The Better Day

Marie called it a Family Mission, because if you called it Driving Forty-Five Minutes to Look at a Twenty-Dollar Puppy advertised by a woman whose Facebook profile picture was a cartoon angel, then everybody began asking practical questions.

Did they need a puppy?

Was twenty dollars suspiciously cheap?

Would a puppy chew the sectional?

Would Robert, who had said, “I’m not saying no, I’m saying let’s not confuse impulse with destiny,” regard the arrival of a live animal as impulse or destiny?

Family Mission solved all that. Family Mission meant snacks in the Lexus, everybody getting a vote on the puppy’s name, no phones except for pictures, and Marie singing the old songs she had loved before her children started saying music from the 1990s sounded like grocery-store music.

“We are creating a memory,” she announced.

“You missed the turn,” said Noah.

“I know, honey.”

The navigation lady, calm to the point of mockery, instructed Marie to proceed three miles and make a legal U-turn.

“It was back there,” said Tessa.

“Yes. I’m supported in feeling that I know that.”

“What?”

“Nothing. Look at the corn.”

The corn stood shining on both sides of the county road, tall and theatrical, the green leaves flickering silver underneath when the wind went through. Classic corn. Movie corn. A person could imagine a little girl in a white nightgown stepping out between those rows, or a scarecrow turning its head after the car passed, though Marie did not mention these possibilities because Tessa had once refused to sleep for four nights after seeing half a commercial for a haunted-house attraction.

Instead she said, “Wow.”

Noah said, “It’s corn.”

“Well, yes. But wow, right?”

“No.”

He returned to the video game he had been forbidden to bring but had apparently brought democratically. Something in it exploded. Tessa leaned over to advise him that he needed to collect six ghost rubies before fighting the Sewer King, which was ridiculous because everybody knew the Sewer King was immune to rubies until Level Twelve. Marie knew this not because she let screens raise her children but because she entered their interests. She participated. She had once spent ninety minutes helping Noah design a bakery in which the baker was also a wizard and the customers were wolves. The Noble Baker feeding the Hungry Denizens! Robert had come in, looked at the screen, and gone, “Ho HO!” in that way of his that meant I accept this foolishness because it is ours.

That was family. Not her own family of origin, obviously.

In Marie’s childhood, a Family Mission would have meant her father needed cigarettes and her mother did not want to be left alone with him when he got back. It would have meant Marie sitting between grocery bags in the rear of the station wagon, holding a carton of eggs so they would not break, while her mother smoked with the window barely cracked. There had been dogs—three, technically—but they arrived and disappeared like weather. Pepper went to a farm. Duchess “couldn’t adjust.” The beagle, whose name Marie could no longer remember without feeling disloyal, got out during a storm because the gate latch had never been fixed.

Marie’s children would not have disappearing dogs.

They would have one dog, intentionally selected and properly vaccinated, a dog with a memory-foam bed and a funny Christmas stocking. They would grow up saying, Remember when Mom drove us into the country and we found Pickles? Or Winston. The puppy in the photo looked like a Winston, black except for a white chin, as if he had dipped his face in cake frosting. Marie had already invented a British voice for him.

Terribly sorry about the slipper, madam. Appears to have exploded.

Ha ha.

“You’re smiling,” Tessa said suspiciously.

“I’m happy.”

“Why?”

“Because I like being with you.”

Noah groaned, which was, in its way, proof that he felt secure enough to reject affection without fearing its disappearance. Marie had read that somewhere. Well-loved kids could groan. Neglected kids became very quiet and watched the driver’s hands.

The navigation lady directed them onto a gravel lane. The Lexus began making tiny costly crunches.

At the end stood a narrow house with one side stained dark beneath a broken gutter. A blue tarp covered part of the roof. There was a washing machine on the porch, not connected to anything, and a child’s plastic basketball hoop had been weighted down with what looked like an automobile transmission. Three chickens moved under a trampoline without a mat. They were, in effect, trapped inside the skeleton of fun.

“Oh,” Marie said.

The puppy picture had been close-up. Close-up was one of the great deceivers of modern life. You saw a sweet face, a bridal bouquet, an avocado toast. You did not see the countertop behind it.

A dog barked from somewhere around the house. Then another. Then several, all at once, the sound rough and hungry.

“Stay in the car for one second,” Marie told the kids.

“We came to see the puppy.”

“I know, honey. I’m just assessing.”

“Assessing what?”

“The situation.”

She locked the doors after stepping out and felt embarrassed immediately. The woman might be watching. Marie was not prejudiced against poverty. Poverty was structural. She donated automatically at Whole Foods and had corrected Robert for saying “bad neighborhood” in front of the children. Still, safety was not a prejudice. Safety was discernment.

She put on her warm face and approached the porch.

* * *

Callie had solved Bo so perfect that morning.

Not solved him forever, because nobody solved forever, did they? People on television maybe, with their white kitchens and their little jars of lemons. A television mother said, Here’s a system that transformed our mornings, and then every sock in the family learned to fly into the correct drawer. Callie had no system. She had three drawers, two with broken fronts, and somehow Jimmy’s socket wrenches had got in the one for underpants.

But Bo had been solved for today.

The dog cable was thirty feet, plastic-coated and red. Good cable. Jimmy had bought it for Brutus before Brutus bit the electric-meter man and had to go away—not go away to a farm, either, because Callie was not stupid, but away behind the machine shed with Jimmy and his .22 and then not come back. The cable clipped to an old canvas belt around Bo’s waist, not his neck. Important difference. Callie had put a folded washcloth beneath the buckle so it would not rub him.

He could reach the tree shade, the sandbox, the yellow truck, and the water bowl. He could even reach the porch’s bottom step if he stretched.

Freedom with boundaries. That was a phrase from the lady at the elementary school. Callie remembered phrases if they sounded like something useful.

Bo hated the pills. On the pills he sat with his mouth open, rolling lint between his fingers, and once he wet himself at the table and did not seem to notice until Jimmy did. Jimmy noticed everything inconvenient.

“You wanted him medicated,” Jimmy had said.

“The doctor did.”

“And the doctor coming over to mop piss?”

No, the doctor had not been coming over. Callie had mopped it.

Without pills, Bo was fast. He had run across Route 16 after a hawk, barefoot, while a gravel truck leaned on its horn. He had climbed the water tower fence. He had crawled beneath the Harpers’ combine because he wanted to touch “the sleeping blades.” Last month Deputy Carr brought him home at two in the morning, sitting in the front seat wrapped in a silver emergency blanket and delighted by the radio.

One more time, Jimmy said, and he would handle it.

Jimmy handled things finally. That was his expression. The dogs, bills, a raccoon in the attic, people who came around selling religion. Callie handled things every day, which nobody counted because if you did a thing every day it turned invisible. Jimmy handled it once, with tools or yelling, and that was Handling.

So: cable.

Bo could be outside, where he wanted. No medicine. No road. No sleeping blades. He spent the morning pushing his truck through dust and singing one long made-up note. Happy. He was happy, what the heck else did a mother want?

Callie watched him from the kitchen window while she rinsed two glasses and a bowl. The sink held a flat basketball with a crack in it because Bo had decided it needed washing. Beside the sink, on a cookie sheet, lay a greasy crankshaft Jimmy had promised was not greasy anymore.

The mother dog was under the table nursing what remained of the litter. Four puppies sold, one promised to Jimmy’s cousin, one dead behind the couch before Callie knew it was sick, and Winston left. The Facebook woman had called him Winston, though Callie had never named him. Naming was how you got yourself into trouble.

Twenty dollars. She could buy Bo’s cereal, the kind with marshmallow dinosaurs, and laundry soap. Maybe cigarettes, if Jimmy saw the money first. Better if he did not.

She had cleaned for the puppy woman. She had. She swept the main path from the front door through the living room. She folded a blanket over the hole in the couch. The trash bags by the recliner were tied shut, and tied-shut trash was practically not trash, just bags waiting to go somewhere. She sprayed Vanilla Meadow until the room smelled like a candle drowning in dog.

The woman had messaged in complete sentences.

Hello, Callie! My children and I would love to visit around noon, if that works for you.

Callie had answered: yup sounds good.

Then, because yup sounded unfriendly: He is real sweet.

She nearly added God bless, but did not want to seem weird.

When the shiny gray SUV came down the lane, Callie saw herself reflected in its side: stretched thin, hair in a knot, one pink flip-flop and one black because she had quit looking for matches. The SUV was so clean it seemed not to have traveled through the same world.

Bo shouted, “Moon car!”

“Stay where you are,” Callie called.

He ran toward it anyway. The cable unreeled through the dust and snapped him to a stop beside the tree. Not hard. He only rocked backward and laughed, then leaned forward against it as if he were a sled dog.

The driver sat there a while.

“Come on, Winston,” Callie murmured, lifting the puppy. “Time to make cereal.”

* * *

Marie heard the woman before she saw her.

“Hello? You the puppy people?”

She came through the crooked screen door carrying the black puppy against her chest. She was younger than Marie expected—maybe thirty, though some faces were made older by cigarettes and fluorescent lighting—and wore mismatched flip-flops. Her T-shirt said BEACH PLEASE beneath a picture of a sunset.

Marie raised her hand brightly.

“Hi! Yes. I’m Marie. We spoke on Facebook.”

“Callie.”

“And this must be Winston.”

Callie looked down at the puppy as if surprised by his promotion. “Sure. Yeah.”

He was smaller than the photograph. His belly was round, his legs uncertain. One ear stood up and the other folded inward. Adorable, unfortunately. Marie felt the children watching through the windshield, their longing almost physically pressing against the glass.

Then a boy came around the side of the house pulling something red behind him.

He was perhaps six. Perhaps eight. He wore only shorts, and there was dirt on his stomach and around his mouth. He ran toward Marie with an expression of such open excitement that she smiled automatically.

“Hi there!”

The red line jerked straight.

The boy reached its end.

For one moment Marie’s mind refused the arrangement. There was a tree, there was a cable, there was a belt around the child. These facts remained separate, because once they joined they became impossible.

The boy bent forward, pulling with his whole body.

“Moon car,” he said.

Marie looked at Callie.

Callie shifted the puppy. “That’s Bo.”

“He’s—” Marie stopped. Supported in feeling what they felt did not mean speaking every first thought. “Is he attached to that tree?”

“He runs.”

Marie waited for the rest.

“He’ll run clean to the highway,” Callie said. “This way he gets outside.”

The puppy licked Callie’s wrist. Behind them, something large moved inside the dim house and scratched itself against the doorframe. The smell reached Marie now: feces, damp cloth, fryer grease, fake vanilla.

Bo dropped onto his knees beside a metal water bowl. Before Marie understood, he lowered his mouth and drank.

“Bo, use your cup,” Callie said, not moving.

The cup lay on its side in the dirt.

Marie’s whole childhood rose up inside her, righteous and hot. All the adults who had noticed nothing. The teacher who had seen the bruises on Marie’s arm and asked whether she was clumsy. The neighbor who had heard her father screaming and turned up his television. People always explained why it was not their place.

Well, here she was. The Adult Who Did Not Look Away.

She imagined saying it clearly: Unhook him now. She imagined taking the child in the Lexus, though that was probably legally kidnapping. She imagined the social worker arriving with a clipboard, the cable photographed as Evidence Item A. She would be calm, not dramatic. A responsible citizen. A mother protecting a child, any child, because motherhood did not stop at the property line.

Callie held out the puppy.

“You want him?”

Winston—or the nameless puppy, or whatever he was—wagged his entire rear half. Marie did not take him.

“I don’t think so.”

“You can hold him.”

“No, thank you.”

The woman’s face changed slightly. Not shame. Something flatter.

Marie had to get her children away. Any confrontation might endanger them. That was not cowardice; that was prioritisation. She would call Child Welfare from the car. She would give the address, assuming the address had displayed correctly. Maybe take a picture? No. Photographing a restrained child felt grotesque. Also, Callie was watching.

“We’ve changed our minds,” Marie said.

“Okay.”

“It’s just not the right fit.”

“Okay.”

Marie wanted to say more—something that let Callie know she had been seen—but the boy was making a siren noise, and the dog inside began barking, and suddenly Noah had opened the passenger door.

“Is that him?”

“Back in the car.”

“But he’s cute!”

“Now, please.”

Tessa peered around him. “Why is that kid on a leash?”

Callie looked away.

Marie felt the situation become a lesson, one she had not prepared but would manage with honesty at an age-appropriate level. “We’re leaving.”

They left.

As the Lexus bumped down the lane, Tessa began to cry. Not hard; betrayed tears, puppy tears. Noah said they had driven practically to another state for nothing.

“I know you’re disappointed,” Marie said. “You are allowed to be disappointed.”

“You said we were getting him.”

“I said we were meeting him.”

“You said we could name him.”

“We can discuss this when everybody’s regulated.”

“What does that mean?”

“It means stop yelling.”

That had not sounded democratic. Marie took a breath.

In the mirror, the house grew smaller. Bo was a pale flicker beside the tree. Callie still stood on the porch holding the puppy.

Marie’s hands trembled, which was how she knew the matter was serious and not merely judgment. She was not judging their house. The house had nothing to do with it. People could be poor and wonderful. She knew that. She had read whole essays.

At the highway, her phone found two bars.

She searched CHILD WELFARE REPORT LINE while Noah killed the Sewer King and Tessa sniffed against the window.

“You did the right thing,” Marie whispered to herself.

“What?” said Noah.

“I said we’ll get ice cream.”

Both children brightened.

There. Not bribery. Repair.

* * *

After the shiny car vanished, Callie stood holding the puppy until its nails scratched red lines down her arm.

Bo said, “Moon car gone.”

“Yep.”

“Moon car back?”

“No.”

He considered this, then returned to the sandbox. In three seconds the loss had passed out of him. Lucky.

Callie carried the puppy inside. The mother dog dragged herself from beneath the table, ribs showing, teats loose and gray. She sniffed him and turned away. Even she was done.

From the bedroom came Jimmy’s alarm, though it was one in the afternoon. He had worked late. Or stayed up late. Work had been involved earlier in the day, so it all counted.

Callie set the puppy in a laundry basket. It climbed out.

She put it back. It climbed out again, tipping the basket over and rolling with it, feet waving. Under other conditions that might have been funny. What the heck, it was funny. She laughed once, sharp, and covered her mouth.

Jimmy appeared in the doorway wearing jeans and no shirt.

“They take it?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Said it weren’t a fit.”

Jimmy looked toward the puppy, then at Bo through the window. Callie saw the connection happen in his face.

“They got a problem with my house?”

“No.”

“My dogs?”

“She didn’t say.”

His jaw shifted. “That thing’s not eating another day.”

“I know.”

“You been feeding the good kibble?”

“No.”

He walked to the refrigerator, opened it, stared, and slammed it shut. “Handle it before I got to.”

That sounded like mercy if you arranged the words correctly. Jimmy did not want to kill a puppy. Who did? It bothered him afterward, though his bothered looked like anger and could last all evening. Callie could spare him that. A wife was supposed to know the hidden soft places in a man.

She waited until he went to shower.

Then she found a cardboard box, put the puppy inside, and carried it past Bo.

“Baby dog,” he said.

“Yep.”

“Bo hold?”

“Not now.”

“Bo hold baby dog.”

He ran after her and reached the end of the cable. It pulled him back. The folded washcloth remained under the belt, exactly where she had put it.

Callie drove Jimmy’s old Buick because the truck had no gas. The puppy climbed from the box before she reached the county road and crawled beneath the brake pedal. She swore, pulled over, and set him on the passenger seat.

He licked the air conditioner vent.

“Somebody’ll find you,” she told him.

Cornfields stretched everywhere, shining under the afternoon sun. Plenty of farmhouses. Plenty of barns. People dumped cats out here and cats got by. Dogs were smarter than cats, according to Jimmy, except when they were stupider. This puppy had climbed from a basket twice. A natural survivor.

Callie turned onto a field road and drove until the house was hidden.

The corn stood taller than she was. She carried the puppy ten rows in, not so far nobody would hear him, just far enough he would not follow the car right off. The ground was cool and shadowed. Dry leaves whispered overhead.

She put him down.

He sat on her foot.

“No.”

She moved him beside a corn stalk. He tumped over, stood, and came back.

“Quit.”

Again she set him down, farther this time. He stared up at her with the white frosting chin, not understanding that she was the one person trying not to have him shot.

Callie hurried away.

At the car she heard him crying, small squeaks swallowed by all that corn. She shut the door. The crying disappeared.

Somebody would find him. Farm kids. A nice old man checking irrigation. The shiny-car woman might come back after she got over herself, though Callie knew she would not. Women like that did not come back. They went somewhere clean and told the story.

Callie drove home.

Bo was still outside, safe beneath the tree. He had taken off his shorts and filled them with sand. Jimmy had not noticed. Jimmy was watching television with the volume high, and the mother dog slept under the porch.

Callie knelt to fasten Bo’s shorts again. He touched her cheek with a sandy hand.

“Good day,” he said.

His word. Not hers.

Callie looked at the highway beyond the yard, where he was not. She looked at the machine shed, where he was not. At the place beneath the combine, the water tower, the back seat of a deputy’s car—all the terrible places from which she had, one way or another, kept him.

No medicine. No trucks. Sunlight all day. A bowl of water, shade, his yellow toy, his mother checking from the window.

She had solved today so perfect.

Whatever else she had done wrong—and yes, there were things, everybody had things—she had given Bo one happy, safer day.

She had.

Callie had.

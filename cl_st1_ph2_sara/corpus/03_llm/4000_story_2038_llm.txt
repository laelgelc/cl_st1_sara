# Blasé Billy

I first met William Drayton in the smoking-room of the Pelican, at eleven o’clock on a June morning. This was an hour at which respectable men were engaged in regretting breakfast and disreputable men had not yet begun to remember it. Drayton sat by the window, dressed as carefully as though he had an appointment with the Chancellor of the Exchequer, and looked out upon Piccadilly with the expression of a man who had already seen everything worth seeing and had found most of it badly arranged.

“Mornin’,” I said.

He inclined his head.

“Fine day.”

“Is it?”

“Everybody says so.”

“Then it must be wrong.”

I took the chair opposite him. My family has always called this persistence; an old governess, less blinded by affection, called it wearing.

“You’re Drayton, aren’t you?”

“‘Fraid so.”

“Blasé Billy?”

He looked faintly pained.

“That is what my enemies call me.”

“I thought your friends called you that.”

“My dear fellow, one’s friends are one’s enemies who have not had the courage to declare themselves.”

This was my introduction to the Hon. William Cecil Wychwood Stanley Drayton, second son of Lord Runnymede, cousin to three dukes, nephew to a bishop, and connected by marriage with so many persons of consequence that he could not enter a drawing-room without encountering either a relative or a scandal.

His nickname suited him. He had been everywhere, done everything, and disapproved of the arrangements. He had hunted in Leicestershire and complained that the foxes lacked originality; shot in Scotland and objected to walking uphill for birds which could be bought more economically in Bond Street; crossed the Atlantic and pronounced America too new; visited India and found it too old. He had spent six months in Africa, where, he told me, the lions were disappointing and the missionaries overfed. Constantinople reminded him of a costume ball held over a drain; Venice was damp; Paris obvious; Vienna fat; and Monte Carlo, though immoral, had none of the freshness which should belong to sin.

He was twenty-nine, which is a trying age for a man who has exhausted existence at twenty-three.

Billy knew the correct tailor for every form of insolvency, the exact button to be worn at a royal garden party, the divorce history of every handsome woman in London, and the probable Cabinet future of every stupid man. He could distinguish old lace from new morality at twenty yards; knew which countesses received actors, which actresses received countesses, and in what houses one might mention God, money, or honest work without causing unnecessary embarrassment. He never read the newspapers because they told him what he had heard the night before, and he never read books because they told him what somebody had thought the century before.

Nevertheless, I liked him. Beneath his boredom there was intelligence, beneath his insolence good temper, and beneath his perfectly fitting waistcoat, though none of us then suspected it, a heart.

Our acquaintanceship advanced because I refused to be discouraged. Billy said I had the social instincts of a bailiff. I replied that a bailiff, at least, is admitted to the best houses. Before long we lunched together, went to first nights together, and stood side by side at crowded parties, preserving the grave expression with which Englishmen face amusement.

It was at Lady Pomeroy’s that I first saw Gerty Lovell.

Lady Pomeroy’s parties were among the accepted hardships of the season. Everybody went because everybody else went, and everybody else went because Lady Pomeroy would have been wounded if they had not. Her staircase was narrow, her champagne warm, and her rooms decorated with the portraits of ancestors who seemed to regard the guests as a regrettable decline in the family fortunes.

Gerty stood near the conservatory doors, listening to a young attaché explain a Balkan crisis of which he understood less than she did. She was not, perhaps, beautiful in the manner which causes poets to abandon regular employment, but she was lovely enough to keep several young men from attending to theirs. Her hair was a pale brown with gold in it; her eyes were grey; her figure possessed that fragile perfection which costs a dressmaker many anxious nights and a father several hundred pounds a year.

She had no father capable of paying them. The late Colonel Lovell had left his daughter a famous name, a trunk of unpaid accounts, and an elderly aunt in Kensington. The aunt supplied religion, lunch on Sundays, and advice in quantities which made other forms of provision impossible. Gerty supplied the rest by an art practised by women who have nothing and appear to possess everything. Her gloves were always fresh, her gowns always right, her cab always waiting when anybody important was looking. How this was accomplished no one knew, and society respects no mystery so much as the mystery of solvency.

She was said to be shallow. Society had tested her depth by asking what she thought of a new tenor, a royal engagement, two doubtful marriages, and the sleeves to be worn that autumn. She had replied exactly as the last clever person had replied, which proved that she had no ideas of her own. Original opinions, of course, are intolerable in a woman until they have become fashionable; then they are indispensable.

“Who is Miss Lovell?” I asked Billy.

“A compilation.”

“Of what?”

“Other people’s remarks, Madame Vernier’s gowns, and advertisements for violet scent.”

“You admire her?”

“Immensely. She spares one the fatigue of surprise.”

At that instant Gerty turned, saw him, and smiled.

Billy’s face altered.

The alteration was minute. His eyeglass did not fall; he did not clutch the table; he merely ceased, for perhaps two seconds, to look bored. In Billy this was equivalent to a public declaration accompanied by fireworks.

“Do you know her?” I asked.

“Slightly.”

“Introduce me.”

“No.”

This refusal, being unreasonable, interested me. Five minutes later he had crossed the room and was talking to her. The young attaché drifted away with the Balkan crisis still unsettled.

During the next month Billy met Gerty everywhere. In London one can avoid a creditor, an old schoolfellow, or the consequences of one’s conduct, but one cannot avoid a woman whom one wishes to meet. He found her at dinners, dances, race meetings, exhibitions, river parties, and charitable bazaars at which the rich purchased useless objects in order that the poor might acquire useful pennies.

His manner remained languid, but his movements became energetic. He would complain bitterly of an entertainment and arrive before anybody else. He discovered merit in plays which Gerty wished to see, music which she wished to hear, and people whom she wished to visit. Once, after declaring that amateur theatricals combined the conceit of the stage with the incompetence of private life, he drove fifteen miles into Surrey to watch Gerty speak seven lines as a dairymaid.

I went with him.

“You’re in love,” I said on the return journey.

“My dear chap, don’t be indecent.”

“You sat through three acts of Lady Bellingham’s comedy.”

“I have survived worse.”

“You applauded.”

“I was killing a mosquito.”

“There are no mosquitoes in Surrey.”

“That shows how effectively I killed it.”

A week later I found them at the Lyric, in the back of Lady Feversham’s box. The stage contained a moon, a painted lake, and a baritone assuring a soprano that his passion was eternal. Billy, who ordinarily regarded operatic love as an unpleasant form of shouting, was watching Gerty instead. She was not watching the stage either.

Afterwards he left a glove in the box.

It was not his glove; it was hers. He returned for it with a promptness singular in a man who had once allowed his luggage to remain in Cairo for four months because he could not be bothered to write a telegram. I happened to go back at the same moment, having left my coat—through stupidity, which is less romantic but more common.

I found them in the darkened corridor. Billy was kissing her.

I withdrew with the tact of a man who intends to mention the matter at breakfast.

Next morning he came to my rooms.

“Well?” I said.

“Well what?”

“How was the glove?”

“A little tight.”

“When is it to be?”

He walked to the window and inspected the chimneys.

“I’ve asked her to marry me.”

“And she?”

“Has consented.”

“My dear Billy!”

He turned upon me with suspicious severity.

“If you say ‘lucky dog,’ I shall leave.”

The phrase had, in fact, risen naturally to my lips, requiring no assistance from thought.

“I was going to say that I hoped you deserved her.”

“That would be more offensive, but less conventional.”

The engagement astonished everybody. Lord Runnymede considered it imprudent; Lady Runnymede considered it impossible; Gerty’s aunt considered it providential; and the Pelican considered it temporary. Billy had about fifteen hundred a year, expensive habits, and expectations dependent upon an elder brother who enjoyed rude health and mountain-climbing. Gerty had beauty, breeding, and seven pounds in the Post Office Savings Bank. It was felt that two persons so inadequately provided for had no right to be so happy.

“They’ll be ruined,” said Captain Vane in the smoking-room.

“Certainly,” said Billy. “But I have been solvent for nearly thirty years, and the monotony is becoming oppressive.”

“She’ll bore you in six months.”

“Then I shall have had six months which did not bore me.”

“She only thinks about clothes.”

“That is a sound subject. Clothes exist.”

“And what will you live on?”

“My income.”

“My dear fellow, nobody lives on his income.”

“Then we shall die young and escape your conversation.”

They married in November.

I had expected matrimony to expose their mistake; instead, it exposed ours. The change in Billy was not violent enough to be vulgar, but it was unmistakable. He ceased to cultivate weariness as an accomplishment. He sold two hunters, resigned from a club which charged him for being bored, and took a small house in Green Street. He still dressed perfectly, but no longer spoke of his tailor with the emotion other men reserve for their mothers. He began to arrive punctually. Once I heard him admit that a dinner had been pleasant, though he corrected himself immediately and said the soup had been tolerable.

Gerty changed too, and more prettily. Before marriage she had laughed whenever laughter was expected, smiled whenever a photographer might have wished it, and expressed affection with the careful moderation of a hostess distributing ices. Now she laughed from happiness, smiled from habit, and touched Billy’s sleeve when she spoke to him, as if reassured by the mere fact that he was there.

They still went everywhere. Gerty loved parties, music, flowers, jewels, bright rooms, and the small triumphs of social life. Billy loved seeing her enjoy them. She would return from a visit to her dressmaker with the gravity of a diplomat reporting a treaty, and Billy would listen to the fate of a bodice or the disputed frontier of a skirt as though Europe depended upon it.

“You encourage her extravagance,” I told him.

“My dear fellow, economy is the art of spending money on what bores one. Gerty spends it on what delights her.”

“She hasn’t any money.”

“That gives her taste an ideal purity.”

Yet they were not foolish. Their entertainments were small, their carriage hired, and their cook a woman whose chief culinary distinction was loyalty. When Gerty appeared in a new gown, two old ones had generally vanished by some private operation understood only by herself and Madame Vernier. She remained the best-dressed woman in London on an income which would hardly have dressed a dowager’s Pekinese.

I saw much of them. Their house was one of the few in which a man might call without being fed, instructed, or introduced to a prodigy. Billy’s cynicism softened into humour, and Gerty’s borrowed opinions gradually gave place to her own. They were not always profound, but they were hers, and originality became her.

One evening, after dinner, I found them disputing over a landscape Billy had purchased.

“It is atrocious,” said Gerty.

“The dealer assured me it was important.”

“That is what dealers say when a picture is too ugly to be called pretty.”

“You used to admire everything that was important.”

“I used to admire whatever people told me to admire.”

“And now?”

“Now I have you to tell me.”

“That is intellectual progress of a dangerous sort.”

She looked at him and smiled.

“I don’t believe you know anything about pictures, Billy.”

“No,” he said; “but I know what I like.”

“You like me.”

“Exactly. It has destroyed my reputation for judgment.”

It was two years after their marriage that Billy fell ill.

The season had begun with unusual violence. There were three royal functions, two political crises, a succession of American heiresses, and a new dance which enabled respectable people to appear compromised without undergoing the inconvenience of guilt. Gerty was in great request. Her gowns were copied, her phrases repeated, and her presence at a dinner raised the hostess’s spirits and the other women’s suspicions.

Billy had been out of sorts for some weeks, but nobody regarded this seriously because he had made ill health one of his conversational ornaments. Then, at a supper after the opera, he fainted.

The doctors called it pneumonia, spoke of complications, and adopted that professional cheerfulness which terrifies a household more efficiently than mourning. He was put to bed in the back room overlooking the gardens. A trained nurse arrived, followed by bottles, charts, thermometers, and the subdued tyranny with which science takes possession of a private house.

For the first fortnight Gerty scarcely left his door. She abandoned dinners, balls, concerts, and the Duchess of Harborough’s fête, an entertainment which half London would have attended with a broken leg. Cards accumulated in the hall; flowers filled the dining-room; callers whispered on the doorstep and departed looking important. Gerty neither saw nor asked for anyone.

Billy noticed.

Illness stripped him of his fashionable pose. His face grew thin, his voice weak, and his cleverness no longer served to conceal tenderness.

“You ought to go out, Gerty,” he said one afternoon when I was there.

“I would rather stay with you.”

“You’ve stayed with me all week.”

“I’ve stayed with you longer than that. I married you.”

“That was before you knew what it involved.”

“It involved your being ill occasionally.”

“It also involved Lady Harborough’s ball. You wanted to go.”

“I don’t now.”

He looked troubled.

“People will stop asking you.”

“Then they may save their stamps.”

“You care about all that.”

“I care about you.”

It was spoken simply. Three years earlier Gerty would have hidden sincerity beneath a joke, as a woman hides shabby satin beneath lace. Billy smiled, but after she had left the room he said to me:

“She means it.”

“Of course she does.”

“That is the worst of it.”

“The worst?”

“I have taken everything she likes away from her. She was made for lights and music and crowds. Look at this house—darkened rooms, whispering servants, doctors smelling of carbolic. She sits here and watches me breathe. It is damnable.”

“She is happy to do it.”

“That makes it more damnable.”

I argued, but love is the one subject upon which spectators are always logical and participants never are. Billy became restless whenever Gerty remained at home. He asked what invitations she had refused, who had called, what people were saying. Fever magnified his uneasiness until he seemed to believe that the entire London season was standing silent outside his bedroom, reproaching him for detaining its brightest ornament.

The nurse, Miss Hanbury, was a sensible woman with steady hands and no reverence for social truth. It was she who devised the deception.

“Go out for an hour, madam,” I heard her say to Gerty in the passage. “Or let him think you do. He frets after six o’clock because he knows you are giving things up.”

“I cannot go to a ball while he lies there.”

“No. But you can dress for one.”

Gerty stared at her.

Miss Hanbury explained.

That evening Gerty dined downstairs. At ten she put on a silver-grey gown embroidered with crystal, fastened diamonds about her throat, wrapped herself in white satin, and ordered the hired brougham. The servants were told that Mrs. Drayton was going to Lady Marden’s dance.

She entered Billy’s room before leaving.

He looked at her with delight.

“By Jove, Gerty, you are beautiful.”

“Am I?”

“You know you are. Who will be there?”

“Everybody, I suppose.”

“Dance with young Ferrers. He dances well, though he thinks badly.”

“I shall.”

“And don’t come home early on my account.”

“I won’t, dear.”

She kissed him, descended the stairs, entered the carriage, and drove away.

The brougham went twice around the Park. Then it returned by the mews. Gerty slipped through the servants’ entrance, climbed to a little room at the top of the house, and sat there alone.

At two o’clock the carriage came again to the front door. Gerty went down the back stairs, passed through the mews, entered it, and was driven with proper ceremony to her own steps. The bell rang. The footman opened. She came into the lighted hall like a woman returning from pleasure.

Billy was awake.

“Well?” he asked.

“Delightful.”

“Was the Marden woman wearing that green thing?”

“Worse. Emerald velvet, with pink roses.”

“Good heavens.”

“And Lady Clara danced with the Italian prince four times.”

“She’ll marry him.”

“He has no money.”

“That has never prevented an Italian prince from marrying.”

Gerty invented a supper, a cotillon, two proposals, a torn flounce, and a quarrel in the conservatory. Billy listened like a child to a fairy tale. His eyes brightened; his breathing became easier. When she kissed him good night, he whispered:

“I’m glad you went.”

“So am I,” she said.

The performance was repeated.

Every afternoon notes were examined and an engagement selected. Every evening Gerty dressed with elaborate care. Sometimes she wore blue tulle, sometimes yellow satin, sometimes black velvet with pearls. She ordered flowers for her bodice and consulted Billy about her ornaments. At the proper hour she drove away, returned secretly, waited in the upper room, and later made her public arrival at the front door.

The irony of it would have amused Billy had he known. Gerty, who had once dressed for crowded rooms and the admiration of strangers, now dressed for a sick man in half-darkness. Her audience was one; her triumph was to deceive him; and the finest acting of the season took place before no critic who understood it.

Unfortunately, critics who did not understand it were plentiful.

I was dining at the Beaconsfields’ when Mrs. Carstairs said:

“Is it true that poor Billy Drayton is dying?”

“He is very ill.”

“And Gerty goes out every night.”

Lady Beaconsfield lowered her eyes with the satisfaction of a woman compelled by sorrow to discuss another woman’s conduct.

“I saw her leaving for the opera yesterday.”

“So did I,” said Mrs. Carstairs. “White satin and diamonds.”

“A curious costume for anxiety.”

I said that appearances were often employed to conceal facts.

“My dear man,” said Lady Beaconsfield, “facts are what appearances produce.”

This seemed clever enough to end the subject, which is how many falsehoods become respectable.

At the Pelican, Captain Vane was less delicate.

“Drayton’s wife is a little monster,” he said. “She was at the Savoy while the poor devil was at death’s door.”

“Did you see her inside?”

“I saw her arrive.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“She left her house at eleven and came back at three.”

“Your devotion to observation would make marriage hazardous.”

He shrugged.

“We all knew what she was.”

So had I, once. That remembrance silenced me more effectively than his certainty.

I had been away in Paris for ten days when I first saw the whole performance. Returning unexpectedly, I called in Green Street shortly before midnight. The house appeared closed, but as I was turning away a brougham stopped in the mews. Gerty descended in a cloak, spoke to the coachman, and vanished through the side door.

I followed and knocked softly. After some delay Miss Hanbury admitted me.

“Mrs. Drayton has just returned?” I asked.

“She has not been out.”

Then she told me.

Gerty was in the small room above the nursery floor. I went up. She sat beside an unlit grate in a gown of pale gold, her cloak thrown across a chair. The diamonds in her hair caught what little light entered from the landing. On the table lay a plate of sandwiches she had not touched and a society paper from the previous week.

She rose quickly.

“You mustn’t tell Billy.”

“I won’t.”

“He thinks I am at the Wexfords’.”

“So does half London.”

She smiled faintly.

“That is convenient.”

I looked at her face. Paint could not hide its exhaustion. Her eyes were shadowed; the glittering dress made her pallor dreadful.

“You do this every night?”

“When he is well enough to notice.”

“And sit here alone?”

“Sometimes I sleep. Usually I prepare my adventures.”

She pointed to the newspaper.

“It tells me who is supposed to be where. Last night I put the Austrian ambassador at Lady Marden’s, but he was dining at Windsor. Billy nearly caught me. Fortunately, he has never been precise about diplomacy.”

“Gerty, people are talking.”

“I know.”

“They think you heartless.”

“That is because they are kind enough to interest themselves in me.”

“I could explain.”

“No!”

The word came sharply. Then she lowered her voice.

“No. Billy must not hear of it. If there were even a whisper—if someone wrote or called—he would know.”

“But afterwards—”

She turned away.

“We won’t speak of afterwards.”

A little after two the carriage rolled up to the front. Gerty drew on her gloves, arranged her cloak, and examined herself in the glass. The transformation was astonishing. Fatigue disappeared beneath animation; grief took the shape of gaiety. She went down the back stairs, out through the mews, and entered the brougham. Three minutes later she rang at her own front door.

I waited in the hall.

“Well, Mr. Drayton?” she asked brightly when the servant had withdrawn. “What brings you here at this scandalous hour?”

The comedy was for the household as much as for Billy.

She went upstairs. I followed as far as the landing and heard his weak voice.

“Was it good?”

“Splendid. Such music, Billy! And the Princess asked who made my gown.”

“Did you tell her?”

“Certainly not. There are limits even to loyalty.”

He laughed.

That laugh, faint as it was, paid for the jewels, the lonely hours, the lies, and perhaps even the gossip.

Billy improved for several days, then suffered a relapse. I had to go to Scotland on family business and left unwillingly. Before my departure he sent for me.

“You’ve been a good friend,” he said.

This alarmed me more than the doctors had. Billy had never before used a plain phrase when an epigram was available.

“You’ll be about when I’m up again?”

“Of course.”

“Gerty has a dinner tonight. She wanted to give it up.”

“Naturally.”

“I made her go. One mustn’t let illness become selfishness.”

“No.”

He smiled.

“She has enjoyed this season, after all. That is a comfort.”

I looked toward the window. A cab passed below, and somewhere in the street a man was calling evening papers.

“Yes,” I said. “She has made you believe so.”

“What?”

“I said she has every reason to do so.”

My journey lasted longer than expected. Telegrams followed me north: NO CHANGE; CONDITION LESS SATISFACTORY; COME IF POSSIBLE. The last reached me too late.

Billy died shortly after midnight on a Thursday in July.

The newspapers said that Mrs. Drayton had been summoned from a ball and reached the house only minutes before the end. Society accepted the detail eagerly. It supplied exactly the finish society desired: white shoulders, diamonds, music, a breathless carriage, and a husband dying alone. Truth is often rejected for lack of stage management.

I returned the following afternoon. Green Street was hot and silent. The blinds were down. Cards lay in heaps on the hall table, many from people who had spent the morning discussing Gerty’s cruelty and the afternoon expressing sympathy with her grief.

She received me in the drawing-room. She wore black, and looked smaller than I remembered. For a moment neither of us spoke.

“At least,” she said, “he was happy at the end.”

“I am told he suffered little.”

“I don’t mean that. He thought I had come home from the ball. I told him it had been a great success.”

Her voice broke, but she recovered it.

“He asked whether I had danced.”

“What did you say?”

“That I had danced all night.”

She moved to the mantelpiece and touched a photograph of him.

“He said he was glad.”

I found that I, too, had become interested in the window.

After a while I said:

“Gerty, there is something I must do.”

“What?”

“I must tell people.”

She looked puzzled.

“Tell them what?”

“The truth. They are saying vile things. They believe you deserted him night after night. They think you came from a ball when he died.”

“I did come from a ball—as far as Billy knew.”

“You understand me. Miss Hanbury can confirm everything. So can the servants. A word would put the matter right.”

“No.”

“But your reputation—”

She smiled then, not with the smile she had once worn at Lady Pomeroy’s, nor with the brave brightness she had carried into Billy’s room, but with something quieter and older.

“What would you tell them? That I put on my gowns for him, and sat upstairs counting the hours? That I lied because he was afraid of spoiling my pleasure? That he died pleased because he believed I had been dancing?”

“Yes.”

“And then they would admire me.”

“They would be ashamed.”

“For a week. After that the story would be told at dinners. People would praise my devotion between the fish and the entrée. Women I scarcely know would repeat what Billy said to me in his bedroom. No.”

“They have judged you cruelly.”

“They always judged me. Once they thought me charming, and that was not quite true either.”

“You cannot mean to let this stand.”

She looked again at Billy’s photograph.

“It was our secret, though he never knew it. I will not give it to them merely to make them change their minds.”

“But surely it matters what people believe?”

Gerty was silent a moment.

Then she said, very gently:

“Does it?”

That was all.

I had known Gerty Lovell when an invitation could delight her, a cancelled dinner humiliate her, and the wrong sleeve disturb her peace for a day. I had seen her measure life by crowded rooms, approving glances, and the authority of women whom she privately disliked. Yet now London might condemn her, and she would not purchase acquittal by exposing one tender corner of her marriage.

I went away without arguing further.

Society continued to pity Billy and blame his wife. It had seen her carriage, her gowns, her diamonds, and her late return; society is naturally reluctant to surrender evidence merely because it happens to be false. For a time she was omitted from certain invitations, then included in others because sorrow became her and mystery is always fashionable. She accepted little and explained nothing.

People still called her Gerty Lovell, as though marriage had been an episode and widowhood restored her to the familiar figure they understood: pretty, vain, perfectly dressed, and without a fortune.

They had never known her.

For that matter, neither had I.

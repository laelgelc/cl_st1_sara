### The Deacon’s New Stepper

The Widow Stimson never tried to marry Deacon Hawkins.

She said so herself, repeatedly, and no woman with eighty acres of cleared land, twelve good Jerseys, a sugar orchard, and money drawing interest at the county bank ought to be accused without evidence. If she happened to sit beside the deacon at church suppers, it was because the draft struck her corner of the table. If she sent him a mince pie at Christmas, it was because one pie had been left over. If she consulted him about replacing the boundary fence between their farms, why, a boundary fence must be discussed with the man on the other side of it.

Still, it was an unusually long boundary to require three years’ conversation.

The two farms joined from the pike to Miller’s Creek. Under separate management they were the best properties in North Danby; under one management—though nobody but the Sewing Society used that expression—they might have been the finest holding in the county.

“Providence don’t lay out farms that way for nothing,” Mrs. Elder Pomeroy remarked one Thursday afternoon, as she basted red flannel for the heathen.

The North Danby Sewing Society met every second Thursday in the church vestry. Its announced purpose was to provide garments for deserving missionaries, but it also clothed every bare fact in town with several additional layers before sending it abroad.

“I wouldn’t say Providence intended a marriage,” said the minister’s wife, in a tone implying that Providence had privately informed her of exactly that intention. “It is not for us to judge.”

“Certainly not,” agreed Mrs. Peavey, the grocer’s wife. “But he bought raisins Monday.”

The sisters stopped sewing.

“Raisins?” asked Mrs. Doctor Bell.

“Two pounds. The best kind.”

“For himself?”

“He said they were for a pudding.”

The sisters exchanged looks. A bachelor may buy salt pork without exciting comment, but when he buys raisins he has entered upon a dangerous softness.

“Widow Stimson makes the best steamed pudding in Danby,” said Sister Cartridge.

Sister Cartridge was unmarried, but she still possessed faith, hope, and charity—particularly hope.

“Did he say whose pudding?” she continued.

“No. I wouldn’t ask such a thing,” said Mrs. Peavey, who had asked twice.

Mrs. Doctor Bell drew her thread through with a sly smile. “Abigail Stimson was in here yesterday wanting cinnamon.”

At this the Society gave one soft, collective “Oh!”

It was not that they were eager for gossip. Shame on anyone who thought so! They merely considered themselves guardians of the town’s moral weather, and if a matrimonial storm was gathering, somebody ought to observe the clouds.

The Widow Stimson, meanwhile, went serenely about her affairs. She was forty-two, rosy, vigorous, and built upon a generous plan. She could lift a milk can into a wagon without calling the hired man, judge hay at twenty paces, and bargain with a cattle buyer until he went home feeling that commerce was a personal affliction. She dressed soberly, laughed readily, and had been heard to say that a woman who could run a farm could certainly run a husband, though she did not specify any particular husband.

Deacon Hawkins was fifty, tall and spare, with gray whiskers, a solemn Sunday coat, and a reputation for prudence. He was superintendent of the Sunday school, treasurer of the Mite Society, and so reliable that men handed him money without a receipt.

But even the most seasoned oak may contain one lively worm. The deacon’s weakness was horseflesh.

In youth he had loved fast horses with a passion barely distinguishable from sin. Age, office, and dignity had cooled him—or so he said. Yet whenever Squire Hopkins passed him on the pike, the deacon’s fingers tightened on the reins and his lips moved in a manner that might have been prayer, though it was never entered in the church minutes.

For twenty-seven years the squire had owned a horse able to pass anything the deacon drove. Not the same horse, naturally; the Almighty is merciful even to deacons. But Hopkins seemed to possess a wicked instinct for selecting long-legged bays with no respect for ecclesiastical rank.

The previous winter the squire’s mare, Lady Jane, had overtaken the deacon’s gray on Tuttle’s Hill and tossed snow over him from cap to runner. Hopkins had waved as he went by.

“Fine traveling!” he called.

Deacon Hawkins had answered that there was no sense in driving recklessly upon a public highway. For that matter, there was no sense in a red-haired man wearing a green muffler, either, but he kept that judgment to himself.

In November the deacon heard of a black gelding for sale in East Canaan. By December he owned him, after paying a sum he did not mention to the Mite Society. The horse was named Emperor, stood sixteen hands, and could trot as though the road were being pulled under him.

The deacon admired Emperor for a week in private. Then he began needing somebody else to admire him.

He thought first of Widow Stimson. Not because he was courting her—certainly not. She knew horses, and a man considering an animal of importance required an intelligent opinion. Also, the new sleigh had a wide seat, and it would be wasteful to use only half.

He called on a Saturday afternoon.

The widow received him in a blue wool dress with a white collar and looked so comfortable beside the parlor stove that the deacon forgot the opening sentence he had prepared.

“Cold,” he observed.

“Seasonable,” she replied.

“Roads are good.”

“So I hear.”

“Firm underneath and two inches fresh on top.”

“Just right for sleighing.”

The widow waited. The deacon inspected a glass lamp, a braided rug, and a portrait of the late Mr. Stimson, who regarded him from the wall with an expression of permanent surprise.

“I’ve bought a horse,” he said at last.

“So I hear.”

The deacon was disappointed. News traveled fast in North Danby, especially when purchased for three hundred and fifty dollars.

“Thought perhaps you’d like to take a little ride and tell me what you think of him.”

Widow Stimson’s eyes brightened, though modesty lowered them directly afterward.

“I might,” she said. “If you need my judgment.”

“That’s it. Your judgment.”

“And not my company?”

The deacon coughed.

“Your company would naturally accompany your judgment.”

“Then I’ll get my bonnet.”

Ten minutes later they were tucked into the deacon’s new cutter, the widow wrapped in buffalo robes and the deacon holding the reins with the restrained pride of a Roman driving a captured king through the capital.

Emperor stepped out smartly. His black neck arched, his ears pointed forward, and the silver bells shook a bright tune over the snow.

“My!” said the widow.

The deacon tried not to smile. “Moves easy.”

“Easy? He don’t seem to touch the ground.”

“I didn’t buy him for speed.”

“No?”

“General usefulness.”

“Of course.”

“He may have a little speed in him.”

“I shouldn’t wonder.”

“Not that speed’s important.”

“No indeed.”

They passed the church at twelve miles an hour.

The deacon drew Emperor down to nine, remembering his office.

At the Peavey store Mrs. Peavey was arranging canned goods in the window. She saw the black gelding, the green sleigh, the deacon’s gray whiskers, and the widow’s blue bonnet. Before the cutter reached the mill bridge, Mrs. Peavey had put on her shawl and stepped next door to ask Mrs. Doctor Bell whether she needed anything from the store.

“They’ve gone toward the north pike,” she reported.

“How close were they sitting?”

“It was a wide seat.”

“That ain’t what I asked.”

“I couldn’t say. But one buffalo robe covered both.”

Mrs. Bell nodded gravely. In February a shared buffalo robe is nearly equivalent to an announcement.

Out on the pike the deacon let Emperor move. The sleigh hissed over the packed snow; bare maples flickered by; fence posts seemed to jump backward into the fields.

Widow Stimson watched the gelding’s gait with an expert eye.

“He carries his head well,” she said.

“Does.”

“Good shoulders.”

“Very.”

“Strong behind.”

“Plenty of power there.”

“What’ll he do?”

The deacon looked innocent. “Do?”

“To the mile.”

“I haven’t pressed him.”

The widow laughed. “Silas Hawkins, you’ve timed that horse every day since you bought him.”

The deacon reddened above his muffler.

“Well,” he admitted, “he’s gone in two-thirty-seven on this road, and there was a wind.”

“He’ll beat Lady Jane.”

The deacon’s hands moved on the reins.

“I hadn’t thought of Hopkins.”

“Not once, I suppose.”

“Racing on the highway is foolish.”

“Surely.”

“And unbecoming.”

“Especially if you lose.”

The deacon glanced at her. Widow Stimson’s cheeks were pink from the cold, and there was a mischievous spark in her eyes. He decided that a woman who understood horses might, after all, have other excellent qualities.

They reached the long straight stretch beyond Carter’s woods. The pike lay white and level for nearly two miles, with deep drifts banked along either side. Emperor settled into a magnificent trot.

Then bells sounded behind them.

The deacon did not turn. He knew those bells. He had heard them approaching for twenty-seven years.

The widow looked back.

“Red cutter,” she said.

The deacon’s jaw tightened.

“Green muffler,” she added.

“I know it.”

Lady Jane came on rapidly, her nose stretched, her breath smoking. Squire Hopkins sat low behind her, broad, red-faced, and cheerful in the offensive way of a man who expects to win.

“Afternoon, Deacon!” he shouted.

“Afternoon,” returned Hawkins.

“Pretty little horse you got!”

Emperor’s ears twitched. The deacon’s did likewise.

“He’ll do.”

“Want to see how he’ll do?”

“I’m taking Mrs. Stimson for an airing.”

“Don’t let me disturb you.”

The squire drew almost even. Lady Jane’s harness jingled beside them.

“Road’s open,” Hopkins called. “First to the old tollgate?”

The deacon stared ahead.

Widow Stimson saw the struggle in his face: dignity against desire, church office against twenty-seven years of snow in the whiskers.

“You needn’t on my account,” she said sweetly.

That settled it.

The deacon shortened his reins. “Tollgate, then.”

Hopkins laughed. “Right you are!”

Lady Jane sprang forward. Emperor answered before the whip lifted. The cutters leaped, bells broke into a frenzy, and loose snow flew in shining sheets.

For the first quarter mile they ran even. Emperor’s stride was long and smooth; Lady Jane traveled with quick, stabbing steps. The deacon leaned forward, all Sunday-school moderation blown out behind him. Squire Hopkins called something, but the runners screamed too loudly for words.

“Steady, boy!” cried Hawkins. “Steady—now!”

Emperor forged ahead by a nose.

The widow clutched the rail, not from fear but enthusiasm. Her bonnet ribbons streamed. She knew this was improper. She also knew they were winning.

Then the pike began its gradual rise toward the tollgate.

Lady Jane crept up.

The black gelding was carrying two persons; the mare carried one. It was not a matter to mention before a lady, but arithmetic has no manners. Widow Stimson, being built on that generous plan already described, understood the calculation as quickly as anybody.

The squire’s red cutter edged level.

“Come on, Lady!” Hopkins shouted.

Emperor labored. His breath came harder; his splendid stride shortened by inches. The deacon urged him, coaxed him, called upon his pride, his courage, and possibly his religion. The tollgate stood half a mile away.

Lady Jane’s nose went in front.

Widow Stimson felt indignant. Hopkins was taking a mean advantage. To be sure, he had not invited her into the deacon’s sleigh, nor had he arranged the respective weight of the drivers. Nevertheless, the advantage was mean, and something must be done.

She looked at the snowbank beside the road. Recent winds had piled it deep and soft beyond the ditch.

She looked at the deacon. His face was tragic. Twenty-seven years of defeat sat upon his shoulders, and vanished prestige was already fluttering over Lady Jane’s tail.

“Silas!” she shouted.

He did not hear.

The widow gathered the robe around her, braced one hand on the side of the cutter, and waited until they swept beside the deepest drift.

Then she jumped.

There was a muffled “whump,” a cloud of powder, and Widow Stimson disappeared from the race as completely as if the earth had opened for her.

The cutter bounded light.

Emperor felt the difference. Every strained muscle answered. He shot forward with such sudden power that the deacon nearly fell backward into the vacant half of the seat.

“Good boy!” he cried. “Go on, Emperor!”

The black gelding lengthened. Lady Jane’s advantage vanished. They drew even, held even, and thundered toward the tollgate, black neck against bay, green cutter against red.

“Hi! Hi!” yelled Hopkins.

“Now, boy!” shouted the deacon.

Emperor gave his last heroic effort. Victory, which had been perched on Lady Jane’s collar, changed horses. The green cutter swept past the tollgate a full length ahead.

Deacon Hawkins stood up in the sleigh and waved his cap.

“Beat you!” he shouted. “By thunder, I beat you!”

It was not deaconly language, perhaps, but there are moments when office must yield to history.

Squire Hopkins slowed his mare. “You’ve got a stepper there,” he admitted.

“Knew I had.”

“Carries light, too.”

“Carries—”

The deacon sat down.

The buffalo robe lay in a heap. The wide seat was empty.

His triumph turned cold.

“Abigail!”

He looked behind. The white pike stretched vacant toward the woods.

“Where’s Mis’ Stimson?” asked Hopkins.

The deacon made no answer. He pulled Emperor around so sharply the cutter tipped on one runner.

“Abigail!” he cried again.

He drove back at a pace that made the race seem like a funeral procession. What had happened? She had fallen. She had been thrown. She was lying injured, frozen, perhaps dead, while he—shame on him!—had waved his cap over a horse race.

The deacon’s heart pounded harder than Emperor’s hoofs.

“Abigail! Abigail!”

At last he saw movement in the roadside drift. A blue bonnet appeared, then two snow-covered shoulders. Widow Stimson was struggling upright, laughing and blowing snow from her mouth.

The deacon stopped so suddenly Emperor sat back on his haunches.

He flung down the reins and plunged into the drift. Snow entered his boots, filled his sleeves, and packed itself beneath his collar, but he paid it no attention.

“Are you hurt?” he demanded. “Abigail, are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Are you sure?”

“Perfectly.”

“Any bones broken?”

“Not one.”

“Did you fall?”

“I jumped.”

“You—jumped?”

“You were losing.”

The deacon stared at her.

She brushed snow from her bonnet. “Did you beat him?”

“By a length.”

“I knew you would!”

Her face shone with such delight that the deacon suddenly understood several matters which had escaped him during his fifty years: that a horse may be expensive and still worth less than a woman; that adjoining farms are not the only things capable of consolidation; and that when a widow jumps into a snowbank to help a man defeat his enemy, she is not wholly indifferent to him.

“Abigail,” he said.

“Yes, Silas?”

“You might have killed yourself.”

“But I didn’t.”

“You did it for me.”

“And Emperor.”

“Mostly for me.”

“Well,” she said, looking down, “mostly.”

The deacon took her in his arms. It was an awkward embrace, owing to the depth of the drift and the stiffness of his overcoat, but it was earnest.

Squire Hopkins drove by a moment later. He looked once, grinned, and tactfully kept going. A man may lose a race and still carry home something worth telling.

By six o’clock Mrs. Peavey heard that Widow Stimson had been found in a snowbank with Deacon Hawkins. By seven, Mrs. Doctor Bell knew that he had embraced her. By half past seven, the minister’s wife had expressed the hope that all details would be kept from the Sewing Society.

At its next meeting the sisters discussed nothing else.

“I always said it would happen,” declared Mrs. Pomeroy.

“You said she was after him,” Sister Cartridge reminded her.

“I said no such thing. I said their interests were naturally merging.”

“Did he propose in the snowbank?” asked Mrs. Peavey.

The minister’s wife frowned. “We should not pry into sacred matters.”

“Then he did,” said Mrs. Bell.

The Society bent over its sewing, smiling.

And Widow Stimson? She continued to insist that she had never tried to catch the deacon.

Perhaps she had not.

But when a woman throws herself out of a sleigh to lighten a man’s load, it is a mighty good sign that she is willing to help him carry it thereafter.

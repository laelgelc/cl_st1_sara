### William’s Polite New Year

On the last afternoon of the old year William stood outside Mr. Moss’s sweet-shop with his hands in his pockets and his nose pressed thoughtfully against the window.

The window contained everything that a sweet-shop window ought to contain and a good many things that, in William’s opinion, no sweet-shop window had any right to contain unless it intended to give them to him. There were glass jars of Cokernut Kisses, Pineapple Crisp, Rose Rock, Nutty Footballs, Lemon Fizzers, Chocolate Soldiers, Almond Cannons and Mixed Dew Drops. There were slabs of toffee as large as paving-stones. There were striped sticks of peppermint, long and stout and serviceable enough to support a tent. There were sugar mice with pink eyes and liquorice tails. There were chocolate cigars, which William regarded with the tolerant contempt of one who had once eaten six and been unable to look at a real cigar box for a week.

Mr. Moss, behind the counter, watched him mournfully.

Mr. Moss always looked mournful. When business was good he looked mournful because the stock was going; when business was bad he looked mournful because it was not. His black moustache drooped over his mouth, and his shoulders drooped over his waistcoat, and his waistcoat drooped over his trousers. He had the appearance of having received, many years ago, news too depressing ever to be contradicted.

William entered.

“Afternoon,” he said.

“Afternoon,” said Mr. Moss.

William looked round the shop with the air of a wealthy customer who is disappointed by the limited selection.

“How much are those?” he said, pointing at a jar.

“Penny an ounce.”

“I’ve only got a halfpenny.”

“Then you can’t ’ave an ounce.”

“I didn’t say I wanted an ounce,” said William with dignity. “I only asked how much it was.”

There was silence. William transferred his attention to the Pineapple Crisp.

“How much is that?”

“Penny an ounce.”

“An’ those?”

“Penny an ounce.”

“Are they all a penny an ounce?”

“Most of ’em.”

“It’s funny,” said William, “all of ’em bein’ the same price. Seems sort of not fair to the ones that are nicer.”

Mr. Moss sighed.

William took his halfpenny from his pocket and placed it on the counter. It was not a handsome halfpenny. Indeed, it had spent so long in William’s possession that it had acquired the appearance of sharing his habits and fortunes.

“What can I have for that?” he asked.

“’Alf an ounce of anything that’s a penny.”

William considered this. Half an ounce was a contemptible quantity. It was the sort of quantity one might give to a canary.

“It’s New Year’s Eve,” he said at last.

Mr. Moss admitted it.

“People give presents on New Year’s Eve,” pursued William.

“Do they?”

“Yes. Everyone knows that.”

“Who gives ’em?”

“People,” said William comprehensively.

Mr. Moss looked at him. William looked back, grave, patient and full of information.

“Well, no one’s given me any,” said Mr. Moss.

“I would,” said William warmly, “if I had any money.”

“You’ve got an ’alfpenny.”

“That’s all the money I’ve got,” explained William. “I couldn’t give you that, ’cause then I wouldn’t have any.”

Mr. Moss sighed again and reached for the jar of Mixed Dew Drops.

“’Alf an ounce,” he said.

William watched the scales with an expression of anguish.

“A bit fell off,” he pointed out.

Mr. Moss put back the fragment that had fallen from the scoop.

“I meant it fell off on to your side,” said William.

Mr. Moss put it in the bag.

“An’ that little blue one’s not quite on the scale.”

Mr. Moss added the little blue one.

“An’ that red one was sort of touching it before you moved it.”

Mr. Moss added the red one.

By the time William’s scientific observations were complete, the bag contained nearly two ounces.

Mr. Moss twisted the paper at the top.

“Now,” he said, “you make a New Year resolution.”

William regarded him suspiciously.

“What sort?”

“Resolve to be a good boy.”

“I am a good boy.”

Mr. Moss’s moustache moved slightly.

“Well, resolve to be better.”

“Better than who?”

“Better than you are now.”

William considered the proposition. It seemed to him unnecessary and faintly insulting.

“I’ll resolve not to do somethin’,” he offered.

“Resolve to be polite,” said Mr. Moss. “Just for one day. Tomorrow. New Year’s Day.”

William frowned. Politeness was, in his experience, a tiresome arrangement by which adults delayed saying what they meant. Still, he had his eyes on the paper bag.

“If I do, d’you give me these free?”

“I’ll give you those free.”

“An’ my halfpenny back?”

Mr. Moss pushed the halfpenny towards him.

“All right,” said William. “I resolve to be p’lite all tomorrow.”

“Properly polite.”

“Yes.”

“Not just when it suits you.”

William was shocked.

“A resolution’s a resolution,” he said. “I never break resolutions.”

This was true, chiefly because he had never before made one.

He took the sweets and put three into his mouth.

“Are you makin’ one?” he asked indistinctly.

Mr. Moss’s expression became, if possible, more lugubrious.

“I make the same one every year.”

“What?”

“To ask Miss Marjorie Bell to marry me.”

“Does she?”

“Does she what?”

“Marry you?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“She says no.”

“Why d’you keep on askin’ her, then?”

Mr. Moss looked beyond William at the darkening street.

“I’ve asked her every New Year’s Day for ten years,” he said. “She expects it.”

“P’raps she says no ’cause she expects that too.”

Mr. Moss stared at him.

William, encouraged, went on.

“You ought to do somethin’ diff’rent. Say you don’t want to marry her. Then she might say she does.”

“Go ’ome,” said Mr. Moss.

William moved towards the door.

“I don’t think she sounds very sensible,” he said, “if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

“Go ’ome.”

William went home, finishing the Mixed Dew Drops on the way.

That night he contemplated his resolution. He contemplated it in bed, when he should have been asleep, and clothed it in splendour. He saw himself moving among his family like a courtier of ancient days, bowing, smiling, yielding the largest chair and the last potato. His mother would weep with pride. His father would gaze upon him in silent wonder. His brother and sister, humbled by his example, would confess that they had misjudged him. Possibly there would be a medal.

The resolution ceased to be merely a promise made for sweets. It became a stern and shining law. It hovered above him like a kind of god, demanding sacrifice.

At eight o’clock the next morning William entered the dining-room.

His family were at breakfast.

“Good mornin’, Father,” he said. “I hope you’ve slept well, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

His father lowered the newspaper.

“What?”

“I said I hope you’ve slept well.”

“Why?”

William was taken aback.

“’Cause it’s p’lite.”

His elder brother Robert looked at him with deep suspicion.

“What have you done?”

“Nothin’.”

“What are you going to do?”

“Nothin’.”

His mother turned anxiously from the coffee-pot.

“Are you feeling quite well, dear?”

“Yes, thank you very much, Mother, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

His sister Ethel stared at him.

“That doesn’t make sense.”

“It does,” said William. Then he remembered. “It does, thank you very much, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

He sat down and took the toast-rack.

“Pass that,” said Robert.

William drew himself up.

“Would you mind sayin’ ‘please’?”

Robert blinked.

“Pass the toast.”

“Not unless you say ‘please.’ It isn’t p’lite.”

“Give it to me.”

“I’m only tryin’ to improve your manners, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

Robert reached across the table. William snatched the toast-rack away. In the brief struggle that followed, two pieces of toast fell into the marmalade and one into Father’s coffee.

Father rose.

“William!”

William hastily surrendered the toast-rack.

“Here you are,” he said graciously. “Quite all right. Don’t menshun it.”

“Don’t mention what?”

“Me givin’ it to him.”

“You didn’t give it to him. You upset my coffee.”

“I’m sorry you put your coffee where the toast fell into it, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

“Go and wash your hands.”

“They’re clean.”

“They’re filthy.”

William examined them.

“They’re cleaner than Robert’s, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

“Go.”

William retired with dignity. He felt that politeness, properly practised, was a noble but lonely calling.

He returned and seated himself again.

“May I have an egg, please?” he said.

His mother brightened.

“Of course, dear.”

William took two.

“Only one,” she said.

“I said ‘please.’”

“That doesn’t mean you may have two.”

William slowly replaced the smaller egg.

“Thank you very much for lettin’ me have the bigger one.”

“I didn’t.”

“Well, you didn’t say I couldn’t, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

His father laid down the newspaper.

“William, if this extraordinary behaviour is intended to annoy us—”

“It isn’t. I made a resolution.”

“A resolution?”

“To be p’lite all day.”

Robert laughed.

“I thought something had happened to him.”

“I am bein’ p’lite,” said William fiercely, “which is more than what you are, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

“William,” said his mother, “politeness does not consist of saying ‘if you don’t mind me saying so’ after every rude remark.”

“What does it consist of, then?”

“Thinking of other people.”

William looked bewildered. This was an entirely new and unreasonable definition.

“You can’t tell whether anyone’s doin’ that,” he objected.

“You can generally tell.”

“Well, I’m thinkin’ of everyone now.”

“What are you thinking?”

“I’m thinkin’ Father looks cross an’ Robert’s hair wants cuttin’ an’ Ethel’s got marmalade on her chin, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

Breakfast ended abruptly.

At ten o’clock William went out.

He went first to Mr. Moss’s shop, partly to display the success of the resolution and partly because there was always the possibility that Mr. Moss might celebrate it with more sweets.

Mr. Moss stood behind the counter in a black coat, a high collar and a bright green tie. The tie had evidently been chosen to suggest hope, though on Mr. Moss it suggested chiefly sea-sickness.

“Mornin’,” said William. “You look very nice, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

Mr. Moss adjusted the tie.

“Do I?”

“Yes. Except the green doesn’t suit you much.”

Mr. Moss sighed.

“I’m catchin’ the ten-forty train,” he said. “I’m goin’ to see Miss Bell.”

“To ask her again?”

“Yes.”

“She’ll prob’ly say no again,” said William. “Still, you look very nice.”

Mr. Moss took out his watch.

“Mrs. Cripps is comin’ to mind the shop while I’m gone. She should be ’ere now.”

At that moment a small boy entered carrying a note. Mr. Moss opened it.

“Mrs. Cripps’s got lumbago,” he read. “She can’t come.”

“What’s lumbago?” said William.

“Pain in the back.”

“I had a pain in my back once. I still went to school.”

Mr. Moss looked at the clock, at the note and at William.

“You couldn’t stay ’ere ten minutes, could you? Just till I find someone?”

William’s eyes widened.

“Mind the shop?”

“Yes. Don’t touch anything. If a customer comes, tell ’em to wait.”

William drew himself to his full height.

“I’ll look after everythin’,” he said. “You can trust me.”

Mr. Moss hesitated. Then the station clock struck the quarter.

“I’ll miss that train,” he muttered. “Mrs. Brown next door might come in when she’s finished upstairs. I’ll send word. Don’t sell anything. Don’t give anything away. Don’t eat anything.”

“I know how to keep a shop,” said William with cold dignity.

Mr. Moss seized his hat.

“I wun’t be more’n two hours if—if it’s the usual answer.”

“It will be,” said William encouragingly.

Mr. Moss hurried out.

William was alone.

For a moment he stood behind the counter, motionless, conscious of responsibility. The shop was entrusted to him. Its wealth lay all around him. He was master of an Aladdin’s Cave whose treasures surpassed mere jewels, because no one could eat jewels with pleasure.

He surveyed the jars.

Of course he had been told not to touch anything. But the command had plainly been intended for an ordinary boy. A shopkeeper, on the other hand, had to test his stock. It would be dishonest to sell stale sweets.

He removed one Cokernut Kiss and ate it.

It was fresh.

He tested a Pineapple Crisp. That also was fresh.

The Nutty Footballs presented a difficulty. One might be fresh while another was stale. He tested three.

Then he remembered his resolution. It was New Year’s Day, and he had undertaken to be polite. It was surely impolite to accept Mr. Moss’s hospitality grudgingly. He ate a Chocolate Soldier out of gratitude.

The door-bell rang.

A thin woman entered. She had a long nose, a stiff hat and a shopping-bag which she placed upon the counter as though bringing evidence before a court.

“Where is Mr. Moss?” she demanded.

“He’s out.”

“Who are you?”

“I’m mindin’ the shop.”

“You?”

“Yes, thank you very much.”

The thin woman looked him over.

“I wish to purchase two ounces of violet creams.”

William found the jar.

“How much are they?” she asked.

He had no idea. He looked at the label, but the price was written in Mr. Moss’s private hand, which resembled a line of ants after an accident.

“Sixpence an ounce,” he said.

The woman gave a cry.

“Nonsense! I bought them yesterday at threepence.”

“They’ve gone up.”

“Since yesterday?”

“Yes. New Year prices.”

“There is no such thing.”

“There is here.”

“I shall not pay it.”

“Quite all right,” said William. “You don’t have to have ’em.”

The woman stiffened.

“I have always had two ounces of violet creams on New Year’s Day.”

“Well, you can have ’em now. They’re only sixpence an ounce.”

“They are marked threepence.”

William leaned over the label.

“That says sixpence.”

“It distinctly says threepence.”

“It’s upside down,” said William.

The woman turned the jar round.

“Now it says threepence.”

“Yes, but now it’s the wrong way round.”

“This is outrageous.”

William remembered his resolution.

“You’re a very disagreeable woman, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

The thin woman opened her mouth. No sound came from it at first.

“I shall report you to Mr. Moss.”

“Quite all right. Don’t menshun it.”

She swept out, leaving her shopping-bag behind. William placed it under the counter. He did not call her back because, after all, she had been disagreeable.

He ate another Pineapple Crisp to restore himself.

The bell rang again.

A little girl entered.

She wore a white coat with white fur at the neck, white stockings and shining black shoes. Golden curls escaped from beneath a white woollen cap. Her eyes were blue. Her voice, when she spoke, was silvery.

William, who had been attempting to put an entire Nutty Football into his mouth, removed it hurriedly.

“Please,” she said, “may I have a pennyworth of lemon drops?”

William gazed at her.

In one instant he became a different being. He was no longer William, temporary custodian of a sweet-shop with crumbs on his jersey. He was a prince, a knight, a rescuer of maidens from burning castles and swollen rivers. He saw himself carrying the little girl through flames, fighting wolves on a mountainside, and returning her lost white kitten while trumpets sounded.

“What’s your name?” he asked.

“Lucinda.”

William had never heard a more beautiful name.

“I’m William.”

She waited.

“Do you own this shop?”

William hesitated for only a fraction of a second.

“I’m runnin’ it.”

“Oh.”

“You can have anythin’ you like.”

“I only have a penny.”

“You don’t need money,” said William magnificently. “Not here.”

He took a large paper bag and began to fill it. Lemon drops went in first, then Cokernut Kisses, Rose Rock, Chocolate Soldiers, Mixed Dew Drops, peppermint sticks and several sugar mice.

Lucinda watched anxiously.

“I don’t think Mummy would like me to have so many.”

“They’re good for you.”

“Mummy says sweets are bad for teeth.”

“Only cheap sweets,” explained William. “These are special ones. They make teeth stronger.”

He added a slab of toffee.

“Will they really?”

“Yes. They’re what dentists eat.”

Lucinda accepted the bag.

“Thank you very much.”

William flushed beneath his freckles.

“Quite all right,” he murmured. “Don’t menshun it.”

She smiled at him and went out.

For a long moment William stood entranced. Then he put the rejected Nutty Football back into his mouth and resumed the duties of commerce.

Mrs. Brown did not come.

Eleven o’clock passed, then half-past eleven. William began to feel that he had always kept a shop and always would. He arranged three jars according to colour, sampled the Almond Cannons, and made a notice:

NEW YEAR SALE  
EVERYTHINK CHEEP  
SOME THINGS FREE TO PEOPLE I LIKE

He placed it in the window.

Soon Ted and Peter appeared. They had been wandering about in search of William and were arrested by the sight of him behind the counter.

“Who gave you the shop?” said Ted.

“Mr. Moss.”

“What for?”

“’Cause he trusts me.”

“Can you eat the sweets?”

“Course. Shopkeepers always eat their own sweets.”

Peter entered cautiously.

“Won’t he mind?”

“No. I’m helpin’ him. He said to look after everythin’.”

“What can we have?”

William waved his hand.

“Anything you want, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

Ted seized the Cokernut Kisses. Peter chose Chocolate Soldiers. Sam, arriving a few minutes later, devoted himself to Acid Drops. The shop became filled with the sounds of paper bags crackling, jar lids knocking and jaws working.

They played at shopkeeping.

Ted came in as a duke and bought ten pounds of liquorice bootlaces to feed his racehorses. Peter was an explorer fitting out an expedition with peppermint sticks. Sam became a pirate and stole the till, which, fortunately, contained only fourpence and a tram ticket.

William, as proprietor, served them all with pomp.

“Certainly, sir.”

“Thank you very much, sir.”

“Quite all right, sir.”

“You’re a fat-headed idiot, sir, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

It was while Peter was trying to balance a sugar mouse on the end of his nose that Hubert Lane entered.

Hubert was fourteen, large, smooth-haired and unpleasant. He lived in the next road and considered it his duty to remind all smaller boys of his age and size. Behind him stood two companions of corresponding character, though lesser magnificence.

“What’s going on?” Hubert said.

“Nothin’,” said William.

“You’ve been giving away Moss’s sweets.”

“No, I haven’t.”

Hubert pointed to Ted’s bulging pockets.

“He bought ’em.”

“How much?”

“A pound.”

Ted nodded earnestly.

“I paid a pound.”

“You haven’t got a pound.”

“I’m goin’ to pay him later.”

Hubert smiled.

“I’m going to tell Moss.”

William came round the counter.

“You’d better not.”

“Who’ll stop me?”

“Me.”

Hubert laughed.

William’s resolution stirred faintly within him.

“You’re a sneak, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so.”

“And you’re a thief.”

“I’m not. Mr. Moss put me in charge.”

“So you ate everything?”

“We had to test it.”

Hubert picked up an Acid Drop and put it into his mouth.

“Put that back,” ordered William.

Hubert sucked it thoughtfully.

“Put it back.”

“All right.”

He removed the sweet from his mouth and held it out.

William recoiled.

“I don’t want it now.”

“You told me to put it back.”

Hubert dropped it into the jar.

There are moments when diplomacy ceases to be a virtue. William knew this instinctively. He seized the Acid Drop, now possessing qualities beyond those intended by its manufacturer, and threw it at Hubert.

It struck Hubert on the cheek.

Hubert seized a handful from the jar and flung them.

The battle began.

At first it was confined to the shop. William, Ted, Peter and Sam took cover behind the counter and replied with Lemon Fizzers. Hubert and his allies overturned a display-box and bombarded them with Nutty Footballs. A jar of Mixed Dew Drops fell, rolled across the floor and opened beneath the feet of the combatants. The effect was immediate and spectacular.

Hubert slid into a chair. Peter sat down suddenly in a box of toffee. Ted, attempting to retreat, caught his foot in the thin woman’s shopping-bag and brought down a festoon of peppermint sticks.

William fought on.

He stood behind the weighing-machine, hair wild, face scarlet, eyes blazing, hurling Acid Drops with the grave concentration of an artillery officer defending the last fortress of civilization.

“Give in!” shouted Hubert.

“Never!”

A Pineapple Crisp struck William on the forehead. He responded with a Chocolate Soldier at close range.

Hubert retreated into the street.

William followed.

So did the others.

The battle spread along the pavement. Passers-by stopped. A small dog ate enthusiastically from the gutter. Two little boys joined William’s side without understanding the cause. A delivery boy joined Hubert’s because Hubert promised him a packet of liquorice.

The air was thick with confectionery.

Acid Drops rattled against windows. Cokernut Kisses burst whitely on the road. Sugar mice flew through space with their liquorice tails streaming behind them. Rose Rock shattered upon the kerb. A peppermint stick struck a lamp-post and broke in half with the clean, noble sound of a sword.

William fought a noble fight against heavy odds. At least, that was how he saw it. He did not see a small freckled boy, sticky from head to foot, using another man’s stock to pelt three older boys. He saw a leader rallying his faithful band while enemies closed in on every side.

“More ammunition!” he cried.

Ted ran into the shop and returned with Almond Cannons.

They charged.

Hubert’s forces wavered. One Almond Cannon entered Hubert’s collar. Another struck his companion on the nose. The delivery boy deserted.

At that moment a figure appeared at the end of the street.

It was Mr. Moss.

William saw him and stopped.

The battle stopped.

Hubert Lane, remembering an appointment elsewhere, disappeared with his friends. Ted, Peter and Sam melted into the alley beside the butcher’s shop. The two little boys went on eating from the gutter as though they had never taken part in warfare.

William stood alone before the wrecked shop.

The window display had collapsed. The floor was carpeted with broken sweets, paper bags and fragments of glass. A long pink ribbon of toffee hung from the door handle. One jar lay on its side in the road. The notice in the window now read:

NEW YEAR  
EVERYTHINK  
FREE

Mr. Moss approached.

He wore no hat. His green tie had risen over one shoulder. His moustache still drooped, but behind it was a smile so vast and radiant that it transformed his whole mournful person.

“She said yes!” he shouted.

William stared.

“She said yes, William! After ten years, she said yes!”

He seized William’s hand and shook it.

“Well,” said William faintly, “that’s good.”

“I missed the ten-forty. Had to catch the eleven-fifteen. Thought I’d missed ’er. Found ’er at the station. I said, ‘Marjorie, will you marry me?’ She said, ‘Yes, Alfred, I think I’d better.’ Just like that!”

He laughed aloud. It was an extraordinary sound from Mr. Moss. Several people turned to look.

Then he saw the shop.

He looked at the broken window display, the overturned jars, the Acid Drops in the road, the toffee on the door handle and William’s sugar-coated hair.

William drew breath.

“There was a customer,” he began, “an’ she was very rude, if you don’t mind me sayin’ so, an’ then a girl came an’ you’d’ve wanted me to give her some things, an’ then Ted and Peter only had a few, an’ Hubert stole an Acid Drop, an’ when I told him p’litely to put it back he did, but it was wet, an’ then he started the fight—”

Mr. Moss laughed again.

“Quite all right,” he said.

William stopped.

“What?”

“Quite all right. Don’t matter. Nothin’ matters today.”

He looked round his ruined kingdom with the benevolent indifference of an emperor who has just acquired another continent.

“Stock was insured,” he said vaguely. “Some of it. P’raps. Never mind. She said yes.”

He patted William on the shoulder.

“Thank you for mindin’ the shop.”

William swallowed.

“Quite all right,” he said. “Don’t menshun it.”

Mr. Moss entered the shop, still smiling.

William walked away.

At the corner he met Ted, Peter and Sam.

“What did he do?” whispered Ted.

“Nothin’.”

“Didn’t he hit you?”

“No.”

“Didn’t he say he’d tell your father?”

“No. He thanked me.”

The three looked at William with admiration. His mysterious influence over adults had long been acknowledged, but this exceeded even their expectations.

“What shall we do now?” said Peter.

William leaned against the wall. The street moved gently beneath him. His face had become pale beneath the freckles. Within him Cokernut Kisses, Pineapple Crisp, Nutty Footballs, Chocolate Soldiers, Almond Cannons, Rose Rock and Mixed Dew Drops seemed to be holding a battle of their own, conducted without discipline and in total darkness.

“I’m goin’ home,” he said.

“What about your polite resolution?” asked Sam.

William considered it.

Politeness had brought him suspicion at breakfast, an argument with a customer, an acquaintance with Lucinda, command of a sweet-shop, a battle with Hubert Lane and the complete destruction of Mr. Moss’s stock. It had had a fair trial.

“I’ve done it half a day,” he said. “Half a day’s enough for a whole year.”

He set off homeward with slow and careful steps.

At lunch his mother looked at him anxiously.

“You’re very pale, dear. Aren’t you hungry?”

“No.”

“Would you like some roast beef?”

William shuddered.

“Potatoes?”

He closed his eyes.

“Treacle pudding?”

William rose abruptly and left the room.

His father watched the door close.

“I knew that politeness meant trouble,” he said.

And perhaps William had been greedy, dishonest, reckless and destructive. Perhaps his courtesy had been no more than a string of phrases tied, like bright labels, to conduct of quite another kind. But had he not kept a lonely shop, served the public, encouraged friendship, defended property, rewarded beauty and assisted—however indirectly—at the happy ending of a ten-year courtship?

Could any reasonable person demand more of a New Year’s resolution?

I think not.

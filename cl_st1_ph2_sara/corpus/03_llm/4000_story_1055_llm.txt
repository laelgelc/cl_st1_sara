## THE GREAT PANTOMIME OF LIFE

We have frequently heard it asserted, by persons whose gravity of countenance was only equalled by the extreme lightness of their conclusions, that pantomimes are unnatural; that their incidents are preposterous, their characters impossible, and their transformations such as no rational being could witness without a protest. We differ from these excellent persons. We maintain—respectfully, but firmly—that pantomime is not merely an imitation of life, but life itself, judiciously shortened, brightly painted, and accompanied by music.

Take the commonest incident of the stage. A stout gentleman, with a broad red cheek, a capacious waistcoat, a heavy gold chain, a cane with a knob as large as a door-handle, and an air of general ownership in the world, advances along the scene. He is prosperous; therefore he is offensive. He is comfortable; therefore the audience mistrust him. He has money; consequently every boy in the gallery feels himself personally wronged.

The clown creeps behind him, ties his coat-tails to a post, places a board across his path, or removes the chair upon which he is preparing to sit. The prosperous gentleman tumbles. His hat rolls one way, his wig another, his cane a third. Before he can recover his breath, the clown has abstracted his purse, eaten his luncheon, smashed his spectacles, and presented him, with many low bows, with a brick wrapped in his own pocket-handkerchief.

How the audience roar!

Now, why do they roar? Not because a man has fallen, for many men fall daily without awakening the smallest interest; not because property has been stolen, for the respectable portion of the audience would strongly condemn such a proceeding if performed upon themselves; not because spectacles are naturally comic, for spectacles, considered abstractedly, are instruments of benevolent science. They laugh because a prosperous person has been suddenly reduced to the level of ordinary mortality. His red cheek, his watch-chain, his self-possession, and his consciousness of five per cent have all come down together. The clown has achieved, in thirty seconds, that redistribution of fortune which philosophers discuss through thirty volumes and governments postpone through thirty sessions.

Is this unnatural? Walk into the street to-morrow morning. Let a merchant fail who yesterday was reputed rich; let a banker stop payment; let a gentleman with a carriage be discovered to have no legal right to the horses; and observe whether society does not exhibit precisely the same benevolent hilarity. His friends shake their heads with a satisfaction imperfectly concealed; his enemies remember that they always suspected him; persons who never saw him declare that his manners were insufferable; and a lady whom he once neglected to salute communicates, in strict confidence, that his grandmother sold damaged candles in Whitechapel.

The clown has only done openly, and with a red-hot poker, what the world performs privately with a whisper.

Having established this point—and we request the reader to observe that we have established it beyond appeal—we proceed to the pantaloon.

The pantaloon is usually an old gentleman whose years have increased without any corresponding augmentation of his wisdom. His legs are weak, his affections youthful, his temper violent, and his discernment at the disposal of the first painted face that smiles upon him. He hobbles after Columbine; he shakes his cane at Harlequin; he receives boxes on the ear from the clown; he chuckles toothlessly at his own designs; and, whenever he has arrived at the moment when success appears inevitable, he is knocked into a flour-bin, folded in a blanket, or propelled head foremost through a window.

“The creature is absurd,” says a moral reader. “No man of advanced age could conduct himself in so ridiculous a manner.”

Could he not?

See that little brougham drawing up before a house in a fashionable square. Mark the old gentleman within it. His forehead is polished, his whiskers are dyed, his neckcloth is constructed with the rigidity of a public monument, and the hand which trembles upon his silver-headed cane glitters with rings sufficient to ransom a minor principality. He is sixty-eight by the register, forty-two by his conversation, and twenty-five by his intentions.

Observe how he descends; how he pauses to arrange the curl which has wandered from its adhesive moorings; how he smiles at the upper windows; how he pats the cheek of the maid-servant, who detests him; how he whispers to the footman, who despises him; and how, having gained the drawing-room, he seats himself beside a young widow whose attachment to him is exactly proportioned to her detestation of poverty.

The pantaloon to the life!

He gives suppers which he cannot digest, dances quadrilles which he cannot survive, writes verses which nobody can understand, and challenges young men whom he has no desire to meet. His doctor advises quiet; his solicitor recommends prudence; his grown-up daughters implore dignity; but Columbine smiles, and medicine, law, filial affection, gout, rheumatism, and arithmetic are scattered together like the fragments of a pasteboard cottage.

Presently the widow marries Harlequin, the footman publishes the old gentleman’s letters, the servants laugh in the hall, and the pantaloon is left to hobble home in the rain. Yet people complain that the theatre exaggerates!

The mention of Harlequin naturally conducts us to another branch of our subject; though we will frankly own that, on a more mature consideration, the clown possesses stronger claims upon our immediate attention. Harlequin, with his spangled dress, his agile limbs, his mask, his silence, and his magic wand, has at least the grace to be mysterious. The clown is brazen, noisy, hungry, impudent, destructive, and successful. His cry is “Here we are!”—a phrase which, in real life, may be translated, “Here is your property, and here am I to obtain it.”

There are clowns in every profession. Some wear wigs, some epaulettes, some white neckcloths, some rings upon every finger, and some such very plain coats that their honesty becomes positively alarming. They enter by one door, remove the furniture, and depart by another. They borrow with pathos, promise with dignity, spend with animation, and repay with silence. If pursued, they tumble through a trap; if questioned, they turn a somersault; if exposed, they grin; and if imprisoned, they complain of the cruelty of society.

Let us take a slight example.

Some few years since there appeared in the western quarter of London a person who called himself Captain Fitz-Whisker Fiercy. Whether he had ever commanded anything more military than a dinner, or ever faced anything more formidable than a tailor’s account, we cannot undertake to determine. He had moustaches of a cavalry magnitude, a frogged coat, spurs which announced him three streets before he arrived, and an eye-glass through which he surveyed tradesmen as though they were recruits awaiting inspection.

He was accompanied by a confidential servant named Do’em—a short, smooth, pale individual, whose eyes were always lowered and whose hands were always occupied; so that, while one could never accuse him of looking covetously at any article, it was remarkable how frequently the article disappeared.

The captain took a handsome house. He did not pay for it; but this circumstance, far from diminishing the splendour of his occupation, gave it an airy and aristocratic character. A gentleman who pays ready money may be rich, but he has no imagination. Captain Fiercy possessed imagination in abundance.

He summoned an upholsterer.

“My good fellow,” said the captain, passing his fingers through his moustaches, “furnish the place. Nothing gaudy. Simplicity—military simplicity. Rosewood, satin, mirrors, bronzes, and a few cabinets. You understand.”

The upholsterer did understand. He understood that the captain’s father had estates in the north, that an aunt had property in the west, that a cousin had influence at court, and that money, considered as an immediate object, was beneath the notice of the family. These particulars were communicated by Do’em, with downcast eyes, while assisting to measure the rooms.

The upholsterer furnished the house.

A wine merchant was next honoured with a command. The captain desired champagne, claret, hock, port, sherry, brandy, and certain foreign liqueurs whose names he pronounced with so much ease that the merchant felt ashamed of attaching importance to payment. A jeweller supplied rings, studs, seals, chains, and a bracelet intended, the captain explained with becoming delicacy, for a lady of rank. A tailor produced coats without number; a bootmaker armed the heroic feet; a coachmaker provided a cabriolet; a horse-dealer provided a pair of spirited bays; and a bookseller, having learned that the captain was literary, sent several yards of books selected chiefly for the richness of their binding.

Meanwhile Do’em moved softly among them all, opening doors, carrying messages, receiving parcels, signing papers, and dropping incidental allusions to Government House, the Guards, the Duke, the club, the family solicitor, and “the captain’s little temporary embarrassment respecting the remittance.”

For a fortnight the house blazed with light. Carriages drew up; music sounded; corks flew; young gentlemen came to borrow money from the captain, and went away having lent him some; tradesmen waited in the hall and were refreshed by glimpses of noble-looking coats descending the staircase; and the widow opposite, who had watched the preparations through a slit in her blind, informed all her acquaintances that Captain Fiercy was one of the finest men in Europe.

Then occurred THE CHANGE.

One morning a modest broker, acting upon instructions which he considered unnecessary to explain, entered the house and sold the furniture. The mirrors, the rosewood, the satin, the bronzes, and the military simplicity were knocked down under the hammer. The wine went to a tavern-keeper; the jewels to a dealer; the cabriolet to a gentleman in the bill-discounting line; the spirited bays to anybody who would take them; and the books, still uncut and sparkling with ancestral learning, to a decorator of drawing-room tables.

“Extraordinary!” cried the upholsterer.

“Infamous!” said the wine merchant.

“Who could have imagined it?” exclaimed the jeweller.

We could; but we were not consulted.

The tradesmen hurried to the captain’s residence. They found the rooms bare, the windows open, and a solitary kitchen chair placed in the centre of the drawing-room. Upon that chair sat Do’em, smoking a cigar and reading the newspaper.

“Where is the captain?” they demanded.

Do’em pointed with his thumb towards the back of the house.

The tradesmen rushed thither just in time to behold Captain Fitz-Whisker Fiercy, divested of his frogged coat but still faithful to his moustaches, descending over a wall by means of a rope composed of sheets. They pursued him through an alley, upset a costermonger, collided with a baker, scattered three children and a basket of turnips, and finally secured the captain beneath the shafts of a laundry cart.

Do’em, having finished the paragraph before him, walked quietly out by the front door and was arrested at the corner.

There was the clown caught at last! There was the pantaloon cheated, the scenery removed, the banquet vanished, the music stopped, and the performers conveyed in a vehicle not ordinarily associated with dramatic triumph. The captain bowed to the crowd; Do’em winked at a butcher’s boy; the tradesmen declared that justice had been vindicated; and every spectator, forgetting the credit he had foolishly given in his own private affairs, laughed heartily at the credulity of the rest.

We now approach the supernumerary—a person of less glittering pretensions, but of vast social importance.

On the stage, the supernumerary carries a banner, guards a gate, swells a procession, forms one of a crowd, or stands with a broad stolid simper and a dull leaden eye while events of the most astounding nature occur within six feet of him. A fairy rises from the earth; he does not move. A palace changes to a cavern; he remains composed. A demon appears in flame; he adjusts his belt. Everybody rushes off to the right; he rushes with them. Everybody returns to the left; he returns. His chief qualifications are obedience, appetite, and the absence of individual opinion.

Life abounds with these useful creatures.

They fill doorways, applaud speakers, sign addresses, attend dinners, repeat rumours, obstruct pavements, and ask whether you have heard the news. They know nothing, but know it in large numbers. They have faces which suggest that Nature began a thought and was interrupted. Their laugh is always ready before the joke; their indignation waits only for the name of the offender; their principles are delivered with the morning paper and removed with the waste.

Take the man who stands at the corner of a street whenever a crowd has collected. Ask him what has happened.

“Something dreadful,” he replies.

“What?”

He does not know.

“Who did it?”

He cannot say.

“Is anybody hurt?”

He believes so.

“Whom?”

He has not heard.

Yet he remains; and if another person arrives, he immediately assumes authority, shakes his head, and says, “Bad business, sir—very bad business!” Thus does a supernumerary become, by the mere accession of a later fool, a principal authority.

But a new branch of our subject has flashed suddenly upon us with a brilliance that almost compels us to shade our eyes. We allude to politics.

If ordinary life be a pantomime, Parliament is the grand holiday performance; the theatre enlarged, the company doubled, the scenery paid for by the public, and the tricks executed upon a scale of national magnificence.

Enter the House. Behold the benches, the lights, the papers, the restless figures, the whispered consultations, the formal entrances, the prolonged speeches which explain nothing, and the brief answers which answer less. See the minister rise amid cheers from gentlemen who yesterday considered him dangerous, and groans from gentlemen who yesterday considered him indispensable. Mark the solemnity with which one member walks out because he cannot support a measure, and the agility with which he walks in again when informed that his absence may endanger his seat.

Here is Harlequin with the wand of office. A touch—and the scene changes!

A member who has denounced taxation discovers that revenue is the foundation of civilization. Another, who once called a standing army the instrument of tyranny, explains that national honour demands six additional regiments. A third, who declared the proposed bill fatal to liberty when introduced by the opposite side, finds it moderate, necessary, and beneficent when entrusted to the management of his friends.

How is this effected? By argument? By conviction? By mature inquiry?

Bless the reader’s innocence! By the wand.

Harlequin touches the right honourable gentleman, and he sits down. He touches him again, and he rises. A third touch makes him speak for two hours; a fourth causes him to say precisely what he contradicted last week. If the wand be pointed towards the lobby, he votes; if towards the door, he disappears; if held above his head, he cheers; and if withdrawn altogether, he becomes a patriot.

Observe also the parliamentary clown—that merry functionary who throws ink over inconvenient documents, steals clauses from one bill and hides them in another, knocks down an opponent’s argument, and then asks why it is lying in the way. He produces figures from an empty box; balances the national accounts upon the end of his nose; borrows a million with one hand, spends two with the other, and proves, by turning the slate upside down, that the country has saved five.

“Here we are!” cries the clown.

And indeed here we are—paying for the admission.

The pantaloon is not absent. There he sits, venerable in years, irritable in temper, tenacious of place, and enamoured of every youthful scheme that flatters his importance. He has represented the same borough, opposed the same reforms, predicted the same ruin, and mistaken the same lobby for five-and-thirty years. He shakes his cane at innovation; innovation takes away his chair. He pursues popularity; popularity slips behind him and pins a placard to his coat. He attempts to embrace the future, and falls into the arms of the past.

The supernumeraries, meanwhile, occupy the background in admirable order. They cry “Hear!” without hearing, “No!” without knowing, and “Divide!” without understanding the question. One enters late, is told by a colleague that the Government is right, votes accordingly, and goes home with the consciousness of having discharged a constitutional duty. Another sleeps through the debate, wakes at the bell, and preserves the empire by following the coat-tails before him.

We do not say that these gentlemen are useless. Far from it. What would a pantomime be without a crowd? Who would fill the procession, carry the banners, surround the throne, run from imaginary danger, or kneel when the principal performer raised his hand? The supernumerary gives bulk to opinion, noise to argument, and numbers to wisdom. He is the plural of somebody else’s conviction.

The most beautiful portion of the political entertainment, however, is the transformation which ensues when office changes hands.

The curtain falls for a moment. There is a scuffling behind the scenes, a removal of boxes, a confusion of wigs, swords, papers, seals, and salaries. Presently the curtain rises. What a spectacle!

The late Harlequin is now pantaloon. The former clown has obtained the wand. Those who sat upon the right have crossed to the left; those who groaned now cheer; those who cheered now groan. The very measure which was yesterday a national safeguard has become a public calamity, while the proposal recently rejected as monstrous reappears with a new title and is welcomed as statesmanship.

“Shame!” cries the gentleman who called “Hear!” last Tuesday.

“Consistency!” exclaims the statesman who has just crossed the floor.

“Principle!” shout the supernumeraries.

The new Harlequin waves his wand. His supporters become silent upon matters concerning which they were formerly eloquent; his opponents become eloquent upon matters concerning which they were formerly silent. One gentleman is transformed into an ambassador; another into a peer; a third into a chairman; a fourth, who has waited twenty years for recognition, into a disappointed man. Doors fly open, salaries descend, promises vanish through traps, and principles, reduced to convenient parcels, are carried off by attendants.

Is not this pantomime? There are the changes, the tumbles, the disguises, the pursuits, the cries, the blows, the sudden appearances, and the still more sudden disappearances. There is even the moral at the end—though, like many morals, it is generally postponed until the audience has gone home.

And the audience? Why, the audience is ourselves.

We sit before the stage and condemn the performers, precisely as the gallery condemns the clown until he steals the gallery’s orange. We laugh when the rich man falls, until our own chair is removed. We applaud the exposure of credulity, after entrusting our savings to Captain Fiercy. We despise the supernumerary, while repeating what our newspaper has instructed us to think. We ridicule the pantaloon, and dye our whiskers. We denounce Harlequin’s wand, and bow to it the instant it is flourished in our direction.

Perhaps some grave reader will close these pages with the observation that our comparison is fanciful. We entreat him to reconsider. Let him look abroad with an unprejudiced eye. Let him watch the markets, the clubs, the courts, the drawing-rooms, the elections, and the pavements. Let him note how costume passes for character, noise for intelligence, credit for wealth, office for wisdom, age for virtue, and impudence for success. Let him count the masks, observe the wires, listen for the prompter, and mark the trapdoors.

He will discover that the difference between the stage and the world is chiefly this: upon the stage the machinery is occasionally visible, the clown admits that he is a clown, the wand is openly carried, and the spectators know that they have paid for a pantomime.

In life the machinery is concealed, the clown prints a card describing himself as a gentleman, the wand calls itself duty, and the spectators—poor, excellent, indignant spectators!—pay twice: once for their seats, and once for the damage done to them during the performance.

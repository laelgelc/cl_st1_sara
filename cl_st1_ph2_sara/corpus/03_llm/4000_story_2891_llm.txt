# POOR MAN’S PUDDING AND RICH MAN’S CRUMBS

## I. POOR MAN’S MANURE

There are men whose benevolence expands in exact proportion to their distance from its object. My friend Blandmour was one of these. He loved mankind as a constellation, but was sometimes perplexed by the individual stars; and though the sufferings of a hemisphere could draw from him a sigh as sonorous as a church-organ, the toothache of his washerwoman seemed to him an instance of defective fortitude.

Yet he was no hypocrite. His heart was tender, his purse not habitually shut, and his conversation enriched by every amiable maxim which can be entertained beside a good fire. Poverty, according to Blandmour, was a severe but affectionate schoolmistress; Nature, a universal provider; Providence, an economist who wasted nothing; and the poor, if they would but read the lessons laid under their noses, the special pensioners of Heaven.

One February morning we walked together along a country road lately buried by snow. A thaw had begun. The white banks leaned heavily into the ditches; black fences appeared in broken lines, like sentences imperfectly erased; and every step of the horses in the road crushed the snowy crust into a brown and freezing paste.

“How beautiful,” said Blandmour, “is the economy of Nature! Observe that snow.”

“I have observed little else for the last mile,” I replied, lifting one foot, whose boot had admitted a cold argument against the season.

“You regard only its inconvenience. I regard its mission. The Psalmist calls it wool. What a beneficent image! It wraps the earth, preserves the seed, enriches the soil, and in due time descends, fertilizingly moistened, to the roots. Snow, my dear fellow, is the poor man’s manure.”

At that instant a gust swept across the open fields, lifting a shining powder from the acres of William Coulter, a tenant farmer, and depositing it with great impartiality behind the stone wall of Squire Rye’s park.

“Nature’s distribution appears a little irregular,” said I. “Coulter’s manure is blowing upon the Squire’s land.”

Blandmour smiled as one smiles at the vivacity of a child.

“You fasten upon accidents. The general intention is beneficent.”

“The poor man, then, has the intention, and the rich man the manure?”

“Only for the moment. Besides, snow enriches all. It descends upon palace and hovel.”

“But not always through the palace roof.”

He waved away the distinction. Blandmour’s philosophy was a well-furnished apartment, into which no draught from the facts was willingly admitted.

Presently we passed a small field where several men were cutting brushwood. Among them was Coulter himself, a tall, stooping fellow of perhaps thirty-five, though labour, exposure, and anxiety had written upon him the doubtful age of fifty. His trousers were tied below the knees with twine; one boot was split, and bound round with a strip of sacking; his coat had been repaired so frequently that every patch appeared to apologise for the antiquity of the cloth beneath it.

He took off his cap when he saw us.

“Morning, gentlemen.”

“A fortunate thaw, Coulter,” cried Blandmour. “It will carry nourishment into your fields.”

“If it carries some out o’ my boots first, sir, I’ll thank it,” said Coulter, with a modest laugh.

“You see?” Blandmour whispered after we had passed. “Cheerfulness! The natural gaiety of an uncorrupted mind.”

I looked back. Coulter had resumed his labour under the eye of Squire Rye’s steward, who stood dry-shod upon a cart-plank and called to the men whenever their chilled hands slackened.

“Does he cut his own wood?” I asked.

“The Squire’s, I believe.”

“And his own fuel?”

“He gathers what fallen sticks are allowed him.”

“Then Nature has provided him manure in the Squire’s park, and firewood under the Squire’s trees. She is indeed generous, though remarkably attentive to legal boundaries.”

“My dear sir,” said Blandmour, “you have the melancholy habit of reducing poetry to accounts.”

“And you, perhaps, of putting accounts into poetry.”

He laughed good-humouredly, for he regarded my scepticism as a constitutional cloud which the sunshine of his convictions must ultimately disperse.

We came, before noon, to his pleasant house, where a fire of maple logs received us with that strong, hospitable roar which says more for the comforts of civilisation than many discourses upon primitive virtue. While we waited for luncheon, Blandmour continued his catalogue of Nature’s benefactions.

“Take the potato,” said he. “The poor man’s egg! Nutritious, wholesome, easily raised, and needing only a little salt.”

“And a little ground,” I said.

“Ground may be hired.”

“With money?”

“With labour.”

“Which is already hired by someone else.”

He frowned slightly, not from anger, but from the discomfort of finding a stair where his theory promised level ground.

“Still, the potato remains a provision. And rice—consider rice! What an innocent grain, what an inexhaustible friend of the humble household! A little rice, a little milk, a little sugar, and you have a pudding fit for a prince.”

“Or for the poor man?”

“Especially for the poor man. It is his pudding.”

The luncheon bell interrupted us. We sat down to roast beef, warm bread, butter, preserves, and a rice pudding finished with eggs, cream, nutmeg, and a golden skin which crackled delicately under the spoon.

Blandmour pointed to it in triumph.

“There! The poor man’s pudding.”

I tasted it.

“He has done himself very well.”

“You mock; but simplicity is the mother of contentment.”

Perhaps the pudding, or the fire, or the wine which followed it, softened my opposition; perhaps, too, I had been unfair to a charitable nature merely because its charity took a rhetorical form. Yet the phrase clung to me. Poor Man’s Manure; Poor Man’s Egg; Poor Man’s Pudding. They arranged themselves in my mind like labels in a benevolent museum, beneath which actual want might be preserved without offending the visitor.

Within a week I determined to see the Coulters.

## II. THE HOUSE IN THE THAW

Their dwelling stood on one of Squire Rye’s outlying properties, at the end of a lane which began as mud, proceeded as water, and concluded in a mixture having the disadvantages of both. The thaw had now completed the work begun that morning with Blandmour. Snow dripped from every branch; the fields lay soaked and colourless; and the road, deeply grooved by timber-carts, seemed less a road than a long wound in the earth.

I had chosen to walk, partly from scruple at displaying a horse before persons whose whole estate might not have purchased it, and partly from a foolish notion that hardship may be approached more delicately on foot. Before I had gone two miles, the water in my boots corrected this sentiment. Privation borrowed for an afternoon is apt to seem adventurous; privation possessed for life has no such flavour.

The house was lower than the road. This geographical circumstance appeared small, but the weather made a tyrant of it. Every melted bank drained towards the dwelling. Water stood under the sill; the foundation stones sweated; green stains climbed the outer boards; and the roof, patched with shingles, bark, and some pieces of flattened tin, shone with innumerable leaks.

A dog without energy raised its head beneath a cart and let it fall again.

I knocked. A woman’s voice asked who was there; then the door opened.

Martha Coulter was younger than I expected. She had a broad, pale forehead, soft brown eyes, and hair drawn plainly back. Her dress, though much worn, was clean where cleanliness had been possible. She was far advanced in pregnancy, and one hand remained pressed against her side while she greeted me.

“You’ll be the gentleman Will spoke of.”

“I fear he spoke too kindly.”

“He said you passed him with Mr. Blandmour. Step in, sir. ’Tain’t a day to keep a Christian on the sill.”

The room received me with a breath compounded of wet wood, smoke, sour cloth, and something cooking whose nature I could not immediately determine. There was no entry passage. The outer damp entered directly into the family life, spreading across the boards in blackened patches and settling upon the walls, the ceiling, the panes, the chairs—damp, damp, damp. The house seemed not accidentally wet, but constitutionally damp, as though moisture were in its ancestry and mildew its inevitable heir.

A small fire struggled on the hearth. The sticks were mostly moss-grown, and instead of burning they hissed, spat, smoked, and exuded water, like old complainants who had many grievances but no warmth. Martha pushed one deeper into the coals.

“Will gathered ’em after dark,” she said. “The steward don’t like us taking any that ain’t down, though some’s so nigh down they only need looking at.”

“Does your husband return at noon?”

“If the Squire’s work lets him. He’s clearing the north lot. It’s a long pull through the mire.”

She gave me the best chair. Its enfeebled constitution protested in every joint, and I sat carefully, preserving at once my dignity and its life. The other furniture consisted of a deal table, two stools, a shelf of chipped crockery, and a narrow bed partly screened by a faded quilt. On the wall hung a little print of a bright city with gold towers, above which an angel flew through clouds of an astonishing cleanliness.

Martha noticed my glance.

“That’s the New Jerusalem. My sister sent it. It don’t spot so much as you’d think, being paper.”

On a peg near the bed hung two tiny garments.

The question which rose to my lips fell back. There are inquiries which curiosity may call innocent, but sorrow recognises as trespassers before they knock.

“You will dine with us,” said Martha.

“I could not deprive you—”

“Will would take it unneighbourly if you went hungry from his house. There’s pork, and I’ve made a rice pudding.”

The words startled me by their coincidence.

“A rice pudding?”

“Yes, sir. It ain’t such as rich folks make, but it fills. Rice is poor man’s pudding, they say.”

There it was—Blandmour’s phrase, stripped of its napkin and silver spoon, standing in smoke beside the wet hearth.

William arrived half an hour later, bringing half the lane upon his boots. He stopped outside to scrape them, though the floor within could scarcely have been worsened. His face was raw with cold, his hands swollen and scored, and one shoulder of his coat darkened where he had carried wet timber.

“Well, now!” he cried on seeing me. “Martha, you’ve got company fit to shame the house.”

“The house ain’t ashamed,” she said. “Nor me.”

“No more it ought,” he answered quickly, kissing her forehead. Then he shook my hand with grave welcome. “You found us low in the hollow.”

“Geographically,” said I.

“And otherwise, some reckon.” He smiled. “But a man may stand straight in a low place.”

It was not boastfully spoken. In truth, he stooped even while saying it, and I thought there was more dignity in that bent labourer than in many an erect gentleman whose spine had never carried anything heavier than an opinion.

Martha set the meal. The pork lay in a cracked dish, cut into thick pieces. Its colour was doubtful, and an oily rankness rose from it in the heat. William saw me look at it.

“Barrel turned on us,” he said. “The frost went out sudden.”

“It’s good enough fried hard,” Martha added. “The bad’s mostly in the smell.”

I wished to honour their hospitality. I cut a small piece and carried it to my mouth; but the taste, stronger even than the odour, brought an involuntary resistance of the body against the resolutions of the mind. I swallowed with difficulty.

William ate steadily. Martha scarcely ate at all.

“You should take more,” he told her.

“I’ve had some cooking.”

“That ain’t eating.”

“The little one’s whimsical today.”

His face softened. “Whimseys already, has it? Give it a bite o’ potato, then. Poor man’s egg.”

He split the best potato and laid half upon her plate.

The phrase again! But in Blandmour’s dining-room it had sparkled like a polished motto; here it divided one small potato between a labourer and his pregnant wife.

William spoke cheerfully of his work. The Squire was extending a stone wall, draining a meadow, cutting a road, clearing the north lot. Every improvement, to hear Blandmour or the local newspaper, was a blessing to the district; but the blessing seemed to reach Coulter principally in the form of twelve hours’ labour, irregular wages, wet clothes, and rheumatism.

“How is your back?” Martha asked.

“Easy enough when I don’t move.”

“You was groaning last night.”

“Dreaming, maybe.”

“You don’t groan in dreams.”

“How do you know? You’re asleep.”

“I ain’t.”

The simple answer produced a silence. William looked at her, then at the hanging garments by the bed.

“You ought to sleep,” he said gently.

Martha turned to the hearth.

When the table had been cleared of the pork, she brought out the pudding. It occupied a shallow earthen dish, and its surface was grey rather than white. Some grains of rice had burst; others remained hard, showing brown specks at their ends. There had been no milk. Water, a spoonful of molasses, and perhaps some fat supplied its place.

William rubbed his hands.

“Now, sir, there’s Martha’s masterpiece.”

She smiled, but anxiously. “The rice got damp at the store. Mr. Keel sold it cheap. I picked the worst out.”

“And sweetened it,” said William.

“A mite.”

She served me first. The pudding trembled upon the spoon with a leaden reluctance. I tasted it.

A flavour of mould came first; after it, unexpectedly, the sharp stale taste of brine.

Martha saw my face.

“It caught from the pork barrel,” she said. “The sack stood too nigh. I washed it three times.”

“It is—unusual,” I managed.

“Tell truth,” said William. “We ain’t children.”

“I fear the rice is injured.”

“We know that,” Martha replied, not offended. “Mr. Keel knew it too. That’s why we could buy it.”

William took a large mouthful. “It fills,” he said.

Yes; it filled. A stone fills a pocket, smoke fills a room, grief fills a life. Blandmour had not been wrong in the verb—only in all that he supposed it meant.

I laid down my spoon.

Martha tried to laugh. “Gentlefolk ain’t used to poor man’s pudding.”

There was no accusation in her voice, which made the accusation complete. My broadcloth coat steamed by the fire; my gloves, though wet, were sound; money enough for a dozen wholesome dinners lay in my pocket; and within an hour, if I chose, I might rise, cross the lane, gain the road, and return to a chamber where dry sheets, clear glass, and seasoned fuel awaited me. Their discomfort enclosed them. Mine was a visit.

William, perhaps perceiving my shame, turned the talk.

“Mr. Blandmour’s a learned man. He says snow does the land good.”

“So he tells me.”

“I hope it does somebody. Most of mine went over the ridge. Squire’s wall caught it beautiful.”

I laughed despite myself; and Martha, seeing that William meant humour, laughed too. For a moment the room warmed—not from the fire, but from their affection, which passed between them with the quiet economy of people who have little else to exchange.

William soon rose to return to work.

“So soon?” I asked.

“The steward docks an hour if a man gives him fifty minutes.”

“But your clothes are soaked.”

“They’ll dry on me when July comes.”

Martha fetched a strip of cloth and wrapped his wrist, where the skin had split.

“Don’t lift with this hand.”

“I’ll ask the logs to be considerate.”

“And don’t let Briggs hurry you.”

“The Squire’s trees have been growing eighty years. Strange they must all come down this afternoon.”

He kissed her again, took his cap, and went out into the drizzling day. Through the clouded pane we watched him climb the lane, slipping twice but never falling.

## III. MARTHA’S WEATHER

After William left, the room became very still. Not silent: the roof ticked; water dripped into a pan; the sticks hissed; somewhere under the boards a slow trickle moved with secret perseverance. Yet these small sounds only measured the stillness, as a clock measures an empty house.

Martha gathered the dishes.

“You needn’t,” I said. “Sit down. You are tired.”

“A body gets used to tired.”

This common phrase is often taken as evidence that hardship grows light by custom. The truth is less consoling. A burden may cease to surprise the shoulder without ceasing to bruise it.

She sat near the hearth, one hand on her abdomen. The child moved; I saw the cloth stir.

“Strong,” she murmured.

“You have other children?” I asked, before remembering the little garments.

Her eyes went to the peg.

“Had two.”

I could not recall the question. It stood between us, coarse and fully dressed, though I would have withdrawn it naked if I could.

“The boy was first,” she continued. “Daniel. He’d be six come May. Such hair! Will said it was the colour of corn-silk, but I told him no corn ever grew so fine. He took the fever in August.”

“I am sorry.”

“Yes, sir.”

No ceremonial language could enter beyond that “Yes.” She folded her hands.

“The doctor was away at the Squire’s. One of the carriage horses had got cut on wire. We sent twice. He came next morning.”

Her manner remained quiet; it was the quietness, not of wounds healed, but of a life arranged around the place where something has been removed.

“Was there nothing—?”

“We kept wet cloths on him. He wanted water all the while. The women said don’t give too much, but he begged. He didn’t know me at the last. Kept saying he saw ducks in the ceiling.”

The damp boards, reflecting the poor fire, quivered before my sight.

“And the other?”

“Little May. She never got strong. Born in the winter. This house is bad winter-times. Will stopped every crack, but the cold comes through where there ain’t cracks. It comes through the boards themselves.”

“How long did she live?”

“Nine months and four days.”

She knew the days. The rich commonly count the lives of children by years, because time is allowed to round itself; the poor, whose joys are often seized in infancy, preserve the smaller coinage of weeks, days, and hours.

“She had a cough,” Martha said. “Not loud. Just a little cough, like she was asking pardon for troubling us. Nights I held her under my shawl. Will would get up and walk with her, though he’d worked since dark. She liked his voice. He ain’t much of a singer, but babies don’t judge.”

Her mouth trembled into a smile.

“The last night she wouldn’t take. I warmed the spoon and sweetened the water. We’d no milk till morning. There was rice left, and I made it thin, near like broth. Poor man’s pudding, Mrs. Keel called it. May turned her head away. Just turned it, so.”

The phrase, which had seemed to me merely foolish an hour before, now became intolerable. It appeared to sit in the room like a well-fed stranger, praising simplicity beside a dead child.

Martha went on.

“After we buried her, folks said it was mercy. Said she was spared trouble. Maybe so. But there’s different ways of looking at mercy when you’re the one it leaves behind.”

I had no answer. Theology offers many explanations to a bereaved mother; few have the decency of silence.

“Will’s good,” she said. “He don’t speak their names much because he thinks it sets me off. I don’t speak because I think it sets him off. So we’re both careful, careful—and lonesome together.”

Outside, the rain thickened. It drizzled and drizzled down, not with the violence of a storm that rouses resistance, but with a grey continuance which seemed capable of wearing away stone, timber, courage, memory itself. I understood then that there is a weather of the soul resembling this: no thunder, no tragic wind, only the cold persistence of loss, dropping through every imperfect shelter.

“Do you have neighbours?” I asked.

“Half a mile. Mrs. Neal comes when she can. But she’s seven of her own. Will’s away all daylight, and often after. Sometimes I go a whole day not hearing a voice but mine.”

She looked towards the bed.

“You get talking to them that’s gone. It ain’t healthy, maybe.”

“Perhaps it is natural.”

“Mr. Blandmour says Nature’s comforting.”

“Mr. Blandmour says many things from a comfortable chair.”

She gave me a surprised look, then smiled faintly.

“He’s a good man.”

“Yes.”

I meant it. That was the difficulty. Cruelty plainly dressed excites opposition; cruelty wearing benevolence may receive invitations, publish essays, and be thanked by its victims.

Before leaving, I placed some money on the table. Martha saw it at once.

“No, sir.”

“For the child.”

“Will wouldn’t like it.”

“Then call it payment for my dinner.”

She looked at the untouched portion of pudding.

“That weren’t worth paying.”

“It was worth more to me than you know.”

She hesitated, pride contending with necessity. I saw that the contest itself humiliated her and therefore changed my method.

“Your husband did me a service,” I said. “He settled an argument.”

“What argument?”

“Whether snow always falls in the right field.”

Martha considered this, then laughed—a real laugh, brief but bright. She took the money.

“I’ll tell Will it’s philosophy wages.”

“Tell him whatever preserves us both.”

At the door she said, “You won’t tell Mr. Blandmour the pudding was bad? He might think me shiftless.”

“I shall tell him the rice was damaged before you bought it.”

“And the brine got in by mischance.”

“I shall tell him that too.”

I stepped into the lane. Rain touched my face, entered my collar, and gathered on my sleeves. Yet every wet step led me towards dryness. Behind me the Coulter house settled lower in its hollow, holding the smoke, the damaged rice, the tiny garments, the unborn child, and Martha’s loneliness.

That evening I dined again with Blandmour.

“Well?” he asked, when I told him where I had been. “Did you find the good people contented?”

“I found them courteous.”

“And their table? Plain, no doubt, but wholesome?”

“They gave me pork and rice pudding.”

His face shone.

“You see! The poor man’s pudding.”

“The rice was moulded, and tasted of old brine.”

“An unfortunate preparation.”

“An unfortunate purchase. Sound rice cost too much.”

He paused.

“Still, one exceptional case—”

“The child who died after being fed rice-water was exceptional too, I suppose. As was the boy for whom the doctor came late because the Squire’s horse had cut its leg.”

“My dear friend,” he said uneasily, “you are overwrought.”

I looked around his room: the deep rug, the curtained windows, the lamp, the books, the dry walls, the decanter glowing like a captured sunset. On the sideboard waited a pudding white with cream.

“Perhaps,” I said. “Damp affects the spirits.”

He did not answer. Neither did I eat the pudding.

## IV. RICH MAN’S CRUMBS

The next year brought me to London at a season when Europe, having spent twenty years in setting itself on fire, was congratulating itself upon the elegance of the ashes. Napoleon had fallen; Waterloo was a new word in every mouth; princes embraced, ministers illuminated their windows, generals received swords, plate, titles, estates, and dinners; while nations, exhausted by the expense of being delivered, paid heavily for the celebration.

I lodged near the Strand, where splendour and misery walked so close that the silk skirt of one brushed the rags of the other. Carriages rolled towards palaces through ranks of children who searched the gutters. Victorious banners crossed streets in which discharged soldiers begged. Bakers’ windows displayed abundance behind glass to faces that had learned the whole philosophy of hunger.

An acquaintance, Sir Jasper Tallow, offered to show me the civic wonders of a great entertainment at Guildhall. He was an alderman’s cousin, a connoisseur of ceremonies, and a man who could describe the feeding of five hundred nobles without once considering whether anyone else had dined.

“You are fortunate,” he told me. “The Prince Regent attends, with the Duke, foreign sovereigns, ambassadors, officers, and all the illustrious machinery of peace. Such profusion! Such magnificence! And afterwards the poor are admitted.”

“To dine?”

“To receive the remnants.”

“The crumbs?”

“Not crumbs,” said Sir Jasper, offended by the smallness of the word. “Substantial fragments. Rich man’s crumbs, if you choose—but crumbs of a truly imperial character.”

I remembered Blandmour.

“Do they nourish by association with greatness?”

“My dear fellow, a haunch of venison remains venison though royalty has tasted it.”

“And if royalty has eaten the haunch?”

“There are side portions.”

We approached Guildhall through a crowd dense enough to abolish individuality. Beggars, hawkers, apprentices, porters, widows, sailors, labourers, soldiers with empty sleeves, women carrying infants, boys without shoes—all were compressed behind barriers, awaiting the charitable sequel of magnificence. Some had stood since morning. The smell of wet wool, unwashed flesh, lamp-smoke, horse-dung, and hunger—yes, hunger has an odour, though prosperous noses seldom learn it—hung in the narrow streets.

A thin woman asked an officer when the doors would open.

“When their lordships have done.”

“My children ain’t ate since yesterday.”

“Then they’re trained for patience.”

Sir Jasper drew me onward.

“You must not judge the arrangements by the impatience of the lower orders. They possess peculiar social sensibilities. Expectation agitates them.”

“So does starvation.”

Inside, the hall blazed. Light fell from chandeliers upon gold plate, uniforms, jewels, scarlet hangings, polished tables, carved dishes, feathers, orders, chains, and swords. Banners drooped from the roof, memorials of battles in which many now waiting outside had perhaps bled without receiving so much as a napkin from the feast of victory.

The company assembled with a rustling grandeur. There was the Regent, glittering under the burden of ornament; there the great Duke, spare and self-contained; there princes from the continent, representatives of Alexander’s Russia, soldiers who had seen the Cossacks ride into Paris, ministers who spoke of nations as landlords speak of farms. Toast followed toast: to peace, to victory, to sovereigns, to generals, to commerce, to civilisation, to the union of peoples. Nobody proposed the health of those outside.

Dinner advanced in splendid campaigns. Salmon vanished by regiments; soups were routed; beef and venison fell in great divisions; towers of pastry were breached; pheasants lost breast and wing; jellies trembled under attack; wines crossed the table in crimson and amber battalions.

Sir Jasper watched with pious enthusiasm.

“England understands generosity.”

“England appears hungry.”

“Wait until you see what remains.”

I did wait. Long after appetite had ceased, eating continued as ceremony. A nobleman who could consume no more cut experimentally into a pasty and abandoned it. Another shattered the glazed surface of a pudding, tasted one spoonful, and sent it away. Birds were carved for the choicest morsels; the rest, torn and cooling, accumulated upon dishes. Fruit was bitten and left. Bread was crumbled beside plates. Sauces congealed; gravy filmed over; bones appeared under lace cuffs and jewelled fingers.

At last the royal party withdrew amid acclamations. The superior guests followed. Servants began to clear, though “clear” is a misleading term for an operation designed not to cleanse the hall but to prepare its refuse for public gratitude.

Sir Jasper took my arm.

“Now comes the beautiful part.”

## V. THE BLESSED ALMONERS

The tables, lately ordered with mathematical splendour, had become a landscape after battle. Gouged pheasants lay with ribs exposed; pies stood hollow, their crusts broken inward; tongues had been sliced to stumps; hams displayed greasy caverns; custards bore the tracks of exploratory spoons; jellies, once architectural, had collapsed into coloured ruin. Bones, rinds, skins, crusts, spilled sauces, candle grease, broken glass, and fouled linen completed the scene.

“These,” said Sir Jasper, sweeping his hand grandly, “will be distributed.”

“Distributed?”

“Thrown open. The people enter and partake.”

“Do they receive plates?”

He laughed.

“My good sir, one cannot serve an army of paupers à la française.”

“Knives?”

“Unsafe.”

“Portions?”

“Providence portions according to agility.”

We had come, then, from the poor man’s pudding to the rich man’s crumbs; from scarcity romanticised in a wet cottage to waste consecrated in a golden hall.

Officials appeared, some flushed with wine, others important with ribbons and staves. They surveyed the remnants as Roman governors may have surveyed a conquered province.

“A magnificent reserve,” said one.

“Enough for thousands,” declared another.

“More than enough,” said Sir Jasper. “Observe those pasties.”

“They are empty.”

“The crust is excellent.”

“That bird has no flesh.”

“There is nourishment near the joints.”

“The jellies are full of candle snuff.”

“One must not be fastidious in distress.”

This principle—that the distressed surrender not only comfort but taste, cleanliness, pride, and every ordinary claim of the body—has always been exceedingly convenient to those who provide relief.

A clergyman mounted a step and offered thanks for the generosity displayed. His voice rose into the rafters. He spoke of abundance descending from the high to the low; of the great family of the nation; of rulers sharing their bounty with the humblest subjects; of crumbs blessed by gratitude; of Lazarus comforted at the gate of Dives.

I looked up at the banners. In the wavering smoke they seemed to look down upon the waiting poor precisely as Dives looked upon Lazarus—not with hatred, perhaps, but with that serene separation which supposes the gulf to be part of the furniture of the universe.

Outside, the noise increased.

“Open! Open!”

“Bread here!”

“Mind the child!”

“The Squire! the King! the Regent! Anybody—give us meat!”

Sir Jasper smiled.

“Popular enthusiasm.”

The first barrier was removed. A current of human beings filled the passage, stopped at a second gate, and accumulated behind it. Faces appeared between shoulders: white, red, dirty, feverish, desperate. Hands reached through bars before anything was offered, fingers opening and closing as if hunger itself had claws.

An official shouted instructions which no one could hear.

“Orderly! You shall all be admitted orderly!”

The crowd answered with a roar.

A constable near me said, “Once that gate goes, sir, keep to the wall.”

“Is it dangerous?”

“Only if they’re hungry.”

The inner doors opened.

For one suspended instant the mass seemed to inhale. Then it entered.

Not walked—entered, as floodwater enters a breached house. Men, women, and children poured into the hall, stumbled, were lifted by pressure, flung forward, turned sideways, and driven against the tables. The foremost seized whatever lay nearest: a loaf-end, a bird’s carcass, handfuls of pudding, greasy crusts. Those behind reached over them. Dishes fell. A boy crawled beneath the table and emerged hugging a broken pie. A woman caught a strip of meat, lost it, and shrieked as another trod upon her hand.

“Order!” cried the officials.

“Hunger,” I said, though nobody heard me.

Sir Jasper still attempted admiration.

“See how eagerly the bounty is received!”

A one-armed veteran had fixed his remaining hand upon a ham-bone. A larger man struck him in the chest and tore it away. Two girls fought over an orange already bitten through. An old woman scraped gravy from a cloth into her palm and licked it. A child, scarcely older than Martha’s Daniel would have been, held a jelly mould to his face and swallowed what liquid had not spilled, along with dust, wax, and fragments of paper decoration.

One gentleman laughed.

“By God, they forage like Cossacks!”

The veteran heard him.

“We learnt from serving you,” he said.

At another table, a group had discovered several bottles not wholly empty. They fought for them. Wine streamed down faces and clothes; one bottle broke between two hands, opening the palm of a woman who nevertheless sucked the blood and wine together rather than surrender it.

Sir Jasper drew back.

“The creatures are becoming unruly.”

“They were hungry when they entered.”

“Hunger need not abolish manners.”

“Manners are difficult to preserve while being crushed for a gnawed bone.”

He stared at me as Blandmour had done when I objected to snow leaving Coulter’s field. Comfortable theories are cousins everywhere; they differ in dress, not blood.

## VI. CRUMBS IN MOTION

The first rush consumed whatever was visible. Then began the search beneath and behind: under cloths, inside broken pasties, among spilled decorations, even in the refuse baskets which servants had filled before the doors opened. Every concealment became a rumour; every rumour, a struggle.

“There’s beef yonder!”

“No, here!”

“Under the royal table!”

“Hold! hold!”

“Wedge—wedge—in quick!”

“Mind her! She’s down!”

A bench overturned. The crack sounded like a pistol, and panic moved through the crowd faster than understanding. Some surged towards the supposed beef; others, thinking a way out had opened, pushed in the contrary direction. We were caught between.

“Keep close!” cried Sir Jasper.

I was pressed against a column. My hat vanished. An elbow struck my jaw. Someone seized my coat-tail, not maliciously but as a drowning man seizes a rope. The seam tore from waist to hem.

“Back!” shouted a constable. “Back, damn you!”

There was no back. There was only pressure—chest against spine, knee against thigh, breath against breath. The hall that had comfortably contained the splendour of a continent could not contain its hunger.

A woman screamed that her baby was crushed.

“Lift it!” someone cried.

“Where?”

“Here—here!”

Hands rose, bearing an infant above the crowd. It travelled across them, wavering, passed from a butcher’s bloody fist to a girl’s thin arms, from a sailor to a servant standing upon a table. For a moment every face beneath turned upward. Even in that brutal compression, humanity asserted itself—not in speeches from the dais, but in rough hands lifting one helpless creature beyond the danger made by all.

Then a dish flew through the air and struck an official. He fell. His companions laid about them with staves.

The scene altered at once. Want, which had been frantic, became enraged.

“The meat’s gone!”

“They hid it!”

“Rich devils!”

“Give us what you promised!”

A man mounted the table from which the clergyman had praised national fellowship.

“Look!” he shouted, holding up a stripped carcass. “Here’s their charity! Here’s peace after Waterloo!”

A cheer, a groan, and a curse seemed to break from one throat.

Someone cried, “The cellars!”

That word was the spark.

The crowd rushed towards a side door. Constables blocked it. Tables became barricades; benches became clubs; plates and bottles became missiles. The banners above, stirring in the heated air, appeared to recoil from the citizenship below. A portrait of some ancient mayor received a pudding in the face and looked, beneath the streaming custard, more honestly involved in public affairs than before.

Sir Jasper had lost his composure.

“This is ingratitude! Rank ingratitude!”

“For what?”

“For the banquet.”

“They did not eat the banquet.”

“For the remains!”

“They have eaten those.”

“Then what do they want?”

“More.”

The simplicity of the answer enraged him.

A young man sprang upon us, using our shoulders to climb the column base. His foot struck Sir Jasper’s chest; Sir Jasper fell, and I dragged him up before the crowd could tread upon him.

“This way!” called the constable who had warned me.

We fought towards the wall. The word “fought” may suggest aggression; in truth, I fought only for inches of air. My cravat tightened until I tore it loose. One sleeve was nearly pulled off. My boot came down upon something soft; a cry followed, and the shame of it remains with me, though I never saw whom I injured.

At the wall we edged behind a fallen screen. The constable thrust open a service passage.

“Quick, gentlemen!”

Sir Jasper entered first. I followed, but a hand clutched my collar.

I turned. It belonged to the one-armed veteran. His face was grey. Blood ran from his nose.

“Take me through,” he said.

The constable raised his staff.

“No more!”

“He comes with us,” I said.

“Orders—”

“He comes.”

Whether my voice persuaded him, or the next heave of the crowd did, I cannot say. We dragged the veteran into the passage and forced the door shut. Bodies struck it from the other side. The wood shuddered.

The corridor led through kitchens where abundance still reigned in private. Whole loaves stood on shelves. Untouched joints cooled upon racks. Pans of broth, tureens of sauce, baskets of fruit, and trays of pastry remained, reserved perhaps for servants, perhaps for tomorrow, perhaps merely overlooked.

The veteran stared.

“No meat left, they said.”

Sir Jasper adjusted his torn waistcoat.

“These provisions are not part of the distribution.”

The man looked at him.

“Nor are we part of the nation, save when there’s shooting.”

He reached for a loaf. A cook slapped his hand away.

“Thief!”

I took the loaf, paid for it, and gave it to him.

He did not thank me. I am glad he did not. A starving soldier purchasing bread with another man’s embarrassment does not owe gratitude to the bystander who discovers justice six feet beyond a riot.

We passed into the yard. The veteran disappeared into an alley, tearing the loaf with his teeth.

Sir Jasper and I emerged by a side gate into the street. My knees were bruised, my lip bleeding, my coat split, one cuff missing, and my hat gone beyond recovery. Sir Jasper had suffered less bodily damage but more philosophical injury.

“An abominable exhibition,” he said.

“Indeed.”

“Such noble generosity repaid by violence!”

“Perhaps generosity that must be fought for with fists is imperfectly arranged.”

“They received food.”

“Refuse.”

“Food is food to hunger.”

“No. Food is food. Refuse is refuse. Hunger merely compels men to confuse them.”

He shook his head.

“You have democratic tendencies.”

“I have a bruised rib.”

Outside the principal entrance, cavalry had arrived. Horses pressed against the people; constables drove stragglers from the doors; women searched for children; men shouted accusations; broken dishes crunched underfoot. The promised bounty was exhausted. Those who had entered late received nothing. They did not become less hungry because a clergyman had already thanked Heaven for abundance.

A boy stood near the gutter licking grease from a scrap of painted pasteboard.

Sir Jasper pointed to him.

“You see? Even the decorations have value.”

I looked at him, uncertain whether he jested. He did not.

## VII. THE TWO FEASTS

That night, in my lodging, a servant examined my torn coat and declared it ruined.

“Quite ruined, sir. Cloth’s sound, but ripped every way.”

He spoke as if sound cloth torn every way presented a paradox. I thought of Coulter’s patches, each holding another patch together; of William’s boot bound with sacking; of Martha washing damaged rice three times; of the veteran’s single hand closing upon a purchased loaf.

I ordered supper, but when it came I could not eat.

Before me stood two tables.

At the first sat William and Martha Coulter in their low, damp house. The pork was rank; the rice moulded; the pudding tasted of old brine. Yet their best chair had been given to me, the soundest potato divided, and the shame of their insufficiency turned, by affection, into a welcome. No herald announced their charity. No clergyman praised it. No banner leaned from the rafters to bless the gift. They gave from want, and feared only that I might think the giver shiftless.

At the second table had sat princes, dukes, ambassadors, conquerors, legislators, and all the decorated custodians of civilisation. What they could not eat they mangled; what they mangled they abandoned; what they abandoned they called bounty; and when starving people struggled for it, those people were reproached for the absence of grace.

Poor Man’s Pudding and Rich Man’s Crumbs—the phrases answered one another across the year.

Blandmour had found providence in scarcity. Sir Jasper had found charity in waste. Neither had tasted the mould nor fought for the bone. Each stood safely beyond the fact and bestowed upon it an improving name.

It is easy, after breakfast, to admire the frugality of those who have no dinner. It is easy, before a roaring hearth, to discover moral warmth in snow; easy, from a dry bed, to recommend ventilation to a family that stops every crack against the winter; easy, with sound teeth and a full purse, to praise potatoes, rice, crusts, broth, patience, contentment, and the inexhaustible resources of Nature.

We misjudge the poor even in their apparent errors because we omit the body from our calculations. We condemn them for crowding into one close room, forgetting that another room means another fire. We rebuke them for eating spoiled meat, as though wholesome meat were rejected from perversity. We advise fresh air, sobriety, cleanliness, foresight, cheerfulness, and thrift, each counsel proceeding from a condition they cannot purchase. Damp is not an opinion. Cold is not a defect of character. Hunger is no abstraction, but an animal lawyer pleading every instant before the nerves.

Nor does suffering become picturesque because a gentleman gives it a name. Snow may be Poor Man’s Manure; the potato, Poor Man’s Egg; rice, Poor Man’s Pudding. Yet if the manure blows upon the rich man’s field, if the egg is divided because there is but one, if the pudding is compounded of damaged grain, water, molasses, and sorrow, the adjective “poor” has not transformed deprivation into providence. It has merely taught comfort to smile at what it refuses to see.

And Rich Man’s Crumbs? Let the phrase retain its whole magnificence. Let heralds proclaim it. Let officials marshal it, preachers bless it, aldermen account it, and princes depart before it is served. Still, a gnawed wing is a gnawed wing; a hollow pie is a hollow pie; humiliation does not become nourishment because it falls from gold plate.

I do not say Blandmour was wicked, nor Sir Jasper deliberately cruel. Had either seen a dog tormented, he would have interfered. Had Martha fainted before them, each might have opened his purse. Their failure was subtler and therefore more common: they loved an arrangement in which suffering provided occasions for their own agreeable sentiments. They did not desire the poor to starve; they desired poverty to remain interpretable as simplicity, patience, dependence, gratitude, and divine economy.

But the poor themselves are not emblems. They are cold when cold, hungry when hungry, ashamed when shamed, bereaved when their children die. They do not eat metaphors.

Months later, I visited Blandmour again. It was winter. Snow lay over the fields, pure and level beneath the moon.

“Poor man’s manure,” he said, but less confidently than before.

“Some of it,” I answered.

He looked into the fire.

“I sent Coulter a load of seasoned wood.”

“That was better than a maxim.”

“And sound rice,” he added. “Martha wrote that the baby thrives.”

I was glad. Yet even then I would not permit myself the comfortable conclusion that a load of wood, a sack of rice, and a thriving infant had repaired the world. Charity may relieve a wrong without explaining it away. The danger begins when the giver loves his relief so well that he forgets the condition which made it necessary.

Outside, the snow continued falling. It touched Squire Rye’s park and Coulter’s field with the same white appearance, concealing for a few hours the wall between them. Appearance is impartial. Possession is not.

I thought of Guildhall, where banners of victory had hung above men fighting for bones; and of Martha’s kitchen, where grief drizzled through the silence as water through the roof. Between those places lay an ocean, but no moral distance. In both, comfortable observers had mistaken what descended from above for what was justly shared below.

May Heaven preserve us from the benevolence that praises hunger’s appetite, the philosophy that scents flowers in mildew, the patriotism that calls refuse a feast, and the piety that thanks God loudly enough to drown the cry at the gate.

The poor require neither picturesque substitutes nor ceremonial leavings. They require sound food before it is spoiled, warmth before sickness, medicine before death, wages before alms, and dignity before gratitude.

Call these things by no pretty name.

Give them.

The last cloud of dust had scarcely melted into the golden haze lying over the Gabilan Hills when Violante MacGregor heard the riders returning.

She had been seated at the eastern window of the hacienda, where the purple wistaria, prodigal of bloom, climbed from the tiled court and hung its fragrant clusters about the white-curtained casement. Beyond her stretched the warm, beneficent valley: leagues of tawny pasture broken by oak and sycamore; fields where the wild poppies burned like fallen sunshine; cattle standing knee-deep in the silver shallows of the creek; and, farther still, the blue mountains, tranquil as a promise. In that hour the whole ranch seemed incapable of sorrow.

Violante leaned through the wistaria and listened. Presently came the ringing of bridles, the soft thunder of hoofs, and the gay halloo of men who had ridden far and were rejoiced to see home.

“There is the señor!” cried Alice, her young maid, running to the window.

Violante smiled, and the smile changed her face from beauty into tenderness. She was slender and dark, with grave Spanish eyes and that delicate fairness which the Californian sun, though it may deepen, cannot wholly destroy. Her gown was white, and the breeze moved it gently among the purple flowers. Seen thus, she might have been the saintly heroine of some old romance, waiting in an enchanted garden for her warrior’s return.

Yet her warrior, when he sprang from the saddle below, was no velvet-cloaked cavalier, but a broad-shouldered Scotchman in dusty riding-boots.

“Violante!” he called.

“Robert!”

He entered by the outer stair and, a moment later, had taken her in his arms with an ardor which twelve years of marriage had not diminished. Robert MacGregor was hard-headed, fiery-tempered, and Presbyterian to the marrow; Violante was patient, devout, and Catholic with every breath she drew. They had differed concerning saints, fasting, predestination, the management of cattle, and the proper correction of servants; but never had either doubted the other’s love. What are small divisions of doctrine when two hearts have learned the greater creed of faithfulness?

“You have been gone three days,” she reproached him.

“And thought each one a year.”

“That is a Scotch calculation.”

“It is a husband’s.”

He kissed her again, laughing, then glanced toward the court. Several vaqueros were watering their horses. Among them stood Basilio, a small, swarthy fellow with narrow shoulders and extraordinarily bright black eyes. He had ridden in Robert’s service less than a year, and was valued for his skill with unbroken horses; yet Violante had never liked the noiselessness of his movements nor the sudden glitter that came into his gaze when money was mentioned.

Robert drew from beneath his coat a heavy leathern bag.

“Here,” he said, lowering his voice. “The payment from Monterey. Four thousand dollars in gold. Put it in the iron chest until I return.”

“So much!”

“So much, and honestly earned—which makes the burden the heavier.”

The bag fell into her hands with a dull musical chink. At that instant Violante looked through the vines and saw Basilio in the court. He was not regarding his horse. His face was lifted toward the window, and his eyes, fixed upon the leathern bag, glittered with a concentration so fierce that for one swift moment his whole soul seemed naked.

Their glances met.

At once he bent to tighten a cinch.

A slight chill passed through Violante, though the afternoon was warm. She moved back from the window and placed the gold upon a table.

“Robert,” she began, “that man Basilio—”

But a shout rose from below.

“Señor! The black steer has broken from the lower herd!”

Robert uttered an impatient exclamation. The cattle were being driven toward the northern range, and a refractory animal could disorder half the drove. He buckled on his belt.

“I must go. Nicolas says we shall not be absent above two hours.”

“Must you?”

The question was simple, but something in her tone made him turn.

“Why, my heart, you are pale.”

“It is nothing. Only—take Basilio with you.”

“Basilio?”

“Yes.”

He looked at her more closely, then called over the railing:

“Basilio! Saddle again. You ride with us.”

The vaquero raised his head. For the briefest interval anger darkened his face; then he smiled, showing small white teeth.

“Sí, señor.”

Robert kissed his wife’s forehead.

“Lock the gold away. There are twenty men within call, and Alice besides. What can happen in two hours?”

What, indeed? We ask the question so confidently when happiness surrounds us, as though calamity were bound by clocks and needed long preparation! Yet death may cross a threshold between two beats of a loving heart.

The riders swept away, Basilio among them. Violante watched until the dust sank and the last moving speck vanished beyond the cottonwoods.

Then she carried the gold to the iron chest in Robert’s dressing-room, locked it, and hid the key in the bosom of her dress.

An hour passed. The afternoon retained its pastoral serenity. Bees moved drunkenly among the wistaria; white pigeons strutted along the roof; from the distant quarters came the monotonous song of a woman grinding maize. Violante tried to read, but her thoughts returned to Basilio’s lifted face and that famished glitter in his eyes.

At length Alice entered with folded linen.

“The señor will be home before supper,” she said.

“I hope so.”

“Do you still think of Basilio?”

Violante closed the book. “You saw him?”

“I saw his eyes. They were two little knives.”

“Hush. It is sinful to condemn a man upon a look.”

Alice crossed herself. “Then may the Blessed Virgin forgive me, for I have never trusted him.”

A horse neighed from the rear of the house.

Both women listened.

“Perhaps one of the men has returned,” said Alice.

Violante rose and went to the western gallery. The yard behind the hacienda appeared deserted. A bay horse stood near the pepper trees, saddled, its reins dragging. She recognized it as Basilio’s mount.

Her heart gave one violent stroke.

“Where is the rider?” Alice whispered.

They searched the yard with their eyes. No man was visible. The kitchen door stood open; old Marta had gone to the dairy; the laborers were in the farther vineyard. Between the hacienda and the nearest occupied building lay several hundred yards of dazzling, empty sunlight.

“Close the doors,” said Violante.

Alice obeyed, trembling. Violante returned to the bedchamber, walked directly to the eastern window, and looked down.

At first she saw only the purple vine shaking faintly in the breeze. Then a brown hand appeared from beneath the leaves and fastened itself upon a thick branch.

Another hand followed.

Basilio was climbing.

He had removed his boots and moved with the terrible caution of a cat approaching a sleeping bird. A naked dagger was held between his teeth. Its blade pressed against one cheek, and the sunlight ran along it in a white flame.

Violante stepped back before he saw her. Her face had become colorless, but her mind—by what grace of heaven, what inheritance from brave men and steadfast women?—was perfectly clear.

“The pistol,” she said.

Alice stared at her.

“Señora?”

“The small pistol in Robert’s cabinet. It is loaded. Bring it without a sound.”

“But he—he is there!”

“Go.”

The maid fled. Violante stood beside the curtain and heard the vine creak under Basilio’s weight. She knew why he had returned. He had slipped away from the riders, circled through the dry arroyo, and come to take the gold. Would theft satisfy him? No. He had been seen; he knew he had been seen. The dagger answered the question.

Alice returned, holding the weapon in both hands.

Violante took it. She had often fired at a mark with Robert and could strike a silver dollar at twenty paces; but now the pistol felt immeasurably heavy. It was one thing to pierce a painted circle and another to send an immortal soul, bloodstained and unprepared, before the judgment seat of God.

“Open the curtain,” she whispered.

Alice pulled it aside.

Basilio’s head and shoulders were above the sill. The knife lay between his teeth; one knee was twisted among the vine’s old woody stems, while his left hand clutched the stone ledge. He looked up—and into the round black mouth of the pistol.

“Do not move,” said Violante.

The command was uttered quietly. That quietness was more formidable than a scream.

Basilio remained rigid. His eyes widened; the dagger slipped slightly, cutting the corner of his mouth. A thread of blood crept down his chin.

“Take the knife from your teeth,” Violante continued. “Slowly. Hold it by the blade.”

He obeyed, though the movement forced him to throw more weight upon the leg entangled in the vine.

“Now drop it.”

The dagger flashed downward and struck the stones below.

“Señora,” he breathed, “I came only—”

“Do not speak. Alice, go to the lower range. Find my husband.”

Alice clung to the bedpost. “Leave you alone?”

“You must.”

“He will climb in when I am gone!”

Violante’s eyes did not leave Basilio’s face. “No. If he moves, I shall shoot.”

Basilio understood that this was not a threat spoken in panic. The weapon was steady; the finger rested upon the trigger; and behind the gentleness he had despised there stood a resolution colder than steel.

“Alice,” Violante repeated, “take the bay horse. Ride as you have never ridden before.”

The girl began to sob.

“Go!”

Alice ran. A door slammed below; after a breathless interval came the clatter of hoofs. Basilio turned his eyes in the direction of the sound.

“Look at me,” said Violante.

He looked.

Thus began the strangest and most agonizing parley that the old hacienda had ever witnessed: a beautiful woman in white, framed by purple flowers, holding death in her small hand; and below her, half concealed by the same luxuriant blossoms, the swarthy face of a murderer, blood trickling from his mouth, his body suspended between earth and window.

At first Basilio’s position seemed merely awkward. His right foot rested upon a narrow projection; his left knee was caught in a fork of the vine; one hand gripped the sill, the other a branch beside his shoulder. But the muscles soon began to rebel against that unnatural immobility. His bent leg burned; his fingers grew numb; an exquisite torment spread through his back.

“Señora,” he whispered, “permit me to change my foot.”

“No.”

“I cannot remain so.”

“You must.”

“I shall fall.”

“If you descend, I will not fire.”

He glanced down. To descend he must release the ledge, turn his body, and seek with his bare foot a branch hidden by leaves. One false movement would resemble a spring toward the window. He did not trust her mercy, nor did he trust his own balance.

“I cannot descend,” he said.

“Then remain.”

A fly settled upon the blood at his chin. He could not brush it away. His right arm trembled.

Violante watched that trembling with an anguish almost as great as his. She did not hate him. Indeed, hatred might have made her task easy. She saw not merely a robber with a dagger, but a man enduring pain before her eyes, a man whom one involuntary contraction of her finger might hurl dead among the fallen petals. Yet what could she do? Lower the pistol, and he might leap upon her. Fire, and she would become his executioner. Call for help? No one was near enough to hear.

“Basilio,” she said, “why did you come back?”

“For nothing, señora.”

“You carry a dagger for nothing?”

“I feared a snake in the vines.”

Even in terror he lied, and the baseness of the lie offended her more deeply than the intended robbery.

“You saw the gold.”

He was silent.

“You left my husband. You rode here by the arroyo. You climbed toward this room with a knife in your teeth. You meant to take the money.”

“No, señora!”

“And kill me.”

His face seemed to shrink.

“No! By the holy Mother, no!”

“Do not profane her name.”

The hours of childhood returned to Violante: her mother’s dim chapel, the candles before the Virgin, the murmur of prayer in times of drought and pestilence. She prayed now without moving her lips. Holy Mary, strengthen my hand—but do not permit me to shed blood. Protect Robert from what he may find here. Save this wicked man from forcing me to destroy him.

Basilio’s pain increased. Sweat stood in shining drops upon his forehead. His fingers, strained upon the sill, changed from brown to a sickly gray.

“Water,” he begged.

“I cannot give it.”

“One mouthful, señora! For Christ’s mercy!”

“Be still. Help is coming.”

“Help?” He laughed once, a short and dreadful sound. “Nicolas will hang me.”

“My husband will judge you.”

“The señor is fierce.”

“He is just.”

“And I intended evil against his wife! Better that you shoot me now.”

Violante’s hand quivered.

“Do not say that.”

“Shoot, señora. Here.” He moved his chin, indicating his breast.

“Be still!”

The pistol steadied again. Basilio groaned. A cramp seized his imprisoned leg; his body jerked, the branches thrashed, and Violante’s finger tightened convulsively. For one terrible instant both believed the weapon had discharged. But no report came. The silence afterward seemed to roar around them.

“Madre de Dios!” gasped Basilio.

“Do not move again.”

“I did not choose to move. My flesh moves against me.”

There lay the hideous truth. Courage can command intention, but not forever the rebellious muscles. His body, tortured beyond endurance, was becoming his enemy. If it flung him forward, she must fire; if backward, he might break his neck upon the stones.

The sun had crossed the window. Its fierce rays no longer touched Violante, but beat directly upon Basilio’s uncovered head. The scent of wistaria, so sweet an hour before, grew heavy and nauseating. A hummingbird flashed among the blossoms, hovered before the motionless pair as though perplexed by them, and darted away.

Minutes accumulated until they seemed years.

Violante’s arm ached from shoulder to wrist. She dared not support it with the other hand, lest the alteration encourage him. Her eyes burned from watching. Strange fears assailed her. What if Alice were thrown? What if she failed to find the riders? What if Robert returned by another road? What if night came and she still stood there, pointing death at a man who slowly died from pain?

Basilio read some part of this trepidation in her face.

“Señora,” he said, “you are tired.”

She made no answer.

“Your hand trembles.”

“It is steady enough.”

“If I climb in, perhaps you will miss.”

“I do not miss.”

He knew it. He had seen her break bottles from the gatepost for Robert’s amusement. His hope collapsed.

“Then kill me,” he moaned. “Kill me, for the love of God!”

“No.”

“You are cruel.”

Violante flinched as though he had struck her.

Cruel! Was mercy, then, only another shape of torture? By refusing to shoot him, was she inflicting a suffering more dreadful than death? Yet the decision was not hers alone. His life belonged to God; her safety belonged also to Robert, who loved her. She could surrender neither.

“You came to murder me,” she said, not in accusation but in sorrow. “And now you call me cruel because I will not murder you.”

His gaze fell. For a moment shame conquered pain.

“I am a wicked man,” he murmured. “I have been wicked since I was a boy. Hunger first taught me, then money, then bad companions. But I never killed a woman.”

“You intended it.”

“I thought I could. When I saw the bag, the devil entered my eyes. I said: ‘She is alone. She is soft. One blow, and the gold is mine.’ But now I see you, señora—now I see your face—I could not have done it.”

“Would you have discovered that before or after entering the window?”

He wept then, whether from remorse or bodily agony none could say. Tears slipped down his cheeks and mingled with the blood at his mouth.

“Mercy! In the name of the wounds of Our Lord, mercy! Let me go, and I will crawl to the mission. I will confess. I will spend the rest of my life in atonement. I will be your servant—your slave. Señora, señora, the leg is breaking! The fire runs to my heart!”

Violante’s own tears rose, but she forced them back.

“Wait. My husband is coming.”

“Say you forgive me.”

“I forgive you.”

The words came at once.

Amazement changed his face.

“You forgive?”

“Yes.”

“And will not shoot?”

“Not unless you compel me.”

He bowed his head as far as his position permitted.

“Then I am lower than the dust beneath your feet.”

Far away sounded the faint beat of horses.

Violante scarcely trusted her hearing. Basilio raised his head. The sound came again, multiplied now—hoof after hoof striking the hard road.

“They are coming,” she said.

Deliverance was near; yet at the very instant hope relaxed the cord of her endurance. The room swayed. The bright window became a black frame around Basilio’s distorted face. She set her left hand upon the table.

“Hold still,” she whispered.

The hoofbeats thundered into the court. Men shouted. Robert’s voice rang from below:

“Violante!”

She tried to answer. Basilio’s fingers slipped from the sill, and he cried out. Robert burst through the door.

Violante turned toward him. The pistol fell harmlessly upon the carpet.

“Alive,” she said. “Do not kill him—alive—”

Then the sunshine went out, and she sank into her husband’s arms.

When consciousness returned, Robert knelt beside the bed, holding her hand against his lips. Alice wept at the foot of it. From the courtyard came the confused murmur of men.

“Basilio?” Violante asked.

“Safe under guard.”

“You did not shoot him?”

“No.”

“Nor hang him?”

“No, my dearest.”

She closed her eyes in gratitude. Robert’s stern face worked with emotion as he contemplated her pallor. He had heard Alice’s breathless tale during the furious ride home; he had seen the pistol, the fallen dagger, and the blood upon the vine. His first impulse had been to drag Basilio down and kill him with his bare hands. But then Violante, fainting, had pleaded for the villain’s life. Could he stain her heroism with vengeance?

Basilio was brought into the room at his own request. Two vaqueros supported him, for the tortured leg could scarcely bear his weight. His wrists were bound. The blood had been washed from his mouth, but his face retained the grayness of extreme suffering.

He fell upon his knees.

“Señor,” he said, “I deserve death.”

Robert’s hand closed.

“You do.”

“Robert,” Violante murmured.

He mastered himself.

“My wife has forgiven you,” he said. “For her sake, I spare your life. At sunrise you will be taken beyond my land. If you return, you will be delivered to the law.”

Basilio stared at him.

“No punishment?”

“Have you not been punished?”

“Not enough.” He looked at Violante. “She suffered because of me. I meant to rob her—to kill her. If I go free, the sin remains in my flesh.”

“The sin will remain whatever we do to your flesh,” said Robert grimly.

“Then chastise it. Let the men whip me. Let them bind me to a horse and drive me from the ranch, that I may remember mercy with every scar.”

Violante raised herself upon one elbow.

“No! There has been suffering enough.”

But Basilio persisted with a passion almost savage. Remorse, superstition, and the degraded pride which cannot accept forgiveness as a gift contended within him. He seemed to believe that pain alone could restore the balance of his soul.

“I beg it, señor. Before all the men. Let me make atonement.”

Robert hesitated. He despised theatrical supplication; yet he also knew the vaqueros, whose fierce code might interpret complete pardon as weakness. A token punishment, controlled and brief, could end the matter.

“Nicolas,” he said to the foreman, “give him six lashes and release him outside the corral. No more.”

Violante caught her husband’s sleeve.

“Robert, do not.”

“It is his own request.”

“Pain has disordered his mind.”

“Six strokes will not kill him.”

Ah, how carelessly do strong men measure suffering when another body must endure it! Robert meant leniency; Basilio demanded expiation; Nicolas desired discipline; and from these several intentions, none of which openly sought death, death was already fashioning its road.

The vaqueros led Basilio away.

Violante sank back, troubled beyond reason. Presently there came from the corral the sound of a whip, followed by a hoarse cry.

“One,” whispered Alice.

Violante covered her ears. Yet through her hands she heard the second blow, the third, the fourth. After the sixth there was silence.

“It is finished,” Robert said.

Then arose a sudden uproar—the shrill scream of a frightened horse, men shouting, rails crashing, and the thunder of many hoofs.

Violante sprang from the bed.

“What has happened?”

Robert ran to the gallery. Below, men scattered before a herd of plunging horses. Nicolas had not obeyed his order with the merciful exactness intended. Instead of releasing Basilio beyond the corral, he had stripped him to the waist, tied his wrists to the saddle horn, bound his ankles beneath the belly of a half-broken roan, and struck both man and beast with the whip. The maddened animal had hurled itself among the remuda.

“Nicolas!” Robert roared. “Cut him loose!”

Too late. The roan crashed against the inner gate. A score of horses followed, trampling through the wreckage. Dust rose in a choking wall. Basilio, helpless upon the saddle, reeled from side to side, his bleeding back exposed to the sun.

The terrified herd swept across the pasture toward the northern fence. Men mounted and pursued, but the loose horses, infected with common madness, ran as though wolves followed them. The roan bore to the left, broke through a decayed section of rails, and plunged into the apiary.

The hives stood in orderly white rows beneath almond trees, peaceful little habitations from which thousands of industrious creatures went forth to gather sweetness from the valley. Into this diminutive city thundered the crazed horse. One hive toppled; then another. Boxes split beneath iron-shod hoofs. Combs of amber honey burst upon the ground.

For one suspended second nothing happened.

Then the air turned black.

Bees poured from the ruined hives in furious clouds. They enveloped horse and rider, settled upon the roan’s head, filled its nostrils, clustered about its eyes. Basilio’s scream rose above the thunder.

“Help! Help me! Holy Mother—help!”

Violante was already running.

She crossed the court before Robert could seize her, her white dress streaming behind her. Men shouted for her to return. She did not hear them—or, hearing, could not obey. That cry was the voice which had begged mercy at her window; the life endangered was the life she had refused to take. Could she stand safely beneath the gallery while the man died because mercy had been entrusted to crueler hands?

“Basilio!” she called. “Basilio!”

The roan burst from the apiary, blind with terror, striking itself against trees and fences. Bees clothed its neck like a moving mane. Basilio hung forward over the saddle horn. His naked chest and back were dark with blood, dust, and swarming insects.

Violante snatched a horse blanket from the rail and ran directly into the animal’s path.

“Violante!” Robert shouted.

The roan came upon her with foam flying from its mouth. To every watcher it seemed that she must be trampled. But courage is not ignorance of danger; it is the soul’s dominion over the body while danger is fully understood. At the final instant she cast the blanket over the horse’s head and caught the dragging rein.

The animal reared. Its forefeet beat the air above her. She held on.

“Steady! Steady, pobre animal!”

Her voice, which had commanded a murderer, now penetrated the horse’s frenzy. It came down trembling, tried to plunge away, then yielded its head by an inch. Robert and two vaqueros rushed forward, beating at the bees with their hats. They seized the bridle.

“A knife!” cried Violante. “Cut him free!”

No one moved quickly enough. She tore the knife from Robert’s belt. Bees stung her face and hands; one became entangled in her hair. She climbed against the saddle, sawed through the cords at Basilio’s wrists, then cut the thongs beneath the horse. His body slid heavily upon her.

Together they fell.

Robert dragged them beyond the densest swarm while the vaqueros led the horse away. Violante rose, though her hands were swelling and several stings burned like live coals upon her throat.

“Bring water! Send for the doctor!”

Basilio lay motionless in the grass. His skin was grotesquely mottled; countless stings rose upon his face and chest; the whip wounds across his back had opened under the violence of the ride. Yet when Violante knelt and lifted his head, his eyes opened.

“Señora,” he breathed.

“Yes. I am here.”

“You came.”

“Hush. You must not speak.”

“You came—for me.”

His astonishment was more heartbreaking than reproach. He had believed himself outside all human pity, and now the woman he had intended to murder held him against her breast.

“Robert,” Violante cried, “the priest! Send for Father Ignacio!”

The mission was miles away. Even the swiftest horse could not bring the priest in time; she knew it as she spoke, and the knowledge filled her with a terror greater than that inspired by the dagger.

Basilio knew it also.

“I die unshriven,” he said.

“No. Make an act of contrition. God sees your heart.”

“My sins—too many.”

“His mercy is greater.”

“I came to kill you.”

“And I forgive you.”

“I asked for pain. I was proud. I thought I could pay for evil with blood.”

“You cannot pay. You can only repent.”

His breathing became shallow. The fierce sun poured upon his face, and Violante motioned for the men to carry him beneath a great live oak. There, in the green shade, she knelt beside him while Robert stood bareheaded and Nicolas remained apart, staring at the whip in his hand as though it were a serpent.

Basilio’s fingers sought hers.

“Pray,” he whispered.

She began in Spanish, slowly and clearly, the prayer her mother had taught her at twilight long ago. Basilio tried to follow. Twice his voice failed; twice he began again. When she spoke the name of Christ, tears gathered in his swollen eyes.

The valley around them was intolerably beautiful. Poppies stirred in the warm wind; larks sang above the pasture; the broken apiary exhaled the sweetness of spilled honey. Nearby, the wounded roan shuddered under wet blankets. Beyond the trees, the other horses had halted and were grazing as peacefully as though no madness had passed among them.

How could nature remain so tranquil beside human agony? Yet perhaps that tranquility was not indifference, but a sign that pain, however enormous to those who endure it, cannot overthrow the divine order forever.

“Alive,” Basilio murmured suddenly.

Violante bent close.

“What is it?”

“You wished me alive.”

“Yes.”

“For this?”

“For repentance. For hope.”

His gaze moved toward Nicolas, then Robert, and finally returned to her.

“Do not blame them.”

She could not answer.

“I asked it,” he continued. “My sin rode me harder than the horse.”

A convulsion passed through him. Violante supported his shoulders with a strength which astonished the watching men.

“Basilio! Basilio, look at me!”

He obeyed.

“Trust in God,” she said. “Not in your suffering. Not in your fear. In God.”

“In God,” he repeated.

She made the sign of the cross upon his forehead. His lips moved in one last supplication—to the Virgin, perhaps, or to the merciful Christ whose name he had invoked first in terror and now in humility. Then the strained lines of his face relaxed.

“Basilio?”

There was no answer.

“Basilio!”

The hand within hers became heavy.

Violante remained kneeling, unable to comprehend the stillness. All afternoon she had fought to preserve that life: at the window, where a shot might have ended it; in the bedchamber, where vengeance demanded it; in the corral, where chastisement threatened it; and among the bees, where she had risked her own body to rescue it. Yet life had escaped at last, not through her failure of courage or pity, but through that tragic chain by which the sins, follies, cruelties, and even merciful intentions of men unite to accomplish what none had willed.

She bowed her head over Basilio’s breast.

“May God receive his soul,” she whispered.

Then she turned toward Nicolas. The foreman’s stern face had broken; he was weeping.

“I did not mean—” he began.

“No,” she said. “No one meant it.”

That was the most terrible truth.

She rose unsteadily. Robert came to her, his arms outstretched, but for a moment she seemed not to recognize him. Her face was swollen from stings, her dress stained with Basilio’s blood and the crushed gold of honey, her loosened hair falling over her shoulders. The romantic lady who had stood among the wistaria was gone; in her place stood a suffering woman, exhausted and disfigured, yet invested with a moral splendor before which every man there lowered his eyes.

“Take him to consecrated ground if Father Ignacio permits,” she said. “Tell him that Basilio repented.”

“I will,” Robert answered.

“And the horse—save it if you can.”

“Yes, my heart.”

“And never again—never give punishment into another man’s hands.”

Robert bowed his head.

“Never.”

She tried to walk toward the hacienda. After two steps the oak, the pasture, and the kneeling men revolved around her. Robert caught her before she reached the ground.

He lifted her as though she were a child.

As he carried her home across the sunlit field, the hard-headed Scotch Presbyterian bent repeatedly to kiss the unconscious face of his Catholic wife; and his tears, which no bodily suffering of his own could have drawn from him, fell without shame upon her wounded hands.

Behind them, beneath the oak, Basilio lay still while the vaqueros kept silent watch. Above the hacienda the purple wistaria stirred at the open window, and far away over the Gabilan Hills the evening light began to gather—tender, solemn, and golden—upon a world in which mercy had proved stronger than vengeance, though not, alas, strong enough to prevent death.

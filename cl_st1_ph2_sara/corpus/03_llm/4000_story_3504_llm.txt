### The Lavender Parasol

At seventeen Ariel Tabor possessed two indisputable treasures: a disposition which expected happiness, and a wedding gown which had belonged to her mother. The disposition had survived many discouragements; the gown, less fortunate, had endured damp, camphor, three removals, and the ingenuity of Miss Selina Wedge, who charged seventy-five cents for altering it and was afterward willing to admit that the task had been beyond human aid.

Nevertheless, on the evening of the Kinsworthy dance, Ariel stood before the small mirror in her bedroom and believed for nearly five minutes that she was beautiful.

The glass, which hung too high and presented only so much of her as could be gathered into a wavering oval, encouraged this belief. It showed her dark eyes, made brilliant by anticipation; her hair, uncertainly but abundantly arranged; her young throat; and the upper portion of the ancient bodice, where Miss Wedge had disposed three artificial roses so as to conceal a rent in the satin. What the mirror did not show was the skirt, which descended with bridal gravity until, six inches from the floor, it encountered the alterations and broke into a series of impulsive puckers.

Ariel bent backward, forward, and sideways, seeking a complete view; but the glass was loyal to illusion.

Her grandfather, waiting below, called that they should be late.

“I’m coming!” she cried, and gave herself one final, radiant look.

She descended slowly, holding the skirt away from her clumsily resoled slippers. Old Roger Tabor, who had not attended a dance since his wife’s death, surveyed her from the foot of the stairs. He was a tall, defeated man whose beard retained something of its former authority though his coat did not. In Canaan it was said that Roger had been ruined by his brother Jonas; it was also said that he had ruined Jonas. We may observe that Canaan seldom allowed a quarrel to languish for want of two explanations.

Roger’s eyes softened.

“You favor your mother,” he said.

That was precisely what Ariel had hoped. She kissed him, and for the second time believed herself beautiful.

The Kinsworthy house stood on upper Main Street, where the elms were oldest, the lawns widest, and mortgages most discreet. Japanese lanterns swung among the trees, carriages arrived in a glitter of harness, and music escaped through the open windows with a gaiety so assured that no one approaching the place could imagine unhappiness within.

Ariel imagined none.

She entered the ladies’ dressing room with her cloak drawn closely about her and found fifteen young women before a cheval-glass. They were the recognized daughters of Canaan: pink, white, blue, and pale green; smooth of shoulder and expert of hand; their ribbons correctly placed, their slippers narrow, their laughter easy. They knew when a sleeve was wrong by half an inch, when a color had been worn the previous season, and when a hopeful stranger failed in both particulars. Knowledge of this kind is seldom taught, yet nowhere are examinations more severe.

Ariel laid aside her cloak.

There followed no open outcry. Canaan’s young ladies had been educated too carefully for that. There came instead a stillness, small and sharp; afterward a rustle; then two whispers and a cough. Mamie Pike, who was considered generous because she sometimes spoke to unpopular people, hurried forward.

“How—how sweet!” she said.

Ariel laughed.

She had expected admiration and interpreted the silence before Mamie’s greeting as surprise. Then she saw herself in the cheval-glass.

The broad mirror was not loyal. It contained her from the ill-arranged crown of her hair to the round toes of the resoled slippers, and it placed behind her, for comparison, the whole bright assembly. The roses upon her bodice were too large and too red. The waist rose unevenly under the arms; the old satin shone where it should have been dull and was dull where it should have shone; the altered skirt appeared to have been shortened during an earthquake.

Ariel stared.

One of the girls stooped to fasten a slipper and remained stooping longer than necessity required. Another turned away to examine a comb, though her shoulders shook. Mamie Pike touched the largest artificial rose.

“Isn’t it quaint?” she asked.

“Yes,” Ariel answered. “It’s tremendously quaint.”

She laughed again, with such spirit that Mamie looked relieved. Ariel’s face continued to smile while the skin beneath her eyes grew hot. Her first belief in her beauty departed; the second lingered painfully because it had been given her by her grandfather.

She went away from the mirror.

In the ballroom the floor shone beneath chandeliers and swinging garlands. The orchestra, imported from Rouen at considerable expense, wore scarlet coats and an expression of forgiving Canaan for being forty miles from a city. Young men stood near the door with the air of merchants awaiting favorable bargains; young women passed them more slowly than nature intended. Introductions were accomplished, programmes consulted, engagements remembered or invented.

Ariel sat against the wall.

At first she supposed this accidental. She looked over her programme and held her pencil ready. Two young men approached, but one was seeking Mamie Pike and the other a chair. A third bowed to Ariel, said, “Delightful evening,” and immediately fled, perhaps overcome by its delightfulness.

She laughed whenever anyone looked at her.

There are smiles which invite society and smiles which dismiss it; Ariel’s, fixed too firmly upon her face, seemed to warn the whole masculine population that she was enjoying some private joke at its expense. The young men, who feared ridicule even more than they feared the ancient gown, avoided her with increasing care.

Dance followed dance. The chair became an island, and Ariel its sole inhabitant.

Across the room she saw girls no prettier than herself borne away by attentive partners. They did not need to speak cleverly; they scarcely spoke at all. They knew when to lower their eyes, when to lift them, how to place a hand upon a sleeve, how to cross the floor without appearing either eager or unwilling. Ariel watched these accomplishments as an exile might watch the ceremonies of a native religion. They had been admitted to the secret. She had not.

Mrs. Kinsworthy, observing the deserted chair, became disturbed. A hostess may tolerate suffering in her guests, but not evidence that her entertainment has failed. She whispered to Mamie, Mamie whispered to her brother Eugene, and Eugene approached Norbert Flitcroft.

Norbert was a large, pale youth whose mother had taught him that stoutness, properly dressed, amounted to dignity. He had danced every dance and was at that moment engaged in consuming an ice with an ardor otherwise absent from his nature.

“See here,” Eugene said, “you must take Ariel Tabor for the next.”

Norbert looked toward the wall and then down at his ice.

“I don’t see why it has to be me.”

“Because you’re free.”

“I was going to sit it out.”

“With whom?”

“Myself.”

Eugene explained that his mother insisted. Norbert’s face assumed the solemnity of one called upon to rescue a child from a burning structure; and, having finished the ice lest heroism spoil it, he crossed the room.

“May I have this?” he asked.

Ariel rose so quickly that one of the roses trembled.

“I thought you’d never come,” she said sprightly. “I’ve been saving it for you all evening.”

Norbert’s eyes moved toward the doors.

“I didn’t know.”

“You should always know when a lady is waiting.”

This was a sentence Ariel had once read in a novel. In the novel it had been spoken by a duchess to a cavalry officer and had produced charming results. Norbert, being neither charming nor cavalry, merely sighed.

They entered the dance.

Ariel could dance; she had practiced with her grandfather’s umbrella in the dining room, and Joe Louden had once shown her a step behind the Tabor stable. But an umbrella does not resent its partner, and Joe had not held himself at the greatest possible distance. Norbert extended his arms as though pushing a heavy object uphill. Ariel, perceiving his reluctance, became uncertain; uncertainty made her stiff; and his efforts to avoid the puckered hem drove them into a series of angular excursions not contemplated by the composer.

“Are we going too fast?” she asked.

“No,” said Norbert mournfully.

“Too slowly?”

“No.”

“Then we’re perfect.”

“I suppose so.”

She laughed. Norbert winced.

They passed Mamie Pike, who was dancing with a visiting lieutenant. Mamie’s glance fell upon the artificial roses, traveled downward, and returned to Ariel’s face with an expression of compassionate alarm. Ariel laughed more loudly. The lieutenant looked around. So did two other couples.

Then she saw the long mirror between the windows.

It gave her the whole truth publicly: Norbert’s rounded figure bent away in comic endurance; her own face bright with the terrible smile; the wedding gown buckling and billowing about her; the dancers behind them graceful, amused, and secure. For one instant she appeared to herself not as a girl at her first dance, but as a grotesque interruption in someone else’s happiness.

Her foot caught in the altered hem.

She stumbled, seized Norbert’s shoulder, and dragged him sideways into a potted palm. The palm rocked; Norbert recovered it with one hand and Ariel with the other. The orchestra continued, but laughter ran along the edge of the room in a thin, irrepressible wave.

“I’m sorry,” Ariel gasped.

Norbert’s face had become a burlesque of martyrdom.

“It’s all right.”

“I believe the palm did it.”

“What?”

“It reached out and caught me.”

Norbert stared at her. She laughed again, because if she did not laugh she must cry, and she would rather have fallen through the floor than cry before them.

When the dance ended, Norbert brought her back to the chair. He bowed so hastily that he resembled a bottle tipping from a shelf, and before Ariel could thank him he departed at a speed remarkable in one of his construction.

She sat through half the next dance.

Her smile began to hurt. There were little pains at the corners of her mouth, and the lights had blurred into rings of yellow. She told herself that she did not care—she did not care, she did not care. Presently, having repeated this often enough to make disbelief unavoidable, she rose and slipped through a side door to the veranda.

The night received her kindly. Beyond the lanterns the garden lay in shadow, and the music came softened through curtains. She went to the far end of the veranda, where a trumpet vine covered the railing, and leaned against a post.

For a little while she remained very still. Then her shoulders moved.

“Don’t,” said a voice from the darkness.

She turned sharply. Joe Louden sat upon the railing with one knee drawn up, almost hidden by the vine. He was nineteen, lean, brown-haired, and badly dressed in a manner more defiant than apologetic. His coat was too short in the sleeves, his collar had surrendered early in the evening, and one shoe bore a scar of mud. He had not been invited to the dance. No one needed to ask how he had come; Joe had always entered Canaan’s forbidden places by alleys, fences, and unfastened windows.

“I’m not doing anything,” Ariel said.

“All right.”

“What are you doing here?”

“Nothing.”

“You weren’t invited.”

“No.”

“You ought to go away.”

“Probably.”

He did not move. Ariel turned her face toward the garden.

“I wasn’t crying,” she said.

“I didn’t say you were.”

“I was laughing.”

“I heard you.”

That was worse. She pressed her handkerchief to her eyes, furious with him for knowing and grateful that he knew.

“They’re having a beautiful time,” she said at last.

Joe glanced through the window. “Looks foolish to me.”

“That’s because they don’t want you.”

“Likely.”

“If they did want us, we’d think it beautiful. We pretend we despise them because they despise us first.”

“I don’t pretend much.”

“You do. You pretend you like being alone.”

Joe lowered his foot from the railing.

“Well,” he said, “sometimes there’s less trouble in it.”

Ariel turned upon him. “But don’t you want to know how? Don’t you want to know what they know? How to talk, and dance, and wear things, and not be afraid every minute that everybody sees you’re wrong? They never have to think about it. They’re happy without trying.”

Joe considered the ballroom, where Norbert Flitcroft, restored by a new partner and two cups of punch, was moving with comparative buoyancy.

“Maybe they try harder than it looks.”

“No. Happiness fits them. It fits their clothes and their houses and their voices. They wake up knowing what to do. And we’re outside—always, always, always!”

“Let ’em have it.”

“I can’t.” Her voice broke. “I want it too. I do, I do.”

Joe looked at her helplessly. He had fought boys for calling her names, stolen peaches for her, and once walked six miles in rain to bring medicine when Roger Tabor was ill; but tears left him without resource. After some hesitation he took a cleanish handkerchief from his pocket, examined it, and put it back.

“They’re fools,” he said.

“So are we.”

“You’re not.”

“They laughed at me.”

“I know.”

“I saw myself in the mirror. I looked—” She stopped.

Joe’s face hardened. “Norbert’s got no call to laugh at anybody.”

“He didn’t laugh. He only hated dancing with me.”

“That’s worse,” Joe said, with such conviction that she almost smiled.

The music ceased, and a louder murmur came from inside. At the front of the house a carriage drew up with unnecessary violence. Someone spoke to a servant; then Roger Tabor appeared in the doorway.

He looked older than he had two hours before.

“Ariel,” he called.

She ran to him, forgetting the traces of tears. “What is it?”

“Your uncle Jonas is dead.”

For a moment she understood only that an old man she scarcely remembered would never speak to them again. Jonas Tabor had left Canaan after the quarrel with Roger and had not written in eleven years. He had gone West, then South, then—according to Canaan—everywhere money might be improperly made. Reports of mines, railways, and plantations had reached them, but no kindness had.

Roger put one hand upon Ariel’s shoulder.

“The telegram says he left everything to us.”

“Everything?”

“It appears,” said Roger faintly, “that everything is a great deal.”

The dancers had begun to gather behind them. News traveled rapidly in Canaan even when obliged to pass through doors; and already Mrs. Kinsworthy’s distress had altered into solicitude, while Mamie Pike was whispering that she had always found Ariel interesting.

Ariel looked from their suddenly attentive faces to Joe, who had drawn deeper into shadow.

Wealth had not yet taught her anything, altered the wedding gown, or dried her eyes; nevertheless, the room regarded her differently. The change was so immediate that even at seventeen she perceived its comedy.

She held out her hand to Joe.

But he had gone.

Five years later, on a June afternoon, Joe Louden crossed the iron bridge at the edge of Canaan and saw a lady coming toward him beneath a lavender parasol.

Joe had returned to town three days before, though few people knew it and fewer approved. He had spent his absence irregularly—reading law in a city office, working upon a newspaper, sleeping in railway coaches, and acquiring the sort of knowledge which Canaan classified as dangerous because it could not be obtained at the county school. He had returned with one valise, a law book, and no visible prosperity.

Canaan had altered less than Joe. The courthouse clock remained seven minutes slow; the same merchants leaned in the same doorways; Judge Pike’s white house still dominated upper Main Street with pillars intended to recall Athens, though they more nearly recalled a bank. The town had heard that Joe meant to practice law and felt this to be characteristic impudence.

He walked with his hat pushed back, watching the river through the bridge rails, until the lavender parasol lifted.

The lady beneath it was tall and moved with a quiet freedom unknown to the girls of Canaan, whose accomplishments in walking had always included the suggestion that they might stop if properly urged. Her dress was of some pale material which gathered the color of the summer haze; her gloves, hat, and parasol agreed without seeming to have met for that purpose. A little cluster of ivory-colored flowers rested at her belt. There was nothing ostentatious in her toilet, and this, to a practiced observer, would have revealed its expense.

Joe was not a practiced observer. He knew only that she belonged to a world in which people crossed oceans without terror and entered great rooms without asking whether they were welcome.

She looked directly at him and smiled.

He removed his hat.

“Mr. Louden?” she said.

Her voice disturbed a memory, but the lady herself abolished it. Joe knew no one whose beauty possessed that peculiar warmth: dark eyes lighted from within, a clear, olive delicacy of skin, and hair with bronze in its shadows. She carried a faint perfume, not floral exactly, but suggestive of gardens after rain and of places beyond railways.

“Yes,” he answered.

“You don’t remember me.”

“I’m afraid—”

“Then Canaan has changed you.”

“I thought Canaan never changed anybody.”

“It didn’t change me.”

She lowered the parasol. Her smile widened, and for an instant there came into it an old uncertainty, a question asked with laughter lest the answer wound.

Joe stopped in the middle of the bridge.

“Ariel?”

She nodded.

He continued to stare. A wagon approached from behind, and the driver shouted; Joe moved aside without hearing him. Ariel laughed—not the desperate laughter of the Kinsworthy dance, but something full and unguarded.

“You look as though I had come back from the dead.”

“Farther than that,” he said.

“Paris is not so far.”

“It was from here.”

They stood facing each other while the river moved under them, brown and slow. Beyond the bridge the country opened in fields of young corn; the sky was whitened at the horizon, and the distant woods appeared through lavender air. Her dress seemed to have borrowed that far color and brought it near.

“When did you come?” he asked.

“This morning. Grandfather and I arrived on the early train. He has gone to rest, and Judge Pike was shut up with papers, so I escaped.”

“Escaped?”

“You know what houses are like when everyone is glad to see you. They lock all the doors with affection.”

Joe smiled. “You’re staying with the Pikes?”

“Until the Tabor house is ready. Judge Pike still manages the property. He says my affairs require endless signatures and a great many solemn gentlemen.”

“He likes solemn gentlemen.”

“Yes. They make property appear moral.”

Joe looked at her quickly, and she laughed again. She had learned more abroad than dress and posture.

They began walking toward town.

He found it difficult to reconcile her with the girl upon the dark veranda. He could see that girl clearly—the trembling roses, the strained smile, the badly altered satin—but she seemed no more than a figure glimpsed in an old mirror. Yet certain gestures survived. Ariel still tilted her head before asking a dangerous question; still pressed her lips together when she feared she had shown too much feeling; still watched Joe as if his least articulate answers deserved interpretation.

“You went away without saying good-bye,” she said.

“So did you.”

“I sent you a letter.”

“I never got it.”

“I sent three.”

Joe looked ahead. “Where?”

“To your father’s house.”

“That explains it.”

Ariel’s face darkened. Canaan knew that Joe and his father had parted violently, but the town’s account of the matter concerned Joe’s wickedness and omitted causes as irrelevant.

“I thought you would write,” she said.

“I didn’t know where.”

“Grandfather’s address was in every letter.”

“I told you. I never got them.”

“Yes—but if you had wanted to know—”

“I did.”

The words came bluntly. Ariel looked down, and the ivory flowers at her belt trembled with her step.

“We were in Florence then,” she said. “Afterward Vienna, and then Paris. Grandfather liked Florence best. He said the old men there knew how to sit still without looking defeated.”

“And you?”

“I liked everywhere at first. I learned French from a widow who despised my accent and Italian from a young count who admired it. There were lessons in music, history, painting, dancing—especially dancing.” She glanced at him. “I became very accomplished.”

“I see that.”

“Do you?”

He flushed, and she rescued him kindly.

“Grandfather said I tried to swallow Europe whole. I think I wanted never again to enter a room without knowing its rules.”

Joe remembered her cry on the veranda: I want it too.

“And now you know them?”

“I know that every room has different rules, and that the people who belong there break them whenever they please.”

“That’s education.”

“It was expensive enough to be.”

They had reached Main Street. Here Canaan began to observe them.

Mr. Waddell, the pharmacist, was placing a display of patent tonic in his window. He set down a bottle and failed to remove his hand from it. Two clerks came to the door of the dry-goods store. A buggy passed, slowed, and nearly collided with a hitching post as its occupants turned to look.

Ariel greeted everyone with composure. She remembered names, inquired after mothers, and made references to letters received years before. Canaan, which had prepared to forgive her foreign residence on account of her fortune, discovered with gratification that she had returned both beautiful and polite.

Joe received a different share of the attention.

It was not merely astonishing that the unknown lady should walk beside him; it was morally unsettling. Joe Louden had long served the town as an example. Mothers mentioned him when sons resisted instruction; merchants blamed him for broken windows without wasting time upon evidence; young women were warned that a civil greeting might encourage him. Now beauty, wealth, and Paris moved at his side beneath a lavender parasol and appeared not only unafraid but pleased.

At the corner of Main and Walnut, Mamie Pike emerged from a milliner’s shop.

She was prettier than at seventeen and more anxious about it. Her hat contained feathers sufficient for a diplomatic ceremony, and at her throat gleamed the ruby necklace which Mrs. Pike had worn before fright and neuralgia persuaded her to remain indoors.

Mamie looked at Ariel, looked again, and uttered a cry.

“My dear!”

The embrace which followed was warm, prolonged, and slightly obstructed by the parasol. Mamie had written Ariel twice in five years—once to announce an engagement which was afterward dissolved, and once to ask whether sleeves were widening in Paris—but she now declared that separation had been unbearable.

“And you never told us you were coming today!”

“I told your father.”

“Papa tells us nothing about business. He thinks women faint at figures. Come home at once. Mamma is simply dying to see you.”

Ariel turned to Joe. “You will come this evening?”

Mamie’s expression altered by less than an inch, yet in that inch Joe read Canaan’s complete position.

“I don’t think so,” he said.

“Then tomorrow.”

“He must come this evening,” Mamie put in brightly, because refusal might imply that her house was not universally desired. “We shall be quite informal.”

Joe looked at Ariel. “Quite informal” at Judge Pike’s meant that a guest might wear an old evening coat if it was not very old.

“I’ll see,” he said.

Ariel held out her hand. “You always say that when you mean no.”

“You remember too much.”

“Not everything,” she answered softly.

Mamie led her away, talking of dinners, picnics, a musicale, and the summer assembly. Joe stood upon the corner and watched the lavender parasol move toward the Pike house. It passed beneath the elms like a piece of the distant haze, then vanished behind the courthouse.

He became aware that three men were regarding him.

One was Eugene Kinsworthy, now connected with his father’s bank; one was a young attorney named Cummings; and the third was Norbert Flitcroft. Norbert’s stoutness had ripened into importance. He wore a striped waistcoat, a watch-chain broad enough to restrain machinery, and a straw hat whose smallness lent him the appearance of a corked bottle.

“Who,” asked Eugene, “was that?”

Joe put on his hat. “Ariel Tabor.”

Norbert made a sound of disbelief.

“That was never Ariel Tabor.”

“It generally is,” Joe said, “when she says so.”

Cummings stared after the vanished parasol. “Miss Tabor? The heiress?”

Norbert’s mouth remained open. We should not accuse him of remembering the artificial roses at that instant; memory, in a gentleman, can be tactful. He remembered only that he had once danced with Ariel and that this ancient intimacy might now be of value.

“I recognized her,” he announced.

“You asked who she was,” Eugene said.

“I was confirming it.”

Norbert began walking rapidly toward the Pike house.

“Where are you going?” Cummings called.

“I have business with the Judge.”

“So have I,” said Cummings.

Within an hour, business with Judge Pike became an epidemic among the unmarried men of Canaan.

By five o’clock Mrs. Pike had risen from a headache to receive Ariel in the south parlor. By six, three carriages had stopped at the gate. At seven, Mamie was arranging an impromptu dinner, and the florist had been asked to send everything he possessed except funeral emblems. At eight, the Judge’s veranda exhibited so many young gentlemen that a stranger might have mistaken the house for a club or a siege.

Ariel moved among them with the ease for which she had once prayed. Her pale evening dress required no artificial roses; a single chain lay against her throat, carrying a small amethyst. She listened, remembered, smiled, and turned each man’s most ordinary remark into something he was pleased to have said. Eugene described banking; Cummings discussed Europe, where he had spent six days; Norbert recalled their dance.

“I was always afraid you’d forgotten it,” he said.

“No,” Ariel answered. “I remember the palm particularly.”

Norbert laughed with great heartiness, though he did not understand.

From the parlor Mamie watched the conquest and experienced the complicated affection of a friend whose guest is too successful. Judge Pike watched also, estimating possibilities. Mrs. Pike touched her ruby necklace and whispered that foreign air had done wonders.

Only Joe did not come.

Ariel went twice to the veranda when footsteps sounded at the gate. The first arrival was the florist’s boy with additional roses; the second was Norbert, returning after changing his waistcoat.

Late in the evening she stood alone for a moment before the tall mirror in the hall.

It gave back a picture without cruelty: the harmonious dress, the composed shoulders, the dark, luminous eyes; behind her, rooms full of flowers and men waiting for her return. This was what she had desired. She had crossed oceans to learn the language of belonging, and now Canaan listened eagerly to every syllable.

Yet mirrors, even the finest, retain an old habit of omission.

The glass did not show the shadowed veranda of five years ago, nor the boy upon the railing who had called the dancers fools because they had hurt her. It did not show Joe standing alone in an office above a harness shop, looking toward the illuminated Pike house and telling himself that he had no place there.

Ariel touched the amethyst at her throat.

Then Mamie called, and she turned from the mirror. When she entered the parlor, the young men rose together with such sudden devotion that chairs scraped on every side.

Canaan had accepted her at last.

The town would say it was because she had become beautiful; because she was rich; because travel had polished her; because Jonas Tabor’s fortune had at length restored a distinguished family. Canaan would say many things, and say them confidently.

But Ariel, smiling upon her eager court, knew that the town’s admiration was less a verdict upon her than a confession concerning itself. She had changed, certainly; she had learned grace, courage, and the uses of a lavender parasol. Yet somewhere within the accomplished young woman lived the girl in the altered wedding gown, still asking whether happiness might admit her.

And because Joe Louden alone had known that girl and had not laughed, it was toward the dark window beyond all those admiring faces that Ariel looked.

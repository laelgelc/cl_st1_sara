## THE BANKER MAN

It was during that raw interval between winter afternoon and winter night, when Washington appears less a city than a conspiracy of gray stone and naked branches, that Captain Ephraim Walker told me the adventure of the Liberty plates.

We were in my library overlooking Sheridan Circle. The lamps had been lighted, but the curtains were not yet drawn, and beyond the glass the diminished world persisted in vague geometrical masses—the curve of the roadway, the black ribs of the trees, and the pale front of the house opposite. On the low table between us stood a decanter, two glasses, and a silver box of cigarettes which Walker regarded with the suspicion of a man accustomed to detecting hidden mechanisms.

He had come to deliver a memoir concerning certain deficiencies in the protection of diplomatic correspondence. The memoir was a report.

Walker himself was very like one: accurate, compact, and without ornament. As chief of a division of the Secret Service he had apprehended counterfeiters, guarded Presidents, examined threatening letters, and discovered ingenious uses for photographic paper, hollow walking sticks, false-bottomed valises, and widows in mourning. He was efficient. Indeed, his only defect as an officer was that he possessed no imagination whatever.

This, I had once told him, was a considerable advantage.

“Imagination,” I said, “is the mother of false clues.”

“It is also,” he replied, “the mother of detectives in books.”

He was a large man with square shoulders, a smooth, rather heavy face, and steady blue eyes. Nothing disturbed the arrangement of his features. He might have announced the fall of a government in precisely the tone he used to request another lump of sugar.

On this evening I had been discussing the romantic criminal—that gifted fellow, now regrettably scarce, who leaves a white rose beside the rifled safe or sends a polite telegram to the prefect of police before stealing the crown jewels.

Walker listened without encouragement.

“At any rate,” I concluded, “you Americans have never produced a really distinguished master criminal.”

He turned his glass once between his fingers.

“No?”

“You have produced excellent burglars, audacious confidence men, and bankers of almost supernatural resource. But the criminal as artist—no.”

“There was Mulehaus,” he said.

The name was familiar. Otto Mulehaus had been variously described as German, Swiss, Austrian, and Alsatian. He was credited with military service under one flag and maritime service under three. Before the war he had appeared in Buenos Aires as an exporter of hides, in Rotterdam as a ship chandler, and in Chicago as a manufacturer of artificial leather. During the war his activities had become political and obscure. Afterward he devoted himself, with apparent impartiality, to the currencies of the victorious nations.

I smiled.

“A forger.”

“A counterfeiter,” said Walker.

“Merely?”

“He stole the government plates for the five-hundred-dollar Liberty Bond.”

I put down my cigarette.

“That is better.”

“He returned them, too.”

“Repentance?”

Walker’s expression did not change, but I observed a minute hardening about his mouth.

“No,” he said. “Expenses.”

He drank, set the glass carefully on the table, and after a pause began to speak.

---

“You remember the Liberty loans,” said Walker. “Every engraving plant, every bank, every post office, and every government department concerned with the bonds had been watched because there was a market for good counterfeits before the ink was dry on the genuine paper. The plates I’m talking about were not ordinary reproductions. They were the plates—the engraved government plates from which a particular issue of five-hundred-dollar bonds could be printed.

“How they got out is another story. There were three men in it besides Mulehaus, and two of them went to prison. The third died. None of them knew where the plates had gone.

“That was like Mulehaus. He used assistants for the dangerous part, but he never let them know the end of the operation.

“When the plates disappeared, we moved quickly. We notified every port, railway junction, express office, engraving concern, paper manufacturer, and bank clearinghouse that mattered. We watched men who could print, men who could engrave, and men who knew the market. We took samples from paper mills. We opened crates under warrants. We put agents among bond dealers in New York, Philadelphia, Baltimore, Boston, and Chicago. We had men on the Canadian border and men on ships.

“We knew Mulehaus had planned the theft. We did not know where he was.

“His description was useless. One witness remembered a stout man with a red beard. Another remembered a thin man without any beard at all. In Baltimore he had passed for a minister. In Hoboken he was said to have limped. A Philadelphia hotel clerk swore that he had only one arm, while a barber in Newark had shaved both of them—or, at any rate, had shaved a man with two arms whom we believed to be Mulehaus.

“He spoke English, German, French, Dutch, and enough Spanish to sell things. He could alter his voice. He understood make-up, but he did not depend on it. That point was important, although I failed to understand how important it was.

“A false beard can be pulled off. A manner cannot.

“For three weeks we found nothing. No bonds appeared. No printer approached the known fences. No ship carried the plates, so far as our searches went. Every day increased the chance that Mulehaus would discover some road we had overlooked.

“I had been sleeping at the office and eating when somebody reminded me. The director finally ordered me away for forty-eight hours.

“‘Go where there is air,’ he said.

“I went to Atlantic City.

“It was in February, and the place had that out-of-season look common to summer resorts in winter. Half the hotels appeared to be asleep. The boardwalk was broad, windy, and sparsely inhabited. The ocean had the color of gunmetal. Canvas signs slapped against closed amusement booths, and the rolling chairs moved up and down like invalid beetles.

“I took a room facing the sea. I intended to sleep, but after two hours I found myself standing at the window, examining every man who passed below.

“That is the trouble with police work. It is not an occupation you put into a drawer with your badge.

“In the afternoon I walked north along the boardwalk. The wind was sharp enough to clear my head. A few well-wrapped visitors sat in rolling chairs, pushed by attendants who leaned forward against the weather. The attendants were an assorted crew—Negro boys, old seamen, stranded laborers, and men who had reached that stage of failure at which any uniform, however ridiculous, becomes a professional distinction.

“One of them attached himself to me.

“He was pushing an empty chair. He wore a cap too small for him, a stained military overcoat, trousers polished at the knees, and broken shoes. His beard was the color of dust. His eyes watered in the wind. The odor that accompanied him was composed of whisky, wet wool, stale tobacco, and that sour indefinable atmosphere which belongs to lodging houses where the windows are never opened.

“‘Chair, Governor?’ he said. ‘Nice ride. See the ocean without tirin’ the understandin’.’

“I declined.

“He pushed beside me.

“‘Half a dollar down to the pier.’

“‘No.’

“‘Quarter, then. Special winter rate. Snow, rain, bankruptcy, or domestic trouble don’t interfere with our service.’

“I told him I preferred to walk.

“‘That’s what they all say before the rheumatiz gets organized.’

“He had a flattened cigarette stub tucked behind one ear. He transferred it to his mouth, found it extinct, and flourished it at me while talking. His nose was long and mobile. It seemed to investigate the air independently of the rest of his face.

“I asked him why he was not attending to business.

“‘I am, Governor. You’re business. Leastways, you got the clothes of business.’

“‘And you have the breath of bankruptcy.’

“He considered that.

“‘Bankruptcy’s honest,’ he said. ‘It don’t pretend it can pay.’

“I had been thinking about Mulehaus. I don’t know why I said what I did. Fatigue, perhaps. Irritation. Possibly I wanted to get rid of him.

“‘Can you find men?’ I asked.

“He blinked.

“‘Men?’

“‘A particular man.’

“‘Depends. Husband, witness, or fellow what owes you money?’

“‘A German.’

“‘Plenty of them.’

“‘A former soldier.’

“‘Still plenty.’

“‘He has also been a sailor.’

“‘That narrows the regiment.’

“‘And at one time he worked as a tanner.’

“The attendant stopped pushing. His face assumed an expression of profound official attention.

“‘German soldier, sailor, and tanner,’ he repeated. ‘What name?’

“‘The name wouldn’t help you.’

“‘Might help him.’

“I laughed.

“‘Find such a man and I’ll give you twenty dollars.’

“‘Cash?’

“‘Cash.’

“‘No questions about how I find him?’

“‘As many as I please.’

“‘But the sawbuck twins is mine if he fits?’

“‘Yes.’

“He put out a hand. It was black at the nails and trembled slightly. I did not take it.

“‘You’re on, Governor,’ he said. ‘Where do you roost?’

“I made the mistake of telling him my hotel.

“Then I forgot him.

“That evening I received reports by telephone. Nothing had developed. A suspect detained in Boston was a piano tuner from Milwaukee. A box searched in New York contained machine parts, as the manifest declared. Two agents had followed a Dutch lithographer for thirty-six hours and discovered only that he was unfaithful to his wife.

“I slept badly.

“At ten the next morning the house detective telephoned my room.

“‘There’s a bum in the lobby asking for you,’ he said. ‘He won’t go away.’

“‘Put him out.’

“‘We did. He came back through the kitchen.’

“I remembered the rolling-chair attendant.

“‘Send him up.’

“‘To your room?’

“‘Send him up.’

“He arrived carrying his cap in both hands and moving with an exaggerated humility that made the bellboy follow him suspiciously to the door. His beard looked damper than before. One eye was inflamed. He smelled of coffee, whisky, and the morning.

“‘Found him, Governor,’ he announced.

“‘Found whom?’

“His disappointment was artistic.

“‘Why, your German soldier-sailor-tanner. Banker man.’

“‘What banker?’

“‘The man I found. Looks like a banker. Dresses like one. Eats like one. Gives the waiter a dime and looks like he founded an orphan asylum.’

“I told him to sit down. He chose the edge of a chair, placed his cap between his knees, and glanced at the carpet as if calculating what it would bring in a secondhand shop.

“‘What makes you think he is German?’ I asked.

“‘He counts in German.’

“‘You heard him?’

“‘He was payin’ for breakfast at a lunch room off Atlantic Avenue. Had his money under the table—don’t ask me why. I’m at the counter, studyin’ a sinker, when I hear him say, “Ein, zwei, drei,” quiet-like. Then he catches himself and finishes in English.’

“‘A German banker is not rare.’

“‘This one was a soldier.’

“‘How do you know?’

“The attendant raised his hands.

“‘Governor, did you ever watch a man walk when he thinks nobody’s lookin’?’

“‘Often.’

“‘Then you know his feet remember things his head forgets. This banker man walks common enough in a crowd. But this mornin’ there’s a hand organ outside, bangin’ out a march. He turns the corner alone, hears it, and the left leg goes stiff. Not a limp. A kick. Heel down, toe up, knee locked. Just once. Then he loosens. German goosestep, Governor. He drilled till the bones learned it.’

“That was possible. It was not proof.

“‘The sailor?’ I asked.

“‘He don’t look at the ocean.’

“‘Most sailors do.’

“‘No, Governor. Tourists look at the ocean. They stare at every wave like it’s Sarah Bernhardt performin’ special. Sailors look at weather, smoke, riggin’, and what’s comin’ through the inlet. This banker man never wastes an eye on water. But a steamer shows beyond the pier, and he gives her one look—one!—and knows where she’s bound from the smoke and the way she sits. Then he watches the channel markers.’

“‘He told you this?’

“‘He ain’t told me good mornin’.’

“‘Then you are guessing.’

“‘Sure. Sleuthin’ is educated guessin’, same as medicine, only the corpse complains sooner.’

“I ignored that.

“‘And the tannery?’

“The attendant’s mobile nose wrinkled with pleasure.

“‘Hands.’

“‘What about them?’

“‘Clean. Nails brushed. Pink as a baby’s. But the thumbs is spread and flat, and the first two fingers turn in. Man works wet hides long enough, pullin’ and scrapin’, the hands get shaped to it. You can wash tannin’ out of the skin, but you don’t wash it out of the joints.’

“I had seen tannery hands. Again, his observation was possible.

“‘Where is this man?’

“‘Around.’

“‘That is not an address.’

“‘He’s stayin’ at the Montrose under the name of Mr. Henry B. Carter, Cincinnati. Maybe he’s Carter and maybe Cincinnati’s in China. He wears a black coat, gray spats, pearl pin, and a hat that costs more than my education. Banker man.’

“He repeated the phrase with satisfaction.

“‘What has he done?’

“‘Walked.’

“‘Where?’

“‘That’s worth the twenty.’

“I took out my wallet. He leaned forward, then checked himself.

“‘When I see the man,’ I said.

“His face collapsed into grievance.

“‘Governor, I worked all night.’

“‘Doing what?’

“‘Lookin’ for a German soldier that’s been a sailor and tanner. You think they’re hangin’ in bunches under the boardwalk?’

“‘Show him to me.’

“He sighed, put on his cap, and stood up.

“‘All right. But if he gets suspicious because a swell government gentleman goes paradin’ after him, it ain’t my funeral.’

“I looked at him sharply.

“‘Who said I was a government officer?’

“He opened his mouth, shut it, and worked his nose.

“‘Nobody.’

“‘Then why did you say it?’

“‘I said gentleman.’

“‘You said government gentleman.’

“He scratched beneath his beard.

“‘Well, you got government shoes.’

“‘What are government shoes?’

“‘Good shoes bought by a man that don’t enjoy ’em.’

“That answer was nonsense, but it delayed me. In our profession one becomes accustomed to the sudden accurate intuition of hotel clerks, cab drivers, and bartenders. They recognize an official manner without understanding how. I concluded that he had done the same.

“I changed my collar, took a soft hat, and put my revolver in my overcoat pocket. He watched all this with impudent interest.

“‘Expectin’ the banker to foreclose?’ he asked.

“We went out by a side entrance.

“The attendant had left his rolling chair in an alley. He pushed it before him, explaining that it was his ‘professional alibi.’ We moved south through streets of small hotels and shuttered boardinghouses. He walked with the loose-kneed shuffle of a man whose muscles had been dissolved by liquor. Twice he stopped to pick up cigarette ends. Once he begged a match from a delivery boy and appeared crushed when refused.

“‘Where is Carter?’ I asked.

“‘Walkin’.’

“‘Where?’

“‘He takes the same constitutional every day. Boardwalk first, then Pacific, then up toward the inlet.’

“‘How many days have you watched him?’

“‘Since yesterday.’

“‘You told me you found him last night.’

“‘I found the trail last night. Found him on it this mornin’.’

“‘What trail?’

“He tapped the side of his nose.

“‘Banker man buys things.’

“‘What things?’

“‘Rope. Little pulley. Tarpaulin. Half a dozen bolts. One iron handle. Two bottles of machine oil. And sandwiches.’

“‘That is a miscellaneous crime.’

“‘Depends where he takes ’em.’

“Near a cigar shop he suddenly swung the rolling chair across my path and bent as though adjusting a wheel.

“‘There,’ he whispered.

“A man had come out of a hotel half a block ahead.

“He was fifty, perhaps, broad in the middle, clean-shaven, and carefully dressed. The gray spats and pearl pin were as described. His cheeks had the smooth sedentary fullness one associates with private offices and substantial balances. He carried a cane, not because he required it but because it completed him. Had I been asked to name his profession, I should have said banker.

“‘Carter?’ I asked.

“‘Banker man,’ breathed the attendant.

“We followed.

“Carter went to the boardwalk, turned north, and proceeded at a moderate pace. He looked into shopwindows. He bought a newspaper. He stood for several minutes beside the rail while gulls dipped over the broken water.

“‘You said he never looks at the ocean,’ I murmured.

“‘He ain’t lookin’ at it now.’

“Carter’s newspaper was open, but his eyes passed above it. Far out, a small freighter moved northward, her smoke trailing low.

“‘He is looking at the ship,’ I said.

“‘He’s lookin’ at what’s behind her.’

“I saw nothing behind her but sea and winter haze.

“Presently Carter folded the paper and resumed his walk. He moved well, without stiffness. I saw no sign of the goosestep.

“We kept at a distance. The attendant’s conduct made concealment unnecessary. People avoided him. Since I appeared to be inspecting a rolling chair for hire, no one considered us together.

“Carter descended from the boardwalk and crossed toward the inlet district. The neighborhood became poorer. Hotels gave place to weathered cottages, sheds, boatyards, and stretches of sand where broken spars and rusted cable lay half buried. The wind carried smells of salt, coal smoke, fish, and marsh mud.

“Carter entered a small grocery. The attendant rolled his chair into the lee of a building.

“‘Sandwiches,’ he said.

“Carter emerged with a paper parcel. He walked on to a row of fishermen’s shacks, chose one of gray boards with a patched tar-paper roof, and disappeared around the seaward side.

“‘There’s your man,’ said the attendant.

“‘What is the building?’

“‘Used to belong to a fellow rents skiffs. Storm busted his landing last fall. Place been empty since.’

“‘Did Carter go inside?’

“‘Maybe.’

“‘Did you see him?’

“‘Yesterday.’

“‘What did he carry?’

“‘Nothin’.’

“‘Then why are we here?’

“‘Because day before yesterday he carried somethin’ heavy.’

“I turned on him.

“‘You said you found him last night.’

“‘Found out about him last night. Been seein’ him without knowin’ I’d found him. A man can look at a thing Monday and not see it till Tuesday.’

“‘What did he carry?’

“‘Trunk.’

“‘Alone?’

“‘With another fellow.’

“‘Describe the other man.’

“‘Couldn’t. Saw him from the back. Short coat, cap, ordinary legs.’

“‘Where did the trunk come from?’

“‘Handcart.’

“‘Where is the cart?’

“‘Gone.’

“‘And the other man?’

“‘Gone.’

“‘How long was Carter in the shed?’

“‘Maybe twenty minutes. Then both come out without the trunk.’

“‘Why didn’t you tell me this at the hotel?’

“He looked astonished.

“‘Governor, if I told you the supper before the soup, you’d choke.’

“I had begun to suspect the obvious. The attendant had somehow learned who I was and what reward had been offered. A government notice promising five thousand dollars for recovery of the plates had been circulated in police channels. He might have acquired information about stolen property hidden in the shed and invented the soldier-sailor-tanner story to induce me to act without involving local officers.

“Perhaps the trunk held whisky, narcotics, or stolen merchandise. Perhaps there was no trunk.

“‘You want the twenty dollars,’ I said.

“‘I got ambitions.’

“‘You know there is a larger reward.’

“For an instant his watery eyes became blank. Then he grinned.

“‘How much?’

“‘You tell me.’

“‘Governor, if I knew, I’d be talkin’ to the reward instead of you.’

“Carter came from behind the shed. He no longer carried the parcel. He paused on the sand, struck a match in the hollow of his hands, and lit a cigar. The gesture exposed his thumbs. At that distance I could not inspect them closely, but they did appear broad at the tips.

“He walked inland past us without a glance.

“As he crossed a strip of hard sand, a gust brought the faint rhythm of music from the amusement pier. Some brass band was rehearsing. Carter’s left foot swung forward stiffly. Heel down. Toe elevated. It was an odd, momentary movement, corrected at once.

“The attendant saw me see it.

“‘Bones,’ he said softly. ‘Bones don’t forget.’

“We waited until Carter had disappeared.

“‘Now,’ I said, ‘we shall look at the shed.’

“He seized my sleeve.

“‘Not yet.’

“‘Why?’

“‘Sand.’

“‘What about it?’

“‘Let him get far enough he won’t see us readin’ his mail.’

“He shuffled toward the place where Carter had stopped to light his cigar. The sand was damp and firm. Several impressions crossed it—boots, bare feet, dog tracks, and the thin curved marks of a bicycle.

“The attendant crouched.

“‘Here’s him comin’ out.’

“He pointed to a line of neat shoe prints leading from the shed toward the road.

“‘How do you know they lead out rather than in?’

“‘Sand shoves forward from the toe when you walk. See this little ridge? Out. Besides, heel cuts deeper when the foot lands. Toe cuts deeper when it lifts. A track’s got a front and back same as a chair.’

“He moved a few yards toward the shack.

“‘Here’s where he went in.’

“I saw another line of prints, older and less distinct.

“‘Those may belong to anyone.’

“‘Same shoes. Left heel’s got a nick like a bite. Banker man’s left heel’s got it too.’

“He found the nick in the fresher outward track and placed a dirty fingernail against it.

“‘Very well,’ I said. ‘He entered and came out.’

“‘Today, yes. Now look here.’

“Near the back of the shed the ground had been trampled. He traced two parallel sets of impressions, partly obscured by windblown sand.

“‘Day before yesterday,’ he said. ‘Banker man and ordinary legs.’

“‘You cannot date a footprint to the day.’

“‘Rain night before last. These was made before the rain stopped but after it got light. Crust around the edges, soft underneath. Yesterday’s prints is sharp. Today’s is sharper.’

“He indicated one set.

“‘Banker man. Same nick. Other fellow over there.’

“‘They walked to the shed. That agrees with your story.’

“‘They walked heavy.’

“‘How can you know that?’

“He gave me a pitying glance.

“‘Spacing.’

“He laid his hand between two successive prints.

“‘Man takin’ a common step at their height spreads farther. Carry weight, he shortens up. Both sets is deep at the outside, because the load hangs between ’em and pulls their bodies inward. Left fellow takes little side steps where his end swings. Banker man’s right shoe slides twice. Trunk was too heavy and handles too narrow.’

“‘You saw the trunk.’

“‘Sure.’

“‘Then the tracks prove nothing you did not already know.’

“‘They prove I ain’t lyin’.’

“That, I admit, was a point.

“We went to the shed. The door was secured by a new padlock. One side faced the inlet; the opposite side stood against a hummock of coarse grass. There were no windows, only a square opening beneath the eaves covered by wire mesh.

“The attendant examined the lock without touching it.

“‘Banker man’s,’ he said.

“‘How do you know?’

“‘Old door, old staple, new lock. He bought one of them bolts.’

“‘You said he bought six.’

“‘Other five is inside, I expect.’

“I took a small pick case from my pocket.

“‘Government shoes and burglar tools,’ he remarked. ‘Ain’t civilization wonderful?’

“The padlock was cheap. I opened it in less than a minute.

“Inside, the place smelled of damp rope and rotting wood. Light entered through cracks in the boards. A broken oar, two lobster pots, a coil of useless line, and a rusted stove stood against the walls. The center was empty. There was no trunk.

“The attendant did not appear discouraged.

“He sniffed. He peered under the stove. He struck the walls with his knuckles, tested loose boards, and finally dropped to his knees on the packed-earth floor.

“‘Where is your trunk?’ I asked.

“‘Here.’

“‘I see no trunk.’

“‘That’s why they hid it.’

“‘There is no room beneath this floor. We are nearly at water level.’

“‘Room enough for a flat trunk.’

“He crawled slowly, studying the earth. At one point he stopped and brushed away a sprinkling of sand.

“‘Dug here.’

“I looked. The floor appeared uniform.

“‘It has been packed,’ I said. ‘You cannot tell.’

“‘That’s what packin’s for.’

“He got up, went to a corner, and picked up a dented galvanized bucket.

“‘Need water.’

“‘For what?’

“‘To see what don’t want seein’.’

“We left the shed, locked it, and walked to a public pump beside a boatyard. The attendant filled the bucket. On the way back he spilled water over his broken shoes and cursed them with an eloquence suggesting long acquaintance.

“I was increasingly uncomfortable. If Carter returned and found us, I could hold him, but on what charge? Walking stiff-legged? Possessing flat thumbs? Hiding sandwiches? As for the shed, I had entered without a warrant.

“Still, Mulehaus had served in the German Army. He had gone to sea. He had worked in hides. Those three facts had never been published together.

“The attendant unlocked the door with the key to his rolling chair. Apparently my use of the pick had rendered the lock cooperative.

“He carried the bucket inside and poured the water gently over the section of packed floor he had selected.

“At first nothing occurred. The earth darkened. Water collected in shallow hollows and crept toward the walls.

“‘Your experiment is a failure,’ I said.

“‘Wait.’

“We waited.

“A small bubble rose near the center of the wet patch and broke. Then another appeared six inches away. In a moment a crooked line of bubbles trembled across the floor.

“The attendant’s nose became active.

“‘There she is.’

“‘What causes it?’

“‘Loose dirt holds air. Ground around it’s packed hard by years. Water soaks down. Air comes up where somebody’s dug and filled. Bubbles draw the edge for you.’

“He took a clasp knife from his pocket and marked the line. The disturbed area was rectangular, about four feet by two.

“We found a shovel behind the stove.

“The top layer had been tamped and disguised with dust from the old floor. Beneath it the soil was loose. At eighteen inches the shovel struck wood.

“We cleared away the dirt.

“It was a black trunk, low and broad, wrapped in tarred canvas. Iron handles had been bolted to both ends. The attendant ran one filthy finger over the metal.

“‘There’s your handle and bolts. Banker man believes in home improvements.’

“Together we lifted the trunk. It was heavy enough to account for the tracks. The lock yielded to a blow from the shovel.

“Inside were two wool blankets, a sailor’s sweater, three bricks, and a quantity of old newspapers.

“The attendant stared at them.

“‘Well,’ he said, ‘maybe the banker reads in bed.’

“I examined the trunk. Its outside depth was greater than its inside depth by at least four inches.

“‘False bottom,’ I said.

“‘Governor, you’re learnin’.’

“The interior lining was secured by screws painted black. We removed them with the blade of his knife. Beneath the panel lay a package wrapped in oiled silk and fitted so tightly that a canvas loop had been provided to lift it.

“I knew what the package contained before I opened it.

“The steel plates were covered with grease. Even in the dim light I could see the fine reverse engraving—the border, the denominational figures, the portrait, and the elaborate government lettering. There were ancillary pieces as well: seal plates, serial devices, and guide marks.

“The whole missing set.

“It was a hell of a thing.

“For three weeks we had searched trains, ships, banks, warehouses, and printing plants. We had photographed suspects and examined paper under microscopes. The plates lay under eighteen inches of dirt in a deserted skiff shed, discovered by a drunk who pushed rolling chairs.

“I turned on him.

“He was sitting on the overturned bucket, rubbing his nose with the back of his wrist.

“‘Who are you?’ I said.

“‘Me? I’m temporarily connected with the passenger transportation industry.’

“‘How did you know those plates were here?’

“‘Didn’t.’

“‘You knew there was a trunk.’

“‘Saw it.’

“‘Why were you watching Carter?’

“‘Wasn’t. I sleep around here sometimes. Warm enough in the sheds if the wind’s right. Saw him and the other fellow cart the trunk. Banker man around a poor neighborhood is like a silk hat on a pig. You notice.’

“‘Then why did you wait until I offered twenty dollars?’

“‘Before you offered, it was somebody’s trunk. After you offered, it was employment.’

“‘How did you know what I wanted?’

“‘You said German soldier, sailor, tanner.’

“‘That did not tell you about the plates.’

“‘Still don’t. What are them things?’

“He leaned forward with an expression of almost childlike curiosity.

“I covered the steel with the oiled silk.

“‘Government property.’

“‘Knew you had government shoes.’

“‘You are lying.’

“His face became mournful.

“‘Governor, a man in my condition ain’t got strength for the truth all day.’

“I drew my revolver and held it down by my side.

“He looked at it and slowly lifted both hands.

“‘Now don’t get nervous. Firearms makes me thirsty.’

“‘Stand up.’

“He obeyed.

“I searched him thoroughly. His pockets contained string, matches, two cigarette stubs, a spoon, a brass washer, a hotel soap wrapper, seven cents, and a small flask holding about one swallow of bad whisky. There was no weapon, no key, no paper, and no identification.

“His hands trembled. His body beneath the overcoat felt thin and poorly nourished. The beard was dirty, uneven, and apparently real. When I tugged it, he yelped.

“‘That’s attached, Governor.’

“I made him roll up his sleeves. His arms were grimy. One bore an old blue tattoo of an anchor. Another had a white scar running from wrist to elbow.

“‘You were a sailor,’ I said.

“‘Me and half the respectable drunks on the coast.’

“‘Army?’

“‘They took me, but they give me back.’

“‘Tannery?’

“‘No, Governor. I got limits.’

“I studied his thumbs. They were narrow, not flattened.

“A sound outside stopped the questioning.

“Someone was approaching across the sand.

“I put the attendant behind the door and took cover beside it. The footsteps paused at the padlock. Metal clicked. The door moved inward.

“The man who entered was not Carter. He was a young fisherman in hip boots, carrying a coil of new rope. I seized him before he could retreat.

“He struggled, swore, and then went limp when he saw my badge.

“His name was Leon Dacey. He admitted that Carter had paid him ten dollars to help move a trunk from a handcart into the shed. He knew nothing of the contents. That morning Carter had told him to bring rope at three o’clock and wait. A motor launch was expected after dark. Dacey was to help put the trunk aboard.

“‘What launch?’ I asked.

“He did not know. Carter had said it would show two green lights, one above the other.

“The attendant, listening behind me, gave a soft whistle.

“‘Transfer,’ he said. ‘Launch takes it outside. Freighter picks it up. Your banker don’t need to sail.’

“That explanation fitted. We had agents aboard outgoing vessels and inspectors at the docks, but an innocent freighter could take an object transferred beyond the harbor line. It was a simple method, dependent chiefly upon timing.

“I telephoned from the boatyard office. Within forty minutes six agents and two local policemen arrived. We removed the plates, replaced their weight with scrap iron, restored the false bottom, buried the trunk, and put the shed in order. Dacey agreed to cooperate.

“We waited for Carter.

“He did not come.

“At dusk a motor launch appeared beyond the inlet, showed two green lights, and held position. It remained there fifteen minutes, then turned seaward.

“We searched the Montrose Hotel. Henry B. Carter had vacated his room at noon, leaving no luggage and no forwarding address. The signature on the register was false. A barber remembered shaving him, but could add nothing. The gray spats, pearl pin, black coat, and expensive hat had vanished with the banker man.

“I put every available officer after him. Stations were watched. Roads were covered. Descriptions went out by telegraph.

“We found the cane in a trash barrel three blocks from the hotel. Inside it was a rolled chart of the inlet and shipping channel.

“We never found Carter.

“At nine that evening I returned to the shed. The rolling-chair attendant was waiting outside, sheltered from the wind by the wall.

“‘Twenty dollars,’ he said.

“‘You’ll get it.’

“‘Now?’

“‘Now.’

“I gave him two ten-dollar bills.

“He held them toward the last gray light as if examining rare documents. Then he folded them carefully and placed them inside his shoe.

“‘Banks ain’t safe,’ he explained.

“‘There is also a government reward.’

“His nose twitched.

“‘How much?’

“‘Five thousand dollars for information leading to recovery of the plates.’

“He sat down abruptly in the sand.

“‘Five thousand?’

“‘Yes.’

“‘Dollars?’

“‘Yes.’

“‘Same kind as these?’

“‘The same kind.’

“He took off his cap.

“‘Governor,’ he said reverently, ‘I always believed in government, even when it was arrestin’ me.’

“His claim presented a problem. Reward claims require names, addresses, sworn statements, and proof. He disliked all four. After prolonged argument he called himself James W. Bilkins. He had no address. The local police knew him as Soapy, but could not say whether that was a first name, last name, or professional criticism.

“His statement was taken. It was the most remarkable official document I ever received. According to Bilkins, he had spent the night ‘pursuin’ clues between drinkin’ intervals’; had identified Carter by ‘his military leg, salt-water eye, and leather thumbs’; and had discovered the plates through ‘wet science.’

“The department attempted to reject the claim on several grounds. I insisted.

“The man had found the plates. Without him we should not have recovered them. Whatever his methods or motives, the reward was his.

“Three weeks later the Treasury issued a check for five thousand dollars payable to James W. Bilkins.

“We could not find him to deliver it.

“He had left his rolling chair with another attendant, settled a lodging-house debt of one dollar and eighty cents, and disappeared. The bartender who last saw him said Bilkins had been sober. This circumstance made the bartender remember him particularly.

“We advertised discreetly. At last he presented himself at our Philadelphia office. He was still filthy, still bearded, and still wearing the military overcoat. An agent escorted him to Washington.

“I received him in my office.

“He looked around at the steel files, wall maps, telephones, and framed commissions.

“‘Nice place, Governor. Everybody here suspicious?’

“‘Most of us.’

“‘Must be restful.’

“I showed him the check.

“His hands began to tremble.

“‘All mine?’

“‘Subject to identification.’

“His face lengthened.

“‘I told you who I am.’

“‘You told me you were James W. Bilkins.’

“‘Ain’t that enough? A man oughtn’t have to be more than one person to collect honest money.’

“We had obtained two old arrest records under the name Soapy Bilkins, one for vagrancy and one for drunkenness. The fingerprints on those records matched his. That satisfied the accountants.

“I handed him the check.

“He turned it over.

“‘What do I do with it?’

“‘Deposit it in a bank.’

“He looked wounded.

“‘After all I done for the government, you send me to a banker?’

“One of my men took him to a bank. The bank, after telephoning us twice, opened an account. Bilkins drew out five hundred dollars in cash and requested the rest in hundred-dollar bills. This was refused until the check cleared.

“The next morning he withdrew the balance.

“Then he disappeared again.

“We continued looking for Carter and Mulehaus. Fingerprints from the hotel room were blurred or belonged to employees. The hair found in a comb was human, gray, and not attached to any useful person. The waiter from the lunch room remembered that Carter spoke cultivated English and drank hot water before breakfast. A clerk at the hardware store recalled the purchase of bolts, pulley, oil, handle, and tarpaulin, but could not identify photographs.

“The motor launch was discovered abandoned near Barnegat. It had been stolen. The freighter seen offshore proved to be Norwegian and innocent.

“The investigation settled into paperwork.

“Then, two months later, I received a letter.”

Walker stopped and refilled his glass.

Outside the library windows, Sheridan Circle had disappeared into darkness. I drew the curtains and returned to my chair.

“A letter from Carter?” I asked.

“From Mulehaus,” said Walker.

He took a folded paper from his pocketbook. It was not, I judged, the original, but an official photographic copy. He handed it to me.

The letter was typed upon cheap paper. There was neither date nor address.

> My dear Captain Walker:
>
> Permit me first to congratulate you upon the recovery of the Liberty Bond plates. They were excellent plates, and I regretted abandoning them.
>
> You had, however, made their removal from the country unnecessarily difficult. Your men were on the trains, at the docks, aboard the vessels, and even among the crews. I learned that three of the ships available to me carried your agents. Thus the plates ceased to be property and became evidence.
>
> Evidence is expensive to keep.
>
> I therefore placed the plates where you could recover them, but I could not safely inform you in my own character. You might have misunderstood my public spirit.
>
> Mr. Henry B. Carter served to attract attention to the shed. He was a useful banker, but bankers are watched when they enter poor neighborhoods. Unfortunately, you came nearer to arresting him than I expected.
>
> Mr. James W. Bilkins was more successful. No one watches a dirty man unless he asks for money. You will remember that Bilkins did ask for money. You gave him twenty dollars, and afterward the Government of the United States gave him five thousand.
>
> This sum has covered the principal expenses of the operation, including the original acquisition of the plates, the hire of assistants, costumes, transportation, and certain unavoidable entertainment charges.
>
> I trust, therefore, that our accounts may be considered balanced.
>
> You made one error, Captain. A disguise is not something a man puts on. It is something he permits other men to believe.
>
> With admiration for your honesty in paying rewards,
>
> OTTO MULEHAUS

I read the letter twice.

Then I looked at Walker.

“The hobo?”

“Mulehaus.”

“And Carter?”

“Mulehaus.”

“The same man?”

“The same man.”

“But you saw them within a few minutes of each other.”

“I saw Carter walk past us. Later I saw the attendant beside me. There was an interval while Carter disappeared toward the town and the attendant waited with me near the shed. Enough time, as subsequent experiments proved, if clothes and materials had already been hidden in one of the neighboring shacks.”

“The beard?”

“Real hair attached with spirit gum and worked into his own stubble. He let it remain for weeks. The dirt was real. So was the smell.”

“The trembling hands?”

“Partly acted, partly produced by withholding alcohol after establishing a genuine temporary dependence. The department physician believed that possible.”

“And the scar? The tattoo?”

“His own.”

“Then he had indeed been a sailor.”

“Yes.”

“And a soldier?”

“Yes.”

“And a tanner?”

Walker’s mouth tightened.

“Yes.”

I laughed.

He did not.

“But, my dear Walker, you searched him.”

“I searched a hobo. That was what he intended.”

“You pulled his beard.”

“He expected me to.”

“You examined his hands.”

“I examined Bilkins’s hands after Carter’s hands had directed my attention elsewhere. Flattened thumb pieces were part of Carter’s make-up. Bilkins’s hands were his own. I accepted the contradiction as proof that the two men were different.”

“And all those deductions—the march, the ocean, the shoe prints, the tannery fingers?”

“Prepared.”

“Entirely?”

“Not entirely. That was the clever part. The tracks were real. The trunk was real. The soil had been disturbed. Water would reveal it. He had arranged the facts so that every conclusion could be demonstrated. Once I verified two or three, I supplied confidence for the rest.”

“The lunch-room counting?”

“The cashier did not remember it. Only Bilkins reported it.”

“The hand organ and the goosestep?”

“Carter knew Bilkins would mention them.”

“The ship?”

“A convenient ship.”

“The banker man,” I said.

“Yes.”

“And the other fellow who helped carry the trunk?”

“A hired laborer, probably. Not Dacey; another man. We never found him.”

I returned the letter.

There was something magnificent in the economy of the performance. Mulehaus, discovering that the stolen plates could neither be used nor transported, had converted a dangerous burden into a source of income. He had created a suspect, discovered that suspect, interpreted the clues, guided the law to its own property, submitted to examination, established a legal identity through genuine arrests, and collected an official reward. He had even caused Walker to insist upon payment.

“But why the twenty dollars?” I asked.

Walker looked toward the decanter.

“Vanity, perhaps.”

“No. Mulehaus does not strike me as vain in small sums.”

“Then as a test. If I refused twenty, I might refuse to support the reward claim. Or perhaps he needed ready cash.”

“For whisky?”

“For expenses,” said Walker.

“And you never caught him?”

“Not on that case.”

“On another?”

Walker rose and buttoned his coat.

“That,” he said, “would be another memoir.”

He placed the photographic letter in his pocketbook and moved toward the door. At the threshold I detained him.

“One question. When did you first suspect that Bilkins was Mulehaus?”

“When I received the letter.”

“Not before?”

“No.”

“Then he was right. No one watches a dirty man unless he asks for money.”

Walker considered this with his usual grave precision.

“He asked,” he said. “I watched him.”

“But you watched him as a dirty man.”

For the first time that evening, Captain Walker smiled.

It was not a large smile, nor a cheerful one.

“Exactly,” he said.

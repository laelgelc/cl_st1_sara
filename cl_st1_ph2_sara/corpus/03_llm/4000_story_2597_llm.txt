For three months I have slept only beneath a shaded lamp, and even then sleep comes in brief, convulsive intervals, broken by the suspicion that some vast and colourless thing has paused above my bed to observe the motions of my breathing. Darkness is no longer emptiness to me. Air is no longer vacant. I cannot cross a room without imagining that I penetrate innumerable lives, nor lean against a wall without wondering what soft, patient organisms may be gliding through the masonry and through my own substance at that instant.

The physicians call this condition nervous exhaustion. One has named it traumatic paranoia; another, with the grave complacency of his profession, has assured me that a hallucination may preserve an internal consistency more persuasive than truth. They agree that I must forget Crawford Tillinghast, the machine in the attic, and all that occurred during the last hour of his life.

They have not explained the servants.

It was after ten weeks of silence that Tillinghast summoned me. During those weeks I had neither visited nor written to him, though there had once been no man whose company I preferred. Our estrangement had arisen from his researches—researches so audacious in conception, so contemptuous of every humane restraint, that I had felt compelled to condemn them with a bitterness I afterwards regretted.

Tillinghast had long maintained that the five acknowledged senses are not instruments of knowledge, but merciful limitations upon it. He argued that evolution had shaped them, not to reveal existence, but to conceal whatever did not assist the survival of our brutish ancestors. Around us, he said, there might extend worlds without number, each occupying the same space yet inaccessible because mankind lacked the appropriate organs of perception.

“What does the blind grub know of colour?” he once demanded. “What does a mollusc know of music? You call the invisible unreal because you cannot see it, and the inaudible unreal because you cannot hear it. That is philosophy fit for a nursery.”

His theories concerned atrophied nerves, rudimentary glands, and minute structures within the brain whose functions anatomy had never satisfactorily determined. He spoke especially of the pineal gland, though he would not state what rôle he assigned it. Electricity, ultra-violet radiations, and vibrations of an order he refused to define were to awaken these dormant centres. His attic became crowded with dynamos, glass globes, copper spirals, prisms, batteries, resonating chambers, and instruments so delicate that a passing cart in the street disturbed their needles.

At first I admired the intellectual splendour of the scheme. Later, when his voice acquired its feverish tremor and he began to speak of “burning doors through nature,” admiration yielded to alarm. Our final interview ended in violence of language. I told him his studies were unwholesome, that no truth justified the deliberate ruin of the mind which apprehended it, and that he had become less a scientist than a fanatic kneeling before an unknown altar.

He ordered me from the house.

Ten weeks later his letter arrived. It consisted of seven words:

*It is accomplished. Come tonight, or never.*

The handwriting was scarcely recognizable. Certain letters had been retraced until the paper was almost pierced, and beneath the sentence lay a brownish stain which I first took for rust. No messenger had brought it; I found it thrust beneath my door shortly after dusk.

Despite every dictate of prudence, I went.

Tillinghast’s house stood in an ancient quarter where steep roofs and leaning chimneys seemed to huddle against the modern city. His family had occupied it for generations, and its blackened stones retained something of the morbid dignity of a tomb. On that night no light showed from any window. The iron knocker returned a flat and funereal sound, yet the door opened before I could strike a second time.

Tillinghast stood within.

The alteration in him was so profound that for several moments I could not speak. He had always been lean, but now his body seemed consumed from within. His night-robe hung from angular shoulders like drapery upon a scaffold; his hands were pale claws, each joint offensively prominent. Sparse black hair adhered to his skull in damp, irregular strands. Most frightful of all were his eyes—enlarged, glassy, and protruding, with a lustre suggesting not disease alone but prolonged contemplation of some spectacle too enormous for the human countenance to contain.

“Well?” he whispered. “Do you admire the result of caution?”

I asked whether he was ill.

“Ill? I have been remade.”

His lips drew back in a smile. The gums had receded, making his teeth appear unnaturally long. In the wavering candlelight he looked less like a living scholar than a gargoyle imperfectly taught to imitate mankind.

I glanced beyond him. The hall was dusty, the furniture shrouded, the air stale with an odour of hot metal and decaying flowers.

“Where is Hartwell?” I asked. “And the others?”

The household had consisted of three servants: Hartwell, an elderly butler; Mrs. Ames, the cook; and a young maid called Eliza. Tillinghast stared at me with an expression of malicious amusement.

“Gone,” he said.

“Dismissed?”

“Gone.”

He closed and locked the door, then slipped the key into his robe.

At that gesture I nearly seized him and forced my way out, but curiosity—that most glittering and treacherous of poisons—restrained me. I followed him through rooms whose darkness seemed thickened by neglect. Our candle produced only a small, yellow island, and beyond it the corners appeared to retreat into disproportionate distances. Once I heard a faint rustling inside the wall, though there were no rats in that house, and once a current of cold air touched my face while the candle flame remained perfectly still.

We ascended two flights. On the final stair a remote musical vibration became audible, so low that I felt it in my teeth before my ears perceived it. It resembled the note of an organ sustained beneath an ocean, yet within it fluttered countless smaller tones, thin and rapid as the beating of membranous wings.

“You hear only the machine,” Tillinghast said. “For the present.”

The attic laboratory occupied nearly the whole upper floor. Heavy curtains covered the windows. Candles stood upon shelves and packing cases, but their flames were diminished by a sickly violet effulgence proceeding from the apparatus at the centre of the room.

That apparatus bore little resemblance to the untidy construction I had last seen. It had become a squat, black, metallic mass, crowned by a cluster of glass tubes and faceted crystals. Three immense coils surrounded it like the ribs of some extinct animal. Between them revolved a wheel whose rim was invisible except when minute sparks ran along it. Above the wheel hung a translucent globe full of cloudy radiance, and within that globe a needle of reddish fire quivered without touching the glass.

Cables crossed the floor. Copper plates had been fixed to the walls and ceiling. There was also a chair fitted with straps, though whether intended for restraint or experiment I could not tell.

“You succeeded,” I murmured.

Tillinghast’s head jerked towards me.

“Succeeded? You still think in the language of laboratories. Success is a word for discovering a new salt or measuring a star. I have enlarged reality.”

He moved to the machine and caressed one of its housings with repulsive tenderness.

“You told me the dormant senses might be incapable of interpretation. You feared madness, injury, death. You were right in every detail and wrong in your conclusion. Danger does not abolish truth. If an ocean lies beyond a door, shall we remain in a closet because we may drown?”

“You have seen something?”

“Seen? The word is contemptible. I have seen, heard, touched, and received impressions for which no human verb exists. There are colours that strike like wind, sounds that open vistas, odours which possess shape and duration. Distance is not what we suppose. Solidity is a local superstition. Matter is only one rhythm among infinities.”

As he spoke, his arrogance swelled into something almost regal; yet whenever his gaze wandered to the dim region behind my shoulder, terror twitched across his face.

I asked again about the servants.

For a time he made no reply. Then he laughed softly.

“They interrupted me on the third night. Hartwell complained of the current. Mrs. Ames had heard voices in the pipes. The girl was weeping because she believed someone walked through her chamber after midnight. Fools! They inhabited a miracle and complained of draughts.”

“What happened?”

“The regulating plate slipped. Only for eight seconds—perhaps nine. The vibrations passed through the house at full intensity.”

He leaned nearer. His breath was sour and fever-hot.

“I saw them change. Not physically; no, that would have been simple. Their outlines grew uncertain, as though projected upon several moving screens. Behind them—through them—other outlines appeared. Hartwell shouted and ran. That was unfortunate. Motion attracts attention.”

I demanded that he speak plainly.

“They were taken.”

The low vibration of the machine seemed suddenly louder. I looked towards the locked door.

“Taken by whom?”

“Not whom.”

His smile returned, but his eyes remained terrified.

“Did you kill them, Crawford?”

He recoiled as if insulted.

“I opened the door. What entered was not my responsibility.”

I advanced upon him, resolving to secure the key, but he placed one hand on a brass lever.

“Do not compel me to begin before you are prepared.”

There was such dreadful confidence in his manner that I stopped. Beneath my coat, in an inner pocket, I felt the reassuring weight of the revolver I had carried because of the letter. Tillinghast noticed the movement and chuckled.

“A firearm! Excellent. Bring gunpowder against metaphysics.”

He extinguished the candles one by one.

I confess that I begged him not to proceed. All my earlier fascination had become loathing, and the darkness seemed already crowded with a malign expectancy. Yet he would neither relight the candles nor unlock the door. Instead he bade me stand beside a chalk mark near the centre of the floor.

“You wished my experiments abandoned,” he said. “You called me diseased. Tonight you shall judge with educated senses.”

The lever descended.

At first nothing occurred save an intensification of the subterranean note. Then the violet globe brightened, not by casting light outward, but by drawing darkness inward and staining it with an impossible hue. The colour was not precisely violet. It occupied some alien interval beyond familiar gradations, and my brain translated it as violet only because no truer conception lay within memory.

A tingling began in my forehead. It penetrated deeper, becoming a cold pressure behind the eyes. Every tooth vibrated separately. My skin seemed to loosen from the flesh beneath it, while the boundaries of my body—those unquestioned limits by which one distinguishes self from universe—grew vague and permeable.

“Do not resist,” said Tillinghast. His voice reached me as a succession of blue, angular flashes. “Resistance tears the nerves.”

The room altered.

No object vanished, yet each became transparent to other, contradictory impressions. The walls remained walls while extending into immeasurable distances. The ceiling was at once a few feet above me and remote as an astronomical abyss. Lines bent without movement; corners opened like mouths; shadows acquired thickness and drifted across the floor against the direction of the accursed radiance.

Then the attic was overlaid by another world.

I saw a vast, dim region crowded with columns resembling vegetable trunks, mineral spires, and the pillars of drowned temples. They possessed no fixed perspective. Some appeared microscopic and infinitely distant at the same instant; others passed through the laboratory, the house, and perhaps the earth itself without disturbance. Between them streamed luminous currents in which shapes swam as fish swim in water, though the currents crossed solid objects and intersected one another at angles impossible to geometry.

My first impulse was to cry out. No sound came. I no longer knew whether sound required breath.

Creatures drifted near us.

The smaller were translucent disks fringed with trembling filaments. They gathered around the machine like moths around a lamp, overlapping its metal casing and one another without collision. Within their gelatinous bodies flickered points arranged like constellations. Occasionally one opened a circular fold, and I received an impression compounded of a bell, a bitter taste, and the memory of falling from a great height.

Beyond them moved beings less easily described. They were masses of semi-fluid articulation—ropes, bladders, fans, eyes, and jointed lattices continually dissolving and recombining. Some possessed organs which might have been mouths, though they opened into places rather than interiors. Others resembled colossal medusae whose trailing tissues penetrated the floor. Once an enormous segmented shadow passed across the room, and the alien landscape darkened beneath it as a field darkens under a cloud.

Yet these prodigies, hideous though they were, inspired less horror than the implication of their abundance. The air had never been empty. Every room, street, garden, and human gathering was thronged with invisible life. We had breathed it, walked through it, slept among it. Our ignorance had been a membrane thinner than soap-film, and Tillinghast had torn it.

“Now,” he said, “do you understand?”

I turned towards him and nearly collapsed.

The vibration had revealed what ordinary sight mercifully concealed in his altered body. Something pulsed around his head, attached to him by fine luminous threads that entered the skull. His flesh appeared riddled with moving channels. Small pale organisms clustered along his nerves, feeding or listening; and behind his eyes, visible through the bones, the pineal gland blazed like a diseased star.

He saw my disgust.

“Do not look at me,” he snapped. “Look outward. There are universes within the width of your hand.”

The scene shifted without transition. The laboratory floated above an abyss of green radiance in which enormous architectures rose and toppled with the slowness of continents. Then we seemed enclosed within a scarlet wilderness of fibres, as though the house had become lodged inside the organ of some cosmic animal. Far away, choirs of metallic voices chanted equations whose meaning hovered at the threshold of comprehension. Each partial understanding threatened to dissolve my personality into something colder and more inclusive.

I shut my eyes.

It made no difference.

Perception no longer depended upon the eyes. I saw through my whole nervous system. Indeed, I sensed that another faculty had opened in the centre of my brain—a rudimentary organ awakened after geological ages of sleep. Through it came impressions of neighbouring dimensions: innumerable worlds interpenetrating our own, crowded with life, conflict, appetite, and purposes beside which human history was no more consequential than mould spreading upon forgotten bread.

“Enough,” I whispered.

Tillinghast laughed.

The sound emerged from several directions and crossed my body like a draught.

“Enough? This is the vestibule! You have not yet perceived the intelligences. Some are larger than planets. Some inhabit duration as we inhabit space. Some pass through our lives from birth to death in a single movement and never suspect that we are alive.”

“Turn it off.”

“Why? You discouraged me. You pitied me. You told others I had surrendered reason. Is your reason comfortable now?”

His vindictiveness, naked at last, restored some fragment of my will.

“You brought me here for revenge.”

“For education,” he replied; but his smile admitted the truth. “And perhaps for companionship.”

At that instant his expression changed. He stared beyond me, and all triumph drained from his face.

“Remain perfectly still.”

The command was scarcely audible.

“What is it?”

“Still!”

Among the drifting organisms a disturbance had begun. The smaller creatures scattered, folding themselves into narrow streaks and disappearing through the walls. A region of the alien landscape dimmed as though some immense body approached between us and its luminous vapours.

Tillinghast crouched beside the machine.

“They learned the vibration,” he muttered. “They have followed it each time. I altered the frequency, but they learned. They are not mindless—not all of them.”

The darkness behind me thickened.

I felt, rather than saw, a presence entering the attic from a direction for which our world has no name. Its approach produced a crawling pressure along my spine. Something explored the room with invisible extensions, touching table, chair, apparatus, and walls. Where it passed, the semi-fluid creatures vanished.

“Was this what took the servants?” I asked.

“One kind. Perhaps.”

“Stop the machine!”

“If I stop it too quickly, the sensory centres may remain open. Or burn out. Or—”

A soft impact sounded behind me.

It was like wet cloth dropped upon stone.

Tillinghast’s eyes gleamed with sudden malice. Though terror shook him, his hatred of me had not diminished; rather, it had found in the approaching horror its final and most exquisite instrument.

“Movement attracts them,” he whispered. “Hartwell ran. The girl struggled. You must not move—not even when it touches you.”

I stood rigid, fighting the spasms of every muscle. A smell like ozone mingled with opened graves filled the room. Above my right shoulder appeared three curved points, colourless but perceptible as absences cut from the alien glow. They moved delicately, almost inquisitively.

Tillinghast began to laugh.

“Look behind you,” he said. “Look! It has opened itself.”

I would not turn.

“You came for proof. There is proof enough to embrace you. Look, coward! See what inhabits the inch between your back and your shadow!”

The curved points descended. A sensation colder than ice touched my coat without pressing the cloth. At once there entered my mind an image—not transmitted by sight, but imposed whole upon consciousness—of Hartwell’s face stretched across a translucent membrane, his mouth opening and closing soundlessly while things fed upon the recollections within his brain.

My paralysis broke.

Tillinghast shrieked with delight. “Now it sees you!”

I drew the revolver. The presence behind me rushed forward with a noise like a locomotive heard beneath the sea.

I did not fire at it. Some remnant of reason told me that bullets belonged to our narrow order of matter and could not injure what had no stable relation to it. Instead I aimed at the violet globe.

Tillinghast sprang towards me.

The first shot cracked the glass. The second struck the revolving wheel. On the third, the apparatus burst into a fountain of red sparks, and the laboratory convulsed.

There was no explosion in the ordinary sense. Rather, every impossible world collapsed through every other. The green abyss folded into the walls; the scarlet fibres whipped away into measureless distance; the columns, currents, organisms, and looming predator became thin layers of light, then lines, then points. A scream arose—not from Tillinghast alone, but from a multitude of throats distributed across realities whose conjunction had been violently severed.

For one final instant I saw the creature behind me.

No description is possible. Size, shape, and position were attributes it did not continuously possess. Yet I perceived appetite in it—ancient, automatic, and immeasurable—and I knew that the human race had always moved through the hunting-ground of such beings, spared only because it was blind.

Then darkness returned.

When I recovered consciousness, dawn had entered through curtains torn down by the police. I lay beneath a table with the revolver still in my hand. The machine was a charred heap. Tillinghast was dead beside it, his face distorted by an expression of such absolute fear that even the officers avoided looking at him.

The medical examiner found no wound. Death, he said, had resulted from apoplexy induced by excitement. My own condition was attributed to shock and to hypnosis practised by a homicidal lunatic. The stains in the attic, the electrical damage, and my confused testimony were incorporated into a report whose calm vocabulary rendered the whole affair almost commonplace.

I asked them to search the house.

They did. Cellar, chimneys, cupboards, sealed rooms, courtyard cistern, and garden earth were examined. Advertisements were placed; railway stations notified; relatives questioned. Tillinghast’s accounts showed that no wages had been paid and no travelling money supplied. Hartwell, Mrs. Ames, and Eliza had left behind their clothes, papers, and every personal possession.

The authorities eventually declared that the servants had fled after witnessing their master’s insanity.

Perhaps the physicians are right. Perhaps a diseased man hypnotized me and furnished my suggestible mind with a pageant assembled from overheard theories, Gothic shadows, and terror. I endeavour daily to accept this charitable explanation.

But no bodies were ever found.

And sometimes, when the night is perfectly still, I feel a cold, delicate pressure upon my shoulder—as though something which once perceived me has not wholly forgotten where I am.

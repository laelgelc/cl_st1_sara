The church at Bellweather was older than any person who attended it, and appeared, indeed, to consider the congregation a recent and rather troublesome addition to its arrangements. Its pillars leaned very slightly, as old gentlemen lean when they will not admit to requiring sticks; its carved angels had lost noses, wings, and in one unfortunate instance a whole celestial head; and the monuments upon its walls displayed marble warriors in ruffs, ladies in stone crinolines, and shields bearing lions gules, martlets sable, and bends argent, concerning which Ernest had once asked his Aunt Emmeline so many questions that she had forbidden heraldry on Sundays.

There were high box pews smelling of dust, hassocks, damp cloth, and the peculiar varnish used by churchwardens. There was a pulpit like a dark wooden castle, over which the sounding-board brooded as if waiting to fall upon the clergyman whenever his doctrine became too adventurous. There were red curtains near the vestry, black boards lettered with gold, and a font of grey-veined marble beneath the western window.

It was the font that Ernest loved.

Four little marble figures supported its heavy basin. Three were angels, though one had a chipped wing and the other two looked as if they had been kept after school in Heaven. The fourth was a child without wings. He wore a short carved garment, and his bare feet pressed the stone pedestal. His head was bent beneath the bowl, one shoulder raised to bear the weight; and although his face was beautiful, it was not the comfortable beauty of the fat cherubs on Aunt Lavinia’s Christmas cards. He looked patient. That was much worse.

“He must be very tired,” Ernest said.

Aunt Emmeline, who had been arranging his Geneva collar while Mrs. Meadows, the pew-opener, hunted for Aunt Lavinia’s dropped spectacle-case, replied that marble did not become tired.

“But he has held it up for hundreds of years.”

“Marble does not feel.”

“How do you know?”

This was an improper question, not because it was rude, but because Aunt Emmeline did not know. She therefore said that Ernest was old enough to understand the difference between what was alive and what was not.

Ernest was eight. He understood fewer differences than his aunts supposed and more resemblances than they would ever have believed.

He lived with Aunt Emmeline and Aunt Lavinia in a square yellow house half a mile from the church. They were good women. This must be said immediately, because grown people are terribly unfair to aunts in stories. They loved Ernest; they had promised his dying mother to educate, preserve, discipline, warm, cool, strengthen, correct, and generally prepare him for life; and they performed all these duties with such completeness that very little life remained to be prepared for.

Aunt Emmeline saw to his mind. Aunt Lavinia saw to his body. Between them he had catechisms, copy-books, flannel waistcoats, mutton broth, scales, Gregory’s powder, moral histories, early bedtimes, and walks during which he must not run because running heated the blood.

Did you ever take Gregory’s powder? If you did, you know that no child who has swallowed it can entirely believe in the benevolence of Providence for at least twenty minutes afterward.

Ernest was not beaten. His aunts disapproved of violence. When he was naughty, he was reasoned with, and the reasoning lasted so long that he sometimes wished they would slap him and have done. Punishment is objectionable precisely because it is punishment, whether it wears a birch rod or a kind expression.

At church Ernest sat between the aunts in their curtained pew. He could see the marble child through a narrow opening whenever Aunt Lavinia’s bonnet did not intervene. During sermons he imagined climbing over the pew door, crossing the cold flags, and saying:

“You needn’t hold it any longer. I’ll help.”

He imagined the child easing the basin from his shoulder. They would creep into the churchyard, where the yew trees made green caves, and play robbers among the tombs. They would sail to China in a pew, using a hassock for provisions and the brass collecting-dish for a compass. On wet days they would hide behind the organ and eat raspberry jam from the jar.

The jam was an important detail. Ernest’s aunts allowed it on alternate Thursdays, in moderation. In imagination, moderation does not exist.

The marble child grew dear to him. Ernest knew every curve of his cheek and each curl cut into his stone hair. He knew, too, though no one had told him, that the child was lonely. Adults believed loneliness required an empty house. They did not know that it may sit between two loving aunts at dinner and receive second helpings of rice pudding.

The first time the marble hand moved was on the Sunday of the great September storm.

Morning had been yellow and breathless. By eleven o’clock purple clouds were packed behind the church tower, and the light through the windows had the bruised colour of plums. The congregation rustled uneasily. Ladies smelled salts; gentlemen glanced toward the roof and then pretended they had only been considering the commandments. The clergyman preached upon obedience, while the thunder commented from a distance.

Ernest watched the child.

A sudden flash poured through the western window. For one instant the angels, the basin, and the bowed figure shone white as snow. The thunder broke directly overhead, and Aunt Lavinia caught Ernest’s wrist.

In that same instant the marble child lifted one hand from the font and reached toward him.

Only a little way. Only the breadth of a leaf, perhaps. But it moved.

Ernest made no sound. If he told, Aunt Emmeline would say it was the lightning. Aunt Lavinia would feel his pulse. The doctor might come, and doctors asked questions to which they already possessed answers.

The hand returned to its place.

“All the same,” Ernest thought, “marble has moved.”

This did not astonish him as much as it would astonish you. He knew that people had been turned into pillars of salt and that stones could cry out if required. These things occurred in Scripture and were therefore respectable. Why should not a marble child move his hand, especially in a thunderstorm?

After the service, while the aunts discussed the weather beneath the porch, Ernest slipped back.

The church was darker now. Rain hammered the roof. The red curtains lifted and fell in the draught, and the silence between the thunderclaps was more alarming than the noise. Ernest went to the font.

The child’s stone fingers were in their usual position.

“I saw you,” Ernest whispered. “You needn’t be afraid. I won’t tell.”

The marble eyes looked downward. A raindrop had fallen through a crack and lay upon one cheek like a tear.

Ernest wiped it away.

Then Aunt Emmeline called, and he ran out, feeling that a promise had been accepted.

After that the ordinary world became less certain.

Three weeks later Ernest was walking in the shrubbery behind the yellow house. His aunts walked in front, discussing whether his cough was constitutional or merely wilful. Ernest trailed behind with his hands clasped at his back, because Aunt Lavinia considered pockets harmful to posture.

Golden light lay in flat pieces between the laurels. A blackbird turned the leaves with the important air of a housemaid searching for a lost sixpence. Beyond the iron fence stood a little boy in a white tunic.

He had bare feet. His hair curled close to his head.

Ernest stopped.

The boy smiled.

“You came!” Ernest cried, and thrust himself between two bars of the fence. “I knew you would. Wait—please wait!”

The aunts turned at his voice. Aunt Emmeline saw only an empty path beyond the railing and a child with one stocking torn by ironwork.

“Whom were you addressing?”

“The boy from church.”

“What boy?”

“The marble one.”

There followed an exchange between the aunts of the sort grown persons imagine children do not notice.

“He has been reading too much.”

“He has hardly read anything but the abridged life of Archbishop Cranmer.”

“That may have been too much.”

Aunt Lavinia put a cool hand upon Ernest’s forehead. Aunt Emmeline spoke of imagination as if it were a regrettable eruption. Ernest looked through the bars. The path was empty, but two wet leaves bore the clear prints of small bare feet.

He said nothing about these. He was learning.

That night he dreamed—or, if you prefer, believed he dreamed—that someone tapped upon the nursery window. The room was on the second floor, which made tapping less likely but not impossible in dreams.

The marble child stood outside among the ivy.

Ernest opened the window, and they went down together, not by the stairs, where Sarah slept lightly, but by walking along a shaft of moonlight. They crossed the lawn and entered the wood.

There the trees had become pillars, and the branches arched overhead like a church roof. Foxgloves burned like altar candles. The brook sang through a stone channel, and little pale moths went to and fro as seriously as a congregation. Ernest and the child played hide-and-seek among the beeches. They ran until Ernest’s blood beat happily in his ears; they climbed, shouted, and stained their fingers with blackberries. No one warned them about damp grass.

“Do you have to go back?” Ernest asked.

“Yes.”

“Because of the font?”

“Yes.”

“Does it hurt?”

The child considered this gravely. “Only when you remember me.”

“That ought to make it hurt less.”

“It does.”

Ernest took his cold hand. “I’ll always remember.”

When Sarah came in at seven, she found blackberry stains on Ernest’s nightshirt and dead leaves beneath his pillow. Aunt Lavinia attributed these to fever. Aunt Emmeline attributed them to deception. The doctor prescribed a tonic.

Ernest knew better, and so did the marble child.

Winter narrowed the world. Rain striped the nursery windows; the fields became brown and difficult; church smelled more strongly of wet wool. Ernest waited each Sunday for the small stone head to turn. Sometimes he thought it did. Sometimes he was sure the child smiled. Sometimes nothing happened, and those Sundays were the longest.

His aunts grew concerned by his silence.

“He needs companionship,” said Aunt Lavinia, as if she had discovered a new medicinal principle.

Accordingly, they invited the vicar’s nephew, a large boy named Percy, who possessed a watch, a penknife, and the certainty that everything belonged to him. Percy broke Ernest’s wooden horse, called the font child a “stone dummy,” and ate three pieces of seed-cake without asking. When Ernest struck him, Aunt Emmeline was grieved, Aunt Lavinia was shocked, and Percy was given the largest surviving piece of cake because he was a guest.

Ernest was required to apologize.

“I am sorry I struck you,” he said. This was true. He wished he had pushed Percy into the pond.

That evening he stood at the nursery window and thought of the marble child bearing the enormous bowl.

“I can’t endure it,” he whispered. “You must come, you must.”

But no one tapped.

The trouble occurred in March.

Ernest had been forbidden to visit the church alone. The prohibition was reasonable: the river was swollen, storms were frequent, and Mrs. Meadows had reported finding him once asleep in a pew after evening prayers. His aunts explained all this with grown-up, crinolined kindness. Ernest listened, agreed, and was more miserable than if they had been unjust.

On a Thursday afternoon Aunt Emmeline discovered that he had drawn the marble child in the margin of his Latin grammar. The figure was badly drawn, but unmistakably burdened.

“This preoccupation must cease,” she said. “It is neither healthy nor reverent.”

“He is my friend.”

“He is a carved support.”

“He isn’t.”

Aunt Emmeline sighed. “Ernest, obstinacy does not transform fancy into fact.”

“No,” said Ernest. “But being grown-up does not transform facts into fancy.”

There was a terrible stillness.

Aunt Lavinia dropped her knitting.

Ernest was sent to his room. He was told to reflect, which is the safest rebellion grown people permit, because they suppose reflection will always arrive at their conclusions.

Outside, the afternoon darkened. Clouds marched low over the fields. The wind worried the chimney and flung twigs against the panes.

Ernest reflected.

He reflected that the child was alone. He reflected that no one believed either of them. He reflected that if he stayed at church, he could sleep in the large pew near the vestry and live on the biscuits Mrs. Meadows kept for her tea. Water could be obtained from the pump. Raspberry jam presented a difficulty, but not every plan is perfect.

He put on his coat, filled his pockets with two buns, a ball of string, and the sixpence Aunt Lavinia had given him at Christmas, then climbed out by the pantry roof. The descent scraped his knees and tore his cuff. Freedom is almost always harder on clothes than discipline.

Rain began before he reached the lane.

The church door was unlocked. Mrs. Meadows had been polishing the brass lectern and was now in the vestry, where she sometimes slept after her work because her cottage stood beyond the flooded meadow.

Ernest slipped inside unseen.

The building received him with cold, ancient darkness. The windows were dim patches of violet and blood-red glass. Canopies brooded over stone knights. The pews seemed to draw together, whispering among themselves. Wind breathed beneath the doors.

At the font, the marble child waited.

“I’ve come to stay,” Ernest said. “Forever, if you like.”

The stone head lifted.

Slowly—so slowly that each movement seemed to have been asleep for a hundred years—the child lowered his shoulder. The basin remained suspended above him as if held by invisible hands. His feet loosened from the pedestal. One pale foot touched the pavement, then the other.

He stood before Ernest.

His skin was white, but not the dead white of stone. It held a faint radiance, like moonlight seen through milk. His eyes were grey. His curls moved when the draught passed.

Ernest’s throat ached with happiness.

“I knew,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Will you play?”

“Yes.”

“Will you come home with me?”

“No.”

“Then I shall live here.”

The child put both arms around him. They were cool, but no longer hard. Ernest hid his face against the white shoulder and felt, for the first time he could remember, perfectly understood. That is rarer than being loved, though the two things are often confused.

“You may stay,” said the child. “No one will separate us. When the tower falls, we shall go together.”

Ernest drew back. “Falls?”

“The lightning is coming.”

As if summoned, thunder rolled beyond the hills.

“Will it hurt the church?”

“It will break the roof and the tower. The stones will fall. This body will become dust.”

“And me?”

“You will be with me. You need not be afraid.”

Ernest was not afraid. He had wanted forever, and here it was offered without lessons, tonics, apologies, or alternate Thursdays.

The child took his hand.

Then he said, “The old woman is asleep in the vestry.”

“Mrs. Meadows?”

“Yes.”

Another flash whitened the windows.

“And your aunt is coming along the lane,” the child continued. “The one whose bonnet has purple ribbons. She will reach the porch before the fire comes down.”

“Aunt Lavinia!”

“Yes.”

Ernest saw it all as plainly as if a curtain had been drawn aside: Mrs. Meadows nodding beside the vestry stove, her spectacles low on her nose; Aunt Lavinia struggling through rain, calling his name, her ridiculous purple ribbons beaten flat against her cheeks; the tower above them, waiting.

“We must tell them.”

“If you go,” said the child, “you cannot remain with me.”

“Come too.”

“I belong here until the stone is broken.”

“I’ll carry you.”

“You cannot.”

“You must come, you must!”

The marble child’s face was calm, but its calmness hurt more than weeping.

“If you leave now, you will save them. If you stay, we shall not be parted.”

Thunder struck so near that dust fell from the arches. Somewhere high above, a bell gave one startled note.

Ernest loved his aunts. He did not always like them, which is not the same matter. He loved Mrs. Meadows too, although she smelled of lamp oil and called him “Master Earnest.” But he loved the marble child more than any creature in the world, because the child knew the rooms in his heart which even kindness had kept locked.

He could remain. It would be easy.

That was why he could not.

Ernest seized the child’s hands and kissed them.

“I have to go.”

“Yes.”

“You understand?”

“Yes.”

“Will I ever see you again?”

The child laid one white finger against Ernest’s breast.

“My home was never the font,” he said. “It is here, if you keep it.”

Then Ernest ran.

He burst into the vestry. Mrs. Meadows sat asleep in a horsehair chair, the kettle singing beside her.

“Wake up! The church is falling!”

She opened one eye. “Now then, Master Ernest—”

He dragged at her sleeve. “Lightning! You must come!”

Perhaps it was his face. Perhaps it was the crack that sounded above them like a giant breaking timber across his knee. Mrs. Meadows rose.

They reached the nave as Aunt Lavinia entered the porch, wet, breathless, and entirely without propriety.

“Ernest!”

“No time!”

He caught her hand. Mrs. Meadows caught the other. They stumbled out into the churchyard while the wind tore branches from the yews.

“Farther!” Ernest cried.

Aunt Lavinia attempted a question. The sky answered.

Light descended upon the tower—not like an ordinary flash, but like a white tree growing downward from Heaven. For one suspended moment every stone, leaf, and raindrop stood distinct. Then came a noise beyond noise.

The tower split. The roof rose and folded. Walls that had endured kings, wars, harvests, weddings, plague, and innumerable sermons broke apart like a slice of cake beneath a careless knife. Dust rolled across the graves. Bells plunged through beams. The western window burst outward in a shower of crimson, sapphire, and gold.

Aunt Lavinia fell to her knees.

Mrs. Meadows covered her head with her apron.

Ernest looked back.

Through the dust came a radiant child in a short white garment. He walked beside Ernest, holding his hand. He was no longer bowed. Behind his head shone something—not wings exactly, Mrs. Meadows said afterward, but the idea of wings, which may be brighter.

Then the dust reached them, and Ernest knew nothing more.

For three weeks he lay ill in the yellow house.

There were doctors, whispered consultations, mustard poultices, jellies, thermometers, and patent medicines in blue bottles. Aunt Lavinia scarcely left him. Aunt Emmeline, who never wept in public and only rarely in private, sat by the bed at night with her face hidden behind a book she did not turn.

Ernest burned with fever. He talked of falling bells, blackberries, pews sailing to China, and a child who must not be left beneath a burden. Sometimes he held his right hand tightly closed. No persuasion could make him open it.

The church was a ruin. The font had stood directly beneath the fallen masonry, and when men dug toward it they found only fragments, chips, and white powder mixed with lime. Of the four supporting figures nothing recognizable remained.

“Providential,” said the doctor, “that the boy woke the woman.”

“Providential indeed,” said Aunt Emmeline.

“No doubt shock accounts for the delirium.”

“No doubt.”

Aunt Lavinia said nothing. She watched Ernest’s closed hand.

On the twenty-second morning his fever broke. Sunlight lay across the counterpane. A thrush sang in the wet garden with immoderate cheerfulness.

Ernest opened his eyes.

“Aunt Lavinia?”

“My darling.”

“Did you get out?”

“Yes, because of you.”

“And Mrs. Meadows?”

“Quite safe.”

He smiled. Then his fingers relaxed.

In his palm lay a little finger of white marble, broken neatly at the second joint.

Aunt Lavinia took it before Aunt Emmeline entered. What she did with it no one knew. Ernest never asked. Certain proofs are weakened by being displayed in glass cases and discussed after dinner.

Many years later Aunt Emmeline told the story to sympathetic visitors. She told it very well. Ernest had been a sensitive child, she said; highly imaginative, regrettably lonely despite every attention, and subject to feverish impressions. He had run away in a fit of childish disobedience. By an extraordinary coincidence, his alarm had saved two lives. His subsequent illness had embroidered the event with fantasies.

At this point the sympathetic visitor always said, “How remarkable!”

“Yes,” Aunt Emmeline would reply, with the gentle triumph of one who has repeated a narrative until it has become furniture. “And poor Mrs. Meadows insisted to her dying day that she saw an angel leading him from the church.”

“An angel?”

“A figure in white. Smoke, reflected lightning, shattered glass—you understand.”

Once, when Mrs. Meadows herself was present, she set down the teacup she had been carrying and said:

“Smoke don’t take a little lad by the hand, ma’am. Nor broken glass neither. I seen that blessed child plain. White as the Judgment, bright as Easter, and walking where the stones come down.”

Aunt Emmeline smiled as people smile at servants, children, and inconvenient truths.

Ernest, who was grown by then, did not argue. He remembered a cold hand becoming warm within his own, a choice made in the dark, and one white finger pressed over his heart.

The aunts remembered thunder, illness, and naughtiness forgiven.

Mrs. Meadows remembered an angel.

But Ernest remembered his friend.

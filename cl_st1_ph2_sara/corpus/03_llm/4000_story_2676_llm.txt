The electric fire burned behind three bars of copper, making the office seem warmer than any room had a right to be in November. It shone on the dark carved desk, on the brass corners of the bookcase, and on a green leather armchair that was deep enough to swallow a man whole. Even the carpet looked warm. Red and blue flowers, thick as cabbages, spread beneath the furniture.

Mr Harcourt stood with his back to the fire, rocking gently from heel to toe while old Ellison lowered himself into the chair.

“There now,” said Harcourt. “That’s better than your draughty little room upstairs, eh?”

“Y’are very comfortable here,” Ellison piped.

He peered over the raised collar of his overcoat. During the five years since his seizure he had grown smaller and smoother, like an old apple kept in a drawer. His wife and daughters allowed him into town on Tuesdays only. They dressed him, gave him his fare, and warned him not to miss the four-twenty. Once in the city he called upon former acquaintances, who received him with the patience reserved for invalids and children.

Harcourt, who was older by a year, felt magnificently preserved beside him. He squared his shoulders inside his broad coat.

“Still at the helm,” he said. “Every morning at nine. Never missed a day except for influenza.”

“Wonderful,” said Ellison. “Wonderful.”

His watery gaze wandered round the office. Harcourt followed it with satisfaction. He had lately had the room done up: new carpet, new armchair, new electric heating. The desk had belonged to an Italian prince, according to the dealer. Whether this was true did not matter. It looked as if it had.

On the wall above it hung the photograph of a young man in uniform.

Ellison stared at the photograph without appearing to see it. His mouth opened slightly.

“Ah,” he said.

Then nothing followed.

Harcourt waited. The old fellow had brought something with him; it was there, fluttering behind the pale dome of his forehead, but he could not catch it. Better give him time.

“A drop of something?” Harcourt asked.

“My wife won’t hear of it.”

“Nor ought she.” Harcourt moved to a cabinet. “This isn’t ordinary stuff, though. Medicine. Came from Windsor, this did. One spoonful, and a man could walk to Scotland.”

Ellison’s face sharpened with pleasure. He watched the little key turn and the square bottle come out. There was something delicious in being forbidden a thing and receiving it from another man.

“Only a finger,” he whispered. “She’d smell it.”

“Let her.” Harcourt filled two glasses generously. “Here’s to us. Still on our pins.”

Ellison coughed over the first mouthful, blinked, and then smiled. A faint colour rose beneath his loose skin.

“That warms yer,” he said. “By Jove, that warms yer.”

“Nothing like it.”

For a moment the two men sat in the glow, pleased with the whisky, the office, and themselves. The traffic outside pressed by in a muffled, continuous stream. Somewhere beyond the partition a typewriter struck briskly and stopped.

Ellison placed both hands on the knob of his stick.

“What was it?” he murmured. “There was something the girls told me. Something I particularly meant to—”

He closed his eyes. Harcourt regarded him almost tenderly. The mind, when it grew old, was like a room from which servants had begun carrying away the furniture. One reached for a familiar chair and found an empty patch upon the carpet.

Suddenly Ellison opened his eyes.

“Belgium!” he cried. “That was it.”

Harcourt ceased rocking.

“The girls went over last month,” Ellison continued. “Mabel and Rose. Tour, y’know—Bruges, Brussels, all that. But they made a point of going to Ned.”

His voice altered on the name, becoming both proud and apologetic. Ned had been dead six years.

“They found his grave beautifully kept. Rose says there are flowers all along the paths, and the stones as white as new teeth. You wouldn’t believe there was a war there at all.” He paused, looking into the empty glass. “And who d’yer think they found quite near him?”

Harcourt’s thick fingers tightened behind his back.

“Philip,” said Ellison. “Your boy. Not a stone’s throw from our Ned. Mabel copied the number. She said she stood between the two of ’em.”

The office, for all its glowing bars, became suddenly enormous and cold.

“Did she?” Harcourt said.

“Yes. She thought you’d like to know. Very well looked after, she said. Couldn’t be better. Neat as a garden. Only—” Ellison frowned. “Only she was shocked at what they charged for a little pot of jam at the hotel. Ten francs! Plain currant jam, mind yer. Enough for two bits of toast. It’s robbery. These foreigners think because you’ve come to see the graves they can charge what they please.”

Harcourt made a sound that might have been agreement.

“Ten francs,” Ellison repeated. He planted his stick, struggled upright, and permitted Harcourt to help him into his gloves. “Well, I must be getting along. The girls’ll have a fit if I miss that train.”

At the door he turned.

“Thought you’d like to know.”

“Very thoughtful. Thank them for me.”

“Certainly. Certainly.”

Harcourt watched the old man move down the passage with cautious, rubbery steps. The office messenger, sitting on his stool, sprang up to see him into the lift. For a second they looked like an elderly gentleman being led away by an attentive dog.

Then the lift gate clanged.

Harcourt returned to his room and shut the door. He crossed to his desk, pressed the bell, and when the messenger appeared, breathless and eager, said, “I’m not to be disturbed for half an hour. Whoever it is.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Not even Mr Briggs.”

“No, sir.”

The boy backed out, impressed. A great decision was to be made, no doubt. Perhaps a dismissal. Perhaps some transaction involving thousands.

Harcourt lowered himself into the green chair and covered his eyes.

Now he would think of Philip.

He had done this often in the first years. He had only to shut the door and say the boy’s name inwardly, and grief came upon him, full and black, rising so violently that he could scarcely breathe. It had seemed then that time could do nothing. People had said it would. Time, they said, was merciful. Merciful! As if the years were tradesmen bringing repairs. He had despised them.

Philip had been his only son, the purpose towards which every contract, every new branch, every long day at the office had secretly tended. Harcourt and Son: the brass plate had existed in his mind before the child could walk. At twenty-one Philip had begun coming in on Mondays, sitting at the smaller desk by the window, learning the ropes. He had been liked at once. Too gentle, perhaps, too ready to listen to excuses, but that would have passed. A man hardened in business. There had been time.

Harcourt remembered the young figure crossing the patterned carpet, remembered the quick smile given to the clerks, the clear “Morning, Father,” at the door.

Then war had taken him.

The telegram had arrived at eleven-fifteen on a wet Tuesday. Harcourt could still see the envelope upon the blotting paper, though the blotting paper itself had long since been burned. For weeks afterward he had walked with the sensation that his fat body was only a shell and that the slightest touch would split it open.

Six years. Was it possible?

He waited.

His hands remained over his eyes. He made himself see the grave: the white stone, the narrow grass path, Ellison’s daughters standing between Philip and Ned. But the picture would not deepen. It stayed small and far away, like an illustration in a guidebook.

“My boy,” he whispered.

Nothing came.

He lowered his hands and stared at the carpet. Surely he was not becoming hard. Surely grief did not simply wear out, like cloth at an elbow. A father did not lose his only son and then—recover. The word disgusted him. Yet where was the pain? He searched himself with increasing impatience, as though looking through desk drawers for a missing paper.

His gaze fell upon the photograph.

The face in it was handsome, grave, and unfamiliar. The photographer had arranged Philip’s cap at a brave angle. There was no smile. Harcourt had never cared for the picture; it did not look like the boy. Still, it ought to have helped.

He leaned forward.

Something moved in the broad glass inkpot on his desk.

A fly had fallen into the ink. It struggled in the blue-black pool, beating its wings uselessly, sinking, rising, and sinking again. Its legs clawed at the smooth glass. Each effort seemed weaker than the last.

Harcourt forgot the photograph. He took up his pen, slid the nib beneath the fly, and lifted it out. A heavy drop fell back into the pot. He carried the creature carefully across the desk and set it upon the blotting paper.

For some seconds it lay there, a shapeless black speck.

“Come on,” he said. “Never say die.”

A front leg stirred. Then another. The fly raised itself, trembling. It drew one leg over a wing, then the other, scraping away the ink. It rubbed its head with its forelegs. It cleaned under and over, under and over, pausing when the labour became too great, beginning again when strength returned.

Harcourt watched with admiration.

Plucky little devil! That was the way. No fuss, no surrender. Knocked flat one minute and putting itself to rights the next. If men had half that spirit there would be less whining in the world.

Gradually the wings separated. A faint transparent edge appeared beneath the stain. The fly tested them, lifting and lowering each one. Soon it would be off.

An idea occurred to Harcourt.

He dipped his pen into the ink. Holding it above the blotting paper, he allowed one drop to swell at the nib. The fly stood directly below, still grooming itself.

The drop fell.

It struck the creature flat.

For a moment Harcourt felt a small shock. He had not expected the drop to be so large. It spread in a round, shining stain, with the fly at its centre. But almost immediately a leg pushed through.

“There you are,” he said, relieved. “That’s it.”

Once more the fly began.

The task was harder now. Its movements were slow and uncertain. It dragged its legs through the ink, wiped them, dragged them again. One wing remained glued to its body. With immense care it worked the wing free.

Harcourt bent so close that his breath stirred the edge of the paper.

Extraordinary. There was courage for you—not words, not medals, not speeches, but the plain business of getting up. Life knocked you down; you got up. Life struck again; you got up again.

When the fly had almost finished, he refilled the pen.

“Ready?” he murmured.

The second drop fell upon its back.

This time the fly did not move at once. Harcourt waited, the pen suspended in his hand. At last its forelegs appeared. They worked feebly at the head. The body rose, collapsed, rose again.

“Good fellow,” Harcourt whispered.

He experienced a tenderness so strong that it made him smile. The tiny thing depended upon him; he alone witnessed its trial, understood its valour. He would see it through.

The fly cleaned one wing, then rested. Cleaned the other, then rested longer. Its whole body pulsed.

Harcourt looked into the inkpot. One more test, he thought. Only one. It would prove the quality of the creature. After that he would let it go.

He gathered a smaller drop.

“Last time,” he said.

The drop descended.

It covered the fly completely.

Harcourt set down the pen. He waited for the black mound to stir.

Nothing happened.

He tapped the blotting paper with one finger.

“Come on.”

The fly remained still.

He nudged it gently with the corner of the pen. Its legs were folded beneath it. Its wings, newly cleaned, were fixed forever in the ink.

The fly was dead.

A wretched feeling passed through Harcourt. It was not sorrow exactly, nor fear, but something cold and shameful that seemed to rise from the richly coloured carpet and settle inside his chest.

He lifted the blotting paper, folded it quickly over the body, and dropped the bundle into the wastebasket.

Then he pressed the bell.

The messenger appeared at once, bright-eyed, prepared for importance.

“Fresh blotting paper,” Harcourt said. “And look sharp.”

“Yes, sir.”

The boy glanced at the desk, wondering what had happened to the clean sheet he had placed there that morning, but Harcourt’s face discouraged questions. He hurried away.

Harcourt leaned back and passed a hand over his forehead. He felt tired now, deeply tired. There had been something he meant to think about, something he had required half an hour alone for.

What was it?

He stared at the photograph, at the inkpot, at the empty place on the desk.

For the life of him, he could not remember.

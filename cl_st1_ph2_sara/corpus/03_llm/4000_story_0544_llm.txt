### The Bandits in the Attic

On Thursday afternoon Martha was left alone in the house.

This was not because her family had forgotten her. Her mother had gone to a meeting of the Ladies’ Improvement Circle, her father was at his office, her elder brother was attending a baseball game, and the cook and housemaid were enjoying their day out. Martha had been invited to spend the afternoon with a respectable little girl named Clara, but Clara had caught the mumps, and there is no pleasure in visiting a person whose face is shaped like a pudding.

So Martha remained at home with instructions not to touch the stove, not to answer the door to strangers, and not to go into the attic.

The last instruction was unfortunate.

There was no reason Martha should wish to touch the stove. She knew that strangers at the door were usually selling brushes or asking for the gas bill, and she had no use for either. But until she was told not to go into the attic, she had scarcely thought of it. Afterward she could think of nothing else.

We are all curious, though grown persons frequently rename their curiosity “investigation” and thus make it respectable. Martha had not yet learned to do this. She simply said to herself, “I wonder what’s up there,” and went to see.

The attic occupied the whole upper part of the house and smelled of dust, dried apples, camphor, and old rain. A row of narrow windows admitted slanting beams of afternoon sunshine, in which the dust specks danced as busily as people at a ball. There were trunks belonging to grandmothers, boxes belonging to nobody, broken chairs, rolled carpets, picture frames without pictures, pictures without frames, a birdcage with no bird, and a tall hat that had once belonged to Martha’s grandfather and now belonged chiefly to the moths.

Martha examined everything.

She opened a box of Christmas ornaments, tried on a bonnet decorated with three faded cherries, and wound the handle of a music box which played half of a tune and then sneezed. After this she found a basket filled with old keys.

There were large keys and little keys, bright keys and rusty keys, crooked keys, hollow keys, keys with square holes, keys with round holes, and keys that appeared never to have opened anything in their lives. Martha carried the basket from trunk to trunk and tried them wherever she discovered a lock.

Most failed. One opened a desk containing tax receipts. Another opened a tin box filled with buttons. A third unlocked a suitcase in which lay six woollen stockings, all for the left foot.

At the darkest end of the attic stood a chest she had never seen before.

It was long, broad, and deep, and was bound with strips of black iron. Its wood had once been red, though travel, age, and rough handling had darkened it to the color of dried blood. On the lid appeared a painted crown, two crossed knives, and the words:

**TO WALTER BELL, CHICAGO, AMERICA  
WITH EXTREME CARE  
FROM NAPLES, ITALY**

Walter Bell was Martha’s uncle.

He had departed for Europe three years earlier to study ancient ruins, volcanoes, and inexpensive hotels. He wrote regularly at first. Then his letters became shorter and less frequent, and at last they stopped altogether. No one knew where he was. Martha’s mother feared he had fallen from a mountain; Martha’s father believed he had forgotten to buy stamps; and Martha privately suspected that he had married a queen.

The chest had arrived six months ago without explanation and had been placed in the attic. A locksmith had been sent for, but he had gone to the wrong house, and after that everyone had become busy with other matters.

Martha knelt before the lock.

“This,” she said, “must be the very thing I’m not to meddle with.”

She selected a long brass key.

It would not enter.

She tried a flat silver key.

It entered but would not turn.

She tried a black key with three teeth, then a tiny key tied to a blue ribbon, then a key shaped rather like a fish. Nothing happened.

At last, from the bottom of the basket, she drew a heavy iron key whose handle formed the letter V.

It entered the lock.

It turned.

There was a deep click inside the chest.

Martha drew back.

For a moment nothing occurred. Then the lid trembled. It rose the width of a finger, fell, rose again, and remained balanced as though something underneath were pushing very hard but had not enough room to push comfortably.

A muffled voice said, “Beni, remove your elbow from my eye.”

Another replied, “It is not my elbow.”

“Then whose elbow is it?”

“Possibly Lugui’s.”

“’Tis so!” cried a third voice.

The lid flew open.

A thin man sprang from the chest, struck his head against a rafter, bowed to Martha as if the accident had been planned, and twirled the ends of an enormous black mustache. He wore a scarlet sash, a purple coat, yellow boots, and enough knives to equip a kitchen.

A second man climbed out after him. He was of middle size and had green trousers, a blue waistcoat, silver earrings, and a red handkerchief knotted around his head. In one hand he carried a pistol; in the other, for no apparent reason, he held a lemon.

Last came a short, fat man in an orange cloak. He had to be pulled by the arms and pushed from behind, which was difficult because no one remained behind to push him. At last he rolled over the edge, landed on the floor, and announced:

“’Tis so!”

The three stood in a row and bowed.

“Permit us to introduce ourselves,” said the thin man. “I am Victor the Merciless, terror of the southern roads, master of the midnight pass, and captain of this noble company.”

“I,” cried the middle-sized man, striking his breast, “am Beni the Bloodthirsty, from whose glance strong men collapse and weak men complain to the authorities.”

“And I am Lugui,” said the fat man.

There was a pause.

“Add something impressive,” whispered Beni.

Lugui considered. “I eat much.”

“’Tis so,” Victor said gravely.

Martha looked at them. “Are you bandits?”

The three men appeared deeply pleased.

“Child,” replied Victor, “you have recognized us.”

“You look like bandits,” said Martha. “Especially around the trousers.”

Beni glanced proudly at his green legs.

Victor looked about the attic. “And now, young signorina, inform us in what region we find ourselves. Are we in Calabria?”

“No.”

“Sicily?”

“No.”

“The mountains beyond Salerno?”

“No. You’re in Chicago.”

The bandits stared at her.

Beni dropped his lemon.

“Chicago!” he shrieked.

“Chicago, America?”

“Yes,” said Martha.

Victor pressed both hands to his forehead. Lugui sat on a hatbox, which immediately collapsed.

“Confusion to our foes!” cried Beni. “We have crossed the ocean in a box!”

“An unprofessional method of travel,” said Victor. “I tender you my regrets, comrades. We entered that chest to surprise the wealthy Signor Bell at his Italian villa.”

“Uncle Walter hasn’t any villa,” said Martha. “He once had a room over a bakery.”

“That may explain the smell,” observed Lugui.

“Where is this Signor Bell?” demanded Beni. “Let him appear, so we may surround him, seize his gold, and leave him tied melodramatically to a tree.”

“He’s missing.”

The bandits consulted one another in whispers.

“This is awkward,” Victor admitted. “A bandit requires victims. Without victims, he is merely a brightly dressed gentleman with irregular habits.”

“You could get work,” Martha suggested.

The three recoiled.

“Work!” exclaimed Beni.

“Honest employment,” Martha added.

Beni staggered against a trunk. Lugui covered his ears. Victor became pale beneath his mustache.

“Do not speak those dreadful words before my men,” Victor said. “They are sensitive.”

“You might drive a trolley car.”

“And announce the streets?” cried Beni. “Never!”

“You could be motor men.”

“We should be required to stop for passengers,” Victor said. “A bandit stops for no one.”

“You might open an Italian restaurant.”

Lugui brightened, but Victor frowned.

“No. The cooking of food for honest payment would stain the honor of our profession.”

“Besides,” Beni said, “Lugui would consume the profits.”

“’Tis so.”

Martha considered. “Perhaps you could become politicians.”

Victor’s eyes narrowed. “Do they rob people?”

“Papa says they do.”

“Do they hide in mountains?”

“No. Mostly in offices.”

“Do the police chase them?”

“Not often.”

The three bandits looked offended.

“Then they lack romance,” said Victor. “We must not abandon our high calling.”

“But there aren’t many travelers to rob in this attic,” Martha pointed out.

Victor surveyed the trunks, old furniture, carpets, and broken lamps. Then he looked toward the attic stairs.

“What lies below?”

“Our house.”

“Is it wealthy?”

“I don’t know. We have two bathrooms.”

Beni gave a low whistle.

Victor straightened his sash. “Comrades, fortune has not deserted us. We shall descend, plunder this palace, and return before nightfall.”

“You mustn’t,” Martha said. “It would be wicked.”

At the word wicked, Victor smiled with satisfaction.

“Ah!”

“And naughty,” Martha added.

The effect was astonishing.

Beni’s mouth fell open. Lugui looked ashamed. Victor drew himself up stiffly.

“Signorina,” he said, “we are desperate criminals. We have been called cruel, lawless, bloodthirsty, infamous, and poorly groomed. But never—never—have we been called naughty.”

“Well, you are,” Martha replied.

Beni wiped away a tear. “She wounds us.”

“Nevertheless,” said Victor, recovering himself, “duty is duty. Forward!”

They marched downstairs.

Martha followed because it seemed better to watch them than to leave them alone among the family silver.

In the parlor Victor rolled up the carpet and attempted to carry it under one arm. Beni filled his sash with china shepherdesses, brass candlesticks, and the glass grapes from the mantel. Lugui removed the lace covers from the chairs and put them over his head.

“These are not valuable,” Martha said.

“They are mysterious,” Lugui answered.

In the dining room they found more suitable booty. Victor took the silver forks, silver spoons, and silver sugar bowl. Beni seized a cut-glass dish, a carving knife, six napkin rings, and the bell used to summon the maid. Each time he moved, the bell jingled, and he jumped.

Lugui discovered a mince pie in the pantry.

“This,” he declared, “is treasure.”

“That’s for supper,” said Martha.

“Then supper has been captured.”

He tucked the pie beneath his cloak.

The raid continued. The bandits took Martha’s father’s overcoat, her mother’s best hat, a copper kettle, the parlor clock, three umbrellas, a framed photograph of Aunt Julia, a Bible, a sofa cushion, a pair of skates, a vase containing artificial peonies, and a china dog with one blue eye.

They carried these things upstairs in three journeys.

On the first journey Victor bore the carpet, overcoat, and clock. Beni carried the silver and decorations. Lugui brought the pie.

On the second journey Victor brought two dresses and the kettle. Beni brought the umbrellas, Bible, and skates. Lugui brought the pie.

On the third journey Victor carried the cushion, vase, and china dog. Beni carried Aunt Julia, the napkin rings, and the best hat. Lugui still carried the pie, though it had grown noticeably thinner.

“You’re taking silly things,” Martha said when the attic floor had become covered with household property.

“A true bandit takes everything,” Victor replied.

“What will you do with Aunt Julia’s picture?”

“We shall demand a ransom.”

“Aunt Julia won’t pay it.”

“Then she is a woman of iron.”

Beni picked up the copper kettle. “This object pleases me. It shines.”

“It leaks,” said Martha.

He dropped it.

At that instant the doorbell rang downstairs.

The three bandits froze.

The bell rang again.

Victor drew a pistol. Beni drew two knives and accidentally cut a feather from Martha’s mother’s hat. Lugui drew the pie from his cloak.

“Who approaches?” Victor whispered.

Martha went to the attic window and looked down. On the front step stood a delivery boy holding a parcel. She knew at once that he had brought her mother’s new curtain material.

But Martha also saw her opportunity.

“Oh!” she cried.

“What is it?” demanded Beni.

“The police.”

The bandits turned white.

“How many?” Victor asked.

Martha pretended to count. “One hundred and twelve.”

“S’blood!” cried Victor.

“S’bananas!” cried Beni.

“’Tis many!” cried Lugui.

“They’re all heavily armed,” Martha continued. “Forty have rifles, thirty have swords, twenty carry cannons, and the rest are lawyers.”

At the word lawyers, the three criminals shuddered.

“What do they want?” Victor asked.

“They inspect attics on Thursdays.”

“This is Thursday?”

“Yes.”

“Betrayed by the calendar!” moaned Beni.

The doorbell rang a third time.

“They know we’re here,” said Martha. “They’ve surrounded the house. Some of them may be coming up the back stairs.”

Lugui dropped the remains of the pie into the copper kettle.

“Save us, signorina!” Victor begged.

“You’re terrible bandits,” Martha reminded him.

“Terrible, yes,” Victor said, “but not unreasonable.”

“You were robbing our house.”

“A misunderstanding.”

“You took the silver.”

“For safekeeping.”

“And the mince pie.”

Lugui clasped his hands. “Permit me to return what remains.”

“You called honest work dreadful.”

“We were misinformed,” Beni said quickly. “I have always longed to announce the stops on a trolley car.”

From below came the faint thump of the parcel being set on the porch.

“They’re bringing up a cannon,” Martha said.

Victor seized her hand. “Noble child, conceal us! If we escape, songs shall be sung of you in distant lands.”

“I don’t want songs. But you can get into the chest.”

The bandits looked at the chest, then at one another.

“It was cramped,” Beni said.

“Your elbow was in my eye,” Victor told him.

“It was Lugui’s.”

“’Tis so.”

The doorbell rang once more, long and loudly.

“Into the chest!” ordered Victor.

Lugui climbed in first. This required considerable arrangement. He sat down, stood up, turned sideways, removed his orange cloak, put it on again, and finally folded himself into the left-hand corner.

Beni entered next, carrying three knives, two pistols, the lemon he had recovered, and Martha’s mother’s hat.

“Leave the hat,” Martha said.

“It gives me courage.”

“It’s too large.”

With a groan, he surrendered it.

Victor climbed in last. He placed one boot beside Lugui’s ear and the other beneath Beni’s arm. His mustache stuck over the edge.

“You’ll have to tuck that in,” Martha said.

“A bandit’s mustache is his banner.”

“The police will see it.”

Victor hastily tucked it under his chin.

Martha tried to lower the lid, but it would not close.

“Somebody’s too high.”

“It is Lugui,” said Beni.

“’Tis not!”

They shifted. The chest creaked. A pistol went off inside, making a neat hole through the painted crown on the lid.

“Careless!” cried Victor.

“I tender you my regrets,” Beni replied.

Martha pushed again. The lid descended another inch and stopped.

“What now?”

“The pie,” Lugui confessed.

“Throw it out.”

There was a sorrowful silence. Then a crushed piece of crust appeared through the opening.

Martha removed it, leaned all her weight upon the lid, and forced it down. A muffled chorus of complaints arose from within, but she held the lid firmly and turned the iron key.

The lock clicked.

Just then the delivery boy, tired of waiting, walked away.

Martha stood beside the chest and listened. There was whispering inside.

“Is it dark?” asked Beni.

“’Tis so,” said Lugui.

“Silence,” ordered Victor. “We are escaping.”

After a while there came a faint munching sound, which proved that Lugui had retained some portion of the pie.

Martha placed the key in her pocket.

The attic was now in dreadful disorder. The carpet lay across two trunks. The silver was mixed with Christmas ornaments. The kettle contained pie filling. Aunt Julia’s picture leaned upside down against the birdcage, and the parlor clock had stopped at twenty minutes past four, as though it wanted no further part in the afternoon.

Martha carried everything downstairs.

She carried the forks and spoons, the sugar bowl and napkin rings. She carried the dresses, the overcoat, the umbrellas, and the Bible. She carried the peonies, the china dog, the sofa cushion, the skates, and Aunt Julia. She dragged the carpet, wiped the kettle, straightened the hat, and put the clock back on the mantel, though it afterward struck thirteen whenever it pleased.

By the time Martha’s mother returned from the Ladies’ Improvement Circle, the house looked almost ordinary.

“Did you have a quiet afternoon?” she asked.

“Pretty quiet,” Martha said.

“And you didn’t go into the attic?”

Martha hesitated. From far above came a muffled cry:

“Confusion to our foes!”

Mrs. Bell looked toward the ceiling.

“What was that?”

“The pipes,” said Martha. “They act awfully queer on Thursdays.”

Her mother accepted this explanation, since pipes, like children, are often blamed when no better answer is available.

As for the chest, it remained locked in the attic, and Martha never opened it again. Sometimes, on very still afternoons, she heard Victor making speeches, Beni complaining about somebody’s elbow, and Lugui asking whether supper had arrived.

Thus Martha learned that curiosity may lead to adventure, and that quick thinking may get one out of it. But she learned something else as well: when three Italian bandits rob your house, it is much easier to lock them in a chest than it is to carry all the stolen property downstairs.

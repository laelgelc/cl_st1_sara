The night on which Oberon burned his stories was one of those bitter nights when Winter, no longer content to pinch the noses of honest citizens and glaze the gutters for the destruction of elderly gentlemen, appears to have entered into a private compact with the winds for the general chastisement of mankind.

The snow had ceased at dusk; but the cold increased after it, tightening the town as a screw tightens a coffin-lid. The pavements rang beneath the foot. Every pane was blind with frost, every pump wore an icy beard, and the moon, sailing among torn black clouds, looked as hard and polished as a silver coin which no charitable hand had ever warmed. The wind came roaring down the narrow streets, caught me by the skirts, turned me half about, and then, discovering that I persisted in my course, drove me toward Oberon’s lodging with such violence that I may be said to have arrived under sail.

I crossed the market-place like a small vessel making dangerous water. Carts thundered past me; signs creaked overhead; chimney-pots groaned; and once a slate, detached from some invisible roof, struck the stones at my heel and flew into fragments. I took this for an omen, though of what I could not then determine. A man commonly interprets an omen after the event; before it, he is merely annoyed.

Oberon inhabited the upper floor of a wooden house in Griffin Street, over a maker of coffins and opposite a dealer in lamps—a position which, as I often told him, placed him conveniently between death and illumination. He never thanked me for the observation. On this evening I found the outer door unfastened, climbed the crooked stair, and knocked twice.

“Enter,” cried a voice of sepulchral magnificence.

I entered and saw my friend seated before an enormous fire.

The room was poor, but ambitious. Books in uncertain battalions occupied the chairs, the table, the window-sill, and much of the floor. A plaster bust of Shakespeare, cracked across the forehead, presided over the mantelpiece; beneath him stood an empty bottle, two full bottles, a chipped glass, and a brass candlestick whose flame was beaten sideways whenever the wind found entrance through the casement. The fire alone was magnificent. It leaped and bellowed in the chimney, making the mean walls alternately golden and black.

Oberon sat in a faded dressing-gown, his hair disordered, his cheek flushed, and his eyes fixed upon a heap of manuscripts tied with blue, red, and yellow ribbons. I knew those packets. I had seen some of them born, watched others languish through infancy, and had even carried one—an unfortunate romance in three volumes—to a bookseller who returned it with such speed that I suspected he had hurled it from an upper window.

“You are late,” said Oberon.

“The wind opposed me.”

“The wind!” he exclaimed. “The wind is a critic, then. It seeks to drive every man backward.”

“Not every man. I saw it greatly assist a hat.”

He frowned, but pushed a glass toward me. I took it, for the room, despite the fire, retained in its corners several colonies of cold.

“You find me,” he said, “at the close.”

“The close of the bottle?”

“The close of a career.”

“Ah. Which career?”

He waved his hand toward the manuscripts. “That fever, that folly, that splendid degradation which I have called authorship.”

As no printer had yet called it authorship, I judged his definition liberal; nevertheless, there are hours when contradiction is cruelty, and others when it is merely useless. This was both.

He rose and began to pace the room, stepping over books with the instinctive accuracy of a cat among flower-pots.

“Look at them,” he said. “There lie eight years of servitude. Eight years! While other men ate, I imagined banquets; while they loved, I constructed heroines; while they slept, I argued with phantoms. I have poured my youth into those pages as the alchemist poured his estate into the furnace—and with the same result: smoke, ashes, and creditors.”

“Not wholly the same. The alchemist occasionally exploded.”

“Pooh! Pish! Pshaw! You jest beside an open grave.”

I glanced at the coffin-maker’s sign visible through the frozen pane. “An upper grave, perhaps.”

He stopped before me.

“You do not understand. No one understands. The booksellers least of all. I have taken these tales from counter to counter, from sanctum to sanctum, from one upholstered tribunal of ignorance to another. One says the public dislikes sorrow; another, that the public will tolerate nothing else. One demands action; another complains that my action fatigues him. A third praises my imagination with the air of a physician admiring the complexion of a dying man, and regrets that imagination cannot be made commercially useful.”

“Did none offer to publish?”

“One offered—on condition that I should pay for paper, printing, advertisements, binding, storage, carriage, and the probable demolition of his reputation.”

“A cautious patron.”

“A villain! A cold-blooded trafficker in immortality! He weighs genius by the pound, rejects Homer for bulk, Dante for gloom, and Shakespeare because the plays are already known.”

Here Oberon drained his glass, replenished it, and held it up against the fire as though examining some ruby medicine for despair.

“They have poisoned me,” he continued more quietly.

“The booksellers?”

“The tales.”

This answer, being less reasonable than the former, alarmed me more.

“They have poisoned my happiness,” he said. “They creep after me. Their unwritten endings whisper beneath my pillow; their discarded scenes grin from the walls; their phrases return at dinner and make the meat taste of ink. There is something demonic in them—yes, demonic! I conceived them, but they have possessed me. I am no longer their author. I am their habitation.”

“You are also their landlord. Evict them.”

“I shall.”

He pointed to the fire.

Until that moment I had regarded his despair as one regards a thunderstorm heard at a safe distance: impressive, musical, and unlikely to wet one’s coat. But now I looked from the manuscripts to the grate and perceived that his rhetoric had acquired an intention.

“You cannot mean to burn them.”

“I can. I do. I must.”

“Why?”

“Because they are false idols.”

“Then sell them as antiquities.”

“Because they are deformed offspring.”

“That is no reason to roast them.”

“Because they are fiends!”

“Fiends tied with blue ribbon?”

“The devil has worn finer decorations.”

He seized the uppermost packet, but did not immediately cast it in. His fingers lingered on the knot. On the wrapper was written, in a large hand, *The Kingdom Under Glass: A Romance*. I remembered the summer in which he had composed it. We had then lodged near a lake whose surface, at dawn, lay so still that mountains seemed to descend beneath it into another sky. Oberon, looking into that incorporeal country, had declared that a drowned kingdom existed below, inhabited by pale kings, bells without sound, and women who remembered the future. For six weeks he had been happy.

“This one came to you by the lake,” I said.

He stared at the title.

“Yes,” he murmured. “The morning was blue. Do you remember the reeds? They shivered though there was no wind. I saw the palace distinctly—every tower inverted, every window blazing downward into the deep.”

“And you wrote ten chapters before breakfast.”

“Twelve.”

“Eleven and a preface.”

A reluctant smile troubled his mouth. For a moment the room softened. He untied the ribbon and turned the pages. Here were his corrections, his vehement marginal commandments, his arrows, insertions, excommunications, and repentances. A manuscript after revision resembles a battlefield upon which grammar has defeated the author at considerable cost to both.

He read a passage under his breath. The old light entered his eyes.

“There was beauty in the conception,” he said.

“Much beauty.”

“The queen who could speak only in reflections—”

“Excellent.”

“And the bronze divers descending by moonlight—”

“Unforgettable.”

“You said they delayed the plot.”

“They delayed it beautifully.”

His smile vanished. “Yet they refused it.”

“Booksellers are not bronze divers.”

“They called it obscure.”

“They fear water.”

He flung the packet upon the table.

“No! You tempt me backward. That pleasure was the bait; this misery is the hook. Inspiration! What is it? A cup of water discovered in a desert, delicious because one is perishing, and gone before one knows whether it was water or a mirage. Composition is enchantment; revision, awakening; publication—ha! publication is the locked gate before which the naked dreamer discovers a notice forbidding trespass.”

He snatched another bundle.

“Here is *The Bell of Saint Orison*. I heard that bell in sleep for three years. Here is *The Widow’s Lantern*, written while my mother lay dying. Here, *The Last Masque of Nebuchadnezzar*—rejected as improbable by a man who publishes parliamentary speeches! Here are my martyrs, my witches, my gray abbots, my children born old, my dead who would not remain decorously buried. Shall they pursue me forever?”

“They have remained very quiet on the table.”

“That is their cunning.”

He drank again. The fire struck upward, impatient as a creditor.

I attempted persuasion. I spoke of time, revision, changing taste, future recognition, and the many admirable works which had endured neglect before triumphing over posterity. These arguments, being ancient, respectable, and of no practical use whatever, affected him as rain affects a statue.

“Posterity!” he cried. “The graveyard of unpaid accounts! Must I dine on future praise? Shall I warm myself at unborn admiration? Will a bookseller in the year two thousand send me royalties backward through eternity?”

“Postal arrangements may improve.”

“Mock me! It is fitting. I am already a comic figure—the lean poet at the fat merchant’s door, the conjurer whose fairy coin turns to leaves at noon. But I shall be free. Tonight I perform my own deliverance. Let Alexandria burn again, though its library occupy one table and contain no work approved by reviewers!”

Before I could prevent him, he thrust *The Kingdom Under Glass* into the grate.

The ribbon caught first, curling into a small blue serpent. Then flame seized the wrapper, raced along the edges, and opened the packet leaf by leaf. The close-written pages blackened; sentences glowed crimson for an instant, each word legible in fire, then broke into scales of ash. Oberon leaned over them with a countenance compounded of priest, murderer, and cook.

“There,” he said hoarsely. “The kingdom has fallen.”

“It appears to burn upward.”

“The laws of nature insult me even now.”

He added *The Bell of Saint Orison*. The fire rang no bell, but gave a hollow report as air escaped between the sheets. Then followed *The Widow’s Lantern*, which produced, by an accident of ink or gum, a green flame. At this Oberon recoiled.

“Did you see?”

“Copper in the coloring, perhaps.”

“It was her lantern!”

“It was chemistry.”

“Chemistry is the superstition of materialists.”

Packet after packet went in. I rescued one from his hand and asked whether he had no copy.

“None.”

“No draft?”

“Burned last spring.”

“No notes?”

“Torn.”

“Then at least allow me to preserve this.”

He read the title: *The Fiend of Ashenbridge*.

His face altered.

“That least of all.”

“Why?”

“Because it contains him.”

“Him?”

“The thing I made and could not unmake.”

I had read part of that tale. Its villain was a shadowy being who entered houses through chimneys, feeding upon unwritten thoughts and leaving sparks in men’s brains. I had considered him picturesque, though too fond of speeches.

“Oberon,” I said, “your fiend is ink.”

“Ink is merely darkness made fluid.”

“And paper?”

“The grave-cloth of ideas.”

“Your definitions grow dangerous.”

He tore the packet from me.

“The bookseller laughed at him. Laughed! He said my monster could not frighten a child of seven. Let him go, then. Let him return to his element.”

He hurled the tale into the heart of the blaze.

For several moments neither of us spoke. The papers swelled, collapsed, and sent forth a multitude of little flaming fragments. The room filled with the bitter odor of scorched ink and sealing-wax. Outside, the wind struck the house broadside, making the rafters complain.

Oberon watched with terrible attention.

“There!” he whispered.

“What?”

“The bridge.”

I saw only two charred sheets leaning together.

“And beneath it—the river! The horsemen! Look, look! The black tower is opening.”

The burning leaves, lifted and folded by heat, did indeed assume fleeting architectures. A turret sprang up and perished; a face glimmered in profile; a procession of red figures ran along a margin and plunged into darkness. The imagination which had created the tales now fought to preserve them, transforming their destruction into one final, magnificent edition.

Oberon extended both hands.

“My people,” he cried. “They are coming out! The queen beneath the lake—her glass crown melts! The widow lifts her lamp. Old Father Anselm walks through the coals. Ah, my little prince, I had forgotten you!”

I confess that I, too, saw something—not what he named, perhaps, but enough to understand him. Flame is the oldest romancer. Give it a rag, a twig, a crumpled page, and it builds cities more splendid than ours, peoples them, conquers them, and destroys them before the sober eye can object.

Then a black shape rose in the grate.

It gathered itself from the burning manuscript, a crooked body with long arms and a pointed head; two holes shone where eyes might have been. It leaned toward us, wavering, enlarged, and for one absurd yet unpleasant instant seemed to grin.

“The fiend!” shouted Oberon.

A gust descended the chimney. The shape burst apart. Half flew into the room as ash; half shot upward in a whirl of sparks.

Oberon staggered back and struck the table. The bottle fell, but I caught it—a service to literature for which I have never received acknowledgment.

“It is done,” he said.

The rage had left him. His face, a moment before illuminated by sacrifice, now appeared exhausted and old. He sank into the chair and stared at the grate, where red embers consumed the last corners of his labor.

“What have I done?”

“What you repeatedly announced you would do.”

“Eight years.”

“Yes.”

“Gone.”

“Very nearly.”

He dropped to his knees and thrust the tongs among the coals. He dragged out a smoking fragment, slapped it with his hand, read three surviving words, and uttered a sound more piteous than any in his fiction.

“‘And she remembered—’ Remembered what? I shall never know!”

“You wrote it.”

“But I have forgotten!”

He searched frantically through the ash. Black leaves broke at his touch. A sentence, glowing along one edge, vanished while he read it. He caught at scraps as a miser might catch banknotes in a gale.

“My kingdom! My bells! My dead! Fool—assassin—barbarian! Worse than the caliph who burned Alexandria, for he may at least have disliked reading. I have murdered the only witnesses that I ever lived.”

“You remain alive.”

“For what? To eat, sleep, grow bald, and be forgotten by persons who never heard of me? A man’s life is a corridor between two locked doors unless he hangs some picture on the wall. Mine were poor pictures, perhaps—but they were mine!”

A distant cry arose in the street.

Oberon did not hear it. He sat amid the ashes, mourning with an extravagance which would have been ludicrous had it not been sincere—and which, being sincere, was only partly ludicrous.

The cry came again, nearer and multiplied.

“Fire!”

I went to the window. Against the frosted glass a reddish pulse appeared and vanished.

“Fire!” shouted voices below. “Wake! Fire!”

A bell began to clang. Another answered from beyond the market-place; then another, until the whole frozen town seemed filled with iron tongues. Doors burst open. Men ran half-dressed into the street. Women called from upper windows. The wind, delighted to exchange cold mischief for hot, roared among the roofs.

I opened the casement. Smoke descended upon us.

“The roof,” I said.

“What?”

“Our roof is burning.”

We ran to the landing. Already feet thundered on the stairs. The coffin-maker appeared in his nightshirt, carrying a mallet in one hand and a child in the other. Behind him came his wife with a clock, then his apprentice with nothing but his boots.

“Out!” he shouted. “The sparks have fired the shingles!”

Oberon stood motionless.

“Sparks?” he repeated.

“From your chimney, curse you! The roof’s ablaze!”

A burning fragment sailed past the stair window like a crimson moth and lodged beneath the eaves of the neighboring warehouse. Almost instantly a thread of flame ran along the dry wood. The wind seized it, flattened it, stretched it, and flung it onward.

We descended into Griffin Street. Above us Oberon’s roof was a red, ragged crown. Fire streamed from it horizontally, as banners stream in battle. The lamp-dealer’s sign ignited; his stock, faithful to its profession, contributed an astonishing brightness. Across the lane, sparks settled upon a stable. Horses screamed. Men formed lines at the pumps, but the water froze upon the stones or flew backward in the gale. Shingles leaped from roof to roof. Windows reddened, cracked, and burst.

“Your manuscripts,” I said. “The burning leaves went up the chimney.”

Oberon looked at me, then at the town.

The conflagration had already crossed the market-place. Bells clamored; engines rattled; wheels thundered over the iron pavement. Citizens rushed about with blankets, buckets, ladders, portraits of ancestors, cages of birds, ledgers, cradles, and objects chosen in that sublime confusion which reveals what each soul considers indispensable. One banker saved a stuffed owl and abandoned his account books. A philosopher carried three volumes of metaphysics into the street, returned for the fourth, and found the staircase gone.

The fire mounted higher. It shone upon the snow, turning winter into a ghastly summer. Chimneys stood black against the blaze like monuments erected before the battle had ended.

Oberon’s despair slowly changed.

His shoulders straightened. His eyes widened. The flames of Griffin Street entered them and found congenial lodging. He began to laugh—not loudly at first, but with a low, amazed delight.

“What is it?” I demanded.

“They refused them.”

“This is hardly the moment—”

“They said my tales had no power.”

“Oberon—”

“They said they would kindle no interest!”

A roof fell with a roar, sending a tower of sparks into the heavens.

He sprang upon a water-butt and lifted both arms toward the blazing town.

“Behold!” he cried. “No publisher would print me, no critic praise me, no public buy me—but I have my readers now! Roof, window, steeple, street—every one illuminated! They called my imagination cold. Cold! Ha! It has set the town on fire!”

Then, while buckets passed beneath him and respectable citizens shouted for him to come down and make himself useful, Oberon stood exultant amid the snow and sparks, crowned by that dreadful light—an author at last successful, though in an edition which no bookseller could return.

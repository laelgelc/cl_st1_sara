In the spring of 1774, when the forests of Ohio were still broad enough to swallow the roads of the white man and hide the villages of a nation, there were many tribes dwelling between the river and the lakes, and many ancient causes of jealousy among them. The Iroquois and the Algonquin peoples had not forgotten their former wars; the Shawnees distrusted the Mingos, the Delawares remembered injuries from both, and the Wyandots, Ottawas, Miamis, and scattered bands of other nations held themselves apart according to old alliances and older grievances. Yet a danger greater than their mutual hatreds was creeping upon them. Cabins appeared where hunting lodges had stood; trees were girdled and died; smoke rose from new clearings; surveyors passed through the woods with chain and compass, measuring lands which no man had asked the tribes to sell.

Thus necessity began to accomplish what friendship had failed to do. The separate nations discovered that the same flood threatened every village, and that the white advance did not pause to inquire whether its victim spoke an Iroquois or an Algonquin tongue. They did not become one people, for such unions are not made in a day, nor even in a generation; but they came at last to feel that their misfortunes were common, and from this feeling arose two remarkable leaders, separated by years, unlike in many qualities, yet joined in the unhappy purpose of defending a country which was passing from their race.

The first of these was Logan, a Mingo chief, whose Indian name has been differently given by different writers. It is said that he took the name Logan from a Pennsylvania gentleman whom he admired; whether this is wholly true or not, it agrees with what we know of his early affection for the whites. He was no enemy of the settler merely because the settler had a pale face. He entered the cabins of the border as a friend, shared his game with hungry families, and was proud of the confidence placed in his word. His generosity was not the careless generosity of one who gives what he does not value. He gave what was precious—food in a season of scarcity, protection in a time of fear, and faith when faith between the races was becoming rare.

At one time a white woman, living in a lonely cabin, had brought forth a child, and Logan, hearing that she lacked necessary articles, went to see her. The men of the household were absent, and she may well have been frightened when the tall chief appeared at the door; but he spoke quietly, looked at the infant, and presently went away. Some days afterward he returned with a pair of little moccasins, carefully fashioned by his own hands for the baby’s feet.

“White child cannot walk in woods with bare feet,” he said.

The mother laughed and wept together, as mothers sometimes do, and the infant, knowing nothing of tribe or color, stretched its hands toward the bright beads. It is a small incident, but I have given it because small deeds often show character more clearly than battles. A man may be brave while thousands look on; it is something nobler to be gentle when there is no witness but a woman and a child.

Such was Logan before revenge entered his heart. He loved friendship, and he was loved in return by many who had sat beside his fire. But the frontier at that period was inhabited not only by courageous and industrious settlers, but also by reckless men, who considered the killing of an Indian a deed requiring neither excuse nor repentance. Some had suffered cruelly and brooded upon their wrongs; some feared the tribes and struck from fear; some were merely brutal. They called every Indian savage, yet in their treachery they were themselves more savage than those whom they condemned.

In 1774 a party of Virginians enticed several Indians into their power near the Ohio and murdered them. Among the dead were members of Logan’s family. The exact number and relationship have been disputed, as such matters commonly are where tidings travel by frightened messengers and are repeated about a hundred fires; but it is certain that those dearest to him were slain, and that they were slain not in open battle, but through perfidy.

When the news reached Logan, he did not at first speak. He sat apart, with his blanket drawn about him, while the village murmured. An old man placed food near him, but he did not touch it. A child came close, was frightened by his face, and stole away. At sunset he rose and went alone into the woods.

It is easy for one who has never endured such an injury to condemn the vengeance that followed. We must condemn it, because innocent blood cannot pay for guilty blood; but we may condemn with pity. Revenge gnawed at Logan’s heart until the gentle friend of the cabins became the terror of the border. He vowed that he would take life for life, and during the summer his warriors passed swiftly through the settlements, striking at exposed homes and lonely travelers. Men were killed, houses burned, and families driven in panic toward the stronger stations.

Yet even in this season of wrath, Logan did not become the indiscriminate enemy of mankind. He believed himself engaged in a personal vengeance rather than a war against all whites. He could not always control those who followed him, and innocent persons suffered; nevertheless, again and again the older nobility of his character broke through the darkness which grief had cast over it.

Among the captives taken by the Indians was Major William Robinson. He was carried to a village, where a crowd gathered about him, and it was determined that he should die. Some cried for the stake; others flourished their tomahawks and recounted injuries committed by white men. Robinson stood bound, expecting each moment to be his last. He had perhaps heard many tales of Indian cruelty, and he now saw enough to persuade him that the worst of them might be true.

Then Logan pushed through the circle.

“This man is mine,” he said.

A warrior protested that the prisoner belonged to all, for all had enemies to avenge.

“No,” answered Logan. “You kill him, you kill Logan’s prisoner. Then you make Logan your enemy.”

There was a long dispute. Rage is noisy, but authority may speak low. Logan loosened Robinson’s bonds, placed himself beside him, and faced the assembly. The crowd yielded at last, not because its anger was gone, but because the chief’s courage and reputation were greater than its anger. He took the prisoner to his lodge and guarded him.

As the story goes, Logan asked Robinson to write a few words for him. The chief wished the whites to understand that he was not murdering from mere delight in blood.

“Tell them,” he said, “that Logan’s family was killed. Tell them he sought those who killed his blood. Tell them he does not wish war with every white man.”

The message was written, and Logan fastened it where it might be found. There is something deeply affecting in this effort of an injured man to distinguish his vengeance from cruelty. He was doing wrong, yet he wished even his enemies to know that he had once loved them and had not forgotten justice altogether.

The troubles widened into what has been called Lord Dunmore’s War. The Virginia troops crossed into the Indian country, and at Point Pleasant a severe battle was fought. The tribes were compelled to treat for peace. The chiefs came to the appointed place, but Logan did not appear. Perhaps pride withheld him; perhaps he could not endure to stand among men whose countrymen had destroyed his home; perhaps, having taken the revenge he had vowed, he felt only the emptiness which revenge always leaves. Lord Dunmore sent a messenger to him, and Logan delivered the speech by which his name has chiefly been remembered.

He said, in substance: “Ask any white man whether Logan ever entered his cabin hungry and was not fed, or whether a white man came to Logan cold and was not clothed. Ask whether he ever turned from friendship. He loved the whites, and men pointed to him as the friend of white men. Then his family was murdered, and not one of his blood was spared. He sought vengeance; he killed many; he rejoiced that he had taken payment. Yet do not think he fears death. Who remains to mourn for Logan? Not one.”

Whether every word has reached us exactly as it was uttered, no one can now say. The message passed through an interpreter and was afterward written down, and some have questioned portions of it. But the sorrow in it is Logan’s sorrow, and the proud loneliness is his. It is not the boast of a triumphant warrior. It is the cry of a man standing in the ashes of his household, who has discovered that the blood of his enemies cannot restore the dead.

I think few speeches in our early history reveal more in fewer words. He does not deny his killings; he does not beg pardon; he does not excuse cruelty by calling it war. He places before his hearers two pictures—the open lodge of the generous Logan, and the empty lodge of the avenger—and between them lies the crime which changed him.

Peace came, but it brought Logan no peace. The white settlements continued to spread, while the tribes were pushed from one uncertain resting place to another. A man whose happiness had depended upon friendship and family now possessed neither. He wandered, drank deeply, and became at times violent and suspicious. Whiskey was one of the most destructive gifts carried to the Indians by the traders; it mastered strong men whom hunger, cold, and battle could not subdue. Logan, once dignified and self-controlled, sank beneath it.

His end was as lonely as his speech had foretold. He quarreled with another Indian, or was thought to threaten him, and was killed. Thus the man who had saved white captives, befriended white settlers, and avenged his murdered kindred, died by the hand of one of his own race, homeless and broken. There was no family to bend above him, no child wearing moccasins made by his patient fingers, no household fire to which his feet might turn.

We come now to another figure, greater as a statesman and soldier, though, in my judgment, not more admirable as a man. Tecumseh, the Shawnee, was born while the struggle for the Ohio country was growing fierce. From youth he saw the cabins multiplying, the hunting grounds narrowing, and treaty following treaty, each surrender presented to the tribes as final, yet each followed by a demand for more.

He became a warrior, and hatred of encroachment burned steadily within him; but he did not believe that hatred excused baseness. He opposed the torture of prisoners, a custom practiced in those days with barbarous ingenuity, and, when his companions prepared the stake, he sometimes cut the bonds himself. He protected women and children, and condemned the killing of the helpless. He was a savage, if we use the word as his enemies used it; but in mercy he was far less savage than many white men who boasted of civilization while taking scalps or shooting down unarmed families.

On one occasion a prisoner was dragged into camp and condemned to torment. Brush was gathered, and the victim, exhausted by wounds and travel, was tied to a tree. The warriors began their dance. Tecumseh entered the circle, struck the burning brand from a man’s hand, and cut the rope.

“Brave men fight warriors,” he said. “They do not burn one man who cannot fight.”

Some muttered that their relatives had died by white hands.

“Then meet the white warriors,” he answered. “Do not make yourselves women by tormenting a bound man.”

The expression belonged to the rough speech of the camp, but its meaning was plain. Courage, in Tecumseh’s ideal of conduct, required restraint as well as daring. He could kill in battle without hesitation, yet he despised cruelty after victory.

He possessed those gifts which cause men to follow: a tall and commanding form, great endurance, a fiery eloquence, and a mind capable of looking beyond the quarrels of a single village. He saw that no tribe could save itself alone. If the Shawnees sold land, the Delawares were weakened; if the Miamis yielded, the Wyandots stood exposed. He therefore advanced a bold doctrine—that the earth had been given to all the tribes together and could not rightfully be sold by a few chiefs. A single nation might sell its own blankets, he said, or its own horses; it could not sell the rivers, the forests, and the bones of the dead.

In this work he was assisted by his brother, commonly called the Prophet. The two were unlike. Tecumseh appealed to courage, patriotism, and reason; the Prophet appealed to fear and wonder. He declared that he received commands from the Master of Life, denounced many white customs, and promised supernatural protection to those who obeyed him. He urged the people to cast away whiskey and to return to the older ways, which was wise; but he also claimed a divine authority which made opposition seem wicked, and this claim was used for cruel purposes.

The brothers established a settlement to which followers came from distant nations. Messengers traveled north toward the lakes and south beyond the Ohio, carrying belts and speeches. The Prophet’s fame opened ears which politics alone might not have reached, while Tecumseh’s eloquence gave the movement dignity and purpose.

Three times, it is said, he addressed men who had come doubtful; and three times they asked him to continue. He spoke of the graves beneath the fields, of the game fleeing before axes, of the streams muddied by cattle, and of children who would one day ask what had become of their inheritance.

“Will you tell them,” he cried, “that you sold it for a keg and a blanket? Will you tell them that, while the white people stood together, the red people quarreled? The branches are broken one by one. Bound together, they may resist the storm.”

This was the great purpose of his life: not vengeance for a single house, as with Logan, but union for a whole race. He traveled among Northern and Southern tribes, enduring hunger, suspicion, and danger. Some listened and promised warriors; some feared American power; some had private arrangements which they would not abandon; and some disliked the Shawnees more than they disliked the settlers. The old rivalries were a wall which even Tecumseh could not wholly break down.

General William Henry Harrison, governor of the Indiana Territory, understood the danger of the confederacy. He treated Tecumseh with respect but rejected his doctrine concerning the common ownership of land. At one council the discussion became so heated that warriors sprang to their feet, and for a moment bloodshed seemed certain. Tecumseh stood sternly before the governor and declared that unjust treaties could not bind the nations that had not made them.

Harrison answered that the tribes were distinct peoples and that each might sell its own territory.

“No,” said Tecumseh. “The Great Spirit gave this country to all. No one tribe may give it away.”

Neither convinced the other, for behind their words stood two irreconcilable powers. Harrison represented a young republic eager for farms, roads, and states; Tecumseh represented peoples who knew that every American advance, however lawful it appeared upon paper, made their own future smaller.

While Tecumseh journeyed southward to strengthen his alliance, he warned his brother not to begin war. The confederacy was not ready. Its scattered members had not all pledged themselves; weapons and food were insufficient; and a premature battle might destroy in a morning what years of labor had built. The Prophet promised obedience.

But vanity is a poor guardian of wisdom. When Harrison marched toward the settlement, the Prophet permitted himself to believe—or pretended to believe—that his sacred power would protect the warriors. He assured them that American bullets would be harmless. During the darkness before dawn, the Indians attacked Harrison’s camp near Tippecanoe. They fought with courage, but surprise did not bring victory. The soldiers held their ground; the warriors withdrew; and the settlement was burned.

The defeat was more than a military loss. Men who had trusted the Prophet’s promises had seen companions fall beneath the bullets which were to have been turned aside. His supernatural authority collapsed. A mystery may survive argument, but it seldom survives a battlefield covered with those whom it promised to protect.

When Tecumseh returned and saw the blackened cabins, his anger was terrible. His brother came before him with explanations, but Tecumseh seized him by the hair and shook him.

“You had one command,” he said. “Wait. You have ruined all.”

Yet even then the enterprise was not wholly dead. The War of 1812 soon brought Britain and the United States again into conflict, and Tecumseh allied himself with the British, not because he loved them, but because he believed they offered the only remaining power by which American expansion might be checked. The British promised support and spoke of protecting Indian lands. Tecumseh accepted the alliance, though he watched them with the suspicion taught by long experience.

He distinguished himself in the campaigns about Detroit and the western lakes. British officers admired his intelligence, discipline, and personal courage. He did not crouch behind others in battle, nor claim honors after danger had passed. He was first where the fire was hottest, and last to seek safety. Yet his noblest actions in that war were not feats of arms.

After one engagement American prisoners were brought in, and some of the British-allied warriors began killing the wounded. Tecumseh rushed among them and drove them back, threatening those who continued. He then turned upon a British officer and demanded why the soldiers had allowed the massacre.

The officer replied that it was impossible to control the Indians.

Tecumseh’s eyes flashed. “Then put on women’s clothes,” he said. “You are not fit to command men.”

The rebuke was severe, but deserved. It is too easy for civilized commanders to profit by barbarity and afterward claim that they could not prevent it. Tecumseh would not accept such cowardly excuses. If the British were his allies, they must bear the responsibilities of alliance; if they called themselves superior, they must prove it by conduct.

At another time women and children belonging to the enemy came within the power of his followers. Tecumseh placed guards around them and ordered that no one should harm them or rob them of necessary clothing. A warrior complained that the whites had not always spared Indian families.

“We do not learn honor from those without it,” Tecumseh answered.

Here again appears the breadth of his character. He hated a policy, resisted an invading population, and fought its armies; but he did not make hatred of helpless individuals the measure of patriotism. The distinction is one that many educated nations have failed to preserve.

The British naval defeat on Lake Erie changed the balance of power. Their supply line was broken, and General Procter decided to retreat from Detroit into Canada. For some time he concealed or softened his intention, but Tecumseh quickly understood what was happening. He had seen provisions removed and preparations made; he knew that the British, who had urged the Indians to stand, were preparing to abandon the country.

At a council he rose to speak. His manner was controlled at first, but grief rather than anger gave force to his words. The Indians, he said, had listened to their British father. They had fought beside him and trusted his promises. Now the ships were lost, and the British proposed to flee without striking a proper blow.

“You told us to hold fast,” he said. “You gave us arms and called us your children. Where now is that courage? The animals carry their tails behind them, but you carry yours between your legs.”

He reminded Procter that the tribes had no distant cities to receive them, no secure farms farther east, no king across the ocean. Their homes were the ground being surrendered. The British might retreat and remain British; the Indians who retreated left their country behind.

Procter promised to make a stand, but continued withdrawing. Tecumseh had little choice except to follow, for his warriors lacked supplies and could not oppose the Americans alone. The retreat was disorderly, and discouragement spread among the allied force. Tecumseh, who had spent his life urging divided nations to stand together, now found himself dragged backward by the very allies upon whom his last hopes depended.

The Americans under Harrison overtook the retreating army near the Thames River in Canada. Procter placed his troops badly and soon fled from the field. Tecumseh remained with his warriors in the swampy woodland. He wore no general’s uniform. Some say he laid aside conspicuous ornaments so that he might fight as an ordinary warrior; some say he was recognized by his commanding figure. It is certain that he did not flee.

The American line advanced. Smoke gathered among the trees, rifles cracked, and men fought at close range in brush and mud. Tecumseh called to his warriors and moved wherever the resistance weakened. Then he fell. Who killed him has never been established beyond dispute, though several afterward claimed the honor. When darkness came, his body was not certainly identified, and the place of his burial remains unknown.

There is a fitting melancholy in this uncertainty. No carved stone marks his grave; but the lands for which he struggled, the rivers beside which he spoke, and the names of the nations he tried to unite are his monument. He flashed across the troubled history of the West like a meteoric light—brilliant, restless, and soon extinguished. With him perished the strongest hope of a broad Indigenous confederacy capable of checking the American advance.

The Prophet survived his brother for many years, but never recovered his former authority. The defeat at Tippecanoe had exposed his claims, and Tecumseh’s death removed the great spirit whose nobility had partly concealed the Prophet’s weakness. Yet superstition does not vanish at once. Fear lingers after faith has departed, and the Prophet continued for a time to accuse enemies of witchcraft.

These accusations were terrible weapons. Disease, crop failure, sudden death, or military defeat might be blamed upon some unpopular man or helpless woman. The accused could seldom prove innocence, for the very denial might be called evidence of secret power. Thus private hatred clothed itself in religion, and murder pretended to be justice.

Among those accused was Leatherlips, an aged Wyandot chief known for dignity and friendly relations with the whites. His enemies charged him with witchcraft and sent men to execute him. Friends urged him to escape, but he would not. Perhaps he believed flight would confess guilt; perhaps he was tired of seeing the old order fall; perhaps he knew that escape would only postpone his death.

He dressed himself carefully and walked with his executioners to a place near a grave prepared beneath the trees. He asked for a little time. Then he knelt, raised his face, and spoke quietly to the Great Spirit. There was no wild pleading, no attempt to purchase life by betraying another. When he had finished, he signaled that he was ready. A blow of the tomahawk ended his life.

I wish that every boy and girl who thinks courage belongs only to the battlefield might remember that old chief walking calmly toward his grave. He had no army, no hope of rescue, and no applause; yet he preserved his self-command when those who condemned him had surrendered both reason and mercy.

The persecutions continued until they touched a heart bold enough to resist them. A young warrior learned that his sister had been accused. The agents of the Prophet came to seize her, expecting the family to bow before their pretended authority. Instead, the brother stood in the doorway with his weapon in his hand.

“You shall not take her,” he said.

They answered that the Prophet had named her and that disobedience would offend the higher powers.

“The Prophet has made brother fear brother,” said the young man. “He has made us kill our wise men and our old women. If his power is great, let him come himself. My sister shall live.”

Others gathered, and some who had been silent found courage in his words. The agents withdrew. From that hour the accusations lost much of their terror, for tyranny built upon superstition lasts only while each victim believes himself alone.

Thus ended, not at once but by degrees, the dark influence which had accompanied the nobler attempt at union. Tecumseh had sought to bind the tribes against a common political danger; the Prophet, by fear and witchcraft, turned them against one another. One appealed to courage, the other to terror; one wished to save a people, the other to preserve authority. Their partnership had given the movement strength, but it had also planted within it the cause of destruction.

Between Logan and Tecumseh there are many comparisons to be made. Tecumseh possessed the greater intellect for affairs, the wider plan, the firmer discipline, and the more splendid military fame. Logan’s purpose narrowed after his family’s death to personal revenge, and his later years were clouded by drink and despair. Yet I place Logan, in the gentler qualities, even above the great Shawnee. Tecumseh protected captives because his ideal of honor commanded it; Logan protected them while grief and vengeance gnawed at his heart. Tecumseh loved his people with a statesman’s fire; Logan had once loved across the boundary of race, entering the cabins of strangers as a friend.

Both were defeated. The families, villages, alliances, and hunting grounds they tried to preserve disappeared or passed into other hands. But failure does not prove a cause ignoble, nor victory prove its possessors just. The white men brought farms, towns, schools, laws, and a civilization powerful enough to master the wilderness; they also brought broken promises, treacherous killings, whiskey, and hunger. The Indians defended home and kin with courage; they also tortured prisoners, killed the innocent, and sometimes surrendered judgment to cruel superstition. Nobility and savagery were found on both sides, as they have been found wherever frightened men contend for land.

It is well, therefore, to remember not merely who gained Ohio, but who suffered while it was gained. Let us remember Logan beside the little child whose moccasins he had made; Logan before the empty lodge, asking who remained to mourn him; Tecumseh cutting a prisoner loose and rebuking a commander who excused massacre; Leatherlips walking beneath the trees toward his grave; and the unnamed young warrior standing before his sister’s door.

The forests have fallen, the council fires are cold, and roads pass over the old war paths. Yet these figures remain, stern and sorrowful, upon the borderland of history. They ask no false praise and need no sentimental excuse. It is enough that we judge them as we ought to judge all men—not by race, not by victory, not by the names their enemies gave them, but by the cruelty they resisted, the mercy they showed, the faith they kept, and the human hearts which beat beneath the paint, the uniform, or the blanket.

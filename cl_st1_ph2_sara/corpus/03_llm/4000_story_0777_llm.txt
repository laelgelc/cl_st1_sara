### The Roots of the Flag

There were seven of us that afternoon in Lyon Hartwell’s studio, though I remember that for the first hour we seemed continually to miscount ourselves, because Bentley, whose departure had brought us together, was forever vanishing behind screens, into the little bedroom, or down the corridor in search of some book, glove, address, or unconsidered obligation. His luggage stood near the door in discouraged resignation: two strapped boxes, a leather portmanteau rubbed pale at the corners, and a long case containing, as he assured us, three canvases which America would either crown or bury. Over the case lay his winter coat, although the September day was warm; and upon the coat a gray hat had been crushed beneath somebody’s portfolio.

Bentley had received, forty-eight hours before, a cable from his father. It contained six words, four of which were imperative. Illness, a difficulty with the family property, and that grave distrust of cables which makes their obscurity more alarming than explicit calamity, had combined to send him home. We had made a luncheon of the occasion, at a restaurant in the rue Notre-Dame-des-Champs, where the meal stretched extravagantly through the middle of the day; where a perspiring waiter brought us warm wine, and three young Poles at the next table cried repeatedly for beer; and where, by common consent, no one spoke of the likelihood that Bentley would never return.

Paris was full of returns just then. Along the boulevards the chestnuts had become a burnished brown, and the women had begun to wear dark blue and purple after the pale looseness of summer. At every corner one encountered an acquaintance newly arrived from Brittany, Spain, England, or some inexpensive village in the Midi, sunburned, energetic, and already recounting the work he intended to do. Studios were opened and swept; casts emerged from their shrouds; canvases, newly stretched, shone bluishly in the high windows. The city had taken up its old business with that crisp, impersonal vitality which makes one’s private interruption seem almost an impertinence.

Bentley’s going was therefore the more disquieting. He was moving against the tide, and we felt, though we would scarcely have admitted it, that the current which carried him westward might prove stronger than the one that had brought him to us.

Hartwell had invited us to finish the afternoon at his place, partly because he lived near the restaurant, and partly because he alone possessed a room large enough to contain our melancholy without making it absurd. He was then at the beginning of the reputation that afterward became so secure; yet even in those years, when public commissions came slowly and critics confused severity with coldness, there was about him an authority independent of success. He was older than any of us by perhaps twelve years. His great shoulders, which appeared bronze-like under a rough brown coat, his narrow hips, his deliberate hands, and his head—small in proportion to the powerful frame, with hair already retreating from the temples—gave him less the appearance of an artist than of one of the workmen whose bodies he modeled. Only the eyes contradicted that first impression. They were gray, watchful, and, when he listened, almost painfully attentive.

His studio looked north across a confusion of zinc roofs and chimneys. The upper window admitted a gray light in which every solid object became important: the white edge of a plaster cast, the green-black shoulder of a bronze, the wet glimmer of clay. There were few pictures on the walls, for Hartwell distrusted decoration; but a faded Anatolian rug hung behind the stove, and on a shelf above a row of tools stood several terra-cotta heads, Roman fragments, two tarnished cups, and the wooden bugle of a French infantryman. The place smelled of damp clay, tobacco, and that faint mineral chill which clings to stone even in summer.

After luncheon we were disposed among chairs, packing cases, and cushions. Carver, who always collapsed after eating, lay full length upon a divan. Little Morris balanced on the edge of the model’s platform, swinging one leg; while Bentley wandered from group to group with the distracted geniality of a host at his own funeral. At first we spoke too much. We discussed the Salon, a notorious portrait, the chances of war in the Balkans, and the dangerous cleanliness of American cities. Dullness yawned through our animation. Every subject, however remote, presently found its way to steamers, distances, relatives, or the price of living in New York.

It was Morris who saw the covered figure in the corner.

“What have you there, Hartwell?”

Hartwell, seated near the stove with his pipe unlighted between his fingers, looked over his shoulder.

“Something I have been trying.”

“May we?”

He hesitated, then nodded. “Pull the cloth away.”

Morris climbed down, caught the brown sacking, and dragged it from the clay. Dust rose and floated through the slanting light.

The figure was that of a boy in a soldier’s blouse, moving forward against an invisible force. He was scarcely more than fifteen; one knew this not only from the delicate throat and unfinished body, but from a certain audacity in the lifted head, as though courage had come to him before knowledge. The uniform hung loosely from his narrow chest. His left sleeve was empty and pinned back. With the remaining hand he grasped the staff of a flag, whose heavy folds, massed close to him, gave the slender body something of their own upward sweep. Hartwell had not modeled the colors as a banner streaming magnificently in wind; they drooped, torn and weighty, about the boy, and seemed at once his burden, his shelter, and the visible shape of his resolve.

We became quiet. Even Bentley stopped sorting addresses.

Carver rose from the divan and went close to the clay. “It is for the Pennsylvania commission?”

“No.”

“A study for it?”

“Perhaps. Not officially.”

Morris, who had a sculptor’s impatience with reticence, walked around the platform. “The sleeve is extraordinary. It makes the whole thing happen. Where did you get him?”

Hartwell struck a match. The little flame lit his profile, cutting it clean against the gray window; then it sank into the bowl of his pipe.

“He was my uncle,” he said.

None of us had known that Hartwell had an American uncle, or, for that matter, any American connection beyond the accident of citizenship. He had been born in Florence, educated in France and Germany, and, except for two years which we vaguely understood he had spent in the United States, had lived all his life in Europe. His English was precise and uncolored; his French carried a Tuscan softness; and his manner toward patriotic exhibitions of any sort was marked by the mild forbearance of one who has seen too many frontiers to admire them.

Bentley leaned against the long packing case. “Your uncle was in the war?”

“In the Civil War. He enlisted at fifteen.”

“And lost an arm?”

Hartwell looked at the figure. “He lost both.”

Morris’s swinging foot became still.

Hartwell smoked for a moment, and we heard, from the courtyard below, the concierge shaking a mat and calling irritably to her cat. Beyond the roofs a bell struck four, the notes flattened by distance. I thought the conversation finished; Hartwell was not a man whom one urged into reminiscence. Then Bentley said, with an attempt at lightness which deceived none of us:

“You never told us that story.”

“No,” Hartwell replied. “I had not meant to tell it now.”

He rose, crossed to the window, and stood with his back toward us. The city beyond him was a delicate arrangement of slate, smoke, pale sky, and the yellow upper walls of houses touched by declining light. He knocked the ashes from his pipe upon the sill.

“But I suppose,” he continued, “this is as good a time as another. Bentley is going home. Going home is always an experience.”

He returned to his chair, drew it nearer the platform, and for a while contemplated the clay boy, not sentimentally, but with the searching dissatisfaction of a workman who has not yet secured his meaning.

“My father,” he began, “went abroad at twenty to study Art—with a capital letter, you understand, as Art was always written in our family correspondence. He had money enough, an agreeable person, and the conviction, commoner then than now, that genius declared itself chiefly through reluctance to engage in business. He modeled two ingenuous marbles in Rome, married the daughter of an English archaeologist, and never returned to America except on brief visits. Before I was born he had abandoned sculpture for collecting. He knew a good deal, bought well, and died respected; but I sometimes think his surest artistic judgment was exercised upon his own work.

“My mother died when I was young. Our houses were in Florence, Munich, and, later, Paris. America was represented to me by an eagle on certain documents, by my father’s occasional profanity, and by gifts sent at Christmas from two maiden aunts in Pennsylvania—maple sugar, embroidered slippers, preserved fruits, and once a mechanical bank in the shape of a negro, which my English grandmother promptly removed.

“I knew that the family had been settled in western Pennsylvania since before the Revolution. I knew there was a brick house, a hill, some oil property, and a graveyard containing a wearisome abundance of Hartwells. But these facts had no more relation to me than the ancestry of a person one reads about in a county history. My actual loyalties were local and successive: to the Arno seen from one window, to a square in Munich, to the Luxembourg in rain. I belonged to the streets I walked in, to the museums where I worked, to friends, masters, habits. Blood and soil appeared to me rather theatrical inventions, convenient to novelists and generals.

“Then, when I was twenty-eight, my father’s elder sister became helpless through paralysis. The other aunt had died; there was no close relative in America; and the old woman refused to leave the house. I had just quarreled with a man in whose studio I was working, had very little money, and possessed more self-importance than occupation. It was arranged that I should go over, settle certain matters, and remain until a companion could be found.

“I expected six months. I stayed two years.”

He paused while his pipe went out, but did not relight it.

“The voyage was bad, and New York in August appeared to me an ingenious machine for producing heat, noise, and indignation. From there I traveled westward through a succession of landscapes which seemed less inhabited than attacked. Towns burst out of the earth without warning; forests were cut open; raw banks of clay bled beside the tracks; locomotives drew black seams across an enormous sky. I had never seen such waste, or such force. Europe, even where it is ugly, has usually had time to compose its ugliness. That country had not. Everything was exposed—the labor, the appetite, the wound.

“Near Pittsburgh the air thickened. Furnaces colored the low clouds; mills flung out fire at noon; derricks creaked on the hills like black insects pumping at some buried heart. The rivers were leaden and burdened with refuse. Pipes crossed the ravines, sweating and splashing; slag heaps smoked beside patches of corn; and through it all there moved trains, wagons, barges, men—endless processions of energy, each pursued by another energy like the beam of a searchlight. I hated it. Yet I could not look away.

“The Hartwell house stood twelve miles beyond one of the river towns, on a slope above a tributary that had once been clear. The stream was brown when I saw it, and on damp evenings an oily film gathered among the reeds. The valley had been gashed for a narrow-gauge track; two wells worked beyond the orchard; and at night the flare from a refinery turned the clouds a dull crimson. Still, the house had escaped immediate destruction. It was square, red, severe, with white stone lintels, and it faced a lawn descending to old locusts and chestnuts. Behind it the garden had gone back to disorder. Purple asters choked the paths; pale roses flowered in exhausted clusters; vines pulled at the summerhouse, and the fruit trees, black with age, leaned away from the advancing smoke as though they already knew themselves doomed.

“My aunt occupied a room on the ground floor. She was a small woman with an immense head of white hair, and paralysis had drawn one side of her face downward, so that her expression, even in pleasure, retained something of grief. Her speech came slowly. At first she called me by my father’s name; later, and more frequently, by her younger brother’s—Lyon, my own name, the name of the boy there.

“She liked me to read the local newspaper aloud. She followed accidents, lawsuits, church suppers, deaths; and if I attempted to shorten a report of county commissioners, she detected it. Her memory for the recent past was almost gone, but remote events returned with tyrannical brightness. She spoke of people dead sixty years as if they had just left the room. Sometimes, when evening came and the machinery in the valley began its pounding, she would turn her head toward the window and say, ‘The boys are late from the creek.’ I never knew which boys she meant.

“It was in the sitting room that I first saw my uncle’s portrait. You must remember that until then he had been no more to me than a name, repeated inconveniently at banks and consulates. The picture hung above a narrow bookcase, in a bad oval frame. It had been painted from a daguerreotype, I suppose, and painted badly: the flesh was pink, the shadows brown, the buttons too brilliant. Yet the head held me.

“He was perhaps thirteen in the portrait. His hair was fair, brushed high from a broad forehead; his mouth was large and grave; the gray-blue eyes looked straight outward with that innocent composure which old photographs demanded of children. He wore a black jacket and a stiff collar. One hand rested upon a table beside a closed book, the fingers spread carefully as though he had been told not to move.

“The face resembled my father’s, but without his charm and without his weakness. It resembled mine also. That disturbed me. A chance arrangement of forehead, cheek, and jaw seemed a trivial basis for emotion; nevertheless, I often found myself looking up from the newspaper to meet those solemn, crudely painted eyes.

“‘He was the best of us,’ my aunt said one evening.

“I asked how he died.

“She became agitated and could not tell me. Later I heard the story from a man named Ralston, who had served in the regiment and kept a hardware shop in town. He was very old, but straight, with hands enlarged by work and a voice that had outlived several sets of teeth. When he learned who I was, he examined me for a long while and said, ‘You favor him about the brow.’

“My uncle enlisted in 1862. He was fifteen and small for his age. The recruiting officer refused him, so he went to another town and lied. The family brought him home once; he ran away again. At last my grandfather consented, or ceased to prevent him. Because he could play the cornet and because the colonel knew the family, he was attached first to the band; but he despised that safety and contrived, before long, to be made a color sergeant.

“Ralston told me that he had never seen a boy so proud of a flag. Not proud in the ceremonial way—he did not flourish it or make speeches. He cared for it as for a living charge. On marches he watched the sky for rain; in camp he kept it from the mud; and under fire he carried it upright when older men crouched.

“The regiment was engaged in one of those battles whose names we used to memorize and have since confused. There was a field rising toward woods, a stone wall, batteries on the ridge, and grain high enough to hide men who fell. The colors went down twice. My uncle took them the second time and moved ahead with the line. A shell burst near him and tore away his left arm above the elbow.

“Ralston saw him fall. Before anyone could reach him, the boy got upon his knees. The flagstaff had slipped beneath his body. He freed it, pushed the butt into the ground, and, using his shoulder and right hand, raised himself against it. They told him to lie still. He shook his head.

“‘Cut the sleeve away,’ he said.

“Someone cut it. They bound the stump, but he would not release the staff. The regiment had halted under the wall; officers were shouting; men were firing from their knees. Then the line moved. My uncle went with it, carrying the flag in one hand.

“‘Hang me,’ Ralston said, and his old voice broke there, ‘if he wasn’t smiling.’

“They advanced perhaps thirty yards. Another shell struck. It tore away the other arm and opened his side. He died before they carried him back. The flag was found across him.”

Hartwell’s account of the battle had been given in an even voice, without enlargement; and it was this economy, I think, that made the image intolerably clear. We saw the grain, the wall, the smoke dragging along the ground; the boy’s emptied sleeve; the childish body propped against the staff while the regiment, delayed but not saved, gathered itself for another movement.

Hartwell bent forward, his elbows on his knees.

“I was horrified,” he continued, “but I was not yet moved in the way Ralston expected. That ashamed me. He had given me something sacred to him, and I could respond only with imagination—the professional imagination, perhaps, which sees posture, weight, the action of muscles, and even in pity searches for a composition. I thought of the mutilated figure. I did not think of my uncle.

“For months I tried to think of him. I read the regimental history. I went to the family plot, where his name had been cut beneath that of my grandparents, although his body lay in a national cemetery in Virginia. On Memorial Day old soldiers came with little flags. Schoolchildren recited. A bugle sounded from the hill—thinly at first, then with a brightness that seemed to pierce the smoky air. I knew what I was supposed to feel. The bugle was the golden throat of all that sacrificed boyhood; the flags trembled among the stones; the old men stood bareheaded. Yet the meaning remained general. I was touched by age, ceremony, loss; not by kinship.

“That failure accompanied my whole life there. I performed my duties, and I believe I performed them decently. I read to my aunt, kept the accounts, dismissed a dishonest tenant, repaired the north roof, and learned more than I wished about oil leases. But the house never became mine. I moved through its rooms as through furnished history. The chairs had supported bodies whose blood was said to be mine; the worn stair rail had passed under their hands; their portraits darkened upon the walls. Nothing answered me.

“Outside, the country pressed upon us with a vitality almost violent. A new mill was built downstream. Roads widened; orchards disappeared; foreign laborers crowded the town, Poles, Slavs, Italians, men with bright shirts and exhausted faces. At night the furnace glare pulsed against my bedroom ceiling. The valley never slept. Hammers crashed, derricks complained, steam escaped with long sighs; now and then, between these mechanical outcries, one heard a boatman calling from the river, and the human voice seemed frailer than water.

“I resented the place for denying me the quiet beauty I had been taught to recognize. Yet gradually I suspected that the fault lay not wholly in the place. Its ugliness had magnitude. The black hills, the burning sky, the rivers carrying away days and weeks beneath their scum, the immigrant streets, the trees doomed before the advancing works—all were parts of something brutal, prodigal, and young. It was not Europe unfinished. It was another intention.

“My aunt declined. Six months became a year; the year extended toward two. There were mornings when she did not know me, and afternoons when she spoke to me with perfect lucidity about my father’s childhood. Once she said, ‘Lyon planted the sycamore because he wanted a tree that would grow faster than he did.’

“I asked which tree.

“She pointed toward the lawn. Near the lower wall stood a sycamore much taller than the house, pale-limbed, immense, its bark flaking in patches of cream and green. I had noticed it every day without knowing that the boy in the portrait had put it into the earth. That information made a slight breach in my indifference. It was the first thing he had done which I could still see living.

“Afterward I often worked beneath it. I had begun to model again, at first with no seriousness—heads of workmen, a child from the town, studies of hands. From under the sycamore I could see above the roof the flag on the courthouse two miles away, when the wind opened it; and nearer, at the end of our lawn, my aunt’s small flag hung from a pole that had leaned there since the war. It was raised only on holidays. The gardener, who was nearly as old as the flag, took charge of it.

“On the second Memorial Day of my stay, rain began before dawn and continued until afternoon. My aunt was ill and slept heavily. The exercises at the cemetery were postponed; the town band, imprisoned somewhere in the weather, sounded at intervals like a brass cupboard falling downstairs. I spent the morning writing letters, then went to the attic in search of a blanket.

“The attic covered the whole house. It was not romantic. There were no picturesque spinning wheels arranged for discovery, but boxes of tax receipts, broken bedsteads, jars, dead moths, school slates, trunks whose straps had rotted, and a family accumulation of objects saved long after affection for them had disappeared. Rain whispered over the shingles. Where the roof leaked, bowls had been placed to catch the water, and each received its drop with a different note.

“In a corner behind a cradle I found a small black trunk marked L.H. The lock had been forced, perhaps fifty years before. Inside were a boy’s things: a blue cap, two collars, marbles in a tobacco pouch, a jackknife with one broken blade, fishing line wound about a card, a bird’s egg packed in cotton, and several schoolbooks tied with string. The clothes smelled of dust and camphor. The cap was no bigger than the cap of a child.

“I sat down beside the trunk. I remember that I was irritated with myself for expecting a revelation from rubbish. The relics of childhood are usually meaningless to everyone except the person who preserved them, and he was dead. I opened an arithmetic book. The sums were neat at the beginning and desperate toward the end. In a geography he had colored the states with such violence that blue crossed into red and yellow engulfed several lakes. There were horses in the margins, cannon, boats, profiles with prodigious noses.

“The last book was an old school edition of Virgil, used before he went to war. On the flyleaf he had written his name three times—first carefully, then larger, then in a hand that tried to imitate an officer’s signature. Several passages in the Æneid were marked, not where I would have expected a master to mark them, but beside references to armor, ships, and banners. At the back of the volume, across two blank pages, he had drawn an American flag.

“It was a very bad drawing. The staff bent inward; the stripes were unequal; the canton contained perhaps twenty stars, some with six points, some with eight, and one resembling an asterisk. He had attempted to shade the folds and had rubbed the paper almost through. Beneath it, in round schoolboy writing, were words from the anthem:

“‘And the star-spangled banner in triumph shall wave
O’er the land of the free and the home of the brave.’

“He had written ‘triumph’ incorrectly, scratched it out, and written it again.

“That correction did what the portrait, the grave, and the regimental history had failed to do. Suddenly I knew him.

“I do not mean that I understood his motives, or that the mysteries of courage and death became plain. I knew something smaller and more absolute: the pressure of his hand upon that paper, the labor of his tongue between his teeth as he made the stars, his impatience at the crooked staff, his humiliation at the misspelled word. The hero vanished for an instant, and in his place was a boy drawing in a schoolbook—a vain, earnest, restless boy who had planted a tree because he wished to grow quickly, who had run away twice to war, who loved a flag before he knew what it could demand of him.

“The thing broke through me. I put my hand upon the page where his had rested. It was not imagination now, or at least not imagination as I had known it. The fifty years between us contracted. His youth seemed not past but interrupted; and the life in my own hand, warm upon the faded paper, appeared to continue something that had been violently cut away.

“I carried the book downstairs. My aunt was awake, though the room had grown dark around her. When I showed her the drawing, she smiled.

“‘He did flags everywhere,’ she said. ‘On the barn, on the copybooks, on my music. Father whipped him once for cutting up a red shawl.’

“Then she touched the page with the fingers she could still move.

“‘Poor Lyon,’ she said. ‘He thought he was going to come back taller.’

“That was all.

“Toward evening the rain stopped. The clouds separated low in the west, and a tarnished yellow light passed over the valley, touching the wet roofs, the dripping locust bark, the derricks on the hill. Mist lifted from the river in smoky blue-green folds. The postponed procession went to the cemetery; from far away I heard the band, then the bugle, clearer than on the year before.

“The gardener had raised the flag on our lawn. There was almost no wind, and it hung dark with rain. I took the Virgil and went down to the sycamore.

“The earth beneath the tree was soaked. Water fell from the leaves in slow, heavy drops; roots broke the surface like the backs of submerged animals. I sat against the trunk, where the pale bark was cool through my coat. Above me the boughs moved with long sighs, and beyond them, whenever a little air descended the hill, the wet flag stirred, loosened, and fell again.

“I remained there after dark. Lights came on in the valley one by one; the refinery flame reddened the clouds; trains moved along the river, their windows passing in a luminous chain. The mills began their night labor. Ordinarily that crashing had seemed to me the voice of an invading power, indifferent to house, tree, grave, and all the delicate accumulations by which men persuade themselves that life has continuity. That night I heard continuity in the noise itself. The force which had felled forests, opened hills, crowded the towns, and blackened the streams was terrible, but it was not alien. It had been in the boy also—the haste, the prodigality, the refusal to wait for maturity.

“I thought of him going forward with the staff pressed against his body; of the tree growing here while he lay buried in Virginia; of my grandfather walking beneath its first thin branches; of my father escaping to marble courtyards and measured light; of myself returning unwillingly, a stranger equipped with opinions. The country had not asked me whether I approved of it. It had merely taken me back.

“There are realizations which the mind approaches for years and receives finally through the body. As I sat there, I felt the great roots under me; not figuratively, but with my spine and shoulders, through the soaked ground and the rough trunk. The earth seemed to have grasped me. I was no longer looking at an ancestral place; I was within it—held by blood, error, affection, violence, and the obscure fidelity of the dead. For the first time in my life I understood that one may belong to what one has resisted, and that belonging is not admiration. It is recognition.

“A wind came up from the river. The flag lifted above the lawn, slowly at first, then opened. I could not distinguish its colors in the darkness. I heard only the soft shock of the cloth and the rope tapping the pole.

“That sound has never left me.

“My aunt died in November. I settled the estate the following spring, leased the house, and returned to Paris. I brought the schoolbook with me. For years I made studies and destroyed them. Whenever I attempted the obvious thing—the charge, the wound, the triumphant banner—the boy disappeared and a public monument took his place. I had to find him again as I found him in the attic: before the legend had hardened over him.

“This”—Hartwell looked toward the clay—“is nearer. Not near enough.”

No one answered. The room had darkened while he spoke, and the young soldier now existed chiefly in silhouette: the small head, the forward shoulder, the upright staff, the flag gathered in deep masses beside the body. From the street came the roll of wheels and the thin cry of a newspaper seller. Carver, who had remained standing near the platform, moved back quietly, as though his nearness to the figure had become an intrusion.

Bentley was still leaning against his packing case. His face, usually quick with amusement, had assumed a gravity that made him look younger and less certain. He glanced once toward his luggage, then at Hartwell, but whatever he had intended to say was prevented by a knock.

The concierge entered without waiting. She announced that the cab had arrived, that the driver objected to waiting, and that, if monsieur wished to catch the train, the boxes must descend immediately. Behind her appeared the driver himself, red-faced and skeptical, followed by a boy in an apron. At once the studio’s silence was broken into practical fragments. Straps were tested, coats found, coins sought, the long case condemned as impossible and then carried away. Bentley discovered his gray hat beneath the portfolio and laughed at its condition; no one else did.

We went down with him.

In the courtyard the evening had sharpened. A pale strip of sky remained above the roofs, and the lamps, newly lighted, shone on the wet stones. The driver arranged the luggage with resentful ingenuity, tying the canvas case beside him and wedging the portmanteau beneath Bentley’s feet. We stood in an awkward semicircle while the usual phrases were exchanged—letters, addresses, the certainty of return. Bentley shook hands with each of us. When he came to Hartwell, he held his hand a moment longer.

“I suppose I shall know it when I see it,” he said.

Hartwell looked at him steadily. “Perhaps not at first.”

Bentley got into the cab. The driver gathered the reins, the horse slipped once upon the stones, and then the wheels passed under the archway into the street.

We followed as far as the gate. The cab moved eastward, small among omnibuses and lamps, then turned across the boulevard and was lost in the flowing brightness of Paris. None of us waved after it had disappeared. We remained there, listening to the traffic, each occupied, I think, by a journey longer than Bentley’s—the passage from one shore to another, from inheritance to knowledge, from the painted face of a stranger to the pressure of a familiar hand.

Hartwell was the first to turn back toward the stairs.

“Come,” he said quietly. “It is cold.”

Then, after a moment, as though concluding not his story but the afternoon:

“Going home is always an experience.”

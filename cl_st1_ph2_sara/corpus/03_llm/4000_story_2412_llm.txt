### EDITORIAL NOTE

The following narrative is transcribed from the sole surviving copy of *The Red Basket: Being the Account of an Arm and Certain Other Matters*, printed without license at East Liverpool in the year 2412.

Possession of the pamphlet was made a felony under Article IX of the Labor Tranquillity Act of 2415, wherein it was declared unlawful for any bonded person “to read, write, repeat histories, preserve genealogies, or communicate matter tending to diminish respect for ownership.” Of the alleged thirty thousand copies, twenty-nine thousand, nine hundred and eighty-three were officially burned. Sixteen were subsequently found and destroyed. The remaining copy was discovered in 2861 inside the false bottom of an iron flour chest excavated near the ruins of Pittsburgh.

The work purports to record a speech delivered by a wandering storyteller to factory slaves sometime between 2390 and 2400. Although the speaker’s name is nowhere given, records of the Vanderwater Textile Combine, particularly Ledger H-77 and the private correspondence of Roger Vanderwater III, confirm the principal incident.

Modern readers should remember that, in those days, slavery was not considered an embarrassment. It was an institution. The distinction is important.

—Institute of Early Democratic Antiquities,

### THE TALE OF THE ARM

Listen, my brothers.

Come closer. The night is cold, and if you sit so far from the stove you will hear your teeth better than you hear me. Put no more coal on. Smoke is a tattler. Light is a tattler. Even a warm chimney may carry news to the patrol.

You at the door—have you wedged it?

Good.

And if there comes the scrape of a boot outside, I am a drunken fiddler and you are fools who paid to hear me. That is no crime yet. Not quite.

I promised you a tale of an arm. You have worked twelve hours, and some of you fourteen, and you do not want a sermon. I know. Your backs ache. Your bellies complain. Your eyes are full of lint. Yet I must tell you something before I tell you of the arm, because an arm cut from a man is only meat unless you know who owned the knife.

Not the blade, brothers. The knife.

First, can any man here read?

Do not look at the floor. I am not an overseer. I ask no names.

Can any man make sense of the little black marks stamped on a notice? Can any woman read the number entered beside her wage? Can one among you write, “This is mine,” and know whether the clerk has changed it to, “This belongs to my master”?

No?

That is why you are slaves.

You suppose you are slaves because the guards have guns, because the mills have walls, because the law says your children inherit your labor bond. Those things help. But the first chain is ignorance. A man who cannot read must swallow whatever history his master feeds him. If the master says, “My family has ruled since God laid the first brick,” the slave must nod. If the master says, “You consented to this debt,” the slave must bow. If the master says, “There is no law against what I do,” the slave cannot open the book and call him liar.

Books are little things. Paper is weak. A child can tear it.

That is why masters fear it.

Now I will tell you of the Vanderwaters, whose towers rise over the Western Manufactories and whose mark—a silver V over three waves—is burned into bales, engines, cattle, and men. You have heard that the Vanderwaters descend from admirals. That is written above the gate of their palace. It is carved in marble, so naturally it must be true.

It is not true.

I have read the old records with my own eyes.

The first of them was called Vange. Only Vange. He had no family name, because family names were then an expense reserved for masters, lawyers, and respectable thieves. He was a furnace slave in the Ohio works, a broad fellow, clever with his hands and cleverer with his tongue. The other slaves called him Bill Vange when they liked him, Sly Vange when they did not, and Bloody Vange thereafter.

Have you got that? Vange, Bill Vange, Sly Vange, Bloody Vange. One man. Four names. History often gives a man a new name when he earns a new kind of shame.

This was in 2156, after the Grain Wars and before the Bond Codex, when slavery wore a cheaper coat and pretended to be temporary employment. Men were bound for debts they had never incurred, fed from company stores, housed in company sheds, fined company money for breathing company air, and told they might purchase freedom if they lived to be two hundred.

There rose a rebellion at the Ohio works.

The furnace slaves had hammers. The pit slaves had picks. The women of the sorting sheds carried hooks. For three nights they held the river road, and they might have held it longer had not Vange crept out through the ash sluice and told the militia where the powder was stored.

The militia entered before dawn.

They were well armed. The slaves were well beaten.

Forty-two were hanged from furnace cranes. One hundred and six were sold south into the reclamation marshes. The rest returned to work with iron collars. Vange received a blue coat, twenty hectares, and authority over the widows of the men he had betrayed.

He was a slave.

Remember that.

Bloody Vange learned his letters from the company accountant. He learned figures better. He discovered that a whip could earn more money than a hammer, provided another man’s hand held the hammer. During the Cotton Disturbances he supplied troops. During the Miners’ Petition he supplied names. During the Short Bread Winter he purchased children from starving parents and rented them to the chemical works.

When he died, he owned seven hundred slaves and called himself William Van de Water, because villains, like bad meat, are often improved in the market by a foreign label.

His son joined the words. Vanderwater.

His grandson bought a judge.

His great-grandson bought the court.

His descendants bought newspapers and ordered printed therein that their ancestor had been Commodore Willem Vanderwater, who crossed the Atlantic with three ships and defended civilization from mutineers. The three ships were three ore carts. The Atlantic was an ash ditch. The mutineers were hungry slaves.

This is fact.

Why, then, do the masters forbid you to read history? Because a slave who reads may discover that the master’s grandfather stood beside him in chains. Worse, he may discover that the grandfather escaped those chains by fastening them upon his brothers.

The Vanderwaters grew rich. Rich is too small a word. A rich man owns a house. The Vanderwaters owned counties. They owned the rain that fell into their reservoirs and the mud that clogged the workers’ boots. Their mills stretched from Lake Erie to the Cumberland barrens. Their private trains crossed public roads without slowing. Their family law became commercial law, commercial law became national law, and national law became the lock upon your door.

Now we come forward two hundred years, as a man crosses rotten boards—carefully, because every plank is soaked in blood.

Roger Vanderwater III inherited the textile works called Hell’s Bottom.

Some of you have seen Hell’s Bottom. Some of you were born there. It crouches in a valley where the sun arrives late and smoke arrives early. Eight brick mills surround a canal black as shoe grease. In summer, the dye vats stink of vinegar and dead dogs. In winter, steam freezes upon the windows, so that the slaves labor all day behind white glass and never see whether the sky is blue.

The spinning rooms employed children because children’s fingers could dart between moving teeth. The carding rooms employed women because women, said the managers, had patient lungs. The press floors employed men until the presses shortened them.

Six thousand slaves slept in barracks upon the slope. Their wages were paid in stamped tin, redeemable at the Vanderwater store. Their meat was mostly memory. Their blankets were mostly holes. Yet every week three coins were deducted from each worker for the Mutual Distress and Injured Laborers’ Relief Fund.

A fine name, is it not?

If a man lost a hand, the fund was to feed him.

If a woman was burned by acid, the fund was to buy medicine.

If a child was drawn into the rollers, the fund was to bury what could be found.

The slaves possessed these rights under Section Twenty-Seven of the Industrial Mercy Ordinance. They possessed regular slave rights. They had no access to the law, no lawful advocate, no permission to read the ordinance, and no money that could be spent outside the company store—but they possessed rights. The masters said so.

Hell’s Bottom was governed by two overseers, Clancy and Munster.

Clancy was thin and red, with a neck like twisted wire. He enjoyed striking mouths because teeth, as he explained, were evidence of insolence.

Munster was large, pale, and soft. He did not often strike a slave himself. Violence tired him. He employed assistants and watched from a chair.

These two hell-hounds administered the Relief Fund. They administered it into their pockets.

They bought a hunting lodge in the northern woods. They bought mechanical horses. Clancy bought a gold tooth for every natural tooth he had lost to drink. Munster bought a bath cut from green stone, although he washed only on election days. When an injured slave applied for aid, the clerk reported that the fund was exhausted, the application improper, the injury self-inflicted, or the applicant dead.

The last reason was convenient, for it could be made true.

Among the pressmen was Tom Dixon, who had a wife named Ann and two little girls. Tom was a quiet man. Quiet men are praised by masters because masters mistake silence for contentment.

One morning the locking pin on Press Four cracked. The press came down while Tom was clearing cloth from beneath it. His left arm remained on one side. Tom came away on the other.

The foreman wrapped the stump in waste cotton and sent him to the barrack infirmary. The arm was thrown into a scrap pail.

Tom did not die.

This was considered troublesome.

Ann went to the Relief Office. She waited in the rain from sunrise until noon. Clancy came out picking his gold teeth.

“What do you want?”

“My husband’s aid, sir.”

“What aid?”

“The money deducted for injury.”

Clancy laughed. “Your husband damaged company machinery through carelessness. He owes nine months’ wages for interruption of production.”

Ann showed him the blood upon her dress.

“Take that rag away,” he said.

She did not move quickly enough, so he struck her.

The workers heard. They gathered before the office after the evening bell, not in rebellion—do not imagine banners and guns—but in that hesitant manner of slaves who know they are right and have been trained to apologize for it.

They asked for the accounts.

Clancy asked which of them could read accounts.

No one answered.

He smiled.

Then a man stepped forward. We shall call him Elias, though that was not the name under which he was registered.

“I can read them,” Elias said.

A dangerous sentence.

Munster ordered him seized. They took him into the discipline shed and beat him with a braided transmission belt. They beat him until Clancy grew bored and Munster grew hungry. Then they threw him beside the ash heaps with three broken ribs, a split cheek, and bleeding deep inside, which is the kind of bleeding an overseer likes because it leaves less stain upon company ground.

But Elias lived through the night.

He had learned to read from an old woman employed in the packing room. She had once been a schoolteacher before the Education Restrictions. She taught him with labels cut from chemical drums and scraps of shipping paper. CAUSTIC. EXPORT. PROPERTY. DEATH.

A small vocabulary, perhaps, but sufficient to describe a factory.

Elias obtained ink from a tally clerk. He obtained paper from the wrapper of a machine part. Lying upon his stomach because his broken ribs would not permit him to sit, he wrote a petition.

He wrote of the stolen deductions.

He wrote of Tom Dixon.

He wrote the names of eleven children killed in the spinning room and denied burial money.

He wrote of Munster’s green bath and Clancy’s golden mouth.

And then he asked who would carry the petition to Roger Vanderwater.

No one volunteered.

Do not condemn them, brothers. Courage is easy in a warm tale told long afterward. At Hell’s Bottom a slave found with writing could be blinded. A slave approaching the Vanderwater palace without a pass could be shot before he reached the outer hedge. Each man had a wife, a child, an old mother, or merely a life that was miserable but still his own.

Elias said, “I will carry it.”

“You cannot walk,” Ann Dixon told him.

“I can walk once.”

Now, each spring Roger Vanderwater offered a silver medal for the finest strawberries grown upon his estates. This pleased him. He bred strawberries as other men bred dogs, and spoke more tenderly of their bruises than of injuries among his workers.

A gardener at Hell’s Bottom had raised a new kind, white at the crown and crimson below, each berry large as a child’s fist. They were to be delivered to the palace in a cedar basket lined with blue silk.

Elias heard of this.

Then he went to the scrap house.

Tom’s arm still lay in the pail.

Yes, brothers, we have come to the arm.

It was a white arm, for the blood had drained from it, crushed above the elbow and blackened at the torn end. The hand was broad, with two fingers bent from an old loom injury. Elias took it to the washroom and cleaned away the grease.

It was well washed, of course. One must be neat when presenting a grievance to a gentleman.

He dressed himself in the gardener’s spare coat. He set the strawberries in the cedar basket, red and shining, fruit fit for the tongue of a master. In their midst he laid Tom Dixon’s arm. He folded the petition and pressed it into the dead fingers. As those fingers would not willingly hold it, he tied them shut with white thread.

Blood-red berries. A bloodless arm. Black words upon brown paper.

Body and book.

Have you got that?

At dawn Elias drove the gardener’s cart toward the palace. The guards saw the silver V stamped upon the basket cloth and waved him through. A poor man carrying nothing is suspicious. A poor man carrying luxury to a rich man is invisible.

The Vanderwater palace stood upon a hill above clean air. Its windows were made from old sea glass. Its eastern hall contained a forest of indoor orange trees. Water ran uphill there by machinery, merely to demonstrate that Roger Vanderwater could afford to insult nature.

Elias passed carpets soft as moss. Remember, he had slept thirty years upon boards. He passed a breakfast room where enough food had been set out to feed a barrack. Remember, he had eaten nothing for two days because the beating made him vomit.

A servant led him to the private bedchamber.

Roger Vanderwater lay beneath a green canopy. The walls were painted with naked angels flying through gold clouds. The floor was warm. Scented air entered through hidden vents. It resembled Paradise, if Paradise had required six thousand slaves to maintain the temperature.

Roger had a headache.

He had attended a banquet the night before and drunk three bottles of yellow wine. Consequently, he felt himself acquainted with suffering.

“What is it?” he asked.

“The prize strawberries from Hell’s Bottom, master.”

“Bring them near. The light hurts my eyes.”

Elias carried the basket to the bed.

Roger lifted the blue silk.

He saw the berries first. Then the arm.

For a while he did not speak. Perhaps his mind, accustomed to receiving reports in columns of figures, could not at once understand a fact with fingernails.

Then he shouted.

Servants ran in. Guards threw Elias upon the floor. One placed a pistol at the back of his neck.

Roger Vanderwater stared at the petition clenched in the hand.

“What obscenity is this?”

“A message,” Elias said.

“Whose arm?”

“Tom Dixon’s.”

“Who is Tom Dixon?”

“One of your slaves.”

Roger looked offended. “Do you expect me to know six thousand names?”

“No, master. That is why I brought one arm.”

The guards kicked him.

Roger ordered them to stop—not from mercy, but because noise increased his headache. He took the petition from the fingers. The paper left a faint brown mark upon his thumb.

He read.

This is important, brothers. He read.

The arm frightened him, but the words directed his fear. Without the words he might have called for a bucket and ordered the thing removed. Without the arm he might have laid the paper aside for a secretary. Together they compelled him. Flesh made the petition urgent. Writing made the flesh speak.

He read of three coins taken every week from six thousand workers.

He made a calculation.

Then he made another.

The sum was large.

Roger sat upright.

“Is this true?”

“Yes.”

“If it is false, you will be flayed.”

Elias answered, “I have been sorry all my life that I was born. You can threaten me with nothing.”

The room became quiet.

A master does not know what to do with a slave who no longer fears losing life, because life is the master’s final coin.

Roger sent for his private auditor, a dry old creature named Peeble, whose loyalty arose from hatred of everyone equally. He ordered the gates closed. He ordered Clancy and Munster detained before they could hear of the inquiry.

For three days Peeble examined the ledgers of Hell’s Bottom.

He found false deaths, false burials, false medicines, false pensions, false seals, and one false physician who was in fact Munster’s cousin and a dealer in racing birds. He found that Clancy charged the Relief Fund for “moral bandages,” a product not known to science. He found that Munster’s green bath had been entered as a public fever basin, though no slave had been permitted within two hundred paces of it.

Peeble enjoyed himself greatly.

Roger Vanderwater rode to Hell’s Bottom.

The slaves were lined in the yard. They expected punishment. That is what justice usually means when a master arrives with cavalry.

Clancy and Munster stood beneath the office clock. Clancy’s gold teeth no longer shone, for he had spent the night trying to swallow certain pages of the ledger. Munster wept into his collar.

Roger spoke from the steps.

He said the Relief Fund was a sacred trust. He said corruption offended the ancient honor of the Vanderwater name. We, who know of Bloody Vange, may smile at that honor, but the slaves did not smile. Cavalry stood behind them.

Roger ordered the hunting lodge sold. He ordered the mechanical horses sold. He ordered the green bath broken up and placed in the workers’ washhouse.

Then he punished the overseers.

Clancy had knocked out workers’ teeth. Roger ordered every gold tooth drawn from Clancy’s mouth, together with two natural ones that Peeble said were probably innocent but poorly documented.

Munster had watched beatings from a chair. Roger ordered him strapped to that same chair and flogged.

Thereafter both men were sent to Press Four. Clancy lost three fingers before the week was out. Munster lost a foot the following month. They applied to the Relief Fund.

Their claims were denied because their injuries had interrupted production.

You may laugh. It is permitted here.

What of Elias?

Roger could have hanged him for approaching the palace in disguise. Instead he spared him, which the official family history calls an act of magnificent compassion.

Elias was already dying.

Roger offered him the position of overseer. Think of that. The master believed every brave slave desired only a whip and somebody beneath it. Bloody Vange had desired so. Why not every man?

Elias refused.

Roger offered money.

Elias asked that Tom Dixon receive aid, that Ann be released from her inherited debt, that the names of the dead children be entered honestly, and that a worker elected by the barracks keep a second copy of the fund accounts.

Roger agreed to the first three requests. The fourth troubled him.

“A worker cannot keep accounts,” he said. “Workers cannot read.”

“One can be taught,” Elias replied.

At last Roger allowed a single licensed reader, selected by management, to inspect the books twice a year. For a time only, the fund was honest. For a time only, injured men ate. For a time only, children who died were buried under stones bearing their true names.

Do not mistake this for freedom.

Roger returned to his palace. New overseers came. The green bath cracked in winter. The licensed reader disappeared. Ten years later the deductions rose from three coins to five, and the workers were forbidden to ask why.

A good master dies.

A law is amended.

A memory fades.

Then the whip returns from the cupboard.

But the petition remained in the Vanderwater archives. Peeble copied it into his audit. The audit was copied by a clerk. The clerk’s daughter carried a copy beyond the district. Words travel in strange pockets, brothers. They hide under bread, inside boots, behind holy pictures, within love letters. A printed word can wait a hundred years in darkness and still speak when light finds it.

And Elias?

He returned to the barracks. He lived nine days.

During those nine days he taught a boy to shape letters with a burnt stick upon the floor.

He taught him A from the slant of a roof.

He taught him O from the mouth of a man crying out.

He taught him T from a hammer.

He taught him the words ARM, BLOOD, BOOK, SLAVE, MASTER.

That boy was his son.

That boy was me.

Yes. Look well. You expected history to remain safely dead, did you? My father carried Tom Dixon’s arm into the palace. My father wrote the petition. I know. I saw his ribs black from the beating. I saw him cough blood upon the letters he made for me. I have since read Peeble’s audit with my own eyes. The masters preserved it to prove Roger Vanderwater’s justice. They did not understand that it proved everything else.

When I was grown, they offered me a place in the office because I could read.

Later they offered me a whip.

I took neither.

I became what you see: an old man with a cracked voice, bad boots, and more forbidden papers than good sense. I go from mill to mine, from mine to field, from field to barrack. I tell the tale of the arm. Then, if my hearers have courage, I teach them letters.

Why?

Not so you may petition for a kinder master.

Not so the Relief Fund may remain honest for a year.

Not so one Clancy may lose his teeth while ten others polish theirs.

I teach you because history is the inventory of what was stolen. I teach you because a man who reads a contract may discover the trap, and a woman who writes a name may keep the dead from being erased. I teach you because masters are few and slaves are many, and the chief arithmetic of mastery is that the many must never learn to count themselves.

The Vanderwaters say mastery is ancient.

We shall read how it began.

They say slavery is natural.

We shall write how it ends.

They say they own the factories, the fields, the courts, the soldiers, the rivers, and your children yet unborn. Perhaps they do. But they do not own the meaning of the black marks unless you surrender it to them.

Come here, you by the stove.

Take this charcoal.

Do not tremble. It is only a stick. It weighs less than a chain and may prove stronger.

Make a line downward.

Now another across it.

That is T.

T for Tom.

T for truth.

Again.

And you at the door, keep listening for boots. We are not free because we have learned one letter. We shall not be free because one master is shocked into justice by one dead arm. We shall be free when every slave can read the record of his bondage, write the name of his enemy, count the number of his brothers, and imagine a world containing neither slave nor master.

Make the letter again.

Good.

The night is long, my brothers.

We have time.

### POSTSCRIPT TO THE 3118 EDITION

Comparison with Vanderwater Archive Document 44-C confirms that an injured worker named Thomas Dixon lost his left arm at Press Four on May 17, 2387. The same archive contains an unsigned petition bearing chemical traces of human blood and strawberry pollen. An audit conducted three days later records the dismissal and judicial mutilation of Overseers Patrick Clancy and Horace Munster.

The identity of the petitioner remains uncertain. Company death rolls list four literate workers as having died during that month, none under the name Elias. Such discrepancies were common, since bonded persons could be renamed upon sale, marriage, transfer, punishment, or clerical convenience.

The storyteller’s hope was not immediately fulfilled. Literacy among industrial bondsmen remained punishable for another eighty-six years. The Vanderwater holdings expanded. Hell’s Bottom continued operating until its destruction during the General Rising of 2479, when workers seized the mills, opened the archives, and printed the first uncensored labor histories upon company presses.

Among the Rising’s earliest proclamations was a sentence of six words:

**WE HAVE READ IT FOR OURSELVES.**

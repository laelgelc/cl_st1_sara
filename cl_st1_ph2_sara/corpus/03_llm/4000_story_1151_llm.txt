Whenever I tell Eddie Houghton’s story it behaves like one of those letters that has gone clear around the world only to come back stamped in purple ink—UNCLAIMED, NO SUCH PERSON, RETURN TO SENDER. I send it out hoping somebody will take it in and understand it, and back it comes, battered at the corners, with another official mark across Eddie’s name.

Maybe you won’t want it either.

Still, I have to tell it, because every time I pass the whitewashed wall beside Ransom’s Feed Store and see that blue-and-gold recruiting poster blooming there, as fresh as if no boy had ever suffered, something hot rises behind my eyes. The poster shows a clean young sailor in white, standing on the deck of a ship. Beyond him is a sea like blue glass, an island with green palms, a city of marble towers, and a girl in a red scarf waving from a quay.

SEE THE WORLD, it says.

LEARN A TRADE.

MAKE A MAN OF YOURSELF.

And down in one corner, in smaller letters, it says the service offers travel, education, health, comradeship, and opportunity.

Whenever I see it I think of Eddie, and everything goes crimson.

You remember where Ransom’s wall is—the place where the streetcar turns by the Methodist church. In summer the dust settles yellow on the hitching posts there, and in winter the north wind comes down Main Street as if it has a personal grudge against the town. That wall used to advertise stock tonic, corsets, and a brand of plug tobacco which, according to its picture, could make an eighty-year-old farmer leap a rail fence. We laughed at those advertisements. We had sense enough for that.

But we did not laugh at the sailor.

He looked so clean.

Eddie looked clean, too. That was one reason everybody liked him. He worked at Bell’s Drug Store, and Mr. Bell called it working, though Eddie’s mother always said he had “accepted a position.” There is a difference in our town. A boy takes a job at the lumberyard, but if he wears a white coat and stands behind a glass counter, he has accepted a position.

Eddie’s coat was always white. He had brown hair that would not stay flat, gray eyes, straight shoulders, and the pleasant kind of face which made old women tell him their symptoms and young women invent symptoms to tell him. He knew how much peppermint to put in Mrs. Cole’s stomach mixture and how little soda to use in Judge Parmenter’s phosphate. He could wrap a bottle in paper so neatly that the corners came together like folded wings. He remembered who wanted chocolate and who wanted vanilla, and he never grinned when Miss Tibbetts asked for complexion cream, though everybody knew Miss Tibbetts had been buying complexion cream since the Cleveland administration.

The girls liked him, naturally.

On warm evenings the marble-topped tables at Bell’s would be crowded with girls who could have made lemonade at home for one-tenth the price. They ordered slowly and ate more slowly, and one chocolate sundae might last from seven o’clock until Mr. Bell began turning out the side lights at nine. Eddie would come from behind the fountain, set down the dish, say, “There you are, Miss Helen,” and smile.

That smile was responsible for more melted ice cream than the July weather.

He had played halfback at the high school and had once run eighty yards against Rockville with three men hanging on him, though by the time the story had been told for five years the distance had become a hundred and twenty yards and the number of Rockville men had risen to seven. He played tennis, sang a respectable tenor in the Presbyterian choir, and could dance without counting under his breath. He was not brilliant, if by brilliant you mean one of those boys who can tell you the capital of Bulgaria and then forget to shut the chicken-house door. But he was quick, decent, cheerful, and ambitious in a vague, restless way.

That restlessness was the crack the recruiter got his knife into.

Eddie lived with his mother in the little yellow house beyond the water tower. His father had died when Eddie was eleven, leaving a photograph in a walnut frame, a silver watch that did not run, and debts enough to keep Mrs. Houghton sewing until midnight for six years. Eddie knew what she had done for him. Everybody did.

Their yard had three apple trees, a vegetable patch, and a porch half-covered with purple clematis. In August there would be sweet corn stacked by the kitchen door, tomatoes on the pantry sill, and bread cooling under a white cloth. The front room held Eddie’s school pennants, his tennis racquets, and the photograph of the football team in which he sat in the center with his hair wet and his face solemn. There was also a green cushion Josie Morehouse had made for him, though Eddie claimed not to know who had made it.

He knew.

Josie was not one of the girls who made a sundae last two hours. She came into Bell’s only when she had something to buy, and when she asked for tooth powder she meant tooth powder. She had dark eyes, a quiet mouth, and a way of looking at Eddie that made him suddenly busy with spoons.

Everybody knew about Josie except Eddie, and perhaps Eddie knew most of all.

“You’d better marry that girl,” Mr. Bell told him once.

Eddie turned red clear to his collar. “What girl?”

“That’s a poor beginning,” said Mr. Bell. “If you don’t know which girl, I can’t help you.”

Eddie polished the fountain taps so hard that they shone like gold.

He was twenty when the recruiting office opened over Gately’s shoe store. A flag appeared above the entrance, and the white wall by Ransom’s was covered with colored pictures. There were ships sliding through moonlit water, sailors examining machinery, sailors studying books, sailors playing baseball under palm trees, sailors smiling beside pyramids, sailors feeding bananas to monkeys, sailors being welcomed by foreign children who seemed to have nothing in the world to do but wait on docks for American battleships.

That was some picture gallery.

Not one sailor was seasick. Not one scrubbed a filthy deck. Not one had a bruised mouth, an infected hand, or a look in his eyes that suggested he had seen anything worse than a sunset. Their uniforms were spotless; their officers were fatherly; even the cannon looked educational.

The recruiter was Chief Petty Officer Larkin, though we called him Captain until we learned better. He had a broad chest, a smooth red face, and a gold tooth which showed whenever he laughed—and he laughed often. He carried himself as though Main Street were the deck of his private cruiser and we were all natives who had gathered to watch him land.

He came into Bell’s one afternoon, looked Eddie over, and said, “There’s a likely young fellow.”

Eddie laughed. “Likely for what?”

“For something bigger than mixing ice-cream sodas.”

Now Eddie was proud of his work, but there are ways of naming a thing that make it shrink in front of you. The chief had the gift. He could turn Bell’s Drug Store, which had seemed clean and important in the morning, into a narrow room smelling of medicine and stale syrup. He could make our whole town look like a spot a man ought to apologize for having been born in.

“You ever been to China?” he asked.

“No.”

“South America?”

“No.”

“France? Egypt? Honolulu?”

Eddie shook his head.

“Ever seen the sun rise over Gibraltar?”

“I’ve seen it come up over the water tower,” Eddie said.

We laughed, and the chief laughed loudest. But he laid one hand on Eddie’s shoulder.

“A fine-looking boy like you ought to have a chance. Travel from port to port. Learn electricity, engineering, wireless. Good pay, good food, the finest companions in the world. You go in a boy and come out a man.”

He said “from port to port” as if it were part of a hymn.

After that he dropped into Bell’s every day. Sometimes he brought pamphlets. Sometimes postcards. There was one of Venice, with blue water under bridges; one of a harbor crowded with sails; one of a tropical beach where the sand looked powdered with gold. Eddie kept them under the cash drawer and took them out when trade was slow.

He began to see himself everywhere but home.

He saw himself in white on a shining deck, leaning on a rail while bright islands rose from the sea. He saw himself learning complicated machinery, earning promotions, coming back with a chest full of ribbons and knowledge enough to command the respect of men who had never gone beyond the county line. He forgot that machinery has grease on it. He forgot that ships have places no poster shows. He forgot—no, that is not fair. He did not forget his mother or Josie. The chief merely made them seem like reasons a timid boy would give for refusing his life.

“Your mother won’t want to hold you back,” Chief Larkin said.

“No,” Eddie answered slowly. “She wouldn’t.”

“And as for a girl—”

“There isn’t any girl.”

“Of course not.” The chief showed the gold tooth. “But if there were, she’d think more of a fellow in uniform than a fellow tied to his ma’s apron strings.”

Eddie did not like that. I saw his face change.

“My mother never tied me to anything.”

“Just an expression, son. No offense meant. A man has to cut loose sometime.”

A man has to cut loose. There was another of his hymns.

When Eddie told his mother, she went on shelling peas. She sat on the back porch with a blue bowl in her lap, and the peas struck the china one by one.

“If you think it’s right,” she said.

“I don’t know yet.”

“You’ve been thinking about it.”

“Yes.”

“You want to go?”

“I want to see something. Learn something. I don’t want to spend my whole life—” He stopped.

“Behind Mr. Bell’s fountain?”

“There’s nothing wrong with Bell’s.”

“No.”

“Only a fellow ought to have a chance.”

She split another pod with her thumb. “You’ve always had a chance here, Eddie.”

He thought she meant to reproach him. She meant that she loved him and had worked to give him a chance, but love is often clumsy when it is frightened.

“So you don’t want me to go.”

“I didn’t say that.”

“What do you want me to do?”

She looked at him then. “I want you to do what you can live with.”

Poor woman. She did not know there would be no answer to that.

Josie heard about it before Eddie told her. Our town could carry news from the depot to the cemetery faster than the telegraph, and improve it on the way. By supper time people said Eddie had enlisted, by eight o’clock they said he was going to submarine school, and by morning Mrs. Peavey had heard he was engaged to a French admiral’s daughter.

Josie came to Bell’s near closing.

“Is it true?” she asked.

“I haven’t signed yet.”

“But you’re going to.”

“Maybe.”

She studied the pattern in the tiled floor. “When?”

“Soon, if I do.”

“You’ll see a lot.”

“That’s what they say.”

“From port to port?”

He smiled because she had imitated the chief, but the smile did not last.

“Josie, this town’s all right. You know I think so. But sometimes it seems—small.”

“It is small.”

“I don’t mean the people.”

“No.”

“I could learn a trade.”

“You already have one.”

“I could learn a better one.”

“Yes.”

He wanted her to argue. Anybody could see it. He wanted her to say she needed him, to make staying seem brave instead of cowardly. But Josie had too much pride to bargain against the world.

At the door she said, “Your mother will miss you.”

“Will you?”

She looked straight at him. “Yes.”

He took one step toward her, then stopped. That was Eddie. He could run through Rockville’s whole football team, but one quiet girl in a blue hat could bring him up short.

He enlisted the following Monday.

And he went.

We gave him a supper in the church basement. There was ham, scalloped potatoes, six kinds of cake, and a speech by Mayor Givens about the proud tradition of American manhood. Chief Larkin sat beside Eddie and looked pleased, as though he had personally invented the navy.

Mr. Bell presented Eddie with a fountain pen. The football club gave him a watch. The choir sang. Mrs. Houghton sat with her hands folded, and Josie poured coffee until every cup on every table was full.

At the depot Eddie wore a new blue suit and carried a brown suitcase. His mother adjusted his tie though it did not need adjusting. Mr. Bell told him his position would be waiting if he ever wanted it. We shouted jokes about mermaids and rum and foreign princesses. Eddie laughed.

The train began to move.

He leaned from the platform and waved until the curve took him. Mrs. Houghton did not cry then. She only stood with one hand raised after there was nothing left to wave at.

The letters came cheerfully at first.

Dear Ma, the food is pretty good and there are fellows here from all over. We get up earlier than I expected. Don’t worry about me.

Then:

Dear Mr. Bell, you would laugh to see me trying to make a bunk the navy way. It has to be tight enough to bounce a coin. I could use one of your chocolate sodas.

And to Josie:

I saw the ocean today. It is bigger than it looks in pictures. I wish you could see it.

That letter she kept.

Afterward the letters changed. Not at once, and not so anybody could point to a sentence and say, There, that is where the boy’s heart broke. A word would be crossed out. A page would end too soon. He began saying he was tired. He asked about foolish little things—the clematis, Mr. Bell’s old cat, whether the high school had beaten Rockville, whether the corn had come in.

He wrote more and told less.

I don’t know all the things Eddie suffered. His mother showed us portions of the letters after he was dead, and Josie showed nobody hers. There were matters he hinted at and then covered over, as a decent boy covers something foul before his mother enters the room.

But we learned enough.

The fine companions Chief Larkin had promised were not all fine. Some were men who had been broken before they came and had decided to break others for sport. They knew words Eddie had never heard, mouth-heavy, filthy words that left dirt in the mind after the sound was gone. They laughed at kindness. They stole from the weak. They boasted about women as if women were things bought, used, and thrown into alleys. They found out that Eddie was ashamed of them, and shame amused them.

“Drug clerk,” they called him.

“Choir boy.”

“Mother’s darling.”

They hid his clothes, fouled his bedding, took his money, and accused him of losing it. They made him do work twice, then said he had done it wrong. When he would not join them in tormenting a sick recruit, they gave him the sick recruit’s share too. There are a hundred ways for men in a closed world to punish one man who refuses their law, and not half of them leave a mark an officer cares to see.

Perhaps some officers were decent. I hope so. Perhaps some sailors befriended him. I hope that too. I am telling Eddie’s story, not writing a report for the government, and I only know what reached us through his frightened, guarded sentences. Yet whenever people say, “Surely it cannot have been so bad,” I want to ask how bad a place must be before a boy begins to dream of the smell of his mother’s bread as though it were heaven.

The ship was not the white ship on the wall. It had rust in its corners, heat in its metal rooms, crawling things, foul air, and passages where a man could not escape another man’s breath. The sea was not blue glass. Sometimes it was black, sometimes gray, sometimes green and sickening, and it heaved under him until he thought his bones were loose.

He saw ports, yes.

From port to port.

He saw warehouses, gutters, saloons, diseased faces, hungry children, and women waiting under lights that made their skin look dead. He saw sailors pour ashore with pay in their pockets and return bruised, robbed, and laughing about things that made Eddie turn away. He saw cruelty treated as experience and disgust treated as innocence. Every shining postcard had an alley behind it, and somehow Eddie was always shown the alley.

He wrote Josie once:

You used to think I was foolish because I didn’t like dirty jokes. Maybe you didn’t think it, but I thought you did. I don’t care now. A fellow can get used to anything if he tries, but maybe there are things he ought not get used to.

That was the fight in him. People said afterward that he was weak because he deserted and weaker because he died. They did not understand. Eddie had been fighting every day to keep from becoming what he hated. If he had learned to curse as they cursed, strike as they struck, laugh as they laughed, he might have survived. He could not pay that price.

His mother received a letter in November.

I dreamed last night I was coming up the walk and you had the kitchen window open. I could smell the bread. There was rain on the clematis and my old racquet was on the porch. I woke up before you saw me.

She read it twice, went into the pantry, and cried where nobody could hear.

By December Eddie’s face had altered. We know because he sent a photograph. The uniform fitted him well. His cap sat at the proper angle. Behind him was a painted canvas showing a warship and a romantic cloud.

At first Mrs. Houghton said, “Doesn’t he look handsome?”

Then she looked closer.

His mouth was tight. His cheeks had sunk. His eyes stared beyond the camera, as though the photographer had told him to watch something dreadful and keep still.

Josie put the picture face down.

He deserted in January.

Even the word sounds ugly, doesn’t it? Deserted. It makes you think of a coward throwing away his rifle while brave men stand to their duty. But there was no battle. Eddie did not abandon comrades under fire. He merely walked away from a place that was destroying him.

He had been refused leave. Somebody had struck him—perhaps more than struck him. He had gone to a superior and been laughed at. That much we pieced together. Then, on a cold rainy night, with fifteen dollars and his fountain pen in his pocket, he went through a gate and kept walking.

He bought civilian clothes from a secondhand dealer. The trousers were too wide, the coat was shiny at the elbows, and the hat had belonged to a man with a larger head. He boarded a train west.

He was going home.

That seems the most terrible part to me—not the hotel, not the bottle, not even the last letter. He was going home with the trust of a child who has awakened from a nightmare and knows that one familiar room will make everything right.

On the train he sat beside a traveling man named Corbett, a stranger who later told the authorities what happened. Eddie looked so ill that Corbett offered him a sandwich.

“You’ve been sick, son?”

“A little.”

“Going far?”

“Home.”

“Where’s that?”

Eddie named our town.

Corbett noticed the sailor’s mark on one of Eddie’s garments, or perhaps Eddie told him. Accounts differed. Anyway, the truth came out.

“You’re absent without leave?” Corbett asked.

“I left.”

“Got papers?”

“No.”

Corbett was not cruel. If he had been cruel, Eddie might not have believed him. He was a practical man, used to tickets, schedules, regulations, and consequences.

“They’ll be looking for you.”

“I know.”

“You can’t go home.”

Eddie stared at him. “Why not?”

“That’s the first place they’ll look.”

“My mother’s there.”

“Yes, but—”

“I’m going to explain.”

“To whom?”

“The authorities. Anybody. I couldn’t stand it. They’ll understand when I tell them.”

Corbett took off his spectacles and wiped them.

“Son, desertion means prison. They won’t just let you go back to the drugstore.”

Eddie said nothing.

“You’d better give yourself up. It may go easier.”

“How long?”

“I can’t say.”

“Years?”

“Maybe. I don’t know.”

“I only want to go home.”

Corbett told us Eddie repeated that several times, softly, with wonder rather than grief.

“I only want to go home.”

There it was. The yellow house, the purple clematis, the bread under its cloth, the soda fountain, Josie’s dark eyes, the worn tennis racquet, all within a day’s journey—and farther from him than China.

At the next junction Eddie got off the train.

Corbett thought he meant to surrender. Eddie even thanked him for the sandwich and shook his hand.

“It’s better you know,” Corbett said.

“Yes,” Eddie answered. “It’s better.”

He walked three blocks through dirty snow to the Midland Hotel, which was not much of a hotel and never had been. The carpet in the lobby had a path worn down its center. A fly-specked palm leaned in a brass pot. Behind the desk sat a clerk reading a racing sheet.

Eddie signed the register as Edward Hall.

“Luggage?” the clerk asked.

“No.”

“Pay in advance.”

Eddie paid.

The room cost seventy-five cents. It had a narrow bed, a washstand, a cracked pitcher, and wallpaper patterned with brown roses. Outside the window was a brick wall so close a man could almost touch it.

He wrote three letters.

One was to his mother.

One was to Josie.

One was to Mr. Bell, because Mr. Bell had given him the fountain pen and a position and had always treated him fairly.

Then he went to a drugstore and bought carbolic acid.

Do not ask me what he said to the clerk. Perhaps he named some harmless use for it. Perhaps the clerk did not ask. Bottles pass over counters every day, neatly wrapped, their corners folded like wings.

Eddie returned to the Midland.

I cannot tell you what he thought in that room. Nobody can. Maybe he heard the train whistles and imagined each one carrying him nearer home without moving him an inch. Maybe he laid out the secondhand coat carefully because his mother had taught him not to drop clothes on the floor. Maybe he looked at the brown roses until they blurred.

He drank the acid.

The chambermaid found him in the morning.

They identified him through the letters.

The news came to us just after noon. Mr. Bell was filling a prescription when the telephone rang. He listened, set the receiver down, and stood with both hands on the counter.

“What is it?” I asked.

He looked at Eddie’s empty place by the fountain.

“It’s the boy,” he said.

Mrs. Houghton did not scream when they told her. She sat in the front room beneath the football picture, very straight, while the minister spoke about God’s mysterious will. When he finished, she said, “There was nothing mysterious about it.”

Josie came after dark. She removed her coat, went to Mrs. Houghton, and knelt beside her chair. Neither of them spoke for a long time. Then Mrs. Houghton touched Josie’s hair.

That was all.

The chief had left town by then. His office stood empty, though the flag remained above it for a week. People blamed him, and perhaps we had a right. But we had laughed at his jokes. We had admired the postcards. We had eaten ham in the church basement and applauded when the mayor talked about manhood. The chief sold the dream, but all of us helped wrap it.

Eddie’s body came home under a gray sky.

There was some question about church burial because suicide was still a word people lowered their voices over. There was more question about desertion. Mayor Givens, who had praised Eddie when he left, said the circumstances were unfortunate. Mrs. Peavey said no mother should have to bear such shame. Judge Parmenter said discipline had to be maintained.

Then the letters were read.

Not all of them. Never all. Mrs. Houghton let the minister read enough to certain people, and enough traveled from kitchen to kitchen for the town to see what had happened. We saw the clean boy inside the dirty ship, trying to keep hold of himself. We saw him longing for corn, rain, bread, tennis, his mother’s lamp. We saw that his desertion had not been treachery but flight, and his death not contempt for home but despair of ever reaching it.

We forgave him.

Mr. Bell forgave him, though he said there was nothing to forgive.

The minister forgave him and buried him in consecrated ground.

The football club forgave him and carried the coffin.

The women who had whispered forgave him and brought food to Mrs. Houghton’s house.

Judge Parmenter forgave him.

Even Mrs. Peavey forgave him, which was practically an act of Congress.

Josie never said she forgave him. Love does not use that word when it understands.

At the funeral she stood beside Mrs. Houghton. On the coffin lay Eddie’s high school pennant and a wreath shaped like an anchor, though I wished somebody had thrown the anchor away. When the service ended, Josie placed one small sprig of purple clematis beside his hands. She had forced it in a hothouse out of season.

Afterward Mr. Bell kept Eddie’s place open for three months. He hired no new clerk. Sometimes, when trade was brisk, he would turn toward the fountain and say, “Eddie, take care of—”

Then he would stop.

Mrs. Houghton lived many years. She kept the yellow house and the garden. Every spring the clematis climbed over the porch until the boards were almost hidden under purple flowers. Josie married no one. That is not to say she sat in black beside a window; she taught school, traveled once to California, laughed often, and lived a useful life. But the green cushion stayed in her room, and Eddie’s letter stayed locked in her desk.

As for the town, we went on being small.

The streetcar still turned by the Methodist church. Bell’s Drug Store changed hands twice. The football team beat Rockville and lost to Rockville. Girls continued to order sodas they did not need for boys they pretended not to notice. Mothers baked bread, boys became restless, and smooth men came from elsewhere to tell them that life began beyond the county line.

Then, one bright morning, a new poster appeared on Ransom’s whitewashed wall.

A clean young sailor stood on the deck of a ship. Behind him the sea shone blue as glass. There were palms, marble towers, a girl waving from a foreign quay. His uniform was white. His face was fearless. Not a stain touched him.

SEE THE WORLD.

LEARN A TRADE.

MAKE A MAN OF YOURSELF.

Below that came the old promise—travel, education, health, comradeship, opportunity—and somewhere, though the words were not printed, I could hear Chief Larkin’s confident voice saying, From port to port.

People passed it on their way to dinner. Boys stopped and looked.

The sailor smiled down at them.

The sea sparkled.

The poster had not changed at all.

But I had.

I saw a narrow hotel room behind the island, a brick wall behind the marble city, brown roses behind the white uniform. I saw a secondhand coat folded on a chair. I saw three letters beside a cracked pitcher and a boy who had traveled far enough to learn that the world could take away his home without moving it one inch.

That is why, whenever I pass Ransom’s wall and see that bright sailor smiling, everything goes crimson.

I think of Eddie Houghton.

And I see red.

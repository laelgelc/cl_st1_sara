“Yes, gentlemen, I killed him!”

There is no need for the clerk to dip his pen and stare at me as though I had announced an eclipse. I killed him deliberately, in full possession of my faculties, after reflection, and with an excellent Belgian revolver. I fired once. Economy, gentlemen, is a virtue.

I do not repent.

You wish to know his name. That is your first mistake. He had a name, naturally: two Christian names, a surname ending in the usual manner, a rank, a ribbon in his buttonhole, and a visiting-card ornamented with flourishes. But what have these to do with the matter? I did not kill a school inspector, a husband, a railway passenger. I killed something older, more numerous, more indestructible.

I killed the Russian intelligent.

Do not smile, Your Excellency. I see by your smile that you misunderstand me already. You imagine a pale student in spectacles, whispering forbidden verses and dying of consumption. Nothing of the sort! That one may be harmless. My creature is healthy. He has a damp hand, a loud opinion, a soft hat, and a breath compounded of tobacco, onions, and moral superiority. He lives everywhere. He multiplies without marriage. Cut him in half and each portion becomes a member of a committee.

All my life he has pursued me.

In childhood he came to our house after dinner, crushed my father’s fingers, slapped me between the shoulders, and cried:

“Well, young man! Growing? Studying? And what do you intend to be—eh? A useful citizen, I hope!”

Then, without waiting for the reply, he took three lumps of sugar, stirred his tea until the spoon rang like a fire-bell, and explained the education of children to my mother, who had seven, though he himself had none. He said corporal punishment degraded the human personality; five minutes later he boxed the servant’s ears because the saucer was cracked.

At school he was the visitor who examined our copybooks. He would pat the master on the shoulder before us and say:

“Routine, my dear fellow, routine! We must awaken thought!”

Then he asked me the date of the Battle of Poltava, and when I answered correctly he looked offended.

“Dates are not knowledge,” he said. “The spirit, my boy, the spirit!”

Had I answered incorrectly, he would have said that ignorance of national history was the ruin of Russia. You see the perfection of the system. It admits no escape.

Later, in the government office, he sat opposite me. He hummed while I calculated, drummed on the table while I wrote, borrowed my knife to sharpen pencils and returned it with half the blade broken. He read every document over my shoulder.

“Confidential?” he would ask, already reading it.

“Yes.”

“Ah! Then I shall not inquire.”

And he continued reading.

At eleven he declared that Russia lacked conscientious workers. At twelve he went away to luncheon. At two he returned smelling of cognac and dictated a memorandum on punctuality. He called everyone “dear colleague,” especially those whose promotion he prevented.

On railway journeys he is inevitable. You enter an empty compartment at night, place your coat beneath your head, extinguish the lamp, and offer a prayer of gratitude. At the next station the door opens. A soft hat appears.

“Anyone here?”

You remain silent.

“Sleeping? Splendid! I also sleep extremely well in trains.”

He sits upon your feet, lights a cigarette under the notice forbidding smoking, and begins:

“Travelling far?”

You say, “Yes.”

“On business?”

“No.”

“For pleasure, then?”

“No.”

“Aha! A secret mission!”

Then comes the elbow, the confidential breath, the history of his digestion, his cousin’s lawsuit, the corruption of railway officials, and a theory that all accidents are caused by Jews, nihilists, or the absence of religious instruction—according to which newspaper he bought at the station.

He cannot see silence without wanting to improve it.

Once, on the Volga, I stood at sunset watching the water turn copper beneath a violet sky. The islands floated in mist; a bell sounded from some invisible monastery; for one minute the world was vast, solemn, and clean.

He came up behind me, gripped my coat-button, and said:

“Very pretty! Quite like a picture. Though, between ourselves, the Rhine is more cultured.”

More cultured!

He then threw an orange peel into the river and told me an anecdote about a merchant who mistook a bishop for an actress.

At an art exhibition he stands so close that his beard touches the canvas. He reads the title aloud—“Morning in a Pine Forest”—and immediately explains:

“Those are bears. The artist wishes to symbolise Russia.”

If the painter uses bright colours, he says, “French influence.” If dark colours, “Our national melancholy.” If he understands nothing, he sighs, “There is an idea here.” If the artist is dead, he worships him; if alive, he knows the man personally and assures you that he beats his wife.

At the theatre he arrives late, causing twelve people to rise. He treads upon eleven pairs of toes, drops his opera-glasses beneath the thirteenth chair, and whispers the plot to his companion in a voice suitable for commanding artillery.

“Now she poisons herself.”

“She stabs herself,” says the companion.

“It makes no difference. Symbolically, poison.”

During the aria he coughs. During the death scene he unwraps a sweet. When the curtain falls he cries, “Bravo!” and adds in the lobby that the performance lacked sincerity.

Literature offers no refuge. Lend him a book and it returns swollen with rain, stained with tea, its corners bent, its margins inhabited by exclamation marks. Passages you love are underlined in blue pencil. On the page where the hero dies he has written: “Rather exaggerated.” He praises Tolstoy’s simplicity in sentences that last twenty minutes. He condemns Dostoevsky as unhealthy, but knows every scandal concerning Dostoevsky’s publishers. Pushkin, he says, is “eternal”; what he means is that he once memorised eight lines at school, of which six have escaped him.

He writes, too. God help us, he writes!

He sends letters to newspapers beginning, “As a disinterested observer…” He publishes reminiscences of men he met once in an anteroom. He signs petitions against censorship and secretly asks the censor to suppress an enemy’s pamphlet. He demands freedom of speech, then speaks for forty-five minutes without permitting interruption.

If you live in town, he complains that you are severed from nature. If you go to the country, he arrives on Saturday with two friends, a guitar, and no luggage.

“Don’t trouble about us,” he says, while your servant kills the last chicken.

He occupies the best room, frightens the horse, leaves bottles beneath the lilac bushes, and carves his initials into a birch tree. At midnight he sings of the beauty of silence. At dawn he fires at crows. On Monday he departs with your umbrella and tells everyone that country hospitality is declining.

Even love—yes, gentlemen, even love!—was not safe.

Once I sat beside a young lady beneath acacias. Her hand trembled near mine. The moon was above the roof, and I had reached that sacred instant when one word may alter two lives.

From behind the hedge came a throat being cleared.

“Hm-hm! Forgive me! Romantic situation?”

He emerged smiling, placed himself between us, and began discussing the emancipation of women. He told the young lady that marriage was an obsolete institution, then asked whether her dowry included the orchard. She married him six months later. I was invited to the wedding.

Can you wonder that my nerves suffered?

Physicians prescribed rest. The first physician tapped my knee, asked whether insanity existed in my family, and recommended cold baths. The second said cold baths were dangerous and recommended travel. The third, whom I met while travelling, said travel exhausted the nervous system and advised useful employment. Each charged ten roubles and told me money was not everything.

I endured. I avoided clubs, theatres, exhibitions, steamers, committees, gardens, weddings, funerals. At funerals he is particularly active. He shakes the widow’s hand and says, “We must all die,” as though imparting private information. I even stopped reading newspapers, but he came wrapped around the cheese.

And then occurred the journey for which you propose to hang me.

The carriage was crowded. People sat upon boxes, baskets, coats, and one another. An old woman stood in the passage holding a cage with a green bird. A soldier slept upright. I had purchased a ticket and, by one of those miracles which prove that Providence occasionally loses attention, found an empty place.

Opposite it sat the deceased, his wife, and a boy of perhaps ten. The empty seat beside them was covered with parcels: a shawl, a cake-box, two pillows, a tin teapot, and a wooden sword.

I moved to sit down.

“Occupied,” said the official.

“By whom?”

“A gentleman. He has gone to the refreshment-room.”

At the next station no gentleman appeared. A woman carrying an infant asked for the place.

“Occupied,” he repeated, spreading his coat over it.

At the third station I asked the conductor.

“No one has a ticket for that seat,” the conductor said.

The official reddened.

“There was a gentleman. He must have entered another carriage. How should I know? Am I responsible for all Russia?”

His wife looked out of the window. The boy smiled. They arranged themselves comfortably across the stolen space.

Presently the child began playing with the wooden sword and struck his father’s knee.

“Stop that, Kolya,” said the official.

The boy struck again.

“Kolya! A well-bred boy obeys his parents. Remember, truth and honour are a man’s greatest treasures. Never lie, whatever advantage a lie may bring you. A liar is lower than a thief.”

Gentlemen, I felt something inside me become perfectly quiet.

I leaned forward.

“Pardon me,” I said. “What of the gentleman at the refreshment-room?”

“What gentleman?”

“The gentleman whose seat you preserved with such devotion.”

He stared. His wife whispered to him.

“Sir,” he said, “this is a family matter.”

“It was a public seat.”

“Are you calling me a liar?”

“I am repeating your lesson to the child.”

Then he rose. He was a large man, though his largeness had the soft, inflated quality of bread. He seized my sleeve.

“I know your sort!” he cried. “Nothing sacred! No respect for family, rank, or education! Always criticism, criticism, criticism! You think because you read little books you may disturb decent people. An intel-li-gent! That’s what you are—an intel-li-gent!”

An intelligent!

He—the damp-handed, seat-stealing, proverb-spouting creature—had dared to give me his own name.

I freed my sleeve, opened my travelling bag, took out the revolver, and shot him through the heart.

There was screaming, naturally. The bird escaped from its cage. The soldier awoke and saluted. The dead man’s wife called me a monster, though five minutes earlier she had assisted in hiding the cake-box beneath the fraudulent coat. I gave the weapon to the conductor and sat down in the empty place.

That is all.

Sentence me. Exile me. Hang me, if your machinery requires it. I do not ask mercy, and I do not offer madness as an excuse. I acted with calmness, clarity, and, I maintain, social purpose.

Only one thought disturbs me.

The boy, Kolya, watched everything. As they carried away his father, he picked up the wooden sword, pointed it at the old woman with the birdcage, and ordered her to move because the seat was “reserved.”

He is ten.

In another fifteen years he will slap strangers on the shoulder, explain honour to clerks, discuss sincerity at the theatre, and carve his name upon birch trees.

Gentlemen, what if I killed only one?

# Billy Malowney’s Return

Fancy, if you will, a road wandering westward through the low green country at the melancholy close of an autumn day: a road white with old limestone dust, ribanded between blackthorn hedges, and descending by many an idle turn toward a river that moved through the valley with the grave composure of one who had heard all the griefs of mankind and found no language sufficient to answer them. The rain had passed an hour before. Every leaf held a separate crystal; the fern was dark and shining; and the distant mountains, half dissolved in vapour, seemed less like enduring hills than the recollection of hills once loved and long since lost.

Above them the evening gathered its faded magnificence. Bars of tarnished gold lay along the west, crimson islands burned among violet seas of cloud, and one pale star waited, modest and solitary, over the ruins of an abbey whose roofless arches were clothed with ivy. The rooks wheeled homeward, quarrelling in the air as they went; cattle called from the misty meadows; and from some unseen cabin came the thin smoke of a turf fire, ascending straight for a little space before the damp breeze bent it among the alders.

It was there, beside the broken abbey wall, that I first heard the history of Billy Malowney.

I had taken shelter from a renewed shower beneath the arch of what had once been the south window. In the grass at my feet lay stones carved with saints’ faces, their features softened by centuries of rain into expressions of divine patience. A stream, brown with peat, ran under the wall and gave back in trembling fragments the mournful splendours of the sky. As I stood listening to it, there entered beneath the arch an old man carrying a hazel stick and wearing a coat so patched that the original cloth appeared only as an occasional rumour among repairs.

He saluted me with dignity, shook the drops from his hat, and looked first at the weather, then at me, and lastly at a gravestone beneath the elder bushes.

“A soft evenin’, sir,” said he, “though it’s little softness there was in the people that’s lyin’ yonder.”

I asked whose graves they were.

“Och, all sorts,” he replied. “Saints, sinners, landlords, tenants, an’ two bailiffs that’ll have settled their differences by this time, unless they’re keepin’ separate establishments below. But that stone beyant, wid the top knocked off it—that’s where Molly Donovan would have been put, only Billy Malowney came back from the dead an’ prevented the expense.”

Perceiving, perhaps, that I doubted either the event or his interpretation of it, he seated himself upon a fallen capital.

“Is it the story ye’d be wishin’?” said he. “Well, then, I’ll give it to ye as I had it from my mother’s uncle, that knew Billy well, barrin’ a small disagreement over a greyhound, an’ there was no more truthful man in the parish whenever the truth was convenient to him.”

He filled a short pipe, held it upside down, discovered his mistake without embarrassment, corrected it, and began.

“Well, now, Billy Malowney was the pleasantest poor man that ever owned nothin’. He had a black head of hair, a blue eye, a red waistcoat, a leg that was the wonder of every dance from Ballycarrick to Knockaderry, an’ a fortune consistin’ of a clay pipe, a cracked fiddle, three shirts—one on him, one washin’, an’ the third in dispute with a widow woman that said it belonged to her late husband—a terrier dog named Bonaparte before ever the true Bonaparte was heard tell of, an’ a fine broad field of expectation that never grew so much as a thistle.

“His father had left him the blessin’ of God, his mother had left him her good looks, an’ his landlord had left him out of the farm. So there he was, as free as the wind, which is a grand condition for a poet or a bird, but a troublesome one for a man that’s thinkin’ of matrimony.

“An’ thinkin’ of it he was, day an’ night, sleepin’ an’ wakin’, fastin’ an’ atin’, since the first Sunday he saw Molly Donovan comin’ out from Mass in a blue cloak, wid a white ribbon under her chin an’ a face that would bring the birds down from the bushes to look at it. Molly wasn’t a showy girl, do ye mind. She had no gold about her, except what was in her hair when the sun touched it; no diamonds, except the tears that came into her eyes when she laughed too hard; an’ no pearls, barrin’ her teeth, which were of more practical use than any necklace ever imported from Spain.

“Billy looked at her.

“Molly looked at Billy.

“Then both of them looked away, which is the first chapter in every love story since Adam blamed the apple.

“‘Who’s that?’ says Billy to Patsy Langan.

“‘That’s Molly Donovan,’ says Patsy.

“‘An’ what’s she worth?’ says Billy.

“‘Four hundred pounds, six milch cows, fourteen acres of meadow, a feather-bed, two silver spoons, nine pigs at present prices, a press of linen, an’ the big black pot when her mother dies,’ says Patsy.

“‘I wasn’t askin’ about the money,’ says Billy, offended.

“‘Then why did ye ask what she was worth?’ says Patsy.

“‘I meant the worth of her heart,’ says Billy.

“‘That isn’t in the inventory,’ says Patsy, ‘but ye may be sure her father has a valuation on it.’

“Old Donovan had, too. He was a substantial farmer, with a house that possessed two chimneys, though only one of them drew smoke and the other mostly drew admiration. He had cattle in the field, oats in the haggard, bacon in the rafters, money in the bank, suspicion in his mind, an’ the rheumatism in his left knee, by which he could foretell rain more accurately than the priest’s almanac.

“Mrs. Donovan was a small, brisk woman that could hear a whisper through three doors an’ remember it for twenty years. She had raised seven children, buried two, married three, sent one to America, an’ kept Molly by her as the pride of the family and the hope of the furniture.

“So when Billy began comin’ round of an evenin’, leanin’ over the gate an’ discoursin’ on the moon, the weather, the price of calves, an’ the immortality of love, old Donovan says:

“‘What property have ye, Billy?’

“‘Property?’ says Billy. ‘I’ve the world before me.’

“‘Then take the road into it,’ says Donovan, ‘an’ don’t be stoppin’ at my gate.’

“‘I have two strong arms,’ says Billy.

“‘So has the scarecrow,’ says Donovan, ‘an’ he’s better dressed.’

“‘I have an honest heart,’ says Billy.

“‘Hearts don’t pay rent,’ says Donovan.

“‘I have prospects,’ says Billy.

“‘Where?’ says Donovan, lookin’ behind him.

“‘In the future,’ says Billy.

“‘Then marry in the future,’ says Donovan, ‘for ye’ll get no wife in the present.’

“An’ he shut the door.

“But a shut door, my dear sir, is only an encouragement to a young man in love, particularly if there’s a window to the house. Molly’s room looked upon the orchard, an’ every second night Billy was under the apple tree, makin’ a ladder of his feelings an’ climbin’ it in whispers.

“‘Molly, jewel,’ says he, ‘would ye come away wid me?’

“‘Where to?’ says she.

“‘Anywhere,’ says he.

“‘What would we live on?’ says she.

“‘Love,’ says he.

“‘Billy,’ says she, ‘ye ate three plates of potatoes at supper last Thursday.’

“‘That was before my love reached its present strength,’ says he.

“‘An’ would love milk the cow?’ says she.

“‘It would.’

“‘Cut the turf?’

“‘Like a machine.’

“‘Mend the roof?’

“‘As good as a slater.’

“‘Buy shoes for the children?’

“‘Molly,’ says he, ‘ye’re gallopin’ through the family before we’ve so much as crossed the church door.’

“Then she’d laugh, an’ he’d laugh, an’ they’d be talkin’ an’ sighin’, an’ sighin’ an’ talkin’, till Mrs. Donovan would open the door below an’ throw out a basin of water for no domestic purpose whatsoever. Billy learned the exact range of that basin, and could step aside from it without interruptin’ a sentence.

“For near a year the courtship went on, half by moonlight an’ half by conspiracy. Molly loved Billy as steadily as a candle burns in a quiet room, but Billy’s love was more like a bonfire on a windy hill—blazin’, leapin’, scatterin’ sparks, frightenin’ the neighbours, an’ liable to set fire to anything foolish enough to come near it.

“Now there was a wake one night at the house of Darby Finn, who had died at the age of ninety-two from the consequences of arguin’ that he was as young as ever. The whole parish came to assist the family in lamentation, conversation, tobacco, porter, tea, bread, ham, pipes, keening, cards, and such other spiritual comforts as are required when a decent man is removed from society.

“Billy came in a green coat borrowed from his cousin Mick, an’ Molly came wid her mother. There were candles round Darby, an’ a plate of salt on his breast, an’ Darby himself looked more peaceful than he’d ever looked while able to contradict people.

“For the first hour Billy behaved beautiful. He shook hands wid the widow, praised the deceased, drank to his memory, drank to his resurrection, drank to the health of those still waitin’ their turn, an’ then sat beside Molly.

“But there was present a certain Cornelius Doolan, who owned twenty acres, a horse, a jauntin’-car, an’ a face like a wet turf. Mrs. Donovan favoured him on account of the acres and overlooked the face as a temporary inconvenience. Cornelius sat on Molly’s other side an’ offered her a glass of cordial.

“‘She doesn’t take cordial,’ says Billy.

“‘I asked the lady,’ says Cornelius.

“‘An’ the lady’s answer is in my keepin’,’ says Billy.

“‘Ye’ve little enough else to keep,’ says Cornelius.

“‘I keep my temper,’ says Billy.

“‘It’s the only thing ye haven’t spent,’ says Cornelius.

“That brought the company round them, for there’s no assembly so sorrowful it won’t make room for a quarrel. Molly whispered to Billy to be quiet, but whisperin’ quietness to a jealous man is like recommendin’ sobriety to a duck in a pond.

“Cornelius asked Molly for the next dance.

“‘There’ll be no dancin’ at a wake,’ says Molly.

“‘There might be when the old people go,’ says Cornelius.

“‘There’ll be none wid you,’ says Billy.

“‘Did she appoint ye her mouth?’ says Cornelius.

“‘No,’ says Billy, ‘but I’m prepared to shut yours.’

“‘Billy!’ says Molly.

“‘I’m only conversin’,’ says Billy.

“‘Then converse without your fists,’ says she.

“‘I never raised a hand,’ says Billy, raisin’ both.

“Cornelius laughed. Billy told him not to. Cornelius laughed again to establish his independence. Billy stepped forward, Molly caught his sleeve, the borrowed coat tore from shoulder to elbow, an’ Billy—thinkin’ everyone was laughin’ at him, when indeed most were laughin’ at the coat—turned on Molly.

“‘Go dance wid him, then,’ says he.

“‘I will not,’ says she.

“‘Marry him, maybe,’ says Billy.

“‘Perhaps I will,’ says Molly, for she had pride too, and pride is love’s red-headed cousin, forever makin’ mischief in the family.

“‘Then joy go wid ye,’ says Billy.

“‘An’ wid yourself,’ says Molly.

“‘I’ll never speak to ye again,’ says Billy.

“‘Ye’ve said that seven times since Easter,’ says she.

“‘This is the eighth an’ last,’ says he.

“Out he went, tearin’ the other sleeve himself so they’d match.

“He proceeded to Flaherty’s public house to drown his affliction, though affliction, like a cork, is hard to drown and generally rises on top of the liquor. There he drank first to Molly’s happiness, then to Molly’s misery, then to his own indifference, an’ lastly to the confusion of Cornelius Doolan, who wasn’t there but suffered terrible defeats in Billy’s imagination.

“At the table beside him sat Sergeant Mulrooney of His Majesty’s army, a wide man in a red coat, with whiskers like two brushes and a genius for discoverin’ soldiers inside civilians who’d never suspected the thing themselves.

“‘A fine spirited fellow,’ says the sergeant.

“‘Who?’ says Billy.

“‘Yourself,’ says he.

“‘I am,’ says Billy.

“‘Too spirited for this little place,’ says the sergeant.

“‘Far too spirited,’ says Billy.

“‘A man like you ought to see the world,’ says the sergeant.

“‘I was thinkin’ that,’ says Billy.

“‘France,’ says the sergeant.

“‘I despise it,’ says Billy.

“‘Spain,’ says the sergeant.

“‘Never liked the sound of it,’ says Billy.

“‘Portugal,’ says the sergeant.

“‘I’d have no objection to Portugal if Portugal behaved itself,’ says Billy.

“‘Glory,’ says the sergeant.

“‘Fill the glass,’ says Billy.

“The sergeant filled it. Then he spoke of uniforms, drums, banners, foreign ladies, captured treasure, roast beef every day, and Napoleon Bonaparte, who, according to the sergeant, was waitin’ in a state of personal terror for Billy Malowney to enlist.

“‘He’s been askin’ after ye,’ says Mulrooney.

“‘Bonaparte?’ says Billy.

“‘Constantly. “Is Malowney in the army yet?” says he. “No,” says his generals. “Then I’m safe another night,” says he, an’ goes to bed, though not to sleep.’

“‘The villain,’ says Billy.

“‘Will ye leave him comfortable?’ says the sergeant.

“‘Never!’ says Billy, an’ struck the table.

“Mulrooney produced a shillin’ before the glasses stopped jumpin’. Billy took it to buy another drink, and when he woke next morning he had a headache in his skull, a shillin’ in his pocket, and His Majesty’s service all around him.

“Molly heard of it before noon. She ran to the barracks, but the party had marched. She ran to the hill beyond the town, and there saw the red coats movin’ on the road below, small and bright between the hedges. Billy was among them, sick with drink, sick with sorrow, and sick with the motion of the drum.

“She called his name.

“He heard her, turned, and broke from the line.

“‘Molly!’ says he.

“‘Billy!’ says she.

“They came together at the ditch, but Sergeant Mulrooney caught Billy by the tail of the coat.

“‘Release me,’ says Billy, ‘or I’ll dismantle the British army from the bottom upward.’

“‘Ye’re enlisted,’ says Mulrooney.

“‘It was a mistake,’ says Billy.

“‘The army thrives on mistakes,’ says the sergeant.

“Molly held out her hands.

“‘I didn’t mean it,’ says she.

“‘Nor me,’ says Billy.

“‘I’ll wait for ye,’ says Molly.

“‘Don’t,’ says Billy, for there’s nothin’ makes a woman more determined to wait than tellin’ her not to. ‘Marry some rich fellow.’

“‘I’ll marry no one.’

“‘Forget me.’

“‘I will not.’

“‘Then remember me handsome,’ says Billy, ‘for this uniform is killin’ me under the arms.’

“The drum sounded. Mulrooney hauled him away. Molly stood on the hill till the red coats were gone into the rain, and Billy looked back till the bend of the road took her from him. That was the last they saw of one another for seven weary years.

“Well, Billy crossed the sea, which disagreed with him so violently that he vowed to conquer France if only to prevent the necessity of returnin’ by water. He was sent to Portugal, Spain, and every place where the sun was hot, the wine was sour, the roads were absent, and the French were forever occupyin’ hills that decent men were then obliged to climb.

“At first Billy was no soldier. He saluted officers he liked and neglected those he didn’t. He cleaned his musket with butter because it gave a beautiful shine, though the ants carried it halfway from the camp. He put his left foot out when the regiment put its right, an’ when the corporal cursed him for bein’ contrary, Billy said:

“‘If the whole battalion is wrong, am I to abandon my principles?’

“But when the bullets began, he altered entirely. He had always been brave through ignorance; now he became brave through anger. The first ball knocked the pipe from his mouth.

“‘Who done that?’ says Billy.

“Several thousand Frenchmen answered at once.

“‘Come over here till I speak to ye separately!’ says Billy.

“Then he charged, an’ the regiment, seein’ him go, followed to prevent him from claiming the whole victory. He climbed a wall, took a gun, captured a drummer, disarmed a captain, rescued a lieutenant, lost his hat, found a better one, and came back askin’ if the battle was soon to begin.

“After that there was no holdin’ him.

“Wellington himself—an’ I’m givin’ ye the account exactly as it reached Ballycarrick, where truth is never damaged by excessive plainness—Wellington himself would send for Billy before every engagement.

“‘Malowney,’ says he, spreadin’ the map on a drum, ‘the French are here, here, and here.’

“‘Why are ye lettin’ them stand about like that?’ says Billy.

“‘I was waitin’ for you,’ says Wellington.

“‘Very considerate,’ says Billy. ‘Keep the army quiet while I discourage them.’

“Then Napoleon, from a hill sixty miles away, would clap a spyglass to his eye.

“‘Who is that in the damaged hat?’ says Napoleon.

“‘Billy Malowney, sire,’ says Marshal Soult.

“‘Order a retreat,’ says Napoleon.

“‘But he is alone, sire.’

“‘Then we may retreat in good order,’ says Napoleon.

“Whether that happened precisely, I wouldn’t swear on a wet day; but Billy did fight like a lion whose tail was after bein’ trod on. Letters came home from men of the parish tellin’ how he’d carried a wounded officer three miles, taken three prisoners wid an empty gun, and stood alone in a breach until the rest came up.

“Molly read every letter till the folds went thin. She kept them tied with the white ribbon she’d worn the first Sunday Billy saw her. Old Donovan pretended not to listen when they were read aloud, though he always found business near the hearth at the proper time. Mrs. Donovan said bravery was a fine thing but acreage safer.

“Suitors came to Molly. Cornelius Doolan came first, in a new hat and a cart of his own.

“‘I have twenty-four acres now,’ says he.

“‘Then ye’ve plenty to occupy ye,’ says Molly.

“‘I could put ye over a good house,’ says he.

“‘I’m not a roof,’ says Molly.

“‘Billy may never return,’ says he.

“‘Then I’ll never marry,’ says she.

“After Cornelius came James Keane, who owned a mill but breathed through his mouth; Terence Goggins, who had money in Dublin and a laugh like a gate without oil; a widower from Ennis with six children, two cows, and an opinion on every subject; and a schoolmaster who wrote Molly a poem comparin’ her to nine goddesses, three planets, the month of May, and a well-constructed sentence. She refused them all.

“‘Girl,’ says her mother, ‘are ye determined to waste your youth?’

“‘I’m determined to keep my word,’ says Molly.

“‘Words are wind,’ says Mrs. Donovan.

“‘Then mine blows only one way,’ says Molly.

“Three years passed, and then came news of a battle more savage than the rest. There had been smoke enough to hide the daylight, cannon enough to crack the firmament, cavalry enough to trample Europe flat, and such confusion that generals lost armies, armies lost roads, and Billy lost the tobacco from his pocket.

“They said he had been sent with a small party to hold a broken wall. The French came on by hundreds. Billy fired till his barrel was hot, fought till his bayonet bent, then stood on the wall singing ‘Garryowen’ in order, he said, to give the enemy some notion of civilisation. A shell burst near him. When the smoke cleared, Billy was down, and before his comrades could reach him the position had been overrun.

“A man from Kilfinnan wrote home that Billy Malowney was dead.

“When Molly heard it, she said nothing. She took the letter, walked out of the house, crossed the orchard, and sat beneath the apple tree where Billy had so often stood. It was spring, and the branches were white above her. The petals fell upon her hair and shoulders, and still she sat, as if the light touch of every blossom were another gentle hand laid there in pity.

“At sunset her mother found her.

“‘Come in, child,’ says she.

“‘In a little while,’ says Molly.

“‘Ye’ll catch your death.’

“‘No,’ says Molly. ‘Death doesn’t always come when it’s called.’

“She wore black after that. The laughter didn’t leave her entirely, for no warm heart can become altogether winter; but it came seldom, like a bird deceived by a bright hour in December. Every Sunday she went to Mass, every evening she tended the house, and on Billy’s birthday she walked alone to the hill where she had seen him march away.

“Old Donovan softened, though he wouldn’t confess it.

“‘He was an idle blackguard,’ says he.

“‘He was,’ says Mrs. Donovan.

“‘But a brave one,’ says Donovan.

“‘So they say.’

“‘An’ he might have improved.’

“‘He’d need time.’

“‘Well, he has eternity,’ says the old man, an’ blew his nose fiercely.

“Now, my dear sir, Billy wasn’t dead at all. Death had come lookin’ for him, certainly, and had gone so far as to measure him for the box, but Billy had never been punctual for an appointment in his life, and wasn’t goin’ to begin with the last one.

“The shell had torn his coat, broken two ribs, cut his head, damaged one ear, and driven half a pound of Spain into his left leg. He lay senseless while the armies surged away, an’ woke after dark under a dead French grenadier of extraordinary size.

“‘Get off me,’ says Billy.

“The Frenchman made no answer.

“‘Then I’ll move myself,’ says Billy, ‘for there’s no civility in this country.’

“He crawled from the field, was found by peasants, hidden in a barn, fevered for six weeks, and came near departin’ the world three separate times. Each time, according to Billy, Saint Peter looked over the gate and says:

“‘Is that Malowney?’

“‘It is,’ says Billy.

“‘We’re quiet here at present,’ says Saint Peter. ‘Try him below.’

“Then the divil looked up and says:

“‘Is that the fellow that fought at the breach?’

“‘The same,’ says Billy.

“‘Send him back,’ says the divil. ‘I’ve trouble enough.’

“So Billy recovered.

“But the regiment had marched, his papers were lost, the people at home had been told he was dead, and Billy himself knew none of it. He spent months in hospital, went lame for near a year, and after many complications was discovered by authority to be the same William Malowney who had been officially killed with distinction.

“‘Ye can’t be Malowney,’ says a clerk.

“‘Why not?’ says Billy.

“‘He’s dead,’ says the clerk, pointin’ to the paper.

“‘Then the paper’s mistaken,’ says Billy.

“‘Official papers are never mistaken.’

“‘Feel my pulse.’

“‘Irregular evidence.’

“‘I’ll kick ye.’

“‘Assault proves nothing.’

“Billy limped round the desk and supplied such proof that the clerk admitted the possibility of resurrection.

“In time he received a medal, arrears of pay, and a pension of twenty-eight pounds a year for life. He remained abroad while the claim was examined, reconsidered, misplaced, rediscovered, copied, sealed, forwarded, returned, and finally settled by a gentleman who died of old age immediately afterward.

“Seven years had passed before Billy saw Ireland again.

“He landed on a grey morning when rain drifted over the harbour and the green shore rose beyond it like the dear face of a friend seen through tears. He knelt and kissed the ground, then stood up spittin’, because the road had recently been used by cattle.

“‘Sweet native earth,’ says he, ‘but it could be cleaner.’

“He travelled home by cart, foot, coach, and argument. The nearer he came, the slower he went. His courage, which had carried him against cannon, failed before the thought that Molly might be married. Every child by the roadside seemed likely to be hers; every cabin might contain a husband; every prosperous-looking man offended him by the possibility of being the fellow.

“At last he reached the hill above Ballycarrick. Evening lay over the valley. Smoke rose from the chimneys. The church bell sounded. He saw Donovan’s farm among the trees and the old apple orchard behind it.

“‘If she’s married,’ says Billy to himself, ‘I’ll bless her an’ go away.’

“Then he thought about that.

“‘I’ll bless her,’ says he, ‘after I’ve had a look at the husband.’

“It happened that the Donovans, all except Molly, had gone to the great fair at Kilmora. Old Donovan went to sell cattle, Mrs. Donovan to prevent him from sellin’ them too cheaply, their son Larry to buy a horse, Larry’s wife to prevent him from buyin’ one too dearly, and the servant girl, Biddy, because fairs were instituted by Providence for servant girls to attend.

“Molly remained to mind the house.

“She was sittin’ near the window sew­ing when she heard a step in the yard. The latch lifted. A tall, weather-beaten man stood in the doorway, lame in one leg, scarred at the temple, dressed in a faded blue coat, and holdin’ his hat as if it were the only courage left to him.

“Molly rose.

“‘God save all here,’ says the man.

“She caught the table.

“‘Billy?’ says she.

“‘What’s left of him,’ says he.

“She stared, white as the cloth under her hand.

“‘Are ye livin’?’

“‘I was when I came through the gate.’

“‘They said ye were dead.’

“‘They exaggerated,’ says Billy.

“Then she crossed the room slowly, touched his sleeve, touched his scar, and laid her hand against his cheek.

“‘It’s warm,’ says she.

“‘Molly, jewel,’ says he, an’ his voice broke.

“At that she put both arms around him.

“For a while there was no speech between them, and if ye ask me what passed in their hearts, I’ll tell ye it was too deep for an old man’s story and too sacred for a stranger’s curiosity. The seven years fell away. The battles, the bitter waiting, the spring blossoms falling on black cloth, the long hospital nights, the roads, the doubts, the loneliness—all were gathered into that one embrace, as rivers are gathered into the sea and lose their separate sorrow there.

“Afterward there was talk enough.

“‘Why didn’t ye write?’ says Molly.

“‘I did, twice.’

“‘No letters came.’

“‘Then the French took them.’

“‘Did the French want news of my health?’

“‘They wanted everything, the greedy villains.’

“‘An’ were ye true to me?’

“‘Molly Donovan,’ says Billy, puttin’ a hand on his breast, ‘I never looked at another woman.’

“At that exact moment he remembered a widow in Lisbon, a wine-seller’s daughter near Salamanca, and two sisters in Porto who had all considered his looks attentively.

“‘Never looked twice,’ says he, amendin’ it.

“‘An’ I never married,’ says Molly.

“‘I knew ye wouldn’t.’

“‘Ye told me to.’

“‘That was military strategy. I meant the opposite.’

“She gave him food: ham, bread, milk, cold potatoes, butter, and half an apple tart. Billy ate as only a returned hero can eat when love has preserved his heart but done nothing for his stomach. He was finishin’ the tart when wheels sounded on the road.

“Molly looked from the window.

“‘Merciful Heaven! They’re home.’

“‘Who?’

“‘Father an’ mother—an’ Larry—an’ all of them.’

“‘Grand,’ says Billy. ‘I’ll speak to your father.’

“‘Ye will not.’

“‘I faced Napoleon.’

“‘Father’s in a temper after fairs.’

“‘I faced three French battalions.’

“‘Mother’s with him.’

“‘Where’ll I hide?’

“That was the measure of Mrs. Donovan.

“Molly looked about. The kitchen had no concealment except under the table, where even love couldn’t mistake Billy for furniture. The chimney was narrow, the loft ladder creaked, the yard was full of geese, and the pigsty already contained a sow, eleven young pigs, a broken wheel, two sacks, and a travelling tinker asleep in the corner, unknown to anybody.

“‘Upstairs,’ says Molly. ‘Quick.’

“She hurried Billy to the best bedroom and pushed him into a great oak clothes-press built into the wall.

“‘Stay quiet,’ says she.

“‘I’ll be silent as the grave,’ says Billy, not recognisin’ the unfortunate comparison.

“Molly locked the press and slipped the key into her pocket just as the family entered below in a storm of voices.

“The fair had gone badly. Old Donovan had sold no cattle because every buyer was a robber. Mrs. Donovan said he’d have sold them all for buttons if she hadn’t watched him. Larry had bought a chestnut horse with one white foot and a moral character yet to be established. Larry’s wife said it was lame. Larry said it was tired. Biddy had lost a shawl, gained a ribbon, and brought home Father O’Flaherty, whose mare had cast a shoe three miles back.

“The priest was a large, comfortable man, brave in doctrine and cautious in practice. He would have confronted any heresy in Christendom, provided it approached in daylight and gave reasonable notice.

“‘Molly,’ says Mrs. Donovan, ‘why are ye red?’

“‘The fire,’ says Molly.

“‘There’s no fire on your face,’ says her mother.

“‘I was bendin’ over it.’

“‘Why are there two plates?’

“‘I was hungry.’

“‘Two knives?’

“‘One was blunt.’

“‘Two mugs?’

“‘One was thirsty.’

“Mrs. Donovan narrowed her eyes, but Father O’Flaherty asked for supper, and suspicion yielded temporarily to bacon.

“Upstairs, Billy sat in the dark among Sunday coats, lavender bags, quilts, boots, a parasol, the family deeds, and Mrs. Donovan’s best bonnet. He had marched all day, eaten enough for four, and spent every remaining ounce of wakefulness in reunion. The press was warm. The coats were soft. Billy closed his eyes for what he intended to be half a minute.

“Below, they ate, argued over the horse, discussed prices, abused the weather, and heard Biddy’s account of seven fights, three proposals, a stolen goose, and a man who had swallowed a penny while cheering.

“Then, from the room above, came a low, mournful sound:

“Rrrrrrrr—hooooo—grrrrrrrr.

“Every spoon stopped.

“‘What was that?’ says Mrs. Donovan.

“‘The wind,’ says Molly.

“‘There’s no wind,’ says Larry.

“‘Then it stopped,’ says Molly.

“They listened.

“Rrrrrrrr—hoooo—khrrrrrr—Molly—

“Billy had spoken her name in his sleep.

“Mrs. Donovan crossed herself.

“‘Sweet Mother preserve us,’ says she.

“Old Donovan’s face loosened around the mouth.

“‘It came from upstairs.’

“‘Rats,’ says Larry.

“‘What rat says Molly?’ says his wife.

“‘A knowledgeable rat,’ says Larry, though he moved closer to the priest.

“Again the sound rolled down, deeper this time, because Billy’s head had fallen against the bonnet box.

“‘Moooolly—tell the serrrgeant—’

“Molly felt the key burn in her pocket.

“‘Billy Malowney,’ whispers Mrs. Donovan.

“‘Nonsense,’ says old Donovan, who had turned the colour of skimmed milk. ‘Billy’s dead.’

“‘That’s the point,’ says Larry’s wife.

“Father O’Flaherty raised one hand.

“‘Be calm,’ says he, backin’ toward the door. ‘The Church has encountered manifestations before.’

“‘Then go up an’ encounter this one,’ says Donovan.

“‘Spiritual matters require preparation,’ says the priest.

“‘What preparation?’

“‘A lantern, holy water, a strong assistant, an’ perhaps the arrival of daylight.’

“From above came a bump as Billy shifted, followed by a long groan and the words:

“‘Donovan—old villain—’

“Old Donovan gave a cry.

“‘He’s callin’ me!’

“‘Why wouldn’t he?’ says Mrs. Donovan. ‘Ye kept him from the girl.’

“‘You kept him too.’

“‘I was only followin’ your commands.’

“‘Ye never followed one in forty years.’

“‘Don’t quarrel before the spirit,’ says the priest. ‘It might take sides.’

“Biddy began to keen. The dog crawled under the dresser. Larry seized the poker. His wife seized Larry. Molly begged them all to be sensible, which persuaded everyone that she was under supernatural influence.

“‘Look at her,’ says Mrs. Donovan. ‘Not a bit afraid!’

“‘Maybe he’s appeared to her already,’ says Biddy.

“‘Have ye seen him, child?’ says her father.

“Molly hesitated.

“The whole room recoiled.

“‘She has!’ cries Larry.

“Father O’Flaherty opened his prayer book.

“‘Depart,’ says he toward the ceiling, ‘from this house, troublesome spirit, an’ return to—return to the place appointed for ye.’

“Upstairs Billy snored.

“‘He refuses,’ says Biddy.

“‘I gathered that,’ says the priest.

“They formed a procession for the stairs: Father O’Flaherty first by ecclesiastical rank, Larry second with the poker, old Donovan third with a candle, Mrs. Donovan fourth because no power earthly or otherwise would keep her from knowin’ what happened, Larry’s wife fifth, Biddy sixth, and Molly last, wringin’ her hands and attemptin’ to invent a confession that wouldn’t kill her parents from shame.

“At the top landing they paused.

“The bedroom door stood open. Beyond it the press gave a small shake.

“‘In there,’ whispers Donovan.

“‘So I perceive,’ says the priest.

“‘Open it.’

“‘You’re master of the house.’

“‘You’re master of spirits.’

“‘Only in a general pastoral capacity.’

“Larry extended the poker.

“‘Open it wid this.’

“‘A poker is not canonical,’ says Father O’Flaherty.

“Then Billy, disturbed by their whisperin’, woke suddenly in darkness. Forgettin’ where he was, he pushed at the doors. They were locked.

“‘Molly!’ he roared. ‘Let me out!’

“The procession broke.

“Father O’Flaherty went backward over Larry. Larry sat on Biddy. Biddy bit him through his breeches. Mrs. Donovan struck her husband with the candle. Larry’s wife threw the holy water into the priest’s face. The dog, hearing universal distress, ran upstairs and added his voice.

“‘He wants Molly!’ screamed Mrs. Donovan.

“‘Offer him somebody else!’ cried old Donovan.

“‘Open this confounded box!’ shouted Billy.

“‘He’s in torment,’ says Biddy.

“‘So am I!’ says Larry.

“Father O’Flaherty, recoverin’ his feet, began Latin with great authority and little arrangement.

“‘Exi—spiritus—ab hoc—bedroom—’

“‘That’s not Latin,’ says Donovan.

“‘It’s understood Above,’ says the priest.

“Billy struck the door from inside. The bonnet box fell. Mrs. Donovan’s bonnet landed on his head.

“‘Molly!’ says he. ‘Either release me or send in air!’

“Molly could bear no more.

“‘Stop!’ she cried. ‘He’s not a ghost.’

“Everyone became still.

“‘What is he, then?’ says her mother.

“‘Billy.’

“‘Billy what?’

“‘Billy Malowney.’

“‘That’s the ghost,’ says Donovan.

“‘No. It’s Billy alive.’

“‘Alive where?’

“‘In the press.’

“‘Why?’

“‘Because I put him there.’

“Old Donovan looked at his daughter, the press, the priest, and the ceiling, as if hopin’ one of them would explain the others.

“‘Open it,’ says he.

“Molly turned the key.

“The doors flew apart, and out stepped Billy, scarred, limpin’, rumpled, and crowned with Mrs. Donovan’s best bonnet. A black coat hung from one shoulder. A lavender bag had caught in his belt.

“No apparition rising from a tomb could have looked half so destructive to human reason.

“Mrs. Donovan screamed.

“Father O’Flaherty flung the prayer book.

“Larry thrust with the poker.

“Billy seized it.

“‘Bad luck to ye!’ says he. ‘Is this the welcome for a veteran?’

“‘Speak the truth,’ says the priest from behind the bed. ‘Are ye of this world?’

“‘I am, unless Ballycarrick has moved.’

“‘Prove it.’

“‘I owe Mick Langan three shillin’, I kissed Nora Healy behind the chapel when I was sixteen, an’ your reverence once fell asleep in Donovan’s haystack after the bishop’s dinner.’

“Father O’Flaherty emerged.

“‘It’s Billy,’ says he. ‘No spirit would possess such unnecessary information.’

“Then came explanations, exclamations, questions, answers, tears, laughter, blessings, reproaches, and Billy tellin’ the whole seven years in five minutes while Mrs. Donovan continually interrupted to ask whether the bonnet was ruined.

“Old Donovan remained stern.

“‘Ye’ve returned alive,’ says he. ‘That’s evident.’

“‘Thank ye,’ says Billy.

“‘But ye’re still without property.’

“Billy drew himself up.

“‘I have a pension from His Majesty.’

“The room altered.

“‘A pension?’ says Mrs. Donovan.

“‘For life,’ says Billy.

“‘How much?’ says Donovan.

“‘Twenty-eight pounds a year.’

“Mrs. Donovan sat down.

“‘Certain?’

“‘As long as I breathe.’

“‘An’ are ye healthy?’ she asked anxiously.

“‘He was dead ten minutes ago,’ says Larry, ‘an’ now ye’re worried for his constitution.’

“Old Donovan rubbed his chin.

“‘Twenty-eight pounds,’ says he.

“‘A year,’ says Billy.

“‘Regular?’

“‘Regular as the Government can manage.’

“‘Which isn’t sayin’ much,’ says the priest, ‘but it has divine possibilities.’

“He drew Donovan aside.

“‘Listen to me. Ye opposed them when the lad had nothin’. Ye separated them, an’ still they remained faithful. War couldn’t end it, distance couldn’t cool it, death itself made an announcement and was contradicted. Are ye now prepared to resist love, Providence, His Majesty’s army, and twenty-eight pounds per annum all at once?’

“Donovan considered.

“‘It’s a powerful combination,’ says he.

“‘It would flatten a stronger man than you,’ says the priest.

“Mrs. Donovan was already askin’ Billy whether the pension continued to a widow.

“‘Mother!’ says Molly.

“‘I’m considerin’ every eventuality,’ says she.

“So old Donovan gave in with the dignity of a fortress that opens its gates after secretly discovering the defenders are hungry.

“‘Take her,’ says he to Billy. ‘But mind—if ye break her heart—’

“‘I’ll mend it,’ says Billy.

“‘Ye can’t mend hearts.’

“‘I kept mine seven years under gunfire.’

“Molly put her hand into his.

“‘He’ll do,’ says she.

“They were married three weeks later, an’ such a weddin’ was never seen before or since, though several have injured themselves attemptin’ the resemblance. There were five fiddlers, two pipers, a blind harpist, a drummer who arrived by mistake from a political meeting, and a flute player that knew only one tune but played it in four keys to prevent monotony.

“Donovan killed a pig, Mrs. Donovan baked for six days, Larry supplied the uncertain chestnut horse, which carried the bride to chapel sideways, and Father O’Flaherty performed the ceremony with unusual speed because Billy’s history had taught him not to take continued life for granted.

“The company ate three hams, four geese, fourteen fowls, a mountain of potatoes, a lake of broth, twenty loaves, nine apple tarts, a currant cake strong enough to support a child, and a quantity of butter that caused the dairy market to rise in neighbouring counties. They drank porter, punch, whisky, tea, more whisky, and a bowl of something invented by Larry which removed speech from two men and restored it to another who had been silent since harvest.

“Cornelius Doolan attended in his best coat and danced with a widow before midnight. James Keane from the mill fell asleep under the table. Terence Goggins laughed till the dog attacked the gate. The schoolmaster composed an ode to the bride, lost the third page, recited the fourth twice, and was thrown lovingly into a hayrick.

“Billy danced on his wounded leg until the fiddler begged mercy. Molly laughed as she had not laughed since girlhood. Old Donovan, after denying three times that he’d taken drink, attempted to embrace the priest and instead embraced the clock. Mrs. Donovan guarded the silver spoons until she mislaid one in her own pocket and accused the bridegroom.

“At two in the morning Father O’Flaherty stood upon a chair to call for order. The chair collapsed, which produced more order than his speech could have done. At three, Larry rode the chestnut horse into the kitchen to prove it wasn’t lame. The horse departed through the pantry, where its opinion of the wedding cake was discovered too late.

“At dawn Billy and Molly slipped away to the orchard. Mist lay white over the grass, and the eastern sky was opening softly above the hills. The apple tree was old now, twisted by weather, but it still spread its branches above the place where they had whispered as boy and girl.

“‘Seven years,’ says Molly.

“‘Gone like a bad dream,’ says Billy.

“‘An’ will ye stay home?’

“‘Till they carry me out.’

“‘They carried ye out once already.’

“‘Then they’ll have to take greater care the next time,’ says he.

“They lived together for more than forty years. Billy’s pension arrived—sometimes early, sometimes late, once in another man’s name, and twice not at all—but his good humour was more dependable. He rented a little farm, raised cattle badly, children successfully, and potatoes according to the will of Heaven.

“There were, by the best account, twelve young Malowneys: seven boys, four girls, and one whose arrival occurred during such confusion that the family always disagreed whether he made twelve or thirteen. They inherited Billy’s courage, Molly’s constancy, Donovan’s suspicion of low prices, Mrs. Donovan’s interest in other people’s affairs, and a general inability to remain silent when a story was being told incorrectly.

“Billy lived long enough to describe his campaigns to grandchildren who believed half of them, which was twice what their parents believed and only a quarter of what Billy required. In his final version he captured Napoleon personally, released him out of courtesy, advised Wellington on the management of Europe, and survived death chiefly because Molly had not given permission for it.

“An’ maybe,” said the old man beneath the ruined arch, tapping the ashes from his pipe, “that last part wasn’t altogether a lie.”

The rain had ceased while he spoke. Night had come gently into the valley, extinguishing field after field until only the river remained visible, carrying upon its darkened surface the wavering image of the evening star. From a distant farmhouse came laughter, faint yet clear in the moist air; and nearer, among the ivy and the graves, a blackbird gave one final, liquid phrase before settling into silence.

“Where did Billy and Molly live?” I asked.

The old man pointed with his stick toward a cluster of trees beyond the river.

“Yonder, where the two chimneys are. One of them never drew properly.”

“And are they buried here?”

“They are,” said he, indicating a broad stone almost hidden by moss. “Molly went first. Billy followed within the year, though he complained that after escapin’ the French, the doctors, the Government, an’ the Donovan family, it was a poor conclusion to be defeated by loneliness.”

He rose, replaced his hat, and looked once more toward the stone.

“There’s stories that leave a man laughin’,” he said, “an’ stories that leave him grievin’; but the best of them do both, for that’s the way of the world itself. Ye’ll find sorrow sittin’ at every weddin’ if ye look for it, an’ some bit of comedy winkin’ at the wake. Billy knew that, though he’d never have had the language to say it.”

With that he bade me good evening and passed down the road, his stick sounding on the wet stones until mist and darkness received him.

I remained a little longer among the ruins. The ancient trees leaned above the graves, their branches woven together as though in an affection too old for speech. Water whispered under the broken wall. The clouds parted in the west, and for one moment a dying crimson shone through the abbey window, kindling every drop upon the ivy and setting the river aflame beneath it. Then the colour faded; the valley sank into shadow; and the long road home lay pale under the rising moon.

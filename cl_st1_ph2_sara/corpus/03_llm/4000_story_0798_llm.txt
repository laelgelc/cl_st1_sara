# The Measure of a Name

The Blue Vein Society of Wellington was not, as its officers frequently explained, an organization founded upon complexion. There was no article in its constitution requiring that a member’s veins should be discernible through the skin; nor did the committee on admissions possess any instrument by which such a qualification might have been tested. Membership depended, according to the society’s printed circular, upon character, culture, and congeniality. It was nevertheless observed that nearly all the Blue Veins were light enough to satisfy the picturesque condition from which their popular name was derived.

The members themselves deplored the title, though not, perhaps, the distinction it implied. They preferred the formal designation, the Wellington Society for Social Improvement. Their critics called them Blue Veins, mulatto aristocrats, and several less agreeable things. But criticism is often only admiration compelled to stand outside the gate. It had happened more than once that the most ardent opponents of the society, having been elected to membership, discovered that its standards were both necessary and wise.

To do the Blue Veins justice, they represented the most cultivated portion of Wellington’s colored population. Their men were physicians, teachers, merchants, postal clerks, and minor officials; their women were accomplished in music, needlework, church administration, and the difficult art of maintaining attractive homes upon limited incomes. They gave concerts for worthy causes, subscribed to books, encouraged temperance, and took pains that their children should pronounce their words correctly. They believed that the progress of their race depended upon the visible success of its best representatives.

This theory had much to recommend it. If, in practice, their definition of “best” happened to include straight hair, refined manners, education, property, and a complexion approaching that of the dominant race, the coincidence was rarely discussed. There were among them several darker persons whose talents or wealth overcame the disadvantage of color; but, generally speaking, a candidate who possessed neither money nor a light skin required virtues of an almost heroic order.

Mr. Ambrose Ryder was the acknowledged leader of this society. His leadership rested upon no formal election. It had arisen naturally from the combination of qualities that made him, in the opinion of his associates, the embodiment of their ideals. He was prosperous without ostentation, dignified without stiffness, charitable without publicity, and sufficiently reserved to permit imagination to enlarge his importance.

He was a bachelor, apparently somewhat past fifty, though his precise age was unknown. His skin was of a warm, pale brown, his features regular, and his hair, which had receded from a high forehead, was fine and only lightly touched with gray. He wore a short, carefully trimmed beard. His clothes were never conspicuous, but a tailor would have recognized their quality. He had begun his career in Wellington as a laborer in a wholesale warehouse; later he became a clerk, then a bookkeeper, and at length the firm’s trusted cashier. Economy and several prudent investments had enabled him to purchase a comfortable brick house on Wellington Avenue, in a neighborhood where colored residents were still few enough to be remarked upon.

The house expressed its owner with unusual fidelity. The parlor was furnished in dark wood and green velvet. The curtains were heavy, the carpets soft, and the pictures, though not costly, had been chosen with intelligence. There was a steel engraving of Lincoln, a portrait of Frederick Douglass, and a framed view of an English abbey whose name Mr. Ryder pronounced with confidence, though he had never visited England. His library contained Shakespeare, Milton, Tennyson, several histories, a well-used Bible, and a number of volumes that had been purchased from a sense of duty and remained uncut.

Mr. Ryder was fond of poetry. At Blue Vein entertainments he was sometimes persuaded to recite, and his resonant voice gave distinction even to verses that might otherwise have seemed overly familiar. His taste ran toward noble sentiments, faithful love, courageous renunciation, and those elevated griefs which are more agreeable in literature than in life. It had never occurred to anyone that his own history contained material for romance. He spoke little of his early years. What was known was that he had come North before the end of the war, had educated himself, and had risen by perseverance. The society considered this sufficient. A past, like an old coat, may be excused in a man who has replaced it with something better.

For several years Mr. Ryder had been regarded as the Blue Veins’ most desirable bachelor. That he remained unmarried was attributed to fastidiousness rather than indifference. He enjoyed the society of cultivated women, paid them impartial courtesies, and avoided entanglement. Recently, however, his impartiality had shown signs of yielding before the attractions of Mrs. Molly Dixon.

Mrs. Dixon was a widow of twenty-eight. Her late husband, a railway clerk, had left her a modest income and no children. She had been educated at a Northern institute, read French with some ease, played the piano agreeably, and possessed that composure which can make even silence appear accomplished. Her complexion was fair enough that strangers frequently mistook her ancestry, and her hair, of a soft brown, fell in smooth waves about a face more distinguished by intelligence than by beauty. She was gracious to everyone, condescending to no one, and had earned the affection of persons who might otherwise have resented her advantages.

Mr. Ryder admired her first for her mind, then for her manners, and finally, though he would have reversed the order in any public account, for the lightness and delicacy of her person. His admiration was generally understood, and Mrs. Dixon’s reception of it was thought encouraging. Their marriage, should it occur, would place them at the summit of colored society in Wellington. It would also, Mr. Ryder believed, represent a principle.

“We must lift ourselves,” he had once said to a friend, “not by denying the race, but by demonstrating its highest possibilities.”

The friend, who was darker than Mr. Ryder and had twice been rejected by a white insurance company, had answered that the race appeared to have many heights and very few elevators. Mr. Ryder had smiled, but the remark had displeased him.

He did not deny his people. On the contrary, he spoke often of racial advancement. Yet his philosophy of advancement was selective. Society, in his favorite figure, was a vessel struggling against a contrary sea. The educated and prosperous were the lifeboat; if they overloaded themselves with every unfortunate claimant, all might sink together. At other times they were an anchor, a shield, or a bulwark. His metaphors varied, but their practical conclusion remained the same: progress required standards, and standards required exclusion.

It was perhaps unfortunate that so many excluded persons possessed complexions darker than his own. This, he maintained, was a result rather than a cause.

For the first Thursday in October, Mr. Ryder planned an entertainment more elaborate than any he had previously given. Its announced purpose was to celebrate the autumn season, but his intimate acquaintances suspected another intention. It was believed that, during the evening, he would ask Mrs. Dixon to become his wife.

The supper was ordered from the best caterer in the city. Flowers came by train from a greenhouse forty miles away. Additional lamps were placed in the hall and parlor; musicians were engaged; the silver was polished until the spoons seemed to contain small, distorted moons. Mr. Ryder inspected every arrangement with care. He had prepared a toast for the supper and, in the privacy of his library, rehearsed both it and a shorter, more personal speech.

On the afternoon of the ball, he stood at his desk reading over the final lines. Beside his hand lay a small velvet box containing a ring.

A servant entered and said that an elderly colored woman wished to see him.

“What is her business?”

“She says she is looking for somebody, sir. She won’t tell me more.”

Mr. Ryder frowned. The hour was inconvenient, and the house already trembled with preparations. Chairs were being moved in the dining room; from the kitchen came a clatter of dishes and the mixed fragrance of coffee, spice, and roasting meat.

“Did she give a name?”

“Liza Jane Taylor.”

The name produced no visible change in his face, though the fingers resting upon the desk drew together.

“Where is she?”

“At the side door.”

Mr. Ryder looked toward the velvet box. Then he closed it and placed it in a drawer.

“Bring her to the back veranda,” he said. “I will speak with her there.”

The veranda overlooked a small garden, now touched by autumn. The last roses drooped beside the walk, and red leaves had gathered against the fence. Mr. Ryder waited with one hand upon the railing. When the servant led the visitor toward him, he saw first a faded red kerchief, then a bent figure in a coarse gray dress.

She was a very dark woman, small and wiry, perhaps sixty years of age, perhaps older. Labor and weather had written upon her face in a hand more severe than time’s. Her shoes were dusty, the hem of her dress patched, and one of her hands grasped a worn carpetbag. Yet her eyes were lively and searching; and though she looked tired, she carried herself with an independence that poverty had not destroyed.

“Is dis Mistuh Ryder’s house?” she asked.

“I am Mr. Ryder.”

She examined him frankly from head to foot.

“You’s Mistuh Ryder?”

“That is my name. What can I do for you?”

“Well, suh, I hopes you kin ’scuse me comin’ dis-a-way. I been axin’ roun’, an’ a man down by de depot, he say you knows most all de cullud folks in dis town. Say ef anybody kin he’p me, you kin.”

Mr. Ryder inclined his head. “Whom are you looking for?”

“My husban’.”

The answer was so direct that he paused.

“How long has he been missing?”

“Twenty-five year, come nex’ spring.”

He looked at her again. “Twenty-five years is a long time.”

“Yas, suh. Hit’s a mighty long time when you counts it, but it ain’t so long when you’s waitin’. One day jes’ lays down side o’ de nex’.”

“What was your husband’s name?”

“Sam Taylor.”

The leaves along the fence stirred, though there was little wind. Mr. Ryder’s hand tightened upon the railing.

“Where did you last see him?”

“Down in Miss’ippi, on de Carroway place. Dat was fo’ de surrender. Sam belonged to Marse Abel Carroway, an’ I belonged to his sister, Miss Lucy. Dey plantations j’ined. Me an’ Sam got married by de preacher, same as folks could in dem days, an’ we had nigh on two good years.”

“You were slaves?”

“Yas, suh. We was slaves.”

She said it without emphasis, as one might name a country from which a long journey had begun.

“What happened to him?”

The old woman set down her carpetbag and rested both hands upon its handle.

“Marse Abel was hard on Sam. Sam, he warn’t no great hand fo’ work, I ain’t gwine tell no story ’bout dat. He could sleep sweeter in de shade dan any man I ever knowed. But he warn’t bad. He’d sing whilst I worked, an’ make me laugh when I was fit to cry. Marse Abel had him beat one day, an’ Sam run away.”

“Do you know where he went?”

“Naw, suh. Some say he went up river. Some say he crossed over wid de Yankees. Marse Abel come to our cabin an’ say Sam be shot ef he come back. I waited. Den de war got worse, an’ peoples was sold an’ scattered, an’ Miss Lucy took me off to Alabama. When freedom come, I went back. De cabin was burned, Marse Abel was dead, an’ nobody knowed nothin’ certain.”

“And you have searched for him ever since?”

“Not every day wid my feet,” she replied, with a flash of humor, “’cause feet got to eat. I cooked. I washed. I nussed chillen dat done growed up an’ had chillen dey own. But every place I went, I axed. Every traveler come from up North, I axed. Every preacher, soldier, railroad man, drummer, anybody looked like he knowed de road, I axed him ’bout Sam Taylor.”

“There are many men of that name.”

“Dat’s so. I found five. One was too young, two was too black, one was a preacher—Sam never had no call dat-a-way—an’ one had only one eye. My Sam had two eyes when he left, an’ I reckon he’d mention losin’ one.”

Despite himself, Mr. Ryder smiled faintly.

“What did your husband look like?”

“He was brown-skinned. Not light as you, suh, but lighter’n me. Had straightish hair an’ a little scar by de lef’ ear, where a mule kicked him when he was a boy. He was tall an’ slim. Pretty, too. Sam knowed it.”

“After twenty-five years, a man might change considerably.”

“Reckon he would. I done changed some myself.”

She smiled, and the smile briefly restored the girl she had been—not in feature, but in spirit.

“Suppose he is dead?” Mr. Ryder asked.

“He mought be.”

“Suppose he married again?”

She considered this more gravely.

“Den he done wrong, lessen he knowed I was dead. But I wouldn’t bust up no home. I jes’ wants to see him. Wants him to know I ain’t forgot.”

“Why have you come to Wellington?”

“A woman in Chicago tell me she heard tell of a Sam Taylor lived here years ago, a man come up from de South an’ changed his name. She couldn’t ’member de new name. I had some money saved, so I come. I been here three days.”

Mr. Ryder’s voice was careful. “A great many fugitives changed their names.”

“Dat’s why I carries dis.”

She opened the carpetbag and searched beneath several neatly folded garments. At length she brought out a small parcel wrapped in a piece of calico. From it she removed a tarnished metal case and handed it to him.

It contained an old photograph, dim with age. A young man stood stiffly beside a rustic chair, one hand resting upon its back. His coat was too large, and his expression combined uneasiness with pride. The picture had faded at the edges, but the face remained visible.

Mr. Ryder held it near the light.

“Where did you get this?”

“Sam give it me. A picture man come through de county. Sam done odd jobs fo’ him, an’ he took Sam’s likeness. Only picture he ever had. I kep’ it hid when Miss Lucy took me away. Sewed it in my dress.”

Mr. Ryder did not answer.

The young man in the photograph possessed darker skin and fuller cheeks than Mr. Ryder; his hair was abundant, his clothes rough, and no cultivation had yet disciplined his expression. But beside the left ear, partly concealed by the hair, was the faint line of a scar.

The old woman watched Mr. Ryder’s face.

“You ever seen him, suh?”

“No,” he said.

The word came after too long an interval.

She sighed, though without surprise. Repeated disappointment had not destroyed her hope; it had merely taught hope to travel on tired legs.

“I ’lowed maybe you hadn’t. But you’ll keep a lookout?”

“Yes.”

“Tell de folks, please, dat Liza Jane’s huntin’ Sam Taylor. Tell ’em she ain’t wantin’ nothin’ from him. I got two hands yet, an’ I kin work some. Jes’ tell him Liza Jane ain’t forgot.”

Mr. Ryder returned the photograph. Their fingers touched, and he drew his hand back as if the metal case were hot.

“Where are you staying?”

“Wid a washwoman on Pine Alley. Name’s Mis’ Watkins. I give her a little fo’ my pallet.”

He took out his pocketbook.

“I cannot promise to find your husband, but you may need assistance.”

She glanced at the money and shook her head.

“I ain’t beggin’, suh.”

“I did not suppose you were.”

“Mis’ Watkins say you’s a good man. She say you done he’ped folks before. But I got enough till Monday, an’ Monday I’m gwine take in washin’.”

He did not press the matter.

“If I learn anything, I will send for you.”

“Thank you, suh. You reckon I ought to stay in town?”

“For a day or two.”

She wrapped the photograph again, placed it carefully beneath her clothes, and closed the bag.

“I sho’ hopes you finds out somethin’. You got a face makes a body trust you.”

Mr. Ryder looked away toward the dying roses.

The servant came to conduct her out. At the garden gate she turned.

“You ain’t never heard tell o’ de Carroway place, has you?”

“No.”

“Nor Miss Lucy?”

“No.”

“Nor a boy name Sam?”

“No.”

She nodded, as though closing one more door among hundreds, and went down the alley with the carpetbag swinging at her side.

Mr. Ryder remained on the veranda until the servant reminded him that the florist required instructions. He gave them, inspected the table, approved the arrangement of lamps, and answered several questions with his customary precision. No one observed anything unusual except that, when asked whether the musicians should play in the hall or the rear parlor, he was obliged to have the question repeated.

Afterward he went to his bedroom and locked the door.

The room contained a tall mirror in a walnut frame. Before it Mr. Ryder stood for a long time.

He first looked at his clothes: the black coat fitted smoothly; the linen at his throat was white; his narrow watch chain was of good gold. Then he studied the face above the collar—the broad forehead, the controlled mouth, the pale brown skin, the beard arranged to conceal the lower cheeks. He raised his hand and touched the small scar behind his left ear.

The gesture seemed to strip away years.

A cabin appeared in his memory, not as a complete picture but in fragments: rain upon a wooden roof; the smell of corn bread; a girl laughing as she chased a hen from the doorway; a red kerchief; a hand, darker than his own, laid upon his arm. Then came a field at dusk, blood beneath a torn shirt, the threat of a whip, and a night flight through cane and mud. He remembered promising to return. He remembered intending to return.

War had swept him northward. Freedom had brought hunger, opportunity, danger, work, and a new name. At first he had inquired after Liza Jane. Later he had assumed she had been sold farther South. Still later he had permitted himself to believe her dead. There had been no single hour in which Sam Taylor ceased to exist. He had diminished gradually, as a road behind a traveler narrows and disappears.

Ambrose Ryder had been built carefully upon the place where Sam had stood.

Mr. Ryder sat beside the window.

The legal question was uncertain. Slave marriages had been imperfectly recognized, and twenty-five years of separation might excuse almost any conclusion. No court could compel him to acknowledge the woman. No witness in Wellington knew his former name. Liza Jane herself had looked into his face and failed to recognize him.

There was Mrs. Dixon. There was the society. There was his position, earned through discipline and sacrifice. A public acknowledgment would invite ridicule from enemies and discomfort from friends. It would join his cultivated present to a past he had not so much denied as outgrown. He could provide for Liza Jane secretly. He could send her money, secure a comfortable room, and allow her search to end in charitable uncertainty.

This would be kind.

It would also be false.

Toward evening he unlocked the desk and removed the velvet box. He held it for a moment, then slipped it into his waistcoat pocket.

At nine o’clock the first carriages arrived.

The house, illuminated from cellar to garret, fulfilled every promise of its preparations. Lamps shone through colored glass; flowers softened the dark furniture; music moved from room to room with the guests. The women wore silk, satin, and lace in colors selected with due regard for complexion. The men appeared in evening coats, some less comfortable than others in collars of fashionable height. There were judges of small courts, principals of schools, two physicians, a newspaper editor, a dentist, several ministers, and a young lawyer whose recent admission to the bar had been celebrated as a racial victory, though he had not yet obtained a client.

Mrs. Dixon arrived shortly after nine. She wore a gown of ivory silk with pale blue trimming, and a string of pearls lay at her throat. Her appearance produced a murmur of approval discreet enough to be flattering. Mr. Ryder received her at the door and offered his arm.

“You have made your house beautiful,” she said.

“It lacked only its most distinguished guest.”

She smiled. “That is a dangerous compliment. Every lady who enters after me will prove you insincere.”

“Then I shall make no further compliments this evening.”

“I should not wish your other guests to suffer on my account.”

Their exchange was precisely of the sort upon which observers build engagements. Mrs. Dixon’s hand rested lightly upon his sleeve; Mr. Ryder’s manner toward her possessed an attention distinct from his general courtesy. Yet she soon noticed that he was absent-minded. Twice he failed to hear remarks addressed to him. During a quadrille he stood alone near the library door and appeared to listen for some sound beyond the music.

“Are you unwell?” she asked when they met again.

“Not at all.”

“You seem troubled.”

“Only occupied with my toast.”

“Then it must be an important one.”

“It may be.”

She studied him. “Will it surprise us?”

“I think it will surprise me.”

Before she could ask what he meant, supper was announced.

The guests entered the dining room, where the table, enlarged for the occasion, glittered beneath the lamps. There were oysters, fowl, ham, salads, breads, preserves, cakes, and ices; the abundance pleased even those who believed it vulgar to appear impressed by food. Mr. Ryder seated Mrs. Dixon at his right. Conversation rose easily. The editor told a political story; the young lawyer corrected him upon a point of law; one physician disputed with the other about sanitation; and several ladies discussed the proposed admission of a family whose father was dark but prosperous, while the mother was light but rumored to have worked as a seamstress before marriage.

No one mentioned complexion as a qualification. They spoke only of suitability.

When the dishes had been removed and the glasses filled, the company grew quiet. Mr. Ryder rose.

He stood straight and composed, one hand resting upon the table. His prepared toast lay folded in his pocket, beside the ring. He did not take it out.

“My friends,” he began, “I had intended this evening to speak upon a subject agreeable to us all. I had prepared certain phrases concerning beauty, companionship, and the happiness that may attend a fortunate choice. Those phrases no longer suit my purpose.”

Several guests glanced toward Mrs. Dixon. She lowered her eyes, then raised them again.

“Instead,” he continued, “I shall ask your judgment upon a case. It is not a case from the law books, though law enters into it. It is not a tale from a poet, though love and constancy are concerned. Suppose that, many years ago, before freedom came to our people, there lived upon neighboring plantations a young man and a young woman. They were slaves. They possessed neither property nor legal names that the world was bound to respect. Yet they loved each other and were married according to the custom permitted them.”

The room became very still.

“Suppose the young man was not remarkable. He was neither learned nor industrious. He had, perhaps, more charm than wisdom and more hope than discipline. His master treated him cruelly. After a beating, and under threat of worse punishment, he fled.

“Suppose he intended to return for his wife. But war intervened. Armies moved; plantations changed hands; men and women were scattered like leaves before a storm. He escaped to the North. He found work, learned to read more fully, acquired the manners of a different society, and began another life.

“Suppose he changed his name.”

A glass touched a plate with a faint click. No one moved to retrieve it.

“Suppose the years prospered him. He rose by labor and economy. He gained the confidence of honorable men. He secured a home and a respected position. He became, perhaps, a leader among people who valued education and refinement. The ignorant fugitive disappeared, and in his place stood a gentleman.”

Mr. Ryder paused. His face had lost its usual color.

“Suppose that, at first, he sought news of his wife. Suppose that no news came. He knew she might have been sold, might have died, might have married another after emancipation. As the years passed, he ceased to inquire. At length he persuaded himself that the duties of the present had canceled the promises of the past.”

Mrs. Dixon’s gaze did not leave him.

“Suppose, meanwhile, the woman had not forgotten. She had no money, no education, no friends of influence. She could not print advertisements or employ detectives. She had only her memory and her feet. She worked in kitchens, at wash tubs, beside sickbeds. Wherever she went, she asked after him. For twenty-five years she searched.

“Suppose age bent her back. Suppose hardship darkened and roughened a face that had never been protected from the sun. Suppose her speech remained the speech of the plantation, because freedom had given her ownership of herself but no leisure for schooling. Yet she kept the young man’s picture. She kept it hidden through war, removal, hunger, and poverty. She refused other husbands because she believed the first might still be alive.”

Here Mr. Ryder’s voice changed. The cultivated cadence remained, but beneath it sounded another rhythm, older and less guarded.

“Suppose she say, ‘I ain’t beggin’ nothin’ from him. I jes’ wants to see him. Jes’ wants him to know Liza Jane ain’t forgot.’”

A woman at the farther end of the table drew a quick breath.

“Suppose,” Mr. Ryder continued, “that she came at last to the city where the man lived. Suppose she stood at his door and told her story. Suppose she showed him the old photograph, but did not know him. He had changed too greatly. Time, comfort, clothes, and a new manner had hidden him more completely than any disguise.”

He lifted his hand and touched the scar behind his ear.

“Suppose he recognized himself.”

No one now doubted that this was more than a moral anecdote. The guests looked from Mr. Ryder to Mrs. Dixon and back again.

“Suppose this man loved another woman—a younger woman, educated, beautiful, fitted in every respect to adorn his home and share his position. Suppose he had meant, on that very evening, to ask her to become his wife.

“Suppose no one knew his former name. Suppose he could deny the old wife and remain secure. He might provide for her as an act of charity. He might send her elsewhere. He might permit her to continue searching until death persuaded her that she had searched in vain.

“Suppose the law would protect him.

“Suppose society would forgive him.

“Suppose his own heart would not.”

The last sentence fell quietly, but it seemed to alter the air of the room.

“My friends, what should that man do?”

No one answered at once.

The moral question was clear; the social consequences were equally clear. It is easy to admire loyalty in the abstract, especially when it wears clean linen and speaks correctly. It is more difficult when loyalty appears at the side door in a patched dress and threatens the arrangement of one’s dining room.

The Blue Veins had built their respectability as a shield against insult. They knew the world’s contempt for poverty, darkness, and the marks of bondage, because many had spent their lives avoiding its blow. Some had parents or grandparents whom they rarely mentioned. Some had altered pronunciations, revised birthplaces, and allowed old acquaintances to become strangers. Mr. Ryder’s question did not accuse them, but it passed among them like a mirror.

The young lawyer cleared his throat, perhaps intending to distinguish legal from moral obligation. His wife laid a hand upon his sleeve, and he remained silent.

At last Mrs. Dixon rose.

Her face was pale, but her voice was steady.

“He should acknowledge her.”

A murmur of assent followed.

“He should,” said one of the physicians.

“Yes,” said the editor.

“He must,” said an elderly minister, who had himself been born enslaved.

The answer moved around the table, gathering strength as it went.

Mr. Ryder bowed his head.

“I thank you,” he said. “Your judgment agrees with mine.”

He left the dining room.

For several moments the guests sat in a silence too serious for whispering. Mrs. Dixon remained standing, one hand resting upon the back of her chair. No one offered her consolation, because no one knew whether consolation would insult her pride or increase her pain.

The music had stopped in the parlor. From the street came the sound of carriage wheels and, farther away, the call of a night vendor. The lamps burned steadily over the untouched glasses.

Then footsteps approached in the hall.

Mr. Ryder entered first. His bearing was erect, but the elaborate composure of the evening had left him. Beside him walked Liza Jane.

Someone had given her a clean black shawl, but she still wore the gray dress and red kerchief. Her dusty shoes moved uncertainly over the carpet. She held the carpetbag before her with both hands, as if it were a defense against the silk, silver, light, and astonished faces. At the threshold she stopped.

“Go on,” Mr. Ryder said gently.

She looked up at him.

“Dey’s a heap o’ fine folks in here, suh.”

“They are my friends.”

“Did you find out somethin’ ’bout Sam?”

“Yes.”

Her grip tightened upon the carpetbag.

“Is he—he ain’t dead?”

“No.”

“Den where is he?”

Mr. Ryder turned toward the assembled company. For an instant the old instinct of concealment rose in him. It was visible only as a slight hesitation, but in that hesitation lay the whole distance between the Carroway plantation and Wellington Avenue, between Sam Taylor and Ambrose Ryder.

He crossed it.

“My friends,” he said, “this is Liza Jane Taylor.”

Then he looked at the old woman.

“Liza Jane, do you remember the scar a mule made behind Sam’s left ear?”

Her eyes widened. The carpetbag slipped from her hands and fell soundlessly upon the rug.

He bent his head and drew back the hair above his ear.

She stared. Her lips moved, but no sound came. She touched the scar with one trembling finger, then searched his face as though age were a veil she might lift by looking hard enough.

“Sam?”

The name was barely spoken.

“Yes, Liza.”

She gave a little cry, neither wholly sorrow nor wholly joy. Her knees failed, and he caught her. For a moment she leaned against him, the red kerchief resting upon his formal coat. His eyes closed.

When he raised his head, he addressed the company.

“Ladies and gentlemen,” he said, “permit me to introduce my wife.”

No applause followed. Applause would have been smaller than the moment and too easy for those who had only witnessed its cost. The guests rose instead.

Mrs. Dixon came forward first.

Mr. Ryder watched her approach with an expression in which gratitude, regret, and apprehension were mingled. She stopped before Liza Jane, whose hand still clutched his sleeve.

“I am Molly Dixon,” she said.

Liza Jane looked from the young widow’s ivory gown to Mr. Ryder’s face. Whatever she perceived there, she understood enough.

“You’s mighty pretty, honey,” she said softly.

Mrs. Dixon’s composure trembled, but did not break.

“And you,” she answered, taking the old woman’s hand, “have been very faithful.”

Liza Jane shook her head.

“I jes’ kept lookin’.”

Sometimes a life’s highest virtue is described most truly by its simplest act.

The minister came next and welcomed her. Then the physician, the editor, and their wives followed. Not all did so with equal warmth. Human prejudice is not dissolved in a single noble scene; it retreats, considers, and often returns by another door. Some guests were moved chiefly by Mr. Ryder’s sacrifice, some by Liza Jane’s devotion, and some by the consciousness that history had entered the dining room and was observing their manners. Yet they came forward.

One by one, they spoke to her.

The young lawyer recovered the carpetbag. A lady who had lately opposed the admission of a dark family adjusted Liza Jane’s shawl. The caterer brought a chair, and Mrs. Dixon herself placed it beside Mr. Ryder’s.

The ring remained in his pocket.

Later, after most of the guests had gone, Mr. Ryder stood with Liza Jane in the quiet parlor. The flowers had begun to droop in the heated room. Empty glasses and folded napkins remained upon the table; the musicians’ chairs stood abandoned near the window. Outside, the last carriage rolled away.

Liza Jane sat beneath the engraving of Lincoln and studied the objects around her with cautious wonder.

“You done mighty well, Sam.”

“I have been called Ryder for many years.”

She considered this.

“Reckon I kin learn it.”

He sat opposite her.

“There is much I must tell you.”

“Got time now.”

The words were without reproach, which made them harder to bear.

“Yes,” he said. “We have time.”

She opened the carpetbag and took out the old photograph. After unwrapping it, she held it beside the lamplight and compared the young face with the older one before her.

“You sho’ was pretty,” she said.

Mr. Ryder smiled.

“So you told me once.”

“I told you plenty times. You was lazy, too.”

“So you told our guests.”

“I ain’t tell ’em half.”

Her laughter was thin with weariness, but unmistakably merry. He laughed with her, uncertainly at first, and then more freely than anyone in the Blue Vein Society had ever heard him laugh.

Presently she grew serious.

“I knowed you warn’t dead.”

“How could you know?”

“Didn’t know it in my head. Knowed it somewheres else.”

He looked at the photograph.

“I should have searched for you.”

“Maybe so.”

“I should have returned.”

“Couldn’t, maybe.”

“I could have tried longer.”

“Dat’s so.”

Her agreement carried neither cruelty nor false comfort. It was judgment without vengeance.

He lowered his eyes.

“Can you forgive me?”

She studied him for a long moment.

“You Sam?”

“Yes.”

“Den I done forgived you a thousand times already.”

The lamp flame wavered. In the tall mirror across the room were reflected the cultivated gentleman in evening dress and the old dark woman in her faded kerchief. They did not resemble the youthful figures who had parted twenty-five years before. Time had altered them unequally, as it alters all travelers according to the roads they take. Yet the mirror now contained them together.

Mr. Ryder rose and offered her his arm.

“Come,” he said. “I will show you your home.”

She placed her hand upon his sleeve. At the foot of the stairs she hesitated, looking upward.

“Dat’s a powerful lot o’ steps.”

He smiled and stood nearer, so that she might lean upon him.

“We will take them slowly.”

And so they did.

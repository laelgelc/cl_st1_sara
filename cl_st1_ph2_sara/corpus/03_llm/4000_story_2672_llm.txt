All morning the house forgot how to be a house.

First two men came carrying chairs upon their shoulders, six at a time, with the thin gold legs sticking up and trembling. They went through the hall sideways and said, “Steady there!” though nothing was moving except themselves. Then a boy brought baskets of roses—red roses, white roses, and little yellow ones curled tight as buttons. Then came a man with a black bag.

“Is that the concert?” Moon whispered.

Sun did not answer. The man had put his ear close to the piano and struck one note over and over. Perhaps the concert was inside, and he was asking it to come out.

Ping! Ping! Ping!

Moon stood on one foot and struck the air with her finger.

“Ping!” she said.

“Don’t,” said Sun. “You’ll muddle him.”

They watched from behind the stair rails until Nurse discovered them.

“Back to the nursery, you naughty ducks. What would your mother say?”

“But it is our house,” Moon protested.

“Not today it isn’t,” said Nurse, gathering Moon under one arm. “Today it belongs to the company.”

That was extraordinary. Sun looked down through the rails at the hall, which was full of coats and baskets and green leaves. If it did not belong to them, perhaps by evening it would belong to somebody else altogether. Perhaps they would wake tomorrow and find the nursery made into a railway station.

After luncheon Mother came upstairs with three pins between her lips. She smelled of powder and roses, although the roses had not yet been put into her hair. Behind her walked a pale lady carrying a long box.

“You must be angels this afternoon,” Mother said. “Perfect little angels. No crumbs, no ink, no romping.”

“We never romp,” said Sun.

Moon was romping at that very moment, going round and round the table with one glove on and one glove off.

The pale lady opened the box, and inside lay Moon’s frock, puffed and folded like pink cream, and Sun’s new suit, blue and much-too-stiff-at-the-neck. There were white socks, shining shoes, and a sash which Moon immediately dragged through the water jug.

“Oh, really!” Mother cried. “I knew it. I absolutely knew it.”

She went away saying she had a thousand things to do, though Sun had never seen even a hundred things at once.

While Nurse cleaned the sash, the children were permitted to look into the dining room.

Sun entered first.

He stopped.

The room shone so brightly that for a moment he thought there must be snow in it. The great white cloth covered the table from end to end; the silver dishes lay upon it like little moons; tall glasses winked green and red; and every napkin stood up in a pointed shape, wearing a rose.

“Look!” Moon breathed. “They have gardens on their heads.”

In the centre was a round mirror with ferns at its edge. Upon the mirror floated china swans and tiny lamps. The swans could not truly be floating, because Nurse said mirrors were not water, but they looked cold and proud, and one had a silver beak bent down as if it were drinking the light.

There were dishes of almonds, green sweets, red sweets, sugared fruits, little cakes with silver balls, curled sandwiches, grapes shining like eyes, and oranges cut into baskets. There were pale jellies, dark jellies, creams with cherries on their caps, and a great fish lying asleep under a silver net.

But at the far end—Oh!

Sun caught Moon’s hand.

It was a house. A little pink house made of ice pudding.

Its roof was white and softly ridged. Sugar snow lay upon the chimneys. Four yellow windows looked out from the pink walls, and there was a green path leading to a white door. The handle of the door was a brown nut, no bigger than the end of Moon’s finger.

Moon stretched toward it.

“No,” Sun said quickly. “You mustn’t touch their door.”

“Who lives there?”

“Little people.”

“What little people?”

“The ice people, of course.”

He bent close, but not too close. Perhaps, if he waited, someone would come to one of the yellow windows. A very small lady in a white apron might peer out. A little boy might run down the green path. Inside there would be cold beds, cold spoons, cold fires that burned blue.

Moon breathed upon the roof and made one patch shine.

“You’re melting their snow!” Sun pulled her back.

He walked slowly round the table. Round and round he went, seeing each thing again from another side. The roses bowed to him. The spoons lay still and bright. The salt cellars were glass birds with silver heads. Everything was waiting. Everything knew that something wonderful would happen when the lamps were lit.

“Sun! Moon!” Nurse called. “Come away this instant!”

The dining room door closed, and the little house disappeared.

At six o’clock the bells began.

First the front bell, then Mother’s bell, then the pantry bell, then the front bell again. The house rang and rang as if invisible hands were pulling it in every direction.

The children, dressed and brushed and painfully clean, waited on the nursery sofa. Moon’s sash spread around her like a pink puddle. Sun’s collar pressed his chin whenever he swallowed.

Below, wheels crunched upon the gravel. Doors opened. Voices blossomed in the hall.

“Who is it?” Moon asked at every sound.

“How should I know?”

“That one is a queen.”

“No.”

“Yes. I heard her dress.”

At last Nurse took them down.

The drawing room was full of people who had changed themselves. The men were black and shining, with two coat-tails behind like beetle wings. The ladies were white, blue, purple, and gold. Their shoulders came out of their dresses, and jewels sat in their hair like drops of coloured rain.

Mother sailed toward them. She wore a green gown, and her face was almost Mother’s face, only brighter and farther away.

“Here are my precious babies!” she called.

The people turned.

“Oh, the darlings!”

“Which is which?”

“What enormous eyes!”

“Come and kiss me, sweet!”

Sun was passed from hand to hand. A lady with a long white feather kissed his forehead and left a damp moon there. A large gentleman asked whether he was going to marry his sister. Everyone laughed, though Sun had not said anything.

Moon enjoyed it. She spread her skirt, copied the ladies’ smiles and said, “How perfectly lovely!” to a man’s buttonhole.

Then a young man with yellow hair crouched before them.

“Would you like a puppy?” he asked.

Moon screamed, “Yes!”

“A white one?”

“Yes!”

“With a blue ribbon?”

“Yes, yes!”

The young man looked suddenly over his shoulder. A lady in silver had beckoned to him.

“I shall fetch it,” he said, and vanished among the dresses.

Moon watched the door.

“He has gone for it.”

“He hasn’t,” Sun said, though he wished he had.

Then Father appeared. His cheeks were red and he was laughing before he reached them.

“Hallo, my two comets! Showing everyone how beautiful we are?”

He lifted Moon high in the air. She kicked her shining shoes and cried, “Higher!” Mother said the guests were waiting and Nurse said bedtime, and Father kissed them both with a loud smack.

“Off you go, little stars. Dream of the music.”

The concert began after they were put to bed.

It came through the floor in soft waves. The piano ran very fast, then stopped, then ran again. A woman sang as if she were calling from a long way off. Sometimes everyone clapped, and once Father cried, “Bravo!” louder than all the rest.

Moon sat up.

“The puppy will be waiting.”

“There isn’t a puppy.”

“There is. He went to fetch it.”

“Nurse said we were not to move.”

Moon slipped out of bed.

Sun listened. Nurse was in the next room, breathing heavily. Downstairs the music had ended. There were voices, chairs scraping, and a great clatter of plates.

Moon put on her slippers.

“I’m only going to see if he came.”

Sun could not let her go alone.

They crept into the passage. The stair carpet felt rough beneath their feet, and the banisters were cold. Halfway down they saw the guests leaving the drawing room, moving in a bright stream toward the dining room.

The men offered their black arms. The ladies trailed behind them, glittering and whispering. At the door everyone stopped and cried out.

“Oh, exquisite!”

“What a table!”

“Too beautiful!”

Sun’s heart lifted. They had seen the house.

The guests moved round and round the table just as he had done. A lady bent over the swans. A man pointed at the little lamps. Someone laughed at the pink roof. They all understood.

Then they sat down.

Sun and Moon hid behind the curtains in the dark morning room. Through a crack they watched servants hurry past with trays. Warm smells rolled through the hall: soup, fish, roast meat, hot bread, sweet wine. Knives and forks chattered. Glasses rang. The guests laughed, and the laughter grew louder and looser, rising above the opening and shutting of doors.

Moon became tired and leaned against Sun.

“When will they bring the puppy?”

“Never.”

“You’re cross.”

“I’m not.”

They might have fallen asleep there, but Father found them.

He drew back the curtain and stared. For one second he looked like Father on ordinary mornings. Then his face opened into a tremendous smile.

“Stowaways!” he cried. “Little midnight thieves!”

“Sh!” said Sun.

Father laughed so hard he had to hold the wall. His collar was open, and his hair, usually smooth, stood up in two horns.

“Come along. Come and see the battlefield.”

“What is a battlefield?” Moon asked.

“Where the brave have eaten too much.”

He took one of their hands in each of his and marched them into the dining room.

At first Sun thought it was the wrong room.

The white cloth had become wrinkled and stained. Red wine lay in a dark pool beside an overturned glass. Napkins were crushed into balls. The roses had fallen from their pointed folds and were scattered among bones, peels, crumbs, nutshells, torn paper, and little greasy lakes.

The fish had been opened. Its silver net was dragged aside, and its head stared from a dish of cloudy sauce. The jellies leaned over, cut and trembling. One china swan lay on its side upon the mirror-lake. Wax had run down the tiny lamps like tears.

The shining spoons were dirty. The glasses did not wink now; they gaped.

And the house—

Sun could not breathe.

The roof was broken. One chimney had vanished, and a spoon had cut deep into the pink wall. The yellow windows had run into streaks. The white door hung crooked over the green path, which had melted into a puddle. Half the house had fallen inward, showing nothing inside but pink cream and crumbs.

Moon gave a happy cry.

“The handle!”

Before Sun could stop her, she reached down, pulled the brown nut from the broken door and popped it into her mouth.

“Delicious,” she said, chewing.

Father roared with laughter.

“That’s my girl! Now you, Sun. Have a bit of roof.”

He scooped some melting pudding onto a plate. It slid from the spoon with a soft, wet sound.

Sun stepped back.

“No.”

“Oh, come on. It’s splendid.”

“No!”

Moon licked sugar from her finger. “It’s only pudding.”

Only pudding.

Sun looked again at the table. In the morning everything had stood so straight and perfect. The roses had nodded. The birds had drunk from the bright lake. The ice people had been safe behind their yellow windows. And the guests had walked round admiring it. They had said beautiful, exquisite, too beautiful—and then they had done this.

Broken—broken. All of it spoiled.

His throat closed. Tears sprang hot into his eyes.

“It’s horrid,” he whispered.

Father’s smile remained, but it no longer seemed merry.

“What did you say?”

“Horrid!” Sun cried. “It’s all horrid! You’ve smashed everything. You ate their house!”

Moon stopped chewing.

Mother appeared in the doorway, holding up her green skirt. “What is this noise?”

“Your son,” said Father, “has decided to insult the supper.”

“I knew they should never have been brought down.” Mother pressed two fingers to her forehead. “I said so from the beginning.”

Sun pointed at the ruined roof, but the words tumbled and would not arrange themselves.

“It was lovely—and now—and they lived—”

“Enough,” Father said.

His voice was flat. The horns of hair no longer looked funny.

“But look!”

“I said enough. Go upstairs.”

Sun stood still.

“Upstairs, sir. At once.”

Moon slipped her hand into Father’s. She was watching Sun as if he had become strange.

No one looked at the house.

Sun turned and ran. Past the dark morning room, past the chairs against the wall, up the long staircase he ran, with his stiff collar cutting his neck and the damp mark of the feather lady’s kiss still cold upon his forehead.

Behind him Mother said, “Really, what an extraordinary child.”

Then the dining-room door shut.

Upstairs, the nursery was quiet and unchanged. The two little beds waited beneath their white covers. Moon’s discarded glove lay on the floor. The water in the jug shone in the moonlight.

Sun climbed into bed without taking off his shoes.

Far below, someone laughed. A servant dropped a tray. The piano gave one startled note.

Ping!

Sun buried his face in the pillow, but he could still see the broken windows, the fallen chimney, the little brown door without its handle, slipping slowly into the pink, pink lake.

## THE LAST VOYAGE

I have never been shipwrecked, frozen into the polar ice, speared by savages, roasted for supper, or compelled to eat my boots; and, if the truth must be spoken, I have generally found the crossing from Dover sufficiently heroic for my ambition. Yet there are hours when a man who has travelled no farther than prudence and a tolerable purse will permit may wander beyond Columbus, suffer with Franklin, escape with Bligh, and perish with nameless sailors upon rocks which no chart remembers.

Such an hour was the last hour of the year.

The wind had worried the house since dusk. It shook the casement, descended the chimney with a hollow cry, and now and then flung a handful of rain against the glass, as if some wet traveller demanded admittance. But the curtains were drawn, the lamp burned softly, and the fire—my ruddy, hospitable, English fire—built towers and caverns of its coals, populated them with shadows, destroyed them, and built them again. My chair stood within that charmed circle where the feet are warm, the conscience disposed to tenderness, and every hardship endured by another man becomes remarkably vivid.

Upon the table lay several books of voyages, open or half-closed, with maps protruding from them like tongues weary of telling marvels. I had been reading at random; and now, without the assistance of print, the old travellers appeared and vanished as they pleased.

Columbus stood for an instant against the crimson horizon of the grate, watching a weed drift by his vessel; Bruce strode through Abyssinian splendours, magnificent and doubted; Mungo Park, solitary beneath an African tree, listened to a poor woman singing of the white stranger whom no mother would pity; Franklin moved, already spectral, through the ice; and little groups of dusky islanders seemed to kneel upon coral sands beneath the Southern Cross.

Then the fire sank, and a baser shape arose.

There was once an escaped convict—I shall not dishonour the dead by giving him a name—who fled with several companions into a wilderness where hunger followed them more faithfully than any gaoler. They had carried provisions; they wasted them. They hoped for settlements; there were none. Day after day the forest shut them in, the sun stared down, and every living creature fled before their famished steps. At length one man disappeared; then another. Of those who remained, the strongest looked upon the weakest, not as upon a brother, but as upon food.

The convict survived.

He was taken, questioned, condemned, and afterward, through one of those strange turns by which justice sometimes loses hold of the very hand it has grasped, escaped again. Again he entered the bush; again men accompanied him; again he returned without them. When he was finally secured, there was found in his pocket a portion of salted pork.

Salted pork!—while human flesh had been cut, cooked, and consumed upon that dreadful journey.

I could imagine famine clouding the understanding until affection itself appeared through a red mist; I could imagine men casting lots with trembling hands and afterward praying beside the body of him whom the lot had condemned. God judge such extremity, not I! But the little lump of pork, preserved while a companion was murdered, remained in my mind like an accusing stone. Hunger had been pleaded; the pork denied the plea. It was not necessity that stood before me, hollow-eyed and staggering, but appetite selecting its morsel.

A coal fell upon the hearth, and the figure broke apart.

In its place came a ship under the palm-trees of Otaheite, her yards black against a sky so tender that mutiny beneath it seemed doubly wicked. I saw the *Bounty*; saw Fletcher Christian with anger, fear, and resolution contending in his face; saw Captain Bligh driven over the side into the launch with eighteen men and provisions miserably small. Bligh was a mariner of astonishing endurance, and a commander whose temper might have driven Job himself to thoughts of rebellion. This does not excuse mutiny; neither does mutiny convert tyranny into virtue.

Away went the crowded boat upon an immeasurable ocean. A few pieces of bread, a little water, some wine, a quadrant, a compass—these were the possessions of nineteen souls, exposed to sun, rain, thirst, and waves that might at any instant have written their brief history in foam. Yet they sailed more than three thousand miles, measuring life by spoonfuls, sleeping in wet garments, bailing when they should have slept, and looking daily upon death until his countenance grew familiar.

Then, beyond them, I saw Pitcairn: a high, lonely island rising from the Pacific, where the mutineers burned their ship and endeavoured to burn their past. But no man destroys his past with pitch and flame. It lives in the blood, sits at table, wakes at midnight, and teaches the children their father’s secret in the very silence maintained to conceal it. Violence followed violence; the guilty slew one another; and at last an old seaman remained among women and children, reading to them from the Bible, while the sea rolled round their narrow world.

How strange that the Book neglected in the crowded ship should become law upon the solitary island! How strange that men voyage across half the globe seeking liberty, only to discover that without duty liberty is another prison!

The gale outside strengthened. The windows trembled; and from the red hollows of the grate arose the tall stern of an East Indiaman, labouring under a press of weather near a black and iron coast.

I remembered the account because it had been written with that dreadful calm which belongs to official testimony. No poet had adorned it; no preacher had enforced its warning. Names, hours, bearings, distances, and positions of the wreck were set down; and through the exact words one heard the sea.

The ship, homeward bound, carried passengers, soldiers, women, children, and a rich cargo. Her captain had with him his two daughters, young ladies returning to England after long separation from home. On the evening before the disaster the wind blew hard from the southward; by midnight it increased to a violent gale. The vessel, unable to gain the desired offing, was driven toward the coast. Soundings were taken repeatedly. The leadsman called the decreasing depth; each cry was an announcement that the land, invisible in darkness, drew nearer.

At two in the morning breakers were heard.

Orders followed rapidly. The topmasts were struck; cables ranged; anchors let go. For a short time the ship rode, pitching heavily, while the seas swept her decks. Then one cable parted. The second held, dragged, caught again, and dragged once more. Guns were fired as signals of distress, but their flashes showed only rain and foam. At length a dark mass appeared under the lee—the cliff itself—and almost immediately the vessel struck.

She struck upon rocks beneath a precipice, her bow toward the land, her stern exposed to the whole fury of the sea.

According to the statement of a surviving officer, several men attempted to reach a cavern in the cliff by passing along the bowsprit and thence over broken rocks. Some succeeded. Others were torn away. The captain remained in the round-house with his daughters and certain passengers, believing that the after-part of the ship, which had not yet separated, offered greater security than the exposed bow.

Imagine that chamber: lamps swinging, furniture overturned, water entering at every seam, children crying, women clinging to one another, and beneath all the thunderous blows which shook the great vessel from stem to stern. The captain’s daughters sat beside him. He had commanded hundreds of men, crossed oceans, faced enemies and storms; but he could not command one wave to spare those two beloved heads.

A coxswain, who had gained the rock, called through the darkness that the ship must break. His voice was heard between the seas. Some endeavoured to leave the round-house. One gentleman descended toward the quarter-gallery, was struck by wreckage, and disappeared. A woman reached the deck, but, seeing the gulf between the stern and the rocks, turned back. The captain was urged to make the attempt.

He refused to abandon his daughters.

At about four o’clock the timbers amidships opened. Those in the cavern could distinguish, whenever the waves withdrew, the black form of the wreck bending. The poop rose strangely; the stern-gallery shuddered; the ensign-staff inclined toward the sea. Then came a wave greater than the rest.

There was one universal shriek.

The after-part of the ship vanished.

For a little while broken spars, chests, garments, and human forms were seen in the whitening water; then these also were gone, and nothing remained where the round-house had been but the surf beating against the cliff.

One universal shriek—and after it the wind, which had roared before they were born and would roar after their names were forgotten.

I looked toward my own door. It was closed; the house was safe; no salt water crept beneath it. Yet I thought of fathers who, at that very hour perhaps, sat among their children, impatient because one laughed too loudly, or another disturbed the newspaper. Oh, fortunate man, if you knew your fortune! The hand that pulls your sleeve may tomorrow be beyond the reach of kings.

The fire brightened suddenly and showed me, not the sea, but a wilderness—vast, stony, burnt by day and frozen by night. Across it moved the survivors of another wreck.

Their vessel had gone to pieces far from any settlement. Those who gained the shore possessed a small quantity of biscuit, a gun with little powder, two blankets, a kettle battered upon one side, and some fragments of sailcloth. They knew that human habitations lay somewhere beyond the wilderness. Where, none could say with certainty. Behind them was the sea; before them, mountain, forest, marsh, and desert. They chose the unknown road because the known shore offered only a slower death.

Among them was a child of seven years.

His father had been lost when the mast fell; his mother had been washed from a spar almost within reach of the boat. Thus, in one black hour, he became an orphan, and inherited no property in the world except the pity of exhausted men.

The steward took him first.

He was not a large man, nor one whom a painter would have selected for a hero. He had a narrow chest, a quiet voice, and hands accustomed to plates, bottles, account-books, and cabin linen. But he lifted the boy upon his shoulders, knotted a piece of sailcloth round him, and said, “We shall carry him in turns.”

No one disputed it. The child became their sacred charge.

During the first days, while strength remained, they made good progress. One man carried the kettle, another the gun, another the biscuit; two supported an injured passenger; and the steward bore the boy. At noon they gave him a double portion of water, though every eye followed the cup. At night they wrapped him in the driest blanket, though strong men lay shivering on the earth. When he cried for his mother, the steward told him she had gone before to prepare a home; and when he asked how far away that home might be, the steward answered, “Not far, if we are brave.”

Their route soon entered a forest where no track could be found. Thorny creepers tore their clothes; insects fastened upon their faces; rain collected in the hollows and turned the ground to mire. They crossed streams swollen by the storm, passing the child from arm to arm above the current. Once the man who received him slipped, and all uttered a cry—not for the man, who could swim, but for the little boy held high above the brown water.

Food diminished. The biscuit, green with wet, was divided crumb by crumb. They found berries, but feared poison; roots, but could not always soften them; shellfish near a river, but only enough to sharpen hunger. A bird was shot, and fourteen men watched while the steward plucked it. The flesh, scarcely larger when cooked than a man’s hand, was apportioned among them. The child’s share was the breast.

There were many who had wives; there were many who had children; there were many whose minds, weakened by famine, must have pictured tables at home—bread newly cut, meat steaming in its dish, apples, milk, the common plenty formerly received without gratitude. Yet not one snatched the orphan’s morsel.

Is courage only the firing of cannon? Is greatness only the planting of flags where no flag has flown? I tell you that when a starving sailor turned his eyes from that scrap of meat and watched a child eat it, a greater victory was won than many for which cities have illuminated their windows.

On the twelfth day the injured passenger could walk no farther. They made a shelter beneath a leaning rock and laid beside him a little water. He begged them to proceed.

They waited by him one day; they waited by him two days.

On the third morning he died. They covered his face, placed stones upon the body, and resumed their march, fewer in number and poorer by the water they had willingly spent. The child looked back until the grave disappeared among the trees.

Afterward fever entered the company. It touched one, then another. Men who had marched silently began to speak to persons not present; one called continually to a sister dead twenty years; another insisted that he could hear church bells. They supported them while support was possible. When it was not, they rested.

Thus the wilderness conquered them, not by one great assault, but by inches.

A beast followed their camp at night. They saw its eyes beyond the fire and heard it move among the leaves. Their powder was nearly gone. The strongest kept watch with clubs while the others slept; and always the boy lay in the centre, the steward’s arm beneath his head.

One evening they found oranges growing near an abandoned hut. There were seven fruits, small and bitter; but they shone among the leaves like lamps of paradise. The men laughed when they discovered them. Laughed!—those torn, bleeding, hollow-cheeked spectres, who had forgotten the sound of merriment. They carried the oranges to two sick women and to the child. The steward pierced one rind and squeezed the juice, drop after drop, into the boy’s mouth.

“Is it England?” the little fellow asked.

“Nearly,” said the steward.

Their shoes failed. Some wrapped their feet in bark, some in strips cut from the sailcloth, and some walked barefoot until every stone was marked with blood. The boy, seeing this, begged to walk too; but after a few steps he sank down. The steward lifted him again.

“You are tired,” said the child.

“No,” said the steward.

He was lying; and may that falsehood be entered to his credit in Heaven.

Snow met them in the hills. Their wet garments stiffened; the wind penetrated every covering. They burned roots, brushwood, a portion of the blanket, and at last the wooden stock of the gun. The fire gave little heat. Still the men crowded less closely about it that the child might have air and warmth.

One by one they died. One fell upon the path and did not rise. One slept beneath a tree and could not be awakened. One wandered during the night, following imaginary lights, and was found at dawn beside a frozen pool. The survivors ceased to speak of burial. They had not strength to dig, nor stones enough to cover the dead. They folded hands, whispered prayers, and went on.

At length only five remained: the steward, the child, two sailors, and a woman whose husband had died in the forest.

The woman was seized with fever. They carried her for half a day upon sailcloth, though every mile so gained consumed the strength which might have saved them. At sunset she opened her eyes and understood.

“Leave me,” she said.

They would not.

“Then leave me tomorrow.”

They waited through the night. In the morning there were four.

The country beyond the hills appeared more open, but no habitation greeted them. Their last food was gone. They boiled leather cut from a belt; they chewed candle-ends saved by the steward from the wreck—little pieces of wax once intended to illuminate a cabin table, now divided with the solemn exactness of sacramental bread.

The child could no longer sit upright. His face had become strangely old; only his eyes remained young.

That evening they made a fire in a hollow. The wind devoured it from one side while they fed it from the other. The sailors went to seek wood. The steward sat with the child wrapped against his breast.

“Will my mother know me?” the boy asked.

“Yes.”

“Though I am so dirty?”

“She will know you.”

“Will you come?”

“If God permits.”

The child considered this. Presently he said that he could see a light. The steward raised his head, hoping wildly for a house; but there was only the last flame trembling among the ashes.

“What light?”

“There—behind you.”

The steward turned. Darkness lay over the wilderness.

When the sailors returned, he was singing—not a seaman’s song, but some simple cradle hymn remembered from childhood. His voice was faint and frequently broken. The boy seemed asleep.

They put fresh branches on the fire. They warmed his hands. They called his name once, twice, many times.

And the child was dead.

The steward would not surrender the body that night. He held it until morning, smoothing the hair, covering the little feet, and speaking occasionally as if sleep alone separated them. When the sun rose, the sailors made a grave with sharpened sticks. The steward placed the child within it, together with the better portion of his own coat.

Three days afterward they reached a station.

Men ran out with food and blankets. The rescued sailors ate, wept, and asked that help be sent for companions who could no longer receive it. The steward drank a little broth. Then he turned his face toward the wall.

“He was not mine,” he said, “but I think his mother will thank me.”

Before sunset he was dead.

The clock upon my mantel began to strike the quarter before midnight.

The imagined wilderness withdrew; the drowned ship went down again; Pitcairn faded; the convict passed into darkness with his untasted pork. I sat alone before a failing fire, and all those outward voyages—the crossings of oceans, the traversing of deserts, the battling with hunger, tempest, mutiny, ice, and savage solitude—seemed suddenly no more than figures of that last journey which every child begins upon the day of birth.

Death is the exile from which no traveller returns to amend his preparations.

I imagined a dying man—not a murderer, not a tyrant, but one such as we meet daily; respected, occupied, decently kind, and always postponing goodness until a more convenient season. He lies in a familiar room. Medicines stand beside him; footsteps are softened on the stair; those who love him speak hopefully beyond the door. But he knows.

He is alone.

Then neglected duties gather round the bed. There were many. A letter unanswered; there were many. A reconciliation delayed because pride desired the other to speak first; there were many. Love received as a right and never returned with gratitude; there were many. Harsh words remembered by the wounded when the speaker had forgotten them; there were many. The poor sent empty from the door, the lonely left unvisited, the child dismissed impatiently, the old friend allowed to drift away—there were many, a million, a host more terrible than armies, because each bears his own face.

He would rise now; but the hand cannot lift itself from the coverlet. He would speak; but the tongue produces only a murmur. He would cross the city, cross the world, crawl upon bleeding knees to ask forgiveness; but the room has become wider than the wilderness, and the few feet between his bed and the door are farther than Bligh’s ocean.

The moment comes.

What would he give then for one common day—one grey morning, one rainy afternoon—such as he had squandered by thousands? What treasure would he not pay to call back the friend, to kiss the child, to confess the wrong, to perform the small kindness once dismissed as troublesome? But the ship has parted from the rock. The universal shriek may be silent in his throat; nevertheless, the stern has fallen away, and the shore recedes.

Outside, the old year stormed toward its death. Each year, I thought, is a wave. We see it rise, glitter, break, and vanish, and because the next shore appears unchanged we say that nothing has happened. But the waves do not pass without effect. They lift us. Quietly, irresistibly, they raise the sea upon which we shall at last be borne away.

The clock raised its warning.

Father of all mankind, teach us before that voyage! Let us not preserve the pork while our brother perishes; not burn the ship and hope to burn the sin; not sit secure in the round-house while love waits unspoken beside us. Give us rather the steward’s weary shoulders, his divided morsel, his merciful falsehood, his song beside the dying fire. For if we have fed one little one, carried one weak creature, forgiven one injury, or warmed one desolate heart, we shall not have travelled altogether in vain.

Midnight struck.

The old year was gone beyond recall.

I rose, stirred the embers into flame, and went to write a letter which should have been written long before.

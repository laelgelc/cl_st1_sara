# Nelly’s Little Hospital

## I. A New Kind of Charity

It was one of those June mornings when the whole world seems to have washed its face in dew, put on its greenest gown, and come singing to the windows to wake sleepy people. Roses peeped into the breakfast-room; robins held a concert in the elm; the wind wandered about with pockets full of clover-scent; and the sunshine laid a golden path across the carpet to the couch where Will lay looking pale and weary.

He had come home from the war with a wounded shoulder, a fever-worn face, and eyes that had seen more sorrow in one year than many see in a lifetime. The wound was nearly healed, the surgeon said; but courage heals less quickly than flesh, and Will, who had formerly been the merriest lad in the house, now often lay silent for hours, thinking of comrades left behind in crowded wards, or beneath the grass on distant fields.

Nelly sat near him picking lint. Her small fingers pulled the old linen into soft, snowy heaps, while her brown head bent so earnestly over the work that one would have thought the fate of the army depended upon her diligence.

“Will,” she said presently, “were the hospitals very dreadful?”

“Some were,” he answered. “But they would have been worse without the nurses, the Sanitary people, and all the kind women who sent bandages, broth, shirts, and comforts.”

“Did the soldiers like to be washed and fed and tucked up?”

“Most of them did. A sick man is glad enough to have some one care for him, even if he is six feet high and wears a sword.”

Nelly reflected upon this, drawing out a thread nearly as long as herself.

“Were there any animals there?”

“Plenty. Horses chiefly; poor fellows, many of them worse hurt than their masters. There were camp dogs too, and mules, and once a pet crow that belonged to the drummer-boy. But we had no proper hospital for them.”

“Why not?”

“I suppose every one was too busy with men.”

Nelly’s hands stopped, and the long thread hung from them like a fine white line of thought.

“Mamma,” she cried, “why shouldn’t there be hospitals for animals?”

Her mother, who was sewing beside the open window, looked up with the quiet smile that always encouraged a good purpose without promising to remove all its difficulties.

“There should be kindness for every suffering creature,” she answered. “But an animal hospital requires knowledge, patience, clean quarters, proper food, and a faithful nurse.”

“I could be faithful,” said Nelly quickly.

“Could you rise early, work when it was hot, handle unpleasant things, and give up play if your patients needed you?”

Nelly glanced toward the garden, where her hoop leaned invitingly against the porch and two little neighbors were already calling to one another beyond the hedge. Then she looked at Will’s pale face, at the lint in her lap, and at a blue-winged moth beating vainly against the upper pane.

“I think I could,” she said, less quickly, but more bravely.

Will smiled for the first time that morning.

“Here is Surgeon Nelly ready for duty. What regiment do you intend to mend?”

“All the hurt creatures I can find,” she answered. “Birds, bugs, butterflies, and—well, perhaps toads, if they are very sick. I shall have wards, and beds, and medicine, and a bell, and an amb’lance, just like the great hospitals.”

“Not in the house, my dear,” said mamma, who knew that Nelly’s charities were apt to overflow their appointed limits.

“The old summer-house!” cried Nelly. “Nobody uses it, and the woodbine makes it cool, and there are shelves for the bottles.”

“The roof leaks.”

“Tony can mend it.”

“The floor is dusty.”

“I can scrub it.”

“There are spiders.”

Nelly hesitated. She did not like spiders, especially when they descended unexpectedly by invisible threads.

“They may stay if they are not sick,” she said at last. “Perhaps they can help carry things.”

Will laughed outright, and the sound was so pleasant that mamma’s eyes brightened.

“You may try the plan for one month,” she said. “But remember, pity without wisdom sometimes does harm. You must learn before you doctor, and you must not imprison healthy creatures merely for the pleasure of nursing them.”

“I’ll only take the really afflicted ones,” promised Nelly. “Oh, mamma, thank you! Will, will you be my head surgeon?”

“I shall be consulting surgeon, since I cannot walk far yet.”

“And Tony shall be assistant and contraband,” added Nelly, whose military vocabulary was more abundant than exact.

At that moment Tony himself appeared beyond the window, pushing a watering-can before him through the grass. He was the gardener’s son, a stout, freckled lad of twelve, with honest gray eyes, rough hands, and a private acquaintance with every nest, burrow, pool, and berry-patch for half a mile around.

Nelly rushed out to tell him the news. Before she had finished, Tony set down the watering-can and whistled.

“That’s a queer notion,” he said. “You’ll have more patients than you bargain for. Things are forever getting pecked, stepped on, frozen, drowned, or eating what disagrees with ’em.”

“Then they need me very much.”

“Maybe they do. But don’t go putting caterpillars into feather beds, Miss Nelly. They’d rather have leaves.”

“That is just the sort of thing you must teach me. Will you help?”

Tony looked pleased, though he rubbed his nose and pretended to consider the matter like a man offered an important public office.

“I’ll help with the rough work. I don’t know about being a contraband, though.”

“You can be steward, keeper, and second surgeon.”

“That sounds better. When do we begin?”

“Now,” said Nelly.

Good purposes, like seeds, should be planted while the heart is warm; yet they must afterwards be watered by steady labor, or they will never bloom. Nelly’s hospital began that very hour.

## II. The Summer-House Ward

The old summer-house stood at the lower end of the garden, half hidden by lilacs and a mighty woodbine that had climbed over the roof, dropped green curtains across the doorway, and sent inquisitive tendrils through every broken pane. Once it had been a pleasant retreat, with rustic chairs, a painted table, and shelves for books or fruit; but damp had spotted the walls, ants had established several flourishing towns beneath the floor, and spiders had hung gray hammocks in every corner.

Nelly entered armed with a broom, Tony followed with hammer and nails, and for two lively hours there was such a sweeping, knocking, sneezing, scurrying, and splashing that the wrens in the lilac-bush sat with their heads on one side, wondering what new species of storm had fallen upon the neighborhood.

The spiders were carefully removed to a shed, where, as Tony observed, “they could catch flies without frightening the sick ones.” The ants could not be persuaded to leave, so their principal road was fenced off with bits of board. Tony patched the roof, fixed wire netting over the lower windows, and drove pegs into the wall for baskets. Nelly scrubbed the table until its faded roses appeared again, washed the shelves, and tied back the woodbine curtains with blue ribbon.

Then began the furnishing, which was the pleasantest part.

A row of small boxes became private rooms for delicate patients. A large basket lined with moss was the bird ward; a shallow pan, furnished with pebbles, water, and a floating leaf, was prepared for frogs and other damp persons; glass-covered tumblers, with holes pricked in paper tops, were set apart for insects; while an old foot-bath, half filled with sand, formed a sunny apartment for such reptiles as might require warmth and retirement.

There were acorn-cups for medicine, clam-shells for food, soft rags for bandages, match-boxes for beds, and tiny splints made from thin pieces of cedar. On the shelf stood bottles labeled WATER, MILK, HONEY, and WORM TEA—the last containing not worms, as its name seemed to announce, but a weak herb mixture which mamma declared might sometimes be useful and could seldom be dangerous.

Nelly also provided flaxseed, crumbs, sugar, meal, berries, seeds, bits of apple, lettuce leaves, and a saucer of earth, because Tony said certain patients preferred their dinner alive, others fresh and green, and still others buried.

“You cannot feed a bird, a beetle, and a turtle on the same bill of fare,” mamma told her. “Kindness asks, ‘What would please me?’ but wiser kindness asks, ‘What does this creature truly need?’”

So Nelly studied. Will lent her a book about birds, mamma found another concerning insects, and Tony supplied information which had never entered any printed volume. He knew where the swallows obtained mud, how the field mice hid grain, what leaves silkworms relished, and which beetles were harmless though fierce in appearance. Some of his opinions were less exact; but when they differed from the books, Nelly wrote both down in a little blank volume called the Case Book, and awaited further evidence.

At the top of the first page she printed:

**HOSPITAL FOR SICK AND WOUNDED ANIMALS.  
NELLY, SURGEON.  
TONY, STEWARD.  
WILL, CONSULTING OFFICER.  
NO HEALTHY CREATURES ADMITTED.  
KINDNESS TO ALL.**

“That last rule is easy enough with doves,” said Will when she read it aloud. “How will it be with hornets?”

“We should love our enemies,” replied Nelly seriously.

“At a respectful distance, I hope.”

“Yes,” she admitted; “and perhaps with a tumbler over them.”

Meanwhile Will had undertaken the ambulance. Nelly’s old toy cart was brought from the attic, where it had lost one wheel, half its paint, and all its dignity. Tony repaired the wheel; Will, using his sound arm and much advice from Nelly, fitted a canvas cover over bent hoops; mamma made a red-cross flag no larger than a handkerchief; and Nelly lined the bottom with cotton, moss, and two little cushions from her doll’s cradle.

When completed, the vehicle looked so important that even the cat walked around it twice and sniffed with respect.

“It is modeled on the wagons of the Sanitary Commission,” said Will.

“It is the nicest amb’lance in the country,” Nelly declared. “How softly it goes! A butterfly could ride in it without rumpling one feather.”

“Butterflies haven’t feathers,” said Tony.

“I mean wings, of course. Surgeons cannot always stop for little words.”

Will painted **U.S.A.H.** upon the side.

“What does that mean?” Nelly asked.

“United States Animal Hospital.”

She clapped her hands, then ran to fetch mamma, cook, the gardener, and every person who could properly admire the establishment. Even grave old Caesar, Will’s army dog, was invited to inspect it. He sniffed the cart, looked at Will, and wagged his tail as if memories of rough roads and groaning men had stirred beneath his grizzled coat.

“You shall come too, Caesar,” Nelly whispered, patting him. “You have been a soldier, so you will know how to behave on an expedition.”

Thus, one bright morning, the little procession set forth: Nelly in a broad hat with her Case Book and bandages in a basket; Tony drawing the ambulance; Caesar walking beside it with martial gravity; and the sun, wind, birds, and flowers all apparently interested in the first day’s work.

## III. The First Expedition

The lane beyond the garden was full of pleasant possibilities. Buttercups held up golden cups to catch the sunshine; daisies looked innocent and useful; ferns waved green plumes along the wall; and every tuft of grass seemed to conceal a sufferer waiting for rescue.

Nelly walked slowly, peering right and left.

“We must not wish them to be hurt,” she said; “but I hope we find one or two, or the hospital will be disappointed.”

Tony had secretly foreseen this difficulty. He knew that wounded creatures were plentiful when one did not want them, but singularly scarce when an ambulance came seeking them. Therefore he had risen early, made a round of the meadow, and arranged several discoveries with as much care as a general plans a campaign.

Their first patient, however, needed no contrivance.

A fly had become entangled in a dusty cobweb stretched between two rails. The spider was absent, but the fly struggled so violently that each effort bound another leg or wing in the sticky cords.

“Oh, poor prisoner!” cried Nelly.

“It’s only a fly,” said Tony, though he stopped.

“Only a fly can suffer.”

With a grass stem and the point of her scissors, Nelly broke the web, lifted the captive, and laid it upon a leaf. Dust and silken threads covered the little black body; one wing was bent, and two legs appeared hopelessly tied.

“I cannot carry so tiny a patient in the cart. He would be lost in the bedclothes.”

“Put him in this,” said Tony, offering a pill-box.

Nelly lined it with a fresh leaf, and the fly was admitted as Number One.

A little farther on they found a pale-green caterpillar lying curled beside the path.

“He looks very ill,” said Nelly.

“He’s been eating,” Tony replied. “Caterpillars generally look ill after they’ve been eating, because they generally are eating.”

But this one had a dark spot upon its side and moved feebly when touched. It was placed in a tumbler with several varieties of leaves, for no one knew its particular taste.

Next appeared an emerald beetle turned helplessly upon its back, waving six bright legs toward heaven. Nelly righted it, but it immediately rolled over again.

“Sun-stroke,” she decided. “The poor gentleman’s armor is too hot.”

“More likely something pecked him,” said Tony, pointing to a crack in the shining shell.

The beetle was set in a mossy box, where it lay with such a proud and melancholy air that Nelly named it General Green.

These small successes made her eyes shine; yet Tony feared the expedition was proceeding too slowly. He gave a peculiar whistle near a hazel-bush, and Caesar trotted forward, sniffed beneath it, and discovered a young bird.

Tony had found the nestling that morning after a storm. One wing drooped, but it was lively enough to open an enormous yellow mouth and demand food from the entire world.

Nelly knelt, full of pity.

“Perhaps its mother is near.”

They waited and watched from behind the wall. Presently a distressed sparrow darted from the hedge, chirping sharply, but dared not approach while Caesar remained.

“There she is!” cried Nelly. “We must not rob her.”

“The nest’s split and blown down,” said Tony. “We can mend it and tie it back, or take the young one home till the wing’s stronger.”

The broken nest lay under a thorn-tree, soaked and flattened. After grave consultation they placed it, nestling and all, in the ambulance, while the mother followed overhead, scolding them as kidnappers commonly deserve.

“Do not be frightened,” Nelly called to her. “We are the Sanitary Commission.”

The sparrow appeared unconvinced.

At the meadow brook, Tony discovered two field mice in a deep earthen jar which had been thrown among the rushes. They had evidently fallen in while hunting grain and could not climb the smooth sides. One crouched motionless; the other sprang again and again, only to slip back.

“Healthy creatures,” said Tony. “They don’t belong in the hospital.”

“But they may be exhausted. We shall feed them and let them go.”

He turned the jar carefully, but the mice were too weak or terrified to escape. So they were put in a covered basket with grass and grain.

A turtle came next, sitting in the mud with his head out and an expression of ancient disapproval. There was no visible wound.

“He’s sound as a stone,” Tony said.

“Perhaps he has rheumatism. He walks very slowly.”

“They always do.”

Nelly knew that healthy creatures must not be admitted; still, the turtle seemed so dignified and so well suited to be an interesting patient that she was reluctant to leave him.

At that instant Tony turned the creature gently, showing a small crack near the edge of the shell.

“There now. A cart may have run over him.”

This was true; though Tony did not mention that he had noticed the turtle the previous evening and saved it for the expedition. Nelly lifted it with both hands.

“You shall be Commodore Waddle, because you look like an old naval officer. He must ride in the large ward.”

The commodore was placed in the foot of the ambulance, where he promptly walked over General Green’s box, pushed aside the caterpillar tumbler, and tried to climb upon the bird’s nest.

“He is not very considerate for an invalid,” said Nelly.

“Sick folks ain’t always,” replied Tony.

They were turning homeward, well satisfied, when Caesar stopped beside a heap of stones and growled. Something moved there—a long, dark, glistening shape, twisting weakly in the dust.

“A snake!” cried Nelly, retreating so suddenly that she sat down among the buttercups.

Tony seized a stick.

“Don’t kill it!” she called, though she was pale with fear.

“It might bite.”

“Will it?”

Tony examined it from a cautious distance.

“Only a harmless black snake, I reckon. Its back’s hurt. Somebody’s struck it with a stone.”

The creature attempted to escape, but its injured body dragged behind it. Its bright eyes and darting tongue made Nelly shiver. She hated snakes—or thought she did—and all the nursery tales of wicked serpents, poisonous fangs, and dreadful coils came crowding into her mind.

“We might leave it,” she whispered.

“It’ll die,” said Tony.

This was the first severe trial of the hospital rule. Doves were soft, beetles were beautiful, and even mice possessed bright eyes and velvet ears; but the snake was cold, creeping, and terrible. Nelly wished some older person would decide for her. Then she remembered Will’s stories of nurses who had cared for rebel soldiers as tenderly as for their own blue-coated brothers.

“If kindness is only for things we like, it is not the best kind,” she said, chiefly to herself. “And if we must love our enemies, we ought at least to bandage them.”

Tony lowered his stick.

“How’ll you carry him?”

“In the long basket, with the cover fastened very tightly.”

Together they pushed the snake upon a strip of bark, lifted it into the basket, and tied the lid with cord. Nelly’s hands trembled, but she did not run away.

As the ambulance moved home, its passengers made a strange company: fly, caterpillar, beetle, sparrow, mice, turtle, and snake; some born to hunt the others, some clothed in shell or feather, some ugly, some beautiful, but all now helpless, and therefore united under one flag.

Many divisions disappear when suffering enters; for pain speaks a language understood in every country and by every living heart.

## IV. Busy Wards

The summer-house had never seen such activity.

The fly was cleaned with a camel’s-hair brush and a drop of water. Nelly freed the bound legs, spread the bent wing as well as she could, and left the patient upon a rose leaf with a grain of sugar beside him.

General Green received a moist moss bed and was shaded from the sun. The caterpillar chose a willow leaf after rejecting apple, grape, currant, and lettuce with the silent obstinacy of a spoiled child. Commodore Waddle was given the foot-bath ward, though he spent most of his time trying to escape over its edge.

The bird caused greater anxiety. Its wing was not broken, Tony thought, but bruised and strained. They placed it in a basket nest, fed it softened crumbs and tiny worms, and hung the basket where the mother sparrow could visit. She soon did so; first scolding, then watching, and finally darting in with a morsel for her hungry child.

Nelly was delighted.

“She understands! She knows it is a hospital.”

“She knows that bird wants its dinner,” said Tony. “That’s near enough.”

The mice ate greedily and revived, but one breathed with a faint clicking sound, and both were strangely tame. Mamma, when consulted, feared that cold and fright had harmed them more than appeared. They were lodged in a deep box covered with wire, supplied with cotton, grain, water, and a little wooden house.

Last came the snake.

Tony wanted to attend to it alone, but Nelly would not accept all the credit while escaping the disagreeable duty. She stood at the end of the table, with Caesar at her side and a blanket wrapped around her skirts, though no one could explain what protection the blanket offered.

The snake’s injury was serious. A heavy blow had crushed part of the spine, and the lower body was already cold and still. No bandage could mend such harm; nevertheless they washed the wound, laid the poor creature in warm sand, and placed a shallow dish of water near its head.

“It doesn’t look wicked now,” Nelly said softly.

“It never was wicked,” Tony replied. “It was only a snake.”

This plain remark remained in her mind. Human beings often give evil names to whatever they fear or do not understand, and then consider themselves excused from mercy. But the creature in the sand had not chosen its shape, its food, or its manner of moving; it had merely lived according to the law given it.

Nelly entered each case in her book, adding observations in large, wavering letters:

**No. 1. Fly. Rescued from the enemy’s lines. Wing sprained. Temper patient.**

**No. 2. Caterpillar. Complains of stomach or side. Eats willow.**

**No. 3. General Green, beetle. Armor cracked. Does not speak.**

**No. 4. Sparrow. Young and noisy. Mother attends.**

**Nos. 5 and 6. Field mice. Starved and frightened. Very pretty.**

**No. 7. Commodore Waddle. Shell wounded. Tries to desert.**

**No. 8. Black snake. Badly hurt. Not poisonous. I was afraid, but did it.**

When Will came down on his crutches that afternoon, the hospital was in full operation. Nelly hurried him from ward to ward, explaining symptoms, diets, and prospects with such solemnity that he listened as respectfully as if the patients had worn uniforms.

At General Green’s box he bowed.

“A gallant officer, evidently. Has he been under fire?”

“Under a hen, perhaps,” said Tony.

At the turtle ward, Will tapped the shell.

“Commodore, discipline must be maintained. No walking over the other patients.”

The commodore drew in his head, which Nelly interpreted as submission.

Then Will came to the snake. His amusement vanished when he heard how Nelly had conquered her fear.

“That was bravely done, little sister.”

“Not very bravely. I sat down first.”

“Soldiers sometimes fall back before they advance. The important matter is where they stand at last.”

She slipped her hand into his. Since coming home, Will had often spoken as if courage belonged wholly to the days before his wound; but now there was something of the old brightness in his face.

That night Nelly woke twice, once dreaming that the turtle had marched into the house carrying the mice upon his back, and again imagining that the snake was coiled under her pillow. Each time she considered calling mamma; each time she remembered that hospital nurses must not disturb the family for private alarms, tucked the blanket firmly around her, and went to sleep again.

At dawn she hurried barefoot to the summer-house.

The fly had vanished. For one dreadful moment she feared a spider had eaten him; then she found the pill-box open and saw a fly washing its face upon the window.

“Is it my patient?” she wondered.

He spread both wings, buzzed once around her head, and sailed into the garden.

Nelly chose to believe he had paused to thank her.

General Green also improved and was released beneath the rose-bush. The caterpillar ate for three days, then attached itself mysteriously to a twig and became so still that Nelly believed it dead.

“Don’t bury that one,” Tony warned. “It’s changing.”

“Into what?”

“Wait and see.”

Waiting is one of the medicines nature often prescribes, though impatient hearts dislike the dose. Nelly waited.

The sparrow soon fluttered from its basket to a low branch. Its mother led it away through the hedge, chirping encouragement. Commodore Waddle’s shell was cleaned and covered with a thin protecting plaster, which made him look as if he wore a white medal. After a week he was returned to the brook, where he entered the water without farewell and disappeared under a green roof of duckweed.

“He might have looked back,” Nelly said.

“Turtles don’t have much manner,” Tony consoled her.

Not all the cases ended happily.

The weaker mouse died on the second night, curled beside its companion in the cotton house. The other lived three days longer, refusing food at the last, though Nelly offered grain, milk, apple, and the smallest crumb of cake. When she found it cold one morning, she sat very still with the tiny body in her palm.

“I tried so hard,” she said.

Mamma, who had come with her, put an arm around her shoulders.

“Care cannot always save, dear. If it could, no mother would lose a child and no nurse a soldier. But care can warm, feed, comfort, and prevent loneliness. Those are not little things.”

The snake died also, quietly, in the warm sand. Nelly no longer feared it. She touched the smooth black head and felt sorrow that any creature should have suffered from idle cruelty.

Tony made three small coffins from cigar-box wood, and they buried the mice together under a fern, with the snake a little apart beside the stone wall. Nelly planted violets over the mice and laid a flat white pebble upon the snake’s grave.

“Why give him the best stone?” Tony asked.

“Because no one liked him,” she answered.

There was no grand funeral. Caesar attended, Will watched from the garden seat, and a thrush sang so sweetly in the apple-tree that the humble graves seemed honored enough. Nelly read a verse about God’s care for the sparrows; then she added, in her own words, “And I think He remembers mice and snakes too.”

Mamma did not correct her.

## V. The Hospital Family

After the first expedition, patients arrived without being sought. News of the institution spread among the children, and every day brought a basket, box, cap, apron, or pair of dirty hands containing some unfortunate creature.

A lame grasshopper came in upon a cabbage leaf. A bat, found hanging from a shutter in broad daylight, was admitted for observation and discharged at dusk with no illness except sleepiness. Three drowned bees were laid in the sun, where two revived and flew away, while the third received burial beneath the thyme. A toad with a scratched back spent a night in the damp ward and ate six flies, thereby proving himself convalescent. There was a white pigeon with a torn breast, a squirrel stunned by a fall, a half-starved kitten from the mill, and a butterfly whose wing had been caught by a thorn.

The butterfly gave rise to the prettiest contrivance in the hospital. Since it could not fly, Nelly made a bed of rose petals in a match-box and shaded it with a leaf. At first she proposed feeding it bread and milk; but her book instructed her to offer honey-water, which the patient sipped through its delicate curled tongue.

“How shall it take exercise?” she asked.

Tony suggested tying it to a string, but Nelly indignantly rejected such treatment. Will made a litter from a card and four threads; Tony caught two long-legged spiders and placed them before it as bearers. The spiders, being independent persons unacquainted with military discipline, immediately departed in opposite directions, overturning the invalid among the rose petals.

Will laughed until his shoulder hurt, and even Nelly was obliged to smile as she rescued the butterfly.

“Spiders are poor contrabands,” she said. “We shall carry it ourselves.”

The kitten became hospital cat and was named Mercy, though her conduct did not always justify so Christian a title. She slept in the sun, washed every patient who came within reach, and showed a professional interest in the bird ward which made wire covers indispensable. When reproved, she blinked with an air of injured innocence, as if she alone understood the proper treatment of sparrows.

The pigeon, however, became Caesar’s friend. The old dog lay outside the summer-house, while the bird walked about him, picking crumbs from between his paws and sometimes perching upon his back. Thus a soldier dog, a white dove, and a small brown nurse often kept watch together while the roses looked in at the window and the wind turned the pages of Nelly’s book.

Her days settled into a useful order. She rose early and visited the wards; she washed dishes, renewed leaves, supplied water, and recorded changes; she ate her breakfast, studied her lessons, and picked lint beside Will; she returned at noon to feed the birds and insects; she played in the afternoon unless some serious case required attendance; and before sunset she made a final round, tucking covers over baskets, closing wire doors, and wishing each patient good night.

There were failures, mistakes, and discouragements. She once gave a young rabbit too much clover and passed an anxious night because of it. She nearly drowned a beetle by supposing all insects liked baths. A family of worms escaped from their box and were discovered in mamma’s work-basket among the silk. Mercy ate an unnamed patient during Nelly’s absence, leaving only two feathers and an expression of pious meditation.

But each mistake taught caution. Mamma never laughed when Nelly was sincerely grieved; Tony never said “I told you so” more than once; and Will helped her make better cages, safer covers, and clearer entries in the Case Book.

The caterpillar, forgotten by all but Nelly, rewarded her patience. One morning the dull case split, and out came a soft, crumpled creature, which rested, slowly spread broad brown-and-golden wings, and presently flew to the roof.

“It was not dead at all!” cried Nelly. “It was making itself beautiful in the dark.”

Will watched the butterfly sail through the woodbine into sunlight.

“A pleasant sort of hospital discharge,” he said.

Nelly thought of the soldiers who did not recover, the mice beneath the fern, and the snake by the wall; then she thought of Will, pale but stronger each day.

“Perhaps many things are making themselves new where we cannot see,” she said.

Will looked at her, but did not answer. Some hopes are too sacred for easy words.

## VI. The Circle Widens

The hospital soon did more good outside its walls than within them.

Children who came merely to stare remained to help. Little Annie Page, formerly afraid of every beetle, learned to carry harmless ones upon a leaf. Ned Brown stopped robbing nests because Nelly showed him a mother bird searching for her young. Two sisters organized a branch ward in their father’s barn, where they cared for an aged hen, four orphaned ducklings, and a lamb with a sore foot.

Even rough Sam Pike and his companions, who prided themselves on throwing straight stones and setting cunning traps, were affected, though not at once.

Nelly found them one afternoon near the brook, with a frog tied by one leg to a stick.

“What are you doing?” she cried.

“Teaching him to march,” said Sam.

“He cannot march. He is hurt.”

“He’s only a frog.”

Nelly had heard those words before.

“Will says ‘only’ is often a cruel word. A fly is only a fly, and a frog is only a frog, and a poor boy may be only a poor boy; but each one feels what happens to him.”

The boys laughed, and Sam made the frog jump. Nelly’s cheeks grew red, yet she did not scold. Anger might have pleased them, while pity made them uncomfortable.

“Will saw men tormented in prison,” she continued. “He says a brave soldier never hurts a helpless enemy. There are five of you and one tied frog. That does not look brave.”

This argument struck home. Boys who could resist a sermon often surrendered to the fear of seeming cowardly.

“We weren’t hurting him much,” muttered Sam.

“Then it will cost nothing to let him go.”

Sam cut the string. The frog made one crooked leap, then another, and vanished into the rushes.

“He jumps queer,” said the smallest boy.

“Because his leg is injured. Bring him to the hospital if you can catch him gently.”

They could not find that frog, but the next day Sam appeared at the summer-house with a robin in his cap.

“Cat got it,” he explained. “I took it away. Thought maybe you could mend the wing.”

Nelly did not remind him of yesterday. She received the bird with such confidence in his improved character that he was obliged to deserve it. Sam held the patient while Tony set the wing; he came back to inquire after it; and before the summer ended he had broken two traps, defended a snake from stoning, and brought a bag of meal for the hospital mice.

Love makes friends not by praising what is wrong, but by discovering and encouraging what may become right.

The grown people smiled at Nelly’s establishment, but they, too, carried away a lesson. Farmer Page mended the sharp wire that cut his cattle. Mr. Brown ordered his hired man not to overload the old gray horse. A lady who had worn bright birds upon her bonnet looked troubled after visiting the sparrow ward and chose ribbons thereafter.

One evening a group of women met at Nelly’s house to pack a box for a military hospital. There were shirts, slippers, towels, preserves, books, bandages, lint, and little bags holding needles, thread, and buttons. Nelly’s summer-house was discussed, and some one called it “a pretty child’s play.”

Will, sitting near the window, spoke up.

“It began in play, but it has done work. That child has relieved more suffering this month than many who pity the world all their lives and never lift a finger.”

The ladies looked toward Nelly, who sat sorting rolls of bandage, unaware that she was being praised.

Will continued more slowly: “A hospital for beetles may seem a small affair when men are dying by thousands. Yet the spirit is the same. Whoever learns to care for the least helpless thing is preparing to serve the greatest. We need lint and broth, certainly; but we also need hearts that do not grow hard from hearing too much sorrow.”

One of the women had lost a son in battle. She wiped her eyes and placed a little Bible in the soldiers’ box.

“Let the children go on with their charities,” she said. “The world will need them.”

That evening Nelly carried an armful of fresh lint to the summer-house and placed a little packet beneath the flag.

“This is not for the animals,” she told the pigeon, the kitten, and a convalescent chipmunk. “It is for Will’s soldiers. But you have helped make him cheerful while I picked it.”

The white pigeon cooed, Mercy purred, and the chipmunk stuffed both cheeks with corn. It was not a formal acknowledgment, yet Nelly accepted it.

## VII. Will’s Cure

All summer the hospital prospered, but its most important patient was never entered in the Case Book.

Will had returned from the army restless, discouraged, and secretly ashamed of his weakness. His wound pained him; he started at sudden noises; he dreamed of battlefields and awoke calling names no one in the household knew. Worst of all, he felt useless. Other men were still serving, while he lay beneath roses and listened to bees.

At first Nelly’s enterprise merely amused him. Then it interested him; afterward it employed him; and at last it taught him.

He made cages, read natural histories, painted labels, and constructed ingenious splints. He advised Nelly when a patient must be freed and when protection was necessary. Children came to him with questions, and he discovered that his experience, patience, and knowledge still possessed value.

More than this, the small sufferers shamed him out of self-pity without making him ashamed of honest pain.

A bird with its breast torn open endured the dressing in silence and sang three days later. A squirrel with a broken paw learned to climb by another way. The turtle carried its cracked shell without complaint. Even the snake, dying from a cruel wound, had struck at no one.

“They bear a good deal,” Will said one morning.

“They cannot tell us how it hurts,” answered Nelly.

“Perhaps that is why they do not complain.”

“You do not complain very much.”

“I have done enough of it in my thoughts.”

Nelly looked troubled.

“Thoughts do not trouble other people unless they make your face cloudy. Yours was very cloudy when you came.”

“Was it?”

“Yes. But now it is nearly sunny.”

Will laughed and took up his crutch. That day he walked as far as the brook. A week later he went to the village. By August he rode on horseback; and though he returned pale and tired, he had begun to hold himself like a soldier again.

Mamma watched the change with grateful eyes. She knew that time, rest, and wholesome air had helped his body; but Nelly’s little charity had reached the hidden wound. In caring for weak things, Will ceased to despise his own weakness. In teaching courage, he recovered courage. In sharing useful work, however humble, he regained his place in the living world.

Near the end of summer a letter came from his regiment. There was an opening for him in a lighter duty, and if the surgeon approved, he wished to return.

Mamma grew pale as she read, but she did not say no. Love that guards too closely may become another kind of prison.

Nelly listened silently. The war had seemed like a dreadful country into which Will had once disappeared and from which he had returned hurt. She wanted to hide his boots, burn the letter, and tell the surgeon that his shoulder ached every day. Instead she asked, “Do you think you ought to go?”

“I think I can still be useful.”

“Then I suppose you must.”

Her voice trembled, and Will drew her close.

“I shall not forget what Surgeon Nelly has taught me.”

“I have not taught you anything.”

“Oh, yes. You taught me that a cracked shell need not prevent a fellow from walking; that a bruised wing may mend if one tries it gently; that enemies deserve mercy; and that being of use is a very good medicine.”

Nelly smiled through tears.

“You may be consulting surgeon from the army. I’ll send reports.”

“Do. Especially concerning Commodore Waddle, if he reenlists.”

The days before Will’s departure passed swiftly. Uniforms were brushed, shirts marked, letters written, and a strong little traveling-case packed. Nelly prepared lint with redoubled industry, as if every soft thread might guard him. Tony said little, but polished Will’s boots and brought Caesar’s old collar from the attic.

Caesar was too old to return to service. He seemed to understand this, for he followed Will from room to room and lay with his nose upon the packed trunk.

On the final evening Will asked Nelly and Tony to meet him at the summer-house.

They found the door closed.

“What has happened?” cried Nelly. “Is there a new patient?”

“One,” said Will, “but not the sort you expect.”

He opened the door.

Over the entrance hung a new flag, bright and neatly painted. Upon it were the letters:

**THE MERCY HOSPITAL  
FOR ALL SMALL CREATURES**

Inside, the shelves had been repaired and painted white. New wire covered the windows; a cupboard stood beneath the table; the Case Book had a red leather cover; and above the principal ward hung a framed picture.

Nelly stared, then burst into laughter.

Will had drawn the hospital staff in grand procession. Nelly marched first, wearing an enormous surgeon’s apron and carrying a spoon like a sword. Tony followed with the ambulance, in which Commodore Waddle sat upright beneath the flag. Caesar wore a nurse’s cap; Mercy carried a bandage in her mouth; two mice peeped from a basket; General Green rode upon the back of the snake; the butterfly reclined upon a litter borne, at last successfully, by four obedient spiders; and behind them came frogs, birds, beetles, rabbits, bats, and caterpillars enough to fill a regiment.

“Oh, Will, it is beautiful!” Nelly cried.

“It’s mighty like us,” said Tony, delighted. “Only I never had ears as big as that.”

“Those are signs of wisdom,” Will assured him.

Beneath the picture, printed in dark letters, was a motto. Nelly read it aloud:

> “He prayeth best, who loveth best  
> All things both great and small;  
> For the dear God who loveth us,  
> He made and loveth all.”

The summer-house grew quiet. Outside, the sun was drawing rosy curtains across the west; swallows flew homeward through the golden air; the brook repeated its small evening prayer among the stones; and every leaf upon the woodbine seemed to listen.

“That is the rule we were trying to write,” Nelly said at last.

“Yes,” answered Will. “Your hospital has been preaching it all summer.”

“I did not preach.”

“You did better. You practiced.”

Tony examined the motto with unusual gravity.

“Does it mean we’ve got to like mosquitoes?”

Will’s eyes twinkled.

“It means we must remember that even mosquitoes have their place; but I see no command to let them bite us.”

“That’s a comfort,” said Tony.

They made the evening round together. The pigeon’s wound was healed, and it would be released in the morning. The chipmunk was ready for the woods. A small green frog sat in the damp ward; two orphaned wrens slept under a fold of flannel; the hospital kitten curled beside Caesar; and upon the highest shelf stood the empty chrysalis from which the butterfly had flown.

At each ward Will saluted and offered some last piece of advice.

“To the pigeon: use your liberty better than men commonly do. To the chipmunk: save honestly, but do not hoard all the corn. To the frog: avoid boys with strings. To Mercy: love your patients, but do not eat them. To Caesar: guard the surgeon. To Tony: keep the roof tight. And to Nelly—”

He paused.

“To Nelly,” she prompted softly.

“Keep your tender heart, but train your head and hands to serve it. Feeling sorry is a beginning; knowing what to do is better; doing it faithfully is best of all.”

Nelly promised.

The next morning Will went away. There were tears, but no despair. His face was cheerful, his step firm, and beneath his coat he carried a little packet of Nelly’s lint. Caesar watched the carriage disappear, then returned slowly to the summer-house and lay beneath the flag.

Nelly stood at the gate until the dust settled and the road was empty. The garden seemed changed; the roses had lost a little color, and the wind no longer sang. But sorrow did not send her to hide. From the hospital came the sharp cry of a hungry wren.

“Duty calls,” said mamma gently.

Nelly wiped her eyes and ran down the path.

The young birds needed feeding; the frog’s water required changing; Mercy had overturned the meal; Tony was waiting with a basket containing a lame cricket; and on the table lay fresh linen to be picked for soldiers whose names she would never know.

So the little surgeon went to work.

Autumn came with scarlet leaves and silver mornings. Many patients departed for woods, fields, ponds, eaves, and burrows; others did not recover, but none died uncared for. Will’s letters arrived regularly, full of cheerful counsel and inquiries about the wards. He wrote that he, too, had found a hospital to serve, and that whenever pain or weariness tempted him to be gloomy, he remembered Commodore Waddle and carried his cracked shell a little more bravely.

The neighborhood children continued their work. Traps disappeared, stones were less often thrown, nests were guarded, and tired horses sometimes found water waiting by the roadside. Not every cruel habit vanished, nor did every child become a saint; but many hands grew gentler because one small girl had first stretched hers toward a struggling fly.

And the old summer-house, once dusty and useless, remained a pleasant place where compassion learned to be industrious. Roses peered through its windows; birds sang over its roof; the wind brought news from meadow and wood; and the flag stirred above the door as if saluting every humble patient carried in.

There Nelly discovered what wise people sometimes forget: that no good work is too small when love directs it; that courage may tremble and still be courage; that the strange, ugly, troublesome, and feared have claims upon mercy as surely as the beautiful; and that a heart trained to tenderness toward the least of God’s creatures will not easily grow hard toward any human sorrow.

For pity is a lovely flower, but service is its fruit; and he who would truly love all things, both great and small, must not merely sigh over suffering, but stoop, learn, labor, and help.

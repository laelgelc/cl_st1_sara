### The Education of Three Bears

When the only woman Richard Herrick had ever loved married a gentleman who manufactured collapsible perambulators, Herrick did not shoot the gentleman, enter a monastery, or marry the woman’s best friend. The last course would have been the usual one. Statistics, if properly encouraged, will prove that a rejected man marries either his beloved’s most intimate companion or somebody whom he meets in a railway accident. But Herrick had no humor, and therefore no respect for statistics.

He retired instead to the country.

This was possible because he had seven million dollars, a gentle disposition, and no profession. Poverty may drive a disappointed lover into poetry, journalism, or the manufacture of collapsible perambulators; wealth permits him to suffer scientifically. Herrick bought four thousand acres of mountain, meadow, woodland, and water in northern New York, and announced that he intended to devote the remainder of his life to Nature.

His estate was called Briarcliff, although there were no briars near the house and the nearest cliff belonged to another county. Here he constructed a model farm, a model dairy, a model poultry-yard, and several gardens representing different stages of civilization. There was an Italian garden for dignity, an English garden for sentiment, a Dutch garden for order, and a wild garden for Nature, in which eleven men were employed to prevent anything natural from happening.

Beyond these lay the game preserve, Herrick’s holy of holies. It comprised deep woods, rocky ravines, mysterious caves, tiny gurgling streams, wild laurel, silver birches, solemn hemlocks, and a lake so clear that visiting young women invariably compared it to a jewel and visiting young men invariably fell into it. A fence twelve feet high surrounded the preserve. Herrick regretted the fence, because it suggested restraint, but his superintendent pointed out that without it Nature would wander into the turnip fields and be shot by the neighbors.

Herrick loved every creature upon the place. He loved the deer, although they ate his roses; the raccoons, although they entered his corncribs; and the foxes, although they made his model poultry-yard look like a diagram illustrating military defeat. Once, after a porcupine had gnawed the handles from six expensive garden tools, Herrick ordered salt placed in the woods for it.

“The animal is seeking nourishment,” he said.

“The animal is seeking handles,” replied Kelly.

Kelly was a frequent visitor at Briarcliff. He had a cynical and combative disposition and had been born with the conviction that every noble sentiment concealed either indigestion or a land speculation. He and Herrick had been at college together. Friendship between men of opposite temperaments is common, for each keeps the other supplied with material.

Jackson, another college friend, had a frivolous disposition. He invented facts because established facts seemed to him insufficiently entertaining. When Herrick spoke of the brotherhood of life, Jackson asked whether membership included dues. When Herrick declared that the call of the wild sounded in every living heart, Jackson said that in his heart it generally sounded like a dinner gong.

These two men preserved Herrick from becoming a prophet. They could not cure him, but they kept down the symptoms.

It was during one of Herrick’s infrequent returns to New York that the affair of the bears began.

Jackson had persuaded him to attend a music hall. Herrick objected to music halls on the ground that their air contained patchouli, cigarette smoke, steam heat, and moral carelessness in almost equal proportions. Jackson replied that moral carelessness was the only one of the four ingredients which did not cause headaches.

Kelly met them in the lobby.

“What are we to see?” Herrick asked suspiciously.

“Life,” said Jackson.

“Rot!” said Kelly. “We’re to see twelve turns, nine of them bad, two imported, and one prohibited in Massachusetts.”

They entered while a young woman in silver scales was singing about a fisher-lad whom she had met, loved, and abandoned between sunset and the second chorus. After her came an acrobat, a ventriloquist, six melancholy girls dressed as champagne bottles, and a comedian who struck his mother-in-law with a plaster ham. Herrick endured these manifestations of civilization with gloomy patience.

Then the curtain rose upon Madame Zichy and her Imperial Russian Bears.

Madame Zichy was neither a madame, a Zichy, nor Russian. Her real name was Hannah Bloom, she had been born in Paterson, New Jersey, and she spoke to her animals in a language composed of German endearments, Polish threats, English commands, and pure personal invention. She was a stout, capable woman dressed in green velvet, with yellow boots and a feathered cap. She carried a silver whistle, a short whip, and the expression of one who had no intention of being contradicted by man or beast.

Behind her shuffled three brown bears.

The first, Bruno, was enormous. He wore a red military coat with brass buttons, a small cap fastened under his chin, and an expression of grave civic importance. Walking upright, he resembled a prosperous gentleman at a seaside resort who had mislaid both his waistcoat and his waist-line.

Clara, his mate, was smaller and lighter. She wore a blue skirt, a wreath of artificial roses, and white cotton gloves through which her claws projected with disconcerting candor. She moved with the prim precision of a dancing teacher who has long despaired of her pupils but remains civil for financial reasons.

The cub, Ikey, wore trousers, one suspender, a derby hat, and no coat. His face was sharpened by perpetual curiosity. Even before he did anything, the audience began to laugh, which is the first and rarest gift of the natural-born comedian.

At a blast from Madame Zichy’s whistle, Bruno mounted a wooden chair and balanced a ball upon his nose. Clara turned a somersault. Ikey attempted to imitate her, rolled sideways into Bruno’s chair, and received a cuff which knocked off his derby. He sat down, picked up the hat, examined the dent, and looked reproachfully at the orchestra.

The audience applauded.

Ikey bowed.

“He thought of that himself,” Jackson said.

“He did not,” said Herrick.

“He did,” said Kelly. “That bear has timing.”

Next Bruno and Clara rode bicycles around the stage. Bruno pedaled with massive solemnity; Clara rang her bell whenever she overtook him. Ikey followed upon a scooter, pushed too hard, crossed the footlights, and was caught by the conductor. Before being returned to the stage he snatched the conductor’s baton and led the orchestra through eight bars of a march.

The applause became thunderous.

Herrick sat rigid.

“Remarkable,” Jackson said. “I have known conductors with less authority.”

“It is degrading,” Herrick answered.

“For the conductor, perhaps.”

“For the animals.”

Madame Zichy now arranged the two elder bears in the center of the stage. The orchestra began a waltz. Bruno put one paw about Clara’s waist, Clara rested one gloved paw upon his shoulder, and together they revolved with astonishing grace. Their costumes, their serious faces, and their heavy bodies produced an effect at once ridiculous and touching. The audience laughed, but it also leaned forward.

“They dance better than the people at your house parties,” Kelly observed.

“They have been beaten into it,” said Herrick.

Madame Zichy did not strike them. Indeed, she stood to one side smiling, while Bruno guided Clara through a turn of considerable delicacy and Clara finished with a curtsy.

Then came the banquet scene.

A table was brought on, set with three plates, three napkins, and three brown bottles. Bruno sat at the head. Clara sat opposite him. Ikey climbed into a high chair, tucked his napkin under his chin, and rapped upon the table with a spoon.

Madame Zichy filled three glasses. Bruno drank his in one swallow and extended the glass. Clara sipped delicately. Ikey seized his bottle, hugged it to his chest, and turned his back.

The audience shouted encouragement.

Madame Zichy approached. Ikey hid the bottle under the table. She went around the table; he went around the other way. She blew her whistle. He placed the bottle upon his head and pretended not to know where it was. At last he drank from it, wiped his mouth with Clara’s skirt, and descended from the chair.

He then became intoxicated.

His knees weakened. He attempted to embrace Bruno and was repelled. He addressed the orchestra with one paw raised, quarreled with a chair, apologized to it, put his derby on backward, and walked with immense caution toward a door that was not there. Finally he saw two imaginary policemen, uttered a squeal, and crawled under the table, taking the cloth, dishes, bottles, and Bruno’s dignity with him.

The house shook with laughter.

Herrick rose.

“I have seen enough.”

“You haven’t,” Jackson told him. “The bears now imitate members of Congress.”

“Come away.”

In the restaurant afterward, Herrick refused supper and ordered mineral water, which is the manner in which wealthy men punish waiters for the sins of society.

“I cannot understand,” he said, “how intelligent people can find pleasure in such an exhibition.”

“Intelligent people weren’t finding pleasure in it,” said Kelly. “We were.”

“Those poor brutes!”

“The largest poor brute weighs six hundred pounds and drinks lager by the quart.”

“They are unwilling slaves.”

Jackson buttered a roll.

“You think Bruno dreams of the forest?”

“I know it.”

“What sort of forest?”

“A forest untouched by man. Great trees, open glades, caves among the rocks, running water—”

“Central heating?”

“Don’t be an ass.”

“Then no central heating. I merely wished to make the dream complete.”

Herrick leaned forward with that expression of grave enthusiasm which caused business agents to open notebooks.

“You saw the way they looked toward the wings.”

“They looked toward Madame Zichy,” Kelly said. “She had the sugar.”

“They looked beyond her. They heard the call of their own world. Can you imagine what it must mean to a wild creature to be imprisoned under electric lights, covered with grotesque clothing, compelled to mimic the vices of men, and surrounded by laughter?”

“Ikey likes the laughter,” said Jackson.

“No animal likes ridicule.”

“He doesn’t consider it ridicule. He considers it applause.”

“Rot!” Herrick exclaimed, borrowing Kelly’s favorite weapon and handling it badly. “That cub is taught to drink, taught to stagger, taught to make a clown of himself. If he were placed in natural surroundings, every debasing trick would fall from him.”

Kelly set down his glass.

“Why don’t you buy them?”

Herrick looked at him.

“Buy them?”

“Yes. You have the forest. You have the caves. You have running water, laurel, privacy, and eleven gardeners to keep the wilderness wild. Buy the bears, turn them loose, and watch the debasing tricks fall.”

Jackson’s eyes brightened.

“A beautiful experiment. Science, philanthropy, and vaudeville combined.”

Kelly had meant the suggestion as a blow. Herrick, having no humor, received it as illumination.

“I could do it,” he said slowly.

“Of course you could,” Kelly replied, now alarmed.

“The preserve is fenced.”

“It will need to be.”

“There are caves above the north lake. Beechnuts, berries, roots, fish—everything bears require.”

“Except an orchestra,” Jackson said.

Herrick was silent for half a minute. When he spoke, the scheme had passed through hope, theory, and moral obligation and had become destiny.

“I shall see Madame Zichy tomorrow.”

Kelly stared at him.

“You can’t be serious.”

“I have never been more serious.”

“That,” said Jackson, “is what makes Richard so dangerous.”

Madame Zichy received Herrick the next morning in a dressing room containing three trunks, seven wigs, a gas stove, a canary, and Bruno, who was eating a bun.

She heard Herrick’s proposal without surprise. Show people are accustomed to proposals which would astonish bankers, bishops, and the police.

“You want to buy the act?” she asked.

“The animals.”

“Without me?”

“Certainly.”

“Without the bicycles?”

“Most certainly.”

“Without the table, chairs, military coat, skirt, hats, bottles, scooter, ball, and banquet service?”

“Without any apparatus associated with their captivity.”

Madame Zichy considered him.

“What do you want them for?”

“To set them free.”

“In the street?”

“On my estate. Four thousand acres.”

“Fence?”

“Twelve feet.”

“Good fence?”

“The best.”

“Then they are not free.”

“They will be free within natural limits.”

“Ah,” said Madame Zichy. “Like married people.”

She turned to Bruno.

“Would you like to be free, old man?”

Bruno held out his paw for another bun.

“You see,” Herrick said softly. “He begs.”

“He always begs before lunch.”

“It is pitiful.”

“It is profitable.”

She named a price. Herrick did not bargain. People who bargain may desire an object, but only those who pay the first price can feel that they have rescued it.

Before the agreement was completed, Madame Zichy insisted upon explaining the bears’ habits.

“Bruno is quiet unless somebody takes his food. Clara is jealous when Bruno dances with anybody else. Don’t let him. Ikey gets into things. Lock up your beer.”

“There will be no beer.”

“Then lock up somebody else’s. He’ll find it.”

“He will return to the diet intended by Nature.”

“Nature never had Ikey boarding with her.”

“You trained him to drink.”

“I trained him to drink from the glass. The rest he invented.”

“The staggering?”

“All his.”

“The chair?”

“All his.”

“The imaginary policemen?”

“He used to stop after the chair. One night a policeman came behind the scenes about a license. Ikey watched him. Next show we had two policemen.”

Herrick smiled sadly. To him this proved only how innocence imitates corruption when deprived of better models.

“Within a week,” he said, “he will have forgotten it.”

Madame Zichy looked at Ikey, who had entered, opened a trunk, and put on a blond wig.

“Maybe,” she said. “But if he starts a matinee, send me a ticket.”

The news of Herrick’s purchase reached Briarcliff before the bears did. Country news travels by telephone, servants, tradesmen, and moral disapproval, and has no need of newspapers.

The superintendent threatened resignation.

The head gardener demanded wire protection for the Italian garden.

The dairyman said bears upset cows.

The poultry manager said everything upset hens.

The chef announced that he would climb a tree at the first alarm. When informed that bears could climb trees, he turned pale, reflected, and said he could not climb one after all because of the oven.

Herrick listened patiently.

“These are domesticated animals,” he explained. “They have gentle dispositions.”

“Then why put ’em in the preserve?” asked the superintendent.

“To restore them to Nature.”

“If they’re gentle, Nature won’t do ’em any good.”

Kelly and Jackson arrived for the liberation, bringing with them Miss Waring, Mrs. Fenwick, a lawyer named Price, and two young men who had heard there would be danger. Both young men wore new boots and intended to stand close enough to the danger to describe it afterward.

Miss Waring was the best friend of the lady who had married the perambulator manufacturer. Statistics had not yet completed their work upon Herrick, but they had established communication. She was quiet, observant, and sympathetic, and she understood that a man who cannot laugh at himself must occasionally be laughed at very gently by somebody else.

“You approve?” Herrick asked her as the company waited near the preserve gate.

“I approve of your kindness.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is safer than approving of your judgment.”

Jackson applauded.

“There speaks civilization—encouraging the motive, insuring against the result.”

Herrick’s arrangements for the release were elaborate. The bears would arrive in three wheeled cages. These would be backed against an opening in the preserve fence. Ropes attached to the cage doors would pass over pulleys to a protected station forty yards away. At Herrick’s signal the doors would rise simultaneously, and Bruno, Clara, and Ikey would step from bondage into the ancestral wilderness.

At least this was the plan.

In practice, one cage arrived backward, one pulley stuck, and Ikey removed the rope from the inside of his door and pulled three yards of it into the cage.

“He thinks it’s a game,” said Jackson.

“He has been trained to think everything is a game,” Herrick replied bitterly.

“An enviable education.”

Madame Zichy had come to say farewell. She wore no velvet and no feathers, but a plain traveling dress. Without the stage lights she appeared older, smaller, and less formidable. Clara pressed her nose against the bars when she approached. Bruno made a low sound in his throat. Ikey reached out and took her glove.

For a moment Herrick felt an uncomfortable suspicion that affection, even when associated with bicycles and brown bottles, might not be entirely artificial.

Madame Zichy recovered her glove.

“No foolishness,” she told Ikey. “You are going to be a wild bear.”

Ikey put the glove on his head.

The spectators withdrew behind a barricade of logs which Herrick had ordered built for people who did not believe the bears dangerous. Kelly climbed onto the roof of a tool shed. The two young men in new boots discovered an interest in the distant lake.

Miss Waring remained beside Herrick.

“You had better go back,” he said.

“Are you going?”

“No.”

“Then I won’t.”

Herrick’s expression softened. Statistics advanced another inch.

The keepers repaired the rope, straightened the cages, and fled. Madame Zichy stood by the wagon.

“Come behind the barricade,” Herrick called.

“They won’t hurt me.”

“They are wild animals.”

“Yesterday they were nature’s children.”

“They are both.”

“Like married people,” Jackson called from safety.

Herrick raised his hand.

The three doors lifted.

Nothing happened.

Beyond the cages, the preserve lay green, shadowed, and inviting. Sunlight came through the beeches in long shafts. A stream flashed over stones. Laurel crowded the slope, and high above it a dark cleft in the rocks promised shelter. The air smelled of earth, water, pine, and the wholesome decay by which Nature prepares nearly everything for something else.

Bruno sat in his cage.

Clara scratched her ear.

Ikey pulled at the lifted door.

“They do not understand,” Herrick whispered.

“They’re waiting for the music,” Kelly said.

Madame Zichy walked forward.

“Go on, Bruno.”

The great bear turned his head.

“Go on, Clara.”

Clara dropped to all fours.

“Ikey!”

Ikey released the door and looked at her.

Madame Zichy clapped her hands sharply.

“March!”

Bruno stepped down from the cage.

He paused upon the forest floor. His nose rose. He drew in the strange air. Clara came beside him, hesitant at first, then eager. Together they moved toward the stream.

Ikey remained behind.

The cub looked at Madame Zichy. He looked at the bears. He looked at the audience behind the barricade. Then he jumped from the cage, ran three steps, turned a somersault, and waited.

No one applauded.

“Don’t!” Herrick said fiercely.

They remained silent.

Ikey waited another moment. His small eyes traveled from face to face. Then Clara called from the edge of the trees. He dropped to all fours and raced after her.

The three bears entered the wood.

Bruno plunged into the stream and struck at the water. Clara tore at a rotten log, scattering bark. Ikey climbed a young birch, which bent almost to the ground under his weight and flung him into a patch of ferns. He emerged, shook himself, and galloped in circles.

A strange excitement seized the onlookers. Even Kelly descended from the shed. Madame Zichy shaded her eyes with one hand.

Bruno rose against a tree and ripped away a strip of bark. Clara thrust her muzzle deep among the roots. Ikey found a cluster of wild berries and ate them so rapidly that he swallowed several leaves.

Then, as if some signal had passed among them, they moved uphill. Brown backs showed between the laurels, vanished, appeared again near the rocks, and were lost in the deep shade of the hemlocks.

Herrick took off his hat.

No one laughed.

“They have gone home,” he said.

Madame Zichy nodded.

“Yes.”

There were tears in her eyes, though she denied them later and said a cinder had blown from the wagon.

Jackson cleared his throat.

“A touching triumph,” he said. “Though Bruno might have thanked the management.”

Kelly put his hands in his pockets.

“Wait until dinner.”

But his tone lacked conviction.

Herrick spent the afternoon patrolling the preserve fence with the superintendent. They found no attempt at escape. Near the north lake they saw Bruno’s tracks in the mud, claw marks upon a beech, and the ruins of a rotten stump. At sunset one of the keepers reported that all three bears had been seen near the rocky caves.

“They were digging,” he said.

“For roots?” Herrick asked.

“Might’ve been.”

“Of course they were.”

“Might’ve been trying to get to China.”

Herrick returned to the house radiant.

At dinner he described each piece of evidence in the manner of an explorer announcing the discovery of a continent. The guests ate upon the long terrace, which overlooked the lawn and the darkening valley. Beyond the last fields the preserve rose like a black island beneath the stars.

“They reverted instantly,” Herrick said. “The moment they entered the forest, inherited instinct asserted itself.”

“Ikey did a somersault,” Jackson reminded him.

“A last reflex of servitude.”

“He waited for applause.”

“And did not receive it. That was the decisive moment. When he heard Clara call, the artificial life fell away.”

Kelly carved a peach with unnecessary violence.

“You saw a bear eat berries. Don’t build a religion on it.”

“It was not merely what they did. It was the manner in which they did it.”

“Bears have been eating berries for some time.”

“Not these bears.”

“You don’t know that.”

“Madame Zichy said they had been in captivity since cubhood.”

“Then they’re no more fitted for the wilderness than Jackson is.”

“I am beautifully fitted,” Jackson said. “I carry matches.”

Herrick ignored them. His triumph had made him expansive.

“Training touches only the surface. Beneath it lies the essential creature, waiting. Civilization says, ‘Dance’; Nature says, ‘Live.’ Civilization puts a cap on Bruno, a skirt on Clara, trousers on Ikey. Nature strips away these grotesque distinctions and says simply, ‘Be bears.’”

“Nature,” said Kelly, “also says, ‘Eat the nearest thing.’ I hope your nearest thing isn’t the chef.”

The chef, serving coffee, dropped a spoon.

“There is no danger,” Herrick assured him.

“I know, sir. But I remember the oven.”

After dinner the guests entered the drawing room. Miss Waring played the piano. She played well, which means that those who wished to listen could listen and those who wished to talk could talk without committing a social offense. Jackson and Mrs. Fenwick played cards. Kelly read a sporting paper and contradicted it aloud. Herrick stood near the open windows, watching the moon rise above the preserve.

Miss Waring joined him when she had finished.

“You are happy tonight,” she said.

“I am.”

“I am glad.”

“Kelly thinks I am a fool.”

“Kelly thinks everybody a fool. It saves classification.”

“And Jackson thinks the whole affair a joke.”

“Jackson thinks everything a joke because he is afraid something may not be.”

Herrick looked at her with surprise. Miss Waring had a way of saying the exact thing in a voice which made it sound accidental.

“And what do you think?”

She considered the moonlit lawn.

“I think you wished to give happiness to three creatures who could not explain what happiness meant to them.”

“But surely freedom—”

“Is one kind of happiness.”

“The greatest kind.”

“For you, perhaps.”

“For every living thing.”

Miss Waring smiled.

“You are very certain about every living thing.”

“Some truths are universal.”

“Food?”

“Yes.”

“Safety?”

“Yes.”

“Companionship?”

“Certainly.”

“Habit?”

Herrick hesitated.

“Habit is not a truth. It is often a chain.”

“Chains may become familiar.”

“That does not make them desirable.”

“No. But taking them away does not always make one grateful.”

He knew she was no longer speaking entirely of bears. A man who has loved unhappily suspects all conversations of doing the same.

“You mean that people may prefer what has hurt them?”

“I mean that people—and perhaps bears—do not arrange themselves according to our theories. It is inconsiderate, but general.”

Herrick looked toward the woods.

“They were happy today.”

“Yes.”

“You saw them.”

“Yes.”

“They will become wholly natural.”

“Perhaps.”

The word annoyed him. A philanthropist can endure opposition better than reservation. Opposition gives him something to conquer; reservation merely waits.

From the drawing room came the opening measures of a waltz. Jackson had seated himself at the piano and was playing with more enthusiasm than accuracy. Mrs. Fenwick protested. Kelly demanded something with a tune.

The music floated out through the windows and crossed the terrace. It moved over the formal gardens, the white road, and the broad lawn, becoming softer as it traveled toward the dark preserve.

Herrick listened.

“That tune,” he said suddenly.

Miss Waring recognized it.

“It was the bears’ waltz.”

Jackson, who had an excellent memory for anything likely to cause trouble, played the melody again.

Herrick stepped toward the window.

“Stop that!”

Jackson looked over his shoulder.

“Why?”

“It is associated with their captivity.”

“Then we will rehabilitate it.”

“Play something else.”

Jackson changed to a march. Unfortunately, it was the march to which Bruno had balanced the ball upon his nose.

“Something else!”

Jackson played the fisher-lad song.

“Is that natural enough?” he called.

Before Herrick could reply, there came from the lawn a scream.

It was the scream of a woman who has unexpectedly encountered either death or a mouse, the vocal distinction between these emergencies being small. The blood of everyone who heard it turned to water. Chairs fell backward. Kelly dropped his paper. The chef, in the pantry, started for a tree, remembered both bears and oven, and crawled under a table.

A maid rushed through the open doors.

“The bears!”

Mrs. Fenwick screamed because the maid had screamed. The two young men in new boots seized the fireplace irons. Jackson stopped playing.

Herrick ran to the terrace.

Moonlight flooded the stone balustrade, the steps, and the lawn beyond. For one instant nothing moved. Then a dark shape rose beside a marble urn.

It was Bruno.

He stood upright, immense and solemn, his fur silvered at the edges by the moon. Clara came from behind him. Ikey appeared last, carrying something in both paws.

“Don’t move,” Herrick commanded.

No one had intended to.

The bears advanced toward the lighted windows.

“They came back,” Kelly whispered.

“They heard the music,” Miss Waring said.

Herrick descended one step.

“Bruno!”

The bear stopped.

In that grave, expectant pause, Herrick’s faith made its final effort. The animals had returned, certainly, but perhaps from affection. Perhaps freedom, rightly understood, included the right to revisit one’s benefactor. Perhaps they had come to establish some nobler relation between man and beast, a relation unspoiled by domination, applause, costumes, brown bottles—

Jackson’s fingers, resting nervously on the piano keys, struck three notes of the waltz.

Bruno moved to Clara.

Clara rose upon her hind legs.

Bruno placed one forepaw about her waist. She rested hers upon his shoulder.

“Don’t play!” Herrick cried.

But it was too late. Jackson, fascinated, completed the phrase. Bruno and Clara began to dance.

They revolved upon the moonlit terrace with the same earnest grace they had displayed beneath the electric lights. Bruno guided. Clara followed. At the turn she inclined her head with professional delicacy. They needed no military coat, no blue skirt, no artificial roses. The forest had stripped away their costumes, but it had not stripped away the waltz.

Kelly made a sound like a man choking and praying not to recover.

Herrick stood motionless.

Ikey had meanwhile climbed onto a terrace chair. The object in his paws proved to be a crystal decanter stolen from the drawing-room table. He pulled out the stopper with his teeth.

Herrick sprang forward.

“Ikey!”

The cub hugged the decanter to his chest and turned his back.

Jackson sat down abruptly at the piano.

“No,” Herrick said. “No one is to encourage him.”

Ikey looked over his shoulder. Every face was fixed upon him.

Slowly, with exquisite consciousness of his audience, he lifted the decanter and drank.

Then he wiped his mouth upon the back of the chair.

His knees weakened.

He attempted to embrace Bruno and was repelled without interruption to the dance. He addressed the moon with one paw raised. He quarreled with the chair, struck it, became ashamed, and apologized. He put the empty decanter upon his head like a derby, took three cautious steps toward a door that was not there, and stopped.

Across his furry face passed sudden horror.

He had seen the imaginary policemen.

With a squeal, Ikey dropped to all fours and crawled under the terrace table, taking with him the cloth, the coffee cups, a vase of roses, and the last remains of Richard Herrick’s theory.

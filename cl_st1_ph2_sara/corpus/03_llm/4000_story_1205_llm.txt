The rain had ceased toward noon, but the country kept the strange brightness which sometimes follows a long wetting in April. The trunks of the maples were black as if newly charred; every young leaf showed a green so luminous that the branches seemed hung with bits of colored glass; and the unpainted cottages, their boards sodden and dark, stood out against the pale sky with a vivid and unnatural distinctness. Water dripped from the lilac bushes and ticked upon the flat stones before the doors. Along the road the wheel-ruts were full to the brim, and here and there a last year’s leaf sailed solemnly in a yellow puddle.

In the Tollets’ kitchen the damp had entered in spite of the fire. Lucy Tollet had set Alfred’s boots upon the hearth, and steam arose from them with a smell of wet leather. She had spread his coat over two chairs, carefully turning the sleeves, that both sides might dry alike. The kitchen was small and exceedingly neat. A square table, scoured to a whiteness which no wood had any natural right to possess, stood under the west window; six blue plates were ranged above the mantel; and a red geranium, lately repotted, leaned toward the glass as if it too suffered from confinement.

Alfred sat beside the stove with his feet wrapped in an old gray shawl.

“I don’t want no more tea,” he said.

Lucy, who had already taken the teapot in her hand, set it down softly.

“I thought mebbe a little hot tea’d warm ye, Alferd.”

“I be warm enough.”

His face contradicted him. It was thin at the best of times, with a pinched authority about the mouth; now his skin had a yellow-gray look, and the hollows under his eyes were dark. He shivered so that the chair gave a faint answering rattle against the floor.

“Ye hadn’t ought to set in them wet clo’es all the way from Barlow’s,” Lucy ventured.

“I’ve got ’em off, hain’t I?”

“Yes, Alferd.”

“Then what’s the use talkin’?”

Lucy smoothed the front of her calico dress. She was a small woman, so slight that her head appeared almost too large for her shoulders, though this appearance came partly from the smooth bands of brown hair drawn down over her ears. Her eyes were mild and anxious, and had acquired, during eighteen years of matrimony, the habit of seeking Alfred’s face before they decided what to see. He was the fixed star of her modest firmament, the magistrate of her conscience, the sole autocrat of all her little Russias.

She stood a moment, listening to his breathing.

“I guess I’ll warm the soapstone fur the bed,” she said.

“I ain’t goin’ to bed at four o’clock.”

“No, Alferd. I only thought—”

“Don’t think so much. It tires other folks.”

Lucy took the soapstone from behind the stove nevertheless, wrapped it in a strip of flannel, and placed it where it would heat gradually. Alfred saw her do it, but offered no further objection. His eyelids drooped. The rain began again, lightly at first, brushing the window with soft finger-tips.

By evening he was in bed, and by morning he had a fever.

The village doctor lived six miles away and had gone to attend a woman beyond the mountain. Lucy sent Ezra Pike’s boy after him, but the boy returned at dusk with mud to his knees and the news that the doctor could not come before the next afternoon. Meanwhile Alfred refused boneset tea, onion syrup, hot mustard for his feet, and every other remedy which Lucy tremblingly proposed.

“I don’t believe in dosin’,” he declared. “Folks kills theirselves with medicine, then lays it to Providence.”

“But ye’re reel sick, Alferd.”

“I know how sick I be.”

Lucy believed this as she believed almost everything Alfred said concerning himself, the weather, politics, Scripture, potatoes, taxes, or the proper depth at which beans should be planted. Yet the sight of his face upon the pillow weakened her faith in his judgment. He lay with his eyes shut and his hands outside the coverlet, the knuckles showing sharp and pale. At intervals a chill shook him, though the feather-bed was piled with quilts.

Toward noon Lucy put on her hood and ran across the wet lots to her sister Hannah’s.

Hannah Bell lived in a low brown house behind the village store. She was a widow of twelve years’ standing, tall, angular and capable. She owned three cows, loaned money upon occasion, read the county paper from first column to last, and entertained no doubts which had not already been settled in her own favor. When Lucy entered, Hannah was cutting carpet-rags at the kitchen table.

“For the land sake!” she cried. “What be you out in this slop fur? Set down. You look like a pullet that’s been drug through a brook.”

“Alferd’s sick.”

“Wa’n’t he sick yisterday?”

“He’s wuss. He shakes, an’ then he burns, an’ he won’t take nothin’. Doctor Lane can’t git thar till to-morrer.”

Hannah snipped through a breadth of faded green wool.

“Give him gentian.”

“He won’t take it.”

“Don’t tell him.”

Lucy stared at her.

Hannah laid down the shears. “Put it in his victuals.”

“Why, Hannah!”

“What?”

“That’d be deceivin’ him.”

“It’d be curin’ him. I put gentian in Bell’s cider every spring fur ten year, an’ he never knowed it. Said my cider had more snap’n anybody’s in town.”

Lucy looked toward the window. Beads of rain clung to the small panes, turning the store-shed and its stacked barrels into wavering shapes.

“I couldn’t,” she whispered.

“Then let Alferd shake his teeth loose. Some men hev got to be managed like balky horses. They don’t need to see the hand on the rein.”

“But ef he should find it out—”

“He won’t, unless you tell him. An’ you’d tell the meetin’-house clock it was slow ef you’d sot it back yourself to save a funeral.”

Lucy’s eyes filled.

“Oh, don’t commence cryin’,” said Hannah. Her voice was rough, but she rose and opened the cupboard. “Here. I’ve got a paper of it. Stew a mite—not enough to taste strong—an’ put it in his broth. Gentian’s good fur fever, good fur stomach, good fur most everything but a weak conscience.”

She folded a spoonful of the dried root in brown paper and thrust it into Lucy’s hand.

Lucy carried it home under her shawl as if it were stolen silver.

Alfred was asleep when she entered. The kitchen had grown cold, and the fire was nearly out. She laid the parcel upon the table, then instantly covered it with her work-basket. After mending the fire, she went to the bedroom door and stood looking at her husband.

Illness had stripped him of his authority without making him less dear. Indeed, his weakness drew from her a devotion almost terrible in its intensity. The thoughts of Lucy Tollet had never ranged far; they followed Alfred like sheep, crowding after him through every gap of the day. If he coughed, they coughed in sympathy; if he frowned, they huddled together in fear; if he praised the biscuits, they fed upon that praise for a week. Now, seeing him helpless, she felt both the agony of a subject whose king is fallen and the secret strength of one who alone can lift him.

She returned to the kitchen and opened the paper.

The gentian consisted of dry yellow-brown pieces, innocent-looking and woody. She put three fragments into a tin cup, poured boiling water over them, and set the cup behind the teakettle. Presently a bitter smell arose, thin but penetrating. It seemed to Lucy that the whole house must smell it, that Alfred must wake and call from the bedroom, “What be you a-doin’?”

He did wake.

“Lucy!”

She caught the cup so suddenly that some of the liquid spilled upon the stove and hissed.

“I’m comin’, Alferd!”

“What smells?”

“Nothin’—I mean, I scorched a mite of—of broth.”

“Don’t give me no scorched broth.”

“No, Alferd.”

She strained the gentian through a clean rag. Her hands shook. Into Alfred’s chicken broth she put one spoonful, then, fearing it too much, added water; fearing the water would weaken the broth, she added chicken; and by the time she carried the bowl into the bedroom it held a compound so elaborately balanced that Hannah herself could not have improved upon its discretion.

Alfred opened his eyes.

“I don’t want it.”

“Jest taste, Alferd. I took sech pains.”

He turned his head away.

Lucy stood with the bowl between her hands. Steam warmed her face. Had he commanded her to empty it, she would have obeyed and perhaps felt relieved; but he merely lay silent, and his silence left a little space in which her own will, such as it was, might move.

“Ef ye don’t take somethin’,” she pleaded, “ye’ll git weaker. Jest three swallers. Fur me, Alferd.”

He turned back with an irritable sigh.

“Set it here.”

She fed him slowly. At the third spoonful he frowned.

“Got a queer taste.”

“It’s the chicken,” said Lucy, and the lie, once spoken, seemed to stand in the room like another person.

Alfred ate half the broth.

During the next two days she gave him gentian in tea, gravy, porridge and a custard which she made with the last of the winter eggs. She used very little, but in her conscience each yellow drop increased and multiplied. The doctor came at last, prescribed rest, warmth and a tonic, which Alfred refused, and said the illness must take its course.

The illness took its course with unusual docility. Alfred’s fever abated. In a week he sat by the kitchen window; in two he walked as far as the wood-pile; and before the orchard bloomed he had begun to complain of the crooked rows in Lucy’s early peas.

“Ye’re yourself ag’in,” she said, trying to smile.

“I’d hev been myself sooner ef folks hadn’t pestered me with slops.”

Lucy turned toward the sink.

Her happiness at his recovery was poisoned by the means of it. She had saved him—perhaps—but she had deceived him certainly. Hannah laughed when consulted.

“For the land sake, don’t make a hangin’ matter out of a teaspoonful of bitters.”

“He said the chicken tasted queer.”

“Well, it did.”

“I told him ’twas the chicken.”

“Then you told him near enough. It was in the chicken, wa’n’t it?”

“Hannah, a thing ain’t true jest because ye can hitch it to another thing.”

“A thing ain’t wicked jest because you can cry over it, neither.”

But Lucy could not be comforted. The gentian spread backward and forward through her days. When Alfred thanked her once for a particularly light biscuit, she felt she accepted gratitude under false pretenses. When he remarked that a man ought to be able to trust his own household, in reference to Ezra Pike’s hired hand stealing cider, her needle stopped midway through a seam. At meeting, words such as deceit, secret and confession detached themselves from the minister’s discourse and flew at her like blackbirds.

Spring passed into summer. The damp green country grew dusty; daisies whitened the mowing fields, and the Tollets’ bean-poles stood in straight ranks under Alfred’s restored supervision. Lucy churned, baked, weeded, sewed and suffered. At night she lay beside Alfred and listened to his breathing. Sometimes she formed the words: “Alferd, I put gentian in your victuals.” But his breathing was so peaceful that she could not disturb it.

In August, while she was preserving blackberries, her burden became too heavy.

Alfred came in from the barn and washed at the sink. The hot kitchen smelled of berries and sugar; purple jars stood upon the table, and Lucy’s thin face was flushed from the stove.

“Ain’t dinner ready?” he asked.

“Yes, Alferd. It’s kep’ warm.”

“Then set it on.”

She brought ham, boiled potatoes and cucumbers dressed with vinegar. Alfred sat down. Lucy remained standing behind her chair.

“Ain’t you goin’ to eat?”

“I—I’ve got somethin’ to tell ye.”

“Tell it arter dinner.”

“I can’t wait no longer.”

He put down his fork.

“Well?”

Lucy twisted a corner of her apron.

“When ye was sick—last spring—an’ ye wouldn’t take the medicine—Hannah, she said—though I ain’t layin’ it onto Hannah, fur I done it—”

“Done what?”

“I put jest a mite of gentian in your broth.”

Alfred looked at her without speaking.

“An’ in your tea,” she added, because truth, having once escaped, dragged every lesser truth behind it. “An’ the porridge. An’ once in the custard. Oh, Alferd, ’twa’n’t much! Hannah said ’twould help, an’ ye was so sick, an’ I was scairt ye’d die.”

“You put stuff in my victuals unbeknownst to me?”

“Yes—but—”

“After I’d said I wouldn’t take it?”

“It wa’n’t the doctor’s medicine. ’Twas only gentian.”

“What difference does that make?”

Lucy’s lips trembled.

“I couldn’t see ye shake so. I thought—I thought the Lord wouldn’t count it sech a lie ef ’twas to make ye well. But I’ve felt awful. I’ve wanted to tell ye every day.”

Alfred pushed back his chair. His face, still lean from illness, had become rigid.

“So that’s the kind of woman I’ve been livin’ with.”

“Oh, Alferd!”

“A man don’t know what he’s eatin’ in his own house.”

“I never put nothin’ else in, not ever.”

“How do I know?”

The question struck her more cruelly than an accusation. She caught the table edge.

“Ye know because I say so.”

“You said ’twas the chicken when I tasted it.”

Lucy began to cry.

Alfred rose. He lifted his plate, carried it to the sink and emptied the untouched dinner into the swill-pail. He did this deliberately, without looking at her.

“I won’t eat no more of your cookin’,” he said.

“For the land sake, Alferd, don’t say that! I’ve cooked fur ye eighteen year.”

“Eighteen year don’t prove nothin’ about what you done last spring.”

“What’ll ye eat?”

“I’ll cook fur myself.”

“Men-folks can’t live cookin’ fur theirselves.”

“I guess men-folks can live without bein’ dosed.”

He went into the shed and returned with a small iron spider which had belonged to his mother. He scrubbed it with sand, though it was already clean, and set it upon the stove. Then he cut two slices of ham and fried them.

Lucy watched from beside the table, her tears falling silently upon her apron.

“Can I git ye a plate?” she asked.

“No.”

“A knife, then?”

“I know where knives be.”

He ate the ham from the spider with a fork.

Thus began Alfred’s domestic independence. The next morning he walked to the village store and bought a brown teapot, a loaf of baker’s bread, a wedge of cheese and two salt mackerel. Mr. Pottle, the storekeeper, wrapped each article with impatience, for Alfred was particular about the string.

“What you settin’ up, Tollet—a grocery?” he asked.

“My affairs is my affairs,” said Alfred.

“They be till they git into Pottle’s store. Then they generally turn into everybody’s.”

Before night the village knew that Alfred Tollet had taken to cooking for himself because Lucy had poisoned him. By the second day the poison had become “doctor’s stuff,” and by the third it was reported at the north end that Lucy had administered a white powder during a quarrel. Hannah went into the store and corrected the public account with such vigor that Mr. Pottle threatened to sell her no more molasses.

“Poison!” she cried. “’Twas gentian. I give it to her myself. Ef gentian was poison, half the old men in this town would be planted under stones.”

Mr. Pottle tied up a pound of rice.

“I only tell what folks says.”

“Then charge folks fur it. It’s all they git here worth carryin’ away.”

At home Alfred established his provisions upon the upper pantry shelf. Lucy’s cakes and pies remained below, untouched by him. Every morning he made tea in the brown pot, cut baker’s bread, and fried salt pork or mackerel in the spider. The smell of his independent meals filled the kitchen with a harsh, greasy smoke. He would not allow Lucy to wash his cup. After eating, he scalded it himself and set it upside down upon a separate towel.

At first Lucy tried entreaty.

“I won’t never do it ag’in, Alferd.”

He said nothing.

“I’d take an oath to it.”

“A woman that’ll deceive once can deceive twice.”

“But I told ye.”

“Arter four months.”

“I was afraid.”

“You’re generally afraid enough before you do things.”

Then she attempted service in secret. She left kindling split beside the stove, filled the kettle, put a fresh towel beneath his cup. Alfred removed the towel and split his own wood. His stubbornness, once merely the bony framework of his character, had become its entire visible body.

The summer waned. Goldenrod appeared along the fences; dust whitened the burdock leaves; the nights turned cool. Lucy moved about the kitchen like a little penitent ghost, preparing meals which Alfred would not touch and eating scarcely enough to sustain herself. The separation of their food divided every hour. A marriage may survive many great wounds while bleeding to death through the daily cut of an unused plate.

Hannah came often and spoke her mind.

“Alferd Tollet, you’re makin’ a fool of yourself.”

“I didn’t ask your opinion.”

“You got it without askin’. Most useful things come that way.”

“She put it up to Lucy to dose me.”

“I did, an’ I’d do it ag’in. You was sick an’ contrary.”

“I’ve got my own notions.”

“You’ve got enough of ’em to furnish a town hall.”

Lucy would pull at Hannah’s sleeve.

“Don’t, Hannah. You make him feel wuss.”

“Him! What about you? You’re gittin’ thin as a broom-straw.”

“I’m well enough.”

“You’re a natural-born doormat, Lucy, an’ Alferd wipes his feet according.”

Alfred’s face darkened. “Ef you can’t speak decent in my house, you can leave it.”

Hannah stood up.

“I can leave it. The Lord give me legs an’ twelve years’ practice usin’ ’em without askin’ a man which way.”

She tied her bonnet under her long chin. At the door she turned to Lucy.

“An’ you can come with me whenever you’ve gathered sense enough to fill a thimble.”

Lucy shook her head, horrified. Leaving Alfred was a thought more impossible than leaving her own skin.

Yet it was Alfred who brought the thought into possibility.

One October evening he came home with flour and a new rolling-pin. He had decided, he said, that bought bread cost too much and he would bake.

Lucy was paring apples by the stove.

“I can bake enough fur both,” she ventured.

“I hain’t asked ye to.”

“But ye can trust bread, Alferd. Gentian’s bitter. Ye’d taste it in bread.”

He looked at her.

“That ain’t the question.”

“What is the question?” she asked, with a suddenness that surprised them both.

“The question is I don’t want nothin’ you prepare.”

The apple-peeling broke between her fingers.

Lucy’s face altered. It did not harden, for she had no hardness in her; it seemed rather to become smaller and stiller, as a flower closes under cold rain.

“Then I guess ye don’t want me,” she said.

Alfred turned the rolling-pin in his hand.

“I didn’t say that.”

“What am I here fur ef I can’t do fur ye?”

“That’s fur you to decide.”

He meant, perhaps, no more than to close the discussion. Alfred’s words were doors which he commonly shut without considering who might remain outside. But Lucy had for months eaten shame, slept with silence and watched the brown teapot proclaim her uselessness from its separate towel. Something within her yielded.

“All right,” she said.

The next morning, while Alfred was in the far lot, she packed her clothes. She folded her two best dresses, her nightgowns, her wedding shawl and the unfinished gray sock she was knitting for him. At sight of the sock she sat down upon the bed and wept; then she wrapped it with the yarn and put it at the bottom of the trunk.

She baked six apple turnovers before she left. Alfred would not eat them, but she set them on the pantry shelf in a row, covered with a clean cloth.

Hannah received her without surprise.

“I wondered how long it’d take,” she said. “Put your trunk in the front room. Mind the sill.”

“I ain’t leavin’ him fur good,” Lucy explained. “Only till he wants me.”

“Then you may wear out that front-room bed.”

Alfred came home at dusk. He saw the turnovers, the empty peg where Lucy’s shawl had hung, and the geranium gone from the window. He walked through the house, opening doors. Then he cooked his supper. He ate one of the turnovers after midnight, standing in the pantry without a lamp.

Winter settled early. Snow banked against Hannah’s north wall, and the road rang under runners. Lucy earned a little by sewing shirts for Mr. Pottle’s wife and by binding quilts. Her small hands were never idle. She sat near Hannah’s kitchen window, where the light fell cold upon her work, and stitched until her eyes ached.

She asked no one about Alfred, because everyone told her. He had bought a cook-stove catalogue. He had burned a batch of bread. He was living mostly on crackers, cheese and mackerel. He had been seen sweeping his doorstep. He had taken his washing to old Mrs. Crane, though Lucy had always whitened his shirts beautifully.

“He’ll git scurvy,” Hannah predicted.

“Cheese is nourishin’,” Lucy said.

“Not when it’s et with spite.”

At Christmas Lucy knit Alfred a pair of mittens and left them at the store. He wore them, but sent no message. In February he slipped on the ice before the meeting-house. Lucy heard of it within an hour and put on her hood.

“Where you goin’?” Hannah demanded.

“To see ef he’s hurt.”

“He walked home.”

“Mebbe he’s worse arter.”

“Mebbe he’ll send fur you ef he is.”

Lucy stood with one hand on the latch. After a moment she took off her hood.

That was the hardest act of resistance in her life, and nobody praised her for it.

The winter passed. Snow shrank from the stone walls; roofs dripped; the road loosened into brown mud. Nearly a year had gone since the illness, and six months since Lucy left home. To her it seemed both an eternity and a single unfinished day. She had not ceased being Alfred’s wife merely because she slept in Hannah’s front room. Her mind still set his breakfast, counted his shirts, worried over his cough in east winds. Separation had taken her body from his house but left the best of her there, wandering from stove to pantry in useless attendance.

One mild evening in April she could endure it no longer.

Rain had fallen in the morning, and the wet hedges shone under a clearing sky. Hannah was in the shed straining milk. Lucy put on her brown shawl and went out without speaking.

The road to the Tollet place ran between low fields newly ridged for planting. Frogs called from the ditches. Here and there a maple showed red buds, and over the western hill a band of yellow light lay beneath the clouds. Lucy walked fast until she saw the house; then her steps shortened.

Nothing had changed, and everything had. The kitchen chimney smoked. Alfred’s boots stood outside the door, muddy and unblacked. The lilacs were budding. Through the window she saw the red geranium, which she had brought back secretly during the winter and left on the sill when Alfred was away. It was alive, but leaned crookedly toward the glass.

She knocked.

Alfred opened the door.

He had grown thinner. His shirt hung loose at the neck, and there was gray in his beard which Lucy did not remember. Behind him the kitchen looked disorderly rather than dirty: newspapers on a chair, a cracked bowl beside the sink, wood chips scattered before the stove. The brown teapot sat on the table.

“What d’ye want?” he asked.

Lucy’s courage failed so entirely that for a moment she could not answer.

“I—come to see ye.”

“You’ve seen me.”

“Alferd, don’t.”

He moved as if to close the door, but she put one hand against it.

“I can’t stay to Hannah’s no longer.”

“Why not?”

“She’s good to me. ’Tain’t that. But it ain’t home.”

“You left of your own accord.”

“You said ye didn’t want nothin’ I prepared.”

“I didn’t tell ye to go.”

“I thought ye meant it.”

“You’re always thinkin’ things I don’t say.”

Lucy’s eyes filled, but she kept her hand upon the door.

“Take me back, Alferd.”

He looked past her at the wet road.

“I won’t deceive ye. I won’t put so much as salt in your porridge unless ye see me. I’ll taste everything first. I’ll—oh, Alferd, I’ll do any way ye want! I know I done wrong. I know I hadn’t ought to gone contrary after ye said no. But I was scairt. Ye was so sick, an’ your hands shook on the quilt—”

“That’s enough.”

“Don’t send me off. I’ve tried to bear it. I’ve worked, an’ Hannah’s been good, but nights I keep thinkin’ I hear ye call. I wake up an’ don’t know where the stove is. I can’t—I can’t seem to belong thar.”

Alfred’s hand tightened on the door.

“You’d better go back before it gits dark.”

Lucy stared at him. Then she drew her shawl over her head.

“Yes, Alferd,” she said.

She turned and went down the path.

Alfred closed the door.

He stood in the kitchen while her steps diminished along the road. The room was very quiet except for the ticking clock and a faint simmer from a pot upon the stove. On the table lay a loaf of his own bread, heavy and split across the top. The geranium cast a crooked shadow upon the wall.

He lifted the lid of the pot, replaced it, and went to the window.

Lucy had reached the bend by the alder bushes. She walked with her head bowed, a small brown figure in the wet and shining landscape. Beyond her, the sunset had turned the puddles to strips of fire.

Alfred took his hat from the peg.

Lucy heard him when he was halfway down the road, but she did not turn. She supposed some wagon was approaching until his voice came sharply behind her.

“Lucy!”

She stopped.

Alfred was breathing hard when he reached her. He had forgotten his coat, and the evening wind pressed his shirt against his narrow chest.

“What be you runnin’ fur?” she cried. “Ye’ll ketch cold.”

“I wa’n’t runnin’.”

“You be all out of breath.”

“The road’s soft.”

They stood facing each other. Water dropped from the alder twigs. Somewhere beyond the pasture a cow lowed to be milked.

Alfred looked toward the Tollet house and then toward the village.

“You’d better come back,” he said.

Lucy made a little movement but did not yet dare believe him.

“D’ye mean—home?”

“I don’t mean Hannah’s.”

“Oh, Alferd!”

He frowned, though less severely than before.

“I hain’t felt reel rugged lately.”

“Ye hain’t?”

“No. Stomach’s poorly. Food don’t set right.”

Lucy’s whole face changed. Anxiety, hope and the old devoted purpose returned to it together, like lamps lighted in a dark house.

“Ye’ve been eatin’ them salt mackerel. I knew they’d hurt ye. How long ye felt so? Do ye hev chills? Does your head ache? Alferd, your hands be cold!”

He withdrew them with dignity.

“’Tain’t nothin’ serious.”

“Doctor Lane—”

“I don’t want no doctor.”

She caught her breath. For one instant the old difficulty stood between them, but now it had altered its shape.

Alfred looked away. When he spoke, his voice was curt and almost impatient, as if the words had been forced upon him by some practical necessity for which he declined all sentimental responsibility.

“I thought mebbe,” he said, “you could stew me up a little gentian.”

Lucy gave a sob that was half a laugh. She caught his sleeve in both hands.

“Oh, Alferd!”

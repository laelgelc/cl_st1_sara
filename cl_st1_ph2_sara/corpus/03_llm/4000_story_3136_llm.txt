### The Shape in the Light

Timothy O’Connor began life with every intention of becoming a saint, and ended it, with less success, as a journalist. Between these ambitions lay poetry, theology, three love affairs, one marriage, innumerable whiskies, and a conviction—never quite sincere—that the universe had been designed by nobody in particular for the amusement of men who could write leading articles.

At nineteen he had wished to take orders. He possessed then a narrow, luminous face such as the Italian painters reserve for the Beloved Apostle; blue eyes of an almost offensive candour; and hands so fine that old priests regarded them as already consecrated. He wrote hymns to the Virgin, sonnets to dead kings, and a philosophical drama in which the Soul conversed with Eternity upon a mountain. At twenty-two he discovered Baudelaire, Burgundy, and the daily press. The mountain disappeared; Eternity was reduced to eight hundred words; and the Soul, having missed the last train, slept at a club.

Journalism did not corrupt him immediately. No profession can accomplish in a morning what character has resisted for years. Tim fought against his better nature with patience, ingenuity, and considerable expense. He learned to sneer at virtue, though never in the presence of a virtuous person; to ridicule religion, though he removed his hat when a priest passed; to praise cruel books, though cruelty made him ill; and to lose at cards with a generosity that ruined only himself. In a world of cultivated ruffians he remained a perfect gentleman, which was his first misfortune. His second was that he knew it.

His prose became famous. It had the polish of silver and the edge of broken glass. Cabinet ministers feared his parentheses; actresses treasured his adjectives; and obscure poets, whom he murdered on Thursday, invited him to supper on Friday, because an execution by O’Connor was preferable to immortality under another man’s name. His employers paid him handsomely and irregularly. Tim spent the money before it arrived, partly from optimism, partly from habit, and partly because Jim O’Malley considered solvency a Protestant affectation.

O’Malley was less a man than an Irish institution, founded for the encouragement of conversation and the suppression of closing hours. He had published one volume of verse, lost two fortunes, and assisted at the death of seven magazines. To young writers he was Mæcenas; to publicans, Providence; to the police, an annual inconvenience. He loved Tim with that expansive Celtic devotion which borrows five pounds from the beloved object and forgets the transaction from motives of delicacy.

Rick Dodson completed the trinity. Dodson was a critic, a cynic, and, on Sundays, a remorseful Presbyterian. He had a red moustache, an appetite for paradox, and the melancholy eyes of a man who has discovered that cleverness is not a private income. He and Tim abused one another daily and would cheerfully have died for each other, provided the matter could be arranged without an early breakfast.

It was among these assiduities that Tim met Celia Vane.

She had pale golden skin, yellow eyes, straw-coloured hair, and a small scarlet mouth which smiled as if it had just discovered a secret dishonour concerning you. Her beauty possessed neither warmth nor modesty; it was the beauty of a costly little animal sleeping upon stolen silk. She admired Tim’s reputation, his hands, and his presumed salary. Tim admired the fatal inclination of her shoulders and mistook appetite for mystery—a common error among poets, who have given the Eternal Feminine credit for much that belongs properly to the digestive system.

They married after six weeks.

Celia desired luxury, publicity, jewels, suppers, and the visible envy of other women. Tim provided what he could and invented the rest. She amused herself with his friends, despised his poems, pawned his mother’s crucifix, and complained that his drunkenness was provincial because he sometimes wept. He, meanwhile, cultivated degradation as another man might cultivate orchids. He drank more, gambled recklessly, wrote brilliant obscenities he did not believe, and sought the company of persons beneath him with the humility of a penitent. Yet the old purity clung to him. It survived dissipation as a white flower survives in a ruined chapel, inconvenient, unexplained, and faintly reproachful.

Celia eventually departed with a financier whose moral character was so secure that nobody had ever found it. Tim gave them his blessing and paid for their railway tickets. That evening he produced the funniest column of his career. Dodson read it twice, laughed once, and went out to find him.

Tim was in O’Malley’s back room, surrounded by bottles and admirers. His face had grown thinner, but its saintly delicacy remained, insulted rather than destroyed. He recited verses, imitated bishops, and declared marriage to be the sole sacrament administered by the Devil. At two in the morning he distributed his remaining money among three strangers and borrowed cab fare from the waiter.

It was after Celia’s flight that his fear of darkness began.

At first this appeared merely another idiosyncrasy in a man rich in them. Tim slept with the electric lamps burning. He refused unlighted stairways, would not cross an empty square after midnight, and once broke a window because a servant had accidentally locked him in a pantry. Naturally his companions were delighted. Nothing is so refreshing to the well-bred as a friend’s terror, especially when it can be discussed over supper.

“What do you expect to meet?” Dodson asked him one night at the newspaper office. “A creditor?”

“Creditors are substantial,” said Tim. “They cast shadows.”

“A husband, then?”

“Too improbable.”

“The Devil?”

Tim smiled. “I know the Devil. He is an excellent conversationalist and never comes unless invited.”

“Ghosts?”

At that word Tim’s smile vanished. He turned toward the electric globe above the desk, as though assuring himself of its loyalty.

Dodson, whose affection was often betrayed into cruelty by embarrassment, laughed.

“My dear Timothy, you have spent fifteen years proving that there is no immortal soul. A ghost would destroy your collected works.”

“There are no ghosts,” Tim said.

“Then you cannot fear them.”

“I do not fear ghosts.”

“What do you fear?”

Tim took up a pencil, broke it, and examined the pieces with grave attention.

“What I fear,” he said at last, “is Fear.”

Dodson applauded. “Excellent! Greek in its simplicity, French in its uselessness. We shall print it under your portrait.”

But Tim did not smile.

“You think fear is a sensation,” he continued. “It is not. It is a thing. It waits until one is alone; then it puts on a shape.”

“What shape?”

“If I knew that, Rick, I might endure it.”

There followed several weeks during which Tim’s condition improved sufficiently to deceive everyone except those who loved him. He worked hard, drank with method, and preserved a little court of young decadents who copied his waistcoats and misunderstood his despair. He no longer mentioned darkness, but carried two boxes of matches and a silver candlestick even at noon. O’Malley presented him with a pocket electric lamp and christened it the Light of Faith.

“You see,” said O’Malley, “science has at last justified theology. Press the button, and behold—revelation!”

“Until the battery fails,” Tim replied.

“All revelations fail in the end. That is why churches employ sextons.”

One wet November evening Dodson took Tim to see *Faust*. The performance was magnificent in the modern manner: the scenery cost more than Goethe earned in his lifetime, the angels were selected for their legs, and Mephistopheles wore a crimson cloak which reconciled several ladies to damnation. Tim watched in silence. At the words concerning the Eternal Feminine he shuddered and looked away.

“Celia?” whispered Dodson.

“Do not be vulgar.”

“I was being literary.”

“That is often the same thing.”

Afterward they walked to the newspaper office. Rain varnished the streets; the lamps burned in long, trembling files; and the city, half drowned in fog, resembled a fallen heaven whose stars had been hung at the level of gin shops. Tim seemed exhausted. Dodson installed him in the editorial room, switched on every electric light, and went downstairs to procure whisky and sandwiches.

At the foot of the stairs he met a printer with news of a political scandal. There were documents to inspect, denials to invent, and a duke to protect from the consequences of his handwriting. Dodson was detained perhaps twenty minutes. During this interval a fuse failed.

The office became dark.

When Dodson returned, carrying a tray and swearing at the government, he saw no blackness. The door stood partly open, and through it poured a light more pure than any electrical brilliance—a soft, white radiance that seemed not to illuminate the room but to abolish everything unworthy of being seen.

He stopped upon the threshold.

Tim stood beside the desk, rigid, one hand pressed against his heart. Before him, or above him—for the laws of space appeared to have withdrawn out of respect—was a figure of intolerable beauty.

It had the form of a young man, though its tenderness was almost feminine; it wore no wings, no crown, no emblem by which theology could classify it and turn wonder into furniture. Its face was Tim’s face as a Raphael might have imagined it: serene, ardent, unstained. About it hung the fragrance of lilies after rain. Its garments were white, yet not the white of cloth or snow, but of mercy made visible. The eyes regarded Tim without anger.

That absence of accusation was terrible.

The room had become glacial. Dodson could not move or speak. He understood, with the immediate certainty granted in nightmares and sacraments, that this beauty meant no harm, threatened no punishment, demanded no repentance. It merely existed. Against it, all the cleverness of their lives—every epigram, every bottle, every elegant profanation—seemed small and noisy, like laughter overheard beside a deathbed.

Tim uttered a sound, not quite a cry.

The figure extended one luminous hand.

Then the electric lamps sprang back to life. The apparition vanished. The office, with its torn proofs, dirty glasses, tobacco smoke, and portraits of dishonest statesmen, returned to possession of itself.

Tim fell.

Dodson revived him with whisky, which was the only spiritual remedy available at that hour. For some minutes Tim stared before him; then he began to tremble so violently that the glass rattled against his teeth.

“You saw it?” he whispered.

“I saw something.”

“Do not lie to comfort me.”

“I never comfort anyone, Tim. It establishes a precedent.”

“What did you think it was?”

Dodson hesitated. “An angel, perhaps.”

“No.” Tim’s voice was suddenly calm. “An angel belongs to God. That belonged to me.”

“To you?”

“It was the shape of all the good I might have done.”

Dodson looked away.

“My unwritten poems,” Tim continued, “my unspoken kindnesses, the faith I mocked because I had not courage to obey it; every clean desire I strangled before it could reproach me; every noble thing I might have become and did not. I feared my sins would take shape. Sins are ugly, Rick, and ugliness can be laughed at. But that—”

He covered his face.

“That was beautiful.”

Dodson attempted some answer, but wit, which had served him admirably against politics, religion, marriage, and death, proved unequal to goodness. He placed a hand on Tim’s shoulder. It was a clumsy gesture and therefore sincere.

They took him home before dawn. The morning was grey, muggy, and offensive; carts splashed through the gutters, factory whistles proclaimed the triumph of commerce, and a drunk slept peacefully beneath a poster announcing the resurrection of a musical comedy. Tim looked at the sky and murmured, “Even daylight can be dark.”

Three days later he was found kneeling in his room before the extinguished grate, speaking gently to someone who was not there. After that came doctors, consultations, euphemisms, and at last the private asylum, where madness was treated with lawns, punctual meals, and invoices of remarkable sanity.

Tim lived there for seven months. He became mild, courteous, and almost happy. He drank no alcohol, wrote no journalism, and asked frequently for flowers. Sometimes he spoke of the radiant Shape, but without terror.

“It waits for me,” he told Dodson during their last interview.

“Then let it wait. You were never punctual.”

Tim smiled. “I think it has waited all my life.”

He died patiently and sweetly one spring morning, while sunlight lay across his bed. A priest had been with him through the night. Thus religion, which Tim had dismissed in several brilliant articles, returned without resentment and closed his eyes.

O’Malley arranged a high mass, borrowed money for flowers, and delivered an oration at supper which lasted until the candles sank in their sockets. Dodson said little. At midnight the two men walked past the cemetery wall.

“He hated the dark,” O’Malley muttered. “It is a damned unkind place to put him.”

Dodson looked toward the graves, where the black trees moved against a clouded sky.

“We should have buried him under an electric lamp.”

“The authorities would object.”

“Authorities always object to illumination.”

They stood there a moment longer, two ageing jesters without an audience. Then, from beyond the wall, a faint whiteness rose among the trees—perhaps mist, perhaps moonlight, perhaps nothing that a reasonable man could not explain.

O’Malley removed his hat.

Dodson did likewise, though he afterwards denied it.

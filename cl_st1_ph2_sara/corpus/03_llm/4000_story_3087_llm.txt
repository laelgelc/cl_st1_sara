## I. The Camp

At four o’clock in the afternoon the stamps were dropping in the mill with the steady, ponderous cadence of artillery heard at an immense distance. The sound never ceased. It was the pulse of the camp, the iron diapason beneath all lesser noises—the whine of the saw in the carpenter’s shed, the clatter of ore cars upon the trestle, the cries of the stable boys, the sudden bark of a foreman among the sorting floors.

Toward evening, however, the other sounds diminished one by one, while the stamps continued, unhurried and implacable.

The camp of the Blue Arroyo Mine occupied a shelf of the Sierra foothills, seven miles from the nearest wagon road and twenty-three from Campo Seco, where stood the express office, the bank, the telegraph instrument, and the office of the district assayer. Above it rose a wilderness of pine and chaparral; below, the slope fell through live oak and manzanita to the gorge of the San Gregorio. The superintendent’s cottage, the office, the bunkhouse, the cook shack, the blacksmith shop, and the long corrugated roof of the mill had been set down without reference to beauty or convenience, but solely according to the necessities of grade, water, ore, and timber. Nevertheless, at sunset the place had its beauty.

The western sky burned vermilion behind the firs. Wood smoke drifted blue and level across the roofs. The smell of hot grease and crushed rock mingled with the sweeter odors of apples stored beneath the cookhouse, witch-hazel along the ditch, and tar-weed drying in the dust. Tree-toads began their staccato chorus from the creek; an owl called three times from the black woods; and the last light faded from the mill windows like receding music.

On such evenings Lockwood would sit on the steps of the superintendent’s office, one heel hooked upon the lower tread, watching the day expire.

He was twenty-eight years old, a graduate of a technical school in the East, and for eleven months superintendent of the Blue Arroyo. He was tall, spare, clean-shaven, and rather pale; his hands, though scarred now by drill steel and timber splinters, retained an incongruous whiteness. He had read much, travelled little, and lived less. In the presence of machinery he was exact, prompt, and self-possessed. Before a broken pump, a squeezed stope, a bad fuse, or a mutinous gang he knew precisely what to do. But there are emergencies for which no technical school provides formulas.

It must be understood that the camp was isolated beyond the ordinary meaning of that word. In winter the road went out under slides. In summer the dust lay half a foot deep between the ruts. Letters came twice a week, newspapers once, visitors almost never. The same forty-odd men ate together, worked together, quarrelled, gambled, and slept within sound of the stamps. A trivial event became history; a new face became an epoch. Under such conditions personal impressions, denied the correction of variety, take on an enormous importance. A gesture repeats itself in the mind; a look acquires significance; a woman’s voice, heard once in the dusk, may reverberate for days.

Felice Zavalla was the only young woman at Blue Arroyo.

She was the wife of Chino Zavalla, boss of the night shift underground—a compact, silent fellow with heavy shoulders, a black mustache, and the grave, attentive manner of a man accustomed to obeying danger. Chino had worked in quartz mines from Sonora to Grass Valley. He knew timber, powder, and men. If a hanging wall began to talk in those faint, dry cracklings that precede collapse, Chino heard it first. If a laborer turned sullen or a driller careless, he detected the change before the foreman. He neither drank nor gambled. His loyalty to the mine was absolute and impersonal.

Felice had come to the camp in May. By June every bachelor on the property had found an excuse to pass the Zavallas’ cabin.

She was not tall, yet she gave an impression of height because of the erect carriage of her head. Her hair was black and abundant; her skin held the warm pallor of ivory under lamplight; her eyes, unexpectedly green, seemed at one moment candid and at another opaque. She dressed better than the wives in Campo Seco, though no one knew how she contrived it. A red ribbon, a white sleeve, a silver comb—she understood the value of a single bright note amid the brown dust, gray rock, blue shirts, and blackened faces of the camp.

Lockwood avoided her from the beginning.

At least he called it avoidance. He no longer took the path by the Zavalla cabin unless business required it; but business, singularly enough, required it almost every day. He asked Chino questions that could have been asked at the change-house. He inspected the ditch behind the cabin, though the ditch had given no trouble. He stopped to discuss the price of hay with the stableman where Felice could be seen hanging linen in the yard.

She had a trick of looking at him steadily while he spoke, as if his least commonplace observation concealed a private message intended for her alone. Then, at the precise instant when he believed that some understanding had passed between them, she would turn away, smile at Chino, and become merely the shift boss’s wife.

This ambiguity was the hook. Lockwood’s imagination swallowed it whole.

He did not at first admit that he loved her. He classified his sensations with the industrious dishonesty of an intelligent man determined to deceive himself. Her presence interested him aesthetically; he was lonely; he felt responsible for the welfare of the employees’ families; the charm of a refined woman was naturally welcome in a rough camp. Such were the names he gave the thing.

But the thing itself grew.

When he awoke, her image was already waiting. During the morning inspection it accompanied him underground, moving beside him through the drifts where candles burned in niches and water dripped from the lagging. At the noon assay he would see her green eyes in the flare of the furnace. Her voice threaded itself through the stamp-mill’s thunder, fine and persistent as a harp-string vibrating beneath an orchestra.

Once, returning from the upper workings, he found her seated under the pear tree behind the cabin, mending one of Chino’s shirts.

“You work too much, Meester Lockwood,” she said.

He laughed. “Somebody has to.”

“Chino, he say you never stop. Always the mine, the mill, the books. You got no time to be happy.”

“I’m happy enough.”

“No.” She drew the needle through the cloth and looked up at him. “You are not happy.”

The remark was nothing. Yet the pity with which she invested it—or which he imagined she invested it—struck through him like chilled steel. For the remainder of the day he repeated the words. You are not happy. By midnight he had transformed them into a confession.

Thereafter the entanglement tightened rapidly. A flower left on his desk became a signal, though Felice said the wind had blown it through the window. A song in the Zavallas’ cabin was sung for him, though half the camp heard it. When she asked whether he would go to Campo Seco for the fiesta in September, he detected disappointment in her face when he answered that the mine could not spare him.

He never touched her. He never spoke one word that could have been repeated as evidence against him. Yet in the secret chambers of his mind he had already betrayed Chino a hundred times.

That was the monstrous feature of it. Chino trusted him.

“Boss,” the shift foreman would say, standing in the office doorway with the lamp smoke still upon his face, “number seven raise she look bad. I put two more sets. You come see?”

Or, “Boss, Jim Fallon he make trouble again. Better you talk him.”

Or simply, when Lockwood had remained too long underground, “You go eat. I watch.”

His confidence was as direct as the grip of his hand. It imposed no conditions, anticipated no treachery. And because it was perfect, it became at moments almost intolerable.

Lockwood began to wish that Chino would leave the mine.

The wish appeared first in an innocent form. Chino deserved a place of his own; he might take a lease in Nevada; he might return to Mexico; he might accept a foremanship at the Red Elephant. From this it was only a step—one imperceptible, noiseless step—to wishing Felice free.

Free. He would use no more definite word.

But thought, once started down an incline, does not stop where conscience sets a signboard. Now and then the mind rushed onward of itself, beyond absence, beyond separation, to the one final freedom. At such instants Lockwood recoiled, ashamed and frightened. Then Felice would pass beneath his window, lifting her face to smile, and the madness rode and roweled him once more.

## II. The Gold Brick

The Blue Arroyo cleaned up every six weeks.

For five days the amalgamators scraped the plates, retorted the quicksilver, and melted the sponge in a graphite crucible. The product of that particular run was a rough yellow bar weighing a little over two hundred pounds and worth, according to the office estimate, thirty-eight thousand dollars. It was not a pretty object. Its top was blistered, its corners irregular, and particles of black slag adhered to one side; nevertheless it represented the labor of the stamps, the drilling of a hundred holes, the consumption of powder and candles, the handling of sixteen hundred tons of ore, and the wages of every man in camp.

On Friday morning the brick was to go to Campo Seco.

The usual arrangement was simple. Lockwood and Chino carried it between them in a shallow iron box slung from a pole. At the stable it was packed into a leather case and buckled behind the saddle of the stronger horse. The two men rode by the north trail to Digger’s Gap, descended to the county road, and reached town before noon. After delivering the bullion at the bank they transacted the camp’s business and returned next day with currency for the payroll.

No one had attempted to rob them. Still, every saloon from Campo Seco to Auburn knew approximately when Blue Arroyo cleaned up, and the district had lately become unsafe.

Three weeks before, the Reno Kid had held up the stage near Dutch Flat. He was said to be a Texan; again, an Oregonian; again, an escaped convict from Carson. Nobody knew. What was known was that he rode a flea-bitten gray, handled two revolvers with unusual quickness, and shot to kill. The driver of the Dutch Flat stage had hesitated before throwing down the express box. The Kid’s first bullet had entered just below his collarbone; the second, fired after the box was surrendered, had broken his spine.

Hicks, the mine manager, came up from San Francisco on Thursday to inspect the books and attend the cleanup. He was a small man of fifty, colorless in dress and manner, with a close gray mustache and eyes that never appeared to blink. The camp regarded him with awe. Hicks wasted neither motion nor language. He could dismiss a foreman, reject a requisition, and detect an error in an assay sheet without raising his voice.

That afternoon Lockwood accompanied him to the mill. They were crossing the trestle above the rock breaker when a loose plank turned under Lockwood’s boot. He fell heavily, caught himself against the handrail, and drove a projecting spike through the leather at the side of his foot.

It was not a grave injury, but it bled freely and swelled before evening. The camp doctor, who also served as storekeeper, dentist, and postmaster, cleaned the wound with carbolic, wrapped the foot, and ordered Lockwood to keep off it for forty-eight hours.

“Then the brick waits,” Lockwood said.

“No,” said Hicks.

They were in the assay office. The bullion lay upon the platform scale under a canvas cover. A lamp burned on the desk, throwing Hicks’s shadow against the whitewashed wall in a hard, angular silhouette.

“Payroll is due tomorrow,” he continued. “The men were promised cash Saturday night.”

“I can ride.”

“You cannot put that foot in a stirrup.”

“Send Carson with Chino.”

“Carson is needed at the hoist. Chino can go alone.”

Lockwood looked at him. “With thirty-eight thousand?”

“He is trustworthy.”

“That isn’t the point.”

“The route is known only here and at the bank. He will leave before daylight and take the south cutoff from Digger’s Gap. No stop at the hotel. No talk.”

Lockwood hesitated. “There’s been trouble on the roads.”

“There is always trouble on roads. Chino carries a rifle?”

“A Winchester.”

“Then settle it.”

The words closed the discussion like the jaws of a steel trap.

Chino raised no objection. He came to the office at four the next morning, while the camp was still dark, drank coffee standing, and inspected the straps on the bullion case himself. The brick had been divided into two packages to balance the load, one on either side of the saddle, under the skirts. Chino’s Winchester hung in a scabbard beneath his right leg; a revolver was strapped outside his coat.

Lockwood watched from the office porch, his injured foot in a felt slipper. The cold before dawn sharpened every sound—the ring of a buckle, the horse’s breathing, the scrape of a hoof.

“You keep to the south cutoff,” Lockwood said.

“Yes, boss.”

“Don’t stop at Digger’s.”

“No.”

“If anybody falls in behind you, don’t wait to see what he wants.”

Chino smiled a little. “Maybe nobody know I go.”

“Maybe.”

Felice appeared in the doorway of the cabin, a shawl over her head. She crossed the yard and gave her husband a packet of food. Then, placing one hand on the saddle, she spoke to him in a low voice. Chino bent toward her. She kissed his cheek.

Lockwood turned away.

A moment later the horse moved out, walking at first, then taking a slow trot beyond the stable. The hoofbeats went down the trail, became faint, merged with the rumble of the stamps, and were gone.

“Good man,” Hicks remarked from the office door.

Lockwood said nothing.

Felice was returning to the cabin. Halfway across the yard she looked back. Her eyes met Lockwood’s. For one instant she paused, the shawl loosened about her black hair, the pale dawn behind her. It seemed to him that the look contained anxiety, appeal, and something more—something hidden, something that acknowledged the same thought he had not dared to formulate.

Then she went inside.

## III. The Warning

At half past ten Hicks left for the upper tunnel with the master mechanic. Lockwood remained in the office, checking time sheets, his foot propped upon a box. The morning grew hot. Through the open window came the pulverizing roar of the mill and, at intervals, the bell signals from the hoist: one—stop; two—lower; three—men aboard.

His mind followed Chino’s progress over the route.

By five Chino would have reached Pine Saddle. At six he would be crossing the old burn. At seven, Digger’s Gap. The south cutoff saved three miles but descended steeply through a canyon choked with buckeye and scrub oak. From the canyon to Campo Seco was open country. If all went well, he would deliver the gold before ten.

At eleven a stable boy galloped into camp on a lathered mule, bringing the mail pouch from the road. There was also a telegram.

The nearest instrument was at Campo Seco, but urgent messages for the mine were relayed by telephone to Morrison’s ranch, six miles below Blue Arroyo, and from there sent up by rider. The envelope was addressed to Hicks. In his absence Lockwood opened it.

The message, from the sheriff, read:

RELIABLE INFORMATION RENO KID KNOWS BLUE ARROYO SHIPMENT DUE TODAY. BELIEVED WAITING SOUTH OF DIGGER’S GAP. SEND NO GOLD. WARN COURIER. KID WILL SHOOT.

Lockwood read it twice.

For a moment the office, the desk, the bright window, and the mine buildings beyond appeared preternaturally distinct. He heard the ticking of the assay balance behind him. He saw a fly walking along the rim of the ink bottle. The telegram trembled slightly between his fingers.

Chino had been gone seven hours.

If the message was true, he had ridden directly into the ambush.

Lockwood’s first thought was practical. Send men after him. But nearly every horse was in the lower pasture, four miles away. Hicks had taken the manager’s bay to the upper tunnel. The stable held only the old buckskin used for hauling the mail, a strong animal but rough-gaited and vicious.

He reached for the bell rope to summon the stableman.

Then he stopped.

The movement was arrested midway, as if another hand had caught his wrist.

There are crises that approach with warning—announced by sleepless nights, by argument, by gradual temptation. There are others that fall like a knife upon a knot. In one stroke all the tangled threads are cut; the secret nature stands exposed.

Chino might be killed.

The fact entered Lockwood’s mind without disguise. Not transferred, not dismissed, not divorced. Killed.

Felice would be free.

He sat motionless.

Outside, the mill roared on. Men crossed the yard. A team drew an ore wagon past the window, the harness chains clanking; the driver called to the lead horse. The ordinary life of the camp enclosed the office, while within that square room Lockwood descended, in a few seconds, through all the strata of his moral being.

He had only to do nothing.

No one could blame him. The telegram had arrived late. He was injured. There was no fit horse. Chino might already be in town; he might already be dead. To pursue him now would be useless, reckless, perhaps fatal. Reason provided these arguments instantly, neatly, in order. They came to him like clerks placing documents upon a desk.

And underneath them sounded the other voice.

Do nothing.

Felice’s eyes were before him—the green, unfathomable eyes, the look across the dawn-struck yard. He saw the shawl slipping from her hair. He remembered: You are not happy.

Suppose the Kid waited in the canyon. Suppose Chino came around a bend, the laden horse climbing slowly. A voice from the brush. Hands up. Chino’s hand moving toward the Winchester. A flash.

Then silence.

Lockwood’s heart beat violently, not with horror alone, but with hope.

The discovery appalled him. He had believed himself tempted; he now found himself consenting. All his education, his standards, the inherited habits of honorable men, the whole laborious structure he called character—these were not destroyed; they were thrust aside. Passion had not argued against them. It had merely stepped past them, like a brutal workman shouldering through a line of children.

He let the telegram fall.

For perhaps twenty seconds he remained so. It was enough. In those seconds Chino’s death became possible, advantageous, desired. Lockwood saw himself after the funeral—grave, helpful, protective. He saw Felice in mourning. He saw time passing, propriety satisfied, suspicion lulled. At last he saw her in his own house.

The vision was complete.

Then another memory came—not Felice, not any maxim or religious injunction, but Chino in number seven raise, standing beneath a loosened slab while Lockwood crawled past the bad ground. Chino had held the timber with both hands and said quietly, “Quick, boss. She come.”

The slab had fallen after Lockwood cleared it. Chino’s shoulder had been bruised for a month.

The recollection drove home like a rivet.

Lockwood snatched the bell rope and pulled it until the stableman ran from the harness room.

“Saddle the buckskin.”

The man stared at the felt slipper. “For who?”

“For me. Move!”

“He’ll pile you, Mr. Lockwood.”

“Saddle him!”

Now action came with the onslaught of a whirlwind. Lockwood thrust the sheriff’s telegram into his pocket, buckled on a revolver, filled a canteen, and took a short carbine from the office rack. His boot would not go over the bandaged foot; he slit the leather down the side, forced it on, and tied it with cord. The pain leaped to his knee, sharp and nauseating.

No matter.

The buckskin came sidling from the stable, ears flattened, eyes rolling. Lockwood mounted from the porch rail because he could not bear weight upon the injured foot. At the pressure of the girth the horse exploded into motion, plunged twice across the yard, then bolted for the trail.

“Tell Hicks!” Lockwood shouted.

The camp flew behind him.

## IV. The Pursuit

The north trail to Digger’s Gap was twelve miles of broken grade. For the first three it followed the ore ditch, twisting in and out of ravines where ferns brushed the rider’s knees and the air smelled of wet stone. Then it climbed through pine forest to the ridge.

Lockwood rode for all they were worth.

The buckskin had a mouth like iron and a gait that drove the saddle against the wound at every stride. Pain became first a stabbing, then a burning, then a monstrous separate life fastened to his body. Sweat ran into his eyes. Branches whipped his face. Twice the horse stumbled; once he swerved from a shadow and nearly went over the trail edge.

Lockwood neither checked nor spared him.

At Pine Saddle he found the road empty. In the dust were many tracks—wagon wheels, cattle, two shod horses travelling east. He dismounted awkwardly and examined them. One set might have been Chino’s, but the ground was too trampled to be certain.

He shouted.

No answer came except the diminishing echo: “Chino—Chino—ino.”

He rode on.

Now the country opened. The pines thinned, giving way to manzanita and gray-green brush. Heat quivered above the red earth. Far below, the San Gregorio showed between the hills like a strip of beaten tin. Beyond it the lower country spread under a yellow haze, and somewhere in that haze lay Campo Seco.

At the old burn he passed a woodcutter’s camp. An elderly man stood beside a crosscut saw, his shirt dark with sweat.

“Rider through here this morning?” Lockwood called.

“Mexican fellow?”

“Yes.”

“About six. Headed for the Gap.”

“Anybody behind him?”

The man considered. “Saw a gray horse maybe an hour later. One rider.”

The words struck Lockwood between the shoulders.

“What kind of man?”

“Couldn’t say. Kept to the trees.”

Lockwood drove in the spurs.

Once more the buckskin gathered himself; once more the trail streamed backward under pounding hoofs. The whole world narrowed to the way ahead—the pale ribbon of dust, the quick turns, the shadows beneath the scrub oak. At every bend Lockwood expected to see a rider lying in the road, a horse standing with trailing reins, a dark stain upon the ground.

Nothing.

At Digger’s Gap the trail divided. The north branch descended by the toll road; the south cutoff plunged into the canyon. Lockwood found Chino’s tracks unmistakably in a patch of soft dirt: the broad print of the mine horse’s left forefoot with one corner of the shoe missing.

The tracks took the cutoff.

Beside them, partially obliterating them, appeared the prints of another horse.

A gray? There was no way to tell.

Lockwood stopped long enough to throw water upon the buckskin’s neck and give him three swallows from the canteen. His own hands shook. The sun had passed noon. If Chino had maintained his pace, he should have reached town hours ago. But a bullion-laden horse moved slowly on the descent. The second rider might have overtaken him in the canyon.

He began the descent.

The cutoff was scarcely a road. It fell in switchbacks between walls of decomposed granite, crossed the dry bed of the creek nine times, and entered a mile-long gorge where buckeye branches joined overhead. The shadows there were green and close. The sound of the buckskin’s hoofs changed from a hard ringing to a muffled thud.

Lockwood drew his revolver.

Now every stump became a crouching man; every jay’s cry tightened his finger upon the trigger. He saw signs everywhere: a broken twig, dust newly disturbed, a pebble rolling from the bank. The nervous force that had carried him from camp altered its character. It was no longer urgency, but pursuit—the primitive concentration of the hound upon a scent, of the cat within reach of its spring.

“Chino!”

The canyon flung back his cry.

No answer.

Halfway down he found a place where two horses had stopped. The dust was churned. There were no blood marks, no cartridge cases. One rider had dismounted; a boot heel showed near a boulder. Then both horses had gone on.

At the canyon mouth he came upon a boy driving goats.

“Did a man pass?” Lockwood demanded.

The boy nodded.

“Two men? One on a gray?”

Another nod—or perhaps only fear.

“How long?”

The boy pointed toward the sun and then shrugged. Lockwood flung him a coin and rode on.

The open road beyond the canyon ran straight between dry fields. Campo Seco’s church spire showed five miles away. Here the buckskin began to fail. Foam streaked his bit; his stride shortened; the muscles of his quarters quivered. Lockwood checked him at last, because to kill the horse before reaching town would serve nothing.

He had time now to think.

Suppose Chino was safe. Suppose the gray rider was a rancher, the sheriff’s information false, the Reno Kid fifty miles away. Then what would Lockwood say? That he had crippled a horse and reopened his wound because of a warning that arrived too late? Hicks would call it bad judgment. Yet Hicks’s judgment mattered little. The essential fact remained: he had ridden.

He seized upon this as proof of virtue. The temptation had come, and he had overcome it.

But conscience is a precise accountant. It does not credit the final act without entering the preceding intention. He had hesitated. He had hoped. For those seconds in the office, he had allowed Chino to ride toward death while he sat still.

Nothing could erase that.

By three o’clock he reached Campo Seco.

The main street lay white and glaring between false-fronted buildings. Wagons stood before the feed store; dogs slept under the hotel veranda; from a saloon came the mechanical jangle of a piano. Lockwood rode first to the bank.

Chino had delivered the gold at ten minutes after eleven.

“Came in alone,” said the cashier. “Signed the receipt and left.”

“Left for where?”

“Couldn’t say. He drew your payroll and packed it in the saddlebags. Said he’d start home after he fed his horse.”

At the livery stable Lockwood found that Chino had indeed fed and watered the mine horse. He had left town at one o’clock.

“Anybody watching him?” Lockwood asked.

The hostler spat thoughtfully. “A stranger asked which road he’d take.”

“What stranger?”

“Lean man. Brown hat.”

“Horse?”

“Gray.”

The Reno Kid.

“Did Chino see him?”

“Don’t reckon.”

“Which road did the stranger take?”

“North toll road, maybe twenty minutes ahead.”

Lockwood stood gripping the stable door.

The explanation seemed obvious. The Kid had missed Chino on the way in—perhaps because the boy with the goats, frightened and confused, had indicated two riders where there was only one. Learning in town that the gold had been exchanged for currency, he had gone ahead to wait on the return route.

The payroll was worth less than the bullion, but it was still a prize. And Chino, once stopped, could identify the robber.

The Kid would shoot.

Lockwood turned the buckskin toward the street. The animal resisted, exhausted.

“You can’t take him back,” the hostler said. “He’s done.”

“Give me another.”

“Nothing here but boarders’ stock.”

Lockwood pulled out his watch. Half past three. The toll road joined the Blue Arroyo trail at the Gap, fifteen miles away. Chino had ninety minutes’ lead; the Kid something more.

He could telegraph the sheriff—but the sheriff was out with a posse. He could hire men—but by the time they assembled, darkness would have fallen.

There was one horse in the stable’s rear stall, a narrow-chested bay mare belonging to the bank manager.

“Her,” said Lockwood.

“She ain’t for hire.”

Lockwood threw two twenty-dollar gold pieces upon the ledge. “Then she’s bought.”

Five minutes later he was riding north.

## V. The Shot in the Dark

Evening overtook him on the toll road.

At first the sun hung behind him, a red disk sinking through dust. The fields flamed, darkened, and lost their color. Then the hills closed around the road. Shadows collected under the oaks, joined across the ravines, and rose like water.

The bay mare was willing but unfamiliar with mountain going. Lockwood could not press her as he had the buckskin. Moreover, his foot had swollen until the boot cords cut into the flesh. He rode with the leg free of the stirrup, every jolt sending a pulse of sickness through him.

At intervals he saw hoofprints in the dust, but the road had been travelled all day. Twice he dismounted to examine them; twice he found nothing certain. The Kid, if he had gone this way, might be behind him. Chino might have taken the cutoff again. Both might already be dead. Uncertainty magnified danger until it occupied the entire landscape.

The moon rose late—a thin, distorted crescent over the ridge. It did not illuminate the road so much as sharpen the darkness around it. Tree trunks flashed white, then disappeared. Granite outcrops resembled crouched animals. The wind moved in the pines with the long respiration of the sea.

At eight he reached Miller’s Crossing, where the road forded the San Gregorio. The water came only to the mare’s knees, yet the current shone black, broken into quicksilver around the stones. On the farther bank Lockwood saw fresh tracks where a horse had climbed dripping from the stream.

One horse.

He called again: “Chino!”

The wind and water answered.

Beyond the crossing the road ascended for six miles to Digger’s Gap. It was here, Lockwood knew, that an ambush would most easily be made. The road passed between a steep bank and a drop into the river gorge. A single rider hidden among the rocks could command two hundred yards of approach and escape by any of three cattle trails.

Lockwood took the carbine across the saddle.

Now the world became a succession of sounds. The scrape of the mare’s shoes. The creak of leather. The wind’s rush and pause. The infinitesimal ticking of insects in the grass. Once, far up the slope, a stone clicked against another and went bounding downward.

He stopped.

Silence.

He went forward.

The moral struggle was over, or seemed to be. Fear, fatigue, and pain had simplified him. He no longer thought of Felice, of Hicks, of judgment, or of himself. There was only Chino somewhere upon the mountain and another man who intended to kill him. The problem had become physical, clean, direct. Reach him. Warn him. If necessary, fight.

This simplification brought relief. Action had burned away the vapor of desire. Lockwood wondered, dimly, how he had ever allowed Felice to become the center of his life. Against the immensities of the night—the black forest, the remote stars, the miles of empty trail—her glances and half-spoken phrases appeared trivial, almost absurd.

Yet this detachment was temporary. It was the anesthesia of danger, not cure.

The road bent sharply around a granite shoulder. Ahead lay a straight ascent of perhaps eighty yards, pale beneath the crescent moon. At its upper end the trees met over the road, forming a tunnel of darkness.

The mare lifted her head.

Lockwood heard hoofbeats.

They were faint at first, descending toward him—a measured walk, then a stumble, then the metallic strike of a shoe upon stone. A rider was coming from the Gap.

Lockwood reined the mare against the bank and waited.

The hoofbeats drew nearer. He could see nothing within the black tunnel, but the sound was unmistakable. One horse. Slow. Cautious.

The Reno Kid would naturally approach so.

Lockwood eased the hammer of the carbine to full cock.

His mind raced through the facts. Chino had left town first. The Kid had gone ahead. If one rider now descended alone, it must be the outlaw returning after the ambush. Chino would be behind—robbed, perhaps dead. The approaching man might carry the payroll. He might shoot on sight.

The hoofbeats stopped.

For one second there was no movement anywhere.

Then came a faint click.

A revolver cocking!

Lockwood’s nerves discharged all at once.

“Throw up your hands!” he shouted.

The answer was a violent movement in the dark. Horse and rider lunged sideways; metal flashed.

Lockwood fired.

The carbine’s explosion tore the night apart. Flame leaped before his face; the mare reared, striking the bank; echoes crashed from ridge to ridge in enormous, diminishing salvos.

From the road ahead came a cry and the heavy fall of a body.

Lockwood dragged the mare under control. Powder smoke stung his nostrils. For an instant he remained fixed in the saddle, the carbine raised, listening for a return shot.

None came.

He dismounted, dropped the reins, and advanced. His injured foot gave way under him; he caught at the bank, recovered, and limped onward.

A horse stood across the road, trembling. A man lay beneath its shoulder.

“Don’t move,” Lockwood said. “I’ll shoot again.”

The man groaned.

Something in the sound stopped Lockwood’s breath.

He went closer. Moonlight slid between the branches and touched the rider’s face—the black mustache, the broad cheekbones, the familiar blue flannel shirt.

“Chino!”

Lockwood fell to his knees beside him.

Chino’s eyes opened. They were dazed but conscious.

“Boss,” he whispered. “Why—”

The rest dissolved in a gasp.

The carbine dropped from Lockwood’s hands. For a second he could neither think nor move. The whole pursuit—the telegram, the gray horse, the imagined ambush—collapsed inward like a mine stope after the timbers are shot away.

He had warned Chino of the Reno Kid by shooting him himself.

“Where are you hit?”

Chino’s hand pressed his left side. Blood darkened the shirt below the ribs.

Lockwood tore the cloth open. In the uncertain light he could see only the wound’s entrance, a small black hole with blood welling from it.

“Did it go through?”

“I don’t know.”

“Can you move your legs?”

“Yes.”

“Who was behind you? The man on the gray?”

Chino stared without comprehension. “No man.”

“The Reno Kid—he followed you from town.”

“I see gray horse by stable. Rider go south.”

South.

The hostler had been wrong. Or Lockwood had heard only what fear expected to hear.

“Then why did you cock your gun?”

“My horse stumble. I catch the saddle ring.”

The click—nothing but the snap of metal against metal.

Lockwood sat back upon his heels. A coldness spread through him, deeper than fear.

He had shot Chino.

Not by design. Every circumstance proved it an accident. Yet beneath the accident lay the earlier wish, the moment in the office, the hidden desire that Chino should die. That desire now rose like a witness from concealment and accused him.

Had he truly mistaken Chino for the outlaw? Incontestably.

Had some deeper instinct recognized the rider and fired? Impossible.

Was it impossible?

The question entered and could not be expelled.

Chino moved, groaning. The sound restored Lockwood to action. He folded a bandage from his neckerchief, packed it against the wound, and bound it tightly around Chino’s body. There was no exit hole he could find. The bullet might be lodged in the muscles; it might have entered the abdomen. If so, every minute mattered.

The bay mare had remained near the bank. With enormous difficulty Lockwood raised Chino, got him upright, and half lifted, half pushed him into the saddle. Chino sagged forward over the pommel.

“You hold on,” Lockwood said. “Hear me? Hold on.”

“Yes, boss.”

Lockwood took the reins and started toward Blue Arroyo on foot.

The camp lay ten miles beyond the Gap. The road rose for another two, then descended. Lockwood’s wounded foot could scarcely bear the ground. Nevertheless he walked. There was no alternative.

Now—step by step, yard by yard—the night ride became a march of expiation. The moon sank. Clouds covered the stars. Chino’s blood soaked through the bandage and spotted the mare’s flank. At every quarter mile Lockwood stopped to tighten the cloth, to give him water, to make certain he still breathed.

Once Chino whispered, “The money.”

“It’s safe.”

“Saddlebags.”

“I have them.”

“Men get paid.”

“Yes.”

Even wounded, perhaps dying, he remembered the payroll.

Lockwood bent his head.

Near midnight they reached the Gap. From there the camp trail led downward through the pines. Lockwood fired three shots at intervals—the emergency signal agreed upon for accidents underground or on the road.

For a long time there was no answer.

Then, far off, a rifle replied.

Lights moved among the trees below. Voices called. Hicks had sent men searching after dark, and now four of them came up the trail with a lantern and a litter.

Hicks himself walked ahead.

He took in the scene with one sweep of his cold gray eyes: Chino on the mare, Lockwood bloodied and limping, the carbine, the saddlebags.

“What happened?”

“I shot him.”

The men stopped.

Hicks’s gaze fixed upon Lockwood’s face and seemed to penetrate it like a scalpel.

“Accident?”

“I thought he was the Reno Kid.”

“Is he alive?”

“Yes.”

“Then explanations wait. Put him on the litter.”

No more was said.

## VI. The Escape

They reached Blue Arroyo after one in the morning.

The camp was awake. Men stood in groups outside the bunkhouse; lanterns swung from hooks; the stamps had been shut down, and the resulting silence seemed louder than the mill. News had travelled ahead of the litter in that mysterious fashion by which disaster announces itself. Felice waited before her cabin, dressed, her hair coiled high, a blanket drawn around her shoulders.

When she saw Chino, she did not cry out. She stepped backward and pressed one hand against the door.

The doctor ordered the men to carry him inside. Chino was placed upon the bed. Lamps were lit; water heated; instruments laid out upon a towel. Hicks cleared the room of everyone except Felice and Lockwood.

“You stay,” the doctor said to Lockwood. “I may need help.”

Lockwood stood near the wall, one hand braced against it. His boot was full of blood. He felt neither pain nor fatigue now, only a profound suspense.

Felice watched while the doctor cut away Chino’s shirt. Her face was pale but composed. Once she looked at Lockwood, and the look was so strange—so intent, so searching—that he turned away.

The doctor examined the wound with his fingers, washed it, and followed the bullet’s path with a probe. Chino, revived by brandy, clenched his teeth but made no sound.

“Well?” Hicks asked from the doorway.

“Can’t tell yet. Bullet struck a rib and ran around the body. If it stayed outside the cavity, he’ll be all right. If it turned inward—”

He left the sentence unfinished.

Felice crossed herself.

The doctor asked for more hot water. Felice went to the kitchen. Lockwood followed, though he did not know why. Perhaps he intended to confess the whole chain of events; perhaps he wished only to escape the sight of Chino’s blood.

In the kitchen a single lamp burned beside the stove. Steam clouded the windows. Felice set a kettle upon the table, then turned.

“You shoot him,” she said.

It was not a question.

“Yes.”

“You know it was Chino?”

“No. I thought he was a robber.”

She continued to look at him. Her green eyes, which had once seemed inexhaustible in suggestion, were perfectly clear now.

“You thought it was robber,” she repeated.

“There was a warning. The Reno Kid knew about the gold. I rode after Chino. On the road, in the dark, I heard him coming. I called to him. He moved—I fired.”

Felice came one step nearer.

“No,” she said softly. “You shoot Chino.”

Lockwood stared.

“I’ve told you.”

A smile appeared at the corners of her mouth. It was slight, secret, almost tender.

“For me,” she said.

The room contracted around him.

“What?”

“You no need tell lies to me.” Her voice fell still lower. “I understand.”

She glanced toward the bedroom door, then placed her hand upon his sleeve.

The touch was light. It had the effect of a blow.

“You think now he die,” she whispered. “After—when wait little—we go away. Yes?”

For several seconds Lockwood could not comprehend the words. He heard them individually, yet their meaning seemed too enormous, too grotesque, to assemble. Then suddenly they sprang together.

She believed he had deliberately tried to kill her husband.

She approved.

The revelation came not gradually but with the violent illumination of lightning. In that instant the woman he had loved disappeared. Or rather, what disappeared was the woman his desire had created—the lonely, subtle, tender creature who understood him, who suffered in silence, who waited helplessly for fate to free them both. That woman had never existed. In her place stood Felice: beautiful still, composed, practical, and willing to step across Chino’s body toward whatever future she imagined Lockwood could provide.

His passion did not diminish. It died.

The extinction was absolute. One moment the image of her had filled his life; the next it was an empty shape, a painted screen kicked down by a single word. He felt no grief. He felt horror—then an immense, almost intoxicating relief.

Yet it is only fair to observe that Felice, too, had deceived herself. She had seen Lockwood’s attention, his silence, his jealousy, his presence near her cabin. She had read his guilty wish more accurately than he had supposed. Knowing that wish, she now interpreted the shot according to it. The treachery she revealed was not born in the kitchen; it had been nourished by the same glances and evasions upon which Lockwood had built his romance. Each had mistaken the other, and each mistake had encouraged the darker truth.

But at that moment Lockwood was incapable of such distinctions.

He took her hand from his sleeve.

“Listen to me,” he said.

His voice was so altered that her smile vanished.

“I rode to save him. Do you understand? I got the warning after he left. For one minute—” He stopped, mastered himself, and went on. “For one minute I thought of letting him go. I knew what might happen, and I did nothing. Then I went after him. I rode to Campo Seco and back. I shot him by mistake.”

She watched, now uncertain.

“I don’t believe.”

“I don’t care what you believe.”

“You love me.”

“No.”

The word fell between them, simple and final.

Felice’s face changed. The warmth went out of it. Her eyes narrowed, and for the first time Lockwood saw in them neither mystery nor tenderness, but calculation wounded in its vanity.

“You afraid,” she said.

“Yes,” he answered. “But not of you.”

From the bedroom came the clink of an instrument against the basin. The doctor called for the lamp to be brought nearer.

Felice reached for the kettle. Lockwood took it before she could touch it and carried it into the other room.

Chino lay with his eyes closed. His face had gone gray under the brown skin. The doctor was bending over the wound, his sleeves rolled above the elbows. Hicks stood at the foot of the bed, immovable as a post.

“Hold the light,” said the doctor.

Lockwood held it.

The probe followed the bullet’s course along the rib. There was blood, a small quantity; then the doctor’s forceps closed upon something. He drew steadily. Chino groaned once.

A flattened bullet dropped into the basin.

The doctor straightened and exhaled.

“Lucky,” he said. “Struck the eighth rib, travelled under the skin, and stopped in the back muscles. Never entered the cavity.”

“He’ll live?” Lockwood asked.

“He’ll be cursing us by breakfast and walking in three weeks.”

The tension broke. Hicks’s shoulders lowered by an almost imperceptible fraction. One of the men outside laughed when the news was passed through the door; then the yard filled with voices.

Felice sat beside the bed and took Chino’s hand.

The gesture might have been tenderness. It might have been fear, gratitude, possession, or merely the instinct to resume a role suddenly restored to her. Lockwood did not attempt to interpret it. He had done with interpretations.

The doctor now turned his attention to Lockwood’s foot.

“You’ve made a mess of this,” he said. “Sit down.”

While the boot was cut away, Hicks came to stand beside him.

“Your report,” the manager said.

Lockwood gave it without excuse: the telegram, the ride to town, the hostler’s information, the road, the approaching horse, the supposed click of a weapon, the shot. He omitted only the pause before he had ordered the buckskin saddled. That belonged to another account, one that no manager could audit.

When he had finished, Hicks considered him for a long moment.

“You acted late,” he said.

“The telegram came late.”

“I don’t mean the telegram.”

Lockwood looked up sharply.

But Hicks’s expression gave nothing away. Perhaps he referred only to the pursuit; perhaps his cold intelligence had divined more. He took the payroll saddlebags from the corner.

“The money is intact,” he continued. “Chino will recover. The sheriff caught the Reno Kid at Morrison’s ranch at nine tonight. He never came near the Gap.”

“I see.”

“You should not have fired without identifying the target.”

“No.”

“You should not have ridden in your condition.”

“No.”

“Nevertheless, if the warning had been correct, your action might have saved Chino’s life.”

Lockwood gave a short, bitter laugh. “Instead I nearly killed him.”

“Yes.” Hicks’s eyes rested upon him. “A man may do the right thing for mixed reasons and still do it badly. That does not make it the wrong thing.”

He turned to go, then paused at the door.

“You have had a lucky escape.”

Lockwood glanced toward the bed. Chino breathed evenly. Felice sat beside him, her face lowered.

“Yes,” Lockwood said. “A lucky escape.”

Hicks could not know the full meaning of the words.

Dawn came before the doctor finished bandaging Lockwood’s foot. The first light touched the cabin windows, gray at first, then faintly yellow. Outside, the camp resumed its order. The cook struck the breakfast triangle. Men drifted toward the mess house, talking in low voices. At six the mill foreman opened the water gate.

The stamps began again.

One—then another—then the whole battery, dropping in ponderous sequence until the familiar thunder filled the valley. The sound entered the cabin, shook the cups upon the shelf, vibrated in the floor and walls. It was the pulse of Blue Arroyo, indifferent to love, temptation, suffering, and escape.

Lockwood stepped outside on crutches.

The storm of the night had passed, though there had been no rain. The pines stood black against the growing sky. Mist lay in the San Gregorio gorge. Smoke rose from the cookhouse chimney, carrying the odors of coffee, wood fire, and hot bread. Far up the ridge an owl gave its last measured call; nearer at hand the stable horses stamped for their grain.

The world had not changed. But it had been restored to proportion.

The mine was there—the levels to inspect, the south drift to survey, the pump to overhaul, the payroll to distribute. Hicks would return to San Francisco. Chino would recover. Felice would remain his wife, and Lockwood would arrange, as soon as it could be done without cruelty, for Chino’s transfer to another property. Not to possess her. Not to punish her. Simply because the three of them could not continue together in that narrow camp where every path crossed every other and silence magnified all things.

He looked toward the eastern ridge.

The sun had not yet risen, but the sky above it was clear and pale. He understood now how close he had come—not merely to killing Chino, but to consenting to his death; not merely to betraying a trusted man, but to binding himself to a woman who could welcome murder as proof of devotion. The bullet had travelled around the rib and stopped. His intention, too, had travelled near the vital part of him and, by some last resistance, had failed to enter.

Lucky. The word was inadequate, almost frivolous.

Yet no other word would serve.

He had escaped Chino’s death. He had escaped Felice. Above all, he had escaped the man he had been for those twenty seconds in the office.

Behind him the cabin door opened. Felice stood upon the threshold.

Their eyes met.

There was no signal now, no hidden confession, no magnetic entanglement. She was only a woman in the cold dawn, tired, frightened, and unreadable; he was only the superintendent, bandaged, ashamed, and alive to his responsibilities.

She closed the door.

Lockwood turned toward the office and moved slowly across the yard while the stamps roared on.

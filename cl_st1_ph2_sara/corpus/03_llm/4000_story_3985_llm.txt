## THE MAN BETWEEN THE POSTS

### Dramatis Personæ

**CLARENCE TRESILLIAN**, younger son of the Earl of Runnymede. A Greek god with no marketable qualifications.

**ISABEL RACKSTRAW**, an angel with opinions.

**MR. JABEZ RACKSTRAW**, her father. A multi-millionaire City man, Radical politician, and licensed football maniac.

**THE EARL OF RUNNYMEDE**, a peer of the realm who has mislaid most of his income.

**LADY RUNNYMEDE**, his wife. American by birth, decisive by temperament, and accustomed to meeting disaster with a hatchet.

**MR. PUGSBY**, secretary of the Houndsditch Wednesday Football Club.

**VARIOUS ATHLETES, JOURNALISTS, DOMESTICS, AND PERSONS WHO KNOW THE OFFSIDE RULE.**

There. In the old days an author would have consumed seven pages introducing these people one by one, comparing Isabel’s eyes to woodland pools, Clarence’s shoulders to those of the Apollo Belvedere, and Mr. Rackstraw’s chin to something out of the later Stone Age. But the modern editor has no reverence. He stands over the artist with a watch in one hand and a blue pencil in the other.

Brevity, brevity. Let us on.

It was at the Countess of Sevenoaks’s charity bazaar in Belgrave Square that Clarence Tresillian first beheld Isabel Rackstraw.

The bazaar was in aid of Distressed Gentlewomen, though a glance at the gentlemen present suggested that distress had not been confined to one sex. Young men were being compelled to purchase pincushions, hand-painted blotting-paper, raffia baskets, and small china objects of a nature which Providence had wisely left obscure. Clarence had already acquired a woollen cigar-case, three buttonhooks, a photograph of the Matterhorn, a bottle of scent called Passion’s Whisper, and a stuffed rat in a red jacket.

He bore these reverses with the composure of his race.

Then Isabel looked at him across the lavender stall, and the world became a better, nobler place. The stuffed rat ceased to matter.

She was tall, fair, and possessed of that dewy softness of eye which has sent stronger men than Clarence into the bouillon. At the moment she was selling paper flowers at half a guinea each. She held out a rose.

“You will buy one?”

Clarence, who would have bought the Albert Hall if she had been selling it, inclined his head.

“Delighted.”

“One guinea.”

“The label says half a guinea.”

“The label,” said Isabel, “was written before lunch.”

Clarence paid. Their fingers touched. Somewhere in the dim machinery of the universe a cog slipped.

“Thank you,” said Isabel.

“Not at all.”

This was not much as dialogue, but many lifelong entanglements have begun with less. Romeo, had he possessed Clarence’s restraint, might have been spared a good deal of unpleasantness at the end of the fifth act.

They met again near the bran tub. Five minutes later Clarence had won a tin whistle, a framed text, and a wax banana. Ten minutes later they were talking as if they had known one another from infancy. At the end of half an hour Clarence was in love.

At the end of forty minutes he learned that she was the only daughter of Jabez Rackstraw.

The name struck him like a bullet.

Jabez Rackstraw was known throughout England as the man who had made twenty million pounds out of breakfast foods, municipal lighting, Argentine tramways, and the general inability of the public to read small print. He was also the most violent Radical in Parliament. He had once declared that the House of Lords should be converted into a public swimming-bath, and that every duke ought to be taught a useful trade, preferably road-mending.

To Clarence, however, these were details. Love is a steamroller. Politics, finance, and social prejudice lie down before it and make little squeaking noises.

“I say,” he said, as Isabel pinned the paper rose to his coat, “would you care to come to Ranelagh on Thursday?”

“Yes.”

“Capital.”

“My father may object.”

“Why?”

“He objects to nearly everything.”

Clarence considered this.

“Would Friday be better?”

Isabel laughed. The sound went through him like a corkscrew.

“No. I mean he may object to you.”

“But he hasn’t met me.”

“That would not handicap Father. He distrusts the aristocracy on principle.”

“Oh?” said Clarence, with the mild concern of a man informed that it may rain in Peru.

“He says the hereditary system is a boil on the body politic.”

“I’m only a younger son.”

“He regards younger sons as the pus.”

Clarence adjusted the wax banana beneath his arm.

“Still,” he said, “Thursday at three?”

“Thursday at three.”

Such was the beginning.

There should now follow a lyrical account of the succeeding weeks: of drives beneath whispering trees, of twilight meetings, of hands clasped in the moonlight, of the pale stars looking down like silver trouser-buttons upon two young hearts. I could do it. Nobody handles a pale star with greater technical finish. But space presses.

Brevity.

Clarence and Isabel loved each other.

Mr. Rackstraw did not know.

The Earl of Runnymede did.

And the Earl was worried.

One morning, while Clarence was eating kidneys in the dining-room of the family house in Grosvenor Square, his father entered with the expression of a man who has just found a black beetle in the treasury chest.

“Clarence.”

“Good morning, Father.”

“I have bad news.”

Clarence lowered his newspaper. The sporting page had been discussing the doubtful form of Houndsditch Wednesday’s goalkeeper, and he disliked interruptions while national affairs were in progress.

“Yes?”

“We are ruined.”

“Completely?”

“Practically.”

“Too bad.”

The Earl stared. He had expected his son to reel, grip the table, or at least stop eating kidney.

“Do you understand? We are ruined.”

“You said.”

“The South American investments have collapsed.”

“All of them?”

“Every one.”

“That seems careless.”

“They were considered exceptionally sound.”

Clarence returned to the sporting page.

The Earl’s voice rose.

“Our income has fallen to fourteen thousand a year!”

This time Clarence looked up.

The Runnymedes had hitherto existed on rather more than sixty thousand. Fourteen thousand was not poverty in the strict dictionary sense, but to them it represented something between a famine and an invasion of locusts. At fourteen thousand a year one cannot maintain three houses, a yacht, two shooting-boxes, six hunters, a private cricket-ground, and an elderly aunt at Monte Carlo. Economies become necessary. Chilling word—economies. It creeps into a home like damp.

Lady Runnymede entered briskly. She was a small woman whose chin suggested that, had George Washington tried any nonsense with her, the United States would still be a colony.

“Well?” she said. “Have you told him?”

“I have.”

“Has he fainted?”

“No.”

“I never knew an Englishman who would faint when it could be useful. Clarence, your father and I have been talking this thing over. There’s only one course.”

“Sell Aunt Agatha?”

“Work.”

Clarence’s fork stopped halfway to his mouth.

“Work?”

“Work.”

“What sort?”

“Any sort. Get out and hustle. Make money.”

Clarence looked pained.

“I don’t think I know how.”

“Then learn.”

“I was at Oxford.”

“Exactly. You’ve had every handicap. But you’re healthy, you’re twenty-seven, and you have the shoulders of a freight elevator. There must be something you can do.”

Clarence reflected. He could shoot, ride, dance, play polo, select a cigar, and tell when a wine had been chilled too long. He could also keep goal.

At school and at the university he had been a phenomenon between the posts. For three seasons Oxford’s goal had been less a goal than a sealed tomb. Forwards had hurled themselves against Clarence and rebounded like peas from a cathedral.

“I can play footer,” he said.

Lady Runnymede’s eyes sharpened.

“Professionally?”

“I suppose so.”

“Then do it.”

The Earl emitted a sound like a dying harmonium.

“A Tresillian—a professional footballer!”

“Why not?” said his wife. “Your grandfather made money out of mines.”

“That was different.”

“He never went down them.”

The Earl paced the room.

“The newspapers—”

“Needn’t know. Clarence can use another name.”

“What name?”

Lady Runnymede surveyed her son.

“Jones.”

“Why Jones?”

“Because nobody has ever looked twice at a Jones.”

Thus, with the simple majesty of great decisions, Clarence Tresillian became George Jones.

Mr. Pugsby, secretary of Houndsditch Wednesday, received him without enthusiasm.

Houndsditch Wednesday occupied premises behind a fried-fish shop in the Mile End Road. Its office contained three chairs, two cracked cups, a calendar displaying a young woman in insufficient bathing costume, and a framed photograph of the club’s triumph over Clapton Orient in a snowstorm in 1903.

Mr. Pugsby was a round man with a cauliflower ear and the suspicious eye of one who has been offered too many promising inside-lefts by their aunts.

“You want a trial?”

“Yes.”

“Played before?”

“A little.”

“Where?”

“Oxford.”

Mr. Pugsby’s expression suggested that Clarence had said Mesopotamia.

“Amateur stuff.”

“Certainly.”

“We want a pro.”

“I require money.”

This touched Mr. Pugsby. It was the correct professional spirit.

“You don’t look like a Jones.”

“No?”

“You look like one of them chaps in cigarette advertisements.”

“Sorry.”

“Can you stop a ball?”

“Usually.”

Mr. Pugsby sneered. Ten minutes later, on the practice ground, he ceased to sneer. After twenty minutes he had become reverent. At the end of half an hour he was telephoning the club chairman in a voice which ripped the air into a million shreds.

Jones was engaged at once.

His first appearance for Houndsditch Wednesday was against Tottenham Hotspur. Before the match the newspapers described him as an unknown amateur recruit. After the match they called him the Find of the Century, the Human Wall, the Miracle of Mile End, and Britain’s Answer to the Decline of Civilisation.

He saved a penalty in the first five minutes. In the seventeenth he flung himself full length and turned a shot round the post with the tips of three fingers and a portion of eyebrow. In the second half he stopped six shots in forty seconds, picked a seventh out of the air, and looked faintly surprised when the opposing centre-forward collided with him and had to be removed on a stretcher.

Houndsditch won by one goal to nil.

Clarence left the field amid a roar which caused elderly horses in Whitechapel to sit down abruptly.

He became, in the phrase of the sporting press, a Gibraltar among goalkeepers.

Office boys whose grandmothers had already died four times developed fresh bereavements on the afternoons when Houndsditch played at home. Clerks vanished from banks. Respectable fathers climbed fences. Poets wrote odes to Jones’s left hand. A Cabinet Minister, questioned on foreign policy, replied absently that what the country needed was more weight at half-back.

And among the most fervent worshippers of the mysterious Jones was Jabez Rackstraw.

Rackstraw’s politics were violent, his business methods Napoleonic, and his football emotions beyond human control. He owned nine hundred and twelve football programmes, the boots worn by a famous Scottish international in 1888, two goal-post splinters, a referee’s whistle with a disputed pedigree, and the ball used in the legendary Cup Final when Bolton scored after a dog ran on the field.

But the jewel of his collection was not in his possession.

It belonged to Sir Septimus Barge, the Manchester cotton magnate: the original cap worn by the goalkeeper in the first international match between England and Scotland. Brown, moth-eaten, and resembling a deceased tea-cosy, it was to Rackstraw what the Holy Grail had been to medieval knights.

Sir Septimus would not sell.

“I wouldn’t take fifty thousand pounds for it,” he had said.

Rackstraw had offered sixty.

Sir Septimus had laughed.

From that hour relations between the two men had been strained.

On Saturdays Rackstraw stood behind the Houndsditch goal, purple with passion, bellowing advice. He loved Jones. He worshipped Jones. He spoke of Jones at breakfast, luncheon, dinner, and during debates in Parliament. Isabel, who knew the secret, listened with mixed emotions.

“That fellow Jones,” Rackstraw told her one evening, “is the finest working man in England.”

“Is he a working man?”

“Of course. He earns his wages by the sweat of his brow.”

“I thought goalkeepers tried not to perspire. It makes the ball slippery.”

“Don’t be frivolous. Jones is labour in its noblest form. No inherited title. No idle luxury. No pampered upbringing. Just a plain son of the people.”

Isabel’s spoon trembled against her coffee-cup.

“What would you say if he wished to marry me?”

Rackstraw stared.

“Jones?”

“Yes.”

“My dear child, don’t be absurd.”

“You admire him.”

“As a goalkeeper.”

“Not as a son-in-law?”

“A goalkeeper is one thing. A son-in-law is another.”

“What sort of man must I marry?”

“A worker. A fighter. A man with iron in his soul. Not some chinless puppy from a diseased aristocracy.”

Isabel thought of Clarence’s chin, which was excellent, and was silent.

Love, meanwhile, did not run smoothly. It took taxicabs, missed trains, and was frequently held up by fixtures. Clarence could see Isabel only on Sundays and occasional evenings, for professional football is a jealous mistress and insists on early bed.

They became engaged privately beside the Serpentine.

“I’ll speak to your father,” said Clarence.

“When?”

“After the championship final.”

“Why after?”

“He’s likely to be in a better temper if we win.”

“You think football will influence him?”

“My darling,” said Clarence, “football is the only thing that does.”

The championship final—Houndsditch Wednesday against Manchester United—was fixed for the last Saturday in April. England ceased to conduct business. Men who had not previously known that Houndsditch possessed a football club discovered ancestral connections with the district. Red-and-white ribbons appeared in buttonholes from Whitechapel to Putney. The King was reported to have inquired discreetly about the odds.

Then catastrophe strode onstage.

On the Thursday morning Clarence awoke with a temperature of a hundred and three.

The club doctor pronounced influenza.

“I can play,” said Clarence.

“You can’t stand.”

“I could lean against a post.”

“You’d die.”

“Would that affect my form?”

“You’re not playing.”

The news spread through London like fire through a fireworks factory. The Stock Exchange weakened. A man in Holborn bit a policeman. Three thousand people surrounded the Houndsditch offices and demanded the doctor’s execution.

Rackstraw received the tidings in his private room in the City. He sat motionless for eleven seconds. Then he rang for his secretary.

“Wilkins.”

“Yes, sir?”

“Cancel everything.”

“The board meeting?”

“Everything.”

“The deputation from Leeds?”

“Everything.”

“Your speech at the Free Trade League?”

“Burn it.”

“Yes, sir.”

“And find me the address of the doctor who says Jones can’t play.”

That afternoon he sent Clarence grapes, beef tea, champagne, six medical specialists, and a note reading:

FOR HEAVEN’S SAKE RECOVER.

Clarence did not recover.

Without Jones, Houndsditch Wednesday was a body without a soul, a pudding without suet, a government department without sealing-wax. Their reserve goalkeeper, a willing fellow named Higgs, had courage, enthusiasm, and the tactical intelligence of a damp sponge.

Manchester United won four goals to one.

The effect on Houndsditch was appalling. Public-houses closed from grief. A tram conductor forgot to collect fares. Grown men sat on kerbstones, rocking gently. Mr. Pugsby was found at midnight addressing a stuffed owl.

Rackstraw returned home with fire flashing from his eyes.

“Robbery!” he roared. “A hollow, fraudulent, contemptible victory!”

“It seems quite conclusive,” said Isabel. “Four goals to one.”

“Conclusive? With Jones ill? If Wellington had had influenza, would you call Waterloo conclusive?”

“I’ve never thought about it.”

“I shall challenge them to a return match.”

“There are league rules.”

“Rules!” Rackstraw spoke the word as though it were something found beneath a stone. “I’ll arrange a private match. I’ll make Barge accept.”

“How?”

“The cap.”

Isabel understood. When football collectors talk of “the cap,” no further identification is necessary.

Two days later Rackstraw and Sir Septimus Barge met at the Vulcan Club.

They began with insults and ended with terms.

A private match would be played three weeks later at a neutral ground. Rackstraw would put up his most precious relic, the ball from the dog-interrupted Bolton final. Sir Septimus would stake the first international goalkeeper’s cap. The winner would take both.

“Provided Jones plays,” said Sir Septimus.

“He’ll play.”

“And provided both teams are at full strength.”

“They will be.”

Sir Septimus smiled unpleasantly.

“Then prepare to lose your ball.”

Rackstraw laughed like a Chinese cracker exploding in a biscuit-tin.

The stage was set.

Three weeks is a long time in romance and a short time in training. Clarence recovered. He saved shots, punched medicine-balls, ran round the ground, and ate chops. The sporting papers published daily reports of his condition. His pulse became a matter of national interest.

On the Wednesday before the match he called at Rackstraw’s house in Park Lane.

He came not as Jones but as the Honourable Clarence Tresillian, in a morning coat of exquisite cut. The transformation was substantial. Jones, in sweater and shorts, looked like a splendid young workman. Clarence, in a silk hat, looked capable of inheriting large tracts of Scotland without moving a muscle.

Rackstraw received him in the library.

“Yes?”

“My name is Tresillian.”

“I know. Runnymede’s son.”

“Exactly.”

“What do you want?”

“Your daughter.”

Rackstraw sprang from his chair like a terrier released from a cat.

“What?”

“I wish to marry Isabel.”

“You!”

“Yes.”

“A peer’s son!”

“A younger one.”

“An idler!”

“Technically.”

“A parasite!”

“So some people say.”

“A piece of social wreckage with a title at one end and debts at the other!”

Clarence considered.

“I haven’t many debts.”

“Don’t quibble. How dare you come here?”

“By appointment.”

Rackstraw struck the desk.

“My daughter shall marry a working man.”

“I work.”

“At what?”

Clarence paused. The moment had come, but he was not a man to spoil an effect by hurrying it.

“I keep goal,” he said.

Rackstraw’s mouth opened.

Clarence removed his gloves.

“For Houndsditch Wednesday.”

There was a silence.

A coal shifted in the grate with the embarrassed air of one who wishes it were elsewhere.

“You,” whispered Rackstraw, “are Jones?”

“Yes.”

“Jones?”

“Yes.”

“The Jones?”

“There are several, of course, but I imagine you mean me.”

Rackstraw walked round him twice. He peered at Clarence’s face as though searching for a concealed moustache. Then his expression hardened.

“This is a trick.”

“No.”

“You are the son of an earl.”

“I’ve never denied it.”

“You called yourself Jones.”

“My mother suggested it.”

“Why?”

“We needed money.”

Rackstraw recoiled as if Clarence had uttered an obscenity.

“Needed money? Your father has fourteen thousand a year.”

“So I understand.”

“And you call that needing money?”

“It has caused inconvenience.”

Rackstraw breathed heavily. His political convictions rallied like troops after a surprise attack.

“No. Absolutely not. I admire Jones. Jones is a hero. But Clarence Tresillian is a useless excrescence on the face of democracy. You cannot marry Isabel.”

“I see.”

“You will leave this house.”

“Certainly.”

“And you will understand that the subject is closed.”

“Quite.”

Clarence picked up his gloves.

“Of course,” he said, “there is the match on Saturday.”

Rackstraw’s eyes narrowed.

“What about it?”

“I may not play.”

The millionaire became rigid.

“Not play?”

“No.”

“You are fit.”

“Perfectly.”

“You must play.”

“Must?”

“You gave your word to Pugsby.”

“To do my best for the club. But this is a private match. My contract does not cover it.”

Rackstraw’s face passed through crimson and arrived at purple.

“You can’t refuse. The arrangements—the crowd—the newspapers—”

“A pity.”

“The cap!”

“Ah, yes. The cap.”

Rackstraw came round the desk.

“Listen, Tresillian. If you don’t play, Houndsditch will lose.”

“Probably.”

“Higgs couldn’t stop a balloon.”

“He has uncertain hands.”

“I shall lose the Bolton ball.”

“And fail to win the cap.”

Rackstraw’s lips moved silently.

Clarence looked at the clock.

“I must be going.”

“Wait.”

Clarence waited.

Rackstraw drew a deep breath.

“Are you attempting to blackmail me?”

The word hung in the room, dark and melodramatic. Outside, a motor-horn bleated in Park Lane. Somewhere in the house a maid dropped a spoon.

Clarence regarded the question judicially.

“I suppose that is one way of putting it.”

“You threaten to throw the match unless I consent to your marriage with Isabel?”

“I shouldn’t throw it. That would be dishonest. I simply shouldn’t take part.”

“You know what the result would be.”

“I have my suspicions.”

Rackstraw stared at him.

The gaze lasted so long that a less well-bred young man might have shuffled. Clarence did not shuffle. The Tresillians had faced Norman invaders, Tudor monarchs, hostile creditors, and American heiresses. They did not shuffle.

Then an extraordinary change came over Jabez Rackstraw.

The purple faded from his cheeks. His brows relaxed. His jaw softened. Slowly, like sunlight appearing over a gasworks, admiration dawned in his eyes.

“You cold-blooded young devil,” he said.

Clarence inclined his head.

“You come into my house—my house—and put the screw on me over the thing I value most in the world.”

“Second most,” said Clarence politely. “There is Isabel.”

Rackstraw waved this aside.

“You take advantage of confidential information, exploit my position, threaten financial and emotional loss, and demand a controlling interest in my family.”

“That sums it up.”

“Do you know,” said Rackstraw, “I’ve been looking for a man like you for fifteen years?”

Clarence blinked.

“My business has grown too large. I need someone young. Someone with nerve. Someone who understands leverage. The fellows in the office are clerks. Excellent clerks, but they think a contract is a moral obligation. You—”

He jabbed a finger at Clarence.

“You have vision.”

“Thank you.”

“You have audacity.”

“Very kind.”

“You have the instincts of a first-class City man.”

Clarence looked modestly at the carpet.

“I’ve had little experience.”

“Experience can be acquired. Ruthlessness cannot. It is a gift.”

He began to pace the room, his hands behind his back.

“The wheat market has been on my mind. There is a position developing in Chicago. Properly handled, it might be possible to corner the available supply.”

“Would that be legal?”

Rackstraw stopped.

“You have much to learn.”

“Yes.”

“But the foundations are there. You see the essential point. You know when the other fellow has the goods and when he hasn’t. You had the goods just now. I hadn’t. You squeezed.”

“I did rather.”

Rackstraw turned abruptly.

“You may marry Isabel.”

“Thank you.”

“On conditions.”

“Name them.”

“You play on Saturday.”

“Naturally.”

“You play as you have never played before.”

“I’ll do my best.”

“Your best? I don’t want your best. I want miracles. I want Barge broken. I want Manchester forwards carried home in small parcels. I want that cap.”

“I’ll bear it in mind.”

“And Monday morning you come to my office.”

“What for?”

“Work.”

Clarence winced almost imperceptibly.

Rackstraw saw it and grinned.

“Yes, work. Real work. Nine-thirty.”

“Could we make it ten?”

“Nine-thirty.”

“Very well.”

They shook hands.

At that moment Isabel entered. She looked from one to the other.

“Well?”

Rackstraw pointed to Clarence.

“You can have him.”

Isabel’s eyes shone.

“Oh, Father!”

“Don’t thank me. He has blackmailed me, insulted my principles, and forced me into surrender.”

“Clarence!”

“Nothing serious,” said Clarence.

Rackstraw slapped his future son-in-law on the shoulder with such force that a lesser man would have entered the fireplace.

“He’s hot stuff. The peerage is rotten, but there are exceptions. Be ready on Saturday, my boy.”

“I shall.”

Rackstraw hurried out, muttering “Wheat” and “Chicago” under his breath.

Isabel turned to Clarence.

“What happened?”

Clarence took her hands.

“Your father refused.”

“And then?”

“I reasoned with him.”

“What did you say?”

“That unless he consented I shouldn’t play in the match.”

Isabel stared.

“Clarence!”

“It seemed the simplest argument.”

“And he agreed?”

“Immediately, after reflection.”

She tried to look shocked. Love fought with laughter. Love lost.

“You’re perfectly horrible.”

“I know.”

“And Father likes you for it.”

“So it appears.”

She came closer.

“Will you win his cap?”

“I shall endeavour to prevent the other side scoring.”

“Clarence.”

“Yes?”

“You had better.”

The private match was played before sixty thousand people, though the ground had been designed to hold thirty-five thousand and a moderate-sized dog. The remaining spectators accommodated themselves on roofs, walls, trees, telegraph poles, and one another.

Rackstraw sat beside Sir Septimus Barge in the directors’ enclosure. Between them, under glass, lay the two relics: the Bolton ball, bald with age, and the first international cap, brown and sacred.

Sir Septimus smiled.

“Still confident?”

Rackstraw looked toward the Houndsditch goal, where Clarence stood calm and immense in a green jersey.

“I have no worries.”

“You’ll have several before five o’clock.”

“I shall have the cap.”

Sir Septimus chuckled. It was the chuckle of a man unaware that Destiny is standing behind his chair with a sandbag.

The whistle blew.

Manchester attacked at once.

Their centre-forward, a short, broad person built like a safe, charged down the field. He shot from twenty yards. Clarence caught the ball and tucked it under his arm with the absent-minded ease of a gentleman accepting a theatre programme.

The crowd roared.

Two minutes later came another shot, low and vicious. Clarence went down like a felled tower and held it.

Then the storm broke.

Manchester attacked from the right, from the left, and, to outward appearance, from beneath the soil. Shots came at Clarence high, low, spinning, swerving, and filled with personal malice. He punched them over, tipped them round, smothered them, caught them, and on one occasion appeared to dismiss a particularly venomous drive by looking at it.

At half-time there was no score.

Rackstraw had bitten through the head of his walking-stick.

The second half began.

Houndsditch, inspired by the knowledge that there are limits even to what can reasonably be asked of a goalkeeper, attacked. Their outside-right centred. Their centre-forward missed. The ball struck a defender, a spectator, and possibly a passing bird before rolling into the net.

One goal to nil.

For the remaining thirty minutes Manchester besieged Clarence.

With seven minutes left, their inside-left broke through. Only Clarence stood between him and immortality. The forward shot. Clarence sprang sideways, horizontal in the air, and turned the ball against the post.

It rebounded to the centre-forward.

He shot.

Clarence, still on the ground, flung up a hand.

The ball struck it, rose like a startled pheasant, and dropped toward the net.

Clarence twisted, scrambled, and caught it on the line.

The sound that followed was not a cheer. It was a national convulsion.

Rackstraw rose from his seat and waved both arms. His hat flew backward and struck a bishop. Sir Septimus sat frozen.

The whistle blew.

Houndsditch had won.

The crowd poured onto the field. Clarence vanished beneath a tidal wave of humanity. He was raised on shoulders, dropped, raised again, embraced by strangers, struck affectionately by policemen, and presented with a baby whose parent temporarily lost interest in it.

In the enclosure Rackstraw seized the cap.

“Mine!” he cried.

Sir Septimus looked at the Bolton ball.

“Keep your wretched ball too.”

“No. A wager is a wager.”

“I don’t want it.”

“You’ll take it.”

“I won’t.”

“You will if I have to send it by registered post every morning for the rest of your life.”

History does not record how this dispute ended. History is often weak on essentials.

Four years passed.

Four years are easily disposed of by the trained narrator. An amateur would tell you about the wedding, the honeymoon, Clarence’s retirement from professional football, his first morning in the City, and the birth of an heir. He would describe Rackstraw’s speeches, Lady Runnymede’s diamonds, the Earl’s relief, Isabel’s gown, the bridesmaids’ emotions, and the bridegroom’s difficulty in finding his gloves.

We are made of sterner stuff.

Brevity. Let us on.

On a June afternoon Clarence sat in the library of a large house near Ascot. He was broader than formerly and wore the grave expression of a man who has just bought three million bushels of wheat by telephone.

Isabel sat by the window. At her feet played a small boy of three, who was attempting to kick a silver inkstand between two chairs.

Above the mantelpiece, in a glass case of its own, rested the first international goalkeeper’s cap.

Rackstraw entered with a sheaf of telegrams.

“Chicago is moving,” he said.

Clarence looked up.

“How far?”

“Two cents since noon. Barge and the Liverpool crowd are short. If we hold another week, we have them.”

“Unless the Argentine shipments arrive early.”

“They won’t.”

“Are you sure?”

“No. That’s the beauty of it.”

Clarence nodded thoughtfully.

The small boy kicked the inkstand. It passed between the chairs and struck Rackstraw on the ankle.

“Goal!” cried the child.

Rackstraw looked down at him with pride.

“Good lad. Strong on the right foot.”

“He takes after Clarence,” said Isabel.

“Let us hope,” said Rackstraw, “that he has Clarence’s business conscience.”

Isabel smiled.

“Has Clarence one?”

“Exactly.”

Rackstraw moved to the mantelpiece and gazed at the cap. His features softened. Millionaire, politician, wheat operator, and enemy of hereditary privilege though he was, he could still become devotional before a moth-eaten piece of brown cloth.

Clarence joined him.

“Worth it?” he asked.

Rackstraw glanced at his daughter, his grandson, the cap, and the telegrams from Chicago.

“My boy,” he said, “it was the best bargain I ever made.”

Outside, the summer breeze whispered in the trees. The sun sank in a lake of gold. Birds sang their evening hymns. A lesser artist would linger.

But we know better.

Brevity.

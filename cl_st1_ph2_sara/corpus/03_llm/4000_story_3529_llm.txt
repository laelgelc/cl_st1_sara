# The Spark in the Thatch

> “Then came Peter to him, and said, Lord, how oft shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? till seven times?  
> Jesus saith unto him, I say not unto thee, Until seven times: but, Until seventy times seven.”

Iván Shcherbakóv was a prosperous peasant. He had three sons. The eldest was married, the second was soon to be married, and the youngest was a strong lad who could already guide a plough and manage a horse. Iván’s wife was careful and hardworking, and his eldest son’s wife kept the house clean and knew how to bake, spin, milk cows, and tend poultry.

There was no idler in the family.

They had three horses and a foal, two cows with calves, a dozen sheep, pigs, hens, carts, ploughs, harrows, and enough grain to last until the next harvest. Their taxes were paid on time, their roof did not leak, and their barns were full. Iván himself was still strong. He could mow all day, eat his supper, and then go out again to mend a fence or look after a sick animal.

Only Iván’s old father no longer worked. For seven years he had suffered from a disease of the lungs and lay most of the time on the stove. He coughed and breathed with difficulty, but his mind was clear, and he saw what went on in the household.

Next door lived Gabriel the Lame. He was called lame because he dragged one foot a little, though he too could work well enough. His farm was not so rich as Iván’s, but he had what was needed: a pair of horses, a cow, several sheep, and sons who helped him.

The two households had once lived as neighbors should.

If Iván’s people needed a sieve, they went to Gabriel’s and took it. If Gabriel’s horse broke through a fence and strayed into Iván’s rye, Iván drove it out without making trouble. If one family lacked flour until market day, the other lent some. The women helped one another at weddings and funerals. The children played together, and the men shared tools and labor.

“Keep what is ours safe,” the old people used to say, “but do not suspect a neighbor. A lock may guard a chest, but goodwill guards a yard.”

So they lived peaceably.

Then one spring an egg went missing.

Iván’s daughter-in-law had a speckled hen that began laying early. The young woman was saving eggs for the Easter feast. Each morning the hen flew into the cart shed and laid in a corner behind an old shaft. The daughter-in-law had counted the eggs and knew how many there ought to be.

One day she heard the hen cackling, but she was kneading bread and could not leave the dough. She said to Iván’s youngest boy:

“Tarás, run and fetch the egg from the shed.”

Tarás went out, looked behind the shaft, and returned empty-handed.

“There is no egg there,” he said.

“Did you look properly?”

“I looked everywhere. The hen must have laid somewhere else.”

The daughter-in-law wiped the flour from her hands and searched the shed herself. There was no egg. She examined the hay, the cart, the manger, and even the corner behind the barrel.

Just then Tarás said:

“I saw Gabriel’s old mother near our shed. Perhaps she took it.”

That was all that was needed.

The daughter-in-law went to the fence and called to Gabriel’s mother.

“Granny! Did your hens come into our yard?”

“No,” said the old woman. “Why should they?”

“Our speckled hen laid an egg, and now it is gone.”

“What is that to me?”

“Only this: you were seen by our shed.”

Gabriel’s mother understood what was being hinted at.

“Do you think I steal eggs?” she cried. “Thank God, our hens lay enough for us. We do not search other people’s sheds.”

“I did not say you stole it.”

“You said it plainly enough.”

“If the shoe fits, put it on.”

Then Gabriel’s wife came out carrying a pail.

“What are you screaming about?”

“Your mother has taken our egg.”

“Taken your egg? Perhaps your hen laid a golden one! We have no need of your eggs.”

“Who asked you? Go about your work.”

“You go about yours, and teach your children not to tell lies.”

Now Iván’s wife came running, and Gabriel’s daughter-in-law followed. In a moment four women were speaking at once. One accused, another denied, a third remembered an old grievance, and the fourth added an insult. They called one another thieves, beggars, liars, and worse. Gabriel’s wife set down her pail, but in the pushing it overturned and the water ran into the dust.

“You did that on purpose!” she cried.

“You pushed me first!”

They caught at each other’s sleeves. One tore a kerchief loose, another scratched a cheek, and the children stood around shouting advice.

Iván returned from the field and forced himself between the women. Gabriel came from his yard and seized Iván by the collar.

“Teach your women manners!” Gabriel shouted.

“You teach yours not to steal!”

Gabriel struck at him. Iván caught Gabriel’s beard and pulled out a handful.

People came running and separated them.

That was how it all began.

The egg was found the next day beneath a stack of firewood, where the hen had made a fresh nest. But by then no one cared about the egg. Gabriel cared about his beard, and Iván cared about the blow he had received before the neighbors. The women cared about the insults, and the children repeated everything they had heard.

Gabriel wrapped the hairs Iván had torn out in a piece of paper and went to the district office.

“I have worn this beard for forty years,” he said, “and that robber has torn half of it away. I demand justice.”

Iván was summoned. He too made a complaint, saying Gabriel had struck him and torn his shirt.

The officials listened, wrote down their statements, took fees, and sent them away. Gabriel was fined. He appealed. Iván lodged another complaint. Witnesses were called. Some said they had seen Gabriel strike first; others said Iván had begun the fight. Every witness remembered only what favored the household with which he was friendly.

From that time the two men watched one another as cats watch at a mouse hole.

If Gabriel’s calf slipped through the fence, Iván shut it in his shed and demanded payment for trampled grass. If Iván’s geese wandered near Gabriel’s oats, Gabriel chased them with a stick and broke a wing. When a cart pin disappeared, Gabriel accused Iván’s sons. When a harness strap was cut, Iván blamed Gabriel.

The women met at the well and quarreled. They went to rinse linen at the stream and quarreled there. Their tongues did more work than their hands.

“Thieves!”

“Drunkards!”

“Court-haunters!”

“Wait, your turn will come!”

The children learned quickly. At first they had played together; now they threw clods across the fence and called one another the names their mothers used. Even the little ones, who did not understand the words, screamed them with delight.

Iván’s old father heard everything from the stove.

“My children,” he said, “what are you doing? Is an egg worth all this sin?”

“No one asks you,” Iván’s wife answered. “You lie there and do not know how they torment us.”

“I know enough. A quarrel is like a weed. Pull it while it is small, or it will seed the whole field.”

But no one listened.

A year passed, and then another. The first case had hardly ended before the second began. Gabriel accused Iván’s middle son of stealing a bridle. Iván charged Gabriel with damaging a haystack. Gabriel was ordered to pay a fine; Iván lost an appeal; then Iván won another case and recovered costs.

They traveled to the district town so often that they knew every clerk, guard, and petitioner. They carried documents in their coat linings and could speak of petitions, appeals, witnesses, and legal terms better than of sowing and harvest. Each went to court sure that justice meant punishing the other.

Court business costs both money and time. While Iván waited in corridors, his ploughing was delayed. While Gabriel searched for witnesses, his hay was rained upon. They paid scribes, bought food on the road, and treated men with vódka so that they would testify. A fee here, a gift there, a lost day, a lame horse, an unsown strip—none seemed much by itself, but together they consumed more than the egg, the beard, the bridle, and the damaged hay were worth twenty times over.

The old father tried to make Iván count the cost.

“You were once a good farmer,” he said. “Your land was ploughed before your neighbor’s, and your stacks stood straight. Now you know which clerk takes a silver coin and which one prefers a bottle, but you do not know where your young mare limps. Is this wisdom?”

“Gabriel gives me no peace,” said Iván. “If I let him go unpunished, he will grow bolder.”

“You say the same of him that he says of you. One dog bares its teeth, the other bares its teeth, and then each claims it bites only because it was bitten first. But a man has understanding. Let the other bark. You need not bark with him.”

“He must answer for what he has done.”

“And you? Have you nothing to answer for?”

Iván turned away.

The feud continued for six years. By then no one could remember all its causes. Old wrongs were mixed with new ones. A missing rake was added to the egg; a broken axle was added to the beard; a child’s bruised head was added to the rake. Every misfortune found an owner in the next yard.

One summer Gabriel’s cow was found among Iván’s cabbages. Iván’s daughter-in-law drove it out with a switch. Gabriel’s wife came shouting, and the two women fought. The next morning Iván found three cabbage plants uprooted and swore Gabriel had done it. Gabriel swore he had not.

In autumn, a sack of flour vanished from Gabriel’s barn. He complained that Iván’s sons had taken it.

“What would we do with your musty flour?” said Iván. “Our own grain is better.”

“Your pride is better, certainly,” Gabriel replied, “but your conscience is worse.”

They went to court again.

There was an old judge there who had served many years and had seen many fools destroy themselves by demanding justice. He read the papers, looked at Iván, and said:

“You men live side by side. You must borrow fire from one another, share the road, water cattle at the same place, and pray under the same church roof. Why do you come here every month?”

“He began it,” said Iván.

“He lies,” said Gabriel.

The judge raised his hand.

“The civil law can decide a fine, but it cannot make peace in your hearts. Withdraw your complaints and settle at home.”

“What would be the use?” asked Iván. “He takes kindness for weakness.”

“And he says the same of you. One of you must be the first to stop.”

“Let him stop.”

Gabriel said, “Let Iván stop.”

The judge sighed and sent them away according to the law, because men can be advised to act wisely, but they cannot be forced to love one another.

That winter the quarrel became worse.

Gabriel’s horse slipped its tether one night and entered Iván’s yard. Iván’s sons caught it and tied it in the barn. In the morning they demanded compensation for oats the horse had eaten.

“It ate no more than a handful,” said Gabriel.

“A handful of grain and half a manger of hay.”

“You are robbers.”

“You are lucky we do not take the horse for what you owe us.”

Gabriel paid, but as he left he said:

“You will choke on that money.”

A week later one of Iván’s sledges was found with its runners hacked. Iván had no proof, but proof is not needed by a man whose mind is already made up. He went to Gabriel’s gate and shouted:

“You lame devil! Come out!”

Gabriel came out, and they fought in the snow until their sons pulled them apart.

The old father heard about it and called Iván to the stove.

“Iván,” he said, “come closer. I cannot speak loudly.”

Iván bent down.

“Do you remember how you lived when Gabriel’s father was alive?”

“We lived well enough.”

“If his flour ran short?”

“We lent him some.”

“If your wheel broke?”

“He gave me his cart.”

“And did you become poorer through it?”

“No.”

“Then why are you poor in spirit now, though your barns are still full?”

Iván frowned.

“You speak as if I alone were guilty.”

“I do not speak of Gabriel’s soul. God has given me no charge over it. I speak of yours. If a hole opens in your roof, do you climb onto Gabriel’s house to mend his thatch, or do you mend your own?”

“My own.”

“Then mend what belongs to you. His anger is his hole. Yours is yours.”

Iván made no answer.

In the spring, during a family celebration, Iván’s eldest daughter-in-law went to fetch water. She was heavy with child and walked slowly. Near the well she met Gabriel, who had been drinking. Words passed between them. The young woman told him that everyone knew he had once stolen a horse harness and escaped punishment only by bribing a clerk.

Gabriel flushed.

“You lying crow!” he shouted.

She laughed at him.

Then Gabriel struck her with the back of his hand. She fell against the well frame and cried out. Other women came running and took her home. She was ill for several days, though the child was not lost.

Iván was furious.

“Now he will not escape,” he said. “To strike a woman—and a pregnant woman! I’ll have him sent away in chains.”

He lodged a complaint. The doctor examined the young woman. Witnesses gave evidence. Gabriel said she had insulted him and that he had only pushed her, but the court found him guilty.

Because he had already been fined for previous disturbances, the magistrates sentenced him to be flogged.

Gabriel stood pale while the sentence was read. He was no longer young, and public punishment was a deep disgrace. He gripped the rail and said nothing until the guard led him out. As he passed through the corridor, Iván followed behind and heard him mutter:

“They may tear my back, but something of Iván’s will burn worse than my back.”

Iván stopped.

“What did he say?” he asked the guard. “You heard him threaten to burn my property.”

The guard shrugged.

“Men say many things after sentence.”

Iván returned to the courtroom.

“Your Honors, he has threatened arson. I ask that this be added to the record.”

Gabriel was brought back. He denied the threat.

“I said nothing of the kind.”

“I heard you!” cried Iván.

“You hear whatever brings harm to me.”

The old judge was present that day. He looked at Gabriel’s white face and trembling lips, and then at Iván, who stood flushed with triumph and anger.

“You both listen,” said the judge. “There is still time. Gabriel, ask forgiveness. Iván, withdraw the charge. The punishment has not yet been carried out.”

The younger clerk whispered that such a settlement did not conform to procedure after sentence, but the judge waved him aside.

“There is a law above our papers,” he said. “Make peace, and both of you will gain.”

Gabriel turned his face away.

Iván thought: “If he bowed before everyone and admitted his guilt, perhaps I could forgive him.” But Gabriel did not bow.

Instead he said:

“I am nearly fifty. I have a married son and grandchildren, and this man has brought me to the whip. Am I to ask his pardon too?”

His voice broke. He covered his mouth, then suddenly glared at Iván.

“Let him remember me.”

Iván’s pity vanished.

“You see?” he said to the judge. “A wolf remains a wolf.”

The sentence stood.

When Iván returned home, everyone wanted to hear what had happened. His wife was pleased. His daughter-in-law said Gabriel had received only what he deserved. The sons talked of watching the barns.

The old father called Iván.

“What sentence was given?”

“Twenty strokes.”

The old man closed his eyes.

“Is your heart lighter?”

“He threatened to burn us.”

“I asked if your heart was lighter.”

Iván did not answer.

The old man struggled to sit up. His thin hands shook, and his breath whistled.

“You think the court has settled matters,” he said. “It has settled nothing. It has put another log on the fire.”

“What should I have done? Let him strike my daughter-in-law?”

“You should have stopped your daughter-in-law from insulting him. You should have stopped your wife when she first quarreled over the egg. You should have stopped yourself when you pulled his beard. You ask what you should do now? Go to him. Say, ‘Brother Gabriel, we have both sinned. Forgive me.’ Invite him in tomorrow. Set tea on the table and put out a little vódka. Tell the women and children that the quarrel is ended.”

Iván stared at him.

“You want me to bow to the man who threatened to burn us?”

“You are not bowing to his threat. You are bowing before God.”

“He will boast that I was afraid.”

“Let him boast today. Tomorrow his heart may soften. If it does not, you will still have guarded your own soul.”

“You do not know him.”

The old father gave a weak smile.

“And you know him? For six years you have seen nothing in him but an enemy. Hatred is smoked glass; look through it, and even the sun appears dark.”

Iván paced the room.

“What if he really sets fire to the place?”

“Then watch against fire if you must, but first put out the fire in your heart. A spark in dry thatch is easy to crush. Leave it, and no bucket will save the house.”

The old man lay back, exhausted.

Iván went into the yard. Evening had fallen. The horses chewed quietly in the stable. A cow shifted her chain. The barns stood dark against the sky, and the house smelled of bread and warm milk. Iván looked over the fence at Gabriel’s yard.

For a moment he thought of going there.

He imagined himself opening the gate and saying, “Enough, neighbor. We have both behaved badly.” He imagined Gabriel’s surprise. Perhaps the old judge and his father were right. Someone had to be first.

Then he heard Gabriel’s daughter-in-law telling someone in the lane that the magistrates had been bribed and that Iván would yet weep tears of blood.

Iván’s heart hardened again.

“There!” he thought. “They are planning evil, and my father tells me to offer tea.”

That night the wind rose. It was harvest time, and everything was dry. Straw lay in the yards, sheaves stood near the barns, and the roofs were thatched. Clouds covered the moon. The wind came in gusts, rattling gates and lifting loose straw along the lane.

At supper Gabriel’s threat was discussed again.

“We should take turns watching,” said Iván’s eldest son.

“If he comes, we will catch him,” said Tarás.

Iván’s wife crossed herself.

“God preserve us from such a neighbor.”

The old father heard them.

“Preserve yourselves from anger,” he murmured. “That is the nearer danger.”

After supper the family lay down. Iván tried to sleep, but every sound disturbed him. A horse stamped. A shutter creaked. Something scraped along the fence.

“He may come tonight,” Iván thought. “The wind is just right for a villain.”

He rose, put on his coat, and went outside.

The night was black. The wind rushed through the lane and hissed in the thatch. Iván walked around the house, checked the shed, and looked behind the barn. Nothing moved.

He was ashamed of his fear.

“The old man is right,” he thought. “I see Gabriel in every shadow.”

He went toward the gate. Then, near the corner of the barn, he saw something darker than the darkness move low along the fence.

Iván stood still.

The shape crouched. For an instant a tiny light appeared and vanished. Then it appeared again, shining on a hand, a sleeve, and the side of a face.

It was Gabriel.

Iván’s blood surged.

“So I was right!”

Gabriel held a burning twist of straw. He bent beneath the eaves of Iván’s shed and thrust the flame into the dry thatch. The wind caught it. A thin tongue of fire ran upward, disappeared, and then glowed red among the straw.

Iván sprang from behind the corner.

“I have you, villain!”

Gabriel dropped the straw and ran.

Iván could have pulled the burning handful from the roof. It was still small. A man’s cap could have beaten it out. But Gabriel was escaping, and Iván wanted to catch him and prove his guilt before the whole village.

He rushed after him.

Gabriel reached the gate. Iván caught the back of his coat, but the cloth tore in his hand. Gabriel twisted free, seized a fence rail, and struck Iván on the side of the head.

There was a flash before Iván’s eyes. He fell to his knees. By the time he rose, Gabriel had vanished into his own yard.

“I’ll show you!” Iván shouted. “You will not escape now!”

Then the wind roared behind him.

Iván turned.

The corner of the shed was burning. The small red glow had become a sheet of flame. It ran over the roof as though oil had been poured on the thatch. Burning straw whirled into the air and fell upon the house.

Iván cried out and ran back.

“Fire! Get up! Fire!”

His sons came rushing from the house. The women screamed. Someone dragged out a chest; someone untied a horse; someone fetched a bucket. Iván climbed onto the shed with a fork, but flame burst through the roof in front of him and drove him down.

“One need only pull out the burning straw!” he kept shouting. “One need only pull it out and trample on it!”

But it was too late.

The wind carried sparks across the yard. The house roof caught, then the barn. The animals bellowed and tore at their ropes. Sheep crowded into a corner. Hens flew shrieking through smoke. The mare broke loose and galloped into the lane with part of her tether dragging behind.

People came with buckets, but the well was far and the flames moved faster than men. Water thrown on one place hissed uselessly while fire seized three others.

A burning bundle flew over the fence and landed on Gabriel’s roof.

Gabriel’s family ran out. They shouted that Iván’s fire was destroying them. Gabriel himself climbed a ladder and tried to tear away the thatch, but the wind flung flame into his face. He fell, rose, and staggered back.

Soon his house was burning too.

From roof to roof the fire traveled. A shed on the opposite side of the lane caught; then another cottage; then a row of barns. Cattle ran loose. Children cried for their mothers. Men hacked down fences, dragged carts away, and shouted contradictory orders. Women carried icons, blankets, kettles, and cradles. One old woman saved a sack of empty pots and left her grain behind.

The village bell rang wildly.

Iván ran into his burning house to rescue what he could. He dragged out a bench and a chest, though the chest contained only winter clothes while money lay forgotten in a cupboard. His beard was singed, and his hands blistered. Again and again he cried:

“Brothers, the thatch! Pull off the thatch!”

No one listened. Each man had his own roof to save.

Then Iván remembered his father.

The old man had been lying on the stove.

Iván rushed toward the house, but flames filled the doorway. His eldest son and two neighbors came around the side carrying the old father on a wooden bench. They had pulled him through a window. His shirt was scorched, and one side of his body had been burned. He breathed in short gasps but was conscious.

They carried him across the lane to the village Elder’s house, which stood beyond the path of the fire.

By dawn, half the village was black earth, smoking beams, and fallen chimneys. Iván’s house, barns, tools, grain, carts, and livestock were destroyed. Only one horse and a cow had been saved. Gabriel had lost nearly everything as well. Eleven other households had burned.

People wandered among the ashes looking for iron pots, tools, and half-burned grain. Smoke stung their eyes. Children sat wrapped in blankets. The homeless argued over whose sparks had started the fire.

Iván stood in the road with a torn coat on his shoulders. His hair and eyebrows were singed. He seemed not to understand what people said to him.

“One need only have pulled it out,” he repeated. “One need only have pulled out the straw and trampled on it.”

Toward noon the Elder’s wife came for him.

“Your father is asking for you. He may not live long.”

Iván went into the Elder’s house.

The old man lay beneath the icons. His burns had been covered with linen and grease, but his face was gray. Each breath caused him pain. He motioned for the others to leave.

Iván knelt beside him.

“My son,” the father whispered, “who burned the village?”

Iván’s anger returned at once.

“Gabriel did. I saw him. He pushed burning straw under the eaves. I caught him, but he struck me and ran. If I had held him—”

The old man looked at him steadily.

“Who burned the village?”

“I tell you, Gabriel. I saw him with my own eyes.”

The father closed his eyes, then opened them again.

“Iván, I am standing at the door of death. Do not lie to your own soul. Before God, who burned the village?”

Iván was silent.

Outside, someone struck a charred beam with an axe. A child cried. The smell of smoke came through the window.

The old man waited.

Then Iván remembered the tiny flame in the thatch. He remembered how easily he could have pulled it out. He remembered his father’s warnings, the old judge’s words, the years of complaints, the women at the fence, the handful of beard, and the missing egg that had been found beneath the woodpile.

His strength left him. He bowed his head to the floor.

“Father,” he said, “I burned it. Gabriel put in the fire, but I let it spread. I saw the spark and chased the man. I wanted revenge more than I wanted to save the house. Forgive me.”

The old father moved one burned hand and laid it upon Iván’s head.

“Praise God,” he whispered. “Now you see.”

Iván sobbed.

“What shall I do? We have lost everything. So many homes are gone. I have ruined my sons and grandchildren.”

“You still have life. While there is life, there is a road back.”

“Tell me what to do.”

“Do not expose Gabriel.”

Iván lifted his head in surprise.

“But I saw him.”

“Conceal another man’s sin, and God will help thee bear thine own.”

“He burned half the village.”

“And if he is flogged, or sent away, will the houses rise from the ashes? Will your grain return? Will my burns heal?”

“No.”

“Then do not feed the fire again. If people ask, say you do not know how it began.”

Iván hesitated.

“He may think I am afraid.”

The old man’s face showed both pain and patience.

“You are still thinking of what he may think. Think of what God sees. You pursued Gabriel and left the flame. Do not pursue him again and leave your soul burning.”

Iván bent lower.

“I will obey.”

“Remember,” said the father, pausing for breath, “one angry word is straw. Another is wind. A third is flame. Put out the first spark. If a man injures you, seek peace before sunset. If he will not listen, seek it again. Forgive, not with the tongue only, but from the heart.”

His hand slipped from Iván’s head.

He moved his lips in prayer, drew one long, difficult breath, and died.

Iván wept beside him. He wept for his father, for his house, for the ruined village, and for the six years he had spent guarding his pride while neglecting everything that might have brought peace.

That afternoon officials came to inquire into the fire. Villagers gave many accounts. Some said it began in Iván’s shed. Others said a spark had blown from a chimney. One boy claimed to have seen a stranger in the lane. Gabriel remained among his family and avoided Iván’s eyes.

The district officer questioned Iván.

“You were the first to raise the alarm. How did the fire begin?”

“I do not know,” said Iván.

“Did you see anyone near the shed?”

Iván felt Gabriel watching him.

“No. I saw the roof burning.”

“Had Gabriel threatened you?”

“We had quarreled. Angry men say foolish things.”

The officer asked again, but Iván would say no more. There was no proof, and the inquiry ended.

That night the homeless families slept in barns, sheds, and neighboring villages. Iván’s household was given space in the Elder’s outbuilding. Gabriel’s family sheltered nearby beneath a lean-to.

Iván saw Gabriel standing alone beside the road. His face was blackened by smoke, and a strip of cloth covered one burned hand. For a moment neither spoke.

Gabriel lowered his eyes.

“Iván,” he began, but could not continue.

Iván felt the old anger stir. He saw again the hand thrusting fire into the thatch. Then he remembered his father asking, “Who burned the village?”

He said:

“Gabriel, we have both suffered enough. Let what has happened be buried.”

Gabriel looked up.

“You did not tell them?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“My father told me to put out the fire.”

Gabriel understood. His mouth trembled.

“Iván, I—”

“Say no more. We must build shelters before the rain.”

Iván turned away, not because he hated Gabriel, but because he feared that if they spoke too much both might lose courage. Yet from that moment the weight in his heart began to lift.

The rebuilding took months.

At first every family thought only of itself, for winter was approaching. Men cut timber, split boards, gathered surviving tools, and borrowed horses. Women cleaned scorched grain, patched clothes, and cooked in common kettles. Those whose houses had escaped the flames shared space with those who had lost everything.

Iván had once possessed more than many others, but now he stood in the same need. A neighbor lent him an axe. Another gave him seed grain. A widow whose shed had not burned sheltered his cow. He began to understand again that no household lives by itself.

Gabriel came one morning with his sons.

“We are raising our frame today,” he said. “But your beam is ready and rain is coming. We will help you set it first.”

Iván looked at him.

“As you wish.”

They worked side by side. At first they spoke only of the task.

“Lift there.”

“Hold the rope.”

“The notch is too shallow.”

“Bring the adze.”

By noon the first frame stood. Iván’s wife brought bread, onions, and a jug of weak beer. She hesitated when she saw Gabriel sitting among the workers. Then she remembered what Iván had said and placed bread before him.

“Eat, neighbor.”

Gabriel crossed himself.

“May God reward you.”

The women were slower to forget than the men. They had stored up six years of words, and words are light to carry but hard to throw away. Yet Iván forbade quarrels in his household.

When his daughter-in-law spoke bitterly of Gabriel, he said:

“He struck you, and he sinned. You insulted him, and you sinned. The matter has burned enough.”

When Tarás mocked Gabriel’s youngest boy, Iván took him by the shoulder.

“Would you throw sparks into new thatch?”

“It was only a word.”

“The village burned from what was only a spark.”

The boy fell silent.

Gabriel too restrained his family. Once his wife began accusing Iván’s people of taking more than their share of donated timber. Gabriel stopped her.

“Count our own boards,” he said. “Leave theirs to them.”

Little by little the old habits returned, but this time they were chosen with care.

If Gabriel needed a horse, Iván lent his surviving mare. If Iván’s ploughshare broke, Gabriel gave him one until market day. Their sons cut hay together. The women baked in the same oven while the new houses were being finished. The children, seeing that the elders no longer cursed one another, soon began playing across the fence again.

The village did not become rich at once. A fire consumes in an hour what generations have gathered. There were fewer cattle, poorer clothes, small harvests, and debts to pay. Iván wore the same patched sheepskin coat for several winters. Gabriel’s family lived under a rough roof that leaked during spring rains.

But work done in peace prospers more than abundance wasted in hatred.

Iván no longer lost days in court. He did not buy vódka for witnesses or pay clerks to write complaints. He sowed on time, mended his tools, and watched his animals. Gabriel did the same. When one family had too much work, the other helped. After several years both farms were sound again.

People sometimes asked Iván privately how the fire had started. He always answered:

“It began with anger.”

Some laughed and said they meant the actual flame.

“So do I,” he replied.

He never told what he had seen that night.

Gabriel knew this, and the knowledge worked upon him more strongly than punishment would have done. A flogging might have made him count his wounds and dream of revenge. Iván’s silence made him count his own guilt. He ceased drinking heavily. He became quieter and no longer hurried to answer every insult.

Once, many years later, a boundary dispute arose between their sons. A spring flood had washed away two markers, and each young man claimed a strip of meadow. Their voices rose, and Tarás seized Gabriel’s son by the sleeve.

Iván came upon them.

“What is this?”

“The meadow is ours,” said Tarás.

“It is ours,” said the other.

Iván looked at the narrow strip. It would yield scarcely one cartload of hay.

“Divide it,” he said.

“Why should we give away what belongs to us?” Tarás protested.

Iván stooped, picked up a dry stem, and held it before them.

“Do you know what this is?”

“Straw.”

“And what can straw do?”

They understood and lowered their eyes.

Gabriel, who had followed from behind, said:

“Let Iván’s people mow the strip this year. We will mow it next year.”

“No,” said Iván. “Let the young men mow it together and divide the hay.”

So they did, and before evening the dispute was forgotten.

From then on, whenever anger began in Iván’s household, he did not ask first who was right. He asked what could be done to restore peace. Sometimes he yielded a measure of grain, sometimes he apologized, sometimes he invited the offended man to tea. People said he had grown soft in old age, but they also chose him to settle disputes, because he cared less for victory than for an end to hatred.

He would listen to both sides and say:

“If you bring two burning sticks together, neither puts out the other. Separate them, and cover the flame.”

Or he would say:

“A debt of money may be paid with money. A debt of insult grows when payment is demanded.”

He did not speak as one who had never sinned. He spoke as one who had watched a whole village burn while he chased the man who lit the spark.

Iván and Gabriel lived beside each other until they were old. Their new houses stood where the former ones had stood, with a fence between the yards and a gate in the fence. The gate was seldom fastened. Children ran through it, women borrowed bowls and sieves, and the men shared carts, pasture, and labor.

When Gabriel’s cow calved and his household had more milk than they could use, a jug went across to Iván’s. When Iván slaughtered a sheep, Gabriel received a portion. At weddings the families sat at the same table. At funerals they prayed side by side.

They lived not without offenses, for no family and no village is free from them. A careless child still broke a tool. A wandering animal still entered another man’s field. Women still grew sharp when tired, and men still spoke too loudly after drinking. But Iván did not allow a wrong to spend the night and wake stronger in the morning.

“Go now,” he would say. “Do not wait for pride to put on its boots.”

And if the offender was himself, he went first.

Thus Iván learned what his father had tried for years to teach him: that forgiveness is not weakness, nor is peace purchased by proving another man guilty. The man who returns injury for injury ties himself to the wrongdoer, and both drag the same chain. But he who forgives from the heart breaks the chain for two.

A fire is easiest quenched when it is only a spark. So it is with anger. Stamp it out at once, and the house stands, the cattle feed, the children sleep, and neighbors remain neighbors. Let it lie in the dry straw of pride, and the wind will do the rest.

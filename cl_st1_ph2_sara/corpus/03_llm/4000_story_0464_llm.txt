# Roy and the Greasewood Root

All that afternoon the heat had lain upon the Colorado prairie like a heavy blanket. The sagebrush drooped in the white glare; grasshoppers sprang from Scamp’s hoofs with a dry whirring sound; and even the little gray prairie dogs, usually so ready to sit upright and scold a passing rider, had retired to the cool depths of their burrows.

Roy Milford pushed back his broad hat and looked toward the mountains.

The peaks, which at noon had shown purple and clear beyond the rolling mesa, were disappearing behind a bank of blackish clouds. Above them, long streamers reached eastward, as though the storm were marshaling its forces before marching out upon the plains. Now and then a pale flash quivered in their depths, followed, after a long interval, by a muttering of thunder.

“We’re going to get wet, old fellow,” Roy said.

Scamp twitched one ear backward but did not increase his pace.

The pony was small, wiry, and mouse-colored, with a black mane and a disposition of his own. He and Roy had quarreled more than once. Scamp objected to the quirt, to sudden mounting, and especially to being taken away from good grass. Yet their disagreements never lasted. Roy had owned him for three years, and each understood the other almost as well as two boys who had ridden and camped together.

Roy was fourteen, brown from the Colorado sun, and sufficiently familiar with the prairie to feel at home twenty miles from a house. He knew where antelope watered, where coyotes denned, and where the wild plums ripened first. On many a solitary ride he had imagined himself a scout carrying dispatches through hostile country, a sheriff pursuing outlaws, or a hunter returning with meat to a starving camp.

This afternoon, however, he carried nothing more romantic than a small sack of nails tied behind the cantle. His father needed them for repairing the north corral, and Roy had promised to return before supper.

Another roll of thunder sounded, nearer than the first.

“We’ll have to hustle,” he told Scamp.

The pony broke into an easy lope.

The Milford ranch lay southeastward, beyond a low mesa and two dry watercourses. By following the wagon road Roy must go around the head of a deep arroyo. There was another route, three miles shorter, which entered the arroyo at a break in its western bank and followed the sandy bottom until a cattle trail climbed out on the opposite side.

Roy hesitated when he reached the branching tracks.

His father disliked having him ride through the arroyo when storms were near. An arroyo is a watercourse common to the dry regions of the West. For much of the year it may contain no water whatever. Its floor is sand, gravel, and sun-baked adobe, while its sides, cut by the torrents of many seasons, sometimes rise as straight as walls of masonry. But rain falling upon distant hills drains rapidly into such channels, and a place that has been dry for months may become, in a few minutes, a furious river.

Roy knew this. He also knew that the arroyo offered shelter from wind and rain, and that the storm still appeared to be over the mountains.

“If we take the road, it’ll catch us sure,” he reasoned. “Down there we’ll miss the worst of it, and save three miles besides.”

Scamp did not approve. At the break in the bank he stopped and sniffed the air. Then he turned his head as if proposing that they continue along the open road.

“Oh, quit your fooling!” Roy said. “You’d rather get home to your oats, wouldn’t you?”

He touched the pony with his heel, and Scamp went reluctantly down the slope.

The arroyo was perhaps fifty feet deep and in places not more than thirty feet wide. Its yellow walls were patched with gray clay and streaked where former rains had trickled down. Greasewood bushes clung to shelves or leaned over the rim, their dusty green branches armed with stiff twigs. Here and there a sagebrush had found lodging; elsewhere rabbitbrush, yucca, and bunch grass thrust from cracks that appeared too poor to nourish anything.

Greasewood was the rancher’s enemy. Its roots went astonishingly deep in search of moisture, and when one of the tough bushes took possession of a field it was difficult to remove. Roy had spent many an unhappy hour chopping and pulling them from a patch near the Milford garden. The brown taproots were often thicker than his wrist and as tough as hempen rope.

“Pesky stuff!” his father had said only the previous week. “If greasewood could be persuaded to grow fence posts instead of thorns, a man might have some use for it.”

The arroyo twisted from side to side, so that Roy could see only a short distance ahead. Its floor was firm near the entrance, but farther on Scamp’s hoofs sank into loose sand. Lizards darted beneath stones. A jack rabbit bounded from behind a bank of tumbleweed, startled Scamp, and vanished around the next bend.

The light faded rapidly.

Roy glanced upward. The narrow strip of sky between the prisonlike walls had changed from blue to a smoky greenish gray. Lightning ripped across it; thunder struck almost at once, rolling along the arroyo with a crash magnified by a hundred echoes.

Then down came the rain.

It did not begin with separate drops. It descended in columns and sheets, as though a million buckets had been overturned at one instant. Water streamed from Roy’s hat brim and ran cold down his neck. Scamp lowered his head, blinking and snorting, while the rain drummed upon the saddle and beat the dust from the greasewood leaves.

For five minutes horse and rider could scarcely see. The walls appeared and vanished behind moving curtains of gray. Little rivulets sprang from every crack, carrying clay, stones, dead twigs, beetles, and clumps of grass into the bottom. The thirsty sand drank eagerly at first; then its surface darkened, softened, and became mud.

Roy allowed Scamp to walk. Galloping over such uncertain ground might lame him, and there was no use hurrying now. They were already soaked.

“It can’t keep this up long,” the boy said, though the roar of rain drowned his voice.

Presently the downpour slackened. The thunder moved eastward, and the strip of sky overhead grew lighter. Water still dripped from every ledge, but Roy could see the farther wall clearly again.

He laughed with relief.

“That was a soaker, Scamp! Mother’ll say I ought to have had sense enough to come in.”

The pony did not respond. His ears stood rigidly forward.

Roy felt the muscles under the saddle tighten. He leaned to look past the next turn, but saw only wet sand, scattered brush, and the brown walls.

“What is it?”

Scamp gave a low, uneasy whinny.

Then Roy heard it—a faint, continuous murmur, so deep that at first it might have been mistaken for distant thunder. It did not cease. It grew louder, filling the narrow passage with a steady vibration. Small stones at the foot of the bank trembled. A trickle of muddy water crept between Scamp’s hoofs, though the rain had nearly stopped.

Roy’s heart seemed to turn over.

He knew that sound.

The storm had not spent all its fury where he was. Far up the drainage basin a cloudburst must have fallen upon the hills, and every ravine was pouring its burden into the arroyo. The flood was coming.

For an instant Roy sat motionless, listening. Then he jerked the reins.

“Come on, Scamp!”

The nearest exit lay less than a mile ahead, where the cattle trail climbed the southern bank. Behind them the entrance was farther away. Roy did not need to choose twice.

Scamp leaped into a gallop.

Around one bend they raced, then another. Mud flew from the pony’s hoofs. Roy crouched low over the wet mane, guiding him past boulders and heaps of driftwood. The sack of nails thumped against the cantle. The murmur behind them became a roar.

He dared one backward glance.

A wall of brown water swung around the last curve. It was not smooth like a river. Its front boiled with white froth and carried sagebrush, branches, fence rails, and rolling stones. Behind the first wave rose another, higher and blacker, striking from wall to wall as if furious at every obstruction.

It looked to Roy like Death itself pursuing him.

“Run, boy! Run!”

Scamp ran. Never had the little pony traveled so swiftly. His neck stretched straight, his nostrils flared red, and his hoofbeats came in quick, desperate strokes. Yet the sand, softened by rain, dragged at him. Water spread beneath his feet—first a film, then an inch, then enough to splash against his fetlocks.

Where was the cattle trail?

Roy recognized a split rock that stood near the last bend before it. Hope leaped within him. Fifty yards more—only fifty! He could already see the slanting break in the wall.

Then Scamp stumbled.

The pony recovered, but that lost instant was enough. The first muddy wave struck his hind legs. He plunged forward, regained footing, and struggled on; the water rose to his knees.

Roy pulled him toward the trail.

A mass of uprooted brush whirled past and struck Scamp’s shoulder. He swerved. Another wave came roaring around the bend and lifted both pony and rider sideways.

Scamp could no longer touch bottom.

He swam bravely, his head and part of his neck showing above the turbid water. Roy clung to the saddle, kicking loose from the stirrups lest he be trapped beneath the pony if Scamp rolled. The exit was opposite them, but the current swept them past it as easily as it might carry a straw.

“We can’t both make it,” Roy thought.

The saddle and his weight were dragging Scamp down. The pony’s nose dipped, rose, then dipped again. Roy remembered the oats waiting in the barn, the way Scamp rubbed his forehead against Roy’s shoulder, and their last foolish quarrel beside the plum thicket.

He loosened his grip.

“Go on, old fellow!” he cried. “Get out if you can!”

A projecting ledge flashed past. Roy sprang toward it.

His fingers scraped wet clay and found no hold. He dropped into the torrent. Brown water closed above his head.

He was turned over and over, battered against sand and gravel, and borne along with a force beyond resistance. He did not know which way was upward until his shoulder struck the bank. He kicked, rose for one choking breath, and saw Scamp’s black mane disappear amid the foam.

Then another wave seized him.

The boy thought of home. He saw his mother placing preserves upon the supper table, his little sister May watching at the window, his father coming from the corral and asking, “Roy back yet?” They would search the road after dark. They might never know what had become of him.

“I’m going to drown like a rat in a ditch,” he thought.

Terror took away his strength. He beat the water wildly and swallowed mud. Something struck his ribs. Something else tore the sleeve of his shirt.

Then, because there was nothing else to do, he said the few prayers he knew. He could not form all the words, but the attempt steadied him. Panic loosened its grip. He remembered that a swimmer who fought the current wasted his strength. If he could keep near one wall, some root or ledge might offer a hold.

He turned upon his back, lifted his face, and let the flood bear him.

A greasewood bush hung from the bank ahead. Half the earth beneath it had been washed away, exposing a long brown root that curved downward like a rope.

Roy reached.

His first hand missed. The root struck his palm, slid through his fingers, and was gone to the length of his arm.

He clutched again.

This time his fingers closed.

The current snapped his body straight and dragged at him with terrific force. Pain shot through his shoulder. His grip slipped an inch; then another. He caught the root with his other hand and held on.

The flood roared past, hurling sticks against his legs. The bank above was nearly perpendicular, ten or twelve feet high, and softened by rain. Each time Roy tried to draw himself up, the current pulled him down again.

But the root held.

He wrapped one arm around it and rested. His feet found the wall, though the clay crumbled beneath them. Inch by inch he lifted himself until his chest rose above the strongest current.

A wave struck. His right foot slipped, and for a dreadful second he hung by both hands, swinging over the flood. The root bent but did not break.

“Tough as rope,” Roy gasped. “Father said so.”

He dug his toes into a crack and climbed again.

The exposed root reached upward to a shelf two feet below the rim. Roy pulled until his hands burned and his wounded shoulder seemed on fire. Wet clay filled his nails. His knees scraped the wall. Once he paused and looked down.

The sight nearly undid him. The arroyo had become a solid, rushing mass of brown, covered with foam and wreckage. A dead sheep turned slowly in the torrent. A cottonwood limb struck the bank beneath him and was snapped away.

He looked up instead.

The greasewood bush trembled above him. Rainwater fell from its narrow leaves upon his face. He grasped a smaller root, then a branch. The branch pierced his palm with a thorn, but he welcomed the pain; it meant he had another hold.

He gained the shelf.

For several moments he lay upon it, panting. The rim was still above his head, and the shelf itself was no wider than a feed trough. Cracks were opening in the soaked adobe. Pebbles slid beneath his elbow and vanished.

The whole bank might fall.

Roy rose to his knees. He seized the greasewood close to its base and pulled. The bush held fast, anchored by roots buried deep beyond sight. With one foot against the wall, he dragged himself upward.

Inch by inch—an inch gained, half an inch lost—he climbed.

His hand reached the rim. The sod broke away. He caught another handful farther back, kicked frantically, and rolled over the edge upon the prairie.

He had scarcely crawled six feet when there came a dull tearing sound. Part of the bank, shelf, greasewood, and all, slid into the arroyo. The torrent swallowed it and roared onward, as though in anger because its prey had escaped.

Roy lay face downward in the wet buffalo grass.

After a while he sat up. The storm had passed eastward, where lightning still flickered under a purple-black sky. In the west a strip of sunset shone beneath the clouds, turning every wet grass blade to copper.

“Scamp!” he called.

There was no answer.

He walked along the arroyo, calling at intervals, but darkness came quickly. His legs shook; his shoulder ached; and the prairie seemed to tilt beneath him. He could no longer tell whether he was following the direction of home. At last he found a dry place under the lee of a low ridge, curled himself among the sagebrush, and fell asleep.

Near midnight two riders discovered him. They were Milford and Mr. Bowen from the neighboring ranch, searching with lanterns.

“Here he is!” Bowen shouted.

Roy awakened beneath his father’s coat, with a strong arm around him.

“Scamp?” was his first word.

“Home,” Milford replied. “Got there an hour ago, near spent and without you. That’s how we knew you were in trouble.”

Roy tried to explain, but his father told him to save his breath.

The next morning he awoke in his own bed. Sunlight lay upon the adobe wall; May sat beside him, guarding a tray loaded with toast, preserves, eggs, and a wedge of cake which had no proper place at breakfast.

Mother Milford kissed him twice before permitting him to eat. His father stood near the window, listening while Roy told the whole story—from Scamp’s warning at the arroyo entrance to the collapse of the bank.

When Roy finished, his father cleared his throat.

“You had no business going into that arroyo with a storm coming.”

“No, sir,” Roy admitted.

“But you used your head when it counted. And you gave Scamp his chance.”

“Is he hurt?”

“Scraped and worn out. He must have caught the cattle trail after the water carried him past. He’s been looking toward the house ever since sunrise.”

Roy smiled. “I reckon we’re square now.”

May pointed to the bandage on his hand. “What made that hole?”

“A greasewood thorn.”

“Greasewood!” exclaimed his father. “One of those pesky bushes?”

“The root was hanging out of the bank,” Roy said. “If it hadn’t been there, I couldn’t have climbed out.”

Milford looked through the window toward the field where he and Roy had lately spent so much labor grubbing the stubborn shrubs. His weather-browned face softened.

“Well,” he said slowly, “I’ve called greasewood about every mean name a plant can be called. But any bush that saves my boy’s life has earned the right to grow where it pleases. I don’t reckon I’ll ever willingly pull up another one.”

From the barn came a shrill, familiar whinny.

Roy pushed aside the covers, despite three voices ordering him to remain still. Limping to the window, he saw Scamp standing in the corral, bandaged, muddy, and very much alive.

The pony lifted his head. The boy raised his sore hand.

Between them stretched the bright, rain-washed prairie, while beyond the corral the humble greasewood bushes glittered in the morning sun.

By half past two the sun had reduced the village street to a broad stripe of yellow dust, upon which neither wagon nor man seemed willing to venture. The horses stood under the tavern shed with their heads hanging solemnly over the hitching rail. Three hens occupied the shade beneath Major Tom Boldin’s bench, scratching at intervals with the slow confidence of creatures that knew the world to be empty of consequence.

The major sat tilted against the tavern wall. His hat covered his eyes, his mouth was open, and from his throat came a small, regular sound. Since the younger men had gone away to the war, the major had assumed many burdens. He corrected the women’s reports of battles, advised old men concerning national policy, inspected strangers, and explained to boys the use of artillery. The village, having little else to do, accepted this guardianship without formally appointing him to it.

At the far end of the street a boy appeared.

He ran in a manner which announced calamity long before he could give it a name. His bare feet struck the dust in quick slaps. One suspender had fallen, and his shirt inflated behind him like a banner of retreat. A dog followed for twenty yards, barking in the belief that the occasion was public, then stopped and stared.

The boy came upon the tavern porch with such violence that the hens fled beneath the bench in an insanity of fear.

“Maj’r! Maj’r Boldin!”

The major awoke in a tempest. His hat fell to the ground. His arms flew outward, and for one dreadful instant he appeared to be defending himself against the tavern wall.

“Eh? What? Where?”

“There’s a reb in th’ woods!”

The major stared. Sleep remained upon his face, but the word had penetrated.

“A what?”

“A rebel! A great big one! He’s layin’ over there past Beasley’s corn. I seen him. He’s got whiskers—awful whiskers. An’ a coat all tore. He looked right at me!”

Three women emerged from the general store. Old Peter Witheby lifted his head from a chair near the blacksmith shop. Two small boys who had been conducting a private war with green apples abandoned their ammunition and approached.

The major rose. He did this slowly, for he had already begun to understand that the village was observing him.

“Now, Samuel,” he said, “you want to be certain. This is a grave matter. You hain’t mistook some stump?”

“A stump don’t roll over an’ cuss.”

A silence came upon the porch.

“Did he have a gun?” asked the major.

“I—I couldn’t see. He was layin’ on it, maybe.”

The major looked down the peaceful road. At its end, beyond the last white house, Beasley’s corn stood in a dark green mass, and farther off the woods made a blue-black wall against the pale sky. This familiar prospect had in a moment become a map of invasion.

Mrs. Cady pressed one hand to her breast. “Mercy! A rebel right here!”

“I always said they’d come,” declared Mrs. Hooper, who had never said it.

Peter Witheby advanced, his hands clasped importantly behind him. He was a narrow old man with a long gray chin whisker. “Now, hol’ on a minnet. Hol’ on a minnet. Le’s get at th’ rights of this. Where’d yeh see him, Sammy?”

“By the big hick’ry. I was huntin’ blackberries, an’ I heard a noise, an’ there he was.”

“What sort of noise?”

“A rebel noise.”

This answer affected them profoundly.

The major cleared his throat. He knew that somewhere in his house there was a rifle, a long, ancient weapon which had not been fired since a Fourth of July seven years before, when it had bruised his shoulder and broken a kitchen window. The rifle now appeared in his mind as the natural instrument of authority.

“I’ll investigate,” he announced.

The women cried out together.

“You mustn’t go alone, Major!”

“He might shoot!”

“Suppose there’s a whole army in there!”

The possibility of an army caused the boys to look down the street with shining eyes. It caused the major to look toward his house.

“Nonsense,” he said. “If there was a force of any size, I’d have received information.”

Nobody inquired from whom he would have received it. His manner had already created vague but splendid arrangements of scouts, dispatches and military intelligence.

He strode across the road. Behind him the villagers followed as far as his gate, where they gathered in a whispering knot. He entered his house and was absent for several minutes. During this period Mrs. Boldin’s voice could be heard, rising sharply, then the major’s, deep and official, then the crash of some tin article in a closet.

At last he reappeared carrying the rifle. It was longer than dignity required and dust lay thick in the corners of its stock. A powder horn hung at the major’s side. He had put on his old blue coat, whose brass buttons testified to campaigns that had been largely conversational.

Mrs. Boldin stood in the doorway.

“Tom, you come back here! That gun hain’t fit to shoot.”

“It’ll shoot,” said the major.

“It might shoot you.”

He ignored this treason. Descending the walk, he kept his face turned toward the distant woods. The villagers made way for him. They admired the grave set of his jaw, although his fingers were busy discovering whether the hammer of the rifle would remain cocked.

Peter Witheby stepped forward.

“I’ll go with yeh,” he said.

The major glanced at him. “You armed?”

“No.”

“Well—”

“I’ll go as far as seems judicious.”

This phrase won Peter a place in the expedition. The two old men started down the street, the major bearing the rifle over his shoulder and Peter walking half a step behind. The boy Samuel followed. Behind Samuel came the two apple warriors, Mrs. Cady, Mrs. Hooper, the blacksmith’s wife with a baby, four girls, a limping dog, and a number of persons who had hurried from houses upon hearing that something was occurring.

“Stay back!” commanded the major.

The procession stopped.

He and Peter went on. After they had gone thirty yards, the procession resumed.

The dusty road led between low fences and gardens gone heavy with August. Bees moved among the hollyhocks. A harness jingled in a stable, and from an orchard came the sleepy wrangle of sparrows. Yet the rumour had touched all things. Every fence corner offered concealment; every rustle in the weeds became the guarded movement of an enemy. The women spoke in quick whispers. The boys searched the fields with faces stern from imagined service.

At Beasley’s lane the major halted.

“Where now?” he asked Samuel.

The boy pointed. “Straight on, then through th’ corn.”

The cornfield rose beyond a rail fence. Its long leaves shifted and whispered beneath a wind too slight to be felt upon the road. Above it the woods waited, inscrutable and cool.

The major examined the field. “You went through there?”

“Yessir.”

“See any tracks?”

“I wasn’t lookin’ fer tracks. I was lookin’ fer berries.”

Peter placed one foot upon the bottom rail, but did not cross. “Hol’ on a minnet,” he said. “We ought to consider. If this man’s armed, he’s likely selected a position commandin’ th’ approach.”

The major frowned. He had not thought the rebel so competent.

“Likely he’s a picket,” continued Peter. The crowd was near enough to hear him, and his voice acquired authority. “Could be th’ rest of ’em’s beyond th’ ridge. Could be hundreds.”

Mrs. Hooper gave a little moan. The baby began to cry.

“If they was hundreds,” said the major, “the corn would show signs.”

Everyone looked at the corn. It swayed with a dark and secret unanimity.

“There!” whispered one of the girls.

The whole company recoiled.

“What?” demanded the major.

“Somethin’ moved.”

“Where?”

“In there.”

The major brought the rifle from his shoulder. At once Peter got down from the fence. Samuel moved behind Mrs. Cady. The dog barked twice at the corn and then, deciding against an unsupported assault, retired between the blacksmith’s wife’s skirts.

A blackbird rose from the field, making a harsh complaint. It flew above their heads and disappeared toward the village.

Peter laughed. “Only a bird.”

There was general laughter, loud and relieved. Several persons explained that they had known it was a bird from the first. The major lowered the rifle.

“You people must keep quiet,” he said severely. “Nervousness is more dangerous than bullets.”

He climbed the fence. His coat caught upon a splinter, and for a moment he hung with one leg on either side, bearing the rifle across his arms. Peter, after glancing at the crowd, climbed after him.

“You stay here,” the major told Samuel.

“But I got to show yeh th’ place.”

“You can point from here.”

“I can’t point through corn.”

This was true. The major considered sending Peter with the boy, but Peter had already advanced several paces and was studying the soil with an appearance of expert caution.

“Come along, then,” said the major. “Keep between us.”

Thus arranged, they entered the field.

The corn closed behind them. The road, the women, the children, and the white houses disappeared. They moved within narrow green aisles where the air was hot and smelled of earth. Dry blades rubbed against their coats with furtive cracklings. Grasshoppers sprang from underfoot. Once a field mouse rushed across the path, and the major swung the rifle so suddenly that Samuel ducked.

“S-s-sh!” said Peter.

“I hain’t said anything.”

“S-s-sh anyhow.”

They advanced ten yards.

“Much farther?” asked the major.

“Aw, yes,” said Samuel.

The major paused to adjust the powder horn. “You’re certain he was a rebel?”

“He looked like one.”

“What d’you mean, looked like one?”

“Why—he looked bad.”

“That don’t prove nationality,” said Peter.

The boy’s certainty began to weaken under this military examination. “He had gray pants.”

The major and Peter exchanged glances. Gray pants were significant.

“Did he have any marks on his coat?”

“It was brown. Or maybe blue. It was dirty.”

“Did he speak?”

“He said, ‘Git out o’ here, yeh little red-headed nuisance.’”

“Southern accent?” asked the major.

Samuel reflected. “He was mad.”

The three went on.

From the road the villagers could see only the tips of the corn shaking above the course of the expedition. This evidence of progress filled them with mingled pride and grief. Mrs. Boldin had arrived breathless and indignant, but upon learning that her husband had already entered the field, she became pale and spoke of him as though he were dead.

“I told him,” she said. “I told him that rifle would be his end.”

“It may not be th’ rifle,” said Mrs. Hooper darkly.

The boys climbed the fence rails and gazed across the corn. Each desired to witness bloodshed, provided it occurred at a respectful distance. The girls held hands. The baby ceased crying and stared with the calm eyes of one for whom war and peace were equal inconveniences.

In the field, the major’s progress had lost its first grandeur. His hat had been knocked crooked by the leaves. Burrs adhered to his trousers. Perspiration moved down his face in shining paths. Peter’s gray whisker had caught a strand of corn silk and waved it like a decoration.

“Hol’ on a minnet,” Peter said.

Again they stopped.

“What is it?” whispered the major.

“I thought I heard—”

They listened.

The corn murmured. In the meadow beyond, insects shrilled without pause, a vast tiny machinery labouring in the heat. Then came a dull sound from the direction of the woods.

“Uhhh—ah!”

Samuel’s mouth opened.

“That’s him!” he breathed.

Peter looked at the major. The major looked at Peter.

“Sounded wounded,” Peter said.

“Could be a signal.”

“Could be.”

The sound came again, mournful and barbaric.

“Uhhh—grr—ah!”

The major tightened his grip upon the rifle. He pictured the hidden rebel crouched among roots, perhaps surrounded by maps, messages and weapons. If captured, the man might prove an officer of rank. There would be questions from military authorities. Newspapers might speak of Major Thomas Boldin, whose vigilance had saved a loyal village. He saw himself refusing rewards with modest firmness.

“Forward,” he said.

Peter did not move. “Hol’ on a minnet. We oughtn’t rush him.”

“I didn’t say rush.”

“You said forward in a rushin’ tone.”

“I said forward in a cautious tone.”

“Well, I’m cautious.”

At last they reached the far edge of the field. Before them lay a meadow, bright and open, sloping toward the woods. A single hickory stood near the tree line. Its shadow was black upon the grass.

Samuel pointed. “Over there.”

“Where?” asked the major.

“Past that tree.”

“Where past it?”

“In th’ woods.”

The woods appeared to the two old men as a fortress built by nature for the encouragement of rebels. Vines hung in loops between trunks. Brambles guarded the openings. Under the leaves darkness collected in silent companies. The meadow between was perfectly empty, which somehow made crossing it an act of exposure and sacrifice.

The major crouched a little.

“You see anything?”

“No,” said Peter.

“S-s-sh!”

“I only answered yeh.”

The major studied the tree line. A cicada burst into violent speech above them. Peter sprang sideways and collided with Samuel.

“Thunder!” he muttered. “What infernal thing was that?”

“Locust,” said the boy.

“I knew it.”

They moved into the meadow.

The villagers at the fence saw them emerge from the corn, three small figures in the gold light. A solemn admiration possessed the crowd. The major led, bent under his rifle; Samuel followed; Peter came last, turning often to examine the rear. To those watching, every motion had meaning. When the major stopped to free his boot from a vine, the women believed he had discovered a track. When Peter slapped at a mosquito, the boys imagined a signal.

“There they go,” whispered Mrs. Cady. “Lord preserve ’em.”

Halfway across the meadow Samuel halted.

“I hain’t goin’ no farther.”

The major turned. “Why not?”

“Because that’s where he seen me.”

“You said you seen him.”

“Well, he seen me see him.”

The boy began backing toward the corn.

“Come here,” ordered the major.

But Samuel had fulfilled his obligations to the campaign. He turned and ran. His red head moved rapidly over the grass and vanished among the stalks.

The major and Peter remained.

“This complicates matters,” said the major.

“It does,” Peter agreed.

They both gazed at the woods.

“Course,” said Peter after a time, “we don’t know exactly where he is now.”

“No.”

“Might’ve gone.”

“Might.”

“Then again, he might be watchin’ us this very second.”

The major’s eyes traveled slowly over the shadows. He felt that every leaf possessed an eye.

“Best plan,” Peter continued, “would be for one man to stay here an’ cover th’ edge while th’ other circles around.”

“Very good,” said the major. “You circle around.”

Peter shook his head. “I don’t know th’ ground like you do.”

“I don’t know it any better.”

“You’ve hunted squirrels here.”

“So’ve you.”

“Not since eighty-one.”

They considered another plan.

“Maybe,” said Peter, “one of us ought to go back fer assistance.”

The major looked toward the corn. The crowd could now see them clearly. Retreat across the meadow would be observed, discussed, and perhaps interpreted. He imagined Mrs. Boldin’s mouth.

“We can’t abandon the operation.”

“I didn’t say abandon. I said reinforce. There’s a difference. Military difference.”

“Who’d you get?”

“Well—there’s Amos Pike.”

“Crippled.”

“Jonas Bell.”

“Deaf.”

“Deaf men can shoot.”

“They can’t hear orders.”

Peter rubbed his beard. “Then I could fetch my shotgun.”

“You said it was broke.”

“Only th’ left barrel. Right barrel’s as sound as—well, it shot last winter.”

Again the groan came from the woods, louder now.

“Uhhh—Lord—”

Both men ducked.

“That was words,” Peter whispered.

“Yes.”

“Did yeh hear what words?”

“No.”

“Sounded like a prayer.”

“Rebels pray.”

The thought was discouraging. An enemy who prayed before firing possessed a terrible deliberation.

The major swallowed. “I’ll advance to the hickory. You cover me.”

“With what?”

The major looked at Peter’s empty hands.

“That’s th’ point,” said Peter. “I can’t cover yeh proper. I’ll go back an’ get my gun. I’ll bring Amos too. He can load, even if he can’t walk fast.”

The major’s face reddened. “You intend to leave me?”

“Only for tactical purposes.”

“You’re afraid.”

Peter stared at him. The accusation struck away some of his fear and replaced it with offended dignity.

“Afraid? I come as far as you did, hain’t I? I crossed th’ corn. I crossed half this meadow. If I was afraid, would I be standin’ here?”

At that instant there was a crackle among the trees. Peter took three swift steps backward.

“Only,” he added, “there hain’t sense in two unarmed men—”

“I’m armed.”

“There hain’t sense in one armed an’ one unarmed man both gettin’ killed. I’ll fetch help.”

Before the major could answer, Peter turned and walked toward the corn. For the first ten yards he maintained a grave pace. Then a grasshopper sprang against his neck, and his walk became a trot. He entered the corn at considerable speed.

The major stood alone in the meadow.

Behind him was the village, though hidden by the corn; before him was the inscrutable wood. The old rifle grew heavy. He tried to remember whether he had loaded powder before the ball or ball before powder. In the heroic scenes he had imagined, weapons had never raised such administrative difficulties.

Yet Peter would reach the crowd. There he would explain. Every face would turn toward the meadow. The major felt the eyes of the whole village upon his back.

He advanced.

At the hickory he stopped and rested the rifle against the trunk. The shade was cool, and this coolness seemed to belong to the enemy. He could see flattened grass leading between two bushes.

“Come out!” he called.

The woods gave no reply.

“I know you’re there!”

A jay screamed overhead and fled deeper among the trees.

The major cocked the rifle. The hammer produced a small, doubtful click.

“Come out, or I’ll fire!”

There was a stirring beyond the bushes, followed by a voice.

“Go ’way.”

The major’s heart struck him.

“Show yourself!”

“Go ’way, I tell yeh. Lemme sleep.”

The tone was hostile, but there was something in it which touched the major’s memory. He peered through the leaves. Upon the ground lay a large man in torn brown clothes. His boots were muddy. One arm encircled a stone jug with the tenderness of old affection. A red handkerchief covered part of his face, and gray whiskers spread upon his chest.

The major stared.

The man rolled over. The handkerchief fell away.

“Milton Jacoby!”

The sleeper opened one eye. “Who’s that?”

“Milton, you drunken fool!”

“Tom?” The man considered this with immense weariness. “Tom, yeh make more noise’n a sawmill.”

The major lowered the rifle. For some seconds he stood without motion. Glory, dispatches, captured officers and public gratitude departed from him in a quiet procession. In their place appeared the crowd at Beasley’s fence.

He looked back toward the meadow.

Peter had returned to the road and was addressing the villagers with violent gestures. Amos Pike stood beside him holding a shotgun. Mrs. Boldin had climbed the bottom fence rail. All waited.

The major turned again to Milton Jacoby, who had already replaced the handkerchief upon his face.

“You’ve caused an alarm,” said the major.

Milton snored.

The major stepped from the woods and began to cross the meadow. At first he walked. Then he perceived Peter and Amos entering the corn, and behind them several boys. If they came farther, they might discover him advancing without a prisoner. He therefore hurried. His hurry increased as he neared the field, until he appeared to the watchers as a man fleeing the jaws of tragedy.

The crowd erupted.

“He’s comin’!”

“Where’s th’ rebel?”

“Is he wounded?”

“Did he shoot him?”

Mrs. Boldin cried, “Tom!”

The major burst from the corn, scattering leaves and silk. He climbed the fence with less ceremony than before and landed in the dust among them.

“Well?” demanded twenty voices.

The major leaned upon the rifle and breathed.

“It’s all right,” he said.

“What happened?”

“Where is he?”

“Was there more’n one?”

Peter pushed forward, carrying Amos Pike’s shotgun. “Did yeh engage him?”

The major straightened. “There was no rebel.”

A deep disappointment passed through the company.

“No rebel?”

“Then what was it?”

“Samuel seen him!”

The major adjusted his coat. “The individual in the woods is Milton Jacoby.”

For one moment there was silence. Then one of the boys laughed.

“Milton!”

“Drunk again, I suppose,” said Mrs. Hooper.

“Layin’ by th’ hickory?” asked Amos Pike. “Why, that’s where he always goes.”

Mrs. Cady smiled with relief and superiority. “I thought all along it was likely Milton.”

Samuel’s face became red. “He looked like a rebel.”

“Rebel!” cried Peter. “Milton Jacoby! Anybody could tell Milton’s holler half a mile off.”

The villagers began to laugh. Their fear, having lost its object, sought someone upon whom to avenge the indignity of its existence. They looked at the major’s dusty coat, his crooked hat, the burrs on his trousers, and the ancient rifle trembling slightly in his hand.

“You marched clear through that corn after Milton?” called a woman.

“With an army behind yeh!” said Amos.

“An’ me fetchin’ a gun!” exclaimed Peter, laughing louder than any of them. “Major, yeh might’ve known. You’ve heard Milton snore often enough.”

The major glared at Samuel. “The boy gave a false report.”

“I said he looked like one,” Samuel protested.

“Well, he don’t,” said Peter. “Not to anybody with judgment.”

They turned toward the village, cheerful now, rehearsing the absurdity as though they had never trembled at the blackbird, the locust, the swaying corn or the solemn woods. The boys fought for the privilege of carrying the rifle. The women spoke of supper. Chickens scratched beside the road, insects sang in the meadow, and the woods resumed their ordinary place at the edge of the fields.

Behind the others walked Major Tom Boldin, bearing his own weapon. From every group ahead came bursts of laughter and the repeated verdict that he might have known.

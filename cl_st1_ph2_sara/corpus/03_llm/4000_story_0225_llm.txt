## The Elder-Tree Mother

There was once a little boy who had caught a cold; and he had caught it in a manner which no sensible boy could recommend to another.

He had gone out with dry stockings and sound shoes, but on his way home he had discovered a gutter filled from curb to curb with rainwater. It was much too broad to jump over, and much too deep to step through; therefore, of course, he stepped through it.

“No one saw me,” he said afterward.

But the water had seen him, and had crept into his shoes. The wind had seen him, too, and had blown about his wet ankles; and before night the cold had climbed from his toes to his throat and settled there.

Now he lay in bed with a red nose and hot cheeks, and his mother sat beside him.

“How did your feet become wet?” she asked.

“They were not very wet,” said the boy.

“How wet were they?”

“Only up to here,” he replied, pointing first to his ankles, then, on remembering the truth, a little higher.

“So you went into the gutter.”

“I only meant to see how deep it was.”

“And did you find out?”

“Yes,” said the boy. “It was deeper than I expected.”

His mother shook her head; but she smiled a little, for she was glad he had at least told the truth. Then she brought a brown earthen teapot filled with elder-flower tea, which is an excellent drink for a cold if one keeps warm afterward and does not go exploring gutters again.

The steam rose from the spout, and the room smelled of summer hedges. Outside, the rain tapped the windowpanes; inside, the curtains were drawn, the candle burned quietly, and the boy had his hands beneath the coverlet.

Just then the old man from the floor above came in. He lived alone and possessed two walking sticks, a silver snuffbox, and more stories than any cupboard could hold. Whenever children saw him, they said, “Tell us something!” and he generally did; but he never told a story until it was ready.

“Good evening,” he said. “So here lies our young water-inspector!”

“I did not know the gutter was so deep,” said the boy.

“Knowledge is often wetter than ignorance,” replied the old man. “But perhaps you have learned enough for one day.”

“Tell me a story,” begged the boy.

“A story?” The old man seated himself beside the stove. “That is easily asked. But stories ought not to be shaken out of one’s sleeve like crumbs. The best ones come of themselves.”

“Can a story come by itself?”

“To be sure. Sometimes it knocks at the door; sometimes it whispers in the chimney; and sometimes”—he leaned toward the teapot—“it grows out of elder-flower tea.”

The boy looked at the pot. Nothing appeared there except the brown lid and the white steam.

“I see no story,” he said.

“Wait. It is putting forth green shoots.”

And, indeed, something green pushed from beneath the lid. It rose like a twig in April, divided into branches, and spread above the teapot. Leaf followed leaf, fresh and tender; clusters of white blossoms opened, and their fragrance filled the room. The tree grew wider and taller until its branches reached the bed curtains, and beneath its leaves sat a little old woman.

She wore a black gown with tiny white flowers upon it, and a large cap tied under her chin. Her eyes were bright blue, and she smiled as if she knew every child who had ever hidden wet stockings.

“Who is she?” whispered the boy.

“The Elder-tree Mother,” said the old man. “Some call such a creature a dryad—that means the spirit who lives in a tree—but she has lived so long among kettles, gardens, and good people that she is quite at home indoors.”

The old woman lifted one finger.

“Hush,” she said. “There are others beneath the tree.”

Then the boy saw that the bedroom wall had opened like a curtain. Beyond it lay a small garden in the warm evening light. In the center stood a great elder tree, much larger than the one growing from the teapot; and beneath it sat an old sailor and his old wife. Both wore golden crowns.

“Are they a king and queen?” asked the boy.

“They are holding their golden wedding,” said the Elder-tree Mother. “That means they have been married fifty years. Such a day does not come quickly. One must travel toward it through fair weather and foul.”

The old sailor had white hair and a brown, weather-beaten face. His wife’s cheeks were wrinkled, but her eyes were clear. Around them stood children, grandchildren, and little great-grandchildren, all talking at once. On the table were cakes, flowers, and two glasses of wine.

The company had been noisy; now they became still.

“Do you remember,” asked the old woman, “the first time we played together?”

“Do I remember?” cried the sailor. “You wore a blue frock, and I had a wooden sword.”

“No, it was a red frock.”

“It was blue.”

“The sash was blue.”

“So it was!” said he. “Then we are both right, and that is the best way to remember.”

They laughed, and the Elder-tree Mother shook the branches above them so that the fragrance fell like invisible rain.

“We lived in neighboring houses,” said the wife. “Between the gardens there was a low fence, which we were forbidden to climb.”

“And therefore we climbed it every day,” said the sailor.

“You tore your jacket upon it.”

“And you mended it so badly that your mother knew at once.”

“You said the thorn had done it.”

“The thorn could not defend itself.”

Then they remembered how they had made a ship from a wooden tub. The boy had been captain, and the girl had sat beneath an umbrella as the passenger. Their sea was a duck pond, their foreign port was the far bank, and their cargo consisted of three apples and a pewter spoon.

“The tub sank,” said the old wife.

“Only halfway.”

“That was enough for me.”

“But I rescued the apples.”

“You rescued one apple. The ducks ate the others.”

The children around them laughed. The little boy in bed laughed too, though his mother told him not to throw off the coverlet.

“After that,” continued the sailor, “we went to school, and then the world grew larger. Yet it was not as large as it seemed, for wherever I went, I met something that reminded me of home.”

“You went away very early,” said his wife.

“Yes. I was scarcely more than a lad when I first sailed. The harbor smelled of tar and rope; gulls screamed over the masts; and the flag moved in the morning wind. You stood upon the quay with your father.”

“And I had an elder blossom in my hand.”

“You gave it to me.”

“I said, ‘Keep this, and do not forget us.’”

“As if I could forget!”

The old sailor drew from inside his coat a little book with tarnished clasps. Between two pages lay a dried elder blossom, yellow with age, but still perfect.

“I carried it across the sea,” he said. “I saw mountains blue with distance, cities with towers and domes, and shores where the sun burned white upon the stones. I heard languages I could not understand and songs I never learned. Yet whenever I opened my book, the flower told me plainly: ‘There is a garden at home, and someone waits there.’”

“And did someone wait?” asked the boy in bed.

The old wife beneath the tree seemed to hear him.

“I waited,” she said. “But waiting is not sitting still. I worked, I sewed, I tended the garden, I helped my mother, and every evening I looked down the road. In winter I thought, ‘Perhaps by spring.’ In spring I thought, ‘Perhaps before the elder blooms.’”

“And one year,” said the sailor, “I returned while it was blooming.”

“You came without warning.”

“I wanted to surprise you.”

“You frightened the hens instead. They flew over the fence, and one sat upon the pump.”

“But you knew me.”

“Not at first. You were brown as a walnut and wore a beard.”

“A very fine beard.”

“A very untidy beard.”

“And yet you married it.”

“I married what was behind it.”

The two old people looked at one another, and for a moment their faces appeared young.

They spoke of their wedding day, when rain had fallen in the morning and sunshine in the afternoon. They spoke of the little house they rented, where the roof leaked above the flour bin but nowhere else; of the first cradle; of the second voyage; of letters that crossed one another upon the sea; of anxious nights and joyful mornings; of sons who grew tall and daughters who wore white confirmation dresses; of an empty chair when one beloved child did not return; and of new small hands that afterward clasped theirs.

Their memories did not come in order. Memories seldom march like soldiers. One called another; a song brought back a room, a ribbon brought back a summer, and the scent of elder blossom opened doors which time had shut.

At last the evening sun shone red through the branches. The old sailor took his wife’s hand.

“Fifty years,” he said.

“Fifty years,” she repeated.

“It went quickly.”

“Not always.”

“No,” he said. “Not always. But now it seems so.”

The Elder-tree Mother bent over them and laid a flowering wreath upon each golden crown.

“What happened next?” asked the boy.

“What always happens next,” said the old man softly. “The youngest become old, the old close their eyes, and what they loved passes into other hearts.”

But the Elder-tree Mother clapped her hands.

“That is true,” she said, “yet truth need not sit in a dark corner. Come, little boy! The story has shown you one life; now you shall travel through another.”

In the same instant she was no longer an old woman. Her black gown became a dress white as elder blossom, her cap flew away like a cloud, and there stood a young girl with green leaves in her hair. Her eyes were the same clear blue eyes, but they shone with morning.

She sprang lightly onto the bedrail.

“Will you come?”

“I have a cold,” said the boy.

“A cold cannot travel where we are going.”

“My mother says I must stay beneath the blankets.”

“Then we shall take the blankets with us.”

She touched them, and they became a white cloud. She touched the old man’s walking stick, and it became a spirited little horse, black as coal, with a silver mane.

“Hurrah!” cried the boy.

He mounted, and the maiden seated herself behind him. The horse leaped through the window without breaking a pane, and away they flew above the roofs.

Below them lay the countryside in spring.

The beech woods had just put forth their leaves, so pale and delicate that the sunlight passed through them. White anemones covered the ground like fallen stars. Larks sang over the fields; the plow turned the dark earth; children rolled colored eggs upon a green slope. From gardens came the scent of damp soil, violets, and young thyme.

“See!” said the maiden. “This is home waking.”

They skimmed over a castle reflected in a lake, over red-roofed villages and narrow roads where carts moved slowly between hedges. At every cottage the people seemed to know the horse, and they waved handkerchiefs from the doors.

Then spring deepened into summer.

The fields grew high and golden. Cornflowers shone blue among the grain, and poppies burned red. Cattle stood knee-deep in cool streams. Along the coast, sails moved like white wings, and the sea glittered so brightly that one had to narrow one’s eyes.

At evening the smell of new-mown hay rose from the meadows. Young people danced near a haystack while a fiddler played; old people sat upon benches and kept time with their feet. The moon came up, round and yellow, and every pond held another moon within it.

“How beautiful!” cried the boy.

“It is beautiful because you see it,” said the maiden. “Many ride through summer with their eyes shut.”

They passed the places where kings had slept, where soldiers had marched, and where nameless men and women had planted orchards whose fruit their grandchildren ate. The maiden knew each hill and church tower. She knew which window had held a lamp for a traveler, which bridge had carried a bride home, and beneath which tree a child had first read a book.

Then summer became autumn.

The woods flamed red, green, and gold. Apples bent the branches; nuts rattled into baskets; wild geese wrote dark letters across the sky. The wind ran through the reeds, and the sea, no longer blue and smiling, lifted gray shoulders beneath the clouds.

The boy was taller now. His legs nearly reached the little horse’s stirrups.

“Where are we going?” he asked.

“To the harbor,” she replied.

There stood a ship ready to sail. Men carried chests aboard; ropes creaked; mothers gave advice that could not be followed upon the ocean; fathers said little and gripped hands firmly.

The maiden plucked an elder blossom from her hair and gave it to him.

“But elder does not bloom in autumn,” he said.

“It blooms wherever remembrance requires it.”

He placed the flower in a hymn book and shut the clasps.

“Keep it,” she said. “You will see many lands. Admire them all, learn from them all, and do good where you can; but do not imagine that distance makes home smaller.”

The horse vanished, and the boy stood upon the deck, no longer a child but a young sailor. The maiden remained upon the quay. He waved; she waved; the ship moved out beneath the flag, and the town became a cluster of roofs, then a gray line, then only a memory.

He sailed southward, where the air smelled of oranges and the stars seemed larger. He saw marble palaces, dark forests, burning deserts, and islands green in a violet sea. Storms tore the sails; calm weather held the ship motionless for days. Once he thought he would never return. Then he opened the hymn book, and although the elder blossom was pressed flat between its pages, its fragrance rose fresh and sweet.

Immediately he saw the spring woods, the moon over the hayfield, the red roofs, and a garden enclosed by a low fence.

Years passed. He returned home, and a young woman waited near an elder tree. Whether she was the maiden who had carried him through the seasons, or another with the same blue eyes, he could not tell; and in the best stories one should not always tell everything.

They married.

The boy, who was now a man, built a house with windows toward the sea. Children played upon its floor. Wet boots stood in the passage, as wet boots will where children live, though he often told them the history of a certain gutter. There were birthdays, illnesses, voyages, bills to pay, stockings to mend, letters to read twice, quarrels that seemed important at noon and foolish by supper, and quiet evenings when the kettle sang on the stove.

Winter came.

The trees stood white as coral. Snow lay upon the fields, and sleigh bells rang along the roads. Behind the windows, candles shone; apples roasted on the hearth; children danced around the Christmas tree while their elders watched and remembered other children dancing in other rooms.

The sailor’s hair turned gray. His children grew up and went away, then returned with children of their own. The house became crowded again, then quiet again, as houses do.

Winter changed to spring, spring to summer, and the years flew faster than the magical horse.

At last the sailor and his wife sat beneath a great elder tree. Their hands were old, and their faces were lined, but they leaned toward one another just as young people do when sharing a secret.

Around them stood sons and daughters, grandchildren, and little great-grandchildren. On the table were flowers, cakes, and two glasses of wine. Upon their heads were golden crowns, for it was their golden wedding day.

“Do you remember,” asked the wife, “when you rode away upon a black horse?”

“I remember a ship,” said he.

“There was a horse before the ship.”

“Perhaps I dreamed it.”

“And the girl with leaves in her hair?”

“I remember her best when I smell elder blossom.”

He opened an old hymn book. Between its pages lay the flower she had given him long ago. It was not brown and withered, but white and fresh, as though plucked that morning.

Then the branches stirred, though there was no wind. The young maiden stepped from the tree.

No one appeared astonished. On golden wedding days many guests arrive from far away, and some come from farther than others.

She placed a wreath upon the old wife’s crown and another upon the sailor’s.

“Do you know me?” she asked.

“You are the Elder-tree Mother,” said the wife.

“You are the girl from the road,” said the sailor.

“You are springtime,” cried a grandchild.

“You are the one who lives in the teapot,” said a very small great-grandchild, who perhaps knew more than the others.

The maiden smiled.

“I am Memory,” she said. “I sit in flowers and old songs, in familiar rooms and books that have traveled far. I keep what love has truly seen. I make no life free from sorrow, but I prevent sorrow from having the last word. And when a tale comes of itself, I am often the one who opens the door.”

She touched the elder blossom. At once the garden filled with scenes from the old couple’s life. There they were as children beside the forbidden fence; there was the tub-ship upon the pond; there was the harbor, the wedding rain, the leaking roof, the first cradle, the long table at Christmas, the empty chair, and the hands of generations joined beneath the tree.

The old sailor looked carefully.

“But that is the other couple’s story,” he said.

“It is yours,” replied Memory.

“It resembles theirs.”

“All true lives resemble one another in some things,” she said, “yet no two are the same. One flower grows beside another; both are elder blossoms, but each has opened to its own sun.”

The evening light became golden. The old couple closed their eyes, not from weariness only, but because happiness itself sometimes asks for silence. The crowns shone among the white blossoms, and all the children stood very still.

Then the tree folded its branches. The garden shrank, the seasons swept backward, the ship became a stick, and the cloud became a coverlet.

The little boy opened his eyes.

He was in his own bed. Rain still tapped the panes. The old man sat beside the stove, and his mother stood with the brown teapot in her hands. There was no tree growing from its lid—only a thin line of steam.

“Well?” asked the old man. “Was it a proper story?”

“Did you tell it?” asked the boy.

“Perhaps.”

“Did I dream it?”

“Perhaps.”

“Did the Elder-tree Mother really come?”

The old man took a pinch of snuff and looked extremely wise.

“That is the wrong question,” he said. “You should ask whether you will remember her.”

“I shall,” said the boy. “And when I am old, I will tell the same story.”

“No,” replied the old man. “You will tell your own, and it may sound like this one.”

The mother poured another cup of tea.

“Drink while it is warm,” she said. “Stories are all very well, but colds must be attended to.”

The boy drank. The tea smelled of summer hedges, moonlit hayfields, distant harbors, winter rooms, and a garden where two golden crowns shone beneath white blossoms.

He looked into the teapot, but it was quite ordinary.

“Where has she gone?” he asked.

“Gone?” said his mother. “The Elder-tree Mother does not go away. She remains inside the pot until someone is quiet enough to see her.”

And because the boy was warm now, and tired from so long a journey—whether taken in a dream, in a story, or upon a black horse—he closed his eyes.

The rain went on tapping at the window, but beneath that small sound he thought he heard leaves whispering, and an old voice that was also young saying:

“Remember.”

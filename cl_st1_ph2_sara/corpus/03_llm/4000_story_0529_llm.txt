# The Christmas the Daemons Stole Santa

Beyond the last village of the North Country, where the snow lies white and smooth for half the year and the stars appear close enough to be polished with a cloth, there is a pleasant place called the Laughing Valley.

The brook that runs through it chuckles over bright pebbles; the wind whistles among the pines; the sunbeams dance upon the grass; and even the little blue flowers seem to smile at those who pass. In the very center of the valley stands a broad, cheerful house with many windows, many chimneys, and a door wide enough for the largest toy-cart ever made. There our good old Santa Claus lives with his faithful assistants, Nuter, Peter, Kilter, and Wisk.

Nuter was slow and deliberate and never tied a ribbon until he had measured it twice. Peter was a crabbed fellow who grumbled at everything, although he always did his work well. Kilter was thoughtful and clever, with a head full of lists and plans, while Wisk was nimble, merry, and so quick that he could fill a stocking before a child had finished turning over in bed.

All year they labored in Santa’s workshops, fashioning dolls and drums, sleds and wagons, tin trumpets, wooden animals, puzzles, picture books, and every other toy that may properly delight the heart of childhood. Around them worked the ryls, who painted the toys; the knooks, who guarded the animals; the pixies, who stitched soft garments for the dolls; and many willing fairies who carried messages wherever messages were required.

Now, upon one side of the Laughing Valley rose a bleak mountain.

Its lower slopes were gray, its upper crags were black, and deep within its stony heart were five caves. The first belonged to the Daemon of Selfishness. Beyond it lay the Cave of Envy; beyond that, the Cave of Hatred; and beyond that again, the Cave of Malice. The fifth and last was the Cave of Repentance.

This arrangement was no accident. A person who becomes selfish soon envies what others possess; one who envies may learn to hate; and hatred, if cherished, often ripens into malice. After malice has wrought its mischief, Repentance may follow, although it sometimes arrives late and is not always welcome.

The first four caves opened only into one another and into twisting passages full of shadows, loose stones, and deep pitfalls. But from the Cave of Repentance a narrow path wound upward and at last emerged into fresh air and sunshine. What lies beneath the lowest tunnels I do not know, and I hope neither you nor I shall ever have reason to discover.

The Daemons who inhabited these caves were greatly troubled by Santa Claus.

“He is ruining our business,” complained the Daemon of Selfishness. “Every Christmas he gives things away for nothing. Children see his example and begin giving to one another.”

“Worse than that,” said the Daemon of Envy, whose green eyes were always fixed upon what belonged to someone else, “the children are pleased with what they receive. Contentment is the enemy of Envy.”

“They love him,” growled the Daemon of Hatred. “Where love enters, Hatred must retreat.”

“And he teaches them kindness,” hissed the Daemon of Malice. “A kind child is of no use to me at all.”

The Daemon of Repentance sat at the edge of the gloomy council and sighed.

“I cannot very well assist you unless you first do something wicked,” he explained. “That is the difficulty of my position.”

The others told him to hold his tongue.

After much discussion they determined to tempt Santa into their caves. If they could persuade him to become selfish, envious, hateful, or malicious, they believed his influence over children would be destroyed.

So one autumn morning the Daemon of Selfishness went down into the Laughing Valley. He found Santa in the great storehouse examining rows of newly finished toys.

There were woolly lambs with silver bells, red engines with golden wheels, dolls in silken gowns, and little brown bears whose arms opened whenever a child desired a hug.

“Good morning,” said Santa pleasantly. “What can I do for you?”

“That is always your question,” answered Selfishness. “What can you do for others! Have you never considered what others might do for you?”

“Frequently,” replied Santa. “Nuter keeps my records, Peter mends my harness, Kilter plans my journeys, Wisk makes me laugh, and the dear children reward me by being happy.”

“But you give away all these beautiful toys,” urged the Daemon. “Keep them! Lock the doors and enjoy them yourself.”

Santa looked at the thousands of dolls, drums, balls, and wagons.

“What should I do with so many toys?” he asked.

“You would possess them.”

“Ah, but possession without use is a poor pleasure,” said Santa. “A drum is silent until a child beats it, a doll has no adventures until a child loves it, and a sled cannot laugh as it flies downhill. These things become valuable only when given away.”

The Daemon scowled and returned to his cave.

Next came the Daemon of Envy.

He met Santa near the reindeer meadow, where the deer were tossing their antlers beneath the bright sky.

“There are merchants in the cities,” Envy whispered, “who sell toys at great profit. They dwell in handsome houses, wear rich garments, and receive gold for the very things you give away. Surely you envy them.”

“Not at all,” answered Santa. “They must pay rent, count coins, keep shop, and worry over what remains unsold. I have the Laughing Valley, faithful friends, a strong sleigh, and more work than I can finish. Why should I envy anyone?”

“But some of their toys are finer than yours.”

“Then I am glad,” said Santa. “The more excellent toys there are in the world, the happier the children may be.”

Envy could make nothing of such a fellow and departed in disgust.

The Daemon of Hatred tried next.

“There are children who say they do not believe in you,” he declared. “There are grown people who mock your name, and misers who refuse to give even a crust to the poor. You should hate them.”

Santa shook his white head.

“To hate unbelievers would not make them believe,” he replied. “To hate mockers would not make them gentle, and to hate misers would not make them generous. Besides, a heart filled with hatred has little room left for Christmas.”

The Daemon ground his teeth, but Santa only smiled, so Hatred also went away defeated.

At last Malice climbed down from the mountain.

“You are growing old, graybeard,” he said. “Each year your journey becomes harder. Why not place stones in chimneys, break the merchants’ windows, frighten the reindeer, and spoil Christmas altogether? Then everyone would be as miserable as you ought to be.”

“My dear fellow,” replied Santa, “I am not miserable.”

“You must be tired.”

“I am often tired.”

“Then you should wish others to suffer.”

“When I am tired,” said Santa, “I wish others to rest. When I am cold, I wish others warmth. When I am hungry, I wish others food. Misery shared is still misery, but kindness shared grows greater with the sharing.”

Malice stamped so hard that three icicles fell from the roof.

“You are impossible!” he cried.

“That,” Santa answered cheerfully, “is what Peter says whenever I change the packing lists.”

Thus every temptation came to naught, and the four wicked Daemons were more offended than before.

Christmas Eve drew near. The workshops rang with hammers, bells, songs, and Peter’s complaints. Nuter checked every label twice. Kilter arranged the gifts by country, city, village, street, and house. Wisk flew from room to room so swiftly that his cap trailed behind him like a red flame.

At sunset on Christmas Eve, Santa’s great sleigh stood ready. Its sides shone crimson, its runners flashed like moonlit ice, and behind the seat rose a mountain of sacks stuffed with gifts. Ten reindeer pawed the snow, sending up clouds of sparkling powder.

“Everything loaded?” asked Santa.

“Everything,” said Nuter.

“Everything except common sense,” muttered Peter. “No sleigh should carry this much.”

“The route is marked,” said Kilter.

“And the bells are tuned!” cried Wisk, giving them a shake.

Santa climbed into the seat.

“Guard the valley until I return,” he told his assistants. “And keep the fire burning.”

Then the reindeer bounded forward, the runners sang upon the snow, and sleigh, toys, and driver swept up into the starry sky.

High above the valley Santa flew, following a silver road of moonlight. He passed over sleeping farms, dark forests, frozen rivers, and villages whose windows glowed like golden beads. But when he approached the bleak mountain, the Daemons were waiting.

Selfishness, Envy, Hatred, and Malice had woven a black rope from shadows and stretched it across the air between two peaks. The rope could not be seen against the night, even by eyes as experienced as Santa’s.

The leading reindeer struck it and stumbled in the air. The sleigh tipped sharply; the bells clashed; and Santa was thrown into a bank of snow upon a narrow ledge.

Before he could rise, the four Daemons sprang upon him.

“Take your hands off my coat,” said Santa sternly. “You will wrinkle the fur.”

They bound his arms with the shadow rope and dragged him through a crack in the mountainside. The reindeer, frightened but unhurt, tore free and sped away with the sleigh.

“Now,” cried Selfishness, “the children will receive nothing!”

“They will envy those who have more than they,” said Envy.

“They will hate Santa for forgetting them,” said Hatred.

“And they will become spiteful and cruel,” added Malice.

“That is a mighty clever plan,” Santa remarked, “except that clever wicked plans are apt to contain their own defeat.”

The Daemons thrust him into a rocky chamber and rolled a great stone across the entrance.

“We shall see!” they shouted.

The runaway reindeer reached the Laughing Valley shortly before midnight. The empty driver’s seat and the broken harness told their own fearful story.

Wisk was the first to discover them.

“Santa is gone!” he cried.

Peter hurried from the workshop, grumbling, but when he saw the empty sleigh his crabbed face became pale. Nuter examined every strap. Kilter questioned the reindeer, for he understood their speech, and soon learned where the disaster had occurred.

“We must rescue him at once,” declared Wisk.

“At once,” agreed Peter. “I have always said that mountain should be pulled down.”

Nuter looked at the toy sacks.

“The children are waiting,” he said slowly.

The four assistants became silent.

They loved Santa dearly, and every impulse urged them to hasten to his aid. Yet they knew what Santa himself would command.

“His work must come first,” said Kilter at last. “If we abandon the journey, the Daemons will have accomplished their purpose.”

Peter frowned and rubbed his nose.

“I dislike good sense when it delays a rescue,” he said. “Nevertheless, good sense is good sense.”

So the assistants harnessed the reindeer again. Nuter took the reins, because his hands were steady. Kilter sat beside him with the route book. Peter guarded the sacks, and Wisk prepared to leap down chimneys.

Away they flew.

They had never made the entire Christmas journey without Santa, and at first matters did not go smoothly. Nuter drove so carefully that they nearly fell behind the clock. Kilter spent precious moments studying each address. Peter complained that every chimney was too narrow, too crooked, too smoky, or built in the wrong place. Wisk moved with such speed that he occasionally entered the correct house and filled the wrong stocking.

At one cottage he left a sailor doll for a boy who had asked for a drum and carried the drum three streets farther to a girl who had hoped for a doll. In a city apartment he gave a wooden elephant to a baby and a silver rattle to the baby’s astonished elder brother. Peter dropped a jumping toy into a flour barrel, and Nuter placed a pair of skates beneath the tree of a child who lived on a warm island and had never seen ice.

Yet these errors were few.

The assistants remembered Santa’s methods. They entered quietly, moved gently, and gave careful thought to each sleeping child. They left books where minds were curious, warm garments where rooms were cold, games where brothers and sisters might play together, and toys sturdy enough to endure much love.

Wisk even paused beside one small bed where no stocking had been hung. The child’s family was poor, and the bare room contained neither tree nor decorations.

“There is no name on the list,” whispered Nuter from the chimney.

“There is a child,” Wisk whispered back. “That is name enough for me.”

He placed his own little silver whistle beneath the pillow.

By dawn, nearly every child upon Santa’s route had received a gift, and laughter began to rise with the sun. It rose from cottages and palaces, from farms and ships, from snowy towns and warm southern villages. It rose so brightly that the Daemons heard it through the mountain walls.

“What is that noise?” demanded Hatred.

“Children,” answered Santa from behind the stone.

“They cannot be happy!” cried Envy.

“They sound happy,” said Santa.

Malice hurried through the tunnels and peered out from a crack in the cliff. Far below, the reindeer were returning to the Laughing Valley with four weary assistants in the sleigh.

“We have been tricked!” he screamed.

“No,” replied Santa. “You merely supposed that kindness depends upon one person. A good example is a seed, and seeds have a habit of growing.”

When Nuter, Peter, Kilter, and Wisk reached home, they allowed themselves no rest. Kilter sent messengers to all the friendly immortals of the North Country. The fairies carried word to the forests, the pixies to the hills, the ryls to the valleys, and the knooks to every place where beasts dwelt under magical protection.

Before noon an extraordinary army gathered in the Laughing Valley.

There were fairies in shining ranks, pixies in pointed caps, sturdy gnomes with crystal hammers, knooks riding shaggy bears, ryls carrying bright spears, and nymphs crowned with evergreen. Frost spirits came glittering out of the air; river folk climbed from beneath the ice; and great birds circled overhead, beating their wings like drums.

Peter surveyed them with approval.

“We shall crack the mountain open,” he said.

“No,” answered Kilter thoughtfully. “Santa may be inside the part you crack.”

“Then we shall crack it carefully.”

Wisk drew a tiny sword.

“We shall march through every cave until we find him!”

Nuter climbed onto the sleigh and raised one hand.

“For Santa,” he said.

“For Santa!” cried the assembled host, and the sound shook snow from the pines.

Meanwhile, in the deepest portion of the mountain, the Daemon of Repentance had become very unhappy.

As I have said, Repentance cannot act until wrongdoing has occurred. But once the kidnapping was accomplished and the wicked Daemons began boasting of it, he had ample cause to awaken.

He thought of Santa lying bound in the cold chamber. He thought of the anxious assistants and frightened reindeer. He thought also of the children who might have awakened to empty stockings, although, as it happened, they had not.

“I regret this business,” he said.

“Then stop thinking about it,” snapped Selfishness.

“I regret helping you tie the rope.”

“You tied only one knot,” said Envy.

“It was an excellent knot,” said Repentance sadly. “That makes it worse.”

Hatred pushed him aside. “Regret is useless.”

“Not if it leads to repair,” replied Repentance.

He slipped away through the tunnels and went to Santa’s prison. The great stone was heavy, but Repentance knew a hidden lever in the wall. He pressed it, and the stone rolled aside.

Santa sat upon the floor, humming a Christmas song. The shadow rope still bound his arms, although it had loosened somewhat, for shadows become weak in the daytime.

“I have come to release you,” announced Repentance.

“I hoped you would,” said Santa.

“Did you know I would repent?”

“One can never be certain,” answered Santa. “But your cave has an exit into the sunshine, and that seemed promising.”

Repentance untied the rope.

“I did wrong,” he confessed. “I listened to the others because I wished to belong to their council. Then I helped capture you, although I knew it was wicked.”

“And now?”

“Now I wish to undo what I can.”

“Then show me the way out,” said Santa kindly. “A regret that only sighs is a poor thing, but a regret that repairs damage may become useful.”

They passed through the Cave of Malice, where sharp stones jutted like teeth; through the Cave of Hatred, where every sound returned as an angry echo; through the Cave of Envy, whose walls glittered with false jewels that vanished when touched; and through the Cave of Selfishness, where heaps of hoarded treasure had gathered dust because no one enjoyed them.

At last they entered the Cave of Repentance. Though dark at its lower end, it grew brighter as they climbed, until they came to the narrow path leading into open day.

Santa stepped outside and drew a deep breath of cold, clean air.

At that very moment the rescue army appeared on the snowy plain below. The fairies flashed, the gnomes lifted their hammers, the knooks urged their bears onward, and Nuter, Peter, Kilter, and Wisk rode at their head.

“There he is!” cried Wisk.

The army rushed forward with such joy that Santa was nearly buried beneath embraces, wings, caps, paws, and evergreen garlands.

Peter grasped his hand.

“Are you injured?”

“Only annoyed.”

“Good,” said Peter. “Stand aside while we destroy the Daemons.”

The gnomes raised their hammers. The frost spirits prepared an icy storm. The fairies drew their shining bows, and the bears growled at the cave mouths.

But Santa stepped between his friends and the mountain.

“No,” he said.

“No?” repeated Peter, astonished.

“We cannot destroy these Daemons,” Santa explained. “Selfishness, Envy, Hatred, and Malice have existed a very long time, and hammering a mountain will not rid the world of them. Indeed, if you bury them here, they may creep out elsewhere.”

“Then we shall punish them,” cried Wisk.

“They have already failed,” said Santa. “The children received their gifts, their scheme came to naught, and they now know that I have loyal friends. Besides, Repentance has freed me, and we should not answer one repaired wrong with another injury.”

The Daemon of Repentance stood uncertainly near the cave entrance.

Santa turned to him.

“You did badly at first and well afterward. Remember which action brought you into the sunshine.”

Repentance bowed his head.

The army lowered its weapons, though Peter did so reluctantly.

“I had hoped to crack something,” he complained.

“You may crack the ice on the reindeer trough when we return,” Santa promised.

Thus the great host marched back to the Laughing Valley, with Santa riding in the sleigh and the banners of the fairies floating above him. The brook laughed louder than ever when he arrived; the wind whistled a welcome; the sunbeams danced upon the snow; and every bell in the workshops rang without being touched.

But Santa did not rest long.

Kilter brought him the list of doubtful deliveries, and together they examined it.

“A sailor doll instead of a drum,” said Kilter.

“We shall exchange them.”

“An elephant for a baby.”

“The baby may keep it if it is pleased.”

“A silver rattle for an elder brother.”

“Give him the jumping toy from the flour barrel.”

“Skates sent to a warm island.”

Santa smiled.

“Pack a wheeled sled and a book about snow.”

That night, although Christmas had passed, Santa harnessed two reindeer and made a quiet second journey. He corrected each mistake, exchanged the drum and doll, rescued the jumping toy from the flour, and delivered the wheeled sled to the island child.

He also visited the poor cottage where Wisk had left his silver whistle. There Santa placed a fine wooden flute beside the child’s bed, and on returning home he gave Wisk a new whistle made of gold.

“You acted without a name on the list,” Santa said.

“I feared I had broken the rules,” replied Wisk.

“You remembered the reason for the rules,” said Santa, “which is better.”

As for the four wicked Daemons, they sat for many days in their gloomy council chamber, listening to the sounds of happiness drifting from the world outside.

“Our plan was perfect,” grumbled Selfishness.

“Except that Santa’s friends completed his work,” said Envy.

“And an army came to rescue him,” added Hatred.

“And Repentance betrayed us,” snarled Malice.

“I corrected you,” said Repentance from the doorway. “There is a difference.”

After that the Daemons never again interfered with Santa’s Christmas journey. They still whispered to selfish hearts, envious minds, hateful tempers, and malicious tongues, for such is their nature and their place in the world. But they remembered the fairies, the gnomes, the knooks, the ryls, the nymphs, the bears, the frost spirits, and, above all, the loyalty of four humble assistants who had done their duty when their master was absent.

And Santa continued to travel each Christmas Eve, while the sleigh bells rang over mountain and forest and sleeping town.

For to give is to loosen the grasp of Selfishness; to be content is to close the door on Envy; to love is to silence Hatred; and to do kindness is to defeat Malice. If we fail in these things, Repentance may yet show us a path upward—but it is wiser, and certainly pleasanter, to remain from the beginning in the fresh air and sunshine.

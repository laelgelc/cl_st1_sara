In those days Lykeham and I were young, which is to say that we possessed every vice except avarice and called the deficiency virtue. We rose at noon, worked when driven to it by the grossest threats, drank as an intellectual exercise, and maintained that a clean collar was the mark of a wasted morning. In another thirty years our idleness will be described as contemplation, our insolence as independence, and our inability to digest supper as temperance. Age is the most obliging of biographers.

Lykeham was beautiful in the manner of persons who are aware of the fact. His hair curled without assistance; his nose had the arrogance of a Roman conqueror; and his eyes, which were brown and rather too large, seemed always to be admiring the effect they produced. Women considered him poetic. Men called him Lykeham.

One February night he entered my rooms at half-past one, bringing with him cold air, cigar smoke, and an expression of cataclysmic importance.

“I have seen a goddess,” he said.

“Did she see you?”

“She knew me.”

“That is less reassuring.”

He flung his coat upon my Aristotle and himself upon the sofa. Great Tom had ceased to count the hours, but a man in the room below was attempting to play a Spanish song upon a guitar. He possessed only three chords and distrusted one of them.

“You are profane,” said Lykeham.

“I am sleepy.”

“I have passed through fire.”

“You look damp.”

“It is raining.”

“So much for the fire.”

He sat up and regarded me with the patient sorrow of Apollo inspecting a satyr.

“You have never loved,” he said.

“I have frequently desired. Love appears to be desire corrected for publication.”

“No, no. Desire is a match; love is the conflagration, the burning of Troy, the final splendour of the world.”

“You ought not to go to London unattended.”

This, it appeared, was precisely what he had done. He had gone up two nights before upon business which, when stripped of its decorative foliage, proved to be the acquisition of a waistcoat. Having bought the waistcoat, he dined, drank champagne from motives of loneliness, and went to a theatre because the actress’s name suggested depravity. The performance was one of those modern comedies in which everybody is married and no one regards the circumstance as conclusive.

It was during the second act that he saw her.

“Across the house,” he said, “in the first tier. Pale—not merely pale, but luminous. Her hair was dark as the spaces between the stars, and her throat—”

“Was presumably attached to her shoulders.”

“Her throat,” he repeated, ignoring me, “was the column on which the night rested. She wore silver-grey. There were diamonds at her breast, but they seemed coarse beside her. Everything else in the theatre—paint, jewels, uniforms, damned women, bald men—became dust. She was cold and ardent, remote and intimate, passionately virginal—”

“Virginally passionate would be neater.”

“Virginally passionate, then. Burning ice.”

“That has been used.”

“Not of her.”

He had stared. She had turned her head and looked directly at him. He knew at once, he said, that she had been waiting through centuries of mortal ugliness for this encounter.

“How did you know?”

“By her eyes.”

“What did they do?”

“They widened.”

“You were wearing the waistcoat?”

“Yes.”

“That may explain it.”

At the interval he crossed the theatre. There are men who can traverse a crowded foyer as ships move through water, leaving lesser creatures agitated in their wake. Lykeham was such a vessel. He reached her as she stood alone beside a marble pillar, and neither of them, according to him, experienced surprise.

“You have come,” she said.

“I have come,” he replied.

This dialogue struck me as inadequate, but Lykeham maintained that the greatest emotions reject syntax. He offered no explanation of how she had escaped her companions, or whether she had any; indeed, the mortal machinery surrounding her had apparently vanished. They went out together before the third act, which perhaps proves divine wisdom more certainly than anything that followed.

They walked beneath the London lamps. Rain had polished the streets, and the lamps were repeated beneath their feet in long, trembling shafts of gold. She would not tell him her name. He would not tell her his. Names, she said, belonged to the accidents of incarnation.

“That sounds like a woman concealing a husband,” I remarked.

“She was unmarried.”

“How did you discover that?”

“I knew.”

“Again by the eyes?”

“By the whole immortal candour of her.”

They supped in a private room at an hotel. She ate hardly anything, drank white wine, and asked him whether he remembered Delos. Lykeham, who had obtained a third in Classical Moderations chiefly by attributing everything to Euripides, said that he remembered it as one remembers a dream. This pleased her. He then asked whether she remembered the vale of Tempe. She closed her eyes and shivered.

“Daphne,” he whispered.

“Phœbus,” she answered.

At this point the guitar beneath us committed a shrill and lingering outrage.

“You see?” Lykeham cried.

“I see that mythology has suffered another assault.”

“You fool! We recognized one another. We were gods in exile, clothed for a few hours in human bodies.”

“The exile appears to have included a good supper.”

He rose and paced between the fire and the window. “There were moments when she seemed to draw light from hidden sources. Her hands were cool, but where she touched me I burned. We kissed.”

“Divinely?”

“Do not sneer.”

“I am asking for classification.”

“Her lips were—”

“Spare me the fruit and flowers.”

He spared me nothing. Her lips were pomegranate, autumn rose, snow kindled by sunrise, and a wound in heaven. His own conduct, though enveloped in Homeric mist, appeared to have been energetic. They passed the night together. At dawn she stood at the window, clothed in the colourless London morning, and seemed less a woman than the last brightness of the moon before day.

“Will you find me?” she asked.

“In every life,” he said.

Then she kissed his forehead and departed.

“You did not ask her address?”

“Gods have no addresses.”

“Hotels do.”

He looked genuinely wounded. “You reduce revelation to the Post Office.”

“I should like revelation to be forwarded.”

For the next fortnight Lykeham lived upon tobacco and metaphysics. He attended no lectures and wrote several poems, all of which contained laurel. He became thinner, though not, I thought, less pleased with his appearance. At dinner he would break off in the middle of a sentence, gaze toward the dark window, and murmur, “She is somewhere beneath the same moon.” Since the moon was usually hidden by Oxford cloud, this gave him an excuse for melancholy without requiring astronomical accuracy.

I did not believe his story. This must be clearly stated, not because my disbelief does me credit, but because subsequent events gave it an elegance it did not possess at the time. I believed that Lykeham had encountered a handsome and adventurous woman, that champagne had promoted her to Olympus, and that vanity had supplied both parties with suitable pedigrees. The gods, I felt, had enough troubles without undergraduates.

A fortnight after his return we dined with Carteret at Magdalen. It was an oppressive entertainment. Carteret had discovered Socialism, and required us to hear about it between the soup and the port. At ten Lykeham and I escaped and set out toward our college by the longer way, because the shorter led past a proctor whom Lykeham had insulted in a sonnet.

The night had cleared. A late moon hung above the plain, almost full and extraordinarily white. Oxford behind us was a congregation of towers and chimneys, black beneath a faint, smoky radiance; before us the meadows extended into a tranquillity so complete that each hedge seemed drawn in ink. From far off came the heavy murmur of the weir. Peewits cried over fields we could not see, and once a horse moved behind a gate with the soft, deliberate noise of an animal dreaming.

Even Lykeham was silent. Beauty sometimes imposed upon him the restraint which friendship could not.

We had crossed the bridge and followed the road for perhaps a mile when he stopped so abruptly that I collided with him.

“There,” he said.

“Where?”

“On the hill.”

I saw only a pale strip of road, a row of pollarded willows, and, above them, the moon.

“She is calling.”

“No one has spoken.”

He seized my arm. His face had altered. The theatrical exaltation was gone; he looked frightened, hungry, and absurdly young.

“Can’t you hear her?”

I heard the weir, the wind moving among dry reeds, and my own digestion protesting against Carteret’s principles.

Then Lykeham left the road.

There was a gate three yards away, but passion has never respected agricultural convenience. He went over the hedge head-first, vanished, swore, rose, and began running across the field. I followed through the gate. Friendship may be incapable of sharing another man’s pleasure, but it has a vulgar appetite for his disasters.

He ran with astonishing speed. The moonlight showed him now bounding over furrows, now floundering in ditches, now mounting a bank and immediately descending it on the wrong side. He crossed a second field, frightened sheep into a white, flowing panic, tore through a thorn hedge, and lost one shoe in mud of profound and ancient character. I pursued at a pace governed equally by curiosity and breathlessness, calling upon him to stop, wait, explain, or at least use gates. He answered only once, with a cry that may have been “Daphne!” and may have been pain.

Beyond the fields lay a little wood. Lykeham plunged into it. Branches cracked; leaves rattled; an owl protested in basso profondo. I entered more cautiously and found myself among hazels and young beeches, where the moon came down in broken coins. Ahead, Lykeham crashed through the undergrowth with the reckless confidence of a boar who has misunderstood Greek poetry.

Then the noise ceased.

I went forward. The trees opened upon a glade, and across it ran a path of moonlight, broad, unbroken, almost material. At its farther end stood a woman.

I have no wish to make myself ridiculous, and therefore record only that which ridicule cannot amend. She was tall; her dress was pale; her hair was dark. The moon was behind her, yet her face was visible. No mist softened her, no distance magnified her. She stood in that wet Oxfordshire wood with the composure of one to whom forests, darkness and solitude were domestic things.

Lykeham stumbled into the glade. His coat was torn, his hat gone, one foot bare, and mud had reached the Olympian line of his waistcoat.

She smiled.

“So,” she said, “you have found me.”

At the sound of her voice the wood seemed to listen. The little noises of the night drew back; even the weir became remote.

Lykeham held out his hands. “I knew you would call.”

“And you came running.”

“Through the world, if need be.”

“Through two hedges,” I murmured, but neither attended.

She came toward him along the path of moonlight. It gathered about her feet and seemed to cling to her dress. When she reached him, she touched his tangled hair with a tenderness so grave that my mockery failed me. He bowed his head. Then she embraced him, torn coat, mud, vanity and all, and kissed him upon the mouth.

At that instant I knew her.

Not her mortal name—if she possessed one—nor the London hotel, nor any useful particular. I knew the older shape beneath the woman: the silver huntress, queen of the silent hour, mistress of hounds and secret woods. She was Cynthia, whom poets flatter because they fear the dark.

But Lykeham?

He had been wrong about Apollo. Of that I felt certain. Apollo was sunlight, measure, music, intolerable competence. Lykeham had no measure, and his musical gifts were confined to requesting other people to stop. Yet there was a lover of Cynthia—I remembered horns, trees, pursuit, some scandal among reeds—but the name evaded me.

The woman raised her face. For one second she looked past Lykeham and directly at me. Her eyes were clear, cold and amused. She had known all along that I was there.

A cloud crossed the moon.

The glade darkened.

When the light returned, Lykeham stood alone.

He said nothing on the way home. This was so unprecedented that I did not disturb it. He limped beside me with his shoe in his hand, occasionally touching his lips. At the college gate he turned.

“You saw her?”

“Yes.”

“And now you believe?”

“I believe you embraced a woman in a wood.”

“A goddess.”

“Very probably.”

He smiled with exhausted triumph. “Daphne,” he said. “And I—Phœbus.”

I permitted him this happiness until morning.

Then, while he slept, I went to the library and consulted Lemprière. The old volume opened reluctantly, breathing dust and moral disapproval. I read of Cynthia, of the moon, of woods and lonely hills; I pursued cross-references through Diana, Selene and Arcadia. At last I found the lover whom memory had hidden from me—the woodland deity, lustful, vain, noisy, sudden in pursuit; the terror of nymphs, the patron of flocks, the creature whose music disturbed the noon and whose lower extremities rendered the wearing of shoes at once difficult and unnecessary.

I carried the book to Lykeham’s rooms.

He lay amid cigar ash and laurel poems, looking pale and beatified. On the chair beside him stood the muddy shoe.

“Well?” he asked. “Have you discovered who I am?”

I placed the open volume before him and indicated the passage.

He read.

His divinity survived for perhaps eight seconds.

Then he looked at me, at the book, and finally at his bare, rather hairy leg.

“Not Apollo?” he whispered.

“No.”

“Who, then?”

I confess that the pleasure with which I answered was neither scholarly nor humane.

“PAN,” I said. “GOAT-FOOT.”

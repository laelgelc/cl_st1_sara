## THE RECORD OF BEXAR SCRIP NO. 2692

Whenever you visit Austin, if your affairs leave you an idle hour between the Capitol and the river, turn aside from the newer thoroughfares and examine the old General Land Office. Do not be satisfied with a glance at its exterior, though the thick walls, narrow windows, and sombre masonry may well arrest you; go in, if permission can be obtained, and stand beneath the vaulted passages where the footsteps of clerks, claimants, speculators, surveyors, widows, heirs, and men whose names have long vanished from the tax rolls once echoed from morning until dusk.

Much has been done to improve the building. Improvement is a word with which every generation excuses its vandalism. Walls once dignified by stains have been scraped and whitened; doors of seasoned oak have yielded to lighter conveniences; desks at which the public domain was divided into leagues and labors have been carried away; and certain shadowed recesses, formerly serviceable to spiders, rats, and historical reflection, have been made offensively clean. Yet something of the elder structure remains. On a wet afternoon, when the halls are deserted and rain whispers against the panes, the building appears to draw inward upon itself, as if remembering the secrets entrusted to it.

Those secrets were numerous.

The history of land in Texas is not merely a history of soil, timber, water, boundary stones, and chains stretched over the prairie. It is a history of paper. Before a settler’s cabin could become his castle, before a widow might call the field beyond her door her own, before a merchant could mortgage a league or a railroad acquire a right of way, there must be a certificate, a transfer, a survey, a set of field notes, an entry upon a docket, and, finally, a patent bearing the seal of the State. Between the first promise and the last assurance lay many doors; and beside those doors waited the patient, the ignorant, the desperate, and the cunning.

There were honest agents, certainly. There were also land sharks.

The knavery, the double-dealing, the cross purposes, the forged assignments, the altered dates, the purchased affidavits, the false surveys, the perjury, the bribery, and the respectable theft practiced in those days would furnish matter for a library. Claims overlapped like shingles. Dead men sold land. Children signed transfers. Rivers changed their courses to accommodate field notes, and oak trees appeared upon treeless plains whenever a surveyor required a corner. The public domain, vast as it seemed, was consumed repeatedly on paper; and many an innocent family learned too late that occupation, cultivation, taxes, memory, and the graves of its dead weighed less before the law than a document hidden in a pigeonhole.

Among the agents who flourished during this period was Mr. Lucian Sharp.

He was a person of excellent standing. He paid his notes at maturity; he contributed to the church; he subscribed to public improvements; he spoke with feeling of the sacredness of contracts; and no man more quickly removed his hat when passing a lady in mourning. His clothes were dark, his linen exact, his handwriting elegant. He never raised his voice in a business office. Men said that he was sharp by name and sharp by profession, and intended the phrase as praise.

It must not be supposed that Mr. Sharp regarded himself as dishonest. Few men begin with so severe an opinion of their own conduct. He had merely learned to distinguish between morality and law, and afterward between law and procedure, and afterward between procedure and opportunity. The frontier separating honesty from dishonesty is not marked by a fence. It is crossed first in thought, then by a step so small that the traveler may turn and still see his former ground; but presently the road descends, the landmarks disappear, and when he looks back there is a wilderness behind him.

Sharp had crossed that frontier years before.

The tract which was to complete his fortune lay west of Austin along a clear branch of Shoal Creek. It contained rich bottomland, cedar slopes, a spring that did not fail in summer, and a ridge which advancing settlement would soon make valuable. Upon it lived Mrs. Mary Harris and her son Edward. Her husband, Captain Thomas Harris, had received a land certificate for military service, had occupied the property, and had died believing his title secure. The widow planted a garden, rented part of the lower pasture, and took in sewing. Edward, who was nineteen when our account begins, worked wherever work could be had and intended, when money permitted, to study law.

But the original certificate could not be found.

A copy of the field notes existed. The county records mentioned the survey. Several old neighbors remembered Captain Harris’s occupation. None of these sufficed. The file which should have contained the certificate was absent from its proper place, and the tract, for purposes convenient to Mr. Sharp, appeared vacant.

Sharp filed upon it.

He had made his application with caution. One affidavit established abandonment; another cast doubt upon the Harris survey; a third declared that the land had never legally passed from the public domain. The evidence was false in spirit, defective in fact, and adequate in form. The clerks saw papers in order. The Commissioner saw no adverse certificate. Sharp’s patent approached completion by that majestic process through which government advances slowly when justice waits and rapidly when error has acquired the correct endorsements.

The law was on his side.

One afternoon in November, while searching among old empresario papers for an instrument unconnected with the Harris tract, Sharp drew from a crowded compartment a thin packet coated with yellow dust. A careless clerk, perhaps twenty years earlier, had placed it behind the file of a neighboring survey. The string had broken. The outer sheet had folded upon itself. Across the upper corner, in faded ink, appeared the notation:

**Bexar Scrip No. 2692.  
Original Grantee: Thomas Harris.**

Sharp did not move.

The great file room stretched around him with its presses, shelves, drawers, bundles, and leaning maps. A clerk coughed in the corridor. Somewhere below, a door closed. Sharp read the certificate once; then he read the transfer attached to it; then the field notes, which enclosed, beyond dispute, the spring, bottom, ridge, cabin, and cultivated acres claimed in his application.

The certificate was genuine.

For perhaps half a minute the respectable Mr. Sharp considered carrying the packet to the proper desk. He imagined the clerk’s surprise, the cancellation of his pending patent, the inquiry into his affidavits, the laughter of rivals, the whispered word fraud, the widow restored, the son triumphant. He saw his own name passing from admiration to suspicion. Reputation, which had been built from innumerable observances, might be ruined by one piece of paper.

The certificate must be destroyed.

Yet destruction within the office was dangerous. Missing papers sometimes produced questions; ashes produced more. Sharp listened, looked toward the doorway, and slipped the packet beneath his coat. Unfortunately, the Harris papers had been tied within a larger jacket containing references, memoranda, and earlier correspondence. To remove the certificate alone would leave its outline and number behind. He therefore took the entire file.

He did not know that Edward Harris had seen him.

Edward had come to inquire once more about his father’s claim. He had been sent from one desk to another and finally told that the search could not be continued that day. While descending the passage, he saw Sharp in the file room. There was nothing remarkable in this, for agents entered there under the tolerance allowed to familiar men; but Sharp’s sudden stillness, his glance toward the corridor, and the angular swelling beneath his coat were remarkable. Edward stepped behind a partly open map cabinet and waited.

Sharp came out carrying nothing in his hands.

“Good afternoon, Mr. Sharp,” said Edward.

Sharp turned so sharply that the name seemed to strike him between the shoulders.

“Good afternoon, young sir.”

“You have been examining the western surveys?”

“I examine many surveys.”

“Perhaps you found my father’s file.”

“I did not.”

Edward smiled with a courtesy that had no warmth in it. “Then I shall not detain you.”

Sharp descended the stairs. Edward watched him go. On the dusty floor of the file room lay a short fragment of red tape, newly broken, and beside it the clean rectangular mark left by a packet removed from long-settled dust.

It was closing time.

The clerks gathered their coats and memorandum books. Iron shutters were fastened. Keys turned. The last visitor was urged toward the door. Edward descended with the others, spoke briefly to the porter, then doubled back through a passage used by the copying clerks and concealed himself behind rolled maps in a disused alcove. He believed Sharp would return. He could not have explained why, except that suspicion, once awakened by danger to those we love, possesses an intelligence quicker than reason.

The building emptied.

Evening entered by degrees. The pale squares of the windows faded; the corridors became channels of darkness; the rooms, stripped of human voices, recovered their ancient character. A clock below ticked with unnecessary loudness. Pigeons stirred beneath the roof. A rat passed behind the wainscot with the dry rustling of paper. Outside, a dog barked, paused, and barked again.

Edward waited.

At eight o’clock a key grated in the side door. Sharp had borrowed it on the pretense that an urgent abstract must be completed before morning. He entered with a shaded lantern, locked the door behind him, and stood listening. The old building returned his breath to him from the arches.

He climbed to the second floor.

Sharp’s intention was simple. In a small room adjoining the old survey vault stood a stove used during severe weather. He would burn the certificate, crush the ash, return the empty jacket to some remote shelf, and report, if necessary, that he had discovered nothing. But guilty plans, however carefully composed in daylight, undergo strange alterations in deserted places. Every sound became a witness. Every locked door seemed to conceal an officer. The maps hanging from rollers stirred in the draft like pale men turning to observe him.

He reached the room, set down the lantern, and drew out the file.

“Bexar Scrip No. 2692,” said a voice behind him.

Sharp wheeled around.

Edward Harris stood in the doorway.

For several seconds neither spoke. The lantern cast their shadows against the wall, enlarging them until the agent and the boy appeared as two distorted figures already struggling.

“You have no right here,” Sharp said.

“I might say the same of that file.”

“You have followed me and entered a public building unlawfully.”

“I remained to prevent a greater wrong.”

Sharp placed one hand upon the packet. “Take care, Harris. Suspicion is not proof, and slander is punishable.”

“I require no suspicion now. I saw the name upon the jacket.”

“You saw nothing.”

“I saw my father’s name. I saw the number. Tomorrow the Commissioner shall see both.”

Sharp’s face altered slightly; not enough to destroy its civility, but enough to show the thing beneath.

“You are young,” he said. “You do not understand these matters. The certificate may be invalid. There may be transfers of which your mother knows nothing. Your father’s occupation proves no title. Let the file remain with me tonight, and I will examine it fairly.”

“Fairly?”

“Yes.”

Edward looked at the coat, the locked door behind them, and the stove. “You are Sharp by nature as well as by name.”

The insult was mild when compared with the truth, but truth gives even mild words an edge.

Sharp took a step forward. “Give me your word that you will leave this building and speak to no one until I call upon you tomorrow.”

“I shall give no such word.”

“You will regret your obstinacy.”

“My mother has regretted your honesty long enough.”

Edward moved toward the file. Sharp seized it first. They stood on opposite sides of the table, each with a hand upon the stained jacket.

“Release it,” Sharp said.

“No.”

“I warn you.”

“And I warn you, sir. If you destroy that paper, you destroy a widow’s home.”

There are moments when a man’s whole moral history gathers itself into one decision. Sharp might yet have released the file. Exposure would have followed; wealth would have diminished; reputation would have suffered; but life, that patient creditor, would still have permitted payment. Instead he saw only the patent, the land, the inquiry, the disgrace. The lesser crimes stood behind him and drove him toward the greater.

He struck Edward across the mouth.

The boy staggered, recovered, and cried out. Sharp sprang upon him. Courtesy, churchgoing, punctual debts, polished linen, and all the outward ceremonies by which society measures virtue fell from him like a torn disguise. He became an animal, with blazing eyes and cruel-looking teeth. They overturned the table. The lantern rolled but did not go out. Edward gripped the file against his breast and shouted for help.

No help came.

Sharp drew the knife he used for cutting tape and scraping seals. Edward saw the blade and caught his wrist. They swayed against the shelves; bundles fell around them; an old map unrolled with a hollow rattle. Sharp drove the knife once, felt it strike paper, cloth, and flesh, drew it out, and struck again.

Edward made a low sound.

Then he sank.

The clock below continued ticking.

Sharp stood over him, the knife in his hand. Blood spread across Edward’s coat and darkened the file he still held clasped to his breast. One corner of the certificate protruded from the torn jacket. The blade had passed through it.

For the first time that night, Mr. Lucian Sharp understood what he had done.

He knelt, spoke the boy’s name, felt for a pulse, and recoiled. Then fear, practical and commanding, displaced horror. He cleaned the knife upon a fallen wrapper. He lifted the lantern. He examined the corridor. He tried to pull the file free, but Edward’s arms had stiffened around it in the last convulsion, and the packet was slick with blood. A noise from the street caused Sharp to extinguish the light.

Darkness closed upon them.

* * *

The next morning a clerk noticed that a table in the old survey room had been moved. He also noticed a dark smear upon the floor, but supposed it to be ink. Sharp arrived at his customary hour, complained of headache, and departed before noon.

Edward Harris did not return home.

Search was made along the roads, at the river crossings, in gambling rooms, stables, camps, and boardinghouses. Mrs. Harris came to the Land Office and asked whether her son had visited it. Several clerks remembered him. The porter believed he had left before closing. Mr. Sharp, questioned because Edward had spoken with him, declared that the boy had seemed agitated and perhaps intended to go east in search of employment.

This explanation satisfied no mother, but it satisfied the public.

A young man’s disappearance upon the frontier was not rare. He might be drowned, murdered by strangers, enlisted under another name, or lured toward distant gold. Rumor performed its charitable cruelties. Someone had seen Edward near San Antonio; another had met him traveling toward Galveston; a third reported that he had quarreled with his mother. Each false sighting prolonged her hope and sharpened its end.

There were, nevertheless, curious circumstances.

A copying clerk found Sharp’s memorandum book near the unused spiral stairway leading to the roof space. The stair had long been closed because its upper door warped in wet weather. Dust upon the steps had been disturbed. A faint trail crossed the boards above the file vault, where the roof descended so low that a man must crawl. When these things were mentioned, Sharp laughed and said he had gone up in search of an old survey map.

No one crawled farther into the darkness.

For three nights the body of Edward Harris lay beneath the roof, between a disused chimney and the outer wall. Pigeons cooed a few feet away. Below, clerks copied patents, compared field notes, sharpened pens, and complained of dust. The living machinery of title proceeded under the dead.

On the fourth night rain fell heavily. Sharp entered by the side door once more. He carried rope, canvas, and a small handcart. How he brought the body down the spiral stair; how he passed it through the rear court; how far he pushed the cart beside Shoal Creek; and in what lonely interval between rain and dawn he dug the grave, no witness afterward could say. The storm erased wheel marks. The rising water disturbed the bank. By morning the building had swallowed one crime and the earth another.

The file went with the corpse.

Sharp had intended to recover it, but when he unfolded the canvas beside the shallow grave, the blood had glued paper, cloth, and flesh together. The knife thrusts had pierced the jacket; the certificate was clotted and torn. Dawn threatened. A wagon sounded on the distant road. He covered everything.

Thus Bexar Scrip No. 2692 passed from archive to grave.

* * *

The Harris claim failed. Without the original certificate, the widow could establish neither the validity nor the priority of her title. Sharp received his patent. Legal notices were served; delays were granted; compassion was expressed; possession was taken. Mrs. Harris left the cabin where her husband had died and where her son had last eaten supper. She survived less than two years.

Sharp prospered.

The city advanced toward his land. Streets crossed the old pasture; stone houses rose upon the ridge; mills valued the water; merchants valued the roads. Sharp sold parcels, retained others, lent money upon mortgages, and became one of those substantial citizens whose early shrewdness is softened by later wealth into legend. He served on committees. He advised young men to preserve their credit. At public dinners he spoke of the pioneers, the sanctity of property, and the duty owed by one generation to the next.

Yet melancholy settled upon him.

He disliked closed rooms. He would not enter the Land Office alone. The cooing of pigeons disturbed him, and once, when a clerk dropped a bundle of files behind him, he collapsed against a desk. In rainy weather he rode toward Shoal Creek and returned after dark with mud upon his boots. His acquaintances attributed these peculiarities to overwork, dyspepsia, or the loneliness of an unmarried man.

Society is generous in explaining the sorrows of the rich.

Sharp died respected. His obituary praised his probity, enterprise, benevolence, and intimate knowledge of the land system. It remarked only that his later years had been clouded by an unaccountable gloom.

Seven years after his death, three men digging along Shoal Creek in search of buried Mexican silver struck a flat stone beneath the roots of a cedar. The stone covered no treasure vault. Below it lay rotted canvas, fragments of clothing, and a human skeleton doubled into a narrow grave.

The authorities were summoned.

In the remains of a coat they found brass buttons of an old pattern, a corroded knife mark upon one rib, and a packet of papers compressed into a black mass against the breastbone. The outer leaves had decayed; the inner sheets, protected by cloth and dried blood, still bore portions of writing. A blade had pierced the packet twice.

An elderly clerk from the Land Office separated the fragments with water and a camel-hair brush. He recognized the ruled form of an early certificate, part of a surveyor’s return, Captain Thomas Harris’s name, and the boundaries of the tract once claimed by his widow. Upon the torn file jacket, where blood had preserved what dampness might otherwise have erased, remained this broken notation:

**B—x— S—rip N— 2—92.**

The old clerk supplied the missing letters.

The skeleton was Edward Harris.

The file was the missing file.

And the grave, like the archive, had kept its record.

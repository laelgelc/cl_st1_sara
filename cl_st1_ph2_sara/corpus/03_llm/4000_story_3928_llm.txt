# The Sheriff’s Two Cartridges

The road from Cheyenne to Deadwood was not, in those days, a road in the sense that a citizen of Boston or Philadelphia would have understood the word. It was rather an agreement among teamsters that certain ruts went in the desired direction. At intervals the agreement became uncertain, divided into several opinions, wandered over a low ridge, descended into a creek, and resumed on the farther side with all the confidence of a statesman who has forgotten his previous remarks.

In dry weather the road was dust. In wet weather it was gumbo. In winter it was a white vacancy.

Billy Knapp drove it in all three conditions and held no preference among them, except that dust could be coughed up, mud could be cursed, and snow, when deep enough, had to be shoveled. Billy was a broad, leather-colored man with a mustache like a buffalo robe hung out to dry, and he had driven stages so long that he seemed to sit on the box even when standing on the ground.

On the morning with which we are concerned, he was taking the northbound coach across the last long rolls before Deadwood. Snow from an earlier storm lay in the hollows, the grass tops showing through it like whiskers on an old chin. Overhead, the sky had that hard gray look which the plains sometimes assumes before turning malicious.

Inside the coach rode four passengers, of whom only one matters. His name was Mr. Horace Pender, and he had come from Connecticut to inspect a mining concern in which he had invested an inheritance and a great deal of faith. He wore a wool suit suitable for a January funeral in Hartford, a high collar, polished boots, and spectacles that flashed whenever he asked a question.

He had asked Billy forty-three questions since breakfast.

The first five had concerned the distance to Deadwood. The next eleven concerned Indians, outlaws, road agents, buffalo, wolves, and the comparative danger of each. After that he had passed to revolvers.

“Do you carry a firearm, Mr. Knapp?” he called through the forward opening.

“Yep.”

“What kind?”

“Loaded.”

“I mean the manufacture.”

Billy spat over the wheel.

“Manufactured to shoot.”

This answer failed to satisfy Mr. Pender, but it postponed him. Beside Billy sat the express messenger, Jake Mulvern, with a short-barreled shotgun across his knees and a revolver under his coat. Jake guarded a small ironbound strongbox bolted inside the coach. It contained banknotes from Cheyenne, payroll money for two mining outfits, and certain papers which no robber would have valued after reading them.

Jake was a lean man, not much older than thirty, with steady eyes and a habit of rubbing his thumb along the shotgun’s lock. He had lost one partner to road agents near Hat Creek and did not intend to lose another.

There is a difference between that resolution and good judgment. The difference is often discovered too late.

The stage had just entered a shallow cut between two bare swells when a horseman came over the ridge ahead. He wore a buffalo coat turned hair-side out, a hat pulled low, and a black scarf across his face. His horse was lathered, though the morning was cold.

He did not wave his weapon or indulge in any of those flourishes beloved by dime novels. He simply leveled a Winchester.

“Time!” he called.

Billy pulled the leaders back so sharply that the wheel team nearly sat down. The coach stopped with a thump that pitched Mr. Pender against an elderly passenger and caused a valise to open underfoot.

Jake Mulvern raised his shotgun.

The robber fired.

Jake rolled sideways off the box. The shotgun discharged into the sky, where it did no harm whatever. Jake struck the frozen ground and lay still.

Billy looked down at him. Then he looked at the outlaw.

“You made that unnecessary,” he said.

“Likely,” said the masked man. “Throw down your gun.”

Billy took the revolver from beneath his seat and dropped it beside the wheel.

“Now climb off. Easy.”

Billy climbed off. He did it easily.

The robber moved his horse to the coach door and ordered the passengers out. Mr. Pender emerged first, because curiosity had placed him nearest the latch. He stepped down with both hands raised, his spectacles crooked and his dignity badly jarred.

“Sir,” he said, “are you aware that murder is a capital offense?”

“Been rumored,” said the robber.

The other passengers descended. The masked man had them stand in a row, then directed Billy to remove the strongbox. Billy found a cold chisel and hammer beneath the seat and knocked the box from its bolts.

“Why don’t you jump him?” Mr. Pender whispered while Billy worked. “There are five of us.”

“Because he’s got the rifle,” Billy whispered back.

“But numerical superiority—”

“Don’t stop bullets.”

The robber heard that and laughed.

When the box was down, he made Billy load it across the saddle behind him. Then he collected Jake’s revolver and ammunition belt but left the shotgun where it had fallen.

“Ain’t you going to rob the passengers?” Billy asked.

“No.”

“Polite of you.”

“I’m in a hurry.”

“So was Jake.”

For an instant the robber’s eyes hardened above the scarf. Then he turned his horse and rode south, passing around the ridge. Within half a minute he had vanished into the broad, folding country.

Mr. Pender was the first to move.

“He is escaping!”

“That’s generally the next part,” said Billy.

They lifted Jake into the coach. He had been struck high in the chest and was dead before he reached the ground. Billy covered him with a lap robe, recovered his revolver, and climbed back to the box. His face showed nothing, but he drove the remaining miles without answering questions.

Toward noon the wind began.

It came out of the northwest in long, level blows, combing snow from the slopes and carrying it across the road. The sky lowered. The distant ridges faded, then the nearer ones. By the time the stage rolled down into Deadwood, the horses were white to their eyes and the coach resembled a traveling snowbank.

The news reached the sheriff’s office before Billy did, because news in a frontier town commonly traveled faster than anything except credit. Men gathered at the stable, the hotel, the saloon, and finally outside the little false-fronted building where the newly elected sheriff kept two chairs, one desk, three wanted notices, and a stove with strong political convictions against drawing properly.

Sheriff Alfred Sweet came out while they were carrying Jake Mulvern inside.

The voters of Lawrence County had chosen Alfred partly because they knew him and partly because his opponent had been arrested two days before the election. At first sight he did not appear to be a formidable officer. He was short, narrow in the shoulder, pink of cheek, and bald over the entire top of his head. His remaining hair, pale and fine, made a fringe above his ears. Seen from behind at a respectful distance, he might have been mistaken for a small boy carrying an elderly gentleman’s scalp.

His eyes prevented such mistakes at close range.

“What happened?” he asked.

Billy told him.

“How long a start?”

“Four hours, near enough. He rode south.”

“Horse?”

“Bay. Fifteen hands. White stocking on left hind. Blowin’ hard when he stopped us.”

“Man?”

“Medium height. Maybe a mite over. Winchester, likely forty-four. Buffalo coat. Knew how to hold the gun.”

Alfred pulled on his gloves.

“What was in the box?”

The express agent, who had arrived red-faced and breathless, named the sum. It was enough to buy a good ranch, a poor mine, or several elections.

“Notes registered?” Alfred asked.

“Most of them. Numbers on file.”

“Then he can’t spend ’em here.”

“He might change them in Cheyenne.”

“He can’t go back the way he came. Every station south will have word by tomorrow.”

A dozen men had already volunteered for a posse, and another dozen were waiting to be asked so that they might refuse modestly and then accept. Alfred looked from them to the thickening snow.

“No posse,” he said.

There was a silence.

Billy scowled. “What’s that mean?”

“Means I’m goin’ alone.”

“It means you’re cracked.”

“A crowd’ll bury the trail. Besides, he’ll watch for a crowd.”

“You won’t find a trail in this.”

“Not near town.”

“Then where?”

Alfred turned to the map nailed on the office wall. It showed a great many rivers accurately and a great many roads hopefully.

“He’s got banknotes he can’t pass in Deadwood, Cheyenne, or any camp that gets telegraph service. He needs somebody who’ll trade clean money for dirty at a discount. Nearest likely place is Pierre. Sooner or later he has to work east.”

“Pierre’s a long way.”

“Longer without money.”

Billy considered this. “He might lie up.”

“He might. But his horse was spent, the storm’s comin’ west to east, and there ain’t much shelter on the flats. He’ll strike for one of the creek bottoms, change mounts if he can, and then bend east.”

One of the volunteers laughed.

“You figger all that from a bay horse blowin’?”

Alfred buttoned his coat.

“No. I figger some from the horse and some from the fact that he ain’t likely to sit in a snowdrift till spring.”

He selected a Winchester from the rack, examined the action, and filled his belt with cartridges. Then he packed coffee, bacon, matches, a blanket, and oats in his saddlebags.

“How many rounds?” Billy asked.

“Forty.”

“Take more.”

“Weight.”

“Cartridges don’t weigh much.”

“Everything weighs much after fifty miles.”

That was Alfred’s way. He did not reject advice because it came from another man, nor accept it for the same reason. He seemed to possess a small private set of scales behind his eyes. On one side he placed words; on the other, facts. Words did not often prevail.

He rode out at one o’clock on a rangy gray gelding named Deacon. Snow swept down the street behind him and shut the town like a curtain.

For the first ten miles Alfred did not hunt the robber’s trail at all. This puzzled those who later heard of it, but only because they imagined tracking to be a matter of putting one’s nose near a hoofprint and following faithfully. On the open plains a man who does that will eventually arrive where the horse went, provided wind, snow, rain, darkness, hard ground, branching trails, livestock, and his own death do not interfere.

Alfred rode southeast to intercept rather than follow.

The storm thickened. Snow moved not downward but sideways, hissing against his coat, collecting in Deacon’s mane, and finding every opening in his clothing with the skill of water. The land came and went in gray glimpses. A low slope rose before him, remained beneath the horse for some time, and descended into nothing. The next slope was precisely like it. In such country a man could travel all day and believe himself standing still while the world rolled under him.

Near dusk Alfred found what he had expected: the trail of a horse moving east through a sheltered draw.

It was nearly filled, but not entirely. Here and there the bay’s left hind hoof had punched deeper than the others. On one patch of frozen mud, blown clear by wind, the white-stockinged leg had left no white mark, naturally, but its shoe was chipped at the outer heel. Billy had not mentioned that. Billy had not needed to.

Alfred followed until dark, then stopped beneath a clay bank, fed Deacon, ate frozen bacon, and slept for two hours with the reins looped around his wrist. At midnight the storm broke. The clouds tore apart, and stars appeared in rents of black sky, hard and innumerable. Cold came down immediately. It tightened the saddle leather, sealed the snow crust, and made each breath hang white before horse and rider.

He went on by starlight.

The outlaw had found a ranch line shack sometime before dusk. He had forced the door, eaten, and changed horses. Alfred knew this because the bay stood in the lean-to, trembling and empty-eyed, while a dun cow pony was gone. The owner had also been absent, which was fortunate for both parties.

The dun’s tracks led east.

By dawn Alfred was six miles behind. By noon he was three. The outlaw’s new mount was sturdy but slow; Deacon was weary but long-legged. The two disadvantages canceled each other until Alfred began saving distance at the creek crossings.

A fleeing man, you see, tends to think of the country behind him. A pursuing man thinks of the country ahead. The outlaw crossed each bend of Willow Creek where he reached it. Alfred cut across the loops. Water always meanders when permitted, and so do men who do not know they are being measured.

Late that afternoon Alfred saw smoke.

It rose in a thin blue line beyond a shelf of cottonwoods. He dismounted half a mile away, led Deacon into a hollow, tied him, and went forward on foot. Snow muffled his steps. Along the creek the bank had crumbled into little walls and alcoves, with stiff yellow grass between. The smoke came from one of these alcoves.

The outlaw had built a fire scarcely larger than a hat. He lay beside it wrapped in his buffalo coat, his head on the strongbox. The dun pony grazed on cottonwood bark nearby. The Winchester rested against the bank, an arm’s reach away.

An arm’s reach is a short distance when a man is awake. When he is asleep, it may be the width of a county.

Alfred stepped into the alcove and took the rifle.

“Wake up,” he said.

The outlaw opened his eyes. He did not move.

“Morning?” he asked.

“Near sundown.”

“Then let me sleep till morning.”

“You can sleep in Deadwood.”

The man looked at the Winchester in Alfred’s hands, then at the revolver covering him.

“You alone?”

“Yes.”

“That’s discouragin’.”

“Roll over.”

The outlaw obeyed. Alfred searched him, removed Jake Mulvern’s revolver, and fastened his wrists with handcuffs. Then he opened the strongbox, which had been pried at one corner. The packets of notes were still inside.

“You hadn’t got it open,” Alfred observed.

“Hadn’t found the right argument.”

“Saved me countin’.”

The outlaw sat up. He had a narrow, sun-darkened face and gray eyes. A thin scar ran from one corner of his mouth toward the jaw, giving him the appearance of smiling at remarks he had not yet heard.

“What do they call you?” Alfred asked.

“Depends who’s callin’.”

“What’ll the judge call you?”

“Probably prisoner.”

“Name.”

“Cal Rusk.”

Alfred knew it. Rusk had robbed in Colorado, Wyoming, and Nebraska, but until that morning had not been known to kill. He had a reputation for planning carefully, riding hard, and surrendering only after other arrangements failed.

“You killed the messenger,” Alfred said.

Rusk looked into the fire.

“He lifted the scattergun.”

“You shot before he had it level.”

“You would’ve.”

“No.”

Rusk glanced up. “No?”

“I’d have let the box go.”

“That why they elected you sheriff?”

“That’s why I’m alive to serve.”

Rusk considered this and nodded once. It was not remorse, but it was recognition, and among such men recognition often had to do the work of more expensive virtues.

Alfred made coffee and fried bacon. He unlocked one cuff and allowed Rusk to eat with his right hand. Then he fastened both wrists again, tied a lead rope between the cuffs and his saddle horn, and mounted.

“You treat all murderers this handsome?” Rusk asked.

“Only the hungry ones.”

“You might regret feedin’ me.”

“I generally regret bacon.”

They rode west until darkness stopped them. During the night they heard wolves, the cracking of creek ice, and once the far, hollow drumming of hooves. Alfred sat up at that sound, but it faded toward the north.

At daylight they found the tracks.

Six ponies had passed along the ridge. Their riders were Sioux, moving quickly, and one of the pony trails—the dun’s—joined theirs from the east.

Alfred looked at Rusk.

“Where’d you get that horse?”

“Borrowed him.”

“From where?”

“Camp near the Moreau.”

“What kind of camp?”

Rusk rubbed the scar beside his mouth.

“Had lodges.”

“You stole a Sioux pony.”

“Mine was wore out.”

Alfred examined the tracks. The party had circled during the night, found the trail, and moved ahead of them. Their purpose needed no explaining. A good pony was property, livelihood, status, and sometimes the difference between eating and starving. Horse stealing on the plains was not treated as a clerical misunderstanding.

“How far?” Rusk asked.

“Close.”

“Can you tell that from tracks?”

“No. I can tell it because they’re over there.”

Six mounted figures stood on the ridge to the north, dark against the pale sky.

They remained still for a moment. Then one lifted an arm. The riders separated, three moving east and three west.

Alfred turned Deacon toward the creek.

Rusk ran beside the saddle, his handcuffs linked to the horn.

“You might consider untyin’ me,” he said.

“I’m considerin’.”

“Consider fast.”

The creek lay a quarter mile away. Its banks offered some cover, though not enough to satisfy a rabbit. Alfred drove Deacon down the slope. Behind them came a yell, then the quick gathering thunder of ponies.

Rusk stumbled.

Alfred cut the lead rope, leaned down, and seized the chain between the handcuffs. Rusk caught the stirrup with his free hand and ran in long, desperate leaps.

An arrow hissed past and planted itself in the snow.

Another struck Deacon’s saddlebag. The horse lunged. Rusk lost the stirrup, rolled, regained his feet, and kept running.

They reached the creek bank as a rifle cracked from the ridge. Snow jumped near Alfred’s boot. He dismounted behind a shoulder of clay no higher than a table and dragged Rusk down beside him.

“Now,” said Rusk, breathing hard, “have you concluded your thinkin’?”

Alfred unlocked the cuffs and handed him Jake Mulvern’s revolver.

Rusk stared at it.

“Loaded?”

“Six.”

“You’re trustin’.”

“I’m countin’.”

Rusk spun the cylinder. “Same thing, sometimes.”

“No.”

The first three riders came from the west, sweeping down the slope in a line. They bent low along their ponies’ necks, showing little but shoulders, heads, and rifle barrels. Hooves threw frozen clods behind them. Their coats streamed. They seemed less like separate horsemen than parts of one violent shape rolling over the earth.

“Hold till they’re close,” Alfred said.

“I know rifle work.”

“You’ve demonstrated it.”

Rusk’s face tightened, but he said nothing.

The charge came on. One rider fired too soon; the shot slapped dirt from the bank. Another rose in the stirrups, bow drawn. Alfred waited. At a hundred yards Deacon began to tremble. At eighty he tried to back away. Alfred hooked the reins beneath his knee and laid the Winchester across the bank.

At sixty yards he fired.

The lead pony stumbled, recovered, and swerved. Its rider slipped sideways, hanging for a moment by one leg, then fell clear. Rusk fired twice. The second rider’s hat flew away. The third loosed an arrow and wheeled so sharply that his pony sat on its haunches.

The arrow struck Deacon high in the neck.

The gray horse screamed, reared, and collapsed against the bank. Alfred barely pulled his leg free. Blood darkened the snow.

The remaining riders circled out of range.

Rusk looked at the horse. “That leaves one.”

“The dun.”

“The one they came for.”

“Yep.”

“Life does arrange things neat.”

Alfred put a final shot into Deacon. The horse went still.

He then did something Mr. Pender would have found shocking but any plainsman would have understood. He and Rusk dragged Deacon’s body lengthwise against the low bank, piled saddlebags and frozen earth beside it, and made a breastwork. A dead horse is poor company, but excellent cover. There are times when sentiment must stand behind ballistics.

The second charge came from the east.

This time four riders advanced while two remained back, firing from long range. The riders did not come directly at the creek. They crossed, turned, and raced along its edge, shooting downward as they passed.

Alfred and Rusk crouched behind Deacon. Bullets thudded into the horse, struck clay, whined away, and clipped dry stalks over their heads.

Rusk fired once.

A rider jerked backward but stayed mounted.

Alfred fired twice, carefully. One pony went down in a great rolling confusion of legs, snow, and blanket. Its rider sprang free and ran crouched toward the ridge while the others covered him.

Then there was quiet.

Not true quiet, of course. The wind moved through the grass. Deacon’s saddle creaked under his weight. Somewhere upstream, ice cracked with the solemn report of a pistol. But after gunfire, ordinary sounds seem to return one at a time and look around cautiously.

Rusk reloaded his revolver from Jake’s belt.

“How many rifle shells?” he asked.

Alfred checked.

“Twenty-seven.”

“You started with forty?”

“Some used huntin’, some persuadin’ you.”

“I was persuaded by the barrel.”

“The ammunition gave it authority.”

Rusk had eighteen cartridges for the revolver, including the six in the cylinder. Alfred gave him the robber’s Winchester, which he had tied across Deacon’s saddle, and divided the rifle ammunition. Both weapons took the same caliber, though the cartridges were not quite interchangeable with every arm as casually as fiction sometimes suggests. In this instance they were close enough, which was fortunate. Mechanics, like Providence, occasionally favors the desperate.

The Sioux withdrew to the ridges.

Alfred counted seven now. There had been six at first, so at least one more had joined them, perhaps from a larger camp. One man on foot tended the rider Rusk had hit. Another caught the loose pony. They did not waste movement, and they did not leave.

“They’ll wait till dark,” Rusk said.

“Maybe.”

“Then crawl in.”

“Maybe sooner.”

“Cheerful little cuss, ain’t you?”

“Been elected.”

Rusk glanced at Alfred’s uncovered head. During the scramble his hat had vanished, and the winter sun shone pinkly on his bald crown.

“You best keep that down,” Rusk said. “They can see it signalin’.”

Alfred put on Deacon’s saddle blanket like a hood.

“Better?”

“You look like a bald pup under a rug.”

“You look like a murderer.”

“That’s a poor sort of answer.”

“It’s accurate.”

Around noon two riders approached carrying a white cloth. Alfred and Rusk watched them come.

One was an older man in a dark wool coat, the other young and bareheaded despite the cold. They stopped beyond easy revolver range. The older man called in Lakota. Alfred understood only a few words, but the meaning was plain enough when the man pointed at the dun pony grazing farther downstream, then at Rusk.

“They want the horse thief,” Alfred said.

Rusk peered over the breastwork. “Tell ’em you can have the horse.”

“It’s their horse already.”

“Tell ’em they can have it more peacefully.”

Alfred knew some sign language and a little traders’ speech. He stood enough to be seen, pointed to the dun, spread his hands, then indicated that he and Rusk meant to travel away.

The older man shook his head. He pointed at Rusk again, then drew one finger across his throat—not theatrically, but as a statement of intended settlement.

“What’d he say?” Rusk asked.

“You ain’t included in the peaceful arrangement.”

“Tell him I object.”

“I expect he knows.”

Alfred pointed to the badge beneath his coat, then toward the west, trying to explain that Rusk was a prisoner under law. The older man laughed once. It was not a cruel laugh. It merely expressed an opinion of Deadwood’s jurisdiction along Willow Creek.

He spoke again, indicating Alfred could leave.

Rusk watched him. “They offerin’ to let you go?”

“Looks like.”

“Well?”

“I’m sheriff.”

“So I heard.”

The older man waited. Alfred shook his head.

The two riders turned away.

Rusk settled behind the horse.

“That was foolish,” he said.

“Likely.”

“You could’ve gone.”

“Without the money?”

“They’d probably let you take it.”

“Without you?”

Rusk’s gray eyes rested on him.

“No,” Alfred said.

The answer was neither affectionate nor heroic. It had the tone of a clerk declining to leave a ledger half balanced. Yet Rusk nodded as if something of consequence had passed between them.

The next attack came on foot.

At first Alfred saw nothing. The ridge was empty, the flats still, the grass scarcely moving. Then a tuft of yellow grass shifted against the wind. Farther left, a patch of snow darkened and vanished. A head rose no higher than a prairie dog’s, then went down.

“They’re crawlin’,” he said.

“How many?”

“All.”

This sort of advance is worse to endure than a charge. A mounted man crossing open ground gives the eye employment. He comes fast, enlarges visibly, and offers decisions in quick order. A crawling man can spend an hour crossing a hundred yards. You may watch the place where he was and never see where he is. Your mind supplies ten enemies for every one hidden, and each clump of grass develops hostile intentions.

Alfred fired at movement near a rock.

Nothing happened.

Rusk fired at a strip of blanket and received a shot in reply. The bullet passed through the buffalo coat at his shoulder without touching skin.

“That cloth cost money,” he complained.

“You stole the money.”

“Not for long, apparently.”

They waited.

The sun moved west. A wind came up the creek, sharp enough to make their eyes water. The Sioux used every wrinkle in the land: shallow drainage lines, snow hollows, weed beds, the shadow under a bank. Now and then a rifle muzzle showed and smoke puffed. Bullets struck the breastwork with thick, sickening impacts. Deacon’s body shuddered beneath them.

Alfred fired when he had a target. Rusk did likewise. Neither shot rapidly.

By midafternoon Alfred had twelve cartridges.

Rusk had nine.

At perhaps eighty yards, a warrior rose to one knee and fired. Alfred’s hat—retrieved and set low over his ears—jumped from his head.

Rusk shot before the man could duck. The warrior fell behind the grass.

“Got him,” Rusk said.

Alfred felt his scalp. A groove along the crown bled freely, as scalp wounds commonly do, even when the damage is mostly to vanity.

Rusk looked over and grinned.

“Parted your hair.”

“I ain’t got any.”

“Then he wasted the shot.”

Alfred tied a bandanna around his head. Blood ran behind one ear and froze there.

The crawlers came closer.

They no longer needed to expose themselves to shoot. The ground dipped within fifty yards of the creek, giving them a shallow trough. From there they kept up an irregular fire while two or three worked toward the flanks.

Alfred recognized the plan. They would pin the defenders in front, gain the creek above and below, then fire along the bank. The breastwork that protected them from the prairie offered no protection from either side.

“Watch upstream,” he told Rusk.

“You?”

“Down.”

Ten minutes passed.

A face appeared over the bank downstream. Alfred fired. The face vanished, and a body slid back with a rush of loose snow.

Upstream Rusk’s Winchester cracked twice.

“Two there,” he said. “Still there.”

“Hit?”

“Don’t know.”

“You generally know.”

“I know when it’s useful.”

Another rifle shot cut the edge of the bank. Clay sprayed Alfred’s eyes. He wiped them clear and fired at a moving elbow. His bullet snapped through grass, and a man rolled sideways, rose, and ran bent double for cover. Alfred fired again.

The man fell.

Seven cartridges remained in Alfred’s belt.

Rusk had five.

Toward sunset the firing stopped.

The sky turned pale green above the west, then yellow along the horizon. The cold deepened before the sun was gone. The dun pony, which had wandered into the creek brush, stood trembling with its reins caught on a willow.

Rusk looked toward it.

“Could make a run.”

“Not together.”

“I hadn’t proposed together.”

“I know.”

“Thought you might.”

“No.”

Rusk smiled with the scarred side of his mouth. “Sheriff, if we live, I ain’t goin’ peaceable.”

“I know that too.”

“You got a heap of confidence in knowin’.”

“It saves surprise.”

Darkness would help the Sioux more than it helped the two men. They knew the ground around the breastwork and could approach from every direction. Alfred and Rusk could not retreat without crossing exposed snow, nor remain without being surrounded.

Rusk opened the Winchester’s action.

“Three,” he said.

Alfred inspected his belt.

“Four.”

“That’s seven.”

“Arithmetic’s comfortin’.”

“We need more comfort.”

The first shot of the final attack came from upstream. Rusk answered. A second came from the front. Alfred saw the flash and fired toward it.

Then the grass rose.

Men seemed to appear everywhere—not in a wild rush, but in short, deliberate bounds from cover to cover. One ran ten yards and dropped. Another fired over him. A third slid into the creek. Their voices carried, sharp and urgent, across the snow.

Rusk fired.

“Two,” he said.

Alfred shot at a figure crossing left to right.

“Three.”

The Sioux moved nearer.

Rusk fired again.

“One.”

A bullet struck him high in the left arm, spun him around, and knocked the Winchester away. He caught it with his right hand, sat against Deacon, and laughed through clenched teeth.

“Didn’t improve my aim.”

Alfred fired at the man who had shot him. The figure dropped behind the bank.

“Two,” Alfred said.

Rusk levered his last cartridge into place. A warrior came up from the creek scarcely thirty yards away. Rusk fired. The man fell backward.

“Empty.”

Alfred shot downstream. Someone cried out.

“One,” he said.

Rusk drew the revolver, forgetting for an instant it had been emptied during the earlier fighting. He snapped it once and threw it aside.

The attack paused.

This often happens at the edge of success. Men who have advanced under fire know approximately how many shots have been fired, particularly when their lives depend upon the count. They listen to the different voices of the guns. They note pauses, reloadings, misfires. The defenders’ last cartridges make a peculiar announcement, though no word is spoken.

The Sioux called to one another.

Three figures rose in the grass. Another appeared upstream. A fifth crouched behind a dead pony. They had concluded that the two men were empty.

Rusk looked at Alfred.

“You got that one?”

Alfred nodded.

“Save it for yourself,” Rusk said. “They may take time with a sheriff.”

“What about you?”

“I’m wanted by everybody. Be impolite to favor one jurisdiction.”

Alfred laid the rifle across the horse. His face was gray with cold and loss of blood. Rusk’s wounded arm hung useless, and his buffalo coat was dark to the elbow.

The attackers began forward.

Then, from the western ridge, came a rifle shot.

It struck the snow between the advancing men.

A second shot followed, then a third, and suddenly the ridge was full of horsemen—twelve, fifteen, perhaps twenty—coming down at a hard gallop. Their coats flew, rifles flashed, and their horses threw snow in white sheets. At their head rode Billy Knapp on a black mule that appeared furious with the weather, the Sioux, Billy, and life in general.

The rescuers yelled as they descended.

The Sioux vanished into the grass, rose again near their ponies, and withdrew eastward. They fired twice at the newcomers, not in panic but to check pursuit. Billy’s men answered. One pony went down; its rider climbed behind another. Within minutes the party had reached the far ridge and dissolved into the gathering dusk.

Billy pulled up beside the breastwork.

He looked at Deacon, the bullet-cut bank, Alfred’s bloody head, and Rusk’s arm.

“You look poorly,” he said.

“Been busy,” Alfred replied.

“We followed after the storm. Found your line-shack sign.”

“Told you no posse.”

“You was outvoted.”

“I’m sheriff.”

“Not of weather.”

Men dismounted, brought blankets, bound Rusk’s arm, and gave Alfred coffee from a flask. Mr. Pender had not joined the rescue party, though he had offered detailed advice concerning its organization.

Rusk drank half a cup, then looked toward the dun pony.

Everyone else was occupied. Billy had gone to examine the fallen warriors. Two men were counting the recovered money. Another held Alfred upright while a fourth tied a fresh bandage around his scalp. The dun stood twenty yards away, reins still tangled in willow.

Rusk moved without warning.

He flung the coffee into the face of the man beside him, sprang across the creek, snatched the reins free, and vaulted into the saddle. His left arm was useless, but he rode well with one hand.

“Hey!” Billy shouted.

Rusk kicked the dun. The pony lunged up the bank and broke west.

Men scrambled for rifles, but they were in one another’s way. Rusk was already forty yards off, bent low over the pony’s neck.

Alfred pushed aside the man bandaging him.

He raised his Winchester.

There was a click of the lever, then a shot.

The dun pony folded in full stride. Its forelegs went under, its hindquarters rose, and Rusk sailed over its head into a drift. He disappeared entirely, then emerged sitting upright with snow in his hair and an expression of grave betrayal.

Billy turned toward Alfred.

“I thought you was empty.”

“So did he,” Alfred said.

Rusk staggered to his feet.

Alfred levered the rifle again and aimed at his chest.

The outlaw stopped.

Billy stared at the weapon. “You said one before they charged.”

“I did.”

“And you had two?”

Alfred nodded.

Rusk looked from the dead pony to the sheriff. His gray face slowly rearranged itself into that crooked smile.

“You let me think we was both empty.”

“Yep.”

“You let them think it.”

“Yep.”

“Those two shots might’ve helped when they come in.”

“Might’ve.”

Rusk’s smile vanished.

“You saved ’em for me?”

Alfred lowered the rifle only after Billy had placed the handcuffs on Rusk again.

“I was elected to bring you back,” he said. “Fightin’ Sioux was extra.”

### THE REPAIR OF CHRISTMAS

I had been commanded to write a Christmas story.

That is a simple sentence, and it has the cheerful sound of an invitation to dinner; but those who have written Christmas stories know better. A Christmas story must contain snow, though the weather be wet; a fire, though coal be scarce; a child, preferably golden-haired; a miser who repents before the pudding; and some manifestation of Providence sufficiently surprising to be interesting but not so surprising as to alarm the family reader.

I had all these things except the story.

The fire had sunk to one red eye. Rain rattled upon the panes, and the wind came round the house like a creditor who knows you are at home. On the hearth-rug lay my little boy’s wooden horse. It had lost one wheel, its mane was scorched, and a crack ran through its painted body. He had brought it to me after supper.

“Can you mend him by Christmas morning?”

“Certainly,” I had said, with the magnificent confidence of a father who has not examined the damage.

Then he had gone to bed, leaving the horse with me; and I, leaving the horse untouched, sat down to mend Christmas.

I wrote at the top of a sheet:

“On Christmas Eve the snow lay white upon—”

Then I remembered that snow lay grey upon roads in wartime, and was speedily trampled into mud by boots, wheels, ambulances, and guns. I tore up the sheet.

I began again:

“Old Nathaniel Grudge had never loved a child—”

But I reflected that the world already contained too many men who had never loved a child, and that few of them could be cured by a pudding. I tore up that sheet also.

At last I threw down my pen.

“There is no Christmas,” I said aloud. “Commerce strangled it, greed sold the body, and war buried the remains.”

“Tut, tut,” said somebody behind me.

I turned.

An old gentleman sat in the high chair by the clock.

You will tell me, no doubt, that you once saw an old gentleman appear mysteriously in your aunt’s conservatory at Leeds, and that afterwards three spoons were missing. Pray reserve the anecdote. This was not your gentleman. Mine was very old indeed. His beard flowed over his breast like a white stream, and his robe seemed woven of smoke, moonlight, dust, and cobwebs. In one hand he held an hourglass. The sand in it did not fall steadily, but hurried, paused, climbed a little, and fell again, as though every grain had private business.

“You entered without knocking,” I said.

“So did you,” he answered.

“I beg your pardon?”

“Into the world.”

“That was not my fault.”

“It rarely is.”

He leaned toward the fire and warmed his transparent fingers above it.

“You’re Father Time, I suppose.”

“People call me so when they wish to blame me for their faces.”

I glanced at the clock. Its pendulum moved, yet made no sound. Instead I heard a faint whispering from the old man’s beard—summer leaves, bells, hoofbeats, crying babies, marching feet, wedding music, the shutting of doors.

“You said there is no Christmas,” he continued.

“I said it has been destroyed.”

“Dear me! And you are sure?”

“As sure as a man can be of anything after reading the newspapers.”

“A poor source of eternity.”

“It is gone,” I insisted. “It was eaten by shopkeepers long before the war. They wrapped it in gilt paper, priced it, advertised it, and sold it by the yard. Then came hatred, cannon, wire, submarines, bombs. What room is left for peace and goodwill?”

“Not much room,” he admitted. “But both have lived in smaller quarters.”

“I mean the true Christmas.”

“Ah, the true Christmas! Describe it.”

I did so. There was a country house with holly in every room, servants bearing steaming dishes, snow outside, a great log upon the hearth, children singing, poor people grateful for blankets, and all quarrels suspended until Boxing Day.

Father Time smiled.

“That is a very young Christmas.”

“It is the traditional one.”

“Traditional! My dear fellow, I remember when your traditional Christmas was a dangerous novelty. Before that there were midwinter fires on hills, and before those—well, never mind. A tribe beside an ice-bound river once shared its last haunch of venison with strangers. That was Christmas, though they had no word for it. An Egyptian clerk wrote a seasonal tale in hieroglyphics and complained that the editor altered the ending. A Babylonian humorist baked jokes upon clay tablets.”

“Were they good jokes?”

“No. Humour improves more slowly than weapons.”

He peered at my blank paper.

“And now you declare the festival dead because you cannot begin a story.”

“I declare it dead because the world has murdered it.”

“No,” he said softly. “The world has wounded it.”

The wind struck the window. Somewhere in the street a motor backfired, and I started; the sound had been too much like a rifle.

Father Time looked toward the door.

“He is outside.”

“Who?”

“The witness for the defence.”

There came a timid knock.

It was not the knock of a supernatural person. It was the knock of a beggar who fears refusal, of a traveller uncertain of his welcome, of one who has knocked at many doors and seen curtains drawn quickly across warm windows.

“Open,” said Father Time.

I opened.

A figure stood upon the step beneath the rain.

At first I took him for some ruined actor escaped from a provincial pantomime. He wore a red coat, but it had faded to the colour of dried blood and hung in strips at the hem. The fur was grey with mud. One boot was bound with wire, the other with a bandage. His cap drooped over his eyes, and from beneath it streamed wet white hair. A sack sagged across his shoulders, patched with canvas, hospital cloth, and what looked horribly like the torn sleeve of a child’s coat.

He lifted his face.

I knew him then, though the cheeks were hollow, the lips blue, and the eyes—those famous merry eyes—darted from hedge to window, from window to rooftop, watching for dangers no one else could see.

“May I come in, sir?” he asked. “Only for a moment. I’ll leave if there’s trouble.”

“Come in, Father Christmas.”

He flinched at the name.

“Hush! Not so loud.”

The old man by the clock rose.

“Welcome, Nicholas.”

Father Christmas stared at him, and some of the fear left his face.

“Time! Is it truly you?”

“It usually is.”

They embraced. Father Christmas trembled so violently that drops flew from his beard.

“He is frozen,” said Time. “Do something.”

This was unfair. One expects apparitions to manage their own temperatures. Still, I stirred the coals, added the last decent lump from the scuttle, and drew the high chair close. Father Christmas sat with an apologetic heaviness. Water gathered beneath him.

“My wife will be delighted,” I murmured.

“She has forgiven worse,” said Father Time. “I remember the blue vase.”

I ignored this indecent knowledge of domestic history and fetched a towel.

When I tried to take Father Christmas’s sack, he clutched it.

“No! Don’t touch!”

“I only meant to put it down.”

“Wires,” he whispered. “They fasten things to wires. Toys, tins, bodies—anything. You lift them, and then—”

His hands opened suddenly.

“Boom,” said the wind in the chimney.

He slid from the chair and crouched behind it.

We waited. The clock resumed its ticking, very gently.

“It’s only the wind,” I said.

“Only the wind,” he repeated, but remained hidden.

Father Time bent beside him. “There are no mines here.”

“Are you certain?”

“As certain as I may be in a writer’s study.”

After a while we coaxed him back into the chair. I wrapped the towel round his shoulders. His clothing smelt of wet earth, smoke, hospitals, horses, and long roads.

“What he requires,” said Time, “is a drink.”

“I have no drink.”

Father Time raised his eyebrows.

“None.”

He looked toward the bottom cupboard.

“That is medicine.”

“Of course. It has been medicine since the first monk put berries into spirits and assured the abbot he had a cough.”

I produced the bottle.

Father Christmas watched the glass being filled. His nostrils stirred. He took one suspicious sip, then a larger one.

“Schnapps?” he asked.

“Brandy.”

“Ah! Brandy!” He swallowed and shut his eyes. “There was a widow near Arras who gave me brandy. Her roof was gone, her two sons were gone, half her village was gone—and still she gave me the bottle she had hidden for winter. ‘You are colder than I am,’ she said.”

“Did it warm you?”

“For an hour.”

He held out the glass.

I filled it again. Warmth began to creep into his face. The fire crackled bravely, performing beyond its means. Father Christmas extended his boots toward it and gave a long sigh.

“That’s better. Oh, that’s better! A hearth! A chair! A roof that stays where a roof ought to stay!”

His gaze travelled about the room: books, pipe-rack, curtains, toy cupboard, family photographs. At the sight of the toys he smiled—faintly at first, as if testing an expression long unused.

Then he saw the broken horse.

“What’s this?”

“My son’s.”

“And you let it lie in that condition?”

“I was occupied with the death of Christmas.”

“Pooh!”

It was not a powerful “Pooh!” but it contained a trace of the old authority. He set down his glass, took up the horse, and turned it expertly in his hands.

“Cracked axle. Loose flank. Mane singed by a candle, I’ll wager.”

“A nursery campaign.”

“There are always campaigns in nurseries, but the casualties should be mended before bedtime.”

From an inner pocket he drew a little roll of tools: awl, pincers, screwdriver, brush, glue-pot, tiny hammer. They were tarnished but immaculate. His fingers, which had shaken at the sound of the wind, became steady over the toy.

“More light, pray.”

I brought the lamp.

“Not in my eyes, man! Here—hold the wheel. No, the other way. Have writers no practical intelligence?”

“Very little. It interferes with criticism.”

He snorted. Father Time chuckled into his beard.

For ten minutes there was peace in the room. The rain softened. The old clock ticked. Father Christmas worked, humming a tune that appeared to know every chimney in Europe. He straightened the axle, fitted the wheel, pressed the split wood together, and bound it with a strip from his own ragged cuff.

“Children,” he said, “are hard on horses. They make them jump over books, charge table legs, and pull loads no sensible animal would attempt. Excellent masters, children.”

“Have you many deliveries this year?” I asked.

The hammer stopped.

His smile vanished.

“Deliveries?”

I regretted the question, but it was too late. He looked at his sack.

“Some.”

He opened it slowly.

There were toys inside, though few were whole. A doll without a face; a trumpet crushed flat; a box of bricks gritted with plaster; a woollen dog scorched along one side. There were also books—dozens of them, swollen by rain, torn by fragments, stained with mud and darker things. Names had been written upon the flyleaves in many hands.

“To Emil, from Mother.”

“For Jeanne, with Papa’s love.”

“To my brave little Stefan.”

“For Lucy, Christmas, 19—”

The year had been burned away.

“I carried them,” said Father Christmas. “Across roads, over fields, through towns with no windows. I came too late. Always too late.”

He lifted the faceless doll.

“This was for a girl in a cellar. She had made a bed for it from a cigar box. The shell came before I did.”

He laid it down and picked up a red picture book.

“A father sent this from the front. He was dead before the parcel left the depot. The boy died in the influenza ward. The book went on travelling. It knew neither of them was waiting.”

“Enough, Nicholas,” Father Time murmured.

“No, he must hear. He says Christmas is dead.”

Father Christmas rose. His new warmth had become fever. He dragged objects from the sack and placed them upon my table like exhibits before a court.

“A skipping rope for a child who lost her legs. A tin engine for two brothers under a fallen wall. A paint-box for a hospital where the windows burst inward. Here—a little wooden flute. The boy played it while the guns were quiet. When the guns began, he played louder. Then the roof—”

His voice failed.

From beyond the house came the grinding of a lorry changing gear. To him it was evidently something else. He dropped flat, covering his head.

“Machine guns!”

“No,” I said. “No, you’re safe.”

“Down! Keep the children down!”

I knelt and touched his shoulder. He fought me blindly, then seized my coat.

“Why do they put children near the guns? Why do they put guns near the children? I took them oranges once. Oranges! Can you remember such folly? Nuts, drums, sugar mice! Now I carry bandages. I fill stockings with bread. I bring candles to cellars so mothers can count who is left.”

His head turned toward the window.

“Do you hear them?”

I heard only the wind, but the wind had found a thin place in the world. Through it came, or seemed to come, voices: a child calling from a station platform, another crying beneath stone, another asking whether Father Christmas could find an address after the street had ceased to exist.

He tore off his cap.

“Give them back!” he cried. “Do you hear me, Time? Give me back my children! The laughing ones, the greedy ones, the solemn ones who write in their best hand! Give me back the child who asks for a kingdom and is satisfied with a whistle. Give me back their beds and their bowls and their mothers’ songs. I cannot go on knocking at empty houses!”

He swayed.

Father Time caught him before he struck the floor. Together we laid him upon the hearth-rug. The broken toys lay around him, a pathetic court of wounded subjects. His face seemed suddenly ancient—not old in Time’s serene fashion, but worn out, used up, ravaged by every winter of the war.

I looked at Father Time.

“Well?” I demanded. “Is this your defence? You have proved my case.”

“No.”

“You brought me Christmas himself, and he is dying.”

“He is suffering.”

“What distinction can matter to him?”

“All distinctions matter to me.”

He covered Father Christmas with my coat and sat beside him.

“You spoke of commerce,” he said. “Yes, greed has often hired musicians and called the noise a carol. You spoke of hypocrisy. Yes, men have given pennies in December and taken pounds in January. You spoke of war. Against that charge I have no jest.”

The room grew paler. Dawn had begun its slow assault upon the curtains.

“But Christmas is not a season supplied by shops,” Time continued. “It is not snow, nor plenty, nor the comfortable remembrance of comforts. It is a compact renewed in darkness—that the helpless shall be sheltered, the hungry invited in, the lonely addressed by name.”

“A compact we have broken.”

“Then renew it.”

“With whom? The dead?”

“With the living first.”

I glanced at Father Christmas. His lips moved. Perhaps he dreamed of sleigh bells; perhaps of guns.

“What can one man do against all that?” I asked.

Father Time’s eyes sharpened.

“That question has excused more wickedness than any lie I know. You cannot silence every gun. You cannot rebuild every school tonight. You cannot restore one child whose little book lies in that sack. Therefore, you conclude, you may do nothing. A comfortable conclusion—and wholly false.”

“What would you have me do?”

“Stand between the children and hatred where you can. Keep from them, for one hour more, the poison that adults drink so eagerly. Tell them courage without teaching cruelty. Let them know sacrifice, but not delight in suffering. Give them games while there is still room to play, songs while there is breath to sing, stories in which mercy is not laughed out of court.”

He pointed to the sack.

“Those gifts failed to arrive. Send others. Food. Books. Kindness. Send letters to children you do not know. Welcome the frightened. Share your fire. And when a small thing is broken, mend it.”

“That will not end the war.”

“No. But it may preserve the world for which the war is said to be fought.”

The first bird called outside—a thin, doubtful note.

“And the dead?” I whispered. “What of them?”

Father Time bowed his head.

“I do not spend lives,” he said. “Men do that, then charge the account to me. Yet I keep what they purchased dearly: lessons, warnings, courage, love. Much is squandered. Much is forgotten. But not all. There may come a day when children now hidden in cellars build houses with broad windows; when men who have heard the guns refuse to point them again; when borders become roads instead of wounds. I promise no easy paradise. I promise only that sacrifice need not be barren if the living make it bear fruit.”

The clock struck six.

At the first stroke, Father Christmas opened his eyes. At the second, he sat up. At the third, he reached for the wooden horse and tightened the binding round its body. At the fourth, he placed it carefully in my hands.

“It will hold,” he said, “if treated kindly.”

At the fifth, the room filled with a grey brightness in which both figures seemed made of breath upon glass.

At the sixth, they were gone.

The chair stood empty. My coat lay folded on the hearth-rug. The bottle was lighter than it had been, which proves nothing supernatural, as my wife later observed. Upon the table remained the damp mark of a sack, but that, too, faded as the fire died.

Only the horse was indisputable.

One wheel turned smoothly. The crack had been drawn together, though the narrow scar remained. Around its body was tied a strip of faded red cloth.

You may say I had fallen asleep at my desk. Very well. I have no wish to quarrel with you before breakfast. A dream may enter without knocking, drink a man’s brandy, rebuke his politics, and leave him with work to do. Reality has often accomplished less.

I sat until the house began to stir. Pipes complained in the walls. A door opened overhead. Small feet crossed the landing, paused, and then came pattering down the stairs.

Before my son reached the study, I took glue, paint, and a little brush from the cupboard. Father Christmas had repaired the axle and bound the crack, but the horse still lacked part of its mane, and there were ugly burns along its flank.

The world also was scorched, split, and short of wheels. I could not mend the world before breakfast.

But I could begin.

So when my son opened the door and asked, “Is he ready?” I set the horse beneath the lamp, drew up my chair, and said:

“Not quite. Come and help me.”

For three evenings the upper window remained lighted after the other houses had gone dark, and each time I passed I looked for the square of yellow above the shop-sign. Father Flynn had suffered another stroke, the third, and my aunt said there could be no rallying from it. Yet the lamp continued to burn behind the blind as if somebody sat there keeping watch upon a lesson not yet finished.

On Thursday morning the blind was drawn down.

I knew before anyone told me. The milk-boy had left his cart at the corner, and two women stood beneath the sign whispering, their shawls pulled close though the morning was mild. One crossed herself. I went on without stopping, counting the cracks between the paving stones, but the word paralysis sounded with every step. Father Flynn had taught it to me. He had made me divide it slowly into syllables, tapping the table with one thick finger.

“A deprivation of motion,” he had said. “The will commanding, and the body refusing.”

At breakfast Mr. Coyle sat by the fire with his boots stretched toward the grate. He was an old friend of my uncle’s and came often enough to be treated as one of the furniture. His pipe had gone out, though he continued drawing at it, making his cheeks hollow.

“So the poor gentleman’s gone at last,” he said.

My aunt set down the pot of stirabout.

“God rest him.”

“Aye,” said Mr. Coyle. “It’s a release, no doubt.”

He looked across at me, but I bent nearer my bowl. The skin on the stirabout had wrinkled and broken where the milk touched it.

“He took a great interest in that lad,” my uncle said.

“Too great, maybe,” Mr. Coyle answered.

There was a short silence. My spoon struck the side of the bowl, and I thought the sound indecently loud.

“What d’you mean by that?” my aunt asked.

Mr. Coyle pressed tobacco into his pipe with the blackened tip of his finger.

“Nothing against the man. Nothing of the kind. But an old priest in that condition, and a young impressionable boy—it isn’t altogether healthy. A child wants companions of his own age. Exercise. Fresh air. Not all that business about sins and vestments and the dead.”

“He instructed him,” said my aunt. “And never asked a penny.”

“I’m not denying it. A learned man, by all accounts. Only there was something peculiar there toward the finish.”

He puffed several times before remembering the pipe was unlit. I waited for him to explain, though I hated him for making me wait. He had a broad, damp nose and yellow hairs upon his ears. In my mind I called him a stale old crow, a meddler, a rag-picker. I would not look up, for fear he might see that I was listening.

“Peculiar how?” my uncle asked.

Mr. Coyle glanced at me again.

“Ah, well. These matters aren’t for the breakfast table.”

I filled my mouth until I could hardly breathe. It seemed to me that if I swallowed I would have to speak, and if I spoke my anger would come out thin and childish. Mr. Coyle began talking of the price of coal, but the word peculiar remained in the room after the other words had faded. It hung above the table like smoke.

When I went into the street, the sky was white and low. A card had been fixed to the door beneath Father Flynn’s window. The printing was bordered in black, and I read it twice:

THE REVEREND JAMES FLYNN  
LATE OF THE CHURCH OF ST. CATHERINE  
AGED SIXTY-FIVE YEARS  
R.I.P.

The age surprised me. He had always seemed much older, ancient almost, because of the deep furrows beside his mouth and the grey folds under his eyes. The card made him finite. Sixty-five years could be counted. I counted backward, trying to imagine him as a boy, but could picture only the heavy face grown smaller upon a child’s body.

All that day I expected grief to arrive. I waited for it at school and again when I passed the church railings. Instead there came a lightness which made me ashamed. There would be no more evening summons to the back room, no more reciting the responses while he corrected me, no more watching his hand tremble above the breviary. The hours ahead appeared suddenly empty and mine.

Yet whenever I admitted this freedom to myself, his face rose before me.

During the last months his left eyelid had drooped and his mouth remained partly open. Sometimes, in the middle of instruction, a thread of moisture gathered at the corner of his lip. He wiped it with a red handkerchief and continued speaking as though nothing had happened. His breath smelt of snuff and old wine. When he said the names of the sacred vessels—ciborium, monstrance, pyx—his voice became low and careful, and the words seemed to belong not to speech but to some cabinet of dark, shining instruments.

He taught me the divisions of sin, the conditions necessary for a valid confession, the colours proper to each season. White for joy and glory, red for blood and fire, violet for penance, black for the offices of the dead. I liked the exactness of these distinctions. They suggested that every act, even the secret ones, had been measured and placed in its proper drawer.

But at times he would cease speaking and stare toward the fireplace. I would wait with the catechism open upon my knees. The room would grow so quiet that I heard the soft workings of his mouth.

“Father?” I would say.

Then he would start, look at me suspiciously, and ask what question we had reached.

That night I dreamed I was in a country where all the houses were chapels. A priest sat behind a pierced screen, but when I knelt I could remember no sin. The priest whispered my name. I saw one side of his face through the lattice, immense and grey, and his lips moved in a smile which begged me to understand something. Beyond him burned a red lamp. I felt that he wanted to confess to me, though I had no authority to hear him. When I woke, the room was dark and my hands were clasped upon my chest.

On Friday evening my aunt said we must pay our respects. She had wrapped herself in her good black shawl and carried a small bunch of white flowers folded in damp paper. I asked whether my uncle was coming.

“He’ll call tomorrow,” she said. “Men don’t think of these things in time.”

We walked after dusk. The rain had stopped, leaving the pavements black and shining. Warm light spilled from the public houses, and through an open door I heard laughter and the clatter of glasses. It appeared strange that people could be drinking within a few yards of the dead. At the corner a fishmonger was washing his slabs, sending pinkish water along the gutter.

The shop below Father Flynn’s rooms was shut. Its old lettering—TEA, WINE AND PROVISIONS—showed faintly beneath the dust. My aunt pulled the bell. After a delay we heard slow steps descending, then bolts withdrawn one by one.

Nannie opened the door. She was smaller than I remembered and wore a black dress that seemed too heavy for her shoulders. She took my aunt’s hands between hers.

“Ah, you came,” she whispered. “That’s good of you. And the young gentleman too.”

Her eyes were red at the rims, but her face had the blank, polished look of someone who had repeated the same greetings all day.

We followed her upstairs. The passage smelt of flowers, candle grease and shut rooms. At the landing a door stood open. Two candles burned beside the bed, their flames pale in the brown light.

My aunt touched my shoulder and guided me in.

Father Flynn lay in a polished coffin raised upon supports. He wore his vestments, though they fitted stiffly across his chest. His hands had been folded around a chalice. The metal looked cold and useless between his swollen fingers. I had seen him hold a chalice at Mass, lifting it with such deliberation that the whole congregation seemed to rise with it. Now it lay sideways a little, as if it might slip but would never be allowed to fall.

His face was larger in death. The cheeks had settled heavily, and the nostrils were black above the white moustache. They had closed his mouth, giving him an expression of stern patience. Powder dulled the marks upon his skin, yet a faint discolouration showed near the temple. I thought he looked imposing, but not peaceful. He seemed to be enduring the prayers around him rather than receiving them.

Nannie knelt, and my aunt knelt beside her. I took the remaining place. The boards pressed sharply through my trousers. We said the prayers in whispers. I knew all the responses, yet once I answered too soon and heard my own voice alone.

While my aunt prayed, I examined the chalice. It was not the one from St. Catherine’s. This was plain, almost light-looking, perhaps borrowed for the purpose. The thought disturbed me. A priest should not be buried holding an imitation, though I could not have said why.

I lowered my eyes, afraid he might open his.

When the prayers ended, Nannie led us into the room across the passage. Eliza was seated at a round table on which stood a bottle of sherry, several small glasses and a plate of pale crackers. She rose with effort and embraced my aunt. She was broad and high-coloured, with wisps of grey hair escaping beneath her cap.

“Well, he’s gone,” she said. “Poor James. We done all we could.”

My aunt murmured that he was in a better place.

“That’s what we must believe. He had a beautiful death, very quiet. Not a struggle out of him. Only for Father O’Rourke coming when he did, I don’t know what we’d have done. He arranged everything. The notices and the undertaker and the clothes. Poor Nannie was no use at all.”

Nannie smiled faintly as though this were a fault they had long agreed upon.

Eliza poured sherry. My aunt accepted half a glass. I was given a little in the bottom of mine, though I had not been asked whether I wanted it. The smell was sharp and dusty. Beside my hand the crackers lay in a fan. I feared that if I took one, its breaking would be heard in the room with the coffin, so I declined when Nannie offered the plate.

“Take one, child,” my aunt said.

“I’m not hungry.”

“He’s shy,” said Eliza. “James was very fond of him.”

They all looked at me. I took a cracker and held it without biting.

“He had hopes for you,” Eliza continued. “He said you had a serious turn of mind.”

I felt my face grow warm. Mr. Coyle’s words returned to me: not altogether healthy. I wondered whether the sisters knew what he had meant.

My aunt asked about the illness.

“It was the third stroke carried him,” Eliza said. “The doctor told us from the start. The first left the hand trembling, the second took the leg, and after this last one there was nothing to be done. Paralysis, complete. He knew us, though. I’m certain he knew us.”

“He couldn’t speak?” my aunt asked.

“No, not to say speak. There were sounds. Once he made a sign toward the window, but what it meant—well, God knows. He was resigned.”

Nannie repeated, “Resigned, he was.”

They sipped their sherry. The grate contained no fire, only a little grey heap with a red paper flower stuck in it for ornament. We all looked toward it.

“He had a hard life,” my aunt said.

“Ah, the duties were too much for him,” Eliza replied. “That was the truth of it. People imagine a priest has nothing to do but say Mass and take his dinner. They don’t know the responsibility. The sacraments, the sick calls at all hours, the confessions. Always somebody’s soul depending on you.”

“He was very exact,” said my aunt.

“Too exact, maybe. He’d worry over a thing another man would let pass.”

Eliza turned her glass by the stem. The sherry left yellow lines upon the sides.

“It began with the chalice,” she said.

Nannie made a slight movement, but Eliza went on.

“What chalice?” my aunt asked.

“One belonging to the church. A fine old one. Silver, with work around the foot. He was putting it away after Mass and it slipped.”

“Was it destroyed?”

“Not destroyed. The bowl was cracked near the stem. They said it could be repaired. And there was no disrespect intended, none whatever. It was empty too. That’s important. Nothing consecrated in it.”

“An accident, then.”

“Of course it was an accident. But James took it to heart. He said his hands had betrayed him.”

I remembered the definition: the will commanding, the body refusing.

“After that,” Eliza said, “he wasn’t the same. He kept to himself. He’d sit in that chair there for hours, though the room might be cold, turning over the pages of his office and never reading a line. If Nannie spoke, he’d look up as if she’d called him from a great distance.”

Nannie folded her hands tightly.

“And he got notions,” Eliza added.

“What sort of notions?” my aunt asked.

Eliza glanced toward the open door.

“Religious notions. Scruples, Father O’Rourke called them. James would question whether he’d said the words correctly, whether his intention was right, whether a confession had been properly made. He’d go back over ceremonies that were finished weeks before.”

“A tender conscience,” said my aunt.

“Yes. But there’s tender and there’s troubled.”

The word troubled was spoken almost soundlessly. Rain began again, faintly ticking against the window. Somewhere below, a pipe knocked in the wall.

Eliza filled her glass, though it was not empty.

“The night they found him,” she said, “we thought he was in bed. Nannie brought up his tea and the room was empty. His coat was gone, but not his hat. We waited. Ten o’clock came, and eleven. I said he might have been called to the hospital, though he’d no business going in his condition.”

“He could scarcely manage the stairs,” Nannie said.

“At last we sent for Father O’Rourke. He came straightaway, good man that he is. The church was locked, but the side door had not caught properly. They searched the sacristy and behind the altar.”

Eliza stopped. Her fingers pressed against the stem of the glass.

“Well?” my aunt said.

“They heard a sound.”

Nannie lifted her eyes to her sister.

“Not crying,” Eliza said. “At first Father O’Rourke thought it might be crying. It came from the confessionals.”

The rain tapped more distinctly. I became aware of the cracker softening in my palm.

“He was inside one of them,” she continued. “Sitting where the priest sits, in the dark. His hat was on the floor and his breviary open across his knees. Wide awake he was.”

She paused again.

“And laughing,” Nannie said.

Eliza looked displeased that the word had been supplied.

“Well, not laughing as people laugh. A little sound to himself. Soft-like. But he wouldn’t answer them. Father O’Rourke called his name and took him by the shoulder, and James only looked through the grille into the empty side, smiling away.”

My aunt set down her glass.

“God preserve us.”

“That’s when Father O’Rourke knew,” said Eliza. “There was something gone wrong.”

No one spoke. From the room opposite came the smell of lilies, thick and sweet, and beneath it another odour I could not name. I pictured the empty confessional: the narrow seat, the polished ledge, the pierced screen. I had knelt there often while Father Flynn’s shadow waited on the other side. In my mind he sat alone, hearing the sins of no one, laughing very softly into the dark.

Nannie rose to attend to the candles. As she crossed the passage, the boards gave a long creak. Eliza watched her go.

“He never said what happened,” she whispered. “Not afterward. If he remembered it, he kept it between himself and God.”

My aunt crossed herself. I did the same, a moment too late.

When we left, Nannie kissed my forehead at the door. Her lips were cold. Eliza told my aunt to come again after the funeral, when things had settled, though her manner suggested that nothing would ever settle.

Outside, the rain had freshened. My aunt opened her umbrella and drew me under it. We walked close together, avoiding the streams along the kerb. At the corner I looked back. The upper room was lighted once more, but now I knew the lamp burned for the corpse.

My aunt said, “You must remember him in your prayers.”

I promised I would.

Yet as we went home I felt again that secret loosening, as if a knot had been cut somewhere inside me. It was not happiness. The dark streets seemed wider than before, and that width frightened me. I thought of the catechism closed forever, of the unanswered questions, of the chalice resting idle upon his breast.

Above the roofs, a strip of cloud had opened. A few stars showed through, weak and remote. I tried to imagine Father Flynn beyond them, restored to speech and motion, all mysteries made plain to him. But the only figure I could summon was the one in the confession-box: awake in the darkness, his fallen book upon his knees, listening at the grille where no penitent waited, and smiling at whatever had come to confess.

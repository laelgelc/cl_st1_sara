### The Long Way In

Tub had been waiting beside the church since before daylight. Snow covered the steps and softened the stone animals on either side of the entrance. He stood under the bare branches of a maple, stamping his feet and watching the road.

At seven-thirty a truck came around the corner. It was moving too fast. The back end swung out, straightened, then swung again. Tub stepped away from the curb. The truck followed him. He dropped his rifle and jumped into the snowbank.

The truck stopped with its bumper against the bank.

Kenny leaned across the seat, laughing. His mouth was wide open. Through the cracked windshield his face looked painted there.

“You should have seen yourself,” he said.

Frank sat beside him. He was laughing too, but quietly.

Tub climbed out of the snow. He brushed at his coat. “You could have killed me.”

“Not you,” Kenny said. “A normal man, maybe.”

“You were supposed to be here an hour ago.”

“We had to get gas.”

“For an hour?”

Frank rolled down the window. “Get in, Tub. We’re burning daylight.”

Tub picked up his rifle. The barrel was packed with snow. He opened the passenger door and looked at the seat.

“No room,” Kenny said.

Frank had a thermos between his boots and two packs on his lap. Kenny’s rifle lay across the seat. Tub went around to the back. A length of chain, two spare tires, and a shovel were sliding around in the bed.

“You want me to ride back here?”

Kenny looked in the mirror. “You want us to leave the chain?”

Tub climbed in. The truck started before he had settled himself. He fell against the side and struck his elbow. Kenny waved through the rear window.

Snow blew into Tub’s face. He sat on one of the tires and held the rifle between his knees. The road crossed the edge of town, passed a school with dark windows, and entered open country. The fields were white except where the wind had scraped them bare. Black dirt showed in long cuts. Every few miles they passed a farmhouse with smoke lying flat above the chimney.

After half an hour the truck stopped at a filling station. Frank got out and came around back.

“You alive?”

Tub climbed down.

“Barely.”

“We didn’t know it would be this cold.”

“It’s January.”

Frank rubbed his hands together. He wore yellow gloves with leather patches on the palms. “Kenny said the heater was fixed.”

“The heater is fixed,” Kenny called. “It just doesn’t work.”

Inside the station, Frank bought coffee. Kenny bought cigarettes and a sweet roll wrapped in plastic. Tub stayed outside by the truck. When Kenny came out, he held the sweet roll in front of Tub’s face.

“Want a bite?”

“No.”

“You sure?”

“I ate.”

“What’d you eat?”

“Breakfast.”

Kenny peeled the wrapper down. “What kind of breakfast?”

Tub looked across the road.

Kenny took a bite and chewed slowly. “Gland food?”

“Leave him alone,” Frank said. He had taken the lid off his coffee and was blowing on it. “We’re here to hunt.”

“I’m only looking after his health.”

“You don’t know anything about my health,” Tub said.

“I know you’re the only man I ever saw who can gain weight walking uphill.”

Tub opened the truck door. “Move your rifle.”

Kenny stared at him. Then he pushed the rifle toward Frank.

Tub got in. Frank sat in the middle. Kenny drove with one hand and ate the sweet roll with the other.

They left the highway and turned onto a narrow county road. Snow rose almost to the fence wires. At a gap in the trees, Kenny pulled over.

“This is it,” he said.

“This is what?” Tub asked.

“The place.”

There was no house in sight. A logging road went up through the woods. Someone had driven into it earlier, but the tracks had filled with new snow.

Frank took out the thermos. “We should make a plan.”

Kenny opened his door. “Here’s the plan. Find deer. Shoot deer.”

“We ought to work the ridge,” Frank said.

“You work the ridge.”

“You said we.”

“I changed my mind.”

Tub climbed down from the cab. He looked into the truck bed for his pack.

“What’s this?” Kenny said.

He had picked up a paper sack tucked behind the spare tire.

“Nothing,” Tub said.

Kenny opened it. Inside were four sandwiches wrapped in wax paper.

“Breakfast,” Kenny said.

“Give me those.”

Kenny held the sack above his head. Tub grabbed at it. The bottom tore out, and the sandwiches fell into the snow.

Frank stepped between them. “That’s enough.”

Kenny looked down at the sandwiches. “You said you ate breakfast.”

“So?”

“So these are what? Emergency medicine?”

Tub knelt and picked them up. Snow had stuck to the wax paper. One sandwich had opened, showing thick pink meat and yellow mustard.

“Don’t start,” Frank said.

“I’m not starting anything,” Kenny said. “Tub started it years ago.”

Tub pushed the sandwiches into his pack.

“You know what your trouble is?” Kenny said. “Your glands are located in your refrigerator.”

Tub stood up. His face felt hot, though the wind had gone through his coat.

“My doctor says—”

“Your doctor says whatever keeps you coming back.”

“Shut up.”

Kenny smiled. “Make me.”

Frank raised both hands. “Save it for the deer.”

They loaded their rifles and entered the woods.

The snow was deeper under the trees. It had formed a crust during the night, but the crust would not hold Tub. Each step broke through. The snow caught him above the knees. Kenny and Frank moved lightly ahead, sometimes walking on top.

They followed a game trail along the lower slope. The trail bent through brush and young pines. Tub’s coat snagged on branches. His breathing grew loud.

“Keep it down,” Kenny called.

Tub stopped to catch his breath.

Frank looked back. “You all right?”

“He’s sending signals to the herd,” Kenny said. “They can hear him in Canada.”

Tub bent over with his hands on his thighs. Sweat had gathered under his cap. He could feel it running behind his ears.

“You go ahead,” he said. “I’ll catch up.”

“We know,” Kenny said.

Frank hesitated. Then Kenny said something to him, and they moved on.

Tub watched their orange caps pass between the trees. He stayed where he was until they disappeared.

The woods became quiet. Snow slipped from a branch and struck the ground with a soft weight. Somewhere above him a crow called once.

Tub began climbing again.

He found their tracks, but they had widened the distance. Every so often one of them had stepped aside and kicked snow into the trail. Tub could not tell which. He kept his rifle across his body and pushed forward.

At the ridge the wind was stronger. The trees thinned, and the snow had been driven into hard waves. Tub saw Frank and Kenny below him, entering a stand of birch. He called out.

Kenny turned and put a finger to his lips. Then he pointed ahead.

Tub crouched. His knees began to shake. He saw nothing among the trees.

For ten minutes no one moved. Then Kenny stood.

“Gone,” he said.

“What was it?” Tub asked when he reached them.

“Buck.”

“How big?”

“Twelve points,” Kenny said. “Maybe fourteen. Hard to count with all that gold hanging off him.”

Frank adjusted his sling. “It was a doe.”

“I saw antlers.”

“You saw branches.”

“Same family.”

Tub wiped his mouth with his glove. “Why didn’t you shoot?”

“It was too far.”

“You shoot at anything.”

Kenny looked at him. “Not anything.”

They crossed the ridge and entered a long hollow. The snow there was untouched except for the marks of mice and birds. They spread out. Frank took the left side, Kenny the middle, and Tub the right.

Tub’s side was the steepest. The ground fell away under brush and loose stones. Twice he slipped. The second time his rifle went off his shoulder and landed in the snow. He dug it out and worked the action to make sure it was clear.

Kenny appeared above him.

“Having fun?”

“I’m fine.”

“You look fine.”

Tub started climbing toward him.

Kenny moved on.

Around noon they stopped beside a frozen creek. Frank sat on a fallen log and poured coffee. Kenny lit a cigarette.

Tub took one of his sandwiches from the pack and held it behind his knee as he unwrapped it.

Kenny watched him.

“What?” Tub said.

“Nothing.”

Tub ate quickly. The bread was wet from the snow. Mustard got on his glove.

Frank passed him the thermos. “Only one cup.”

Tub drank. The coffee was sweet and nearly cold.

“Any sign down there?” Frank asked.

“Some tracks.”

“Fresh?”

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t know fresh tracks?” Kenny said.

“They were tracks.”

“Could’ve been your own.”

“They were deer tracks.”

“How do you know?”

“They weren’t wearing boots.”

Frank laughed. Tub did too. Kenny looked from one to the other and tossed his cigarette into the creek.

“You boys finished?”

The sky had lowered. Gray clouds pressed over the treetops. A few flakes began to fall.

Frank said, “We should circle back.”

“We haven’t covered the north side,” Kenny said.

“We won’t make it before dark.”

“We’ll make it.”

Tub finished the sandwich. “I’m going back.”

Kenny stood over him. “You’re what?”

“I’m going back to the truck.”

“We came to hunt.”

“You hunted. I walked behind you.”

“That’s because walking next to you causes tides.”

Frank said, “Knock it off.”

Tub looked at Frank. “You heard him all day.”

“He’s kidding.”

“I don’t care.”

Kenny put his cigarette pack away. “Poor Tub.”

“Leave him be,” Frank said.

“Poor little Tub with his glands and his secret sandwiches.”

Tub rose from the log. Kenny stepped back, smiling.

“What’re you going to do?” Kenny asked.

Tub picked up his rifle.

Frank got between them. “We’re going back. That’s settled.”

Nobody spoke on the return. The new snow covered their earlier tracks and made the way hard to follow. Kenny led them across the creek, then up a slope that ended in a wall of thorn bushes. They had to turn around.

“It was the other crossing,” Frank said.

“I know.”

“You said you knew this place.”

“I do.”

Tub leaned against a tree. His legs trembled under him.

Kenny looked over. “Don’t die yet. We’d need a winch.”

Tub said nothing.

They reached the truck at three. Snow covered the hood and filled the bed. Kenny started the engine, but it took several tries. The heater blew air that smelled of dust.

Frank unfolded a road map across his knees.

“There’s another place west of here,” he said. “Farm country. We can ask permission.”

“It’ll be dark,” Tub said.

“Not for two hours.”

“I’m done.”

Kenny backed onto the road. “You can stay in the truck.”

“Fine.”

“With the food.”

Tub watched the woods move past. His reflection hovered in the side window, pale and broad. Behind it the fields stretched away under the falling snow.

They drove west. The road narrowed, rose, and dropped. At the bottom of a hill they passed a red barn and an old house with a porch sagging in the middle. Kenny braked.

A man was splitting wood beside the barn. He set down his axe when they pulled in.

Frank got out. “We’re looking for someplace to hunt.”

The farmer rubbed his beard. He was small and wore a green wool cap. “Not much around.”

“We’d stay away from the buildings.”

The farmer looked into the truck. His eyes rested on Tub.

“You boys had any luck?”

“Not yet,” Frank said.

“You can try the upper pasture. Sometimes they come down along the orchard.”

Kenny opened his door. “How do we get there?”

The farmer pointed toward a gate. “Follow the fence. There’s a break at the top.”

A dog came from behind the barn. It was a black shepherd with white around its muzzle. One hind leg dragged slightly. The dog stopped near the farmer and looked at the men.

The farmer scratched its head.

Frank said, “We appreciate it.”

Tub remained in the cab while the others unloaded their rifles. Kenny came around and opened Tub’s door.

“Let’s go.”

“I said I was done.”

“You’ll get stiff sitting there.”

“I’m already stiff.”

The farmer looked toward them.

Frank said, “Come on, Tub. Last push.”

Tub climbed out.

The farmer caught Kenny by the sleeve and spoke to him in a low voice. Tub could not hear what was said. Kenny looked at the dog, then at the farmer. The farmer nodded.

“What’s that about?” Frank asked when Kenny joined them.

“Nothing.”

They followed the fence toward the upper field. The farmer stayed by the woodpile. The dog came after them.

“Go home,” Kenny said.

The dog kept coming.

The pasture sloped upward, open except for weeds poking through the snow. Beyond it stood an orchard. The trees were twisted and black, their lower trunks painted white.

They crossed the fence one at a time. Frank climbed through the wires. Kenny vaulted over. Tub tried to step across, caught his trouser leg, and fell sideways.

Kenny laughed. “Man overboard.”

Tub pushed himself up. Snow had gone down the back of his coat.

“Hold the wire,” he said.

“You got across, didn’t you?”

The dog waited until Tub stood, then slipped under the fence.

In the orchard they found tracks, but they were old. Kenny followed them anyway. The light was fading. Snow hung blue between the rows of trees.

Frank whispered, “We should drive the far edge.”

Kenny did not answer.

They reached the top of the orchard. Beyond it, the land fell toward another line of woods. There were no deer.

Kenny kicked snow from around a fence post. “Waste of time.”

“You wanted to come,” Tub said.

“I wanted to hunt. I didn’t want to nurse you through a nature walk.”

Frank said, “Let’s call it.”

Kenny lifted his rifle and fired into the fence post.

The report struck the orchard and came back from the hills. Snow fell from the branches. The dog crouched.

“What the hell?” Frank said.

Kenny worked the bolt. “Hate that post.”

He turned and fired into an apple tree. Bark jumped from the trunk.

Frank grabbed at the rifle. “Stop it.”

Kenny pulled away.

The dog had started down the row. Kenny watched it.

Then he raised the rifle again.

The dog looked back.

Kenny fired.

The animal rolled once and lay still. Its legs pointed toward the trees. Against the snow it looked flat and black, like a rug thrown out to cool.

Frank stared at Kenny. “You shot the dog.”

“That’s right.”

“Why?”

Kenny lowered the rifle. “I hated it.”

Tub felt the cold under his coat. He had his rifle in both hands.

Kenny looked at the dead dog, then at the tree, then at the post. He turned toward Tub.

“And I hate you.”

The rifle began to come up.

Tub fired.

Kenny’s body folded. He dropped his rifle and sat heavily in the snow. For a moment he looked surprised. Then he rolled onto his side and pulled his knees up.

Frank knocked Tub’s rifle away. “What did you do?”

“He was going to shoot me.”

Kenny made a high sound through his teeth.

Frank ran to him and knelt. “Where are you hit?”

Kenny pressed both hands against his stomach. Blood moved between his fingers.

“He was going to shoot me,” Tub said again.

Frank opened Kenny’s coat. “Give me your scarf.”

Tub did not move.

“Your scarf, Tub.”

Tub unwound it and handed it over. Frank pushed it against the wound.

Kenny’s face had gone white around the mouth.

“You shot me,” he said.

“You said you hated me.”

“I was kidding.”

“You shot the dog.”

“The farmer told me to.”

Tub looked down the orchard.

“What?”

“The farmer told me to do it,” Kenny said. His teeth were shaking. “The dog was sick.”

Frank tied the scarf around Kenny’s middle. “Why didn’t you say so?”

Kenny closed his eyes. “You didn’t ask.”

The farmer was running up the pasture. His boots broke through the snow. He stopped at the fence and climbed between the wires.

“What happened?”

“He got shot,” Frank said.

The farmer looked at Tub, then at Kenny. “Accident?”

Tub picked up his rifle.

“He thought I was going to shoot him,” Kenny said.

“Were you?”

“No.”

The farmer walked over to the dog. He stood above it with his cap in his hands.

“I asked him to put her down,” he said.

Tub stared at the snow by Kenny’s hip. Blood had spread into it. At the edges it was pink and thin.

“We need a hospital,” Frank said.

The farmer pointed toward the road. “Fifty miles east. County hospital in Mercer.”

“Fifty?”

“Maybe more in this weather.”

“Call an ambulance.”

“My phone line’s been out since yesterday.”

The farmer helped Frank lift Kenny. Kenny shouted once and then bit down on his glove. They carried him between them. Tub followed with the rifles.

At the fence Kenny’s coat caught on the top wire.

“Hold him up,” Frank said.

“I am.”

“Higher.”

The farmer pulled the wire down. They passed Kenny over it. His boot struck Tub in the face. Tub nearly dropped his legs.

“Sorry,” Kenny said.

The farmer opened the truck’s tailgate. They cleared the spare tires and chain to one side, then laid Kenny in the bed. Frank brought blankets from behind the seat and covered him.

“Keep pressure on that,” Frank said.

Kenny had both hands over the scarf. “I know.”

Frank touched the wound through the cloth. “Could be worse.”

“How?”

“It’s on the right. Your appendix is over there, but I think the bullet missed it.”

“My appendix is gone.”

Frank nodded. “Then it definitely missed.”

The farmer brought a sheet of paper with directions written in pencil. “Road forks after Simpson’s mill. Bear left. Don’t take the mountain route.”

Frank put the paper on the dashboard.

Tub climbed into the middle. Frank drove. The windshield wipers pushed heavy snow to the sides, where it froze in ridges. Kenny’s face showed briefly in the rear window when the truck bounced.

They had gone two miles when Kenny knocked on the cab.

Frank slowed. Tub opened the small sliding window.

“What?” he called.

“I’m cold.”

“You’ve got blankets.”

“They slid off.”

Frank stopped. Tub got out and pulled the blankets over Kenny. One had caught under the spare tire. He tugged it free.

“How are you?” Tub asked.

“I’ve been better.”

“We’re taking you to the hospital.”

Kenny looked up at him. Snow had landed on his eyelashes. “No kidding.”

Tub tucked the blanket under his shoulders.

As they started again, Frank said, “You shouldn’t have shot him.”

“I know.”

“You don’t shoot a man because he says something.”

“He raised the rifle.”

“He was fooling around.”

Tub watched the road. “He fooled around with the dog.”

“The farmer asked him.”

“I didn’t know that.”

“You could have waited.”

“For what?”

“To see.”

Tub rubbed the place on his cheek where Kenny’s boot had struck him. “Easy for you to say.”

Frank drove without answering.

The snowfall thickened. The headlights filled with white specks. At times the road disappeared altogether. Frank leaned close to the windshield and followed the dark line of the ditch.

They passed a boarded store, then a cemetery. Snow capped the low stone wall. Frank turned at the next fork.

Tub picked up the directions. The pencil marks were faint.

“Was that Simpson’s mill?”

“I didn’t see a mill.”

“There was a building.”

“A mill has water.”

“Maybe the water’s frozen.”

Frank took the paper from him and held it near the dash light. “This isn’t right.”

“What?”

“It says the first fork after the mill.”

“That was a fork.”

“It was more like a bend.”

“You turned.”

“I can turn back.”

Kenny knocked again.

Frank stopped. Tub opened the window.

“What is it?”

“I’m freezing.”

“We’re moving as fast as we can.”

“I can’t feel my feet.”

Tub looked at Frank.

Frank said, “There should be a tavern up ahead. We’ll warm him there.”

“Warm him how?”

“Get him inside.”

They drove another ten minutes and saw a neon sign through the snow. Only half the letters worked. The sign said OOMS.

The building stood alone beside the road. Three trucks were parked in front. Smoke came from a metal chimney.

Frank pulled in. “We’ll ask about the hospital.”

They went around back. Kenny lay with his face turned to one side. The blanket under him was dark.

“You’re bleeding through,” Frank said.

“I know.”

“We’re stopping a minute.”

“Don’t stop.”

“We need directions.”

“I’m going to the hospital.”

“That’s right.”

Frank and Tub lifted him. Kenny cried out and grabbed Frank’s collar.

“No,” he said. “Put me down.”

“It’s warm inside.”

“Just go.”

Frank lowered him back into the truck. “All right. We’ll be quick.”

Tub looked at the blankets.

“He needs them,” Frank said.

“I didn’t say anything.”

Inside, the tavern was hot and smelled of wet wool, beer, and frying onions. A television hung above the bar with the sound turned off. Men in work clothes sat beneath it.

Frank showed the directions to the bartender.

“Mercer?” the bartender said. “You’re headed the wrong way.”

Frank looked at Tub.

“How wrong?” Tub asked.

“Depends where you turned.”

“Can you draw it?”

The bartender found a paper placemat. He drew three roads and marked one with an X.

“Stay right at the quarry,” he said. “Left takes you into the mountains.”

Frank folded the placemat.

Tub stood near a heater fixed to the wall. His trousers began to steam. Feeling returned to his feet in small needles.

“We should go,” Frank said.

“In a minute.”

“Kenny’s outside.”

“I know where he is.”

Frank looked toward the door. Then he ordered two coffees.

They stood at the bar and drank. The cups were thick and white. Tub held his in both hands.

Frank said, “You scared me back there.”

“He scared me.”

“I mean when you fired. You didn’t even think.”

“I thought.”

“No. If you’d thought, you wouldn’t have done it.”

Tub drank his coffee.

Frank’s face softened. “He treats you badly. I know.”

“You laugh.”

“Sometimes.”

“All the time.”

“I get carried along.”

“That’s what you call it?”

Frank pressed his lips together. “You want me to apologize?”

“No.”

“I’m apologizing anyway.”

Tub looked at him. “Fine.”

Frank put his hand on Tub’s shoulder. “There’s more going on with me than you know.”

Tub took another drink. “There’s a man bleeding in the truck.”

“I know that.”

“He could die.”

“You think I don’t know?”

They finished their coffee and went outside.

Snow had already covered their tracks. Kenny’s eyes were open.

“Hospital?” he asked.

“We got directions,” Frank said.

“I’m going to the hospital.”

“Yes.”

They climbed into the cab. Frank unfolded the placemat and put it beside the farmer’s paper.

When he shut his door, the wind caught both sheets. The farmer’s directions blew under the seat. The placemat flew through the open window and disappeared into the dark.

Frank sat still.

Tub said, “Go get it.”

“Where?”

“It just went out.”

Frank opened the door and searched beside the truck. He walked into the road and bent over twice. Snow moved across the pavement in pale streams.

At last he returned.

“Gone,” he said.

“You saw what he drew.”

“Mostly.”

“Mostly?”

“There’s a quarry.”

“Where?”

“We’ll see signs.”

They drove on.

For several miles the road was level. Then it began to climb.

Tub said, “This looks like the mountain route.”

“No, it doesn’t.”

“There weren’t supposed to be mountains.”

“Everything around here is mountains.”

The engine strained. Snowbanks rose on either side. No houses showed in the trees.

Kenny knocked weakly.

Tub opened the window.

“Where are we?”

“Almost there.”

“Is that true?”

“We had to take a detour.”

“I’m going to the hospital.”

Tub looked at Frank. “Yes,” he said. “You are.”

The road wound upward. Wind shook the truck. A wiper blade came loose and slapped against the glass. Frank stopped and tied it down with wire from the glove compartment.

While he worked, Tub stood beside the bed.

Kenny watched him. “You meant to do it?”

“What?”

“Shoot me.”

“I thought you were going to shoot me.”

“You always say that.”

“I only said it twice.”

Kenny’s lips moved. “You’re crazy.”

“You pointed your gun.”

“At the ground.”

“It was coming up.”

“I was kidding.”

Tub pulled the blanket higher. “You shouldn’t kid like that.”

Kenny closed his eyes.

Back in the cab, Frank said, “What did he say?”

“He thinks I’m crazy.”

“Are you?”

“No.”

Frank nodded, as if Tub had settled it.

The truck crested the hill and went down through thick woods. The descent was steep. Frank used the brakes too much. A bitter smell entered the cab.

“They’re heating up,” Tub said.

“I know.”

“You want to stop?”

“If we stop, we may not start.”

They kept going. At the bottom they came to another road. There was no sign.

Frank turned right.

“Why right?” Tub asked.

“I have a feeling.”

“You had a feeling at the last turn.”

“That was different.”

“How?”

“It didn’t work out.”

Tub leaned back. The heater gave off a weak warmth. His wet clothes made the seat cold beneath him.

After a while they saw lights ahead. A diner stood at a crossroads, its windows bright in the snow. A row of small colored bulbs blinked around the roof.

Frank pulled into the lot.

“No,” Tub said.

“We need to ask.”

“We already asked.”

“And we lost the map.”

“Then ask and come back.”

Frank looked at him. “I need to talk to you.”

“About what?”

“Something important.”

Tub glanced through the rear window. Kenny’s shape lay under the blankets.

“Not now.”

“Now.”

Frank went inside. Tub stayed in the truck for a moment, then followed.

The diner was nearly empty. A waitress sat at the counter reading a magazine. She stood when they came in.

“Kitchen’s closing,” she said.

“Coffee,” Frank told her. “And something to eat.”

Tub said, “We don’t have time.”

“You need food.”

“I have food.”

Frank smiled slightly. “I know.”

They took a booth near the front window. From there Tub could see the truck under the parking-lot light. Snow gathered on the roof and hood. The bed was hidden by the cab.

The waitress brought menus.

Frank did not open his. “Pancakes,” he said.

“How many?”

“Bring four orders.”

The waitress looked at Tub.

“Four orders,” Frank repeated. “All for him.”

Tub felt his face warm.

“Frank.”

“What?”

“I don’t want four orders.”

“You’re hungry.”

“I’m not.”

Frank leaned across the table. “Don’t lie to me.”

The waitress waited.

Tub said, “Bring one.”

“Four,” Frank said. “And extra syrup.”

The waitress walked away.

Tub stared through the window.

Frank lit a cigarette, though an ashtray on the table said NO SMOKING. “I have to tell you something.”

“Tell me outside.”

“I can’t.”

“Why?”

“Because it’s cold.”

Tub looked at him.

Frank took a long breath. “I’m leaving Nancy.”

Tub turned from the window. “You’re what?”

“I’m leaving her.”

“For who?”

Frank tapped ash into his coffee saucer. “You know the girl who watches our kids.”

“No.”

“You’ve seen her. Roxanne.”

“The little one with braces?”

“They’re off now.”

“How old is she?”

“Fifteen.”

Tub sat back.

Frank said, “Don’t look at me that way.”

“What way?”

“You don’t understand.”

“She’s fifteen.”

“It isn’t about age.”

“What’s it about?”

“Connection.”

Tub looked outside again. A truck passed on the road, moving slowly through the snow.

Frank lowered his voice. “She sees me. That’s the thing. Nancy looks at me and sees a paycheck and someone who forgot to take out the garbage. Roxanne sees who I actually am.”

“She’s a kid.”

“Physically, yes. Spiritually she’s older than either of us.”

“Either of who?”

“Me and Nancy.”

The waitress set down two coffees. Frank crushed his cigarette in the saucer.

Tub said, “Does she know you’re leaving your wife?”

“She knows how I feel.”

“That’s not what I asked.”

Frank stirred his coffee. “I’m trying to be honest with everyone.”

“You haven’t told Nancy.”

“The timing has to be right.”

“And the girl?”

“She’s afraid. Her parents are narrow people.”

“Maybe they know she’s fifteen.”

Frank’s face changed. “I thought you’d understand.”

“Why?”

“Because people judge you too.”

“For being fat?”

“For being different.”

Tub laughed once.

Frank shook his head. “Forget it.”

“No, go ahead.”

“I opened myself to you.”

“You told me you want to run off with a child.”

“That’s ugly.”

“It’s true.”

“Not the way you say it.”

The waitress brought the pancakes. Four plates filled the table. Each held a stack with a square of butter melting on top. She set down a metal pitcher of syrup.

Frank pushed the first plate toward Tub.

“Eat.”

“I’m not hungry.”

“Eat anyway.”

Tub looked at the pancakes. The smell rose warm and sweet.

Frank said, “You think I don’t know how it sounds? I fight it every day. I ask what it means. I ask whether it’s selfish. I ask whether denying love is any less selfish.”

Tub poured syrup over the stack. It gathered in the plate and darkened the edges.

Frank watched him. “There.”

Tub cut a wedge and put it into his mouth.

The pancakes were soft in the middle and crisp at the rim. The syrup was hot.

Frank smiled. “Good?”

Tub nodded.

“Then stop pretending.”

Tub ate another bite.

Outside, the snow passed through the parking-lot light in straight lines.

Frank said, “You know what I hate? All the lies.”

Tub kept eating.

“People build their lives out of lies and call it duty. They stay miserable because they’re afraid of what others might say.”

Tub put down his fork.

Frank said, “What?”

“I don’t have a gland problem.”

Frank stared at him.

“I never did.”

“What did the doctor say?”

“There wasn’t any doctor.”

Frank looked toward the counter. The waitress had gone back to her magazine.

Tub said, “I eat.”

“Everybody eats.”

“I eat all the time.”

Frank waited.

“I stop on the way to work,” Tub said. “I get eggs and sausage. Then doughnuts. At lunch I eat what I brought, then I go out and get something else. Sometimes I sit in the car so nobody sees me. At night I wake up and go into the kitchen. I don’t turn on the light. I know where everything is.”

Frank’s expression had become tender.

Tub looked down at the pancakes. “I hide food in the garage. I’ve got candy bars in my toolbox. Nancy asked me once why I keep a wrench in the kitchen drawer. It isn’t a wrench. It’s chocolate wrapped in a rag.”

“Why lie about it?”

“Because Kenny.”

“To hell with Kenny.”

“And you.”

Frank shook his head. “Not me.”

“You laugh.”

“I won’t anymore.”

“You say that now.”

“I mean it.”

Tub lifted his fork.

Frank pushed the second plate toward him. “No shame.”

“I’ve had enough.”

“You just told the truth. Don’t crawl back into a lie.”

Tub looked at Frank. Then he poured syrup over the second stack.

Frank reached across the table and squeezed his wrist. “You’re all right, Tub.”

Tub ate.

The waitress refilled their coffee. She glanced at the empty first plate and the three remaining stacks but said nothing.

Frank talked about Roxanne. He said she had a pure way of seeing things. She liked songs nobody her age listened to. She had read a book about reincarnation and believed some people met again through many lives.

“She thinks we knew each other before,” Frank said.

Tub chewed and nodded.

“That’s why age doesn’t matter in the usual sense. Maybe I’m fifteen to her. Maybe she’s forty to me.”

Tub swallowed. “Maybe you were both dogs.”

Frank laughed.

Tub laughed too.

They laughed until Frank began coughing. The waitress looked over.

The third plate went down more slowly. Tub loosened his belt beneath the table. Frank kept pouring syrup whenever a dry patch appeared.

“Finish it,” Frank said.

“I can’t.”

“You can.”

“I’ll be sick.”

“No, you won’t.”

Tub finished.

The fourth stack sat untouched.

Frank leaned back. “I think that’s enough.”

Tub looked at him. “You ordered it.”

“I got carried away.”

Tub took a pancake from the stack with his fingers and folded it in half. Syrup ran over his thumb. He ate it while Frank watched.

“That’s the spirit,” Frank said.

When they paid, Tub asked the waitress for directions to Mercer.

“Mercer?” she said. “That’s south.”

Frank said, “We know.”

“You’re going north.”

“We had to come around.”

She gave them directions. Frank wrote them on a napkin.

Outside, the cold seized Tub’s damp clothes. His stomach pressed against his belt. He walked slowly to the truck.

Kenny had pushed one arm from beneath the blankets. His hand was bare and covered with snow.

Frank brushed it off. “Kenny?”

Kenny opened his eyes.

“We’re going now,” Frank said.

“I’m going to the hospital.”

“Yes.”

“Straight there?”

“Straight.”

Kenny’s eyes moved toward Tub. “You eating?”

Tub wiped syrup from his thumb onto his coat. “No.”

Kenny smiled faintly. “Liar.”

Frank tucked Kenny’s arm under the blankets. “How’s the pain?”

“Bad.”

“That means you’re alive.”

“Is that what it means?”

Frank looked at Tub. “We need these.”

He pulled one blanket away from Kenny.

“What are you doing?” Tub asked.

“The heater’s worse.”

“He’s freezing.”

“He has two.”

Kenny said, “Three.”

“You have two now.”

Frank took the second blanket and handed it to Tub.

“This leaves him one,” Tub said.

“It’s a thick one.”

The remaining blanket was thin and gray. It covered Kenny from his shoulders to his boots, but it did not reach the sides of the truck bed.

Frank climbed into the cab and wrapped one of the blankets around his legs. Tub stood with the other in his arms.

Kenny said, “Tub.”

“What?”

“Don’t.”

Tub looked toward the diner. Through the window the waitress sat under the bright lights, turning a page of her magazine.

He climbed into the cab and closed the door.

The blanket smelled of dog hair and gasoline. Tub spread it over himself.

Frank started the engine. “Better?”

“Yes.”

They drove from the diner.

For a while neither spoke. Tub held the napkin directions beneath the dash light. The ink had spread where snow melted on the paper.

“First left after the bridge,” Tub said.

“I see it.”

They crossed a narrow bridge. Frank turned left.

The road became packed snow. It ran between high banks and rows of pines. No tire tracks showed ahead.

“Did she say this road?” Tub asked.

“First left.”

“There were two roads before the bridge.”

“She said after.”

“I know.”

“So this is it.”

Behind them came a faint knocking.

Tub opened the window.

Kenny’s voice was hard to hear over the engine. “How much longer?”

“Not long.”

“Can you see it?”

“See what?”

“The hospital.”

“Not yet.”

“I’m going to the hospital.”

“Yes.”

Tub closed the window.

Frank drove with one hand. The other rested on the blanket over his lap.

“You meant what you said?” Tub asked.

“About what?”

“Not laughing anymore.”

“Yes.”

“Kenny will laugh.”

“He can laugh alone.”

Tub watched the snowbanks pass in the headlights. “If he makes it.”

Frank looked at him.

“I said if.”

“He’ll make it.”

“You don’t know.”

“No.”

The truck slid on a curve. Frank turned into the slide, then back. The rear end struck the snowbank and bounced free.

They laughed. It came out at the same time.

Frank said, “That was close.”

Tub laughed harder. His stomach hurt. He pressed both hands against it.

Frank began laughing too. For a mile they could not stop. Each time one quieted, the other made a sound and started again.

Kenny knocked.

Frank wiped his eyes. “What now?”

Tub opened the window.

“I heard you,” Kenny said.

“We almost went off the road.”

“You laughing?”

“No.”

“I heard you.”

Frank called back, “Save your strength.”

Kenny was quiet for a moment. Then he said, “What happened with the dog was funny.”

Tub looked at Frank.

Frank said, “What?”

“He says the dog was funny.”

Tub leaned closer to the opening. “You mean when I shot you?”

“No,” Kenny said. “You thought I was going to shoot you.”

“You said you hated me.”

“I did hate you.”

Tub closed the window.

Frank drove another mile. “What did he say?”

“He said he hated me.”

“He’s in pain.”

“He said it like he meant it.”

“He says things.”

Tub stared ahead. “So do you.”

“What’s that supposed to mean?”

“Nothing.”

Frank’s mouth tightened. Then he reached over and struck Tub lightly on the shoulder.

“No secrets now,” he said.

Tub looked at him.

Frank struck him again. “No secrets.”

Tub pushed his hand away. “Watch the road.”

The road forked. One branch climbed through the trees. The other dropped into an open valley.

Tub unfolded the napkin.

“What does it say?” Frank asked.

The lower half had torn away.

“I can’t tell.”

“Mercer would be down.”

“Why?”

“Towns are in valleys.”

“Some are on hills.”

“Not hospitals.”

Frank took the lower road.

The valley was wide and empty. Wind swept snow over the pavement. Fences appeared and vanished beside them. Far off, one farmhouse showed a yellow square of light.

Tub shifted under the blanket. He was warm now. His eyes felt heavy.

“Stay awake,” Frank said.

“I am.”

“Talk to me.”

“About what?”

“Anything.”

Tub considered. “How are you going to tell Nancy?”

Frank watched the road. “Carefully.”

“What does that mean?”

“I’ll explain that no one is at fault.”

“She’ll think someone is.”

“That’s because people need blame.”

“You don’t?”

“I’m trying to get beyond blame.”

Tub closed his eyes.

Frank shook his arm. “Stay awake.”

“I’m awake.”

“You were snoring.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were.”

Tub opened his eyes. The farmhouse was gone. There were no lights anywhere.

Kenny began knocking again. The sound was weak and regular, like a branch tapping the side of a house.

Tub did not open the window.

After a time the knocking stopped.

Frank said, “You should check him.”

“You check him.”

“I’m driving.”

“Stop.”

“We can’t keep stopping.”

Tub turned and looked through the rear glass. He could see only the gray blanket lifting slightly in the wind.

“He’s moving,” Tub said.

“You sure?”

“Yes.”

They drove on.

The clouds began to break. A few stars appeared above the black ridge. Then the sky opened in the north, clear and hard. The stars looked close enough to strike.

Tub found the North Star above the trees. He remembered being shown how to find it from the two stars at the edge of the Dipper. His father had told him that people once trusted it to take them home.

“There,” Tub said.

Frank glanced up. “What?”

“The North Star.”

“So?”

“It means north.”

“I know what it means.”

“We’re going north.”

Frank looked at the compass fixed to the dashboard. The needle trembled. “Northwest.”

“Mercer is south.”

“We’re circling.”

“Are we?”

Frank did not answer.

Tub searched the floor for the farmer’s directions. His hand found an ice scraper, an empty cartridge box, and a crushed paper cup. He reached under the seat until his fingers closed on paper.

He pulled it out.

The sheet was wet and torn, but the farmer’s writing showed near the top: MERCER—EAST TO MILL, LEFT AT FORK. A thick pencil line pointed away from the mountains.

Tub held it under the dash light.

Frank said, “That’s no good now.”

“We should turn around.”

“There’s nowhere to turn.”

“We passed driveways.”

“Miles back.”

“Then back up.”

“In this weather?”

“We’re going the wrong way.”

Frank’s jaw worked. “The road will connect.”

“You don’t know that.”

“All roads connect.”

“Not to the hospital.”

Kenny spoke from the truck bed. They could barely hear him.

Tub opened the window.

“What?” he called.

The answer came thinly.

“I’m going to the hospital.”

Tub held the farmer’s paper in both hands. The pencil line shook with the truck.

“Yes,” he said.

Kenny lay still beneath the single blanket. Above him the North Star burned in the clear space between clouds, the old guide called Help to Sailors.

The truck kept going north. They had left the hospital behind a long time ago.

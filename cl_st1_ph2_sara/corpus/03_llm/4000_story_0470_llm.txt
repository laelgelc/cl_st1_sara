# A Bare Fighting Chance

Again Tom Collins walked past the entrance of the *Washington Evening Star*.

Again he turned at the corner, came back slowly, and studied the brass letters upon the glass doors as though he had never seen them before.

He had seen them often enough. During the past fortnight he had presented himself at nearly every newspaper office in Washington, and at several of them twice. At each he had received substantially the same answer.

“No vacancy.”

“No experience? Then we can’t use you.”

“Try us again in six months.”

Six months! Tom thrust a hand into his pocket and counted, by touch, the coins there. Twenty-seven cents would scarcely maintain a healthy nineteen-year-old for six months. Indeed, it promised a doubtful dinner and a still more doubtful breakfast. Starvation, when regarded from a comfortable chair, may appear a romantic misfortune; but to a young fellow with broad shoulders and an excellent appetite it is a plain and immediate nuisance.

He stopped once more before the entrance.

Another failure and—what?

There was no answer to that question. His room rent was two days overdue. His landlady had been patient because he reminded her of a son in Ohio, but sons in Ohio did not pay Washington rents. Tom had already sold his watch and the better of his two suits. The suit he wore had been brushed until brushing could do no more for it.

This time he went in.

The elevator carried him to the third floor, where a long passage trembled with the vibration of presses below. Boys hurried through swinging doors. Bells rang. Typewriters clicked in a score of different keys, producing a tremendous and disorderly tune. Somewhere a man shouted for copy, and another man shouted that he had sent it five minutes before.

Tom’s heart quickened. The confusion did not repel him. It seemed to him the most splendidly purposeful confusion in the world.

A copy boy, small, freckled, and immensely important, looked him over.

“Want somebody?”

“The city editor.”

“Got an appointment?”

“No.”

The boy grinned as one who had discovered a dangerous lunatic.

“Name?”

“Thomas Collins.”

“Business?”

“I want work.”

The grin broadened.

“Wait.”

He vanished through a gate in the railing. Beyond it Tom could see rows of desks buried beneath papers, paste pots, telegraph slips, and battered reference books. In the centre sat a large man with a fierce and bristling mustache. A black pipe projected from one corner of his mouth. He was cutting a column from another newspaper with shears large enough, Tom thought, to trim a hedge.

The boy whispered to him. The large man glanced up, and his eyes met Tom’s. It was not an encouraging glance.

“Tell him no,” he said.

Tom heard it distinctly.

The boy returned.

“Mr. Pike says there ain’t anything.”

Tom had expected the answer; nevertheless, it struck him with the force of a door slammed in his face. He thanked the boy, turned, and walked halfway to the elevator.

Then something in him rebelled.

For two weeks he had been civil, humble, and easily dismissed. He had accepted the judgment of clerks who had not read a line he had written and editors who had not permitted him to speak three sentences. He had been told that he lacked experience, as if experience were an article a man might purchase before breakfast.

He turned so suddenly that the copy boy nearly collided with him.

“Mr. Pike!” Tom called.

The typewriters seemed to pause, though in truth they did not. The large man removed his pipe.

“Well?”

“I should like to speak to you myself.”

“You have spoken. What is it?”

“A job.”

“Haven’t one.”

“How do you know you haven’t one for me?”

Mr. Pike laid down his shears. Several reporters nearby became interested without appearing to do so.

“Because,” said the editor, “I know my business.”

“I don’t doubt it, sir. But you don’t know me.”

“No,” returned Mr. Pike, “and at present I’m bearing up wonderfully under the deprivation.”

A reporter coughed into his hand.

Tom’s face grew hot, but he stood his ground.

“I’ve been trying every paper in the city. They all say I have no experience. I can’t get experience unless somebody lets me work. I don’t ask you to hire me because I need the money. I ask you to give me a fair chance to show whether I’m useful.”

Mr. Pike took the pipe from his mouth and regarded him more closely.

“How old?”

“Nineteen.”

“College?”

“One year.”

“Why only one?”

“My father died.”

“Ever written anything?”

“For a weekly paper in Pennsylvania. Farm notices, school meetings, an obituary, and part of a fire.”

“Part of a fire?”

“The press broke down before we printed the rest.”

This time the reporter’s cough sounded suspiciously like a laugh.

Mr. Pike scowled at him, then picked up a blue pencil and drew a vindictive line through half a page of copy.

“Know shorthand?”

“Yes, sir.”

“How fast?”

“One hundred and ten words a minute if the speaker behaves reasonably.”

“Speakers never do. Know the city?”

“Fairly well.”

“Know politics?”

“Enough to learn more.”

“Every young fool knows politics.” Mr. Pike pointed his pencil as if passing sentence. “Do you know Senator August?”

“I know who he is.”

“Have you met him?”

“No, sir.”

“Good. That puts you on the same footing as the rest of mankind. Now listen.”

He leaned back, and the men at neighboring desks ceased even pretending not to hear.

At noon that day there had been a private conference at the Willard Hotel. Senator Jonathan August, senior senator from a Western state and chairman of an important party committee, had met four other leaders to consider the proposed currency bill then dividing their party. Eastern Democrats feared inflation; Western and Southern members demanded an enlargement of the circulating medium; and the Administration, anxious to offend no one, was in a fair way to offend everybody. For three weeks rumors had multiplied. That morning’s conference was expected either to unite the factions or split them beyond repair.

Every newspaper in Washington wanted the result.

Every newspaper had failed to get it.

“The Associated Press man tried,” said Mr. Pike. “The senator told him the weather was pleasant. Jenkins of the *Courier* tried. He got a remark on the condition of Pennsylvania Avenue. Our own man waited two hours and learned that Senator August prefers gray gloves. The conference is over, the politicians are scattered, and nobody will say whether they agreed, quarreled, or threw the currency bill out of a window.”

He bent suddenly toward Tom.

“Get me the result.”

Tom stared at him.

“From Senator August?”

“From Senator August.”

“When?”

“Now.”

“But if experienced men—”

“You wanted a fair chance.”

“Yes, sir, but—”

“Was it a fair chance you wanted, or a comfortable chair?”

Tom straightened.

“Where is the senator?”

Mr. Pike’s mustache moved slightly. Perhaps it concealed a smile; perhaps not.

“He left the Willard ten minutes ago. Find him. Get a statement in his own words. Bring it here before five o’clock. If it is worth printing, I’ll pay you space rates. If it is the truth, and exclusive, I may let you come back tomorrow.”

“Is that an offer of a position?”

“It is an offer of a forlorn hope. Take it or stop blocking the aisle.”

Tom took his hat from beneath his arm.

“I’ll take it.”

“Then why are you standing there?”

Tom ran.

At the Willard he found three discouraged reporters in the lobby and a clerk who had no reason to protect the senator now that he had gone.

“Senator August left for the Union Station,” the clerk said. “He is taking the two-twenty express west.”

Tom looked at the clock.

It was fourteen minutes past two.

The distance to the station was not great for a carriage, but Tom possessed twenty-seven cents, and a carriage driver would not be moved by professional enthusiasm. He dashed into the street, dodged a cable car, and ran.

His hat blew off at the first corner. A colored messenger boy caught it neatly and held it out. Tom snatched it with a breathless “Thanks!” and went on. A stout gentleman stepped from a shop and was obliged to jump back. A policeman shouted something. Tom neither heard nor cared.

Six minutes!

The station clock showed nineteen minutes after two when he burst through the doors. A gate was closing at the end of the concourse.

“Western express?” he gasped.

“Track four. She’s moving.”

Tom raced past the gatekeeper, who made an unsuccessful grab for his sleeve. The long train had begun to glide beneath the iron roof. Porters were lifting the steps. At the rear of the last Pullman, a conductor swung aboard.

“Wait!” Tom cried.

The conductor did not wait. Trains, unlike editors, cannot be argued with.

Tom ran beside the last car. For several seconds it gained on him; then he caught the brass rail, found the lower step with one foot, and swung himself up. The conductor seized his collar and dragged him onto the platform.

“Are you trying to get killed?”

“No, sir,” Tom panted. “I’m trying to become a reporter.”

The conductor looked as though he considered the first ambition more reasonable.

“Ticket?”

Tom put a hand into his pocket.

Then he remembered.

In the excitement of pursuit, he had overlooked the minor fact that railway companies expected passengers to pay. His twenty-seven cents would not carry him far toward the West.

“I—haven’t bought one.”

“Cash fare, then.”

“How far is the next stop?”

“Brenton Junction.”

“What is the fare?”

“Eighty cents.”

Tom’s spirits descended even more rapidly than his breathing.

“I have twenty-seven.”

“Then you’ve got trouble.”

“I must see Senator August. It is a matter of national importance.”

“Of course it is. It generally is when a man hasn’t a ticket.”

“Let me speak to him. If he refuses to see me, put me off at Brenton. I’ll send the fare to the company tonight.”

“You’ll send it from the middle of Maryland, I suppose.”

“I’ll leave my coat.”

The conductor glanced at the coat and evidently concluded that the railway could struggle on without it.

“I’ll leave my shoes,” Tom added.

“What good would that do me?”

“None, sir. But it would prove I mean to pay.”

The conductor’s stern mouth twitched.

“You newspaper fellows are all touched in the head.”

“I’ve only been one for half an hour.”

“That explains why you’re worse than the others.” He jerked his thumb toward the car. “Go on. If the senator throws you out, don’t expect me to slow down.”

Tom entered the Pullman.

Senator August was not difficult to identify, though he did not resemble Tom’s old mental picture of a senator. There was no tall hat, no flowing black coat, no bundle of state papers beneath his arm. He sat beside a window in gray tweeds, reading a magazine. He was erect, broad-chested, and white-haired, with a military mustache and a face at once shrewd and kindly.

Tom paused to recover his breath and arrange his collar. Then he approached.

“Senator August?”

The senator looked up.

“Yes?”

“My name is Thomas Collins. I represent the *Evening Star*.”

This was not exactly false, though Tom felt the phrase wore a coat rather too large for it.

“No interviews,” said the senator, and returned to his magazine.

“I beg your pardon, sir, but I must ask one question.”

“You may ask it. I shall not answer.”

“What was decided at the conference this morning?”

“Nothing for publication.”

“Did the committee agree upon its policy?”

“Nothing for publication.”

“Will you support the currency bill in its present form?”

The senator lowered the magazine.

“Young man, six reporters asked me those questions before luncheon. Three more asked them after luncheon. One followed me to the station, and another sent his card into my compartment. Do you possess any reason why I should answer you when I refused them?”

Tom considered inventing one. A dramatic plea about the public’s right to know might sound well. A hint that another senator had already spoken might draw a denial. But there was something in August’s level gaze that made tricks seem both foolish and cheap.

“No, sir,” Tom said. “I haven’t.”

“Then we understand each other.”

“Yes, sir. But I should like to remain here, if I may.”

“For what purpose?”

“To give you time to change your mind.”

The senator’s eyebrows rose.

“You are persistent.”

“I need to be.”

“I suppose your editor ordered you not to return without a statement.”

“He ordered me to bring one before five.”

“And if you fail?”

“I have no position to lose.”

This interested the senator.

“No position?”

“Not yet. Mr. Pike said he might give me one if I succeeded.”

“Ah! Then this is an examination.”

“A rather stiff one, sir.”

Senator August leaned back.

“How long have you been a reporter?”

“Since about two o’clock.”

The senator laughed outright.

“And you began by boarding a moving train?”

“I nearly began by missing it.”

“Where is your ticket?”

Tom hesitated.

“That is another difficulty.”

Under questioning, he confessed the state of his finances and his bargain—or attempted bargain—with the conductor. Senator August listened gravely, though amusement remained in his eyes.

“My lad,” he said, “either you have remarkable pluck or remarkably little sense.”

“I hope it is partly pluck.”

“So do I. Sit down.”

Tom took the opposite seat.

“I shall not tell you what occurred at the conference,” the senator continued. “The time for a statement has not arrived. Certain gentlemen must be consulted, and no newspaper is to receive an advantage over another.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

“I don’t like it, but I understand it.”

“That is an unusual distinction.”

The senator returned to his magazine. Tom sat quietly.

The train rolled northward through the outskirts of Washington. Rows of brick houses gave way to open ground, and telegraph poles began their measured flight past the window. Somewhere ahead lay Brenton Junction, where Tom would be put off with twenty-seven cents, no news, and a considerable distance between himself and Mr. Pike.

He might make one more appeal. Yet the experienced reporters had appealed, questioned, hinted, and doubtless threatened. His only advantage, if advantage it was, lay in not becoming a nuisance. He watched the fields and tried to think.

The political situation was plain enough. Senator August wished to keep silent until the party leaders had been notified. But silence itself was producing rumors. By evening, the opposition papers might declare that the conference had broken in anger. Markets could react; party men could take sides; tomorrow’s explanations might arrive too late.

If only something forced the senator to speak now!

That was wishing for a miracle, and newspapers could not be run upon miracles. Still, Tom had done all that was possible. He had found his man. He had gained access to him. He was sitting six feet from the secret. Unless—unless fortune favored him, the assignment was hopeless.

The forward door opened, and a trainman entered carrying a yellow envelope.

“Senator August?”

The senator accepted the telegram, tore it open, and read.

His expression changed. The amusement disappeared; two firm lines showed beside his mouth. He read the message again, then glanced at his watch.

“How long before Brenton?” he asked.

“Eight minutes, Senator.”

“Can a telegram be sent from there?”

“Yes, sir. We stop four minutes.”

The trainman went out.

Senator August stared for a moment through the window. Then he turned to Tom.

“You know shorthand?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Have you a notebook?”

Tom had one, together with a pencil worn almost to the metal. He produced them in an instant.

“This message,” said the senator, tapping the telegram, “is from the national chairman. An unauthorized report is circulating in New York that our conference ended in disagreement and that I have resigned from the committee. The report is already affecting two congressional contests. The chairman advises an immediate denial.”

Tom’s fingers tightened around the pencil.

“May I quote you?”

“You may take an official statement. It must be exact.”

“Yes, sir.”

The senator dictated in a measured voice:

“The conference held in Washington this morning by members of the Democratic Congressional Committee reached a complete understanding concerning the currency proposal now before the Senate. Reports of dissension are without foundation. While certain amendments will be recommended, the essential principles of the measure have the support of all persons attending the conference. I have not resigned, nor have I considered resigning, from the committee. A fuller statement will be transmitted tonight to the Associated Press for release tomorrow morning.”

He paused.

“Read that.”

Tom read it aloud. His voice shook on the first sentence and steadied on the second.

“Correct,” said the senator. “Add this: ‘The party enters the approaching campaign united upon the necessity of sound reform and confident of public support.’”

Tom added it.

“Now read the entire statement once more.”

He did so.

Senator August nodded and signed the bottom of the notebook page with a fountain pen.

“You have your news.”

Tom scarcely trusted himself to speak.

“Senator, I—thank you.”

“Thank the national chairman. And perhaps thank that unfortunate habit of yours of boarding trains without tickets.”

The whistle sounded. Houses and sidings appeared outside.

“Brenton,” said the senator. “You had better leave here and telegraph your paper.”

Tom’s delight stumbled over a practical obstacle.

“With twenty-seven cents?”

The senator smiled, drew out a leather pocketbook, and handed him a five-dollar bill.

“I can’t take that, sir.”

“You can repay it when the *Star* pays you.”

“But—”

“The train will not debate the matter. Neither shall I.”

Tom accepted the bill.

“I’ll return every cent tomorrow.”

“I believe you will. One thing more. This statement is yours until eight o’clock tonight. At eight I shall send it to the Associated Press. I cannot properly withhold it longer.”

“That gives us an exclusive.”

“It gives you a bare fighting chance. What you do with it is your affair.”

The brakes ground beneath them. Tom seized his hat and moved toward the door, then turned.

“Senator, when I introduced myself, I said I represented the *Star*. I ought to tell you that Mr. Pike had not hired me.”

“I understood that.”

“Then why did you trust me?”

Senator August folded the telegram.

“Because you told me you had not been hired. A young reporter who is honest when dishonesty might help him is worth trusting with a statement that must be printed accurately. Now go.”

Tom went.

At Brenton Junction he paid the conductor, sent a brief telegram to Mr. Pike—HAVE SIGNED OFFICIAL STATEMENT FROM AUGUST. HOLD SPACE. RETURNING FIRST TRAIN—and discovered that the first train to Washington would not arrive for thirty-five minutes.

Thirty-five minutes!

It was then twenty-eight minutes past three. The local was due in Washington at four-forty-seven, if it kept its schedule. The *Star’s* final regular edition went to press at five.

Tom walked the platform until the stationmaster asked him to stop wearing out the company’s boards. When the local finally appeared, he boarded before it had fully stopped and spent the journey comparing his shorthand notes with the signed statement, though there could be no doubt of the words. He wrote an introduction on copy paper borrowed from the conductor:

“Party unity on the currency question was achieved today at the secret conference of Democratic leaders, according to an exclusive statement made this afternoon to the *Evening Star* by Senator Jonathan August.”

He wrote rapidly, crossed out weak phrases, and wrote again. The train seemed to pause at every farmhouse between Brenton and Washington. At one village it waited while a baggage man rolled three milk cans across the track. Tom regarded those cans with personal hatred.

At last the Capitol dome came into view.

The train entered the station at four-fifty-one—four minutes late. Tom jumped down and ran. This time he had money for a carriage, but none stood immediately at the curb. A streetcar was starting. He caught it, endured two blocks, then sprang off where traffic delayed it and covered the remaining distance on foot.

The office clock said three minutes past five when he burst through the city room gate.

Mr. Pike sat beneath a cloud of pipe smoke. He did not look up.

“You’re late.”

“I have the statement.”

“You’re late,” the editor repeated. “The final plate is on the press.”

Tom’s triumph collapsed. He placed the notebook and his written lead upon the desk.

“It is signed. Senator August says the conference reached complete agreement. They will support the currency bill with amendments. He denies resigning. He won’t give it to the Associated Press until eight tonight.”

Mr. Pike looked up then.

“Until eight?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Who else heard him?”

“No one.”

“Who else has the statement?”

“No one.”

The editor snatched the notebook, read the signed page, and pressed a button.

“Boy!”

The freckled copy boy appeared.

“Take this to the managing editor. Run! Tell him August gives us a three-hour beat and Pike recommends an extra. Then get the copy-cutter.”

The boy vanished.

Mr. Pike seized Tom’s introduction.

“Too many words,” he growled. “The public doesn’t care where you were this afternoon.”

His blue pencil slashed through the sentence.

A man with paste on his sleeves hurried over. Another pulled a telephone toward him and called the composing room. Bells began ringing. Someone shouted, “Hold the front plate!” and from beyond the wall came an answering shout. The newsroom, which had seemed busy before, leaped into a sharper and more magnificent disorder.

Mr. Pike thrust copy paper at Tom.

“Write it. Eight hundred words. First, the result. Second, August’s statement complete. Third, what it means for the bill and the campaign. No autobiography. No adjectives. Make every line earn its type.”

Tom sat at the nearest desk.

His pencil raced. The noise around him ceased to confuse; it drove him forward. Facts arranged themselves in order. The political background he had gathered from weeks of hungry reading now proved useful. He explained the sectional dispute, the danger of a party rupture, and the significance of August’s denial. A copy boy snatched away each sheet as it was finished and carried it to the copy-cutter, who divided it with the enormous shears and sent the slips by pneumatic tube to the composing room.

“Last sheet!” Mr. Pike barked.

Tom wrote the final sentence, read it once, and handed it over.

The editor glanced through it.

“Senator say ‘complete agreement’?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Those exact words?”

“The exact phrase was ‘reached a complete understanding.’”

“Then don’t improve senators. Change it.”

Tom changed it.

Mr. Pike shoved the page at the boy and relit his pipe. For several minutes he said nothing. From below came the gathering thunder of presses beginning a new run.

Tom stood beside the desk, exhausted, dusty, and suddenly conscious that he had eaten nothing since morning.

“Well?” Mr. Pike demanded.

“Sir?”

“Why are you hanging about?”

Tom swallowed.

“I thought—you said if I brought the story—”

“I said I might let you come back tomorrow.”

“Yes, sir.”

Mr. Pike took up his blue pencil and frowned at it as though it had disappointed him.

“Eight o’clock.”

“In the evening?”

“In the morning, Collins. Reporters generally begin before the news is over.”

Tom could only stare.

“Do you mean I’m hired?”

“No,” said Mr. Pike severely. “I mean you are to report here at eight, occupy a desk, take assignments, and draw a salary every Saturday. If you choose to call that being hired, I haven’t time to argue.”

“I’ll be here.”

“You’d better be. And bring back Senator August’s five dollars. The managing editor advanced it when your telegram arrived. We don’t permit our reporters to borrow from their news sources.”

“Our reporters,” Tom repeated.

Mr. Pike’s glare returned.

“Get out and buy yourself a dinner before you faint into the paste pot.”

Tom picked up the five-dollar bill the editor pushed toward him. At the gate, the freckled copy boy met him with the first damp copy of the extra.

Across the top of the page, in black type larger than any words Tom had ever seen connected with his own labor, ran the headline:

**AUGUST DECLARES PARTY UNITED**  
**SECRET CONFERENCE BACKS CURRENCY MEASURE**  
**SPECIAL STATEMENT TO THE EVENING STAR**

Tom stood for a moment holding the warm paper.

Below, the presses roared on, shaking the floor beneath his feet. Newsboys were already crying the extra in the street. The sound came faintly through the windows, rose above the clatter of the city room, and mingled with the steady thunder from below.

At eight o’clock the next morning—indeed, at twenty minutes before eight—Tom Collins reported for work.

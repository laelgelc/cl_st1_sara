# Molly Pitcher

When Mary Ludwig was a little girl, long before the guns of the Revolution had thundered across New Jersey and Pennsylvania, she had one ambition which caused her good mother no small amount of anxiety and her young companions a great deal of merriment.

“I shall be a soldier!” she would declare, standing very straight, with a broom over one shoulder and a battered pewter plate upon her red head by way of helmet. “I shall march with General Washington and fight for my country.”

At this the boys laughed, for in those days it was thought strange enough that any girl should wish to handle a musket, sleep upon the hard ground, or go hungry on a winter’s march. Yet Mary—whom everybody called Molly—did not mind their laughter. She was not a timid child. Her hair was red, her eyes were blue, her cheeks were brown and freckled from sun and wind, and her arms, even in girlhood, were stronger than those of many a lad who boasted loudly of what he meant to do when he became a man.

If her mother placed sewing in her hands, Molly sighed over it, broke the thread, lost the needle, and looked longingly through the window toward the road; but if there was bread to knead, a kettle to lift, a floor to scour, a bundle of wood to carry, or a stubborn calf to lead home from the meadow, she made no complaint. She worked vigorously and cheerfully. She scrubbed and scoured and baked and brewed and spun and washed; she polished the door-knobs until they shone, carried sacks of grain which made other girls gasp, and could hang a week’s washing upon the line before a slower person had shaken out the first sheet.

There was nothing delicate or idle about her. She loved a clean house, a wholesome meal, an honest jest, and a task worth doing. These qualities, though they may seem of little importance beside cannon smoke and charging battalions, were the very qualities which afterward made her useful in the hour of danger. Heroism seldom springs full-grown from a single thrilling moment. It is built, day by day, out of duties faithfully performed.

Molly listened eagerly whenever travelers brought tidings of the quarrels between the Colonies and King George III. She knew little of courts and ministers, taxes and charters, but she understood the plain matter beneath them all: the American people believed they possessed certain rights, and they meant to defend those rights. Her heart was with them from the beginning.

As she grew older, she entered domestic service, as many capable young women did, and for a time worked in a prosperous household. Here she found no lack of occupation. There were fires to kindle before dawn, dough to set, pewter to brighten, vegetables to prepare, linen to wash, and a hundred errands which seemed to increase rather than diminish as the day advanced.

On one occasion Molly, being called away while preparing dinner, suspended a dish of potatoes from a hook above the kitchen table, intending thereby to keep it from a hungry dog. A visitor, entering without heed, struck the cord, and down came dish, potatoes, and all, with such a clatter that the whole household ran to discover the cause. Molly stood amid the rolling potatoes, one hand upon her hip and laughter in her eyes.

“Well,” said she, “they were to be mashed in any event!”

It was difficult to remain angry with one who could meet even misfortune so cheerfully.

Yet beneath this humor there was a serious and steadfast nature. Molly had little patience with vanity and none at all with cowardice. She could endure fatigue, speak her mind, and act without waiting for others when action was required. One can fancy that more than one comfortable person, watching the strong serving-maid at her labor, thought her useful but ordinary. History was to judge otherwise.

Among the young men whom Molly knew was John Hays, a barber by trade, though ready, like many another colonial craftsman, to exchange his peaceful tools for the weapons of liberty. He was brave, industrious, and kindly, and he admired Molly not because she pretended to helplessness, but because she possessed both a warm heart and capable hands.

Their affection grew in the simple fashion of those days. They walked together when work permitted, spoke of the troubled state of the country, and made their plans with more hope than money. In time they were married, and Molly Ludwig became Mrs. John Hays.

Marriage did not transform her into a silent or delicate person. She remained the same energetic Molly, quick to laugh, quick to work, and quicker still to resent injustice. Her devotion to her husband was deep, but it was not the devotion of weakness leaning upon strength. Rather, they stood side by side, each ready to bear a share of whatever burden Providence might send.

That burden was not long delayed.

The quarrel with Great Britain had become war. Blood had been shed at Lexington and Concord; the Declaration of Independence had been signed; General George Washington commanded an army which was often ragged, unpaid, hungry, and discouraged, but which would not consent to surrender the liberties of a new nation. Men left farms, shops, and families to join the Continental forces, and John Hays could not remain behind.

One evening he entered their home with a grave face. Molly, who was kneading bread, looked up at once.

“You have tidings,” she said.

“I have joined the army,” answered John. “I am to serve with the artillery.”

There are women who would have wept, protested, or urged delay. Molly’s hands became still upon the dough, and for a moment her face was pale; but she knew what the decision had cost him, and she would not make duty harder.

“You have done rightly,” she said. “If our country needs you, you must go. And wherever I may be, John Hays, remember that I stand by you.”

It was a promise which she kept in a fashion more literal and heroic than either could then have imagined.

After her husband’s departure, Molly remained for a time near members of her family in New Jersey. There she heard the alarming reports that swept from village to village—of British regiments advancing in splendid order, of Hessian troops in high caps, of American defeats, retreats, and sudden victories. News traveled slowly, often by frightened riders or weary soldiers, and rumor magnified every danger. One day Washington’s army was said to be destroyed; the next, the British were reported in flight.

The winter of 1776 and 1777 was filled with events upon which the fate of independence appeared to hang. General Washington, after crossing the Delaware on Christmas night, struck the Hessians at Trenton; then, threatened by Lord Cornwallis, he moved with daring skill around the British forces and marched toward Princeton.

On January 3, 1777, the Battle of Princeton was fought. The morning was cold, and the frozen roads rang beneath the feet of marching men. Brigadier General Hugh Mercer met British troops under Lieutenant Colonel Charles Mawhood, and a fierce struggle followed. Mercer fell wounded; the American line wavered; then Washington himself rode forward, rallied his soldiers, and turned peril into victory.

But a battlefield after the shouting has died away is no place of glory. The wounded lay upon the hard earth; some called for water, some for their mothers, and some made no sound at all. Blood darkened the frozen ground. Muskets, torn knapsacks, broken ramrods, hats, and cartridge boxes were scattered where men had fallen.

Molly did not remain safely indoors when help was needed. With other women she hurried to aid the sufferers, carrying water, cloth, food, and such simple remedies as were available. She knelt beside friend and stranger alike. She washed powder and blood from faces, bound wounds, held cups to trembling lips, and spoke in that firm, cheerful voice which could give courage without false promises.

“Easy now,” she told one young soldier whose arm had been badly injured. “You have done your part. Let us do ours.”

He tried to smile.

“Are you an army surgeon?” he whispered.

“No,” said Molly, tightening the bandage, “but I have mended many a torn thing in my time, and I do not mean to lose you for want of trying.”

In the confusion following the engagement, word came that British troops were approaching. An American cannon had been abandoned near the road, its crew gone and its ammunition not entirely spent. To leave it usable was to offer aid to the enemy; to remove it seemed impossible, for no team was at hand, and every able-bodied man nearby was occupied with the wounded.

Molly looked from the gun to the road.

She had watched artillerymen before. She knew more of their labor than anyone supposed, for John’s service had sharpened her interest. A cannon did not appear to her a mysterious monster. It was a heavy, dangerous machine, certainly, but a machine governed by order—sponge, load, ram, aim, fire.

“Help me turn it!” she cried.

The women and wounded men stared.

“Mistress Hays,” said one soldier, “the British may be upon us at any moment.”

“For that very reason,” she answered, “we must make them think twice before coming on.”

She made no further ado. Gathering what powder and shot remained, she prepared the gun as well as haste allowed. Her rough skirt impeded her movements, and she caught it up and fastened it out of the way. Her red hair escaped its covering and blew about her face. There was nothing of parade-ground elegance in her appearance; nevertheless, few soldiers ever went to their post with a stouter heart.

The enemy advance guard appeared at a distance.

“Stand clear!” Molly commanded.

She touched fire to the charge.

The cannon leaped and roared. Smoke rolled across the road, and the unexpected ball struck near the advancing troops. They halted, astonished that artillery still guarded the position. Molly reloaded. Again the gun thundered. The British, unable to tell whether a battery or a single piece opposed them, paused and formed cautiously, thus giving the Americans precious time to remove wounded men and necessary stores.

One injured soldier lay too weak to rise. Those about him had gone, believing him dead, but Molly saw his hand move.

“He lives!” she exclaimed.

The British were nearer now. The sensible course, so many would have said, was to save herself. Molly did not reason in that fashion. She bent, took the wounded man across her strong shoulders, and rose beneath his weight. The burden was heavy, the road rough, and cannon smoke stung her eyes; still she carried him, step after step, until friendly hands met her and bore him onward.

The rescued man lived to remember the red-haired woman who had come back when others believed the field deserted.

This deed alone might have won Molly an honored place in local memory, but the great hour of her life was still before her.

As the war continued, she sought every opportunity to be near her husband and to aid the army. The wives who followed Continental camps did not lead easy lives. They washed clothing in icy streams, cooked over smoky fires, nursed sick men, mended uniforms, gathered fuel, and sometimes marched through mud in garments soaked to the knee. They shared scanty rations and poorer shelter, endured military alarms, and received little of the praise granted to those who carried muskets.

Molly performed such labor without complaint. If there was food, she prepared it; if there was none, she tightened her belt like the soldiers. She washed John’s powder-blackened garments, listened to his accounts of artillery practice, and learned the names and uses of the implements about a cannon. The sponge staff, rammer, handspike, worm, linstock, cartridge, wad, and shot became familiar to her. She watched how the men served the piece, how each depended upon the others, and how one moment of carelessness could destroy an entire crew.

Sometimes, to amuse the men, she would seize a stick and imitate the commands.

“Load! Ram home! Stand away! Fire!”

The soldiers laughed and applauded.

“You will command a battery yet, Molly!” one called.

“Then mind your drill,” she retorted, “or I shall have you all polishing harness until midnight.”

They laughed again, but there were a few who noticed that she gave the commands in the proper order.

By June of 1778, the British had determined to withdraw from Philadelphia, which they had occupied during the previous winter, and to march across New Jersey toward New York. Their army, commanded by Sir Henry Clinton, carried a vast train of wagons and supplies. General Washington followed from Valley Forge, where suffering and discipline had transformed his army. The Americans were stronger now, better trained under Baron von Steuben, and eager to prove themselves in open battle.

The British column moved slowly through heat and dust. Washington sent forces ahead to harass it, while his council debated the wisdom of a general engagement. Major General Charles Lee, who had doubts about attacking, was placed in command of an advance force. On June 28, near Monmouth Court House, the armies met.

That day became one of the hottest in the memory of those who survived it. The sun rose fierce and white; no breeze stirred the leaves; dust hung over the roads and fields like smoke. Men in woolen uniforms, carrying muskets, cartridge boxes, blankets, and equipment, staggered under the heat. Horses foamed and fell. Soldiers collapsed without receiving a wound. The very air seemed an enemy.

John Hays served at one of the American cannon, and Molly was nearby with the other camp women. At first she helped with the wounded, but soon another need became urgent.

“Water!” cried a soldier. “For Heaven’s sake, water!”

“The guns must be cooled!” shouted an artilleryman. “Bring water, or we cannot continue firing!”

A spring lay at some distance from the battery. Molly seized a pitcher and ran.

She filled it, hurried back, and gave drink to the gasping men. What remained she poured where the artillery crew directed. Then she turned and ran again.

The sun beat upon her uncovered head. Dust coated her shoes, her skirt, and her throat. Musket balls hissed through the grass; round shot tore the earth; shells burst with dreadful violence. Men shouted for ammunition, for officers, for surgeons, and above every human voice rolled the ceaseless thunder of the guns.

Molly continued.

To the spring. Back to the battery.

To the spring. Back to the wounded.

A drink for a boy whose lips were cracked with thirst; water for the swab which must cleanse and cool a smoking cannon; another journey, another pitcher, another breathless return.

At first the men called, “Water, Molly!”

Then one, seeing her approach through the smoke with the familiar vessel in her hand, raised a glad shout:

“Here comes Molly with her pitcher!”

“Molly with the pitcher!” another echoed.

“Molly Pitcher!”

The cry passed along the line, and a name was born which was to outlive many a title more formally bestowed.

“Make way for Molly Pitcher!” the soldiers called.

She laughed as she passed, though her heart was pounding and her arms ached.

“If you can shout so loudly,” she answered, “you are not yet dying of thirst!”

Meanwhile the battle had taken a perilous course. General Lee’s advance troops, meeting unexpected resistance and receiving confused orders, began to retreat. General Washington rode forward and encountered the retiring columns. His anger was terrible, but his presence restored order. He rebuked Lee, rallied the men, and established a new line upon strong ground. General Nathanael Greene held the right, Lord Stirling the left, and the American artillery was placed where it could strike the enemy with effect.

The British attacked fiercely. Their grenadiers and guardsmen advanced with steadiness, while their cannon answered the American guns. The heat grew more deadly. Men fell at their posts, yet the Continental line held.

John Hays worked like one possessed. He helped load, sponge, and aim his piece, every discharge sending flame from its mouth and driving it backward upon its wheels. His face was black with powder; sweat ran from beneath his hat; his breath came harshly.

Molly reached him with water.

“Drink, John!” she cried.

He took one swallow and shook his head.

“The gun first.”

Together they poured water where it was needed. Then he returned to his place.

“Take care of yourself,” Molly urged.

John smiled wearily.

“Did you not promise to stand by me?”

“Aye,” she replied, “and so I shall.”

She turned toward the spring once more, but before she had gone many yards she heard a cry behind her. Looking back, she saw John sway. His hand slipped from the rammer. He attempted to steady himself against the wheel, failed, and fell beside the cannon.

Molly dropped the pitcher and ran to him.

“John!”

For one dreadful instant she believed him dead; but his heart still beat, and she saw no wound. The heat had struck him down.

Two men dragged him clear of the recoiling gun. Molly knelt, lifted his head, and dashed the last water from the pitcher upon his face.

The artillery officer glanced toward the fallen man and then toward the depleted crew. A cannon without enough hands to serve it was nearly useless, and the British were pressing forward.

“Remove the piece!” came the order. “We cannot work it!”

Molly stood.

“No!” she cried.

The officer turned sharply.

“No?”

“My husband’s post is vacant,” she said. “I will take it.”

“This is no work for a woman.”

“It is work for whoever can do it.”

A ball struck the earth nearby and flung dust over them. The gun crew waited, torn between astonishment and urgency.

“I know the drill,” Molly continued. “I know every motion. Let me stand in John’s place.”

The officer looked at her strong arms, her fearless face, and the advancing scarlet line. There was no time for argument.

“Take the rammer, then!” he ordered. “Serve the gun!”

Molly sprang to the post.

All her life had prepared her for that moment—the sacks of grain lifted in girlhood, the kettles carried, the floors scrubbed, the miles walked, the wounded man borne from danger, the camp labor, the watchful hours beside John and his fellow artillerymen. What others had regarded as common strength became, in an instant, a weapon for liberty.

“Worm the piece!” came the command.

The barrel was cleared.

“Sponge!”

Molly thrust the dampened sponge into the smoking cannon, turned it, and withdrew it.

“Load!”

The cartridge was placed in the muzzle. Molly reversed the staff and rammed the charge home with firm, practiced strokes. Wad and ball followed.

“Run her up!”

The crew heaved at the wheels and handspikes.

“Prime!”

The vent was prepared. The gun was aimed.

“Stand clear!”

Molly stepped aside.

“Fire!”

The cannon roared.

It recoiled violently, but Molly was already moving. Sponge, load, ram, run up, aim, fire! There was no hesitation now. Her skirt was torn, her face streaked with sweat and powder, her red hair loose upon her shoulders. She looked neither like the tidy maid who had polished door-knobs nor like the fanciful child who had worn a pewter plate for a helmet. She looked, in truth, like a soldier.

The men shouted encouragement.

“Well done, Molly!”

“Give them another!”

“Stand by the gun!”

Stand by the gun! The words contained the whole history of her brave heart. She had stood by her family in labor, by her husband in war, by the wounded at Princeton, by the thirsty men beneath the pitiless sun, and now she stood by the cannon when a weaker spirit would have fled.

The enemy returned the fire. Shot whistled overhead. One ball passed so close beneath Molly that it tore away part of her clothing and flung dust against her legs. The soldiers stared in horror, expecting to see her fall.

Molly glanced down, saw that she was unhurt, and laughed.

“Well,” she cried, “that might have been worse!”

Then she rammed home another charge.

Her courage ran through the crew like strong wine. Exhausted men straightened; frightened ones forgot their fear. It was impossible to complain of heat or danger while Molly Pitcher worked beside them without faltering.

The cannon spoke again and again. Its balls tore gaps in the British formation, checked their advance, and helped secure the American line. Elsewhere, General Greene’s artillery enfiladed the enemy with deadly effect, while Washington directed the battle with calm majesty. The British assaulted, recoiled, and formed anew; the Americans held their ground.

History tells us that the Battle of Monmouth lasted through the blazing day and into the evening. It was not a simple victory won by one person or one gun. Thousands fought there; many displayed great valor; scores died from wounds or heat. Yet national struggles are made memorable to the human heart by individual deeds, and among all the brave figures upon that field none was more striking than the powder-stained woman who had taken her husband’s place at the cannon.

Toward sunset, the firing slackened. The British withdrew to a strong position, and during the night Sir Henry Clinton resumed his march toward Sandy Hook. Washington’s army remained upon the field. The Continentals had met some of Britain’s finest troops in open battle and had not been driven away.

Only when the last danger was past did Molly leave the gun.

Her hands were blistered and black. Her dress was scorched and torn. Every muscle ached, and she could scarcely lift the pitcher which had earlier seemed so light. Yet her first thought was of John.

She found him beneath such shelter as could be provided, watched over by a surgeon’s assistant. His eyes opened as she approached.

“Molly,” he murmured, “the gun—”

“The gun is safe,” she said.

“Who served it?”

She knelt beside him, and despite her weariness, a mischievous smile touched her face.

“I did.”

John stared, then gave a faint laugh of mingled wonder and pride.

“I should have known,” he whispered. “You said you would stand by me.”

General Nathanael Greene heard of the courageous woman at the artillery piece, and the story passed swiftly from officer to officer. Men repeated how she had carried water throughout the hottest hours, how she had taken the rammer when her husband fell, and how a British ball had passed within inches of her without driving her from her duty.

Some tales grow larger in the telling, but Molly’s deed needed no ornament. The plain truth was splendid enough.

The following day she was summoned before General George Washington.

One can fancy that, for the first time in many hours, Molly became conscious of her appearance. She had no fine uniform to put on, no polished boots, no officer’s sash. Her best garments were stained by camp life; her hands bore the marks of work; her face showed fatigue. Yet she went without shame, for honest service is a better adornment than silk or jewels.

Washington stood among his officers, dignified and grave. The burden of the whole Revolution seemed at times to rest upon his shoulders, but he never failed to recognize courage in others. The Marquis de Lafayette was said to be among those who heard of her exploit, and soldiers gathered at a respectful distance, eager to see what honor might be given their Molly Pitcher.

“Mistress Hays,” said Washington, “I am informed that during yesterday’s engagement you carried water under fire, aided the wounded, and, when your husband was overcome, took his place at an artillery piece.”

“I did what was needed, General,” Molly answered.

“You remained although the position was dangerous?”

“The men remained, sir.”

“And you believed yourself no less bound to duty?”

Molly raised her head.

“I am an American.”

No long speech could have said more.

Washington’s expression softened.

“Your country has cause to be proud of you.”

He conferred upon her the honorary rank of captain, and from that time many soldiers called her “Captain Molly.” It was also recommended that she receive military half-pay for life, an acknowledgment unusual indeed for a woman of her station. The troops cheered her in no measured terms, and Molly, who had faced cannon fire without trembling, found herself embarrassed by applause.

“Three cheers for Captain Molly!”

The sound rolled across the camp.

She looked toward John, who, though still weak, watched with shining eyes.

“Three cheers for Molly Pitcher!”

Hats flew into the air, and the cheers rang out once more.

The war continued for several years. There were other marches, other hardships, other fateful nights before Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown and independence was secured. Molly did not command armies, make treaties, or sit in councils of state. Such matters are of less importance to us than the fact that wherever duty placed her, she served faithfully.

When peace returned, she and John went back to Carlisle, Pennsylvania. The days of roaring cannon gave way to common labor. There were meals to prepare, rooms to clean, clothes to mend, neighbors to aid, and the countless humble concerns of ordinary life. Molly lived without grandeur. Those who met her in later years may have seen merely a vigorous woman with bright eyes and ready speech. Yet veterans knew her, and when stories of Monmouth were told, her name never failed to awaken pride.

Children gathered to hear how she had run through shot and smoke with her pitcher, how she had laughed when the ball tore her garments, and how General Washington had named her captain. Perhaps some details changed as gray-haired soldiers repeated them beside winter fires, but the heart of the story remained: when men were thirsty, Molly carried water; when the wounded cried, she gave aid; when her husband fell, she took his place; when her country needed courage, she supplied it.

Her greatness did not arise from a love of violence. She sought no quarrel and desired no suffering. The cannon she served was terrible, as all instruments of war are terrible; but the liberty for which that cannon spoke was precious, and she would not allow others to defend it alone.

Therein lies the noblest lesson of her life.

It would have been easy for Molly to say, “This is not my concern.” It would have been customary for her to say, “I am only a woman.” It would have been natural, beneath the burning sun and flying shot, to say, “I have done enough.” She said none of these things. She saw a duty and performed it.

Ours it is to admire not merely the dramatic hour when she fired the gun, but the years of faithful labor which made that hour possible. The girl who carried grain became the woman who carried a wounded soldier. The maid who lifted kettles became the patriot who lifted the rammer. The wife who promised to stand by her husband stood also by her army and her country. Her domestic virtues were not cast aside upon the battlefield; they were enlarged. Industry became endurance, loyalty became valor, and helpfulness became heroism.

Long after generals and politicians of greater rank have faded from popular memory, the name of Molly Pitcher remains. It belongs partly to history and partly to legend, as do the names of many beloved national heroes; but behind the familiar title stands Mary Ludwig Hays, a real woman of strong hands, a cheerful spirit, and an unconquerable heart.

Each generation receives its own summons. Not all are called to serve a cannon, and blessed is the land whose women need never stand amid battle smoke; yet courage is required in peace as surely as in war. There are wounds to bind, burdens to carry, frightened hearts to strengthen, and liberties to guard. The pitcher may become a book, a bandage, a tool, a vote, a plow, or a lamp kept burning through a dark night; but the spirit which carries it must remain the same.

When the hour of trial comes, ours is not to ask whether duty is pleasant, fashionable, or easy. Ours is to stand by the right.

So Molly stood.

And because she stood, because she ran when others fainted, lifted when others despaired, and fired when others would have withdrawn the gun, America remembers her—not as a queen in velvet, nor as a lady protected from hardship, but as she appeared on that memorable June day in 1778: red hair flying, blue eyes bright through powder smoke, strong hands upon the rammer, and the voice of the army ringing joyfully above the thunder—

“Here comes Molly with her pitcher!”

“Make way for Molly Pitcher!”

“Stand by the gun!”

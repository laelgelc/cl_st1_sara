On the morning of the first of April, Julius Barrett rose with a soul athirst for deception.

By eight o’clock he had told his mother that the kitchen chimney was smoking, his little sister that the schoolhouse had burned down, and old Mrs. Pender that her cow was loose in the turnip patch. His mother had not looked up from the bread she was kneading. His sister had reminded him that there was no schoolhouse because it was Saturday. Mrs. Pender had said calmly that Brindle was tied with a new rope and could not get loose without the help of Providence or Julius himself.

At nine he changed the sugar and salt bowls. His father salted his coffee, swallowed one mouthful, and merely observed that boys who tampered with breakfast sometimes found an unexpected flavour in their own dinner.

At ten Julius announced that a bear had been seen behind the forge. Nobody went to look.

The trouble was that Valley View knew Julius. He had enjoyed great success three years earlier, when his face was innocent and his imagination modest, but repeated triumphs had ruined him. A boy cannot cry wolf, fire, flood, mad dog, runaway horse, and escaped circus elephant in one small neighbourhood without impairing his credit.

By noon Julius was desperate.

Then he saw Dan Chester coming up the red road with a bag of nails under one arm, and an inspiration of uncommon wickedness entered his mind.

Dan was seventeen, grave as a deacon, and so strictly truthful that people said he would correct a compliment if it exceeded the facts. If Dan could be made to believe a tale, the tale would travel wearing the best possible character.

Julius leaned over the gate.

“Say, Danny, heard the news?”

Dan stopped. “What news?”

“You haven’t heard? Well, I s’pose they’re keeping it quiet.”

“Keeping what quiet?”

Julius glanced toward the Barrett kitchen window and lowered his voice.

“Thomas Everett and Adelia Williams are going to be married.”

Dan stared at him.

“Git along.”

“True as I’m standing here.”

“It’s April Fool’s Day.”

“I know it. That’s why I didn’t tell you before. You’d think I was fooling.”

Dan shifted the nails. Thomas Everett was forty-six and had been a bachelor so long that Valley View had ceased to regard his condition as temporary. Adelia Williams, who lived with a widowed aunt in the weathered house beyond the mill, was forty or thereabouts and equally unattached. They were acquainted, naturally; everybody in Valley View was acquainted. But nobody had ever detected in them so much as a shared umbrella.

“Who told you?” Dan demanded.

“Ma heard it from Cousin Eliza, and Cousin Eliza had it from Mrs. Everett’s sister. They’re not announcing it till next month. Adelia’s piecing a double-wedding-ring quilt already, and Thomas is fixing up his spare room. Wedding’s to be in June.”

This magnificence astonished even Julius. Details rose under his tongue like bubbles in cider.

“Thomas gave her a brooch Tuesday night,” he continued. “Gold, with a purple stone. They’re going to Niagara afterward. I dunno’s I ought to have told.”

Dan’s disbelief wavered. Julius saw it and put on an injured expression.

“If you think I’m lying, you can ask them yourself.”

Dan did not intend to ask them. Such a question would have seemed impertinent. But when he reached the forge, Lem Turner was shoeing a mare, and after a little conversation about nails, roads, and the prospects of rain, Dan said:

“I hear Thomas Everett is to marry Adelia Williams in June.”

Lem stopped hammering.

“You don’t say!”

“I cannot vouch for June,” Dan replied conscientiously, “but I have reason to believe the engagement is settled.”

An hour later Lem told the miller that the wedding day had been fixed. The miller told his wife that Thomas was repairing his house throughout. His wife told the postmistress that Adelia had ordered her dress from the city. The postmistress, having received a parcel of white cotton addressed to Miss Williams, concluded that it must be for the trousseau.

By supper time Valley View knew that the ceremony would be held in the Methodist church, that Adelia’s niece was to stand up with her, that Thomas had bought a new horse, and that the bridal journey would include Montreal as well as Niagara.

Julius heard the improved version on Monday and nearly perished of delight.

At this point Julius and Dan may be dismissed. Having pushed the first pebble, they stood open-mouthed while the avalanche proceeded without them.

Thomas Everett first heard of his engagement at Beasley’s store.

He had gone in for lamp oil, bootlaces, and a pound of tea. Mr. Beasley measured the oil with a festive expression.

“Well, Thomas, I congratulate you.”

“What for?”

“No need to be bashful.”

“I’m not bashful.”

“Just so. A man doesn’t reach your time of life without knowing his own mind.”

Thomas frowned. “What are you talking about?”

Mr. Beasley winked. “Adelia Williams.”

Thomas nearly dropped the oil can.

“What about her?”

“Nothing, nothing. Fine woman. Excellent cook, too, they say.”

“I’ve no concern with how she cooks.”

“Not yet,” said Mr. Beasley.

The three men by the stove laughed. Thomas demanded an explanation and obtained one in fragments, every fragment more ridiculous than the last. He denied the entire affair with such heat that the men exchanged knowing glances.

“Dang it all,” said Thomas, “I haven’t spoken ten words to Adelia Williams since the church picnic.”

“That’s beginning quietly,” observed one of the men.

Thomas carried his purchases away in indignation.

At the mill the miller asked whether he preferred white flour for the wedding cake. At the post office Miss Pringle handed him his letters and said that June was a pretty month for a honeymoon. At the blacksmith’s Lem Turner promised to put a shine on the buggy without charge. By the time Thomas reached home, prospective matrimony had pursued him through five establishments and halfway across the bridge.

He lived alone in a square brown house on the north slope, with a barn, twelve acres of good pasture, and a kitchen which had not been thoroughly subdued since his housekeeper married a travelling seed agent in February. Thomas had believed himself capable of managing.

That evening he found the bread mouldy, the supper half cold, and one of his clean shirts lying behind the woodbox. A grey congregation of dust had assembled along the mop-board. His temper, already wounded by Valley View, gave way.

“Dang it all!”

He fried two eggs, burned one, broke the other, and sat down to tea under the buffalo-horn hatrack in the hall, which he had long considered ornamental and now suddenly perceived to be monstrous.

Adelia Williams, he reflected unwillingly, kept a very tidy house.

He knew this because the church committee had once met in her parlour. The rugs had lain straight, the geraniums had bloomed as if under discipline, and the tea had been hot without being stewed. Adelia herself had gone about with rosy cheeks and a capable manner, neither flustered nor sharp.

Thomas pushed the burnt egg aside.

That did not mean he wanted to marry her.

The next Sunday brought no relief. Mrs. Pender caught him at the church door.

“I’m real pleased, Thomas. Adelia is a fine woman.”

“There’s nothing in it.”

“Of course not,” said Mrs. Pender soothingly. “Not till it’s announced.”

“It isn’t going to be announced.”

“I admire discretion.”

Inside, Adelia sat three pews ahead of him in a dark blue bonnet. Thomas had seen that bonnet twenty times. He had never before noticed that the ribbon matched her eyes. The discovery annoyed him.

After the service, a group of women surrounded Adelia. Her cheeks grew steadily pinker while she denied, protested, and finally escaped toward the Williams buggy.

Thomas watched her go.

She was a pleasant-looking woman. Not slender, perhaps, but Thomas had never understood why a woman should resemble a beanpole. She had a quick step and a firm hand with a nervous horse. She was sensible. She did not giggle. She had once nursed old Mrs. Larkin through pneumonia and had sent Thomas a jar of preserves when he sprained his wrist.

None of these facts was new. Yet they appeared newly polished, as common coins do after long handling.

The rumour meanwhile flourished. Thomas denied it to everyone, and every denial was received as proof of honourable secrecy. If he became angry, people said he was embarrassed. If he laughed, they said he was happy. If he avoided Adelia, they admired his caution. When he happened to meet her at the post office and lifted his hat, Miss Pringle later reported that their conduct had been “most significant.”

Adelia suffered no less. Girls asked her advice about husbands. Married women offered advice without being asked. Aunt Sophia began counting spare sheets and discussing which bedroom caught the morning sun.

“I am not engaged to Thomas Everett,” Adelia said for the twentieth time.

“No, dear,” said Aunt Sophia. “But if you were, you might do worse.”

“That isn’t the point.”

“He owns his farm clear.”

“Aunt Sophia!”

“And he has kind eyes.”

Adelia picked up her knitting with unnecessary firmness.

She knew about his eyes. They were grey, and they crinkled when he laughed. She knew also that he was punctual in paying bills, gentle with animals, and likely to carry a sick neighbour’s firewood after dark without mentioning it. These observations had accumulated over years without troubling her. Now the village held them up one by one until she could not avoid seeing them.

It was perfectly maddening.

For three weeks Thomas and Adelia met often enough to be uncomfortable and seldom enough to remain curious. They exchanged constrained greetings at church, at the store, and once beside the millpond, where Thomas stepped aside to let her pass and then stood watching the blue feather on her hat until she turned the bend.

He went home cross with himself and spent the evening attempting to make biscuits.

The biscuits would have discouraged a woodpecker.

“If Adelia made them,” he thought, “they’d be fit to eat.”

Then he called himself a fool.

In May his cousin Martha, who had moved to Manitoba eight years before, sent him a large parcel. Inside, wrapped in paper and packed with shavings, was a buffalo-horn hatrack with six brass hooks and a small mirror. Thomas gazed at it in horror. It was larger and more aggressive than the buffalo-horn hatrack he already possessed.

A letter lay beneath.

Dear Cousin Thomas,

We were delighted to hear of your approaching marriage. I have always said you needed a good wife, though I hope Miss Williams deserves you. Please accept this little present. Henry says every married hall ought to have a proper hatrack. Give the bride my love, and kiss her once for me.

Thomas read the letter twice.

Then he looked at the hatrack.

There are limits beyond which even a patient bachelor cannot be driven. He had endured jokes, congratulations, inquiries about curtains, and advice regarding the rearing of imaginary children. But two buffalo-horn hatracks under one roof were intolerable.

He put on his best coat.

“If I’m to be treated as a marrying man,” he muttered, “I might as well see whether there’s any chance of becoming one.”

This resolution, though bold in wording, weakened as he crossed the fields toward the Williams place. Thomas was practical, but no man can propose marriage with the same composure he uses in purchasing a plough. By the time he reached the gate, he had decided merely to discuss the rumour. At the porch, he thought he might ask Adelia who had started it. When Aunt Sophia opened the door, he nearly inquired about the weather and fled.

“Adelia’s in the kitchen,” said Aunt Sophia, with an expression of saintly innocence. “You can go right through.”

Adelia stood at the table cutting pastry into strips. She wore a white apron, and there was flour on one cheek. The kitchen was bright, orderly, and fragrant with apple pie.

When she saw Thomas, the pastry cutter slipped from her hand.

“Good afternoon,” he said.

“Good afternoon.”

“Aunt Sophia said I could come through.”

“So I see.”

There followed a silence in which the clock ticked with malicious emphasis.

Thomas cleared his throat.

“I suppose you’ve heard the latest.”

“I have heard so many latest things that you must be particular.”

“My cousin in Manitoba has sent us a wedding present.”

Adelia’s lips twitched. “Us?”

“A hatrack.”

That did it. She sat down suddenly and began to laugh.

Thomas had never heard her laugh freely. It was warm, deep, and irresistible. His own dignity lasted perhaps three seconds. Then he laughed too—at Martha, at Valley View, at the double-wedding-ring quilt which did not exist, at Niagara, Montreal, the Methodist ceremony, and especially at the enormous horned object awaiting a bride who had never been consulted.

They laughed until Adelia wiped her eyes with the corner of her apron.

“Oh, dear,” she said. “A hatrack!”

“I’ve got one already.”

“Then you are doubly prepared for matrimony.”

“I should think matrimony had enough dangers without duplicate hatracks.”

Their laughter faded, leaving the room easy and companionable. Thomas sat opposite her. The apple pie steamed between them.

“Adelia,” he said, “you didn’t start this, did you?”

“Certainly not.”

“I knew you didn’t. Neither did I.”

“I knew that too.”

“Are you—well, are you promised to anybody else?”

She looked at him. The colour returned slowly to her cheeks.

“No.”

Thomas studied his hands.

“This whole business has been foolish.”

“Very foolish.”

“I was angry at first.”

“So was I.”

“But folks kept talking about you. Everywhere I went, it was Adelia this and Adelia that. I got to thinking.”

Adelia lowered her eyes to the pastry strips.

“I got to thinking too,” she admitted.

Thomas took courage.

“I thought what a capable woman you were, and kind, and good-looking, and—well, pleasant to have about. Then I’d go home and find dust an inch deep and supper not worth eating. I don’t mean I want to marry you just because you can keep house.”

“I should hope not.”

“No. Though it’s no fault in a woman.”

“Nor is a clear farm a fault in a man.”

Thomas looked up quickly. Her eyes were twinkling.

“This isn’t a very fancy sort of courting,” he said.

“At our ages, fancy courting might look foolish.”

“I suppose it might. What I’m trying to say is, they talked about it till I began wishing it was true.”

Adelia’s hands became still.

“Did you?”

“I did. And if you could come to wish the same—well, there’s the house and the farm, and I’d do my best by you. I’ve been alone a long time, but lately it has felt more alone than it used to.”

Adelia looked toward the sunny window. Outside, a robin hopped along the fence, cocking its head as if determined not to miss the answer.

“At first,” she said, “I wanted to leave Valley View and never come back. Everyone staring and hinting—it was dreadful. Then I began noticing whether you were in church. And when you weren’t at the store, I wondered where you were. I suppose that was their fault too.”

“Should we disappoint them?”

She smiled. “It seems scarcely charitable.”

Thomas reached across the table and took her flour-dusted hand.

“Then you’ll marry me?”

“Yes, Thomas. Though not in June. Aunt Sophia could not possibly be ready.”

“And Niagara?”

“We shall see.”

“And the double-wedding-ring quilt?”

“I’ve never pieced one in my life.”

“Thank goodness.”

He rose, still holding her hand, and kissed her with more tenderness than skill. Adelia, remembering Cousin Martha’s instructions, permitted the kiss and then, after a moment’s consideration, returned it.

Thus Julius Barrett’s tremendous falsehood became truth, though not in every particular. The wedding took place in September, the journey extended no farther than the county fair, and Adelia wore no purple brooch. Dan Chester never wholly recovered from learning how the story had begun.

As for the second buffalo-horn hatrack, Thomas hung it in the barn, where it served admirably for bridles and reminded him that even the most useless object may, under Providence and village gossip, lead to a useful conclusion.

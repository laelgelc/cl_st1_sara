### Lost in a London Fog

It was during our first winter in London that my sister Kate and I acquired the wisdom which, like most valuable wisdom, comes too late to be useful on the occasion that produces it.

We had been spending an evening with some American friends in Bloomsbury. The evening itself had been harmless—music, weak tea, strong opinions, and a prolonged discussion as to whether Englishmen understood humour. At half-past ten our hostess looked toward the window and remarked, with that cheerful indifference peculiar to persons who are not obliged to go out in the weather,—

“My dears, the fog is becoming rather thick.”

Rather thick! The opposite houses had already vanished; the lamps were yellow blots; and the panes appeared to have been painted with dirty wool.

“We must go at once,” I said.

“We might stay all night,” said Kate, comfortably.

This was impossible. Our hostess had not asked us to stay all night; and although an American woman may be capable of declaring independence in any quarter of the globe, she cannot, with propriety, establish herself uninvited in another woman’s spare bedroom.

Our lodging was in Colville Gardens, Bayswater. The last omnibus had gone, the Underground Railway was declared inconvenient, and our friends’ brougham had been sent to fetch an aunt from the opera. There remained only the hansom cab.

Now I had never liked hansoms. Their two great wheels, their little black boxes, and their drivers perched invisibly behind gave them the appearance of vehicles invented for flight rather than travel. You entered as a respectable Christian, the doors clapped together over your knees, and you were delivered—if delivered at all—like a parcel whose direction had been imperfectly written.

“I shall not go in one,” I announced.

“Then you may walk,” said Kate.

At that instant a gust drove the fog into the hall, extinguished one gas-jet, and revealed the pavement swimming in mud. I entered the hansom.

The horse was white—or had once been white—and seemed, in that brownish vapour, to be the pale spirit of some horse which had died of overwork and returned to protest against cab fares. The driver was a large, red-faced man, muffled to the ears. He received the address with a slow nod.

“Colville Gardens,” I repeated. “Bayswater.”

“All right, mum.”

There is no phrase in the English language that should inspire more immediate suspicion.

Our hostess tucked a rug about us, our host called final directions up through the trap, and we started. The hansom sprang forward; the little doors rattled; the white horse disappeared as far as his head and reappeared as far as his tail; and the fog closed behind us like the sea above a sinking ship.

For the first ten minutes Kate was delighted.

“It is romantic,” she said.

“It is rheumatic,” I replied, drawing the rug over my shoulders. “And if that man knows where he is going, why does he keep pulling the horse first to one side and then to the other?”

“He is avoiding things.”

“What things?”

“The things we cannot see.”

This explanation did not tranquillize me. We rolled on and on, now over stones, now through mud, now turning sharply into a street so narrow that I expected both wheels to be shaved off against the houses. Lamps emerged one by one, stared dimly at us, and went out behind. Once a monstrous shadow rose directly before the horse; Kate clutched my arm, I uttered a cry, and the shadow proved to be an omnibus standing at the curb-stone.

“Did you think it was a murderer?” she asked.

“No. A cathedral.”

At the end of twenty minutes I poked up the little door in the roof.

“Driver!”

There was no answer.

“Driver!”

A muffled sound came from above. It might have been “Yes, mum,” or it might have been a snore.

“Are you sure you know the way?”

“All right, mum.”

“That is not what I asked. Do you know Colville Gardens?”

“Bayswater, mum.”

He shut the trap.

“He is drunk,” I whispered.

“He is sleepy,” said Kate.

“What possible comfort is that? A sleeping cabman is as dangerous as a drunken one, and less sociable.”

We continued. The reins hung down on either side of us, slack as a useless rudder; the driver remained an unseen power above; and our pale horse bore us into unknown London with the solemnity of an animal conducting two victims to a place of sacrifice.

Presently the hansom stopped.

A policeman stood in a circle of mist under a lamp. Our driver climbed down with extreme deliberation and approached him. Their voices came to us in fragments.

“Colville—”

“Which?”

“Gardens.”

“Bayswater?”

“That’s what the ladies says.”

“You’re a long way off.”

I looked at Kate. She raised her eyebrows.

The policeman gave a series of directions involving a turning, a public-house, a church, a square, and something which sounded like “keep bearing to the left.” Our cabman listened with his mouth open.

“Understand?” asked the policeman.

“Blest if I do!”

“Then follow the road till you come to—”

The rest was swallowed by the fog. The driver returned, climbed to his perch, and shook the reins.

“What did he say?” I called.

“All right, mum.”

We moved off in the direction from which we had come.

It was then eleven o’clock, or near it. I knew this because a distant clock struck, each note arriving damp and solitary, as though midnight itself had lost its way. The streets grew emptier. Shops were shuttered; public-houses spilled out their last noisy companies and closed; even the policemen became rare. Every now and then a figure passed us, enlarged by the mist to gigantic proportions and then instantly reduced to a very ordinary man carrying a basket.

My foreboding fear became articulate.

“Kate,” I said, “we are being abducted.”

“Nonsense.”

“Why nonsense? He does not know London. He does not know Bayswater. He may not even know that we are in the cab.”

“He knows when he is paid.”

“Ah! That is the point. Suppose he intends to demand twenty pounds?”

“We have not twenty pounds.”

“Then he will murder us from disappointment.”

Kate laughed. I never understood the peculiar constitution of my sister’s courage. A real danger could alarm her; but once I had enlarged it to its natural dramatic proportions she became offensively merry.

After another quarter of an hour, the cabman stopped beneath a lamp and descended. He came round to the front, opened one door, and looked in upon us with the grave surprise of a man finding ladies where he had expected luggage.

“Beg pardon, mum,” he said; “but where was it you was a-goin’?”

“Colville Gardens!” I cried. “You have been told six times.”

“Ah,” he said, thoughtfully. “Colville Gardens.”

“Do you know where it is?”

He scratched his head.

“Well, mum, I ain’t long at the cabbin’. Come up from Sussex Tuesday.”

Kate turned her face toward the side of the hansom. Her shoulders began to shake.

“Are you laughing?” I demanded.

“No,” she said faintly. “I am dying.”

The driver leaned confidentially nearer. His breath removed any lingering doubt as to how he had occupied part of the evening.

“I knows London mostly,” he said. “Only this ’ere fog puts a man out. You ladies sit quiet. I’ll find it.”

He closed the door and resumed his perch.

“Sit quiet!” I exclaimed. “What else does he suppose we can do? Shall we leap from the vehicle, harness ourselves to the horse, and navigate by the stars?”

“There are no stars,” Kate observed.

This was true, and depressed me further.

Our condition by this time was not elegant. The fog had entered everywhere. It hung in drops upon Kate’s curls, which had lost their arrangement and lay mournfully about her face. One glove had split at the thumb; her bonnet was awry; and a splash of mud decorated her cheek. She sat with her hands clasped and her eyes half closed, the image of a dilapidated Ophelia who had selected a hansom instead of a brook.

“You look dreadful,” I told her.

“So do you.”

“I cannot. My bonnet is plain.”

Shortly afterward she fell asleep.

I shall never forgive her for that sleep. There I sat, sustaining the combined responsibility of our youth, our sex, our nation, and our luggage—for we had a small parcel of music somewhere beneath our feet—while she slumbered against my shoulder and breathed with enviable regularity. I alone watched the phantom horse trot onward through endless streets. I alone listened to the wheels grind, splash, and grind again. I alone imagined tomorrow’s newspapers:

MYSTERIOUS DISAPPEARANCE OF TWO AMERICAN LADIES.

Believed to Have Entered a Hansom.

No Trace of Cabman, Horse, or Fare.

I rehearsed the information I should give the police if we survived. I could describe the driver: large, red, possibly intoxicated, certainly ignorant. I could describe the horse: white, melancholy, and innocent. I could cite respectable persons who would certify our character, including a senator from Massachusetts, two professors, and an elderly cousin who had once been presented at Court. This last connection seemed especially valuable. Lost women in a London fog might be any thing; but lost women whose cousin had bowed to a princess acquired at once a moral standing.

At last I saw salvation—or, at least, a brass knocker.

A gentleman in evening dress was letting himself into a large, sober house. His gray hair, substantial shoulders, and neatly furled umbrella proclaimed him respectable. Here was England itself: roast beef, Magna Charta, domestic virtue, and an annual balance at the bank. I seized the opening and called out,—

“Sir! Oh, sir, if you please!”

The gentleman turned. The hansom stopped of its own accord, probably because the horse wished to hear what would happen.

I opened the doors and descended into mud nearly to my ankles. Kate awoke and followed, bringing the rug with her like a royal mantle sadly damaged in war.

“Sir,” I began, “we are Americans.”

This was not precisely relevant, but it seemed an excellent foundation.

“So I perceived,” said the gentleman.

“We are staying in Colville Gardens, Bayswater. We left our friends at half-past ten, and this man—who may be a very worthy person in Sussex—does not know London.”

“Don’t say I don’t know London,” protested the cabman, descending. “It’s the fog.”

“It is also the refreshment,” I said.

The gentleman glanced at him, then at us. I suddenly saw ourselves as he must have seen us: two muddy, damp, disordered women emerging near midnight from a spectral cab, one carrying a rug and the other explaining her nationality. I therefore added, with dignity,—

“We are acquainted with several persons of distinction.”

“Of course,” he replied gravely. “But I think what you require at present is a four-wheeler.”

He sent his servant to summon one from a nearby rank. Meanwhile he took the first cabman aside, obtained some account of our voyage, paid him, and dismissed him. The fellow touched his hat.

“No offence, mum. I’d ’ave found it.”

“No doubt,” I said. “By breakfast.”

The white horse and the burly, breezy cherub upon its roof melted together into the fog. For one fantastic moment the empty street seemed to have swallowed them, and I wondered whether they had ever existed. Then the gentleman placed us in a four-wheeled cab driven by a sober-looking elderly man whose hat alone inspired confidence.

“You know Colville Gardens?” he asked.

“Know it well, sir.”

“Take these ladies there directly.”

“Yes, sir.”

The gentleman closed the door and wished us good night. I thanked him with warmth enough to embarrass three nations.

“There,” said Kate, when we had started. “We are safe.”

“Never pronounce that word before reaching the house.”

“But this man knows London.”

“So did the other—mostly.”

The four-wheeler advanced with less poetry and more noise than the hansom. Its windows were blurred, its cushions smelled faintly of damp straw, and every turn caused the vehicle to groan as if criticising our weight; nevertheless, it appeared solid, enclosed, and respectable. I began to hope. Kate arranged her bonnet and attempted to clean the mud from her face with a handkerchief, producing only a larger and more expressive stain.

For perhaps fifteen minutes all went well. Then we slowed. We turned once, twice, three times. We passed a square with a little garden in the middle. Five minutes later we passed the same square again.

“Kate,” I said, “observe that broken railing.”

“I observed it before.”

We passed it a third time.

I lowered the window.

“Driver, is this Colville Gardens?”

“No, mum. Colville Square.”

“But we asked for Colville Gardens.”

“Thought you said Square.”

“The gentleman said Gardens.”

“Very likely, mum.”

“Do you know the Gardens?”

A pause followed—an eloquent, cavernous pause.

“I know ’em when I see ’em.”

I drew in my head.

“We are lost again,” I said.

Kate began to laugh. It was not ordinary laughter, but the calm mirth of despair. I joined her. We laughed until the cab shook; we laughed at Sussex, at Bayswater, at respectable gentlemen, at the British Empire’s roads, squares, crescents, terraces, and gardens, which appeared to have been named for the express purpose of misleading foreigners. The driver, hearing us, urged his horse forward, doubtless convinced that Americans were a dangerous but cheerful race.

Near midnight the horse’s pace declined from a trot to a walk, from a walk to a meditation, and finally ceased. The driver dismounted.

“Can’t go on, mum,” he said through the window. “’Orse is done.”

“Where are we?”

“Couldn’t rightly say.”

There are moments when terror rises so high that it becomes serenity. I had reached that elevation. Across the street stood a house with light shining through the fanlight above its door. Whoever lived there must take us in. I no longer cared whether the inmates were dukes, burglars, or dissenting ministers. I would announce our names, display our music parcel, invoke my cousin and her princess, and ask for two chairs until daylight.

“We shall appeal to that house,” I said.

We descended. The fog had thinned enough to reveal a row of wet railings, a small tree, and three familiar steps. Yet familiarity, at that hour and after such trials, seemed merely another deception. I lifted the knocker; then, finding a bell, rang that also with the determination of a shipwrecked sailor grasping his last spar.

A maid opened the door.

She stared at us.

I stared at her.

“Mary!” I cried.

“Why, miss!”

Behind her appeared our landlady in a dressing-gown, and behind the landlady appeared two of the boarders, a German student with a candle and an elderly widow wearing a nightcap of unusual architecture.

We were at home.

Not near home, not providentially within one street of home, but upon our own steps in Colville Gardens. The second driver, after wandering until his horse could proceed no farther, had stopped by accident at the very haven he professed himself unable to find.

I paid him before the perversity of my fallen nature could suggest that he should pay us.

Our entrance roused the house. Within ten minutes we were established before the dining-room fire, drinking hot tea and narrating our adventure to an audience enlarged by every door that opened. Kate, now entirely restored, imitated the first driver’s “All right, mum” so well that the German student choked upon his toast. I described our rescue by John Bull, the repeated discovery of Colville Square, and my intention of claiming shelter from strangers; and the elderly widow laughed until her nightcap leaned dangerously to one side.

By breakfast the tale had improved. The fog was thicker, the cabman drunker, the streets more deserted, and our escape more miraculous. Kate asserted that I had offered to take the reins. I denied this, but admitted that I had contemplated driving the horse, directing the cab, and governing London generally if only some one would show me Bayswater.

Thus our alarm became entertainment, as most alarms do when viewed from a warm room with toast.

We made two solemn resolutions. The first was never again to enter a hansom after dark. The second was never, under any circumstances, to become lost in a London fog.

We kept the first for nearly three weeks.

The second, I may proudly say, we have kept to this day—for whenever a fog appears, we remain at home.

## The First Chance

Senator Harrison sat down with the agreeable sensation of having performed a difficult public duty without weakness and without ornament. His last sentence—“The majesty of the law cannot be measured by the age of the hand that violates it”—still seemed to hang in the chamber, and two of the reporters in the press enclosure were writing it down. He saw them, though he appeared not to see them. Several senators bent over their desks, one turning the pages of the Journal with a diligence too ostentatious to be real; others whispered together and nodded. Harrison knew that his argument had prevailed.

He had spoken for forty minutes against the resolution recommending a pardon for Alfred Williams, who had been confined in the State penitentiary for twelve years for the murder of his father and stepmother. There had been sentiment enough, Harrison said. The prisoner had been eleven years old when the crime was committed, but murder did not cease to be murder because the murderer was a child. If the Senate advised clemency whenever the circumstances surrounding a crime were painful, punishment would become an inquiry into biography, and law would dissolve into pity.

His county had expressed itself with uncommon force. Meetings had been held in three townships; petitions had come from merchants, farmers, church trustees, and the surviving relatives of the murdered woman. Harrison had received letters declaring that his political future depended upon defeating the resolution. He had not mentioned those letters in his speech. He preferred to believe that he would have taken the same position had every constituent demanded the opposite. It was pleasant to be in harmony with his county, but it was still more pleasant to think himself above the necessity of being so.

He arranged the papers on his desk and looked toward Senator Dorman, who had risen from the third row.

Dorman was a small, gray man whose composure had often been mistaken for mildness. He held no papers. For a moment he rested one hand upon the back of the chair before him and regarded the chamber with an air neither challenging nor apologetic.

“I shall not attempt,” he began, “to answer the senator from Clay County in the language of legal philosophy. He has reminded us of the majesty of the law. I ask only that, before we make that majesty final, we consider upon whom it descended.”

Harrison leaned back. That was cleverly put, though it proved nothing.

Dorman began with the boy’s mother. She had died when Alfred was three. His father, a field laborer who moved from county to county, had left him first with an aunt, then with strangers, then with no one in particular. At six the child slept for several months in a shed behind a livery stable and carried water for the privilege. At seven he was taken back by his father, who had remarried. The new wife disliked him; the father, when sober, ignored him, and when drunk remembered him chiefly as something within reach.

There had been beatings. Dorman did not make much of them. He gave dates, names, the testimony of neighbors, the report of a schoolmistress who had seen the boy arrive with a split lip and remain all day without food. Once, in winter, he had been put outdoors until morning for taking a piece of corn bread. Once his father had held his hand against the hot iron of the kitchen range because a tool was missing. The tool was found later in the barn.

No one had taught Alfred that pain might end except by passing it to another. No one had shown him restraint except as the helplessness of the weaker before the stronger. He had attended school, in all, perhaps seven months. He could not read. He did not know the name of the President. He knew how to hide, how to endure hunger, how to watch the movements of an angry man, and how to hate.

Harrison turned his pencil between his fingers.

These things were deplorable. He had never denied that. But there were thousands of neglected children who did not commit murder. Environment explained; it did not acquit. Indeed, if misfortune were to cancel moral responsibility, then the unfortunate would become a privileged criminal class. The argument was familiar, and Harrison had met it in his speech.

Dorman appeared to concede the point.

“I do not say,” he continued, “that every abused child kills. I say that this child killed. We are not judging every child. We are judging Alfred Williams. It is easy to declare what a boy ought to have been when we have never asked what the world permitted him to become.”

The chamber had grown quiet. Even the reporters had ceased sharpening pencils.

Dorman described the night of the crime without concealment. The father and stepmother had returned late from a country dance. There had been drinking and quarreling. The boy had been accused of neglecting the livestock. A neighbor heard cries and the crash of a chair. Near midnight, while the two adults slept, Alfred took his father’s gun from behind the door and fired twice.

In the morning he was found three miles away, sitting beside a frozen creek, his bare feet wrapped in pieces of sacking. He did not deny what he had done. He answered questions in monosyllables, and when asked whether he understood that his father and stepmother were dead, said only that they could not hurt him anymore.

A sound moved through the gallery—less than a sigh, more than silence.

Harrison frowned. It was precisely this kind of detail that disturbed judgment. The frozen creek, the pieces of sacking, the childish answer—what were they but an appeal to nerves? The essential fact remained two dead human beings. He glanced toward the windows. Beyond them, on the State-house lawn, the maples were tipped with April green. A sparrow dropped from a branch to the grass, pecked there, and flew up again.

He turned back impatiently.

Dorman had passed to the years of imprisonment.

“At eleven,” he said, “Alfred Williams entered the penitentiary. He was small for his age. The records show that he weighed sixty-eight pounds. A guard who was there then tells me that on his first night the boy cried, not because he feared the prisoners, but because the iron door had closed and no one would answer him.”

There had been no reformatory suitable for him, and no private association willing to accept the case. He was placed among men. For two years he worked in the broom shop. Later he was sent to the bindery, where an old prisoner taught him his letters from damaged schoolbooks. He learned slowly, then rapidly. At sixteen he began arithmetic. At eighteen he was reading history. The chaplain had sent Dorman several compositions written by the prisoner—plain, thoughtful pages, without complaint. The warden reported twelve years without a serious infraction. Williams had broken up a fight at risk to himself; had nursed an old convict through fever; had refused an opportunity to assist in an escape.

“There are men outside those walls,” Dorman said, “who have enjoyed every aid to decency and have made less use of it. This boy—this man now—has made a life out of the remnants we permitted him.”

Harrison found the phrase objectionable. We permitted him. The senator had a habit of laying the burden of every sorrow upon society, as though society were a negligent person who might be brought to the bar. Harrison himself had been only twenty-six when Alfred entered prison. He had been beginning his law practice, worried about rent, proud of his first black coat. He had known nothing of a half-starved child beside a frozen creek. How, then, had he permitted anything?

Yet the question came back in another form. Who had prevented it?

Dorman spoke of the prisoner’s future. Employment had been offered at a printing establishment in another county. A clergyman and his wife had agreed to receive him until he could support himself. The warden, who opposed easy pardons, recommended this one. So did the prison physician, the chaplain, and every officer who had known Williams for more than five years.

“We are not asked to declare him innocent,” Dorman said. “We are asked whether punishment must have no relation to the human being punished. We are asked whether twelve years—the whole of adolescence, the whole season in which a child becomes a man—may satisfy justice for an act committed at eleven under conditions that would have distorted a stronger nature than his.”

Harrison’s hand had ceased moving. The pencil lay between his fingers.

The whole season in which a child becomes a man.

He remembered himself at eleven. He had lived on a farm then, in a white house with two elms before it and a pond beyond the pasture. He had owned a brown dog named Jip, whose one white ear made the boys laugh. There had been fishing in June, swimming in August, apples stolen from his own father’s orchard for the pleasure of imagining them stolen. His mother had called him from the door at dusk. His father, a stern man, had once whipped him for lying and afterward sat on the bed to explain why a lie made a coward of the one who told it.

Suppose there had been no explanation—only the blow.

Suppose there had been no mother at the door.

Dorman was saying that Alfred had never seen a circus. That detail seemed absurdly chosen, almost indecorous in a debate on murder. What had a circus to do with justice? Yet Harrison remembered the first one he had attended: the enormous white tent swelling in the wind; horses with silver plumes; the sour-sweet smell of sawdust and animals; his father’s hand closing firmly around his own as the crowd pressed forward. He had been nine, perhaps ten.

Alfred had never seen one. He had never played on a school ball team. He had never owned a knife because it was pretty, nor traded marbles, nor gone swimming with other boys and come home with his shirt stuck damply to his back. He had never slept under the stars from choice. He had never owned a dog.

Why did the dog matter? Harrison could not have said. Murders and pardons were matters for reason; a dog was nothing. Nevertheless he saw an undersized boy moving from shed to kitchen, from kitchen to barn, with no creature in the world running out to meet him.

“Put yourself in his place,” Dorman said quietly. “Not as you are now, protected by education, memory, friendship, and the respect of your neighbors. Put yourself there as he was—eleven years old, ignorant, beaten, alone. Then follow him through the gate. Give him twelve years without a field, without a free road, without a sunset he may walk toward, without the stars except as they appear above a wall. After that, if you can say he has had his chance and thrown it away, vote against him.”

Harrison felt an unreasonable resentment. The appeal was unfair because it was effective. No man could put himself wholly in another’s place, least of all in the place of a child from whom every familiar protection had been taken. Government could not be conducted by imagination. There must be statutes, precedents, fixed consequences. Otherwise each crime would become an occasion for emotion, each criminal the hero of his own sorrowful history.

But perhaps fixed consequences were only the conveniences of men unwilling to imagine.

He looked again toward the lawn. The young leaves trembled in a light that seemed almost green. Two boys were crossing the walk below, one carrying books under his arm, the other knocking off his cap and running with it. Harrison could not hear them through the closed window, but he knew the sound of such laughter.

Alfred Williams had been in prison as long as those boys had been alive.

Dorman’s voice rose, not loudly, but with a gathering earnestness that held the chamber.

“I do not ask you to be sentimental. Sentiment spends itself in tears and leaves the facts unchanged. I ask you to be just—to the dead, certainly, but also to the living. The dead have received all that punishment can give them. It cannot restore them, comfort them, or add one day to their lives. The living man remains in our hands. He has disciplined his temper, educated his mind, and served twelve years. Shall we now tell him that growth is useless? Shall we teach every prisoner in this State that repentance is a door painted upon a wall?”

That was rhetoric, Harrison told himself. Excellent rhetoric. Dorman had always been dangerous when he spoke quietly.

Yet Harrison no longer desired his own side to win.

The discovery came without grandeur and brought him no relief. He had delivered the argument that would defeat the resolution. Men who had been uncertain had approached him during the noon recess and said that he had clarified their minds. Senator Blake had declared that Harrison’s stand showed “the old Roman quality.” His county newspapers would print approving editorials. His renomination, threatened by a faction in the eastern townships, would become almost certain.

And if Alfred remained in prison?

He tried to see the matter impersonally. One prisoner more or less; one application among hundreds; one unfortunate life already shaped beyond correction. Williams might fail outside. He might drink, drift, become violent again. Then those who voted for him would be accused of placing tender theories above public safety. Harrison could imagine the headlines and the speeches at the next election.

But he could also imagine a cell at night.

The imagined prisoner was no longer eleven. He was twenty-three, pale from indoor labor, bending over a book in the light permitted by regulations. Somewhere beyond the wall, spring rain passed over fields he had never crossed. A train moved through darkness toward towns where people opened doors freely and dogs barked in yards. He had not seen the sunrise except through bars. He had not chosen when to sleep, when to walk, when to be silent. And now men in a chamber bright with April were to say that because he had once been given no childhood, he should be given no manhood either.

The boy in the penitentiary had never had a chance.

Dorman ended simply. “I ask you to give him the first one.”

There was no applause; applause was forbidden. But the silence after he sat down possessed the quality of applause suppressed.

The lieutenant governor announced the roll call. Names fell across the chamber in alphabetical order, each followed by “aye” or “no.” Harrison listened with the sharpened attention of a man who waits for a verdict upon himself while pretending it concerns another.

When his name came, he answered, “No.”

The word was clear. It seemed louder than the others.

For an instant he was astonished. Why had he said it? Because he had spoken against the resolution; because a reversal within the hour would look weak; because his constituents had instructed him; because a senator could not surrender judgment whenever another senator made a moving speech. All the reasons stood ready, orderly and respectable.

Still, he had said no.

The clerk continued. Harrison counted. The supporters gained one uncertain member, lost another, gained two from the northern counties. At the end, the resolution had failed by two votes.

Conversation broke out at once. Chairs moved. A messenger hurried toward the lobby. In the press enclosure, heads bent over paper, and Harrison knew what was being written: Dorman’s emotional appeal defeated; Harrison’s argument decisive; clemency refused to boy murderer.

Senator Blake leaned across the aisle and gripped his shoulder.

“That did it,” he said. “Your speech held the line.”

Harrison looked at the hand on his shoulder. “Yes,” he answered.

His speech had held the line. Without it, three or four men would have voted differently. Alfred Williams would remain behind the wall because Harrison had been logical, dispassionate, courageous enough to resist pity.

The presiding officer called the next order of business. A bill regarding county drainage was read by title. Harrison heard the words without receiving their meaning. He was thinking of the prison gate closing at evening. He was thinking of an old man—himself—sitting someday on a porch, perhaps under those same elms at the farm, while his grandchildren played on the grass. Would he remember this April afternoon? Men recalled strange things in age. A face glimpsed in a station, a harsh word spoken to a dead friend, a kindness withheld because there had seemed time enough for it tomorrow.

He would hear Dorman’s question: Had the boy ever had his chance?

He would answer that he might have given it and did not.

The matter could still be reconsidered, but only by a senator who had voted with the prevailing side. He had done so. Parliamentary law had placed the key in his hand.

At once the political consequences appeared. His county convention met in six weeks. The opposition would call him faithless, unstable, governed by tears. The murdered woman’s relatives would sit in the front row at meetings. His speech of the morning would be printed beside his vote of the afternoon. There would be no explaining such a reversal. A man might alter his position after months of inquiry; to alter it after forty minutes of another man’s eloquence looked ridiculous.

He pictured the reporters. He pictured Blake’s surprise. He pictured his constituents reading the evening paper.

Then he pictured Alfred being told that the resolution had failed by two votes.

The drainage bill passed to a committee. Another measure was called. If Harrison meant to act, he must do it before adjournment. The chamber had become unbearably warm. He loosened his collar, then fastened it again. His heart was beating with a violence wholly out of proportion to the raising of a hand and the speaking of a sentence.

He wondered whether courage always felt this much like humiliation.

Twice he half rose and sat down. The second time Blake glanced at him.

“Anything wrong?”

“No,” Harrison said. “That is—yes. I don’t know.”

Blake stared.

The lieutenant governor lifted his gavel. “If there is no further business—”

Harrison stood.

“Mr. President.”

His voice failed to carry. Several senators continued talking.

“Mr. President,” he said again.

The gavel paused. “The senator from Clay.”

Harrison was conscious of every face turning toward him, of the reporters looking up, of Dorman very still at his desk. The prepared phrases by which a public man protects himself had disappeared. He had no speech. He had only the fact that he had been wrong and the intolerable necessity of saying so.

“I move,” he began, “that the vote by which the resolution concerning Alfred Williams was defeated be reconsidered.”

The chamber stirred.

The lieutenant governor asked whether the senator desired to state his reasons.

Harrison gripped the edge of his desk. “I suppose I should.”

Someone laughed softly, then stopped.

“This morning I spoke against the resolution. I believed what I said—or believed that I believed it. I thought the law required us to consider the crime apart from those circumstances which merely excite sympathy. I still think there must be punishment. I do not—” He stopped and looked down at his papers, as though the lost sentence might be found among them. “I do not know how to put this properly.”

No one helped him.

“The senator from Mercer has shown us what the punishment has been. More than that, he has shown us what came before it. I said the prisoner had forfeited his chance. It appears to me now that he never had one to forfeit.”

Harrison swallowed.

“I have voted according to the wish of my county. I know what my county will say if I change that vote. Perhaps they will be right to say it. But I cannot—well, I cannot be one of the men who keep him there forever merely because it is difficult to admit a mistake.”

He turned slightly toward Dorman, then away.

“I was mistaken. I ask the Senate to reconsider.”

For a second the chamber remained silent. Then Blake said, “Second the motion,” with such force that the words sounded like a cheer.

The motion to reconsider carried almost unanimously.

On the second roll call the change was greater than anyone had expected. Men who had sheltered behind Harrison’s certainty seemed released by his confession. One after another they voted aye. Some did so solemnly, some eagerly, one with a defiant glance toward the gallery. When Harrison’s name was called, he said “Aye” in a voice no louder than before.

The resolution passed by twenty-one votes.

This time the presiding officer’s gavel could not prevent the applause. It came first from the gallery, then from the floor itself—a disorderly burst quickly checked, but sufficient to bring color to Harrison’s face. Dorman crossed the aisle and took his hand.

“That was a brave thing,” he said.

“No,” Harrison replied. “It was cowardice.”

Dorman smiled, thinking perhaps that modesty demanded contradiction. “You stood against your county and possibly against your career.”

“I was afraid.”

“Of what?”

“Of remembering.”

Dorman’s smile faded.

Harrison gathered his papers. “You made me see myself at seventy, trying to explain to myself why that man had stayed in prison. I was afraid I should see him whenever I had reason to be happy. If I had been brave, perhaps I could have borne it.”

“You are using the wrong word,” Dorman said.

“Possibly. I have used several wrong words today.”

The reporters surrounded him in the lobby. Was his reversal a repudiation of his earlier position? Did he expect political injury? Had he received new evidence? Would he issue a statement? Harrison answered badly. No, there was no new evidence. Yes, he supposed his county might disapprove. No, he had no statement. He had spoken enough—far too much, indeed.

One young reporter asked what particular argument had changed his mind.

Harrison thought of legal responsibility, prison discipline, the reports of the warden and chaplain. But what returned to him was a brown dog with one white ear racing across a farmyard to greet a boy who had never doubted that he belonged somewhere.

“I cannot say,” he answered. “Perhaps it was the whole of it.”

He escaped down the broad stone steps.

The afternoon had deepened, but the sun still lay warmly over the State-house grounds. Rain in the morning had darkened the earth, and from the flower beds came the rich odor of damp soil. The maples were no longer a picture beyond glass. Their small leaves shook above him; sparrows quarreled in the branches; somewhere, unseen, a robin poured out its repeated, confident phrase.

Harrison walked slowly.

His political future had very likely been shortened. He could already hear the charge that he had betrayed his mandate under the influence of a skillful speech. There would be angry letters, resolutions of censure, cold faces in towns where he had once been welcomed. He did not pretend these things were unimportant. A man who said he cared nothing for defeat generally cared most and lied about it.

Yet beneath the anxiety there was quiet.

The world around him seemed engaged in a vast reconsideration. Trees that had stood bare all winter were permitted to begin again. Grass returned through last year’s decay. Birds resumed songs abandoned in the cold. The earth did not ask whether one season had deserved another. It yielded to warmth and brought forth life wherever life remained possible.

At the corner of the grounds, a boy was throwing a stick for a black dog. The animal rushed after it, overshot it, circled foolishly, and came racing back. The boy shouted with laughter.

Harrison stopped to watch.

Tomorrow a message would go to the Governor. After the necessary formalities, another message would pass through the prison office. A gate would open. Alfred Williams, twenty-three years old, would step into a morning not divided by walls. He might fail. He might suffer from freedom as men suffered from any gift they did not understand. He might find the world suspicious, employment hard, memory harder. Pardon would not restore his childhood or raise the dead. It would not turn the past into justice.

It would only give him what others received without knowing they had received it: a road upon which to make the next mistake or the next right choice; a sunset toward which he might walk; darkness with no locked door behind it; the voices of the night; the stars.

A chance. His first.

The dog dropped the stick at Harrison’s feet. The boy called an apology, but Harrison bent, picked it up, and threw it across the wet grass. The dog bounded away under the budding trees.

Then Senator Harrison went on, not triumphant, not certain that he had been brave, but with a tranquil sense that for once his own small act had moved in accord with the renewing earth.

### THE GUN IN THE PASS

At eight o’clock on the morning of the third day the Federal division had advanced no farther than the southern base of Mount Kestrel, although the road beyond it—visible in pale sections through the timber—was known to be unobstructed for nearly six miles. The impediment was not upon the road, but above it. Twelve Confederate guns, disposed along a low ridge, commanded the valley from east to west and made of every open acre a place in which a prudent man would have preferred not to transact even the least important business.

Near the centre of that ridge stood a plantation house, a square, white structure with a pillared veranda and two chimneys. It was distant perhaps two thousand yards. Behind it the country fell away toward the river, where the enemy’s infantry, defeated the previous afternoon, was believed to be crossing in haste. The artillery on the ridge formed the rear-guard and had shown a disagreeable aptitude for its office.

Between the Federal position and the plantation rose a wooded spur of Mount Kestrel. Near its summit was a narrow depression through which ran an abandoned wagon track. The place was marked on no map. By noon it had acquired a name.

The division commander, General Hartwell, stood upon a ledge of gray rock beneath a stunted pine and examined the ridge through his field glass. He was a large, rigid man whose figure appeared to have been constructed for a monument and subsequently condemned to military service. Cannon smoke lay along the enemy’s position in detached white masses. From each mass, at intervals, issued a brief flame; then came the sound—first the thin, malignant whir of the projectile, afterward the deep report of the gun, arriving too late to be of warning.

Colonel Bartram, commanding the brigade on the left, waited at the general’s elbow. Several staff officers, each maintaining the expression proper to his rank and prospects, waited behind them.

“That notch,” said the general, lowering his glass, “would enfilade their right.”

Colonel Bartram looked toward the indicated place. To reach it a battery must ascend a steep acclivity under observation; once there, it would be exposed in front and on both flanks. The notch itself was scarcely broad enough for a single gun. Its sides were strewn with rocks, and the trees above had been thinned by shells until their remaining branches stood out like broken ribs.

“One piece might be placed there,” said the colonel.

“One piece will be placed there.”

The distinction was grammatical, but conclusive.

The general turned toward an aide. “Send Captain Coulter to me.”

No one spoke. The aides exchanged no glances; well-trained officers do not exchange glances when silence will communicate as much and compromise less. Yet all had heard something of Captain Coulter and General Hartwell. In an army, rumor performs the duties of biography and usually does them with greater animation.

Coulter came presently up the slope, saluted, and stood at attention. He was a tall young man, dark-haired and grave, with that composure which may signify either perfect courage or a long familiarity with sorrow. His battery had distinguished itself in four engagements. The newspapers had named him. The general had once named him also—though in a manner not intended for publication.

“Captain,” said Hartwell, with ceremonial mildness, “I have been hearing extravagant accounts of your gunnery.”

Coulter bowed slightly.

“I propose to afford you an opportunity to correct them. You will take one gun into that pass and silence the battery near the white house.”

For one instant the captain did not move. His eyes had followed the general’s hand and rested upon the distant plantation. The change in his face was not such as fear commonly makes. The color withdrew from it slowly, leaving the features sharpened and old. His lips parted, but no sound came.

The general raised his brows.

“You understand the order?”

“Yes, General.”

“You will engage the whole battery. I am told you prefer difficult targets.”

The captain’s gaze remained fixed upon the house. A shell passed overhead with the laborious murmur of some great insect, clipped the top from a pine, and burst beyond the ledge. A shower of needles descended upon the officers.

“May I ask,” Coulter said at last, “whether the occupation of the notch is necessary to the general movement?”

The question was admissible in form, though remarkable in substance.

General Hartwell smiled.

“The necessity of an order, Captain, is assumed by the person receiving it. Its wisdom is the concern of the person giving it.”

Coulter saluted. “Very well, sir.”

As he turned away, the general called after him:

“And, Captain—do not disappoint the confidence of your admirers.”

Coulter halted, but did not look back.

“No, sir,” he said.

The officers watched him descend toward his battery. Colonel Bartram’s face had reddened.

“That position is a slaughter pen,” he said.

“Then your brave Coulter will have ample employment,” replied the general.

There are occasions when subordination is merely the military name of helplessness. Colonel Bartram saluted and went to carry out his part of the operation.

Within twenty minutes a gun, its limber, and two caissons began climbing the abandoned track. The horses strained in the harness, slipping upon stones polished by rain; drivers cursed them with the affectionate ferocity reserved for useful animals. Infantrymen assisted at the wheels. The ascent was made under a desultory fire, for the enemy had quickly perceived the movement without comprehending its full absurdity.

At the notch the gun was unlimbered and run forward by hand. It was a three-inch rifle, black, slender, and singularly delicate in appearance. Through a screen of shattered saplings its crew could see the Confederate line, the white plantation house, and, beyond both, a strip of river shining like steel. The enemy’s twelve pieces were spread in a shallow crescent. Their smoke rose and drifted among them, concealing and revealing the guns as the ink-cloud of the cuttlefish conceals and reveals its living author.

Coulter dismounted and sighted the piece himself.

“Shell,” he said. “Fuse, four seconds.”

The gun was loaded, aimed, and fired.

Its report, confined between the rocky walls of the pass, struck the mountain with a flat concussion. The shell burst above the Confederate right. Men scattered; one gun ceased firing.

Coulter watched through his glass.

“Two points left. Same range.”

The second shell exploded beside a caisson. A wheel sprang into the air, turning lazily, and dropped out of sight. At that, the enemy understood.

Twelve guns were swung toward the notch.

The first salvo was ill judged. Projectiles struck above and below, filling the pass with stone fragments, splintered wood, and dust. One shell tore through a tree and showered the crew with bark. Another passed between the gun and its limber, taking the head from a horse so neatly that the animal remained standing for a second before collapsing, as if in compliance with a delayed order.

The Federal piece fired again.

Its shell entered the veranda of the plantation house. A white column broke in the middle and came down in sections; part of the roof followed it. Coulter lowered his glass abruptly.

“Why the devil are you firing at the house?” cried Lieutenant Maynard.

“The gun beside it,” Coulter answered. “I am firing at the gun.”

His voice was steady, but his hand, resting upon the breech, trembled.

The enemy replied with greater accuracy. The notch vanished in smoke. It seemed no longer a place in the natural world, but the mouth of an engine through which living iron passed screaming—iron whose thoughts were lightnings and whose deeds were death. Trees writhed under impact; rocks split; the earth leaped in black fountains. The men serving the rifle became momentary figures, discovered by each flash and lost again in dust.

When the air cleared, two of them were down. One had been struck in the chest and lay upon his back with an expression of mild surprise. The other, sitting against a wheel, examined with dull curiosity the red ruin below his knee.

“Number three!” called the sergeant.

A man stepped forward.

The gun was sponged, loaded, aimed, and fired.

A Confederate shell burst at the muzzle. The sergeant turned twice and fell. The rammer was flung against the rock wall, where he adhered for an instant in an attitude of grotesque embrace before dropping. Blood covered the sponge staff. The new number three picked it up, found the woollen head clogged with earth, wiped it once upon his sleeve, and thrust it into the smoking piece.

The gun was sponged, loaded, aimed, and fired.

Below the pass Colonel Bartram had established himself where he could observe the action. He sent infantrymen upward in squads to replace losses and ordered another gun prepared. From time to time a messenger came down with requests for ammunition, water, or men. No request was made for permission to retire.

After half an hour the first gun was disabled. A solid shot struck the left wheel, breaking hub and spokes and throwing the piece upon its side. Two men were pinned beneath it. One screamed. The other did not, being dead. Coulter ordered the wounded man dragged clear, then stood beside the wreck while shells burst about him.

A second piece was hauled into the notch.

The infantrymen who brought it saw what had been done there and became suddenly attentive to their footing. The pass was carpeted with severed branches, fragments of equipment, pools of blood, and certain objects which the mind recognized unwillingly as portions of men. A cannoneer’s hand, still wearing a brass ring, had fallen into an open ammunition chest and lay among the cartridges as if selecting one.

Coulter took the lanyard from a dead gunner.

“Ready!”

The second gun spoke.

Its shell struck the roof of the plantation house near the eastern chimney. Bricks sprang upward in a scarlet cloud. The chimney leaned, paused with an almost human indecision, and plunged through the shingles. A dark aperture appeared in the roof.

Coulter bent forward, staring.

“Good shot!” shouted Maynard.

The captain turned upon him with so terrible a face that the lieutenant stepped back.

“I aimed at the battery,” Coulter said.

“Yes, sir.”

“Attend to your duty.”

A staff officer arrived from General Hartwell. His horse had been killed on the ascent, and he delivered his message while wiping blood from one boot.

“The general presents his compliments to Captain Coulter and congratulates him upon the accuracy and rapidity of his fire.”

Coulter’s eyes did not leave the house.

“My compliments to the general,” he said. “Tell him I am grateful.”

The staff officer looked about the notch. A projectile struck the rock overhead and covered his uniform with powdered stone.

“Is that all, Captain?”

“That is all.”

The officer descended with alacrity. He had not eaten since dawn and found himself, during the entire return, thinking persistently of supper. Such matters have no respect for tragedy.

By two o’clock the duel had become famous along the line. Men in sheltered positions crawled forward to see it. They beheld, high upon the mountain, a torn and draggled cloud of blue smoke that flashed within itself and continually gave birth to thunder. From the opposing ridge twelve white clouds answered. Between them the valley lay green and peaceful, except where shells touched it and caused the earth to blossom black.

The second Federal gun lasted twenty-seven minutes. A shell burst beneath the axle. The piece rose bodily, turned part way over, and came down across three men. One was cut nearly in half by the rim of a wheel. Another, flung clear, tried to stand, discovered that he had but one foot, and laughed—a thin, confidential laugh which no one afterward cared to remember.

A third gun was sent up.

Colonel Bartram intercepted Coulter at the lower turn of the track while the new piece was being dragged into position.

“Captain,” he said, “you have done enough.”

Coulter saluted. His uniform was torn at the shoulder, his face black with powder, and a narrow line of blood ran from his temple into his collar.

“My orders are to silence the battery.”

“You have lost thirty men.”

“Thirty-four.”

“And two guns.”

“Yes, Colonel.”

Bartram studied him. “Why did you hesitate when the order was given?”

Coulter looked toward the plantation. Its veranda had disappeared. One chimney remained, and smoke issued from several windows. The Confederate gun beside it was still firing.

“I did not hesitate, sir.”

“You did.”

The captain’s face hardened. “Then I ask the colonel’s pardon for a breach of discipline.”

“Coulter, is that house known to you?”

A long silence followed. Down in the valley a wounded horse was crying with a cadence curiously like a child’s call.

“The general is observing us,” said Coulter.

Bartram turned. On the distant ledge Hartwell stood motionless with his glass raised.

“You have not answered me.”

“No, sir.”

“You mean it is not known to you?”

“I mean I have not answered.”

The colonel’s anger came quickly, but found no useful object. He had heard the story: Coulter, while stationed before the war at a Southern arsenal, had married the daughter of a planter; General Hartwell, then a major, had also admired the lady and had not borne rejection with philosophy. Later she had remained in the South. Some said she was dead, some that she had abandoned her husband, and some—those being the majority—that she had betrayed military information to her people. Rumor had provided evidence suitable to every conclusion except the truth.

Bartram lowered his voice.

“Is your wife in that house?”

Coulter’s lips moved. Before he could reply a shell exploded upon the track. The concussion threw both officers against the bank. A fragment struck the leading horse of the third gun, ripping open its belly. The animal fell among the traces, and the drivers sprang to cut it free.

Coulter rose.

“My gun is waiting, Colonel.”

He went upward without another word.

The third piece was brought into action by men who had ceased to resemble soldiers. Their garments were scorched and stiff with blood; their faces, blackened by smoke, showed the eyes in startling whiteness. They moved with the rigid promptitude of mechanisms. When one fell, another advanced. When the gun was sponged, it was loaded; when loaded, it was aimed; when aimed, it was fired. Death might interrupt a man, but it could not interrupt the drill.

The sponge head had been shot away. A cannoneer wrapped a strip of blanket around the staff and plunged it into a bucket. The bucket contained no water. It had been filled from the wound of a dead horse. With that sanguinary instrument they cleansed the gun.

The gun seemed to bleed and breathe.

Coulter aimed always toward the Confederate piece nearest the house. His shots struck the earth before it, the caisson behind it, the garden wall beside it. One passed through an upper window. Another entered at the veranda level, and a moment afterward the floor of the house appeared to rise in fragments through the roof. Yet the enemy’s gun continued to fire from the smoke, retreating a few yards whenever its position became untenable, as a spiteful creature might withdraw step by step while retaining its fangs.

At three o’clock the Federal infantry advanced against both flanks of the ridge. Bugles sounded in the woods; brigades moved forward in blue lines, visible briefly in the open and then swallowed by trees. The Confederate guns divided their attention, but six continued to engage the notch. Their service was now less rapid. Coulter’s single rifle, aided by the pressure of the general attack, had made their position costly.

A courier came from Hartwell with a second message.

“The general directs that Captain Coulter maintain his fire. The enemy is wavering.”

“Tell the general,” said Coulter, “that I see it.”

The courier, a boy of nineteen, stared at the bodies in the pass.

“Captain, shall I bring back more men?”

“They are already coming.”

They were. A fresh detachment climbed over the wreckage, stepping where it could. One soldier paused beside a dead comrade, removed the man’s cartridge pouch, and continued upward. It was not callousness. In battle, the dead relinquish property with admirable promptness, and the living become practical heirs.

The third gun fired twelve rounds.

On the thirteenth, a Confederate shot struck its muzzle. The rifle burst. A wedge-shaped fragment passed through Lieutenant Maynard and buried itself in a tree. He remained standing for a breath, looking down at the hole in his body, then folded carefully to the ground.

Coulter was thrown into a heap of broken spokes. Men found him conscious, though blood ran from both ears.

“Another piece,” he said.

Colonel Bartram, arriving at that moment, heard him.

“No.”

Coulter struggled upright. “The general’s order—”

“I countermand it.”

“You cannot countermand the division commander.”

“I can suspend the execution of an order when its object has been accomplished.”

“The battery has not withdrawn.”

As he spoke, the Confederate line began to move. Limbers came up through the smoke; guns were attached and drawn away by teams at a gallop. The piece beside the plantation fired once more. Its shell passed over the notch and burst harmlessly among the trees. Then it too disappeared behind the house.

The rear-guard was retiring.

Bartram pointed.

“Your object is accomplished.”

Coulter did not look toward the guns. He looked only at the house. Flames had caught in the western rooms, but burned uncertainly, smothered by plaster and fallen timber.

“I request permission to accompany the advance,” he said.

“You are injured.”

“I can ride.”

“Your horse is dead.”

“I can walk.”

The colonel regarded him with a compassion sharpened by suspicion.

“Go, then.”

The ridge was occupied shortly before sunset. Its abandoned works contained five disabled guns, several dead horses, and many men whose allegiance had ceased to be a military fact. The retreating Confederates had not destroyed the plantation house, perhaps because the Federals had nearly done so for them.

General Hartwell established headquarters beneath an oak near the garden. He received reports, issued orders, and made no inquiry concerning Coulter. His face wore the impassive satisfaction of a man whose judgment has been confirmed by events. That the result might have been obtained at a smaller price was a speculation belonging to another department.

Colonel Bartram entered the house with two officers and an orderly. The front rooms were wrecked. Furniture lay overturned beneath fallen plaster; portraits hung awry upon walls punctured by fragments. In the dining room a table remained set for three persons. One plate was broken. A loaf of bread, gray with dust, lay beside a vase of fresh flowers.

“No one here,” said the orderly.

He had been studying a door beneath the staircase and thinking that a house of this size must possess an excellent cellar. He hoped, with the innocent optimism of thirst, that it might also possess casks.

The door was jammed. Two soldiers forced it open. A stair descended into darkness.

Bartram took a lantern and went first.

The cellar smelled of damp earth, smoke, and wine. Several shelves had collapsed. Bottles lay broken on the floor, their contents mingling with water that dripped steadily from above. Near the farther wall was a mattress, a chair, and a little store of food. Plainly the place had served as a refuge.

“It is not bomb-proof,” the colonel said.

The remark was technically exact.

The lantern light fell upon three figures beneath a gap in the ceiling. A man sat upon the floor with his back against the masonry. His uniform was burned and blackened; his head drooped forward. In his arms he held a woman. Against the woman’s breast lay an infant wrapped in a blue shawl.

All three appeared dead.

One of the officers crossed himself. The orderly forgot the casks.

The shell had entered through the upper rooms, torn through two floors, and burst beneath the cellar ceiling. A timber had fallen across the woman’s legs. Her face was untouched and very white. The child’s small hand rested against her throat.

Bartram raised the lantern.

The seated man opened his eyes.

For a moment no one moved. Then he lifted his head with great effort. Powder had darkened his face almost beyond recognition, and blood from a wound in the scalp had dried along his cheek. His expression was tranquil—not with peace, but with that exhaustion which has passed beyond grief and sits in its vacant place.

“Coulter,” whispered the colonel.

The man looked at him without comprehension.

“Captain Coulter, can you hear me?”

His arms tightened about the woman and child.

Above them sounded the tread of troops, the rolling of wheels, the calls of men preparing a bivouac. Somewhere in the garden an axe began its regular blows. The army had arrived and was making itself comfortable.

Bartram knelt.

“Who are these people?”

The man’s eyes moved to the woman’s face. He touched her hair with one blackened hand.

“My wife,” he said.

The colonel bowed his head.

“And the child?”

Coulter did not answer. No answer was required.

From outside came a cheer. The captured ridge had been reported to the division, and men were applauding the success. The sound entered the cellar faintly through the broken floor, diminished by earth and masonry to a murmur like distant water.

Bartram glanced upward, then at the ruin around him. Understanding came not as a flash, but as a cold and orderly assembly of facts: the captain’s pallor upon receiving the command; his refusal to explain; the terrible precision of his fire; the general’s compliments; the white house serving as an aiming point to twelve guns and one; the private grievance wearing the uniform of tactical necessity.

“Did the general know?” he asked.

Coulter’s gaze returned slowly to him.

“Yes.”

The word was nearly inaudible.

Bartram rose, and for one instant his hand went to his sword. It is possible that he imagined some act by which justice might be made immediate and simple. But authority, like armor, is strongest against those compelled to honor it. His hand fell.

“We will carry them out,” he said.

“No.”

“They cannot remain here.”

“No.”

The captain bent over the woman, shielding her from hands that had not yet touched her.

Bartram spoke gently. “This house—whose house is it?”

Coulter looked about the cellar as if seeing it for the first time: the broken shelves, the scattered provisions, the cradle overturned beneath the stair, the familiar walls opened by artillery.

Then he looked at the colonel.

His voice, when it came, was clear.

“This is my home,” he said.

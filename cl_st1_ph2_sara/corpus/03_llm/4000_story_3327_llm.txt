### THE GENTLE SPELL

There are persons who seem to possess a certain quiet power, for which philosophy has found no name. They neither command nor persuade; they make no display of superior wisdom, and are perhaps themselves unconscious of the influence they exert; yet anger grows ashamed in their presence, obstinacy relaxes, and even the brute creature submits as if some familiar voice had spoken within its heart. Such a person was the maiden of whom I am about to relate a few simple incidents. Her name has escaped my memory—or, if remembered, it matters not now to record it. I knew her only for a night and a morning; but through all the years that have since passed, I have never forgotten her gentle hand.

I was travelling, late in autumn, through a wild and thinly settled district, when I lost the road. How this occurred it is needless to explain. The evening had fallen suddenly; a cold rain swept across the fields; my horse had gone lame, and I was both hungry and weary. For more than an hour I had seen neither cottage nor traveller. At length, through a clump of black hemlocks, I perceived a faint light, which appeared and disappeared as the wind tossed the branches.

Guided by this uncertain beacon, I came presently to an ill-hung gate, beyond which stood a low farmhouse. The building had a cheerless aspect, yet the red gleam from its windows promised warmth; and a column of smoke, driven almost horizontally from the chimney, assured me that human beings were within.

I had scarcely entered the yard when a huge dog sprang from beneath a wagon and rushed upon me with a fury that made my blood run cold. His chain stopped him within a few feet of the place where I stood, but he reared against it, showing his teeth and uttering savage cries.

“Down, Brutus!” shouted a man from the doorway. “Down, I say!”

The dog only raged the more.

“What d’ye want?” demanded the man.

“A lodging for the night,” I replied. “I have lost my way, and my horse is lame.”

“We don’t keep tavern,” he answered bluntly.

“I am aware of that; but the nearest inn—”

“Ten mile, if it’s an inch. You can’t get there to-night.”

The information gave me little comfort, for his tone implied no willingness to shelter me. At that moment the door opened more widely, and a young woman came out into the rain.

“Father,” she said, “the gentleman is wet and tired.”

“That ain’t our lookout,” returned the farmer. “Brutus! Will you be still?”

She passed him without reply and advanced toward the dog. I called out hastily, for the animal seemed beside himself with rage; but she laid one fair hand upon his rough head.

“Poor Brutus,” she murmured, “what has frightened you?”

The change was instantaneous. His cries sank into a low whine; his rigid body relaxed; and, after looking into her face, he crouched at her feet and licked the fingers that rested between his eyes.

I could but look on in wonder. The rain fell upon her uncovered hair, and the wind fluttered her plain dress; yet she appeared wholly unconscious of herself.

“Father,” she continued, turning toward the doorway, “we cannot send him farther on such a night.”

The farmer frowned. She went to him, and, placing the same hand upon his arm, looked up quietly into his face.

“He may have my room,” she added. “I can sleep with mother.”

“Well, well!” he said, though less roughly. “Ask your mother. She’ll have enough to say about it.”

The mother did indeed object. I heard her voice before I crossed the threshold.

“Another mouth to feed! And no bed aired, and supper cleared away! Your father ought to know better.”

“He has had no supper, mother,” said the girl.

“That’s no fault of mine.”

“No, mother; but he is hungry.”

There was no argument in the words, and yet they prevailed where argument might have failed. The maiden drew near, smoothed the woman’s disturbed cap, and kissed her weathered cheek.

“I’ll warm the supper, and there shall be no trouble for you.”

“Do as you please, Ruth,” replied the mother, with a softened countenance. “You always do.”

Thus I learned her name; and thus, through agencies so apparently insignificant—a touch, a look, a few gently spoken words—I obtained shelter where authority or payment might have pleaded in vain.

The room into which I was admitted was large and low, with a broad fireplace at one end. The farmer sat near the blaze, smoking a short pipe; his wife moved noisily among the dishes; two boys struggled over a broken whip; and a little girl, perhaps five years old, cried bitterly because she had been sent from the table for disobedience.

“Stop that noise!” exclaimed the mother.

The child screamed louder.

“I’ll give you something to cry for,” said the farmer.

He made a movement as if to rise; but Ruth, who was placing food before me, turned her head.

“Let me, father.”

She crossed the room, knelt beside the little offender, and drew her close. The child resisted, declared that she hated everybody, and struck at the arm encircling her. Ruth neither threatened nor reproved her.

“You are tired, Lucy,” she said. “Your heart is tired too. Come, let us rest it.”

What mysterious meaning the child discovered in these words I cannot tell. Her clenched hand opened; her head sank upon Ruth’s shoulder; and presently the angry cries were changed to sobs, the sobs to sighs, and the sighs to peaceful breathing. Ruth carried her to a bench near the fire, covered her with a shawl, and returned to her household duties as if nothing remarkable had occurred.

The boys, meanwhile, had renewed their quarrel. The elder twisted the whip from his brother and pushed him violently against the wall.

“I’ll break your head!” he cried.

“Tom!” said Ruth.

It was not a loud voice. The farmer’s command would have shaken the rafters; hers scarcely crossed the room. Yet the boy stopped.

“He tore my whip,” he muttered.

“Perhaps he did. But you will not hurt him.”

She laid her fingers lightly upon his closed fist. He looked first at them and then at her face; the resentment died out of his own, and he dropped the whip.

“He may have it,” he said, with the air of making a great sacrifice.

“No,” replied the younger boy, suddenly magnanimous. “We’ll mend it together.”

Here was another victory; and I began to ask myself whether some charm lay hidden in that delicate hand. The thought was childish, no doubt, yet men have believed in magic upon evidence far less persuasive.

After supper, a neighbour entered to escape the storm and warm himself before proceeding homeward. He and my host soon fell into political discussion. What question divided them I have forgotten; perhaps it was of great importance then, though time has long since buried it beneath a score of later controversies. The neighbour praised a certain public officer; the farmer denounced him as a rogue. One assertion brought another, voices rose, fists struck the table, and opinions gradually hardened into personal rancour.

“You call me a liar again,” cried the neighbour, “and I’ll teach you manners!”

“You!” shouted the farmer, springing up. “I’d like to see you try!”

The mother caught one of the younger children and drew back. The boys stared eagerly, delighted by the prospect of violence. I was about to interfere, deeming it best to prevent a quarrel whose consequences might be serious, when Ruth glided between the two men.

She did not rebuke them. She merely rested her hand upon her father’s sleeve.

“Father,” she said, “Mr. Hale must not go home angry on such a night.”

“He called me—”

“I know. He did not mean it as you think.”

Then she turned to the neighbour.

“And you know father speaks quickly when he is earnest. Sit down again. The cider is warm.”

There was a moment of silence. The neighbour looked foolishly at the floor; the farmer rubbed his chin. Ruth stood between them—not opposing either, not defending either, but seeming to draw from both that better nature which passion had obscured.

“I reckon I spoke too sharp,” said Mr. Hale.

“And I reckon I did,” replied the farmer. “Sit down, neighbour.”

In five minutes they were discussing the harvest; in ten, they laughed together. Had a strong man thrust himself between them, both might have turned upon him; had a moralist delivered a discourse, neither would have listened. But the spell of gentleness had gone forth, and the turbulent waters were still.

Before retiring, I saw Ruth move quietly through the room, setting every small disorder right. She drew her mother’s chair nearer the fire, placed a cushion behind her father’s shoulders, mended the child’s broken toy, and carried a saucer of food to the dog outside. Nothing that she did was important in itself; nothing appeared designed for observation. Yet each little act seemed a thread in the invisible bond which held that rough household together.

I slept soundly in the room she had yielded to me, and rose at dawn. The rain had passed, though heavy clouds still travelled across the pale sky. My horse’s lameness had increased, and the farmer offered to send me to the village in his small cart.

“Tom!” he called. “Bring out Dapple.”

The pony called Dapple was a shaggy, stubborn creature, with a thick neck and a dull eye. Whether he disliked the cold morning, the muddy road, or merely the authority exercised over him, I cannot say; but he refused to leave the stable.

Tom pulled at the halter.

“Come along, you brute!”

Dapple planted his feet.

The farmer seized the rope, uttered a harsh command, and dragged with all his strength. The pony moved half a step, then threw himself backward.

“Fetch the whip!” cried the farmer.

Tom obeyed with alacrity. The whip descended once—twice—three times. Dapple’s ears lay flat; his eyes flashed; and he kicked so violently that we all sprang aside.

“Beat him again!” shouted the farmer.

The scene had become painful. Force had awakened force; anger had met resistance, and resistance increased with every blow. The pony, who at first had been merely obstinate, was now dangerous.

Then Ruth appeared at the stable door.

“Please don’t strike him, father.”

“He’s got to learn who’s master.”

“He is frightened.”

“Frightened! He’s ugly—that’s what he is.”

She came forward slowly. Dapple watched her, trembling, with his head raised and the white of his eye showing. She spoke his name, not as a command, but almost as a greeting.

“Dapple—poor fellow.”

She touched his neck.

At once his head descended. She stroked the place where the whip had fallen, murmuring words too low for me to hear. The pony breathed heavily, nosed her sleeve, and stood quiet.

“Now come,” she said.

Taking the halter loosely in her hand, she walked from the stable. Dapple followed as meekly as a lamb.

The farmer stared after them. Tom concealed the whip behind his back. As for me, I confess that the incident affected me more deeply than its simplicity would seem to warrant. All the morning’s violence, with its noise, effort, and pain, had accomplished nothing; a soft word and a compassionate touch had conquered without humiliation.

When the cart was ready, I took leave of the family. Ruth was nowhere to be seen, having gone, her mother said, to carry broth to an aged woman who lived beyond the hill. I expressed to the farmer my astonishment at the influence she possessed.

“A strange power your daughter has,” said I. “Dog, child, man, and beast—none seems able to resist her.”

He looked toward the road by which she had disappeared. His heavy features softened, and for a moment he was silent.

“There ain’t much strange about it,” he answered at last. “She’s good. Everybody and everything knows it—and loves her.”

The explanation was brief, but it was sufficient.

As the cart bore me away, I reflected upon the events of that single night. We speak much of power, and commonly mean by it the ability to compel. We admire the strong arm, the stern will, the loud command; yet how often do these awaken only fear, resentment, or rebellion! There is another potency, quieter and more enduring: the power which does not bruise, because it seeks not victory for itself; which enters closed hearts without breaking them; which makes obedience free, and repentance unashamed.

Ruth’s hand had no magic in it, save the magic of a loving spirit. Its touch was gentle because her heart was kind; her voice soothed because no selfish anger sharpened it. Even the brute creature felt that she came not to master, but to understand.

And is there not a lesson for us all in this? The world is full of stubborn wills, wounded hearts, and angry voices. Shall we meet them only with the whip and the threat? Or shall we remember that gentleness is not weakness, but strength disciplined by love? Of all the instruments entrusted to human beings, none is so often despised, and none, rightly used, is more powerful, than a gentle hand.

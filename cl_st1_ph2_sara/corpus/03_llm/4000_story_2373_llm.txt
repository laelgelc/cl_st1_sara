# The Romance of Number Fifty-Six

It was upon the fifteenth of January—an anniversary which I have since learned to regard with emotions curiously divided between tenderness and amusement—that I found myself in the back room of Ah-Yen’s laundry, drinking a species of tea whose fragrance suggested violets and whose flavour suggested nothing that I had hitherto encountered in either the vegetable or mineral kingdom.

Outside, the winter rain whispered along the panes; within, a tallow candle leaned disconsolately over its brass socket and shed a wavering illumination upon the bare table, two wooden chairs, a cold iron stove, and the innumerable shadows which hung like black draperies from the rafters. Ah-Yen sat opposite me, smoking a long pipe. For some minutes he had preserved an unaccustomed silence, and I, being unwilling to disturb that grave inward ceremony by which a man prepares himself for confidence, had confined my attention to the candle and the tea.

At length he drew from beneath his coat a folded paper, which he opened with care and bestowed upon the table between us.

It contained the portrait of a young man.

I use the word portrait in a liberal sense. The face was oval, the hair abundant, the nose Roman, the mouth Greek, and the chin expressive of a moral firmness which the eyes, being full of infinite sadness, appeared to contradict. No living youth, I thought, had ever combined so many national excellences in a single countenance.

“You have never shown me this before,” said I.

“No,” replied Ah-Yen. “I do not often show him. To-day is the anniversary.”

“Of his death?”

“Of what I must believe to have been his death.”

There was something in the qualification which interested me. Moreover, I had recently been reading the publications of a favourite novelist, in whose pages a gentleman of extraordinary penetration deduced the whole career of a stranger from a patch upon his sleeve or the clay adhering to his boot. The conversation, therefore, seemed to offer possibilities of an analytical nature.

“Was he a relation?” I asked.

Ah-Yen shook his head.

“A friend, then?”

“My best friend.”

“And yet you are not certain that he died?”

“I never saw him,” said Ah-Yen.

I confess that I looked at him in some astonishment. He received my scrutiny without resentment, folded his hands upon the table, and contemplated the imaginary young man with so melancholy an affection that I had not the heart to smile.

“You find it strange,” he continued, “that I should speak of friendship where there had been neither clasp of hand, exchange of speech, nor even meeting of eyes. Yet acquaintance is not always intimacy. There are men whom we encounter daily and never know; there are others whose souls are revealed to us by signs which the careless would throw into a basket and send out with the washing.”

“The washing?”

“Precisely. He was Number Fifty-Six.”

He leaned back, and the candle, catching the thin smoke of his pipe, set a pale cloud between us. When he spoke again his voice had assumed the measured cadence of one who has often repeated a mournful history to himself, perfecting its periods in solitude.

“You know,” said he, “that in my profession a customer is not at first a man, but a number. The world may call him by a name honoured in Parliament, whispered in drawing-rooms, or inscribed upon the brass door of a legal chamber; to us he is Seven, Forty-Two, or Ninety-Eight. Yet numbers, like masks, conceal only from those who are content not to look beneath them.

“Fifty-Six entered my life upon a Saturday in October, six years ago. I did not then perceive the importance of the event. A bundle was left at the outer counter by a boy, together with the written direction and the usual deposit. It contained two shirts, four collars, three pairs of cuffs, a cotton night garment, one handkerchief, and a pair of socks.

“There was nothing extraordinary in this inventory; nevertheless, even then I observed certain indications. The shirts were inexpensive but well chosen. They had been mended at the elbow, not by a professional hand, but by some woman who cared more for durability than elegance. One collar was slightly frayed, while the other was new and of modest height. Upon the left cuff appeared several figures in pencil—‘3x,’ a fraction, and what seemed to be the beginning of a proposition concerning two trains which departed from different stations at unequal hours.

“‘A student,’ I said to myself, ‘living away from home, economical through necessity, and preparing for an examination.’

“My assistant suggested that the figures might have been written by a clerk calculating the price of herrings. I dismissed the suggestion. A clerk engaged in herrings does not employ algebra, and if he does, he should not.

“The following Saturday brought three shirts. On the cuff of one were written the words: ‘Custom—consideration—acceptance.’ On another: ‘No liability without—’ The sentence ended at the buttonhole.

“Then the truth became clear. He was at the university, but his studies were not mathematical. He had turned his attention to law.

“You may ask why the first cuff contained algebra. I answer that his preliminary examination had required it. You may ask why his linen was sent to me rather than washed within his college. I answer that he lodged outside the gates. You may ask what university he attended. That I could not determine with certainty, for the stains of one university resemble, to the unpractised eye, those of another; but the point was immaterial. The young man himself had already begun to emerge from anonymity.

“During the winter I followed his progress. Week after week the cuffs bore compressed memorials of knowledge: maxims in Latin, dates, references to celebrated cases, fragments of legal definition. He had a habit of writing upon the inner surface, no doubt during lectures, where the notes escaped the observation of the professor. That fact alone showed ingenuity. A dull student writes upon paper, which anybody may read; a gifted one converts his apparel into a private library.

“His means, I judged, were narrow. He sent no waistcoats, and his handkerchiefs were few. The shirts, though clean when they left me, returned with a regularity which testified less to abundance than to industry. Sometimes there was ink upon the fingers of the cuffs; sometimes candle grease upon the sleeve. Once a shirt-front was faintly scorched.

“‘He has fallen asleep over his books,’ I said. ‘Ambition has exhausted the body, though it cannot subdue the mind.’

“I confess that from that evening I felt an interest in him which the receipt of his weekly account could not wholly explain. I had come to this country with little besides a box, two excellent irons, and the conviction that starch, properly understood, was the foundation of civilized society. My days were occupied; my evenings were not. Other men sought society in public houses or clubs. I found mine in the linen basket.

“Do not laugh. There is more character in a shirt than in many faces. The face has learned concealment; the shirt has not. A man may tell you that he is sober, prudent, faithful, and free from vanity, while his cuffs, collar, and waistcoat, speaking independently, refute him in every particular.

“At Christmas, Fifty-Six’s washing ceased for three weeks. I knew that he had gone home. When it returned, there came with it six shirts instead of three, and one of them had been repaired with thread identical to that used in the first bundle. His mother was still living.

“That knowledge touched me deeply. I pictured the modest house, the shaded lamp, the mother bending over the torn seam while her son spoke with hopeful embarrassment of his studies. The thread was brown where white would have been proper; they were poor. Yet the stitches were close and firm; they loved one another.

“Spring arrived. The examination drew near. I knew it by the increasing multitude of notes and by the irregularity of the bundles. One Saturday there were five shirts; on the next, only one. What did this signify? That in the first week he had changed frequently because anxiety induced perspiration, while in the second he had remained for several days in one shirt because intellectual concentration made him indifferent to appearances.

“Then came a bundle of unusual magnitude—seven shirts, eleven collars, and a white waistcoat spotted with wine.

“He had passed.

“The wine was not of a fine quality; but youth, when it drinks to victory, does not inquire too closely into the year. Upon the waistcoat was a smear of mustard, upon one cuff the impression of a cigar, and in the pocket of a light overcoat which accompanied the washing I discovered a paper rosette bearing the words ‘Honour to Our Champion.’ His companions had given a supper. He had made a speech. He had perhaps been carried upon their shoulders. I sat in this room until midnight, smoking and thinking of him, and I assure you that I shared his triumph.

“The change in his circumstances was gradual but unmistakable. The modest collars grew taller. Coloured neckties began to appear. A pair of cuffs fastened by imitation gold links was followed, in due course, by cuffs requiring links of genuine silver. He had entered legal chambers; perhaps as a pupil, perhaps as a junior clerk awaiting admission. His first professional income was small, but hope enlarged it.

“For some months his life proceeded with the beautiful regularity of a well-kept ledger. There were three shirts each week, four in warm weather, and occasionally a blue flannel garment which showed that he rowed upon the river or walked in the country. He was diligent, respectable, and alone.

“Then love came.”

Ah-Yen paused, and for the first time since beginning his narrative he relit his pipe. Outside, a cab rolled through the rain with a hollow and diminishing thunder. I glanced at the portrait, whose eyes seemed to have grown sadder.

“You discovered that from a shirt?” I asked.

“From six,” replied Ah-Yen, “and a handkerchief.

“It was in April. The winter had been long, but on that Saturday the sun entered the shop and rested upon the counter as though it, too, had brought a parcel. Fifty-Six’s bundle contained four white shirts of a quality superior to any he had hitherto possessed. The fronts were severely plain, the cuffs narrow, and the collars neither so low as to suggest timidity nor so high as to imply conceit. There was also a crimson silk handkerchief.

“Now a young man may buy one white shirt from necessity, two from prudence, and three from extravagance; but when he purchases four at the beginning of spring, together with a crimson handkerchief, he is in love.

“The next bundle confirmed it. There were traces of violet powder upon a shoulder, a drop of tea upon one cuff, and, caught in the button of the other, a single golden thread.

“‘He has been in a drawing-room,’ I said. ‘The lady wore a pale dress embroidered at the sleeves.’

“My assistant maintained that the powder came from a barber and the thread from a tobacco pouch. I told him that if Providence had designed him for the interpretation of evidence, it would not have given him the imagination of a flat-iron.

“In the succeeding weeks the signs multiplied. The shirts became more numerous, for he changed before his evening visits. The collars rose by half an inch. Never underestimate half an inch in a collar. It may represent an increase of confidence, a rise in income, or the desire to impress a woman whose opinion has suddenly become more important than comfort.

“He had begun to wear flowers. I found green stains near the buttonhole and once the faint brown outline of a crushed stem. His gloves—though gloves are not ordinarily within my province—were sent by mistake in a coat pocket, and upon the right one was a rent which had been neatly sewn with pale-blue silk.

“She had mended it.

“Ah, my friend, the smallest stitch may bind two destinies! I saw them then as clearly as I see you: the young lawyer with his grave brow and Roman nose; the gentle girl bending over the glove, while he, pretending to examine a picture upon the wall, watched her reflection in the glass. She was fair. I had decided that from the golden thread. Her eyes were blue. The silk established it. She was accomplished, for the stitches were minute; affectionate, for she had taken trouble; and not wealthy, for a rich girl would have ordered her servant to mend the glove and the result would have been inferior.

“Summer strengthened his happiness. Seven shirts came regularly each Saturday. There were white waistcoats, light-grey trousers, boating flannels, and neckties so cheerful that a less charitable observer might have called them gaudy. Once there was grass upon both knees.

“He had proposed.

“I do not pretend that every man who kneels upon grass is offering marriage. Some seek lost coins; some examine insects; some merely fall. But consider the circumstances. The season, the white waistcoat, the grass upon the knees, and, above all, a circular stain upon the breast where a small head had rested—what conclusion could reason admit except one?

“She had accepted him.

“I closed the shop early that night and walked beyond the town, where the river lies between the meadows. It pleased me to think that somewhere under the same evening sky my unknown friend walked with his betrothed. I had never seen him; I had never heard his voice; yet his happiness had become a lamp in my own solitude.

“The engagement lasted through June and July. Its history was written in spotless linen. False shirt-fronts, which he had occasionally worn during the poverty of his student days, disappeared entirely. He would no longer practise deception, even in dress. There was moral elevation in the change.

“In August came the first shadow.

“The bundle contained only five shirts.

“You may say that five are sufficient for a week. Materially, perhaps; emotionally, no. He had been sending seven. Two shirts had vanished. Either there had been two fewer meetings, or at two meetings he had not considered it necessary to change. The latter hypothesis was inconsistent with love in its period of hope. Therefore, they had met less often.

“One collar was broken at the edge, as though torn impatiently from the neck. The crimson handkerchief, absent for many weeks, returned. A white waistcoat bore upon its front a grey streak which I identified as rain.

“They had quarrelled in a garden and he had walked home alone.

“The next week seven shirts returned, and my fears subsided. Youthful lovers quarrel that they may enjoy reconciliation. One of the shirts carried a trace of face powder near the shoulder. She had wept and been forgiven.

“But the reconciliation was incomplete. In September the number fell again—to four. The collars grew lower. The neckties lost their gaiety. A blue flannel shirt, hitherto associated with happy excursions, returned with mud upon one sleeve and a tear across the breast. He had gone alone to some place connected with their former joy and had thrown himself upon the earth in despair.

“I suffered with him. Each Saturday I awaited the boy who brought his bundle. The bell above the outer door would ring, and my heart would answer it. If the parcel was large, I breathed again; if small, the room darkened.

“October brought a severe crisis. Among three shirts appeared a false front.

“Think of it! The man who, in happiness, had clothed himself in integrity had returned to sartorial deceit. What moral disintegration lay before me! Poverty may excuse a false front; despair adopts it as a symbol.

“Moreover, the laundry account, formerly settled each month, remained unpaid for six weeks. His professional work was neglected. Grief had invaded the chambers of law. Briefs lay unopened; clients waited in corridors; learned seniors shook their heads. He, meanwhile, sat at his desk drawing her initials upon blotting paper.

“I knew the initials.”

“How?” I asked.

“Upon one cuff,” said Ah-Yen, “there appeared the letters ‘E. M.,’ enclosed in a heart and crossed twice through.”

“Could they not have referred to a legal case?”

“Legal cases are not enclosed in hearts.”

The rebuke was so just that I apologized.

“By November,” he continued, “hope had nearly departed. There came two shirts, one collar, no cuffs, and a black necktie. The tie had been knotted badly. She had returned his letters.

“You observe that I say letters though none appeared in the washing. Their absence proved the fact. Had he retained them, scraps of paper, sealing wax, or ribbon must eventually have accompanied a pocket. Nothing did. Therefore he had destroyed them.

“On the following Saturday I received six handkerchiefs.

“Of what my poor friend suffered during that week I can give you no idea. Six handkerchiefs! And all of them crumpled beyond ordinary usage. One bore marks of teeth. A man does not bite linen unless the heart is resisting an intolerable cry.

“I pictured him pacing his room at midnight, the fire dead, the lamp untrimmed, while the city slept in ignorance of his anguish. Perhaps her family had opposed the match. Perhaps a wealthier rival had appeared. Perhaps she had discovered that his income, though sufficient for four white shirts, could not support the establishment she desired. I was at first inclined to blame her; but justice forbade a premature verdict.

“The next evidence compelled one.

“A shirt returned bearing upon its cuff a spot of wax—scarlet wax, such as is used upon formal invitations. In the pocket was a minute fragment of engraved card. Only three letters remained: ‘—riage.’

“She had sent him an invitation to her marriage.

“Cruelty could go no further.

“Again my assistant argued that the fragment might have borne the word ‘carriage.’ I pointed out that no young man bites six handkerchiefs because a carriage has been announced. He had no answer.

“December descended upon him. The season which had once restored him to his mother now found him solitary. He did not go home. Had he done so, the familiar brown thread would have reappeared. Instead there came coarse shirts purchased ready-made, their buttons insecure, their seams without affection.

“His mother, I concluded, was dead.

“Misfortune seldom travels alone. Love had betrayed him; ambition had failed; now the last tenderness of home was extinguished. His laundry diminished to one shirt a week. Sometimes the shirt was worn for several days. Once it arrived without a single button at the throat. His collar was the lowest I had ever known him to wear. The proud young lawyer who had faced the future in silver links had ceased to care whether the world beheld his neck.

“I tried to help him.

“You know that it is against our regulations to communicate with customers except upon matters of account; yet I placed in his parcel a fresh handkerchief, uncharged. It was white, with a narrow border. On the paper about it I wrote: ‘Courage. Night is not eternal.’

“The handkerchief did not return.

“For a little while I hoped this meant recovery. The next bundle contained three shirts, one of them white and almost new. There was a flower stain at the buttonhole. Had she repented? Had he met another whose gentleness would heal the wound? I dared not believe it, yet I watched.

“The week after, nothing came.

“Nor the next.

“On the third Saturday a bundle arrived so large that the boy could scarcely carry it. It contained every article I had ever associated with Fifty-Six: the student shirts, the high collars of ambition, the boating flannels of love, the false fronts of despair, the crimson handkerchief, the white waistcoat stained at his celebration, and much else besides. It was as though his whole life had been gathered from drawers and cupboards and cast before me for judgment.

“At the bottom lay one shirt.

“I shall never forget it.”

Ah-Yen’s voice sank, and his pipe, which had long since gone out, trembled slightly between his fingers.

“It was a white shirt, of the finer kind purchased when he first fell in love. Across the breast spread a great crimson stain. Near the heart the linen was pierced by a small round hole, blackened at the edge.

“I understood.

“He had put on the shirt in which he had been happy. Perhaps he wished to die dressed for that vanished season. He had seated himself at his table, placed her portrait before him, written a few calm words to the world, and fired a pistol into his heart.

“The parcel contained all his washing because the landlady, clearing his room after the inquest, had sent everything together without knowledge of its history. There could be no other explanation.

“I washed the shirt myself.

“At first I thought to preserve the stain; it seemed the final signature beneath his tragic record. But professional duty prevailed. I applied cold water, then spirits, then a preparation of my own. The crimson faded slowly. It resisted me near the seams and remained longest about the wound. When at last the cloth was white, I felt as though I had erased the only message he had ever addressed to me.

“The hole I could not remove.

“No laundry came the following Saturday, nor on any Saturday thereafter. His account remained unpaid. The boy who had formerly brought the bundles did not return, and the written address had been lost during a change at the counter. Fifty-Six was dead.

“A year later, upon this date, I drew the portrait.”

He touched the paper gently.

“You had never seen him,” I said. “How did you determine his features?”

“From his linen.”

I waited for further explanation.

“A high collar,” he continued, “requires a long neck; a long neck is seldom associated with a round face. His cuffs showed him right-handed, yet several stains fell toward the left, proving an energetic gesture; this suggested a prominent nose. The quality of his student repairs indicated gentleness, which I placed in the mouth. Ambition gave him the chin; suffering, the eyes. The hair I bestowed upon him because the forehead appeared too large without it.”

I could not deny that the reasoning possessed a certain architectural completeness. It was, indeed, an airy castle; but every balcony corresponded with a window, every tower rose from a plausible foundation, and the whole had been inhabited so long by Ah-Yen’s affections that to call it empty would have seemed an act of vandalism.

For some time neither of us spoke.

The candle guttered; the rain had ceased; from the outer shop came the faint medicinal perfume of soap and hot starch. Ah-Yen folded the portrait and returned it beneath his coat.

“You think me foolish,” he said.

“On the contrary,” I replied, “I think you have shown a faculty of observation which many celebrated investigators might envy.”

“Observation is nothing,” said he. “It is the heart which joins the facts.”

That sentence remained with me after I had left the laundry.

The air outside was keen, and the wet pavement shone beneath the lamps. I walked slowly toward my lodgings, reviewing the strange history I had heard. Certain portions of it seemed to awaken faint and elusive associations, as when one enters an unfamiliar house yet recognizes in its passage the scent of childhood.

I, too, had studied law. I, too, had been careless enough to write notes upon my cuffs during lectures. Six years before, being somewhat reduced in circumstances, I had sent my linen to a laundry in that very district. These coincidences, though curious, were scarcely alarming. Hundreds of young men study law; scores write upon their cuffs; all, unless they possess devoted sisters, must eventually employ a laundry.

Still, as I proceeded, other recollections emerged.

There had been a supper after my examination. A white waistcoat had suffered severely from wine and mustard. I had once bought four shirts in April, not because I was in love, but because a dealer offered them at a reduction when purchased together. The crimson handkerchief had been forced upon me by an aunt who believed strong colours suitable to a pale complexion.

I stopped beneath a lamp.

The powder upon the shoulder—I remembered it. A barber, with an enthusiasm exceeding his skill, had emptied half a box upon me before I could protest. The golden thread had come, if I mistake not, from the cord of a tobacco pouch. As for the glove repaired in pale-blue silk, I had mended it myself, using the only thread obtainable from my landlady; the stitches had been minute because I was ashamed of them, not because the work had been performed by a fair-haired girl with blue eyes.

The grass upon both knees admitted of an explanation more humiliating than romance. I had lost a sixpence after a picnic and sought it with an industry sharpened by poverty. The circular mark upon my breast had been left by the damp bottom of a beer mug.

There had, it was true, been a young lady.

Her initials were not E. M., nor were her eyes blue; and though I admired her for nearly a fortnight, I never proposed marriage. Our relations ended without tragedy when she informed me that she preferred my friend Barlow, who possessed a better tenor voice and an uncle in the Colonial Office. I had drawn E. M. inside a heart as a memorandum of the maxim *ex turpi causa non oritur actio*, abbreviating it in a manner which no doubt obscured its legal origin. I crossed it out because the lecturer altered the subject.

The six handkerchiefs had accompanied a violent cold.

The fragment ending in “riage” had undoubtedly come from an advertisement for a second-hand carriage which I had copied on behalf of Barlow’s uncle. I had bitten one handkerchief while extracting a stubborn cork from a bottle of liniment.

My pulse quickened as memory advanced, washing away romance with an efficiency equal to Ah-Yen’s preparations.

The brown repairs had not been my mother’s. My mother, who was then living abroad and who regarded sewing as an outrage upon woman’s progress, had never mended anything in her life. The stitches were the work of Mrs. Briggs, my landlady, who used brown thread upon all colours from motives of economy.

The rise and fall of my collars had expressed no moral development. Fashion raised them; discomfort lowered them. The false shirt-fronts had returned because my quarterly allowance was late.

My supposed ruin in December had been merely an attack of influenza combined with the conclusion of my clerkship. I had not gone home because there was no home to go to; my mother was travelling in Italy and sending me postcards of monuments with illegible comments upon the weather.

Then came the final recollection.

I had left town suddenly to assist an elderly cousin in settling an estate. Before departing, I gathered every washable article in my rooms and sent the enormous accumulation to the laundry. Among them was a shirt upon which I had overturned a bottle of red copying ink. Near the breast was a round blackened hole produced, not by a pistol, but by the falling ash of a cigar smoked while I was reading in bed.

The shirt had returned spotless, except for the burn.

I had worn it for another year.

My absence had lasted several months. On returning, I moved to another quarter and employed a laundry nearer my chambers. There was no death, no inquest, no faithless bride, no extinguished legal genius. There had been only ink, influenza, cigar ash, a lost sixpence, and an innocent quantity of dirty linen.

At the corner of my street I stood motionless, staring through the clear winter dark.

A final uncertainty remained. Numbers are treacherous things, and six years form a mist through which the past cannot always be read with confidence. Yet I had preserved, in an old dispatch box, several faded receipts from that period. Upon reaching my rooms, I lit the lamp, opened the box, and searched among leases, lecture notes, unpaid bills, and letters whose importance had diminished more rapidly than their dust increased.

At last I found it.

The paper was yellow, the ink faint, the total three shillings and ninepence. At its head, enclosed in a blue circle, stood the number:

**56.**

I sat for a long while with the receipt in my hand.

My first impulse was to return immediately to Ah-Yen, place the evidence before him, and restore myself to life. It seemed intolerable that a respectable man, moderately prosperous and in passable health, should be commemorated annually as a suicide, particularly by a friend to whom he had never been introduced.

I imagined the interview.

“My dear Ah-Yen,” I should say, “your student did not die. He sits before you.”

Then I should explain the barber’s powder, the tobacco-pouch thread, the picnic sixpence, the cold, the advertisement, the copying ink, and the cigar. Each proud tower of deduction would collapse beneath some petty fact. The fair-haired girl would vanish into a badly repaired glove; the engagement would become a beer mug; the pistol-shot would dwindle to an ember dropped through drowsiness.

Truth would triumph.

Yet what, precisely, would truth gain?

Ah-Yen had constructed from my laundry a life more ardent, more disciplined, and infinitely more affecting than the one I had actually led. His Fifty-Six loved deeply, suffered nobly, struggled with ambition, cherished his mother, and died with tragic consistency. I had passed my examination by moderate exertion, admired a girl for thirteen days, caught a cold, spilled ink, and gone to visit a cousin about an estate.

Moreover, the imaginary youth had been Ah-Yen’s companion through many solitary evenings. His hope had brightened the dim back room; his despair had called forth a kindness which asked neither acknowledgment nor reward. Even the unreturned handkerchief—the gift whose exhortation I had never seen, because it had probably been placed in another customer’s parcel—had been offered from the heart.

To destroy such a friendship merely because one of the friends had never existed seemed an unnecessarily literal employment of fact.

I put the receipt back into the dispatch box.

Each year since then, upon the fifteenth of January, I have contrived to visit Ah-Yen. We sit in the bare room behind the laundry while the candle leans over the table and the winter weather troubles the panes. He brings out the portrait; I study with proper gravity the Roman nose, the Greek mouth, the chin of ambition, and the eyes into which he has put all the suffering I was fortunate enough to escape.

Sometimes he adds a recollection.

“Well do I remember,” he will say, “the courage with which poor Fifty-Six endured his examination.”

And I, who remember chiefly an illicit note upon my cuff and a providential question concerning contracts, incline my head.

Or he says, “No woman could have forgotten a love so faithful.”

At this I sigh, though whether for the imaginary lady, for Ah-Yen, or for myself, I cannot absolutely determine.

I have never told him that Number Fifty-Six lives. Nor do I think I shall. There are truths which cleanse, and truths which merely bleach; and I have learned from my friend that a stain, interpreted with sufficient tenderness, may sometimes contain more humanity than a spotless fact.

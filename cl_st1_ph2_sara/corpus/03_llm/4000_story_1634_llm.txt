### The Long Road Through Montopolis

At six o’clock on an October evening, when the last saffron rays of the sun had climbed from the courthouse cupola and were withdrawing, with something of wounded dignity, from the second-story window of the *Montopolis Clarion*, I sat alone in my editorial chair and composed an obituary for a man who was not dead.

This was not, as strangers to journalism may suppose, an extraordinary undertaking. Old Major Pettigrew had been ill for three days; his son-in-law had purchased twenty inches of memorial space; and it is among the duties of a prudent editor to keep one column ahead of Providence. I had got the Major through the Mexican War, two marriages, the building of the Baptist annex, and a regrettable term in the legislature, and was considering whether “ripe in years and public honours” might survive the fact that everybody knew his age and nobody remembered his honours, when a shadow darkened the door.

The *Clarion* office possessed one window, two chairs, seven hundred pounds of type, a paste-pot of uncertain ancestry, and a door which had not shut properly since the flood of ’89. Any shadow entering there was therefore entitled to immediate attention.

The man on the threshold was old beyond the ordinary arithmetic of age. His coat hung upon him as ivy hangs upon a ruin; his trousers had accumulated the soils of several climates; and his shoes, cracked, curled, and whitish at the seams, suggested that their owner had walked over every road in the world and complained of none. His beard was long and grey, his face brown and scored, and his eyes shone under tangled brows with a brightness unpleasantly independent of the declining light.

He smelled of wet wool, tobacco, dust, and those back rooms in museums where objects too venerable to classify await the invention of a suitable label.

“Are ye the iditor?” he asked.

I admitted the charge.

He entered, closed the door by lifting it bodily on its hinges—a trick known only to myself and the office devil—and took the chair reserved for subscribers in arrears. The chair revolved beneath him. He went halfway round, came back, and fixed me with a look combining reproof and eternity.

“Me name,” he said, “is Michob Ader.”

I reached automatically for my pad. A newspaper man will take notes at his own execution if permitted to choose the headline.

“How do you spell Michob?”

“Spell it anny way ye like. It’s been spelled be betther men than ye, an’ worse. There was a monk in Bohemia put two h’s into it, an’ I knocked him down. I was younger thin.”

This had possibilities. The harvest festival had filled Tuesday’s local column, but Wednesday remained deficient except for a runaway Jersey cow and Mrs. Penfield’s sister from Waco. A very old stranger, properly handled, might furnish six inches under the heading, “A Venerable Pedestrian.”

“Where are you from, Mr. Ader?”

“From everywhere.”

“Immediately?”

“From Cincinnati.”

The firmamental darkness, which had now taken possession of the sky, was less discouraging than this answer.

“And where before Cincinnati?”

“Constantinople. Paris. Rome. Jerusalem. Thereabouts.”

“Commercial traveller?”

He leaned forward.

“I’m the Wanderin’ Jew.”

There are declarations which leave a professional interviewer without a suitable follow-up question. If a citizen says he has raised a beet weighing nineteen pounds, one asks whether the seed was imported. If he has seen a sea serpent, one inquires concerning the condition of his eyesight and the size of his flask. But immortality is a subject on which the ordinary forms of local journalism provide no guidance.

I put down my pencil.

“The Wandering Jew,” I repeated.

“The same,” said he. “Though there’s been impostors. Wan fellow in Prague made a comfortable livin’ out av me name till I exposed him. Another in New Orleans had cards printed. A black-haired felly he was, wid a wart on his nose. I niver trusted a man wid a wart where ye could see it.”

He removed a shapeless hat. His head was bald except for a few silver locks that strayed across it like late snow upon a hill. In repose he resembled Father Time’s younger brother after an unsuccessful search for employment.

“You claim,” I said, choosing the verb with editorial caution, “to have lived nearly two thousand years?”

“Claim, is it? I’ve lived them. Every wearisome wan, includin’ leap years. Do ye think I’d be takin’ credit for it?”

“And you cannot die?”

“I’ve tried.”

“How?”

“Every way that was respectable. I wint soldierin’. I wint sailin’. I stood under a church steeple in a thunder-storm wid a tin basin on me head. Nothin’. A cannon-ball took me through the middle at Waterloo, an’ all I got was dyspepsia.”

He sighed with the fatigued resentment of a taxpayer discussing the county board.

I resumed my pencil. The item was growing.

“What caused your—ah—condition?”

The old man’s comic alertness vanished. For an instant his eyes seemed to look not at me, nor at the office, but through both, along some immeasurable road.

“’Twas so commanded,” he said.

Outside, a cowbell sounded from the river pasture. The little noise came clear and solitary through the dusk.

“By whom?”

“By Him.”

There was no need to ask which Him.

“He was passin’ me door,” Ader continued, “carryin’ the cross. The soldiers were cursin’ Him, an’ the crowd was pushin’, an’ He was white wid the dust av the road. He leaned against me wall. ‘Let me rest,’ says He—not in the grand words the preachers use, but quiet, like a man askin’ a kindness av another man. An’ I—”

His fingers tightened upon the brim of his hat.

“Well?” I prompted.

“I struck Him. ‘Go on,’ says I. ‘Ye’ll rest whin ye’re dead.’”

The paste-pot, which had been making small digestive noises in the warmth, gave a sudden pop. I confess I started.

Ader looked down.

“He turned His eyes on me. There was no anger in them. That was the worst. ‘I go,’ says He, ‘but thou shalt wait till I return.’ Thin the soldiers drove Him on. An’ I’ve waited.”

The silence that followed was too long for comfort and too sincere for copy. I sharpened my pencil, though it needed no sharpening.

“You were a shoemaker, according to the legend.”

“I was. Good work, too. Sandals that would outlast a government. I made a pair for Pontius Pilate.”

This restored the atmosphere to a level at which I could breathe.

“Indeed?”

“Bad feet he had. Narrow at the heel, broad at the conscience.”

I wrote that down.

“Pilate,” Ader went on, “has been misrepresented. He wasn’t cruel by disposition. Weak, that was all, an’ troubled wid his household. His wife was forever dreamin’. Ye know the kind—gets up at breakfast an’ tells ye she saw a red bird flyin’ backward over a well, an’ thin ye mustn’t sign papers that day. She warned him about the trial, but he’d an appointment wid the aqueduct commissioners at noon an’ wanted the thing finished.”

“You attended the trial?”

“I was outside sellin’ heel straps. Business is business.”

He settled more comfortably in the revolving chair, turned one complete circle, and began to warm to history.

“I knew Nero, too. A pleasant young man before he took up music. Music has ruined more rulers than war. He couldn’t sing, but no wan had the constitutional courage to mention it. Rome didn’t burn because he wanted scenery for a poem, as they tell ye. His housekeeper left a pan av goose fat on the stove while she wint next door to discuss a divorce suit. Nero was practisin’ the fiddle, so naturally he smelled nothin’. By midnight six districts were gone, an’ the anti-tobacco society blamed the Christians because wan av them had been seen wid a cigar.”

“That account,” I remarked, “differs from the accepted authorities.”

“Authorities!” He snorted. “Men who came two hundred years later an’ wrote books in rooms. I was there wid a bucket.”

He proceeded through the centuries with magnificent disrespect for chronology and reputation. Cleopatra, according to him, had possessed a violent aversion to snakes but an unfortunate passion for patent complexion washes. Charlemagne snored so loudly that ambassadors mistook it for a declaration of war. Columbus discovered America while attempting to escape three Genoese creditors and spent the voyage insisting that the world was pear-shaped, “because he’d money invested in a map showin’ it so.” Shakespeare was a capable actor but borrowed jokes. Napoleon detested short jokes.

“I told him at Austerlitz,” said Ader, “‘Bonaparte,’ says I, ‘Russia is no place for a man who buys his overcoat ready-made.’ Would he listen? He would not. Great men niver listen till they’re on an island.”

The temptation to print him entire was considerable. I imagined Montopolis at breakfast, the Judge choking upon his buckwheat cakes, Reverend Spilman denouncing the degradation of sacred history, and the subscribers who paid in pumpkins demanding a second instalment. Yet there was in the old man’s talk an element that resisted the easy classification of drunkenness, lunacy, or genius. His anachronisms were flagrant, but his details sometimes arrived with the dust still on them. He described the colour of dawn on the Bosporus before the Turkish guns opened; the stink of wet leather in a London plague year; the tiny golden bees embroidered on a Frankish king’s cloak. He spoke of battles not as maps and dates, but as cold feet, lost buttons, horses screaming in mud, and men asking for their mothers in languages they had forgotten they knew.

Then he spoiled everything by asserting that Julius Caesar had once tried to sell him a corner lot.

After an hour I became impatient.

“Mr. Ader,” I said, “your reminiscences have a certain song-book liveliness, but they do not prove your identity.”

“Proof!” he cried. “What would prove it to ye? A receipted bill from Methuselah? A lock av Solomon’s hair? I had both, but a landlord in Smyrna took the box.”

“You might tell me something not found in books.”

He looked at me strangely.

“There’s plenty not in books.”

“What became of Pilate?”

That question, idly spoken, changed him.

The humour drained from his face. His eyes narrowed, and he raised one hand as if to ward off a sight approaching from behind my shoulder.

“Don’t ask.”

“Why not?”

“I saw him after.”

“After what?”

“After he died.”

The press in the rear room gave a settling creak. From the garden below came the dry whisper of cornstalks rubbing together.

“They buried him,” Ader said slowly, “but the earth wouldn’t hold him. They put him in a river, an’ the water boiled around the stone. Years later I found him in a mountain cleft where the snow niver melted. He was sittin’ upright, though there was little enough left to sit. His face was black an’ swollen; his fingers had worn to bone from washin’ one hand against the other. Washin’, always washin’. An’ when the moon came over the ridge, his eyes opened.”

The old man’s voice sank to a whisper.

“‘Is the blood gone?’ says he.”

A coldness passed over my scalp with the deliberate feet of an insect.

“What did you answer?”

“I told him yes.”

“Was it?”

“No.”

Ader covered his face. His shoulders shook.

I waited for the theatrical recovery, the wink, the request for fifty cents. None came. Instead a low sound escaped him—not the picturesque sob of the platform, but the broken, shameful breathing of an old man who has kept grief too long and has lost the art of releasing it.

“I struck Him,” he said. “He only wanted rest. Wan minute at me door, an’ I wouldn’t give it. I’ve walked the roads since thin. Through rain, through wars, through towns where no wan knew me. I hear His step behind me whin it’s quiet. I see His face in every poor soul that asks shelter. But the door is always shut, an’ me hand is always lifted.”

He raised his head. Tears had made white channels through the grime upon his cheeks.

“’Twas so commanded.”

My pad lay untouched. There are matters which a newspaper may expose without illuminating, and wounds which printer’s ink can only blacken.

I offered him money. He refused it with dignity. I offered food. He shook his head. When I offered whiskey, however, his immortality became less inflexible.

There was a bottle in the lower drawer, kept for snakebite, sudden chills, bereaved subscribers, and other emergencies recognized by the profession. I poured him a modest quantity. He drank it, looked reproachfully at the glass, and drank another.

“Whiskey,” he observed, “has changed for the worse since the Irish invented it.”

“You were present?”

“I held the horse.”

Ten minutes later he slept in the subscriber’s chair, his mouth open and his hat clasped to his breast. At eleven I woke him. He stared about the office with no recognition, muttered that he must go on, and stumbled down the stairs.

From the window I watched him cross the square. The moon had risen, laying a pale road over roofs, cottonwoods, and the white courthouse front. A flock of wild geese moved across it in a ragged wedge. Their cries descended thinly, like voices calling from an enormous distance.

Ader stopped and lifted both arms.

“Look!” he called. “There they go—the souls av them that stood by the cross! Pilate! The soldier! The thief! All wanderin’!”

Then he hurried after their shadows until the darkness beyond the livery stable received him.

I did not finish Major Pettigrew’s obituary. The Major, with his usual contrariness, recovered before morning.

At nine the next day I related the adventure to Tom Beasley, our compositor. Tom listened while distributing type, his lower lip thrust out in the manner of a man giving equal consideration to theology and punctuation.

“What’d you say his name was?”

“Michob Ader.”

Tom dropped an *e* into the wrong box.

“Long grey whiskers? Old army coat? Talks like he swallowed Ireland?”

“Yes.”

“That’s Mike O’Bader.”

“Who?”

“Mike O’Bader. Shoemaker. Shop down by the freight depot.”

I experienced the peculiar resentment of a journalist whose mystery has been locally identified before publication.

“Is he insane?”

“Only when he gets enough liquor. Then he’s the Wanderin’ Jew. Sometimes he’s been it three nights runnin’. Sheriff locked him up last spring for tellin’ Pharaoh to let his people go in front of the Elite Restaurant.”

“Why didn’t I know him?”

“He don’t come uptown sober, and you don’t go downtown ever.”

This was true, but impertinent.

By ten o’clock Montopolis had supplied the remaining particulars. Mike O’Bader was sixty-eight, perhaps seventy. He had repaired shoes for thirty years in a narrow shop beside the freight tracks. Ordinarily he was silent, industrious, and as harmless as a last year’s almanac. At intervals, generally after the receipt of pension money, he drank with mournful thoroughness and became Michob Ader, witness to history and victim of Heaven. Children followed him; adults laughed; the sheriff provided lodging when weather required. By Monday he returned to his bench and remembered nothing, or professed not to.

The explanation was complete, reasonable, and disagreeably inadequate.

Whiskey may confer courage, tenderness, eloquence, and an illusory friendship with the Chief of Police. I had not known it to provide a consistent mythology extending over thirty years. Nor did it explain the anguish in the man’s voice when he spoke of the shut door.

I visited his shop.

It was a dingy place with one clouded window. Boots and shoes hung from the walls like dark, mutilated birds. The air was thick with leather, glue, lamp smoke, and poverty. Mike sat bent over a child’s shoe, driving wooden pegs with a small hammer.

In daylight he looked older, smaller, and entirely mortal.

“Good morning, Mr. O’Bader.”

He glanced up.

“Mornin’.”

“I believe you called at my office last night.”

“Did I?”

“You said your name was Michob Ader.”

His hammer paused, but only for a fraction.

“I do be talkin’ foolish whin I drink.”

“You told me you were the Wandering Jew.”

“So they say.”

“Where did you learn the story?”

“Books, maybe.”

There were books on a shelf behind him: a torn Bible, a history of Rome, a volume of medieval legends, three patent-medicine circulars, and a railway timetable dated twelve years before. Dust lay upon them all except the Bible.

“Do you remember telling it?”

“No.”

“Do you remember saying that you struck Christ and refused Him rest?”

The hammer missed the peg and struck his thumb. He made no sound.

“I’ve work to do, sir.”

His face had gone grey.

I apologized and left. At the door I looked back. He was not working. He sat with the little shoe in his hand, staring at it as though it had brought him news.

For the next two days I made inquiries. Small towns disapprove of privacy as an urban affectation, and Montopolis could recall the courtship, debts, ailments, and political errors of every person who had lived within six miles of the courthouse since Reconstruction. Yet concerning Mike there was surprisingly little.

He had come from the East as a young man with a wife and one daughter. The wife died early. The girl, Ellen, grew up “wild,” which in Montopolis meant that she read novels, laughed loudly, and once wore a red ribbon to prayer meeting. At seventeen she disappeared with a travelling circus that had pitched its tents beyond Miller’s pasture.

There the story ended. Some said she became a bareback rider; others, an actress; Mrs. Judkins believed she married the man who swallowed swords, though she could produce no evidence beyond a lifelong distrust of cutlery.

“What happened afterward?” I asked.

Nobody knew.

On Saturday I found Uncle Abner Pike sunning himself behind the feed store. Uncle Abner was eighty-seven and remembered everything, although seldom in the order in which it occurred. He had a white beard, a cane polished by seventy years of argument, and the town’s respect, which he valued less than its chewing tobacco.

“Mike O’Bader?” he said. “Sure. Made me a pair of boots in ’76. Worst boots I ever owned. Never wore out.”

“I’m asking about his daughter.”

“Ellen.”

“You remember her?”

“Everybody did. Purty gal. Too purty for a town with that many women in it.”

“She ran away with a circus.”

“Ran away with a horse trainer. Circus just made it sound more damnable.”

“Did she ever return?”

Uncle Abner cut a careful crescent from a plug of tobacco.

“Once.”

The word was spoken so casually that I nearly passed it.

“When?”

“Must’ve been thirty-five years ago. Winter. Cold rain. She come in on the night train wearin’ a thin dress and shoes busted through. Had a baby, they said, but no baby with her. Maybe it died. Maybe there never was one. Town had both stories by breakfast.”

“What did she do?”

“Tried to get a room at Miz Carter’s boardinghouse. Miz Carter wouldn’t take her. Deacon Bell told her decent folks had daughters to consider. Some boys followed her down Main Street, laughin’. Men’ll forgive a sinner if she sins where they can’t see her.”

I felt the old man’s pale eyes upon me.

“Where did she go?”

“To Mike.”

The feed-store yard, with its sacks, barrels, and scratching hens, seemed suddenly to hold the attentive stillness of a courtroom.

“She went to his house after midnight,” Uncle Abner continued. “I lived across the lane. Heard knockin’. Heard her call, ‘Papa, let me in.’ Mike opened the door.”

“And?”

“He hit her.”

“With what?”

“His hand. Back of it, I reckon. She fell off the step. He told her she’d disgraced her mother and him, and she could go back to them she’d chosen.”

Uncle Abner examined the end of his cane.

“She asked just to stay till mornin’. Rain was near sleet. Said she was tired and had nowhere else. Mike shut the door.”

A freight train clanged beyond the warehouses. Each coupling struck the next with a diminishing series of iron blows.

“What became of her?”

“Found her two days later in the millpond. Reeds caught her dress.”

“Suicide?”

“Might’ve slipped. Might’ve meant it. Water don’t testify.”

“And Mike?”

“Buried her. Quit drinkin’ for near ten years. Then one night he got full and started hollerin’ he’d seen Jerusalem burn. Been wanderin’ ever since, in his way.”

I returned to the office by the long route.

Montopolis wore its Saturday respectability like a freshly ironed collar. Women bargained for beans before the grocery. Men discussed cotton prices beneath the awning of the bank. The church bell rehearsed one solemn stroke and stopped. Nobody mentioned Ellen O’Bader. The town had driven her from its doors, then entered the incident under “miscellaneous” and closed the account.

In my office the late sun was laying amber glory upon the paste-pot.

I took from the shelf a dictionary of legend and read the ancient tale again: the burdened figure pausing before a craftsman’s house; the request for rest; the blow; the refusal; the sentence of endless travel. Scholars disagreed about the wanderer’s name, his trade, his country, and the century in which the story first appeared. Scholars are fortunate. They can disagree about names and dates while leaving the wound untouched.

Mike had read the legend, certainly. Drink had loosened it from memory. Madness had dressed him in its rags. These were facts, or respectable substitutes for facts.

But why had the old fable entered him so completely?

Because somewhere in the frozen midnight of his mind a tired outcast still stood at his door.

She had come through contempt and rain. She had asked not forgiveness, not restoration, but one night’s shelter. He had raised his hand. He had told her to go on. Then he had shut the door and remained inside while she continued down the road toward dark water.

Afterward he made shoes for other people’s children.

That evening I saw Mike again. He was walking beside the railway, sober, carrying a sack of bread and leather scraps. His shoulders were bent, and his steps were short. Yet the straight rails before him caught the sunset and ran like two burning roads to the world’s edge.

“Mr. O’Bader,” I called.

He stopped but did not turn.

For one weak moment I considered telling him that I knew. It was the journalistic impulse in its most indecent form: the desire to open every closed room merely because one possesses a pencil. I said nothing.

A flock of geese passed high above us, going south. Mike lifted his head. Whether he saw birds, souls, or only the gathering weather, I cannot say.

“Long road,” he murmured.

Then he went on.

Years later, a young reporter who had heard some fragment of the story asked me whether Mike O’Bader truly believed himself to be the Wandering Jew.

I told him belief was not the point.

“Then he wasn’t?”

I looked past the courthouse, past the dingy shoemaker’s shop, toward the road where an old man had walked under the burden of one shut door.

“He is,” I said.

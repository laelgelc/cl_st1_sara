### THE ROMANCE OF AUGUST MOSES

When James Harcourt took charge of the little Native school at Te Rimu he was twenty-four, newly married, tolerably certain of the perfectibility of mankind, and possessed by what his wife called a literary ambition and what the inspector, who had read one of his articles, called “a tendency to adjectives.”

The school stood above a brown river which behaved quietly in summer and carried away fences in winter. Behind it were the master’s cottage, a lean-to washhouse, two peach-trees, and a hill covered in fern and flax; beyond the hill lay the pa, a grocery store, three whares, a meeting-house with a tin chimney, and most of the population of Te Rimu. The nearest township was twelve miles away by a road which crossed the river twice, lost itself in manuka, and emerged at last beside an accommodation-house where swaggies, drovers, committee-men, and horses were refreshed upon different terms.

Harcourt had come from the city with several books, a violin, a box of flower-seeds, and opinions regarding Universal Brotherhood. He believed, among other things, that the Māori was naturally truthful, children were naturally eager to learn, and poverty invariably ennobled those who endured it. He had read *Westward Ho!* at an age when such things are dangerous, and there remained in him a hunger for adventure which no school register could wholly satisfy.

Mrs Harcourt—Ellen—had fewer theories. She taught the infants, kept the accounts, baked bread twice weekly, and knew that the pump required three strokes before it gave water. She was kind without making a profession of it, which was perhaps why kindness cost her more than it cost James.

The first person to interest him at Te Rimu was Sarah Moses.

She was entered in the register as Sarah; the pa called her August, though nobody could explain why. One explanation was that she had been born in August; another, that she had been found in that month beneath a flax bush; a third, that her father had admired a man named Augustus who once drove the mail-coach. As her father was dead, her mother was dead, Augustus—if he had existed—was unavailable, and the flax bush maintained its proper silence, the matter remained among those mysteries which give distinction to a small settlement.

August was perhaps sixteen, perhaps nineteen. She was tall, broad-shouldered, untidy, and moved with a heavy deliberation which Harcourt at first interpreted as the repose of a primitive dignity. Her hair fell over her forehead; her dark eyes looked past a speaker rather than at him; her feet, when she attended school, were generally bare and commonly muddy. She sat in the senior class, could read slowly, wrote an enormous hand, and brooded.

She brooded over arithmetic, over sewing, over dinner, over the weather. If praised, she brooded; if corrected, she brooded more deeply; if left alone, she appeared to brood upon the injustice of not being noticed. The younger children feared her a little, but followed her when she went home, for she occasionally produced lollies from obscure folds of her dress.

There were stories about her.

The stories came first from the children, afterwards from the washerwoman, and finally, with additions, from old Rangi Heke, chairman of the School Committee, who said that August had been badly used by her relatives. She lived with an aunt, two uncles, three cousins, an old woman who might have been a grandmother, and a succession of babies whose parentage was clear to everyone except the babies. August worked, they said, and received little food. Her clothes were taken from her. Her aunt beat her. Her uncle sent her barefoot to fetch flour in winter. She slept near the door. A more experienced man would have divided these statements by four and waited for further evidence; Harcourt multiplied them by the dark magnificence of August’s eyes.

Here was tragedy at his threshold. Here, moreover—though he was ashamed of this part of the reflection and therefore dwelt upon it—was copy.

He saw a romance. He saw a silent orphan, oppressed by coarse relatives, hiding gratitude beneath a savage reserve; a young schoolmaster, representing civilisation at its gentlest, winning her confidence; a transformation through love, soap, and regular instruction; perhaps, at the end, a marriage to some worthy young farmer, with August looking back from a veranda upon the whare of her bondage. He had no intention of marrying her himself—Ellen’s presence made that unnecessary—but a novelist need not personally marry all his heroines.

One wet afternoon August remained after school. She had no shawl, and a bruise showed upon her arm. Harcourt observed it. Ellen observed him observing it.

“Who did that?” he asked.

August pulled down her sleeve.

“No one.”

“Was it your aunt?”

August brooded.

“You needn’t be afraid,” said Harcourt, gently. “We are your friends.”

“My aunt,” said August at last.

That settled it. Cruelty to an orphan was foreign, Harcourt felt, to the true communal spirit of the Māori; therefore the aunt was an exception, and exceptions, as everybody knows, are often the most useful facts in a romance.

He went up to the pa.

The rain had stopped, but water lay in the cart-ruts, pigs investigated the grass before the meeting-house, and smoke came sideways from the whares. August’s aunt, Mere Moses, received him upon a box. She was a stout, lively woman with an eye which took account of his boots, umbrella, watch-chain, and probable income before he had completed his greeting.

Harcourt spoke of August’s health, education, prospects, and the obligations imposed upon us by our common humanity.

Mere listened.

“She lazy,” she said.

“She is unhappy.”

“She eat plenty.”

“She says she is beaten.”

Mere laughed so loudly that two children and an uncle came to the door.

“You take her,” she said. “You like.”

Nothing could have been more convenient, though Harcourt did not then see that convenience may be shared by opposing parties. He returned to the cottage with August walking behind him, carrying a small sugar-bag containing one dress, a comb without half its teeth, and a broken photograph of an unknown soldier.

Ellen made up a bed in the washhouse room.

For three weeks the experiment was a triumph.

August washed herself, submitted to having her hair combed, wore one of Ellen’s old print frocks, and became, in Harcourt’s notebook, “almost handsome when the cloud of suspicion lifts.” She rose early, lit the kitchen fire, peeled potatoes, carried water, scrubbed the passage, and sat after tea with a mending-basket upon her knees. She spoke little, but her silence now appeared grateful rather than sullen. Once she smiled at Ellen. Harcourt recorded the smile.

He began a story called “The Child of the Pa.” Its heroine, Aroha, was slender where August was massive, swift where August was slow, and capable of expressing in three polished sentences emotions which August communicated by staring at the stove. This was not falsification; it was Art.

The Harcourts decided that, when they returned to Auckland at the end of their two-year term, August should go with them. She might enter domestic service, train as a nurse, or assist in some noble work among her people. Harcourt even pictured her—faithful and grateful retainer—bringing tea into his study while he corrected the proofs of “The Child of the Pa.”

Then the sugar began to go.

Sugar goes quickly in any household, and at first Ellen blamed the damp. Afterwards half a ham disappeared, together with tea, candles, a tin of golden syrup, two bars of soap, six handkerchiefs, and a packet of raisins reserved for Sunday pudding. August had been sent to the store with seven shillings and returned, after four hours, with tobacco instead of rice, no change, and a message that the grocer had made a mistake.

Harcourt went to the grocer.

“I never make mistakes that size,” said the grocer.

August explained that she had lost the change at the ford.

The next week she lost a pound of butter on the road; then a loaf; then Ellen’s scissors, though the road had no apparent connection with the scissors. Harcourt reminded himself that the conception of individual property develops slowly among simple communities. Ellen bought a lock for the pantry.

August brooded.

She had also taken to visiting the pa. She went to borrow a needle and remained five hours; went to carry a message and returned next morning; went to inquire after a sick cousin who, it transpired, had been fishing in excellent health. Her excuses were numerous but lacked architecture. She had been detained by rain when no rain had fallen, by an old woman who was dead, and by a horse which had followed her. On one occasion she stated that she had not gone to the pa at all, although Harcourt had met her coming out of Mere’s whare with the missing tablecloth under her arm.

“That is our tablecloth,” he said.

August looked at it.

“No.”

“It has Ellen’s initials on the corner.”

August examined the initials as if Ellen had placed them there by some recent and underhand proceeding.

“Mere give it me.”

Harcourt took it home. He was annoyed; but annoyance, being incompatible with his position as rescuer, soon altered itself into sorrow. The poor girl had never been taught accuracy of statement. Fear produced falsehood in undeveloped natures. Dog-like affection might coexist with confused ideas of ownership. He wrote these phrases down, and Ellen, finding the notebook open, shut it with unnecessary firmness.

Winter came. The river rose, the roof leaked above Standard Two, and Ellen fell ill after a washing-day conducted in a southerly gale. The doctor from the township said it was pleurisy, ordered rest and warmth, accepted two guineas, and departed before the road became impassable.

August was now indispensable. She was to make tea, keep the fires going, attend to the infants’ dinner, and bring Ellen her medicine every four hours.

She made the first tea. She forgot the second. On the third morning Harcourt found the kitchen fire out, the kettle empty, and August asleep in the washhouse room beneath Ellen’s best counterpane, which had been removed from a chest.

He woke her.

“Ellen has been calling.”

August sat up and brooded.

“You were to give her medicine at six.”

“I give it.”

“The bottle hasn’t been opened.”

“She not want it.”

“You were to light the fire.”

“No wood.”

“There is half a cord by the shed.”

August considered this information without pleasure.

Harcourt lit the fire, boiled the water, taught thirty-seven children, cooked mutton badly, and sat up most of the night beside his wife. August went to the pa to fetch Mere, who, she said, possessed a certain herbal remedy. Neither August nor Mere appeared until the following afternoon, when they arrived with three cousins, ate bread and jam, and recommended that Ellen be wrapped in wet fern.

After this August’s decline in usefulness was rapid. Gratitude had tired her. She slept late, broke plates, left clothes in the rain, and once put kerosene into the milk-jug. When spoken to she either denied the event, blamed a child, or took to the bush until supper-time.

Still Harcourt defended her.

“She has been damaged by harshness,” he told Ellen.

“Then kindness has not repaired her,” said Ellen.

“We must be patient.”

“You may be patient. I should like my stockings back.”

One Friday the attendance fell from thirty-nine to nineteen. On Monday there were eleven children; on Tuesday, seven. Rangi Heke came to the school, removed his hat, scratched his head, and said there was talk.

“What talk?”

Rangi examined the blackboard.

“Talk about missus.”

“What about her?”

“You can look at it two ways.”

This was Rangi’s favourite contribution to disputed questions. A cow in the school garden, a missing committee cheque, and a fight at the Christmas sports could all be looked at two ways. He would not say more, but he nodded toward the pa.

Harcourt went up to the pa.

He found August in Mere’s whare, surrounded by fourteen attentive relations. On the floor stood a bottle of Ellen’s raspberry vinegar; beside it were the missing raisins. August, who had been speaking with animation, became silent when he entered.

Mere smiled.

“Come in, Mr Schoolmaster. August tell us all about your house.”

It appeared that August had indeed told them all about his house, and rather more than the house contained. Ellen, she said, drank gin in bed, beat August with a poker, refused food to visitors, mocked the old people, and had buried a newborn child beneath the peach-tree. Harcourt stole money from the children’s penny fund, kept August locked in the washhouse, and intended to take her to Auckland to sell her. The school soup was made from diseased meat. The Bible lessons were designed to bewitch the pa. The inspector was a policeman in disguise.

These details, delivered during several evenings and supported by gifts from the Harcourt pantry, had not unnaturally affected attendance.

Harcourt looked at August. She looked past him.

“Come home,” he said.

“No.”

“You will come at once.”

Mere interposed. “She frightened. You cruel.”

Harcourt, who had imagined himself rescuing August from Mere, now perceived that Mere had lent him August during the expensive winter months, retaining both her loyalty and a profitable interest in his groceries. The revelation was less grand than betrayal in a romance, but more irritating.

“Very well,” he said. “She can remain here.”

He returned to the cottage, collected August’s sugar-bag, added the broken comb and soldier, and carried the whole up to the pa. Ellen’s print dress he also surrendered; Ellen’s counterpane he did not.

Thus ended the experiment in Universal Brotherhood.

Or rather, thus began its more active phase.

The next morning a letter was pushed under the school door. It consisted of four sheets written by three hands and signed by eleven persons, two of whom were infants. It accused Harcourt of murder, theft, cruelty, insult, kidnapping, starvation, sorcery, and “other bad things too much to put.” It concluded with a request for the loan of five shillings.

Harcourt sent no reply.

That night stones struck the cottage roof. On the following night someone imitated a dying cat beneath Ellen’s window. A dead eel was placed in the water-butt. The school gate vanished. Language of a personal character was shouted from the road, and a voice, which Harcourt recognized as Mere’s, promised to pull Ellen’s hair out “one by one, all night.”

Ellen was still weak. She bore these attentions with composure until, going to the washhouse at dusk, she found August standing by the tank with a knife in her hand.

The knife may have been intended for cutting flax; it may have been intended merely to be seen. August said nothing. She held it against her skirt and brooded.

Ellen backed toward the cottage.

James came round the corner carrying firewood. He put it down.

“Give me that knife.”

August did not move.

“Give it here.”

She threw it into the grass and ran.

Harcourt followed as far as the gate, then stopped. The old Harcourt would have reflected upon inherited impulses, tribal pressure, wounded affection, and the symbolic significance of steel. The new Harcourt shouted after her:

“If you or any of your people come inside this fence again, I send for the police. If another stone touches this roof, I have the whole lot of you charged. Do you understand?”

August disappeared into the fern.

The threat had some effect. For a week there was silence. Then Rangi came down to ask whether Harcourt truly meant the police.

“Yes.”

Rangi looked toward the pa.

“Police very expensive.”

“Not to me.”

“You angry?”

“Yes.”

“You can look at it two ways.”

“I am looking at it one.”

Rangi nodded, and from that hour regarded Harcourt with a respect which months of benevolence had failed to arouse.

There was one final try-on.

At eleven o’clock on a moonless Saturday night, two men came through the school paddock. They had been drinking at the accommodation-house and were full of courage borrowed on credit. One was August’s uncle Tame; the other was a cousin who had signed the abusive letter with a cross. They kicked the gate, beat upon the cottage wall, and demanded that Harcourt come out to be killed.

Ellen sat up in bed.

“Don’t go,” she said.

“I must.”

“You said that about the pa meeting, and came home without your hat.”

“This is different.”

He took the old fowling-piece from above the kitchen door. It had not been fired since he bought it from the grocer, and the grocer had advised him not to stand too near it when it was. Nevertheless, a firearm seen at night acquires virtues independent of its mechanism.

Harcourt stepped onto the veranda.

The men advanced across the yard, swearing.

He raised the gun.

“The first man over that path gets the charge.”

They stopped.

There followed a discussion in which Tame asserted that he feared neither gun, police, schoolmaster, nor death, but showed a marked preference for asserting it from behind the gate. Harcourt stood still. Presently the cousin remembered an appointment elsewhere. Tame, after a final speech addressed to colonial tyranny, fell over the gate and was assisted away.

Harcourt remained on the veranda until the voices had died beyond the ford. Then he carried the gun inside and discovered that it was not loaded.

Ellen made tea.

Next day there were twenty-six children at school. By the end of the week there were forty-one. Mere sent a message saying the whole affair had been a joke. Rangi proposed at the committee meeting that a vote of confidence be passed in the master, but, as two members thought confidence might involve expenditure, the proposal was allowed to lapse.

As for August, she left Te Rimu soon afterwards and went to keep house for a road labourer named Wilson, six miles up-country. Harcourt heard of it through the grocer and experienced, despite everything, a flutter of his old literary ambition. Here, perhaps, was the final act. Wilson might be rough but honest; August, disciplined by suffering, might awaken to tenderness; there could be flood, sacrifice, remorse, a death-bed reconciliation—or, better, a wedding.

He made inquiries.

For three months August cooked, washed badly, ate well, and gave no trouble. Wilson paid her ten shillings a week. At the end of that time she left his dinner in the oven, put her wages in her pocket, and walked home.

Harcourt met her on the road beside the river. She wore a red shawl and carried the same sugar-bag. The afternoon light lay upon the flax; the ranges were blue; a novelist might have done something with the scene.

August stopped.

“Good-bye, Jim,” she said.

“Good-bye, August.”

“You got any tobacco?”

“No.”

She went on toward the pa.

And that was all.

There was no repentance, no gratitude, no ruin, no redemption, not even an explanatory letter. The Child of the Pa remained unfinished at page forty-seven. Harcourt had designed a romance, but August, with that inconvenient independence common to persons selected as material, had declined to live it.

He stayed at Te Rimu for three more years.

They were peaceful years. He locked the pantry, kept the gun cleaned, answered gossip promptly, and no longer mistook vagueness for mystery or sullenness for tragic depth. This made him, perhaps, less amiable in theory and more useful in practice. He learned which families needed help, which merely asked for it, which promises would be kept, and which required a witness. He lent money rarely and tools never. When he said school began at nine, he rang the bell at nine; when a committee-man’s pigs rooted up the garden, he drove them into the committee-man’s whare.

The people of Te Rimu came to trust him. They brought sick children to Ellen, invited the Harcourts to feasts, consulted James over letters and land notices, and once, during a flood, worked all night beside him to save the school bridge. Rangi declared him “a hard man, but straight,” which was the finest testimonial Te Rimu bestowed upon anyone.

Harcourt’s earlier faith in universal kindness did not return, though ordinary kindness survived it and improved in health. He ceased writing about noble simple natures, having discovered that natures were neither noble nor simple for long together. If he judged the pa too harshly at times, the pa judged him with equal freedom, and the balance was perhaps nearer justice than his old benevolence had been.

Years later, in a city drawing-room, someone asked whether he had encountered romance among the Māori.

“Once,” he said.

Then he told the story of August Moses: the bruise, the rescue, the sugar, the counterpane, the raspberry vinegar, the knife, the unloaded gun, and the red shawl going home beside the river. His listeners laughed in the proper places and found the conclusion disappointingly small.

But Harcourt knew that this was its truth.

He had gone to Te Rimu expecting to teach a people and write a book. Instead, a silent girl with muddy feet had taught him to lock his pantry, examine his motives, and distrust any story in which the author imagines himself the hero.

Poor August! She had been neglected, indulged, borrowed, employed, accused, defended, and interpreted by everybody. Her relatives had used her; Harcourt had meant to use her more elegantly. Between Mere’s appetite for groceries and the schoolmaster’s appetite for copy there may not, after all, have been so vast a moral distance as the schoolmaster once supposed.

Yet August herself escaped both designs. She went home, asked for tobacco, and brooded on—unrescued, unredeemed, and obstinately her own.

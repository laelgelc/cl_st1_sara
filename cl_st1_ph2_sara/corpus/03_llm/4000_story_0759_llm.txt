## PHILO GUBB AND THE FORTY-CENT CHICKEN

When Philo Gubb, Correspondence-School Detective and Decorator of Interior Walls with Paper of Every Description, received the telegram from Syrilla, his finances were in a condition that might have discouraged a less resourceful man. He owed Mr. Higgins for eleven rolls of gilt-edged bedroom paper, he owed the Hotel Magnificent for three dinners which he had eaten there while disguised as a prosperous cattle-buyer, and he owed Syrilla eight dollars and twenty cents borrowed at various times for strictly professional purposes.

The telegram read:

> COME AT ONCE HAVE DECIDED EVERYTHING SYRILLA

The cost of traveling to Syrilla was four dollars and eighty-six cents, unless Philo Gubb went by freight, in which case the cost was uncertain and depended upon whether he was discovered before or after crossing the county line. As an honest detective, Mr. Gubb did not consider freight-car riding proper except when shadowing a desperate criminal who happened to be riding in the same freight car. Nor did he wish to arrive before Syrilla with coal dust in his ears. A detective must be cautious, but a lover should be clean.

He therefore placed the telegram in the inner pocket of his coat and sat down on his paste-board to consider the situation.

The room in which he sat was at once his sleeping-room, his office, his paper-hanging headquarters, and his laboratory of criminal identification. Along one wall stood rolls of wall-paper. Along another stood a narrow bed. Between the bed and the door were a paste-kettle, seven brushes, a trunk containing disguises, a shelf of detective books, two enormous revolvers, a dark lantern, a magnifying glass, three sets of false whiskers, a rubber nose, and a rusty horseshoe which Mr. Gubb had picked up near the scene of a burglary and retained because it might some day prove to be a clue.

On the door was a card:

> PHILO GUBB  
> DETECTIVE WORK AND PAPER-HANGING  
> DONE BY THE JOB OR BY THE DAY  
> STRICTLY CONFIDENTIAL

Below this, in pencil, he had added:

> NO DIVORCE CASES UNLESS PAPERING IS INCLUDED

Mr. Gubb took a sheet of paper and made a list of his available assets. There were fourteen cents in his right trousers pocket, a Canadian ten-cent piece in his left, and two postage stamps in his vest. The Canadian coin was not legal tender in Riverbank, although it had been tendered to Mr. Gubb often enough. The stamps were not cash, but they represented correspondence, and correspondence had taught him the detective profession. He could not despise them.

What he required was a case.

He had just reached this conclusion when there came a firm knock at the door. Mr. Gubb instantly swept the telegram beneath a roll of wall-paper, placed one of the revolvers on his knee, opened his magnifying glass, and said, in the deep voice recommended in Lesson Nine of the Rising Star Correspondence School of Detecting:

“Enter—if your conscience permits!”

The woman who entered was Mrs. Amos Smith, of Cherry Street. She was a square, capable woman with red arms, a black bonnet, and the expression of one who had no objection to mysteries provided they could be cleared up before dinner.

“You Gubb?” she asked.

“I am Philo Gubb,” he replied, “deteckative and paper-hanger. Which branch of my profession is desired?”

“Chicken,” said Mrs. Smith.

Mr. Gubb narrowed his eyes.

“Living or dead?”

“Stole.”

“Ah!” said Mr. Gubb. “Then the deteckative branch.”

Mrs. Smith sat down on the paste-board without being invited.

“It was my speckled hen,” she said. “Best layer I had, except she hadn’t laid much lately. She was there when I shut the coop last night, and she wasn’t there when I opened it this morning. I looked every place. Feathers outside the fence. Somebody took her.”

Mr. Gubb drew a notebook from his pocket.

“Name of missing party?”

“She didn’t have any name. I don’t name hens. It makes ’em too independent.”

“Age?”

“Hen age.”

“Height?”

“She was a hen!”

Mr. Gubb wrote rapidly.

“Complexion—speckled. Occupation—laying, but lately unemployed. Any gold teeth, scars, tattoo marks, or secret-society emblems?”

Mrs. Smith rose.

“If you’re making fun—”

“A deteckative never makes fun during office hours,” said Mr. Gubb. “He eliminates. The correspondence lessons state that every fact, no matter how small, should be wrote down, because you cannot tell which fact the guilty party has overlooked. What was the intrinsic cash valuation of the missing fowl?”

“Forty cents.”

Mr. Gubb’s pencil stopped.

“Forty cents?”

“I’d ask fifty in the market and take forty.”

He looked at the telegram hidden beneath the wall-paper. Four dollars and eighty-six cents was a long distance from forty cents. Even if he recovered the hen and retained the entire value as his fee, he would still be four dollars and forty-six cents short, not counting incidental expenses or clean collars.

“My minimum charge,” he said, “is five dollars a day and expenses.”

“You won’t get five dollars from me.”

“Then,” said Mr. Gubb thoughtfully, “possibly the guilty party will pay.”

Mrs. Smith considered this. She had no objection to a thief being charged for his own detection.

“If you can get it out of him, you can keep it,” she said. “I want my hen.”

“Madam,” said Mr. Gubb, rising, “the case is as good as solved.”

This was not strictly true, but it was professionally helpful.

Mrs. Smith led him to her property on Cherry Street. The house was small and yellow. The chicken yard lay behind it, with the coop close to the alley fence. Beyond the alley was the abandoned Jefferson Brick-Yard, containing weeds, broken drain tile, a rain-water pond, three roofless sheds, and five old kilns that had not burned a brick since Jefferson’s youngest son ran away with the circus and the elder Jefferson discovered that brickmaking without a son involved too much personal labor.

Mr. Gubb first examined the coop. He went around it three times, once stooping, once erect, and once with the magnifying glass held against his eye. He found several chicken tracks, a boot mark made by Mrs. Smith, a red thread from his own necktie, and one small white button.

“Ha!” he exclaimed.

“That come off my nightdress yesterday,” said Mrs. Smith. “I looked for it.”

Mr. Gubb returned the button.

He then searched for fingerprints. He had learned that fingerprints were generally found on windows, glasses, revolvers, safes, polished tables, and the throats of murdered men. The chicken coop offered no polished surface. Its door was made of rough boards and its latch of rusty iron.

“The criminal selected this coop,” said Mr. Gubb, “because it was impossible to leave prints.”

“He selected it because the hen was in it,” said Mrs. Smith.

“That may have influenced him.”

Near the fence lay six speckled feathers. Mr. Gubb picked them up separately, numbered them, and placed them in six envelopes. One envelope would have held all the feathers, but evidence gains importance when divided.

“He went that way,” said Mrs. Smith, pointing toward the alley. “I saw the feathers first thing.”

“I had already deduced the direction,” said Mr. Gubb. “The feathers are on this side of the fence. Had he went toward the house, they would not be on that side unless the wind changed.”

“There wasn’t any wind.”

“Exactly! That proves he did not depend on wind.”

Having completed his outdoor examination, Mr. Gubb asked to see the remaining chickens. Mrs. Smith opened the coop. Eleven hens and one rooster came out, stepping over Mr. Gubb’s shoes and looking at him with the grave suspicion poultry reserves for strangers and empty feed pans. He examined them carefully. Several of their tails brushed his hat, which needed brushing.

“The guilty party,” he announced, “will return.”

“What for?”

“For the rest of the chickens.”

“How do you know?”

“It is the law of criminal repetition,” said Mr. Gubb. “A man who steals one chicken steals chickens. If he stole one because he was hungry, he will become hungry again. If he stole one because he likes chickens, there are eleven more to like. If he stole one by mistake, he may return to correct the mistake. In all events, he returns.”

“You going to sit in the coop?”

“No. A criminal’s eye is quick. He would perceive me. I shall deteck unseen.”

He turned toward the brick-yard. The nearest kiln stood less than thirty feet beyond the alley. It was a broad, low structure of old brick with a plank roof, half fallen in. From its top the chicken yard could be observed through gaps in the weeds, while the observer himself remained invisible except to anyone who happened to look directly at him.

“This is the strategic point,” said Mr. Gubb.

“It looks rotten,” said Mrs. Smith.

“The trained deteckative does not permit personal comfort to interfere with duty.”

“I was thinking it might cave in.”

“The trained deteckative,” Mr. Gubb added, “also tests his strategic point before dark.”

The construction of an old-fashioned brick kiln is simple, although it may appear unnecessarily complicated to a man falling through one. The walls are thick; the burning chambers open below; and above them lies a drying-floor, which, in the Jefferson kiln, was covered by loose planks, straw, dust, and fragments of roofing. One could reach the top by mounting a ladder in the shed, crossing a beam, stepping upon a projecting course of bricks, and pulling oneself through a hole. It was not an easy route, but neither is the path of justice.

That evening Mr. Gubb prepared himself.

He wore a black suit because night is black. Over this he put a light-gray linen duster, because the correspondence lesson warned detectives not to be obvious. He attached brown side-whiskers to his bird-like cheeks, darkened his eyebrows with burnt cork, and carried both revolvers, the dark lantern, a bottle of cold coffee, six cheese sandwiches, a coil of clothesline, a pair of handcuffs, a magnifying glass, a paper-hanger’s smoothing brush, and a small can of paste.

The paste was not detective equipment, but Mr. Gubb had often found that if he left paste behind he needed it.

At nine o’clock he climbed the kiln and arranged himself above the chicken yard. Through a crack between two planks he could see the coop. The hens were asleep. Mrs. Smith’s house became dark at ten. The moon rose at eleven. At twelve a cat passed through the alley.

Mr. Gubb instantly leveled a revolver.

The cat sat down and washed one ear.

Mr. Gubb lowered the weapon. A detective must be cautious, but a cat is not a chicken thief until proved guilty.

At one o’clock he drank the coffee. At two he ate three sandwiches. At three the brown whisker on his left cheek loosened and hung under his chin. At four the rooster crowed, and at four-five Mrs. Smith’s kitchen light appeared.

Nothing—absolutely nothing—happened!

At breakfast Mrs. Smith gave him fried potatoes, ham, biscuits, coffee, and three kinds of preserved fruit. She did not give him eggs because, as she said, with forty-cent hens walking out every night she could not afford to scatter eggs among detectives.

“He didn’t come,” she said.

“He was suspicious,” said Mr. Gubb.

“Of what?”

“Of me.”

“He couldn’t see you.”

“That,” said Mr. Gubb, “is what made him suspicious. A criminal expects to see what he can see. When he cannot see it, he knows it is concealed.”

Mrs. Smith stared at him.

“You going up again tonight?”

“Undoubtedly. The first night’s failure will render him careless. It is one of the principles of deteckation that when nothing happens the first time, something is more liable to happen the second time, because there are fewer times left.”

During the day Mr. Gubb slept in his room. Before sleeping he reread Syrilla’s telegram and calculated that a five-dollar fee would leave fourteen cents after purchasing a railway ticket. Fourteen cents was not much with which to begin the most important conversation of his life, but love, like detective work, must often proceed without sufficient working capital.

That afternoon the newspaper contained a small item:

> POLICE SEEK WIXY WILSON  
>   
> Authorities believe two members of the Sand Ridge gang may be in this vicinity. William “Wixy” Wilson and a man known as Sandlot Sam are wanted for questioning concerning the disappearance of Henry “The Chicken” Murgatroyd, associate of Mother Smith, alleged receiver of stolen property.

Mr. Gubb did not read this item. He read the advertisement beside it, which offered twelve cans of assorted soup for one dollar.

He studied the list with interest. There were tomato soup, bean soup, pea soup, oxtail soup, chicken soup, mock-turtle soup, vegetable soup, celery soup, beef soup, consommé, cream-of-corn soup, and one can whose label had been damaged and might contain soup or varnish. Mr. Gubb wondered whether twelve cans could sustain him on the journey to Syrilla and afterward, if afterward lasted no more than six days.

He fell asleep wondering why chicken soup cost no more than pea soup when a chicken was worth forty cents and peas, individually, were worth almost nothing.

That night he returned to the kiln.

He had repaired his side-whisker with wall-paper paste, and it adhered so firmly that it drew the skin whenever he moved his mouth. This gave his face an expression of painful determination suitable to detective work. He took his former position, arranged his revolvers within reach, and settled down to watch.

At ten o’clock an owl flew over the coop.

At eleven a board under Mr. Gubb cracked sharply.

At twelve he ate a sandwich.

At one he heard footsteps in the brick-yard, but by then his chin had descended upon his chest and his eyes were closed. Mr. Gubb was asleep.

The footsteps belonged to two men. One was tall and narrow, with a cloth cap pulled over his ears. This was Sandlot Sam. The other was thick, red-faced, and unsteady. This was Wixy Wilson. Wixy carried a bottle, from which he drank whenever he remembered it and sometimes when he did not.

They came beneath the kiln roof and stopped directly under Mr. Gubb.

“This’ll do,” Sandlot whispered. “Nobody comes here.”

“Nobody but chickens,” said Wixy, and laughed.

“Shut up! This ain’t no laughin’ business.”

“It was to the Chicken.”

“He ain’t laughin’ now.”

Wixy took another drink.

Above them Mr. Gubb breathed gently through his nose. The loose plank beneath him sagged a little, dropping dust upon Sandlot’s cap.

“What was that?” Sandlot demanded.

“Kiln’s got dandruff.”

“Listen, Wixy. We got to get out. Mother Smith’s put the whole bunch after us.”

“She can’t prove nothin’.”

“She don’t have to prove it. She knows we took the Chicken out to Miller’s marsh.”

“We took him for a ride.”

“And he didn’t ride back.”

“That was his fault.”

“You hit him.”

“You held him.”

“Because you said only scare him!”

“He scared easy,” said Wixy. “Fainted permanent.”

Sandlot seized Wixy’s coat.

“You drunken fool! Mother Smith wanted the Chicken alive. He knew where the Marston diamonds was hid. You cracked him with a coupling pin, and now she thinks we took the diamonds.”

“Maybe you took ’em.”

“I never!”

“Maybe Chicken swallowed ’em.”

“He didn’t swallow no diamonds.”

“Then why’d they call him Chicken? Chickens swallow gravel.”

“They called him Chicken because he’d steal corn from a blind widow and then run from her dog.”

Above them Mr. Gubb stirred, but did not wake.

Sandlot continued in a tense whisper.

“There’s five hundred on us. Five hundred cash to the man that finds out what happened. Mother Smith told Denver Pete, and Denver Pete told Long Jake, and Long Jake is in town. We got to split.”

“Split what?”

“Split up! Separate! You go west and I go east.”

“I don’t like east.”

“Then you go west.”

“I don’t like west.”

“Go north.”

“Cold.”

“Go south!”

“Hot.”

“Then stay here and get killed.”

Wixy considered the alternatives and drank.

“I ain’t afraid of Mother Smith.”

“You was afraid yesterday.”

“I was sober yesterday.”

Sandlot released his coat.

“You got any money?”

“Plenty.”

“How much?”

Wixy slapped his inside pocket.

“Enough.”

“You took Chicken’s roll!”

“Found it.”

“On him.”

“Where else would I find it? Money don’t grow on marshes.”

Sandlot stepped back.

“You told me he was broke.”

“He was after I found it.”

“You give me half.”

“No.”

“I helped.”

“You held him. That ain’t skilled work.”

“I’ll tell Mother Smith.”

“You tell her and I tell her you done it.”

“I didn’t hit him!”

“But you held him where I could.”

For a moment neither spoke. Sandlot’s hand moved toward his coat pocket. Wixy’s hand moved toward his hip. Above them the sleeping detective smiled. In his dream, Philo Gubb had reached Syrilla’s town and had been brought before a judge because his purchase of twelve assorted soups constituted a trust in restraint of trade.

“You have combined tomato, bean, pea, oxtail, chicken, mock-turtle, vegetable, celery, beef, corn, consommé, and doubtful varnish,” said the judge, “thereby creating a Soup Monopoly.”

“I bought them retail, Your Honor,” said Mr. Gubb.

“I order this trust dissolved!”

Mr. Gubb searched his pockets for turpentine, benzine, alcohol, and other dissolving fluids.

“You must dissolve yourself!” cried the judge.

In his effort to obey, Mr. Gubb turned over.

Below, Sandlot said, “What’s that?”

A fragment of brick dropped between them.

“Somebody’s up there!” Sandlot gasped.

Wixy looked upward, but the darkness and the bottle interfered with his vision.

“Chicken ghost,” he said.

Sandlot did not wait to identify the ghost. He ran through the kiln opening, crossed the yard, and vanished among the sheds. Wixy shouted after him, stumbled over a broken drainpipe, cursed the pipe, sat down against the kiln wall, and finished the bottle.

He was still sitting there when sleep overcame him.

Some time before dawn Mr. Gubb awoke. His neck hurt, one leg was numb, and he had a conviction that a dangerous person stood directly below him. This conviction was correct, but his reason for it was that the broken bottle had rolled across a brick and made a faint tinkling sound.

He listened.

A loud snore rose through the floor.

Mr. Gubb’s professional faculties became instantly active. He reached for his dark lantern and pushed open the slide. The beam showed only a crack, straw, and one of his own knees. He crawled toward the edge of the roof.

The proper manner of descending from the Jefferson kiln was to reverse the process by which one had ascended: first the hole, then the projecting bricks, then the beam, then the ladder. Mr. Gubb, however, had forgotten which hole he had used, and in searching for it he placed both hands upon a plank that appeared firm.

The plank was not firm.

It rose at one end, descended at the other, and allowed Mr. Gubb, the dark lantern, a revolver, two sandwiches, the paste can, and much ancient straw to pass into the chamber below.

Wixy had just time to open his eyes.

Mr. Gubb landed upon him with both knees.

Wixy’s head struck the brick floor, his eyes closed, and his body became limp. The lantern went out. The paste can opened and spread a thin coating over the rear portion of Wixy’s coat. One revolver discharged into a pile of broken tile.

Mr. Gubb lay still for several seconds, conducting a mental inventory. He found himself bruised but alive. He found a man beneath him, apparently dead. He found one false whisker adhering to his cheek and the other adhering to the man’s collar.

“The culprit!” he exclaimed.

He rose cautiously and relighted the lantern. Wixy did not move.

Mr. Gubb’s first thought was that he had killed the chicken thief. His second was that a dead thief could not pay a five-dollar fee. His third was that correspondence detectives should know how to revive unconscious criminals, but that the lesson on revival had been included in the Advanced Course, for which he still owed three dollars.

He dragged Wixy to the open air, laid him beside the rain-water pond, and applied such methods as occurred to him. He poured water on Wixy’s face. He loosened Wixy’s collar. He slapped Wixy’s hands. He held the soup advertisement beneath Wixy’s nose, but printer’s ink had no restoring effect.

At last he filled his hat with pond water and emptied it over Wixy’s head.

Wixy opened his eyes.

“Don’t hit me again,” he murmured.

“I have no intentions of hitting you provided you act peaceable,” said Mr. Gubb. “You are caught red-handed, although at present your hands are more muddy than red.”

Wixy blinked at him. In the gray light before sunrise, Mr. Gubb’s appearance was impressive. Straw stuck from his hair. One side of his face wore a large brown whisker. The other was bare and bird-like. A revolver protruded from each pocket. Around his waist was a coil of clothesline, and in his hand he held a lantern.

“Who’re you?” Wixy asked.

“Philo Gubb, deteckative.”

Wixy closed his eyes again.

“Mother Smith,” he said.

Mr. Gubb started.

“So!” he exclaimed. “You know Mrs. Smith?”

Wixy opened one eye.

“Know her? Sure I know her. She sent you.”

Mr. Gubb was gratified. The promptness with which the criminal mind recognized defeat proved the value of his correspondence training.

“She engaged me,” he said solemnly, “to deteck you.”

“I knew she would.”

“You admit your acquaintance with the chicken?”

Wixy turned his head.

“Listen, mister, I can explain about the Chicken.”

“Proceed, but remember that any statements made in my presence may be used against you, for you, around you, or in the newspapers.”

“I didn’t mean to kill him.”

Mr. Gubb frowned.

“Then you admit the chicken is dead?”

Wixy swallowed.

“He was when we left him.”

This was serious. Mrs. Smith had asked for her hen, not its remains.

“How did you kill the chicken?” Mr. Gubb asked.

“It was an accident.”

“With an ax?”

“No.”

“By wringing the neck?”

“No.”

“Then how?”

“Coupling pin.”

Mr. Gubb had heard of foxes killing hens and dogs killing hens, but never of a chicken being killed with railroad equipment.

“You struck the chicken with a coupling pin?”

“Just once.”

“What was it doing at the time?”

“Talkin’ too much.”

Mr. Gubb looked at Wixy sternly.

“Chickens do not talk.”

“This one did. All the time.”

“Possibly you mistake clucking for talking.”

“He was tellin’ Sandlot he’d squeal to Mother Smith.”

“A squealing chicken is also unusual.”

“He was unusual all right. Toughest old rooster in the state.”

Mr. Gubb nodded slowly. Mrs. Smith had called the missing bird a hen, but owners were sometimes mistaken. She had also said it was speckled. Wixy’s reference to a tough old rooster suggested either criminal slang or poor poultry knowledge. Both were common among lawbreakers.

“Where are the remains?” he asked.

“Miller’s marsh.”

“You buried the chicken?”

“Near enough.”

“Plucked?”

“What?”

“Were the feathers removed?”

“I didn’t see no feathers.”

“Then,” said Mr. Gubb, “you concealed it entire.”

Wixy looked at him with growing uneasiness.

“What kind of a guy are you?”

“A deteckative kind.”

“You talk like you’re crazy.”

“Criminals often think their captors crazy,” said Mr. Gubb. “It is because they cannot understand superior reasoning.”

Wixy sat up. He touched the back of his head and winced.

“What does Mother Smith want?”

“Payment,” said Mr. Gubb, deciding that recovery of a dead hen from Miller’s marsh would involve more expense than the hen justified. “Payment for the chicken and payment for my professional services.”

“I ain’t paying her.”

“Then I turn you over to the police authorities.”

Wixy’s face lost color.

“You wouldn’t.”

“It is my sworn and paid duty.”

“You ain’t been paid yet.”

“That is why payment is the next subject.”

Wixy looked toward the sheds. Sandlot was gone. The town lay beyond the brick-yard, and the first smoke was rising from breakfast chimneys. A train whistled at the river bridge. Wixy put a hand inside his coat.

Mr. Gubb drew both revolvers.

“Remove the hand empty!” he commanded.

“I’m gettin’ money!”

“In that event, remove it full.”

Wixy brought out a fat roll of bills. He held it tightly.

“How much?”

Mr. Gubb had considered this matter. His regular charge was five dollars a day and expenses. He had worked two nights, but the first had produced no result. Charging for unsuccessful time seemed to him professionally proper, yet Mrs. Smith had offered no fee at all. He required four dollars and eighty-six cents for the railway fare. Five dollars would meet this and leave fourteen cents. He therefore chose the simple amount.

“Five,” he said.

“Five hundred!” Wixy cried.

Mr. Gubb was startled, but a detective must never show surprise. He stroked the whiskered side of his face.

“You consider it excessive?”

Wixy licked his lips.

“Mother Smith said five hundred reward. That’s for knowin’ what happened—not for lettin’ me go.”

“The payment includes my deteckative work and the chicken,” said Mr. Gubb.

“And you keep your mouth shut?”

“My professional mouth,” said Mr. Gubb, “is always confidential when circumstances justify confidentialness.”

Wixy examined his face. The single whisker, stern eye, revolvers, straw-covered shoulders, and official phraseology convinced him more than a badge would have done. Badges can be purchased. A face such as Mr. Gubb’s must be born.

“You’ll tell her I paid?”

“I will give Mrs. Smith a full report.”

“But you won’t tell the cops?”

“Not unless required.”

“You won’t go to Miller’s marsh?”

Mr. Gubb considered the mud, distance, and probable condition of the chicken.

“Not if the financial settlement is satisfactory.”

Wixy slowly counted five hundred-dollar bills from the roll. He extended them, then drew them back.

“This squares it with Mother Smith?”

“It squares the chicken,” said Mr. Gubb, “and my fee.”

“Same thing,” Wixy muttered. “Here.”

Mr. Gubb accepted the money. Never before had he held five hundred dollars at one time. He had not known a forty-cent chicken could increase in value so rapidly after death. Possibly coupling-pin killing was rare and enhanced the specimen’s worth. Possibly Mrs. Smith had underestimated her property. Possibly the bird had been insured.

He placed the bills in his inner pocket beside Syrilla’s telegram.

“You are at liberty to depart,” he said.

Wixy rose unsteadily.

“My hat?”

Mr. Gubb found the hat beneath a board.

“My whisker?” he asked.

“What whisker?”

Mr. Gubb saw the missing disguise attached to Wixy’s collar, but professional pride prevented an explanation. He pulled it free and put it in his pocket.

“An evidence,” he said.

Wixy backed away.

“If Sandlot comes around, you ain’t seen me.”

“If he has also stolen chickens,” said Mr. Gubb, “he will be deteckated in due course.”

Wixy stared at him, decided that further conversation was dangerous, and hurried across the brick-yard toward the railway tracks.

Mr. Gubb watched until he disappeared. He then drew out the five bills and examined them by lantern light. They seemed genuine. The correspondence lesson on counterfeit money advised bending a bill, smelling it, examining the portrait, and comparing the engraving with a known genuine specimen. Mr. Gubb had no hundred-dollar specimen for comparison, but the portraits looked like men accustomed to having their portraits on money.

He climbed out of the kiln and crossed the alley.

Mrs. Smith was feeding the hens. She counted them as Mr. Gubb entered the yard.

“Eleven,” she said. “He didn’t come.”

“He came,” said Mr. Gubb.

“You got my hen?”

“The chicken is deceased.”

Mrs. Smith put down the feed pan.

“Fox get her?”

“No. A man named Wixy struck the chicken with a railroad coupling pin because it talked too much.”

Mrs. Smith’s mouth opened.

“He then took it to Miller’s marsh with a party named Sandlot,” Mr. Gubb continued. “The chicken threatened to squeal to Mother Smith regarding certain diamonds. Mother Smith offered five hundred dollars to learn the circumstances, and Wixy has paid the amount.”

He displayed the money.

Mrs. Smith sat down on an overturned bucket.

“Five hundred dollars?”

“Five hundred.”

“For my hen?”

“For the chicken and professional services. The exact division was not itemized.”

Mrs. Smith took one bill, held it to the light, rubbed it, and handed it back.

“What did this man look like?”

Mr. Gubb described Wixy.

“I never saw him.”

“He stated you knew him.”

“I don’t.”

“He called you Mother Smith.”

“I ain’t anybody’s mother except Elmer’s, and Elmer’s in Nebraska.”

“He said you employed other parties to seek him.”

“I employed you, and I’m beginning to regret it.”

Mr. Gubb’s bird-like face became thoughtful.

“There may,” he said, “be two Mrs. Smiths.”

“There’s likely two thousand.”

“And possibly,” he continued, “there may be two chickens.”

“There’s likely millions.”

“One chicken being a speckled hen worth forty cents—”

“That’s mine.”

“—and the other being a tough old rooster able to talk, squeal, know the location of diamonds, carry a roll of money, ride to Miller’s marsh, and be struck with a coupling pin.”

Mrs. Smith stared at him.

“Philo Gubb,” she said, “that other chicken was a man.”

Mr. Gubb did not answer immediately. He recalled Wixy’s words one by one. The Chicken had talked. The Chicken had ridden. The Chicken had possessed money. The Chicken had known about diamonds. The Chicken had been held by Sandlot while Wixy struck him. These facts, considered separately, were unusual in poultry. Considered together, they suggested a conclusion.

“Madam,” he said at last, “that is a possible theory.”

“Possible!”

“It will require investigation.”

Mrs. Smith stood up.

“What about my hen?”

Mr. Gubb looked toward the coop. Eleven hens scratched industriously in the dust. The rooster crowed. Beyond the alley the old kiln leaned in the morning sunlight, revealing no sign that it had assisted justice.

“The hen case,” he said, “remains open.”

“And the five hundred dollars?”

“That,” said Mr. Gubb, placing a protective hand over his inner pocket, “was paid by the guilty party for the chicken, the deteckating, and confidential expenses.”

“My chicken?”

“He believed so at the time.”

“I want forty cents.”

Mr. Gubb considered this a reasonable demand. He took out his purse, found the fourteen American cents and the Canadian dime, and counted them twice.

“I will remit the balance,” he said, “following my return from Syrilla.”

“You’ll remit it now.”

“My available working capital—”

“Now!”

With a sigh, Mr. Gubb detached one of the hundred-dollar bills.

“I regret I have no small change.”

Mrs. Smith did not take it.

“You mean to stand there and tell me you let a murderer go?”

“I let a chicken thief go.”

“He wasn’t a chicken thief!”

“He confessed to killing the Chicken.”

“A man!”

“The point,” said Mr. Gubb, “was not entirely clear during the preliminary interrogation.”

Mrs. Smith pointed toward the gate.

“You get out of my yard.”

“But, madam, I must obtain further particulars regarding the missing hen. Did she have enemies? Was she associated with any suspicious roosters? Did she know of any concealed diamonds?”

Mrs. Smith entered her kitchen.

Mr. Gubb followed as far as the step.

“Also,” he called, “do you happen to know a party named Long Jake?”

The door slammed.

Mr. Gubb stood looking at it. Then he took Syrilla’s telegram from his pocket, smoothed it, and placed it beside the five hundred dollars. His financial difficulties had been solved, his chicken case remained professionally active, and he had, without knowing it, obtained a confession in a murder inquiry which the police had not yet solved.

These were satisfactory results.

Still, as he walked toward the railway station, his brow was wrinkled. One feature of the case troubled him deeply.

If the Chicken was a man, Mrs. Smith’s hen was still missing.

And a detective must be cautious.

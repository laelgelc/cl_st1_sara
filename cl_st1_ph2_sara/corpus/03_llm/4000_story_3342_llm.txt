## TWO CHAMBERS

There are diseases which enter the dwelling like visible messengers of sorrow, and command, by their very aspect, the pity of every beholder; and there are others which, though unseen, breathe through every apartment, darken every countenance, and chill the warm current of domestic love. Of the latter kind is selfishness when joined with bodily suffering. We will look in upon two chambers, and learn whether pain must needs make all things painful around it.

The first was spacious and richly furnished. Heavy curtains, of a soft crimson colour, fell from the ceiling to the floor; the carpet yielded noiselessly beneath the foot; a fire burned without smoke upon the marble hearth; and near it stood a couch so skilfully contrived that the weakest frame might rest upon it without weariness. Books, flowers, costly essences, and every delicate provision which affection or wealth could obtain were placed within reach.

Upon the couch reclined Mrs. Harcourt.

She had long been an invalid. Her face, once handsome, was pale and wasted; her hands trembled; and a real, unfrequently severe, pain oppressed her side. We would not speak lightly of that pain. God alone knows the long hours which the sick are sometimes called to endure, when the household sleeps and darkness seems to stand still. But Mrs. Harcourt had another malady, more grievous than the weakness of her body. Her thoughts, like blood that ceases to circulate and gathers dangerously about the heart, flowed ever inward upon herself.

A little boy, perhaps five years of age, stole into the chamber. He carried a wooden horse, whose scarlet saddle was somewhat the worse for wear.

“Mamma,” he whispered, “may Harry sit by you? Harry will be very still.”

His mother opened her eyes.

“Who let that child come in? Nurse knows that I cannot bear him in the morning.”

“I will not make a noise,” said Harry, stopping at once. “See, mamma, my horse’s leg is broken. Papa says I tried to mend it very cleverly.”

“Do not bring broken toys here. You distress me beyond measure. Go away—go directly!”

The child’s lip quivered.

“May I kiss you first?”

“No. Your hands are cold. Ring for nurse, some one. Why am I never obeyed?”

Harry went out as silently as he had entered; yet, in closing the door, the latch clicked.

“There!” exclaimed Mrs. Harcourt. “That noise has gone through my head. I shall feel it all day.”

Was the click so violent? No. But the ear that listens continually for annoyance will hear thunder in the falling of a pin.

Presently two young girls came bounding up the stairs; then, remembering their mother’s illness, checked their steps and entered softly. Clara held a paper in her hand, and Lucy’s eyes shone with the difficulty of keeping good news unspoken.

“Mamma,” began Clara, “we have returned from our examination, and Miss Fielding says—”

“Not now,” interrupted Mrs. Harcourt. “I cannot attend to school affairs. Lucy, do not stand between me and the fire.”

“But, mamma,” said Lucy, “Clara has won the silver medal.”

“Put it on the table. The fire is too hot. No—it is going out. Why has no one sense enough to keep the room at one temperature?”

The girls exchanged a glance.

“And I was first in drawing,” Lucy ventured to add.

“Must you both talk? I told you I could not attend. Take those rustling frocks away.”

The medal, which had seemed so bright in the school-room, lay dull enough upon the table. Clara took it up again, and the sisters withdrew. They had not ceased to love their mother; children’s hearts are slow to close against a parent. But joy, repeatedly repulsed, learns to seek another door.

A servant entered with a vase of early violets.

“Miss Clara gathered them for you, ma’am.”

“They smell too strongly.”

“They have scarcely any scent, ma’am.”

“Do not contradict me. Put them outside. And draw the curtain; the sun strikes directly upon my face.”

The maid obeyed. A broad ray, which had fallen across the floor and touched a portrait of the three children, disappeared. The pictured faces sank into shadow.

So it was with every thing that entered that chamber. Sunshine was too glaring; shade was depressing; flowers were offensive; the absence of flowers showed neglect. The clock ticked too loudly, but, when removed, she continually asked the hour. The pillows were first too high and afterward too low. The tea was hot enough to fever her, and, when cooled, was unfit to drink. If the house were quiet, she declared herself abandoned; if a door opened or a young voice sounded below, she was sacrificed to the thoughtlessness of others.

Mr. Harcourt came home early each day. Business, society, and recreation were alike resigned that he might sit beside his wife. He entered now with the gentle manner which long sorrow had made habitual.

“How have you been, my dear?”

“How should I be? The children have been allowed to disturb me twice; Ellen brought violets, though she knows I cannot endure them; and the cook has sent up broth with some abominable flavour.”

“I am sorry. Shall I read to you?”

“You always choose something exciting.”

“Then I will sit quietly.”

“You know that silence makes me nervous.”

He drew his chair nearer.

“The physician believes the milder weather will strengthen you. If you are able next week, we might drive beneath the elms.”

“And be shaken to pieces by the carriage! Henry, I sometimes think no one understands that I am ill.”

Her husband looked at the wasted face, and compassion checked the reply that rose within him.

“No one forgets it, Maria.”

“Then why am I tormented from morning until night?”

He took her hand; she withdrew it, saying his ring hurt her finger. There came no opiate to her suffering from the tenderness she refused to receive. Her family gave every thing they knew how to give—time, quiet, obedience, delicacies, money, watchfulness; but she demanded not only their service, she demanded that their whole existence should take the colour of her pain.

At dinner the children spoke in whispers. Harry’s spoon fell, and he turned pale with alarm. Clara hid her medal in a drawer. Lucy ceased to practise her music because one wrong note had brought a message from above. Mr. Harcourt moved about his beautiful house like a man who fears to awaken grief sleeping behind every door.

Mrs. Harcourt was alone.

Not literally; servants waited, children hovered near, and a faithful husband watched every change. Yet selfishness had built around her a wall more effectual than stone. Love came to the entrance, but complaint kept it without. Her bodily disease enfeebled her; her worst disease was mental. By continually asking, “What do I suffer? What disturbs me? What attention have I failed to receive?” she magnified each pain, and added to it the fever of impatience.

Let us leave the curtained room and cross but two streets.

At the upper window of a small house sat another mother. We enter by a narrow stair. The chamber is poorly furnished. Its paper is faded; one of the window panes has been mended with a strip of brown paper; the fire is small, and the arm-chair bears marks of long service. Here are no silken cushions, no silver bell, no costly essences. Yet the afternoon light comes freely in, and rests pleasantly upon a jar containing three sprigs of wall-flower.

Mrs. Ellis was younger than Mrs. Harcourt, but scarcely stronger. A slow disease had taken the colour from her cheek and power from her limbs. She could not stand without assistance, and often, when she smiled most tranquilly, pain lay beneath the smile.

A baby began to cry in a cradle near her.

“Poor little Robin!” she said, bending as far as her weakness permitted. “Mother hears you.”

Her eldest daughter, Susan, came from the adjoining room.

“Let me take him, mother. You are tired.”

“I am tired, dear; but his little troubles are great to him. Bring him here, and we will divide the burden between us.”

The child was placed upon her lap. He caught at the ribbon of her cap, and his tears ceased.

“There,” she said, “he has found a better remedy than all the apothecary’s bottles.”

“Does he hurt you, mother?”

“A little, when he presses against my side; but he does not know it. We must not blame ignorance as if it were unkindness.”

Susan arranged a pillow behind her.

“You always make excuses for us.”

“Nay, my child, I sometimes have cause to ask excuses of you. Sickness is apt to make the temper weak as well as the body, unless we keep a good guard over it.”

At that moment feet were heard upon the stairs—not cautious, terrified feet, but the eager steps of children returning home.

“Mother! Mother!” cried Jane and Bess together, entering with books, bonnets, and a breath of the fresh March wind. “We have such news!”

“Then bring it quickly,” said Mrs. Ellis, “for good news should not be kept waiting.”

“Jane has gone above me in arithmetic,” said Bess, with generous haste, “and the mistress praised her before every one.”

“And Bess read without missing a word,” returned Jane.

Mrs. Ellis looked from one glowing face to the other.

“My two scholars! I shall soon be ashamed to confess how little their mother knows. Come, let me see the copy-books.”

They gathered about her chair. Robin pulled one page and Bess talked too rapidly; Jane corrected her, Susan laughed, and for some minutes the chamber was full of confusion.

“Are we too noisy?” Jane asked suddenly.

“You are noisy,” replied the mother, “but I like the sound. It tells me that my children have health and spirits. There may come days when I shall long to hear these voices and cannot. Why should I make sadness beforehand?”

“But your head aches,” said Susan.

“Yes; so speak one at a time, if you can—which I know is very difficult when four happy persons have but one mother to hear them.”

They tried; they failed; they tried again; and the mother’s quiet cheerfulness did more to subdue their voices than twenty fretful commands could have done.

Across the lane, workmen were repairing a roof. The hammering had continued since dawn.

“I wish they would stop,” Susan said. “It must weary you sadly.”

Mrs. Ellis listened.

“The sound is not musical in itself,” she answered, “yet I have made a meaning for it. Every stroke reminds me that some family will have a dry roof before the rain returns. We bear many things better when we remember that they are useful to somebody.”

Was this mere pretence? Did the hammering give her no pain? It did. Once or twice she pressed her hand upon her forehead; but she would not make her children hate the necessary labour of poor men because her nerves were weak. Self-command does not deny suffering; it refuses to enthrone it.

At dusk Mr. Ellis returned from his day’s work, carrying a small parcel.

“I have brought an orange for mother,” he said.

“An orange!” cried Robin’s little sister. “A whole one?”

“A whole one,” said Mrs. Ellis gravely; “but I fear it has too many owners.”

“It is all for you,” her husband replied. “The doctor said you should have fruit.”

“And the doctor is a wise man. Therefore I will take my medicine after supper, when it can be cut into six portions.”

“Five,” said Susan. “You must have two.”

“We will submit the question to parliament.”

The orange was placed upon the mantelpiece, where it shone like a little golden lamp. Had Mrs. Ellis desired it? Yes. Had she sometimes longed, with the craving of disease, for food which poverty denied her? Yes. Virtue is not the absence of desire, but its government.

Her husband sat beside her.

“You have suffered to-day,” he said in a low voice.

“A little more than yesterday.”

His face clouded.

“Do not look so, William. Pain is heavy enough when one bears it in the body; I cannot consent to see it repeated in your countenance.”

“I wish I could take it from you.”

“You take half of it whenever you come home kindly; and the children take the rest in pieces, so that very little remains for me.”

She spoke cheerfully, yet her eyes filled with tears. His hand closed over hers, and neither withdrew it.

Here was sympathy accepted, not extorted; gratitude expressed, not demanded. Love moved freely through that humble room, warming each heart in turn. The children learned gentleness without learning fear. They knew their mother was ill; they brought her shawl, hushed the baby when she slept, and saved choice morsels for her plate. Yet they were not taught to consider happiness an offence against her weakness.

On the following Sunday Clara Harcourt, walking with her governess, carried a basket to the Ellis family. Mrs. Harcourt had ordered that some fruit, which did not please her taste, should be given to the poor.

Clara entered the little chamber and found Jane reading aloud while the others listened.

“Come in, my dear,” said Mrs. Ellis. “You bring the open air with you.”

Clara placed the basket upon the table.

“Mamma sends these.”

“That is very kind. Pray give her my thanks.”

Clara looked wistfully at the children grouped around their mother.

“Do they not tire you?” she asked.

“Sometimes,” Mrs. Ellis replied, smiling; “and I sometimes tire them. We must make allowances on both sides.”

“May they tell you things when your head aches?”

“Certainly. Their thoughts require air and sunshine as much as flowers do. Yet they are kind enough to speak softly when the pain is severe.”

Clara was silent. Then, drawing the medal from beneath her cloak, she said, with hesitation, “I won this at school.”

Mrs. Ellis examined it as though it had been a royal treasure.

“You must have worked diligently. Your mother will be proud.”

Clara’s eyes fell.

“She was too ill to hear about it.”

The poor invalid understood more than the child had said.

“Then tell her again, dear. Sick persons sometimes lose a pleasure at one hour which they may gladly receive at another.”

Clara carried away these charitable words; but she carried also the remembrance of a pale mother whose poverty had not narrowed her heart.

We cannot tell whether Mrs. Harcourt ever learned the lesson offered so near her door. Habits of thought, long indulged, become chains, and chains are not broken by one good resolution. Yet the contrast remains for us.

One woman possessed every comfort and found an injury in each; the other wanted many things and discovered blessings among those that remained. One shut out the sunlight because it offended her eyes, and, with it, shut out the confidence of her children; the other welcomed their voices until affection made music even of noise. One measured love by the amount of sorrow displayed before her; the other proved love by sparing those around her unnecessary sorrow.

Yes, care is a blessing. The mind must have some object beyond its own sensations, or pain, disappointment, and fear will gather within it as stagnant waters gather in a neglected pool. Useful thought circulates through the affections; it carries life to every duty, and returns with healing to the heart.

Bodily weakness may confine the feet, but it need not imprison kindness. The hand that cannot labour may still beckon a child nearer; the voice that is scarcely strong enough for speech may bestow praise; the eye dimmed by suffering may brighten at another’s joy. Thus may the feeblest person become the centre, not of gloom, but of consolation.

And when such a one is gone, the household will remember, not merely that she suffered, but that, suffering, she loved.

## I

Prince Stepan Kasatsky was twenty-six when an event which appeared to others no more than an unfortunate conclusion to a brilliant engagement altered the whole course of his life.

His father, a retired colonel of the Guards, had died when Stepan was twelve, leaving his widow a moderate fortune, a daughter, and precise instructions that the boy should be educated in the Corps of Pages. The mother would have preferred to keep him near her, but she submitted to her husband’s will; and Stepan, who already regarded obedience to a severe rule as a form of distinction, entered the Corps.

He was remarkable not merely for his height, strength, and handsome face, but for the persistence with which he accomplished everything he undertook. If another boy knew French better, Stepan worked until his pronunciation became faultless. If another surpassed him in mathematics, he sat up at night until he stood first. He danced well, rode superbly, fenced with cold precision, and bore pain without complaint. Yet these virtues, which delighted his superiors, did not make him loved by his companions. They felt in him that hidden reproach which the successful man directs toward all who are content to be ordinary.

His one dangerous defect was anger. It came upon him suddenly and almost always when his pride was touched. Once, when an instructor mocked him before the class, Stepan seized an inkstand and would have thrown it had not two boys caught his arm. The matter was concealed because he was valuable to the Corps and because the instructor had been unjust. Stepan wept afterward—not from remorse, as he believed, but from humiliation at having lost command of himself.

On leaving the Corps he entered a Guards regiment. There, too, he succeeded. His uniform was faultless, his accounts exact, his horses the best that could be maintained without vulgar extravagance. He neither drank heavily nor gambled foolishly. He avoided the coarse adventures of his comrades, partly from fastidiousness and partly because he intended to preserve himself for a life more elevated than theirs.

The centre of that life was the Emperor.

Men may submit to a sovereign for many reasons. Some submit from fear, some from interest, some because obedience relieves them of thought, and a few because the person of the sovereign has become for them an embodiment of order, greatness, and their own highest aspirations. Kasatsky belonged to the last class. Nicholas Pavlovich, with his erect figure, broad chest, measured step, and sonorous voice, appeared to him not as an ordinary man but as the visible form of Russia itself.

The first time the Emperor addressed him at a review, Stepan felt a happiness so sharp that it resembled pain.

“Your company manoeuvres well, Lieutenant.”

“Glad to serve Your Majesty.”

The words were conventional, but Stepan repeated them to himself for weeks. He would gladly have died then and there had the Emperor commanded it. More difficult duties—patience, mercy, freedom from vanity—had not yet been required of him.

By his twenty-fifth year Kasatsky had every reason to expect rapid advancement. He was known at Court, received in the best houses, and was spoken of as a possible aide-de-camp. Yet he was not satisfied. The people of high society may, I think, be divided into four groups: those who know they belong to it and despise everyone outside it; those who belong to it but fear they may not; those who do not belong but are admitted often enough to hope; and those who pretend to despise what they cannot enter. Kasatsky, though a prince and an officer of the Guards, belonged inwardly to the second group. His family was old but not fashionable, his fortune sufficient but not great, and he lacked that careless assurance which is usually acquired not by merit but by seeing one’s parents accepted without question.

It was therefore important to him that he should marry not merely a good and beautiful woman, but one whose position would confirm his own.

Countess Mary Korotkova seemed everything he desired. She was a maid of honour, beautiful, graceful, and surrounded by that atmosphere of Court intimacy which to Kasatsky was almost sacred. She had pale hair, calm grey eyes, and a manner in which reserve appeared tenderness and calculation appeared modesty. Men who knew more of society than Kasatsky spoke of her past with smiles, but he did not understand them. His very purity made him credulous.

He fell in love with her. Or rather, his love contained several feelings which he could not separate: delight in her beauty, gratitude that she preferred him, pride in approaching the Court through her, and a genuine longing to give himself entirely to another person. Because the last feeling was genuine, he believed all the others were equally noble.

Mary accepted him. During the months of their engagement Kasatsky experienced a gentleness previously unknown to him. He imagined domestic life, children, service, and sacrifice. He spoke to her of truth.

“There must be nothing hidden between us,” he said one evening.

They were sitting in a shaded linden alley at her mother’s estate. Beyond the trees the sunset had faded, nightingales trilled, and the leaves moved softly above them.

“Nothing,” Mary answered.

“You know all about me. There is little to know. I have lived badly only in my thoughts.”

She smiled and lowered her eyes.

“And you?”

“Stepan, do not ask foolish questions.”

“I ask because I wish to love everything in your past.”

She became pale. He saw it even in the dusk.

“What is it?”

“Nothing. There was something. Before I knew you.”

He took her hand.

“You loved someone?”

She did not answer.

“Well, that can be forgiven. It is not even something to forgive.”

“Not love,” she whispered.

He released her hand.

The nightingale continued to sing. From the house came the sound of a window being closed.

“Who was it?”

She rose, but he caught her wrist.

“Who?”

“Let me go, Stepan.”

“Tell me.”

She looked at him then with terror and, what was more intolerable to him, pity.

“The Emperor.”

At first he did not understand. The Emperor was to him outside those relations in which ordinary men stood to women. Then he understood everything: the smiles, the hints, her mother’s eagerness for the marriage, Mary’s silences.

“You were his mistress?”

“Don’t say that.”

“Is it true?”

She began to cry.

“It was before I knew you. I wanted to tell you. Maman said—”

“Don’t touch me!”

She had stretched out her hand. His face had become white; the muscles of his jaw trembled.

He walked toward the house, then stopped.

“You knew what he was to me.”

“Stepan—”

“You knew.”

He left that night.

The blow wounded him in three places at once: in his love, because the woman he had worshipped had deceived him; in his pride, because others had known what he had not; and in his devotion to the Emperor, because the man whom he had regarded as almost divine was suddenly revealed as a man who had possessed his promised wife and afterward approved the marriage.

Had Mary’s former lover been an ordinary officer, Kasatsky might have challenged him, killed him, or been killed, and the directness of action would have brought relief. But one could not challenge the Emperor. Nor could he cease at once to revere him. The contradiction was unbearable.

Within a fortnight Kasatsky resigned his commission. Friends attempted to dissuade him.

“Mon cher, you are making a tragedy out of what is, after all, only life,” said a regimental comrade.

“That life is yours. It is not mine.”

His sister came to him and wept. His commanding officer offered leave. A person close to the Court hinted that advancement still awaited him. These efforts strengthened his decision, because every attempt to retain him allowed him to feel superior to those who remained.

Then, astonishing everyone, he entered a monastery.

## II

There were sincere reasons for this act. Kasatsky believed in God, had prayed as a child, and in moments of danger had felt the need of a power higher than the Emperor. The discovery of human corruption in what he had worshipped turned him toward that higher authority. If earthly greatness was false, heavenly greatness must be true.

But there was another reason. By becoming a monk he placed himself above the world that had humiliated him. Mary Korotkova, the courtiers, his comrades, and even the Emperor pursued rank, pleasure, and reputation; he renounced all three. They could pity the disappointed bridegroom, but they could not easily pity the man who had rejected everything they valued.

He gave the larger part of his property to his sister, reserved a small sum for the monastery, and travelled to a distant religious house famous for its starets. There, beneath the low whitewashed arches of the church, amid the smell of wax, incense, damp stone, and old woollen cassocks, he felt for the first time that his suffering might be made useful.

The starets to whom he submitted himself was an old man of no impressive appearance. His beard was sparse, his voice weak, and his eyes often watered. Yet he possessed a quality Kasatsky had never encountered: he did not seem interested in the impression he produced.

“You wish to serve God?” the old man asked.

“Yes.”

“And are you prepared to obey?”

“I am.”

“It is easier to give away an estate than to obey the brother who tells you where to put a broom.”

Kasatsky flushed.

“I will obey in everything.”

The starets sighed, as though he had heard this promise before.

The novice’s life suited Kasatsky. He rose before dawn, attended the offices, worked in the bakery and garden, learned the order of chants, and fulfilled every instruction exactly. If told to carry water, he carried more than anyone else. If ordered to read, he read until he knew the passage by heart. When his body demanded sleep, he rejoiced in conquering it.

He believed he had found peace. In fact, he had found a new field for his old striving.

At first obedience was easy because the starets’s authority seemed indisputable. Kasatsky loved him as he had loved the Emperor, though with a purer love, and he submitted with gladness. The danger began when others praised his submission.

Visitors heard of the Guards officer who had abandoned the world. Ladies who would once have been pleased to receive Prince Kasatsky at a ball now asked to glimpse the novice at church. He disliked their curiosity, but he also noticed it. When they looked at him, tall and pale in his black garment, he lowered his eyes more humbly than was necessary.

After three years he received the tonsure and the name Sergius. Later he was ordained. His former life seemed remote; yet at unexpected moments it returned—the flash of epaulettes in sunlight, the pressure of a horse under him, Mary’s pale face, the Emperor’s voice. These memories no longer caused the same pain. He had transformed them into evidence of his sacrifice.

For seven years he remained in the first monastery. Then the starets directed him to another house nearer the capital.

“Why must I go?” Sergius asked.

“Because you would rather remain.”

This answer pleased him. It seemed profound, and because it demanded sacrifice he accepted it.

The second monastery was richer and more frequented by persons of rank. Its Abbot was a courteous, able man with plump hands, a protruding stomach, and a satisfied smile. He spoke French to aristocratic visitors and was skilled in obtaining donations without appearing to request them.

At first Father Sergius reproached himself for judging him. Then he became convinced that his judgment was righteous discernment.

The Abbot treated him with favour. His birth, appearance, and history were useful to the monastery. He was asked to preach when distinguished guests attended, placed where he could be seen during services, and summoned to receive pious ladies who desired advice from one who understood both the world and renunciation.

Sergius obeyed, but his obedience became increasingly troubled. He detected vanity in the Abbot and resented being used to satisfy it. He did not detect with equal clarity the vanity in his own resentment.

The quarrel came on a summer feast day. A general, formerly a comrade of Kasatsky’s and now high in favour, arrived at the monastery with his family. During dinner he expressed a desire to see Father Sergius.

The Abbot sent for him.

Sergius was at the time reading in his cell. The messenger, a young monk, entered hurriedly.

“His Reverence asks you to come to the guest chamber.”

“For what purpose?”

“A general wishes to meet you.”

“Tell His Reverence I am occupied.”

The monk looked frightened.

“He ordered me to bring you.”

Sergius went. In the guest chamber stood the Abbot, the general, two ladies in pale dresses, and a young aide-de-camp whose whiskers smelled of cigar smoke. Wine stood on the table.

“Here is our Father Sergius,” said the Abbot with a pleased smile. “You wished to see him.”

The general examined Sergius as he might have examined a curious weapon.

“Why, Kasatsky! I should hardly have known you.”

Sergius bowed.

The younger lady whispered something in French, and the other smiled.

A heat rose through him.

“Was I summoned only to be exhibited?”

The room became silent.

“My dear Father,” said the Abbot, “these are benefactors of the monastery.”

“I asked whether I was summoned as an exhibit.”

“You forget yourself.”

“No. It is you who forget what this garment means.”

The Abbot’s face darkened.

“You may go.”

Sergius clenched his fist within his sleeve and left.

That evening, when the anger had passed, he was horrified. He had insulted his superior, offended guests, and felt in his clenched fist the same violence that had once driven him to seize the inkstand. Nothing had changed. The uniform had become a cassock, worldly ambition spiritual ambition, but Stepan Kasatsky remained.

He asked forgiveness. The Abbot granted it with a magnanimity which irritated him even more.

Soon afterward Sergius petitioned for permission to withdraw to a hermitage belonging to the monastery. The request was eventually approved. People said he sought solitude in order to conquer himself. This was true. It was also true that in solitude no inferior man would command him.

## III

The hermitage stood in a forest some miles from the monastery, near the grave of an ascetic who had lived there half a century before. It consisted of a small wooden chapel, a cell, and a shed. An old lay brother brought bread, oil, and firewood twice a week.

The first months were difficult. Father Sergius had expected silence to be full of God, but often it was merely silence. He prayed, read the Psalter, worked, and made prostrations. Yet thoughts entered precisely because there was nothing to interrupt them.

At times he doubted everything. Perhaps, he thought, the Church was only another regiment, with ranks, uniforms, rewards, and punishments. Perhaps prayer was speech addressed to emptiness. Perhaps he had left the world not for God but because he could not endure defeat.

These thoughts terrified him less by their content than by their persistence. He fought them as he had once fought fatigue: by increasing effort. He lengthened his prayers, ate less, slept on boards, and in winter refused additional firewood.

Gradually a certain peace came. Whether this peace arose from faith, habit, exhaustion, or pride in his endurance he did not ask. He knew only that his life had acquired strict order and that reports of his severity were spreading.

Visitors began to appear. He received few of them. Refusal increased their desire. It was said that Father Sergius possessed unusual spiritual power, and people who would not have crossed a street to aid a hungry neighbour travelled many miles to see a man who ate almost nothing.

Six years passed.

One winter evening a party of wealthy people left the nearby town after supper to drive in sledges. There were two men, three women, and a divorced lady named Makovkina, celebrated for her beauty and recklessness. She was thirty, dark-eyed, strong, and accustomed to regard every prohibition as a challenge to her attractiveness.

The night was clear. The runners hissed over smooth snow, the harness bells tinkled, and the women laughed beneath their furs. Someone pointed toward the forest.

“That is where the handsome hermit lives.”

“Kasatsky?” asked Makovkina.

“Father Sergius. They say women are not admitted after sunset.”

“Then I shall be admitted.”

A wager followed. Before her companions understood that she was serious, she had ordered the driver to stop, climbed from the sledge, and walked toward the narrow track leading to the hermitage.

Snow entered her shoes. At first she laughed; then, as the bells receded, she became angry and frightened. The forest was darker than she had expected. At last she saw a small light.

Father Sergius was praying when he heard knocking.

“Who is there?”

“A traveller. I have lost my way.”

The voice was feminine.

He remained still.

“For Christ’s sake, open! I am freezing.”

He crossed himself, went to the door, and opened it.

She stood there wrapped in white fur, her cheeks flushed, snow shining on her veil. One foot was bare, for she had lost a shoe.

“I cannot remain outside,” she said. “Unless holy men allow women to freeze.”

He admitted her to the outer room and gave her a blanket.

“You may stay here until morning. I shall be in the chapel.”

“Please help me. My foot is numb.”

He placed a lamp on the bench but did not approach.

“You can warm it yourself.”

She laughed softly. He saw at once that she was not lost. The knowledge angered him, and beneath the anger something else stirred.

“I came on a wager,” she said. “There. I confess. You must forgive me.”

“You should leave.”

“In this snow?”

She removed her wet stockings and began rubbing her feet. Her movements, careless and assured, exposed the shape of her legs beneath her dress. Sergius turned away.

“I shall go to the chapel.”

“Father Sergius.”

He stopped.

“Do you fear me?”

“No.”

“Then why do you run away?”

Her voice had changed. It was lower and tenderer. She knew the change and relied upon it as a marksman relies upon a familiar weapon.

He entered the chapel and shut the door. His heart beat violently. He began to pray.

“Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy upon me.”

From the other room came the rustle of clothing.

“Father Sergius!”

He prayed more loudly.

“I am ill. Truly. Come to me.”

He knew she was lying. Then he thought: What if she is not? This thought was welcome because it gave him a reason to enter, and its welcome nature revealed the danger.

He looked around. Beside the wall lay an axe used for splitting kindling.

In former struggles he had conquered desire by fasting and prayer, and each victory had increased his confidence. But now the woman was close, beautiful, and deliberately summoning him. He pictured her bare shoulders, though he had not seen them. He pictured entering, reproving her, then forgiving her. His mind prepared every step toward sin in the language of duty.

Suddenly he placed his left hand upon the chopping block, raised the axe, and struck.

The first sensation was not pain but a heavy shock. A finger fell onto the wood. Blood spread darkly across it. Then the pain came, fierce and clean.

He wrapped his hand in his robe and returned to the outer room.

Makovkina sat on the bench, her dress loosened at the breast. She smiled, but when she saw his face the smile vanished.

“What have you done?”

“I have given myself a reminder,” he said. “You wished to destroy your soul and mine. Go.”

Blood dripped onto the floor.

She stared at the maimed hand and began to tremble.

“Forgive me.”

“God will forgive you.”

“Forgive me!”

He turned away.

At dawn her companions returned and found her walking alone on the road. She said nothing of the night. A year later she entered a convent. She did not become a perfect woman; people do not change so simply. But the sight of the severed finger remained with her, and whenever vanity or anger rose in her, she remembered the blood on the wooden floor and was ashamed.

For Father Sergius the event had consequences equally serious and less beneficial. The story became known. Makovkina herself concealed it, but servants saw his wound, and rumour supplied what fact withheld. The hermit who had cut off his finger to preserve chastity became famous.

He had conquered temptation. And that conquest became a greater temptation.

## IV

Pilgrims came in increasing numbers. At first Father Sergius resisted. He begged the monastery to protect his solitude, but the Abbot, who had forgiven the old quarrel and understood the value of sanctity better than many understand its danger, arranged that visitors should be admitted on appointed days.

A shelter was built. Then a larger chapel. Merchants gave money; noblewomen donated lamps, vestments, and embroidered coverings. The forest path became a road. Father Sergius had fled the world, and the world established itself around his cell.

Among the visitors was a woman whose fourteen-year-old son had suffered from fever for many months. She begged Sergius to lay his hand upon the boy. He refused, saying that healing belonged to God, not to man. She remained outside through the night, kneeling in the mud.

The next day he received her. He prayed over the boy and touched his head. Within a week the fever ceased.

It might have ceased in any case; such things occur. But the mother declared a miracle, the boy confirmed it, and the story spread. Other sick people came. Some recovered, more did not, but the recoveries were remembered and the deaths explained by insufficient faith.

Sergius himself did not claim miraculous power. Indeed, he spoke against such claims. Yet when a sufferer improved after his prayer, he felt a secret warmth; and when one failed to improve, he searched anxiously for causes. Thus he denied the power in words while becoming inwardly dependent upon the belief that he possessed it.

His days were no longer his own. He rose early, prayed, received petitioners, blessed children, counselled merchants, listened to confessions, and allowed the sick to touch the edge of his garment. He ate little, but the little was prepared with care. He slept on a narrow bed, but the linen was clean. An attendant guarded his hours. Another kept the accounts. Obedience, poverty, and solitude surrounded him in organised forms from which their substance had departed.

He was now fifty. His beard had grown grey, his face was thin, and the missing finger, shown unintentionally whenever he blessed someone, inspired reverence.

Sometimes during a service he felt genuine tenderness. An old peasant woman crossing herself awkwardly, a child staring at the candles, or the words of a familiar prayer would suddenly break through the crust of habit, and he would weep. But at other times he stood before hundreds of kneeling people and thought only of how he appeared to them.

“It is not I; it is God,” he repeated.

Yet he took satisfaction in saying it well.

The division within him grew painful. Formerly temptation had appeared as anger, doubt, or sexual desire, and he could oppose it. Now temptation appeared as holiness itself. The more humbly he bowed, the more they revered him; the more he protested his unworthiness, the more certain they became of his sanctity.

There are men who seek approval by boasting and men who seek it by self-abasement. The second are considered humbler only because their method is more refined.

Father Sergius did not know how to escape. If he dismissed the pilgrims, he would be praised for loving solitude. If he received them, he would be praised for charity. Even his weariness acquired religious importance.

One day a merchant arrived with his daughter, a young woman of twenty-two. He was stout, red-faced, and elaborately respectful.

“Holy Father, she is weak in her mind,” he said. “The doctors can do nothing. She has strange attacks. She laughs, cries, cannot sleep. We have brought her to your mercy.”

Sergius saw the daughter standing behind him. Her face was broad and pale, with heavy fair hair and large, unfocused eyes. There was something childish in her expression and something boldly physical in the way she looked at him.

“Bring her tomorrow with the others,” he said.

The merchant fell to his knees.

“Privately, Father. She fears people. Only privately.”

Sergius hesitated. He was tired, and the hesitation itself made him feel guilty.

“Bring her this evening.”

After sunset the woman was led into the reception room adjoining his cell. Her father remained outside.

“What troubles you?” Sergius asked.

She smiled.

“Everything troubles me.”

“Do you pray?”

“Sometimes.”

She moved closer.

“My father says you cure people by touching them.”

“It is God who heals.”

“But you touch them.”

He felt the old danger at once. It astonished him because the woman was neither so beautiful nor so artful as Makovkina. He had resisted beauty at thirty-nine; at fifty, honoured as a saint, he ought not even to have noticed this confused young person.

He raised his maimed hand.

“Kneel.”

She knelt. He placed his hand upon her head and began the prayer. Her hair was warm beneath his palm. She lifted her face and looked directly at him.

“Your hand trembles,” she said.

“Be silent.”

“Are you afraid of me?”

The words were almost the same as those spoken years before, but now there was no axe, no dramatic struggle, no clear enemy. There was only fatigue, vanity, the pressure of her body as she rose, and the sudden collapse of what he had believed permanently conquered.

She put her arms around him.

He yielded.

Afterward she slept in the outer room while he sat in his cell. Dawn had not yet come. The lamp burned low, and from beyond the wall he heard her steady breathing.

At first he could not understand what had happened. The man who had cut off a finger, who had blessed thousands, who was spoken of as a saint, had committed the ordinary sin of an ordinary man. Yet even in his horror he was concerned with what people would say. The discovery of this concern completed his humiliation.

“There is no God,” he thought. “If there were, He would not have allowed this.”

But another thought answered: “You did not serve God. You served their opinion.”

He opened a chest and took out a peasant coat kept for work. He cut his hair and beard with scissors. For a moment he looked at the razor. He could draw it across his throat and end the lie.

He carried it outside. The sky was whitening above the trees. Birds had begun to call. He placed the blade against his neck, but fear stopped him—not fear of death alone, but fear that suicide too would be a final gesture, a last assertion of his exceptional despair.

He dropped the razor.

Without speaking to anyone, he left the hermitage and walked into the forest.

## V

For a day and a night he walked almost without stopping. He ate bread given by a peasant and drank from a stream. His feet blistered. The physical pain was welcome, but he no longer trusted suffering; he had learned that a man may suffer theatrically even when no one sees him, because an imagined audience remains within.

On the second evening he lay beneath a birch tree. He intended to sleep briefly, then decide whether to continue living.

In a dream he saw himself as a boy at his mother’s estate. A thin girl with untidy hair was playing the piano badly in the next room. Everyone called her Pashenka. She was a distant relative, Praskovya Mikhailovna, mocked by the children because she was awkward, eager to please, and liable to cry when laughed at. Once Stepan had led the others in persuading her that she was being admired by a visitor; when she discovered the joke, she had hidden in the garden.

In the dream a voice said: “Go to Pashenka. Learn from her what you sought from yourself.”

He woke before sunrise. The dream seemed absurd. He had not heard of Praskovya for many years. Then he remembered that a pilgrim had once mentioned a poor widow of that name living in a provincial town not far away.

By noon he was walking toward it.

He found her after two days’ inquiry. She lived in a small wooden house at the edge of town with her daughter, her daughter’s husband, and three grandchildren. The plaster had fallen from the stove; children’s stockings hung near it; a sour smell of cabbage, medicine, and damp clothes filled the rooms.

Praskovya Mikhailovna was nearly sixty. Her thin hair was streaked with grey, her shoulders stooped, and she wore spectacles tied with black thread. She did not recognise him.

“I am Stepan Kasatsky,” he said.

She stared. Then her hands flew to her breast.

“Stepan? Prince Stepan?”

“Do not call me prince.”

“But—Father Sergius!”

“Do not call me that either.”

She led him into a narrow room where a bed, a broken chair, and a table left scarcely enough space to stand. She was agitated and ashamed of the disorder.

“If I had known! We have nothing prepared. Masha is ill, and the baby has been crying all night. My son-in-law—well, he is unwell too.”

“Let no one know who I am.”

She looked at him more carefully and saw his peasant coat, cropped beard, and exhausted face.

“What has happened?”

“I have sinned. I have deceived everyone. I came because I dreamed I must learn from you.”

“From me?” She laughed, but at once covered her mouth so as not to wake the baby. “You see what I am. I give music lessons for twenty kopecks and quarrel when they do not pay. I lose patience with the children. Yesterday I was angry because Misha spilled the milk.”

He listened.

She told him her life without suspecting that it contained anything worthy of attention. She had married for love against her family’s wishes. Her husband drank, squandered what little they had, and sometimes struck her. She had reproached him bitterly and now remembered those reproaches more painfully than the blows. After his death she went to live with her daughter. The daughter was sickly; the son-in-law, once employed in an office, had lost his position and drank. Praskovya taught music to merchants’ children, mended clothes at night, cooked, nursed the babies, and managed the debts.

“Do you pray?” Sergius asked.

“Badly. Sometimes morning comes and I have said nothing. Then while kneading the bread I remember and say, ‘Lord, forgive me.’ What sort of prayer is that?”

“Do people know what you do for them?”

“Who? Masha knows, I suppose. But she is ill. And Ivan is not himself when he drinks.”

“Do you resent them?”

“Of course. Then I am ashamed. What good am I if I add anger to their trouble?”

A child cried in the next room. Praskovya rose immediately.

“Forgive me. I must go.”

Through the half-open door he watched her lift the baby, quiet it, stir the stove, give medicine to her daughter, and speak gently to her son-in-law, who entered with a swollen face and avoided her eyes. No one thanked her. No one admired her. Her work was not called sacrifice because it was required every day and performed among dirty plates, unpaid bills, and crying children.

Sergius remembered the crowds waiting for his blessing, the chapel built around his reputation, the awe inspired by his missing finger. He had performed difficult acts before witnesses and believed them service to God. Praskovya performed small acts no one noticed and believed herself a sinner.

Then he understood—not completely, for no important truth is understood completely at once, but enough to alter his direction—that he had lived for men while pretending to live for God, whereas she lived for God precisely because she had forgotten to consider how her life appeared to men.

He had wished to be the first officer, the most devoted subject, the purest lover, the most obedient monk, the severest hermit, and the holiest starets. The object changed; the wish to be first remained.

Even his repentance had demanded greatness. He had thought his fall terrible because it was the fall of Father Sergius. To Praskovya it would have been simply another human sin requiring sorrow, amendment, and mercy.

She returned carrying a cup of weak tea and a piece of bread.

“It is all we have.”

“It is enough.”

She sat opposite him.

“What will you do?”

“I do not know.”

“Return to the monastery.”

“No.”

“Then where will you go?”

He looked at her worn hands.

“Where I am not known.”

She began to cry.

“Pray for me, Pashenka.”

“You must pray for me.”

“No,” he said. “You pray for me.”

He left before evening, refusing money but accepting bread she wrapped in a cloth. At the gate she crossed him as one crosses a traveller or a child. That blessing, given timidly by a poor, overworked woman who thought herself unworthy, affected him more deeply than all the solemn ceremonies of his former life.

## VI

From that time Stepan Kasatsky became a wanderer.

At first the old habits persisted. When he approached a village, he imagined how the peasants would respond if they knew who asked bread at their doors. When he helped an old man lift a cart from the mud, he caught himself thinking: “This is true humility.” The thought spoiled the act.

But gradually, through hunger, weariness, repetition, and the indifference of those among whom he moved, these thoughts weakened. The world does not long preserve a man’s heroic conception of himself when he is dirty, footsore, and obliged to wait outside a kitchen for a crust.

He travelled with pilgrims, labourers, discharged soldiers, beggars, and women going to holy shrines. He learned to ask without eloquence and to receive refusal without indignation. Sometimes he gave away the bread he had just obtained; sometimes hunger made him keep it, and he saw that he was neither angel nor monster but a man.

In one village he stayed with a soldier’s widow whose roof had partly fallen in. He repaired it and remained to dig her garden. In another he spent three weeks caring for a sick peasant who had no family. He washed him, carried out the foul bedding, and listened to his complaints. No one regarded these actions as remarkable. That was their benefit.

He knew the Scriptures and began teaching children their letters. Since he no longer preached at them, they listened. He wrote petitions for peasants who could not write, read letters from sons in the army, and settled small disputes when both parties trusted him. If asked who he was, he said:

“A servant of God.”

This answer had once implied distinction. Now it meant only that he had no other name to give.

For eight months he wandered. The less he sought peace, the more often peace came—not as exaltation, not as certainty, but as a quiet release from the necessity of measuring himself. Sometimes while walking at dusk, hearing cattle bells beyond a field or seeing smoke rise from a cottage, he felt that life did not require him to be extraordinary. It required only that he respond to the person and task before him.

Yet he did not imagine himself cured. Desire returned. Anger returned. Vanity returned in subtler forms. But now, instead of making dramatic war upon them, he recognised them, was ashamed, prayed briefly, and continued his work.

One autumn day he was walking with several beggars near a district town when the police stopped them.

“Passports.”

The others produced papers of one kind or another. Kasatsky had none.

“Name?”

“Stepan.”

“Family name?”

He hesitated.

“I have none that is useful to you.”

The constable struck the ground with his stick.

“Occupation?”

“Wandering.”

He was taken to the police station. There, under questioning, he might have revealed his identity. His sister was still living; former acquaintances would have intervened; Church authorities would have reclaimed him. For an instant he pictured the astonishment caused by the discovery that the famous Father Sergius had been found among beggars. Even disgrace offered him the possibility of becoming exceptional again.

He remained silent.

The official, a tired man with ink on his cuffs, classified him as a vagrant without papers. He was sentenced to exile in Siberia.

The journey was long. Prisoners quarrelled, guards cursed, chains clinked, and disease passed through the convoy. Kasatsky did what little he could. He shared food with a feverish youth, prayed with a dying thief, and carried the bundle of an old sectarian when the guards did not object.

No revelation attended these acts. Often he was disgusted, impatient, or afraid. Once, after giving his ration to another prisoner, he regretted it all night. But in the morning, when he saw the man alive and able to walk, the regret passed.

In Siberia he was assigned to a settlement where a prosperous peasant agreed to take him into his household. The peasant, Nikita, possessed a large family, several cows, and more work than hands.

“You can read?” Nikita asked.

“Yes.”

“Write?”

“Yes.”

“Work?”

“I can learn.”

Kasatsky lived in a small room near the stable. He worked in the garden, kept accounts, taught Nikita’s children, and during winter evenings read aloud to the family. When illness came, he nursed whoever was sick. He had no special skill, but he was patient, and patience is often the greater part of nursing.

The children loved him because he did not demand reverence. To them he was simply Stepan, the old man with the missing finger who knew stories of soldiers, palaces, saints, and distant cities. They sometimes asked how he had lost the finger.

“I was proud,” he said.

They did not understand and assumed it had happened in battle.

Years passed. News from European Russia reached the settlement rarely and late. The famous hermit Father Sergius was said by some to be dead, by others to have disappeared because of a secret crime, and by still others to be wandering in a hidden state of sanctity. Legends accumulated around the empty place he had abandoned.

Kasatsky did not hear most of them. When he did hear his old name, it seemed to concern another man—not because he had ceased to be that man, but because he no longer needed to defend or condemn him.

One spring morning Nikita’s youngest daughter fell ill with inflammation of the lungs. Kasatsky sat beside her for two nights, changing the cloth on her forehead and giving her water. On the third morning the fever lessened. The child opened her eyes.

“Stepan, did you cure me?”

“No.”

“Then who did?”

“God, perhaps. Or your own life.”

She considered this and fell asleep.

He went outside. Snow still lay in the hollows, but water ran beneath it, and the birches shone in the pale sun. From the yard came the sound of an axe and a woman calling to the cattle.

Kasatsky stood for a moment, breathing the cold air. No pilgrims waited. No one knelt for his blessing. No history would record that he had spent two nights beside a sick child, and he felt no wish that it should.

Nikita called from the shed:

“Stepan! When you have time, the grey mare’s harness needs mending.”

“I am coming,” he answered.

And he went.

He had once sought God in grandeur: in the Emperor’s majesty, in renunciation, in obedience, in solitude, in suffering, and in the reverence of crowds. All these had contained something of what he sought, but each had also reflected his own image back to him, magnified and disguised.

Now there was no image. There was a torn harness, a child recovering from fever, lessons to be prepared, and an old peasant coughing in the next house. There was work which another person needed and which he could do.

Whether he had at last found the true life he did not ask. To ask it would already have been to turn from the work toward himself.

He threaded the needle, bent over the leather, and began to mend it.

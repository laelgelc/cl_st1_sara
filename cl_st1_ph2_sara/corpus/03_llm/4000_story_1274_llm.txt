There were six positions advertised that morning, and by three o’clock the young woman had applied for all of them and received, in one form or another, the same answer.

At the first place the manager told her she lacked experience; at the second, that she had too much education for the work; at the third, that a girl with relatives in Chicago would be preferred. Two offices had already filled their vacancies, though the notices were still in the paper, and at the sixth a tired clerk looked at her shoes, her carefully brushed coat, and the Colorado address on her letter of reference, and said they wanted someone who understood the city.

She came out of the building with eleven dollars in her purse—two five-dollar bills and one silver dollar—and no understanding of the city at all.

Chicago moved about her with the dreadful assurance of something that could not stop to ask whether one small human being was keeping pace. Elevated trains roared overhead; streetcars clanged; men and women hurried by with their faces turned toward engagements, wages, meals, homes. The girl stood on the pavement for a moment, holding her gloves in one hand, and thought that even the wind seemed to know where it was going.

She had been in Chicago eight weeks. During that time she had learned the price of a hall bedroom, the price of coffee and rolls, the price of having a dress cleaned, and the greater cost of letting it go shabby. She had learned that advertisements reading “Young woman wanted” brought forty young women to one narrow stairway; that references from respected people in a Colorado town meant little to a superintendent born in New York and promoted in Illinois; and that courage, like shoe leather, wore thin when used upon pavements.

Yet there was one place where she could go without spending anything and without being required to explain why she had come. That place was the public library.

She entered it now, partly because her room at the boarding house would be cold until supper, partly because she could not bear to count her money again, and chiefly because, in a certain alcove of the reading-room, the Denver newspaper waited upon its wooden rod.

The paper was sometimes two days old. That did not matter. Its very name was a comforting companionship. She read the reports of snow in the passes, of cattle shipped from the western slope, of a school election in a town she knew, of a wedding at which the bride’s aunt had once taught her geography. She read advertisements for seed, mining machinery, irrigated land, and excursions into the mountains. Even the names of streets gave her anchorage.

They were not Chicago streets, stretching grimly between walls. They were streets down which one might look, at the proper turning, and see the far blue lift of the hills.

This afternoon the paper was already in use.

An elderly woman sat beneath the shaded lamp, holding it close to her face. She was poorly dressed, but with that careful neatness which makes poverty more touching because it has tried so hard not to be seen. Her black bonnet had been mended at the crown; her gloves were gray at the finger ends; and a thin wisp of hair, almost white, had escaped beside one ear. The paper trembled in her hands.

The girl took a magazine from the table and waited.

Five minutes passed, then ten. The old woman did not turn the page. She bent nearer, drew back, and bent near again. Once she raised her hand and rubbed her eyes with the knuckles, like a sleepy child.

The girl ceased pretending to read.

“Are you from Colorado?” she asked softly.

The woman started. For an instant she looked frightened, as though she had been accused of something; then her face changed.

“Why—yes. Yes, I am. Leastways, I was.”

“So was I.”

“You don’t say!”

The words escaped almost joyfully. The woman lowered the newspaper, and the two looked at each other with the immediate interest of exiles who discover that they have stood beneath the same sky.

“What part?” the girl asked.

“Denver for a spell. Before that, up toward the mountains. Not in ’em exactly, but close—close enough to see ’em every clear day. You?”

The girl named her town.

“I knowed it,” the woman said. “Went through there once with my husband. ’Twas in June, and there was light streamin’ all over everything. Funny what a body recollects. I couldn’t tell you the year—not certain—but I recollect that light.”

She smiled and lifted the paper again, but the smile faded as she tried to read.

“Would you like me to find something for you?” the girl asked. “My eyes are good, and I know how closely they print some of those columns.”

The woman’s fingers tightened upon the wooden rod.

“I was just lookin’ at the weather.”

“In Denver?”

“In the mountains.”

The girl drew her chair nearer.

The report was brief. Fair weather was predicted over most of the state, with lower temperatures after sunset and a possibility of snow at higher elevations. She read it aloud. Then, because the woman continued to wait, she read an item about a new road, another about tourists returning early from a mountain hotel, and a little description of the evening light after a storm.

At the words “purple shadows advancing across the eastern slope,” the woman gave a low breath.

“That’s it,” she said. “That’s how they come.”

“The shadows?”

“Slow at first. You don’t notice ’em unless you’re watchin’. Then one peak goes dark, and then another. The snow holds the light longest. Seems like it don’t want to give it up.”

Her voice had fallen to a whisper. Several readers glanced toward them and away again.

The girl said, “It makes me homesick to hear you.”

“I oughtn’t to talk so. Young folks shouldn’t be made downhearted.”

“Oh, I was homesick before.”

“Are you workin’ here?”

“I am looking for work.”

“That’s hard.”

“Yes.”

The answer was short, for the six refusals were still too near. But when the older woman’s hand moved uncertainly across the newspaper, the girl was ashamed of the bitterness in her own voice.

“Have you been in Chicago long?” she asked.

“Seven years come November. My sister’s girl brought me after my husband died. She meant kind. They all meant kind.”

“But you would rather be in Colorado.”

The woman looked at her quickly.

“Did I say that?”

“You did not need to.”

For a moment neither spoke. Around them pages turned, chairs scraped, and the great room preserved its ordered quiet—a quiet made, not of rest, but of many people keeping their troubles to themselves.

At last the woman said, “I’d like to see it once.”

“You will.”

“No.” She folded her hands on the newspaper. “No, I won’t.”

“Why not?”

“Costs money.”

“Your relatives—”

“They ain’t got it. My niece’s husband’s been laid off twice this winter. Three children, rent due, doctor bills from when the baby had pneumonia. They feed me. I won’t say they don’t. I sew when there’s light enough, and I mind the little ones. But a train ticket clear out there, and then up where I want to go—why, it’s foolish even to think on.”

“It isn’t foolish.”

“Ain’t it?”

“No.”

The old woman looked down.

“I’m goin’ blind,” she said.

There was no tremor in the statement. That came afterward, in the hand that reached up as if to arrange the edge of her bonnet and failed to find it.

The girl leaned nearer. “Blind?”

“Doctor says so. I thought it was age. Print slippin’ around, faces gettin’ cloudy, lamps wearin’ halos. But it ain’t age—not just age. He says there may be months. Might be less. Can’t tell.”

“Oh!”

“It don’t hurt much. That’s something.”

The girl’s eyes filled. She laid her hand over the worn glove resting on the paper.

“Does your family know?”

“They know about my eyes. They don’t know about—about this other.”

“The mountains?”

The woman nodded.

“They wouldn’t understand,” she said apologetically. “They was all born here except my niece, and she was little when she left. She says, ‘Auntie, you can look at pictures.’ She means kind. There’s a calendar at home with Pike’s Peak on it, but it ain’t the same. A picture’s flat. It don’t wait for you. It don’t change while you’re lookin’.”

“No,” the girl said. “It isn’t the same.”

“I knowed you’d know what I mean.”

The words passed between them like a clasp of hands.

Slowly, with many pauses, the woman told her story. She had gone west as a bride when the settlements were smaller and journeys longer. She and her husband had lived first in two rooms behind a store, then in a frame house with a pump that froze every winter. They had worked, saved, lost, begun again. Three children had been born to them. One had died as a baby; one, a boy of twelve, had been taken by fever; the daughter had lived to marry and had then been buried beside her father.

“All there,” the woman said. “Every one of ’em.”

“In Colorado?”

“Under the mountains. Not right under, you understand. But where you can see ’em.”

She spoke of her husband coming home at dusk, his shoulders bent from labor, pausing before he entered to look west. She spoke of washing days when the wind snapped the sheets, of the children calling from the road, of snow piled high against the shed, and of summer evenings when the whole range seemed to float in rose-colored air.

“When my man died,” she said, “I thought there wasn’t anything left that knowed me. Then I went outdoors. They was there. Same as always. I don’t mean I thought rocks had sense. I ain’t ignorant. But they’d seen us come young, and they’d seen us get old. They’d seen the children. Somehow it rested me.”

The girl’s hand tightened over hers.

“And now,” the woman continued, “I wake nights tryin’ to see ’em in my mind. Sometimes I can. Sometimes there’s only a kind of gray. That scares me. I don’t mind darkness so much if I can carry the right things into it. But suppose I forget? Suppose the last thing I see clear is a brick wall, or the washtub, or somebody’s back in a streetcar?”

“You must go home.”

The woman gave a little, broken laugh.

“On what?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Train folks don’t take longin’ instead of tickets.”

“Someone must help.”

“Who? My people can’t. The charity lady came once about spectacles, but when I tried to tell her, she said their funds was for necessities. Maybe she’s right. Mountains ain’t bread.”

“They may be a necessity to you.”

“Not the kind they put on forms.”

She drew her hand away gently and began folding the paper, though it belonged upon the rod.

“I shouldn’t have said all this. You’ve got your own worries. I could tell by your face when you came in.”

The girl thought of the eleven dollars.

It was all she possessed between herself and failure. Five dollars would pay part of another week at the boarding house. Five dollars meant carfare to distant offices, clean collars, stamps for applications, perhaps the meal she might otherwise omit. Prudence spoke clearly. It had spoken all day in the voices of clerks and managers and landladies. It said that no one in Chicago would save her if she did not save herself.

Then she looked at the woman’s dim, searching eyes.

From those eyes the mountains were already receding. Day by day the familiar world narrowed: print vanished; faces clouded; lamps became blurred circles. Somewhere beyond the walls of the library, beyond the smoke and miles and winter plains, the peaks still held their snow against the western light. They were there, and this woman, who had given them the trust of a lifetime, might lose them forever for the want of a railway ticket.

The girl opened her purse.

The two five-dollar bills lay beside the silver dollar.

She took out one bill.

“I want you to have this.”

The woman stared at it, then at her.

“No.”

“Please.”

“No, child. I couldn’t.”

“It is mine to give.”

“You said you was huntin’ work.”

“I shall find it.”

“You don’t know that.”

“I know I can spare this.”

But she did not know it; and because both women understood, the gift became greater and harder to accept.

The old woman pushed the bill back with trembling fingers.

“Five dollars! Why, five dollars’d carry you a long way if you had to go home yourself.”

“Not far enough,” the girl said. “And perhaps it will carry you farther.”

“I can’t take it.”

“Then let me use it for you.”

“How?”

“I do not know yet. But I will think of something.”

Hope came painfully into the old face, so painfully that the girl wished for an instant she had not awakened it.

“You mustn’t promise,” the woman whispered. “Promises hurt when they can’t be kept.”

“I will not promise what I cannot do. I only ask you to meet me here tomorrow at four.”

The woman hesitated.

“At the Denver paper?”

“At the Denver paper.”

When she had gone, the girl sat for some minutes without moving. Then she asked the librarian for a sheet of paper, an envelope, and permission to place a notice in the alcove.

The librarian was doubtful. Notices were not ordinarily permitted. Appeals for money were especially irregular. Rules existed because there were many needs and too few walls.

The girl listened, then told the story.

Before she had finished, the librarian removed her spectacles, polished them, and said the notice might remain for three days.

The girl wrote:

TO COLORADO PEOPLE:

An old Colorado woman in Chicago is losing her sight. Before darkness comes she longs to see the mountains once more—the mountains near which her husband and children are buried. She has no money for the journey. Her relatives are poor.

Those who have been homesick will understand.

I give five dollars. Will others who love Colorado help send her home?

Contributions may be placed in this envelope or left at the desk.

She signed no name. The woman’s name she did not know.

She placed the five-dollar bill in the envelope, fastened it beneath the notice, and went out with one silver dollar between herself and the next unknown day.

At four o’clock on the following afternoon, the old woman waited beside the Denver paper. The girl did not come.

At half past four she was still waiting.

By five, fear had conquered the little hope she had permitted herself to feel. Perhaps the girl had found work. Perhaps she had regretted the five dollars and removed the notice. Perhaps she had been kind only for one emotional hour, as people sometimes were, and had awakened next morning embarrassed by their own generosity.

The librarian approached.

“You are the Colorado woman?”

“I reckon so.”

“This was left for you.”

It was a note in the girl’s handwriting.

I have been sent to an office where there may be work and cannot return before closing. Please do not lose courage. The notice is still there.

The old woman turned toward the alcove. She could no longer read the words upon the wall, but she saw the pale square of paper and, beneath it, the envelope bulging against its pin.

“How much?” she asked.

The librarian counted.

There were the five-dollar bill, three one-dollar bills, six half-dollars, twelve quarters, and many dimes and nickels. There were also two postage stamps and a streetcar token, put in by someone who had possessed nothing else convenient to give.

“Fourteen dollars and eighty-five cents,” said the librarian.

The woman gripped the edge of the desk.

“All that?”

“Since yesterday.”

“From rich folks?”

The librarian looked toward the reading-room.

A laundry worker came each evening to read the Pueblo news. A waiter from a cheap restaurant followed the mining reports. Two laborers shared the Denver paper because neither could afford a subscription. A seamstress had given fifty cents and afterward walked home. A boy who swept an office had put in three nickels, saying his mother lived near Leadville. There were prosperous readers, too, but the envelope did not record which coin came from which hand.

“From Colorado people,” the librarian answered.

During the next two days, the notice seemed to acquire a quiet life of its own.

Strangers paused before it. Some read and passed on, only to return after they had visited the newspaper tables. Some spoke to the librarian in low voices. One man laughed roughly and said that the old lady was not the only person who wanted a free trip west; twenty minutes later he came back and thrust a dollar into the envelope without looking at anyone.

A woman in a fur-trimmed coat left five dollars. A bent old miner left twenty-five cents.

“It’d buy me tobacco,” he said, “but it might buy her a mile of mountains.”

A young mother contributed ten cents and explained that she could not give more because her baby needed milk.

A hotel porter stood staring at the notice for a long while.

“I used to see the range every morning,” he told the librarian. “Never thought much about it then.”

“How much do you wish to give?”

He smiled uneasily.

“Week’s pay would be nearer what I feel. Fifty cents is nearer what I’ve got.”

The girl, meanwhile, had obtained temporary employment addressing circulars. It would last only four days, and the wage was small, but when she returned to the library the next evening there was a new steadiness in her step. She found the older woman surrounded by three people, all talking of Colorado.

They had never met before.

One had lived in a mining camp; one had taught school on the plains; one had been born in Denver but carried away as a child. Their memories did not agree in detail, yet they spoke as relatives speak at a family table. Snowstorms grew deeper, skies bluer, distances wider; and whenever one faltered, another said, “I know what you mean.”

The old woman sat among them with both hands clasped, listening.

When the girl touched her shoulder, she turned and began to cry.

“I didn’t know,” she said. “I didn’t know there was so many that remembered.”

On the third evening the envelope disappeared.

The librarian had removed it for safekeeping, but this was not known at once. A rumor passed among the readers that the money had been stolen. Indignation spread. Men who had never spoken to one another demanded an inquiry. A copy boy employed near the library declared that newspapers ought to be told.

By noon next day a reporter from a Chicago paper arrived, questioned the librarian, copied the notice, and tried to question the old woman. She became frightened and would say only that she did not want to be made a public spectacle.

The young woman was firmer.

“You may write about what the contributors have done,” she said, “but you must not make her longing seem ridiculous.”

The reporter, who had expected an easy little item for an inside column, looked at her, looked at the old woman, and put away the joking phrase he had intended to use.

That afternoon the story appeared under a modest heading:

COLORADANS IN CHICAGO HELP BLIND WOMAN SEE MOUNTAINS

It was copied by a news service. Two mornings later it reached Denver.

In the city room of the Denver newspaper, the managing editor found the clipping on his desk with a note from the telegraph editor: “Isn’t this one of ours?”

He read it twice.

Around him men were writing of elections, strikes, markets, crime, and the possibility of war in a distant country. Telephones rang. A messenger hurried in with a report of a fire. The editor held up the clipping.

“Find out who she is,” he said.

“How?”

“Wire Chicago. Ask the library. Ask everyone. And see what a railroad will do.”

The city editor, a lean man who was supposed to have no sentiment because he corrected sentimental copy with a blue pencil, took the clipping.

“Not much of a story,” said a younger reporter. “Old woman wants to come home.”

The city editor turned.

“Whatever makes human beings less strange to one another is a story,” he said. “Most of the rest is only news.”

By evening the railroad had offered transportation from Chicago to Denver. Readers of the Denver paper added enough for the mountain journey, a room, meals, and such care as the woman might need. A physician who had once lived near her old town volunteered to meet her. Someone located the cemetery. Someone else found a farmhouse where she might stay.

The money collected in the library—far more than the girl’s five dollars now—was placed in trust for clothing and her return, if she should wish to return.

But she did not speak of returning.

When the girl told her, the old woman sat very still.

“You mean I’m goin’?”

“Yes.”

“Clear home?”

“Clear home.”

“And up where I can see?”

“Yes.”

“Before—”

“Before your sight is gone.”

The old woman lifted both hands to her face. Her shoulders quivered, but for some time she made no sound. Then she reached outward blindly until she found the girl and drew her close.

“You done it.”

“No. We did it.”

“You begun.”

“I gave five dollars.”

“You understood.”

That, after all, was the gift for which no account could be kept.

The day of departure came bright and cold. People gathered at the station: the librarian, several contributors, the reporter, the miner who had sacrificed his tobacco, the hotel porter, the young mother with her baby, and strangers who had read the notice but never seen the woman before.

The old woman wore a new gray coat. She carried a small bag and the Denver newspaper folded beneath her arm.

The girl walked beside her to the train.

“Ain’t you comin’ someday?” the old woman asked.

“Yes.”

“When?”

“When I have earned the fare.”

“You’ll earn it.”

“I believe I shall.”

The woman touched her face, tracing it gently as though already committing it to memory.

“I can’t see you plain,” she said. “But I’ll recollect.”

The train began to move. Hands waved. The old woman leaned toward the window, and the girl walked beside it until the platform ended.

For two days the train carried her westward.

Across Illinois, Iowa, Nebraska, and the long plains of eastern Colorado, she watched what she could: towns passing like blurred thoughts, fields opening beneath the winter sky, telegraph poles rising and falling at the edge of sight. The farther she traveled, the quieter she became.

Then someone in the car said, “There they are.”

At first she saw only brightness—a pale interruption along the horizon. She pressed her face close to the glass.

Slowly the brightness gathered shape. A white summit stood clear, then another, and another. The mountains rose out of distance, vast and familiar, not flat like the calendar, not fixed like a picture, but changing under clouds and sun.

The woman did not speak.

Tears ran down her cheeks, and she made no effort to wipe them away.

In Denver she was met by people whose names she had never heard and whose kindness she could not understand except by accepting it. They took her to rest, then drove her toward the place she had described so many times in the library alcove.

They reached it before sunset.

The farmhouse was not the one she had known, and the road had changed. There were new fences, new roofs, telephone wires, and automobiles where wagons had once gone slowly through dust. The cemetery gate had been replaced. Several names upon the stones were already beyond her power to read.

But the mountains were there.

She stood before the graves of her husband and children with one hand upon the iron fence. No one hurried her. No one told her not to grieve. The mountains had witnessed her youth, her labor, her love, her losses; now they received her again without surprise.

Afterward they led her to a chair facing west.

The sun descended through clear, streaming light. Snowfields shone above the darkening earth. For a little while every ridge was distinct, every hollow blue. She looked and looked, as if the soul itself had opened wider than failing eyes.

Slowly the light withdrew from the lower slopes.

Slowly the purple shadows advanced.

One peak darkened, and then another. The highest snow held the radiance longest, seeming, as she had said, unwilling to surrender it. At last the final brightness faded, and night came softly over the range.

The old woman closed her eyes.

Within the darkness she saw the frame house, sheets leaping in the wind, children running along a road, and her husband pausing at the gate to look west. She saw the mountains behind them all—not as rocks without sense, but as the oldest and most comforting friend of memory, changeless enough to hold what mortal hearts could not.

Far away in Chicago, the young woman sat in the library alcove reading the Denver paper. Upon the front page was a small account of the journey and a message dictated by the old woman:

“I seen them. Tell all the homesick ones I seen them before the sun went down.”

The girl read the words twice. Then she looked around the room at the workers, strangers, and solitary people bending beneath the lamps. Chicago no longer seemed wholly impersonal. Somewhere under its noise and haste, unseen as roots beneath frozen ground, fellowship waited for the touch that would awaken it.

And under the Colorado night, with the beloved shapes fixed forever in her memory, the old woman rested in that satisfying calm that men call peace.

The publishers have a rule—whether printed in black ink and nailed above the editorial desk, or merely communicated by the lifting of an eyebrow—that a heroine must not enter a story improperly dressed.

I do not mean improperly in the moral sense. Morality is permitted to arrive late, after the second installment, and sometimes does not arrive at all. I mean that the reader must be told what the heroine has on. You may introduce a young man with nothing more than a name and an appetite. You may say, “Henry came in,” and Henry is accepted, hat, trousers, collar, and all. But if you write, “Marcella entered,” somebody immediately demands to know whether she wore blue voile, white organdie, biscuit-colored broadcloth, or one of those simple little frocks whose simplicity costs eighty-seven dollars.

The illustrator demands it first. How can he draw Marcella if you leave her suspended in the atmosphere like a disembodied virtue? The publisher demands it next, because women buy magazines and women, according to publishers, study the heroine’s sleeves before consenting to feel anxiety about her soul. Finally you demand it yourself. Do not deny this. If Marcella steps onto a moonlit balcony, you want to know whether the moon shines on chiffon or satin.

Therefore Sophy Epstein must be described by her dress.

This is unfortunate, because Sophy had only one.

It was black, or had once been black, and it fitted her with an accuracy not wholly attributable to the dressmaker. There had been no dressmaker. Sophy and a girl named Tillie Rosen had cut it from a paper pattern upon the kitchen table in Mrs. Epstein’s flat, where the gravy stains on the oilcloth gave several of the pieces an adventurous bias. It was tight at the waist, tighter at the hips, short enough to display two narrow ankles, and cut in front to a V of such daring depth that every man in Klein’s Cut-Rate Shoe Emporium became, on beholding it, a student of geometry.

The dress had other qualities. It did not show dirt. It could be worn to work, to a dance hall, to a funeral, or—if Sophy ever found time for one—to church. A ribbon altered its politics. A string of glass beads raised its social standing. A spot of powder on the shoulders prepared it for evening.

It was, in short, a highly efficient dress.

Louie Baer disliked it immediately.

Louie had come from Oskaloosa, Iowa, where dresses knew their place and remained there. They rose to the throat, descended to the shoe tops, and gave no information not demanded by law. In Oskaloosa Louie had sold footwear for Mr. T. E. Bixby, who kept a walnut ruler under the counter, attended the Methodist church, and considered the fitting of a shoe a sacred trust.

Louie believed likewise. A customer might lie about her age, her weight, her husband’s salary, and the natural color of her hair; but when she said she wore a four-B, Louie’s face became grave. Feet were facts. He would kneel, measure, test the arch, and say, “Madam, you require a five-C,” with the calm bravery of a physician announcing appendicitis.

Chicago received him without enthusiasm.

Chicago receives five thousand young men every week who have been assured at home that the city is waiting for them. The city is generally looking the other way.

For nine days Louie called upon shoe houses from State Street to the river, carrying letters which said he was sober, industrious, honest, and acquainted with the McKay stitch. Honesty was not then in brisk demand. Neither was the McKay stitch. On the tenth morning his landlady informed him that the room rent was due; on the tenth afternoon he entered Klein’s Cut-Rate Shoe Emporium and accepted nine dollars a week.

Klein’s occupied a long, narrow room beneath an elevated railway. Every three minutes a train passed overhead and shook dust into the children’s shoes. The front window contained twenty-seven pairs of ladies’ footwear, each resting upon crimson plush and each marked with a price ending in ninety-eight cents. Nothing in the store had ever cost a round sum. Klein believed that humanity would resist two dollars to the death but surrender instantly to one dollar and ninety-eight cents.

The shoes were equally diplomatic. Pasteboard soles masqueraded as leather. Heels concealed private weaknesses. Patent-leather pumps shone with the desperate brilliance of people who know they will not last.

Mr. Klein himself was a broad, pale man with diamonds on two fingers and a permanent dampness about the lips.

“You sell quick,” he told Louie. “No sermons. Lady says five, give four-and-a-half if it don’t hurt too loud. Everything genuine. Understand?”

“No, sir,” said Louie.

Klein stared.

“I mean—yes, sir.”

Thus Louie entered metropolitan commerce.

He learned many things. He learned that a shoe could be “Parisian” without ever having left Milwaukee. He learned that a woman who could scarcely walk in a pump would buy it if told that her foot looked two sizes smaller. He learned that the proper answer to “Is this real calf?” was not necessarily the answer taught in Oskaloosa. He learned to say “special line,” “factory surplus,” and “just your style.”

He did not learn to lie well. His ears reddened before the falsehood emerged, thereby making it useless except to customers of defective eyesight.

Sophy Epstein worked at the ribbon counter near the rear, though “ribbon counter” gives too festive an impression. There were shoestrings, buckles, polish, insoles, hose, and three cards of pink ribbon left from a season when pink ribbon had briefly threatened to become essential to civilization. Sophy sold all these, wrapped parcels, answered the telephone, carried stock, soothed angry customers, and, when Mr. Klein’s bookkeeper failed to appear, added columns of figures. For this she received seven dollars.

She was nineteen, small and thin, with cloudy dark hair, gray eyes, and a mouth that seemed always to have heard the beginning of a joke. Her beauty was not the restful sort. It flashed, contradicted itself, challenged you, and then withdrew. One minute she looked like a child who ought to be given a bowl of soup; the next, like a young woman who had already learned that bowls of soup are seldom given without questions.

The men in the store called her Soph, Baby, Queenie, Toots, and several other names not bestowed at baptism.

“Say, Soph,” cried Gus, the window trimmer, on Louie’s first day, “ain’t you cold in that ballroom wrapper?”

“Cold?” said Sophy. “With you around? You’re a human furnace, Gus—mostly smoke.”

The clerks laughed.

When she bent to lift a carton, Benny Loeb whistled. Sophy straightened slowly.

“You practising to call dogs, Benny?”

“Only the good-lookin’ ones.”

“Then whistle in a mirror.”

She could defend herself. Indeed, she defended herself so continuously that the defense had become part of her manner, like the bangles on her wrist. Yet Louie noticed that after the laughter she sometimes turned away quickly. He noticed, too, how Klein brushed against her when passing in the narrow aisle, though there was room enough for a piano.

One evening Klein called her into the stockroom.

Louie, sorting misses’ button boots outside, heard Klein say, “Come on, Sophy. Don’t be so high-toned.”

Then came the scrape of a box, a sharp slap, and Sophy’s voice:

“You try that again and I’ll feed you them patent leathers—buckles first.”

She came out with one cheek crimson. Seeing Louie, she stopped. Her hand went at once to the low V of her dress—not covering it, precisely, but touching it as if she had suddenly remembered it was there.

Louie looked down at the boots.

Sophy waited for a grin, a wink, or a question. Men’s curiosity, she had found, was an actor who could play every part from friend to magistrate. But Louie only said, “That six is in the five-and-a-half box.”

“What?”

“This boot. It’s marked wrong.”

“Oh.”

She returned to her counter. For the rest of the evening she was unusually silent, which disturbed everybody and caused Gus to ask whether she had swallowed a shoehorn.

Louie’s courtesy troubled her more than the insults. An insult could be struck back. Courtesy left no convenient handle.

The next day he said, “Good morning, Miss Epstein.”

She looked behind her.

“Who you talkin’ to?”

“You.”

“Then cut out the Miss. Makes me feel like I’m forty and own a parrot.”

“All right—Sophy.”

“That’s better, Iowa.”

She had named him Iowa before learning his surname, and the name stuck. She mocked his careful speech, his shiny Sunday tie, and his habit of saying “Excuse me” to customers who stepped on him. But when a woman complained that Sophy had short-changed her, Louie quietly produced the sales slip and proved otherwise. When Benny hid a dead beetle in her cash drawer, Louie removed it before she opened the drawer. When Klein asked her to stay late for “inventory,” Louie announced that he, too, would remain.

Klein discovered that the inventory could wait.

This went on for three weeks. Nothing in the behavior would have interested a motion-picture director. There were no moonlit balconies. There was one alley behind the store, but it smelled of cabbage and contained a cat of violent politics. Nevertheless, Louie began to think about Sophy while brushing his teeth, which is one of the least romantic yet most reliable signs of affection.

He admired her quickness. He pitied her thin wrists. He disliked her slang, her powder, her cheap bangles, the company she kept, and most particularly the dress.

One Saturday the store remained open until eleven. Near ten, trade slackened. Gus and Benny went to the corner for coffee. Klein was arguing by telephone with a wholesaler who wished to be paid. Sophy sat on a stool, rubbing one foot.

Louie approached her.

“Does it hurt?”

“Naw. I’m rubbin’ it for luck.”

“You ought to wear a wider shoe.”

“Listen to Professor Foot.”

“You’re in a three-A. You need a four-B.”

“I need four bucks. The B can stand for breakfast.”

Louie smiled, then became serious. Seriousness came upon him with no warning and sat heavily.

“Sophy, may I say something?”

“You usually do.”

“I wish you wouldn’t wear that dress so—so open.”

Her hand ceased rubbing the foot.

“What’s it to you?”

“I don’t like the way those fellows look at you.”

“They got eyes. Want me to collect ’em in a cup?”

“They don’t show you respect.”

At that word her face changed. A shutter came down behind the gray eyes.

“Respect! Say, Iowa, did you buy a job lot of respect before you left the farm?”

“I’m not trying to insult you.”

“You’re doin’ swell without tryin’.”

“I only mean you could put something in there. A piece of lace. A yoke, maybe.”

“A yoke!” Sophy laughed. It was not her regular laugh. This one had edges. “Hear him! Go get me a yoke, Gus—white lace, handmade by starving duchesses.”

“I didn’t say white.”

“No, black. Black’s refined. Black takes me right into society.”

“Sophy—”

She sprang from the stool.

“You think I like wearin’ this rag every day? You think I don’t know it’s shiny under the arms and split under the belt? What d’you suppose I do nights—sit in a limousine and fan myself?”

“I never said—”

“I get home after twelve Saturdays. There’s Ma coughin’, two kids in one bed, and the sink piled to the ceiling. We got one iron, and you got to heat it on the stove. Coal costs money. Soap costs money. Hot water’s a celebration. If I wear a white waist, it’s dirty Tuesday. Then what? Come Wednesday in a barrel?”

Louie stood very still.

Sophy’s voice dropped, but its bitterness increased.

“This dress don’t show dirt. I brush it. I sponge the spots with benzine. I sew it when it busts. It’s the only thing I got that don’t look like I came out of a rag bag.”

“You might have it cut higher.”

“Sure. I’ll ask the dressmaker I keep in the linen closet.”

“You and your friend made it, didn’t you?”

“Tillie’s workin’ nights now. Anyway, there ain’t cloth.”

“A little lace wouldn’t cost much.”

“A little lace wouldn’t cost much,” she mimicked. “Seven dollars a week don’t cost much either, if you ain’t buyin’ food.”

Louie flushed. He had thought himself poor. Sophy was showing him that poverty has grades, departments, and postgraduate courses.

She touched the beads at her throat—a glittering string of imitation jet.

“You think these are foolish too, don’t you?”

“I didn’t say so.”

“You looked it. They cost thirty-nine cents. I skipped lunch twice. That’s foolish, huh?”

“Yes,” said Louie honestly.

“Well, maybe it is. But when I put ’em on, people see the beads before they see the dress is old. A girl’s got to look like somebody in this town. If she looks hungry, everybody lets her stay hungry.”

Louie had no reply.

Sophy went on, because once pride begins confessing its injuries it must talk fast or stop altogether.

“And if a fellow likes the look of me and takes me to supper, what’s the crime? I ain’t askin’ him to buy the restaurant. A chop’s a chop. You can eat respect when they put gravy on it.”

“I’d take you to supper.”

“That ain’t the point.”

“Why not?”

“Because you’d sit there lookin’ like I was your Sunday-school class.”

The elevated train roared overhead. A tower of shoe boxes trembled. One fell, struck the floor, and burst open, revealing a pair of bronze slippers of extraordinary ugliness.

Louie picked them up.

“When I first came here,” he said, “I had two shirts.”

Sophy folded her arms.

“Congratulations.”

“I washed one every night in the bowl at my room. The bowl was about this big.” He made a circle with his hands. “The landlady wouldn’t give me hot water. I scrubbed the collar with shaving soap and hung the shirt over the bed.”

Sophy’s mouth twitched.

“One morning it was still wet, so I put it on wet. It dried on me while I walked to State Street.”

“That why you’re shaped so funny?”

“Probably.”

She almost smiled.

“I wore the cuffs until the edges frayed. I trimmed them with scissors, but one got crooked. A manager saw it when I applied for work. He kept looking at that cuff while I told him I knew fitting, stock numbers, and children’s corrective shoes. He said they had no opening.”

Louie turned the bronze slipper in his hands.

“I wanted to tell him I was clean. The cuff was clean. It was only worn out. But saying that would have made it worse.”

Sophy’s arms loosened.

“Yeah,” she said. “It always makes it worse.”

For a moment they possessed that rarest of luxuries—complete understanding. Naturally, neither knew what to do with it.

Louie put the slippers back in their box.

“Still,” he said, “I wish you’d put in the lace.”

Sophy stared at him.

Then she snatched the box from his hands and shoved it onto the shelf.

“Still! You pig-headed cornfield! I tell you the whole story of my life, with orchestra accompaniment, and you come back with lace!”

“I don’t mean—”

“Ferget it.”

“Sophy—”

“I said ferget it. Go sell a shoe.”

She marched to her counter. The conversation was concluded with all diplomatic relations severed.

On Monday Sophy wore the black dress unchanged.

On Tuesday she wore it unchanged.

Wednesday, Thursday, Friday, and Saturday found the V still pursuing its reckless journey. If Louie glanced at it, Sophy caught him and smiled sweetly.

“Nice cool weather, ain’t it, Iowa?”

He never mentioned lace again.

Their acquaintance, however, survived. Poverty makes quarrels expensive; persons who work ten feet apart cannot afford the luxury of eternal estrangement. Sophy resumed teasing him. Louie resumed protecting her without admitting that he did so. He brought an apple for lunch one day and discovered, after waiting until four, that he was not hungry and Sophy was. She ate it without thanking him, which was her way of accepting the delicacy of the transaction.

She taught him Chicago.

She taught him never to buy a newspaper from the boy with the bandaged arm, because the bandage moved from arm to arm according to fatigue. She taught him that a restaurant advertising “home cooking” usually had a cook who had left home under painful circumstances. She showed him where coffee was five cents, where neckties were reduced after six, and which elevated guard would hold the door for a girl running upstairs.

He taught her shoes.

He taught her that a high instep could make a cheap pump torture, that children’s feet ought not be forced into sizes left over from last season, and that when a tired woman said, “Anything comfortable,” she was to be believed instead of sold the pair with the largest commission.

“Shoes is your religion, ain’t they?” Sophy asked.

“They’re important.”

“Sure. Souls can go barefoot, but feet got to be saved.”

Even Mr. Klein noticed Louie’s ability. Customers returned and asked for him. This annoyed Klein, who preferred customers to return only after their purchases had become too worn to exchange.

Then, one rainy afternoon, a stout man with a reddish mustache entered the store. His umbrella dripped upon Klein’s mosaic floor—a floor so called because three colors of linoleum had been patched into it.

“Louie Baer!” he exclaimed.

Louie looked up from a pair of button shoes.

“Mr. Hanford!”

The man was Elias Hanford, formerly of Oskaloosa and now proprietor of Hanford’s Family Shoe Store on North Clark Street. His shop had polished oak fittings, honest leather, and a mechanical rocking horse for children. He had come to Klein’s because the rain had driven him beneath the awning, but Providence often works under awnings.

They talked. Mr. Hanford asked questions. Louie answered. Klein hovered, moist and suspicious.

That evening Hanford took Louie to dinner and offered him fifteen dollars a week, with an increase to eighteen if he proved satisfactory.

Fifteen dollars! Respectable stock! Regular closing at six except Saturdays! No pasteboard announced as calf!

Louie accepted.

He was to begin Monday.

On Friday he told Sophy.

She was arranging boxes of shoe polish.

“Hanford’s?” she said. “That swell joint with the wood horse?”

“Yes.”

“Fifteen a week?”

“Yes.”

“Gee. You’ll be wearin’ spats.”

“I don’t think so.”

“Don’t forget us lowlifes when you’re president of the shoe trust.”

“I won’t forget you.”

Sophy dropped a tin. It rolled beneath the counter.

“Who asked you to remember?”

“Nobody.”

“Then don’t get mushy.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You had the symptoms.”

She knelt to retrieve the tin and remained hidden longer than necessary. When she rose, her grin was in place.

“Better watch Hanford. Respectable stores is dangerous. First thing you know, you’ll marry a lady customer with a bunion and a bank account.”

Louie wanted to say something. He did not know what. A declaration would have been premature, presumptuous, and impossible at nine dollars a week. At fifteen it became only premature and presumptuous.

“I may come back,” he said.

“What for? Our shoes?”

“To see everybody.”

“Everybody’ll be thrilled.”

Saturday was his last day.

At nine-thirty Sophy had not appeared. This was unusual. Klein complained, Benny suggested that she had eloped with a millionaire, and Gus offered odds that the millionaire would return her before lunch.

Louie said nothing. He sold shoes. He sold a pair of gray pumps to a woman who wanted brown, and when she returned them ten minutes later, he apologized so earnestly that she bought the brown pair as well. Such is the commercial value of misery when properly directed.

At eleven the door opened and Sophy entered.

She was breathless, her cheeks pink from the wind. She wore the black dress.

But across its deep V, from shoulder to shoulder and throat to breast, there now extended a yoke of black lace.

It was not the lace of duchesses. No starving noblewoman had blinded herself over it in a Belgian tower. It consisted, if truth must invade the scene, of three separate remnants skillfully persuaded to look acquainted. One piece bore roses, another spiderwebs, and the third a design suggesting damaged cabbages. The seams had been joined with tiny, painful stitches. At the throat a narrow strip of black ribbon concealed a place where the supply had failed.

It was cheap. It was uneven. It was beautiful.

Sophy marched behind her counter as if nothing had occurred.

The store became silent.

Gus recovered first.

“Holy smoke! Soph’s put a fence around the property.”

Benny leaned across a display case.

“What’s the matter, kid? Reform wave hit town?”

Sophy removed her hat.

“Reform nothin’. It’s cold.”

“Cold? It’s sixty-two.”

“My neck’s delicate.”

Klein emerged from the stockroom and surveyed the alteration with disfavor. The dress had been, in his opinion, an asset to trade.

“Vat’s this funeral curtain?”

Sophy smiled brilliantly.

“This? Latest thing. All the swell dames is wearin’ ’em. La Valliere neck effect. You wouldn’t know, Mr. Klein. It’s French.”

Klein distrusted France but feared appearing ignorant of it.

“Looks cheap,” he muttered.

“Cost enough.”

“How much?” Benny asked.

“None of your business.”

“Bet she made it.”

“Bet I didn’t.”

“You did too. Look at them stitches.”

“They come from Paris that way, rube.”

The clerks laughed. Sophy laughed louder. Only Louie, at the fitting stool, did not laugh.

He saw the lace. He saw also the needle puncture on her left forefinger, the faint blue shadows under her eyes, and a loose thread near the ribbon where, in haste, she had failed to knot it. He imagined the kitchen after midnight: the coughing mother, the sleeping children, the smoking lamp, the scraps spread upon stained oilcloth. He imagined Sophy bending over them, swearing when the thread broke, perhaps throwing the whole thing down, perhaps picking it up again.

The reader may imagine these things too. That is one privilege the reader possesses over the characters. Louie could not ask. Sophy would have denied every stitch.

At noon he found her alone beside the stock shelves.

“It looks nice,” he said.

“What does?”

“The lace.”

“Oh, that.” She examined a polish tin. “I told ’em. My neck gets cold.”

“Yes.”

“And it’s stylish.”

“Yes.”

“All the girls’ll be wearin’ ’em.”

“I expect so.”

She glanced at him suspiciously.

“If you think I done it on account of what you said—”

“I didn’t say that.”

“Because I didn’t.”

“All right.”

“I’d been plannin’ it for months.”

“Of course.”

“Tillie give me the scraps. I hadda use ’em or throw ’em out.”

“That was sensible.”

She set down the tin with violence.

“Don’t stand there agreein’ with me!”

“I’m sorry.”

“And quit lookin’ at me like that.”

“How am I looking?”

“Like—like a shoe clerk in a stained-glass window.”

Louie smiled.

Sophy’s defiance faltered. For one second she looked nineteen—not streetwise, not daring, not equipped with jokes and beads and a dress that did the work of three dresses, but simply nineteen and uncertain.

“Honest?” she asked.

“Honest what?”

“It looks all right?”

“It looks very nice.”

“Not tacky?”

“No.”

“Gus says the middle piece don’t match.”

“Gus wears purple socks.”

“That’s true.”

She touched the ribbon at her throat. Her fingers trembled once and became still.

“You’ll be in tomorrow to get your pay?”

“Yes.”

“I won’t be here. Ma’s takin’ bad. I’m stayin’ home.”

“I’m sorry.”

“She ain’t dyin’. She just likes attention.”

“I’ll come by sometime.”

“To the flat?”

“If you don’t mind.”

Sophy’s gray eyes met his.

“Bring fruit,” she said. “Not flowers. Flowers is no good to eat.”

“I’ll bring fruit.”

“And don’t wear spats.”

“I won’t.”

“And don’t think this lace means anything.”

“I don’t.”

“It don’t.”

“I understand.”

“You don’t understand nothin’, Iowa.”

“Maybe not.”

She turned away, but not before he saw her smile.

At six o’clock Louie left Klein’s Cut-Rate Shoe Emporium. Mr. Klein shook his hand and said he could return at the old wage if Hanford discharged him. Gus gave him a cigar of criminal manufacture. Benny borrowed a quarter.

Sophy remained behind her counter, although her mother, it appeared, had postponed taking bad until Sunday.

Louie paused at the door.

“Good night, Sophy.”

“Good night, Mr. Baer.”

It was the first time she had called him that.

The elevated train thundered overhead. Dust descended. The window shoes trembled upon their crimson plush, displaying their false soles, their weak heels, and their little white cards proclaiming impossible bargains. Outside, Chicago shoved and shouted beneath the lamps. Everybody was selling something—shoes, ribbons, labor, beauty, honesty, or the appearance of having eaten lunch.

Louie stepped into the crowd.

Behind him Sophy adjusted the black lace yoke. Then, discovering Benny watching, she scowled.

“Whatcha starin’ at?”

“Nothin’.”

“Well, keep on starin’ at it.”

And she lifted her chin, now modestly, stubbornly, splendidly covered to the throat.

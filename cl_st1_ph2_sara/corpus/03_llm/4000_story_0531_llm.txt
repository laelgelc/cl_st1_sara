## Prince Lilimond’s Day of Rule

> Beggars in tatters, beggars in tags,  
> Beggars who flourish in velvet and rags;  
> Some cry for supper, and some cry for gold—  
> Look twice, little Prince, ere thy treasure be told.

Very fair and sweet was little Prince Lilimond, only son of King Florimund, who ruled over the pleasant kingdom of Valedore. His hair was yellow as ripe corn in the sunlight, his eyes were blue as the flowers that grow beside a brook, and his manners were so courteous that the oldest Duke in the realm declared he had never known the Prince to forget either a title or a kindness.

But that which was best in Lilimond was his tender heart. He could not see a lame dog without desiring to carry it home, nor hear that a woodcutter’s cottage had burned without sending his own blankets to the family. If a page cut his finger, Lilimond fetched the salve; if an old soldier stood bareheaded in the snow, the Prince would remove his own fur cap and thrust it upon the veteran.

Now a tender heart is a noble possession; yet, like a fine horse, it must be guided by judgment, lest it carry its owner into a ditch.

Valedore was a prosperous kingdom. Its wheat fields rolled golden from hill to hill; its orchards were rosy with apples; its rivers turned many mills, and merchants came from distant countries to buy the silks, wines, clocks, and carved chests made by the King’s subjects. The roads were well kept, robbers were scarce, and even the poorest village had a fountain and a bell.

King Florimund was not unkind. He loved justice, good dinners, hawking, tourneys, bright banners, and long evenings when minstrels sang before the fire. He left much of the business of government to his councilors, who were grave men in robes of crimson and black. Because the kingdom flourished, the King believed that all was as it should be.

One autumn morning Prince Lilimond rode out with his tutor, Master Borland. Now this Borland was a learned and gentle man, who could speak seven languages, name every star in the northern sky, and write a poem upon any subject from roses to rhinoceroses. Yet he knew little of cheats, idlers, or crafty persons, for he supposed that every man’s heart was as honest as his own.

As they passed through the eastern gate, an old beggar stretched forth a trembling hand.

“Alms, noble Prince!” he cried. “Have pity upon one who hath not tasted bread since yesterday. The royal taxes have taken my last penny.”

Lilimond gave him the silver clasp from his cloak.

A little farther on, a woman in a torn gray shawl sat beside the road with two children.

“Succor us, gracious lord,” she wailed. “We starve beneath the burden of the King’s taxes.”

The Prince emptied his purse into her lap.

Before noon he had heard the same complaint from a blind fiddler, a man with a bandaged foot, three barefoot boys, two bent old women, and a fellow who carried a sleeping infant and wept so loudly that the infant awoke and wept also.

At last Lilimond turned a troubled face toward his tutor.

“Master Borland,” said he, “can it be true that my father’s people starve?”

“Methinks it must be true if so many declare it,” replied Borland sadly. “For although one tongue may lie, surely twenty tongues cannot all lie together.”

This sounded reasonable to the Prince, though it was not so reasonable as it sounded.

When Lilimond returned to the palace, he went straight to the Great Hall. There sat King Florimund beneath a canopy of cloth-of-gold, listening while the Master of Revels described a tournament that was to be held upon the Prince’s twelfth birthday.

“Father,” cried Lilimond, forgetting ceremony in his distress, “the people are starving!”

The King raised his eyebrows.

“Which people?”

“The people of Valedore, sire. I have seen beggars at the gate and upon the roads. They say that your taxes have taken their bread.”

“Nonsense!” said the King.

“They were dressed in rags.”

“Rags may be put on as easily as velvet.”

“One man was blind.”

“He may have been blind,” said the King, “but blindness doth not prove that my taxes caused it.”

“There was a poor woman with hungry children.”

“Were they hungry?”

“They said so.”

“Nonsense!” repeated the King, though not angrily. “Valedore’s taxes are lighter than those of any neighboring realm, and no man unable to labor is refused bread at the parish house. My councilors have examined these matters.”

“Your councilors sit upon cushioned chairs,” said Lilimond, with more courage than courtesy. “They do not hear the cries outside the gate.”

At this the Lord Treasurer frowned, the Chancellor coughed behind his hand, and the First Chamberlain looked at the painted ceiling. But the King loved his son and forgave him.

“My dear child,” said he, “when thou art older, thou wilt learn that a kingdom cannot be governed by every cry heard beside a highway. Some complain because they suffer; others complain because complaint is easier than work.”

“But if there be even one who suffers through us, should we not help him?”

“Of a surety. Yet help given foolishly may feed dishonesty and leave honesty hungry.”

Lilimond shook his head.

“It seemeth to me, sire, that gold is plentiful in the palace, while misery is plentiful beyond it.”

“Nonsense!” cried the King for the third time. “Come, let us speak of thy birthday. Thou shalt be twelve years old next Thursday, and I have promised thee whatsoever gift thou desirest, provided it be within my power and contrary neither to honor nor law. Wouldst thou have a white charger? A jeweled sword? A ship with purple sails?”

Then a bold idea entered Lilimond’s mind.

“My father,” said he, kneeling before the throne, “I ask neither horse, sword, nor ship. Grant me instead the government of Valedore for one day, with power to command whatsoever even the King might command.”

A whisper ran through the court.

The Lord Treasurer dropped his ivory counting rod. The Chancellor said, “Your Majesty!” in a voice of alarm, and the Queen’s ladies fluttered like startled pigeons.

King Florimund looked long at his son.

“That is a grave gift for a child.”

“You gave your royal promise, sire.”

“So I did,” said the King; for kings, above all men, must keep their promises. “Thou shalt rule from sunrise until sunset upon thy birthday. Yet remember, Lilimond: a crown is heavier than it appears when lying upon velvet.”

“I shall remember,” replied the Prince, though in truth he thought chiefly of the good he would accomplish.

All that week he and Master Borland made their plans. They counted villages upon maps, consulted tables of population, and calculated the number of poor persons likely to require aid. Borland, who trusted every written figure and every spoken lament, concluded that one large coffer of gold would suffice.

Then did the Prince command heralds to ride throughout Valedore and make this proclamation:

“Hear ye! Hear ye! Upon the twelfth birthday of His Highness Prince Lilimond, every beggar who presents himself at the Royal Palace before noon shall receive one piece of gold, enough to buy bread for many weeks. Let none who suffer hunger fail to come!”

The heralds blew silver trumpets in market squares, beside village wells, before monastery gates, and at lonely crossroads. The news flew faster than the heralds. Peddlers carried it over the hills; boatmen shouted it along the rivers; shepherds repeated it from one valley to the next.

And wherever the news went, men began to reckon.

“A gold piece for asking!” said some. “Why should we not ask?”

Thursday dawned clear and cold. Before the sun had wholly risen, bells rang from every tower of the palace. The King entered the throne room wearing his crown and carrying the scepter of Valedore. Courtiers, ministers, captains, and judges stood in two glittering rows.

Prince Lilimond came forward in white satin and a mantle of blue velvet. He was pale with solemnity, but his eyes shone.

King Florimund removed the crown from his own head and placed it upon the boy’s golden hair. It slipped down over one eyebrow, and a page had to fit a small cushion beneath it; nevertheless, no one smiled.

“From sunrise until sunset,” proclaimed the King, “Lilimond is ruler of Valedore. His word shall be obeyed as mine.”

“Long live King Lilimond!” cried the court.

The boy seated himself upon the throne, and King Florimund took a plain chair at his right hand.

“Open the palace gates,” commanded Lilimond. “Bring forth the gold, and let every beggar receive his piece.”

The First Chamberlain bowed, but he did not move.

“Your Majesty,” said he, “I beg leave to report that there is some difficulty outside.”

“What difficulty can there be?”

“The multitude is greater than was expected.”

“How many have come?”

“It cannot easily be counted, sire.”

“Then look from the western balcony,” said King Florimund quietly.

Lilimond went to the balcony, followed by Borland, the Chamberlain, and half the court.

Never had he beheld such a sight.

The great square was filled from wall to wall. People crowded the steps, the fountains, and the roofs of nearby houses. Beyond the square, the road appeared black with newcomers. They came singly and by twos and threes; they came in families, companies, and entire wagonloads. There were men on crutches, women carrying babies, boys with torn caps, old soldiers, barefoot girls, blind fiddlers, traveling tinkers, fortune-tellers, laborers, and persons of no occupation that any man could name.

Tramp, tramp, tramp came the feet upon the road. Babies cried, donkeys brayed, and dogs barked until the bronze lions over the gate seemed ready to bark in answer.

Lilimond’s joy gave way to astonishment.

“Are all these people starving?”

“So they declare,” said Borland, though less confidently than before.

The Lord Treasurer approached, looking whiter than his parchment collar.

“Your Majesty, the first coffer hath been brought forth, and six others stand ready. Yet if each person in that square receives one gold piece, seven coffers will not suffice, nor seventy. We should empty the treasury before the rear of the procession entered the city.”

“Then empty it,” said Lilimond, gripping the scepter. “I have promised.”

The Treasurer looked as though a sword had pierced him.

“Sire, without gold we cannot pay the guards who defend the borders, repair the bridges, purchase grain in a season of famine, or maintain the hospitals and parish houses. To relieve one day’s petitioners, Your Majesty would imperil the whole kingdom.”

Lilimond’s face grew red.

“Shall I break my word?”

The First Chamberlain bent low. He was an elderly man named Lord Ardwell, whose silver buckles were always polished and whose mind was even more orderly than his garments.

“May it please Your Majesty,” said he, “your proclamation promised gold to every beggar. It did not promise gold to every person who chose to dress as one. Examine the claimants, sire, and give to those whose need is true.”

“Would that not be cruel delay?”

“It is not cruelty to separate want from greed. Indeed, it is mercy; for whatsoever gold is taken by a deceiver cannot be given to an honest sufferer.”

The words were much like those King Florimund had spoken, but Lilimond was now upon the throne, and advice sounds different when one must answer for the result.

“So be it,” he said. “Bring the claimants into the Hall of Judgment, one at a time.”

The first was a huge fellow in a torn brown cloak. His cheeks were ruddy, his shoulders broad, and his hands smooth and strong.

“Why dost thou beg?” asked Lilimond.

“Because I am too weak to labor, my King,” groaned the man, leaning heavily upon a stick.

Lord Ardwell dropped a silver coin beside him.

The man stooped and snatched it up before it ceased ringing.

“Thou art quick for one so weak,” observed the Prince.

“My weakness comes and goes, sire.”

“What labor hast thou sought?”

“None, Your Majesty. Labor would worsen my condition.”

A murmur of laughter passed through the court.

“Take him to the palace garden,” said Lilimond, “and let him weed one bed. If he cannot do it, he shall receive gold and food. If he can, he shall be offered honest work.”

At once the fellow threw away his stick and fled so swiftly that two guards could scarcely open the door in time.

The second claimant was a woman wrapped in patched shawls. She bent almost double and spoke in a cracked whisper.

“Pity a poor widow, gracious King. I possess nothing in this world.”

As she bowed, a pearl necklace slipped from beneath her shawl.

“Nothing save pearls,” said Lilimond.

The woman clutched the necklace.

“They are but glass, sire.”

The royal jeweler examined them.

“Orient pearls of excellent quality, Your Majesty. Worth three hundred pieces of gold.”

Then the woman straightened; and all perceived that she was neither old nor bent, but the prosperous wife of a merchant.

“I meant no harm,” she cried. “I thought that since Your Majesty intended to give gold away, one piece more or less could matter little.”

“One drop matters little,” answered Lilimond, “yet enough drops will drain a fountain. Go, and be ashamed.”

The merchant’s wife departed with her shawl drawn over her face.

Next came a man with both eyes bandaged.

“Alas!” he moaned. “I have been blind from birth.”

“Then how didst thou find thy way from thy village?” asked the Prince.

“My faithful dog guided me.”

At that moment a palace hound wandered near. The man leaped upon a bench and shouted, “Keep that beast from me!”

“How knewest thou it was a beast?” asked Lilimond.

The man tore off his bandages and ran.

After him appeared six boys who claimed to be orphans. Upon questioning, it was discovered that they all had living fathers and mothers and had been promised sweet cakes by an alehouse keeper if they returned with gold. Then came a stout farmer wearing rags over his good woolen clothes; a tailor who had sewn patches upon a new coat that very morning; and a young nobleman disguised with soot, who wished to win a wager from his companions.

The Prince ordered the soot washed from the young nobleman’s face, whereupon three Lords recognized their nephew. Their indignation was great, though not so great as his embarrassment.

Hour followed hour. The crowd outside still roared, shuffled, whistled, quarreled, ate sausages, and complained of delay.

Some claimants were poor indeed, but not helpless. To these Lilimond offered work upon royal roads, in orchards, kitchens, stables, and workshops. Many accepted gladly. Others became angry.

“I came for gold, not work,” said one.

“Then thou camest for treasure rather than succor,” replied the Prince. “There shall be no gold for thee.”

Shortly before noon, Lord Ardwell whispered, “Your Majesty, the boldest cheats came first, believing that no questions would be asked. Yet thousands remain. We cannot examine them all before sunset.”

Lilimond thought awhile. Then he commanded the heralds to go into the square and proclaim:

“Let every person hear! Whosoever hath falsely claimed hunger, infirmity, widowhood, orphanhood, or other misfortune in order to gain royal gold shall be punished for deception. But whosoever is truly in need shall receive food, aid, and honest consideration.”

The trumpets sounded. The proclamation was cried once, twice, and three times.

For a moment the multitude stood still.

Then a curious change came over the square.

A lame man tucked his crutch beneath his arm and walked away. Two blind men recovered their sight and hurried after him. A woman with four borrowed children returned them to their astonished parents. Bandages vanished, bent backs straightened, and ragged cloaks were cast aside to reveal fur collars, good leather belts, silver buckles, and even velvet gowns.

Some slipped down alleys. Some climbed walls. Some departed with dignity, pretending they had merely come to watch. Others ran outright.

The tramp, tramp of arriving feet became the patter and clatter of retreat. Donkeys turned, carts creaked away, and dogs raced after their masters. Within half an hour, the square that had seemed packed beyond all counting was almost empty.

Only a small company remained near the fountain.

Lilimond descended from the throne and went among them himself.

There he found a mason whose arm had been crushed by a falling stone, and who could not work until it healed. There was a widow with three pale children; parish records proved that her husband had died of fever a fortnight before. There was an old soldier who had lost a leg defending the northern pass. There were two sisters whose cottage and spinning wheels had burned, a deaf woman abandoned by cruel relatives, and several aged laborers who had no family and no strength remaining.

The Prince questioned them gently, and Lord Ardwell verified each account. Not one wore hidden pearls; not one refused suitable work; not one asked for more than was needed.

To each Lilimond gave a gold piece. He also ordered food for the hungry, medicine for the sick, tools for those able to resume a trade, and places in the royal almshouses for the infirm.

When all had been served, the Lord Treasurer counted what had been spent.

“Your Majesty,” said he, smiling for the first time that day, “less than one quarter of the first coffer is gone.”

Lilimond looked at the six unopened coffers and then at the vast square where thousands had lately clamored.

He was silent a long while.

At last he returned to the throne room. The afternoon sun slanted through the high windows and made bars of gold upon the floor. King Florimund still sat in the plain chair, saying nothing. He had neither mocked the boy’s errors nor interfered with his commands, for he knew that certain lessons cannot be given like sweetmeats; they must be earned.

Lilimond removed the crown. His hair was flattened beneath it, and he looked weary beyond his twelve years.

“My father,” he said, “I believed that every rag covered misery, every complaint proved injustice, and every outstretched hand deserved gold. I thought your councilors hard-hearted because they asked questions before giving aid.”

“And what thinkest thou now?” asked the King.

“I think that questions may protect the needy from those who would steal their portion. Yet I would not become so suspicious that I refuse pity to all because many deceived me.”

King Florimund smiled.

“Thou hast learned in one day what some rulers fail to learn in fifty years.”

“I have learned also,” continued Lilimond, “that taxes maintain roads, guards, hospitals, bridges, and stores of grain. Gold in a treasury is not idle if it stands ready against famine or war. Yet, sire, perhaps we should send honest officers through the kingdom to seek those who are too weak or ashamed to beg.”

“That is wisely spoken,” said the King. “We shall do so.”

The Prince held out the crown.

“Sunset hath not yet come, but I return this willingly. It is too heavy for me today.”

King Florimund did not take it at once.

“Art thou certain? There remains nearly an hour of thy reign.”

“Of a surety, sire. I have commanded enough for one birthday. I would rather be your son until I am fit to be your successor.”

Then the King rose, placed the crown upon his own head, and embraced him before the assembled court.

“Let it be recorded,” he proclaimed, “that Lilimond ruled less than one day, spent less than one coffer, relieved every true petitioner who came before him, frightened a thousand rogues, discovered four pearls, six false orphans, eleven sham cripples, and one exceedingly foolish nobleman.”

At this the court laughed, and even Lilimond laughed with them.

That evening there was a feast in the Great Hall. The tables shone with silver, and the minstrels sang of the Day of the Vanishing Beggars. Yet before Lilimond tasted his birthday cake, he sent baskets of meat, bread, apples, and warm pudding to the poor families who had remained in the square.

For compassion had not left his heart; it had merely opened its eyes.

Years passed, as years do in kingdoms and cottages alike. Lilimond grew tall and grave, though he never lost his courtesy. He studied laws as diligently as he had once studied stars. He visited farms without announcing his coming, inspected parish houses, spoke with craftsmen, soldiers, widows, merchants, and laborers, and listened not only to what men said but also to what their lives revealed.

Master Borland continued to believe nearly every sad tale he heard, so Lilimond made him Royal Comforter, in which office his kindness did much good. Lord Ardwell remained Chamberlain and taught the Prince how accounts were kept, stores measured, claims tested, and relief distributed without waste or insult.

When King Florimund died at a venerable age, Lilimond became King in truth. The crown no longer slipped over his eyebrow, but he remembered always how heavy it had felt upon his twelfth birthday.

He ruled generously, though never carelessly. No starving person was turned from his gate; neither was idleness rewarded as though it were misfortune. Those unable to work received succor. Those able and willing were given work. Those who sought alms by fraud learned that mercy is not blindness, while officials who treated the unfortunate harshly learned that judgment is not cruelty.

Thus Valedore prospered through many peaceful years.

And if you ask whether it is better to give than to receive, the answer is surely yes. Yet the wise giver looks well at what he gives, to whom he gives it, and whether his gift will heal a sorrow or nourish a fault. Gold cast blindly into a crowd may be caught by the greediest hands; but kindness guided by judgment reaches those who need it most.

So remember the old rhyme:

> Beggars in tatters, beggars in tags,  
> Beggars who flourish in velvet and rags;  
> Give with a warm heart, but look ere you do—  
> For mercy is noblest when wisdom looks too.

### THE HUMAN BLOODHOUND

On the night of September 17, 1891, the west-bound express of the Denver and Rio Grande Railway halted for water at Texas Creek, an isolated station lying between the brown ridges of the Arkansas Valley and the eastern wall of the Sangre de Cristo Range. There was no town at Texas Creek in the proper meaning of the word. A section-house, a water tank, a telegraph office, and several weather-beaten cabins comprised the settlement; beyond these stood the mountains, silent under the stars, and the long rails glimmered east and west like twin knife-blades.

The train had scarcely lost its motion when four masked men came from behind the tank.

Two boarded the locomotive, where they covered the engineer and fireman with six-shooters. A third guarded the passenger coaches, walking beside them and warning the occupants to remain within. The fourth, a broad-shouldered man who moved with an uneven but remarkably rapid stride, forced the express messenger to open his car. Beneath his long coat he carried dynamite, fuse, and caps in a canvas sack.

The messenger surrendered the contents of the way safe, but declared that he did not know the combination of the through safe, which contained a shipment of gold moving east from the mines. This refusal did not disturb the masked man. He ordered the messenger and baggage-master from the car, laid a charge against the safe door, and touched the fuse with a cigar.

The explosion shook the little valley. Window glass fell from the section-house; frightened horses broke their halters; and a cloud of smoke, splintered timber, express papers, and fragments of iron burst through the open door of the car. Before the passengers understood what had happened, the robbers had gathered the gold into rawhide sacks, mounted horses concealed in a dry wash, and disappeared toward the southwest.

The entire hold-up occupied less than twenty minutes.

News of the outrage went over the wire to Pueblo, Cañon City, Denver, and every railway point at which officers might be found. At Cañon City it reached Robert Banks, deputy United States marshal, before midnight. Banks asked four questions: the direction taken, the number of men, the kind of horses, and the probable weight of the plunder. Having received such answers as could be given, he ordered saddle stock, summoned six men whom he knew to be reliable, and sent for three bloodhounds kept by a ranchman below Florence.

There are officers who pursue criminals as a duty, and there are officers in whom pursuit is an instinct. Banks belonged to the latter order. He was not a large man, nor did he possess that loud authority which passes for courage in settlements where courage is seldom tested; but he had ridden from the Platte to the Pecos, knew cattle brands from altered running-irons, could distinguish a shod horse from a barefoot pony after three days of wind, and had the patient, level temper without which endurance is merely stubbornness. Men said that he never hurried and never stopped.

Before daylight he reached Texas Creek.

The trail was found in the wash where the robbers had mounted. Four horses had gone out; two were sharp-shod, one carried a broken shoe upon the near hind foot, and the fourth made an irregular impression, as though burdened more heavily on one side. The bloodhounds took the scent from a glove dropped near the track, strained upon their leashes, and ran southward with deep, eager notes.

Banks examined the ground before following.

“They know this country,” he said.

The route selected proved the truth of his judgment. Instead of taking the open valley toward Wet Mountain Valley, where telegraph messages might bring ranchmen against them, the fugitives entered the rough foothills, crossed stony ridges on which tracks could scarcely be seen, and descended into narrow gulches where running water destroyed scent. They avoided wagon roads, abandoned the ordinary passes, and traveled by game trails known chiefly to hunters, prospectors, and men who had reason to keep away from settlements.

During the first day the posse gained nothing. The robbers had eight hours’ start, fresh animals, and the urgency of the gallows behind them. Banks’s men, on the other hand, were compelled to examine every division of the trail, for four mounted men crossing a cattle range may easily be confused with cowboys riding to a roundup. The dogs could be trusted where the scent remained; but cold rock, water, and the dust of wandering stock repeatedly baffled them.

A bloodhound is not a magician. He follows what exists, and where wind, water, heat, or cunning has erased the record, his sorrow is as honest as his failure. Yet you can believe the dog when you doubt your own eyes, and this fact has sent many a criminal to the penitentiary.

Toward evening the oldest hound, a scarred red dog called Major, recovered the line at the mouth of a cedar canyon. The robbers had dismounted there and led their horses over bare slabs for half a mile. At the end of the rock they crossed a patch of damp earth, and Major bent to it with a cry that brought the younger dogs behind him.

The posse rode until midnight.

They camped without fire in a grove of dwarf oak, each man sleeping with his bridle rein looped about his wrist. At three o’clock Banks was awake. The stars still burned above the black comb of the range, but a pale bar in the east announced morning, and within half an hour the horses were saddled.

All that day the trail climbed.

The eastern Rockies rise from the plains with a grandeur not apparent upon maps. A line that occupies the width of a finger may represent sixty miles of broken mesa, volcanic dike, timbered canyon, and granite wall. Trails turn back upon themselves; streams run first east, then south, then disappear underground; and a man may look across a gorge at a point which requires a day’s riding to reach. To the inexperienced such country is confusion. To the plainsman it is a book, though one in which many pages have been torn away.

Banks read it steadily.

At noon he found where one of the robbers’ horses had stumbled. A splash of blood marked a stone, and hair clung to a thorn. Two miles farther on there were signs that the animal had been relieved of its load. The gold had been divided among the other mounts, while the injured horse continued under a lighter saddle.

“This will delay them,” one of the posse remarked.

“It will make them steal another horse,” Banks answered.

Before sunset they came to a small rancho occupied by a Mexican herder, his wife, and several children. The corral gate hung open, and the best two horses were gone. The herder, greatly excited, explained in broken English and rapid Spanish that four Americanos had appeared shortly after sunrise. One limped. Another wore a black beard. They had paid for food with a gold coin, taken two horses without permission, and left their weary animals in exchange.

“Which way?” Banks asked.

The herder pointed toward the southwest.

“Por la sierra. Siempre por la sierra. The lame one, señor—he say they go to Santa Fe. But they no go Santa Fe. I think this.”

Banks thought so also. Criminals frequently tell a lie which contains enough truth to be remembered. Santa Fe lay south, but it was a railway and telegraph center, and no man carrying stolen bullion would willingly enter it. The statement was meant to travel after them.

The abandoned horses supplied another clue. One bore a Colorado brand altered with a running-iron; the other carried saddle marks showing long use by a rider whose weight rested heavily in the right stirrup. Banks measured the latter impression and compared it with the uneven trail found at Texas Creek. The lame man had ridden that saddle. His lameness was not fatigue but an old deformity.

On the third day a deputy from Pueblo overtook the posse with information gathered from railway men and former offenders. Among the names submitted was that of Alfred “Peg-Leg” Eldridge, gambler, stock thief, sometime prospector, and suspected participant in two previous train robberies. Eldridge had lost part of his left foot in a gun accident years before, though he concealed the injury so well that it appeared only after long exertion. He knew southern Colorado and northern New Mexico from his days as a horse trader. More important still, he had relatives by marriage near the western border of the Chickasaw Nation.

Banks folded the written report and placed it in his pocket.

“Peg-Leg,” he said. “That is our man.”

Eldridge was in many respects a child of circumstances, though circumstances cannot carry a six-shooter or light a dynamite fuse. He had come to manhood during the disorder following the Civil War, when horses changed owners without bills of sale, courts sat irregularly, and the boundary between enterprise and theft was sometimes fixed by the number of armed friends a man possessed. He learned to ride before he learned to read; he could sleep in snow, cross a desert by starlight, and find water where another man saw only sage and sand. When the frontier hardened into law, Eldridge did not harden with it. Customs which had once been tolerated became crimes, and the free rider found himself an outlaw.

In his calling he was an artist. He understood that pursuit is defeated not by speed alone, but by compelling the pursuer to waste time. A false trail of five miles is better than a lead of five miles, because the first exhausts both horse and judgment. Thus he doubled through sheep ranges, drove loose cattle over his tracks, crossed streams repeatedly, and chose camps where no smoke could betray him. He left no papers and permitted no careless fires. The robbers ate jerked beef while riding and slept by turns.

But Banks also understood time.

For two days the fugitives moved west along the shoulders of the Sangre de Cristos, then turned abruptly south. The posse followed across a high plateau where frost silvered the grass at dawn and the midday sun burned through their coats. On the fourth night sleet came over the range. The trail vanished beneath it, and one of the younger hounds, after working until his paws bled, lay down and refused food.

The men expected Banks to halt. Instead he led them to the nearest sheltered valley, where soil might preserve the hoofprints beneath overhanging timber. At daybreak Major found the scent. The old hound moved slowly now, but his certainty remained. His long ears dragged through wet leaves; his flanks trembled; and from time to time he lifted his head as though listening to a voice beyond the hearing of men.

That afternoon a posse horse went lame. Another had a saddle sore deep enough to show white flesh. Banks divided the loads, left one man at an isolated ranch with the disabled stock, and purchased three ponies at prices which the government might afterward object to but which necessity approved. Man-hunting is exciting sport when described beside a stove; upon the trail it consists principally of hunger, lost sleep, sore animals, wet blankets, and the unending examination of dust.

By the sixth day they crossed into New Mexico Territory.

Here the country opened into wide valleys bordered by dark mountain chains. Spanish settlements lay along the streams, their adobe walls appearing at a distance like natural outcrops of the earth. The robbers passed around these habitations, keeping to the mesas. Once they approached within three miles of a station on the narrow-gauge line, but waited in a lava field until a freight train had gone through and darkness covered their crossing.

Banks reached the rails the next morning. He found a faint cut upon one tie where a new horseshoe had struck, and beyond the right-of-way Major gave tongue. The marshal sent two men to the station for food and information while he continued with the others.

The station agent remembered four horsemen watching from the north the previous evening. A section hand had seen them afterward, traveling east rather than south.

This report appeared to contradict the trail, which ran south from the railway. Banks examined the section hand separately and learned that the men he had observed rode unburdened horses and one wore a white hat. None of the robbers wore white, and their animals carried gold, blankets, food, and ammunition.

“Cowboys,” Banks said. “Our men went south.”

The pursuit continued.

On the seventh day the first bloodhound failed. His paws were torn, his eyes inflamed by dust, and his breathing came in painful gasps. Banks lifted him across a saddle and carried him to a sheep camp. Some of the men smiled at the tenderness, for they had seen the marshal remain unmoved before wounded criminals; but Banks washed the animal’s feet himself and paid the shepherd to keep him.

“A dog does not know why he is asked to die,” he said. “That is a difference between dogs and officers.”

Major and the remaining hound went on.

The trail now entered a country of ancient lava, where black ridges twisted across the plain like rivers arrested in the act of flowing. Hoofprints disappeared upon the stone. The heat rising from those desolate beds gave warnings of water that was not there, while ravens circled in the cloudless sky. Eldridge kept to the lava for nearly twelve miles, and at its southern edge divided his party. Two riders went east; two went west.

The hounds chose west.

Banks dismounted and studied both routes. The eastbound horses were heavily shod and had passed through sand in a straight line, making no effort at concealment. Those going west stepped irregularly and had been led for part of the distance. There were marks of dragging brush.

He called Major back and tried the eastern trail. The hound showed interest for a quarter mile, then turned aside. The westward scent was stronger because two of the fugitives had carried blankets belonging to all four. Eldridge had anticipated the dogs and divided the scented equipment as well as the men.

The marshal faced his first grave uncertainty. The gold might have gone east while Eldridge drew the pursuit west; or Eldridge himself might be east, leaving two lesser men as sacrifice. Banks knew that choice, once made, would cost a day if wrong.

He chose east.

Several miles onward, the trail descended into sand, and there Banks found a gray thread caught upon greasewood. The thread matched the torn lining of a saddle coat left by the limping rider at the Mexican rancho. Farther on, a peculiar print appeared—the left boot shorter and broader at the toe than the right.

Now it became a hunt of man by man.

Banks sent two members of the posse after those who had gone west, instructing them not to attempt an arrest unless reinforced. With the remaining officers and Major he followed Eldridge eastward. The route curved south before night, confirming his belief that the Chickasaw Nation, not Santa Fe or Arizona, was the ultimate refuge.

Forcing a tired horse an extra hour has left many an amateur rider afoot. Eldridge knew this and stopped long enough to graze his animals in hidden parks; Banks knew it and did the same. Each man husbanded horseflesh while driving himself without mercy. The gap between them remained from ten to twenty hours. Banks found camps where ashes were warm enough to stain the hand, yet never saw smoke ahead. A frightened deer, loose stone, or distant glimmer sometimes promised contact, but the promise failed.

On the ninth day the second hound collapsed after drinking alkali water. Banks remained beside him for an hour, though every minute was precious, and watched the animal die beneath a cottonwood. Major stood nearby with lowered head.

The old dog alone remained, and age was overtaking courage. His pads had worn raw, his ribs showed, and his voice, once deep enough to fill a canyon, had become a hoarse cough. Still he pulled at the leash when given the scent.

That night the fugitives raided a horse herd near an abandoned mining camp. They selected three of the strongest mounts, turned loose their exhausted stock, and left a twenty-dollar gold piece nailed to the corral post. Payment did not alter the theft, but it revealed Eldridge’s temper. He did not regard himself as a common robber. Men of that breed maintain distinctions which the law does not recognize.

Banks secured replacement animals from the same herd after leaving a signed voucher. The ranch owner, encountered six miles away, reported that one of the thieves had addressed the lame man as “Al.” The identification was complete.

During the next three days the chase passed through a region in which the earth seemed to have been built for concealment. Red mesas rose above winding valleys; canyons split into a hundred tributary gorges; sandstone towers stood in serried columns, their shadows forming Gothic arches through which a rider might pass unseen at noon. Far below, a river shone like a strip of metal hammered into darkness. Wind moved among the cliffs with the solemn utterance of an organ, and clouds gathered upon distant summits like councils of gray-robed judges.

To ordinary eyes this wilderness offered no road. Eldridge found one.

He descended by an old Indian path, crossed the river upon a shelf where the water reached the saddle skirts, and ascended through a break scarcely wider than a pack horse. Banks followed. One deputy’s mount fell on the ascent and rolled twenty feet, breaking its own neck and injuring the rider’s shoulder. The man protested that he could continue, but the marshal left him with food and directed another officer to conduct him to a settlement.

The posse that had begun with six men was reduced to three.

Their clothing hung in strips, their faces were black with sun and dust, and their eyes had the vacant brightness of exhaustion. They had traveled hundreds of miles, often upon two hours’ sleep, and had eaten whatever could be carried or purchased at lonely ranchos. Yet Banks rose each morning before the others and examined the animals.

The fugitives suffered also. Their stolen horses had begun to fail, and the weight of the gold, though divided, was unrelenting. Gold is convenient wealth in a bank and cruel freight in the mountains. It cuts pack ropes, bruises animals, and cannot be eaten. Twice Eldridge buried part of the shipment, marking the places by signs which he expected to remember. Banks discovered one cache where fresh earth had been disguised beneath a dead fire, but the cavity was empty. The robbers had reconsidered and carried the treasure on.

On the thirteenth morning Major could not rise.

Banks knelt beside him in a grove of piñon. The veteran dog attempted to pull forward, dragging his useless hindquarters through the needles. His nose remained upon the trail.

“No more, old fellow,” Banks said.

He made a bed of blankets in the shade and gave the dog the last of their fresh water. A ranch lay eight miles north. One officer was sent there for assistance, while Banks waited, his hand resting upon the hound’s scarred head.

By afternoon Major was removed on a travois. He survived, but his service was ended.

The last visible instrument of pursuit had failed. The robbers possessed a full day’s lead, fresh horses, and a choice of a thousand trails. Banks’s companions believed the chase finished. In the practical sense, it was.

The marshal did not pretend otherwise.

He rode to the nearest telegraph point, arriving after dark on the fourteenth day, and sent messages to sheriffs, railway detectives, Indian police, and deputy marshals across New Mexico, Texas, and the Indian Territory. He described Eldridge, the suspected companions, the stolen horses, and the probable destination. He then compared every fact accumulated during the pursuit: the southern course, the avoidance of large settlements, the attempts to suggest Santa Fe, the eastern turn from the lava beds, and Eldridge’s family connections.

The robbers were not wandering. They were going home.

Near the Washita River, not far from the western edge of the Chickasaw Nation, lived a former associate of Eldridge named Harlan Voss, who maintained a small horse ranch and enjoyed a reputation for asking no questions. Every autumn a race meeting was held within twenty miles of Voss’s place. Gamblers, cattlemen, Choctaws, Chickasaws, white tenants, horse traders, and fugitives under assumed names attended it. A man might enter such a crowd unnoticed, exchange horses, hear news from three territories, and arrange for the disposal of gold.

Banks ended the direct pursuit.

Some declared that Peg-Leg had beaten him. The marshal returned two borrowed horses, settled accounts for supplies, and allowed the surviving members of the posse to go home. He spent a day sleeping, another day writing reports, and on the third boarded a train east. Telegrams went ahead of him under names having no connection with the marshal’s service.

Eldridge, meanwhile, had done precisely what Banks expected. Believing the trail lost in New Mexico, he and one accomplice crossed the Texas Panhandle by night, rested at remote cattle camps, and reached Voss’s neighborhood before the race meeting. The other two robbers separated from them somewhere south of Las Vegas. One was afterward reported in Sonora and the other in Idaho, but neither was captured, and their true names never became certain. They went out of the record as men sometimes went out of the frontier—suddenly, completely, and forever.

Two weeks after the mountain pursuit ended, the race grounds beside the Washita presented a lively spectacle. Wagons stood in double rows around the course. Blankets, saddles, bridles, ammunition, patent medicines, and whiskey were offered from makeshift booths. Bookmakers shouted odds; cowboys raced through the crowd; women in bright dresses watched from spring wagons; and every hitching rail carried saddle stock from three states and two territories.

Shortly before the principal race, two strangers arrived in a mud-stained buggy. One appeared to be a stock buyer, the other a gambler of no great prosperity. They separated without greeting and moved among the spectators.

One of these strangers was a United States marshal.

Banks had cut his beard, changed his hat, and exchanged his accustomed dark coat for a cheap linen duster. With him was Deputy Marshal Sam Rusk of the Indian Territory, a quiet officer who knew Voss and could identify several of Eldridge’s associates. They had spent the previous night in a hay shed, after approaching the race grounds on foot.

Eldridge stood near the judges’ platform.

He wore a gray suit, polished boots, and a pearl-buttoned waistcoat. No mask concealed his face. He seemed cheerful, and his slight lameness was visible only when he turned quickly. Beside him lounged a narrow-faced man believed to have guarded the passenger coaches at Texas Creek.

Banks did not approach at once.

An arrest in such a crowd might produce a general battle. Many spectators carried revolvers; some were Eldridge’s friends; others would join a fight from habit without knowing its cause. The marshal therefore waited until the race had begun.

The horses broke together under a roar from the crowd. Men climbed wagon wheels and rails. Hats flew upward. Every eye followed the runners as they came around the first turn in a cloud of yellow dust.

Banks stepped behind Eldridge and closed one hand upon his right wrist. At the same instant he pressed a revolver beneath the outlaw’s coat.

“United States marshal,” he said. “Do not move.”

Rusk seized the accomplice.

For one second Eldridge’s body stiffened. His left hand started toward his waist, then stopped. He looked at the racing horses, at the shouting spectators, and finally over his shoulder into Banks’s face. Recognition came slowly, followed by an expression almost of amusement.

“So you are Banks,” he said.

“I am.”

“I reckoned you were still in the mountains.”

“That was useful.”

The irons went on without a struggle. Before the crowd understood what had occurred, the prisoners were inside the buggy, and two mounted deputies, concealed among the horse traders, had closed behind them. The favorite won by half a length. Men disputed the finish for an hour, and many never learned that Peg-Leg Eldridge had been taken within thirty yards of the judges’ stand.

The stolen gold was only partly recovered. Some had been buried, some divided among the missing men, and some expended in the long flight. Nevertheless, the express messenger, engineer, Mexican herder, ranchmen, station employees, and members of the posse supplied a chain of testimony which the defense could not break. Tracks, altered brands, stolen horses, gold coins, and the peculiar left boot all took their places before the jury. The mountains had preserved more evidence than Eldridge supposed.

He was convicted of train robbery and sentenced to a term longer than his reasonable expectation of life. When the judgment was pronounced, he listened without visible concern. Men who gamble professionally must learn to accept the turn of a card, though few accept it without bitterness.

Several weeks later, while being conducted to the penitentiary, Eldridge removed one of the pearl buttons from his waistcoat. He gave it to the guard with instructions that it be sent to Marshal Banks. Wrapped around the button was a scrap of paper upon which he had written:

“To the human bloodhound: Keep this for remembrance. If I had known at the Washita that you were on my trail, there would have been no court business between us. One or both would have gone over the road in a box.”

Banks read the message, placed the pearl button in his desk, and made no reply.

Peg-Leg had been correct in one respect. The bloodhounds were left behind in the mountains. One died, one was crippled, and old Major never again followed a trail. Yet the pursuit did not end when their voices became silent. The man who had studied beside them possessed their patience without their dependence upon scent; he could lose the hoofprint and retain the purpose, abandon the trail and still reach its destination.

The wilderness had hidden the fugitives, distance had protected them, and cunning had delayed the law. None had defeated it.

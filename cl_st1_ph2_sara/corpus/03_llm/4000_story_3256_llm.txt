It was during one of those winter evenings when conversation, having exhausted politics, the theatre, and the scandals of the law courts, turns almost inevitably toward subjects which no one believes and everyone desires to hear, that Doctor Tribourdeaux related the following history.

We were assembled before the fire in Madame de R——’s little salon. Outside, snow was falling upon the boulevard; within, the lamps burned softly behind rose-coloured shades, and the comfortable precision of the room seemed to deny the possibility of all disorder, whether natural or supernatural.

Tribourdeaux, then a physician of considerable reputation, had been discussing fear as a pathological phenomenon.

“Fear,” he said, “does not necessarily arise from danger. It is nourished more often by uncertainty. A visible enemy provokes courage; an unknown correspondence between two facts, neither of which is alarming in itself, may destroy the balance of the most disciplined mind.”

“That is an excellent preface to a ghost story,” said our hostess’s niece.

“To a clinical observation, perhaps,” replied the doctor.

“You shall call it whatever you please, provided somebody disappears.”

Tribourdeaux smiled, though with less amusement than civility.

“Somebody did disappear.”

The young woman drew her chair nearer.

“You see!” she exclaimed. “Doctor, we are listening.”

He remained for a moment with his eyes fixed upon the fire. Though nearly sixty, Tribourdeaux still possessed the upright figure and composed gestures of the army surgeon he had once been. His broad forehead, severe mouth, and habit of qualifying every assertion gave him the air of a magistrate examining even his own memories under oath.

“At the time of which I speak,” he began, “I was twenty-eight years old, and believed myself incapable of superstition.”

“Which means,” observed the magistrate Valmont, “that you were admirably prepared for it.”

“Possibly. It was in 1863. I had been attached to a military hospital near Orléans, and, finding the quarters assigned to me intolerably noisy, I rented rooms in an old building beyond the more frequented streets, not far from the Loire.

“The house had formerly belonged to a family of provincial importance. Alterations made by successive owners had divided it into several apartments, but no ingenuity could conceal its original grandeur or subsequent decay. The stone staircase was broad enough for a palace, while the walls beside it were stained by damp. Carved doors opened upon empty chambers. The courtyard was overgrown with weeds, and the iron gate, when moved, uttered a prolonged complaint which could be heard throughout the building.

“My own apartment occupied half of the second floor. It consisted of a study, a bedroom, and a neglected salon which I used chiefly for storing books and surgical cases. The remaining half, separated from mine by a locked corridor and a masonry partition, had long stood vacant.

“There was, however, one communication between the two residences which the architect had failed to abolish. A balcony extended across the entire façade. A wooden trellis, covered in summer by a dead and leafless vine, divided my portion from that belonging to the neighboring apartment. It was high enough to discourage an ordinary visitor, but not high enough to stop a determined man.

“Please note the arrangement carefully. My bedroom opened upon this balcony by means of a long window. The corresponding room next door was separated from mine by no more than twelve or fifteen feet. From my bed, if its curtains were drawn, I could see the neighboring window, though not the interior beyond it unless a lamp was burning.

“The concierge, an old woman named Berthe, lived on the ground floor. Besides her and myself, the house had no regular inhabitants. Berthe assured me that the adjacent rooms had been locked for nearly a year. The proprietor resided in Paris, and no tenant, she said, would endure the loneliness or the rats.

“I found both tolerable. For two months nothing occurred which the most exacting rationalist might not have approved.”

Here Tribourdeaux paused.

“And then the light appeared?” asked Madame de R——’s niece.

“Yes. Then the light appeared.

“I returned late one evening from the hospital and, while undressing, observed a pale radiance behind the lace curtains of the neighboring bedroom. It was not the yellow, vacillating light of a candle. It was steadier and whiter, though this distinction may be an embellishment imposed by memory.

“My first supposition was that the apartment had been rented. The fact was ordinary; only Berthe’s failure to mention it surprised me. I went to bed, heard light movements through the wall—or imagined that I heard them—and slept.

“At breakfast I asked Berthe about our new neighbor.

“She crossed herself.

“‘There is no new neighbor, Monsieur le Docteur.’

“‘Then somebody entered the rooms last night.’

“‘Impossible. I have the keys.’

“She produced them from beneath her apron. They were heavy, rusted things, and she declared that they had not left their hook.

“‘You dreamed the light,’ she said.

“I disliked the explanation, not because it was impossible, but because old women should not diagnose physicians. That evening I made a point of watching. At eleven o’clock the neighboring window remained black. At twenty minutes after eleven the room became illuminated.

“I stepped onto the balcony. The curtains were closed, and I saw only the shadow of what appeared to be a human figure passing once across them. I called out. The light immediately vanished.

“On the following morning Berthe and I inspected the apartment. Dust lay evenly upon the corridor floor. The entrance door was locked; the windows were secured; the fireplaces were cold. There was furniture, but it stood under grey covers. In the room adjoining mine we found neither lamp nor candle, neither ashes nor footprint.

“I concluded that the light had come from some external reflection. This hypothesis was physically unsatisfactory, for the house opposite was unoccupied and the street lamp could not have produced the observed angle. Nevertheless, an insufficient natural explanation is generally preferable to a sufficient impossible one.

“The light returned the next night.”

“And the cat?” asked the young woman.

Tribourdeaux looked at her sharply.

“Your impatience anticipates the order of my deposition. The cat appeared that same night.

“I had extinguished my lamp and was reading beside the fire when I heard a faint scratching at the balcony window. Supposing a branch had touched the glass, I ignored it. The sound was repeated—three deliberate strokes, a pause, and three more. I opened the window.

“An Angora cat entered.

“It was the most extraordinary animal I had ever seen. Its fur was not merely tawny, but gold: the yellow of a silkworm’s cocoon under sunlight. Along its back the hair was long and fine, and each movement sent through it a ripple of metallic lustre. The head was small, the limbs slender, the paws tufted with soft fur. Its eyes were green, of a peculiarly luminous green, and seemed in the darkness to preserve, if not to generate, a faint phosphorescence.

“It displayed no fear. After looking at me with an attention almost indecorously human, it sprang upon my bed, turned twice on the coverlet, and lay down near my pillow.

“I confess that I was charmed. I had always liked animals, and this one possessed, beneath its strangeness, a singular sweetness. When I touched its head, a vibration passed through its body and into my fingers like a weak electrical current. It purred, but the sound was deeper than the ordinary purring of a cat and appeared to proceed from its breast.

“I allowed it to remain.

“In the morning it was gone. The window was closed. I searched the apartment, but there was no trace of it.

“This circumstance was not, in itself, mysterious. A cat can pass through openings which escape human observation. I found no such opening, but did not presume that because I had failed to discover one it did not exist.

“The next night the light returned at twenty minutes after eleven. At midnight the cat scratched at my window. This time I examined the balcony before admitting it. The stones were wet from rain; no tracks were visible on them. Beyond the trellis was darkness.

“The animal entered, slept in precisely the same place, and disappeared before morning.

“This continued for eight nights.

“The recurrence began to disturb me. Events which, occurring once, are insignificant may, by exact repetition, acquire the authority of a message. Each evening the white light appeared behind the lace curtains. Each night the golden animal came to my window. Each morning it had vanished, although I sometimes slept with my hand resting lightly across its body.

“On the ninth night I resolved not to sleep. At about four in the morning the cat rose. Its eyes opened and fixed themselves upon mine. I was fully awake; of that I am certain. It slipped from beneath my hand, crossed the coverlet, and leaped toward the floor.

“For one second it passed through a bar of moonlight. Then it was gone.

“I rose instantly. The doors were locked, the chimney guarded by an iron screen, the window latched from within. I examined the room with a candle until daylight. I found nothing.

“Hallucination was an obvious hypothesis. I had been working hard, sleeping badly, and treating several cases of fever. Tactile hallucinations are uncommon but well attested. Yet the hairs which clung to my black coat were real. I examined one under a lens. It was unquestionably the hair of an animal.

“I then determined to investigate the light.”

Tribourdeaux leaned back. His listeners had become silent.

“On the following evening,” he continued, “I dressed in dark clothes and waited upon the balcony. Shortly after eleven the neighboring room grew bright. By placing a chair against the trellis, I succeeded in climbing over it, though not without tearing my sleeve and sacrificing some of the dignity appropriate to my profession.

“The curtains were imperfectly joined. Through the opening I saw a woman.

“She was seated before a mirror, combing her hair.

“Even after so many years I find it difficult to describe her without seeming to arrange my description for the purpose of what followed. I must therefore ask you to remember that I am stating impressions as they occurred, not inventing correspondences retrospectively.

“She was perhaps twenty-two years old. Her skin had an ivory pallor. Her hair, which descended below her waist, was of a warm golden colour, not blonde in the ordinary sense, but yellow, lustrous, almost metallic. As she drew the comb through it, the whole mass rippled like the fur of the Angora beneath my hand.

“She turned her head.

“Her eyes were green.

“That is a common enough colour. Yet there was in hers the same curious luminosity I had observed in the animal’s eyes. They seemed phosphorescent, not because they actually emitted light, but because they gathered and retained it with unnatural intensity.

“She rose and moved toward the window with a smooth, sinuous step. I attempted to withdraw, but my foot displaced a fragment of plaster. She pulled aside the curtain.

“We looked at one another through the glass.

“She showed no alarm. On the contrary, an expression of recognition passed over her face.

“‘It is you,’ she said.

“I heard the words distinctly.

“She opened the window.

“My position was ridiculous. I stood upon a forbidden balcony after midnight, spying upon an unknown woman. I bowed and began an apology.

“‘Mademoiselle, I beg you to believe that circumstances which I cannot explain—’

“‘It is you,’ she repeated.

“‘Have we met?’

“She smiled.

“‘Perhaps.’

“Her voice was low, with a slight foreign accent which I could not identify. Up close, her resemblance to the golden cat became less definite but more troubling. It lay not only in the colour of her hair and eyes, but in the inclination of her head, the quiet vigilance of her expression, and a peculiar suppleness by which each movement seemed to continue after her body had become still.

“She invited me to enter.”

“Very imprudent,” murmured Valmont.

“On her part or mine?”

“On both.”

“Undoubtedly. The room, which that morning I had seen buried beneath dust sheets, was now furnished with elegance. A lamp stood on a low table; the covers had disappeared; a fire burned in the grate. There were flowers, books in several languages, and a faint perfume resembling heliotrope. Either the transformation had occurred within a few hours or I had entered a different apartment from that inspected with Berthe.

“The young woman called herself Linda. She gave no family name. When I asked how long she had occupied the rooms, she replied, ‘Not long.’ When I asked whether she lived alone, she said, ‘Usually.’ To every inquiry concerning her country, her relatives, or her acquaintance with me, she returned an evasion or that same disturbing phrase:

“‘It is you.’

“I left after an hour, knowing less than when I entered and desiring greatly to know more.

“The cat did not visit me that night.

“Ah!” exclaimed our hostess’s niece.

“Yes,” said Tribourdeaux. “I remarked the fact.

“On the next evening Linda admitted me by the balcony window. She showed no surprise at my return. We spoke of music, medicine, and certain obscure philosophies with which she possessed an acquaintance unusual in a woman so young. She knew German and Italian, quoted Paracelsus, and laughed when I mentioned transmigration.

“‘You believe only in what you can dissect?’ she asked.

“‘I believe in what has a causa sufficiens.’

“‘And love? Have you dissected that?’

“‘Frequently,’ I replied. ‘Its anatomy is depressing.’

“She laughed again and stretched her hands toward the fire. They were small, narrow hands. I remember thinking that her fingers, bent in the light, resembled claws sheathed in velvet.

“You will say that my imagination had already become infected. That is true. The resemblance which had first occurred to me as an absurdity began to govern my observation. I sought it; therefore I found it.

“After I left Linda, the cat came to my bed.

“The following night I remained with Linda until dawn. The cat did not appear. On the night after that, Linda was absent, though her lamp burned; the animal entered my room before midnight. During six weeks this alternation continued. I never saw them together.

“At first I treated the matter as a diverting coincidence. Then I devised small experiments. I tied a narrow green ribbon around the cat’s neck. In the morning the ribbon and animal were gone. That evening Linda received me wearing a high collar of black lace. She refused to remove it.

“‘Your throat is injured?’ I asked.

“‘No.’

“‘Then why conceal it?’

“She placed one finger upon her lips.

“On another occasion the cat scratched my wrist while playing. The wound was trivial. The next night Linda saw the bandage and became agitated.

“‘Who did that?’

“‘You did.’

“She stared at me, then turned away.

“‘Do not say such things.’

“‘Why not, if they are true?’

“She faced me with dilated pupils.

“‘Because you may come to believe them.’

“Her words should have cured me. Instead, they became evidence.

“I questioned Berthe. She swore that no tenant had been admitted, no rent paid, and no food carried upstairs. Yet Linda’s rooms contained fruit, wine, candles, fresh flowers. Once, while Linda slept upon a sofa, I searched discreetly for letters or papers. There were none. Her clothing bore no maker’s mark. In a small cabinet I found only a comb, a silver hand mirror, and several strands of golden hair.

“I took one of those hairs and compared it with the specimen removed from my coat.

“Under magnification they were, of course, distinguishable. Human hair and feline hair differ in structure. I knew this, and the microscope confirmed it. I experienced profound relief.

“Then, that same night, I awoke to find the cat sitting upon my chest, its green eyes within a few inches of my own, and all my relief vanished.

“You must understand the psychological process. I did not believe that Linda changed into a cat. I regarded such a transformation as biologically and metaphysically impossible. Matter cannot reorganize itself in that fashion without violating every observed law. Yet beside this rational conviction there developed another belief, silent, incorporeal, and inaccessible to argument. My intellect denied; my nerves affirmed.

“I loved Linda. I feared the cat. Soon I feared Linda and loved the cat. The two sentiments crossed until I could not determine their object.

“My sleep deteriorated. At the hospital I committed errors in simple calculations. I heard scratching where there was none. The sight of yellow silk caused palpitation. I began to dread sunset, while spending each day in expectation of it.

“Linda perceived the change.

“‘You watch me as if you were waiting for something,’ she said.

“‘I am waiting for certainty.’

“‘Certainty is cruel.’

“‘Uncertainty is worse.’

“She came close to me. Her hair brushed my cheek with the faint electrical sensation I had felt when stroking the Angora.

“‘If you loved me,’ she whispered, ‘you would not ask what I am.’

“‘If you loved me, you would tell me.’

“She smiled sadly.

“‘Perhaps I have told you.’

“That night, after I returned to my room, the cat did not come. Nor did Linda appear on the following evening. For three days both were absent. I became convinced that she had gone, and my distress was such that I would have welcomed either woman or animal without question.

“On the fourth night I heard the familiar scratching.

“I opened the window. The Angora entered, wet with rain. Around its neck was the green ribbon I had tied there weeks before.

“That circumstance destroyed what remained of my self-command.

“‘You had only to seize the cat,’ said Valmont, ‘and carry it into Linda’s room.’

“I attempted it. The animal became frantic whenever I approached the dividing trellis. It twisted from my hands with a violence disproportionate to its size, and once bit me to the bone. When Linda was present, her doors were locked from the inside; when the cat was present, she could not be summoned. I engaged Berthe to watch the corridor while I guarded the balcony. We discovered nothing.

“There remained one experiment.”

Tribourdeaux’s voice had altered. Its measured quality persisted, but beneath it we heard a strain like that of metal under pressure.

“I obtained hydrocyanic acid from the hospital laboratory.

“No one interrupted him now.

“My intention, as I formulated it, was scientific. If the cat died and Linda remained alive, the association would be broken. If Linda disappeared at the instant of the animal’s death—well, I refused to state the alternative even to myself.

“I prepared a small portion of meat and introduced into it a quantity of the acid sufficient to kill the animal almost immediately. Hydrocyanic acid interferes with cellular respiration; in concentrated form its action is rapid, producing collapse, convulsion, and death. I give you these details because I considered them then with the cold precision of a physician. That precision was cowardice. As a matter of fact, I was preparing to kill a creature which trusted me.

“The cat came shortly after midnight.

“It was unusually affectionate. It rubbed its body against my legs, sprang onto my desk, and touched my hand with one velvety paw. For a moment I abandoned my purpose. Then it turned its green eyes toward me, and in their fixed brightness I imagined knowledge, mockery, perhaps Linda herself concealed and watching.

“I placed the poisoned meat before it.

“The animal sniffed, looked up at me, and ate.

“For several seconds nothing occurred. It licked one paw. Then the body stiffened. The forelegs spread; the back arched violently; the claws tore at the carpet. A tetanic convulsion passed from the hindquarters to the head. The mouth opened.

“The cry it uttered was not the cry of a cat.

“It was a woman’s cry—low at first, then rising into an articulate sound of horror and reproach.

“I heard my name.

“Tribourdeaux!”

The doctor had spoken the name in a voice so unlike his own that Madame de R—— started and covered her mouth.

“I seized the animal,” he continued. “Its fur seemed to burn my hands. The green eyes remained open, but their luminosity faded while I watched. The limbs relaxed. The heart ceased.

“She was dead.”

“You mean the cat,” whispered someone.

“I mean that the cat was dead.

“I waited for reason to return. It did not. Instead there arose in me an absolute conviction that I had murdered Linda.

“I wrapped the body in a cloth, carried it downstairs, and left the house. Rain had ceased, but a mist lay along the river. I walked without meeting anyone until I reached the Loire. There, after weighting the cloth with a stone, I threw the bundle into the current.

“It disappeared without a splash that I could hear.

“When I returned, the neighboring window was illuminated.

“I climbed the trellis. The room was empty. A lamp burned on the table. The fire had fallen low. Linda’s cloak lay over a chair; her jewels were before the mirror; a half-read book rested open upon the sofa. In the bedroom the bed had not been disturbed.

“I called her name. I searched every room.

“Linda had disappeared.

“She did not return the next day, nor the next. Her belongings remained untouched. No carriage had come to the house. Berthe had seen no one leave. The proprietor knew nothing of a young woman and denied having authorized occupation of the apartment.

“There was then a further complication. A boatman had seen me wandering near the river after midnight with a bundle beneath my arm. Berthe, questioned by the police, spoke of the mysterious female resident whom she herself had never seen but about whom I had repeatedly interrogated her. The rooms contained a woman’s clothing, yet no woman could be found. There were stains on my carpet—caused by the cat’s final convulsions—which a provincial chemist initially declared to be blood.

“For several days I was in danger of facing a criminal trial.

“The examining magistrate, Monsieur Armand, was a cultivated and compassionate man. When we were alone, he asked me directly whether Linda had existed. I told him what I have told you, omitting nothing, not even the poisoning.

“He listened without ridicule. Then he said:

“‘Doctor, there are three possibilities. You killed a cat, and a woman independently departed. You killed a woman in some manner which your disordered mind has transformed into an allegory. Or you killed something for which the law has no definition. I can prosecute only the second, and I have no body, no witness, no identity, and no proof that this Linda ever lived.’

“The stains were subsequently determined not to be human blood. Inquiries yielded no missing person corresponding to Linda’s description. Monsieur Armand ended the investigation.

“I left Orléans within a month.

“I have never since owned a cat.”

The fire had sunk while Tribourdeaux spoke. Beyond the windows the snow had covered the boulevard, and the room, formerly so secure in its warm order, seemed surrounded by an immeasurable silence.

Valmont cleared his throat.

“There is one obvious explanation,” he said. “The young woman heard of your obsession and amused herself by encouraging it. The cat belonged to her. After its death she discovered what you had done, became frightened, and fled.”

“Without money, clothing, papers, or assistance?” asked Madame de R——’s niece.

“She may have retrieved them later.”

“The rooms were watched,” said Tribourdeaux.

“Then you imagined some of the details. Insomnia, emotional excitement, professional fatigue—a case of obsessional delirium. Your own account admits as much.”

“It admits the possibility.”

The young woman leaned forward. Her earlier gaiety had vanished.

“Doctor, did the cat truly speak your name?”

Tribourdeaux looked toward the dark window.

“I heard it.”

“That is not the same answer.”

“No.”

“And Linda? Was she a woman?”

“I touched her hand. I heard her voice. I loved her. Whether these establish what you call existence, I cannot say.”

Valmont smiled, though uneasily.

“My dear Tribourdeaux, you have constructed the tale too perfectly. The gold hair, the green eyes, the ribbon, the impossible disappearance—these are not evidence. They are literary devices. I suspect that your clinical observation is merely fiction.”

The doctor rose. His face, when he turned from the fire, was very pale. He bowed to Valmont with stiff and ceremonious courtesy, but he did not smile.

“You may take it as you will.”

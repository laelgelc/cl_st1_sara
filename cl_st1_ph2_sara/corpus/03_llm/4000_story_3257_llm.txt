### Kirdjali

In the spring of 1821, when Prince Alexander Ipsilanti crossed the Pruth and called the Christians of the Principalities to arms, many brave men joined him, and many others who merely wished to pass for brave men. There were Greeks in embroidered jackets, Moldavians in sheepskin caps, Serbian fugitives, Bulgarian horse-thieves, Greek students from Odessa who had never heard a shot fired, and Arnouts who had heard too many. Among these last companions of liberty appeared Kirdjali.

Perhaps you do not know his name. In Bessarabia it was once sufficient to silence a crying child.

Kirdjali was a Bulgarian by birth and a brigand by profession. The Turks had burned his village; whether this happened before or after he began robbing them, I do not know. He had wandered through the Balkan passes with a band of twelve men, all admirably mounted and furnished with long guns, yataghans and pistols of Albanian workmanship. They fell upon tax collectors, caravans and country houses. If a Turkish bey refused to pay ransom, they hanged his steward at the gate. If a Christian merchant pleaded his religion, they reminded him that gold had none.

One story concerning him was often repeated at Jassy. A boyar had barricaded himself in a stone house with forty servants. Kirdjali, having only seven companions, piled straw against the doors and sent word that he would smoke them like foxes. The boyar surrendered his money, horses, silver cups and two carriage clocks. Kirdjali returned one clock because it did not go.

Such a man could hardly remain indifferent when an army marched beneath banners bearing crosses and promised to overturn an empire. He entered Ipsilanti’s service with twenty horsemen. His political opinions were not examined. Indeed, at that period opinions stood in no need whatever of examination; a cartridge box and a mustache were accepted as adequate professions of faith.

The Sacred Band wore black uniforms and spoke much of Leonidas. Kirdjali’s men wore whatever they had taken from other people. One had a hussar’s dolman with only three buttons; another displayed yellow Turkish slippers and a French officer’s sword; Kirdjali himself wore a red skullcap, a short green jacket and a white cloak stained by rain, dust and, according to those who knew him, several less innocent substances. He was broad in the shoulders, not tall, black-eyed and calm. His calmness was remarked because everybody about him was shouting.

He listened while an orator explained the regeneration of Greece, the rights of nations and the imminent intervention of the Emperor of Russia. Then he asked where the Turkish treasury was kept.

This question caused some embarrassment.

The rising began with proclamations, church bells and banquets. It ended, as such enterprises frequently end, with bad roads, empty magazines and contradictory orders. The peasants hid their grain. The boyars hid themselves. Commanders disputed precedence while their soldiers sold cartridges for wine. A major, whose name I shall omit because his grandchildren have since become respectable people, threatened the enemy every morning with his finger and retreated every evening with his battalion.

Near the Pruth the insurgents met a Turkish detachment much smaller than rumour made it and much larger than prudence desired. I have heard three accounts of that action, and none agrees with the others. According to one, the Greeks performed prodigies of valour; according to another, they ran away before the first discharge; according to the third, which was given me by a merchant who watched from a hayloft, both statements are true.

The morning was close and grey. Carts blocked the road. Cavalry crowded infantry into a marsh, and two cannon, drawn by starving horses, were placed where they could fire only upon a vineyard. The Turks advanced in tolerable order. The insurgent guns discharged once; one burst its axle, and the other was abandoned because nobody had brought the proper balls.

Kirdjali and his companions remained on the left, behind a low earthen wall. They allowed the Turkish horsemen to approach, fired together and then rushed upon them with drawn yataghans. For half an hour they maintained themselves there. I was told that one of them, named Kantagoni, received a lance in the chest. He seized the shaft with both hands and dragged himself farther upon it until he could reach the lancer with his knife. Both fell dead.

This action, admirable as it was, did not repair the cannon, drain the marsh or inspire the major. The army dissolved. Men threw away muskets, crosses, cockades and even boots. The road to the river became covered with overturned karoutsas, baggage chests and sacred banners.

A karoutsa is a Moldavian vehicle constructed on principles known only to Moldavian coachmakers and Providence. It consists of a shallow wooden box placed upon four wheels, without springs and ordinarily without any visible reason for remaining together. Six small horses may be attached to it, while a driver in a woollen cap lashes all six and addresses each by the name of a Christian saint. In one of these machines three colonels, a priest and the military treasury reached the Pruth before sunset. The soldiers arrived as they could.

Kirdjali had been wounded by a pistol ball below the shoulder. Two companions tied him upon a horse and crossed the river at night. By morning they were on Russian soil.

There the remnants of Ipsilanti’s adventure were disarmed and scattered among the Bessarabian towns. Some became tutors, some gamblers, some police informers. The more dignified lived upon recollections of battles in which they had not been present. Kirdjali settled near Kishinev with a young Bulgarian woman who was called his wife and a child of two years. Whether they had been married by a priest I cannot say. He planted cabbages, traded in horses and, for nearly a year, killed nobody.

I knew an official who saw him often during this period. The official was a thin man with a purple nose and pewter spectacles. His uniform coat was too large, and its brass buttons swung when he walked.

“He bowed to me like a gentleman,” he said. “A very civil fellow. Naturally I kept my hand upon my sword.”

The Turkish authorities did not share this favourable opinion. Lists of Kirdjali’s crimes arrived from Jassy: robberies, abductions, burnt estates, murdered guards and an affair involving a pasha’s favourite nephew. Requests for his surrender followed. At first the Russian administration replied that he was a political refugee. The Turks then replied that he was a common murderer. Papers passed over the Pruth, received signatures, seals and marginal observations. In the end, treaty stipulations prevailed over hospitality.

Kirdjali was arrested at dawn.

He offered no resistance. He allowed the police to search his house and looked on while they overturned the cradle, opened flour sacks and examined the stove. They found one pistol without a flint, a silver-mounted dagger and thirty-seven Turkish beshliks.* This was considered suspicious, though in Bessarabia a man without Turkish money would have been more remarkable.

At the police office he spoke with composure.

“I crossed the Pruth defeated and wounded,” he said. “The Russian Emperor received those who followed Ipsilanti. Since coming under his protection I have not robbed a traveller, stolen a sheep or struck a man. If blood was shed beyond the river, let those who shed none accuse me. But by what justice does Russia answer to the Turks for deeds committed in Turkey?”

The police chief arranged the papers before him and replied that justice was not the subject under consideration.

Kirdjali smiled.

“Then I understand.”

My official was ordered to accompany the prisoner to the frontier. They travelled in a closed karoutsa with two gendarmes. Kirdjali’s wrists were chained; an iron ring was fastened about one ankle. During the journey he spoke little. At the last Russian station, while the horses were being changed, he asked whether his wife had been permitted to keep the house.

“I told him,” said the official, “that I knew nothing of women or houses. It was entirely outside my department.”

When they reached the bank, Turkish soldiers waited on the opposite side. Kirdjali looked at them and then at the Russian post behind him. Suddenly he fell upon his knees. The chains struck the stones.

“Your Honour,” he cried, “they will kill me; that is their affair and mine. But she has nobody. The child has nobody. Do not let them turn her into the road.”

He seized the hem of the official’s coat and wept.

Imagine the embarrassment of a functionary who has fulfilled his instructions exactly and then discovers a matter not mentioned in them. The poor man’s spectacles became clouded. He raised Kirdjali, promised what he could not promise, and afterward collected a small sum for the woman. I mention this because brigands have wives and children, a circumstance frequently overlooked both by poets and policemen.

The Turks received their prisoner with satisfaction. He was taken to Jassy, where a crowd gathered before the prison. Armenians in black caftans, Greek shopkeepers, Jewish boys, Moldavian peasants and Turkish soldiers pressed against one another to see him descend. A dervish cursed him. Two old women made the sign of the cross. Kirdjali looked neither to the right nor the left.

The Pasha examined him on the following day. The proceedings were brief. The charges required half an hour to read, although several villages had sent only the names of their dead. Kirdjali admitted that he had been present at places mentioned in the accusations, but disputed various details. He had burned one estate, not three; he had never stolen the pasha’s nephew’s horse, but had won it fairly at dice; and a certain tax collector, reported murdered, had merely been tied naked to a walnut tree.

The Pasha condemned him to be impaled.

Execution was fixed for Friday outside the city. Until then seven guards watched him in a vaulted chamber near the southern gate. They were Turks and Albanians, stout fellows, well armed and not easily moved by tears. Kirdjali did not attempt to move them by tears.

On the second evening he said:

“Brothers, you will see me die the day after tomorrow.”

They agreed.

“I have sent many men before me. It is just that I should follow.”

The guards approved this sentiment also.

“But what troubles me,” he continued, “is that strangers will enjoy what brave men ought to possess.”

Here he stopped.

One guard asked what he meant.

Kirdjali lowered his voice. Before joining Ipsilanti, he said, he had buried a copper jar beyond the Galata road, beside a ruined fountain. It contained Venetian ducats, jewels, strings of pearls and two handfuls of unset diamonds taken from a merchant of Adrianople. No living soul knew the place.

The guards laughed, questioned him and laughed again. Kirdjali turned his face to the wall.

The next night he resumed the subject. Why should the treasure remain underground? The Pasha had enough. The executioner would take his clothes. His wife was far away. If the seven guards divided the jar among themselves, each could purchase a house, horses and perhaps an office.

Greed is a patient locksmith. By midnight it had opened seven honest hearts.

The guards consulted. One feared a trick. Another observed that a chained man could not trick seven armed soldiers. A third proposed informing their captain and killing him afterward. This suggestion was considered unnecessarily complicated. At last they brought Kirdjali from the chamber, wrapped him in a soldier’s cloak and placed him in a karoutsa.

The moon had not risen. They passed the gate by saying they were transferring a sick prisoner. Beyond the vineyards Kirdjali directed them into a hollow crossed by a dry stream. Frogs sounded in the reeds. Farther on stood a broken fountain, exactly as he had described.

“Under that willow,” he said.

They removed the chain from his ankle but left his hands fastened. Two men began digging with spades brought from the prison yard. The soil was hard. They dug for a quarter of an hour and found stones.

“A little to the left,” said Kirdjali.

They dug to the left.

“No—deeper, brothers. I buried it in haste.”

Perspiration rolled from their foreheads like hail. One cursed him and struck him with the flat of his hand. Kirdjali endured this calmly.

“The roots have shifted the earth,” he said. “Let me feel for the mark.”

They unfastened one wrist. He knelt, examined the ground and pointed to another place. Again they dug. At length even the most credulous guard became uneasy.

“You buried no gold,” he said.

Kirdjali sighed.

“Give me a dagger. I will cut through these roots. If the jar is not beneath them, take me back and tell the Pasha I tried to escape.”

This appeared reasonable.

One of the Albanians handed him a dagger.

What followed occupied less time than the consultation which preceded it. Kirdjali cut through a root, rose, and drove the dagger beneath the Albanian’s ribs. Before the man reached the ground, Kirdjali had taken two pistols from his sash. He fired one over the heads of the others.

“Down!” he shouted. “Fifty men are waiting in the reeds!”

The seven guards had become six; darkness made the fifty probable. They flung away their spades and ran. One lost his turban, another his musket, and a third leaped into the dry stream and remained there until dawn.

Kirdjali took the dead man’s weapons and disappeared.

Three weeks later the governor of Jassy received a letter. It informed him that Kirdjali had returned to his old occupation, that twenty riders obeyed him, and that unless five thousand levs were delivered at a certain mill, the vineyards would be fired, the city gates attacked and the governor personally deprived of his ears before being killed.

The governor declared the letter insolent, absurd and beneath notice. He then sent five thousand levs to the mill.

The city was not burned.

As for Kirdjali, some say he went back to Bulgaria; others that he crossed the Danube and entered Serbian service. My official insisted that he later saw him in Kishinev, standing at dusk outside the house where his wife had lived. But the official’s eyesight was poor, and by then his purple nose had become still purpler.

I do not know the truth. I know only that seven guards went searching for treasure, one found death, and six discovered wisdom.

Such was Kirdjali.

\* A *beshlik* was a Turkish silver coin current throughout the Principalities, where its value, like that of official promises, varied according to time and necessity.

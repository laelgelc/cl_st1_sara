I

You came to me only when the winter dusk had already filled the windows with a deep, watery blue.

You stood at the door, not entering, one shoulder pressed against the frame, your little hands hidden behind your back. Your face, swollen from crying, had that grave and exhausted expression which children sometimes wear after grief, as though in a single hour they have grown old and learned everything sorrow can teach.

I was sitting at my desk and pretending to read.

“Well?” I asked.

You lowered your eyes. One foot moved forward, then back.

“Uncle…”

“What is it?”

Your lips trembled. Pride still held you by the throat, but behind pride stood another power, greater even than your hurt.

“Forgive me, please.”

I waited.

Yes, I waited! Though my whole heart had already turned toward you, though I wanted to lift you up, kiss your wet cheeks, and say that I was the one who ought to ask forgiveness, I remained in my chair. I believed that wisdom required a pause. A stern and just educator must not surrender too quickly.

What vanity there is in virtue when virtue sits comfortably in an armchair!

“Forgive you for what?” I asked.

“For being naughty.”

“And do you love me?”

You looked up at once, frightened by the question.

“I do love you! I do!”

“Then come here.”

You ran to me, but even in that moment of reconciliation your mind did not forget the great longing which had governed the whole day. With your arms around my neck, you whispered into my collar:

“And now will you show me the figures?”

“The figures?”

“Yes. Please. Now.”

Your breath was warm against my cheek. Your hair smelled of sleep, tears, and something light and living—the odor of little birds hidden in a nest. Could anyone be slow in responding to such an appeal?

I kissed your forehead.

“Very well. Bring the paper and the colored pencils.”

You flew from the room. A moment later your joyful cry rang down the passage:

“Mamma! Grandmamma! He is going to teach me!”

It was a cry to make God smile.

And yet, only a few hours earlier, those same rooms had heard a very different music.

For this time it had been too great a quarrel.

II

But it is here that the whole thing began.

You woke that morning possessed by three desires: books, colored pencils, and figures.

The books and pencils were to be bought in town. The figures—the mysterious signs by which grown people counted money, houses, years, stars, and perhaps all the secrets of the world—were to be shown to you by me.

You came running into my room before I had properly dressed.

“Uncle, let us go to town.”

“Not today.”

“To buy the books.”

“I remember.”

“And the pencils. The big box, with the gold horse.”

“I remember that too.”

“And afterward you will teach me figures.”

“Tomorrow.”

Your face changed.

“Why tomorrow?”

Because I was lazy.

That is the whole truth. I did not wish to put on my heavy coat, walk through the wet streets, enter shops, compare boxes of pencils, answer your thousand questions, and then, after returning, bend over a table while you made crooked marks and breathed anxiously upon the paper. I wanted my quiet morning. But grown people rarely tell children the truth when the truth makes them look small.

“It is a holiday,” I said.

You glanced toward the window. The street looked like every other street on every other morning.

“What holiday?”

“A holiday for the shops.”

“All the shops?”

“All of them.”

“Even the bookshop?”

“Even the bookshop.”

You considered this monstrous arrangement in silence. Then, with the persistence of one whose whole soul has been given a single direction, you asked:

“But figures are not in the shops.”

“No.”

“So you can show me figures now.”

I smiled. I was amused by your logic, which was better than mine.

“No, my dear. Lessons begin tomorrow.”

“Why?”

“Because I say so.”

There it was—the final argument of exhausted authority.

You climbed onto my knee, laid one hand upon my shoulder, and peered into my face with shining eyes.

“Please, Uncle. Just one. Show me one figure.”

“Tomorrow.”

“Two figures.”

“Tomorrow.”

“All of them very quickly.”

“Tomorrow.”

You slipped down from my knee. Your mouth tightened. You had not yet begun to cry; you were still trying to understand how the world could contain pencils, books, figures, an uncle who knew them, a whole empty morning—and yet deny them to you.

For you, the wish was not a small wish. Grown people say, “A child will forget.” We mean that the object is trivial to us, and therefore the longing must be trivial to him. But you did not forget. The figures stood before you like bright gates. Behind them lay school, mastery, adulthood, the power to read clocks and count coins and write solemn rows in a notebook. Happiness, happiness! It was all near enough to touch.

And I said: Tomorrow.

III

All morning you followed me.

When I went to the dining room, you came after me. When I stood by the window, you stood at my elbow. When I took up a newspaper, your head appeared beneath it.

“Is that a figure?”

“Yes.”

“What figure?”

“Three.”

“Show me how to make it.”

“Tomorrow.”

Then you drew something in the air with your finger.

“Like this?”

“No.”

“Like this?”

“No.”

“Then show me.”

“Tomorrow, I told you.”

At first you pleaded. Then you bargained. Then you tried ceremony.

You backed away three paces, placed your feet together, and bowed so low that your hair fell over your forehead.

“Dear Uncle,” you said, in a voice copied from some speech you had heard, “I request you very much kindly to teach me.”

Your mother laughed. Your grandmother covered her mouth with her hand. Even I laughed.

This encouraged you.

“I shall be obedient all day. I shall never shout. I shall eat soup. I shall not frighten the cat. I shall give you my tin soldier with the broken gun.”

“A magnificent offer,” I said, “but no.”

At once the bow disappeared.

“You are bad.”

“Take care.”

“You promised!”

“I promised for another day.”

“You always say another day.”

This was not true, but it contained the larger truth children sometimes hurl at us: that to a waiting heart every delay feels eternal.

By noon your disappointment had fermented into mischief. You dragged a chair across the floor merely to hear it scrape. You opened and shut the cupboard. You marched around the table, beating your palms upon your knees. When your mother told you to stop, you stopped for three seconds, watching us, and then began to whistle through your teeth.

I continued to preserve my calm. My calm was now precious to me, not because calm is good, but because it proved that I was master.

At tea you climbed onto your chair and swung your feet.

“Sit properly,” your mother said.

You swung them higher.

The heels of your shoes struck the chair: knock, knock, knock.

“Do you hear?” I asked.

“Yes.”

“Then stop.”

You stopped.

We resumed our talk. Your grandmother poured tea; the spoon rang against the glass; steam clouded her spectacles. Outside, a cart passed through the damp street. For perhaps half a minute there was peace.

Then you jumped down.

You jumped once.

You jumped twice.

On the third jump you stamped both feet and gave a sharp, triumphant shriek.

Your mother caught your arm.

“What has come over you?”

“I am jumping.”

“We can see that.”

“I want to jump.”

“You must not.”

“Why?”

“Because people are having tea.”

“I am people too.”

Grandmother smiled, which was unfortunate. You saw the smile and believed yourself victorious. You stamped again, harder, and the cups trembled in their saucers.

“Enough,” I said.

You looked straight at me.

It was no longer about jumping. It was no longer even about figures. Between us stood two wills, each suddenly ashamed to retreat.

“I shall jump,” you said.

“You shall not.”

“I shall!”

“You will go to the nursery.”

“I won’t!”

“Go at once.”

“No!”

Your face had gone pale, but your eyes burned. You leaped and stamped and shrieked all together, producing not play now but a deliberate outrage, a declaration of war against tea, holidays, tomorrow, and the entire fraudulent order of grown people.

I rose.

You shrieked again.

And I slapped you.

There’s figures for you!

IV

The sound was small. That is one of the shameful things I remember: how small the sound was, compared with everything it destroyed.

You stared at me.

Terror crossed your face like lightning. Your hand went slowly to your cheek. For one second you did not breathe; then your mouth opened, and from you came a cry so naked with disbelief that all the anger left me at once.

I pointed toward the door.

“Go.”

You went.

Not because you had accepted my authority, but because the blow had cast you out of the world in which you had believed yourself loved. You stumbled into the passage, struck the wall with your shoulder, and ran to the nursery.

Then the crying began.

At first it was loud and high, each breath flung upward like a bird dashed against a ceiling.

“A-ah! A-ah! A-ah!”

Then came words.

“Bad uncle! Wicked uncle! I hate him!”

Your mother lowered her eyes to her tea.

Grandmother sighed.

I sat down. My hand trembled, and I hid it beneath the table.

“You must not go to him,” your mother said quietly to Grandmother.

“I am not going.”

“He has to learn.”

“Of course.”

We all agreed. It was necessary. Discipline. Character. Obedience. These solemn words passed among us while a little boy cried alone in an unlit room.

Your voice dropped into a lower register. The cries came farther apart, hoarse now, dragged over the dull knife of exhaustion.

“Nobody loves me… I shall go away… I shall die…”

At this Grandmother moved in her chair.

“He says he will die.”

“He will not die,” I replied.

I spoke coldly because I was afraid that if I spoke naturally, my shame would be heard.

You continued:

“I shall die in the snow… and then Uncle will be sorry…”

There was no snow. The autumn rain had not yet turned. But snow belonged to death in your imagination, and so you lay abandoned among white drifts while I, too late repentant, searched for you with a lantern.

Grandmother almost rose.

Your mother touched her sleeve. “No. If we comfort him now, everything is lost.”

Everything! What was everything? Our dignity? The Empire? The moral structure of the universe? No—only the victory of three adults over one wounded child.

Yet we sat still.

After a while your accusations grew confused. You blamed me for the holiday. You blamed the shops for being closed. You cried that the pencils were yours, though they had not been bought, and that I had stolen your books before they existed. Once, between sobs, you shouted:

“I know figures already!”

That touched me more than the threats of death.

Then even the words ceased. We heard only uneven breathing, a little cough, the rustle of your sleeve across your face.

Finally there was silence.

V

Silence ought to have ended it. Punishment had been given, the rebellion broken, the crying exhausted. One of us could have gone to you.

But pride is a creditor who adds interest after the debt has been paid.

We waited.

The room darkened. The windows, reflecting the interior, no longer showed the street. Grandmother lit the lamp and placed the shade over it. A warm circle fell upon the tea things, while beyond that circle the corners of the room remained cold and uncertain.

At last your mother went to the nursery.

She returned alone.

“He is sitting on the floor.”

“Still crying?” Grandmother asked.

“No. He says he will never come out.”

I felt an absurd stab of offense. Never! After all that had happened, you still dared to set conditions.

Your mother went back and spoke through the half-open door.

“You must ask Uncle’s forgiveness.”

“No.”

“You behaved disgracefully.”

“He hit me.”

“That was because you would not obey.”

“I will never love him again.”

The sentence reached me clearly.

I turned a page of my unread book.

Grandmother called from the dining room:

“Naughty children are loved by nobody.”

A pause followed. Then your answer, faint but firm:

“Mamma loves me.”

“Not when you behave like this.”

“Grandmamma loves me.”

“Not disobedient boys.”

Another pause.

“I love myself.”

Grandmother looked at us, and despite everything a smile pulled at the corners of her mouth. She quickly made herself severe again.

“No one can live loving only himself,” she called.

“I can.”

You were sitting, as I learned later, beside the toy chest with your knees drawn up and your face pressed to them. The nursery had not been lit. Its window was a square of dark blue; the wooden horse, the drum, and the cupboard had sunk into shadow. Near your hand lay a scrap of paper on which you had attempted to draw a figure from memory. It resembled a bent ladder.

Life had spoken to you for the first time in its official voice.

“I want happiness,” you had said.

“Tomorrow,” Life answered.

“But it is here now. I can see it.”

“Tomorrow.”

“Why?”

“Because I say so.”

Then you rebelled, and Life struck you.

“Give in,” said Life. “Bow. Confess. Say that justice was done.”

“No,” you replied. “I will die in the snow.”

Life knows that children do not die so easily. It can afford to wait.

Yet what did I know of Life, I who sat safe beneath the lamp, pleased with the hardness of my heart? I thought I was preparing you for the world. In truth, I was borrowing the world’s cruelty to excuse my own.

VI

Grandmother went at last to the nursery, though she did not embrace you. She stood by the door, preserving on her face the expression of one who brings not comfort but terms.

“Well,” she said, “you may sit there all night.”

You did not answer.

“You may never speak to Uncle again.”

Still no answer.

“And tomorrow he may go to town alone.”

Your head lifted a little.

“He may buy no books.”

You shifted on the floor.

“No colored pencils either. The box with the gold horse will remain in the shop.”

This was cruel, but still you resisted.

“I don’t want them.”

“And the figures,” she added. “Of course you will never learn those.”

There was a long silence.

“Why?” you asked.

“Because Uncle is the only one who can teach you, and you say you do not love him.”

“I can learn myself.”

“How?”

“From the clock.”

“The clock cannot tell you how to hold a pencil.”

“I shall ask Mamma.”

“Mamma is busy.”

“I shall ask you.”

“I have forgotten.”

You understood then that the gate to the bright kingdom still stood in my keeping. The same man who had denied it, lied about the holiday, and struck you was also the man who could open it. What a monstrous confusion love is for a child! We are at once the wound and the bandage, the prison wall and the door.

Grandmother heard your deep sigh.

“Go and ask forgiveness,” she said more softly.

“No.”

“Give in, give in,” whispered the whole dark room.

You rose. Your legs, cramped from sitting, almost failed you. You wiped your face with both hands, smearing tears across your cheeks.

“Will he teach me today?”

“If you ask properly.”

“And will he love me?”

Grandmother’s severity broke for a moment. She bent and smoothed your hair.

“He loves you already, foolish boy.”

You began to cry again, but this crying was quiet. It came not from fury but from relief, and perhaps from the last pain of surrender.

“I said I didn’t love him.”

“You said many things.”

“I do love him.”

“Then tell him.”

You practiced the apology in the passage. Grandmother prompted you.

“Say, ‘Forgive me.’”

“Forgive me.”

“Say, ‘I was naughty.’”

“I was naughty.”

“And no jumping at tea.”

“No jumping.”

“And you must not shout.”

“I won’t shout.”

You took two steps toward my room, then fled back to her skirts.

“I can’t.”

“You can.”

“He will say no.”

“Try.”

Again Life stood before you.

“Bow,” it said.

“Will you give me happiness?”

“At least a drop.”

“Will it still be happiness if I must bow for it?”

“Child, that is the sort I most often give.”

And so you came to my door, one shoulder against the frame, your hands behind your back, your swollen face solemn beneath the gathering dusk.

VII

You returned with the paper, the pencil box, and three books you had collected merely because books belonged to the ceremony. None of them was a copybook. One was a book of animals, one a torn atlas, and one your mother’s household account, which you had taken without permission.

You arranged them carefully on the table.

“May I sit here?”

“Sit beside me.”

You climbed onto the chair. Your feet did not reach the floor, and for a moment they began to swing; then you remembered and held them perfectly still.

I drew a straight mark.

“This is one.”

You stared at it.

“One,” you repeated.

I drew two, with its curved head and slanting back.

“And this?”

“Two.”

“Now you.”

You selected the red pencil, rejected it, chose blue, rejected that, and finally took green. You moistened the point with your lips.

“Not in your mouth,” I said gently.

You pulled it away and smiled at me, cautiously, testing whether gentleness was truly restored.

Your first one leaned so far that it seemed about to fall. Your two curled inward like a sleeping worm.

“No, look,” I said. “Begin here.”

I placed my hand over yours. Your fingers were warm and limp with weariness. Together we drew the line.

“So?”

“So.”

Again you bent over the paper, the tip of your tongue showing at the corner of your mouth. Every mark demanded your whole body: shoulder, elbow, wrist, breath. When the pencil went wrong, your brows contracted; when it went right, your eyes shone.

“I made it!”

“Yes.”

“Is it truly two?”

“Truly.”

You laughed. The laugh still caught slightly in your throat after so much crying, but it was laughter. Your mother appeared at the door. Grandmother looked over her shoulder. Neither spoke. They had come to see whether peace, that shy animal, had returned to the house.

You wrote three. Then four. Four troubled you because its lines would not meet.

“It is broken,” you said.

“Try again.”

You licked the pencil before I could stop you, pressed so hard the tip snapped, and looked at me in terror.

I laughed.

At once you laughed too.

How little was needed! One laugh, one hand laid over yours, one permission granted—and the whole terrible day began to shrink back to its true size. Yet it did not disappear. Pain never becomes nothing merely because happiness follows it. Somewhere beneath the lamplight remained the dark nursery, the slap, the hoarse voice declaring itself unloved.

“Uncle,” you asked suddenly, “was it really a holiday?”

I hesitated.

“No.”

“Why did you say it was?”

Here was my opportunity to recover authority with another lie. Instead I answered:

“Because I did not want to go to town.”

You considered this.

“That was naughty.”

“Yes.”

“Very naughty?”

“Very.”

You nodded, satisfied by the restoration of moral order.

“Then you must ask forgiveness too.”

Your mother drew breath. Grandmother looked down. Perhaps they feared that the whole fortress of discipline would collapse if an adult apologized to a child.

I turned toward you.

“Forgive me.”

You gave me your hand immediately.

“I forgive you.”

No pause, no lecture, no proof of repentance. You forgave with the careless magnificence of the rich.

Then you leaned against my arm and went on writing. One, two, three, four. The figures wandered uphill and downhill, collided, toppled, and rose again. Around us the evening settled fully upon the house. The lamp shone on the paper, on your bent head, on the green pencil repeatedly traveling toward your lips. Beyond its circle the windows were black, and the town with its closed—or rather open—shops no longer mattered.

You had your lesson.

I had mine.

And though Life would afterward teach you harsher figures—losses to be subtracted, duties multiplied, years divided, joys counted and found too few—that evening you possessed the whole science of happiness. You had desired, rebelled, suffered, surrendered, and been received again into love.

At last your hand slowed.

“This is five,” you murmured.

“No, that is three.”

“It is tired,” you said.

“You are tired.”

You laid your cheek upon my sleeve.

“But tomorrow,” you whispered, “we shall do all the rest?”

“Yes. Tomorrow.”

This time the word did not mean refusal. It meant promise.

Your eyes closed. Your fingers still held the pencil, and on the page, beneath your warm sleeping hand, stood the crooked figures we had made together—poor little signs, uneven and smudged, but bright to me even now, because between every two of them there seemed to be written another lesson, one no child should have had to teach his uncle:

Love must not wait for victory.

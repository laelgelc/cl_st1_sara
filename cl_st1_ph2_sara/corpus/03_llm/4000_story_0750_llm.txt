### The Fragrance of Uncle David

I never saw Judge Harcourt in the exercise of his judicial functions, and I am therefore unable to affirm, upon personal knowledge, that he wore a black robe, terrified counsel, or pronounced sentence with that majestic reluctance which shows the culprit how painful it is to punish him. But family tradition has clothed him in the robe, and I have no wish to strip it off.

He lived in Boston when Boston was a smaller place, but, according to the inhabitants, much larger morally; when a man’s grandfather was a kind of negotiable security, and a respectable name, if properly invested, would maintain several generations in comfort without any conspicuous labor on their part. The Judge’s house stood in Chestnut Street, with brass knockers, stone steps, green shutters, and an air of being able to prove that its bricks had crossed the Atlantic in better company than the bricks of neighboring houses.

The Judge had money. He had also dignity, which he valued more because it could not be accurately counted. He had two daughters—Lucretia and Anne—and one son, David. Anne became my mother; and thus, although David was out of the country many years before I was born, I was brought up in the full illumination of his legend.

David, by all accounts, was beautiful.

I use the word after consideration, though I believe men ought not to be beautiful unless they are prepared to make some return for the inconvenience they cause. He had black eyes, a white forehead, curling hair, and that graceful manner which elderly ladies forgive in advance. He could ride anything, dance with anybody, sing a sea-song, imitate the Governor, and get into debt with equal facility. At fifteen he had already acquired the reputation of a young gentleman of spirit. At sixteen the spirit had become expensive. At seventeen it was generally agreed that a voyage might broaden his character, though no one then contemplated so much broadening as actually occurred.

The first authentic story concerning him—and concerning another person, less admired at the time—is the story of the baskets.

In those days the market was not merely a place where provisions were purchased. It was an institution, and approached in a frame of mind almost religious. Families sent baskets with their names painted upon them, and the baskets waited in rows while beef, poultry, cabbages, fish, and other necessities of a virtuous existence were distributed according to rank and purse. Boston was so honest that a basket could be left unattended. Whether Boston was also so hungry that no one wished to carry away another family’s turnips, I cannot say.

One Saturday morning David went to market with a servant. While the servant debated the age of a goose with an obstinate tradesman, David observed two baskets standing side by side. One was the Harcourt basket, large, varnished, and nearly bursting with evidence of prosperity. The other belonged to Widow Biggs, who lived in a narrow house behind the ropewalk and had a large appetite in the family but a small one in the purse.

The Biggs basket contained, as far as I can find out, a bone, three onions, a damaged loaf, and something wrapped in paper which may have been cheese. Family narrators differ about the cheese. Aunt Lucretia always denied it, perhaps because she thought cheese an extravagance in the poor.

David changed the labels.

It was the work of a moment. The Harcourt basket, with its roast, fowl, butter, eggs, apples, coffee, and a pie of imposing circumference, became the property of Mrs. Biggs; while the Judge’s household received the bone, the onions, the loaf, and the disputed cheese.

David regarded the transaction as wit. It may have been philanthropy in disguise, though his later conduct makes this doubtful.

A butcher’s boy saw him do it.

The boy was named Thomas Biggs—Tommy to his acquaintances—and was the widow’s younger son. He was twelve, short, broad, red-haired, and so freckled that one of the market-men said he looked as though somebody had thrown bran at him while he was wet. He carried meat for Mr. Pomeroy, the butcher, cleaned blocks, swept sawdust, and received instruction from the rear hoofs of animals not completely reconciled to civilization.

Tommy worshiped David Harcourt.

Why a poor, plain butcher’s apprentice should have worshiped the Judge’s handsome son is not difficult to understand. The handsome are frequently adored by those whom Providence has relieved of the responsibility of beauty. David could stand with his hands in his pockets and appear romantic; Tommy could not have appeared romantic on a burning ship at midnight.

When the exchange was discovered, the Judge was incensed. It was not the loss of the dinner, though the Judge had invited two aldermen and a clergyman. It was the insult to Order. The Harcourt household had received Widow Biggs’s bone; Widow Biggs had received the Harcourt pie. Society had been overturned, and there was gravy upon its foundations.

Suspicion fell upon Tommy because he had been seen near the baskets.

Mr. Pomeroy seized him by the collar and demanded the truth.

“Did you change them labels?”

Tommy saw David standing beyond the stall. David did not speak. He only looked at him.

“No,” said Tommy.

“Know who did?”

Tommy hesitated.

“No.”

This second answer was morally different from the first, but Mr. Pomeroy’s hand did not appreciate distinctions. Tommy was cuffed, deprived of his half-day’s wage, and sent away without dinner. The Judge, having learned that the culprit could not be identified, delivered a discourse upon the rising dishonesty of the lower orders. David listened with admirable composure.

Later, he found Tommy behind the market and gave him a silver fourpence.

Tommy would not take it.

“Hot in the collar?” David asked.

“No.”

“Then why not?”

“Didn’t do it for pay.”

“What did you do it for?”

Tommy looked at him as a dog might look at a church steeple—without understanding the architecture, but with perfect confidence in its elevation.

“For you,” he said.

David laughed, put the coin in his own pocket, and invited Tommy to come down to the wharf that afternoon. Tommy went.

I have been particular about this incident because, though no one then knew it, the baskets contained the entire story. David changed what belonged to him for what belonged to another; Tommy accepted the blow; and the Harcourt family ate the bone for years without knowing who had provided the dinner.

The acquaintance continued. David climbed out of the Judge’s stable at night, and Tommy waited in the lane. David wanted a boat; Tommy found one. David quarreled; Tommy stood beside him, though Tommy, being compact and laboriously developed by beef, could lick almost anybody of his size, while David’s courage operated best when there was an audience.

The Judge forbade the intimacy.

“Young Biggs is unsuitable,” he declared.

To the Judge, unsuitability was not a condition but an offense.

David submitted with filial gravity and met Tommy more frequently.

Then came the quarrel which ended in David’s flight. I cannot give its exact cause. Aunt Lucretia said it was cards; my mother said it was a woman; an elderly cousin maintained it concerned a horse; and one paper among the Judge’s effects referred obscurely to “a debt contracted under circumstances dishonorable to this house.” Since David was capable of combining cards, a woman, a horse, and a dishonorable debt in one evening, all accounts may be correct.

The Judge sent for him.

“You will go to your uncle in Worcester,” he said, “and remain there until you have learned duty.”

David replied that Worcester was not far enough away to make duty attractive.

There were words. The Judge used the kind which shook windows. David used fewer, but they were selected for injury. At last the Judge pointed to the door.

“If you cross that threshold in defiance of me, you shall not cross it again.”

David bowed.

“Then I shall save wear upon the step.”

He went upstairs, packed a small bag, kissed Anne, avoided Lucretia—who would probably have held him by the coat—and left the house.

Tommy was waiting at the corner.

Before daylight the two were aboard a brig bound first for Madeira and thereafter, by those uncertain commercial intentions common to brigs, toward any port where profit might be found. David had six pounds, a gold watch, and an excellent coat. Tommy had eleven shillings, a clasp-knife, and the habit of work.

The Judge said David would return within a month.

He did not.

At the end of six months, Mrs. Harcourt received a letter. The writing was David’s, or so the family believed, though Aunt Lucretia afterwards said she had always suspected something in the capitals. It was dated at sea and was brief:

> MY DEAR MOTHER,—  
> I am quite well and hope all at Home are enjoying the Same blessing. The life agrees with me. I have seen Madeira and expect soon to see the Cape. Do not be uneasy. I shall write when opportunity affords.  
>   
> Your affectionate Son,  
> DAVID.

The Judge read the letter twice, said, “Perfunctory,” and placed it in his desk. Mrs. Harcourt kissed it. Anne cried. Lucretia remarked that if David supposed three sentences could excuse six months, foreign travel had weakened his mind.

A year later another letter came from Singapore, accompanied by a carved ivory box. Two years after that came a shawl from Canton, yellow as sunlight and covered with birds which no Boston ornithologist could conscientiously recognize. There followed, at dignified intervals, lacquer trays, tea, porcelain jars, sandalwood fans, a teak cabinet, embroidered screens, preserved ginger, little men cut out of jade, and a red silk robe in which the Judge looked like an indignant idol.

The letters remained restrained.

> HONORED FATHER,—  
> Business carries me into the interior and may delay communication. I trust Mother’s cough has abated. The cabinet accompanying this was made in Ningpo and is considered here a creditable piece. Please accept it with my duty.  
>   
> D. HARCOURT.

Or:

> DEAR SISTERS,—  
> The four cases shipped by the *Celestial Hope* contain articles marked as per enclosed invoice. The blue jars are for Lucretia, the workbox for Anne, and the toys for Anne’s little Boy. I have done reasonably well and expect better.  
>   
> Yours affec.,  
> DAVID.

The “little Boy” was myself.

I therefore knew Uncle David first as a source of packages. Most children are taught that benevolence descends from Heaven. Mine came by ship, paid freight, and smelled of camphor.

Our house gradually acquired an Oriental character which astonished visitors and confused the furniture. A severe mahogany sideboard found itself flanked by two gilded dragons. A porcelain warrior, armed with a green spear, faced my grandfather’s portrait across the dining room, and each appeared to regard the other as a regrettable racial experiment. We had cabinets which opened in unexpected places, tables too delicate to hold anything, and screens which fell whenever a person spoke emphatically near them.

Above all, we had fragrance.

Sandalwood, tea, spice, lacquer, preserved orange, silk, camphor, and the faint dry odor of old ships mingled in our rooms. My mother used to open Uncle David’s letters and declare that she could smell China upon them. Aunt Lucretia said she smelled damp.

The Judge’s attitude toward these munificent consignments was a triumph of parental consistency. He accepted every article, displayed most of them, and never forgave the sender.

“I have no son in China,” he would say, seated in a carved chair sent by that nonexistent relative.

Yet he asked the sailing dates. He read the commercial news. When a vessel from Canton was overdue, he became irritable. Once, during a winter of storms, he walked to the shipping office every morning for three weeks. On the day word arrived that the ship was safe, he punished a servant for smiling.

Mrs. Harcourt died without seeing David again. Her last intelligible words, according to my mother, were, “Tell him I understood.” What she understood was not specified, and in our family such omissions were respected as sacred property.

The Judge survived her six years. David wrote after his death:

> MY DEAR ANNE AND LUCRETIA,—  
> The news of Father’s decease has reached me by the *Mercy Brown*. At this distance words are poor instruments. Whatever passed between us, he was my Father. I beg you will place the enclosed sum toward a suitable stone, without mentioning the source should this be considered undesirable.  
>   
> Your affectionate Brother,  
> DAVID.

The stone was extremely suitable. Aunt Lucretia objected to the money for three days, accepted it on the fourth, and supervised the lettering for two months.

Time, which performs its greatest wonders without consultation, made us all older. My mother married, bore me, received a sandalwood cradle that arrived too late for my use, and died before I reached manhood. Lucretia remained unmarried. I do not say no one asked her. Family pride requires that we assume several men did and were rejected before they could make the mistake public.

She grew angular with age. Her face became a declaration, her nose a dissenting opinion, and her long bony finger an instrument before which tradesmen confessed errors in accounts which were actually correct. Yet she loved the family violently, and Uncle David most violently of all. His absence allowed him to remain young, handsome, misunderstood, and entirely unable to contradict her.

I married Eleanor.

This was a fortunate act, though Aunt Lucretia observed that Eleanor’s people had not been in Boston long enough to become fully dry. Eleanor possessed humor, patience, and the sort of beauty which improves a room without making the chairs feel inferior. At the time the principal events of this narrative occurred, we had been married four years and had one daughter, Mary, aged fourteen months, whose moral nature was excellent except where buttons were concerned.

Then came the letter announcing Uncle David’s return.

It was dated Hong Kong:

> MY DEAR NEPHEW,—  
> Having at length arranged my affairs so that I may absent myself for a period, I intend to take passage for Boston and, if health permits, to arrive sometime in October. Thirty-eight years have elapsed since I left Home. I cannot expect to find much unchanged, nor to be remembered beyond my deserts. If convenient I should be glad to pass my first weeks with you. I travel without ceremony and wish no public notice.  
>   
> Your affectionate Uncle,  
> DAVID HARCOURT.

The letter threw the family into a condition surpassed only by war.

Aunt Lucretia wished to receive him at her own house, but she had recently dismissed her cook for putting nutmeg into a custard without authority and was therefore domestically disabled. My house was selected. Eleanor began opening rooms, counting linen, and wondering what a man who had lived in China ate for breakfast.

“Rice,” said Aunt Lucretia.

“Every morning?”

“They live on it.”

“Perhaps he has changed.”

“David never changed.”

This was the creed of her existence.

The expected man grew in our imaginations as October approached. He was sixty, but we pictured him as a mature edition of the beautiful youth in the daguerreotype—silver at the temples, perhaps, bronzed by tropical suns, grave with experience, but erect, elegant, and touched with the mystery of the East. I expected anecdotes involving mandarins, pirates, typhoons, opium dens, desert islands, and narrow escapes from execution. Eleanor hoped only that he would not require chopsticks.

On the eighteenth of October, during a rain which made romance difficult, a hired carriage stopped before our door. The driver placed upon the pavement one battered leather trunk, two square boxes, a roll of rugs, and a small elderly man in an enormous gray overcoat.

The small elderly man paid the driver, counted the change, looked at the number on our door, and rang.

I opened it.

“Mr. Edward Harcourt?” he asked.

“Yes.”

He removed a wet hat. His hair was thin, reddish-gray, and flattened by the journey. He had pale eyes, a blunt nose, a fringe of whisker, and a scar near his chin. He did not resemble the daguerreotype. He did not resemble a pirate, a mandarin, or even a man who had narrowly escaped execution. He resembled a retired dealer in hardware.

“I’m David,” he said.

I embraced him.

It was the proper action, and I performed it with warmth; but I confess that during the embrace I became aware of an aroma which was neither sandalwood nor spice. It was patchouli—strong, inexpensive patchouli, such as may accompany a person to whom fragrance has been recommended by a barber.

Eleanor came forward. He bowed to her, took both her hands, and said:

“Anne’s boy chose well.”

That conquered Eleanor. Women will forgive much in a man who compliments their husbands indirectly.

Then Mary, awakened by the commotion, was brought down by her nurse. Uncle David looked at her for a long moment. His face, which until then had seemed merely tired, altered.

“Baby?” he asked.

“Ours,” said Eleanor.

He held out one finger. Mary took it, examined it with judicial caution, and put it in her mouth.

“Well,” he said, “that settles it.”

What was settled we did not know. He removed his overcoat.

Both arms emerged.

I mention this because it became important later. At the time we were chiefly interested in helping him out of several additional garments. He wore two waistcoats, a woolen comforter, and under his coat another short coat of quilted silk. Whether he feared the Boston climate or intended to unpack himself gradually was not clear.

His first evening was disappointing only if one expected entertainment. He ate sparingly, praised the beef, asked the age of the house, and answered questions in a manner which ended them.

“Did you ever see the Emperor of China?” I asked.

“No.”

“Were you in Peking?”

“Once.”

“And what did you think of it?”

“Large.”

“Did you encounter pirates?”

“Not willingly.”

“Were you ever in danger?”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

“Got out.”

It is difficult to maintain the pageantry of foreign travel against such artillery.

After dinner he smoked a short black pipe, holding it in the hollow of his hand, and watched the fire. Occasionally he looked at my mother’s portrait. When Eleanor asked if he was fatigued, he said, “Some.” When I offered brandy, he declined it quickly.

“Never touch it?”

“Not now.”

He went to bed at nine.

Eleanor and I remained by the fire.

“Well?” she said.

“He is not exactly as I imagined.”

“No.”

“He may be reserved by habit.”

“Possibly.”

“He has lived among a people celebrated for inscrutability.”

“He was born in Boston,” said Eleanor.

This seemed unanswerable.

Within a week, however, Uncle David established himself in our lives with very little assistance from conversation. He rose at six, walked in all weathers, purchased his own tobacco, and visited the docks. He disliked carriages, sermons over twenty minutes, sweet wine, and being waited upon. He mended his clothes with surprising skill, sharpened every knife in the kitchen, repaired the nursery clock, and taught our cook to preserve ginger, though the first experiment hardened into a substance which cost us a saucepan and part of a tooth.

He was generous in a stealthy, almost criminal way. Money appeared beneath the sugar bowl after a poor cousin called. The nurse found a gold piece in Mary’s sock. A discharged sailor, who had once known him abroad, left our door in tears and a new overcoat. When confronted, Uncle David became evasive.

“Did you give that man fifty dollars?” I asked.

“Owed him.”

“For what?”

“Old matter.”

“Did you also give him your boots?”

“Pinched me.”

The boots were two sizes larger than his feet.

With Mary he was another creature. He had no theory of children and therefore pleased her. He allowed her to pull his whiskers, fed her crumbs, carried her for hours, and manufactured from teak a fleet of small boats on which she immediately sat. If she cried in the night, he appeared in the passage before the nurse. When she was ill with a fever, he neither ate nor slept until the physician declared the danger past.

Then he went into his room, shut the door, and remained there an hour.

He brought Mary presents from the boxes he had shipped—a silver bell, a carved ivory fish, silk shoes, and a squat jade animal concerning whose species experts disagreed. The animal became her favorite, possibly because it was the only toy in the nursery that could not be broken by ordinary Christian methods.

Our visitors found him insufficiently exotic. Mrs. Prendergast came prepared to hear of cannibals and was told that he had never met any.

“Surely,” she said, “you have seen the heathen bow down to idols?”

“Seen Christians do it too,” he replied.

Mrs. Prendergast did not return for three weeks.

The greatest disappointment was his dress. Aunt Lucretia had expected robes. He wore dark trousers, thick boots, and jackets which appeared to have been selected because no one else wanted them. On Sundays he produced a black coat of ancient cut, brushed to a gray polish. He smelled persistently of patchouli.

“Why does he use that dreadful scent?” Eleanor whispered.

“Perhaps it is esteemed in China.”

“It is sold at Mr. Pike’s for twelve cents.”

“Commerce destroys mystery,” I said.

By degrees I ceased asking for adventures. We sat together in companionable quiet, he with his pipe and I with a book. Sometimes he spoke of ports, currents, prices, sickness, ships, and weather. Never of himself. Never, curiously, of his parents, though he often looked at their portraits. Never of the years before he left Boston. If I mentioned the basket story, he shifted in his chair and said:

“Boy’s nonsense.”

“Do you remember Tommy Biggs?”

He looked at the fire.

“Remember him.”

“What became of him?”

“Stayed with me a time.”

“And afterward?”

“Afterward too.”

This answer, while grammatically imperfect, seemed to close the matter.

We had enjoyed—or endured—Uncle David’s presence for nearly two months when Aunt Lucretia came to see him.

Her delay requires explanation. She had gone to Philadelphia shortly before his arrival to nurse a cousin who was not dangerously ill until Lucretia took charge. Letters concerning David reached her, but she refused to return until the cousin could sit up and express gratitude. The railway journey was then interrupted by snow. Thus the meeting on which she had meditated for thirty-eight years was postponed until December.

She sent notice of the hour.

At half past two precisely a carriage arrived. Aunt Lucretia descended in black, carrying a reticule and wearing a bonnet of such architectural firmness that the snow dared not settle upon it. She refused my arm, mounted the steps like a grenadier, and halted in the hall.

“Where is he?”

“In the library.”

“Does he know I am here?”

“He heard the carriage.”

“That is not what I asked.”

“No.”

“Good.”

She removed her cloak. Beneath it she wore gray silk and the amethyst brooch David had sent her twenty years before. I saw that her hands trembled.

“Edward,” she said, “you will not remain.”

“Certainly not.”

“Eleanor may remain.”

This was characteristic. Men, in Aunt Lucretia’s view, interfered with emotion by observing it.

Eleanor and I withdrew to the drawing room, leaving the door slightly open—not from curiosity, I trust, but because old hinges sometimes resist delicacy. Uncle David sat by the library fire with Mary on his knee.

Aunt Lucretia entered.

For one instant she stood motionless. She had prepared speeches: accusation, forgiveness, recollection, family history, and probably Scripture. None was used.

Uncle David rose and set Mary down.

“Lucretia,” he said.

She stared.

Then she pointed at him with the long bony finger which had brought grocers to repentance.

“That ain’t HIM!”

Mary began to cry.

Eleanor hurried in, and I followed at a distance appropriate to a man who had been ordered elsewhere.

“Aunt Lucretia—” I began.

“That man is not my brother.”

Uncle David remained standing. His face had gone gray.

“Lucretia,” he said again.

“Don’t call me that! Who are you?”

He bent, picked up Mary, and gave her to Eleanor. The child clung to his coat, but he gently released her fingers.

Aunt Lucretia crossed to the mantel, where the daguerreotype of the youthful David stood in a velvet case. She seized it, thrust it toward him, and then toward us.

“This is David. Look at the brow. Look at the eyes. Look at the shape of the head. That person hasn’t his head, his eyes, or any feature belonging to the Harcourts.”

Age can alter a head, I thought, and the Harcourt features were not registered property. But she had not finished.

“My brother lost his left arm.”

Silence entered the room.

Even the fire seemed to lower its voice.

“What?” I said.

“His left arm. Above the elbow. I had it from Captain Josiah Bell in 1849. David’s ship touched at Salem. Captain Bell saw him and spoke with him. He had lost the arm in an accident at Macao.”

We looked at the man beside the hearth.

His two hands hung at his sides.

Aunt Lucretia advanced.

“Who are you?”

He did not retreat.

“I’m Tommy Biggs, Miss Lucretia.”

The name seemed to strike the room more violently than the accusation.

I remembered the butcher’s boy. The basket. The whipping. The brig at dawn.

Aunt Lucretia sat down.

She did not faint. She had always regarded fainting as a form of bad manners, since it put the burden of one’s weight upon innocent persons. But the rigidity went out of her, and for the first time in my life she looked old.

Eleanor held Mary close.

I said the foolish thing.

“Then where is Uncle David?”

Tommy looked toward the portrait of my mother.

“Dead.”

“When?”

“Thirty-one years ago.”

Aunt Lucretia made a small sound.

Tommy picked up his pipe from the table, turned it once in his hand, and put it down again.

“Fever,” he said. “Canton River. Buried there.”

“And all these years—” I began.

“All these years,” said Aunt Lucretia, recovering enough strength for anger, “you have written to us in his name?”

“Yes.”

“You have sent gifts in his name?”

“Yes.”

“You entered this house and allowed us to receive you as my brother?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Tommy did not answer.

Aunt Lucretia rose again, fortified by outrage.

“Why?”

He looked at her with the pale, tired eyes which I had thought commonplace.

“Promised him.”

“Promised him WHAT?”

“To look after things.”

“What things?”

“Home.”

Aunt Lucretia’s face reddened.

“You call this looking after us? A deception continued for thirty-one years? Forged letters? False signatures? You let my mother die believing—my father—Anne—”

“Let them believe he was doing well,” Tommy said.

“He WASN’T?”

“No.”

The word fell quietly.

“What was he doing?”

Tommy sat down. His knees seemed suddenly uncertain. Eleanor placed Mary with the nurse, shut the door, and came back. No one asked her to go. Even Aunt Lucretia had forgotten the management of the occasion.

Tommy spoke into the fire.

“David didn’t like writing.”

“We knew that,” said Aunt Lucretia sharply.

“Didn’t like business either. Not steady. Could make money. Couldn’t keep it.”

“Cards?” I asked.

“Some.”

“Drink?”

Tommy’s hand closed.

“Some.”

“Women?”

He glanced toward Eleanor.

“Peculiarities,” he said.

Aunt Lucretia gave a sound between a snort and a gasp.

“Peculiarities!”

“Had ’em,” Tommy said. “Peculiarities.”

The story came slowly, in pieces. We had to draw it from him, and sometimes supply the connections afterward. I give it here with more order than it possessed in the telling, but I will not pretend to words he did not use.

On the brig, David had been charming, idle, seasick, and popular. Tommy worked. At Madeira David spent half his money in two days. At Cape Town he lost the rest at cards and sold the gold watch. Tommy lent him nine shillings and went without shoes for part of the voyage.

In Calcutta David found employment with a merchant, quarreled with him, and left. In Singapore he entered another house, prospered briefly, and disappeared with an actress whose charms, according to Tommy, had been “considerable but not permanent.” Tommy followed, recovered David from fever, paid his debts, and obtained another position for him.

“You did all this?” I asked.

“Somebody had to.”

“Why?”

“Friend.”

David would not write home. At first he said he would write when he had made his fortune; then when he had repaid a debt; then when he could explain a scandal; then not at all.

“He was proud,” Tommy said.

Aunt Lucretia lifted her chin, as if pride were at least a recognizable family symptom.

The first letter had been David’s. The second also. After that Tommy wrote them.

“Did David know?”

“Mostly.”

“What do you mean, mostly?”

“Sometimes knew. Sometimes away.”

“Away where?”

“Peculiarities.”

Aunt Lucretia shut her eyes.

The presents began with the ivory box. Tommy had bought it after a profitable voyage and told David to send it to his mother. David agreed, then gambled away the money intended for freight. Tommy paid the freight. Later he sent the shawl. David signed the letter. Afterward, when David could not be found, Tommy signed for him.

Their fortunes rose and fell, though David contributed more to the falling. He had imagination, daring, and persuasive grace. He could arrange a venture, charm investors, foresee a market, and ruin the entire enterprise before the accounts were closed. Tommy lacked imagination but possessed industry, thrift, and the ability to know when a man was lying—except, apparently, when the man was David.

Together they traded in tea, silk, spices, and shipping. David made opportunities. Tommy made them pay.

The arm was lost during the loading of a vessel at Macao. A cable parted. David pushed another man clear and was caught.

At this point Tommy looked up eagerly.

“Brave,” he said. “Always brave. Finest man in the world when it came sudden.”

No one contradicted him.

After the accident David changed for a time. He drank less. He worked. He spoke of coming home. Tommy had a coat made for him, settled old obligations, and booked passage.

Then David met someone.

“A woman?” Eleanor asked gently.

“Widow,” said Tommy.

“What happened?”

“Wasn’t a widow.”

He canceled the passage.

Years went by. The Judge died. Tommy showed David the letter from Boston. David read it, locked himself in a room, and emerged drunk two days later. The money for the monument came from Tommy.

“Every sum?” I asked. “Every gift?”

“Business money.”

“Whose business?”

“Ours.”

“How much was David’s?”

Tommy considered.

“Hard to say.”

“How much work did he do?”

“Different kinds.”

“How much was yours?”

“Didn’t reckon.”

Aunt Lucretia did.

“It was all yours.”

“No,” Tommy said with unexpected force. “His name helped. People liked him.”

“People liked him, so you spent your life paying for what he squandered?”

“Partners.”

“You were not partners. You were his servant.”

Tommy’s face hardened.

“No.”

“His dupe, then.”

“No.”

“What were you?”

“Friend.”

There was nothing loud in the word. Yet Aunt Lucretia stopped.

David’s final illness came during a summer of heat and fever. He had been absent for weeks. Tommy found him in a room above a riverside tavern, ill, penniless, and attended by persons who departed when asked to assist with expenses. Tommy took him home and nursed him.

“Did he know he was dying?” Eleanor asked.

“Toward end.”

“What did he say?”

Tommy stared at his scarred hands.

“Said he’d made a bad job.”

Aunt Lucretia’s lips moved.

“He asked about home,” Tommy continued. “Asked if his mother kept the ivory box. Asked about Anne. Didn’t ask about Judge till last.”

“And about me?” said Aunt Lucretia.

Tommy looked at her.

“Said you’d know.”

“Know what?”

“That he was no good.”

Her face changed as if he had struck it.

Tommy went on quickly, almost protectively.

“Fever talking. Didn’t mean it. He was fine. You didn’t see him there. Everybody knew him. Could walk into a room—whole place lit up.”

We said nothing.

“Asked me to write,” Tommy said. “Said don’t let ’em know. Not yet. Mother gone, father gone, Anne with her little boy. Said send things. Said keep him—”

He stopped.

“Keep him what?” Eleanor asked.

“Keep him good at home.”

There it was: the whole fraud, if fraud is not too cheap a word for thirty-one years of labor, loneliness, and love.

David died before dawn. Tommy buried him near the river. He paid the doctor, the tavern, the woman who owned the room, and several debts which appeared with impressive promptness after death. Then he returned to the warehouse and continued the business under David’s name.

He sent the letters.

He chose the blue jars for Lucretia because David once said she liked blue. He sent my mother the workbox because David remembered her sewing. He sent me toys, books, ivory chessmen, and later money for my education. He established scholarships anonymously at the school David had disliked. He provided for sailors, cousins, and dependents. Every act of munificence by which the absent David’s character had been enlarged was Tommy’s.

“Why did you come here?” I asked at last. “You might have remained abroad. We never demanded your return.”

“Getting old.”

“That is not a reason to become another man.”

“Wanted to see home.”

“Whose home?”

He looked around the room—the lacquer screen, the sandalwood cabinet, my mother’s portrait, Mary’s jade animal on the rug.

“His,” he said.

“And you thought we would never discover it?”

“Thought Miss Lucretia knew about arm.”

Aunt Lucretia stared at him.

“You thought I KNEW?”

“Captain Bell.”

“Then why come?”

Tommy’s shoulders moved in the faintest shrug.

“Maybe wanted it over.”

He had not planned an imposture, not as a criminal plans one. From all I can gather, he had planned nothing beyond the next letter, the next gift, the next duty. When he decided to return, he wrote that David was coming because he had signed David’s name so long that his own had become almost an impropriety. He knew discovery was likely. Perhaps he hoped for it. Perhaps he wished to enter once, however falsely, the home he had supported from afar. Perhaps he wished only to see whether the people David had left were real.

And perhaps, though he never said so, he wished for a family.

Aunt Lucretia was the first to speak.

“You have made us ridiculous.”

This was not the most important aspect of the case, but it was the one nearest her hand.

Tommy nodded.

“You allowed me to talk to you of childhood—of our mother—of matters no stranger had a right to hear.”

He nodded again.

“You are not David.”

“No.”

“You never were David.”

“No.”

“You never could be.”

“No, ma’am.”

Each answer reduced him. He seemed smaller in the chair, an old butcher’s boy caught beside the baskets.

Then Mary escaped from the nurse and ran into the room.

She went directly to Tommy.

“Unca,” she said, holding up the jade animal.

Tommy did not take it.

Aunt Lucretia turned away.

Eleanor knelt beside him.

“Tommy,” she said, “look at me.”

He obeyed.

“You deceived us. That was wrong.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“You should have told the truth years ago.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“But everything we loved in Uncle David—the kindness, the remembrance, the generosity—was yours.”

“No. His.”

“The letters were yours.”

“His thoughts.”

“The gifts were yours.”

“He’d have sent ’em.”

“But he didn’t.”

Tommy’s mouth trembled.

“Would have. Later.”

Eleanor took his hand.

“He left us thirty-eight years ago. You did not.”

I have loved my wife for many reasons, but never more than then.

I crossed the room and placed my hand upon Tommy’s shoulder.

“You came here as my uncle,” I said. “If you are willing, you shall remain as my uncle.”

He shook his head.

“I am not asking you to continue the lie. You are Tommy Biggs. You were David’s friend. You cared for my mother and for me when you owed us nothing. That seems sufficient relationship.”

“More than sufficient,” Eleanor said.

Mary struck the jade beast upon his knee to emphasize the legal point.

Aunt Lucretia still faced the window.

We waited.

At last she said, “He took a whipping for David when he was a boy.”

“Yes,” I replied.

“He always was a fool.”

Tommy bowed his head.

Aunt Lucretia turned. Her face was wet, which made us all look away, because her dignity in tears was more private than another woman’s undress.

She came to him and held out her hand.

“You are not my brother.”

“No, ma’am.”

“My brother was handsome.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“He had breeding.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

“He did not smell of cheap patchouli.”

“No, ma’am.”

“Why do you wear the vile stuff?”

Tommy touched his collar, embarrassed.

“David used it. Toward last.”

Aunt Lucretia’s hand dropped.

For a second I thought she would embrace him. She did not. Such actions require practice, and she had spent too many years pointing. But she laid her fingers on his sleeve.

“You may stay,” she said.

Tommy looked from her to us, then down at Mary.

“Thank you.”

The crisis seemed over. It was not.

That evening he ate no dinner. He said he was tired and went upstairs. At six the next morning his bed was empty.

His trunk remained, but his papers and a small valise were gone. On the pillow lay the gold fourpence David had offered him behind the market—preserved through all those years—and a note addressed to me:

> DEAR EDWARD,—  
> You have treated me better than I had right to expect. Mrs. Edward also. Miss Lucretia was right. I cannot stay in his place after you know it is not mine, and I do not know how to stay in any other. Tell the Baby I am obliged to her. There are matters abroad requiring attention. I shall write.  
>   
> Respectfully,  
> THOMAS BIGGS.

We searched the docks. His ship had sailed on the morning tide.

Aunt Lucretia blamed me, Eleanor, the weather, the shipping company, Tommy, David, and herself, in that order. Then she took the fourpence and had it mounted under glass, though she said it was merely evidence.

Six months later a final shipment arrived from Hong Kong: twelve chests of tea, rugs, porcelain, books, carved panels, preserved fruits, bolts of silk, a sandalwood desk for Mary, and several cases whose contents no one had ordered and no room could comfortably contain. The house smelled for weeks like the hold of an Oriental merchantman.

The accompanying letter was not signed David Harcourt.

> HONG KONG, 3 MAY 18—  
>   
> DEAR EDWARD AND ELEANOR,—  
> Health is not so good as formerly and the doctor advises that I put my affairs in order. Papers forwarded separately make you executor and leave the property to Mary, excepting certain sums for men in the business and for Miss Lucretia during life. It is not as much as it might have been, but enough. The things shipped are mostly what I had by me. I hope they will not inconvenience you.  
>   
> I have often considered what you said. You were kind, but I was not your uncle. David was. Whatever was wrong in him, he was the finest man I knew. He gave me the chance to see the world. Before that I was carrying beef and couldn’t expect much. I have had a good life.  
>   
> Please tell Miss Lucretia that he spoke of her at the end, and tell the child I remember her. I do not expect to come Home again.  
>   
> Yours with respect and affection,  
> THOMAS BIGGS.

We wrote at once. The letter was returned months later. The firm had dissolved. Tommy had left Hong Kong in poor health, perhaps for a smaller port, perhaps for Japan, perhaps inland. No one could tell us. An agent believed he had died aboard a coastal steamer, but the passenger lists were incomplete. Another man claimed to have seen him in Shanghai. Aunt Lucretia rejected both reports and continued to write him every Christmas until her own death.

We never heard from him again.

Mary inherited his estate and the jade animal, preferring the latter until she reached an age at which her judgment became more commercial. The money educated children he had never seen, repaired our house, sustained relatives who would have been offended to call themselves poor, and established, at Eleanor’s insistence, a fund for the families of sailors lost abroad. Aunt Lucretia permitted his name to be used, though she objected to the size of the lettering.

The objects remain.

Some are beautiful, some valuable, and some so extraordinary that even dealers approach them in silence. The gilded dragons still guard the sideboard. The porcelain warrior still threatens my grandfather. The cabinet from Ningpo conceals its drawers from every generation in turn. The silk has faded; the ivory has yellowed; the old letters, folded and refolded, have become delicate at the creases.

And the house retains a fragrance.

Visitors call it sandalwood. Eleanor says it is the teak chests. Mary, now grown, insists it comes from the cabinet in the hall. On damp afternoons there is certainly camphor in it, and in summer perhaps tea. I sometimes detect the preserved orange, though the last jar was eaten during an unfortunate winter when the cook mistook it for marmalade, and I do not wish to make that breakfast any more pathetic than is necessary.

But beneath those respectable perfumes, fainter now and yet unmistakable, there lingers a trace of cheap patchouli.

It is not the odor we expected from China. It has no romance, no rank, no mystery worth displaying. It belongs to an ordinary man in thick boots and too many coats—a man who spoke badly, looked commonplace, and loved one radiant scapegrace beyond reason. He took the punishment in the market, paid the debts in foreign ports, wrote the letters, sent the gifts, guarded the family pride, and carried another man’s name until it bent him.

David was our uncle by blood, and perhaps blood must be allowed its privileges.

But Tommy Biggs remembered us.

So, when the old rooms grow warm and the carved boxes give up the scents of their long voyages, we do not say that the house smells of China, or of David, or even of the past.

We say that it still smells of Uncle Tommy’s love.

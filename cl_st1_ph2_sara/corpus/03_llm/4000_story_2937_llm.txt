Even after this lapse of five-and-thirty years, I cannot hear the thin music of falling water in a vaulted chamber without recalling that night in the palace of Rajah Daryan Singh; nor can I behold, in any hospital, the calm smile which sometimes settles upon the countenance of a patient newly released from agony, without seeing again the face of Neranya—upturned, blood-darkened, and horribly content.

I was then a surgeon attached to the Residency at Bhurampore. My professional reputation, which has since been magnified beyond its merits, rested chiefly upon a certain steadiness of hand and upon my willingness to undertake operations from which men of greater learning, but less confidence, occasionally recoiled. Thus, when a messenger came at midnight from Daryan Singh, bearing a gold token and entreating my immediate attendance upon a lady of his household, I rode thither without delay.

The patient, a young widow related to the Rajah, had sustained a deep wound of the abdomen by falling upon the jewelled point of a ceremonial dagger. Whether the injury were indeed accidental I never learned. The palace women had stanched the external bleeding with silk and powdered herbs, but internal hæmorrhage had begun; and had I arrived an hour later, no earthly aid could have preserved her. With such instruments as I had brought, and under difficulties which need not here be enumerated, I secured the divided vessel. She recovered.

The Rajah’s gratitude was profuse. He pressed gifts upon me, lodged me in a cool apartment overlooking the inner garden, and would not permit my departure until the danger of fever had passed. He was then a man of perhaps fifty years, handsome, heavy, and indolent, with a courtesy that could charm and a vindictiveness which, when stirred, was terrible. In his affection he seemed lavish; in punishment he delighted not merely in pain, but in the anticipation of pain—the theatre, as it were, of justice.

It was during this enforced residence that I first observed Neranya.

He held no distinguished office. He carried messages, kept certain doors, tended the Rajah’s hunting weapons, and performed those miscellaneous services which belong less to a defined station than to personal favour. He was a short, enormously powerful man, with shoulders disproportionate to his height, long arms, a deep chest, and a head which might have served a sculptor for some malignant philosopher. His intelligence was unquestionable. He spoke little, forgot nothing, and possessed the singular faculty of remaining motionless while every other person in a room had become unconscious of him. His eyes alone betrayed activity. They were black, brilliant, and perpetually watchful.

From the first I disliked him; yet, as a physician may be interested in a disease which he most earnestly desires to cure, I found myself studying him. In those days his devotion to Daryan Singh appeared absolute. The Rajah had but to lift a finger, and Neranya sprang to obey. Once, when a tethered leopard broke its chain in the courtyard, this fellow interposed himself between the beast and his master, receiving a dreadful laceration of the shoulder before the guards killed it. I dressed the wound, and remember his contempt when I told him that the claws had missed the great vessels by less than an inch.

“What is an inch,” his look seemed to say, “where the Rajah is concerned?”

That devotion did not extend to mankind in general. Neranya was feared by the servants, hated by the guards, and detested by a misshapen dwarf named Koro, who entertained the household by mimicry. Koro’s tongue was merciless. He mocked Neranya’s crouching walk, his silence, and the excessive length of his arms; and the Rajah, amused by the contumely, did nothing to restrain him. One morning the dwarf was found behind the elephant stables with his neck twisted so completely that the chin rested between the shoulders. There had been no struggle. Neranya was seen returning from that quarter, and, when accused, neither confessed nor denied the deed.

Daryan Singh’s anger was sharpened by the loss of a favourite plaything. He summoned the household to the courtyard and pronounced sentence with the smoothness of one ordering a dish removed from table. Neranya’s right arm was to be amputated above the elbow.

I protested. Whatever the crime, I said, mutilation was an abominable substitute for justice. The Rajah listened, thanked me for my opinion, and replied that unless I performed the operation, the palace executioner would hack the limb away with a tulwar. Thus was my humanity made the instrument of his cruelty. I yielded—God forgive me!—and removed the arm with all the speed and skill at my command.

Neranya submitted without a cry. Not once did his eyes leave the Rajah, who watched from a shaded balcony. Even when I tied the arteries and folded the flaps over the bone, his expression retained that strange, dog-like fidelity which had formerly astonished me. He seemed less resentful than bewildered, as though the hand which smote him must remain sacred because it was his master’s.

The wound healed favourably. Within a month Neranya returned to his duties, awkward at first, then remarkably adroit with the left hand. But something in him had altered. His obedience persisted, though it had become rigid and mechanical; and at moments I detected in his eyes a smouldering interrogation.

The catastrophe followed soon afterward. Daryan Singh, irritated by some trifling omission, struck Neranya across the mouth before the assembled servants. The fellow staggered, stood still for an instant, and then sprang. His remaining hand closed upon the Rajah’s throat with such force that two guards could not loosen the fingers. I thrust my thumb beneath the constricting tendons and forced the grip apart. The Rajah was uninjured save for bruises; but Neranya’s last arm was condemned.

Again I pleaded, and again my pleading served only to ensure that the barbarity should be cleanly accomplished. The second amputation was made at the corresponding point. This time the man’s gaze did not rest upon his master with devotion. Hatred had entered it—hatred pure, concentrated, and almost luminous. I have seen delirium, mania, hydrophobia, and the furious despair of men condemned to death; but never have I seen any passion burn with so steady and terrible a brightness.

When he recovered, Neranya was retained within the palace. Why he did not receive his liberty I cannot say, unless Daryan Singh feared him even then, or found an agreeable piquancy in compelling the service of the being he had mutilated. A broad leather strap was buckled about Neranya’s chest, and by means of a loop attached to it he carried light objects. He learned to open doors with his teeth, to lift vessels between his chin and shoulder, and, most astonishing of all, to employ his feet as substitutes for hands.

His legs were unusually flexible, his toes long and separated, and his abdominal muscles of extraordinary strength. By lying upon his back he could raise either foot to his mouth; by bracing himself against a wall he could pick up a key, turn it, and set it down without noise. I once watched him thread a cord through a ring with his toes. The performance filled me with admiration, followed immediately by dread.

I warned the Rajah that he was nurturing an unheard-of power for mischief. Daryan Singh laughed.

“A snake without fangs,” he said, “may still practise its coil.”

Those were almost the only words spoken in my presence which remain precisely in my memory.

The Rajah’s sole unselfish affection was centred in his son, Prince Amrat, a boy of eleven. The child was gentle, fearless, and much given to wandering where he had been forbidden. He showed Neranya neither cruelty nor kindness, but that careless curiosity with which children regard deformity. More than once I saw him command the armless servant to exhibit some feat with his feet; and Neranya complied, though the terrible eyes would travel from the boy to the father with a significance that chilled me.

One evening Amrat disappeared.

The palace was searched from roof to cellar. At dawn his body was found in a disused bathing chamber, laid with ceremonial neatness upon a marble bench. I care to say little of what had been done. The throat was compressed, not cut; there were bruises about the ankles; and both arms had been severed close to the shoulders after death. No weapon lay near him, and the door had been fastened from within by a low wooden bar.

Suspicion fell first upon a wandering ascetic who had been seen near the gardens. I examined the chamber and rejected that theory. The dust upon the outer sill was undisturbed; the bar could not have been moved from without; and beneath a copper basin I discovered a narrow-bladed knife, its handle wrapped in cord. Two loops had been tied into that cord at distances exactly suited to the great and second toes.

There were other proofs. A smear upon the floor showed where a bare heel, scarred upon its outer edge, had rested in blood. Neranya possessed such a scar. Moreover, the bruises upon the dead child’s ankles were not finger-marks; they were the oval pressures of toes.

I confronted him in the presence of the Rajah. Neranya neither denied nor acknowledged his guilt. He sat upon the floor, his empty sleeves folded and pinned against his sides, while Daryan Singh bent over him with a grief so profound that for several moments it robbed him even of rage. Then the Rajah began to describe the death he intended for the murderer.

It was to be prolonged over seven days. Heat, thirst, iron, insects, and sleeplessness were to succeed one another with a fiendish ingenuity of which I had not believed him capable. At the conclusion Neranya was to be trampled by elephants, though not before every bone below the neck had been separately broken.

I opposed him with all the authority gratitude had given me. I said that if the prince’s murder were answered by deliberate torment, the palace would contain two monsters instead of one. I threatened to leave at once and report the proposed atrocity to the Residency. Whether my words touched his conscience, his pride, or merely his fear of interference, I know not. He abandoned the seven-day punishment—but the mercy I obtained was dreadful.

Neranya’s legs were broken below the knees and afterward amputated above them.

I did not perform those operations. I remained, however, beneath the same roof; and that fact has since weighed upon me more heavily than any participation of the hand. The palace surgeon did the work badly. Splinters of bone projected through the wounds, fever supervened, and I was called to save the life which the Rajah had forbidden me to defend from mutilation. I trimmed the shattered femora, ligatured the bleeding vessels, drained the suppuration, and by such care preserved Neranya for the fate prepared for him.

When at last the stumps healed, scarcely more remained than head, trunk, and the upper portions of the four limbs. He was a frightful wreck; yet his chest retained its massive breadth, his neck the thickness of a wrestler’s, and his black eyes their undiminished fire.

Daryan Singh caused an iron cage to be made and hung within the great audience hall.

Because the events of the final night cannot be understood without some knowledge of that chamber, I must describe it with precision. The hall was an oblong of nearly eighty feet, paved in polished stone and roofed by a cedar ceiling so lofty that the suspended lamps seemed to float in darkness. Along the western wall ran a gallery or balcony, twelve feet above the pavement, approached by a broad staircase which ascended from the northern end and turned once upon a square landing. The Rajah’s private apartments opened from this gallery.

In the centre of the hall stood a circular fountain. Its basin rose two feet from the floor, and from a carved lotus a slender jet descended continually with a soft, silver sound. Between the fountain and the eastern wall, suspended by four chains from an iron bracket, hung Neranya’s cage. Its bottom was six feet above the pavement. The cage itself was scarcely large enough to permit him to turn. One side opened upon hinges secured by a padlock; food and water were passed through a small aperture.

From the balcony the Rajah could look down upon his prisoner whenever he emerged from his apartments. Beneath the balcony, almost directly opposite the cage, he had placed a low travelling cot upon which, during the hottest weather, he frequently slept in the draught of the hall. Thus the architecture of the room, designed for ceremony and repose, became an engine of contumely: the master above or peacefully reclining below; the limbless enemy displayed between earth and ceiling, watched by all, approached by none.

For several weeks Neranya raved. His curses, though reported to me only in fragments, surpassed every extravagance of hatred. He cursed the Rajah’s body and the bones within it; his ancestors, his descendants, his name, his food, and the shadow cast by his house. He cursed every god who had permitted Daryan Singh to be born, every river that had given him water, every tree that had yielded him shade. He called upon the sun to scorch him, the moon to reveal him, the stars to fall upon him, and all creeping, flying, swimming, and four-footed creatures to unite in consuming his remains.

The Rajah would sit beneath this torrent with closed eyes and an expression almost serene.

Then, gradually, Neranya became silent.

This change did not reassure me. On the contrary, I believed that his mind, ceasing to expend itself in useless fury, had turned inward upon some design. But what design could be formed by a creature without hands or feet, confined in iron six feet above the floor? Escape was impossible. Attack was more impossible still.

Daryan Singh regarded his silence as resignation. He slept beneath the balcony nightly, attended only by two guards at the far doors. Neranya accepted food, drank, and lay for hours as motionless as some wrapped idol. Once I ascended a ladder to examine an irritation upon his left stump. His pulse was slow, his skin healthy, his muscles hard. As I leaned near him, his eyes moved past my shoulder to the cot below the gallery; and in their depths I saw calculation.

That glance caused me, some nights afterward, to keep watch.

I had told no one of my purpose, being unwilling, so I persuaded myself, to expose the man to further punishment upon the evidence of a look. Shortly before midnight I concealed myself behind a pierced screen near the southern end of the hall. One lamp burned upon the gallery. Its stained shade threw a dull amber radiance across the stairs, while the cage remained chiefly in shadow behind the ceaseless veil of the fountain. The Rajah slept upon his back beneath the balcony. A thin coverlet rose and fell upon his breast. The guards crouched by the distant doors, their heads lowered.

For nearly an hour nothing happened.

Then Neranya moved.

At first I perceived only a faint swaying of the cage. Presently his head bent toward his breast, caught the edge of his tunic between his teeth, and drew it upward. By repeated jerks—each so slight that the chains gave scarcely a murmur—he forced the cloth against a projecting rivet. He rubbed it to and fro. The movement lasted perhaps twenty minutes. At length the fabric tore.

He seized the rent with his teeth, twisted his body, and enlarged it. Strip after strip came away. Each he coiled beneath his chin, holding one end in his mouth while, by turning upon his side and pressing it between his body and the bars, he twisted the cloth into a cord.

I watched in amazement. Yet I did not move.

Why? I have asked myself that question through the greater part of a lifetime. I might say that I desired proof before awakening the guards; that I believed his attempt hopeless; that professional fascination held me, as a naturalist may be held by the action of some rare and dangerous animal. All these things are true. None absolves me.

When he had made a cord perhaps eight feet long, Neranya formed a loop by passing the end through a knot and tightening it with his teeth. He then rolled until his back lay against the door of the cage. Raising his thigh-stumps, he drove them beneath his trunk, arched his body, and struck the latch with the crown of his head.

The door did not yield. It was padlocked.

Again he struck, and again. I heard the blunt impact of bone against iron. Blood appeared upon his scalp. He paused, panting, then passed the loop of his cord through the bars, worked it sideways until it caught the padlock, and drew the other end between his teeth. By leaning backward with the full strength of his neck and back, he pulled the lock against the staple. Once—twice—twenty times he strained. The cloth stretched; the staple groaned; but still it held.

How could he accomplish the impossible task?

He altered his method. Turning so that the cord lay across one shoulder, he wedged his right stump between two bars and used it as a fulcrum. His body twisted; the great muscles of his chest rose like cables beneath the skin; a low sound escaped his throat. Suddenly the rusted screws of the staple tore from the wooden frame to which the iron grating had been fixed.

The door swung outward.

Neranya nearly fell, but caught the lower bar between his teeth. For several seconds he hung there, the whole weight of his body sustained by the muscles of the jaw and neck. Then, by a convulsive upward motion, he hooked one thigh-stump over the threshold and rolled back into the cage.

He rested. The blood from his forehead ran along his cheek and fell in slow drops into the fountain basin beneath. Its tiny sound was lost in the water.

His escape from the cage had become possible; descent remained a problem which appeared fatal. The pavement lay six feet below. A fall upon the head might kill him, while a fall upon the side could disable even that extraordinary trunk. He pushed the cord outward until the loop dropped over a scroll of the fountain. He tested it by pulling with his teeth, then slid head foremost through the opening.

His body emerged inch by inch. The shoulders stuck. He exhaled, compressed them, and wriggled farther. For one awful moment he hung across the iron sill, balanced at the waist; then the greater weight of his chest prevailed, and he fell.

The cord tightened. His head stopped less than a foot above the pavement, but the cloth cut deeply into his mouth. He swung, turning slowly, unable to rise and unwilling to release his hold. By arching backward he brought one stump against the fountain rim. It slipped. He tried again, braced himself, relaxed the cord, and dropped sideways upon the floor.

The fall was not loud. The Rajah stirred but did not awaken.

Neranya lay motionless. I believed him stunned. Presently, however, he lifted his head and looked toward the doors, then toward the staircase.

I understood—or thought I understood. He sought freedom. If he could reach the stairs, ascend to the gallery, and enter one of the apartments, he might conceal himself until some door was opened at dawn. The conception was mad; yet all that I had witnessed was madness made practicable by will.

He began to cross the hall.

No description can communicate the horror of that progress. He placed his chin upon the polished floor, thrust with the stumps of his thighs, contracted the abdomen, and dragged the chest forward. Then he rolled slightly, advanced one shoulder, pressed his cheek downward, and repeated the movement. Each effort gained him three or four inches. Sweat streamed from him. The torn remnants of his garment left dark streaks upon the stone.

The fountain concealed him from the guards during the first part of his journey. Beyond it he entered the open space where the amber light touched the floor. There he halted whenever one of the sleepers moved. Once a guard lifted his head. Neranya flattened his face against the pavement and became so still that he seemed a discarded bundle of rags.

The guard slept again.

Foot by foot the wretch approached the staircase. When he reached the lowest step, I expected him at last to fail. The riser was seven inches high—a wall to one who possessed neither hand nor foot. He raised his chin upon the edge, bit the carpet runner, and pulled. The fabric slipped between his teeth. He released it, turned, and drove both thigh-stumps against the pavement. His head and shoulders rose; his breast struck the step; he slid back.

Again he attempted it. Again he failed.

At the fourth effort he caught the carved lip of the tread in his teeth. His neck swelled; the shoulders lifted; the trunk bent until I feared the spine would break. He gained the step and lay across it, breathing in silent gasps.

There were twenty-three steps to the landing.

I counted them.

Upon some he mounted by the carpet; upon others by hooking his chin over the edge; twice he turned and ascended backward, forcing his stumps against the riser and writhing upward through sheer contraction of the back. Each elevation seemed the last that flesh could endure. Each was overcome.

At the landing he rested for so long that I believed his strength exhausted. Then he looked over the balustrade.

Below him slept Daryan Singh.

The truth came to me—not wholly, but as a flash that shows the precipice without revealing its depth. Neranya had no thought of liberty. The gallery doors were guarded from within; he must have known it. The staircase, the height, the position of the cot—these alone concerned him.

For what purpose?

He began the second flight.

I stepped from behind the screen. Even then I did not call out. Some dreadful compulsion urged me to see whether the superhuman monster could complete his design. I followed at a distance, placing each foot carefully, while he mounted with a slowness that was more appalling than haste.

When he gained the gallery, he turned not toward the apartments but toward the balustrade directly above the cot. There was a gap between two carved pillars wide enough to admit his body. He thrust his head through, surveyed the sleeping Rajah beneath, and smiled.

That smile broke the spell which had held me.

I shouted.

Daryan Singh opened his eyes. The guards sprang up. Neranya, hearing my cry, made one final and prodigious movement. He braced his stumps against the gallery floor, drove his shoulders between the pillars, and launched himself outward.

He fell twelve feet.

His chest and abdomen struck the Rajah squarely upon the breast. I heard the ribs break—a dull succession of cracks like sticks beneath a wheel. The cot collapsed, and both bodies rolled to the pavement. Before I could reach them, Neranya had fastened his teeth in the Rajah’s throat.

The guards tried to drag him away, but there were no limbs by which to seize him. His jaws had closed below the angle of the mandible, crushing the windpipe and tearing the great vessels. I thrust my fingers between his teeth and found them locked with the rigidity of iron. One guard struck him upon the skull. Another drew a sword, which I knocked aside lest he sever the Rajah’s head.

At last, by forcing a metal scabbard between the molars and using it as a lever, I broke the bite. Blood covered us all.

Daryan Singh was beyond aid. The sternum had been driven inward; several ribs had pierced the lungs; the throat was destroyed. He breathed once beneath my hand and died.

Neranya lived perhaps two minutes longer. The fall had fractured his spine and ruptured organs within the abdomen. He could not speak, nor do I think he desired to. His eyes, already clouding, turned from me to the dead Rajah. The hatred had vanished from them. In its place was peace.

Then that shattered remnant of a man smiled—gently, almost sweetly—and died.

I have often been praised for preventing Daryan Singh from torturing Neranya to death. I have never accepted the praise. I saved the murderer that vengeance might ripen; I repaired the body which hatred afterward employed; and when warning might yet have averted the final horror, I stood concealed and watched ingenuity contend with the impossible.

Yet I cannot remember Neranya only as a fiend. His crime against the child was atrocious, his revenge murderous, his nature violent from the beginning; but the Rajah, in taking limb after limb while preserving life, believed that he had extinguished agency itself. He forgot that the will has no fixed habitation in hand or foot, and that a man deprived of every instrument may convert his own ruined body into the last and most terrible of weapons.

The cage remained for years above the fountain. Long afterward, when the palace had passed into other hands, I saw it still hanging there, empty and rusted, while the water fell with its old soft murmur into the marble basin. I looked from it to the balcony, from the balcony to the place where the cot had stood, and seemed again to hear the measured dragging upon the floor, the strain at each stair, the snapping of bone, and the final dreadful silence.

But clearest of all, even after this lapse of years, I remember Neranya’s smile.

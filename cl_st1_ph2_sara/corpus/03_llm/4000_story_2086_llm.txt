### The Green Leaf

Rain had been falling since before the lamps were lit, a narrow, persistent rain which passed between the roofs and polished the black street. It gathered upon the iron railings outside Number Seventeen and slipped down them in drops. Above the doorway a placard, loosened at one corner, flapped whenever the wind entered the street:

**VOTE FOR RICHARD TIERNEY  
THE NATIONAL CANDIDATE**

The word *National* had been printed in green. Rain had drawn a pale tail from the last letter.

In the committee room old Molloy sat over the grate, holding a sheet of brown paper before the fire. The room had once been a shop parlour. A counter remained along one wall, and behind it stood empty shelves upon which election bills had been folded in bundles. Two candles in saucers burned on the table, each candle bent a little by the draught. Their light showed a ceiling stained like an old map and a row of damp overcoats hanging from nails.

The fire was made of slack and pieces of a broken fruit box. Whenever Molloy lifted the paper, the flame sank and a smell of wet soot came out into the room.

He wore an ivy leaf in the buttonhole of his rusty coat. His grey moustache was yellow at the ends and his eyelids had the pink, polished look of meat kept too long in water. He bent nearer to the grate and said:

—You’ll not do it, is it?

The fire gave no answer. He waved the paper again.

A thin man in a soft hat stood near the door, rubbing his hands beneath his armpits. His name was Behan, though the others called him the Citizen because he had once spoken for nearly an hour at a meeting where no resolution was carried.

—Give it a poke, said Behan.

—With what?

—The poker.

—There’s no poker.

—There was a poker yesterday.

—There was a shilling on the mantelpiece yesterday too.

Behan looked at the bare mantelpiece.

—That’s politics for you, he said.

Molloy folded the paper lengthwise and inserted it under the grate. A little blue flame travelled along the edge, turned yellow and then vanished.

—A magnificent fire, said Behan. Fit for the Mansion House.

—Fit for Tierney, said Molloy.

He sat back on his heels and began to chew. He had nothing in his mouth, but his jaws moved regularly, as though some small machine had been put inside his cheeks and forgotten there.

Outside, a cart passed, sending a fan of water against the kerb. The hoofs grew faint. Then the rain could be heard again, ticking on the window and dripping from the broken gutter above the door.

Behan removed his hat. His hair lay flat on his skull in separate wet lines.

—What time did he say?

—Six.

—And what time is it now?

Molloy drew a watch from his waistcoat. The watch had stopped.

—A quarter after, he said.

—A quarter after what?

—Whatever hour you like.

Behan walked to the table and examined one of the candles.

—Did he leave the money?

—He left word.

—Word is grand nourishment.

—You can’t spend it, though.

—You can in an election.

Molloy turned his head slowly and looked at him.

—You were up around Dorset Street?

—I was.

—How many houses?

—Every house assigned to me.

—And how many promises?

—Enough.

—Enough for what?

—Enough to satisfy any reasonable man.

—There’s no reasonable men in this ward, said Molloy.

Behan smiled without showing his teeth. He found a cigarette paper in his pocket, smoothed it upon the back of his hand and shook into it the remains of tobacco from a twist. The tobacco was nearly black and clung together in damp threads.

—They’ll vote for Tierney, he said. They may curse him from the hall door, but they’ll vote for him.

—Why?

—Because they know him.

—That’s a queer reason.

—It’s the best reason in Irish politics. A man you know can only rob you in the usual way.

Molloy gave a small grunt. The flame stirred in the grate and disclosed a wet lump of coal.

The door opened and a gust entered with Mr Henchy.

He came in sideways, protecting his hat with one hand and carrying beneath his arm a bundle of handbills. He was broad in the chest and narrow in the legs. Rain shone on his cheeks. His sandy moustache had collected a row of bright drops, and when he spoke the drops trembled but did not fall.

—God save all here, he said.

—He’d need to, said Behan.

Henchy flung the handbills upon the counter.

—There’s patriotism for you! he said. Two hundred of them left and not a dry one among them.

He pulled off his coat and shook it. Molloy held up a hand.

—Not over the fire, man.

—Fire? Is that what you call it?

—It was a fire before you came in.

Henchy hung the coat upon a nail. The nail bent, making the coat descend solemnly until its hem rested on the floor.

—Has he been?

—Who?

—Our noble standard-bearer.

—No.

—Has he sent?

—Word.

—Word! Henchy cried. There’s that word again. I’ve a wife at home and two children, and am I to bring them word? “Here, children, is six shillings’ worth of your father’s word. Take it gently now and don’t swallow it all together.”

He approached the grate and stretched his palms toward it.

—That fellow would skin a louse for the hide and grease, he said.

—You were praising him yesterday, said Molloy.

—I praised the cause.

—You praised his speech.

—I praised the parts of it that weren’t his.

Behan lit his cigarette from a candle. He drew too quickly and the paper flared. He shook it out.

—Tierney’s not the worst, he said.

—Not the worst! said Henchy. He promised us a sovereign each for the polling and ten shillings for the week. Where’s the ten shillings? Tell me that.

—You received five.

—Five is not ten.

—It’s on the road to it.

—It’s travelling backwards.

Henchy sat upon the edge of the counter. His boots were covered with pale mud and one trouser leg was turned up higher than the other.

—I called at headquarters, he said. The clerk was there with his cuffs on and a pen behind his ear. A very fine fellow. “Mr Tierney is in consultation,” says he. “Consultation with whom?” says I. “With important supporters.” “And are the supporters supporting him with money?” says I. “Because he’s not supporting us with any.”

—What did the clerk say? asked Molloy.

—He looked at me.

—That was cheap enough.

—He looked at me as if I’d come to borrow his mother. “Mr Tierney’s engagements are considerable,” says he. “So are mine,” says I. “My butcher has an engagement with me every Saturday night.”

Behan laughed, breathing smoke through his nostrils.

—Did you get anything?

—I got civility. A whole hatful of it.

The street door opened below and closed with a bang. Feet ascended the stairs slowly. All three men became silent. Henchy slid from the counter and put his hand through his damp hair. Molloy stood up. Only Behan continued smoking, though his eyes moved toward the door.

A young man entered. He was tall and heavy in the face, with smooth cheeks and calm brown eyes. His hat was too small for him and sat upon the crown of his head like an object placed there temporarily. He carried a folded newspaper.

—Good evening, gentlemen, he said.

—Good evening, Mr Hynes, said Molloy.

Joe Hynes nodded to each of them. He placed the newspaper on the table but kept one gloved hand upon it.

—Bad night, he said.

—Grand day for the country, said Henchy.

Hynes looked at him.

—How is the canvass going?

—Like a funeral in a flood.

—That bad?

—Worse. At a funeral you know who’s dead.

Hynes smiled. He had a broad, patient smile which seemed to arrive upon his face before he had decided to use it.

—I hear Tierney has the publicans with him, he said.

—He has everybody with him except the fellows working for him, said Henchy.

—Ah, you’ll be paid.

—Who told you?

—No one.

—Then your information is as good as ours.

Hynes moved nearer the fire. He wore a green ivy leaf, darkened by rain, beneath the lapel of his coat. Molloy saw it and touched his own leaf.

—You remembered the day, Joe, he said.

—I did.

Henchy watched them.

—There’s not many remembers, he said.

—There’s some, said Hynes.

—And some that remembers when it suits them.

No one answered. Hynes opened his coat to the weak warmth. On his boots the wet made tiny beads among the grains of mud.

Behan sat at the table and began to tear narrow strips from a spoiled handbill.

—Were you out for Colgan today, Joe? Henchy asked.

Hynes turned his head.

—I was out on my own business.

—There’s a deal of private business abroad at election time.

—There is.

—They say Colgan is doing well.

—Do they?

—They do.

—Who are they?

Henchy slapped his hands upon his knees.

—Ah, now, Joe, don’t be putting the law on me. I merely heard it.

—You’ll hear anything in this city if you stand long enough at a corner.

—And you’ll see anything if you look through the right window.

The candle nearest Hynes guttered. He glanced at Henchy and then at Molloy.

—I called in to see how you were getting on, he said.

—We’re getting cold, said Molloy.

—And patriotic, said Behan.

Henchy lowered his eyes and scraped mud from one boot with the heel of the other. His manner became friendly.

—Colgan’s a decent man, Joe. No one denies it.

—You were denying it last Tuesday.

—I denied he had a chance. That’s another thing.

—He has labour behind him.

—Labour behind him! Henchy said. Labour’s always behind somebody. That’s why it never gets in front.

Behan laughed and struck the table.

—That’s well said.

—It may be well said, Hynes answered, but a workman has as much right to sit in the council as a solicitor or a publican.

—Every right in the world, said Henchy. I’m a labour man myself.

—Since when? asked Molloy.

—Since birth. Wasn’t I born through labour?

Hynes smiled again, but less patiently.

—Colgan works for his living.

—So does Tierney, said Henchy.

—At what?

—At getting elected.

The others laughed. Henchy looked round with a satisfied expression and crossed his legs.

—My private and candid opinion is this, he said. The working man ought to be represented by the working man. No question. But what happens when you put a working man into a fine chamber with a carpet under him and a silver chain round the chairman? He remembers he has a Sunday coat. In six months he’s talking about municipal undertakings, and in twelve he won’t know the price of a loaf.

—You judge everyone by yourself, said Hynes.

—I judge human nature.

—You give it poor marks.

—It has earned them.

Molloy crouched and pushed a folded handbill beneath the coal with his fingers. A green corner bearing Tierney’s name caught fire. For an instant the grate brightened, and the printed face of the candidate blackened inward from the cheeks. Then the paper curled and fell.

—There he goes, said Behan.

Hynes looked toward the window.

—They say Tierney is willing to vote an address to the king, he said.

Henchy’s leg ceased swinging.

—Who says?

—It’s in the *Evening Telegraph*.

He tapped the newspaper.

—Newspapers, said Henchy. Sure they’d print a man’s death before breakfast and his denial before dinner.

—Will he vote for it?

—He’ll vote according to the party.

—That means yes.

—It means according to the party.

Hynes buttoned his coat.

—A Nationalist candidate welcoming the King of England.

—King Edward isn’t Queen Victoria, Henchy said.

—No. He’s her son.

—He’s supposed to be a good sort.

—By whom?

—By those that know him.

—Do you know him?

—Not personally.

—Then your information is second-hand again.

Henchy leaned forward, his moist moustache projecting.

—Now listen, Joe. Sentiment is sentiment and business is business. If the king comes here, money comes with him. Hotels filled, carmen employed, flags sold, drink taken. Would you turn away trade because you dislike the crown on the coin?

—I’d turn away the crown.

—And keep the coin?

—If it was ours.

—Musha, there you are! said Henchy. The old grand gesture. Throw the dinner into the street because the plate has a harp on it drawn by an Englishman.

Hynes picked up his newspaper.

—You’ve changed since last year.

—A man who never changes hasn’t learned anything.

—Or remembered anything.

The room was silent. The rain struck the glass more sharply. One of the overcoats slid from its nail and fell in a heap. Behan looked at it without moving.

Hynes said good evening and went out.

They listened while his steps descended. The street door opened, admitting the noise of a passing tram, and closed again.

Henchy waited several moments.

—What did I tell you? he said.

—You told us several things, said Molloy.

—He’s with Colgan.

—Maybe.

—There’s no maybe about it. He came in to spy out the land.

—There’s not much land here, said Behan.

—He wanted to know our returns. Did you see the way he kept his hand on that paper? A reporter’s trick. He’ll have every word of it down.

—Including yours about the king, said Molloy.

Henchy’s face altered.

—There was nothing disloyal in what I said.

—No?

—Nothing that could be twisted by an honest man.

—Then you’re safe enough in a newspaper.

Behan lifted a strip of paper and held it over the candle until it smoked.

—Joe was always a Parnellite, he said.

—So were we all, said Henchy.

—Were you?

—Was I? I stood in the rain outside the Rotunda the night—

—You were a Conservative then, Molloy said.

Henchy looked offended.

—I was never a Conservative.

—Your father was.

—A man’s not responsible for his father’s politics.

—You wore a blue ribbon at the election.

—That was for a temperance society.

Behan began to cough. He covered his mouth with his fist and bent over the table, his shoulders shaking. When he recovered he said:

—A blue ribbon for temperance! That’s good.

—It is the truth, said Henchy. There was drink destroying the country then as now.

—And did you give it up?

—I gave up the society.

The door opened again before the laughter had ended. A small, elderly man entered with an umbrella held horizontally before him. His face was long and colourless, with a large white moustache brushed out upon either side. Behind him came a thickset man in a hard hat, whose pale eyebrows and heavy, innocent eyes gave him the appearance of a young ox dressed for church.

—Mr Lyons, said Molloy. Mr Crofton.

Mr Lyons closed the umbrella with care.

—An atrocious evening, gentlemen.

—Election weather, said Henchy.

—I don’t remember a wetter October.

—The country’s weeping.

—It has cause, said Mr Crofton.

These were his first words. He said them in a deep voice and then became silent, partly because he had nothing ready to add and partly because he believed quick conversation to be a habit of inferior men.

Mr Lyons hung his umbrella from the counter. Water ran from its point and made a dark pool on the floor.

—Any message from Mr Tierney? he asked.

—Plenty of messages, said Henchy.

—Regarding remuneration?

—That message hasn’t arrived.

Mr Lyons compressed his lips.

—This is hardly satisfactory.

—Hardly would be putting a silk hat on it.

—We have rendered services.

—We have rendered ourselves penniless.

Mr Crofton removed one glove finger by finger. He had formerly worked for a Unionist candidate, but the death of that gentleman and the unwillingness of the local association to pay its accounts had moderated his convictions. He now assisted Tierney without admitting that assistance involved agreement.

—Tierney will pay, he said.

Henchy pointed at him.

—There’s a man of faith.

—He gave his undertaking.

—He gave me the same thing. I tried to pawn it.

Mr Lyons drew a chair to the table and sat down. His trousers were carefully creased, though rain had darkened them to the knees. He surveyed the room with an expression which suggested that he had been invited under false pretences.

—The arrangements here are exceedingly poor, he said.

—The arrangements are national, said Behan.

—There should at least be a proper fire.

—There should at least be wages, said Molloy.

—And refreshment, Henchy added. A candidate who sends men through rain from morning till night ought to provide a bottle of stout.

—Two, said Behan.

—One each.

—That’s more like it.

Mr Lyons looked toward Mr Crofton.

—I believe in moderation.

—So do I, said Henchy. Moderate thirst and plenty to moderate it with.

Mr Crofton sat down heavily. The chair gave a crack but endured.

—How did you find the Royal Terrace district? Mr Lyons asked him.

—Uncertain.

—Hostile?

—Reserved.

—Reserved means hostile behind lace curtains, said Henchy.

—Several expressed dissatisfaction with the corporation, Mr Crofton continued.

—That’s Tierney’s strength. Everyone dissatisfied with the corporation votes to put him into it.

—One householder spoke in favour of Colgan.

—A labourer, I suppose?

—A stationer.

—That’s worse. A man surrounded by paper gets ideas.

Mr Lyons warmed his gloves over the grate.

—Colgan’s programme is quite impracticable. Municipal workshops, fair contracts, direct employment. Where is the capital to come from?

—From the same place the profits go, said Behan.

Mr Lyons glanced at him.

—That is a phrase, not an answer.

—Most answers begin as phrases.

—And end as taxes, said Henchy.

Molloy rose and went to the window. Through the glass he could see the lamp opposite, surrounded by a yellow mist. A boy ran beneath it with newspapers under his coat. Two women passed under one umbrella, their skirts lifted from the mud.

—There’s not much movement, he said.

—They’ll come out after chapel, said Mr Lyons.

—Chapel’s over.

—After the public houses, then.

—They’ll come out for them, said Henchy.

He slapped his pockets as if searching for tobacco. Each pocket produced a separate piece of paper, a key without a bow and a cork. At last Behan pushed the black twist toward him.

—That stuff would poison a rat.

—Have a fill.

Henchy accepted it. Mr Lyons watched him roll a cigarette and said:

—What is the latest estimate?

—Tierney by three hundred, said Henchy.

—Two hundred, said Molloy.

—Colgan by fifty, said Behan.

Henchy looked at him sharply.

—You were saying Tierney was certain.

—I’ve reconsidered.

—Since Joe Hynes came in?

—Since the fire went out.

All eyes turned to the grate. The last redness had withdrawn into the slack. Molloy took a handbill, crumpled it and placed it between the bars.

—Don’t burn too many votes, said Henchy.

—They’re the only ones we can count.

A knock sounded below, followed by a shout. Then came the noise of bottles touching one another. Henchy stood so quickly that his chair overturned.

—What’s that?

Footsteps climbed the stairs, quick and uneven. A freckled boy appeared carrying a wooden crate against his chest.

—From Mr Tierney, he said.

Every face turned toward the crate.

—What is it? Henchy asked, though the black bottles were visible.

—Stout.

—How many?

—Twelve.

—Did he send anything else?

The boy looked blank.

—Any envelope?

—No.

—Any message?

—He said to say he’d be round later.

Henchy took the crate from him with both hands.

—Did he now? That’s the style. Put it here, son. Easy. Easy with it.

The boy wiped his nose on his sleeve.

—Am I to bring back the empties?

—If the country survives.

Molloy found a penny. Mr Lyons found a halfpenny after a prolonged search. The boy received both without gratitude and went away.

Henchy set the bottles along the counter in two exact ranks.

—Now, gentlemen, he said, you see the manner of man we’re dealing with.

Behan looked at him.

—A minute ago he’d skin a louse.

—Ah, I spoke in irritation. A man says things when he’s wet through.

—Your clothes are drying fast.

Henchy selected a bottle and held it toward the candle.

—He doesn’t forget his workers. That’s the point. He’s delayed, no doubt, by business of the highest importance, but he remembers us.

—He forgot the opener, said Molloy.

They searched the shelves and drawers. No opener was found. Behan tried the edge of the counter, striking the bottle cap against it until Mr Lyons protested that he would damage the furniture.

—Furniture! said Behan. This counter was dead before we came.

Molloy found a corkscrew with one arm broken. The bottles were corked beneath their caps. Henchy prised the first cap off with the counter edge, then pushed the cork inward a little and placed the bottle near the dead grate.

—Warm them, he said.

—Warm stout?

—It loosens the cork. Scientific.

Five bottles were arranged before the bars. Their black glass reflected the candles. The men stood round, watching with an attention not previously given to the election.

A cork rose slowly from one neck.

Pok!

It struck the underside of the grate and fell among the ashes.

—There’s the first result declared! Henchy cried.

Behan caught the bottle before it foamed over.

Pok!

A second cork leaped across the floor and vanished beneath Mr Crofton’s chair. Mr Crofton moved his boots apart with dignity.

—Mind your trousers, said Molloy.

Pok!

Mr Lyons gave a start.

—Really, he said.

Henchy laughed.

—Artillery for the national cause!

They poured the stout into cloudy tumblers, two of which had to be rinsed with a little of the stout itself. Mr Lyons wiped his glass with a handkerchief before permitting it to be filled. Mr Crofton accepted his without comment. The weak fire, encouraged by brown paper and the warmth of the bottles, produced a small orange tongue which laid a soft colour upon the men’s faces.

Henchy raised his tumbler.

—To Richard Tierney, he said. A sound man and a coming man.

Molloy looked at him over the rim.

—Coming later.

—No sneering. He’s treated us handsomely.

—With our own wages, perhaps.

—Drink your stout and don’t philosophise.

They drank. The first swallow altered the room. It remained bare and cold, but the cold became a subject for laughter rather than complaint. Henchy wiped the foam from his moustache and praised the quality of the porter. Behan said it had a smoky taste. Mr Lyons said the flavour was acceptable. Mr Crofton emptied half his glass in one pull and placed it down gently.

—A capable candidate, he said.

—There you are, said Henchy. Crofton knows men. He has mixed in high circles.

Mr Crofton inclined his heavy head.

—Tierney has administrative qualities.

—And generosity.

—Within reason.

—Exactly. No vulgar display. Quiet generosity. That’s the best kind.

Molloy filled the glasses again.

—You’ll have him canonised before the crate’s empty.

—Give credit where credit is due. My private and candid opinion is that Tierney is one of the few public men in Dublin with a practical head.

—You said he stole his speech.

—All good speeches are stolen. That’s how you know they’re good.

The stout travelled round. The candles burned more steadily now that the door remained closed. Wet coats gave off a woollen smell, mixed with tobacco smoke and the sweet bitterness of drink.

Mr Lyons unfolded the newspaper left by Hynes, but it was an old evening edition.

—There’s an item here concerning the proposed royal visit, he said.

Henchy leaned back.

—Now we’ll hear treason printed respectably.

Mr Lyons read:

—“It is understood that, should His Majesty honour the Irish capital with his presence next spring, the municipal authorities will take appropriate steps—”

—Appropriate steps, said Behan. Two forward and six back.

—“—to assure the sovereign of the loyalty and goodwill of the citizens.”

Molloy made a sound in his throat.

—Not this citizen.

—Nor me, said Behan.

Henchy drank and considered.

—There’s two ways of looking at it.

—There were three when Joe was here, said Molloy.

—Let the man finish.

Mr Lyons continued:

—“The occasion, it is anticipated, would contribute materially to the commercial life of the city.”

—There, said Henchy. Materially. That’s the word. We can’t live entirely on memories.

—Some men live on promises, said Behan.

—And some on phrases about freedom. I ask you plainly: if the king comes and spends money, who suffers?

—Ireland, said Molloy.

—How does Ireland suffer if a London lord pays four shillings for a room worth one?

—By bowing for it.

—The hotelkeeper can bow. That’s included in the four shillings.

Mr Lyons folded the paper.

—I am no admirer of British rule, he said, but courtesy to the person of the sovereign need not imply political submission.

—Well spoken, said Henchy.

—He represents the system, Molloy said.

—A man represents many things, Mr Lyons answered. Mr Crofton here formerly represented the Conservative interest.

Mr Crofton’s face remained composed.

—I was employed by it.

—And now you’re employed by the National interest, said Behan.

—I am assisting Mr Tierney.

—That’s a narrow bridge, Henchy said, but he crossed it dry.

Mr Crofton looked at him with his mild bovine eyes.

—Political judgment develops.

—There you have it again, said Henchy. Development. We’re all developing.

—Into what? asked Molloy.

No one answered at once. The fire settled with a sigh. Beyond the window a bell rang the hour, each stroke made thin by rain.

Mr Lyons touched the ivy leaf in Molloy’s buttonhole.

—It is the anniversary, of course.

—It is, said Molloy.

Henchy looked down. There was no leaf in his coat.

—I had one, he said. It must have dropped off in the rain.

—They do, said Behan.

—It was pinned here. A fine leaf.

—The faithful leaf, Molloy said.

Henchy did not answer. He poured the remainder of a bottle into his glass and, finding it too much, distributed some to the others.

—Parnell was a great man, he said after a while.

—The greatest we had, said Molloy.

—He had command, Mr Lyons said. Whatever one thinks of his later errors, he had command.

—His errors! Behan said. What errors?

Mr Lyons raised a pale hand.

—I speak historically.

—History’s the name given to a knife after the blood is wiped off it.

—Very striking, said Henchy. Did you make that yourself?

—No.

—I thought not.

Mr Crofton spoke from his chair:

—Parnell understood discipline.

—He understood England, Molloy said. And Ireland didn’t understand him.

—Ireland understood him well enough to betray him, said Behan.

The word remained among them. Henchy stared into his stout. Mr Lyons adjusted the crease of his trousers with two fingers.

—The clergy had influence, he said cautiously.

—Influence! Behan cried. They rang the bell and the sheep came home.

—There were moral considerations.

—There’s always moral considerations when a great man has to be pulled down.

—He was not without fault.

—Who asked him to be without fault?

Henchy raised his hand.

—Now, now. No theology. We’re all Parnell’s men here.

Behan turned on him.

—And welcoming the king.

—That has nothing to do with Parnell.

—It has everything to do with him.

—Parnell himself dealt with Englishmen.

—He dealt with them; he didn’t decorate the streets for them.

—If he were alive, said Henchy, he’d take a practical view.

Molloy looked at him.

—If he were alive, half the practical men in Ireland would be under the table.

A low laugh came from Mr Crofton. It surprised the others and perhaps surprised him, for his face resumed its solemn expression immediately.

The door opened. Joe Hynes stood there again, rain upon his shoulders.

—Come in, Joe! Henchy called. You’re just in time.

Hynes remained at the threshold.

—I left my paper.

—We found it.

Henchy lifted a bottle.

—And we found better news. A drink?

Hynes hesitated. Molloy drew a chair out with his foot.

—Come in out of that.

Hynes closed the door and removed his coat. Beneath it he wore an old black suit, carefully brushed. The ivy leaf upon his lapel had begun to curl at the edges.

—No hard feelings, Joe, said Henchy.

—About what?

—Politics.

—I wasn’t aware of any feelings.

—Good man. Sit down.

A fresh bottle was warmed. They watched the cork.

Pok!

Hynes caught it in one hand.

—A marksman, said Behan.

Stout was poured for him. He drank a little and set down the tumbler.

—We were speaking of the Chief, Molloy said.

—I heard you on the landing.

—And of betrayal, Behan added.

Hynes looked around the room. The smoke had thickened, giving the candle flames red borders. The men’s faces floated above dark coats: Molloy’s moist eyes and working mouth, Henchy’s bright cheeks, Lyons’s white divided moustache, Crofton’s broad pallor, Behan’s narrow, smoke-stained forehead. On the counter the bottles stood like a row of black spectators.

—I wrote a few lines about him, Hynes said.

—About Parnell? asked Molloy.

—For the day.

Henchy struck the table.

—Give them to us, Joe.

—They’re nothing much.

—All the better. We’re no critics here.

Mr Lyons straightened.

—I should be interested to hear them.

Hynes put a hand inside his coat and drew out a folded sheet. It had been folded several times and the outer side was softened by damp. He opened it upon his knee.

—It’s called “The Uncrowned Captain,” he said.

—Silence for the poet, Henchy announced.

Behan extinguished his cigarette beneath his boot. Molloy leaned forward, elbows upon knees. Even Mr Crofton adjusted himself to listen.

Hynes looked once at the paper and then began. His voice at first was low and conversational, but after the opening lines it rose, finding a public rhythm:

> O Erin, in your rain-washed night,  
> Why mourns the western sea?  
> What shadow walks by hill and height,  
> What voice demands to be?  
> No crowned invader calls your name,  
> No lord with sword in hand,  
> But he who bore your secret flame  
> And taught your heart to stand.
>
> He came not robed in borrowed gold,  
> Nor blessed by foreign throne;  
> His eyes were stern, his speech was cold,  
> His purpose was his own.  
> Before the lion’s guarded gate  
> He neither bent nor smiled;  
> He held the patience of our fate,  
> The anger of our isle.
>
> They heard him where the strangers sit,  
> Beneath the painted crown;  
> He spoke, and every lamp was lit  
> In cabin, field and town.  
> The poor man raised his winter head,  
> The prisoner dreamed of day,  
> And Ireland, numbered with the dead,  
> Remembered how to pray.
>
> But not by England’s hand alone  
> Our captain met his doom;  
> The bitter seed by Irishmen sown  
> Bore treason into bloom.  
> The lips that praised him turned to blame,  
> The hands he lifted fell;  
> The priests pronounced a righteous shame,  
> The people tolled the bell.
>
> O darker than the foreign chain,  
> O sharper than the spear,  
> The friend who sells a leader’s pain,  
> The coward’s loyal tear!  
> One kissed his cheek with scripture breath,  
> One swore the cause was pure,  
> Then left him to the public death  
> Alone and still secure.
>
> He passed beneath an autumn sky,  
> No king, yet kinglier far;  
> The leaves were green where he must lie,  
> The clouds concealed his star.  
> And every year the cold winds grieve  
> Along the city wall,  
> While faithful hearts an ivy leaf  
> Wear for the chief of all.
>
> Sleep, captain, till the sleepers wake,  
> Till Ireland’s shame is past;  
> Though little men your banner take  
> And sell its cloth at last,  
> The wind that moves above your grave  
> Still troubles tower and throne;  
> A nation may betray the brave,  
> But cannot make them none.
>
> And if some dawn our children stand  
> Where free men name the free,  
> They’ll know who first, with empty hand,  
> Defied the guarded sea.  
> No royal trumpet then shall sound,  
> No subject knee shall fall;  
> Your name shall be the only crown,  
> Your truth the feast of all.

During the last verse Hynes no longer looked at the paper. His broad face had reddened and his calm eyes shone. The rain continued behind his voice, but more softly now, as if it had withdrawn to the far end of the street.

When he finished, he lowered the page and folded it once.

No one spoke.

Then Henchy brought his palm down upon the table.

—Bravo, Joe! he said.

Molloy clapped slowly. Behan joined him. Mr Lyons tapped his fingertips together in dignified approval, and Mr Crofton, after a moment, struck one great hand against the other three times. The noise filled the little room and made the bottles tremble.

—That’s poetry, said Henchy. By God, that’s the true thing.

—Very spirited, Mr Lyons said. Very affecting.

—The line about the little men, said Behan.

Henchy turned to him.

—What about it?

—Nothing. It’s a good line.

Molloy’s eyelids were wetter than before. He rubbed one with the knuckle of his forefinger.

—You had it right about the leaves, Joe, he said.

Hynes folded the paper smaller and returned it to his pocket.

—It might be improved.

—Don’t touch it, Henchy said. Not a syllable. “Your name shall be the only crown.” That’s a line for you. There’s some fellows writing in the papers wouldn’t produce it if you left them locked in a room for a week with a dictionary.

—Would you print it? Mr Lyons asked.

—Perhaps.

—You ought, said Molloy.

—You must, said Henchy. The country wants reminding.

Behan looked at the placards piled upon the shelves.

—The country gets a deal of reminding.

Henchy ignored him. He filled Hynes’s glass and then the others. The last bottle gave less than a full round, and he tilted it over each tumbler in turn until no drop appeared.

—To the memory, he said.

They stood, except Mr Crofton, who attempted to stand, struck his knee against the table and remained half-raised. Henchy held his glass before him. His sandy moustache was damp with stout.

—To Charles Stewart Parnell, he said.

—Parnell, the others answered.

They drank.

The fire had sunk again. A charred corner of Tierney’s bill showed briefly in the grate and turned to ash. One candle had burned down into its saucer, where the grease lay in a white pool. Its flame leaned toward the men and recovered.

Outside, election cars passed at the end of the street, their drivers calling names made shapeless by distance and rain. Somewhere a cheer arose and broke apart. The men listened without asking for whom it had been given.

Molloy sat with his empty glass between his hands. Behan stared at the table. Mr Lyons stroked one side of his moustache and then the other. Mr Crofton contemplated the wet crown of his hat. Henchy’s lips moved as if repeating a line from the poem, though no sound came.

Hynes looked toward the window. His ivy leaf cast a small, crooked shadow upon his shirt.

For a little while no one spoke of wages, or the king, or the votes still to be brought from the public houses. The room held the warmth the stout had given it, but only faintly. Under the door a thin current of cold air entered and spread along the floor, touching one pair of wet boots after another.

By seven-thirty the cardboard jail had begun to sag.

This was unfortunate because the cardboard jail was the visual centerpiece of Unlocking Hope!, the Rotary Auxiliary’s annual charity gala benefiting Youth Choices, pediatric mobility services, and, for reasons never clearly explained, the replacement of the library’s west gutters. The jail had bars made from paper-towel tubes painted silver and a sign reading BAD DECISONS CAN IMPRISON U. Local Celebrities would parade past it in themed outfits, then be auctioned for “service experiences.” Dinner with a dentist. Golf with a realtor. A ride-along with a pharmacist who owned a restored military Jeep.

Al Roosten, proprietor of Roosten’s Antiques & Distinctive Finds, was offering a Personal Heritage Consultation and Lunch.

His outfit was Robin Hood.

Not the good Robin Hood. Not the rugged forest fellow. A kind of green felt assistant-manager elf with plastic boot covers and a cap that kept tipping left because the feather was too heavy. Earlier he had asked Deena from Youth Choices if he could remove the feather.

“The feather reads really well,” she said.

From where? From outer space?

Now he waited behind a paper screen with the other businessmen while the MC, Lenny Dace, explained into a squealing microphone that the community had “literally assembled in one room tonight.” The businessmen shifted and checked their phones. Hal Brister of Brister Chevrolet wore a cowboy coat with fringe. Dr. Wimsatt, orthodontist, wore a white naval uniform. Dale Donfrey, owner of Donfrey Flooring, wore a tuxedo with red sneakers and somehow looked natural, as if tuxedos with red sneakers had been invented specifically to permit him to stand around being tan.

Al hated Donfrey only in the social sense.

Personally? He barely knew him. Had once sold him a brass umbrella stand. Donfrey had said, “Great little place you’ve got,” while looking around in the careful way successful people looked at unsuccessful places, mentally estimating how long until liquidation.

What made a place little? Square footage? Revenue? Dreams?

Al’s shop had four rooms, technically five if the rear office was counted, which it should be, unless human effort suddenly did not count. His mother had helped choose the wallpaper in the front room. Mag had painted the sign. The letters leaned, yes, but they leaned enthusiastically.

Behind the screen, Hal Brister slapped Donfrey’s shoulder.

“You’re going for five hundred minimum.”

“Five hundred to make me eat chicken with somebody?” Donfrey said.

Laughter.

Effortless laughter. The boys.

Al looked down at his tunic. Across his stomach ran a wide fake-leather belt. The buckle was already peeling, showing white beneath the gold. De-kingifying him. De-thief-of-Sherwood-ifying him. Turning him into the guy who cleaned up after Robin Hood’s horse.

“You okay, Al?” said Dr. Wimsatt.

“Excellent,” Al said. “Just centering.”

“Good man.”

Was he nervous? Not nervous. He had appeared before crowds. In eighth grade he had done a monologue from The Diary of Anne Frank. Not Anne’s monologue, obviously. Mr. Dussel. He had received sustained applause, though Mom later admitted she started it. That did not invalidate the rest. Somebody always started applause.

Onstage Lenny Dace said, “And remember, folks, every dollar you spend tonight goes directly toward giving our youth the tools to literally make the choices that will unlock their futures!”

Applause.

“Youth cannot choose,” Lenny continued, “unless we choose to give them choice.”

More applause, less certain.

A teenage volunteer in a jailbird costume walked by carrying a tray of raffle tickets. Her striped cap said INMATE. One paper handcuff had come unstapled and dragged behind her.

Al had donated a Victorian sewing box to the silent auction. Estimated retail value: $275. Minimum bid: $40. No bids so far, although people were waiting strategically. They knew antiques. They were playing it cool.

“Up first,” Lenny cried, “the man who puts the roar in Brister Chevrolet!”

Hal went through the screen opening, spinning finger-guns. The room erupted. Actual whoops. Not merely clapping but table-pounding, whistles, a woman yelling, “Take it off!”

Hal removed his cowboy hat, bowed, and did something involving his hips.

The room went insane.

Al smiled supportively while an acid wind moved through his stomach.

The auctioneer got Hal to six hundred dollars. Dinner, dealership tour, and a weekend convertible rental, restrictions applicable. Hal returned behind the screen, flushed and pleased.

“That’s how you move the merchandise,” he said.

“Very polished,” Al told him.

“Just have fun.”

Advice from the gifted. Simply leap the chasm, Al. Simply possess cheekbones.

Dr. Wimsatt went next and received five hundred and fifty. Then Bernardo Pell, owner of three fitness centers, came out dressed as a gladiator. Bernardo’s bare legs were brown and hard and largely hairless. At one point he flexed and the ladies from First Community Bank began screaming as if attacked.

Al considered saying, You missed a spot shaving.

Didn’t. Class.

Then Donfrey. Lenny introduced him as “a cornerstone of not just local enterprise but local heart.” Donfrey jogged out, red sneakers flashing. His wife, Elise, stood at a front table and waved both arms. Donfrey kissed his fingertips and flung the kiss to her. People made the warm sound they made when successful married people demonstrated continuing sexual preference.

Seven hundred dollars.

Seven hundred for dinner at the Donfrey home, with wine-pairing by Elise, who had once been in a regional orange-juice commercial and remained, at forty-two, frankly annoying to behold.

Donfrey came back grinning.

“You’re up, buddy,” he said to Al.

Buddy. The term used by men who could not access your name.

Deena straightened Al’s cap.

“Own it,” she whispered.

He stepped through.

At first he saw nothing but lights and tablecloths. His boot cover caught on the edge of the temporary runway. He stumbled, corrected, and in correcting entered a strange half-trot. The feather swung into his eye. He swatted it away, and because his other arm went out for balance, he appeared to be waving.

Someone whooped.

Then several people whooped.

The whole room began clapping with a ferocious, almost protective rhythm. A woman called, “You got this!”

Exactly.

He did.

A heat entered him. Well, there it was. The thing he had suspected but never been provided adequate conditions to prove. Presence. Charisma. Maybe the costume freed him. Maybe the crowd, bored by standardized masculinity—tan, dental, automotive—had been waiting for something subtler. An approachable sensuality. A historical sensuality.

He reached the runway’s end and attempted Hal’s hip move. Not copied. Adapted.

The cheers doubled.

“You go, Robin!” someone cried.

“I’m going!” Al cried back.

Laughter, delighted.

At the turn his boot cover twisted around, so the false toe pointed sideways. He walked back with one green foot apparently broken, but this only endeared him further. He tipped his cap. The feather fell off. Applause became thunderous.

Behind the screen, Deena hugged him.

“You were adorable,” she said.

Adorable being a gateway category to desirable. Babies: adorable. Certain foreign actors: adorable. Al accepted.

The bidding started at twenty-five.

Silence.

Lenny reminded them that Al’s Heritage Lunch Experience included “a personal guided journey through yesteryear, plus soup, salad, or sandwich.”

A woman at the rear raised her card.

“Fifty!” Lenny shouted.

The woman lowered it and looked alarmed.

“Do I hear seventy-five?”

A man bid seventy-five. Possibly Mag’s tax preparer. Then the woman went to one hundred. The man went to one-twenty-five. Lenny attempted one-fifty, but the energy had escaped. Sold for one-twenty-five.

Still. One hundred twenty-five dollars to spend two hours with him. More per hour than many attorneys, depending how lunch was costed.

Behind the screen, Donfrey caught his shoulder.

“You killed, man.”

“Thanks.”

“No, seriously. They loved you.”

“Seemed positive.”

“Absolutely. The way you just went with it? That takes balls.”

Al nodded. Here was an acknowledgment between equals. Donfrey had noticed the bravery, the comic recalibration, the crowd-work. Perhaps after this they would—

“You’re Art, right?” Donfrey said. “Art Rooster?”

For a moment the sounds in the room arranged themselves into one flat metallic note.

“Al,” Al said.

“Al. Jesus, sorry. I knew that.”

“Roosten.”

“Right, right. I bought that urn thing from you.”

“Umbrella stand.”

“Sure. Great piece.”

Donfrey’s phone rang. He glanced at it.

“Excuse me, Allen.”

He turned away.

Allen.

Al stood very still, smiling as if continuing a pleasant exchange. Inside, various departments rushed to contain leakage. Maybe Donfrey was bad with names. Certain high-functioning types were. Their minds moved too fast for labels. Einstein probably called everyone Ted.

On a folding chair beside Donfrey’s garment bag lay a brown wallet and a set of keys with a silver D. The D was tasteful. Not enormous, merely informative. D for Donfrey. D for dick.

Al looked toward the screen opening. Everyone watched the next participant, a television meteorologist wearing a foam sun.

He nudged the wallet with his sideways boot.

It fell.

He nudged the keys.

They jingled once on the floor.

Nobody turned.

The risers supporting the rear volunteer choir were six feet away. Al gave the wallet another nudge, then another. A game, almost. Absurdly easy. The wallet slid over the tarp and vanished into the dark under the first riser. The keys required three kicks. The second kick sent them against a support post. The third put them beside the wallet.

There.

What had he done? Nothing, as yet. Moved items. Prevented tripping. The chair was unstable. Any safety inspector would say personal property did not belong there.

Donfrey ended his call.

“I’ve got to run,” he told Hal. “Where’s my stuff?”

“What stuff?”

“My wallet. Keys.”

“You had them on the chair.”

“I know I had them on the chair.”

Several businessmen began looking. One lifted the garment bag. Dr. Wimsatt checked beneath the chair. Al joined in, bending, making an interested face.

“Maybe backstage?” Al said.

“I came straight from the car.”

“Pocket?”

Donfrey slapped his pockets. His face had changed. The easy tan confidence had been wiped off and replaced by father-face, employee-face, ordinary face.

“Damn it.”

“You got a spare?” Hal said.

“Elise does, but she left already.”

“Call her.”

“She’s meeting Lily at the clinic.”

At the word clinic, Al felt an alertness he disliked.

Donfrey phoned his wife. “Hey, I can’t find my keys. No, wallet too. I know. I know, sweetheart. Can you turn around?”

He listened, rubbed his forehead.

“No, don’t. Stay there. She shouldn’t come out alone after that treatment.”

Al lowered his gaze and pretended to examine beneath the paper screen.

Donfrey’s voice softened. “I know it hurts her. I know. Tell her I’m coming, okay? Just tell her Dad’s coming.”

A pause.

“I’ll figure it out.”

He ended the call.

Hal said, “Lily’s having the procedure tonight?”

“Second casting. They crank the foot over another few degrees. She screamed the whole ride home last time.”

“What about a cab?”

“I need to get there before she’s done. She’s scared.”

Al knew about Lily vaguely. A thin girl around eleven, quiet, one shoe built up high. He had seen her at the street festival standing behind a pottery booth, holding her father’s hand while other children climbed an inflatable wall. Her foot turned inward severely. Clubfoot, or something worse. Al had not stared. Had specifically looked away, which was a courtesy.

Donfrey crouched and looked beneath the chair again.

“Come on,” he said. Not to anyone. To the universe, which had previously been cooperative.

The wallet and keys waited three feet behind him in the dark.

Al could say it.

Right now.

Hey, Donfrey, check beneath the riser.

Then how did he know? He could claim he saw a glint. He could crawl under, retrieve them. No confession necessary.

But what if somebody had seen the kicks? Then retrieval looked like staging. Better to wait until others searched near the risers, at which point he could indicate a likely vector: Things bounce farther than you’d think.

Or confess.

Confession would be staggering.

“Dale,” he could say, using the correct name, “I need to tell you something difficult.”

Everyone would stop.

“In a moment of hurt, after you got my name wrong—not that that excuses it—I acted beneath myself.”

He would kneel, produce the wallet and keys, perhaps with dust on one shoulder.

Donfrey would stare. “You did this?”

“I did. And I am sick about it.”

“But why tell me?”

“Because your daughter needs you. Because some things are larger than pride.”

Silence. Then Donfrey would grip his arm.

“That took guts, Al.”

Later, at the mansion, Elise would refill his wine and say, “You know what Dale values most in a person? Moral courage.”

Dale would sit by the fireplace, one red sneaker crossed over the other. “I misjudged you, Al.”

“You barely knew me.”

“That’s my point. I thought I knew people. Then tonight happened.”

Lily, her corrected foot resting on a silk pillow, would look up from her book. “Mr. Roosten, Dad says you saved him.”

“Your father saved himself, Lily. I merely removed the obstacle I had created.”

Experience-based profundity.

Possibly he would become Lily’s godfather. Was eleven too late for godparenting? Ceremonial mentor, then. She might someday write an essay: The Night a Local Antiques Dealer Taught My Family About Truth.

Donfrey hurried out with Hal, apparently to borrow Hal’s truck.

The moment sealed shut.

Al remained behind the screen with Dr. Wimsatt and the wallet and keys.

He could still act. Retrieve them, chase Donfrey into the parking lot. If he moved quickly, the heroism remained intact. More intact, since physical pursuit added urgency.

He bent as if adjusting his boot cover.

Under the riser, the silver D caught a thread of stage light.

Go.

Instead Al stood, removed his cap, and said to Deena, “Wonderful event.”

* * *

Outside, the night smelled of wet leaves and generator exhaust.

Al crossed the parking lot quickly. Not fleeing. He had reached his maximum networking capacity. This was healthy boundary recognition.

A crow—or perhaps merely a large black bird, as crow certainty required ornithological training—stood on a parking bumper and watched him. Al disliked the bird’s attitude. Judgmental without credentials.

“Go on,” he said.

The bird hopped once.

What exactly did the bird think it knew?

He got into his Caravan. The passenger window was repaired with clear tape after the motor failed, but from ten feet away, at night, it read as glass. He started the engine and backed out.

At the lot exit he could turn left toward the shop or right back toward the hall’s service entrance.

He stopped.

A car behind him honked.

“All right,” Al said. “Jesus.”

He turned left.

The wallet and keys would be found. Obviously. Risers had to come down. Maybe within the hour. Donfrey had transportation. Lily had Elise. No child was abandoned in some medieval pain chamber while Al drove away laughing.

He was not laughing.

Also, the incident might teach Donfrey to secure his valuables. Rich men grew careless due to excess cushioning. A wallet on a folding chair? At a public event? Had Al not moved it, an actual thief might have taken it permanently. In a broader sense, he had protected Donfrey’s identity and assets by temporarily consolidating them in a low-traffic zone.

Mom would have understood.

No, Mom would have said, Albert Joseph, turn this car around.

Would she? Mom had disliked show-offs. Whenever the Channel Eight news featured Donfrey’s commercials—Donfrey Flooring: We’ve Got You Covered!—she’d say, “Look at that grinning baboon.” Her phrase.

Still, she’d say, Turn around.

He pictured her in the passenger seat, hair wrapped in the blue scarf she wore after chemo made it patchy.

“You go back,” she said.

“Why?”

“You know why.”

“I’m asking for a reasoned argument.”

“A little girl needs her father.”

“She has her father. Hal drove him.”

“You made him suffer.”

“For three minutes.”

“You enjoyed it.”

Did he enjoy it? Enjoy was too strong. There had been a corrective satisfaction. Donfrey gliding through life in red sneakers, misnaming people, buying their umbrella stands while calling them urn things. Maybe three minutes of panic restored balance.

Mom looked out the taped window.

“That’s not you,” she said.

“What isn’t me?”

“Mean.”

He had heard this often. There wasn’t a mean bone in him. Too sensitive, that was his problem. As a boy he had rescued worms from sidewalks after rain. Once he kept an injured moth in a shoebox for nine days, feeding it sugar water. Mag called him Moth Nurse and told everybody at school, including Patrice Welker, whom he loved with an intensity that caused digestive malfunction. But who showed the greater moral development, the boy saving a damaged life or the boys laughing about it?

The moth died anyway.

He buried it beneath the lilacs. Mom attended.

“You have a tender heart,” she’d said.

Now imaginary Mom softened. “You made a mistake, honey.”

“Exactly.”

“And mistakes don’t define us.”

“Thank you.”

“But actions after mistakes—”

“No. Don’t switch.”

At the next light he could make a U-turn.

He entered the left-turn lane.

Behind the pharmacy, a giant illuminated mortar and pestle blinked from blue to white. His windshield had begun to fog. In the rearview mirror he looked pale and greenish, the tunic’s pointed collar framing his neck. Still in costume. Driving around dressed as a welfare elf.

The arrow turned green.

He turned left, which was technically the direction of the event if he continued around the block.

There. Motion toward accountability.

Then he passed the next street.

Why? Because a police cruiser sat at the corner. If he returned now, dressed like this, carrying no plausible explanation, and some volunteer had discovered the items, there might be accusations. Theft. Attempted theft. Was kicking legally equivalent to taking? Courts were unpredictable. Civic donor humiliates struggling merchant, merchant relocates property, merchant’s life destroyed. Donfrey would have attorneys. Al had Craig Dummel, who mostly did divorces and once forgot Al’s court date regarding the awning permit.

Better to call anonymously.

He could phone the hall and say, “Have someone check under the rear risers.”

They would ask who was calling.

“A concerned observer.”

“Why were you observing beneath risers?”

Also caller ID.

He could use the gas station pay phone, except pay phones no longer existed, society having eliminated anonymous conscience for corporate reasons.

He drove on.

The town slid past: Pawn Harbor, the florist with three dead mums in a barrel, the old cinema now divided into a vaping shop and tax service. On the courthouse lawn, dry leaves spun in a tight circle. One rose high, escaped, then fell.

Al imagined commanding the leaves. Lifting them with his mind. Sending them against Donfrey’s windshield in a brown furious cloud—not causing an accident, just forcing a reconsideration of arrogance. Then parting them at the crucial second to reveal Al standing in the roadway, arms raised, coat snapping although he wore no coat.

“You?” Donfrey would say.

“I simply mentally eliminated all other possible options.”

Why did his fantasies always require powers or mansions? Why not fantasize about the shop making rent?

The shop would make rent. November had been atypical. People were spending online due to laziness, isolation, and an inability to appreciate grain. December would correct. Holiday browsers. Collectors. He had the French escritoire, priced aggressively at $1,850. True, one hinge was newer. One hinge being newer did not make the whole desk false. Was a man false because he had a filling?

Mag said he should list more items online.

Mag knew healthcare, not antiques. She worked nights at Pine View, came home at six, slept three hours, then studied for her registered-nursing exam with her cheek against the textbook. Good but flawed. Loyal but bossy. Hair unfortunate but potentially fixable. When she smiled, she was handsome. Not handsome like a man. Handsome in the old sense: substantial, open-faced, frontier-capable.

Her sons, Tyler and Brock, came to the shop after school. The boys ate everything and touched objects with damp hands. Al loved them. Had taught Tyler to identify dovetail joints. Had loaned Brock sixty dollars for wrestling shoes and received eleven dollars back, which was, for a fourteen-year-old, practically full restitution.

If the shop closed, where would the boys go after school? To their father’s apartment, where there was a snake and no dining table? The shop was not merely a business. It was an intergenerational cultural sanctuary with retail functions.

Mom had believed in it.

Her last coherent week, he brought photos to the hospital after repainting the front room. She examined each photo as if approving plans for a cathedral.

“Beautiful,” she said.

“The blue isn’t too blue?”

“No.”

“The trim?”

“Beautiful.”

“You can tell me.”

“Al, honey, I’m dying. Why would I waste time lying about trim?”

Then she laughed until she coughed. Crazy old broad.

He wiped his eyes angrily at the stop sign.

This was what Donfrey did not understand. A name was not just a name. Art Rooster. Allen. Great little place. Each misnaming pushed a person half an inch out of the human picture. After enough years, where were you? Standing outside the frame in a green hat while everybody praised your spirit.

And yet Lily’s foot.

He pictured the clinic. White room. Buckles. A doctor turning the foot by means of a metal wheel.

Probably inaccurate. Treatments were more advanced. There might be warm towels. Medication. Cartoons projected on the ceiling.

Still, she had screamed last time.

He saw her squeezing Donfrey’s hand and asking, “Where were you?”

“My keys vanished.”

“Who would do that?”

A shadow crossed the imagined doorway. Al, cap in hand.

“I would.”

Elise gasped. Donfrey rose.

“No,” Al said. “Let me finish.”

Here he gave the speech about wounded pride and radical honesty. Not begging. Stating. Then he placed the keys in Lily’s palm.

She closed her fingers around the silver D.

“I forgive you,” she whispered.

Later the newspaper ran a photo under the headline LOCAL MAN TURNS ERROR INTO EXAMPLE. The story went regional. Youth Choices invited him to speak.

“My message is simple,” he would tell assemblies. “Your worst impulse is not your truest self.”

Students would be silent. Even the wrestling-engorged boys at the rear.

He might develop a program: The Roosten Method. Relocate, Reflect, Restore. Corporate workshops. School districts. Fees reasonable but not insulting. The shop could stay open as a heritage center. Mag could quit Pine View. The boys would attend college without debt. Donfrey would join the advisory board.

Was it too late?

He looked at the clock. Eight-forty-one.

No. The event would continue until ten. The risers remained up until tomorrow, perhaps. He could go back after closing, find Deena, quietly explain. Or arrive before anyone and retrieve the items, then deliver them to Donfrey’s mansion.

That might be best.

Not concealment. Curated confession.

Timing mattered. Raw confession could injure. Properly delivered confession transformed injury into growth. Therapists said things like this constantly, charging by the hour.

He would close the shop early and go.

Except the shop was already closed.

He was only going there because Mag had texted that the front alarm chirped twice and stopped, and because home felt intolerable. He could sit in the rear office, inventory brass candlesticks, and make a deliberate plan.

Deliberate moral action was superior to impulsive moral action. Everybody knew that.

He passed the road that would have taken him back to the hall.

He did not turn.

* * *

Roosten’s Antiques & Distinctive Finds occupied the narrowest building on Merchant Street, squeezed between a barber shop and a vacant insurance office. In daylight the window looked crowded. At night it looked desperate.

A porcelain dog stared outward beside a tarnished tea set. Three wooden ducks occupied a child’s chair. The escritoire loomed in back, elegant but blocked by a rotating postcard stand Mag had purchased at auction without consulting him. In the lower corner, a hand-lettered sign said WE BUY ESTATES, COINS, TOYS, MILITERY.

Military.

Mag had added the sign during his flu week. He had pointed out the error gently. She said nobody cared. Nobody cared being the modern slogan.

He parked behind the shop and sat with the engine running.

On the neighboring property, a chicken pecked beside a rusted air conditioner while a tiny Yorkie watched it through a chain-link fence. The Yorkie trembled with desire to destroy the chicken. The chicken ignored him completely. A useful lesson in power, though for whom?

Al turned off the engine.

Silence entered.

He should call Donfrey directly. His number would be online through the flooring store. Say: “Dale, this is Al Roosten. I believe I saw your wallet and keys get knocked beneath the choir risers.”

Believe I saw. Not a lie, strictly. He had seen them get knocked. By whom was grammatically optional.

Then Donfrey would say, “You’re a lifesaver, Al.”

And Al would say, “How’s Lily?”

That opened the friendship door naturally.

But if Donfrey asked, “Why didn’t you tell me before?”

“I only just processed what I saw.”

Processed. Modern. Unchallengeable.

He took out his phone.

Three missed texts from Mag.

ALARM OK?

ARE YOU THERE

PLZ ANSWER ME ASSHOLE

He typed: Here. Fine. Sensor glitch.

Then he searched Donfrey Flooring. A photograph appeared: Dale and Elise kneeling beside Lily at a charity 5K. Lily wore a purple brace and held up a medal. Donfrey looked exactly as he had while asking the universe to return his keys, only happier.

The company number went to voicemail after hours.

He could leave a message.

He pressed Call.

“Thank you for calling Donfrey Flooring, where we’ve got you covered! Our office is currently—”

Al hung up.

A movement near the dumpster startled him.

An old man came around the corner of the vacant insurance office. He wore three coats, each a different length, and pushed a shopping cart containing plastic bags, aluminum cans, and a pink child’s lamp without a shade. His beard was not a proper beard but gray strings emerging from a face red with cold.

The man stopped when he saw the Caravan.

Al locked the doors.

The old man looked at him.

“What?” Al said, though the glass was closed.

The man remained still.

Here came the request. Money. Food. Access to the dumpster. A life story designed to force participation. Al had met him before, maybe. Or another one. They circulated downtown, leaving bottles, sleeping in doorways, reducing the attractiveness of retail corridors while the city spent eighty thousand on consultant banners that said DISCOVER MERCHANT STREET!

The old man pushed the cart closer.

Al imagined getting out with the tire iron.

Not using it, necessarily. Holding it. Establishing limits.

The man would say something filthy. Al would advance. The man would lunge, making what happened next self-defense. One clean strike to the shoulder. Not head. Al was not an animal.

Or perhaps the man had a knife. Then Al would have to disarm him through surprising speed. Years of suppressed capability exploding outward. Police arriving, examining the knife, saying, “You did what you had to do, sir.”

The newspaper: LOCAL BUSINESSMAN DEFENDS HISTORIC DISTRICT.

Donfrey would see it. Call immediately.

“Al, I’ve been trying to reach you. They found my wallet and keys.”

“Glad to hear it.”

“And now this? You’re full of surprises.”

“We all are, Dale.”

But suppose the old man had no knife. Suppose he was simply weak and Al hit him. The beard would fly. The coats would crumple. Beneath them, what? Ribs. Old undershirt. A body like Mom’s near the end, all hinges and paper.

The old man came alongside the driver’s window.

Al’s fingers closed around the door handle.

The man raised one hand.

Not demanding. Just showing the hand, perhaps. Empty.

His fingernails were dark. A split in one knuckle had dried black. His eyes moved over Al’s green tunic, pointed collar, sideways boot, then up to his face.

For one horrible second Al expected laughter.

The old man’s mouth shifted.

A smile. Barely. Three teeth, maybe four. Not mockery. More like apology for having seen him like this, sitting in a taped-window Caravan dressed as Robin Hood after failing to become good.

Al looked away.

In the shop window, the porcelain dog looked back with its permanent, idiotic hopefulness. The silver tea set caught a weak stripe from the streetlamp. Everything unsold. Everything waiting for someone to say what it was worth.

When Al looked again, the man was still smiling.

Al made his own mouth move.

It felt incorrect at first, a grimace assembled from old parts. Then the old man’s smile weakened, and Al’s did too, until they matched: two small failures of the face, offered without explanation.

The Yorkie barked once behind the fence. The chicken continued pecking.

Neither man spoke.

After a moment the old man lowered his hand and pushed his cart toward the alley, the pink lamp wobbling above the bags like an extinguished little moon.

Al watched him go.

His phone lay lit on the passenger seat, Donfrey Flooring’s number still open on the screen.

He did not call.

He did not delete it either.

For a while he sat in the cold van outside the failing shop, wearing the ridiculous green clothes, his smile gone but not entirely, not yet.

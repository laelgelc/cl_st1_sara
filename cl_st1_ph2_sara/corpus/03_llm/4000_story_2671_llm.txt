At half past nine the telephone rang.

It did not merely ring. It burst into the bedroom, sharp and glittering, while the blind knocked against the sill and the muslin curtains breathed in and out like pale, frightened lungs.

Monica Tyrell opened her eyes.

Ring-ting—a pause—ring-ting-ting!

No. Impossible. Not at this hour.

The room was full of that thin white-grey morning light which made everything appear exhausted: the silver-backed brushes, the little bottle of scent, her stockings lying across the chair like two fainting arms. Outside, the wind tore at the roofs. A cloud went past in strips, like washing ripped from a line.

Again!

Monica pulled the eiderdown over her head. Her heart had begun to beat with the quick, offended violence she knew so well. People did not understand. That was the tragedy. They could be told, warned, implored; one could explain that the hours before eleven were not hours at all but a kind of raw passage through which the nerves had to be smuggled—and still they rang one up.

The maid knocked.

“Telephone, madam.”

“Who is it?”

“I think it is Mr. Ralph.”

Ralph.

For a moment Monica lay perfectly still. Then she sat up, gathering the lace at her throat.

So it was Ralph.

He knew. If there was one person in London who knew how morning affected her, it was Ralph. Had she not said only yesterday, as they walked beside the dark shining railings, “Before eleven, darling, I am hardly a human creature”? And he had pressed her hand and answered in that deep absurd voice of his, “You are never a human creature. You are a flame imprisoned in a woman’s body.”

How beautiful it had sounded then! How false it sounded now!

“Tell him I am coming.”

The passage was cold. A window banged somewhere below—bang!—and the floor trembled beneath her bare feet. At the telephone she saw herself in the narrow glass: tumbled bronze hair, enormous eyes, a white face floating above the blue dressing gown. She looked ill. Interesting, but ill.

“Hullo?”

“Monica! At last. Did I wake you?”

Did he wake her? The cruelty of the question!

“No,” she said softly. “Not exactly.”

“My poor angel, what’s the matter? You sound a thousand miles away.”

“I feel rather more than a thousand miles away.”

“Listen, beloved. I’ve got to see Camden at twelve, so shall we make it one-fifteen at the Marlowe instead of one? I’ve secured the corner table. And afterward—”

The blind in the hall window flapped up with a crack. Monica shut her eyes.

He was talking about lunch.

Lunch! Tables, oysters, those ridiculous scarlet chairs, Ralph leaning forward over the flowers and whispering that her mouth had destroyed his peace. Always his peace, his heart, his life. But her morning—her poor defenceless morning—he trampled into it without a thought.

“Monica? Are you there?”

“Yes.”

“Then one-fifteen?”

“I don’t think so.”

“What do you mean?”

“I mean I don’t think I can lunch with you today.”

There was a little silence. How instantly silence changed him! She could see his brows lift, his hand tighten about the black receiver.

“But why not? We arranged it.”

“We arrange so many things.”

“My dear girl, what on earth has happened?”

Nothing had happened. That was precisely it. Nothing ever happened except that people showed themselves at last. A veil thinned; a word, a gesture, a telephone bell—and there they stood, strangers.

“You rang me in the morning,” she said.

Ralph laughed.

Not loudly; no, worse than loudly. Tenderly.

“My sweet lunatic, forgive me. I had to change the hour.”

“You knew.”

“Knew what?”

“That mornings are unbearable to me.”

“Oh, Monica!”

There it was. Oh, Monica! The whole history of misunderstanding contained in two words. She felt suddenly calm, remote, exquisitely wounded.

“Good-bye, Ralph.”

“Now don’t be absurd. I’ll see you at one-fifteen.”

“No.”

“At one-fifteen.”

“No, Ralph. Not today.”

She replaced the receiver before he could answer.

At once the house became immense. The wind rushed down the chimney, the front door slammed, a pail clattered in the area. Yet through all the noises there spread a marvellous clearness. She stood in the cold passage with one hand on the telephone table.

It was over.

Or perhaps not over—no, one must not be melodramatic—but changed. Decisively changed. She was free of lunch. Free of Ralph’s corner table, his soft bullying, his “my poor angel,” his thick gold cigarette case pushed toward her as if it contained the secret of love.

Free!

The word opened before her like a bright door.

She ran back to the bedroom and pulled the curtains wide. What a day! The wind was not attacking the houses now; it was shaking them awake. Chimney smoke streamed sideways, blue and magnificent. The trees in the square tossed their bare branches as though they had thrown away all caution.

By eleven Monica was dressed. She chose the little black hat with the silver pin and a coat the colour of wet sand. No rouge. Freedom did not rouge itself. It had a clear, almost transparent face.

But what should one do when one was free?

Walk? Take a train somewhere? Sit alone in a station café and drink black coffee among strangers? She imagined Ralph waiting at the Marlowe, glancing at the door each time it opened. Poor Ralph. No—why poor? Had he not rung at half past nine?

Her hair felt heavy under the hat.

Of course. George.

She would have it washed and waved. One should begin liberty with clean hair.

The taxi leapt into the traffic. Everywhere the wind was busy: snatching newspapers from men’s hands, lifting skirts, driving pigeons in a grey whirl above the square. Monica pressed close to the window. How delightful not to be expected anywhere! She might vanish. She might tell the driver, “Go on—go on until the streets end.” She might become a woman seen once in a hotel at Dieppe, wearing pearls at breakfast, whom nobody could afterwards identify.

At Madame Élise’s the glass door usually opened upon warmth, perfume and exclamations.

“Madame Tyrell!”

“Ah, what a wind!”

“Your lovely hair—quickly, before it escapes altogether!”

Today the bell gave one dry note. Marie looked up from the desk and then down again.

“Good morning, madame.”

That was all.

Monica paused. The salon appeared strangely naked. The nickel basins glittered; brushes lay in disciplined rows; from behind a curtain came the steady hiss of hot water. The air smelled of soap, scorched paper and wallflower brilliantine. Two women beneath electric dryers resembled solemn figures wearing metal crowns.

“Can George see me?”

Marie turned a pencil between her fingers.

“I think so, madame. He is not occupied.”

No smile. No little French shrug. Had Ralph telephoned them? The idea flashed by and was dismissed. Even Ralph was not capable of surrounding her with a conspiracy before noon.

“Is anything the matter?”

“Matter, madame?”

“You all seem—” Monica let her glove fall upon the desk. “Never mind.”

Marie bent to pick it up, but too late; Monica had already done so. Their heads almost touched. Marie’s face was powdered rather thickly, and beneath the powder there was a curious swelling about the eyes.

“George will come,” she said.

Monica went into the washing cubicle. She removed her hat and studied herself in the mirror. Perhaps they were offended because she had arrived without an appointment. Yet she often arrived without one. George always said, “For you, madam, we make the time.” He enjoyed doing her hair. He had told her it was unusually responsive.

The curtain moved.

George entered in his white coat.

He was a slight young man with smooth fair hair and mild blue eyes. Usually his presence was like a small lamp being lit. Today he avoided the mirror.

“Good morning, madam.”

“Good morning, George. I’ve committed the unforgivable sin.”

He folded a towel.

“Have you, madam?”

“My hair. Look at it.”

He did look, but without admiration.

“The wind has got into it.”

“The wind has got into everything today.”

“Yes, madam. Very windy.”

He fastened the towel around her shoulders. His fingers, brushing her neck, were cold.

How odd they all were! As though she had entered a house immediately after a quarrel. Monica felt the first fine crack in her happiness. Freedom required a welcoming world. One could not be splendidly free among people who would not look at one.

George turned on the water. It rushed loudly into the basin.

“Not too hot,” she said.

“No, madam.”

“George.”

“Yes, madam?”

“Are you ill?”

“No, madam.”

“Then what has happened?”

“Nothing, madam.”

Nothing. The hateful word. She leaned back, surrendering her head to his hands. He wetted her hair carefully. Warm water moved over her scalp, and for one second she felt soothed, almost boneless.

Then something struck the roof—a branch, perhaps—and scratched across it.

George’s fingers stopped.

“Only the wind,” Monica said.

“Yes, madam. Only the wind.”

There was something in his voice, a flatness too carefully made. Monica watched him upside down in the mirror. His face was pale, pinched at the mouth. He began to rub in the soap.

“Were you caught in it this morning?” she asked.

“Yes, madam.”

“Coming from home?”

“Yes, madam.”

“It must be worse where you live. More open.”

“Very open, madam.”

His hands moved through her hair, lifting it, pressing it, gathering the scented foam. Usually he talked of dances, football, his wife’s cooking, the baby’s new tooth. The baby—

“Is the little girl well?” Monica asked.

His hands ceased again.

The water ticked into the basin.

From the outer salon came the electric hum of a dryer and then Marie’s voice, unnaturally bright: “This way, please, madame.”

George bent over Monica’s hair. She saw his mouth open, but no words came. His face seemed suddenly to lose its structure; it folded inward like wet paper.

“She died this morning,” he said.

Monica did not move.

“What?”

“My little girl. She died this morning, madam.”

The dryer hummed. A bottle clicked against glass. Far away in the street a motor horn gave two impatient cries.

“Oh, George.”

“Yes, madam.”

“But you are here.”

“I had to come in.”

He turned on the tap, though the basin was already full. Water struck water with a bright, unbearable noise.

“She was ill?”

“Only since yesterday. She took bad in the night. The doctor came, but there was nothing.” He swallowed. “Nothing he could do.”

Nothing.

Monica closed her eyes. The warm foam around her head felt obscene, luxurious. A baby had died while the blind knocked in her bedroom, while she listened to Ralph’s voice and thought herself betrayed. Somewhere in a little open house the wind had pushed against the windows, and George had stood beside a cot.

“How old was she?”

“Seven months, madam.”

Seven months. Not even a year. A tiny face, hardly finished; little curled fingers; a mouth soft as the inside of a flower. Monica saw, with sudden terrible clearness, a white cot and a waxen child lying beneath a blanket, while the curtains trembled and the morning entered without pity.

“Oh, George, I am so dreadfully sorry.”

“Thank you, madam.”

“Why did they let you come? Why aren’t you at home with your wife?”

He looked almost apologetic.

“There’s things to arrange. And Madame said if I preferred— But I thought I’d come. I didn’t know what else to do.”

What else to do. Of course. The hours had to be crossed somehow. Hair washed, towels folded, taps turned on; and behind each ordinary movement stood the cot.

Silence came down between them, soft and thick as snow.

Monica wanted to take his hand, but his hands were wet and buried in her hair. She wanted to say something worthy, something simple and true. Yet every phrase approached already dressed for the occasion. Life is cruel. Time heals. She is at peace. Hideous phrases! How did human beings speak when words had been stripped of all meaning?

George rinsed her hair.

“Water all right, madam?”

“Yes.”

“Not too hot?”

“No.”

His courtesy was more dreadful than tears.

When she sat up, wrapped in towels, Marie appeared to lead her to the drying chair. Marie’s swollen eyes met hers for an instant, and now Monica understood the coldness, the lowered voices, the whole salon holding itself rigid around George’s grief.

She could not stay.

“My head aches,” she whispered. “I think I’ll go.”

“But your hair, madame—”

“I’ll put the hat over it.”

She pressed money into Marie’s hand. Too much, absurdly too much. Marie glanced at it, then at Monica, and something like reproach crossed her face.

Outside, the wind struck Monica’s wet head through the hat. She stood on the pavement, trembling.

Ralph.

She must see Ralph. At once. At once!

A taxi came pitching toward her. She raised both arms as though signalling from a wreck.

“The Marlowe Restaurant.”

As they swept away, she saw herself entering late, her hair damp and ruined, her eyes full of knowledge. Ralph would rise. He would understand before she spoke. Perhaps he had always understood; perhaps the morning call had been nothing—a clumsy little knock on the door of love, and she had mistaken it for cruelty.

Seven months.

The taxi turned sharply. Monica clutched the strap.

She would send flowers. Not coloured flowers. No vulgar pinks, no red roses suggesting passion and life. White flowers—lilies, perhaps, and those small starry blossoms whose name she never remembered. A low white basket with satin ribbon. No name. Or only a card:

For the little one, from someone who understands.

No, “someone” was ugly.

From one who understands.

Yes. That was beautiful. Mysterious, but not falsely so. George would read it; his wife would read it beside the white cot. They would wonder who had entered, for one instant, into their sorrow.

Monica leaned forward.

There—a flower shop! The window floated with white: lilies, narcissi, pale roses, masses and clouds of whiteness.

“Stop!” she cried.

But a motor omnibus roared beside them, swallowing her voice, and the taxi had already passed the window. She twisted round. The flowers slid away behind rain-streaked glass, became a soft blur, and vanished.

For a moment she thought of calling to the driver again.

Then the wind caught the taxi broadside, shaking it fiercely, and ahead, beneath the striped awning of the Marlowe, Ralph was waiting.

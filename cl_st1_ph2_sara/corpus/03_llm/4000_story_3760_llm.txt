# The Honeymoon That Waited for Us

There is a common error concerning honeymoons which deserves to be corrected by some competent authority. The world supposes that they must begin on the morning after the wedding, or at least as soon as the bride’s travelling-gown can be buttoned and the bridegroom’s new portmanteau persuaded to close. Thus a sacred season is tied to chronology; love is put into a railway time-table; happiness must depart by the 10.15, whether its luggage is ready or not.

Our own theory was different.

“Why,” said Graygown, “should there be only one honeymoon?”

“Precisely,” I answered. “Why indeed?”

This reply, though lacking the severe apparatus of logic, satisfied the committee of two. We therefore resolved that the first journey after marriage should not exhaust the subject, but merely open it; that every year, if fortune permitted, we would go away together in search of another honeymoon; and that the experiment should continue until either the world or our curiosity came to an end.

Love, like honey, is commonly imagined to keep best when sealed. But perhaps it resembles the bee more nearly than the comb. It must fly abroad, visit strange blossoms, endure a little rain, and return laden with sweetness gathered from many fields. Memory afterwards presses the sweetness into waxen cells, where even the discomforts acquire a pleasant flavour. A wet day, properly aged, becomes hydromel. A bad dinner, recalled across twenty years, may nourish the heart.

Of all our experiments, the Norwegian one proved richest in this retrospective fermentation. It lasted six weeks, and included almost every possible method of locomotion except the balloon and the camel. We went by railway, steamer, carriole, stolkjærre, rowboat, sailboat, and those primitive vehicles which Providence attached to mankind before the invention of wheels. We travelled from Christiania to Throndhjem, not by the shortest route—that would have been a concession to geography—but according to the attractions of mountains, rumours of trout, reports of waterfalls, advice of chance acquaintances, and the agreeable perversity which makes a traveller turn aside whenever Baedeker says, “There is nothing of interest.”

Our baggage was moderate in theory and extensive in fact. Graygown possessed a travelling-bag which seemed externally innocent, but yielded, on proper invocation, slippers, medicines, books, needles, thread, tea, biscuits, string, court-plaster, and the crochet-work that accompanied her through Norway like a small white flag of domestic peace. I carried rods, reels, flies, leaders, landing-net, wading-shoes, and a few garments packed among these essentials as concessions to civilisation.

“You have brought enough fishing-tackle,” said Graygown at Christiania, “to supply a small rebellion.”

“My dear,” I replied, “one never knows what the trout of a foreign country may demand.”

“They will probably demand to be left alone.”

This prediction, like many of her quiet observations, was founded upon an intimate knowledge of human nature and was not without application to fishes.

Christiania gave us the first delicate savour of the North: broad streets washed with a cool light; gardens leaning over walls; blue water at the city’s feet; and beyond it those long, low hills which appeared to be waiting, with Scandinavian patience, for the traveller to come out and look at them. The evenings declined reluctantly. At ten o’clock there was still a golden uncertainty in the sky, as if the day had forgotten some errand and could not quite bring itself to depart.

But we were not city-seekers. A capital is merely the front parlour of a country; polite, well-arranged, and somewhat conscious of company. We desired the back rooms, the kitchen, the hay-field, the mountain pasture, the little chamber under the eaves where the wind tells stories after midnight. So we turned northward, and presently the train carried us beside Mjøsen, a long mirror of pale blue, bordered with farms and dark fir woods. Steamboats crawled upon it like black insects; islands rested in the water; white houses flashed and vanished among the trees.

At Eidsvold we remembered constitutions, independence, and the solemn courage of men who make a nation by sitting around a table. Graygown said the Norwegian assembly had shown superior wisdom in choosing a place where, if political debate became tedious, the members could look out of the window.

“Do you suppose,” I asked, “that the Continental Congress would have finished sooner if there had been a trout-stream beside the hall?”

“No,” she said; “you would merely have had fewer signatures.”

Beyond the lake the country rose and unfolded. Valleys opened like green books; rivers ran through them in silver sentences; farmhouses, red or brown, stood upon the slopes with an air of substantial contentment. Mountains came nearer, at first civilly, then with grandeur. They looked over one another’s shoulders, they gathered in grave councils, they frowned down upon the road; yet wherever a stream sparkled between them, the austere company seemed to smile.

We followed the Gudbrandsdal, that noble avenue of farms, river, and mountain, where history and haymaking live amicably side by side. At times the valley spread broad and pastoral, with grain-fields ripening in squares of yellow-green; at times it narrowed, and the river, compressed between rocks, shouted against restraint. Old houses carried carved doorways and grass-grown roofs. Children stood beside the road and saluted us. Women in the fields straightened themselves and smiled. The horses, small and sagacious, went at their own pace, which no argument could alter.

The Norwegian station horse recognises only three speeds: contemplation, reluctant movement, and return to the stable. On level ground he contemplates; uphill he moves reluctantly; but when his nose is turned homeward, the sagas revive in his blood. Then he becomes the steed of Harald Fairhair, and the passenger must hold his hat.

Our first prolonged halt was made because I heard there were trout in a river which crossed the road two miles below a certain posting-station. The information came from a commercial traveller who had never fished there, but whose cousin had spoken with a man who had seen an Englishman carrying a rod. This chain of evidence seemed to me conclusive.

The river ran swift and clear between alder bushes, with deep amber pools under the opposite bank. Graygown established herself upon a mossy stone, opened her work, and said she would remain until dinner or until I had caught enough trout to satisfy me.

“That,” I told her, “is an indefinite promise.”

“So is marriage,” she replied, and began to crochet.

I entered the stream with the confidence of an apostle and the footing of Nebuchadnezzar. The bottom consisted of round stones, each one carefully greased by the river. Above me a rapid broke into white tongues; below, the current slid towards a dark pool under a cliff. The trout rose freely—always, however, at a respectful distance from my flies. I changed a brown hackle for a coachman, the coachman for a March brown, and the March brown for something scarlet which no natural insect had ever resembled. Still the river preserved its composure.

At last a fish rose close beneath the farther bank. The cast required delicacy, judgment, and exactly two feet more line than could safely be managed. I supplied the two feet.

The fly fell beautifully. The trout took it. I struck, and at the same instant the hook behind me fastened itself in the collar of my coat.

What followed was less a contest with a fish than a theological dispute between free will and predestination. The trout pulled downstream; the coat pulled backward; the current urged me diagonally towards the pool. For three seconds I represented a perfect balance of natural forces. Then the collar tore, the line flew free, and I sat down in the river with a dignity which no language can describe.

Graygown did not scream. She merely laid aside her crochet-work.

“Have you caught him?” she asked.

“No.”

“Has he caught you?”

“To some extent.”

She came down the bank, and while I emptied my wading-shoes she detached the fly from my collar. There was no reproach in her face; there was something worse—the serene expression of a person whose prophecy has been fulfilled.

That evening the innkeeper served us six trout, fried brown in butter.

“Yours?” asked Graygown.

“Spiritually,” I said. “All trout are brothers.”

Thus began the piscatorial portion of our honeymoon, a long series of hopes, deceptions, triumphs, and dinners procured by other men. Yet there were victories. I recall a mountain stream near Skjåk where the water, pale as melted aquamarine, ran out of a gorge and loitered for half a mile through meadows. The day was cloudy, the air soft, and the trout—blessed be their innocent appetites!—rose to a small gray fly as if they had been waiting since the creation of Norway for my arrival.

Graygown followed along the bank, carrying the basket. She had a way of appearing at the proper pool precisely when a fish was ready for the net, and disappearing when there was a fence to climb. By noon we had enough for luncheon. We made a fire under a birch, borrowed a pan from a farmhouse, and cooked the trout with salt and a morsel of butter. No silver dish at Delmonico’s ever bore a feast half so distinguished. The stream supplied music, the birch leaves applauded, and across the valley a row of snow mountains looked on with cold but unmistakable envy.

Our road presently climbed into sterner country. The farms grew fewer; the woods thinned; the air became sharper. Above us rose ranges streaked with snow, and beyond them glimmered the ragged edges of glaciers, like torn cloth-of-silver flung upon the shoulders of the mountains. Waterfalls descended everywhere. Some leaped in one clear shaft and vanished in spray; some went down ledge after ledge, white stairways for the mountain spirits; some hung so lightly upon the precipice that the wind lifted them and scattered them upward.

We passed one fall in a narrow gorge where the whole river hurled itself between black walls. The sound met us before the sight—a deep, continuous thunder, not angry, but immeasurably strong. Graygown and I stood upon a wet platform above the chasm. Far below, the water twisted in green coils, struck a boulder, and exploded into whiteness. Spray drifted around us; little ferns trembled on the rocks.

“What does it say?” I asked.

“It says,” replied Graygown, “that we are very small and lunch is getting wet.”

Here was Diotima translated into practical Norse philosophy. We withdrew to a shelf of rock beneath an overhang, where the lunch-basket was opened. In the presence of a cataract which had been preaching sublimity for ten thousand years, we ate hard-boiled eggs and discussed whether the salt had spilled. Grandeur is an admirable thing, but the human mind cannot live on it continuously. Even Moses, coming down from Sinai, must eventually have wanted supper.

The glaciers exercised a peculiar fascination over us. At a distance they shone pure and smooth, but close at hand their beauty became wild and broken. They were furrowed, stained, cleft into blue caverns, and crowded with pinnacles that leaned like ruined towers. Beneath one of them we ate another memorable meal, spread upon a flat stone beside a torrent newly born from the ice.

The sky was of an intense northern blue; the sun filled the hollow of the valley with saffron light; the glacier descended between dark cliffs, solemn as the robe of some dethroned king. Every few minutes there came a distant report, and a fragment of ice fell somewhere in the upper ravine. The sound rolled slowly from wall to wall.

Graygown placed a napkin over the bread to protect it from inquisitive flies.

“Imagine,” I said, “the ages through which this river of ice has been moving.”

“I am imagining them.”

“Imagine the winters accumulated here, the snowfalls of forgotten centuries, the storms that swept these peaks before St. Olaf was born.”

“I am imagining those also.”

“And now consider that this very water beside us may have fallen as snow in the days of the Vikings.”

Graygown dipped the drinking-cup in the torrent.

“Then it has had time to become cold,” she said.

That cup of water tasted of stone, snow, and the beginning of the world.

It would be impossible—and, let us confess, cruel—to compel the reader to follow every turn of our route. We crossed valleys whose names escaped us before we had learned to pronounce them; we slept in rooms large enough for royal councils and in rooms where opening the umbrella would have been an architectural disaster; we drove beside fjords, climbed over passes, waited for ferries, missed steamers, found boats, dismissed guides, recovered luggage, and ordered meals in a language which grew more supernatural each day.

My Norwegian began with confidence and ended in gesture. I had learned from a phrase-book a sentence meaning, “Will you kindly procure two horses and a carriage to take us to the next station at seven o’clock tomorrow morning?” I pronounced it carefully to an old woman at an inn. She listened with grave attention, crossed herself, and brought me a candle.

Graygown’s method was superior. She smiled, pointed, and employed three words—“værelse,” “kaffe,” and “tak”—with such tact that doors opened, coffee appeared, and the household adopted her. Before I had finished explaining that we desired a room, she had chosen it, aired it, discovered the clean linen, and arranged our possessions.

Her greatest conquest occurred at a farmhouse where we arrived after sunset in a storm. All afternoon the clouds had been collecting among the peaks. They came over the ridges in gray battalions, dragging veils of rain behind them. The fjord changed from blue to lead; the birches turned the pale undersides of their leaves; thunder moved from mountain to mountain like heavy artillery.

Our driver, who had been cheerful until the rain entered the back of his collar, stopped at a lonely farm above the road. The house was dark. A dog objected to us with conscientious fury. Presently a man appeared in the doorway and explained, partly in Norwegian and partly by closing the door, that there was no lodging.

I prepared to argue.

Graygown stepped forward.

What passed between them remains one of the mysteries of diplomacy. She knew perhaps twelve Norwegian words; the farmer knew no English. Yet within five minutes the door was open. Within ten, our dripping coats hung beside the stove. Within twenty, coffee steamed on the table, eggs hissed in a pan, and the farmer’s wife was showing Graygown a chest of homespun linen. I was sent upstairs with a candle and told not to fall through a loose board.

The chamber was enormous. Two small beds stood against opposite walls, separated by an ocean of bare floor. A wooden chair occupied an island near the window. The candle, set midway between the beds, illuminated neither, but merely emphasised the distance.

“We shall have to write to each other,” I said.

“Or signal,” replied Graygown. “Two flashes will mean, ‘Are you awake?’”

“And three?”

“‘Stop talking.’”

Rain beat upon the roof. The wind shook the window and roared in the chimney, but the room grew homey. Graygown put her watch upon the chair, laid her crochet-work beside it, and arranged our books in a little row. These slight acts subdued the wilderness. The vast chamber, which had looked like a barn abandoned by giants, became ours. That was her peculiar gift. She could conquer a farmhouse without hostilities, annex an inn-parlour by laying down a pair of gloves, and make any corner of the world domestic with a hairbrush and a lamp.

In the morning the storm had departed. The peaks stood freshly washed, with new snow along their summits. Drops glittered on every blade of grass. The farmer took me to a shed where, with much pantomime, he displayed an ancient fishing-rod. It was eighteen feet long, weighed nearly as much as a musket, and had a line resembling the cord of a window-sash. He explained that great trout lived in a lake four miles above the farm.

“Four miles?” asked Graygown.

The farmer nodded and pointed upward.

Now, when a Norwegian points upward, the inexperienced traveller thinks he indicates merely elevation. He may mean precipice, bog, snow-field, avalanche track, and the total absence of a path. But the phrase “great trout” had entered my mind. We agreed to go.

Our guide was the farmer’s son, a grave youth named Lars, who carried the rod, provisions, an axe, and apparently no sense of fatigue. The path climbed through dripping birches, crossed a torrent on two logs, and then vanished into heath and stone. Clouds drew around us again. The air cooled. Patches of snow appeared in the hollows, and presently snow began to fall—large, soft flakes drifting through the silence.

“This is midsummer,” said Graygown.

“Norwegian summer,” I explained, “is a state of mind.”

“My feet are in another state.”

We pressed on. The mountain tarn lay in a black basin under the ridge, its water dark as a shield of polished steel. Snow floated down and melted upon it. There was no tree, no flower, no sign of habitation; only the lake, the rocks, and the cloudy sky bending close above us.

Lars put together the ancestral rod and handed it to me. I made a cast. The line moved through the air with the sound of a clothes-rope in a gale and struck the water like a falling branch. No trout rose. A second cast produced equal silence. On the third, the hook caught in my cap.

Lars watched solemnly. Graygown sat upon a stone, wrapped in a waterproof, her crochet-work concealed for once by climatic necessity.

After an hour the snow changed to sleet. The lake preserved its iron indifference. I tried flies of every size and colour. I fished near the inlet, near the outlet, beside the rocks, and through a channel in the weeds. Nothing happened.

“Perhaps,” suggested Graygown, “the great trout have gone down to the farmhouse to see us.”

At last, when even hope had become damp, a small fish took the fly close to shore. I lifted it carefully. It was six inches long, lean, and pale, with the melancholy expression of an exile.

Lars gazed at it. His face remained respectful, but a dimple appeared near one corner of his mouth.

“He is not the largest fish in the lake,” I said.

“No,” replied Graygown. “He is probably the guide.”

We returned in triumph sufficiently moderated by proportion. At the farmhouse the little trout was fried and placed alone upon a dish. The family gathered to admire him. I divided him among six persons, thus performing a piscatorial miracle in reverse: one fish, many guests, and no fragments remaining.

Not all our days were strenuous. There were long, indolent hours upon fjord steamers, when the vessel moved through water smooth as green glass and the mountains opened before us one behind another. A farm would appear at the foot of a cliff, its fields incredibly bright, a handful of emeralds spilled between rock and sea. Then a village came into view: white church, red warehouses, boats drawn upon the shore, children waving from a pier. The steamer whistled; echoes ran among the hills; sacks and passengers were exchanged; and we moved on, carrying the smell of coffee, tarred rope, wet wool, and the clean salt breath of the fjord.

Once, late in the evening, we entered a narrow reach where the mountains rose almost sheer from the water. Their summits still held sunlight, though shadow filled the depths. High above us a waterfall descended in a silver thread, lost itself in mist, reappeared, and fell again. The western sky was pale gold; the east had turned purple. No wind moved. Even the steamer’s pulse seemed hushed.

Graygown sat beside me with her hands folded over the white work in her lap.

“Now,” I whispered, “we are approaching the midnight sun.”

“It is half-past nine.”

“One must begin approaching early.”

But clouds gathered along the horizon, and the sun, with that perversity which celestial bodies occasionally display towards tourists, disappeared before performing the advertised miracle. We never saw it at midnight. We saw it at eleven, glowing red above black ridges; at ten-thirty, turning the fjord to copper; at an hour when respectable suns elsewhere are in bed, gilding the snow-fields and awakening no conscience whatever in the birds. But midnight itself remained veiled.

I complained of this mildly, as a man complains when the universe has failed to consult his itinerary.

“You wished for the sun,” said Graygown, “and you were given the clouds.”

“That is not equivalent.”

“No; but the clouds are very beautiful.”

They were. Behind them the hidden light spread upward in broad beams; their edges burned crimson; the water received the colour and carried it trembling beneath us. A star appeared in the clear green space overhead, then another, blossoming slowly. The fjord became a dark garden where heaven planted lights.

At Stuefloten we found the kind of hospitality that leaves memory permanently in debt. We arrived tired after a day’s driving, walking, ferrying, and waiting for a postboy who was described as “coming immediately,” which in the mountains may mean any time before winter. The farm lay above the road, with a cluster of buildings around a clean courtyard. Flowers crowded the windows. A broad-faced woman came out, took both Graygown’s hands, and welcomed us as if we had returned from America.

There was no question of bargaining, passports, or suspicion. We were hungry; therefore we must eat. We were tired; therefore we must rest. Coffee, cream, brown bread, trout, eggs, and wild strawberries appeared in a procession. The farmer sat with us, speaking a little English acquired from summer travellers, and told stories of snow, reindeer, roads, and an Englishman who had fished three days without changing his clothes.

“He must have been very devoted,” I said.

“He was very wet,” said Graygown.

After supper the daughter of the house brought out an old violin. The farmer played a dance tune, stamping gently with one foot. The room was low and warm, the lamp-light shone on polished wood, and through the open door came the soft noise of the river. Graygown was given a spinning-wheel to inspect; I was shown a gun, two antlers, and a dried trout of heroic dimensions which had been caught, according to the farmer, in a lake not more than five miles away.

I listened, but I did not rise to the bait. Wisdom had entered me by the snowy tarn.

In the morning our hostess packed food for us and refused payment for half of it. When we departed, the family stood in the yard and waved. The road curved upward; the farm diminished; the red roofs sank among the trees; but for an hour we could see the white handkerchief of the youngest child fluttering against the green.

What makes a stranger kind? What ancient law, older than codes and customs-houses, bids a household open its door to two wanderers out of the rain? We carry letters of introduction, passports, recommendations, money; yet the truest welcome asks for none of these. It recognises simply that night is coming, that the road has been long, and that every traveller is somebody’s beloved expected home.

At Throndhjem we encountered history in stone and salmon in water—two attractions between which I divided my allegiance with imperfect justice. The cathedral rose gray and elaborate, crowded with saints, kings, arches, memories, and the long shadow of St. Olaf. We walked through its solemn spaces while coloured light from the windows lay across the pavement. Graygown moved quietly among the pillars. Even my sporting imagination was subdued.

But outside flowed the river.

I had heard that salmon were being taken. The information, unlike many fishing reports, proved true and therefore dangerous. An English angler at our hotel had landed a noble fish the previous day. He described the event at breakfast, measuring the salmon afresh with every cup of coffee.

By noon I had hired a boatman and engaged a stretch of water. Graygown came with us, carrying her faithful work and a book. The river was broad, strong, and darkly luminous. We drifted down while I cast across the current, let the fly swing, recovered the line, and cast again. Hour followed hour. The cathedral tower moved slowly behind the trees; clouds travelled seaward; the boatman smoked.

At last, near a deep run below an island, there came a pull—not the nervous touch of a trout, but a heavy, living resistance. The reel screamed. A silver shape sprang clear of the water, shook itself in sunlight, and fell with a crash.

“Salmon!” I cried.

Graygown closed her book.

For twenty minutes the fish ran, rested, bored into the current, and ran again. I forgot Norway, marriage, theology, and the probable cost of the enterprise. The universe contracted to a line of silk joining one excited man to one exasperated fish. At length the salmon came near the boat. The boatman reached with the gaff.

The hook came free.

The fish paused beside us for one lucid instant, showing his spotted back and disdainful eye; then he sank into the river.

No one spoke.

The boatman relit his pipe.

Graygown opened her book.

“My dear,” I said, “that was a very large salmon.”

“Yes.”

“At least thirty pounds.”

“Certainly.”

“Perhaps thirty-five.”

“By dinner,” she said, “he will weigh forty.”

Years have added to him. He is now, in memory, the leviathan of Throndhjem, a creature scarcely able to turn in the river. Yet I am not sorry that he escaped. A captured fish belongs to the photograph and the frying-pan; a lost fish belongs to literature.

We came home at last by stages too numerous to recount, bearing wooden spoons, photographs, pressed flowers, a carved horse, several pebbles, three unused medicines, and fewer flies than I had taken out. We had been wet, cold, delayed, overcharged, underfed, magnificently fed, lost, found, and occasionally understood. We had seen fjords hold the sky like a chalice, glaciers glow above summer fields, waterfalls write white signatures upon cliffs, and valleys fold their farms into evening peace. We had slept beneath turf roofs and hotel cornices; we had dined with farmers and anglers; we had made temporary homes wherever a lamp could be lighted and Graygown could lay down her crochet-work.

Now, after many years, the separate days have lost their dates. Chronology, that industrious but unimaginative clerk, has mislaid the accounts. I cannot always say whether the glacier came before the waterfall, or whether the snowy tarn preceded Stuefloten. But memory is not a guidebook. It is an album of instantaneous photographs, some blurred at the edges, some overbright, some unexpectedly perfect.

Here is Graygown upon a mossy stone beside the river, waiting while I fall in. Here she stands in the doorway of a conquered farmhouse, commanding coffee without a common language. Here she sits under a glacier, holding a cup of ancient water. Here she is on the steamer at twilight, the white thread moving quietly between her fingers while mountains darken around us. And here—clearest of all—she looks up as I return fishless from some expedition and says, not “What have you caught?” but “Was it beautiful?”

That was the true question.

We did not see the midnight sun. We missed it by clouds, by mountains, by half an hour, by the constitutional reluctance of happiness to appear at the advertised moment. Yet we found what we had gone to seek, though it was never marked upon a map. It waited beside rivers, rode with us through rain, climbed the snowy path, sat down at strange tables, and made its nest in unfamiliar rooms.

The world had told us that a honeymoon must be taken immediately or lost forever. We discovered that it may be renewed wherever two travellers carry love lightly, curiosity warmly, and patience enough to wait for supper. Norway gave us six weeks of wandering; memory has distilled them into a cup that is still sweet.

We missed the midnight sun, it is true.

But we found the honeymoon.

### THE SACRED BRAY  
*Being an Account from the Lesser Chronicles of Bellafonte*

**Editor’s Note.**—For many years the following legend was rejected by serious historians, chiefly because it was found written upon the inner lining of a royal stable-door. Recent scholarship, however, has established that the stable-door is at least three centuries older than the oldest official history of Bellafonte, and is therefore considered much more trustworthy.

There was once, between two green hills and beside a blue river, a dainty little kingdom called Bellafonte. It was so small that a good horse could cross it between breakfast and dinner, provided he were not detained by toll-keepers, public ceremonies, or ministers asking where he was going.

The people of Bellafonte were peaceful. There was no malice, there was no envy, there was no ambition—or, if there was, it had been appointed to an office and drew a salary. The peasants cultivated their vines; the merchants weighed their pepper; the soldiers polished weapons which had not been used in living memory; and the ministers governed with such gravity that nobody dared inquire what they did.

Over this agreeable country ruled King Hubert the Fourteenth, who was also fourteen years old.

Hubert was a rosy, courteous boy, more fond of climbing trees than signing warrants, and much happier feeding sugar to horses than listening to debates concerning the hereditary privileges of the Royal Umbrella-Bearer. Yet he was a king, and therefore obliged to receive every morning three bows from each minister, six petitions from each town, and twelve pieces of advice from persons who had never followed advice themselves.

On the first day of Hubert’s fourteenth year, the Royal Soothsayer appeared before the throne. He wore a purple cap sewn with silver stars, carried seven scrolls beneath one arm, and had arranged his beard in the shape of a question mark.

“Sire,” he said, “the heavens have spoken.”

The ministers trembled, for the heavens seldom spoke without causing additional paperwork.

“During Your Majesty’s fourteenth year,” continued the Soothsayer, “your life shall be preserved by the animal whose song sounds sweetest in your ears. Honor that creature and all its kind, and your dynasty shall endure, your kingdom shall prosper, and your enemies shall be confounded.”

“Have I any enemies?” asked Hubert.

“Not at present, sire,” replied the Minister of War. “But it is wise to confound them in advance.”

The Council immediately met.

Now a plain person might suppose that the prophecy advised the King to wait until an animal saved him, and then to honor it. But plain persons were not admitted to the Council. The ministers decided that the animal must be selected at once, so that suitable statues might be commissioned, laws prepared, priests instructed, feast days named, and offenders punished before the emergency occurred.

Accordingly, it was proclaimed throughout Bellafonte:

> LET ALL CREATURES OF MELODIOUS VOICE BE BROUGHT BEFORE HIS MAJESTY, THAT THE SWEETEST MAY BE KNOWN AND THE FUTURE OF THE STATE SECURED.

For seven days the palace gardens resembled an aviary conducted by a brass band.

There came canaries in golden cages, larks in wicker cages, finches in cages of ivory, and one exceedingly expensive foreign bird that refused to sing because the climate did not agree with its artistic temperament. A blackbird whistled; a thrush poured out a clear little river of notes; a linnet sang as though silver beads were falling upon glass. The Court applauded each performance with exactly the enthusiasm which it believed would please the King.

The Minister of Finance preferred the canary, because it ate little. The Minister of War favored the hawk, although the hawk made no music and bit him on the thumb. The Royal Composer declared that no bird sang correctly, but that several might improve if placed under his instruction for twenty years.

Hubert listened patiently. He liked the linnet; he liked the lark; he liked the blackbird very much. Yet none seemed to him beyond all doubt the sweetest voice in creation.

On the seventh afternoon, while the Court was assembled under striped silk awnings, there came through the palace gate a poor orphan girl named Mara. She was perhaps twelve years old, barefooted, sun-browned, and dressed in a coarse blue gown with a patched apron. Beside her walked a small gray donkey with a white muzzle and ears of astonishing extent.

The donkey’s name was Parsley.

The guards crossed their spears.

“Birds only,” said the captain.

“But the proclamation says creatures of melodious voice,” replied Mara.

The captain could not read, and was consequently much impressed. He permitted her to pass.

Mara led Parsley before the throne and made an awkward curtsey.

“What does your creature sing?” asked Hubert kindly.

“The sweetest song in the world, Your Majesty.”

At this the Chief Chamberlain smiled behind his hand. The courtiers smiled behind their fans. The ministers did not smile, because they had not yet decided whether the King would.

“Let us hear it,” said Hubert.

Mara stroked the donkey’s nose.

“Sing, Parsley.”

Parsley looked about him with simple and marveling curiosity. He observed the jeweled ladies, the embroidered lords, the cages, the throne, and the Royal Soothsayer’s remarkable beard. Then he lifted his muzzle.

“Hee—HAW! Hee—HAW! HEE—HAW!”

Three canaries fell from their perches.

The foreign bird fainted.

The Minister of Music covered his ears and cried that civilization was at an end.

A roar of laughter ran through the garden. Even Hubert laughed at first, for the bray was large enough to have belonged to an animal ten times Parsley’s size. But he stopped when he saw Mara’s face.

“Why do you call that beautiful?” he asked.

“Because I love him,” she answered simply. “And because when I hear him across the fields I know he is coming home. If I am carrying wood, he carries it for me. If I have fallen, he finds me. If I am lonely, he calls until I answer. There is no sound sweeter to me.”

For a moment the garden was quiet.

Then the Chief Chamberlain whispered that affection was not music, the Royal Composer whispered that peasants had no ears, and the Soothsayer whispered that prophecy must never be interpreted by an unlicensed child.

“Remove the interruption,” commanded the Lord High Keeper of Courtly Taste.

Mara was driven from the garden amid laughter, Parsley protesting at every step.

“Hee—haw!”

“Silence that monster!”

“Hee—haw!”

“Take it away!”

Hubert watched them go and felt ashamed; but he was young, the Court was large, and shame is often quiet while ridicule has excellent lungs.

That evening a nightingale sang in the palace rose garden.

It sang beneath the moon, softly at first, then with such clear, liquid sweetness that all who heard it held their breath. Hubert leaned from his window, enchanted.

“That is the voice,” he said. “The nightingale is sweetest.”

Trumpets sounded before dawn.

The nightingale was proclaimed sacred throughout Bellafonte. It became treason to frighten one, sacrilege to disturb its nest, and a capital offense to imitate its song disrespectfully. Marble nightingales were erected in market squares. A silver nightingale replaced the lion upon the royal standard. The College of Avian Interpretation was founded, and six learned men were paid to translate nightingale songs into advice upon taxation, foreign policy, and the price of onions.

Thus was the future made secure.

Some months later, King Hubert rode hunting in the northern woods. He disliked killing things, but was told that hunting encouraged the deer and maintained the ancient liberties of the nobility. During the chase a mist descended. Hubert became separated from his company, and his horse, frightened by a boar, galloped between the trees.

There was a sharp turn, a crumbling ledge, a shower of stones—and then the world rolled over.

When Hubert opened his eyes, he lay at the bottom of a rocky slope. His horse was dead beside him. His left leg was twisted under him, and when he tried to move, pain flashed through his body so fiercely that he nearly fainted.

He shouted.

No answer came.

All afternoon he called until his throat was raw. The mist became rain; the rain became cold; and the light faded among the pines. His hunting party, meanwhile, searched the western valley, because the Master of the Hunt had declared with professional certainty that no king would go east.

Hubert lay helpless.

Presently a nightingale began to sing in a thorn bush above him.

Its music was exquisite. Each note trembled like a star reflected in water.

“Little sacred bird,” whispered Hubert, “fly to my people. Lead them here.”

The nightingale continued singing.

“Please,” said Hubert.

The bird sang a more elaborate passage, which, according to the College of Avian Interpretation, would later be identified as a recommendation for reducing harbor duties. Then it flew away.

A thrush came and sang upon a stone.

“A fine voice,” Hubert murmured bitterly. “Can you fetch help?”

The thrush cocked its head, swallowed a worm, and departed.

A linnet followed, scattering bright notes into the darkening air. Hubert called to it. It paid him no more attention than the others.

Night came.

The boy’s leg burned; his hands were numb; and fear, which court etiquette had never permitted to approach the throne, sat down beside him like an old acquaintance. He thought of the palace, of the warm lamps, of his supper waiting beneath silver covers. He thought of his ministers arguing about the safest direction in which to search. He thought, too, of the barefoot girl driven from the garden.

“If I hear him across the fields,” she had said, “I know he is coming home.”

The forest grew black.

Then—hark!

Far away, beyond the trees, came a faint and ragged sound.

“Hee—haw!”

Hubert lifted his head.

Never had any note seemed so glorious. No silver bell, no cathedral choir, no nightingale beneath the moon could have equaled it.

“Hee—haw!”

“Here!” cried Hubert. “Here! Oh, sing again!”

The cry came nearer.

“Hee—HAW!”

“Sweetest of creatures!” shouted the King. “Divine musician! This way!”

Branches cracked. Stones rattled. Out of the darkness emerged two immense ears, then a white muzzle, then the modest gray body of Parsley.

The donkey had been gathering fallen sticks with Mara when he scented the dead horse upon the wind. Being less educated than the Master of the Hunt, he had gone directly toward it.

Parsley approached Hubert, sniffed him carefully, and uttered a low bray of concern.

“Saved!” whispered the King.

The donkey knelt without instruction. Hubert dragged himself onto Parsley’s back; and since there was neither saddle nor bridle, His Majesty preserved his royal person by holding firmly to the animal’s generous ears.

Slowly, patiently, Parsley climbed out of the ravine.

He carried Hubert through the wet forest, across two fields, and at last to a poor cottage where a candle burned in the window. Mara opened the door.

When she saw the wounded boy upon her donkey, she gasped. When she recognized the King, she nearly dropped the bowl of goat’s milk in her hands.

“You!” said Hubert.

“You,” said Mara.

Then neither wasted time upon ceremony.

Mara laid him on her own bed, set his leg as well as she could, wrapped him in blankets, and ran to alert the nearest village. Parsley brayed from the yard until every sleeper within a mile awoke and came to complain; whereupon they discovered the King and forgot their complaints.

Before sunrise the royal search party arrived. The ministers fell upon their knees. The Soothsayer announced that everything had happened precisely as he had foreseen, though in a deeper and less obvious sense than previously understood.

When Hubert had recovered, he summoned the whole Court.

Mara stood beside the throne in a new blue dress. Parsley stood upon the carpet, eating the tassel of the Lord Chancellor’s robe.

“The prophecy is fulfilled,” declared Hubert. “I chose beauty when I was safe and mistook pleasure for worth. In danger I learned better. The sweetest song is the one that brings hope, and the noblest creature is the one that comes when called.”

The Court applauded with tremendous sincerity.

By royal edict, donkeys were henceforth sacred and inviolable. The marble nightingales were removed from the squares and replaced with stone asses, many of which may still be seen by travelers who have missed the main road. The College of Avian Interpretation became the Academy of Braying, retaining its entire staff, since the learned men explained that their experience was equally applicable.

Parsley was appointed Chief Minister of the Crown. He attended every council, ate the documents laid before him, and made fewer mistakes than several of his predecessors. When his opinion was required, he delivered it loudly; when it was not required, he delivered it louder.

As for Mara, Hubert publicly promised that, when they were both old enough and if she were willing, he would marry her. She answered that she would consider the matter after observing whether he kept his promises and treated his donkeys well. This reply astonished the Court, but pleased the King.

Hubert ruled long and kindly. Mara became his queen, Parsley’s descendants occupied distinguished positions, and Bellafonte prospered beneath laws which were not always wise but were at least announced clearly.

Such is the legend.

It explains why every important work in Bellafonte is begun by a bray; why royal proclamations open with three solemn “hee-haws”; why statues of asses adorn the public buildings; and why, even to this day, an ass is traditionally given a place of honor in the Cabinet.

In this last custom, Bellafonte is not believed to stand alone.

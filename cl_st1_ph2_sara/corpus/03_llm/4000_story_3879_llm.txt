### The Entertainment at the Window

Bailey’s room was not, in itself, a place in which any reasonable man would have chosen to spend three weeks. It contained a narrow brass bedstead, an armchair whose horsehair stuffing had begun to emerge in grey, wiry curls, a washstand, two shelves of books selected by somebody who did not read, and a small table crowded with bottles. There were bottles to induce sleep, bottles to encourage appetite, bottles to diminish pain, and one square blue bottle which, according to the doctor, was not to be touched except in circumstances of peculiar urgency.

On the fourth day Bailey had asked what circumstances the doctor considered peculiar.

“You will know them when they occur,” the doctor had replied.

Bailey, surveying the procession of bottles, said he hoped the circumstances would have the decency to label themselves.

There was also a newspaper five days old, a saucer full of cigar ash, a plate upon which the stalks of grapes had stiffened into brown anatomy, and a pair of crutches leaning in a corner beyond reach. His legs, wrapped in spotless bandages, lay before him on a long footstool. The right one ached. The left one, which the doctor had declared the more troublesome, was generally numb. Between them they made standing impossible and patience unlikely.

But the room had a window.

It was a broad French window opening upon a narrow balcony, and beyond the balcony the ground dropped through a tangle of acacia and hibiscus to the river. Upstream, the water came round a shoulder of dark trees; downstream, it widened into a sheet of hammered brightness, crossed by the black reflection of a church tower. On the opposite bank stood a plantation house with a red roof, several white huts, a strip of maize, and woods whose shadows remained blue even at noon.

The river supplied Bailey with a programme.

At seven there were fishermen in hollowed boats, paddling as silently as conspirators. At eight a launch belonging to the district office went upstream, coughing pale smoke and carrying an official beneath a white umbrella. Later came rafts of timber, sampans loaded with baskets, women washing clothes among the reeds, boys who swam beside an overturned canoe, and once a dignified old gentleman who fell into the water while attempting to beat a goat with a pole.

Bailey had applauded the goat.

There were quieter entertainments. A crocodile showed its corrugated back near a sandbank and submerged before anyone but Bailey had seen it. Two boatmen quarrelled for twenty minutes, separated, and then discovered that each had taken the other’s paddle. A yellow dog crossed the river every afternoon and returned each evening, wet, satisfied, and entirely secretive.

Thus Bailey, who could not go into the world, had arranged for the world to pass before him.

Wilderspin came most mornings to smoke a cigar and discuss the weather.

“You’ll become frightfully observant,” he remarked one day.

“I have already become omniscient. That canoe is carrying plantains, the man in the grey hat is cheating his partner, and the young woman at the landing is expecting a letter.”

“How can you possibly know that?”

“The canoe always carries plantains. The man in the grey hat cheats everyone. And the young woman has looked down the road seventeen times.”

“Perhaps she has a stiff neck.”

Bailey considered this.

“Then it is an unusually romantic stiff neck.”

On the morning the private performance became public, Wilderspin had not yet arrived. The light was clear after rain, and the river, swollen and impetuous, carried leaves, branches, yellow foam, and once the roof of a chicken house. Bailey was wondering whether the chickens had gone with it when five men appeared on the opposite bank.

They came from behind the plantation house at a run. The first was a broad man in white clothes with a red sash or belt. Behind him ran a younger fellow, fair-haired and bareheaded, carrying a gun. The other three were estate servants. One had a boat-hook, one a machete, and the last nothing that Bailey could distinguish.

They stopped near the landing and looked upstream.

Bailey reached for his field-glasses. They were on the table six inches beyond his fingers.

“Confound it.”

He stretched. Pain went sharply through his right leg. The glasses remained where they were, shining with quiet malice.

The men began shouting. Their voices travelled across the river, blurred but urgent. The broad man pointed towards the maize. At once the party ran along the edge of the field and vanished.

Bailey stared at the empty place.

This was intolerable. Five armed men did not enter a morning picture at full speed merely to depart through its left-hand edge. He waited. A flock of small birds rose from the maize. There was another shout, then silence.

A fisherman drifted into view, looked towards the commotion, and immediately paddled himself behind a bend. Bailey disapproved of him. A proper fisherman would have remained and provided information.

Then a sixth man burst out of the maize.

He was perhaps two hundred yards away, but Bailey saw that he wore a dark cotton jacket and loose trousers. He ran unevenly, with one shoulder lowered, and carried something bright in his right hand. Behind him the fair-haired pursuer appeared and raised his gun.

The fugitive swerved.

The report reached Bailey a moment after the puff of smoke. Birds sprang from the trees. The dark-clothed man stumbled, recovered himself, and ran towards the landing.

“Good,” said Bailey. “Now we are getting somewhere.”

The broad man in the red belt emerged with the others. He shouted and waved. The fugitive leapt into one of the boats tied at the landing, struck at its rope with the object in his hand, failed to cut it, and jumped out again. For several seconds he was hidden by a hut. When he reappeared, he was running downstream.

The pursuit followed.

They passed behind a clump of bamboo and were lost.

Bailey waited with growing resentment. The empty river shone; a blue kingfisher dived from a branch; from somewhere behind the plantation came a woman’s scream.

The fugitive returned abruptly, running upstream now, much closer to the bank. The alteration offended all rules of narrative but improved the view. His pursuers appeared one by one. The man with the boat-hook had lost his hat. The broad man had acquired a revolver. The fair-haired youth stopped, knelt, and fired again.

This time the runner spun halfway round. He put one hand to his side, and Bailey saw the flash of the long knife in the other. Then he reached the water.

For an instant he hesitated on the muddy lip. The broad man shouted something. The river ran fast, brown beneath its silver surface, crowded with debris. The fugitive looked back, crouched, and sprang.

He disappeared with scarcely a splash.

The pursuers reached the bank. The fair-haired one fired into the water. The broad man struck his arm down and seemed to abuse him. The three servants spread along the shore, peering among the floating branches.

Nothing happened.

Bailey leaned forward until his bandaged legs protested. A dark head appeared far out, vanished, and appeared again. The swimmer was being carried downstream, but each time he rose he was nearer Bailey’s bank.

“By George,” Bailey murmured.

A third shot slapped across the water. The swimmer went under.

The broad man seized the gun, or tried to. During their struggle the fugitive surfaced beside a drifting log. He held it for a moment. Then, sheltered by it, he kicked towards the near shore.

Bailey’s excitement became complicated. At a distance the affair had possessed the admirable simplicity of sport; the man ran, the others pursued, guns sounded, and all was briskness and colour. But the river had narrowed the moral distance as well as the physical one. Bailey could now see the swimmer’s mouth open as he fought for breath. He could see a red stain spreading from his shoulder into the brown water.

The wounded man reached the reeds below Bailey’s house and dragged himself ashore.

For several seconds he lay there, half in the water. Bailey saw his back rise and fall. Across the river, the armed party rushed to the boats. The fair-haired youth cut one loose while the others climbed aboard.

The fugitive lifted his head.

He looked towards Bailey’s house.

Bailey ceased smiling.

The man pushed himself to his knees, then to his feet. He swayed, clutching the knife, and entered the trees beneath the balcony.

At once the frame was empty.

Across the river the pursuers crowded into the boat and paddled with astonishing disorder. The current took them downstream. The broad man shouted instructions which nobody followed. One servant struck another with his paddle; the boat-hook caught in a trailing branch; the fair-haired youth fired once into the air, whether accidentally or as a signal Bailey could not tell.

“Encore,” Bailey called without conviction.

The woods below remained still.

A few moments later Wilderspin appeared in the doorway.

“What’s all the shooting?”

“You have missed the first two acts,” said Bailey. “Somebody from the plantation has run amok, I imagine. He has been shot and has crossed the river.”

“Crossed? In this current?”

“Quite impressively. He came ashore down there.”

Wilderspin went to the window and looked over the balcony.

“Where is he now?”

“That is the difficulty. He has left the picture.”

Wilderspin looked at him.

“You mean he’s in these grounds?”

“Presumably. But wounded, and in no condition to be sociable.”

Wilderspin did not share Bailey’s confidence. He went to the door and called for Mrs Green. There was no answer. He called again, descended the stairs, and presently returned carrying Bailey’s crutches.

“You had better move into the passage.”

“My dear fellow, if the house catches fire, those instruments will enable me to burn upright.”

“We ought to lock the window.”

“And lose the conclusion?”

“You are an ass.”

“Certainly. But I am an ass who has spent three weeks looking at plantains.”

From the river came confused shouting. The boat had reached their bank below the bend and was no longer visible. Somewhere in the woods a branch broke.

Wilderspin set the crutches beside the chair. He crossed the room, closed one half of the French window, and hesitated over the other. The morning had become very quiet. Even the river seemed to run without sound.

Then a voice called from downstairs.

Wilderspin turned. “Mrs Green?”

There was a hurried tread in the passage, and Mrs Green entered with her bonnet crooked, her face crimson, and both hands pressed to her breast.

“Oh, Mr Bailey! Oh, sir! Such goings-on! Such wickedness!”

“What has happened?” asked Wilderspin.

“The plantation servant—Mr Carbury’s houseman—the one they call Osman—he has killed the cook and cut Mr Carbury, and they say he attacked the overseer with a parang, and the whole estate is out after him.”

“So that is it,” said Bailey. “I thought there was more plot than explanation.”

Mrs Green stared at the open window.

“They say he crossed the river.”

“He did,” said Bailey. “A strong swimmer.”

Mrs Green made a small choking sound.

“I came through the lower path.”

“Then you may have passed him.”

This possibility affected her more deeply than any danger already survived. She sat heavily upon the end of the bed.

“The lower path,” she repeated. “All through those trees. And me not knowing enough to be frightened!”

Wilderspin took her arm. “You must go downstairs and lock the doors.”

“Downstairs?”

“Yes.”

“Alone?”

“I shall come with you.”

Mrs Green rose, but before they reached the door there came a soft scraping from outside.

All three turned.

It was not a dramatic sound. A twig might have made it against the plaster, or a lizard on the balcony rail. It came again: scrape, pause, scrape.

Wilderspin stepped towards the window.

Bailey said, “Don’t.”

A brown hand appeared above the balcony’s wooden rail.

The fingers closed slowly, slipped, and closed again. After them came a forearm streaked with blood. The acacia grew close to the house; one of its branches brushed the balcony, and the wounded man had climbed it from the slope below.

Mrs Green inhaled for a scream but produced only a whistle.

Wilderspin looked about for a weapon. There was none. The poker was downstairs; Bailey’s walking stick had been taken for repair; the largest object at hand was a volume of devotional essays, which might have discouraged a lesser intruder but seemed unequal to this one.

The hand tightened.

A head rose behind the rail.

Bailey recognised the soaked black hair, the dark cotton jacket, the face he had seen only as a speck across the glittering water. Up close, the man looked neither picturesque nor theatrical. He looked exhausted. Mud marked his cheek. Blood had dried from his shoulder to his waist. His eyes were wide but not vacant; they fixed upon the room with a terrible, concentrated need.

The long knife was clamped between his teeth.

Wilderspin pulled Mrs Green into the passage.

“Come away, Bailey!”

Bailey gripped the arms of his chair. To cross the room required the crutches; to reach the crutches required standing; standing was precisely the accomplishment denied him. He made an effort nonetheless. Pain burst through both legs, white and enormous, and dropped him back.

“Go!” he shouted.

Wilderspin hesitated only an instant, then dragged the housekeeper out and slammed the door. Bailey heard him calling from the stair for help.

The Malay—Osman, Mrs Green had called him—raised himself until his chest lay over the balcony rail. For a moment he remained there, breathing with a wet, rattling sound. Then one leg came over.

The process seemed to Bailey to occupy days, weeks, possibly a substantial portion of geological time. The wet trouser caught on a splinter. The man pulled. The wood creaked. One bare foot searched the balcony floor, found it, slipped in blood, and found it again.

Bailey looked at the medicine table.

The field-glasses were still six inches beyond his fingers. Beside them stood three bottles. One was small and green, one square and blue, and the third tall, colourless, half full of a tonic which had achieved nothing but bitterness.

The man freed his leg and crouched on the balcony.

Bailey stretched towards the table. His fingers touched the green bottle and knocked it over. It rolled, struck a spoon, and fell onto the carpet without breaking.

Osman took the knife from his teeth.

“What the devil do you want?” Bailey asked.

The question was absurd, but speech placed a thin wall between them.

The man replied in a language Bailey did not understand. The words were low and urgent. He looked behind him towards the woods, then back into the room. Perhaps he wanted shelter. Perhaps water. Perhaps only escape. His left hand pressed the wound in his side, and blood came between the fingers.

From below, voices approached.

Osman heard them. His face changed. He stepped through the French window.

Bailey snatched the tall bottle and threw it.

It was a poor throw, delivered from the shoulder without balance. The bottle turned once in the air and struck Osman high on the chest. It did not break. It bounced onto the balcony and rolled under a chair.

Osman flinched. Then he advanced.

Bailey felt, with offended clarity, that the entertainment had violated its contract. The river was the stage; the window was the frame; Bailey was entitled to watch. Players were not supposed to climb out of the scenery and bleed upon the carpet.

Osman reached the footstool. His shin struck it. Bailey’s injured legs moved half an inch, and the pain was so violent that the room went black at the edges.

Below, someone shouted, “Up there!”

A fair head appeared among the acacia leaves.

The young man from the plantation stood on the slope with his gun raised. He could see Osman through the open half of the window, but Bailey lay directly beyond.

“Don’t fire!” Bailey shouted.

The gun fired.

The room filled with sound. A pane of glass cracked from corner to corner. Osman jerked upright as though a cord had pulled him, the knife falling from his hand. For an instant he stood perfectly still.

Then he fell across Bailey’s legs.

Bailey screamed. It was not an heroic sound, and he afterwards denied having made it.

The weight pressed upon the bandages. Osman’s face lay close to his knee; one arm hung towards the floor. Blood spread across the white linen, astonishingly bright. Bailey pushed at the man’s shoulder but could not shift him.

Outside, the fair-haired youth scrambled up the slope. Wilderspin was shouting from the hall. Mrs Green was screaming now with all the force she had previously mislaid.

Osman moved.

His hanging hand opened and closed. His eyes turned towards the knife on the carpet. Slowly, with immense effort, he reached for it.

Bailey saw the fingers creep nearer: an inch, a pause, another inch.

The blue bottle stood on the table.

Peculiar urgency, Bailey thought.

He lunged sideways. The chair tipped. His fingertips struck the bottle and sent it skidding towards the edge. He caught it by the neck, swung it once, and brought it down upon Osman’s face.

The glass broke.

For a moment there was no motion except the bright medicine running over Bailey’s hand and dripping from his cuff. Its smell—camphor, spirit, and something sharply medicinal—overcame even the smell of blood.

Osman’s reaching hand relaxed beside the knife.

Wilderspin rushed in, followed by the broad man with the red belt. Between them they lifted the body.

“Easy with those legs,” Bailey gasped. “They were unsatisfactory before.”

They lowered Osman onto the carpet. The broad man knelt and touched his throat. The fair-haired youth came through the window, still holding the gun. He looked extraordinarily young. Without the distance of the river between them, Bailey saw freckles on his nose and a tear in one sleeve.

“Is he dead?” the youth asked.

The broad man nodded.

The youth stared at the body.

“I didn’t mean— I thought he was going to kill him. I couldn’t see properly through the branches.”

“A common defect this morning,” said Bailey.

Wilderspin fetched a blanket from the bed and laid it over Osman. Mrs Green appeared at the doorway, declared that she could not bear to look, and continued looking without interruption.

From outside came the voices of the remaining pursuers. The river went on shining. A timber raft passed downstream, its two boatmen gazing up at the balcony with frank curiosity. The yellow dog emerged from the reeds, shook itself, and trotted towards the village as though the day’s arrangements had proved entirely normal.

The fair-haired youth put down his gun.

“I’ve killed him,” he said. “Good God. I’ve killed a man.”

Bailey looked at the broken bottle in his hand, at the blood soaking his immaculate bandages, at the knife lying within a foot of his chair, and at the covered shape upon the carpet. The blue medicine dripped steadily from his fingers.

Then he looked through the window at the river, where the empty boat from the plantation had broken loose and was turning slowly in the current.

“It’s just as well,” he said.

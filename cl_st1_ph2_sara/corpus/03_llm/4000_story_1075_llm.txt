### THE ENGLISH LABELS

If ever a young man was promoted by means of a portmanteau, a murder, and a mistake in personal identity, I am that young man.

At the time of which I write I was junior clerk in the house of Blenkinsop and Vane, corn merchants, Odessa. I had been with the firm eighteen months, drew a salary which did not encourage extravagance, and occupied a bedroom from whose window I could see, by leaning dangerously outward, one corner of the harbour and three-quarters of a chimney. Yet I had ambitions. I was twenty-three, English, and tolerably sure that the business world was waiting only for an opportunity to recognise me.

The opportunity came on a wet Monday morning.

“Robinson,” said Mr. Blenkinsop, looking over his spectacles, “can you keep your mouth shut?”

I answered that I could.

This was not strictly true. I could keep other people’s affairs as dark as a cellar, but my own importance generally escaped me in some form or another. Still, there are moments when a man rises above himself, and I felt that this was one of them.

“Quite shut?” said Mr. Vane.

“Quite, sir.”

The two partners exchanged glances. I drew myself up.

Mr. Blenkinsop unlocked a drawer and took out a folded paper.

“The firm has information,” he said, “that Ivan Dimidoff, of Solteff, is disposed to sell the whole produce of his southern estates.”

I nodded as if Dimidoff’s southern estates had occupied my thoughts for years.

“The amount is considerable,” continued Mr. Vane. “If we secure it before Koslov Brothers hear of the matter, we shall make a very handsome thing.”

“And if Koslov Brothers do hear?” I asked.

“We shall not.”

There was a meaning in the way he said it which gave me a most agreeable sensation down the back. I had often carried samples, copied contracts, and added columns of figures. I had never before been connected with anything mysterious.

“You will take the noon train to Solteff,” said Mr. Blenkinsop. “Mr. Dimidoff has been informed that a representative will call upon him. You will make the best terms possible, subject to these limits.”

He handed me the paper.

“You must not mention your business to a soul,” added Mr. Vane. “Dimidoff has enemies, and we have rivals. Go quietly. At Solteff wait until you are approached. You understand?”

“Perfectly, sir.”

As a matter of fact, I understood nothing beyond the pleasing certainty that I had suddenly become the confidential agent of a great commercial house. Partnership seemed no longer an impossible vision. I saw myself in a private office, giving sharp orders to bald-headed clerks.

“And, Robinson,” said Mr. Blenkinsop as I reached the door, “don’t get theatrical about it.”

This was uncalled for. I bowed with dignity and went to pack.

My luggage consisted of a brown leather portmanteau which had belonged to my uncle William. He had travelled with it in England, and it was decorated with labels from Liverpool, Birmingham, London, and half a dozen provincial hotels. I had often intended to scrape them off, but they gave the thing an experienced appearance. A portmanteau with labels, like a man with scars, is respected without inquiry.

At the station I bought my ticket in a careless voice and entered an empty compartment. I had taken every precaution. The paper containing my instructions was sewn into the lining of my coat; the name of Dimidoff had not passed my lips; and when a porter asked if I was travelling on business I replied, with great presence of mind, that I was visiting an aunt.

I had no aunt in Russia, but secrecy has its sacrifices.

Just as the train was moving, a man jumped into my compartment. He was short and broad, with a black beard clipped close to the chin and a pair of remarkably bright eyes. His coat was shabby, but his boots were good. I noticed this because a commercial clerk learns to judge solvency from boots.

He settled opposite me and looked first at my face, then at my portmanteau.

“English?” he asked in French.

I admitted it.

“You travel far?”

“Not very.”

“To Solteff, perhaps?”

I looked out of the window.

“Possibly.”

He smiled. It was not an agreeable smile.

“You English are cautious.”

“We find it pays.”

“And your luggage has travelled much.”

He bent forward to examine the labels. There was something confoundedly inquisitive in the act, and I pulled the portmanteau nearer with my foot.

“Birmingham,” he read. “Liverpool. London. Ah!”

There is no reason why a man should say “Ah!” over Birmingham unless he means mischief.

“You are interested in English geography?” I asked.

“In many English things.”

After that he pretended to sleep, but whenever I glanced at him his eyes were open by a fraction. At the first stopping-place he got out, spoke to a gendarme, and returned before the whistle sounded.

“Tom,” I said to myself, “Koslov Brothers are on the scent.”

The idea delighted me. To be watched by an ordinary traveller would have been annoying; to be watched by a rival firm was business romance. I pictured the bearded stranger telegraphing to Odessa: *Blenkinsop agent proceeding Solteff. Young, resolute, dangerous.*

I assumed as resolute an expression as I could and did not speak another word.

It was after dark when we reached Solteff. The station was a low, dirty building illuminated by two lamps which seemed ashamed of themselves. Beyond it lay a confusion of crooked streets, patches of snow, and black roofs. Three ragged drivers quarrelled over me before I had both feet on the platform.

While I defended my portmanteau, I saw the black-eyed man speaking earnestly to a tall official near the ticket barrier. Both looked in my direction.

The dickens! Koslov Brothers had influence.

An unshaven driver in a sheepskin coat seized my luggage.

“Mr. Robinson?” he asked.

I was startled.

“Who wants to know?”

“The gentlemen who expect you.”

This agreed with Mr. Blenkinsop’s instructions. I was to wait until approached. Clearly Dimidoff was conducting the affair with admirable caution.

“Lead on,” I said.

The driver placed my portmanteau in a small carriage and covered it with a rug. This precaution further raised my opinion of Dimidoff. We jolted out of the station yard, turned twice through narrow streets, and passed under an arch into a poorer quarter of the town.

“Is Mr. Dimidoff’s house much farther?” I asked.

The driver looked over his shoulder.

“No names, excellency.”

Quite right. I had nearly blundered at the beginning.

We proceeded for ten minutes, though the carriage made so many turns that we may have covered no more than two hundred yards. At last we stopped before a blank wall. A gate opened without any visible person touching it, the carriage entered, and the gate closed behind us.

The courtyard was dark. Two men appeared. One carried my portmanteau; the other conducted me through a side door and along a passage into a bare room with a barred window.

“You will wait,” he said.

“Certainly.”

He locked the door from the outside.

This was peculiar, but I reflected that wealthy Russian landowners have customs of their own. Dimidoff’s enemies might be watching his servants. Possibly all visitors were locked up until their honesty could be proved. In a country where tea is served in glasses, one must be prepared for anything.

The room contained a chair, a table, and a portrait of the Emperor turned with its face to the wall. I considered that last detail to be in doubtful taste. Then I heard voices beyond the door.

“He has come from England?”

“The labels prove it.”

“Was he followed?”

“The chief’s man watched him from Odessa.”

“Does he suspect?”

“He suspects nothing.”

This was very satisfactory. My discretion had been noticed.

Presently the door opened, and a pale young man entered. He could not have been more than twenty-five, yet his face was worn and his eyes had the feverish brightness of illness. He bowed gravely.

“You are expected,” he said.

“So I understand.”

“I am Alexis.”

“Robinson.”

He hesitated.

“You use your own name?”

I laughed lightly. I had read enough sensational fiction to know how a confidential interview should be managed.

“Names between men of business,” said I, “are useful chiefly on contracts.”

His pale face relaxed.

“You are cautious. It is well. Come.”

He led me through two passages and up a flight of stairs. At the top stood a thick-bearded man of enormous size, wearing a red shirt under his coat. He placed both hands upon my shoulders and kissed me on either cheek.

I confess that this staggered me.

“Welcome, brother!” he cried.

I had never met a land agent so affectionate. Still, I supposed he represented Dimidoff, and I did not wish English manners to injure the negotiation.

“Delighted, I’m sure,” I said, disentangling myself.

The room beyond was a surprise. After the poverty of the courtyard and the barred chamber, I had expected a cellar. Instead, I found myself in a handsome apartment with velvet curtains, gilded chairs, a long polished table, and a chandelier blazing with candles. About twenty persons were assembled, several of them masked. The remainder included two women, three students, a grey-haired man in military uniform, and a small fellow with spectacles who looked like a schoolmaster.

Every eye turned toward me.

The thick-bearded man raised his hand.

“Comrades,” he announced, “the commissioner from England!”

They rose together.

By Jove, thought I, Blenkinsop and Vane had certainly understated the importance of the transaction. I had expected a private talk with a landowner. Instead I was received by a council. Evidently Dimidoff’s estates were held by a syndicate, and I had been mistaken for some superior officer of our firm. There was no harm in that. If they chose to suppose that I was a commissioner, I was not bound to contradict them before prices had been discussed.

A masked man at the head of the table pointed to the chair on his right.

“The English representative will sit beside me.”

I obeyed.

“Your journey was safe?” he asked.

“Except for an inquisitive fellow in the train.”

A stir ran round the table.

“Describe him.”

“Short, black-bearded, bright eyes, shabby coat, good boots.”

“The police chief!” exclaimed Alexis.

There was a dead silence.

I began to perceive that the rivalry of Koslov Brothers had been more seriously regarded than I imagined. Perhaps “police chief” was a nickname given to their agent.

“He spoke to officials at the station,” I continued, determined to show that I had kept my eyes open.

The masked chairman leaned toward the giant.

“Double the guard. No one leaves.”

The doors were immediately locked.

This struck me as excessive, but I admired their business methods. A merchant who locked his partners in until an agreement was reached would save much time.

The chairman then addressed the assembly.

“Comrades, before we hear the report from England, there is a matter of discipline. Alexis Petrovitch, stand forth.”

The pale young man rose. He looked even paler than before.

“You have been accused,” said the chairman, “of correspondence with the agents of authority.”

Alexis gripped the back of his chair.

“It is false.”

“Three letters were found.”

“They were written to my sister.”

“Your sister is in Warsaw.”

“She has friends here.”

“You deny betrayal?”

“Before God!”

The schoolmaster pushed his spectacles up his nose.

“God,” he said, “is not recognised by the association.”

At this several persons nodded.

I listened with growing bewilderment. Correspondence, betrayal, agents of authority—these might relate to the grain contract, but the expressions seemed unnecessarily dramatic. It occurred to me that Alexis had perhaps been selling information to a rival concern. Commercial firms may be severe in Russia.

The chairman turned to me.

“The English commissioner has arrived at a solemn hour. In his country, how are traitors treated?”

This was awkward. I knew how Mr. Blenkinsop treated a clerk who disclosed prices: he dismissed him without a character. I therefore answered:

“In England such a man would certainly be dismissed.”

A murmur of approval passed round the table.

“The English decision is just,” said the chairman. “Let him be dismissed.”

Alexis sprang back.

“No! Hear me! I am innocent!”

Two men seized him.

I had not expected the sacking to occur in my presence. Still less had I expected the condemned clerk to show such terror. He struggled furiously as they dragged him toward a curtained door.

“I served the cause!” he cried. “Nicholas, you know it! I carried the papers across the frontier! I hid Vera from the gendarmes! For pity’s sake—”

The curtain fell behind him.

I looked round the table. Nobody moved. The chairman examined a document. The woman opposite me twisted a ring on her finger. From the next room came a thud, a choking cry, and then a sound like a chair falling.

A minute later the thick-bearded giant returned, wiping a long knife upon a handkerchief.

“Dismissed,” he said.

It is curious how swiftly the human mind can alter its view of a situation. One instant I was the confidential agent of Blenkinsop and Vane among eccentric Russian grain dealers. The next I was seated in a lodge of Nihilists, had assisted at a trial, and had recommended a young man’s death.

My mouth became dry. Every label on my portmanteau rose before my imagination like a witness for the prosecution. *Liverpool. London. Birmingham.* They had mistaken me for an English revolutionary. If I declared myself to be Tom Robinson, clerk, the best I could hope for was disbelief. The worst had just gone through the curtain.

“Tom,” I said inwardly, “if you wish to see Odessa again, you must become the English commissioner and remain so until further notice.”

The chairman folded his hands.

“We may now proceed to the report. Comrade Berger, you have brought the instructions of the central council?”

So that was my name—Berger.

“I have,” said I.

“Written instructions?”

This was a trap, or appeared to be one.

“Not in a form which would inconvenience the police.”

There was a second murmur of approval. The grey-haired officer smiled.

“Excellent. The English have learned caution.”

My courage returned by a degree. Ignorance, when delivered firmly, often passes for reserve.

“You came through Birmingham?” asked the schoolmaster.

I remembered the label.

“Among other places.”

“The Birmingham group remains active?”

“Unusually so.”

“How many members?”

I crossed my legs and considered him.

“My dear sir, do you suppose I carried a list through three frontiers?”

The schoolmaster flushed. The chairman raised a hand.

“The commissioner is right. No numbers.”

“Yet we require guidance,” said the woman with the ring. “Does England approve the enterprise concerning the Livadia?”

I had no notion whether the Livadia was a palace, a newspaper, or a lady. The whole table waited.

“England,” I replied, “approves no enterprise until those on the spot have satisfied themselves that the risk is proportionate to the return.”

This was a maxim of Mr. Vane’s. It had been employed in connection with damp barley, but it sounded well enough.

The officer struck the table.

“The return is liberty!”

“And the risk?” I asked.

“Our lives.”

“Exactly. You have therefore a special interest in calculating it.”

Several heads nodded. The chairman regarded me with respect.

“You speak with the cold wisdom of your nation.”

I bowed.

“Business is business.”

The effect of this innocent phrase was unfortunate.

“Business?” cried the woman. “Do you call the liberation of forty million souls a business?”

“I call any organised undertaking a business.”

“It is a cause!”

“All the more reason to conduct it properly.”

The giant laughed so loudly that the chandelier shook.

“He is right, Vera! We Russians make speeches; the English keep accounts. That is why their ships are everywhere.”

The danger passed, but the questions continued.

“Has the correspondence with Paris been renewed?”

“Indirectly.”

“Through whom?”

“A person whose name I cannot reveal.”

“Is the press at Liverpool still available?”

“If necessary.”

“What of the rifles promised from Manchester?”

“Delayed.”

“By the police?”

“By circumstances.”

“When will they arrive?”

“When the circumstances cease.”

This answer produced a solemn silence. I began to discover that a man may hide a great emptiness of knowledge behind a small fullness of manner. Whenever I was pressed, I looked offended; whenever they asked for names, I invoked security; whenever they sought dates, I spoke of developments. Before half an hour had passed I had become, in their eyes, a model conspirator.

Unfortunately, success made me rash.

The chairman asked whether the English association had funds for an immediate operation. Money was a subject I understood.

“That depends,” I said. “What quantity is required, what security can you offer, and what discount do you expect?”

The schoolmaster stared.

“Security?”

“Every advance requires security.”

“We offer the sacred honour of the association.”

“In Odessa,” said I, “that would not cover postage.”

There was a dangerous movement round the table. The giant ceased smiling.

The chairman spoke slowly.

“Comrade Berger, we have been told that you possess a peculiar humour. Do not carry it too far.”

I apologised. Thereafter I handled finance with less commercial frankness.

Food and wine were brought in at midnight. I ate little, though I drank a glass of brandy. My mind was occupied with escape. The windows opened upon a sheer wall; the doors were guarded; and my revolver, a small six-chambered weapon which I carried when travelling, was in my coat pocket but would be of little service against twenty people.

The wisest course was to continue the imposture until they released me. Yet they had doubled the guard because of the police chief, and every member was to remain until dawn. At dawn, I gathered, the “enterprise concerning the Livadia” would be discussed in detail. One definite question might finish me.

At half past one the chairman called an interval. Groups formed about the room. The giant attached himself to me.

“You have seen much,” he said.

“More than I expected.”

“And you have dismissed many traitors?”

I glanced at the curtained door.

“Not personally.”

“In England you leave such work to committees?”

“Generally to the head of a department.”

He pondered this.

“Your organisation is wonderful.”

He then asked me whether dynamite manufactured in Birmingham was superior to that made in Liège. I replied that the Birmingham article had the advantage of finish, while the continental product was sometimes preferred on account of price. This was pure invention, but it satisfied him completely.

Meanwhile Vera, the woman with the ring, watched me. She was handsome in a severe fashion and had the unpleasant habit of looking as if she could hear one’s thoughts.

At last she came nearer.

“You do not resemble your portrait,” she said.

“A bad photograph.”

“You appear younger.”

“An active life preserves the complexion.”

“And your eyes were darker.”

“Railway travel.”

The giant laughed again, but Vera did not.

“You speak Russian with an accent,” she continued.

“Naturally.”

“Yet Berger’s mother was Russian.”

“His mother may have been a Chinese princess for all the assistance it gives him in grammar.”

The pronoun slipped out. Vera’s eyes flashed.

“Gives *him*?”

I raised my glass.

“One sometimes speaks of oneself objectively. It is an English habit.”

“I have lived in London. I never observed it.”

“My dear madam, London is large.”

She would have pressed the matter, but at that instant three knocks sounded from below: two close together and one after an interval.

Every conversation stopped.

The giant drew a pistol.

Again came the knocks.

The chairman whispered to a guard, who disappeared. We waited in complete silence. My heart beat so loudly that I feared Vera would object to its accent.

Footsteps approached along the passage. The door opened, and the guard entered with a tall, thin man in a travelling cloak. Snow lay upon his shoulders. His face was narrow, his hair fair, and a scar crossed his left eyebrow.

He halted on seeing the assembly.

Then he saw me.

“What is this?” he demanded in English.

No one answered.

The stranger threw back his cloak.

“I am Gustave Berger,” he said.

I have known many uncomfortable moments. I have presented an invoice containing a mistake of three thousand roubles to Mr. Blenkinsop. I have sat upon a lady’s bonnet in a public carriage. I have once addressed a customs officer as a thief before learning that he understood English. But never have I felt quite as I did when the real Gustave Berger entered that room.

Every face turned from him to me.

The chairman rose.

“You are Gustave Berger?”

“I am.”

He produced a packet from inside his coat and flung it on the table.

“Here are my papers and the seals of the English council. My train was stopped by snow. Who is that man?”

Nobody answered because everybody was waiting for me to do so.

I stood up. There are occasions when a lie must be supported by another lie, as a damaged warehouse is propped up by timber.

“An obvious police trick,” I said.

Berger stared.

“What?”

“This fellow,” I told the chairman, “has followed me from England. He has stolen documents and now arrives at the convenient moment to create confusion.”

Berger uttered a short laugh.

“Search him.”

The giant came toward me. I stepped back.

“I object.”

“That need not trouble us,” said Vera.

Two men caught my arms. The giant searched my coat and found the railway ticket, thirty-eight roubles, a pocket-book, the key of my portmanteau, and finally the lining where Mr. Blenkinsop’s instructions had been sewn.

He ripped it open.

The chairman read the paper aloud.

“‘Purchase not above the limit of—’ What is this? ‘Freight to Odessa—quality of southern grain—Dimidoff—’”

There are documents which will not bear a revolutionary interpretation. My instructions were among them.

Berger seized the pocket-book and opened it.

“Thomas Robinson,” he read. “Clerk to Blenkinsop and Vane, Corn Merchants, Odessa.”

The silence that followed was worse than noise.

At last the schoolmaster said, “He is a spy.”

“I am nothing of the sort,” I replied. “I came to buy grain.”

“Grain!” cried Vera.

“Precisely. I was met at the station by one of your men, brought here without being asked a sensible question, embraced by that hairy lunatic, and invited to advise you upon matters of which I know less than I do of Chinese poetry.”

The giant growled.

“You deceived us.”

“You mistook me.”

“You answered to the name of Berger.”

“After I had seen what happened to a man who displeased you! What the deuce would you have answered to?”

This checked them for a moment. Even the chairman appeared to see some reason in it.

Berger did not.

“He has heard names, plans, and addresses,” he said. “Whether he came by accident or design makes no difference.”

Vera nodded.

“The association cannot permit him to leave.”

The word “dismissal” was not used, but it hung in the air.

I tried commerce.

“Gentlemen—and madam—let us look at this practically. You have inconvenienced me and damaged my coat. I, on the other hand, may have caused some embarrassment by my presence. Let the accounts balance. Return my portmanteau, point out Mr. Dimidoff’s residence, and we will say no more about it.”

The giant’s beard spread in a grin.

“He bargains for his life.”

“Certainly. It is the most valuable article I possess.”

The chairman spoke to Berger in a low voice. Berger replied sharply. Others joined them. I caught the words “police,” “before dawn,” and “no trace.” The curtain beside the inner room moved slightly in a draught.

I remembered Alexis.

“Tom,” I thought, “the time for negotiation is nearly over.”

My revolver had been returned to my coat pocket after the search, because the giant had overlooked it in his excitement over the sewn paper. His error was excusable. The weapon was flat, and I had carried it in an inner pocket under my left arm.

The chairman turned.

“Thomas Robinson, your arrival may have been accidental. Your conduct since arriving has shown courage. We regret the necessity—”

“I am much obliged,” I said. “I always prefer a compliment before being murdered.”

“Take him,” said Berger.

The giant advanced, accompanied by two others.

I drew the revolver.

It is astonishing how quickly a large room becomes small when a pistol appears in it.

“Stay where you are!”

The giant stopped. The others scattered from the line of fire.

Berger alone moved toward me.

“He will not shoot,” he said. “An English clerk!”

Now I may be vain, and I was certainly inexperienced, but I have never liked a man to decide what I will not do.

“Keep back.”

Berger sprang.

I fired.

The report shattered a mirror and filled the room with smoke. Berger spun round and fell against the table. Whether the bullet struck his shoulder or chest I could not tell.

At once the giant flung a chair. It caught my wrist, and the revolver flew away. Somebody seized me from behind. I struck backward, received a blow upon the temple, and went down among a confusion of boots, shouts, and overturned furniture.

From far below came a crash.

Then another.

A voice thundered, “Police!”

Shots followed in the passage. The chandelier swung wildly overhead. Vera screamed an order. The chairman tore papers from the table and thrust them into a candle flame. The giant lifted me by the collar, perhaps intending to carry out my dismissal at once, but the door burst inward and men in uniform poured through it.

I saw the black-eyed stranger from the train at their head, a sword in his hand.

“So,” he cried, looking directly at me, “we have the Englishman!”

This seemed the last injustice.

“He is not Berger!” shouted someone.

I attempted to explain that I had never wanted to be Berger, that I was in the corn trade, and that Dimidoff was expecting me. Unfortunately the room turned upside down before I had finished. The floor struck the back of my head, and all business ceased.

When I recovered consciousness, I was in a narrow bed with a clean bandage around my skull and a disagreeable taste in my mouth. Morning light entered through an unbarred window. A police officer sat beside the stove, reading my pocket-book.

It was the black-eyed man.

“You again,” I murmured.

He closed the book.

“Yes, Mr. Thomas Robinson.”

“If you belong to Koslov Brothers,” I said, “your methods are disgraceful.”

He laughed.

“My name is Colonel Gregor Ivanovitch. I am chief of police at Solteff.”

This explained the boots.

He gave me water and advised me not to move too quickly.

“We have watched the society for months,” he said. “We knew an English agent called Berger was expected, but we possessed no reliable description. When I saw your luggage at Odessa—English labels, many English towns—and observed your secrecy, I concluded that you might be the man.”

“So did they.”

“Exactly. Their driver reached you before mine. We followed, surrounded the house, and waited in hope that Berger would arrive. He did.”

“Is he dead?”

“No. The bullet passed through his shoulder. He is under guard.”

I confess that I was relieved. Shooting a man in self-defence is one thing; remembering that you have killed him is another.

“And the others?”

“Captured, with the exception of two who escaped across the roofs. We found sufficient papers to occupy the government for a year.”

“What of Alexis?”

The colonel’s expression altered.

“Dead.”

I looked at the stove.

“He was innocent,” Ivanovitch added. “The letters were indeed to his sister.”

For a while neither of us spoke. The adventure had possessed, until then, the wild unreality of a nightmare or a stage melodrama. The thought of the pale young man beyond the curtain stripped it of that quality. I had said “dismissed,” meaning no more than the loss of employment, and he had died by it. I knew I was not truly responsible, yet the word has never since sounded harmless to me.

The colonel appeared to guess my thoughts.

“Your answer did not condemn him,” he said. “The decision had been made before you entered.”

“That is some comfort.”

“And your evidence may condemn his murderers.”

“That is less cheerful, though I suppose it is proper.”

He shrugged in the Russian fashion.

After breakfast he returned my belongings. The portmanteau had acquired a bullet hole beside the Birmingham label and a dark stain upon Liverpool.

“You should remove those labels,” said Ivanovitch.

“Not for a fortune,” I replied.

At noon, with my head aching and my coat stitched badly across the lining, I remembered the reason for my journey.

“Colonel, what day is it?”

“Tuesday.”

“Dimidoff!”

I tried to get out of bed.

The colonel pushed me back.

“You are not fit to walk.”

“I was sent to negotiate for his grain. Koslov Brothers may already be with him.”

“After last night, you think of grain?”

“That is what I am paid to think of.”

He studied me with amusement.

“You English are a curious nation.”

“So I was told last night. Kindly procure a carriage.”

Two hours later I was driven to the residence of Ivan Dimidoff. It proved to be a plain white house on the respectable side of the town, less than a mile from the lodge. A servant showed me into a sunny library where a stout old gentleman sat beside a samovar.

“Mr. Robinson?” he said. “You are late.”

“I have had a slight misunderstanding.”

“So I hear.”

Colonel Ivanovitch had telegraphed him. The old gentleman examined my bandaged head, torn cuff, and powder-marked coat.

“You went to the Nihilists instead of coming here?”

“They came for me.”

“And they mistook you for an English agent?”

“Yes.”

“And the police made the same mistake?”

“Temporarily.”

Dimidoff laughed until the tea-glasses rattled.

Then we discussed grain.

Perhaps the blow on my head sharpened my abilities; perhaps, having faced a revolutionary council, I was no longer frightened of a landowner. At any rate, I conducted the negotiation with a firmness which astonished even myself. Dimidoff demanded an impossible price. I pointed out the cost of freight. He spoke of quality. I mentioned damp. He referred to rival offers. I asked to see them. He called me impertinent. I said that an impertinent buyer with ready credit was preferable to a polite buyer without it.

For four hours we disputed every clause. At the end he signed for the entire produce of the southern estates at a price two points below Mr. Vane’s maximum.

“You are a hard young man,” Dimidoff said.

“I was taught business last night by people who objected strongly to unsecured advances.”

He laughed again and gave me dinner.

When I returned to Odessa, Mr. Blenkinsop and Mr. Vane received me in the private office. The story had preceded me by telegram, though several details had been confused. One report stated that I had led the police raid. Another that I had been president of the society for three years. A third that I had killed Berger in a duel over the grain market.

I placed the signed contract on the desk.

Mr. Vane read it twice.

“You secured the whole amount?”

“Yes, sir.”

“At this figure?”

“Yes, sir.”

Mr. Blenkinsop looked at my bandage.

“And in the course of doing so you entered a Nihilist lodge, witnessed a murder, impersonated an international agent, shot the genuine article, and assisted the police?”

“I should hardly say assisted, sir. I was unconscious during the useful part.”

Mr. Vane coughed into his hand.

“We have considered your position with the firm.”

Here, for one terrible instant, the word *dismissed* entered my mind.

“We require someone to manage the new Solteff account,” he continued. “The salary will be doubled, with a commission on purchases.”

I believe I thanked them properly. My memory is uncertain. I know that Mr. Blenkinsop shook my hand and advised me, when next sent on confidential business, to distinguish between a landowner’s representative and a political assassin before entering his carriage.

It was sound advice, and I have followed it.

The portmanteau stands in my dressing room to this day. My wife has repeatedly urged me to throw it away, for the leather is cut, the lock is broken, and the old English labels have become nearly illegible. But I retain it. The bullet hole remains beside Birmingham, and across Liverpool is the stain which no amount of cleaning will remove.

Whenever a new clerk joins our firm—and I am a partner now—I sometimes show him that battered article.

“Take care of your luggage, my boy,” I say. “It may carry you farther than you intend.”

And if he laughs, as young clerks do, I let him.

After all, I once thought myself a great secret negotiator because I had been told to keep a grain purchase dark. Before the next day was out I had voted, in perfect innocence, for the dismissal of a man, instructed an international conspiracy, and been very nearly dismissed myself.

There are words, like portmanteaux, which acquire dangerous labels.

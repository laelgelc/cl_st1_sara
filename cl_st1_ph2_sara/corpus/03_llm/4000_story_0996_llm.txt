## The Last Instruction

The planet turned beneath them, gray and silent.

No oceans shone through the atmosphere. No clouds moved over the continents. There was only dust, a thin veil of radioactive particles drifting from pole to pole. The land below had been melted and hammered flat. Mountains had run like wax. Cities had become dark stains.

Nothing moved or stirred.

Everything was silent, dead.

Captain Rinn stood at the forward screen with his hands behind his back. The cold yellow sun lay at the edge of the planet, throwing long shadows from the ruined ridges.

“Another circuit,” he said.

Fomar looked up from the control board. “We have made six.”

“One more.”

“The readings will not change.”

The Captain did not answer. Fomar touched the automatic controls. The ship banked, settling into a lower survey orbit.

Nasha watched the radiation dials. Their red needles trembled near the upper marks. “The upper atmosphere is clearing,” she said. “At this rate the surface might be safe in two thousand years.”

“Safe for what?” Dorle asked.

No one replied.

Tance, the archaeologist, leaned close to the screen. He was a narrow man with quick hands and pale, restless eyes. Since they had entered the system he had hardly slept. He had studied every broken plain and fused riverbed as if something might rise from the slag and explain itself.

“There,” he said suddenly. “Reduce speed.”

Fomar glanced at him. “What is it?”

“A geometric pattern. North of the crater.”

The image enlarged. At first Nasha saw only gray country crossed by black cracks. Then a group of lines came into focus. Streets. Foundations. The lower halves of walls.

“A city,” Tance said.

“A stain,” Dorle corrected.

“Once a city.”

Captain Rinn moved nearer. “Can we land?”

“Radiation is high,” Nasha said. “But our suits can manage five or six day-periods. Perhaps more, if we stay out of the deep craters.”

Fomar examined the wind indicators. “The dust is moving east. There is a stone plain on the western side. Firm enough for the landing gear.”

The Captain nodded. “Take us down.”

The ship passed through the yellow haze.

Below them the city opened like a dead skeleton. A river had once divided it. The water was gone. Two rows of bridge piers stood in the dry channel like broken teeth. Towers had collapsed into their own cellars. A few walls remained upright, stripped and polished by centuries of poisoned wind.

The landing jets lifted a slow cloud of ash.

When the ship touched the ground, the sound rolled across the ruins and came back faintly from the hills. It was the first sound the city had heard in a very long time.

For several minutes the crew waited.

Nothing answered.

Captain Rinn studied the screens. “No movement?”

“None,” Fomar said.

“No transmissions?”

“Nothing above static.”

Tance was already fastening his suit. “I want to see the central ruins.”

Dorle gave a dry laugh. “They have waited perhaps a thousand years. They can wait until we test the ground.”

“There may be records.”

“There may be a hole.”

“A hole can be a record.”

Dorle lifted a hand weapon from the rack. “That is why archaeologists seldom command ships.”

“And why soldiers seldom discover anything.”

“Enough,” the Captain said. “Nasha, Dorle, Tance—you will make the first surface examination. Stay within sight of the ship. Two hours.”

Tance sealed his helmet. “Two hours.”

“Not two hours in each direction.”

Tance looked disappointed. “No.”

The lock opened.

The three explorers went down the ramp into the city.

Their boots sank a little into the gray silt. It was finer than sand and rose around them in slow curtains. Nasha switched on her suit filter and heard the pump begin to work.

The sun hung low and cold. It gave light without warmth.

Dorle knelt and pressed a metal probe into the ground. “Fused underneath,” he said. “This top layer is wind deposit.”

Tance walked to the remains of a wall. He touched its black surface. The stone had melted, run downward, and hardened in long glassy folds.

“Atomic fission weapons,” he said.

“You sound pleased,” Dorle said.

“I am not pleased.”

“You always sound pleased when you identify a method of death.”

Tance turned from the wall. “A natural disaster is anonymous. This was done deliberately. The distinction matters.”

“To the dead?”

“To history.”

Nasha looked along the empty street. Foundations extended on both sides, square and orderly. Here and there a metal beam projected from the rubble. All exposed surfaces were bent in the same direction.

“The blast came from the east,” she said.

Tance nodded. “And another from the south. Perhaps several.”

“Can you tell which side won?”

Tance surveyed the city. “Yes.”

Dorle waited.

“Neither.”

They moved on.

Near the dry river they found the shadow of a person burned into a slab of stone. The body itself was gone. Only the darker outline remained: head, shoulders, one arm raised.

Tance bent over it.

“Do not touch,” Nasha said. “The stone is active.”

He straightened slowly.

They found no bones. No insects. No seeds trapped in cracks. The soil analyzer reported carbon, metals, glass, and complex poisons, but no organic activity. Even the microscopic life was gone.

The planet had not merely been defeated. It had been sterilized.

When they returned, Captain Rinn was waiting at the foot of the ramp.

“Well?” he asked.

Tance removed his helmet. “There was a large civilization. Industrial, urban, technically advanced. The city was destroyed by multiple atomic attacks.”

“Survivors?”

“No.”

“Shelters?”

“Possibly. But no surface life has remained to open them.”

Nasha handed Fomar the sample case. “The contamination is uniform. Whatever happened was not local.”

Fomar put the case into the analysis chamber. “Then our survey is finished.”

Tance stiffened. “We have examined two streets.”

“We came to determine whether the planet could support a station.”

“And it cannot,” Dorle said. “Unless the Government Receiving Station wishes to employ ghosts.”

The Captain looked back at the ruins. “We will remain until the next day-period. Tance may conduct a final search. Then we leave.”

Tance smiled unsuccessfully. “Thank you.”

“It is not a reward,” Rinn said. “I want certainty.”

Beyond them the city stood in the yellow light.

Nothing moved.

Nothing watched them.

Or so they believed.

* * *

They left the next morning.

Fomar raised the ship gently, using only the lower jets. The gray plain dropped away. The city spread beneath them: streets without houses, bridges without water, foundations without rooms.

Tance stood at the rear screen.

“There,” he said. “That circular structure.”

Dorle turned. “What structure?”

“Near the northern ridge. I saw it yesterday.”

The ship climbed through eight hundred meters.

A white flash appeared below.

For an instant it was no larger than a spark. Then the forward screen filled with light. The ship lurched sideways. Metal screamed through the hull. Nasha was thrown against the navigation frame.

Alarms began together.

“Power loss in two!” Fomar shouted. “Steering line cut!”

Another flash rose from the city.

Captain Rinn struck the emergency descent control. “Back to the ground!”

The second projectile passed above them. Its shock wave spun the ship. Fomar fought the manual levers while Nasha dragged herself upright. The floor tilted sharply.

“Landing field is behind us,” Dorle said.

“We will not reach it.”

“Use the riverbed!”

Fomar turned the remaining jet. The ship dropped toward the dead river, trailing smoke and fragments.

“Brace!”

They struck tail-first. The landing supports folded. The hull slid through ash, hit a bridge pier, and stopped with a violent jolt.

For a moment there was no sound except the alarms.

Then Captain Rinn said, “Report.”

His voice was low.

Nasha unfastened herself. Blood ran down one side of his uniform. A strip of metal had entered below his ribs.

“Do not move,” she said.

“I was not planning to.”

Fomar crawled under the control table. “Main pile is stable. Three propulsion cells are dead. Communications—” He opened a panel. Smoke drifted out. “Communications destroyed.”

Dorle checked the hull indicators. “No breach in the living section. Storage compartment open. We have lost food, one pressure suit, and most of Tance’s digging equipment.”

Tance was on his knees near the rear wall. He looked at Dorle. “Most?”

“One brush remains.”

The Captain tried to smile. His face had become gray. “An economy ordered by the universe.”

Nasha cut away the cloth around his wound. The metal fragment was deep. With every breath a thin line of blood appeared.

“Medical couch,” she said.

Dorle and Tance lifted him. He made no sound until they set him down. Then his fingers closed on Nasha’s wrist.

“The shot came from the city,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Survivors?”

“Perhaps.”

Fomar came from the control compartment. “Not survivors. The first shot struck our propulsion bank at long range. The second anticipated our turn. That was a fixed battery with telescopic sights and automatic computation.”

Tance stared at him. “A machine?”

“Most likely.”

“After all this time?”

“The machine does not know about time.”

Captain Rinn’s eyes shifted to the screen. Through the cracked plate they could see the broken piers of the old bridge.

“Can the ship be repaired?”

Fomar hesitated.

“Answer.”

“Yes. If the primary thrust ring is not fractured. Four or five day-periods.”

“And if it is fractured?”

“We stay.”

The Captain released Nasha’s wrist. “Find out.”

Fomar and Dorle went aft.

Nasha prepared an injection. The Captain watched her.

“Will that make me well?”

“It will stop the pain.”

“That was not my question.”

She said nothing.

He looked toward Tance. “You wanted more time in the city.”

Tance lowered his eyes.

“It appears,” Rinn said, “that your request has been granted.”

Nasha gave him the injection.

Outside, gray dust settled over the wrecked ship. High on the northern ridge, something turned a few degrees. It paused. It listened.

Then it became still.

* * *

The primary ring had split along one section, but Fomar believed it could be joined.

“We have alloy sheets,” he said. “We can melt them down and cast braces. The work will be crude.”

“Will crude work?” Dorle asked.

“If nothing fires at us while we rise.”

“That appears to be the problem.”

They stood in the engine space. Broken conduits hung from the walls. The damaged propulsion cells had sprayed insulating fluid over the floor.

Fomar pointed to a row of pressure tanks. “We can restore enough thrust to reach space. But if the weapon struck us once, it will strike us again.”

“Then we must find it.”

“We know where it is,” Tance said. “The circular structure near the ridge.”

“We know where you think it is.”

“I saw it turn before the shot.”

Dorle examined him. “Why did you not mention that?”

“I thought it was a trick of the light.”

“Light rarely fires atomic shells.”

Nasha entered the engine space. “The Captain wants us.”

They found Rinn propped up on the medical couch. His breathing had become shallow. A recorder stood beside him.

“I have transmitted an emergency signal,” he said.

Fomar looked surprised. “The transmitter is gone.”

“The hand unit.”

“It cannot reach beyond the system.”

“No. But it can establish that we survived the landing.” The Captain stopped to breathe. “If another expedition enters local space, the automatic repeater may warn them.”

“It may also attract the gun,” Dorle said.

“That cannot make our position much worse.”

Nasha checked the medical gauge. The Captain saw her expression.

“How long?” he asked.

“Perhaps one day-period.”

“Then do not spend it making polite answers.”

She turned away.

Rinn looked at the others. “The ship must be repaired. At the same time, the weapon must be examined. Nasha will take command.”

Dorle’s eyebrows rose. “Nasha?”

“She has senior navigation rank.”

“I am aware of her rank.”

“Then your difficulty is solved.”

Dorle folded his arms. “Yes, Captain.”

“Tance goes to the ridge. He may recognize the structure. Dorle goes with him.”

“And Nasha?” Tance asked.

“She decides.”

Nasha looked from the dying Captain to the city. “Fomar can begin the repairs alone. I will go.”

Fomar shook his head. “I need Dorle.”

“You need your tools more.”

“That is not certain.”

“It will have to be.”

The Captain touched the recorder. “If you find living inhabitants, do not assume they are hostile.”

Dorle glanced toward the torn side of the ship.

Rinn continued, “If you find a machine, do not assume it is simple.”

Tance sealed his sample bag. “We will return before dark.”

“You will return when you understand what shot us.”

The Captain’s hand rested on the recorder control.

“This is Captain Rinn, survey vessel—” Static broke across the speaker. “We have landed on the third planet of the yellow primary. Surface conditions indicate complete biological extinction. Our vessel has been attacked by an unknown installation. We are attempting—”

He staggered.

Nasha caught him before he fell from the couch.

The recorder continued to turn.

* * *

They crossed the riverbed on foot.

Tance led them north through the ruins. The ridge was only four kilometers away, but rubble blocked the direct route. They passed through squares filled with tilted slabs and streets buried under drifts of metallic ash.

Nasha carried the radiation counter. Dorle carried a heavy cutting torch and two hand weapons. Tance carried the surviving brush.

“You brought it,” Dorle said.

“It weighs almost nothing.”

“It may save us.”

“You see? Archaeology has military value.”

The air was quiet. Their suit radios magnified each breath and scrape of boots. No wind moved at ground level.

They reached a wide avenue. At its end stood the remains of a public building. Six black columns supported part of a roof. On the wall behind them, a carved relief had survived.

Tance stopped.

The relief showed a group of figures gathered around a globe. Their hands touched across the surface. Above them flew narrow machines with long wings.

“An assembly hall,” he said. “Or a temple.”

Dorle looked at the flying shapes. “Aircraft.”

“Yes.”

“The gun may see those as targets.”

Nasha studied the figures. They were not human. Their bodies were low and compact, with three arms and broad heads. But the gesture was clear. The joined hands were not threatening.

“They had ideals,” she said.

“Every civilization has ideals,” Dorle replied. “They are useful during speeches.”

Tance brushed dust from an inscription below the relief. “And wars?”

“Especially during wars.”

They went on.

As the ridge drew near, the circular structure appeared above the rubble. It was a low dome set on a square foundation. Much of the outer shell had been blackened. A long barrel projected through a slit.

The barrel was aimed at the sky.

They crouched behind a fallen wall.

Tance raised a viewing lens. “That is it.”

Dorle adjusted the magnification. “Large bore. Automatic loading chamber beneath. The dome sits on a rotation track.”

“Is it active?” Nasha asked.

As if in answer, the dome moved.

It turned slowly to the west. The barrel elevated. For several seconds it held its position.

Something passed high overhead, a scrap of metal still circling the planet after the ancient war. The gun followed it with calm precision.

A flash erupted from the barrel.

Seconds later a point of light appeared in the upper sky.

The gun lowered again.

Dorle stared. “Active.”

Tance’s voice became almost a whisper. “It has been waiting all this time.”

“No,” Nasha said. “It has been working.”

They remained behind the wall. The dome did not turn toward them.

Dorle lifted one hand above the stones.

Nothing happened.

He stood.

“Get down,” Nasha said.

The gun remained still.

Dorle took several steps into the avenue. “It does not care about us.”

“Perhaps it has not seen you.”

“It saw an object several hundred kilometers above us.”

He walked toward the ridge.

Nasha and Tance followed more cautiously.

The weapon was larger than it had appeared from the ship. The barrel alone was thirty meters long. Armor enclosed its sides. Behind the dome, vents opened and closed as a cooling system breathed.

At the foundation they found a hatch sealed by heavy clamps. Dorle touched the metal.

“No rust,” he said.

Tance pointed to a strip of bright alloy. “It repairs itself.”

Tiny manipulators moved within a recessed track. One carried a drop of lubricant. Another tested a contact point and withdrew.

“The city died,” Nasha said. “This did not.”

Dorle walked around the base. “I see no personnel entrance.”

“There must have been one originally.”

“Perhaps the makers were loaded in through the barrel.”

Tance ignored him. “The control chamber may be below.”

They found a maintenance passage under a slab of fallen masonry. Its door had been crushed open by the ancient blast. Beyond it, steps descended into darkness.

Dorle switched on his lamp. “Stay behind me.”

The stairs led to a circular room beneath the dome. Racks of machinery filled the walls. Relays clicked. Indicator lamps glowed behind cloudy glass. The floor vibrated each time a motor corrected the position of the barrel.

A map occupied one side of the chamber. Colored lines divided continents and seas that no longer existed.

Tance wiped dust from the map. “Defense regions.”

Nasha examined the control panels. “Can you shut it down?”

Dorle opened a cabinet. Inside, sealed modules waited in neat rows. “Perhaps. But if I break the wrong circuit, the magazine may react.”

“Atomic warheads?”

He checked the markings. “At least twelve in the loading assembly. More below, probably.”

Tance had moved to the rear of the room. “There is another door.”

This door was not armored. It was decorated.

Metal figures ran around its frame. They carried instruments, tools, tablets, and small carved animals. At the center was an emblem of an open eye above a flame.

Tance pressed his hand against it.

The door slid aside.

A broad passage extended downward. Unlike the gun chamber, it was silent. The walls were clean. Soft lamps came on ahead of them, one after another.

Dorle lifted his weapon.

“No one is there,” Nasha said.

“The lights disagree.”

“The lights are automatic.”

“So is the gun.”

They descended.

At the bottom they entered a great underground hall.

Rows of sealed cases stretched into the distance. Statues stood on raised platforms. Cabinets contained rolls of thin metallic film. There were books made from sheets of plastic, cylinders, recording crystals, paintings enclosed behind transparent shields. Along one wall, machines waited before a bank of dark viewing screens.

Tance took two steps and stopped.

His breath sounded loudly in the radio.

“What is it?” Dorle asked.

“Everything.”

Nasha moved toward a case. Within it lay small objects of colored glass: animals, houses, figures holding hands. A label was fixed beneath each one.

“A museum?” she asked.

“More than that.” Tance passed from case to case. “Literature. Music. Image records. Scientific documents. Religious texts. This section appears to contain legal history. Those cylinders may be films.”

“Films of what?”

“Their lives.”

Dorle looked back toward the passage. “Guarded by an atomic cannon.”

Tance turned. His face had changed. The excitement was still there, but it had become solemn.

“They knew the cities might be destroyed,” he said. “So they placed their culture underground. They built the vault to survive.”

“And put the gun above it,” Nasha said.

“A guardian.”

Dorle glanced at the statues. “A dragon.”

Tance looked at him.

“In old stories,” Dorle continued, “a dragon sits over treasure. It kills anyone who approaches from the air, presumably because dragons are traditional authorities on flight.”

“The gun is not guarding this place from us,” Nasha said. “It did not fire when we came on foot.”

“It guards against enemy aircraft.”

“The enemy is gone.”

“Yes.”

“The people it defended are gone.”

“Yes.”

Nasha looked along the endless cases. “Then what is it guarding against?”

Dorle answered, “Instructions do not require an enemy.”

They explored the archive.

The films showed cities under bright skies, rivers full of ships, crowds moving through markets. The people of the planet wore loose coverings and decorated their three arms with bands of metal. They raised animals. They built gardens on rooftops. They argued in assembly rooms.

One film showed children playing with a ball in shallow water.

Tance ran it twice.

Another film was military. Columns of aircraft crossed the sky. Soldiers loaded missiles into launch frames. Maps flashed. Borders changed color.

Later films showed shelters, mobilization orders, factories working through the night. The same emblem appeared again and again: an open eye above a flame.

“The northern coalition,” Tance said. “This must have been its national archive.”

“How can you know?” Dorle asked.

“The symbol is on the weapons and government records.”

“Perhaps it means peace.”

“It probably did, at first.”

Nasha found a set of historical charts. Even without knowing the language, the progression was clear. New weapons appeared. Their ranges increased. Cities spread outward. Defensive circles multiplied.

Near the end, the maps were almost hidden under overlapping target lines.

Tance stood before the final chart. “They expected attack from every direction.”

“Were they attacked?” Dorle asked.

“Yes.”

“Then they were correct.”

Nasha looked at the blackened image of the planet. “Being correct did not help them.”

A signal sounded in her helmet.

Fomar’s voice came faintly over the short-range set. “Nasha. Can you hear me?”

“Barely.”

“The Captain is failing. Return if possible.”

She looked toward the dark screens and the preserved faces of the dead.

“We found the weapon,” she said. “It is automatic. We are examining its controls.”

“Can it be stopped?”

“We think so.”

“You had better be certain. The thrust ring can be repaired, but we will have one ascent. Only one.”

“We are coming back.”

Tance did not move.

Nasha touched his shoulder. “We must go.”

“This archive is the greatest discovery of the expedition.”

“The expedition still has to survive.”

“We cannot leave it.”

“We will return.”

Dorle gave a short laugh. “Assuming the dragon permits it.”

Tance looked at the cases. “If we destroy the gun, the vault may be damaged.”

“Then we will destroy it carefully,” Nasha said.

* * *

Captain Rinn was conscious when they returned.

Fomar had rigged braces around the split thrust ring. The repairs covered half the engine compartment, a crude web of alloy plates, welded bars, and fresh cable.

The Captain lay near the open lock so he could see the city. His face was bloodless.

“Well?” he asked.

Nasha knelt beside him. “It is an automatic antiaircraft weapon. Its controls and magazine are under the northern ridge.”

“Crew?”

“None.”

“Other weapons?”

“We do not know.”

“That means yes.”

“It means we found only one.”

Rinn closed his eyes briefly. “Can you disable it?”

Dorle set down the cutting torch. “It ignores ground movement. We can approach the barrel and destroy the rotation system. After that we can enter the magazine and remove the warheads.”

“Will it explode?”

“Not if Tance stays outside.”

Tance frowned. “Why me?”

“You touch things.”

“I discovered the entrance.”

“And touched it.”

The Captain looked at Tance. “What was beneath it?”

Tance hesitated, then began to speak rapidly. He described the vault, the films, the statues, the books and records. As he spoke, the Captain watched the dead city beyond the lock.

“So they saved something,” Rinn said when he had finished.

“Almost everything,” Tance replied. “Everything that made them more than weapon builders.”

“Except themselves.”

“Yes.”

The Captain’s fingers moved against the blanket. “Bring the archive coordinates home.”

“We will.”

“Do not try to take the collection now. Repair the ship. Destroy the weapon. Leave.”

“We can return with a larger expedition,” Nasha said.

Rinn nodded. “And proper equipment. Language machines. Shielded carriers.”

His breathing stopped for several seconds, then began again.

Fomar came forward. “Captain, we must close the lock. The temperature is falling.”

Rinn seemed not to hear him.

“Nasha,” he said.

“Yes?”

“You have command.”

“Until you recover.”

“No.”

She stiffened.

The Captain looked toward the ruined bridge. “Do not make command into a ceremony. It is only the person who must decide while the others object.”

Dorle bowed his head slightly. “That definition will be remembered.”

Rinn almost smiled.

The yellow sun touched the ridge. Shadows crossed the riverbed.

“What were they like?” the Captain asked Tance.

“The people here?”

“Yes.”

Tance searched for an answer. “They made laws. They cultivated legumes. They composed music in five tonal systems. They had elaborate mating contracts. They built excellent storage vaults.”

“Did they know why they fought?”

“There were histories. I could not read them.”

“Perhaps when we return.”

“Yes, Captain.”

The sun sank.

Captain Rinn watched until the last light left the city.

Then he died.

They wrapped him in a field covering and placed him in the ship’s observation compartment. There was no ground safe enough for burial, and Nasha would not leave him in the radioactive dust.

No one spoke for a while.

At last Fomar turned toward the engine space. “The braces must cool before I can test them.”

Dorle checked the charge on the cutting torch.

“We go at first light,” Nasha said.

Tance looked toward the ridge. “To kill the dragon.”

“No,” she said. “To correct an old instruction.”

* * *

They approached the weapon from three sides.

Dorle climbed the rear foundation carrying the torch. Nasha entered the control chamber through the broken maintenance passage. Tance waited below with Fomar near the magazine access.

The gun was awake.

Its listening plates turned toward the upper atmosphere. Telescopic sights moved behind narrow slots. Deep inside the structure, automatic controls compared signals, rejected harmless ones, and searched for the shapes they had been made to fear.

Fomar opened the electrical cabinet.

“Power source is below us,” he said. “A long-life atomic converter. Output is low but steady.”

“Can we remove it?” Tance asked.

“Eventually.”

“Can we stop it now?”

“Yes. But Dorle wants the rotation track locked first. If the gun detects the ship’s test firing, it may shoot before the circuits die.”

Dorle’s voice came over the radio. “I am in position.”

Nasha stood beside the manual override panel. Ancient characters marked the switches. She had traced the cables and believed she knew which lever separated the aiming system from the firing relays.

Believed.

“Fomar?” she said.

“Ready.”

“Dorle?”

“Ready.”

“Tance?”

“I am standing a safe distance away, which seems to be my assigned technical function.”

“Begin.”

Dorle lit the torch.

The blue-white flame entered the outer rotation track. Metal ran in bright streams. The dome moved abruptly, trying to correct its position. Gears ground against the cut.

The gun turned toward the east.

“Faster,” Nasha said.

“I am not painting it.”

A warning light appeared on the panel. Relays began to hammer.

Fomar pulled three power couplings. One broke free. The second held.

“Contact fused!”

Tance raised his hand weapon. “Move aside.”

“Do not fire in here.”

“Then move aside slowly.”

The gun barrel lifted.

Far beyond the atmosphere, the wreckage of the expedition’s lost storage compartment passed over the horizon. The telescopic sights caught it. The target computer began its calm solution.

Nasha gripped the override lever and pulled.

Nothing happened.

She put both hands on it.

The lever moved halfway, then stopped. Behind the panel something clicked. A secondary control took over.

“It has bypass circuits,” she said.

Captain Rinn’s warning came back to her.

Do not assume it is simple.

“Dorle, it is preparing to fire.”

“I know.”

He drove the torch deeper. The rotation track glowed. The dome pushed against the molten metal with immense force.

Fomar struck the fused coupling with a wrench. Once. Twice.

Tance took the wrench from him.

“Your practical method lacks conviction,” he said.

He swung it with both hands. The coupling shattered.

Half the chamber went dark.

The gun fired.

The shell left the barrel with a blast that knocked Dorle from the foundation. He fell into the dust and rolled. Above them the projectile climbed toward empty space.

The dome tried to follow its target. Its cut track seized.

The mechanism groaned.

Then the whole weapon stopped.

Silence returned.

Nasha listened. A few relays continued to click, slower and slower.

“Dorle?”

A pause.

“I am alive,” he said. “But command should record my objection.”

“Recorded.”

“To what?”

“To falling.”

“Yes.”

They entered the magazine.

Twelve atomic warheads stood in a loading rack, each enclosed in a dull silver casing. An automatic hoist had already raised another from the lower store.

Fomar inspected the control lines. “The firing circuit is dead.”

“Can they detonate without it?” Nasha asked.

“With sufficient violence.”

Dorle came limping into the chamber. “Then let us use insufficient violence.”

They worked for six hours.

The warheads were disconnected one by one and carried down the ridge on the gun’s own maintenance sled. Fomar removed their trigger assemblies. Dorle cut through the elevation gears. Nasha smashed the sighting lenses with a steel bar.

Tance stood before the central computation cabinet.

“This contains its memory,” he said.

“Break it,” Nasha replied.

He hesitated. “It is also part of their history.”

“It shot down our ship.”

“So did its makers.”

“I cannot reach them.”

Tance stared at the moving relays. “The machine is the last active artifact of the civilization.”

Dorle handed him the steel bar. “Then archaeology may wish to conduct the ceremony.”

Tance took it.

The first blow cracked the cabinet cover. The second crushed a row of calculation wheels. Sparks spilled onto the floor.

The gun shuddered.

Its vents opened. The barrel moved a fraction and stopped. A low vibration passed through the chamber, like a slow mechanical pulse.

Tance struck again.

The vibration weakened.

Again.

Indicator lights went out in sequence. The listening plates above folded inward. The telescopic sights became dark.

The machine had watched the sky for centuries. It had seen cities burn, armies vanish, oceans retreat, and dust cover the roads. It had remained alert when there was no nation to defend, no enemy to defeat, no living voice to alter its orders.

Now it throbbed painfully under their blows.

Then it was still.

* * *

Fomar tested the repaired engines at noon.

The jets fired for three seconds. The ship strained against the riverbed, but the braces held. No shell rose from the ridge.

Dorle watched the sky. “Nothing.”

“Again,” Nasha said.

Fomar fired the jets for ten seconds.

Dust rolled across the bridge piers. Loose stones danced under the hull. The repaired thrust ring glowed, cooled, and remained intact.

“No attack,” Tance said.

“The dragon is dead,” Dorle replied.

They loaded the detached trigger assemblies into the ship and placed the atomic warheads in a deep crater outside the city. Without their firing mechanisms, they were only dangerous metal. Fomar marked the location for the next expedition.

Tance returned once more to the vault.

He brought back a film cylinder, a small book of transparent pages, and one colored glass figure. It represented a child holding a round object in two of its three hands.

Nasha looked at the items. “The Captain said not to take the collection.”

“This is not the collection.”

“What is it?”

“Proof.”

“The coordinates are proof.”

Tance held up the glass child. “Coordinates do not have a face.”

She allowed him to keep it.

Before departure, they carried Captain Rinn to the control room and fastened him into his command seat. Nasha stood beside him.

“I would prefer the medical compartment,” Fomar said.

“He wanted to leave,” she replied.

Dorle looked at the Captain’s covered face. “Then he should see it done.”

They sealed the ship.

Fomar brought the repaired propulsion cells online. Warning lights flashed across the board. Half the automatic controls did not respond.

“We will climb manually,” he said. “Slowly until we clear the city. Then full thrust.”

Nasha took the navigation levers.

The jets ignited.

The ship rose from the riverbed. Gray dust spread outward in a great circle, covering their footprints and the marks left by the maintenance sled. The broken bridge dropped below.

One hundred meters.

Five hundred.

The northern ridge came into view. The gun remained motionless. Its barrel pointed east, fixed between sky and ground.

“One thousand,” Fomar said.

No flash appeared.

The ruined city widened beneath them. Tance watched the circular structure become small.

“Full thrust,” Nasha ordered.

Fomar opened the propulsion cells.

The ship climbed through the poisoned air. It shook violently, but the improvised braces held. The yellow haze thinned. Stars appeared.

They reached orbit.

Dorle released his straps. “I object to command.”

Nasha glanced at him.

“It has failed to kill us,” he said. “This establishes a dangerous precedent.”

Fomar bent over the engine gauges. “The thrust ring will last until home if no one breathes heavily.”

Tance placed the glass figure on the control table. It caught the light from the dead planet.

“We will return,” he said.

Nasha looked at the world below. “Yes.”

“With a full archaeological unit.”

“Yes.”

“And military engineers.”

Dorle nodded. “Especially military engineers.”

“To protect the archive?”

“To make certain the dragon remains dead.”

They set course for the outer marker.

Behind them the third planet turned beneath its yellow sun. The dark stains of cities moved into night. No lights appeared on the surface.

Nothing lived.

Everything was silent.

The survey ship crossed the system and vanished.

* * *

For two day-periods the damaged gun did nothing.

Dust entered the broken rotation track. The cooling vents remained open. Its telescopic sights stared blindly toward the east.

Deep beneath the computation chamber, however, one circuit still carried current.

It was not part of the aiming system.

It was not part of the loading system.

It did not know about aircraft, nations, victory, or defeat.

It measured damage.

The circuit tested the primary controls. No answer came. It tested the reserve controls. No answer came. It sent a pulse through the sighting assembly, the rotation motors, the ammunition hoist, and the atomic converter.

The results exceeded permitted limits.

The circuit closed a relay.

A signal entered an underground cable.

The cable ran north beneath the ridge, under the ruins and the fused plain. It crossed the dry bed of a lake. In several places the cable had broken, but automatic junctions found alternate paths. The signal continued.

At last it reached a facility buried under a range of melted hills.

Locks opened.

Airless rooms received power for the first time in centuries. Inspection lamps came on above long rows of machinery. Mechanical arms unfolded from walls. Pumps began to move lubricant through sealed lines.

A door opened in the floor.

From it rose a small tracked cart. The cart carried cable, relays, and replacement computation wheels.

A second cart rose behind it. It carried tools, welding equipment, and sections of rotation track.

A third cart appeared. It carried telescopic sights sealed in padded metal boxes.

Then came a larger vehicle loaded with armor plate.

Another followed with motors.

Another with an atomic converter.

Another with twelve silver warheads.

The carts arranged themselves in a line. Their guidance units received the coordinates. Their tracks began to turn.

One after another, they left the underground facility and moved south across the gray plain.

They traveled without lights. They required no road. When they reached broken ground, they separated and found easier paths. When one became trapped in fused stone, the others pulled it free.

They moved with calm efficiency.

Across the dead lake.

Through the poisoned dust.

Past the black remains of towns whose names had been forgotten.

Toward the ruined city.

Toward the silent ridge.

Toward the damaged gun.

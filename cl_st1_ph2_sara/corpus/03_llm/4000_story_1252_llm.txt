Semen Ivanov had suffered much in his time, and suffering had not made him bitter, but only quieter. He had served seven years in the army, had been twice wounded, had caught fever in the marshes beyond the Danube, and had returned to his native village with a medal, a cough, and no strength for the plough. During his absence his father had died, the old hut had burned, and the little strip of land belonging to the family had passed into the hands of a creditor.

For two years Semen wandered with his wife, Avdotya, from one district to another. He worked wherever they would take him: he guarded a merchant’s timber, carted bricks, dug clay, and once spent a winter in a sugar factory. But his chest was bad. If he labored hard, blood came into his mouth; if he rested, there was no bread.

Two children were born to them, and both died. The first, a boy, lived a year and three months. The second, a girl, died before she was christened.

Avdotya wept and said:

“Why does God send us children only to take them back?”

Semen answered:

“Who can know His will? We must bear our portion.”

He too wept, but secretly, and went on.

At last he met an old officer under whom he had served. The officer was now employed upon the railway and remembered Semen as a sober and obedient soldier.

“You are not fit for heavy work,” he said, looking at Semen’s sunken cheeks. “But perhaps they can make a trackman of you. Can you read?”

“A little, your honor.”

“Do you drink?”

“No, your honor.”

“Are you careful?”

“In the service I was not punished for neglect.”

The officer wrote a letter, and by this means Semen obtained a place on the railway.

His pay was twelve rubles a month, and he was given a watch-house beside the line, with a small piece of ground for a garden. To Semen and Avdotya the house appeared almost rich. It had one room, a stove, two windows, a shed for tools, and a well some forty sazhens away. Behind it lay a strip of earth where cabbages, potatoes, onions, and beans might be planted. There was no village near. The nearest station was ten versts in one direction, and in the other direction, after five versts, stood another watch-house occupied by a trackman named Vasili Spiridonov.

Semen thanked the officer, thanked the inspector, thanked God, and began his new life.

His duty was to examine a certain portion of the line every day. He walked from boundary post to boundary post, tested the bolts and spikes, watched whether the rails had shifted, cleaned earth and gravel from the joints, and saw that water passed freely through the culverts. In winter he cleared snow; in spring he watched the embankment, lest the thawed earth should slide; in summer he looked for grass and dry rubbish, which a spark from an engine might set on fire.

He was given a hammer, wrench, crowbar, spade, green flag, red flag, lantern, and a little book of regulations. The red flag meant danger: stop at once. The green meant that the line was clear. At night the lantern served, with red and green glasses fitted to it.

Semen read the regulations slowly. Some words he could not understand, and these he asked about at the station. He learned the times of all trains, the rules concerning signals, the proper distance at which a warning must be placed, and the sound that a sound wheel makes upon a sound rail. He could tell by the humming of the telegraph wires when the weather would change, and by laying his hand upon the steel he could sometimes feel a train while it was still beyond the woods.

Avdotya planted the garden. Semen fenced it with branches, mended the roof, cut a bench, and made shelves. They acquired three hens and a lean cat. For the first time in many years there was order in their life.

“Thank God,” Avdotya would say. “Here at least no one drives us from one lodging to another.”

“Yes,” Semen answered. “It is lonely, but quiet.”

Vasili Spiridonov, the neighboring trackman, often came to them. He was younger than Semen, broad in the shoulders, dark-faced, and strong. His beard grew thick and black, and his eyes, when he was angry, shone as if fever burned behind them. He had a wife and two children, and he too kept a garden beside his watch-house.

At first Semen liked him very much. Vasili worked well. His part of the track was always clean, his bolts tight, and the ditch along the embankment straight as if cut by a rule. If Semen needed help lifting a sleeper or rolling a stone, Vasili came without asking what he would receive for it.

But he was a proud man and could not endure an insult.

One evening they sat on the bench near Semen’s house. The sun had gone down behind the birches; the rails were still red in the west, and mosquitoes moved like smoke over the ditch. Avdotya was hoeing the cabbages.

Vasili said:

“Twelve rubles! Is that money? Flour is dear, salt is dear, kerosene is dear. Boots cost five rubles. And if a child is sick, what then?”

“We live somehow,” said Semen.

“You live because you eat nothing. Your wife is thin as a fence stake, and you cough half the night. Is that living?”

“It is better than begging.”

“Better! They give us a hut, then count the hut as a blessing. They give a strip of clay and say, ‘Plant your cabbages.’ But if a railway cow gets into the cabbages, we must not touch it. If the inspector’s horse tramples the potatoes, we must bow. Everything is theirs—rail, earth, timber, stone, even our time. Only hunger belongs to us.”

Semen looked toward the line.

“You should not excite yourself, Vasili Spiridonovich. A man must serve where God has placed him.”

“There it is again—God! Did God set the wages? Did God make the fines? Last month they deducted three rubles from Afanasi because a sleeper split. He did not cut the sleeper. The sleeper came rotten from the yard.”

“Perhaps they made a mistake.”

“A mistake? Their mistakes always go into their pockets and out of ours.”

Semen sighed.

“Still, little brother, anger does not mend a matter.”

“And meekness does?”

“What can people such as we do?”

Vasili struck his fist upon his knee.

“We can speak. We can complain. We can refuse to be treated like cattle.”

“Speak carefully, then.”

“Why carefully, if the truth is true?”

This was always the difference between them. Semen saw the injustice, but he thought it one of the burdens allotted to mankind, like sickness, frost, and death. Vasili believed that every burden had been laid by some living hand, and he desired to seize that hand and force it away.

In the spring the rains were heavy. Water poured down the embankment, softened its sides, and collected in the low ground near Vasili’s house. The drainage ditch had long needed deepening. Vasili had reported it twice, but nothing was done.

Then a cart belonging to the railway administration came for gravel. The driver took a short way across Vasili’s garden and broke the fence. The horse trampled two beds where young cabbages had been planted.

Vasili ran out and caught the bridle.

“Where are you going?”

“The overseer ordered me.”

“Did he order you to ride over my cabbages?”

“Let go. I have no time.”

“You will turn back.”

They quarreled. The driver turned back at last, but complained that Vasili had hindered railway work. Three days later the inspector came and fined Vasili one ruble.

After this Vasili’s anger never left him. He spoke of the ruble while tightening bolts; he spoke of it at dinner; he spoke of it when he woke at night. The ruble itself was not the whole matter. It was the broken fence, the cabbages, the refusal to listen, and the knowledge that the inspector might write one mark in a book and take away what had cost Vasili several days’ labor.

“I shall complain to the division-master,” he said.

“Do not,” Semen advised. “The division-master will believe his inspector.”

“Then I shall go higher.”

“And who will do your work?”

“My wife can walk the section one day.”

“You may lose your place.”

“Let them take it. A man has one skin. They cannot strip him twice.”

The division-master was expected in June. Before his inspection, the trackmen were ordered to put everything in perfect condition. They straightened posts, painted numbers, cleared weeds, spread fresh gravel, and whitened the guard stones near the culverts. Semen worked from dawn until the evening train had passed. His cough grew worse, but he told Avdotya it was only the damp air.

On the appointed day a special car came behind a small engine. The division-master descended at the milepost. He was a heavy man with a red neck and gold buttons. With him were the inspector, an engineer, and two clerks carrying papers.

Semen stood beside the line, holding his green flag.

The division-master looked at the joints, asked two questions, and passed on. Semen bowed. All had gone well.

Farther down the line, near Vasili’s section, voices rose. Semen could not hear the words at first, but he recognized Vasili’s harsh tone. Then he saw him step in front of the division-master.

“Your honor, I have a complaint.”

“Send it through the inspector.”

“I sent it. He fined me instead.”

“Stand aside.”

“The ditch is not cleared. My fence was broken. The cart went through my garden—”

“Stand aside, I tell you.”

“Your honor, hear me. Are we men or not?”

The inspector seized Vasili by the sleeve. Vasili pulled away. The division-master’s face became purple. He lifted the cane in his hand and struck Vasili across the cheek.

For a moment everybody was still.

Vasili did not strike back. His hands closed, and his head went down like the head of a bull. Then he looked straight at the division-master.

“Very well,” he said. “You have beaten me. Remember that.”

The clerks moved closer. The inspector shouted:

“Go to your house! You are dismissed from the line for the day.”

Vasili turned and walked away.

That evening he came to Semen. A dark stripe crossed his cheek, and the skin near his ear was swollen.

“Did you see?”

“I saw,” said Semen.

“For speaking. For asking that my complaint be heard.”

“It was wrong of him.”

“Wrong? Is that all?”

“What else can I say?”

“Say that I am a man! Say he had no right!”

“He had no right.”

“Then why did you stand there holding your flag?”

Semen was silent. The question hurt him, for he had indeed stood there. Yet what could he have done? Throw down the flag? Strike the division-master? Lose the place upon which he and Avdotya depended?

Vasili began walking up and down.

“I will go to Moscow. I will find the chief office. If they turn me out, I will go to the minister. I will show them the mark.”

“Who will admit you to a minister?”

“I will wait at the door.”

“You have no money for the journey.”

“I will walk.”

“Think of your wife and children.”

“I am thinking of them! Shall my son grow and see his father beaten like a dog?”

Semen put a hand on his shoulder.

“Do not do anything in anger, Vasili Spiridonovich. Sleep first. Pray. In the morning the burden may appear lighter.”

Vasili shook off the hand.

“You have been bent so long that you call bending a virtue. I cannot live so.”

At dawn the next day he left his watch-house. His wife said he had gone to seek justice. He took bread, a coat, and a few kopecks. No one knew whether he had gone toward the station or by the forest road.

Three days passed. Then five.

Vasili’s wife walked his section and looked after the children. Semen helped where he could. He inspected several versts beyond his own boundary, though the extra walking made his legs tremble. He pitied the woman, but he did not ask many questions. She was frightened and ashamed.

On the seventh day she said:

“He has not written.”

“Perhaps he found work on the road.”

“He said he would return.”

“He will return, God willing.”

But Semen himself was uneasy. At night, when trains passed, the windows shook and the red glow from the engine fire moved across the ceiling. He thought of Vasili’s face after the blow and of the words, “Remember that.”

Still, daily work leaves little room for distant fears. The weather became hot. The garden required water. One rail joint had loosened where the embankment curved around a wooded hollow, and Semen reported it, then tightened it temporarily according to regulation. A consignment of new sleepers was expected. The passenger express from the south now passed his section at twenty-eight minutes after five in the afternoon, usually at great speed, because the line beyond the station was open and straight.

One morning Semen cut willow shoots beside the stream. He wished to make handles for two tools and new hoops for a basket. He trimmed the shoots with his knife and left several straight sticks near the shed.

After dinner he took his hammer and wrench and went to examine the line. The sun stood high. Heat shimmered over the rails, and the tar upon the sleepers had softened. There was no wind. Even the telegraph wires seemed asleep.

Semen walked slowly, testing here and there. At the culvert he cleared away a branch. Beyond it he tightened a nut. Then he went toward the curve, where the embankment rose high above a ravine.

As he approached the trees, he heard a sound.

Clink.

He stopped.

Clink—clink.

It was iron striking iron, but not the regular ringing made by a work gang. It came separately, with pauses, as though someone worked secretly and listened between the blows.

Semen stepped off the gravel and went quietly along the slope. Bushes hid the line ahead. He climbed, grasping roots with one hand.

Then he saw a man kneeling beside the outer rail.

A wrench lay on the sleeper. Two nuts had been removed. The fishplate joining the rails hung loose. Beside the man was a short crowbar.

“Vasili!” cried Semen.

The man sprang up.

It was Vasili. His beard was uncombed, his coat torn at the elbow, and his face had become lean. For one instant they looked at each other.

“What are you doing?” Semen said.

Vasili seized the crowbar and thrust it beneath the rail.

“Go away.”

“Have you lost your senses? The passenger train will come!”

“Let it come.”

“There are people in it!”

“People?” Vasili laughed, but his laugh was dry and terrible. “Do they count us as people?”

He pressed upon the bar. The loosened rail moved.

Semen rushed forward and caught his arm.

“Vasili Spiridonovich, save your soul! Whatever they did to you, those travelers did nothing.”

“They ride while we crawl.”

“There will be women, little children—soldiers, workers, people such as ourselves!”

“Stand back.”

Vasili flung him aside. Semen fell upon the gravel, striking his shoulder. Before he could rise, Vasili pulled the rail end outward. It shifted from its place, leaving a black gap between the shining lines.

The express, coming round the curve at speed, would strike the displaced rail. The engine would leave the track, roll down the embankment, and drag the carriages after it.

Semen scrambled up.

“You will murder them!”

Vasili’s face changed, but only for a moment.

“They murdered enough in me.”

He took the wrench and crowbar and ran down the far side of the embankment. Bushes closed behind him.

Semen stood alone.

For several seconds he could neither think nor move. The broken line lay before him. The screws and nuts were scattered among the stones. One fishplate had been thrown down the slope. The heavy rail had shifted nearly half an arshin.

Could he repair it?

He dropped to his knees and seized the rail. It did not move. He set his shoulder against it. Pain passed through his chest; his breath failed. Even a strong man could not align it without a lever and assistance. The crowbar was gone.

He searched for the nuts. One—two—where was the third? It did not matter. The plate itself lay below.

The train! What time was it?

From beyond the forest came the long whistle of the factory at the settlement. Five o’clock.

The express was due at five twenty-eight.

Twenty-eight minutes.

His red flag was at the watch-house.

Semen looked back along the line. The house was more than a verst and a half away. If he ran there, took the flag, and returned, he would lose too much time. He need not return to the break; he could signal from farther along the straight track. Yes, that was possible. He must run toward home, take the flag, then meet the train.

Run—run for dear life!

He started.

The gravel rolled under his boots. His tools beat against his side until he threw down the wrench and hammer. He ran with his arms bent, breathing through his open mouth. At first the house seemed not very far. Its roof showed beyond a cut in the embankment.

But Semen was not a healthy man. Before he had gone half a verst, his chest burned. The line swam before him. He slowed, then forced himself on.

“Dear Lord, give me strength! Only this once!”

He could hear nothing except his breathing and the blood beating in his ears.

Suppose he reached the house in ten minutes. He must find the key if Avdotya had gone to the well. The flag hung by the door—no, he had used it the week before and put it in the shed. Or had he? Then he must run back toward the train, far enough that the engineer could brake before the curve.

The express needed a great distance to stop. On dry rails, perhaps seven hundred sazhens at such speed; perhaps more. The regulations were plain: the signal must be displayed where it could be seen in time.

He stumbled and fell to one knee.

When he rose, black flies moved before his eyes.

The house was still far away.

He understood: he would not reach it and return to a proper signaling point. If he went on, the train would pass him before he could save it. If he turned back, he had no red flag.

He looked around desperately. Green grass, gray stones, the white posts, the endless shining rails. Nothing red.

His shirt was white. His handkerchief was white. A white signal meant nothing.

Again he heard a sound, faint and far away.

A whistle?

No. Perhaps a bird. Perhaps the wind in the wires. Yet there was no wind.

Semen’s thoughts broke apart. He saw the passengers as if the carriage walls had become transparent: a mother pouring tea, a child asleep upon a bench, a merchant counting money, a soldier returning home. None knew that the rail ahead had been torn aside. They trusted the line. They trusted the men who guarded it.

And he was the man.

His eyes fell upon his hand. In that moment his mind became illuminated, as it were, by a thought.

Blood was red.

He pulled the handkerchief from his pocket. Then he remembered the willow sticks beside the house—useless, far away. But near the ditch grew young alders. He scrambled down, broke a straight branch, stripped off the leaves, and tied the handkerchief to its end.

White. Still white.

He opened his clasp knife.

For an instant he hesitated. He knew how weak he was. He knew that sometimes a small wound bled dangerously in a man exhausted by fever and hunger. But there was no other thing to be done.

“In God’s name,” he whispered.

He drew the blade across the inside of his left forearm.

The knife was sharp. Pain flashed to the elbow. Blood rose in a dark line, then ran over his wrist.

He pressed the handkerchief against the wound.

It drank the blood quickly. One corner reddened, then the middle. He squeezed his arm above the cut. The blood was pouring and pouring, hot upon his fingers, darkening the cloth until it hung heavy from the stick.

Enough. Surely enough.

He climbed back to the rails and ran toward the approaching train, away from the broken place. He held the stick high in his right hand.

Now there was no mistaking the sound.

The rails trembled.

Far beyond the straight stretch, behind the low hill, the express gave its whistle—long, powerful, indifferent.

Semen ran.

He must reach the marker before the curve. The engineer must see him against the sky. If he stood too near the break, braking would not help.

His wounded arm hung at his side. Blood ran from his fingers and dropped on the white stones. His face had become cold, though the day was hot.

“Run, run! A little farther!”

The telegraph posts passed him one by one. He counted without knowing why. Three. Four. Five.

The rail hummed beneath his feet.

He reached an open place where the embankment rose above a meadow. Here he turned and faced the train. The line stretched straight for nearly a verst.

He planted his boots apart and waved the bloody flag.

Up—down. Up—down.

The stick felt heavy. The handkerchief clung wetly around it, but enough cloth opened to show red.

The engine appeared in the distance, small and black. Smoke streamed behind it. The brass front shone in the sun.

Semen waved.

“Stop! Stop!”

His voice was nothing. The engine came on.

Had the driver seen? There was no answering whistle. The train grew larger with terrible quickness. He could distinguish the chimney, the lamp, the round boiler front.

He waved with both hands, then cried out because the movement pulled at the wound.

The black flies returned before his eyes. Bells rang in his ears.

What if the blood was too dark? What if the engineer took it for a rag? What if Semen had not come far enough?

He stepped between the rails.

“No farther,” he said. “If he does not see, he will see me.”

The engine whistled.

A sharp blast—then another.

Seen!

But had it time?

Semen waved again. The world tilted. The rails seemed to rise on one side and sink on the other. His knees bent.

He tried to hold the stick higher.

“Dear Lord—do not let it fall.”

The stick slipped in his wet hand.

At that instant someone sprang from the ditch behind him. A strong arm caught him about the body, and another hand seized the falling signal.

Semen saw a dark beard close to his face.

Vasili.

Without a word Vasili lifted the bloody flag high above his head and waved it with all his strength.

The engine screamed. Sparks flew from beneath the wheels. Iron ground upon iron. The carriages struck together with repeated blows; windows flashed; passengers were thrown forward. The great machine came on, slower, slower, breathing steam.

Vasili did not move from between the rails until the engine was almost upon him. Then he dragged Semen aside.

The train stopped.

For a moment there was deathlike quiet. Then doors opened. Voices cried out. The engineer jumped down, followed by the fireman and conductor. Passengers leaned from the windows.

“What is it?”

“Why have we stopped?”

“Is the man injured?”

The engineer ran toward them.

Vasili stood holding the stick. Blood from the handkerchief had run over his hand. At his feet lay Semen, pale and senseless.

“The rail,” Vasili said. His voice was low. “Beyond the curve. It has been displaced.”

The engineer stared, then sent the fireman forward. The conductor ordered two guards to set signals behind the train. Several passengers gathered around Semen. A doctor, who happened to be traveling in the second-class carriage, knelt and bound his arm.

Soon the fireman returned, breathless.

“He speaks the truth. The outer rail is torn loose. We should have gone over the bank.”

A murmur passed through the crowd. Women crossed themselves. A man removed his cap. Someone asked who had discovered the danger. Someone else pointed to the blood-soaked flag.

The doctor looked up.

“He may live, but he has lost much blood. Who cut him?”

“He cut himself,” said Vasili.

“For the signal?”

“Yes.”

The people became silent.

Vasili looked at their faces: the frightened women, the child pressed against its mother, the gray-haired merchant, the young soldier, the conductor, the engineer whose hands still shook. He looked down at Semen, who had called him brother, who had warned him, who had given his own blood to prevent the evil another man had begun.

The bloody cloth moved a little in the warm air.

Vasili broke the stick across his knee and laid it beside Semen. Then he held out both hands to the conductor.

“Bind me,” he said. “It was I who cut the rail.”

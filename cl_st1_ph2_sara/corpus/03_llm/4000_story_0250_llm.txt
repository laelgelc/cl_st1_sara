# The Bachelor’s Nightcap

There is in Copenhagen a narrow street which in old documents is called Hauschen Street, although the clerks who copied the name into Danish books wrote it Hysken Street, and the people, who always shorten words when they have frequently to call them out, at last made of it something scarcely resembling either. Learned men have disputed whether the name came from a little house, a *Hauschen*, that once stood beside the harbour, or from certain German merchants who dwelt there in houses so small that a tall man could almost have laid his elbows upon the opposite walls. Both opinions have supporters, and both may be wrong; that also happens among learned men.

The street lay close to the warehouses and landing places, where vessels came with beer from Rostock, cloth from Flanders, salt from Lüneburg, wine from the Rhine, and spices from countries so far away that those who sold them knew no more of the trees on which they grew than a town sparrow knows of the palm beneath which its grandfather’s grandfather may perhaps have chirped. There were sacks of pepper, ginger, saffron, cloves, aniseed, cinnamon, and nutmeg; there were barrels of dried fish, rolls of leather, bales of wool, iron tools, horn buttons, ribbons, needles, rosaries, painted toys, and knives with handles of carved bone.

Many German merchants settled in that quarter. Their clerks came after them, young men with strong shoes, stiff collars, woollen cloaks, caps with ear-flaps, and knives at their belts; they could reckon in two currencies, drink in three languages, and bargain in any tongue whatever, provided the buyer understood the lifting of three fingers and the shaking of a head. Some grew rich, married Danish maidens, became substantial citizens, and gave silver cups to the churches. Others remained clerks until their backs bent like the handles of old pitchers.

Because the spice merchants dealt particularly in pepper, and because many unmarried clerks lived among the warehouses, bachelors of a certain age came to be called the “pepper gentry.” It was not meant as an honour, although pepper itself was costly enough to be weighed against silver. A grey bachelor might possess a good coat, an iron-bound chest, and a room of his own, yet boys laughed when he passed, maidservants whispered, and married men spoke of him as one who had missed the proper ferry and must now wait upon the bank until Judgment Day.

Such persons were said to wear the bachelor’s nightcap.

Now a nightcap is only a nightcap, you may say: a piece of cloth drawn over the head to protect it from cold and draughts. That is true of most nightcaps; but it is not true of all. A crown may be merely gold until a king has worn it, a ribbon merely silk until it has bound a lover’s letter, and a nightcap merely wool until it has gathered the thoughts of forty lonely years. This must be remembered.

Anthony had two—nightcaps, I mean, not heads; even a pepper clerk requires only one head, though in accounts it is often convenient to possess two. One cap was of brown wool, darned at the crown and edged with faded red braid. The other was white, or had once been white, and ended in a tassel that hung over his left ear. The brown one he wore on ordinary nights; the white one he reserved for Sundays, feast days, and illnesses. In the end, however, illness made every day a feast day of the wrong kind, and so the white cap came to know him best.

He lived at the top of an old house in Hysken Street, in a room where the sloping roof pressed down like a heavy brow. The window looked across chimneys to a strip of harbour, and when the wind blew from the east it brought the smell of tar, salt water, smoke, dried codfish, damp rope, cabbage soup, and sometimes cinnamon from the warehouse below. These scents mingled in Anthony’s room and formed the perfume of his old age.

He had a bed with green curtains, though the green had faded to the colour of boiled herbs; a table with one short leg supported by a folded account sheet; a chest; two chairs, one sound and one unreliable; a little iron stove; a shelf containing a Bible, a hymnbook, a German almanac, and three letters tied with black thread. Over the chest hung a small print of Saint Elizabeth of Thuringia, carrying bread to the poor. The print was badly coloured. The saint’s robe was blue, her cheeks crimson, and the roses in her apron looked rather like red cabbages; nevertheless Anthony held it in great reverence.

He himself was thin and tall, though age had folded him somewhat in the middle. His face had many wrinkles, not set in orderly rows like writing in a ledger, but crossing and recrossing as though sorrow had made calculations there and repeatedly found the sum incorrect. His fingers were long and bony. His eyebrows stood out like two grey brushes; beneath them his eyes were mild, except when someone spoke carelessly of faithless women or unpaid debts. A single tuft of hair rose obstinately above his forehead. It would not lie down, no matter how firmly the nightcap was pulled over it, and this was perhaps the last piece of youthful independence remaining to him.

Every evening Anthony followed the same order. He counted the coals. He examined the window fastening. He tried the door twice. Then, doubting whether twice were sufficient, he tried it a third time. He looked beneath the bed, not because he expected a thief, but because habit is a watchman that continues its rounds after danger has gone home. He placed his wooden shoes beside the stove, folded his coat, and laid his spectacles upon the Bible.

After that he put on the nightcap.

Then the room changed.

No other person would have noticed it. The stove remained black, the frost remained upon the panes, the beams remained cracked, and the mice behind the wall continued their small domestic arrangements. Yet for Anthony the room grew wider; the roof lifted, the walls opened, and through them came old houses, green hills, bells, birds, faces, voices, spring rain, and the smell of apple blossom.

Memory is a strange lantern. It does not light everything at once, nor does it always illuminate the object at which it is pointed. An old man may forget what he ate at noon and remember the pattern of a child’s ribbon seen sixty years before. He may mislay his key and yet hear perfectly the tone in which one particular word was spoken beneath a tree. Anthony’s memories came not in a procession, but in pictures, and each picture seemed enclosed in a tear.

The first was generally Eisenach.

He saw the town lying beneath the Wartburg, with red roofs, gables, church towers, gardens, and steep streets up which carts climbed groaning. He heard the hammers of craftsmen and the bells calling across the valley. Beyond the walls rose the wooded hills, green in summer, copper-red in autumn, and white under winter snow. Saint Elizabeth had walked there, so the old women said. She had carried bread to the poor, and when a suspicious nobleman demanded to see what she concealed in her apron, the loaves became roses.

Anthony’s mother told him that story beside the stove.

“Remember,” she said, “bread given in kindness blossoms in heaven.”

Anthony, who was then eight years old and preferred earthly cakes to heavenly flowers, asked whether honey cakes would become roses too.

“If they are given with the right heart.”

“And if they are eaten with the right heart?”

His mother laughed and kissed him. “Then they become a fat little boy.”

He was not fat for long. Boys stretch as saplings do, sometimes so quickly that their clothes cannot follow them. Anthony became a long-legged, serious child with fair hair and large ears. He was obedient, except when Molly called him; then obedience went in one direction and Anthony in another.

Molly was the daughter of the mayor, whose house stood behind a carved gate. She had dark hair, bright eyes, and the firm manner of one who expects the world to listen. The world usually did. She could jump farther than Anthony, climb faster, and answer before the schoolmaster finished asking. When she commanded a game, all the children played it; when she abandoned it, they discovered that they had grown tired of it too.

One autumn day she came running with a red apple in her hand.

“Anthony,” she said, “we shall plant the pips.”

“Why?”

“So that we may have a tree.”

“But we already have apples.”

“We have this apple. A tree is different. A tree goes on.”

This appeared reasonable, particularly because Molly had said it. They sat beneath the wall of the mayor’s garden and ate the apple, taking alternate bites. Molly bit boldly to the core; Anthony took small bites because he wished the apple to last. The pips lay black and shining in her palm.

“One for you and one for me,” he said.

“No,” answered Molly. “We plant them together. Otherwise there may be two trees, and yours might grow crooked.”

They selected the largest pip and buried it near a sunny corner of the wall. Molly pressed the earth down with the heel of her red shoe.

“Now it belongs to both of us,” said Anthony.

“It belongs to itself,” said Molly. “But when it has apples, half are yours.”

“And half are yours.”

“Certainly,” she replied, as though there had never been any doubt concerning her half.

All winter Anthony watched the spot. He knew very well that a tree would not rise through frozen earth, yet he looked. In spring a green shoot appeared, so delicate that a careless foot might have ended its history. The children built a fence around it from twigs. Molly tied a blue thread to the upper twig.

“So we shall know it.”

Anthony could have known it among a thousand.

The little tree grew. First it was only a stem, then a switch, then a young tree with side branches. The children grew too. The same sun shone upon both, but growth is not the same thing in a tree and in a human heart. The tree stretched upward without doubt. Anthony’s heart stretched toward Molly and was full of doubts.

They went to school; they walked in the meadows; they gathered berries; they listened to travellers tell tales in the market square. Sometimes Molly teased him.

“You will become a merchant,” she said, “and sell ginger to old women.”

“I shall become rich.”

“Then you will wear a velvet coat.”

“And I shall come to the mayor’s house in a carriage.”

“You may come without one. Carriages make a great noise.”

“But if I am rich—”

“You speak a great deal of being rich,” said Molly. “Perhaps you had better begin.”

Then she ran away, and Anthony followed, as he always did.

His father was indeed a prosperous merchant at that time. There were horses in the stable, silver spoons in the cupboard, linen in the presses, wine in the cellar, and good credit in three cities. His mother wore a chain of fine gold on Sundays. Guests came to dinner; geese turned before the fire; the table held white bread, sausages, trout, preserves, and cakes with almonds. Anthony believed prosperity to be part of the house, like the staircase, and supposed it could no more disappear than the front door.

Children think houses are eternal. Merchants know otherwise.

When Anthony was sixteen, the apple tree flowered for the first time. It bore only six blossoms, pale pink at the edges and white within. Anthony stood beneath them with Molly, who was nearly a young woman. Her dark hair was braided and pinned up; he noticed this with surprise and sorrow, as if she had altered it without permission.

“Six flowers,” he said. “Perhaps six apples.”

“Perhaps the wind will take them.”

“Why must you always say what may go wrong?”

“Because you always say what must go right.”

A bee moved heavily among the blossoms.

“Molly,” Anthony began.

But she reached up, touched one flower, and said, “Do not shake the branch.”

He did not speak. There are moments when a sentence remains unspoken so long that it grows roots and becomes part of a man’s whole life.

That summer the mayor received an appointment in another town. Carts came; chests were packed; carpets were rolled; servants ran through the gate. Molly’s family departed with noise and dust. She leaned from the carriage and waved a white handkerchief.

“Take care of the tree!” she called.

Anthony ran beside the wheels as far as the bridge.

“And come back!” he shouted.

Whether she heard him he never knew.

The tree produced three apples that year. One fell green, one was eaten by a blackbird, and one ripened. Anthony wrapped the ripe apple in paper and kept it through the autumn, intending to send it to Molly. But he could not decide what to write with it. At Christmas the apple had shrivelled; by Candlemas it was brown; in spring his mother found it in the drawer and threw it away.

“Why keep what has spoiled?” she asked.

That is a sensible question, but human beings seldom apply it to memories.

Years passed. Anthony learned accounts, measures, exchange rates, the quality of cloth, the smell of spoiled grain, and the many respectable forms dishonesty can assume when dressed in commercial language. He travelled for his father. He wrote letters to Molly and received replies at first: cheerful letters about new acquaintances, concerts, gardens, and dances. She asked after the apple tree, usually in a final line.

He described every branch.

Then her letters became shorter. Then they ceased.

Silence does not always mean cruelty. Sometimes it means only that one life has turned a corner while another remains standing in the street. But to the one who waits, silence has a voice, and it repeats the worst words he knows.

At four-and-twenty Anthony went to the town where Molly lived. He wore a new blue coat, a fine waistcoat, polished shoes, and a hat which had cost more than he afterward admitted. In his pocket lay a little gold ring, and in his mind was a speech he had prepared during three days’ travel. By the time he arrived, the speech had become so perfect that no living conversation could accommodate it.

Molly received him in a bright room overlooking a garden. She was beautiful; at least Anthony thought so, and on this subject his judgment was not impartial. Her features had grown quieter, her manner gentler. She no longer ordered everyone about, because she had learned that soft words can rule more completely than commands.

They spoke of Eisenach, of old neighbours, of the schoolmaster, of the Wartburg, and at last of the apple tree.

“Does it still stand?” she asked.

“It blossoms every spring.”

“How strange!”

“Not strange. It has good roots.”

She glanced at him then, and perhaps she understood. Women are often accused of not understanding what men have never said; this is unreasonable. Molly understood enough.

Anthony took the ring from his pocket, though he kept it hidden in his closed hand.

“Molly,” he said, “all my life—”

She rose quickly.

“Do not, Anthony.”

He became very pale.

“You cannot know what I meant.”

“Yes,” she said. “I know.”

“Then hear me.”

“It would be kinder not to hear.”

“Kinder to whom?”

“To both of us.”

There was no anger in her voice, and that made it worse. Anger is a door that can be beaten upon; calm refusal is a wall.

She told him that she loved another man, a young legal officer whose prospects were good and whose affection she returned. No promise had ever passed between herself and Anthony. She had loved him as a childhood companion, as the boy who had planted the apple pip with her, but childhood was not a marriage contract.

“You knew,” Anthony said.

“I feared.”

“And still you let me come.”

“I did not ask you to come.”

That was true. Truth can be very sharp when newly polished.

He opened his hand and looked at the ring. Molly saw it. Tears rose in her eyes, but she did not alter her answer.

“I am sorry,” she said.

“Keep your sorrow.”

“Anthony—”

“Give it to the man you love. Perhaps he has use for it.”

He left without bowing. On the stairs he passed a young man who greeted him courteously and moved aside to let him go by. Anthony knew at once who it was. The young man had a pleasant face, which seemed a personal insult.

Outside, rain was falling. Anthony walked through it until night. He hated Molly; he loved Molly; he hated himself for loving her; he hated the unknown man for being blameless. These are four heavy companions, and they followed him all the way home.

The ring remained in his pocket. Years later he sold it to buy medicine for his father.

For misfortune had already entered the family house, though no one had yet invited it to sit down. Two ships in which Anthony’s father had invested were lost. A trusted agent disappeared with money. A debtor failed, taking others with him; credit shrank; notes fell due. One horse was sold, then the other. Silver spoons went into a cloth bag and did not return. The good wine was bought by an innkeeper. Chests were carried out instead of in.

Anthony came home still wounded by Molly’s rejection and found a greater wound waiting. His father sat at the account table with both hands over his face. His mother had grown ten years older in one week.

“My son,” said his father, “I have ruined you.”

That sentence washed Anthony’s bitterness away for the moment. A man drowning in deep water does not complain of the rain.

He took charge of the books, wrote to creditors, bargained for time, sold goods, dismissed servants, and worked from dawn until the candles failed. There was no leisure for a broken heart, and this was a mercy. Sorrow, when set to labour, cannot shout so loudly.

Yet ruin has a long tail. Even after the house was sold and the debts arranged, it dragged behind them. Anthony’s father suffered a stroke and became helpless on one side. His mother tended him until she fell ill herself. Anthony supported them both, first by temporary work, then by accepting a clerkship with a German trading house in Copenhagen.

He did not wish to leave Eisenach. The hills held his childhood; the churchyard held his mother, who died before the journey; the house behind the mayor’s gate held nothing of Molly now, but the apple tree still stood against the wall. Its trunk had thickened. One branch had broken in a storm and been roughly sawn away. Yet in spring it was covered with blossoms.

On the morning of his departure Anthony visited it.

The house belonged to strangers. He obtained permission from the gardener, who did not understand why a grown man in a worn travelling cloak wished to stand beside an ordinary tree.

Anthony put his hand on the bark.

“Well,” he said, “you remained.”

A robin sang from the wall.

“You had the better bargain. You were planted and could not follow.”

He found one late apple beneath the leaves, small and spotted. He took it with him, not because it was fine fruit, but because value and price are cousins who seldom visit.

His father travelled to Copenhagen with him and lived there two more years. Anthony lifted him, fed him, read the Bible aloud, and wrote letters at his dictation to persons who no longer answered. The old man died believing that the family fortune might somehow be restored. Hope sometimes remains after reason has packed its chest.

Anthony paid for a modest funeral. Then he was alone.

He worked for Herr Bredemann, a merchant dealing in spices, dyes, lamp oil, and foreign cloth. The countinghouse was dark, and the warehouse darker. Anthony copied invoices, measured bales, checked barrels, disputed with carriers, and kept watch over saffron, ginger, cloves, aniseed, pepper, indigo, and cinnamon. Pepper dust entered his cuffs and made him sneeze; thus he became, in the fullest sense, one of the pepper gentry.

The younger clerks married. Herr Bredemann’s daughters married. Apprentices arrived as boys, grew whiskers, became partners, and moved to larger houses. Anthony remained at the same desk. He was reliable, exact, and quiet; qualities highly valued in a ledger and often overlooked in a man.

Letters occasionally reached him from Germany. From one he learned that Molly had married. From another, years afterward, he heard that she had children. He did not ask how many. Later came news that her husband had been appointed to an honourable office. Anthony folded each letter and placed it in the iron-bound chest.

For a long time he could not forgive her.

He argued with her in his thoughts. He accused; she answered. He produced evidence; she smiled sadly. He repeated the words spoken in the bright room until they wore smooth. Sometimes, during the night, he awoke with his hand closed around the vanished ring.

But resentment, like an unpaid account, changes as years pass. At first it fills the whole page; then other entries crowd around it; at last the ink fades. Anthony came to see that Molly had told the truth and had promised nothing. He remembered not only the woman who rejected him, but the child with the red shoe pressing earth over an apple pip.

One winter evening, many years later, he took the three letters tied with black thread from his chest. They were Molly’s earliest letters, written before silence had begun. He read them and wept.

“I forgive you,” he said.

The room was empty, but forgiveness does not require the presence of the forgiven. It requires only that pride cease guarding the door.

Then he added, “Forgive me too.”

That was harder.

Anthony never married. There had been opportunities, though he hardly noticed them. A baker’s widow once showed him marked favour and sent him warm rolls on three consecutive Sundays. A seamstress with kind eyes mended his cuff without payment. Herr Bredemann’s cook said openly that a quiet man was better than a handsome rogue. But Anthony’s heart had become a room furnished for one absent guest; every chair stood in the wrong place for anyone else.

As he aged, his world narrowed. First he ceased travelling beyond the city gates. Then he no longer crossed the bridge. At last the stairs became so steep that he descended only for work and church. When he could work no more, the firm allowed him a small pension. It was not generous, but generosity is seldom entered under business expenses.

Children in the street called him Pepper Anthony. They laughed at his long coat and nightcap when he leaned from the window. Once a boy shouted, “There goes the old bridegroom!”

Anthony shook his fist, but not very fiercely.

“May you marry six times!” he called back.

The boy considered this a blessing and cheered.

Old age came with its small humiliations: buttons that escaped his fingers, letters that blurred, names that hid themselves, knees that objected to stairs, and sleep that arrived in the afternoon but refused to come at night. His brown nightcap wore through. The white one became thin and soft from use. Into it passed the warmth of his head, the oil of his hair, the smoke of his stove, and, above all, his tears.

For he wept more easily in old age. A hymn could make him weep. An apple in the market could make him weep. The sight of a girl in red shoes could bring tears before he understood why. Yet he was not always unhappy. There are quiet joys even in a lonely life: sunlight on the wall, fresh bread, a letter kindly written, bells across snow, a bird feeding at the sill. Anthony knew them and was grateful.

Then came the great snowstorm.

It began before dusk with small hard flakes driven sideways. By night the wind rushed through Copenhagen, rattling signs, filling alleys, piling snow against doors, and turning the harbour to a darkness of groaning ice. Roof tiles shifted; shutters banged; chimneys roared. Respectable citizens drew their curtains, stirred their fires, and congratulated themselves upon being indoors.

Anthony was indoors too, but there was little comfort in it.

He had been ill for several days. No fire burned in the stove, for the coal basket was empty. The woman who sometimes brought him food had not come; the snow prevented her. He lay beneath two blankets, wearing his coat and the white nightcap. His hands were blue at the nails.

He listened to the storm.

At times he believed someone climbed the stairs. He raised his head, but no one knocked. The steps belonged to the old house settling beneath the wind.

“Tomorrow,” he whispered, “someone will come tomorrow.”

Tomorrow is a strong servant, but it does not always arrive in time.

Frost spread across the window in fern leaves and silver flowers. Anthony watched them grow. The room darkened; yet within his mind another light appeared, faint at first, then golden.

He was again in Eisenach.

Not the Eisenach of any exact year, but the town as memory makes it, with every roof clean, every bell musical, every window bright. The Wartburg stood above the hills. Spring lay upon the valley, though snow beat against the Copenhagen panes.

Someone entered the room.

She wore a blue mantle and carried an apron filled with roses. Her face resembled the badly coloured picture over the chest, and yet it was living, gentle, and full of light.

“Saint Elizabeth,” Anthony said.

It is possible that fever spoke; it is possible that heaven answered. We shall not settle what wiser heads have failed to settle.

The saint looked at the cold stove.

“You have no bread,” she said.

“I am not hungry.”

This was not true, but old people often conceal hunger out of politeness, even from saints.

She lifted a rose from her apron. In her hand it became a red apple.

“Do you remember?”

Then the wall opened, and Anthony saw the tree.

It was no longer the small tree beneath the mayor’s wall, nor the storm-broken tree he had left behind. It rose vast and shining, its roots among the green hills and its branches among the stars. Blossoms and ripe fruit grew upon it together. Birds sang in its crown. Every tear Anthony had shed hung upon the leaves like a pearl of morning dew.

Beneath it stood Molly as a child, her dark hair loose and her red shoe upon the earth.

“Anthony,” she called, “you took long enough!”

He smiled.

“I had accounts to finish.”

“There are no accounts here.”

“No debts?”

“None.”

“No waiting?”

“None at all.”

She held out an apple. Behind her stood Anthony’s mother and father, young and unburdened. His mother wore the gold chain that had been sold. His father’s hands no longer trembled.

The saint touched Anthony’s forehead.

The cold left him. The storm became birdsong. He felt the grass beneath his feet and smelled apple blossom, bread, roses, and the clean earth after rain.

His bony fingers closed around the tassel of his nightcap.

In the morning the snow ceased.

The woman who brought him food climbed the stairs with a bowl of broth beneath her shawl. She knocked, received no answer, and entered. Anthony lay peacefully upon the bed. His eyes were closed. His face, freed from pain, seemed younger. In one hand he clutched the white nightcap, though it was still partly upon his head.

The broth was warm. Anthony was cold.

He was buried in the strangers’ corner of the churchyard. Herr Bredemann’s youngest son paid for the coffin, and two warehousemen followed it. The woman who brought the broth wept. A clerk said Anthony had been an honest man. Another remembered that he never made errors in addition. This was not a splendid funeral tribute, but it was true.

His possessions were sold or given away. The chest went to a sailor. The unreliable chair became firewood. The print of Saint Elizabeth was taken by the woman. The brown nightcap was thrown out.

The white cap remained.

No one wanted it. At last an old dealer in clothes bundled it with worn gloves, stockings, collars, and caps and carried the bundle to a stall near the market. There it lay for years, passing from box to box, until a poor student bought it on a bitter evening because it cost only two copper coins.

He laughed when he put it on.

“Now I am one of the pepper gentry!”

That night he dreamed of a German town beneath green hills. He saw two children planting an apple pip. He felt a love so tender that it frightened him; then came departure, silence, a bright room, a hidden ring, and a woman saying, “Do not, Anthony.”

The student awoke weeping.

On his blanket lay three small pearls.

He sold them and paid his rent. Being a student, he decided to repeat the experiment. The second night he wore the cap again.

This time he saw ships lost at sea, a merchant with his face in his hands, silver spoons carried away, a sick father lifted from bed, a grave beneath winter rain, and a young man bent over accounts while his heart cried out unheard. The sorrow was so heavy that the student tore off the cap before dawn.

Two more pearls fell from it.

He paid his debts, but he did not put it on again.

The cap passed to an actress, who believed tears useful to her profession. She wore it and dreamed of Molly; the next evening she played a forsaken maiden so movingly that the audience sobbed. Yet afterward she said no applause was worth the loneliness she had felt in that narrow room. She gave the cap to a pawnbroker.

The pawnbroker, who valued pearls highly and dreams not at all, wore it for seven nights. Pearls fell each morning, but each day he grew paler. He saw Anthony’s years one after another: the apple blossoms, the rejection, the ruin, the warehouse dust, the letters tied in black thread, the empty chair, the locked door, the snowstorm. On the seventh night he saw Saint Elizabeth and the heavenly tree; but he could not reach it, for the vision was Anthony’s consolation, not his.

He awoke crying, “Take it away!”

By then no one knew whose cap it had been. Stories gathered around it. Some said it belonged to a betrayed prince; others to a sorcerer; others to a miser whose gold had melted into tears. Yet an ancient woman remembered Pepper Anthony and his window in Hysken Street.

“It was his,” she said. “He had two caps—caps, not heads—and the white one knew all his thoughts.”

The cap is said to exist still. Perhaps it lies in a museum drawer, labelled incorrectly. Perhaps it hangs in a theatre wardrobe. Perhaps some thrifty person bought it yesterday and intends to wear it tonight. Old objects travel farther than old people, and they tell their histories only when they choose.

If ever you find a white nightcap with faded red stitching and a tassel bent to the left, do not covet it because pearls may fall from its folds. Pearls are beautiful, but tears paid for them. Do not laugh at the lonely bachelor who checks his door three times, counts his coals, and speaks sharply when young people mock him. You do not know what tree flowers behind his eyes, what name is tied within his heart, what debts he has carried, or what bright room he still enters in his dreams.

Every human head contains a country into which no stranger may step without sorrow.

Therefore, wish for your own sleep, your own thoughts, and your own honest cap, however plain it may be. But never wish for a bachelor’s nightcap; for you may inherit the pearls, and with them all the tears.

### The Establishment of a Demand

Coralio lay along the blue Caribbean with its white houses turned toward the sea and its commercial instincts asleep in the shade.

Its citizens required coffee, tobacco, lottery tickets, guitar strings, red cotton cloth, aguardiente, candles for the saints, and occasionally cartridges for political purposes. These articles moved briskly across counters and through customs. But shoes belonged to the region of luxury, theology, and snow shovels. The richer inhabitants possessed a pair apiece for weddings, funerals, and appearances before the alcalde; the remainder conducted life upon the durable leather supplied by nature.

Into this unpromising market came Mr. Abner Hemstetter, of Ohio, with three hundred and eighty cases of boots, brogans, gaiters, pumps, slippers, congress shoes, button shoes, patent-leather dancing shoes, infants’ shoes, and certain yellow hunting boots calculated to terrify any animal with eyes.

Mr. Hemstetter had been assured by a New Orleans shipping man that Coralio was “opening up.”

A country may open its mines, its ports, its legislature, or a bottle of imported champagne. Coralio had opened nothing but its mouth to yawn.

Mr. Hemstetter rented a building on the Calle Grande, painted above its door the words **EL EMPORIO DE CALZADO UNIVERSAL**, arranged his stock in pyramids, and waited for civilization to begin at the feet.

On the first day, twenty-seven persons entered the store. Eleven wished to sell parrots, eight inquired whether the new American doctor extracted teeth, five rested in the shade, two asked for credit, and one purchased a pair of red slippers after discovering that they could be exchanged for a fighting cock.

The consul of the United States at Coralio was John Atwood, called Johnny by citizens who owed him money and Señor Don Juan by those who intended to. He was young, smooth-faced, quick in action, and gifted with that useful statesmanlike quality which sees no wall where a ladder may be borrowed.

Johnny had a personal interest in the Universal Footwear Emporium. Mr. Hemstetter’s daughter, Rosine, had come south with her father.

Rosine had Ohio eyes, Caribbean color, and a way of looking at Johnny that made the consular salary appear insufficient but not hopeless. She assisted at the store in the mornings and sat in the hotel courtyard in the evenings beneath a flowering tree whose name Johnny never learned. Botany was not then his principal study.

“You encouraged Father to open here,” Rosine reminded him on the tenth evening.

“I furnished him facts,” said Johnny.

“You told him the town was prosperous.”

“It is. Prosperity is widely distributed. Nobody has enough of it to become proud.”

“You said there was an untouched field for shoes.”

“So there is. I never saw a field more untouched.”

Rosine declined to smile.

“Father has invested nearly everything. He says that if business does not improve, we must return home.”

Johnny looked toward the store. A native gentleman was standing before the window, admiring a pair of high black boots as a poet might admire a comet—with pleasure unpolluted by any desire to possess it.

“Business,” said Johnny, “is merely the art of bringing an article and its necessity together. Your father has brought the article. I will attend to the necessity.”

“That sounds like something illegal.”

“No,” said the consul. “Illegal things sound much simpler.”

The next morning Johnny called a conference at the Hotel de los Extranjeros. Present were Billy Keogh, who bought and sold whatever had temporarily escaped government supervision; Captain McClintock, of the fruit steamer *Suwanee*; Goodwin, an American planter; and Dr. Gregg, whose patients recovered whenever their diseases were stronger than his medicines.

Johnny explained the crisis.

“Gentlemen,” he concluded, “this is not charity. It is international commercial cooperation with a matrimonial tendency.”

Keogh tilted his chair against the wall. He was thin, red-haired, and possessed of the expression of a man who had just discovered a second meaning in the Ten Commandments.

“How many shoes?” he asked.

“Three hundred and eighty cases.”

“Then love,” said Keogh, “has put its foot into wholesale trade.”

They marched to the Emporium.

Captain McClintock bought twelve pairs of sea boots, though he had recently purchased sixteen in Mobile. Dr. Gregg took button shoes, tennis shoes, dancing pumps, two pairs of felt slippers, and a child’s bronze kid shoe that he declared useful for holding thermometers. Goodwin selected riding boots, walking shoes, house shoes, and three pairs of patent leather in which he resembled a diplomat standing in ink.

Keogh approached the business as a collector rather than a customer.

“Give me,” he said, “a pair of everything that looks as if it had committed an offense.”

He acquired green slippers embroidered with pink roses, white canvas shoes with rubber soles, hobnailed boots fit for Alpine warfare, delicate pumps for a lady of thirty-seven, and a pair of enormous tan brogans.

“What size do you wear?” asked Mr. Hemstetter.

“Anywhere from six to eleven,” said Keogh, “according to the emergency.”

For four days the expatriate colony supported the shoe trade. Men who had hitherto owned one pair now possessed enough footwear to equip a congressional committee. Shoes hung from bedposts, filled trunks, impeded doors, and appeared upon dining tables among the mangoes. Captain McClintock’s cabin became so heavily shod that the *Suwanee* listed two inches to starboard.

Then the movement ceased.

Mr. Hemstetter examined his ledger.

“You and your friends have been kind,” he told Johnny, “but friendship is not a market. At this rate I shall dispose of the stock in nine years.”

Johnny walked out with Keogh.

“The trouble,” he said, “is not a scarcity of buyers. It is the absence of a reason.”

“Same thing,” said Keogh.

“Not at all. A buyer is a man. A reason is a force. You cannot manufacture men without delay and scandal, but reasons can be produced overnight.”

Keogh brightened.

“Now,” he said, “you are talking the mother tongue of enterprise. What have you got up your diplomatic sleeve?”

Johnny led him to the consulate, unlocked a desk, and produced a dry brown object armed with hooks.

Keogh took it between thumb and finger.

“A cockleburr,” he said. “I have met the family. It clung to me during my youth in Kansas.”

“In my county in Texas,” Johnny explained, “they grow by the acre. Barefoot life is impossible where they flourish.”

Keogh looked toward the sunny street. Children ran over the warm stones. A fruit seller went by without shoes. A policeman lounged at the corner, his naked toes spread with the serenity of a sleeping fan.

Slowly Keogh’s face assumed the radiance of an inventor contemplating mankind’s need for his patent.

“Johnny,” he said, “you have discovered the protective tariff of the sole.”

“I have discovered the necessity for a demand.”

“Same thing, only better grammar.”

Johnny wrote that afternoon to Pinkney Dawson, an acquaintance in Texas who dealt in cattle, hides, pecans, land options, mule teams, and information that had not yet reached the sheriff. The letter requested several hundred pounds of cockleburrs, dry, ripe, and vigorous.

“Ship them at once,” Johnny wrote. “The market here should take all you can gather. Keep the matter quiet and follow instructions exactly.”

Business letters, like prophecies, are most dangerous where they are clear in the wrong place.

Three weeks later the fruit steamer brought six stout sacks addressed to the American consulate. Johnny explained that they contained agricultural specimens for an official investigation. As no one in Coralio had ever known an official investigation to produce anything useful, the sacks passed without question.

At midnight Johnny and Keogh began operations.

They dressed in dark clothes and carried the burrs in canvas bags. Along the Calle Grande they sowed them with the solemn industry of ancient farmers planting grain for an empire. They shook them beneath balconies, around fountains, beside the market, across the plaza, and before the doors of the principal houses. Keogh gave particular attention to the street leading to the government offices.

“Politics,” he whispered, casting a double handful, “should never go unpunctured.”

They worked until dawn painted a silver edge upon the sea. Then they retired to the hotel and slept the sleep of men whose consciences have been replaced by a business plan.

At seven o’clock Doña María Sepúlveda emerged from her house to attend early mass. Doña María was a lady of commanding breadth and ceremonial progress. She moved beneath a black lace shawl like a galleon under mourning colors.

Her first step into the street was stately.

Her second was interrogative.

Her third was not completed.

She uttered a sound of astonished sovereignty, raised one foot, placed the other upon a congregation of burrs, and became at once a complicated monument to betrayed dignity. Her servant tried to assist her, acquired several burrs, and joined in the dance. Together they hopped, revolved, reproached heaven, and finally retreated on hands and knees.

The alarm spread.

A milk boy ran three steps, bounded like a gazelle, overturned his cans, and sat in the road examining his soles with tragic attention. Two fishermen crossed the plaza in a series of leaps unpracticed by any school of navigation. A customs clerk proceeded sideways, choosing each foothold with the care of a statesman approaching a public question. Children wailed; dogs barked; parrots contributed scandal; a mule, perceiving excitement without understanding its source, kicked through the window of a barber shop.

Don Ildefonso Varela, former minister of finance and present authority on all subjects, strode from the café to investigate. A burr entered his left foot; another seized the right. Don Ildefonso rose, folded, and descended like a crumpled cathedral.

By nine o’clock the inhabitants of Coralio had become a nation of acrobats.

They hopped on one foot, then the other; they crawled, limped, shuffled, staggered, clung to walls, rode on the backs of friends, and sent servants ahead with brooms. Men who had endured revolutions without concern were conquered by a vegetable no larger than a walnut. The plaza resembled a battlefield in which the wounded had been forbidden to lie still.

At half past nine a small boy entered the Universal Footwear Emporium.

“How much,” he asked, “for shoes?”

Mr. Hemstetter named a price.

The boy purchased a pair three sizes too large and marched out in them with the pride of a newly armored knight.

At ten came his mother.

At a quarter after ten came the milk boy.

At eleven Doña María arrived in a hired carriage and bought six pairs for her household, refusing to permit economy to expose the Sepúlveda feet again to agricultural insult.

At noon the store was crowded.

The citizens tried shoes upon chairs, boxes, counters, and one another. Size became a philosophical distinction. A man who wore sevens accepted nines and filled the vacancies with cotton. A woman purchased men’s boots rather than await the unpacking of her number. Sandals, brogans, slippers, and hunting boots passed into service without prejudice. Credit was demanded, refused, demanded again, and granted to persons of suitable property or sufficiently alarming relatives.

Mr. Hemstetter stood behind the counter with the elevated calm of a general whose enemy has entered the valley precisely as planned.

Johnny came in near sunset.

“I see trade has improved,” he said.

Mr. Hemstetter removed his spectacles.

“Most remarkable,” he replied. “Some poisonous weed appears to have spread through the streets.”

“Nature,” said Johnny, “often corrects economic inequalities.”

Rosine looked at him sharply.

“What do you know about it?”

“I know,” said Johnny, “that a good shoe is mankind’s strongest argument against the ground.”

That night rain carried some of the burrs into gutters and doorways, and the traffic of the day crushed others. Demand weakened.

At midnight the two apostles of commerce went forth and renewed the crop.

For eight nights they labored. Keogh developed an artist’s touch. He sprinkled lightly before the poorer houses, where one pair per family would suffice, and sowed abundantly near the residences of men known to pay cash. He decorated the approaches to the barracks, the post office, the cathedral, and the club. In front of a rival merchant who had accused Americans of dishonesty, he laid down a brown carpet.

“This,” said Keogh, “is not vengeance. It is intensive cultivation.”

Coralio surrendered to shoes.

Boots clumped through the market. Slippers whispered along the galleries. Brogans struck sparks from the stones. Small boys wore cavalry boots to the knee; old men appeared in white tennis shoes; policemen acquired patent leather; fishermen stood in yellow leggings designed for the Scottish moors. The town had never been so uncomfortable, so protected, or so fashionable.

At the end of the second week Mr. Hemstetter’s shelves stood nearly empty.

Only forty cases remained: odd sizes, winter boots, and certain purple shoes with silver buckles that seemed intended for a theatrical prince undergoing financial difficulty.

One evening Johnny found Rosine in the courtyard.

“You are pleased now?” he asked.

“Father has saved his investment.”

“And you will not go back without me?”

“That depends.”

“On what?”

“On whether you tell me how cockleburrs came to Coralio.”

Diplomacy can evade a demand, postpone it, refer it to another department, or bury it beneath correspondence. But diplomacy in love frequently loses its portfolio.

Johnny confessed.

He described the letter, the sacks, the midnight sowing, the renewal of the crop, and Keogh’s agricultural enthusiasm. Rosine listened with her hands folded.

When he finished, she said, “You deliberately hurt the feet of a whole town to sell Father’s shoes.”

“Only temporarily.”

“You deceived Father.”

“For his benefit.”

“You deceived me.”

“For mine.”

“And you call this business?”

“I call it the establishment of a demand.”

Rosine turned away. Johnny saw his future depart toward Ohio wearing a pair of shoes he had helped to sell.

Then she laughed.

It began unwillingly, escaped control, and finally rang through the courtyard.

“I ought to be ashamed of you,” she said.

“You can attend to that after we are married.”

“I suppose Father must know.”

Mr. Hemstetter received the confession in the rear of the store. He heard it without interruption, drumming his fingers upon the ledger.

“You are an officer of the United States,” he said at last.

“At present.”

“You imported a harmful weed under official protection.”

“A botanical specimen.”

“You scattered it through the streets.”

“With Keogh’s technical assistance.”

“And induced the population to buy my goods.”

“That was the commercial aspect.”

Mr. Hemstetter looked toward the remaining cases.

“You say forty cases are left?”

“Forty.”

“Then,” said the merchant, “I cannot approve your conduct until they are sold.”

Johnny took the rebuke as a conditional blessing.

Three days later he and Keogh sailed with the forty cases and two sacks of burrs to San Mateo, a town eighty miles down the coast where shoes were held in the same esteem as polar bears. History there repeated itself, except that Keogh improved the campaign by sowing the burrs first around the council chamber during a municipal meeting. The councillors emerged in such urgent need of protection that they passed no ordinance against the new footwear shop.

Within ten days the stock was gone.

Johnny returned to Coralio, resigned his consular office, and prepared to sail north with the Hemstetters. Rosine had consented to marry him, though she reserved the right to describe his profession as “agriculture” whenever he became too dignified.

Keogh was appointed acting consul.

On the morning of departure the wharf gathered its usual cargo of freight, farewells, advice, chickens, official stamps, and people who had no business there. Mr. Hemstetter guarded the proceeds of the enterprise in a belt beneath his waistcoat. Rosine wore brown walking shoes. Johnny wore the expression of a man promoted from diplomacy to happiness.

Keogh shook his hand.

“You are leaving me,” he said, “in a country now civilized from the ankles down.”

“Keep out of trouble.”

“My boy, trouble is merely opportunity without its coat on.”

“And if any more sacks arrive from Texas—”

“Throw them into the sea. I have retired from horticulture.”

The steamer carried Johnny, Rosine, and Mr. Hemstetter northward. Coralio returned to politics, coffee, and the daily admiration of its own climate. The burrs disappeared. Shoes remained. Men complained of corns with the pride of progress, and ladies discussed leather as though their ancestors had descended from a Mayflower fitted with a cobbler’s bench.

Twelve days later two schooners entered the harbor.

They rode low in the water. Their decks were piled with bales; their holds were packed to the beams. At the head of the first stood Pinkney Dawson, sunburned, practical, and jubilant.

Keogh went down to meet him.

“Got your goods,” Dawson called. “Had to clean out four counties. Hired schoolchildren, farmhands, two church societies, and every idle man from San Antonio to the border. First lot sold so well, I figured you wanted the market covered. There’s close to forty tons aboard.”

“Forty tons of what?” asked Keogh, although Providence had already whispered the answer.

“Cockleburrs,” said Dawson. “Finest crop in Texas. And there’s another schooner five days behind us.”

Keogh sat down upon a coil of rope.

Before him lay a mountain of brown, hooked, industrious uselessness. Behind him Coralio passed in triumphant footwear—boots pounding, heels clicking, soles slapping, buckles jingling. Dawson explained freight charges, gathering costs, prospective contracts, and the unlimited future of the cockleburr trade.

Keogh listened until the full magnificence of the situation entered his soul.

Then he laughed.

He laughed until sailors leaned over the rails, until customs officers forgot to demand papers, until Dawson suspected tropical fever. He laughed at Johnny’s letter, at Texas enterprise, at supply outrunning necessity, at two shiploads of torment arriving after every foot had been armored against them.

And over his laughter sounded the clump and creak of Coralio’s newly shod citizens, while beyond the wharf the Caribbean, requiring neither market nor salesman, folded its blue waves upon the sand.

### The Exact Measure

Mr. James Duffy lived in a square room at the back of a red-brick house in Chapelizod because he considered the centre of Dublin noisy without being lively and the newer suburbs cheerful without cause. From his window he could see a narrow garden, a wall glazed with damp, and, above the roofs beyond it, the dark rise of the park. The room was furnished with an iron bedstead, a washstand, two cane chairs and a writing table whose drawers gave off a faint medicinal smell. Three white shelves held his books in exact ranks. Philosophy occupied the upper shelf, drama and poetry the second, and on the lowest stood several volumes concerning political economy, bound copies of musical programmes, and a small Italian dictionary.

There were no pictures. Over the bed hung a calendar supplied by the bank, though Mr. Duffy had removed the coloured advertisement from its lower half. On the table stood an ink-bottle, a blotter, a brass candlestick and a sheaf of manuscript paper. Every object had its appointed station. Disorder seemed to him not merely inconvenient but insincere, as if things displaced from their proper positions were attempting to claim a significance they did not possess.

He was cashier in a private bank near College Green. At eight each morning he drank tea, ate two pieces of dry toast and walked to the tram. At one he dined in a plain eating-house where the waiters knew that he required boiled beef on Mondays, mutton on Tuesdays, fish on Fridays and no conversation on any day. After the bank closed he returned by tram, carrying an evening newspaper folded narrowly under his arm. Three nights a week he walked through the park or along the quays. On other evenings he read, copied passages into a notebook, or played fragments of old music upon a violin which he kept in a black case beneath the bed.

He had no intimate friends. Those clerks who had once invited him to supper had ceased doing so, accepting his refusals as evidence of pride; he accepted their cessation as evidence of good sense. He visited a distant cousin at Christmas and received, at long intervals, letters from a brother established in London. These letters he answered conscientiously and without inquiry.

His face was long and brown, with a firm mouth and a pair of attentive eyes in which disappointment appeared to have preceded experience. He wore his hair brushed severely backward. His clothes were dark and durable. Though his body was strong, he regarded it as an instrument requiring maintenance rather than indulgence. Sometimes, during his solitary walks, he seemed to observe himself from a small elevation: a man passing beneath bare branches, turning at the prescribed gate, avoiding puddles, proceeding homeward. He approved the figure’s independence.

The greater part of human intercourse appeared to him improvised. People spoke in order not to hear themselves thinking. They married from timidity, begot children from habit and called the resulting arrangement happiness. Mr. Duffy did not deny that certain minds might meet; he denied that they could remain joined without diminishment. Every attachment, he had written in his notebook, submits the spirit to the accidents of another life.

He attended concerts, however. Music, unlike conversation, imposed form upon emotion and removed the necessity of answering it. One November evening he went to a performance in an assembly-room off Great Brunswick Street. The rain had ceased, but the stones retained a black lustre, and the audience entered carrying the chill of the streets upon their coats.

The room was imperfectly filled. During the first part of the programme Mr. Duffy observed, two seats from him, a woman of about forty whose attention resembled his own in its severity. She was neither fashionably nor negligently dressed. A dark shawl covered her shoulders, and a narrow silver chain descended from her throat. Her face had a faded composure, but her eyes were warm and direct. A heavy man beside her slept intermittently, waking at applause and joining it with noisy promptness.

During the interval the heavy man went in search of refreshment. The woman’s programme fell. Mr. Duffy restored it to her.

“Thank you,” she said. “The floor seems determined to possess it.”

“The floor has heard more of the concert than that gentleman,” he replied, glancing toward the departing figure.

She looked at him, uncertain whether she should be offended. Then she smiled.

“He is not fond of quartets.”

“Yet he applauds them with conviction.”

“He applauds conclusions.”

The bell rang before either could continue. The heavy man returned smelling of whiskey, and Mr. Duffy heard the woman address him as Captain Sinico.

When the concert ended, a sudden shower detained the audience beneath the portico. Captain Sinico discovered an acquaintance among a group of seamen and became absorbed in loud reminiscence. The woman stood a little apart. Mr. Duffy, after a hesitation which he attributed to courtesy, spoke to her about the final piece. Her response was intelligent and unborrowed. She had noticed a phrase in the slow movement which he had thought no one else in the room had heard properly. They spoke until the captain approached and was introduced.

Captain Sinico commanded a small vessel trading between Dublin and Dutch ports. His manner toward his wife was bluff and indulgent, as toward a passenger for whose comfort he had made reasonable provision. He invited Mr. Duffy to walk with them as far as the bridge. The rain had diminished to a fine mist. Before they parted Mrs. Sinico said that she hoped they might meet at another concert.

For several weeks Mr. Duffy regarded this encounter as one of those incomplete incidents which acquire their interest from not being repeated. Then, at a lecture on Scandinavian drama, he saw her again. She was alone. Captain Sinico had sailed two days before.

They sat together. Afterward they walked toward the northbound trams, discussing the lecturer’s sentimental interpretation of the play. Mrs. Sinico laughed readily, but never from politeness. Her laughter gave Mr. Duffy the unusual sensation that his severity possessed an amiable aspect. At the tram stop she told him that she lived with her husband and daughter in a house near the sea at Sydney Parade.

“My daughter studies music,” she said. “Or is studied by it. I cannot decide which suffers more.”

He smiled.

“You play?”

“A little. Badly enough to understand good playing.”

She asked whether he attended the Sunday concerts at the Rotunda. He answered that he sometimes did. Neither proposed an appointment, yet both understood that the next Sunday had been distinguished from other Sundays.

Their meetings became regular without being formally arranged. At first they occurred in public rooms, under the scrutiny of portraits, attendants and dispersed audiences. Later they walked together after concerts, choosing quiet streets where their conversation might proceed without interruption. Mrs. Sinico’s husband was often absent; her daughter, Mary, was occupied with lessons and acquaintances of her own. The practical impediments to friendship were therefore less immediate than the social ones, and these Mr. Duffy dismissed as the inventions of inferior minds.

He called once at her house on the pretext of lending her a book. Captain Sinico received him cordially, mistaking his reserve for distinction. He poured whiskey and spoke at length of fog, freight charges and the stupidity of harbour officials. Mrs. Sinico sat near the fire, listening with an expression which did not quite conceal fatigue. Mary, a pale girl with quick hands, entered and left several times. Nothing in the household suggested scandal; rather, it suggested an arrangement in which each person had been granted a separate solitude.

After that visit Mrs. Sinico sometimes came into the city to meet him. They walked along the canal, through the park, or beyond the last respectable terraces to roads bordered by fields. In bad weather they sat in a small room above a pastry shop where the proprietress, supposing them to be relations, brought tea and withdrew. Mr. Duffy chose a table beside the window. From it they could see umbrellas moving below like dark, agitated leaves.

He spoke to her of books, of music and of the decay of public intelligence. He described his former interest in labour politics and the disgust which had driven him from meetings where men demanded liberty in phrases learned from newspapers. She did not contest his contempt, but asked questions which forced it to become particular. He found himself telling her what he had never told his colleagues or his relations: that he had once intended to write a work on the moral servitude of modern life; that he feared not failure but contamination by approval; that certain music produced in him a grief for events which had not occurred.

She listened without the impatience concealed in most attention. His thoughts, transplanted from the cold privacy in which they had grown, seemed altered by her reception of them. Her mind surrounded them with a quiet fertility. Under its influence his most barren conclusions put forth unsuspected shoots.

Mrs. Sinico spoke less about herself. By degrees he learned that her marriage had been encouraged by an aunt, that Captain Sinico had been kind, that kindness might become a wall as readily as cruelty, and that her daughter’s affection contained the serene selfishness of youth. She did not accuse them. Her very moderation gave her loneliness authority.

One evening, as they walked on an elevated road above the city, the winter sky cleared in the west. Chimneys and church towers stood black against a band of exhausted gold. Lamps appeared below them, one after another, and the windows of distant houses reddened hospitably. Mrs. Sinico paused.

“It looks near from here,” she said.

“What does?”

“Other people’s lives.”

He answered that proximity was an illusion created by light.

She turned toward him with a smile which acknowledged the answer and forgave it. He felt then an impulse to touch her arm. The impulse passed unfulfilled, leaving him both relieved and diminished.

For nearly two years their friendship continued. It became the central fact of his life while remaining absent from its visible arrangements. His meals, accounts, journeys and readings preserved their old order, yet behind them existed a private country into which he admitted only her. He revised his opinions in anticipation of her questions. During the day phrases occurred to him and were retained for their next meeting. At night, when he opened a book, he sometimes saw not the page but her attentive face.

He did not name this dependence. To name a feeling was, in his view, to surrender judgement over it. Their relation was spiritual, exceptional, freed from vulgar appetite. He believed that two minds, disciplined and candid, might achieve a communion inaccessible to persons governed by the body. If her presence stirred in him obscure physical uneasiness, he treated it as one treats a noise in an adjoining room.

The end came in early spring.

They had met in the room above the pastry shop. Rain tapped at the window, and the smell of wet wool mingled with that of tea. Mrs. Sinico appeared distracted. Twice she asked him to repeat himself. He was speaking of a passage he had copied into his notebook, an argument that love, by making happiness dependent upon another, contained grief from its inception.

“You always speak of grief as if foreseeing it were the same as escaping it,” she said.

He looked at her. Her face was pale; the shadow beneath her eyes had deepened.

“Foreknowledge is a kind of liberty.”

“Is it?”

“For those capable of using it.”

She lowered her gaze. He understood that he had wounded her and experienced, beneath his regret, a mean satisfaction that she had recognised his strength.

After a silence he began to explain. He said that the soul was finally solitary; that all efforts to deny this fact ended in possessiveness or deception; that affection was noble only while it demanded nothing. His voice, subdued by the small room, acquired the grave persuasion of a sermon.

Mrs. Sinico leaned toward him.

“James,” she said.

It was the first time she had used his Christian name. Before he could answer, she took his hand in both of hers and pressed it against her cheek.

He felt the warmth of her skin, the slight dampness beneath her eye, the quick movement of blood at her temple. The gesture, simple and desperate, seemed to destroy at once the elevated structure he had made of their friendship. What he had called communion revealed a body, a claim, a future crowded with consequences. He withdrew his hand.

She remained motionless.

“We have been mistaken,” he said.

Her face changed, not dramatically, but as a room changes when its fire goes out.

“Mistaken?”

“In the nature of this association.”

She looked toward the rain-blurred window.

“I have not been mistaken.”

The answer alarmed him more than reproach would have done. He rose, placed money on the tray and said they should leave. In the street they walked apart. At the corner where their routes divided, she asked when she would see him.

“I think it would be better if we did not meet for some time.”

“How long?”

He could not reply without confessing that he intended permanence.

She nodded slowly. “I see.”

He returned home on foot, though rain had entered his shoes. In his room every object appeared to have maintained its station in his absence with exemplary fidelity. He dried his hair, changed his socks and sat at the table. His hand still retained the sensation of her cheek. He washed it in cold water.

During the following days he composed a letter, destroyed it, and composed another. The final letter was concise. He expressed gratitude for a friendship which had enriched him, admitted responsibility for permitting an ambiguity to arise, and stated that continuation would be injurious to them both. He did not mention Captain Sinico. The existence of the husband was a practical argument, and Mr. Duffy wished his decision to proceed from principle.

She answered, asking for one meeting. They met in a small café near the railway. Her distress embarrassed him because it was visible despite her efforts. She spoke of isolation, of the impossibility of returning to a life which their friendship had taught her to understand. He replied that precisely for this reason separation was necessary. A bond founded upon need could not remain pure. All bonds, once they demanded permanence, became bonds to suffering.

“You have made a law of your fear,” she said.

The phrase angered him. He accused her, with controlled gentleness, of confusing emotional violence with truth. She ceased arguing. When they rose, she offered her hand. He touched it briefly.

Outside, a train passed beneath the bridge. Its windows, illuminated and empty-looking, moved across the darkness in rapid succession. Mrs. Sinico watched it until the last carriage had gone.

Mr. Duffy did not look back.

For four years his life resumed its former shape. At first the restored silence felt severe but wholesome, like a prescribed treatment. He avoided the concerts they had attended and altered the direction of his Sunday walks. Gradually these precautions became unnecessary. Her image visited him less often and then only in connection with some opinion she had shared or misunderstood.

He received a promotion of modest consequence. The eating-house changed its proprietor. His landlord repapered the corridor. Mary Sinico’s name appeared once in a notice of a pupils’ recital, and Mr. Duffy passed over it without pause. Time, he concluded, did not heal; it classified. What had once seemed urgent assumed its proper historical dimensions.

One November evening he dined later than usual. The day had been occupied by a discrepancy in the accounts, and his head retained the dull pressure of figures. He sat alone at a table near the wall, eating corned beef and cabbage. The meat was lukewarm, and small islands of grease hardened upon the gravy.

He unfolded the evening newspaper. The first pages concerned parliamentary speeches and a municipal dispute. Turning the sheet, he saw a heading in heavy type:

**WOMAN KILLED ON RAILWAY LINE**

Below it appeared a smaller line:

**SAD OCCURRENCE NEAR SYDNEY PARADE**

He read the name before he understood the sentence containing it.

The report stated that Mrs. Emily Sinico, aged forty-five, wife of Captain Michael Sinico, had been struck by the nine-fifteen train on the previous evening. Evidence at the inquest suggested that she had attempted to cross the line after leaving a public-house. The driver had observed a female figure near the rails, sounded the whistle and applied the brake, but had been unable to prevent the collision.

A railway employee testified that the deceased had lately been seen in the vicinity at irregular hours. The proprietor of the public-house stated that she had been served with a small measure of spirits and had appeared unsteady. Captain Sinico, recalled from his vessel, said his wife had become “rather intemperate” during recent years. Her daughter confirmed that the deceased had sometimes left the house alone after dinner. The jury returned a verdict of accidental death and expressed sympathy with the family.

Mr. Duffy read the report twice. The words did not enlarge or alter; they remained exact, public and indecent.

His first sensation was disgust. The coarse food before him, the stained tablecloth, the testimony of the publican and railway servants became part of a single contamination. So this was what she had become. A furtive drinker wandering near a suburban line, observed by loafers, discussed before a jury. She had degraded the memory of their friendship and proved that beneath her intellectual sympathy there had existed an appetite for disorder.

He pushed away his plate. His stomach contracted.

The decision he had made four years earlier now seemed vindicated with brutal completeness. He had perceived instability where she had claimed devotion. Had he continued their association, he might have been dragged into secrecy, complaint and scandal. Her hand upon his had not been a revelation but a warning. He had escaped.

He paid and went into the street.

The air was cold and smelled of smoke. Trams clanged past, crowded with people returning home. He walked westward, repeating the facts in a judicial sequence: alcohol, deception, railway line, death. Yet the very neatness of the sequence disturbed him. The newspaper had arranged her end as he arranged figures in a column. Nothing remained outside it except what the report could not admit.

He saw again the room above the pastry shop. He felt the pressure of her cheek against his palm.

What life had she returned to after their final meeting? Captain Sinico at sea or speaking of freight; Mary practising scales in an upper room; meals taken beneath a lamp; evenings lengthening without expectation. Had she waited for a letter? Had she met each post with diminishing hope? He had imagined that she would recover the tranquil habits of her former existence, though she had told him that those habits had become intolerable. He had called her distress emotional violence because it required an answer he was unwilling to give.

His disgust turned against itself. It had been prompt, almost eager. Even after death he had judged her first and understood afterward.

He entered the park. The lamps stood far apart, each casting a weak circle upon the path. Beyond them the grass extended into obscurity. His footsteps sounded unnaturally distinct. He walked toward the rise from which they had once watched the city windows appearing in the dusk.

She had loved him. The statement presented itself without ornament. She had loved him, and he had answered her with a theory.

He stopped beside a gaunt tree. A trembling began in his hands and passed into his arms. He placed one hand against the trunk, feeling the wet roughness of the bark. The body he had treated as a servant now accused him in its own language: nausea, cold, constricted breath.

He tried to imagine the last minutes. A public-house door closing behind her. The dark road. The rails shining faintly. Perhaps she had not intended death. Perhaps she had heard the train and misjudged its distance. Perhaps drink had softened the world until danger appeared no more substantial than memory. The report had decided accident, but its decision brought no mercy.

A train sounded far off.

The noise advanced through the darkness, metallic and regular. As it gathered force, its wheels appeared to beat out the syllables of her name. Emily Sinico. Emily Sinico. The repetition entered his nerves. He saw her alone beside the line, her face turned toward the approaching light, and for an instant he believed that she had called to him across the four silent years.

He moved away from the path and climbed the slope. Below him Dublin glimmered, red and yellow, under a low sky. In the hollows of the park he could distinguish couples sitting close together despite the cold. Their obscurity, which once would have seemed vulgar, now protected something from which he had excluded himself. They possessed no principles against sorrow. They accepted the risk of another body’s warmth.

He had believed himself free because no one could wound him. But freedom from injury had required freedom from consolation, dependence, forgiveness and touch. His life, so carefully defended, had contracted around its defender. The room, the shelves, the meals, the measured walks—all were not proofs of independence but articles in a cell.

He listened as the train passed beyond the trees. The pounding diminished. Her name broke into fragments, then into a faint mechanical murmur.

He wondered whether she had thought of him while she drank. The question was intolerable. More intolerable was the possibility that she had ceased to think of him, that even her suffering had ultimately found him irrelevant. He had preserved her within an abstract memory, unchanged and available to his private judgement. Meanwhile she had lived through four winters, had aged, concealed bottles, quarrelled perhaps with her daughter, stumbled along roads, awakened ashamed. She had suffered beyond his knowledge. She had become real where he had refused reality.

He walked until the city lights were hidden by trees. In the deeper darkness he paused and waited for some renewal of her presence: the warmth at his hand, the tone in which she had spoken his name, the patient intelligence of her listening. Nothing came.

He called her inwardly, without words. The darkness did not answer.

A wind moved through the branches and ceased. From the road beyond the wall came the remote sound of a carriage. Then that too faded.

He listened again.

There was no train, no music, no human voice. He could not feel her near him. He could not even recover the pain of her touch.

The night was perfectly still.

Mr. Duffy understood that he was alone.

### The Scarf

The first sign is not the shouting but the onions rolling.

They tumble from a wooden tray, red bulbs skipping over the packed earth of Sabon Gari market, disappearing beneath sandals and plastic slippers. A woman abandons her basket of dried fish. A boy runs with one shoe in his hand. Then the shouting thickens, seems to gather itself from many corners at once, and Chika hears the words Christian and Muslim, hears Igbo, Hausa, unbeliever, brother, enemy, though afterward she will not be certain which words were truly spoken and which ones fear supplied.

Nnedi is beside her one moment and gone the next.

They have come to buy fabric for their mother, something sober enough for the memorial Mass of an uncle neither of them remembers well. Nnedi has rejected three bolts of lace already. She wants blue; Chika says their mother will prefer gold. They are arguing lightly, their usual way, when the crowd presses between them.

“Nnedi!”

Chika sees a flash of her sister’s yellow blouse near a stall of enamel basins. Or thinks she sees it. She pushes after it, gripping her handbag to her chest, but a man slams into her shoulder. Someone is crying that they are coming from the mosque. Someone else says a church has been burned. The traders are dragging down corrugated shutters, gathering money, children, prayer mats. A bottle breaks. Smoke rises beyond the roofs.

“Nnedi!”

Her voice enters the noise and vanishes.

A hand closes around Chika’s wrist. She jerks back, ready to strike, and sees a woman in a faded green wrapper and an orange scarf printed with purple flowers. The woman has a round face, a narrow scar by her left eye, and the same quick terror Chika feels inside her own ribs.

“Come,” the woman says. “Come fast-fast.”

Chika does not move. The woman is Hausa; her accent makes this certain. She is Muslim; the scarf makes that nearly certain. Outside the ordinary boundaries of the university, Chika has often thought in such certainties without noticing them. This one sells onions, perhaps, because onion skins cling to her wrapper and because her hands have the dry, pale dust of onions ground into their creases.

Behind them somebody screams.

The woman pulls harder. “You want them see you?”

Them. The word could mean anybody.

Chika follows.

They hurry down an alley behind the spice stalls, past a gutter choked with black water and orange peels. Chika’s leather sandals slip. Something sharp tears the skin below her knee, but there is no time to look. The woman pushes open a warped wooden door, draws Chika into a dark shop, then closes the door until only a narrow blade of daylight remains.

The place smells of dust, old soap and rust. Empty shelves line one wall. A broken weighing scale lies on its side. Near the back is a tap above a stained basin, and high up, close to the ceiling, is a square window netted with cobwebs.

“Sit,” the woman says.

She spreads a corner of her wrapper over the floor, as if dust matters now, as if there is still a proper way to receive a stranger. Chika sits because her legs are trembling.

Outside, feet pound past. The door shakes once, lightly. Chika presses her palm over the silver cross at her throat. It is not a gesture of belief. She has not believed properly for years, not in the way her mother believes, with Mass on weekdays and holy water by every door. Still, her fingers hold the cross.

“Nnedi,” she whispers.

The woman crouches by her. “Who be Nnedi?”

“My sister. We were together. At the cloth stalls. Then people started running.”

“She young?”

“Twenty-two.”

“Maybe she run another place.”

“Yes.” Chika says it too quickly. “She’ll have found somewhere.”

The woman nods, accepting the statement as something useful, not necessarily true.

Later, Chika will repeat those words in police stations and hospitals: She will have found somewhere. She will tell them Nnedi is twenty-two but looks younger, that she has a small gap between her front teeth, that she was wearing a yellow blouse and blue jeans, that her left ear has two piercings and her right only one. She will show photographs until the photographs bend at the corners. She will visit school halls where survivors sleep on mats, mortuaries where attendants uncover faces with impatient hands, and clinics so crowded that blood darkens the corridors.

She will not find Nnedi.

But now she listens to the market tearing itself apart beyond the door and imagines her sister hiding under a trader’s table, annoyed that dust is settling on her blouse. Nnedi is clever. Nnedi knows Kano better. Nnedi, unlike Chika, speaks enough Hausa to bargain and joke with taxi drivers.

“My daughter too,” the woman says. “Halima. She go sell groundnut near motor park.”

“How old is she?”

“Ten and four.”

“Fourteen?”

“Yes. Fourteen.”

The woman brings a hand to her mouth. Her nails are cut very short. Chika notices a dark line beneath one nail and dislikes herself for noticing it.

“She will have run home,” Chika says.

“Our house far. Two buses.”

“Maybe somebody took her in.”

“Yes. God willing.”

They sit side by side, not touching. Through the slit beside the door, smoke turns the daylight gray. It smells oddly familiar to Chika, like the singed hair when the cook at home burns the skin from a goat’s head over the outdoor fire. That smell had always made her close the kitchen window. Now there is no window to close.

A burst of chanting approaches. The words are indistinct, but the rhythm is not: call, answer, call, answer. Chika’s body tightens.

The woman reaches across her and presses the door fully shut.

Darkness settles around them. Dust floats in the little light from the high window, each grain briefly visible, then gone. Chika hears the woman’s breathing and tries to make hers as quiet.

They come near enough that Chika can distinguish individual sounds: slippers scraping, a metal object dragged over the ground, a boy’s voice stretched high with excitement. Someone bangs on a shutter. Something heavy falls. The woman’s hand finds Chika’s forearm and grips it.

Chika wonders if the mob is Muslim or Christian. The question flashes through her with humiliating urgency, as though one answer might save her. Yet if Christians open the door, will they pause long enough to see the cross hidden beneath her blouse? Will they hear her name? Will they believe her? And if Muslims open it, will they spare the woman and kill Chika beside her? Perhaps the woman will plead. Perhaps she will not. It is absurd to wonder about loyalty between two people who met minutes ago. It is also impossible not to wonder.

The voices pass.

The woman releases Chika’s arm.

“Sorry,” she murmurs.

Chika almost laughs. The woman has saved her and is apologizing for holding too hard.

“What is your name?” Chika asks.

The woman hesitates. “Aisha.”

“I’m Chika.”

“Chika,” Aisha repeats carefully.

The way she says it makes the name feel solid, restored to Chika after the crowd has reduced everyone to labels.

Aisha reaches into the folds of her wrapper and brings out a small plastic bag. Inside are several crumpled naira notes, a key and two sugar sweets in clear wrappers. She checks the notes, counts them without moving her lips, then hides the bag again.

“Your onions?” Chika asks.

Aisha looks toward the door. “Finish.”

“You had a stall?”

“Small table. Not my own stall.”

“Will they steal everything?”

Aisha shrugs. “If no burn.”

There is no self-pity in her voice. This unsettles Chika more than tears might have. Chika thinks of her own handbag, bought at Heathrow, which she is still clutching like a life jacket. Inside are a mobile phone with no signal, a wallet, lip balm, lecture notes, a bottle of perfume, two pens, and the swatch of blue fabric Nnedi preferred. The bag cost more than Aisha may earn in months. Perhaps in a year. Chika begins to calculate, then stops. The arithmetic feels indecent.

“Do you have water?” she asks.

Aisha points to the tap.

The handle resists before giving a rusty squeal. A thin stream comes out, brown at first, then almost clear. Chika cups her hands beneath it. The water smells metallic. She drinks anyway and splashes some over her face.

When she straightens, Aisha is watching.

“You want?”

Aisha comes to the basin. She drinks without letting her mouth touch her hands, then washes her face and forearms. She removes her scarf, revealing neat cornrows and small gold-colored earrings. Without the scarf she looks younger, perhaps thirty-five, perhaps less. Chika had assumed she was near fifty. It is the wrapper, she thinks, and the tired skin around her eyes. It is also Chika’s habit of making poor women old.

Aisha spreads the scarf on the basin to dry the splashed edge. It is cheap polyester, painfully bright even in the dim shop. Chika imagines it hanging among hundreds like it in the market. Two hundred naira, perhaps. Three hundred.

Later, that scarf will lie in the locked bottom drawer of Chika’s desk. She will keep it wrapped in tissue paper, though the dried blood will make one end stiff and brown. For years she will open the drawer only when alone. Sometimes she will lift the scarf to her face, expecting smoke, rust, sweat. Mostly it will smell of paper and the lavender sachets her mother sends from England.

But now it is merely an ugly scarf drying beside a rusted tap.

Aisha lowers herself to the floor and winces. One hand moves to her chest.

“Are you hurt?” Chika asks.

“No.”

“You look uncomfortable.”

Aisha adjusts her blouse. “Breast pain me.”

“Do you have a baby?”

“One year. He still take breast.”

Chika’s fear loosens slightly because here is something named, something bodily and limited, a problem that might have an explanation. “Both breasts?”

“This one pass.” Aisha cups her left breast through the cloth.

“May I look? I’m a medical student.”

Aisha’s eyes brighten with respect so immediate that Chika feels fraudulent. She is only in her fourth year. Her hands still shake before examinations; she forgets drug dosages; she has once fainted in the dissection room and pretended afterward that she had skipped breakfast.

“You be doctor?”

“Not yet.”

“But you know medicine.”

“A little.”

Aisha turns her back to the door and unbuttons her blouse. Her breast is heavy and veined, the nipple split with two raw cracks. Chika leans closer. There is no swelling, no heat that she can detect, no obvious pus.

“How long?”

“Many days. When baby suck, pain well-well.”

“You should clean it gently and let it dry. If you can, put a little purified lanolin on it. Or petroleum jelly if that’s what you have. The baby may not be latching properly.”

Aisha frowns. “Latching?”

“Taking the nipple. His mouth should take more of the dark part too, not only the nipple.”

Chika demonstrates with her thumb and fingers. Aisha watches intently.

“My mother had the same thing,” Chika adds. “When she was feeding my younger brother.”

The lie arrives smoothly. Chika has no younger brother. Her mother never breast-fed her or Nnedi; there had been a nurse with white shoes and bottles lined in boiling water. Yet the lie seems to place a plank across the distance between them. Aisha’s face softens.

“Your mother use what?”

“Warm water. Petroleum jelly. And she changed the baby’s position.”

“I do so.”

“If you get fever, or if the breast becomes hot and swollen, go to a clinic.”

Aisha covers herself. “Clinic take money.”

“There are public clinics.”

Aisha gives her a look that is not disrespectful, only patient. It says Chika knows medicine but not clinics, not the ones where cards vanish unless folded notes are slipped beneath them, where women wait from sunrise until afternoon and are told to return tomorrow.

“Yes,” Chika says, embarrassed. “They can still be expensive.”

Outside, there is a crack like a gunshot. Chika ducks. Aisha does not; she tilts her head and listens.

“Tyre,” she says.

“How do you know?”

“When tyre burn, first it make that sound.”

Chika nods as though she knew this too.

Aisha takes one of the sweets from her plastic bag and offers it. Chika unwraps it. The sugar has softened in the heat. It tastes faintly of orange.

“Take the other,” Chika says when Aisha begins to put it away.

“For Halima.”

The words are simple, but they stop Chika from chewing.

Aisha closes her fist around the sweet. For the first time her composure changes. Her lips tremble. She turns her face toward the wall and makes no sound. Tears slide down beside her nose, one after another, while she keeps her back straight. Chika looks away. It seems important not to touch her, not to say Halima will be fine again, not to make the crying public.

After a while Aisha wipes her face with the heel of her hand.

“My Nnedi is stubborn,” Chika says, because she needs to offer something. “If people tell her to stay hidden, she’ll argue that she knows a safer route.”

Aisha smiles faintly. “Halima too much stubborn. She say she no marry onion seller.”

“What does she want to be?”

“Teacher. Sometime nurse. Sometime woman for television.”

“She could be all three.”

Aisha laughs then, one brief breath. “You people for university think person can be everything.”

Chika cannot tell whether it is admiration or rebuke.

She tells Aisha that Nnedi studies political science, that she reads newspapers from back to front and writes angry notes in the margins. Last week Nnedi complained about an article describing the city as divided along ancient religious lines.

“Nothing here is ancient in the way they mean,” Nnedi had said, lying across Chika’s bed with her feet on the pillows. “People use that word so nobody has to ask who benefits now.”

At the time Chika had told her to move her dusty feet.

Later, newspapers will call the riot spontaneous. They will report disputed numbers: eighty dead, one hundred and twelve, perhaps two hundred. A radio guest will speak of religious violence complicated by ethnic grievances, each word clean and bloodless. Photographs will show blackened cars and men raising sticks. Nobody will photograph Aisha saving the second sweet for Halima. Nobody will list the cracked nipple, the onions in the dust, the cloth swatch in Chika’s handbag.

Nnedi would have circled spontaneous in red ink.

But now Chika still believes she will hear her sister’s key in the front door by evening.

The hours become shapeless. Heat accumulates in the shop. Chika’s blouse sticks to her back, and dust coats her tongue. Sometimes they hear running, sometimes singing, sometimes a silence worse than either. Once a smell of paraffin pushes under the door and both women stand, thinking the building is on fire. It passes.

Chika’s injured leg begins to throb. She looks down and sees that blood has dried from her knee to her ankle in a dark, narrow trail.

Aisha clicks her tongue. “Why you no tell me?”

“It’s just a cut.”

“Sit.”

“I’m fine.”

“Sit, doctor.”

There is humor in the word now. Chika obeys.

Aisha turns on the tap and wets the cleanest end of her scarf. She kneels, lifting Chika’s leg onto her thigh with matter-of-fact strength. The wound is not deep, but grit is embedded in it. Aisha cleans slowly. Each touch burns.

“Sorry, oh,” she says whenever Chika flinches.

“You’re doing it well.”

“I get four children.”

As if that explains all nursing skill, all competence. Perhaps it does explain some of it.

Aisha tears a strip from the end of the scarf. The polyester resists; she grips it in her teeth and pulls until the fabric gives with a harsh rip. Then she wraps Chika’s leg and knots the strip firmly.

The purple flowers disappear beneath the blood almost at once.

Chika thinks of telling her she should not have ruined the scarf. She thinks of offering money. Both impulses seem insulting, though she cannot say exactly why.

“Thank you,” she says.

Aisha pats her ankle. “Small thing.”

Toward late afternoon, the noise recedes. Chika checks her phone again. No signal. The screen shows nine missed attempts to call Nnedi, though none connected. Her battery is almost gone.

“I should look outside,” she says.

Aisha shakes her head.

“It’s quieter.”

“Quiet no mean finish.”

“What if Nnedi is near here?”

“What if bad people near here?”

Chika resents the phrase bad people for its childishness, then sees that it may be the most accurate phrase used all day. More accurate than zealots, rioters, militants, aggrieved youths. Bad people can be anybody. Bad people may pray afterward. Bad people may believe themselves good.

“I’ll only look from the alley.”

Aisha stands between her and the door. She is shorter than Chika, but in the shop her authority has become physical.

“Wait small.”

“I can’t just sit here.”

“You can.”

The bluntness angers Chika. Aisha cannot understand. A daughter is different from a sister, Chika thinks, and immediately knows the thought is monstrous. Love does not submit to such rankings. Yet fear keeps producing them: my sister before your daughter, my grief larger, my education more valuable, my life more visible.

“I have to go,” she says.

Aisha studies her, then steps aside. “No go road. Look only.”

Chika opens the door.

Light strikes her eyes. The alley is empty. Broken onions lie everywhere, crushed into the mud, their sharp smell mingling with smoke. A sandal rests beside the gutter. Far off, a siren rises and falls.

She takes three steps.

At the mouth of the alley, something lies near an overturned wheelbarrow. At first she thinks it is a sack of charcoal. Then she sees the hand.

The body has been burned so completely that it seems carved from black wood. One knee is raised. The fingers curl inward. Heat still shimmers above the torso, and beneath the char there is a sweet, greasy smell that Chika will later encounter outside overcrowded mortuaries and recognize before she allows herself to recognize it.

She cannot tell whether the body is male or female. There is no clothing left, no cross, cap, scarf, beads, tribal marks, nothing by which the living would decide which side owned the dead.

A fly lands on the lipless mouth.

Chika turns and stumbles back. She shuts the door too loudly and leans against it, gagging. Aisha guides her to the floor.

“You see something.”

Chika nods.

“Person?”

She nods again.

Aisha does not ask who the person was. The question would be impossible.

Chika vomits beside the broken scale. There is almost nothing in her stomach, only water and orange sweetness. Aisha holds her shoulders and waits. Afterward she rinses the floor with water from the tap, pushing the thin mess toward a crack by the wall with a piece of cardboard.

“You tell me,” Aisha says softly, “wait small.”

“Yes.”

There is no triumph in her voice. This makes Chika cry.

She cries for the burned person, she tells herself. For terror, for the indignity of vomiting, for the pain in her leg. She does not cry for Nnedi because Nnedi is alive. Nnedi is somewhere, angry and dusty and alive.

Aisha sits beside her until the crying stops.

Night comes quickly. The high window changes from gray to black. Mosquitoes find them. The shop cools, though the air remains stale with dust and smoke. At intervals orange light flickers under the door.

Aisha washes at the tap. Her movements are deliberate: hands, mouth, nostrils, face, arms, head, feet. Then she spreads the remaining part of her scarf on the floor and stands facing a direction Chika cannot verify. There is not enough room, yet Aisha makes room by shifting the broken scale and turning sideways between the shelves.

Chika watches her raise her hands.

She looks away when Aisha bows.

The prayer feels private, not because Chika cannot understand the Arabic but because Aisha’s face has changed. It has become exposed in a way her naked breast was not. Chika stares at the dark square of the window and remembers how she and Nnedi used to exchange looks during family Mass, especially when their mother prayed aloud for wandering children to recover their faith. Nnedi would cross her eyes. Chika would bite her cheek to stop laughing.

She does not think she will become religious after this. Such thoughts belong to testimonies, to neat stories told at church meetings. Still, she knows she will never again watch her mother kneel without remembering that prayer can be the small space a frightened person clears inside terror.

When Aisha finishes, she remains seated on her heels.

“Did you pray for Halima?” Chika asks.

“For all.”

The answer includes Nnedi. It may include the body by the wheelbarrow. It may include the people carrying sticks. Chika does not ask.

They lie down using the wrapper as a mat. Their shoulders touch because the dry part is narrow. Chika rests her handbag beneath her head; Aisha folds an arm beneath hers.

“My husband say girl no need school after primary,” Aisha says into the darkness.

“What did you say?”

“I tell him Halima head strong. If no school, she use strong head trouble everybody.”

Chika smiles.

“He agree?”

“He no agree. But Halima go.”

“How?”

“I pay small-small. Groundnut money. Onion money.”

“You pay her school fees?”

“Some. My brother help.”

Chika thinks of the forms her father signed without reading, the tuition wired before deadlines, the driver waiting outside secondary school when rain threatened. She had called these things normal. Aisha calls them nothing.

“You should be proud of her,” Chika says.

“I am proud.”

“And she’ll be proud of you.”

Aisha is silent. Then she asks, “Your sister, she look like you?”

“A little. People say our mouths are the same. She’s darker. Thinner.”

“She fear easy?”

“No.” Chika pauses. “Yes. But she pretends not to.”

“Halima same.”

They are quiet again.

Later, Chika will wonder whether this conversation happened exactly so. Memory will move sentences, polish some, erase others. She will remember the pressure of Aisha’s shoulder more certainly than any words. She will remember being awakened by shouting and finding their hands clasped. She will remember the smell of old soap in the shelves and how thirst returned immediately after drinking.

She will also remember things she could not yet know: that Nnedi’s yellow blouse would never appear among recovered belongings; that their mother would keep Nnedi’s room unchanged until dust collected around the bottles of perfume; that their father would begin speaking of emigration as if another country could revise what had happened.

But now Chika sleeps for minutes at a time and wakes each time to hope.

Near midnight, gunfire breaks out somewhere beyond the market. It comes in short, hard bursts. Aisha whispers words Chika cannot follow. Chika repeats Nnedi’s name under her breath, not as a prayer, she insists to herself, only as a way of keeping her sister located in the world.

Nnedi. Nnedi. Nnedi.

Aisha hears.

“Nnedi,” she repeats.

“Halima,” Chika says.

They trade the names like objects entrusted for safekeeping.

Before dawn, rain begins. It is brief and heavy. Water rattles on the metal roof and leaks through two holes, darkening the dust. Smoke thins. The rain brings the smell of wet ash, sewage, crushed vegetables.

They sit beneath the shelves and listen.

Eventually voices approach again, but these are tired voices. A man calls that soldiers are at the main road. A woman answers from behind another door. Someone says curfew. Someone says safe. Someone says not safe but safer.

Aisha opens the door a finger’s width. Blue morning enters.

“Finish?” Chika asks.

“For here, maybe.”

Outside, the alley looks smaller. Rain has pasted ash to the walls. The burned body is no longer visible from the doorway; perhaps soldiers have removed it, perhaps it is hidden by the wheelbarrow.

Aisha reties what remains of her scarf over her hair. The torn edge hangs unevenly.

“You go where?” she asks.

“My aunt’s house in Bompai. If I can find a taxi.”

“No taxi. Walk main road. Soldiers there.”

“And you?”

“I go house. Find my children.”

“Halima will be there.”

“Your sister too.”

They both accept the blessings without answering.

Chika reaches into her handbag for money, then stops. She does not want their last act to be payment. Yet perhaps refusing to offer is another privilege, a desire to preserve dignity in a form convenient to herself.

Aisha sees her hand. “Keep.”

“For transport.”

“No transport.”

“At least replace your scarf.”

Aisha touches the torn edge and smiles. “I get another.”

The bloodstained strip around Chika’s leg has dried hard.

“May I keep this?” Chika asks.

Aisha looks surprised. “Dirty.”

“I know.”

“You can throw.”

“I’d like to keep it.”

Aisha shrugs, but her eyes remain on Chika’s face. “Keep, then.”

They step into the alley. At the corner they pause. The road is littered with glass, charred wood, tomatoes split open by feet. Smoke drifts from a row of stalls. Soldiers stand in the distance beside an armored truck, their guns angled toward the ground.

Aisha points left. “You go so.”

“You go right?”

“Yes.”

“Greet Halima for me.”

“Greet Nnedi.”

Chika wants to embrace her but does not know whether Aisha would welcome it. While she hesitates, Aisha takes her hand in both of hers and squeezes once.

Then she walks away, her green wrapper damp at the hem, the bright ruined scarf visible through smoke.

Chika watches until she turns the corner.

Later, there will be no ending to the search, only its gradual change of shape. At first Chika will run from list to list, bed to bed, face to face. She will call Nnedi’s phone hundreds of times until the recorded voice says the number is unavailable. She will inspect bodies and learn that grief can coexist with relief: not her, not her, not her. Each relief will shame her.

Weeks later, officials will advise the family to accept the likelihood of death. No body will be offered. No place will exist for flowers. Her mother will request special Masses. Her father will contact commissioners, police chiefs, a senator he once treated for hypertension. None of these men will know more than the onion seller knew in the dark: a loved person can leave in the morning and become uncertainty by noon.

The riot will acquire a name, then a date, then a paragraph in reports. Analysts will explain it. Politicians will condemn it. The dead will be sorted into estimates and affiliations.

Chika will distrust every clean sentence.

She will think of the burned body, stripped by fire of the signs people kill for. She will think of Aisha’s hands cleaning blood from her shin, of her careful prayer, of the sweet kept for Halima. She will wonder whether Halima came home, whether the baby learned to latch properly, whether Aisha found her onions ground into the market mud. She will have no way to ask.

Years later, when a newspaper commemorates the violence with a photograph of smoke rising above Kano, Chika will read the caption twice. It will describe two communities erupting into conflict. The phrase will make violence sound like weather, as though streets simply combust, as though people are born carrying matches.

She will close the paper.

Then she will open the bottom drawer and take out the scarf.

The fabric will be cheaper than she remembers. The purple flowers will be almost childish. Along one edge, her blood will remain in a brown stain no washing has thinned because she has never washed it.

She will hold it and see, not reconciliation, not a lesson broad enough for speeches, but the narrow shop: dust turning in a shaft of light, two women listening for death, a rusted tap yielding metallic water. She will see Aisha’s breast exposed without shame, her forehead lowered in prayer, her fingers saving a sweet. She will hear two names spoken in darkness.

Nnedi.

Halima.

Outside that room, people had demanded to know who was who, who belonged where, who should run and who should burn. Inside it, for one night, the questions became smaller and harder: Where is your child? Where is my sister? Are you hurt? Do you need water?

The answers had not saved Nnedi.

They had not changed the city.

But Chika will keep the scarf because Aisha tore it. Because Aisha knelt over her wound. Because blood passed into its bright, cheap fibers and made it something no public account could name.

She will fold it again, carefully, and return it to the dark.

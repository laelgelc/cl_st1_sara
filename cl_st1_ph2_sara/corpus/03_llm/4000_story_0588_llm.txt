Cold? Well, some.

I’ve seen mercury crawl down into the bulb and stay there like it owed money outside; I’ve seen dogs freeze standing up, with their noses pointed toward supper; and once, over by Forty Mile, I watched a tin cup of boiling coffee turn into brown buckshot before it hit the ground. But that blizzard at Black Spruce Road-house had special qualifications. It didn’t merely blow. It come at you from all directions, including underneath, and searched your clothes for weak spots with the intimate thoroughness of a customs officer.

There was six of us cached inside Black Spruce when it commenced its emotional activities: me, Billy Joyce; Shorty McCabe; Dutch Henry; French Pete; old Tallow-Face Morgan; and a mail musher named Sims, who was principally whiskers wrapped around a grievance. We’d got the dogs banked under the lee wall and the stove red enough to melt suspicion. Outside, the snow went by horizontal, and the spruce trees screeched like a raft of sinners being hauled over a saw.

Then Annie Black come in.

Ain’t ever seen Annie? Lord! She was a woman built according to specifications which had been changed several times during construction. Tall? Not exactly. Wide? Not continuous. She had angles where ordinary females carry curves, and her face had been weatherproofed by forty-odd years of objections. Her nose went ahead like a survey stake, her chin followed with reinforcements, and her hair—black streaked with iron gray—was twisted into a knot that looked capable of holding a towline.

She shed snow in the doorway and says:

“Which one of you incompetent prairie vermin has got his boots on my bunk?”

Shorty removed his feet.

Not because he was polite. Shorty had once slept through a saloon being dismantled around him, but Annie’s voice had the penetrating powers of a crosscut saw striking a nail.

“This here ain’t your bunk,” says he, though from a safer elevation.

“It is while I’m occupying it.”

“You ain’t.”

“I’m about to.”

That settled the legal ownership without assessment work or appeal.

Annie was notorious all along the upper creeks. Claim jumping was her professional recreation. If a man left his stakes unguarded long enough to sneeze, Annie would relocate his ground, rename the creek, file papers, and be downtown looking for an injunction before he got his handkerchief folded. She’d jumped fractions, side lines, abandoned prospects, and once, so report maintained, a cemetery because she noticed quartz in the headstones.

She had lately been disputing with Ole and Lars Lund over a wedge-shaped fraction on Moosehide Creek. The Lund boys owned Discovery Below, Annie owned—or claimed she owned—Number One Above, and between the two lay a skinny triangle of gravel no wider than a saloon bar at one end and broad as a church promise at the other. Nobody cared for it till Lars sunk a hole and found a pay-streak that washed eight dollars to the pan. After that the fraction developed relatives, ancestors, legal guardians, and eight separate descriptions.

Annie asserted she’d staked it in March.

The Lunds produced papers dated February.

Annie said the dates was forged.

The Lunds produced two witnesses.

Annie said the witnesses was forged.

The case run nine days, consumed four lawyers, eleven bottles of ink, and most of the available profanity in the district. That afternoon the judge decided for the Lunds.

So Annie was not in what you’d call a mood of disappointing affabilities.

She stood over the stove thawing her mitts, and nobody spoke, because we’d all been witnesses, jurors, advisers, or interested spectators in one portion of that lawsuit. Presently I says:

“Well, Annie, law is law.”

She turned her eye on me.

“Billy Joyce,” says she, “you are the only living example of how far a human being can travel with no brains and inadequate dogs.”

“That ain’t relevant.”

“It is refreshing, though.”

“Judge says Lund’s title is good.”

“The judge,” says Annie, “is a stuffed overshoe decorated with whiskers. Ole Lund is a blond thief. Lars Lund is a silent thief, which is the worst kind because he saves all his breath for stealing. Their lawyer is a sewer with suspenders. Their witnesses are two-legged perjuries. And the whole proceeding was a ceremonial assassination of justice.”

Having discharged these oral formations, she poured coffee.

“You hate them Lunds considerable,” observed Tallow-Face.

“Hate?” Annie looked insulted. “That is a pale, parlor word. I have for those men a permanent, reinforced, frost-proof detestation. If they were drowning, I would hand them rocks.”

Then she drank her coffee like it was responsible.

Now about ten o’clock the storm got worse. You may think a blizzard can’t get worse after it has removed the world, but that one started taking inventory. The roof jumped, the stovepipe coughed smoke, and snow squeezed through cracks finer than flour. We’d laid out our blankets and was discussing whether the road-house would go first toward Dawson or the Arctic Ocean when something scratched at the door.

Not knocked. Scratched.

We opened up and a man fell in.

He was an Indigenous guide named John Nanook, a quiet old trail man who knew every creek and cutbank from the Yukon to the divide. He’d lost one mitten, his cheek was white with frost, and his parka had snow rammed into it until he looked upholstered. He tried to speak, but his jaw was frozen.

We rolled him to the stove, rubbed him careful, got warm tea into him, and after a while he says:

“Two men. Back trail. Sled broke by Little Alder. Horses gone.”

“Who?” I asked.

“Lund brothers.”

Annie’s cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

John told it in pieces. The Lunds had started before the blow got bad, hauling records and supplies from town. Their sled struck overflow ice near Little Alder Creek, tipped, and smashed a runner. The horses tore loose. John had come on them while hunting his own dog team, but Lars was hurt and Ole half blind from snow. John had tried to lead them toward Black Spruce. They made maybe a mile. Then Lars went down. Ole stayed with him while John pushed ahead for help.

“How far?” says Annie.

“Three miles. Maybe four. Storm turns trail.”

Nobody answered.

You fellows that have sat indoors in civilization may arrange your faces superior at this point. Three miles don’t sound like distance. But three miles in that blow was a foreign country. The night had no top, bottom, or edges. A man could walk twenty paces from the door and spend eternity trying to locate the house he’d left. Little Alder trail crossed a flat where the wind planed snow hard as iron, then dipped among cutbanks loaded to avalanche. We had one sound dog team, and dogs ain’t machines, regardless of the opinions of men who rent them.

Sims cleared his throat.

“Can’t go till she eases.”

“She won’t ease,” says John.

“Then we can’t go.”

“Meaning,” says Annie, very quiet, “they freeze.”

Shorty poked the stove. “Likely they’re froze already.”

Annie looked around at us. It was an educational look.

“Six men,” she says, “with boots, dogs, rope, lanterns, and a whole warehouse of heroic whiskers. And not one vertebra among the lot.”

“Now listen,” says Dutch Henry. “A man don’t help nobody by gettin’ lost himself.”

“That,” says Annie, “is the favorite syllogism of cowards: because danger exists, virtue is impractical.”

“Big words don’t warm a corpse.”

“No. But movement might.”

She pulled on her frozen mittens.

I says, “Where you going?”

“To fetch them.”

“You said you’d hand ’em rocks if they was drowning.”

“They ain’t drowning. They’re freezing.”

I don’t claim to be governed by logic. No good prospector is; if he was, he’d keep wages. But there are statements that catch a man in his pride like a fishhook in the ear. Annie opened the door, and the storm struck her backward two steps. She lowered her head and went into it.

I swore, grabbed my parka, and followed.

Shorty swore because I had. Dutch Henry swore at Shorty. French Pete said something in two languages and got the dog harness. Within five minutes the whole cowardly, spineless organization was outside, functioning under female management.

Annie knew that trail better than any of us. She tied a rope from man to man, put John Nanook on the sled under blankets, and made me lead with her beside me. We carried a lantern, but it lit nothing except a spinning yellow wall about the size of a hatbox. Snow hit our faces like handfuls of sand. Breathing hurt. The dogs went belly-low, whining, their tails tucked, and every few rods the wind laid the whole team sideways.

“Bear left!” Annie yelled.

“There ain’t no trail!”

“It bears left anyway!”

“How do you know?”

“I’ve got sense!”

That was not a navigational answer, but I bore left.

We nearly missed the turn at Split Spruce. Annie found it by ramming her arm shoulder-deep into a drift and feeling the buried blaze with her fingers. Then she led us over the ridge, counting steps because the landmarks had gone into retirement. At the top the wind picked French Pete up, as far as a compact Frenchman can be picked up, and rolled him twenty feet. The rope fetched him hard.

He come back using language that would blister iron.

“Save your breath,” says Annie.

“I use heem to keep warm!”

Below the ridge John raised his head and pointed. We heard nothing at first but the blizzard blizzing. Then, between gusts, come a doglike howl.

“Ole!” John shouted.

The howl answered.

We found them behind the wrecked sled. Ole had dug a pocket in the lee and stuffed his brother into it. He was lying across Lars to keep the wind off. His eyebrows and beard was solid ice, and when I pulled him up he fought me, not knowing who I was.

“Easy, Ole.”

“Lars,” he kept saying. “Take Lars first.”

Annie knelt and shoved us aside.

Lars was alive, but not much. One leg lay wrong below the knee, his face was gray, and he’d stopped shivering—which is a bad sign, though it looks peaceful. Annie felt his heart, opened his coat, and put her own bare hand against his chest.

“Blankets! Don’t stand there like a row of condemned fence posts.”

We wrapped Lars and lashed him onto the sled. Ole could not walk, so we sat him behind his brother. John went on a travois improvised from the broken sled boards. This distributed our transport facilities beyond what was financially advisable.

The journey back took years.

Halfway up Split Spruce ridge the dogs quit. They didn’t make a speech; they simply folded. We pushed the sled while Annie crawled ahead breaking the crust. Once Tallow-Face disappeared waist-deep into overflow, and his trousers froze stiff enough to have independent political opinions. Once we lost the blazed spruce and circled till we crossed our own rope track. John corrected us from under three blankets without opening his eyes.

Then Lars quit breathing.

Annie heard Ole cry out. She stopped the sled, bent over Lars, and worked on him—pressing his chest, breathing into him, rubbing the cold flesh beneath his shirt. Wind tore at her hood. Her hair came down and streamed straight out like a black flag.

“Come on, you thieving devil,” she says into his face. “You don’t escape me that easy. Breathe!”

He didn’t.

She struck his chest and breathed into him again.

“Your claim’s no good! Hear me? I’ll beat you in the next court. I’ll jump your grave. Breathe, damn you!”

Lars gave a queer little cough.

Ole began to sob. He couldn’t help it; he was frozen, exhausted, and Swedish, which is a heavy combination. Annie snarled at him to save his strength.

We moved on.

When the Black Spruce lantern finally showed, it appeared no farther away than a star and twice as improbable. Sims, who had stayed to mind the house, had hung two lamps outside and kept firing a rifle every minute. We followed the sound. He got us in one at a time, counting like Noah with a limited passenger list.

Annie took possession after that.

We thawed the Lunds gradual, because sudden heat can finish what frost begins. Annie splinted Lars’s leg with kindling and flour sacking. She dosed Ole with broth, slapped him when he tried to sleep too soon, and rubbed his hands until the blood come back. For thirty-six hours she scarcely rested. If one of us offered help, she gave instructions so insulting a man obeyed merely to prove he understood English.

On the second day Ole opened both eyes.

“Vhere am I?”

“In a place you ain’t earned,” says Annie.

He stared at her. “You?”

“Don’t excite yourself. Your gratitude might fracture something.”

“Lars?”

“Alive.”

Ole looked toward the other bunk. Then he caught Annie’s hand and kissed it.

Annie jerked away like he’d put a live coal in her palm.

“Try that again,” she says, “and I’ll reset your brother’s leg diagonally.”

But she went out back of the woodpile and stayed there ten minutes, though it was forty below and no respectable purpose awaited her.

The storm blew itself empty by the third morning. Sky come out hard blue, the hills stood around white and innocent, and every spruce glittered as if the Almighty had been decorating while we slept. We dug out the dogs and roof, found one horse in a willow patch, and settled down to wait till Lars could travel.

That was when the mail arrived.

Sims had a sack cached from his last run, and among the letters was one addressed in a neat hand:

MRS. ANNE BRADSHAW  
BLACK SPRUCE ROAD-HOUSE  
MOOSEHIDE DISTRICT, ALASKA

Sims held it up.

“Who’s Bradshaw?”

Annie snatched it.

Nobody had ever called her anything but Annie Black. Some said Black was a husband’s name, some said it described her business morals, and Shorty maintained it referred to the condition of her temper before breakfast.

She read the letter by the window. I watched the hard color drain from her face.

“What’s wrong?” I says.

“Nothing.”

Then she read it again.

Her hands shook.

Annie’s hands! I had seen those hands hold a mining pan in snow water, swing an axe, point a rifle, and emphasize testimony beneath judicial warning. Shaking was not included in their ground plan.

She folded the paper too careful.

“My daughter is coming,” she says.

You could have heard frost crack.

“Your what?” says Shorty.

“My daughter. Eleanor.”

She gave us a look inviting the first man who smiled to prepare his funeral. Nobody did.

“Where’s she been?” I asked.

“School. Outside. Vancouver first, then Seattle. She finished this spring.”

“You got a grown daughter?”

“I did not mail-order her yesterday.”

“Well, why ain’t nobody heard?”

“Because it was none of your diseased business.”

She began walking up and down. Three strides one direction, pivot, three back. Emotional assessment work.

“She thinks I own the Aurora,” Annie says suddenly.

The Aurora was the best mine on Moosehide, with a steam hoist, bunkhouse, and a pay-streak wide as a wagon. It belonged to a Boston company.

“Why would she think that?” says I.

“Because I told her.”

“Oh.”

“I sent money regular. Told her the mine was prosperous. Told her I had a proper house. Told her I could not visit because operations required me here.”

The road-house took on a silence so stiff you could hang wet socks from it.

“She’s coming to surprise me,” Annie continued. “Traveling with a missionary couple. They’ll reach here tomorrow or next day.”

“Then you tell her,” says Dutch. “Blood’s blood.”

Annie wheeled.

“Tell her what? That her mother washes for mushers when claims don’t pay? That I sleep in road-houses and abandoned cabins? That every drunken loafer from here to Circle has a story about Annie Black? Tell her I’ve spent ten years in courts because men see a lone woman’s stakes and figger Providence left them unguarded?”

Nobody answered that.

She leaned against the table, and all at once the fight went out of her shoulders.

“I was nineteen when Bradshaw married me,” she says. “He had a smile like easy money and about the same durability. We come north in the rush. He drank our outfit, sold my jewelry, and left before Eleanor was born. Later a trader offered me work, then decided wages included privileges. I discouraged him with a shovel. After that they called me hard.”

“Annie—” I began.

“Don’t.”

She looked down at the letter.

“I put Eleanor with good people Outside. Every ounce I took out, every dollar from cooking, sewing, washing, guiding tenderfeet, went to her. I wanted her to have clean rooms and music and books. I sent photographs of the Aurora office cut from newspapers. I wrote about servants I never had and dinners I never ate. Foolish? Certainly. Fraudulent? Likely. But a child needs one fine thing to believe in. I made that thing her mother.”

Her mouth twisted.

“Now she’s coming to inspect the property.”

She laughed once. There wasn’t humor enough in it to feed a mosquito.

Ole Lund had been listening from his bunk.

“Mrs. Bradshaw,” he says.

She looked at him sharply.

“Vat vas de girl’s name?”

“Eleanor.”

“She vill be proud.”

“Keep out of this.”

“She vill.”

“I said keep out.”

Ole subsided, but his blue eyes went busy.

That afternoon Annie announced she was leaving before the girl arrived.

We argued.

She out-argued us.

“I’ll go to Dawson. You tell Eleanor Mrs. Bradshaw has gone to inspect distant holdings.”

“That’s another lie,” says Shorty.

“It has architectural harmony with the others.”

“You can’t run from your own daughter.”

Annie’s face sharpened. “I can spare her.”

She packed her bedroll. But Lars took worse near evening, feverish and wandering, and Annie would not leave him. She sat beside him all night changing cloths and muttering that the Lund family possessed a malicious talent for inconvenient dying.

Next day, near noon, sleigh bells sounded.

Annie froze.

Not the weather kind. Inside.

A dog team swung into the yard, followed by a light cutter. Out stepped a minister with spectacles, his wife in sealskin, and a young woman wearing a blue traveling coat.

She was maybe nineteen. Tall like Annie, but the lines had not hardened. Her hair was dark, her eyes gray, and she moved with a clean, easy confidence that made the road-house look suddenly ashamed of its tin plates and tobacco smoke.

Annie backed into the pantry doorway.

“Don’t,” she whispered to us. “Not yet.”

The girl entered, brushing frost from her collar.

“Good afternoon. I’m looking for Mrs. Anne Bradshaw.”

Now we six men had never practiced organized deception except in connection with claim values and card games. But sometimes a noble fabrication rises full-grown from the communal intellect.

I stepped forward.

“You mean Madam Bradshaw of the Aurora interests?”

Behind me Shorty made a strangled noise. I kicked him.

“Yes,” says the girl eagerly. “She is my mother.”

“Why, certainly. We know Madam Bradshaw.”

“Is she here?”

“Temporarily,” says I, “she is engaged in—medical superintendence.”

French Pete caught the idea.

“Madame, she save three men in terrible storm.”

“Four,” says Dutch Henry. “Count Billy.”

“I was assisting!”

“Mostly by being rescued from your own navigation.”

The girl looked from one to another.

“You are Mr. Joyce, perhaps?”

That startled me. “You heard of me?”

“Oh, often. Mother wrote that you were the most dependable man in the district.”

The entire organization collapsed against walls and furniture.

Annie, hidden in the pantry, made a small sound which might have been choking.

I recovered. “Well. Your mother has judgment obscured by generosity.”

Tallow-Face Morgan stepped up, hat in hands.

“Ma’am, there ain’t a braver woman north of the line. Them Lund boys would be dead but for her.”

“Lund?” the girl said. “The men who tried to steal her mine?”

At this Ole threw off his blankets.

He was still weak, with both hands bandaged, but he stood. Lars, propped behind him, tried likewise and failed halfway.

“No,” says Ole. “Ve did not steal it.”

The girl’s face changed.

Ole continued, slow and careful.

“De court say fraction is ours. Maybe court is right by papers. But papers ain’t all. Your mother, she go into storm ven ve who are enemies lie freezing. She lead men. She bring us back. She give life to my brother.”

He looked toward the pantry.

“Annie Black,” he called, “come out.”

No answer.

“Mrs. Bradshaw,” he says.

Then Annie stepped into the room.

I’ve seen men face guns with less terror than was in her face. She had combed her hair and put on a clean blouse, but the sleeves was worn, her skirt patched, and there was flour on one elbow. She stood straight, because bending was a habit life had failed to teach her.

The girl stared.

Annie tried to speak. Nothing come. All her magnificent collection of hosstyle syllogisms, legal bombardments, and decorative abuse had deserted.

At last she says, “Eleanor.”

The girl’s eyes filled.

“Mother?”

“I’m sorry,” Annie said.

That was all. Two words, small and plain.

“I don’t own the Aurora. I never did. I haven’t a house. I haven’t—most of what I told you. I wanted—”

Eleanor crossed the room and put both arms around her.

Annie remained rigid one second. Then she folded over that girl like a tent struck by weather. Her hands clutched the blue coat, and a sound came out of her unlike anything we had ever heard from Annie Black.

Naturally, none of us watched.

Shorty discovered urgent business with the stovepipe. Dutch Henry examined the weather through a wall without a window. French Pete swore he had smoke in both eyes, though the stove was drawing beautiful. Tallow-Face blew his nose with a report that shook snow off the roof. As for me, I was studying a crack in the floor, being interested in geology.

Ole Lund waited till the first emotional stampede eased.

Then he reached under his mattress and drew out the folded court papers.

“Miss Eleanor,” he says, “your mother also make one mistake.”

Annie lifted her head.

“What are you doing?”

Ole tore the judgment in half.

“De fraction on Moosehide—it belongs to Mrs. Bradshaw.”

“You can’t give it away,” Annie snapped, tears still running and her voice trying desperate to get its old boots on. “Lars owns half.”

Lars spoke from the bunk.

“Yah,” he says faintly. “I give my half too.”

“You’ll do no such fool thing.”

Ole smiled. “Ve already sign transfer. Billy and others witness.”

That was news to me, but I caught up rapid.

“Legally executed,” says I. “Full of whereases, aforesaids, and mining replications.”

Shorty nodded. “Ironclad.”

“De pay-streak is rich,” Ole told Eleanor. “Your mother owns good mine now.”

Annie glared around the room.

“You miserable conspiracy of liars.”

“Yes, ma’am,” says I, “but notice the improved social purpose.”

She looked at Ole, then Lars, then at us. Her anger shook, cracked, and fell to pieces. What remained underneath was a woman none of us had been properly introduced to.

“Why?” she asked.

Ole held up his bandaged hands.

“Because papers ain’t all.”

Eleanor kissed her mother’s cheek.

“I didn’t come for a mine,” she says. “I came for you.”

Well, that finished us.

Shorty kicked the woodbox and swore at it. French Pete commenced polishing the same tin cup inside and out. Sims went outdoors bareheaded, claiming the dogs required immediate moral supervision. Even Annie laughed while she cried, which is a confusing performance and dangerous to witnesses.

That night we had a celebration. Nothing elaborate—beans, bacon, sourdough biscuits, and a bottle Tallow-Face had been preserving for medicinal emergencies. Lars drank broth; Ole had one spoonful of whisky; the minister made a speech; and Annie sat beside her daughter with their hands locked under the table like two claim partners afraid somebody might jump the title.

Did Annie change after that?

Not foolishly.

She married no millionaire, grew no dimples, and never learned to suffer idiots with Christian enthusiasm. She worked the Lund fraction under the name Bradshaw Discovery and took out enough gold to build a house for Eleanor and herself. She sued the lumber company over warped boards, the freighter over damaged machinery, and me over a boundary ditch—though she dropped my case after proving she could win it.

But folks called her Mrs. Bradshaw from then on.

Even me.

Because there are names a person wears like a trail coat—blackened, torn, and fitted to bad weather—and there are names cached underneath, clean and waiting. It took a blizzard, two freezing Swedes, six frightened men, and one girl in a blue coat to uncover Annie’s.

Sentimental? Not me.

I only say that when Eleanor held her and Ole handed over that claim, something fetched loose inside the whole bunch of us—heartstrings, likely—snapping like banjo wires in hard frost. For a minute Black Spruce Road-house felt warm beyond the stove’s capacity, like Christmas, or mail from home, or striking pay after you’d spent your last grubstake.

And if I wiped my eyes, it was smoke.

That stove never did draw worth a damn.

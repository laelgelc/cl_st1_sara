Moonlight touches Soft Shoes at the corner of Friday Street—one pale finger on a russet sleeve—and loses him.

Flowing Boots follow.

They come in thunderous pairs: leather, steel, breath, oath; first one gentleman with a torn plume, then another with blood shining black upon his cheek. Their rapiers flash whenever the moon finds room between the houses. London, which has swallowed bishops, bears, Spaniards, and several thousand excellent intentions, opens its narrow throat to receive the chase.

Soft Shoes darts. A shutter cracks open above him. A citizen’s nightcap blooms, pale and inquisitive, then vanishes at the sight of drawn steel. Soft Shoes leaps a kennel, slips beneath the arm of a startled pie-man, and turns so sharply into an alley that the darkness takes him at a mouthful.

Flowing Boots overshoot.

“Back!”

“There—by the conduit!”

“No, curse him—”

The brothers divide, reunite, collide with a post, and divide again. One loses his hat. The other catches it upon his sword-point and flings it back with a gesture fit for the destruction of Carthage. Unfortunately, the hat falls into a puddle.

Soft Shoes has climbed.

He hangs above them between two overleaning houses, his heels pressed to one wall, his shoulders to the other, a grotesque god of chimneys and laundry. Beneath him the gallants rage up and down, searching doorways and stabbing suspicious shadows. His face, when moonlight uncovers it, is small, pointed, and elfish; terror brightens it without making it less impudent.

A whistle sounds.

The watch comes laboring from Cheapside: three men, one lantern, two pikes, and enough official reluctance to stop an Armada.

Soft Shoes drops behind them.

“Halt!” cries the chief watchman, chiefly because the fugitive is already running away.

“There goes a murderer!” shouts the hatless gentleman.

“A ravisher!” cries his brother.

The word flies farther than the whistle.

Soft Shoes hears it. His stride breaks for half a beat—no more. Then he goes over a stack of baskets, under a horse’s nose, across the lane, and through an arch where the blackness lies folded in several convenient layers.

The watch pursues the brothers; the brothers pursue Soft Shoes; a dog pursues everybody. For one glorious minute London possesses law, vengeance, crime, and criticism in full cry.

Then the city shakes them apart.

Soft Shoes emerges alone beside St. Paul’s, listens, and walks.

It is an extraordinary walk. No innocent man ever attempted so much carelessness. He strolls with his hands behind him, pauses to admire a waterspout, hums three notes from a tavern song, and nearly faints against a wall. Recovering, he bows to the wall as if it had been a duchess.

He reaches a house in Little Carter Lane and knocks: twice softly, once loudly, twice softly again.

Nothing.

He knocks the same message with an added theological objection.

A casement opens.

“Who disturbs Christian sleep?” asks a voice in which Christianity has been postponed until morning.

“An angel.”

“Then fly up.”

“My wing is sprained.”

“Die quietly.”

The casement closes.

Soft Shoes picks up a pebble and throws it. There is a crash, followed by a silence of immense scholarship.

“Wessel,” he calls, “I have broken thy window.”

“I know it.”

“Wilt thou come down?”

“I am considering whether friendship includes glazing.”

A light appears. Bolts are withdrawn with the slow ceremony of a royal burial, and Wessel Caxter stands in the doorway wearing a gown, stockings, and the expression of a man whose mind has been dragged from Arcadia into weather.

Wessel was then thirty-eight, though indolence, reading, and a stomach of premature authority made him appear older. His eyes were dim, his wit was not, and he loved books with that generous promiscuity which excuses ugliness, error, and very bad endings. Posterity remembers him scarcely at all. There is, or was before an energetic churchwarden improved it, a stone at St. Bride’s saying that here lay WESSEL CAXTER, FRIEND TO POETS. It says nothing of the inconveniences poets caused him.

He lifts the candle.

“Will.”

“Wessel.”

“Why art thou bleeding?”

“I thought it fashionable.”

“Why is thy jerkin split?”

“Excess of personality.”

“And why do armed men cry ravisher under my window?”

Soft Shoes glances back.

“They have traced me?”

“As smoke traces fire. Enter, calamity.”

Will enters. Wessel bars the door; then, judging the bar inadequate for poetry, scandal, and two swords, pushes a chest against it. Will helps with frantic vigor. They have just set upon the chest three folios, a chair, and an unnecessary bust of Seneca when fists strike the panels.

“Open!”

Wessel’s eyebrows rise.

“Persistent readers?”

“Open, in the Queen’s name!”

“The Queen,” murmurs Will, “has never knocked so coarsely.”

The door shakes.

Wessel points to the stairs. Will vanishes up them with the obedient speed of a cat expelled from a dairy. Wessel removes one folio, sits on the chest, opens *The Faerie Queene*, and arranges his face into mild allegory.

He is discovered thus when a shutter beside the door is beaten inward and a rapier enters.

“Master Caxter!”

“Sir Rapier.”

“We saw him come here.”

“Many come here. Few return my books.”

“William Sh—”

“You astonish me. I believed him in Shoreditch, ruining a comedy.”

The point withdraws. Through the broken shutter appears an eye surrounded by fury, wet hair, and expensive velvet.

“He has wronged our sister.”

“That narrows London very little.”

“Do not jest!”

The command cracks. Wessel looks more closely.

The gentleman’s cheek is cut. Not with a blade: four parallel scratches mark it. Behind him stands his brother, bareheaded, one arm bound in a linen strip already darkening. Neither has the flushed delight of a tavern quarrel. Their rage is not theatrical rage, which pauses to see whether the gallery has understood. This is sick rage. Helpless rage. Terror put into boots.

Wessel closes the book.

“What wrong?”

The eye disappears. There is hurried speech outside, broken by breath.

“She received him—she knew him—God forgive her—”

“He locked the chamber.”

“She cried out.”

“We broke in.”

“He struck Edmund with a stool.”

“She was on the floor. Her gown—”

The voice fails.

The other brother seizes it.

“He forced her. By God, Caxter, he forced her, and if you hide him, you are partner to it.”

Upstairs, a board whispers.

Wessel hears. The brothers hear.

The rapier comes through the shutter again and drives into the panel beside Wessel’s ear. Seneca topples from the barricade and bursts his plaster head upon the floor, which was perhaps the most practical criticism Seneca had received in years.

Wessel does not move.

“My house,” he says softly, “contains myself, eleven hundred books, two cheeses, and a maid who would defeat you both. Search it and I shall have the law on you.”

“The law?”

“A slow instrument, gentlemen, but one designed for those who put swords through other men’s doors.”

They hesitate. Rank despises the law while remaining curiously attentive to it.

“Send him out,” says the elder.

“If he comes, I shall send him to answer you.”

“He will answer us.”

“I fear that.”

A long silence stands between them.

Then the brothers retreat—not defeated, but recalled by something more urgent than vengeance. Their sister, perhaps. A surgeon. A mother asking where they have gone. Their boots diminish along the lane.

Wessel waits until even the dog has ceased commentary.

“Come down,” he says.

No answer.

“Come down, or I invite them back and lend them a ladder.”

Will appears at the head of the stairs. All the agility has gone out of him. He descends one hand at a time along the rail.

The candle shows a bruise upon his jaw, blood at his collar, and a rent in his shirt. It also shows that he is smiling.

Wessel hates the smile.

“Did you do it?”

Will looks toward the broken shutter.

“Did you force that woman?”

“I went to see her.”

“That was not my question.”

“She sent for me.”

“Nor that.”

“Her brothers hate players.”

“Most sensible men do. Answer.”

Will’s eyes move over the room: books banked to the ceiling; maps curling above the hearth; Seneca murdered on the rushes. Anywhere but Wessel’s face.

“She sent for me,” he repeats.

“And afterward?”

“She changed.”

“Her mind?”

“Everything.”

Wessel rises. The book slides from his lap and falls open upon a woodcut of a knight rescuing chastity from a dragon. Literature has tact of a brutal sort.

“Was she willing?”

Will’s mouth opens. Nothing comes.

Wessel steps away as though some smell has entered with him.

“You can turn any charge into a jest,” he says. “You can make a prince sound like a tapster and a tapster like Julius Caesar. You have five hundred words for moonlight and none for no?”

Will flinches.

“She did not say—”

“Ah.”

“No. Hear me.”

“I have heard too much.”

“It was not as they told it.”

“Then tell it.”

Will presses both hands against his temples. His fingers tremble. For the first time that night he seems his age: young, vain, frightened, and suddenly visited by the bill for all his charms.

“I cannot.”

“You will not.”

“Not so.”

“Take thy distinctions to a lawyer.”

Wessel moves to the door and begins dragging away the chair.

“They will kill me.”

“Perhaps.”

“You believe them?”

“I believe their faces.”

“And not mine?”

“Yours has rehearsed.”

That strikes. Will sits heavily upon the settle. For several breaths he says nothing. Outside, London settles into the sour hour before dawn, when drunkards grow philosophical and honest citizens suspect the universe of bad management.

At last Will asks, “Have you paper?”

Wessel stares.

“Paper.”

“For a confession?”

“For an answer.”

“Speak it.”

“I cannot speak it.”

“Then you have none.”

Will looks up. “Give me ink.”

There are requests so monstrous that refusal becomes difficult. Wessel, who had denied friends money, shelter, praise, and once a clean shirt, could never deny writing materials. He set paper upon the table—cheap paper, from caution—then an ink-horn and three pens.

“Write the truth,” he says.

Will takes a pen.

“What truth?”

Wessel’s disgust becomes almost admiration, but cannot quite make the journey.

“The woman’s.”

He goes upstairs.

Below, the pen begins.

It scratches, stops, scratches furiously. A chair overturns. Will curses a rhyme, though none has yet been invited. The fire collapses into red provinces. Once Wessel hears weeping; once laughter; once a speech delivered in a king’s voice to an audience of mice. Then silence. Then the pen again.

Words gather where action has spent itself.

A Roman camp rises upon Carter Lane. Tarquin mounts. Desire acquires wings, breath, fire, heraldry, and a historical costume broad enough to conceal a London sin. A husband boasts; a listener burns; a woman sleeps beneath the advancing machinery of metaphor. Every stroke of the pen carries Will farther from the chamber and nearer to it. He changes names as thieves change cloaks. He gives the woman eloquence afterward—oceans of it, arguments enough to prosecute gods. Whether this is pity, theft, confession, or the most splendid cowardice in England, no angel enters the record to say.

Wessel does not sleep. He lies above with *The Faerie Queene* unopened on his chest, listening while a deed becomes a poem.

At dawn, the city returns by noises: wheels, bells, a milkmaid’s cry, the first printer’s apprentice abusing the first horse. Gray light comes through the broken shutter and discovers Will at the table.

He is ink to the elbows. His hair stands up like black flame. About him lie discarded pages, blotted battles, violated margins. The agility of the night has poured out of his body; when Wessel enters, Will spills onto the settle like the contents of an inverted bottle.

“There,” he whispers.

Upon the table rests a manuscript of alarming size.

Wessel touches it but does not immediately read. “Is it true?”

Will closes his eyes.

“It is what I can tell.”

“That is not the same.”

“No.”

“Does she forgive you in it?”

“No.”

“Do you?”

Will does not answer.

Wessel turns the first page. At the head, in a hand made grand by exhaustion, stands a title:

**THE RAPE OF LUCRECE**

Below it the poem begins:

*From the besieged Ardea all in post,*  
*Borne by the trustless wings of false desire,*  
*Lust-breathed Tarquin leaves the Roman host,*  
*And to Collatium bears the lightless fire.*

Wessel reads the lines twice.

Outside, somewhere beyond the bells and roofs, two brothers are still searching. Somewhere a woman has awakened into a morning no poem can repair. Inside, William Shakespeare sleeps with his cheek upon a ruined sheet, while the light moves over him—pale, impartial, and pitiless—as if London itself were reading.

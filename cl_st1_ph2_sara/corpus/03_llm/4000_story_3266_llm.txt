### THE LITTLE HANDS AT TRESILLACK

“You have never told us,” said Clara, leaning forward to rescue a chestnut which had rolled dangerously near the fender, “whether you have seen a ghost.”

Miss Emily Le Petyt looked across the fire at her with that expression of courteous surprise which, in another woman, might have meant that the question was impertinent, but in Miss Le Petyt meant only that the answer required arranging.

“I have seen part of one,” she said.

“Part of one?”

“The most important part, as I came to think.”

“A head?”

“No, my dear.”

“Then it was not the most important part.”

“That depends upon the use to which a head is put. I have known several that were no great addition to their owners.”

There was a murmur at this, for we were all intimate enough with Miss Le Petyt to laugh, though not so intimate as to ask whom she meant.

“It was a little ghost,” she continued.

“Little in stature, or little as a story?”

“Both, if you will permit me to begin. I warn you that the house must be described, the water system explained, and Mrs. Carkeek allowed to speak in her own time. A ghost story without plumbing may be told in ten minutes; mine cannot.”

She settled the black lace at her wrists, glanced once towards the drawn curtains, beyond which the December wind moved among the trees, and began.

I went to Cornwall, my dears, because my physician told me not to. He said the journey was long, the climate uncertain, and the inhabitants—though excellent—unaccustomed to the requirements of a lady living alone. He recommended Bournemouth, where, as I pointed out, the inhabitants were altogether too well accustomed to them. I had been overworked, or imagined myself so, and desired quiet; not the quiet of a fashionable watering-place, which consists of being disturbed at regular hours, but quiet of the genuine, uncivilised sort, with no brass band, no German governess pursuing children along the parade, and no obliging acquaintance tapping at the window to inquire whether one is resting.

Tresillack was offered to me through an agent in Truro. It stood, he said, in a secluded coombe within sound, though not sight, of the sea.

You know what “secluded” means in an advertisement. Applied to a villa, it means that the butcher objects to calling; to a country house, that the roof leaks in places inaccessible to a ladder; to a cottage in Cornwall, it may mean anything from half a mile of mud to the total extinction of Christian society. I therefore wrote for particulars.

The particulars were alluring. Tresillack had six bedchambers, two sitting-rooms, a good kitchen, a dairy, a flower garden, a small orchard, and a stream at the foot of the lawn. It was furnished, the rent was absurdly low, and the owner, Farmer Hosking, would let it for a term of years if a respectable tenant could be found. The last clause interested me. Respectability is generally assumed by agents, just as virtue is assumed in epitaphs; when either is expressly demanded, one suspects a history.

I went to see it.

The road from the station climbed between banks so high that the hedges met above the carriage, then dipped suddenly into a green hollow full of ferns, foxgloves, and the sound of running water. There was no sea to be seen, but now and then, beneath the clatter of the wheels, one heard a grave, measured plash, as if some enormous creature breathed beyond the hills. At a turn of the lane the house appeared: low, white, irregular, with grey stone chimneys and roses climbing almost to the eaves. Sunshine lay upon its windows. A brook chattered below the orchard. Nothing could have looked less guilty.

Farmer Hosking met me at the gate. He was a broad, grave man, dressed on that occasion as though he had been persuaded against his judgment that a black coat was necessary to secure a tenant.

“You’ll find her sound, ma’am,” he said, after we had exchanged greetings. “Walls dry, roof mostly new, and water a-plenty.”

“Water a-plenty” should perhaps have warned me; but in London water is either a charge on the rates or an inconvenience from above, and one does not readily conceive it as a supernatural agency.

He showed me the rooms. They were low but cheerful, with deep window-seats and uneven floors polished by generations of feet. The drawing-room looked down the coombe; the dining-room opened by a glass door upon a terrace where white roses grew with indecent abundance. There were cupboards everywhere—cupboards beneath the stairs, cupboards within cupboards, and one little triangular recess in which, Hosking assured me, a former yeoman had hidden during a dispute concerning tithes. The man had remained there two days, though nobody was looking for him.

The furniture was old, plain, and sound. The linen, spread for my inspection, would have satisfied a convent. The brass candlesticks shone. The kitchen dresser was ranged with blue crockery, and not a speck of dust softened the edge of a single plate.

“You keep the house aired?” I asked.

“Mrs. Carkeek do.”

Mrs. Carkeek, summoned from the back premises, appeared with a basket of runner beans upon one arm. She was a small, square woman of perhaps fifty-five, though country women of her kind pass from thirty to seventy without any intervening date that a stranger may safely assign to them. Her eyes were dark and exceedingly attentive; her hair, under a spotless cap, had still more black than grey in it; and her mouth had the shut expression of one accustomed to preserving her own counsel and other people’s jam.

“This lady may take Tresillack,” said Hosking.

“If she do, she’ll want the south room,” said Mrs. Carkeek. “The chimney in the north do smoke when the wind’s easterly.”

“Thank you,” I replied. “Are you in charge here?”

“Since Mrs. Kendall died, I’ve had the tending of it.”

“And would you remain if I took the house?”

At this she looked, not at me, but at Hosking.

“It is a condition,” he said. “Begging your pardon, ma’am, Mrs. Carkeek goes with the place.”

“Like the kitchen range?”

His face reddened, while hers did not alter.

“Not to say that, ma’am. But she’ve been here years, and knows the ways of the house.”

Houses, I observed, do not commonly possess ways more mysterious than smoking chimneys and sticking doors; yet I was so much enchanted with Tresillack that I chose not to quarrel with its condition. Mrs. Carkeek seemed competent, and I had no wish to import a London maid who would call the coombe “dreadful” and spend her evenings predicting murder. I therefore signed the agreement, retained Mrs. Carkeek, and entered into possession in May.

Only afterwards did I hear of the tenants who had preceded me.

There had been three sets within six years, each less satisfactory than the last. The first was a widow who practised what she called magnetic healing and what the neighbourhood called wickedness. She had kept twelve cats, paid no tradesman, and gone away by night, leaving behind her a stuffed crocodile and two hundred pamphlets upon the influence of Saturn on the human spleen. The next tenant, a retired dealer in horses, had used the dining-room for cards and the orchard for transactions which were conducted after dark and ended, more than once, in the magistrates’ court. The last family were named Bream. Mr. Bream claimed some relation to Lord Lytton, though he resembled that nobleman only in a preference for impressive phrases. His wife remained upstairs much of the day; there were several servants who came and went; and two poor little children, belonging, I think, to a sister of Mrs. Bream, lived with them in circumstances which I shall mention presently.

Farmer Hosking had suffered by them all. Rent was unpaid, furniture damaged, fruit trees cut down, and one of the bedroom grates torn out and sold. His anxiety concerning respectability was therefore not a rustic suspicion of my independence, as I had rather grandly supposed, but the natural apprehension of a man who had been cheated three times and did not desire the experience made perfect.

I confess this discovery lowered me a little in my own esteem. One is never so ready to perceive class prejudice as when it is presumed to exist against oneself.

For the first fortnight, however, I thought of nothing but my good fortune. The weather was heavenly. The stream talked all day over its stones; the roses entered at the windows; and at night, when the wind came from the west, the hidden sea sounded at the mouth of the coombe. I read, walked, wrote letters which made my friends envious, and discovered that idleness, properly conducted, is an occupation requiring the whole of one’s time.

Mrs. Carkeek proved excellent, though reserved. A hind came in for the garden, and a girl from the village helped in the kitchen; but the house itself seemed to need scarcely any service. My rooms were always prepared before I entered them. If the morning were cool, a fire appeared; if warm, the windows stood open on the shaded side. The book I had left face downward upon a chair was restored to the table with a marker in its place. Fresh flowers appeared in little bowls—not formal arrangements, but a spray of honeysuckle where its sweetness would reach me, three pinks beside my writing-case, or a wild rose set in the narrow glass over the mantel.

At first I gave Mrs. Carkeek the credit, and thanked her.

“’Tis nothing, miss,” she said.

“It is not nothing. You take too much trouble.”

“No trouble to me.”

This answer was exact, as I now believe; but I understood it conventionally.

There were other attentions. I had a habit, which Clara will condemn, of leaving my shoes wherever I took them off. At Tresillack they were found the next morning cleaned and paired outside my door. A shawl slipped from my shoulders in the drawing-room and was laid across the back of my chair before I remembered it. Once, when I came in drenched from a walk, dry stockings and a warm wrapper were waiting in my bedchamber, though Mrs. Carkeek had been with me in the hall and could not possibly have climbed the back stair ahead of us without a degree of agility which her figure forbade.

“Mary brought them,” I said.

“Mary’s to her mother’s. Gone this hour.”

“Then you did.”

Mrs. Carkeek gave me one of her dark, measuring looks.

“Things gets done here.”

“My dear Mrs. Carkeek, that is a philosophy, not an explanation.”

“Explanations don’t dry stockings.”

She was perfectly right, and I put them on.

Still, the matter began to tease me. I set little traps, not from fear, but curiosity. I left a thread across a drawer. It was gone by morning, and the handkerchiefs within were more neatly folded. I placed a fallen petal beneath the dressing-table; when I returned, the petal had vanished, though neither Mrs. Carkeek nor Mary had been upstairs. I left my writing-room in deliberate disorder—an exercise which cost me nothing—and sat on the terrace with both servants in sight. When I re-entered, the pens had been wiped, the torn sheets removed, and the blotting-paper turned to a clean page.

I began to suspect Mrs. Carkeek of employing some small relative whom she concealed from me out of an excess of Cornish independence. Once I thought I heard a child’s light step in the passage. Once, at twilight, I saw the latch of the china cupboard rise and fall; but when I opened the door there was nobody in the room, and no other exit.

The house did not alarm me. That is important. It was attentive, not watchful; solicitous, not obsequious. There are houses which seem to resent the human creature—doors bite, bells refuse to ring, stairs lie in wait for the ankle. Tresillack received one. I had the absurd impression that it was glad to be decently inhabited again.

The one neglected place was the pantry.

You must allow me to explain its position, or what followed will be no more credible than an apparition in a railway refreshment-room. The kitchen occupied the eastern end of the house. Beyond it lay a narrow passage, with the dairy on the left and the pantry at the end. The pantry was a small, cool chamber, half below the level of the garden, lighted by a barred window. Along one wall ran shelves for china and preserves. Opposite stood a deep porcelain basin with a brass tap above it; but the tap yielded no water.

There was, Mrs. Carkeek told me, another sink in the scullery, so this did not inconvenience her. Yet the basin was well placed for washing fruit, glasses, and delicate china, and I dislike a useless thing almost as much as an ugly one.

“Why is the water cut off?” I asked.

“Pipe’s bad.”

Hosking, when next he came, denied it.

“Pipe was put new seven year ago.”

“Mrs. Carkeek says it is bad.”

He scratched his cheek.

“Do she now?”

“Is there any reason it should not be restored?”

“No reason as I knows.”

That afternoon I sent for the village plumber. He traced the pipe to a small cistern in the roof-space above the passage and found that it had been stopped, not by accident or decay, but with a wooden plug wrapped in cloth and driven firmly into the outlet.

“Somebody meant him not to run,” he said.

“Then somebody may mean differently,” I replied.

Mrs. Carkeek came upon us as he withdrew the plug.

I have never seen a face change so quickly without losing its composure. She did not cry out. She set down the tray she carried and became, for an instant, the colour of the flour in its dredger.

“You shouldn’t have done that, miss.”

“Why not?”

“That water was shut particular.”

“So I perceive. But why?”

She looked at the plumber. He, though up a ladder and encumbered with tools, contrived to appear passionately interested in a cobweb.

“Pipe leaks,” she said.

“It does not.”

“Tap runs.”

“Then it can be turned off.”

She pressed her lips together.

“Best shut as was.”

Now I might have insisted, but there is a point at which insistence with a servant becomes both tyrannical and useless. I told the man to finish his work. The water flowed beautifully—clear, cold, and strong. Mrs. Carkeek watched it enter the basin as Mrs. Gummidge might have watched the sea rise over the last relic of the Old ’Un. Then she turned away.

Nothing happened that day, or the next.

On the third night I awoke.

My room was over the dining-room, on the other side of the house from the pantry, and I cannot say what roused me. There had been a moon when I went to bed, but clouds now moved across it, alternately filling and emptying the room with a dim whiteness. I lay listening.

At first there was nothing but the sea.

Then I heard water.

Not the brook; that sound was continuous, quick, and shallow. This was nearer and intermittent—a small metallic rush, followed by the hollow splashing of water into a basin.

I sat up.

The sound ceased.

After a moment it began again.

There are women who, at such a moment, ring violently for assistance. I have always thought this hard on the assistance, which is generally asleep and as mortal as oneself. Besides, I was mistress of the house. I put on my dressing-gown, took a candle, and opened the door.

The passage was empty.

I crossed the landing, descended the stairs, and entered the kitchen. The moon shone there so brightly that I no longer needed my candle. Beyond the window, the orchard trees moved in the wind; their shadows slid over the floor like long black fingers. The water was still running.

I passed through the narrow passage.

The pantry door stood open.

The room beyond was not wholly dark. Moonlight entered through the barred window and lay across the shelves, the blue jars, the pale basin. The tap ran in a bright thread.

Over the porcelain basin, I saw two hands.

That was all.

Two small hands. A child’s hands.

They were washing.

I do not mean that they made the vague, repetitive motions of something seen in a dream. They were actual hands—or looked actual—with dimples at the knuckles and little rounded nails. Water struck them and broke into drops. They rubbed palm over palm, then each small wrist in turn, gravely and carefully, as children wash when they have been told that cleanliness is next to godliness and suspect that godliness may be inspected before breakfast.

There were no arms beyond the wrists. No body. Nothing reflected in the dark pane above the basin.

Only the hands, washing.

I stood at the threshold, holding my candle, although it had gone out.

The hands passed beneath the running water once more. One reached towards the tap, failed to turn it, and tried again.

This, absurdly, restored me. Terror is an emotion difficult to sustain in the presence of defective plumbing.

“You must press it down, my child,” I said.

The hands became still.

For one second they rested side by side beneath the stream. Then they were gone.

The water continued to run.

I entered the pantry and turned it off.

Yes, Clara, I was frightened. I make no claim to being the Queen of Spain. My knees had become unreliable, my mouth was dry, and I reached my bedroom with less dignity than I had left it. But the thing I had seen was so small, so intent, and so helplessly engaged in an ordinary duty that fear could not occupy me alone. Pity had entered with it.

At breakfast Mrs. Carkeek placed the coffee before me and said, without looking up:

“You heard her, then.”

“I saw her.”

The spoon in her hand trembled once against the saucer.

“Only the hands,” I added.

“That’s all she shows.”

“Whose hands?”

Mrs. Carkeek drew out a chair, though she had never before sat in my presence without invitation. I gave it at once.

“Margaret’s,” she said.

There had been Kendalls at Tresillack for nearly a hundred years. The last owner to live there was Captain John Kendall, who had gone to sea as a boy and returned with a wife of delicate health and one little daughter. Margaret was born in the house. Mrs. Carkeek, then a young woman, had been nurse first and housekeeper afterwards.

“She was a stirring little thing,” Mrs. Carkeek told me. “Never could abide dirty fingers, her own nor no one’s. If I’d set the silver out, she’d be after with a cloth. If her father dropped ash, she’d have brush and pan. ‘House do look after them as looks after house,’ she’d say. Seven year old and solemn as parson.”

Captain Kendall was frequently away. His wife died when Margaret was six. In the winter following, diphtheria came to the village.

“We kept her close,” said Mrs. Carkeek. “But doctor’s boy had it, and doctor came here. Maybe ’twas that. Maybe ’twas the gel from dairy. There’s no knowing. She took bad in January.”

The child had been nursed in the south room. She suffered thirst, as feverish children do, and developed a distressing fancy that her hands were unclean. Again and again she begged to have them washed. When she became too weak to leave the bed, Mrs. Carkeek brought the basin to her.

“Last thing she asked was water. ‘Carky,’ she says, ‘wash my hands, for Mammy’s coming.’ But there wasn’t time.”

Margaret died before dawn.

Captain Kendall, broken by the loss of wife and child, shut Tresillack and returned to sea. He died abroad. The property passed through trustees, then to a cousin, and at length—burdened with some legal complication—to Farmer Hosking, who had bought the surrounding land. Mrs. Carkeek remained.

“When did you first see the hands?” I asked.

“Night after burial. Tap was running in pantry. I went down and there they was.”

“Why in the pantry?”

“She favoured that basin. ’Twas low enough for her. Used to stand there washing strawberries, and herself too, if she’d been in garden.”

“And you cut off the water?”

“Not then. After Breams.”

Her face hardened.

The Bream children, those two little dependants whom I mentioned, had not been treated kindly. Their clothes were poor, though the family entertained; they were kept from the table when guests came, and sometimes shut for hours in the box-room beneath the eaves. Mrs. Carkeek, whose authority had been reduced when the Breams brought their own servants, did what she could, but much was concealed from her.

Margaret, it seemed, did more.

Food disappeared from the pantry—bread, apples, milk in a little jug—and was found beside the children’s bed. The bolt of the box-room repeatedly drew itself back. Once Mr. Bream, in a temper, locked the younger child in a cupboard under the stairs. Before he reached the dining-room the cupboard door stood open, though the key remained in his pocket.

“She couldn’t abide them being hurt,” said Mrs. Carkeek. “Night the little boy was beat, every bell in house rang together. Bream swore ’twas wires, but wires was cut from drawing-room bell. Then his decanter went off table and broke by his foot.”

“Did you see it fall?”

“Didn’t fall. Went.”

Her distinction was delivered with such dryness that I accepted it.

At last a neighbour reported the children’s condition, and their mother’s family removed them. The Breams departed soon afterwards, leaving debts, broken furniture, and a legend that Tresillack was infested with piskies.

“Better piskies than police,” Mrs. Carkeek remarked.

“But why did you stop the pantry water?”

“She was at it every night after they left. Washing, washing. Tap would run till cistern emptied. I told her there was no need, house was quiet now. She didn’t heed. So I plugged pipe.”

“And since then?”

“Things gets done. She’ve always been about. But no hands.”

I was silent for some time.

“You should have told me.”

Mrs. Carkeek looked almost offended.

“Wouldn’t have took house if I had.”

“I might.”

“No, miss.”

“Mrs. Carkeek, you presume upon my cowardice.”

“Beg pardon, miss. I presume upon anybody’s.”

That ended our conversation.

I considered the matter all day. By evening, I had reached a decision which I expect will strike you either as courageous or foolish according to your opinion of ghosts.

I went into the pantry, turned on the tap, and spoke aloud.

“Margaret,” I said, feeling distinctly ridiculous, “you may wash whenever you please, but you must turn the water off afterwards. We cannot empty the cistern every night.”

Nothing occurred. The blue jars maintained their discretion. A fly moved upon the window.

I added, “And thank you for the flowers.”

That night I heard the tap once. It ran for perhaps two minutes and stopped.

From that time Margaret and I understood one another.

I did not often see the hands. Twice, perhaps three times in all those years, I came upon them at the basin; and after the first occasion I never felt terror, though always a certain solemnity. More commonly I knew Margaret by her small services. My spectacles appeared where I could see them. A dropped stitch in my knitting was marked with a pin. On my birthday, which neither Mrs. Carkeek nor the village girl knew, seven white rosebuds were arranged beside my plate.

There was humour in her attentions too. A pompous neighbour, Mrs. Vellacott, came to luncheon and expressed herself strongly upon the impropriety of single women managing property. Each time she laid down her gloves, they were removed to another chair. She suspected me, then Mrs. Carkeek, and finally her own memory. When she departed, one glove was found tucked into the strap of her reticule and the other inside her muff, though the day was warm and she had not brought a muff.

I reproved Margaret afterwards.

“At least,” I said, “you might have chosen equal hiding places.”

A cupboard door clicked twice, which I chose to interpret as contrition.

Children always knew there was something. The rector’s little daughter asked who played with her in the orchard. A fisherman’s boy, brought to the kitchen with a cut knee, ceased crying and held out half his cake to somebody beside him. Once I found three daisies plaited into a chain upon the nursery windowsill, though no living child had been in the house for a week.

Mrs. Carkeek accepted these things without display. She spoke to Margaret when alone, as I occasionally overheard, but never indulged curiosity and disliked visitors who did.

“She bain’t a show,” she said when a gentleman from Penzance hinted that he might spend a night in the pantry with a camera. “And if she was, she wouldn’t show to you.”

I remained at Tresillack for six of the happiest years of my life. The phrase sounds sentimental; nevertheless it is true. I planted the lower garden, repaired the orchard wall, learned enough of the tides to avoid being cut off upon the western sands, and became sufficiently local to distinguish between three kinds of rain. Farmer Hosking ceased to regard me as a temporary lady and consulted me on the school treat. Mrs. Carkeek and I quarrelled regularly over preserves and were devoted to one another.

As for Margaret, I no longer thought of Tresillack as haunted. Haunted is too heavy a word; it suggests an incubus, a clanking wrong, some dead passion that poisons the air. Margaret’s presence made the house lighter. She had been denied the years in which care might have ripened into a vocation, a family, a life of useful love; yet what affection remained in her had not soured. It occupied itself with linen, flowers, lost thimbles, frightened children, and the proper turning off of taps.

One autumn Farmer Hosking came to me with a letter.

We sat in the drawing-room while rain travelled softly down the windows and the first fire of the season smoked in the grate.

“There’s a matter of house, ma’am,” he began.

“Not another magnetic widow, I hope.”

“Colonel Kendall.”

The name made me attend.

Colonel Robert Kendall was Captain Kendall’s younger brother. He had spent most of his life in India, had lately retired, and wished to return to Cornwall with his wife and family. A legal claim of his, long dormant and perfectly just, had been settled; he was prepared to purchase Tresillack at a fair price and make it once more the Kendall home.

“How large a family?” I asked.

“Eight children.”

“Eight?”

“Five gels and three boys.”

I looked around the quiet room. I imagined boots in the hall, bats beneath the stairs, lessons in the dining-room, dolls upon the window-seat, porridge burnt, doors slammed, knees grazed, tempers lost and recovered. Tresillack, which had received me so gently, seemed suddenly to draw a fuller breath.

“When would they come?”

“In spring. But there’s no haste put on you, ma’am. Colonel said particular you was to have what time you need.”

I thanked him and said that I would leave before Easter.

It was not until he had gone that I cried. One may be reasonable and still cry; indeed, reason often provides the best occasion for it. I loved the house. I had expected to grow old there, while Mrs. Carkeek grew no older at all and Margaret continued to improve my habits by invisible means.

That evening I went to the pantry.

“Your uncle is coming,” I said. “He is bringing eight children. You will have your work cut out, Margaret.”

The tap gave one brief silver trickle, though I had not touched it.

Colonel Kendall and his wife visited in February. He was a tall, sun-darkened man with Margaret’s grave eyes, or so Mrs. Carkeek told me afterwards. His wife was warm, practical, and entirely untroubled by eight children, India, Cornwall, or anything else Providence might place in her path.

I told them the history.

You look surprised, my dears, but concealment would have been dishonourable. Also, Mrs. Carkeek would certainly have told them if I did not.

Colonel Kendall listened without interruption. When I described the hands, he covered his face for a moment.

“I was twelve when Margaret died,” he said. “She used to follow me everywhere. I gave her a wooden horse, and she slept with it under her pillow.”

“The horse is in the nursery cupboard,” said Mrs. Carkeek from the doorway.

His wife asked only, “Does she like children?”

“Very much,” I answered.

“Then we shall suit.”

That was all. Some women would have required salts, theology, or a reduction in the purchase price. Mrs. Kendall asked whether the nursery chimney drew well.

My departure was fixed for the last day of March. During the final week every room filled with boxes, paper, string, and the melancholy litter by which a settled life is converted into luggage. Margaret helped. Books I had mislaid for years appeared in obvious places. A silver button missing from my travelling cloak was laid upon its collar. My possessions were packed with such neatness that Mrs. Carkeek accused me of interfering, and I accused her; whereupon we both became silent.

The last night was windless. I slept little. The sea sounded far away, and once I heard a child’s step cross the landing and pause outside my door.

In the morning the carriage came early. My trunks were roped behind it. Mrs. Carkeek stood in the hall in her best bonnet, refusing to cry until I should be out of sight. Farmer Hosking had sent daffodils. The rooms were bare of my smaller things, and already seemed to wait for other voices.

I asked for five minutes alone.

I went through the kitchen and along the narrow passage to the pantry. The blue jars stood upon their shelves. The basin shone. A drop of water gathered at the mouth of the brass tap, swelled, and fell.

“Margaret,” I said.

Nothing moved.

I put out my hands over the basin.

It was not an intelligent action. I did not know whether I expected to see anything, feel anything, or merely make a gesture which no one could witness and therefore no one could call foolish. I closed my eyes.

“Good-bye, my dear,” I said. “Thank you for taking care of me.”

For a moment there was only the cool air of the pantry and the distant chattering of the brook.

Then two small hands rested in mine.

They were cool and damp, as though newly washed. Their fingers curled very lightly about my fingers—not clinging, not pleading, but answering.

For a moment only.

Then there was nothing.

Miss Le Petyt ceased speaking. The fire settled with a soft collapse, and somewhere beyond the curtains the wind moved through wet branches.

Clara was the first to break the silence.

“And the Kendalls?”

“They lived at Tresillack for many years. The children flourished, the furniture suffered, and Mrs. Carkeek declared herself satisfied.”

“Did they see Margaret?”

“The younger ones did, I believe. Or thought they did. Children are not always precise about the frontier between companionship and imagination, and perhaps they are right not to be.”

“And you never went back?”

“Once,” said Miss Le Petyt. “A long while afterwards. There were cricket stumps upon the lawn, a doll’s perambulator in the hall, and eight pairs of shoes in varying degrees of disgrace outside the schoolroom. Tresillack had never looked happier.”

“Were there flowers in your room?”

Miss Le Petyt smiled.

“Seven white rosebuds, my dear, in a little glass beside the bed.”

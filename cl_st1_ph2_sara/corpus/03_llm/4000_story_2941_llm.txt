# The Man in the Sunlight

The inquiry concerning Convict No. 14,208 was begun at nine o’clock on the morning of October 17, in the directors’ room of the State Penitentiary.

There were present the three directors, the prison surgeon, Warden Hale, the deputy warden, the clerk of the institution, a stenographer, and myself. My presence was accidental in so far as the law was concerned, though subsequent events caused me to regard it as one of those accidents by which Providence sometimes selects a witness where justice has neglected to provide one.

The room was long, high-ceiled, and cold. Its two narrow windows, guarded by iron rods, overlooked the eastern yard, where several files of prisoners were marching to the shops. Their striped bodies passed at regular intervals through the pale autumn sunshine; their shoes struck the pavement together; and from time to time the harsh command of a keeper came through the glass with such clearness that the chairman twice turned his head impatiently.

Warden Hale sat at the end of the table.

He was ordinarily a man of commanding presence--large, erect, carefully dressed, with a broad forehead and a pair of cold gray eyes which seldom betrayed either uncertainty or compassion. On that morning, however, he appeared older by ten years. His face had a leaden pallor; the hand that lay upon the table opened and closed continually; and whenever footsteps sounded in the passage he glanced toward the door with an apprehension which no effort of discipline could wholly conceal.

The directors had received certain anonymous communications regarding the treatment of a prisoner who, it was alleged, had been confined for an extraordinary period in an underground cell. These communications were contradictory in detail, illiterate in expression, and violent in accusation; yet the directors, being responsible to the State, had concluded that an examination must be made.

The chairman, Mr. Ellison, a grave and deliberate man, arranged the papers before him.

“Warden,” he said, “you have been informed of the purpose of this inquiry?”

“I have.”

“And you have caused the prisoner known as Number Fourteen Thousand Two Hundred and Eight to be brought here?”

“I have given the order.”

The warden’s voice was firm, but he did not look at the chairman.

“What is the prisoner’s name?”

“Silas Ward.”

“How long has he been under punishment?”

The warden hesitated.

“That depends upon what is meant by punishment.”

“Confinement apart from the general prison population.”

“Twenty-three months.”

The stenographer’s pencil stopped. The prison surgeon lowered his eyes. One of the directors, Colonel Ames, leaned sharply forward.

“Twenty-three months!” he exclaimed. “For what offence?”

“Insubordination; refusal to work; threats against the life of the warden; repeated defiance of authority.”

“Against your life?”

“Yes.”

“And does he still make the threat?”

The warden’s hand closed.

“I believe he does.”

At that moment the door opened.

Two keepers entered, supporting between them a figure which at first I scarcely recognized as human. It was that of a man apparently sixty years of age, though we afterward learned that he had not completed his thirty-eighth year. He was tall, or had once been tall; but disease, hunger, and prolonged crouching had bent him until his shoulders projected beneath his coarse prison shirt like two angular bones. His face was colorless, except where fever had stained the cheek-bones; his lips were blue; a ragged beard covered his hollow jaws; and his eyes, deep-set and unnaturally luminous, moved from one person to another with the watchful ferocity of an animal brought suddenly from darkness.

His hands were yet more dreadful. The fingers were swollen at the joints, the nails broken, the tips flattened and scarred. He held them before him stiffly, as though every movement gave pain.

When his gaze fell upon Warden Hale, the change in him was instantaneous. The bent body straightened; his breath came with a dry, whistling sound; and the eyes which had seemed merely hungry became two points of concentrated hatred.

The warden arose half-way from his chair.

“Keep him back,” he said.

No. 14,208 smiled.

It was not a pleasant smile. There was neither mirth nor triumph in it; only recognition and a terrible patience.

“I ain’t going to touch him,” said the prisoner. “Not here.”

“Place him in the chair,” directed Mr. Ellison.

The keepers seated him near the farther window. A narrow beam of sunshine, passing between two iron rods, fell upon the floor, crossed one worn shoe, and rested upon his left hand. He looked down at it. For a moment the warden, the directors, and the inquiry appeared to be forgotten. He turned his hand slowly, first the palm and then the back, in that slender warmth; and something in the simplicity of the act--something in the astonished tenderness with which he regarded the light--affected every person in the room.

“Move the chair nearer the window,” said the chairman.

One of the keepers obeyed. The sunshine now lay across the prisoner’s breast and face. He closed his eyes and drew a long breath, which ended in a fit of coughing.

The surgeon stepped toward him.

“Not yet,” said the chairman. “Let him recover.”

For perhaps a minute no one spoke. The marching feet in the yard passed and repassed. The stenographer waited with her pencil poised. Warden Hale had resumed his seat, but his attention never left the prisoner.

At last Mr. Ellison said:

“Ward, we have brought you here to tell us why you have been confined. You may speak freely. Nothing you say before this board shall subject you to punishment. Do you understand?”

The prisoner opened his eyes.

“I understand words,” he replied.

“You are charged with refusing to work, resisting the officers, and threatening to kill the warden.”

“I did refuse to work.”

“You admit that?”

“Yes.”

“You resisted?”

“When they laid hands on me.”

“And you threatened Warden Hale?”

The prisoner turned his face slowly toward him.

“I told him I’d kill him.”

“Do you withdraw that threat?”

“No.”

There was a slight movement around the table.

“Ward,” said the chairman, “you are in the presence of men who desire to investigate your case impartially. If you persist in a threat of murder, you prejudice that case at the outset.”

“I ain’t got any case,” he answered. “He took it from me.”

“Who?”

“Him.”

He pointed one scarred finger at the warden.

Mr. Ellison spoke with greater severity.

“You will answer without gestures or abuse. Begin at the beginning. What first caused the difficulty between you and the prison authorities?”

“Tobacco.”

The word was so trivial, so grotesquely inadequate to the spectacle before us, that Colonel Ames uttered an exclamation of disbelief.

“Tobacco?”

“My ration.”

“Proceed.”

The prisoner leaned back. His eyes wandered to the patch of sunlight on the wall, and when he spoke his voice was low and monotonous, as though the story had been repeated so often in the darkness that it no longer required conscious recollection.

“I came here five years ago last May. I was sent up for burglary. I done it. I ain’t saying I didn’t. I was drunk, and I broke into a feed store with another man. He got away. I got four years and six months, with time off for good conduct.

“I behaved myself. Ask the old keepers. I worked in the shoe shop. I never talked back. I never lost a mark. I had ten months coming off, and they told me if I kept straight I’d be out before Christmas.

“We got tobacco every Saturday. Two ounces to a man that used it. The clerk stood at the door of the supply-room, and you went by and gave your number. That Saturday I went along with the rest. I said, ‘Fourteen two-o-eight.’ The clerk looked at his sheet and said I’d had mine.

“I told him I hadn’t.

“He said, ‘You were here ten minutes ago.’

“I said, ‘You’re mistaken.’

“He said he knew me, and I couldn’t get two rations by lying.

“I told him again I hadn’t got it. He called Keeper Doyle. Doyle searched me. There wasn’t any tobacco on me. Then the clerk said I must have passed it to another man. I asked him what man. He couldn’t say.

“I said I wanted my ration.

“He said, ‘Get out, you thief.’

“That was the first of it.”

The prisoner paused. His chest rose painfully.

Colonel Ames said, “You had been convicted of burglary. Surely the word thief could not have possessed such novelty as to drive you into rebellion.”

Ward looked at him, and in that wasted face there appeared an austere dignity which made the director’s remark seem small.

“The court called me a burglar,” he said. “The judge gave me four years and six months for what I done. I took it. But I never stole in this prison. I never begged for what wasn’t mine. When a man’s paying for one thing, that don’t give everybody the right to call him every other thing.”

The chairman nodded slightly.

“What did you do after the clerk called you a thief?”

“I said I wouldn’t leave till I got my tobacco or till he took back the word. Doyle ordered me to go. I said, ‘No.’ He took hold of my arm. I pulled loose. Then three of ’em came, and they put me in the punishment room.”

“For how long?”

“Two days.”

“On bread and water?”

“Yes.”

“What occurred when you came out?”

“They took me to the deputy. He asked if I’d obey orders. I said I would, when they gave me what was mine and cleared the charge. He said the matter was closed. I said it wasn’t closed with me.

“Then they ordered me to the shoe shop. I said I wouldn’t work.”

“Why?”

“Because working meant I admitted they could rob me and call me a thief besides.”

“You had been deprived of two ounces of tobacco,” said the third director, Mr. Bell.

Ward’s head turned quickly.

“No. I’d been deprived of my word. The tobacco wasn’t much. A man’s word is all he’s got in here. You take his name, his clothes, his hair, his liberty; you give him a number and make him walk where you point. If you take his word too, he ain’t a man. He’s a dog with a tag on him.”

The stenographer’s pencil moved rapidly.

Mr. Bell flushed, but asked, “Did you strike an officer?”

“Not then.”

“Later?”

“Yes.”

“Tell us.”

“They took me before the warden.”

For the first time Ward’s monotonous delivery changed. The muscles around his mouth drew tight; his gaze fixed itself upon Hale.

“He sat where he’s sitting now--clean, strong, and sure of himself. The deputy told him I’d tried to draw tobacco twice. I said it was a lie. The warden said, ‘Your record is against your word.’

“I told him my record showed I’d behaved in his prison.

“He said convicts had no rights except what the rules allowed.

“I said the rules allowed my ration.

“He told me not to trifle with him. I said I wasn’t trifling. He said I’d go to work or be punished. I said I wouldn’t go till the charge was taken off the book.

“Then he come around the desk and stood close to me. He said, ‘Ward, I have broken men more obstinate than you.’

“I said, ‘You may break my bones.’

“He said, ‘I’ll break your will.’

“I told him he couldn’t.

“Then he ordered me downstairs.”

“Downstairs where?” asked the chairman.

Ward looked at the warden.

“He knows.”

“Answer the question.”

“To the dungeon.”

A silence followed. Every old prison has some place which its officers mention reluctantly and its inmates name only in whispers; a remnant, perhaps, of an earlier and more brutal age, retained under the pretext that it is no longer used. The dungeon of that institution lay beneath the north wing. It did not appear upon the plans submitted to the legislature, nor in the annual reports, nor in the carefully prepared addresses with which visiting committees were entertained.

Mr. Ellison turned upon the warden.

“Is there an underground cell in this prison?”

“There are several old disciplinary cells,” Hale replied.

“Below the level of the yard?”

“Yes.”

“With windows?”

“Ventilators.”

“With light?”

“A limited amount.”

“How limited?”

The warden did not answer.

The chairman faced the prisoner again.

“Describe the cell.”

“It was about seven feet long and four feet wide. Stone floor. Stone walls. An iron door with a hole near the bottom. There was a pipe in the ceiling, but no window. When the door shut, it was black. Not night black--night’s got air in it, and sometimes stars. This was buried black. You put your hand against your face, and there wasn’t any hand.

“They took my coat and shoes. There was a board to lie on. Once a day they pushed in bread and a tin of water.”

“How long were you first kept there?”

“I don’t know.”

The chairman turned to the clerk.

“The records?”

The clerk opened a ledger with trembling fingers.

“The first commitment was for seven days.”

Ward shook his head.

“It was longer.”

“The book says seven.”

“The book had light to count by.”

There was a murmur which the chairman checked.

“What happened in the dungeon?” he asked.

“At first I walked. Three steps one way, three steps back. I kept doing it so I wouldn’t think. Then my feet swelled. I lay down. I could hear rats in the wall. Sometimes one come through the hole after crumbs.

“I tried to count the meals, but sometimes they brought two close together, and sometimes none for a long while. I sang. A keeper told me to stop. I talked to myself. He told me to stop that too.

“One day--I call it day because they opened the door--they brought me upstairs. The light cut my eyes. I couldn’t see. The deputy asked if I’d work.

“I said, ‘Do I get my tobacco?’

“He said, ‘That foolishness is finished.’

“I said, ‘Not with me.’

“He said, ‘Will you work?’

“I said, ‘No.’

“They sent me back.”

“How many times did this occur?”

“I can’t tell. A good many.”

The chairman looked toward the prison surgeon.

“Did you examine this man during those periods?”

The surgeon cleared his throat.

“Not during the earlier periods. I was informed that he was violent and that the warden did not consider an examination safe.”

Ward laughed softly.

“Violent! I couldn’t stand without the wall.”

“Proceed,” said Mr. Ellison.

“After a while they put irons on me. There was a ring in the floor. They chained one ankle to it and put an iron ball on the other leg. I could sit or lie, but I couldn’t walk my three steps.

“The darkness got inside me then. It filled my mouth when I breathed. It pressed on my eyes. Sometimes I saw colors--red wheels, blue flames, faces hanging in the air. I’d reach for ’em, and the chain would pull me back.

“But I held on to one thing. I said, ‘I didn’t take the tobacco. I didn’t take it.’ I said it till the words didn’t sound like words. I said it to the rats. I said it to the stones. I said it so I’d know who I was.”

His voice ceased. The sunshine had moved from his face, but still rested upon one shoulder. He made a feeble motion toward it and then let his hand fall.

Mr. Ellison said, “You have told us that you struck an officer.”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“The time they put the mask on me.”

Colonel Ames looked up abruptly.

“What mask?”

“A leather thing, with iron over it. It covered my head. There were little holes to breathe through. They said it would keep me quiet.”

The deputy warden shifted in his chair.

“It was an approved restraint,” he said. “Used only when a prisoner endangered himself or others.”

Ward turned to him.

“I was chained to the floor.”

The deputy made no reply.

“They came to take it off,” Ward continued. “I’d worn it--I don’t know how long. My face was sore under it. When they unlocked my hands, I struck Doyle. I didn’t hit him hard. I was too weak. But I meant to hit him.

“They dragged me upstairs. The warden was waiting. He said, ‘Now we will see whether you are master here.’

“I said, ‘I ain’t trying to be master. I’m trying to be a man.’

“He said I’d assaulted an officer and would be whipped.”

The stenographer’s pencil stopped again.

Mr. Ellison’s face became rigid.

“Whipped?”

“Yes.”

“There is no regulation authorizing flogging in a State institution.”

“No,” said Ward. “There wasn’t any regulation in the room.”

The chairman addressed Warden Hale.

“Was this prisoner flogged by your order?”

The warden’s gray eyes met his. For several seconds the two men looked at one another; then Hale said:

“He was subjected to corporal correction.”

“With what instrument?”

“A leather strap.”

“How many blows?”

“I do not remember.”

“I do,” said Ward.

All eyes returned to him.

“They took me to an old storeroom. There was a ladder against the wall. They tied my wrists to a rung above my head. My toes just touched the floor.

“The warden asked, ‘Will you work?’

“I said, ‘When you give me my due.’

“He said, ‘Strike.’

“Doyle struck me.

“The warden asked again.

“I said, ‘No.’

“He told Doyle to strike again.”

The prisoner’s voice had become unnaturally level. It contained no appeal, no ornament, no anger even; and because of this restraint the room in which we sat seemed to disappear, giving place to that hidden chamber, the ladder, the suspended body, and the official standing cleanly apart while another man carried out his will.

“How many?” asked the chairman.

“Thirty.”

“At one time?”

“Ten the first time. Ten two days after. Ten after that.”

The prison surgeon sprang from his chair.

“In his condition?”

Ward continued as though he had not heard.

“After the first ten I fainted. They threw water on me. The warden waited till I opened my eyes. He asked if I’d work.

“I said, ‘No.’

“He said I would answer differently next time.

“The next time I couldn’t climb the ladder, so they lifted me. After six I bit through my lip. I remember the blood running into my mouth. It tasted warm. Everything else was cold.

“He asked me again.

“I said, ‘I didn’t steal.’

“He said, ‘That is not the question.’

“I said, ‘It’s the only question.’

“Then he gave me the rest.”

Here the narrative stopped. The prisoner stared before him, not at any living person, but at some image which the rest of us could not see. His fingers closed upon the arms of the chair. The scarred tips whitened; a tremor passed along his thin frame.

I had seen grief exhibited in courts, remorse in death-cells, fear beneath the surgeon’s knife, and the rehearsed agony by which actors draw tears from crowded houses; but I had never witnessed any scene so terrible as the stillness of that man while he looked backward into the room where his body had been dishonored.

The chairman’s voice, when it came, was subdued.

“And after the third flogging?”

“They cut me down. I fell. The warden stood over me and said, ‘Have you had enough?’

“I told him, ‘I’ll live to kill you.’

“He bent down so I could see his face.

“He said, ‘Then you will remain where you are until that purpose dies.’

“I said, ‘It’ll die when you do.’

“They carried me back.”

“How long ago was that?”

“Twenty-three months.”

“And during the whole of that time you have remained in the dungeon?”

“Most of it. Sometimes they brought me up to ask the same question.”

“What question?”

“Whether I’d work and whether I’d take back what I said.”

“And you refused?”

“I refused.”

“Why did you refuse to work? The original charge had by then become insignificant beside your suffering.”

“To you, maybe.”

“Explain.”

Ward bent forward. His hollow cheeks had reddened; the eyes glowed with that strange light which sometimes accompanies the last expenditure of a failing body.

“If I’d gone to work, he’d have won. Not over a convict--over a man. He’d have made truth mean what he said it meant. He’d have proved a strap can turn right into wrong. I couldn’t let him.

“I knew I might die down there. Some nights I wanted to. I’d put my face against the floor where it was cold and wait for my heart to stop. But then I’d see him asking if I’d had enough, and I’d say, ‘Not yet.’

“He had the keys. He had the keepers. He had the bread and water. He had the dark. But the one thing he wanted was inside me, and I wouldn’t give it.

“No man’s power can make me say I stole when I didn’t. No chain can do it. No whip can do it. No grave can do it.”

He coughed violently. The surgeon supported him while a keeper brought water. Ward drank, pushed the cup away, and looked again toward the warden.

“I held on so I could kill him.”

There it was--not a figure of speech, not the fretful malice of an injured man, but Murder itself, deliberate and patient, standing bodily in the directors’ room. One could almost perceive it between the two men, a dark third presence created by cruelty and nourished through twenty-three months of silence.

Mr. Ellison spoke.

“Ward, whatever wrong may have been done to you, no wrong justifies murder.”

“I ain’t asking it to.”

“You have been heard with patience. This board has authority to remedy injustice. We can remove you from punishment, restore your credits, recommend clemency. But before we act, you must assure us that you will not attempt the warden’s life.”

“No.”

“You refuse?”

“I won’t lie to you. If I get strong, and if I get a chance, I’ll kill him.”

Warden Hale rose.

“You see!” he cried, and the composure which he had maintained broke at last. “You hear him! This is the man whose complaints are to be accepted against officers responsible for the safety of twelve hundred convicts. He is a homicidal madman. He has compelled every measure taken against him.”

Ward turned his head.

“You called me a thief before I was a madman,” he said. “You whipped me before I was a murderer. You made the thing you’re afraid of.”

The warden’s lips moved, but no answer came.

Mr. Ellison struck the table.

“Sit down, Warden.”

Hale obeyed.

The chairman conferred in low tones with the other directors. During this interval the prisoner remained motionless, save for the labor of his breathing. The surgeon knelt beside him, placed an ear against his chest, and then whispered urgently to the chairman.

Mr. Ellison addressed the guards.

“Take this man to the hospital. Not to a cell--to the hospital. Place him in the southern ward, by a window. He is to receive proper food, medical care, and sunlight. No restraint shall be used without the surgeon’s written order.”

Ward looked suspiciously from one face to another.

“Am I cleared?”

“The whole matter will be investigated.”

“That ain’t clearing me.”

“No; but it is the beginning of justice.”

“I don’t want beginnings.”

“It is all we can give you to-day.”

The keepers assisted him to rise. As he passed the warden, his legs failed and he swayed toward the table. Hale recoiled. The prisoner observed the movement and smiled once more.

“Not here,” he whispered. “I told you--not here.”

He was carried from the room.

The investigation did not end that day. The directors examined the punishment registers, supply sheets, hospital records, and private memoranda of the warden’s office. They descended into the underground cells, where the chairman remained less than five minutes before demanding that the doors be removed and the chambers permanently sealed. They questioned keepers separately; and men who at first remembered nothing gradually recovered details when confronted with one another’s statements.

The official record of Ward’s punishment was incomplete. Dates had been altered; medical visits entered which the surgeon denied having made; orders given orally had left no trace except upon the prisoner’s body. The flogging was admitted. The iron mask was produced from a locked cupboard. The ladder still bore dark stains upon one rung.

The directors suspended Warden Hale pending action by the Governor.

Meanwhile the strange history of No. 14,208 escaped the walls. A young reporter obtained some portion of the stenographer’s notes and published an account under a heading which occupied half the width of his paper. Other journals sent correspondents. Ministers preached upon the case. Physicians discussed the effects of solitary confinement; lawyers the constitutional limits of punishment; reformers, who had long been dismissed as sentimentalists, demanded inspection of every penal institution in the State.

The public, which can be indifferent to a thousand anonymous sufferings yet passionately moved by one suffering supplied with a name and face, began to speak of Silas Ward.

He lay in the southern hospital ward, where the sun entered for three hours every morning. At first he followed its progress across the blanket with the absorbed attention of a child. He ate little. His lungs were diseased; the valves of his heart had been injured; his back carried permanent scars; and the surgeon, who had become his untiring attendant, declared privately that freedom itself could not restore him.

Yet the prisoner’s will remained hard.

“Have you changed your intention?” Mr. Ellison asked during one visit.

“No.”

“Warden Hale has been removed.”

“He’s alive.”

“You may be pardoned.”

“I’ll be nearer him then.”

“Ward, this purpose will destroy whatever remains of your life.”

“He destroyed that already.”

No argument moved him. Punishment had not subdued him; justice, arriving in documents and committees, did not soften him. He accepted clean linen, medicine, broth, and sunshine as things which had been withheld and were therefore due; he expressed no gratitude, for gratitude would have seemed to him another surrender.

Then, six weeks after the inquiry, there occurred one of those revelations so simple in cause and so vast in consequence that the mind revolts against the disproportion.

A former prisoner named Amos Trent presented himself at the office of a newspaper in a town two hundred miles distant. He had read Ward’s story. Trent had served in the penitentiary at the same time and had worked in the laundry. In height, build, coloring, and particularly in the shape of the beard then worn by both men, he had closely resembled Ward. Prisoners had frequently called them brothers.

On the Saturday in question, Trent had lost his own tobacco in gambling. Knowing Ward’s number, and counting upon the supply clerk’s haste, he had entered the line a second time, lowered his face, given the number “14,208,” and received Ward’s ration. He had expected the fraud to produce no more serious result than a quarrel at the window. When Ward disappeared into punishment, Trent became frightened. Before he could confess, he was discharged; thereafter cowardice and the fear of prosecution kept him silent.

“I stole it,” he told the reporter. “Ward never touched it. If they want to send me back, I’ll go. I can’t carry him on my back any longer.”

Trent was brought before the directors and repeated his confession under oath. The former supply clerk, shown photographs of the two men as they had appeared in prison, admitted that he could not distinguish them with certainty.

Thus the innocence for which Silas Ward had paid with his health, his reason’s peace, and nearly his life was established at last over a matter worth a few cents.

The Governor issued a full pardon. It recited that the prisoner had been falsely accused; that the punishments inflicted upon him had been unlawful and excessive; and that his conduct prior to the accusation had entitled him to early release. The formal document, with its seal, ribbons, and sonorous legal phrases, was delivered to him in the hospital.

Ward listened while it was read.

“Does it say I didn’t steal?” he asked.

“It does,” replied Mr. Ellison.

“Read that part again.”

The chairman read it.

Ward closed his eyes.

For the first time since I had seen him, the ferocity vanished wholly from his face. He appeared not old, but exhausted and very young. Two tears escaped beneath his lids and traveled slowly into his beard.

After a while he said:

“I told ’em.”

The Governor’s pardon restored his civil liberty, but the surgeon would not permit his immediate removal. He was to remain in the hospital as a guest of the State until arrangements could be made for his care. Several citizens offered assistance. A widowed sister, believing him still vigorous, wrote that he might live with her. Those who knew his condition understood that he would never reach her home.

Warden Hale’s resignation had been demanded and accepted. Criminal proceedings were considered, but delayed. He had remained in his residence near the prison while the directors completed their report. He went little into public. Men who had once courted his favor crossed the street rather than meet him. His wife and children left for relatives in another State.

During those weeks a transformation no less visible than Ward’s ruin occurred in Hale. His erect bearing deserted him; his hair whitened at the temples; and the severe assurance which had once passed for strength became a haunted reserve. Whether he feared prosecution, disgrace, or the prisoner’s threat, no one could say. I believe he feared all three; yet beneath those fears another power was at work--one slower and more terrible than any tribunal--the awakening perception that the man he had sought to conquer had spoken the truth from the beginning.

On the evening before Hale was to leave the prison, he sent a message to Mr. Ellison requesting permission to see Ward alone.

The chairman refused.

Hale came in person.

“I ask ten minutes,” he said.

“For what purpose?”

“To say what should have been said long ago.”

“You know that he threatens your life.”

“I know.”

“He may attempt it.”

“He has not the strength to stand.”

“A weak man can use a weapon.”

Hale’s face changed strangely.

“Yes,” he said. “That is why I must see him.”

Mr. Ellison studied him, then insisted that the surgeon and I remain in an adjoining room with the door partly open. The former warden agreed, upon the condition that Ward should not be told we were there.

It was nearly sunset when he entered the hospital chamber.

Ward occupied a bed beside the window. He had been dressed that afternoon in plain civilian clothes purchased by the directors: a dark coat too large for his wasted frame, a white shirt, and soft shoes. The striped uniform had been taken away. The pardon lay folded on a small table beside him.

Beyond the bars--which had not yet been removed from the hospital window--the western sky burned with October gold. A shaft of light rested across the bed.

Hale closed the door and stood with his hat in his hands.

Ward saw him.

The thin body stiffened beneath the blanket. One hand moved toward the rail, gripping it. His eyes, which had been peaceful, resumed their old glitter.

“So,” he said. “You come.”

“Yes.”

“They told me you were gone.”

“I leave to-morrow.”

Ward tried to raise himself. The effort brought the surgeon to the edge of the adjoining doorway, but Hale motioned him back without turning.

“Don’t call them,” said Ward. “I ain’t afraid.”

“No,” replied Hale. “You were never afraid.”

The acknowledgment seemed to perplex the prisoner more than an insult would have done. He watched silently while Hale placed his hat upon the chair.

“I have come,” said the former warden, “because there is something between us which no board, no Governor, and no court can settle.”

“You know what it is.”

“I know.”

Hale drew the chair nearer but did not sit.

“When you first came before me,” he continued, “I believed the clerk. I believed the record because it was written, and disbelieved you because you were a convict. That was injustice. Afterward, when you defied me, the matter ceased to be tobacco or discipline. It became a contest between your will and mine.”

Ward’s fingers tightened.

“You said you’d break me.”

“I said it.”

“You tried.”

“I tried with every cruel power entrusted to me.”

“You put me in the dark.”

“Yes.”

“You chained me.”

“Yes.”

“You put the iron thing on my head.”

“Yes.”

“You had me tied to the ladder.”

“Yes.”

“You watched.”

At this the warden’s voice failed. He turned toward the window. The last light revealed the deep lines around his mouth.

“I watched,” he said.

“And now you’re sorry because they found you out.”

Hale accepted the blow without protest.

“I was sorry first because I was found out. I will not lie to you. I was afraid of losing my office, my name, my family’s respect. Then I saw you brought before the directors. I saw your hands. I heard you tell what had been done, and I knew that every mark upon you was my work.

“Since then I have had no darkness like yours, no chains, no bread and water; yet I have not passed one unpunished hour. When I close my eyes I see the ladder. When a door shuts, I hear the dungeon door. I have discovered too late that authority over a man’s body is not authority over his conscience, and that an officer may obey every custom of cruelty until cruelty itself becomes his only law.”

Ward breathed heavily.

“Words,” he said.

“Yes. Only words. Had I spoken one right word at the beginning, all this might have been prevented. Spoken now, it can restore nothing. Still I must speak it.”

Hale moved to the foot of the bed. He stood no longer like an official, nor even like an equal who bargains for peace, but like a guilty man before the person he had wronged.

“Silas Ward, you told the truth. You were innocent. I was unjust, arrogant, and cruel. I treated you as a thing because the State had put a number upon you, and when you demanded to be treated as a man I punished you for reminding me that you were one.

“I ask your forgiveness.”

Ward stared at him.

The request seemed to strike against the iron structure which twenty-three months of resistance had built within him. Punishment had shaken that structure but could not overthrow it; vindication had opened its gates but had not dissolved its walls. Now a human voice, stripped of office and command, entered where force had failed.

Yet he resisted still.

“You ask because you’re safe,” he whispered. “You know I can’t get out of this bed.”

“No,” said Hale. “I do not know that I am safe.”

He reached inside his coat and drew forth a revolver.

I moved involuntarily toward the door, but the surgeon seized my arm. Before either of us could intervene, Hale placed the weapon upon the blanket within reach of Ward’s right hand.

It was a heavy service revolver. The metal caught the red sunlight. Hale opened the chamber, revolved it slowly, and closed it again.

“It is loaded,” he said. “You vowed to kill me. You said you would do it if you were free and had the chance. You are free. The chance is here.”

Ward’s hand crept toward the weapon.

Hale stepped back from the bed, folded his arms, and waited.

There was no sound in the room but the prisoner’s labored breathing and, far away, the evening bell calling the men from the shops. The old mechanical procession had begun in the yard; feet struck stone, rank after rank, while within the hospital one broken representative of that multitude held life and death beneath his hand.

Ward grasped the revolver.

At first he could not lift it. He set his teeth, brought his other hand beneath the barrel, and raised it inch by inch. The muzzle wavered toward Hale’s breast.

The former warden did not move.

“Shoot,” he said.

Ward’s eyes blazed.

“You think I won’t.”

“I believe you may.”

“You’re afraid.”

“I am.”

“Then why’d you give it to me?”

“Because I took from you every choice but hatred. I will not take this choice also.”

The weapon steadied for an instant.

I saw Ward’s finger move within the guard. I saw Hale’s face grow pale, though his posture remained unchanged. The sunset touched them both--the punished and the punisher, the accuser and the accused--and seemed, in that suspended moment, to hold them outside every institution and every law, two mortal men alone before the last judgment of one another.

Ward whispered:

“I dreamed of this.”

“I know.”

“Every night.”

“I know.”

“I seen the bullet go into you. I seen you fall.”

Hale bowed his head.

“If that is what remains for me, I accept it.”

The muzzle trembled.

Then Hale raised his eyes and said, with a compassion so simple that it carried no trace of command:

“Silas, I am sorry.”

The prisoner uttered a sound unlike any I had heard from him--not a cry of pain, nor anger, but the bewildered sob of a child awakened from terror. The revolver fell upon the blanket.

“No,” he said. “No--I can’t.”

His hands covered his face.

“I can’t shoot you when you talk to me like that. Why didn’t you say it before? Why didn’t you call me Silas before?”

Hale reached quickly for the weapon and cast it across the room. Then he knelt beside the bed.

Ward was weeping without restraint. The tears ran through his fingers; his thin shoulders shook; all the inflexibility by which he had preserved himself amid darkness, chains, hunger, and the lash melted under the warmth of one sincere human word.

“I tried to be hard,” he sobbed. “I had to be. There wasn’t nothing else. If I’d let go, I’d have been only a baby down there--only a scared baby in the dark.”

Hale took the scarred hands away from his face and held them.

“Forgive me,” he said again.

Ward looked at him for a long while. The hatred faded from his eyes; and as it faded, there appeared behind it the man who might have left prison before Christmas, who might have worked, laughed, grown old, and remembered his punishment as justly earned and honestly completed.

“I forgive you,” he whispered.

At that moment the surgeon entered, for Ward’s face had suddenly become gray.

“Lie back,” he ordered.

But Ward continued to hold Hale’s hand.

“I’m free now?” he asked.

“Yes,” said Hale. “You are free.”

“And cleared?”

“Before every man.”

Ward smiled.

The surgeon placed his fingers upon the wasted wrist, then bent urgently over him.

“You must not speak.”

“I’ve got to go to work,” Ward murmured.

“No,” said the surgeon. “You must rest.”

Ward’s gaze wandered toward the window, where the last bar of sunlight was withdrawing from the bed.

“I’ll go to work--to-morrow.”

His fingers closed once upon the warden’s hand. A faint astonishment passed across his face, as though he had seen some door open where no door had been; then the luminous eyes became fixed, the breath halted, and the body was still.

The surgeon listened at his breast and slowly arose.

Hale remained kneeling. He did not release the dead man’s hand.

No person in the room spoke. Outside, the final file of prisoners crossed the yard, and the great iron gate shut behind them with its accustomed clang.

Thus ended the rebellion of Convict No. 14,208.

The institution recorded his death under disease of the heart; the directors’ report attributed it to prolonged confinement, insufficient nourishment, exposure, and unlawful punishment. Warden Hale’s name was removed from the rolls. New regulations were adopted. The dungeon was filled with stone. Corporal punishment was forbidden in language so explicit that no officer might again conceal brutality beneath the phrase “disciplinary correction.”

These acts were proper; but they were late.

For twenty-three months authority had spoken to Silas Ward in the language of locks, irons, hunger, and blows, and he had answered in the only language left to injured pride--defiance. The State had sought to make him obedient by treating him as a beast; it had thereby created in him the purpose of a murderer. At last one man spoke to him, not as warden to convict, not as master to numbered flesh, but as a guilty soul to another soul; and that word accomplished in a moment what all the instruments of terror had failed to do.

It conquered him--but it conquered him only when there was nothing left to save.

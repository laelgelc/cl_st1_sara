### The Geography of Disgrace

Nicholas was in disgrace. This condition, according to the aunt who governed the household by force of announcement, had been brought about by his obstinate insistence that there was a frog in his bowl of bread-and-milk.

“There cannot possibly be a frog in your bowl,” she had said, employing that tone in which adults mistake confidence for evidence.

Nicholas had continued to maintain that there was.

“Nonsense. How could a frog get into your breakfast?”

Nicholas had not considered this question relevant. The means by which a frog arrived in a bowl had no bearing on whether the frog was presently there. He had therefore repeated his statement with an exactness which the aunt interpreted as insolence.

When the bowl was finally examined, the frog was discovered among the sodden pieces of bread, looking neither ashamed nor surprised. The aunt’s error was complete, but Nicholas’s innocence was not thereby established. He had put the frog there himself, and the older and wiser members of the household, relieved to have recovered some moral footing, fastened upon this circumstance with enthusiasm.

“You see how wicked you have been,” said the aunt.

Nicholas saw rather how mistaken she had been. To him the incident had proved two things: that there had been a frog in the bowl, and that the aunt could be loudly certain about matters of which she knew nothing. The second discovery promised wider usefulness than the first.

Punishment was organised before luncheon. His boy-cousin Bobby, his girl-cousin, and Nicholas’s younger brother were to be taken to the seaside at Jagborough. There would be sands, shrimping, a picnic tea, and all the wholesome delights which are especially wholesome when someone else is forbidden to share them. Nicholas would remain at home.

The aunt described the excursion several times in his hearing, laying particular stress on the probable excellence of the sands.

“They will have a beautiful afternoon,” she said. “And you will stay here and think about your behaviour.”

Nicholas reflected that Bobby’s boots were painfully tight and that the girl-cousin had bruised her knee that morning against the corner of the nursery fender. He did not mention either fact. Adults preferred punishments to possess a theatrical completeness, and it was unkind to disturb them while they arranged the scenery.

The carriage departed amid a bustle of baskets, instructions, and manufactured gaiety. Bobby climbed in with the expression of a martyr entering a public monument. The girl-cousin was helped up because of her knee. Nicholas watched from the side window and observed that none of the travellers appeared overwhelmed by good fortune.

The aunt, however, remained at home. She was not Nicholas’s aunt by any discoverable operation of birth or marriage, but she had long occupied the office by assertion, and the household had surrendered the point through fatigue. She now announced a supplementary prohibition.

“You are not to go into the gooseberry garden.”

“Why should I go there?” asked Nicholas.

“That is not the question. You are forbidden to enter it.”

The gooseberry garden contained two rows of fruit bushes, several currant plants, a leaning pear tree, and very little else. It had never exercised any strong attraction upon Nicholas. But the aunt had convinced herself that, being excluded from the expedition, he would seek consolation by stealing fruit. She stationed herself where the kitchen garden, shrubbery path, and gooseberry enclosure could all be watched with a minimum of movement.

She was a woman of limited invention but heroic perseverance. Once an idea had entered her mind, it might remain there for hours without danger of meeting another.

Nicholas strolled twice toward the garden door and then withdrew when he judged himself observed. On the third occasion he vanished behind a clump of laurel, reappeared near the tool shed, and examined the sky with suspicious care. These manoeuvres cost him little and confirmed the aunt in her self-imposed sentry-duty.

“He means to get into that garden,” she told herself.

Nicholas, meanwhile, had no such intention. The garden was merely a convenient battlefield on which the enemy could be induced to concentrate her forces.

The true object of his campaign lay at the end of a dim passage beyond the schoolroom: the lumber-room.

It was always locked. Children were not admitted there, and no adult entered it unless compelled by some domestic emergency involving chairs, curtains, or ancestral boxes. The key was kept on a high shelf in the library, partly hidden behind a brass candlestick. Nicholas had observed its position months ago. Observation, unlike obedience, was a habit he practised daily.

He waited until the aunt had again moved toward the gooseberry enclosure, then slipped into the library, climbed upon a chair, and secured the key. His departure from the room was conducted with equal discipline; he even replaced the chair at precisely its former angle. Criminal enterprises were so often betrayed by furniture.

The lock of the lumber-room yielded with a reluctant click, and Nicholas entered.

At first the chamber was only shadow and the smell of dust, leather, dried wood, and forgotten cloth. Then, as his eyes accepted the half-light from the narrow window, objects emerged from obscurity with the deliberation of creatures waking in a forest.

There were carved chairs whose arms ended in beasts’ heads; a screen painted with long-necked birds among reeds; candlesticks of tarnished silver and twisted brass; a squat bronze figure wearing a crown too large for him; boxes inlaid with mother-of-pearl; cracked bowls of blue porcelain; a helmet with no warrior; and a small ivory elephant carrying a tower upon its back. These treasures had been judged unsuitable for the rooms downstairs, where everything useful was plain, washable, and discouraging to the imagination.

Nicholas moved among them with reverence. Here was abundance without instruction, beauty without a moral attached to it. No one told him that the birds on the screen were educational, or that the elephant had been provided to improve his character. They existed splendidly for themselves.

But the greatest marvel hung upon the far wall.

It was a tapestry, faded in places yet rich with dark reds, smoky greens, and a blue that seemed deeper for having survived many years of neglect. A huntsman stood in a woodland clearing beside a stricken stag. His bow was lowered, and one hand rested upon the animal’s flank as though he had only just understood the consequence of his skill. Behind him, between the trunks of two trees, wolves were approaching.

Nicholas counted four at first; then a fifth grey head appeared from the woven undergrowth. The huntsman had not seen them. His dogs, if he possessed any, had deserted the scene. There was one arrow left in his quiver and perhaps another lying near the stag, though the threads were worn at that point and certainty was impossible.

Would the huntsman notice the wolves before they surrounded him? Could he climb the oak at his right? The lowest branch was high, but desperation has occasionally improved the jumping powers of men. Perhaps the stag was not dead and would rise at the decisive instant, confusing wolves and hunter alike. Or perhaps there was another huntsman outside the border of the picture, hurrying to the rescue through an unseen portion of the forest.

The matter required prolonged consideration.

From the garden came the aunt’s voice.

“Nicholas!”

He remained before the tapestry.

“Nicholas, where are you?”

The call moved nearer, then farther away. The aunt searched the shrubbery and peered behind the raspberry canes. Nicholas pictured her examining the earth for footprints and felt that the afternoon was proceeding favourably.

On a shelf beneath the tapestry stood a book with heavy clasps. It contained coloured pictures of extraordinary birds. Some had scarlet wings and black crests; one possessed a bill of such preposterous length that it seemed designed by an enemy of balance. Nicholas turned the pages carefully. A moral tale would have explained that the birds were vain, greedy, or disobedient. The book merely admitted that they were magnificent.

Time passed with unusual good conduct.

At length Nicholas decided that prudence required a temporary withdrawal. He locked the door, returned the key to its hiding place, and emerged into the garden regions with the air of one who had spent a dull interval reflecting upon sin.

The aunt was no longer at her post.

This was unexpected. Nicholas walked toward the gooseberry garden but stopped outside its gate, obediently preserving the frontier that had been established for him.

A faint cry came from within.

“Nicholas!”

He listened.

“Nicholas, come here at once!”

The voice rose from the direction of the rain-water tank, an old brick-lined reservoir which stood near the wall and was ordinarily covered. It had lately been emptied for cleaning. The aunt, while searching behind the bushes for her imaginary trespasser, had stepped upon the displaced cover and fallen into the tank.

“Nicholas,” she called again, “I want you to come into the garden immediately.”

“I was told not to enter the gooseberry garden,” he replied.

“That does not matter now. Come at once and help me out.”

Nicholas considered the invitation. “Your voice sounds like Aunt’s voice,” he said, “but Aunt told me I must not come into the garden. Perhaps you are the Evil One trying to persuade me to disobey.”

“Don’t talk nonsense. I have fallen into the rain-water tank.”

“That is what the Evil One would probably say.”

The aunt’s replies lost some of their theological dignity.

“I am your aunt, and I order you to come here.”

“Aunt said I was in disgrace and not to enter the garden under any circumstances. She said disobedient children listened to wicked suggestions.”

There was a pause, during which the prisoner appeared to revise her views on the value of consistent moral instruction.

“Nicholas, dear,” she said at last, with an abrupt sweetness that sat badly upon her voice, “if you fetch the small ladder from the shed and bring it here, I will give you strawberry jam for tea.”

Nicholas’s expression became grave.

“Now I know you cannot be Aunt,” he said. “Aunt told Cook this morning that there was no strawberry jam left.”

“There is some in the store cupboard.”

“That is strange, because Aunt said she looked there herself.”

“I did not—I mean, there may be another jar. Bring the ladder this instant!”

“The Evil One is known to make false promises,” said Nicholas. “And to contradict himself.”

He withdrew before the aunt could produce a reply suitable to her position. She continued to call, but the walls of an empty tank do little to improve either the authority or the temper of a voice.

Nicholas did not feel obliged to seek assistance. He had been explicitly forbidden to concern himself with the gooseberry garden, and it would have shown a regrettable looseness of principle to abandon the rule merely because its author had become inconveniently placed. Presently the kitchenmaid, coming to gather parsley, heard the cries and effected a rescue with the ladder.

The aunt emerged damp, scratched, and carrying bits of dead leaf upon her person. Her dignity had remained in the tank.

By then the seaside party had returned.

The excursion had not fulfilled its advertised programme. The tide, overlooked by the aunt when she planned the punitive expedition, had covered the sands during the useful portion of the afternoon. Shrimping had therefore been impossible. Bobby’s boots had grown steadily more agonising, and he had spent much of the outing complaining beside a bench. The girl-cousin’s injured knee had become painful, and Nicholas’s younger brother had spilled lemonade upon the picnic cake. A stiff wind had supplied the final wholesome element.

At tea, the participants sat in a silence which admitted no favourable interpretation.

The aunt’s hair was still damp at the edges, and a narrow scratch crossed one side of her face. Bobby pushed bread about his plate and occasionally moved his feet with extreme caution. The girl-cousin stared at the untouched jam dish—the store cupboard had, after all, yielded a jar—with the melancholy of one who has travelled far and been insufficiently rewarded.

Nicholas ate peacefully. No one asked what he had done during the afternoon; the aunt had reasons for avoiding the subject, and the others lacked energy.

In the official account, he remained the child in disgrace. Official accounts, however, are often composed by persons who have spent the day mislaying facts.

Nicholas’s thoughts had returned to the tapestry. The wolves were nearer now in his imagination, slipping soundlessly between the trees. The huntsman might yet notice them. There was the oak, the single arrow, the uncertain life of the stag, and perhaps—beyond the woven edge—a companion running through the forest with dogs at his heels.

Or perhaps no rescue was coming at all, and the hunter, having confidently pursued one creature, would discover too late that he had become another creature’s prey.

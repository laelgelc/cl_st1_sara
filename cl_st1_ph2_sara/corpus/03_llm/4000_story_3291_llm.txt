### The Admirals of Knoope

It was in the autumn of 1896, when I had gone into Gelderland in the unprofitable hope of finishing a novel, that I first came upon the house of the Van der Knoopes. The novel—my third, and the one which an indulgent public has since agreed to forget—had reached that melancholy stage when its author, having conducted his people into difficulties which no mortal ingenuity can resolve, begins to suspect that exercise and fresh air may accomplish what art has failed to do. Accordingly, on a mild September morning, I abandoned my manuscript at the inn and set out alone among the wooded hills.

The country was unfamiliar to me, and I had not thought it necessary to bring a map. For two hours this negligence merely enriched the excursion; after three it became inconvenient; and towards evening, when I descended a steep path made treacherous by beech leaves, it assumed the proportions of a calamity. My foot slipped between two roots, and I fell heavily.

The ankle was not broken, but it protested sharply against every attempt to stand. I sat awhile beneath the beeches, hearing acorns drop with an absurdly deliberate sound, and considered the dignity of passing the night in a ditch. Presently, through the trunks below me, I perceived a pair of stone gate-pillars. One still supported a weather-beaten shield; the other was surmounted by nothing more heraldic than a jackdaw.

Beyond them an avenue of Spanish chestnuts descended towards a house. The trees, though hollow in places and propped like old courtiers, retained a sombre magnificence; but the road between them had disappeared beneath grass, nettles and innumerable small mushrooms. I made my way downward with the aid of a fallen branch, hopping when pride permitted and crawling when it did not.

Half-way along the avenue I heard a voice declaiming:

“Now is the winter of our discontent, and the white hart leapt over the moon; whereupon Sir Lancelot, being third cousin to the King of Sweden, unfurled his banner argent, three puddings proper—”

A child in a green dress stepped from behind the bole of a chestnut. She wore no hat; her pale hair had been plaited into two ropes, one of which had partly escaped and hung over her shoulder. In her hand she carried a wooden sword. Three red-admiral butterflies were feeding upon the fallen fruit near her feet.

“Good evening,” I said.

She pointed the sword at me.

“Stand and declare your lineage.”

I gave her my name, which seemed to disappoint her.

“Have you no motto?”

“None that I can remember.”

“That is exceedingly careless. Ours is *Per tenebras fidelis*—faithful through darkness. Uncle Peter says it was granted after an ancestor rescued the Stadtholder from a Spanish cellar. Uncle Melchior says it was a Portuguese ship, but he confuses battles. Are you wounded?”

I admitted as much.

“Then you are either a pilgrim or a defeated king. You may lean upon me, though not heavily. What were you doing in our wood?”

“Seeking a road.”

“We have no road. It was sold.”

She said this with such composure that I did not immediately perceive the practical difficulty of selling a road. Before I could inquire, she had placed my arm across her shoulders and commanded me to advance.

The house disclosed itself by degrees. It was built of dark Jacobean brick, with Dutch gables, stone mullions and a roof of immense red tiles, many of which had slipped so that the surface resembled an old coat with rents at the elbows. Ivy concealed one wing almost completely. The shutters were cracked; several windows had been repaired with brown paper; and from a leaden gutter, detached at one end, a vigorous elder-tree had contrived to grow. Yet above the entrance, amid crowns, dolphins and cannon, the escutcheon of the Van der Knoopes remained distinct.

A dry moat encircled the house. We crossed it by a bridge whose balustrade had lost alternate urns, and entered a courtyard. Here a stone Triton leaned over an empty basin, but turf plugged his conch and snails had silvered his shoulders. A clock in the tower indicated twenty minutes before eleven. It continued to do so throughout my visit.

“My great-uncles are both Admirals,” said my guide. “Uncle Peter is blind, but knows everything. Uncle Melchior sees, but does not always understand it. I am Wilhelmina.”

“And are you mistress of the house?”

“On Tuesdays. At other times we cannot afford one.”

She pushed open the door and called into a cavernous hall:

“Uncle Melchior! I have captured an author.”

This was the first intimation that she knew my occupation. I afterward discovered that she had recognized my name from the advertisement of a Tauchnitz volume, although she had not read the book, owing, as she explained, to its being locked in a cabinet whose key had been lost in 1872.

The hall was noble in proportion and deplorable in condition. No carpet softened its black-and-white pavement; a strip of tapestry hung beside a damp stain shaped like Africa; and the antlers above the fireplace had shed several tines. On the walls, family portraits looked down through obscured varnish. A gentleman in a ruff had developed a long crack across his face; a seventeenth-century lady, attributed perhaps too hopefully to Van Dyck, was perishing in a frame of faded gilt. Everything spoke of distinction patiently yielding to weather, debt and time.

An old man emerged from a door beneath the staircase. He was very tall, thin as a folded umbrella, and dressed in a naval coat so ancient that its brass buttons had become green. Wisps of white hair stood out over his ears. He regarded me, Wilhelmina, my improvised crutch, and finally the muddy trail I had made upon the pavement.

“An author,” she repeated. “He has broken his leg in our territory.”

“Dear me,” said the old gentleman. “Which leg?”

“The left, I think,” I replied.

“Of course, of course. The left.” He seemed relieved by this intelligence. “And you are—?”

I told him.

“Just so. Captain van Ruyter.”

“No, Uncle Melchior. He writes novels.”

“Exactly. I have always said so.”

His name, as I soon learned, was Melchior van der Knoope, formerly a rear-admiral in the Dutch navy. Having examined my ankle with grave incompetence, he proposed sending to the village for a surgeon; but the only horse, Wilhelmina informed him, had gone to have a shoe removed, while the cart was serving as a roof to the hen-house. After some consultation I was installed in a high-backed chair and supplied with brandy from a silver-mounted bottle.

“My brother must be told,” said Melchior. “Peter dislikes surprises. They remind him of the bombardment of Algiers.”

“Was he at Algiers?” I asked.

“No; that was the surprise.”

We entered a long saloon at the back of the house. Tall windows overlooked a terrace, a broken balustrade and lawns falling away to a ha-ha. Roses, no longer trained, had flung themselves in disorder across the glass; Virginia creeper entered through a missing pane and trailed over a cabinet of Delft china. There were Dutch masters upon the walls—at least one genuine Cuyp and a Hobbema which deserved a kinder climate—but mildew had invaded their evening skies.

Beside the cold hearth sat another old man. He was broader than Melchior and more carefully dressed; his white head was erect, and one hand rested upon a stick topped by an ivory anchor. His eyes, open and of a clear grey, turned not towards us but slightly past us.

“Peter,” said Melchior, “a gentleman has arrived. He has injured his left novel.”

“Ankle, Uncle,” said Wilhelmina.

“Certainly. That is what I meant.”

The blind man rose and offered his hand with ceremonious courtesy.

“Admiral Peter van der Knoope, at your service. You must forgive my brother; he has spent his life reading accounts, and figures impair the vocabulary. Wilhelmina, is the gentleman presentable?”

“He has beech leaves in his hair, but they become him.”

“Then we shall not embarrass him by observation. You will dine with us, sir, and remain until you can walk. A Van der Knoope does not permit an injured stranger to crawl back to an inn, whatever modern finance may advise.”

Thus, without further debate, I became the guest of that singular household.

At dinner—served by an elderly woman named Trijntje, who combined the functions of cook, parlour-maid and hostile Providence—I learned something of the family. The Van der Knoopes had inhabited the estate since the reign of Maurice of Nassau, had provided the Republic with seven admirals, two ambassadors and a poet whose Latin verses were, Peter assured me, admired at Leiden. Their fortune had declined through war, litigation, agricultural experiments and the peculiar honesty of Melchior, who had resigned a profitable appointment because the government had overpaid him six guilders. The family now consisted of the brothers, Wilhelmina and a grandson of Peter’s named Frederik, or Fritz.

At the mention of Fritz the blind Admiral’s face changed. Pride warmed it, and the hand beside his plate trembled slightly.

“Our boy is with the Eastern Squadron,” he said. “A lieutenant still, but promotion cannot be delayed. His captain writes of him in the highest terms. Blood declares itself, sir. At sixteen he could distinguish every signal flag by moonlight.”

“At sixteen,” said Melchior, “yes. Or perhaps at fifteen.”

“Sixteen.”

“Of course. Sixteen.”

“He will marry Wilhelmina when he returns.”

Wilhelmina, seated opposite me, looked down at her plate. Until then she had enlivened the meal by explaining that the pike we ate had been descended from the monster in the Book of Job. Now she was silent.

“Is he long abroad?” I asked, for Peter appeared to expect some answer.

“Nearly four years. China, Java, the Levant—wherever duty calls. His letters are infrequent, but a sailor cannot command the post.”

“Nor the ink,” murmured Melchior.

Wilhelmina raised her head quickly.

“What did you say, Uncle?”

“The ink. Very bad in warm countries. It evaporates.”

Peter smiled. “Melchior believes the tropics to be a species of furnace. Fritz wrote last week. You shall hear his account after dinner; it will interest you.”

A small thing occurred then, so small that at the time I scarcely docketed it among my impressions. Wilhelmina’s fingers closed upon the stem of her wineglass, and Melchior, in reaching for the salt, upset it.

After dinner Peter directed her to fetch the letter. She left the room; and since Melchior immediately began an elaborate explanation of the estate revenues, I confess that I did not listen. I was looking through the window at two red admirals which fluttered over the terrace. Their wings, opening upon the grey air, displayed a colour almost insolent amid so much decay.

Wilhelmina returned with a sheet of paper. She handed it to Peter, who passed it to me.

“My eyes have ceased to avail me,” he said, “but Wilhelmina reads all Fritz’s letters aloud. This one describes a storm in the Indian Ocean.”

The handwriting was large, sloping and oddly ornamental. It reminded me of a convent school, though I could not at first have said why. The letter began, “My dearest Grandfather,” and proceeded to relate how the cruiser *Batavia* had encountered a typhoon near Ceylon; how Fritz had remained upon the bridge for thirty-six hours; how the mainmast had been “torn out by the roots”; and how, after saving three seamen and a Maltese cat, he had quoted Tennyson to the captain.

“The mainmast,” I observed, “is a formidable thing to root.”

“There was perhaps a flower-pot attached,” said Wilhelmina.

Melchior coughed.

I read on. The geography was adventurous, the nautical terms still more so. The *Batavia*, having rounded Cape Horn, appeared to reach Alexandria on the following afternoon. Yet the letter possessed a certain breathless charm; and beneath its absurdities there ran a strain of affection which made criticism ungenerous.

“An admirable spirit,” I said, returning the sheet.

“Read the conclusion,” said Peter.

I did so. Fritz sent respectful duty to both Admirals, promised to uphold the honour of the house, and assured Wilhelmina that he carried her likeness next his heart. At this she coloured, though whether from shame or sorrow I could not determine.

As I folded the letter I noticed the watermark: BARTON & SONS, KENT.

“English paper,” I said without thought.

The silence which followed was so complete that I heard the tower clock make an abortive click.

“Obtained at Malta,” said Wilhelmina promptly.

“Of course,” said Melchior. “Malta is almost entirely English paper.”

Peter inclined his head. “The Service carries a man into strange dependencies. Wilhelmina, put it with the others.”

Her eyes met mine for one instant. There was no childishness in them then. They contained apprehension, entreaty and something almost like defiance.

I slept badly in a room where the rain entered at one corner and a portrait of a dead burgomaster watched me from the tester of the bed. My ankle throbbed; shutters tapped intermittently; and somewhere within the house a door opened and shut as if a patient ghost were practising departure. Yet it was not these things that kept me awake. I was considering a letter written on English paper in a young woman’s hand, and the desperate alacrity with which Malta had been produced to explain it.

By morning the swelling had diminished. A village surgeon, conveyed in a wheelbarrow because the horse’s shoe remained unresolved, pronounced the injury a severe sprain and advised two days’ rest. After breakfast Wilhelmina appeared at the library door wearing a broad straw hat and carrying Peter’s ivory-headed stick.

“You are to walk,” she announced.

“The surgeon advised the contrary.”

“He is a republican. Besides, I have something to show you.”

She led me through a kitchen garden where artichokes grew among tombstones of cloches, across the ha-ha by a plank, and into a lane deep with hazel. She adjusted her pace to my limping progress but offered no assistance. Her manner had changed; the woodland sovereign of the previous evening had withdrawn, leaving a grave and rather pale young woman.

The family church stood half a mile from the house. It was a small Romanesque building of brown stone, with a crooked bell-cote and lichens bright as old embroidery upon the roof. The key lay beneath a gargoyle, a hiding place which Wilhelmina defended on the ground that no thief would expect Christians to be so obvious.

Inside, the air smelt of dust, damp hymn-books and the cold mineral breath peculiar to ancient churches. Van der Knoopes lay everywhere: carved upon brasses, kneeling in alabaster, painted upon hatchments whose heraldic beasts had faded into amiable monsters. A marble Admiral, complete with periwig and Roman sandals, reclined above a Latin inscription. Wilhelmina passed him without comment and took me behind the chancel screen.

There, half hidden by the drooping folds of a moth-eaten banner, was a newer tablet.

I read the name first:

**FREDERIK PETER VAN DER KNOOPE,  
MIDSHIPMAN OF H.M.S. ORANJE,  
BORN 3 MAY 1871,  
LOST AT SEA 18 OCTOBER 1889.**

Below it, in smaller letters, were the words: *Multis ille bonis flebilis occidit*.

“He drowned seven years ago,” Wilhelmina said.

The sentence fell quietly in the cold church. There was no thunder, no spectral stirring among the banners; only a fly moved upon the window, and outside a rook gave one coarse cry.

“The ship went down off Sumatra,” she continued. “There were four survivors. Fritz was not among them.”

“And Admiral Peter?”

“Does not know.”

I looked again at the tablet. Fritz had been eighteen. Wilhelmina could scarcely have been more than twenty now, though at moments she had seemed younger by ten years and at others older than any of us.

“He was ill when the news came,” she said. “His sight had just failed. The physician told Uncle Melchior that a shock might kill him. So they said the first report was mistaken; that Fritz had been taken aboard another vessel. Uncle Peter believed it. He wished to believe it.”

“And afterward?”

“Afterward there had to be letters.”

She sat upon the step below the altar rail and folded her hands over her knees.

“At first Uncle Melchior wrote them. They were very short. ‘Dear Grandfather, I am well. The sea is rough. Your affectionate Fritz.’ Uncle Peter complained that the boy had become dull. Then Trijntje tried, but she made Fritz ask twice for a recipe for pickled cabbage. Since I was fourteen, I have written most of them.”

“The Maltese cat was yours?”

“Yes. It was unwise.”

“It was memorable.”

“You must not laugh.”

“I beg your pardon.”

But she herself smiled faintly, and for an instant the church became less cold.

“We have an atlas,” she said, “but many of its pages are missing, and Uncle Melchior’s naval books are all about battles before steam. It is difficult to know where Fritz ought to be, or what he should do when he arrives there. Last year I sent him to Zanzibar, and Uncle Peter said his squadron was in the Baltic. We explained that the orders were secret.”

“Does no one visit who might tell him?”

“Very few people come now. Those who do are warned. The village knows. Even the Bishop knows, though he says deception is a grave sin. He always asks whether Uncle Peter’s heart remains weak before he condemns us.”

“And the marriage?”

Her smile vanished.

“That is the most difficult part. Uncle Peter believes Fritz delays it only until he is promoted. Every year he says, ‘Next spring, Mina.’ And I say, ‘Next spring, Uncle.’”

She rose and went to the tablet, touching the carved letters with one finger.

“Fritz and I were brought up together. We climbed the tower; we sailed boats in the fountain when it still had water. He said he would marry me, but we were children. Perhaps he would not have done so. That does not matter now.”

There are griefs which ask for eloquence, and others before which eloquence is indecent. I said nothing.

By-and-by she turned to me.

“You write stories.”

“Stories of a sort.”

“People believe them?”

“Not in great numbers.”

“But you know how men speak. You know ships, foreign countries, adventures—”

“My knowledge of ships would distress an able seaman.”

“It would satisfy Uncle Peter. You noticed the paper. You noticed the handwriting. One day he will notice something too, though he is blind.”

I understood then why she had brought me to the church.

“You wish me to write Fritz’s letters.”

“One each month. They need not be long. Uncle Peter tires easily now. But they must sound as if a man wrote them—and as if Fritz were happy.”

Behind her hung the decaying banner, its embroidered motto scarcely visible: *Per tenebras fidelis*. Faithful through darkness. It would have been easy to raise objections: that falsehood, once admitted as charity, may become a tyrant; that grief postponed is not grief escaped; that no man, least of all a professional inventor, should meddle in the conscience of a family. I considered several such truths, all excellent in a drawing-room and useless in that church.

“I shall write,” I said.

Her shoulders moved, not quite with a sigh. “Thank you.”

“On one condition.”

She looked alarmed.

“You must correct my naval errors.”

“I know nothing whatever of the navy.”

“Then we are admirably equipped.”

That afternoon Peter asked me to read aloud from one of Fritz’s earlier letters. It concerned a battle with Malay pirates, and included the statement that the young officer had fired a torpedo across the enemy’s bows “as Nelson did at Trafalgar.” I read it without alteration. The old man listened with his head inclined and a smile upon his face.

“A touch of the old blood,” he said when I had finished. “He writes plainly, does he not?”

“With remarkable directness.”

“Mina helps him with his style, perhaps. I detect her fancy here and there.”

Wilhelmina, standing at the window, became very still.

“But the heart is his,” Peter continued. “The heart is unmistakable.”

“Yes,” I said. “That is unmistakable.”

When I returned to England I wrote the first letter. Fritz was then at Nagasaki, where he had attended a reception at which the Japanese Admiral inquired respectfully after the naval traditions of the Van der Knoopes. I kept the weather moderate, avoided masts altogether, and permitted no animal more exotic than a ship’s terrier. The letter ran to four pages. Before posting it I copied Wilhelmina’s final formula: “Until I return, believe me always the devoted guardian of our name and of Mina.”

A week later her answer came.

“Uncle Peter says Fritz has at last learned to write like an officer. Uncle Melchior says Nagasaki is probably in China, but we have hidden the atlas. Please send him somewhere warm next month; the east wind makes Uncle Peter cough.”

And so my secondary career began.

In time I found that writing as Fritz afforded a freedom denied to my published labours. The critics could not reach him; his single reader received every sentence with love; and the world, being imaginary, remained obedient. He cruised among the Philippines, surveyed an island in the Celebes, rescued a Danish botanist from a reef, and was promoted after displaying exemplary coolness during a boiler fire. I studied charts and naval annuals. I learned the difference between port and starboard, and discovered, with some embarrassment, that ships possess neither roots nor drawing-room windows.

Every month I sent the letter to Wilhelmina, who recopied it in a bolder hand. Occasionally she requested amendments. Fritz must mention the birthday of his grandmother; Fritz must not praise the French fleet; Fritz must explain why he could not come home that Christmas. Melchior appended postscripts in which facts struggled against ink blots. Peter dictated messages full of advice on gunnery, honour and the choice of a wife.

I visited Knoope twice in the next three years. The house had sunk further into its green captivity. A winter storm had removed the roof of the orangery; the Cuyp had been sold; three more urns had vanished from the bridge. Yet Peter remained in his chair by the cold hearth, commanding oceans he could not see. He greeted me kindly and spoke always of Fritz.

“Another year,” he said on my final visit, “and the boy will come home. Mina has waited faithfully.”

Wilhelmina was then unmistakably a woman, though she still carried the wooden sword when she walked among the chestnuts. I asked her once whether the waiting oppressed her.

“We are all waiting for something,” she answered. “Uncle Peter waits for Fritz; Uncle Melchior waits for the accounts to balance; Trijntje waits for the roof to fall upon us. I wait for the post.”

Peter died in the spring of 1901, peacefully, after hearing for the third time a letter in which Fritz had been commended by the Queen. Wilhelmina wrote that his hand rested upon the ivory anchor and that, at the last, he appeared to recognize someone entering the room.

“No more letters are needed,” she concluded. “You have been very good to us.”

But I found that I could not dismiss Fritz.

For nearly five years I had known the disposition of his captain, the weaknesses of his engines, the blue islands through which he sailed. I had advanced his career, chosen his friends and preserved his honour. Often, when my own work went badly, I took out the charts and considered where he might be. I imagined him at Singapore under awnings, at Yokohama beneath the rain, or standing upon a moonlit deck while the wake streamed white behind him. The drowned boy had become, through our falsehood, curiously alive; and perhaps all characters loved by their authors are ghosts permitted a little longer in the world.

I wrote one final letter, though there was no blind old Admiral to hear it. Fritz had received orders for home.

Wilhelmina never acknowledged that letter. The estate passed, I believe, to a distant cousin, and the greater part of the house was afterward pulled down. Melchior survived his brother by two years, still seeking the six guilders which the government had long since forgiven. Of Wilhelmina I heard only uncertain reports: that she had gone to Java as a governess; that she had married a schoolmaster in Utrecht; that she remained among the ruins with Trijntje and a roof increasingly open to the stars.

I prefer the last account, though it may be the least true.

Sometimes, on September afternoons, when red admirals settle upon the ivy outside my window, I remember the grassy avenue and the clock that never advanced. Country people used to say that butterflies housed the souls of the departed; and if this be so, the Van der Knoopes, with their seven Admirals and their devotion to impossible returns, should have filled the chestnut grove with scarlet wings.

Then I see Wilhelmina as she first appeared, armed with her wooden sword among the fallen leaves, guarding a kingdom whose road had been sold; and I think of Fritz coming home at last, not across any earthly sea, but through the faithful darkness in which she had kept a light for him.

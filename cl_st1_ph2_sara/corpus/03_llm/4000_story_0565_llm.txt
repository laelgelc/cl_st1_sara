## The Beetle’s Well

Not many years ago there lived, on a stony New England hillside, a farmer named Amos Pike and his good wife, Martha. They were as honest a pair as ever planted corn, paid taxes, or sat through a long sermon without falling asleep; but honesty had not made them rich, nor had hard work persuaded their land to be generous.

Their farm lay upon the slope of a hill where the wind had blown away most of the earth and left the stones behind. Stones were in the pasture, stones were in the garden, and stones appeared beneath the plough as fast as Amos could pry them out. He built a wall with them, and then another wall, and still there were stones enough for three farms.

The house was small and gray. The barn leaned a little toward the east, as if tired of standing upright, and the roof let in rain at one end. Their cow gave thin milk, their hens had a habit of laying in places where no Christian could find the eggs, and the apple trees bore chiefly worms and leaves. Nevertheless, Amos worked from dawn until dark, and Martha worked before dawn and after dark, and between them they contrived to have scarce anything.

The worst trouble was water.

At the foot of the hill, half a mile from the house, ran a cold brook over brown stones. Every morning Martha took two wooden pails and went down the steep path to fill them. Then she climbed home again, resting three times on the way. In summer the sun beat upon her back, in autumn the mud pulled at her shoes, and in winter the path was so slippery that she sometimes sat down without meaning to.

“We ought to have a well,” she would say.

“We ought,” Amos would answer.

“Can we dig one?”

“We can dig,” said Amos. “Whether we can find water is another matter.”

They had once paid a man with a forked hazel twig to tell them where water lay. He walked solemnly over the yard until the twig pointed down beside the wood-box. Amos dug there for six days and found three horseshoes, a broken bottle, and a stone large enough to have a name, but no water.

After that, Martha continued to go to the brook.

One July morning she was coming up the hill with both pails full when she saw a black beetle lying upon its back on a flat stone. The beetle kicked all its six legs in the air, but the stone was smooth and it could get no hold.

“Poor creature,” said Martha. “It works hard and gets nowhere, which is a thing I understand.”

She set down the pails, though they were heavy and though every delay made the climb seem longer, and turned the beetle over with a twig. It stood still a moment, polished and round in the sunshine. Then it spread its wings and flew away.

Martha thought no more of it.

A week afterward, when she was returning from the brook, she came again to the flat stone. There lay the same beetle—or one so like it that no one but another beetle could have told the difference—upon its back, kicking more desperately than before.

“La sakes!” cried Martha. “Haven’t you learned any better?”

She put down her pails and turned it over. This time the beetle did not fly away. Instead, it walked to the highest part of the stone, cleared its throat in a surprisingly distinct fashion, and said:

“Madam, I am deeply obliged to you.”

Martha dropped the twig.

She did not drop the pails, for they were already on the ground.

“Did you speak?” she asked.

“I did.”

“Beetles don’t speak.”

“Most do not speak to human beings,” replied the beetle. “That is because most human beings have nothing to say which interests us. But this is a special occasion.”

Martha bent nearer. The beetle’s black shell shone like a gentleman’s best boots, and its feelers moved gravely as it spoke.

“I am not a common beetle,” it continued. “I am the king of all the insects in this countryside.”

“The king!” said Martha.

“The king,” replied the beetle. “Ants, bees, moths, grasshoppers, crickets, and beetles of every proper sort owe me allegiance. Flies are less obedient, and mosquitoes acknowledge no government when hungry, but that cannot be helped.”

Martha looked around, half expecting to see a court of caterpillars behind the stone.

“You don’t look much like a king,” she said.

“A crown would be inconvenient underground,” answered the beetle. “Royalty is a matter of office, not size.”

This seemed reasonable, or at least as reasonable as anything spoken by a beetle could be.

“You have twice assisted me when you might have gone on about your business,” said the king. “Kindness should not be left unpaid. Tell your husband to dig a well in the yard, twelve feet from your kitchen door and directly in line with the old elm.”

“We stood in need of a well these ten years,” said Martha, “but the ground is all stone.”

“Then he must dig through stone.”

“Will he find water?”

“You will find what is appointed,” said the beetle.

“That isn’t the same as water.”

“It is all the answer I shall give today. Good morning, Madam Pike.”

He opened his wings and rose with a buzz, leaving Martha on the path with two pails, a hot sun, and more questions than she had started with.

When Amos came in at noon, she set before him salt pork, boiled turnips, and the story of the insect king.

Amos listened without interrupting. He was a slow man in speech, and he believed that words, like seed corn, ought not to be scattered carelessly.

“You were tired,” he said at last.

“I was no more tired than usual.”

“The sun was hot.”

“It has been hotter.”

“Perhaps you fell and struck your head.”

“I did not.”

Amos ate another turnip.

“What exactly did the beetle say?”

Martha repeated every word, including the part about flies and mosquitoes.

“That sounds sensible,” Amos admitted. “Especially about mosquitoes.”

After dinner he measured twelve feet from the kitchen door and stretched a line toward the old elm. Then he fetched his pick and shovel.

He dug, and dug, and dug.

The first foot was hard earth. The second was gravel. The third was stones. Beneath those were larger stones, and beneath the larger stones lay a shelf of rock which Amos broke with a heavy iron bar. For seven days he dug, and Martha drew up the dirt in a bucket. At twelve feet there was no water. At fifteen feet there was no water. At twenty feet the bottom was as dry as the parson’s longest sermon.

“I knew it,” said Amos, sitting on the edge of the hole. “We have dug a well without water.”

“Perhaps we must go deeper,” said Martha.

So Amos dug, and dug, and dug, until at last the well was thirty feet deep and as dry as the flour barrel on the day before milling.

That evening the beetle appeared upon the kitchen table.

Martha was mending Amos’s shirt, and Amos was drinking weak tea from a cracked cup. The beetle walked past the sugar bowl, though there was no sugar in it, and stopped beside the cracked teapot.

“Well?” he asked.

“There’s no water,” said Amos.

“I did not promise water.”

“What good is a dry well?”

“That depends upon what is put into it. You must buy a pump.”

“A pump costs money.”

“So does most useful machinery.”

“We’ve only four dollars and eleven cents,” said Martha.

“Buy a pump worth four dollars,” said the beetle, “and keep the eleven cents. A prudent household should never spend its last coin.”

With that he flew out through the open window.

The next morning Amos walked six miles to the village. He bought an old iron pump from a dealer in farm tools, who assured him that it had belonged to a widow and had therefore received gentle usage. In truth it was rusty, stiff, and nearly as old as the widow, but it cost exactly four dollars.

Amos hauled it home in a borrowed cart and set it over the dry well. Martha watched with her hands folded in her apron.

“Try it,” she said.

“You try it. It was your beetle.”

Martha took hold of the iron handle and pushed it down. The pump groaned, the pipe rattled, and something clinked in the spout.

Out rolled a gold coin.

It struck the kitchen step, spun once in the sunlight, and lay still.

Neither Amos nor Martha moved.

“Pump again,” whispered Amos.

Martha raised and lowered the handle. The pump groaned once more, and out came another gold coin, and another, and another. They fell ringing upon the step, bright as little suns.

Amos picked one up and bit it.

“What does it taste like?” asked Martha.

“Metal.”

“That proves little.”

They pumped until twenty gold pieces lay in Martha’s apron. Then Amos made her stop.

“There is something wrong,” he said. “Gold doesn’t come out of dry wells.”

“Water didn’t come out,” said Martha, who thought this worth mentioning.

“It may be stolen money.”

“From whom?”

“That is what troubles me.”

“Perhaps it is fairy gold,” Martha said uneasily. “I’ve heard fairy gold turns to leaves before morning.”

They put the coins in a bowl upon the table and stayed awake as long as they could, watching them. Near midnight Amos fell asleep in his chair. Martha lasted until one o’clock. In the morning the coins were still gold, and not so much as one had turned into a leaf.

Nevertheless, Amos would not spend them.

“We must ask the parson,” he said. “He has read more books than we have.”

They wrapped one coin in a red handkerchief and carried it to the Reverend Mr. Bell, who examined it through his spectacles.

“It appears to be genuine,” said the parson.

“It came out of our pump,” Martha told him.

“Your water pump?”

“Our dry-well pump.”

The parson removed his spectacles, wiped them, and put them on again, but the coin did not improve in its behavior.

“Gold cannot properly come from a pump,” he said.

“So Amos says,” replied Martha.

“Then it must have come from somewhere else before it came from the pump. If it belongs to another person, keeping it would be theft. If it belongs to no person and was made by enchantment, accepting it may encourage dealings of an uncertain moral nature.”

“Could we give it to the church?” asked Amos.

Mr. Bell considered this. He was a conscientious man, and the church roof required shingles.

“I could not knowingly accept stolen property,” he said. “Nor, I think, enchanted property, unless the enchantment were first shown to be respectable.”

The couple went home more troubled than before.

That evening the beetle found them sitting beside the untouched bowl of coins.

“You look,” he observed, “as if poverty had returned before wealth had arrived.”

Amos told him what the parson had said.

“The reverend gentleman’s caution does him credit,” replied the beetle. “But his reasoning is too narrow. We insects travel everywhere. Ants dig beneath doorsteps and along roadsides. Bees visit gardens and fields. Beetles crawl under floorboards. Crickets live near hearths, and moths enter closets, though I have repeatedly discouraged the practice. For generations my subjects have found coins dropped in lanes, lost at fairs, buried by misers, forgotten beneath trees, and hidden by persons who died without revealing the place.”

“Then it does belong to other people!” cried Martha.

“It did,” said the beetle. “But a coin lost beyond recovery has left its former service. We have never taken a penny from a pocket, a till, or an occupied purse. What could be returned was returned when possible. What could not be returned was gathered in the earth beneath your hill. It belongs to no living claimant. By the law of the insect kingdom, which is older than the village by-laws and considerably shorter, I give it to you.”

“Why us?” asked Amos.

“Because your wife twice stopped upon a hard climb to aid one weaker than herself.”

Martha looked pleased, though she tried not to.

“May we spend it honestly?” Amos asked.

“You may.”

“Will more come?”

“So long as the gift is used with sense and accepted with humility.”

“We’ve had little practice with riches,” said Martha.

“Then begin modestly,” advised the beetle. “A full purse makes a loud fool of a quiet person if it is shaken too often.”

He departed, and for several days the Pikes behaved with admirable good sense.

They paid what they owed at the mill. They bought a sound cow, six hens that laid where hens ought to lay, shingles for the barn, flour, coffee, and a new pair of boots for Amos. Martha purchased two stout pails and a blue wool dress of plain quality. They gave five gold pieces to Mr. Bell for the church roof after the beetle’s explanation had satisfied him, though he said a special prayer over the coins before using them.

They also gave food to an old widow who lived beyond the meetinghouse, and Amos quietly paid the doctor’s account of a neighbor whose child had been ill.

Had they continued in this fashion, they might have been comfortable all their days.

But gold came too easily from the pump.

Every morning Amos worked the handle, and the coins came out—one, and another, and another. At first he stopped after ten. Then he stopped after twenty. Soon he filled a wooden box and hid it beneath the bed.

Martha bought a second dress, finer than the first. Then she bought a green silk bonnet with three yellow feathers, though green did not become her and the feathers made her appear to be pursued by a small, alarmed bird.

“No harm in looking decent,” she said.

“None at all,” agreed Amos, who had purchased a silver watch and checked it every few minutes, though farm work went by the sun as it always had.

They returned to town the next week in a new yellow wagon drawn by a glossy bay horse. Amos wore a broad hat tipped backward upon his head and smoked a cigar nearly as thick as the handle of his hoe. He had never smoked a cigar before, and it made him pale, but he would not admit it.

Martha entered the largest shop and asked to see the finest dresses.

The shopkeeper brought out silk, satin, lace, ribbons, and gloves. Martha chose a purple dress with so many folds that it could nearly stand by itself.

“Send it to the wagon,” she said grandly.

“Certainly, Mrs. Pike. And how shall it be paid for?”

“With gold, naturally.”

She laid three bright coins upon the counter. At once every customer in the shop turned to look. The shopkeeper bowed so low that his chin nearly met the purple silk.

Amos bought a red waistcoat, a gold-headed cane, and boots too narrow for him. He paid for each article by drawing coins from a leather purse and letting them chink loudly upon the counter. By afternoon he had told the blacksmith, the tavern keeper, two store clerks, and a man he had never seen before that his well produced gold.

“Not water?” they asked.

“Gold,” said Amos, tilting his hat. “We’ve no use for common wells now.”

Martha told the same story to the dressmaker, the baker’s wife, the apothecary’s sister, and three women standing outside the meetinghouse.

“It comes out by the handful,” she said. “Why, we scarce know where to put it.”

This was not true. They knew perfectly well where they put it: in the wooden box beneath the bed. But pride often begins by enlarging a fact and ends by forgetting where the fact stopped.

When they drove home, a procession followed them. Children ran behind the wagon, neighbors walked after the children, and idle men came after the neighbors. Even the shopkeeper followed partway, tenderly carrying the purple dress because Martha had forgotten to take it.

The whole company streamed up the hill like the tail of a comet.

At the farm Amos demonstrated the pump.

He pushed the handle down, and a gold piece dropped from the spout. The crowd gasped.

He pumped again. Another coin appeared.

“Would you look at that!” cried someone.

“Do it again!” shouted the children.

Amos pumped until fifteen coins glittered on the ground. Martha gathered them slowly, making sure everyone saw.

“We can have as many as we please,” she said.

At that moment a black beetle sat unnoticed upon the stone wall. It watched Amos bow to the crowd and Martha strut across the yard like a peacock in purple silk. Then it flew away.

Among those who had followed the wagon were two men named Cale and Rudge. They possessed strong backs, quick hands, and a settled objection to labor. When they saw the gold, they looked at one another. When Martha mentioned that the house was to be left empty on Thursday while she and Amos attended a cattle show, they looked at one another again.

On Thursday morning the Pikes dressed in their finest clothes and drove to the show. Amos’s boots hurt him, his cigar made him sick, and Martha’s bonnet feathers nodded into her eyes whenever the horse trotted; nevertheless, both believed they were enjoying themselves.

At the show they bought a spotted calf they did not need, a brass lamp too large for their table, six china cups too delicate to use, a painted clock, and a mechanical bird which whistled when wound. Martha ordered an even finer dress and told the seamstress that money was no consideration. Amos treated every man near the tavern to dinner and said he expected soon to build the largest house in the county.

Meanwhile Cale and Rudge climbed the hill.

They entered the Pikes’ house through the pantry window. They emptied drawers, opened cupboards, shook flour from jars, and pulled the bedstead apart. At last they found the wooden box.

It was too heavy to carry, so they poured the gold into two meal sacks. Then they searched for more. They took Martha’s brooch, Amos’s silver watch, the church receipt, and eleven cents from a cup on the mantel. They even worked the pump, but nothing came out.

Being in a great hurry, they did not put the house in order before leaving.

The Pikes returned after dark, with the spotted calf tied behind the wagon and the painted clock balanced between them. The pantry window stood open. The door hung crooked. Feathers from the mattress drifted across the floor.

Amos ran to the bedroom.

“The box!” he cried.

“Is it safe?” called Martha.

“It’s gone!”

She hurried in, tripped over a bed slat, and sat down among the feathers. For a moment they stared at one another.

“The pump,” Martha whispered.

They rushed outside. Amos seized the handle and drove it down.

The pump groaned, but no coin appeared.

He pumped again, and again, and again. Dust came from the spout. A dead leaf fluttered out. Then nothing.

Martha took the handle and worked it until her arms shook.

“Another!” she cried, as if the pump had merely failed to hear. “Give us another!”

But the well remained empty.

The next morning the news was everywhere. Those who had admired the Pikes now laughed at them. People came up the hill to see the broken house and the useless pump. Some said the gold had never existed. Others said it had been fairy money and had flown away. The shopkeeper demanded payment for the purple dress, having forgotten that he had already received it, and the man who sold Amos the spotted calf asked whether he should take the creature back before it vanished too.

At church on Sunday, Amos wore his old coat, for the thieves had taken the red waistcoat. Martha wore the blue wool dress, as the purple silk had been torn during the search. Two boys behind them whispered, “Pump us a penny, Mr. Pike,” and were sharply corrected by the parson.

Humiliation is heavier than a water pail, because a pail may be set down.

For a week Amos scarcely left the house. Martha removed the yellow feathers from her bonnet and burned them. Their new horse had to be sold, as they had no money for feed, and the yellow wagon went with it. The spotted calf was returned at a loss. The china cups, brass lamp, and painted clock were carried back to town, where each fetched less than half its price.

At last Martha went to the brook with her old pails. On the way home she came to the flat stone.

The beetle king sat upon it.

Martha set down the pails.

“I suppose you’ve come to scold,” she said.

“No,” replied the beetle. “You have been scolded by events, which are generally louder and less courteous than I am.”

“Did you take the gold away?”

“I did not. You advertised it to the countryside, and thieves took it.”

“But the pump gives no more.”

“The gift was to continue while used with sense and humility.”

“We helped people at first.”

“You did.”

“We paid our debts.”

“You did.”

“Then we grew foolish.”

“You did,” said the beetle, and his calm agreement was harder to bear than blame.

Amos, who had followed his wife at a distance, now came up the path.

“Can it be restored?” he asked. “Only a little. Enough to mend the house and start again.”

The beetle turned toward him.

“You already possess enough to start again.”

Amos looked at his empty hands.

“I don’t see it.”

“You have strength to dig. Your wife has strength to climb. You know how to plant, mend, milk, bake, and save. No thief carried those away in a meal sack.”

“That is slow wealth,” said Amos.

“It is usually the safest kind.”

Martha looked up the hill at the gray house and the leaning barn.

“Will the well never give gold again?”

“Never to you,” replied the beetle. “Fortune is a traveler. It visits one house, then another, and stays nowhere merely because the chairs are fine. You received it kindly at first, but afterward you dragged it into the road and shouted for everyone to admire it.”

Amos hung his head.

“What should we have done?”

“Taken what you stood in need of. Repaired your farm. Helped where help was useful. Saved without boasting. And when enough was enough, you should have left the pump handle still.”

“Enough is hard to know,” said Martha.

“It becomes harder with every extra coin,” said the beetle. “That is why wisdom must decide before appetite begins counting.”

He spread his wings, but Amos stopped him.

“Your Majesty,” he said, a little awkwardly, “we are sorry.”

“That is a beginning.”

“And thankful,” Martha added, “even though it ended badly.”

“That is a better beginning.”

The beetle flew from the stone and vanished over the wall.

Amos picked up one pail, and Martha carried the other. Together they climbed the hill.

They went back to honest labor. Amos repaired the pantry window with his own hands and straightened the barn as well as he could. Martha planted beans beside the kitchen and sold butter in the village. They never became rich, but their cow prospered, their hens improved their habits, and the land, though still stony, yielded somewhat more when pride no longer prevented them from seeing what it required.

The dry well remained beside the house. Amos removed the pump handle and hung it over the kitchen door, not as an ornament, but as a reminder.

Whenever visitors asked why it was there, Martha said, “To show that what comes up easily may go away easier.”

And Amos would add, “But what a man earns honestly stays in his hands, if not always in his purse.”

Thus they learned that kindness may invite good fortune, but kindness alone will not teach a person how to keep it. Wealth is not wicked, nor is comfort a sin; yet gold loudly displayed calls envy from the road, and abundance without moderation soon becomes another form of poverty. Good gifts should therefore be received with gratitude, used with judgment, and spoken of humbly—for fortune has wings, thieves have ears, and enough is worth more than too much.

### The Road to San Felice

Of course the train had gone.

There was the empty platform, blazing under the white clock, and beyond it the two rails running away together with a glitter that was almost insolent. A scrap of newspaper lifted, turned over, and lay down again. Even the stationmaster, buttoning his green coat as he came, appeared to Mrs. Vale to have arranged his face beforehand in expectation of their failure.

“Gone?” she cried. “But it cannot be gone. We were told eleven-forty.”

“Eleven-fourteen, signora.”

“Eleven-fourteen!” She turned upon her husband. “Edward, you distinctly said eleven-forty.”

“I must have mistaken it.”

“Mistaken it! After I asked you three times? After I said, ‘Do make certain, Edward, because nothing is more hideous than being stranded at one of these little places’? And you assured me—quite calmly, quite finally—that there was no hurry.”

“I’m sorry.”

Sorry! He stood there holding the rugs, her dressing case and his own ridiculous guidebook, his collar wilted, his pale straw hat powdered at the brim. Sorry was apparently sufficient. Sorry restored the train, carried the luggage, reserved their corner seats, and prevented the entire afternoon from being ruined.

Mrs. Vale pressed a scented handkerchief to her lips.

“What are we to do?”

The stationmaster explained that if they drove to San Felice they might catch the evening express. A carriage could be found. It was not far. Three hours, perhaps less, according to the road.

“Three hours!” She gave a little laugh. “In this heat?”

The stationmaster smiled, exposing a tiny gold tooth. He thought the road very beautiful.

“Beautiful,” murmured Mrs. Vale. “They always say that.”

The carriage arrived after half an hour. It was yellow, though the dust had made a serious attempt to turn it grey, and the driver had a red scarf, a cracked whip and two purple boils at the nape of his neck. He greeted them with immense good humour and flung their luggage beneath the seat.

“Not the dressing case!” cried Mrs. Vale. “Edward! Must I attend to everything?”

Edward rescued it. They climbed in. The driver shouted, the little horse shook its bells, and the station, the clock and the smiling gold tooth swung away behind them.

For the first mile Mrs. Vale said nothing. She sat very upright beneath her blue parasol, breathing through her handkerchief. The road was bordered by low vines and dusty cactus. Among the stones, small pink flowers had opened their mouths to the heat.

Then a cart passed them.

The dust rose softly, magnificently, and entered the carriage.

“This is unbearable.” She shut her eyes. “I knew it would be like this. I said, didn’t I, that we ought to remain at the station? But no—you allowed that man to bundle us into this filthy conveyance.”

“I thought you wished to catch the express.”

“That is no reason for suffocating me.”

“No.”

“No? What does ‘no’ mean? Really, Edward, if you could hear the tone in which you speak to me!”

He brushed the dust from his sleeve. It was extraordinarily fine dust. It lay upon his dark coat like ash.

The road climbed between stone walls. Broken jars had been set upside down along one wall, and scarlet geraniums spilled from them in thick, hot clusters. At a bend three children darted from a doorway. They ran beside the carriage, bare feet flashing, hands extended, laughing and calling.

“How dreadful,” said Mrs. Vale. “Tell them to go away.”

The driver laughed back at them. One child, a solemn little girl with black hair cut squarely across her forehead, held up a bunch of crushed yellow flowers.

“She wants you to take them,” Edward said.

“I can see what she wants. I do not require an interpreter.”

He found a coin and tossed it. At once there were six children, then nine, springing out from walls, descending from banks, shrieking, tumbling, their hands bright in the sunlight.

“There! Now see what you have done. Hundreds and hundreds of them! Edward, stop them! That boy touched my skirt.”

The flowers landed in her lap. She seized them by the stems and flung them into the road.

“Poor little things,” said Edward.

Mrs. Vale slowly turned her head.

“Poor little things?”

“I meant nothing.”

“No, you never do. That is precisely the cruelty of it. You drift through life meaning nothing, while somebody else suffers all the consequences.”

The driver cracked his whip. The children fell back, still calling. The solemn girl remained alone in the road, looking at the discarded flowers.

Edward put a cigarette between his lips.

“Oh, must you?”

He took it out.

“No, no—smoke. By all means smoke. You know tobacco gives me one of my heads, but that is a small consideration. If I faint in this dreadful sun, perhaps they will put me under one of those bushes while you finish your cigarette.”

He returned it to the case.

The carriage dropped suddenly into a hollow. Mrs. Vale was thrown against him and immediately away.

“Does that man intend to kill us?”

“It is a bad road.”

“A bad road! I had noticed. Ask him to drive carefully.”

Edward leaned forward. “Piano,” he called.

The driver looked back, beaming. The two boils shone like plums. He shouted something cheerful and drove faster.

“Excellent,” said Mrs. Vale. “You have encouraged him.”

She opened her handbag. From its dove-coloured interior she took a little mirror, a powder leaf, a silver tube, two envelopes, a packet of cigarettes, a flask of scent and three ivory tablets scored neatly down the middle. She considered the tablets, selected one, and swallowed it without water.

Edward watched the objects return one by one. They seemed complete, ceremonial. He imagined them arranged about her in some cool marble tomb: the mirror at her right hand, the scent at her left, the tablets prepared for whatever headache eternity might provide.

“What are you staring at?”

“Nothing.”

“Again nothing.”

She powdered her nose. The horse’s bells rang frantically up the hill.

Beyond the vines, the sea appeared and disappeared—a blue blade, then a shield, then a whole trembling field of light. Edward saw a fishing boat motionless far below. Its sail was the pale brown of an old envelope.

Mrs. Vale lowered the parasol to shield her cheek.

“It is extraordinary that you never consider which side the sun will be on.”

“I can change places with you.”

“And crush my dress while we clamber about? No, thank you.”

The wheel struck a stone. The carriage tilted, righted itself, and went on. There came a small flutter behind them, so light that only Edward looked back. Something blue lay open upon the road.

“Your parasol.”

The carriage stopped after much calling and confusion. The blue speck was already far behind, trembling in the heat.

Mrs. Vale stared at Edward.

“You saw it fall?”

“Yes.”

“And you did not catch it?”

“I couldn’t reach it.”

“My blue parasol—the one from Lyon! You know it cannot be replaced.”

“We’ll turn back.”

“Turn this carriage on a mountain road? Do you want us overturned as well?”

“I’ll fetch it.”

“You would take half an hour. No. I shall go myself.”

She stepped down before he could help her. For a moment she stood beside the wheel, small, elegant and furious, the scented handkerchief clenched in one gloved hand.

“Remain here,” she said. “At least I shall have five minutes away from you. If I do not, I believe I shall go mad.”

She walked back along the road, her cream dress narrowing into the brilliance. The driver climbed down to examine a buckle. He hummed, spat upon the leather, and wandered with the horse towards a patch of shade.

Edward remained in the carriage.

Nothing happened.

The road lay empty between the walls. Dust settled upon the leaves, upon the stones, upon his hands. It seemed to him that he, too, was settling—grain by grain, all weight leaving him. Inside his coat there was no Edward Vale, no husband, no patient answerer. Only a faint human outline filled with grey ash.

A breeze moved over the hill.

It touched the geraniums, turned the vine leaves to their silver undersides, travelled through the dry grass and was gone. Far below, the sea drew a thin white seam along the rocks. It gathered itself, loosened, gathered again.

Then he saw the tree.

It stood beyond the wall in a hollow he had not noticed, an immense plane tree with a trunk divided like two columns and a crown so wide that a house might have slept beneath it. The leaves were dark at the centre, bright at their edges. Here and there the sun entered them and vanished. The whole tree appeared at once perfectly still and full of secret movement, as if innumerable green hands were passing a blessing from one to another.

Edward looked into it.

How long had it stood there? It knew nothing of trains. Nothing of headaches, timetables, ruined afternoons, or the unforgivable side from which the sun happened to shine. It did not accuse the road of dust. It received the dust, the wind, the fierce light; it held them all and remained vast.

From somewhere beyond the tree a woman began to sing.

He could not see her. Perhaps she was working in a garden, perhaps standing at an open window. The first notes were low and wandering. They floated up uncertainly, found the air, and opened. The voice rose, rested upon one clear note, then fell so gently that the silence seemed to bend after it.

Edward closed his eyes.

The song returned. It moved through the leaves and over the wall. It was neither sad nor joyful; it asked nothing. For one suspended instant it seemed addressed to that hollow place within him, and the emptiness answered. Peace entered without haste. It filled his chest, his throat, even his tired hands.

He did not wish to move again.

The driver’s horse stirred its bells. Far down the road a blue shape approached, dipping angrily with every step.

Yet the peace remained. It had been given to him; no one had seen.

That night, on the express, Mrs. Vale sat opposite a large Englishwoman in jet beads and related the history of the missed train.

“My husband, unfortunately, has no notion of time. None. I said to him at breakfast, ‘Edward, are you quite certain?’ But men so dislike being questioned, don’t they? And then that ghastly drive—miles and miles through dust, with a driver covered in disease. I was forced to walk half a mile in the sun because Edward let my parasol fall.”

“How very trying,” said the Englishwoman.

“Trying? My dear, I was beyond speech.”

Edward stood in the corridor outside the compartment. The train roared through the dark, and the window gave him back his faint reflection: hat, moustache, hollow cheeks, one hand resting on the brass rail.

Inside, his wife’s voice rose and fell.

He might open the door. There was his corner seat, his rug, the silver flask she would presently ask him to find. There was the Englishwoman waiting to hear how bravely Mrs. Vale had endured him.

But beyond the glass the darkness streamed, immense and soft. A distant farmhouse showed one lamp, then disappeared. He remembered the tree holding sunlight in its leaves. He heard again the hidden voice floating across the wall, rising, resting, falling away.

The train rushed on. The brass rail trembled under his hand.

He remained where he was, smiling very slightly into the night, and wished that the corridor, the darkness, the song inside the darkness, might continue forever.

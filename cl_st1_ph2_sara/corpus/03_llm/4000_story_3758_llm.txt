## A Day among the Quantocks

It was at Watchet that Dorothea and I deserted our companions.

I say deserted, though there was nothing military in the matter, and no penalty beyond the possible reproaches of six excellent persons who had arranged that we should remain together until Bristol. But organized pleasure has always appeared to me a contradiction in terms. One may organize instruction, meals, and railway journeys; happiness comes upon us by a side road and generally without luggage.

Our party had been set down in Watchet shortly after nine on a morning in June. The programme allowed an hour for the harbour, an hour for luncheon, and then a sober progression eastward; but the day was young, the sky was blue, the Quantock Hills rose greenly behind the town, and Dorothea, who was fifteen and therefore old enough to be adventurous but young enough not to call adventure inconvenient, looked at me with conspiracy in her eyes.

“Papa,” she said, “must we go with the others?”

“Certainly not,” I replied, before prudence had time to interfere.

Thus revolutions begin.

Watchet itself justified rebellion. There was the little harbour, enclosed by its piers and smelling honestly of tar, salt, fish, wet rope, and the deep brown mud left by the retiring tide; there were red and grey houses climbing irregularly from the water; there were boats leaning upon their sides like tired creatures asleep; there were gulls balancing in the fresh wind, and beyond all, the broad Bristol Channel, silver under a procession of white clouds. Far across the water showed, or seemed to show—for I do not pledge myself in regard to distances before breakfast—the faint Welsh coast.

A sailor in a blue jersey was coiling rope beside a boat. He had a face so dark and deliberate that I immediately thought of the Ancient Mariner.

“Look, Dorothea,” I whispered. “There he is.”

“He is not old enough,” said she.

“Suffering preserves the complexion.”

“And the Ancient Mariner was not real.”

At fifteen one is mercilessly literal.

“My dear,” I said, “a person who has lived in the minds of men for a hundred years is at least as real as most of the people who pay rates.”

She considered this, but preferred to watch the sailor. Whether her interest was in Coleridge, seamanship, or the young man’s blue jersey I cannot say, and upon my honour I do not wish to decide.

The association, at all events, was proper. From this coast Coleridge drew something of the atmosphere through which the doomed ship glides—out past church and hill and lighthouse, into that desolation where

> “The ice was here, the ice was there,  
> The ice was all around.”

There was no ice in Watchet that morning. The water flashed pleasantly; two children hunted crabs between the stones; a woman shook a mat from an upper window; and an elderly fisherman informed us that mackerel were “terr’ble onsartain.” Yet the imagination accepts no contradiction from weather. The narrow harbour mouth became for me the gateway of the Mariner’s voyage, and the quiet sea beyond it was already whitening beneath a phantom moon.

Dorothea broke the spell.

“If we are not going with the others, where are we going?”

“To the Quantocks.”

“How?”

That useful syllable has overturned many noble plans. Inquiry produced a small inn, the inn produced a landlord, and the landlord produced, after hesitation, a dog-cart, a brown mare, and a boy whose chief purpose appeared to be the assurance that the mare required no boy at all.

“Her’ll goa straight on,” he said. “Nivver fear. Only don’t ’ee pull at her when her baint minded to stop.”

The instructions were obscure, but Dorothea mounted with confidence and took the reins. I climbed beside her, carrying a basket of provisions which the landlord’s wife had prepared against emergencies. The mare was called Violet, though no reason for the name was apparent; she was brown in front, dusty behind, and philosophic everywhere. We rattled out of Watchet beneath the astonished gaze of our companions, who were then emerging from a shop with picture post-cards.

“Where are you going?” they cried.

“Into poetry!” I answered.

Dorothea touched Violet with the whip—not, I hasten to say, with any violence—and we escaped before further explanations became necessary.

The road rose gently from the sea. Watchet fell behind us, its roofs crowded together beside the shining water; ahead, the Quantocks lifted their long ridges, wooded on the lower slopes and open to the sky above. The morning had that clear, washed look which sometimes follows rain. Every leaf seemed newly created. The grass possessed a conscious gladness; the hedges were deep with dog-roses and honeysuckle; bluebells lingered in shaded places, while foxgloves stood rosy and erect along the banks. Here and there the red earth showed beneath the green, warm as brick in sunlight. Larks climbed over the fields, pouring out song as if the whole blue air must be filled before noon.

There was a stream that dashed and danced and sang beside us for half a mile; there was a flock of sheep flowing over a distant field like spilt cream; there were rabbits that scampered from the roadside, displaying indecorous white tails; there were butterflies wavering above the ragwort, unable, apparently, to arrive at any decision. Above the rounded hills clouds loitered and shifted, and their enormous shadows swept across woodland and meadow.

“This,” I said, “is better than a programme.”

“It depends,” Dorothea replied, “upon whether Violet knows the way.”

Violet did not. At a fork where one road mounted boldly and the other descended between trees, she stopped and looked backward at us. We consulted a man who was clipping a hedge.

“Alfoxton?” I asked.

He leaned upon his shears and examined me.

“Be you seeking Muster Wudswuth’s place?”

“Yes.”

“Then take the high road till you comes by two elms, turn where the white gate was—’tis green now—and ax again.”

These directions, though picturesque, did not bring us directly to Alfoxton. They brought us first to a farm, then to a quarry, and finally to a cottage where three small children, a baby, and a yellow dog rushed out to receive us. The eldest child, a girl of perhaps nine, knew the road perfectly and described it with severe precision. The baby contributed several sounds, and the dog accompanied us for nearly a mile, in spite of Dorothea’s repeated commands to return.

At length we came upon the house, standing with a composed and somewhat withdrawn air amid its trees.

Alfoxton—Alfoxden, if one prefers the other form—is no palace of the imagination. One must not approach it expecting battlements, marble terraces, or a door proportioned to the fame of those who passed through it. Its dignity is domestic. It belongs to the green quiet of the place: the wooded slopes, the lawns softened by weather, the old trees stretching beneficent arms, and the glimpses of hill beyond. A house may be fortunate in its tenants; it may also, after they are gone, appear to remember them.

We obtained leave to wander in the grounds from a lady who regarded our equipage with amusement and our purpose with kindness. Violet was tied in a shady place and provided with water. Dorothea stroked her nose for so long that I again became uncertain whether our pilgrimage concerned poets or horses.

Wordsworth came to Alfoxton in 1797 with his sister Dorothy, and there recovered, if that be not too simple a word, from a season of inward confusion. The French Revolution had first appeared to him like sunrise upon the world. He had hailed liberty with the ardour of youth; then came violence, war, disappointment, and that weary conflict in which generous hope finds itself accused by facts. At Alfoxton the common earth restored him. Dorothy saw, recorded, loved; Coleridge talked and dreamed at Nether Stowey; the hills, lanes, birds, children, beggars, and changing lights became again a language he could trust.

We walked beneath oaks whose leaves made a silvery verdure overhead. Ferns crowded the banks; a thrush flew low across the path; somewhere in the underwood a wood-pigeon repeated its grave, soft note. Through openings among the branches we saw the fields descending toward the sea, placid and fertile, with cattle standing knee-deep in buttercups. The Quantocks behind us rose like steep billows of earth suddenly stayed by a command.

“One can understand,” I said, “how a man might learn to write again here.”

“Did he stop writing before?” asked Dorothea.

“Not altogether. But there are times when a poet writes and times when he discovers what he has to say.”

“Isn’t that the same thing?”

“Very seldom.”

She accepted the distinction provisionally. We sat upon a fallen trunk, and I repeated, not without emotion, lines written of another place but belonging to the same spiritual country:

> “Nature never did betray  
> The heart that loved her; ’tis her privilege,  
> Through all the years of this our life, to lead  
> From joy to joy.”

Dorothea picked a blade of grass and looked through the trees toward the sunlit meadow.

“Dorothy helped him,” she said.

“Yes. More than the world remembered for a long while.”

“I think sisters often do.”

There are remarks which seem small when uttered, yet travel far in the mind. I have since forgotten many formal observations made to me by learned men; I have not forgotten that one.

Brother and sister explored these woods in all weathers. Coleridge came over from Stowey, pouring out schemes, metaphysics, jokes, poems, theology, and plans for universal happiness. The two poets walked prodigious distances, sometimes by night, discussing the destiny of mankind with such animation that sober neighbours began to suspect them. England was then nervous concerning French invasion and domestic sedition. A stranger watching them took notes. Their wanderings, their unusual opinions, even their habit of looking closely at streams and hills, appeared dangerous. Poetry has seldom received a more flattering misunderstanding.

“Were they really thought to be spies?” Dorothea asked.

“Something of the sort. One tale says that Coleridge’s talk of ‘Spy Nozy’ was merely his discourse on Spinoza overheard by an honest man with patriotic ears. I cannot guarantee the tale, but it deserves to be true.”

“And Wordsworth had to leave?”

“The tenancy ended; suspicion did not help. Paradise is often lost through a lease.”

We followed a path into thicker woodland, where the sunlight fell in round golden spaces and the air smelled of earth, fern, and crushed mint. A little waterfall slipped over dark stones, talking to itself in the shade. Dorothea went ahead, light-footed and bareheaded, carrying her hat by its ribbons. For an instant she seemed less my child than a young spirit lent to the summer—a thing of motion, colour, and unconsidered joy. Then she caught her skirt upon a bramble and called to me in language wholly practical.

By the time we returned to Violet, the sun stood high and our basket had acquired importance. But I wished to dine at Nether Stowey; Dorothea wished to drive; and Violet, after a brief dispute concerning direction, consented to proceed.

The road curled along the hills through Holford and past cottages built of warm stone, their gardens overflowing with roses. Women stood at doorways; old men occupied benches with an air of having been appointed to them for life; children waved; hens crossed before us under the protection of Providence. Now the way climbed between banks so high that only a ribbon of blue sky remained above; now it opened suddenly and disclosed the Channel, the distant coast, and a great breadth of country patterned with hedges.

At one descent Violet surprised us by developing speed. The dog-cart swayed; the basket leaped; my hat attempted independence.

“Dorothea!”

“I have her, Papa.”

“You have more courage than experience.”

“So has Violet.”

We reached the bottom with all four wheels, two passengers, and most of the luncheon. It was needed.

Nether Stowey lies comfortably beneath the Quantocks, a village of climbing streets, old houses, gardens, roofs, and unexpected corners. Above it rise the remains of a Norman castle, though “remains” is a generous term. We left Violet at an inn whose sign creaked mildly in the wind and whose landlord promised that no harm should come to her.

“Does she bite?” he asked.

“Not that we know.”

“Kick?”

“Only against literary conversation.”

The landlord looked at me, then at Dorothea.

“Ah,” he said, with the compassion Somerset reserves for harmless lunatics.

Our luncheon was served in a small parlour with a sanded floor and a window full of geraniums. There was cold beef, bread of notable solidity, cheese, pickles, and gooseberry tart. They were few and simple. They were also excellent. Travellers who complain of plain food have generally not earned it.

From the inn we climbed Castle Hill. The path was steep and the noon warm, but every upward turn widened the prospect. At the summit we found no towers, no gate, no mailed sentinel—only banks and hollows, grass, fragments of stone, and the ancient shape of defence impressed upon the earth. Yet imagination repaired the ruin. Timber walls rose; horns sounded; men watched the roads below; smoke climbed from poor huts; and the Norman lord, who has been dust for centuries, looked out across his conquered vale.

Then all vanished, and there remained Dorothea seated upon the grass, a beetle mounting her shoe, and Violet far below flicking her tail in the inn yard.

The view was noble. The Quantocks rolled away in green and purple folds; cultivated fields lay spread like a map toward Bridgewater; villages showed among trees; the Channel shone beyond; and over all moved the immense leisurely pageant of clouds. A hawk hung motionless above the slope. The world seemed at once spacious and intimate, old beyond thought and young in every leaf.

“Coleridge climbed here,” I said.

“Of course he did.”

“And Wordsworth.”

“They climbed everywhere. They had a formidable idea of living near one another.”

It was true. Their friendship required hills, midnight roads, continual visiting, and an expenditure of shoe-leather which would appal a modern literary society.

We descended to the cottage associated with Coleridge. Humble is the proper word for it: low-roofed, white-walled, with small rooms and a small garden, such a dwelling as greatness frequently inhabits before biographers arrive. Here Coleridge lived with his wife and child; here, amid domestic anxieties, scant money, endless projects, and visits from friends, he passed the most fruitful season of his poetic life.

A kindly woman admitted us. The rooms spoke quietly of narrow means—low ceilings, plain hearths, uneven floors, windows through which the village light entered without ceremony. One could scarcely reconcile their dimensions with the immensities that issued from them. Yet why should genius require a large room? The mind carries its own horizons.

In this cottage, or in the lanes and hills about it, were conceived and fashioned poems that have altered the weather of the imagination: “Christabel,” with its moonlit forest and the strange lady Geraldine; “Kubla Khan,” that fragment glittering like a jewel broken from some Eastern crown; and above all the voyage of the Ancient Mariner, with its crime, terror, penance, and final blessing.

I stood by the hearth and heard inwardly:

> “The fair breeze blew, the white foam flew,  
> The furrow followed free;  
> We were the first that ever burst  
> Into that silent sea.”

Outside, a cart passed. A child called to another child. A saucepan clattered in a neighbouring house. So does the supernatural consent to live beside the ordinary.

Dorothea examined the garden.

“Was this where he meant to grow vegetables?”

“Very likely. He had a scheme for supporting himself by literature and market gardening.”

“Did he know anything about gardening?”

“Nothing that would have prejudiced the experiment.”

Coleridge possessed magnificent plans. He would write a great work, conduct a periodical, reform philosophy, preach, educate mankind, cultivate cabbages, and perhaps pay his debts. Each intention was sincere; most were overtaken by another intention. We smile, but affectionately. The world has enough punctual men and too few who have visited Xanadu.

In the garden bees worked among lavender and thyme with a steadiness Coleridge might have admired and could never have imitated. A narrow path divided the beds; climbing roses leaned against the wall; beyond the hedge the hills rose, inviting the walker. I thought of his extraordinary mind, rich, restless, self-consuming—one of those minds that illuminate many roads but have difficulty travelling to the end of any. His life would know illness, dependency, remorse, and frustrated power; yet the best of him survives untouched, perpetually setting sail.

Dorothea had grown quiet. At length she said, “It seems unfair that such beautiful things came when he was unhappy.”

“Beauty is not always the reward of happiness.”

“Then what is?”

“Sometimes beauty is what happiness and unhappiness make together.”

She frowned at this, as she was entitled to do. Fathers are too ready to turn ignorance into aphorism.

Before leaving, we bought a little printed account of the cottage and wrote our names in the visitors’ book. I noticed that the preceding page contained signatures from Birmingham, Boston, Edinburgh, Melbourne, and a place in India I could not decipher. These unknown pilgrims had entered the same low door because a poor poet once imagined a ship beneath an alien star. There is power for you—not the power that commands men to march, but that which brings them freely, long afterward, to stand silent in a little room.

The afternoon was waning when we returned to the inn. Shadows lengthened across the street; swallows flew low; a mellow light rested upon the upper hills. Violet greeted us without enthusiasm but accepted an apple from Dorothea. The landlord gave directions to Bridgewater, adding several cautions that contradicted one another, and we set out.

It was the most beautiful hour of the day. The heat had passed; scents of hay, hedge-rose, and damp earth rose along the road; smoke ascended blue from cottage chimneys. We drove through villages settling toward evening, past women bringing linen from lines, past men returning from fields, past children whose games occupied the entire highway until Violet’s deliberate approach divided them. Church bells sounded somewhere beyond the elms.

Past Stowey’s roofs and the last green shoulder of the Quantocks; past orchards where the young apples hung hard and bright; past a mill-stream that flashed under a stone bridge; past farmyards golden with straw and ponds dark beneath willows; past white cottages, red fields, grazing cattle, and solemn horses looking over gates; past lanes down which the sunlight streamed like visible dust; past a gypsy fire with one thin column of smoke; past a lonely church whose yews kept guard over names already forgotten.

The hills withdrew behind us. I turned often to look back. One ridge remained blue above the hedges, then became a cloud, then was gone.

Dorothea drove without speaking. Her profile against the evening light was still childish in its curve and already touched with the gravity of womanhood. I knew—though she did not—that such days are granted only for a season. A father supposes he leads his child through the world; presently he discovers that they have merely walked together for a little while, and that the road ahead belongs to her.

“Are you tired?” I asked.

“No.”

“Hungry?”

“A little.”

“Sorry we came?”

She looked at me in astonishment. “Of course not.”

It was enough.

Bridgewater received us with lamps beginning to shine in shop windows and the brisker noises of a town. We surrendered Violet and the dog-cart at an inn near the station. Dorothea kissed the mare between the eyes, thereby settling, perhaps, the question of where her chief interest had lain. We bought tickets, secured tea and buns, and entered a railway carriage just as the guard raised his whistle.

The engine drew us rapidly over the country we had spent all day discovering. Fields, houses, canals, and stations flitted past; dusk gathered in the hollows; one by one the lights appeared. The modern world had resumed us. At Bristol our companions were waiting, full of dinner and reproach.

“Where have you been?”

“To Alfoxton and Nether Stowey.”

“In that dog-cart?”

“In that dog-cart.”

“Was it worth missing everything?”

Dorothea answered before I could.

“We did not miss everything,” she said. “We found it.”

And so we had.

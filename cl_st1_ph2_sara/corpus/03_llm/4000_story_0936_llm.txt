# The Discipline of Darkness

## I

When I arrived in London I was in possession of three trunks, two bags, one umbrella, a rug, and that peculiar American conviction that all luggage not held firmly under the knee is already on its way to Constantinople.

The train had entered a vast black shed, where lamps hung in the smoky air like yellow buttons upon the coat of some dead giant. Men moved along the platform with the composure of persons who knew exactly which end of the world was up. I did not. I had been in England perhaps forty seconds, and already I had mislaid the customs, the exit, the hotel, the English currency, and my courage.

A porter approached.

Now, in my country, when a man approaches your baggage with professional intent, you immediately become a general defending a frontier. You spread your arms. You issue commands. You count everything aloud. You accuse innocent citizens with your eye. If one bag disappears behind a post, your imagination follows it through pawnshops, seaports, and the private apartment of a prosperous burglar in St. Louis.

This porter merely touched his cap.

“Cab, sir?”

“Yes,” I said, with the firmness of one who has answered the simplest question in an examination and hopes the remainder will be of the same character.

He gathered my possessions without drama. He did not wrench a handle off, insult my ancestry, or drop the umbrella beneath the wheels of a locomotive. He seemed to understand luggage as a shepherd understands sheep. The trunks followed him morally, although two other men were required to move them physically.

I trotted behind, counting.

“One, two, three—two bags—the rug—where’s the—ah, yes—one, two—”

The porter looked back with patient melancholy.

“They’ll be all roight, sir.”

This was an extraordinary statement. How could they be all right? They were scattered over thirty feet of platform, surrounded by thousands of Englishmen, any one of whom might be secretly yearning for an American trunk containing four shirts, a shaving brush, and a guidebook. Yet the porter believed in the essential safety of things. More astonishingly, things appeared to justify his belief.

At the cab rank stood a row of hansoms, each looking like a small black cannon balanced on enormous wheels. The cabmen sat high above, remote and judicial. They had rugs around their knees and rain upon their hats, and their faces wore the expression of men who had been born knowing the fare to every hotel in Europe.

The porter selected a cabman. There was no shouting. There was no auction. There was no violent discussion in which seven persons claimed that my hotel had burned down and that their cousin owned a better one. The two men spoke briefly.

“Grand Imperial, Charing Cross.”

“Roight.”

That was all. The porter and the cabman then arranged my baggage with a gravity usually reserved for the burial of statesmen. A trunk was lifted. A bag was fitted into a crevice. The rug was folded. The umbrella was placed where it could not commit suicide. Each article found its appointed station in the universe.

I watched, humiliated by competence.

When the porter had finished, I gave him a coin. He touched his cap again, neither groveling nor appearing wounded by the amount. Then he walked away.

I leaned out.

“Is everything there?”

“Yes, sir,” said the porter.

The cabman turned slightly.

“All roight, sir.”

There they were—the porter and the cabman—two men united against my panic. They had no visible affection for me. They had not adopted me. They did not care whether I lived to breakfast. Nevertheless, they had agreed that my trunks should reach the Grand Imperial, and upon this point their honor was apparently engaged.

We started.

I sat in the hansom and expected, during the first quarter-mile, to hear behind me the crash of all my possessions descending into the street. Nothing happened. I looked through the little trap overhead. The cabman’s face appeared upside down, calm and enormous, like the moon inspecting a well.

“Baggage safe?” I called.

“Safe, sir.”

The trap closed.

It was my first lesson in the silence of London.

## II

Rain was falling with professional steadiness. It did not dash itself against the city in romantic fury; it attended to business. The lamps shone through it dimly, each lamp occupying a private sphere of wet gold, beyond which the world ceased.

The hansom ran into darkness.

I could see perhaps twenty yards ahead, perhaps ten, perhaps only the interior of my own alarm. Every pedestrian carried with him a cylinder of vision, a narrow illuminated territory bounded by rain and night. Into my cylinder came a shop window, a policeman, the black wall of a building, the red flank of a bus; then each vanished as completely as if extinguished by decree. Little gas fishes swam toward me out of the sea of darkness, opened round yellow eyes, and slipped behind.

London did not reveal itself. It issued fragments.

I had expected noise. A great city, according to my experience, announces itself like a brass band falling downstairs. New York has a voice and uses it. It roars because roaring is the recognized proof of commercial integrity. Its wagons thunder, its bells assault, its engines scream, its citizens speak as if each man were addressing a deaf convention across a river. I have sometimes suspected that every New Yorker, upon paying his taxes, receives a small cymbal which he is legally required to clash at intervals.

London had all the materials for uproar. Wheels passed us by the hundred. Horses struck the pavement. Buses lumbered near, loaded with human beings. Cabs darted in and out. Somewhere there were trains, bells, voices, and the colossal digestion of the city. Yet over all lay a strange restraint.

It was not silence, precisely. It was an organized murmur, a low drone, as if ten thousand noises had been drilled to speak together in the tone proper for a sickroom. Even the rain seemed to have been cautioned by a policeman.

The effect upon me was sinister.

In New York, noise is companionship. If a cart crashes beneath your window at three in the morning, you know civilization survives. If no one curses for five consecutive minutes, you rise and look out, fearing plague. But here the traffic went on with the secrecy of a conspiracy. Great buses came from the dark like elephants in felt slippers. Cabs passed as swiftly as bullets and with scarcely more comment. Pedestrians emerged, crossed, vanished. Nobody declared himself.

I began to believe that London was artificial. Not false—artificial. It had been constructed out of darkness, wet stone, regulated motion, and the determination not to inconvenience the next man unnecessarily.

The cabman drove with a skill that disturbed me more than incompetence would have done. He threaded the hansom between buses, wagons, islands, lamps, and other hansoms, missing death by distances so small that an American insurance company would have required a microscope to measure them. Yet he did not shout. He did not rise in his seat and tell another driver what sort of man his grandfather had been. He merely guided the horse.

At the top of a hill the horse changed its gait.

The descent shone with rain. The pavement looked like black glass. I prepared for catastrophe. The animal took two careful steps, stretched its forelegs, lowered its hindquarters, and began to slide.

It slid deliberately.

I had never before seen a horse convert itself into a sled. Its shoes scraped faintly. Its body remained solemn. The cabman held the reins with an air of respectable rumination. Down we went, horse, hansom, trunks, traveler, umbrella, and the national credit of the United States, all entrusted to four hooves gliding over a London hill.

I looked upward through the trap.

“Is he falling?”

“No, sir.”

“He’s sliding!”

“Yes, sir.”

“Does he know it?”

The cabman considered.

“’E generally does, sir.”

The trap closed again.

Other horses were doing the same thing. They came down the slope in attitudes of grave resignation, each animal apparently aware that the laws of friction had been repealed by municipal order. At the bottom they resumed walking without pride.

This was not instinct. It was drill.

A wild horse, encountering that hill, would have tried to run, fly, pray, or return to the country. These horses had been educated. Somewhere in their youth they had been informed that London streets were slippery, that alarm was vulgar, and that the correct procedure was to place the feet forward and slide with dignity.

The cab horse had manners.

I was still considering this discovery when we turned into a broader street, where another horse was lying in the roadway.

## III

The fallen animal remained attached to a cab. One shaft pointed upward. The driver had descended and stood near the horse’s head, looking angry in the formal English fashion—that is to say, he appeared as though he might write a letter to the proper authority.

Several persons stopped.

Among them was a young man in an evening coat and a top hat so lustrous that it carried a separate moon upon its crown. He had the air of one bound for dinner, opera, Parliament, or some other institution requiring clean linen. He approached the cabman.

“Why don’t you look where you’re gowing?” he demanded.

The cabman answered. I could not hear the words, but they were evidently the traditional words reserved by cabmen for gentlemen in evening dress.

Another man came up.

“Shouldn’t drive a ’orse like that.”

A third observed:

“Poor beast.”

This shocked me. The horse lay in the rain. The cab was twisted. The driver was alone. Yet the first duty of the bystanders was rebuke.

They gathered and scolded him.

“You cabmen think you own the road.”

“Should know better.”

“Disgraceful, that’s wot it is.”

The driver received these remarks with gloomy fortitude. Perhaps he knew that assistance could not legally begin until blame had been apportioned. Certainly no one moved toward the horse until each had made his moral position clear.

Then, as if a whistle had sounded, they acted.

The young gentleman removed neither top hat nor evening coat. He seized a shaft. The critic of cabmen took the bridle. The defender of the poor beast went to the wheel. Two more men appeared from nowhere. They loosened straps, pushed, pulled, soothed, commanded, and reconstructed the horse.

It was reconstruction rather than rescue. The animal seemed to have come apart into legs, harness, wet hide, alarm, and iron buckles. Under the hands of these men the pieces were assembled. A foreleg was found and placed in a reasonable direction. The shafts descended. The cab returned to its four-wheeled theology. The horse struggled up.

The top hat remained perfect.

This impressed me beyond measure, because I come from a land where, in certain districts, a top hat has historically been regarded not as clothing but as a challenge.

I knew a man in Arizona—at least, I knew a man who knew him, which is the usual legal foundation for an Arizona narrative—who once received a top hat from an uncle in Boston. The uncle had accumulated civilization and wished to distribute some of it among his relatives. The hat arrived in a wooden box, surrounded by tissue paper and moral purpose.

Its owner, Buck Harland, wore it into town.

At the first saloon a prospector looked at it and said, “What’s that?”

“A hat,” said Buck.

The prospector considered this deliberate insult and shot a bottle from the shelf.

Buck shot the lamp.

Another man, believing the lamp had taken sides, shot the mirror. The bartender went beneath the counter. Within thirty seconds the hat had become the center of a constitutional crisis involving six revolvers, three windows, one piano, and a gentleman from Tucson who had merely entered to ask the time.

Buck escaped with the hat uninjured but misunderstood.

The following day he tried again. He wore it lower, hoping to make it less political. A cowboy saw him and remarked that no honest man required so much altitude above the eyebrows. Buck replied that jealousy was a low-down cuss’s substitute for stature. There was further discussion. The sheriff intervened, not against the firearms, which were local, but against the hat, which was foreign.

“Buck,” he said, “you got a right to wear it, but the town ain’t ripe.”

The hat was finally placed upon a fence post outside the settlement, where every citizen shot at it until sunset. In this manner Boston culture was absorbed by the West.

Yet here in London a young man had bent over a fallen cab horse in a shining cylinder and had not provoked so much as an editorial. His hat denoted class, dinner, money, authority, foolishness, dignity, or merely a cold head; but it did not require gunfire. More remarkable still, he had used his gloved hands to push at a muddy cab after first informing the cabman that the accident was entirely his fault.

This ritual interested me.

The Londoner, I concluded, must condemn before he assists. His sympathy passes through a customs office. He cannot simply rush forward in a state of palpable warm-heartedness, for that might expose him to emotional import duties. He therefore rebukes the sufferer, establishes distance, and then helps with enormous efficiency.

The scolding is the receipt.

Our cab moved on. Behind us the rescued horse stood trembling while the young man adjusted his cuffs. I imagined him arriving late at dinner.

“What delayed you, Algernon?”

“Cab horse down.”

“How tiresome.”

“Very.”

And no more would be said, although he had just helped lift half a ton of desperate animal out of the black river of a London street.

The silence of London again.

## IV

At the Grand Imperial a clerk stood behind a counter of formidable breadth. He was pale, neat, and weary. He looked as if he had been waiting since the reign of Queen Anne for someone to ask whether there was a room.

I asked.

He consulted a ledger. This act exhausted the final reserve of his youth.

“Name, sir?”

I gave it.

He turned the book toward me and offered a pen. The pen was attached to the desk by a chain, perhaps because Americans had once visited the hotel before.

While I wrote, the porter and the cabman transferred my luggage. Again I counted. Again each article appeared. I paid the cabman. He touched his hat.

“Good night, sir.”

He did not demand more. He did not explain that the rain had doubled the fare, that my trunks had injured his horse’s opinions, or that Charing Cross had recently been moved farther from the station. He simply departed.

The porter and the cabman had completed their campaign. I almost ran after them to express admiration, but an English hotel entrance does not encourage emotion. It is designed to make every arriving person feel that someone more important has just left.

A bell sounded.

From an alcove emerged an elderly man in uniform. He came toward us slowly.

There are several kinds of slowness. There is the slowness of caution, the slowness of laziness, the slowness of dignity, and the slowness of a man who has forgotten where he was going but continues from principle. This old man embodied all of them.

“Lift,” said the clerk.

The old man looked at me. Then he looked toward the lift. The distance was six yards. We began.

I have crossed prairies in less time.

He did not shuffle. Shuffling implies frequency of motion. His feet advanced with the solemn interval of astronomical events. I followed with my bag. Behind us, hotel servants carried the trunks, halted, rested, formed families, and died.

At last we entered the lift, which was a small cage ornamented in brass. The old man closed the gate. He examined a lever as if it were an artifact dug from Nineveh.

“What floor?” he asked.

“Third.”

“Third,” he repeated.

This information traveled inward. It passed through many departments. Somewhere behind his eyes a committee assembled.

He moved the lever.

Nothing happened.

Then, with a groan of metal and an air of personal sacrifice, the lift began to rise.

The old man stood with one hand upon the lever. Floor by floor, civilization sank beneath us. At the first landing he appeared uncertain whether we had perhaps reached the third by a quicker route. At the second he studied the number. At the third we stopped six inches low, rose nine inches, descended four, reflected, and finally came level.

He opened the gate.

“Third,” he announced.

It was not information. It was a triumph.

I thanked him. He received thanks as the aged receive weather: without surprise.

In my room I discovered that the trunks had arrived before me.

This was supernatural. They had been downstairs when I entered the lift. Unless there existed a second and more energetic old man, the luggage had passed through the walls. I examined the locks. All was intact.

From my window London appeared as a black expanse pierced by yellow points. The rain continued. Wheels whispered below. Occasionally a cab emerged into the light and disappeared. The city seemed less built than submerged. Its lamps were the eyes of creatures living at great depth.

I slept badly, not from noise but from the absence of it. The quiet oppressed me. I felt that the whole city was awake and behaving itself.

## V

Morning did not so much dawn as dilute the darkness.

I went into the street with the patriotic intention of finding London inferior to New York. This is the proper method of foreign travel. A man crosses three thousand miles of ocean to prove that his barber at home understands him better.

At the first crossing, however, my argument encountered traffic.

Buses moved in two dense streams. They were tremendous vehicles, red and yellow, covered with advertisements and topped by citizens sitting in the weather. They rolled forward like domesticated elephants carrying shop signs. Between them ran cabs, wagons, carts, bicycles, and persons who appeared to have private arrangements with death.

I waited at the curb.

A policeman stood in the middle of the crossing.

He raised one hand.

The street stopped.

Not gradually. Not after discussion. It stopped. Buses halted, horses checked, cabs became still, and a wagon driver who had been urging his team with great private enthusiasm lapsed into obedience. The policeman turned slightly and moved two fingers. Another stream advanced.

I watched for resentment. There was none.

In New York, the raised hand of authority is often regarded as the opening statement in a debate. The driver halts, certainly, but he halts in order to explain why halting is absurd. He leans outward. He invokes his destination, his employer, the Constitution, and the defects of every other driver within hearing. If a policeman commands silence, he has merely introduced a new topic.

Here the drivers obeyed.

The policeman did not look brilliant. He did not leap, whistle furiously, or display the strategy of Napoleon. He looked ordinary, composed, and slightly damp. Yet the immense mechanism of the crossing responded to him. His hand rose; iron, wood, leather, horseflesh, commerce, impatience, and human vanity became still.

It was drill, plain, simple drill.

This explanation pleased me so much that I immediately applied it to everything.

The porter had not been a saint. He had been drilled. The cabman was not born courteous. He had been drilled. The sliding horse had been drilled, probably by another horse of severe views. The young man in the top hat had been drilled first to censure and then to lift. The hotel clerk had been drilled to wear despair without permitting it to stain the ledger. Even the ancient lift operator, though his drill had proceeded at a speed fatal to weaker institutions, had ultimately delivered me to the correct floor.

Drill.

It was a cold word, but a warm result.

I crossed when the policeman allowed me. Halfway over I became confused and turned back. This placed me directly in the path of a cab. The driver pulled up with remarkable speed.

“Look where you’re gowing!” he said.

I retreated.

A moment later, when the policeman’s hand moved, the cabman proceeded. He had insulted me only in the established form and only to the necessary degree. He did not pursue me, dismount, or write to my government. His anger had boundaries.

This, too, was drill.

I began to see that London traffic did not depend upon each man being clever. If it had, the city would have been ruins by noon. It depended upon each man knowing the expected gesture. The policeman knew the driver would halt. The driver knew the pedestrian would cross. The pedestrian knew, except when the pedestrian was an American, that the vehicles would come from the direction in which he had neglected to look.

Collective confidence performed what individual genius could not.

An inspired cabman is a menace. A trained cabman is civilization.

## VI

The buses deserved separate study because they appeared to contain all London arranged by altitude.

Inside sat the sheltered classes, dimly visible through glass. Above sat the weatherproof classes, wearing coats, shawls, hats, and expressions of public endurance. The conductor moved between them with a leather purse, calling destinations in a language that had some historical relation to English.

“Bynk! Stryn! Chyng Cryss!”

I boarded one and climbed to the top.

There I sat beside a woman with parcels and opposite a clerk who read a newspaper despite wind, rain, corners, horses, and the probable collapse of Europe. He folded the paper into a narrow architectural structure and never touched another passenger. This feat alone would have qualified him for engineering honors in America.

The bus advanced.

At every stop the conductor appeared.

“Fares, plyse.”

I named my destination incorrectly. He corrected me without joy.

“Not this byby. Next byby.”

“What?”

“Next byby.”

I handed him a coin large enough, in my opinion, to purchase the bus. He returned change with instantaneous accuracy and disappeared down the stair while the vehicle was moving.

No one applauded.

The London citizen was surrounded by little acts of skill which he declined to admire. The driver guided the elephant. The conductor balanced upon it. The policeman commanded it. The passenger mounted and descended without falling beneath it. Everybody performed wonders and regarded wonder as bad taste.

From the upper seat I saw the city in pieces: gray buildings, black railings, wet hats, polished shop windows, chimney pots, squares, statues, and innumerable signs.

The signs soon became a battle.

There were signs for soap, pills, cocoa, tobacco, collars, boots, sauces, teas, tonics, hats, loans, furniture, corsets, cures, and entertainments. They advanced upon the eye horse, foot, and guns. A gigantic lady recommended a powder. A blue infant insisted upon a food. A colonel of impossible redness ordered me to smoke. Letters twenty feet tall shouted the merits of mustard.

Among these armies there occasionally appeared a small necessary sign.

STREET CLOSED.

It was modest, black, and nearly hidden behind an advertisement for a preparation guaranteed to restore the hair of the bald, the courage of the timid, and the appetite of the dead.

Naturally I did not see it.

I walked into the closed street and was informed by a workman that it was closed.

“But there was no sign,” I protested.

He pointed.

There it was, crouching beneath the triumphant hair restorer.

This seemed to me an important social danger. The informative sign, whose purpose is to prevent a man from breaking his neck in a trench, speaks in a whisper. The advertisement, whose purpose is to sell him a biscuit he does not want, attacks with artillery.

Civilization may endure war, famine, politics, and the theatre, but it cannot long survive the concealment of essential directions behind persuasive nonsense.

Suppose a ship at sea displayed, instead of a lighthouse, an immense illuminated announcement:

TRY BINKLEY’S PERFECT SOCKS.

Mariners would perish, but they would perish aware of Binkley.

Or suppose a surgeon, approaching an operation, found the patient’s anatomy covered by placards:

THIS LIVER RECOMMENDS TOMPKINS’S TEA.

The liver might be influential, but the operation would be delayed.

Advertising is organized impertinence. It enters the cylinder of vision without invitation and refuses to leave. A monument may wait quietly for centuries to be noticed. A soap placard seizes the traveler by the coat and cries, “You are dirty!”

London’s restraint had failed upon its walls.

Yet even the advertisements appeared disciplined. They occupied appointed spaces. Their violence was rectangular. They screamed, but in typefaces licensed by convention.

Drill again.

I began to fear that drill was not merely an explanation but a disease of my own mind. Once discovered, it spread. Pigeons drilled in squares. Umbrellas drilled along pavements. Chimneys stood at attention. Rain fell in ranks.

## VII

During the afternoon I walked without a guide, because a guide would have compelled me to look at important things.

Important things are the ruin of travel. A cathedral, knowing itself important, offers little cooperation. It stands there while the visitor exhausts his adjectives. But a cab horse on wet pavement provides instruction.

I watched the pedestrians.

They did not wander. Even when uncertain they maintained an appearance of destination. Men in black coats moved rapidly, each carrying a private appointment like a sealed order. Women guided children through traffic by means of pressure upon the wrist. Messengers slipped between larger citizens. Old gentlemen crossed streets with sublime confidence in the obedience of others.

At narrow places two persons approached. One yielded half an inch, the other half an inch, and civilization passed between them.

This would not have occurred in the West of my top-hat history. There, each person would have yielded nothing, because nothing represented principle. They would have met, explained their families, and eventually widened the road with gunfire.

Londoners surrendered inches and preserved miles.

I do not mean that they were gentle. Gentleness suggests softness, and there was nothing soft in the rush of the pavements. They were trained to economize collision. Their politeness was often no more sentimental than the oiling of machinery. A man said “Sorry” because “Sorry” occupied less time than a quarrel.

Artificial virtue, I called it.

There are persons who dislike artificial virtue. They demand that every courteous act rise fresh and steaming from the human heart. If a man gives up his seat from habit rather than love, they dismiss the act as insincere. This seems to me severe upon the person who receives the seat.

Natural virtue is splendid but uncertain. It may oversleep. It may have indigestion. It may dislike your face. Artificial virtue reports for duty.

The porter did not love me, but my bags arrived.

The cabman did not love me, but he drove me safely.

The top-hatted young man may have despised the entire class of cabmen, yet when the horse fell he put his shoulder to the shaft.

The policeman could not hold personal affection for every pedestrian in London. The strain would have killed him. But he raised his hand, and we crossed.

These things were not warmth. They were form.

Form is commonly accused of emptiness, but an empty cup may still carry water if everyone agrees not to break it.

I became reckless with philosophy.

At another crossing I stood near a small boy. The policeman stopped the traffic. The boy started across. Halfway, he dropped a parcel. Instantly a cabman reined in, a woman caught the boy by the collar, and a gentleman recovered the parcel. The gentleman then said to the child:

“Careless little beggar.”

The boy took the parcel.

“Thank you, sir.”

Everybody resumed.

The rebuke, the rescue, the gratitude—the whole ceremony occupied perhaps five seconds. It had the precision of a military maneuver.

Drill.

Had the gentleman paused to inquire whether he felt genuine tenderness toward the child, the child would have been flattened by a bus. Fortunately, society had relieved him of introspection.

## VIII

Near evening the rain thickened, and the city returned to cylinders.

Each lamp drew a small room in the darkness. Persons entered these rooms, showed for an instant their wet shoulders and pale faces, and left by the opposite wall. A bus emerged like a painted cliff, filled my world, and vanished. Beyond it came a cab. Beyond the cab, nothing.

I found the limitation restful. In New York one is compelled to perceive everything at once. Streets advertise their distances. Towers insist upon the sky. Lights challenge one another across miles. The eye has no privacy.

London granted every man a narrow republic of sight.

Within my cylinder there might be a policeman, a flower seller, a horse’s head, or the door of a public house. Outside it, the Empire could collapse unnoticed. This encouraged modesty. A man could not easily believe himself master of a city which appeared twenty feet at a time.

The sounds also arrived softened. Hooves tapped. Wheels hissed. Voices approached and receded. Somewhere a bell spoke with restraint. The total murmur was immense, yet no separate noise claimed sovereignty.

The silence of London was not the absence of sound. It was the obedience of sound.

I wondered how long an American horn would survive there. Its first blast would probably astonish the lamps. At the second, a policeman would look toward it. At the third, the entire city would silently agree that the horn had been badly brought up.

At a corner two cabs nearly met. Both drivers checked. One said:

“Where d’you think you’re gowing?”

The other replied:

“Mind your own byby.”

Then they passed.

This was warfare reduced to regulation size.

In New York the incident might have developed territorial claims, witness testimony, political factions, and a crowd of experts who had seen nothing. Here the language had been issued, the honor of both sides protected, and traffic continued.

I began to admire the utility of ceremonial anger. It allows a man to discharge rage without making a career of it. The cabman says his sentence and is free. An American often improves his sentence, adds supporting anecdotes, attracts allies, and founds a movement.

London abbreviated emotion.

Of course I knew that beneath this calm there must be fury, grief, ambition, drunkenness, cruelty, and all the other furnaces by which cities are heated. I did not suppose the English had trained the human heart to keep left. But public conduct had been given rails. Men might boil inwardly; outwardly they queued.

This seemed both admirable and terrifying.

A city of spontaneous people may burn down through enthusiasm. A city of drilled people may continue efficiently in the wrong direction. Obedience is a magnificent horse, but someone must still know the road.

I looked at a policeman and wondered whether he knew it.

He was helping an old woman across the street while stopping two buses with his hand.

For the moment, this was sufficient.

## IX

When I returned to the hotel, the weary clerk was still behind his counter. I suspect he had not moved. Perhaps clerks were fitted into these desks during construction and inherited by successive hotel companies.

“Evening, sir,” he said.

There was no surprise in his voice. If I had returned carrying the Tower Bridge, he would merely have asked whether I required it sent up.

The old lift operator emerged.

We undertook the journey.

This time I studied him with new respect. His slowness had seemed personal. Now I saw that it was institutional. He was not merely an old man operating a lift; he was Time employed by the hotel.

We entered. The gate closed after prolonged negotiation. The lever moved. The lift rose with the solemnity of a coffin reconsidering burial.

“Busy day?” I asked.

The old man thought.

“Fair,” he said as we passed the first floor.

At the second floor he added:

“Fair busy.”

At the third, after he had leveled us through the usual series of corrections, he concluded:

“Very fair.”

This conversation had traveled three floors and arrived complete.

In my room the servant had arranged my belongings. The bags stood together. The umbrella hung from a hook. My boots had been removed, cleaned, and returned in a state of moral improvement. Even my disorder had been drilled.

I stood at the window.

Below, the streams of vehicles crossed and recrossed. Policemen lifted hands. Buses advanced. Cabs slipped through openings. Pedestrians gathered, waited, moved. The entire city resembled an enormous machine, but not a machine made solely of iron. It was made of expectation.

Each person expected the others to perform the accustomed act.

This expectation was more powerful than intelligence. No driver needed to invent traffic. No pedestrian needed to negotiate a private treaty with every cab. No porter needed to love every trunk. The system carried them as the street carried rain.

I had come to London helpless over my baggage. That helplessness had been genuine, though perhaps not heroic. I had imagined my trunks stolen, my cab lost, my person misunderstood, and my umbrella exiled. Instead, strangers had moved me through darkness with a courtesy so habitual that they themselves did not recognize it as virtue.

The porter and the cabman.

I repeated the phrase because it seemed to contain the city. The porter and the cabman; the policeman and the pedestrian; the conductor and the passenger; the critic and the rescuer; the clerk and the ancient custodian of vertical travel.

None had displayed genius. All had displayed form.

Drill.

I do not say London was kind. Kindness is a large claim, and cities are too occupied to answer it. I say London had devised certain methods by which unkind persons could behave usefully, impatient persons could wait, angry persons could avoid murder, and indifferent persons could carry another man’s bag to the proper hotel.

This may be the highest practical achievement of society.

Outside, a horse slipped on the wet pavement. For one instant its legs widened; then it recovered, lowered its body, and slid with grave competence down the shining street.

The cabman did not shout.

The policeman did not turn.

The lamps continued to swim in darkness.

Somewhere beyond my cylinder of vision, millions of people were obeying signals, exchanging ritual insults, reading advertisements, losing parcels, lifting horses, and arriving—slowly, perhaps, but usually at the proper floor.

And over it all lay the disciplined murmur, the enormous quiet, the artificial civility of London.

I listened.

It was silence.

But it had been drilled.

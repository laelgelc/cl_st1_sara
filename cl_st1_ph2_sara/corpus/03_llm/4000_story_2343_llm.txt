Harry Chatswood carried the mail from Binalong to Cobble Creek in a spring-cart that had once been painted yellow, and was still yellow in sheltered places—under the seat, for instance, and behind the near wheel, where the mud had not chipped it off. He was mail contractor, carrier, horse-breaker, buyer of hides, occasional drover, and several other things according to the season and the condition of his credit.

Harry was long and lean, with a brown face, a drooping moustache, and eyes that seemed half asleep until you noticed that they had seen everything. He was not what you would call a joker—not in the noisy public-house sense—but he had a quiet, poisonous sort of humour that generally left somebody else explaining matters.

One winter afternoon, about six miles beyond Binalong, he came upon Old Jack walking beside the road.

There are many Old Jacks in the bush. This one was Old Jack Murray, though I never heard anyone call him Murray except the police sergeant, who had reasons for being particular about names. Jack was a harmless, stooping old fellow with a grey beard, a battered cabbage-tree hat, and trousers tied below the knees with greenhide. He lived in a bark humpy beyond the ranges, and earned a little by fencing, stripping wattle bark, minding sheep when nobody else could be got, and doing odd jobs that were generally paid for with old clothes and promises.

He had a blue blanket rolled across his shoulders and a flour-bag under his arm.

Harry pulled up.

“Going far, Jack?”

“Cobble Creek, Harry.”

“Jump up.”

Jack climbed in carefully, as if the spring-cart belonged to the Governor. Harry shifted the mail-bags, and they went on through the cold afternoon, the wheels knocking into ruts and the swingle-bar squeaking whenever the bay horse leaned to the hill.

For a mile or two neither spoke. Bushmen can travel together in silence without quarrelling; new chums cannot, and commercial travellers seldom try.

Then Harry said:

“Been on the spree?”

“No, Harry.”

“Going on one?”

“No, Harry.”

“Then you’re either coming from trouble or going to it.”

Old Jack rubbed his beard.

“I owes Mother Mac fourteen bob.”

“That so?”

“Yes, Harry.”

“What for?”

Jack hesitated—not because he meant to deceive, but because truth sometimes embarrassed him.

“Dhrink,” he said at last, imitating Mother Mac unconsciously.

Harry glanced sideways.

“Fourteen shillings’ worth?”

“Well—there was three drinks one night, an’ a bottle another time, an’ one I had when I was bad with the cold. She said rum was good for it.”

“It is,” said Harry, “if you can afford the cold.”

Jack explained that Mother Mac had trusted him; that she had reminded him twice; that she had called him no better than a blackguard the last time; and that he wanted to pay her before taking a fencing job across the river. He had no money, however. This slight defect in the arrangement appeared to have occurred to him only after he had started.

Harry took out a canvas purse, counted fourteen shillings into Jack’s palm, and closed the old fingers over them.

“Pay me when you can.”

Jack’s eyes watered.

“You’re a white man, Harry.”

“Don’t spread it about,” said Harry. “I’ve a reputation to keep.”

At the next turn they overtook a man walking beside two leather sample-cases. He was a commercial traveller—soft-goods, as it afterwards turned out—and he had been put down by a coach whose driver believed that six miles meant anything between four and twelve. The traveller wore a dark overcoat, buttoned to the chin, and an expression of general objection to the country.

Harry gave him a lift too.

The traveller climbed in with dignity, sat upon a mail-bag, and drew his coat round him as if it were a cloak of silence and civilisation. He had neat whiskers, polished boots, and a collar which looked painfully honourable beside Harry’s red neckerchief and Old Jack’s beard.

Harry tried him with weather, roads, politics, horses, and the price of wool. The traveller answered in syllables. He looked at Old Jack’s flour-bag as if it contained something contagious.

But the wind sharpened after sundown, and the spring-cart jolted the starch out of him by degrees. When Harry mentioned coffee at Mother Mac’s, the traveller became almost human.

Mother Mac lived where the road crossed Stringybark Flat—a road used by bullock teams, selectors, drovers, jackeroos, policemen, and men who preferred not to meet policemen. Her place was a hut of bark slabs and flattened kerosene tins, with a chimney large enough to smoke three rooms and usually doing so. There was a lean-to stable, a crooked stockyard, two dogs under the floor, and a patch of cabbages protected from cattle by bedsteads, saplings, and prayer.

In town she was Mrs MacNamara, a respectable widow. On the road she was Mother Mac, provider of tea, coffee, chops, damper, boiled eggs, sardines, horse-feed, information, headache powders, liniment, tobacco, matches, and—when the customer was known—rum of a murderous strength, brandy with a felonious smell, and whisky that looked capable of giving evidence against itself.

The liquor was concealed in places no excise officer would think of looking, unless he had been an excise officer for more than ten minutes: under the flour, behind the camp ovens, in a hollow log, beneath Mother Mac’s mattress, and sometimes in an innocent-looking kerosene tin labelled SHEEP DIP.

As the cart drew up, Harry turned to the traveller.

“Better let me speak first. She’s shy of strangers.”

Mother Mac came out carrying a tin pint-pot and wiping her hands on her apron. She was a large, quick-moving woman, with iron-grey hair and black eyebrows that could ask a question before her tongue began.

“Good evenin’, Harry. An’ who have ye there?”

Harry nodded towards the traveller and lowered his voice—but not so low that she failed to hear.

“Government man. Excise.”

Mother Mac stopped.

“Phwat?”

Harry was already unharnessing the horse.

“Excise officer,” he repeated carelessly. “Travelling quiet.”

The traveller, who heard only his profession being discussed, bowed.

Mother Mac looked from his polished boots to his sample-cases, from the cases to Harry, and then back to the polished boots. Her face became respectable at once—terribly respectable.

“Come in, gintlemen,” she said. “There’s always a cup o’ tay for any honest man at Mrs MacNamara’s.”

They went inside.

The room was hot, smoky, and crowded with useful objects: saddles, bridles, bags of flour, a cradle with no baby in it, three camp ovens, a cracked clock, bottles of medicine, tins of jam, strings of onions, two rifles, one broken concertina, a churn, a grindstone handle, and a stuffed native cat that had lost one eye and looked the better for it. On the table stood bread, a dish of dripping, a sugar-bag, and a bottle containing something dark.

Mother Mac snatched the bottle away.

“Sauce,” she said fiercely, though no one had asked.

Harry leaned against the mantel, long and comfortable, and lit his pipe.

The traveller warmed his hands.

“Cold evening.”

“Very cowld,” said Mother Mac. “But healthy. Wonderful healthy counthry. No sickness here, barrin’ rheumatics, fever, snakebite, bad lungs, an’ a touch of the liver now an’ again.”

She put the billy on and watched him.

“An’ ye’re travelling for the Government, sir?”

The traveller glanced at Harry, who was examining the fire.

“Not precisely.”

“O-o-oh! Not precisely.”

“I represent a Sydney house.”

“Do ye now?”

“Yes.”

Mother Mac’s eyes narrowed. An excise officer travelling under cover would naturally represent a Sydney house; she knew that much without ever having met one.

“What line?” she demanded.

“Soft-goods.”

“Soft, is it?”

“Drapery.”

“Ye-e-es. An’ perhaps ye go pokin’ into people’s cupboards to see what kind o’—drapery—they keep?”

“I beg your pardon?”

“Sugar, sir?”

“Two spoonfuls.”

“I never keep spirits,” Mother Mac said abruptly.

The traveller blinked.

“Indeed?”

“Never did, an’ never will. I’m a lone, harrd-worrkin’ widow woman, an’ if lies have been told concerning me, I’d like to meet the low blaggard that told them.”

Harry drew on his pipe.

“Coffee smells good, Mother.”

“An’ why wouldn’t it smell good? There’s no law against coffee, is there?”

“Not yet,” said Harry.

The traveller began to suspect that drink might be obtainable, and, being cold, he smiled in a confidential way.

“Could you put a dash of brandy in mine?”

Mother Mac swung round.

“Brandy!”

“If you have it.”

“If I have it! Listen to the man! Harry Chatswood, did ye hear the gentleman insult me beneath me own roof?”

“No offence intended,” said the traveller. “I was prepared to pay.”

“Pay! There’s things that money cannot buy, sir—honesty, respectability, an’ a good name. I’d have ye know that Mrs MacNamara has all three.”

Harry coughed into his hand.

The traveller looked disappointed.

“Rum would do.”

Mother Mac seized the billy as if she meant to brain him with it.

“Rum!”

“Or whisky.”

“Outrageous! Do I look like a publican?”

No one answered. She did, rather.

Mother Mac poured the coffee, clattering the cups in the manner of a woman whose innocence has been wounded. She talked all the while—about licenses, law-abiding citizens, wicked tongues, police corruption, honest widows, and the disgraceful thirst of men from Sydney.

The traveller drank his coffee without improvement.

Old Jack sat near the door, holding the fourteen shillings in his pocket and waiting for a proper opening. Harry, who had almost forgotten him, caught his eye.

“Pay your account, Jack,” he said.

Jack rose obediently.

Mother Mac froze with the teapot in her hand.

“My—my account, Harry?”

“Yes,” said Jack. “The fourteen bob I owes you.”

“What fourteen bob?”

“The fourteen bob for dhrink.”

There was silence. Even the dogs beneath the floor seemed to listen.

Mother Mac put down the teapot.

“Dhrink?”

“Yes, Mother.”

“From me?”

“Yes, Mother.”

“Ye stand there—in front of witnesses, an’ strangers, an’ officers of the Crown—an’ say ye bought dhrink from me?”

Old Jack looked puzzled.

“You give it me.”

“Give ye phwat?”

“Rum.”

“I never had rum in me house!”

Jack scratched his head.

“There was the bottle under the flour.”

Mother Mac made a sound like a bullock choking.

“Under the flour!”

“An’ the square bottle behind the clock,” Jack continued helpfully. “That was brandy, though.”

Harry bent over the fire.

The traveller looked at the clock.

Mother Mac advanced on Old Jack, her apron shaking.

“Jack Murray, ye grey-headed villain! After all I’ve done for ye! Fed ye when ye were hungry, patched yer shirt, let ye sleep in the shed, an’ now ye come here with a wicked fabrication to ruin a lonely widow!”

“But I owes you fourteen bob.”

“Ye owe me nothing!”

“Yes, I do.”

“Nothing!”

Jack took out the coins.

“Three drinks one night, an’ a bottle—”

“Put away yer blood-money!”

“It’s good money,” said Jack, becoming distressed. “Harry lent it me.”

“Then give it back to Harry, an’ may it burn holes in both yer pockets! Rum under the flour, indeed! Brandy behind the clock! Go on with ye! Out!”

She opened the door.

Jack looked at Harry; but Harry’s face was hidden by tobacco smoke.

“Out, I tell ye! Never darken me door again!”

Old Jack pulled his hat down, tucked the money away, and went out. He did not slam the door. Poor devils like Jack seldom slam doors; they have had too many closed against them.

The others followed presently. The traveller thanked Mother Mac for the coffee in a frosty manner. Harry harnessed up. As he climbed to the seat, Mother Mac stood in the doorway, broad, upright, and respectable.

“Good night, Mrs MacNamara,” said Harry.

“Good night, Misther Chatswood,” she replied, with tremendous dignity. “An’ tell yer Government friend he’s welcome to search the premises whenever he likes.”

The traveller opened his mouth, but the cart jolted forward before he could answer.

Old Jack sat behind the sample-cases. He was silent for two miles.

Then he said:

“Harry?”

“Yes?”

“Do I owe her fourteen bob or don’t I?”

“You do.”

“She said I don’t.”

“She was excited.”

“Will I take it back?”

“Not tonight.”

Jack considered this until Cobble Creek. Confusion hurt him more than anger would have done.

Mother Mac, meanwhile, cleared the cups, bolted the door, fed the dogs, and restored the dark bottle to its place behind the flour. She told herself that Jack was an ungrateful old liar and that she had done perfectly right. Then she remembered his face when she ordered him out.

She sat by the fire.

The hut was very quiet after travellers left. Wind worried the bark roof, the clock ticked crookedly, and something small scratched inside the wall. Mother Mac stared into the coals, still holding the dishcloth.

Two big tears rolled down her cheeks—lonely tears, if you understand me; for there are tears caused by grief, and tears caused by temper, but the worst come when a person suddenly hears how empty a hut can be.

“Poor owld Jack,” she whispered. “An’ him tryin’ to pay honest.”

Harry would have been sorry if he had seen her. Harry was not cruel; only humorous at the wrong distance.

Next morning a boundary-rider stopped for breakfast.

“Who was the swell with Harry yesterday?” Mother Mac asked casually.

“Commercial traveller.”

She turned slowly.

“Phwat?”

“Drapery man. Carries ribbons, stockings, collars, that sort of thing.”

“Not excise?”

“Excise!” The rider laughed. “Harry been stuffing you?”

Mother Mac said nothing for a full half-minute, which was a district record. She calculated what the traveller might have drunk, what Harry and Jack would have drunk with him, what they might have bought for the road, and what the damage was to her feelings besides. Then she remembered Old Jack driven into the cold with fourteen shillings in his pocket and shame in his heart.

She took the long-handled shovel and struck the hearth so hard that sparks flew up the chimney.

“Harry Chatswood,” she said, “I’ll be even with ye if I wait till the mail coach runs on wings!”

Old Jack never came back with the fourteen shillings. Perhaps he paid Harry; perhaps he lost the money; perhaps—damfiknow. But Mother Mac forgave him before noon, though he was not there to benefit by it.

As for Harry, she got even with him later, and got even handsomely. It involved a constable, a dead pig that was not dead enough, six bottles labelled cough mixture, and Harry’s yellow spring-cart in the creek.

But that is another yarn, and longer than this one.

Time for a last smoke before we turn in.

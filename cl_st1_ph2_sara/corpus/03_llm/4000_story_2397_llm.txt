# Traditions of Lough Guir

In my young days, before the new road had cut the shoulder of Knockadoon, and while certain wooded islands, now joined to the mainland by reeds and meadow-grass, still showed their dark reflections in the waters of Lough Guir, I passed some weeks of every summer in that pleasant but melancholy country. The visits were made at an age when one accepts a legend with the same unquestioning appetite with which one receives a slice of seed-cake; and I confess that, even now, after the lapse of more years than I care to reckon, the remembrance of those evenings returns to me with a vividness denied to many graver passages of my life.

Our nearest neighbours were the Miss Bailys—Anne and Susan—two maiden ladies of an ancient stock, who occupied a low, irregular house standing among ash-trees at the eastern side of the lake. Their family, though never powerful in the sense in which the Desmonds were powerful, had been seated there for generations, and had contrived, through confiscations, disturbances, bad marriages, and worse lawsuits, to retain a moderate property and an immoderate collection of traditions.

Miss Susan was the younger, though strangers generally supposed the contrary. She was little, brisk, and charitable, wore mittens even in warm weather, and possessed an extraordinary knowledge of preserves, medicines, poultry, and the deficiencies of neighbouring servants. Miss Anne was taller and more ceremonious, with a narrow, handsome face, very dark eyes, and a singular side-long glance which, when she came to the terrible point of a story, had in it something both playful and alarming. I see her still, seated upright beside the drawing-room fire, one thin hand resting upon the head of her ebony cane, while the other, with uplifted finger, prescribed silence to us children.

Their drawing-room was a long apartment with three windows, a Turkey carpet worn almost white in places, and a multitude of objects which seemed to us of great antiquity. There were miniatures in tarnished gold frames, a sword said to have been carried at Aughrim, two little silver cups with lids, and a set of greenish drinking-glasses, fantastically twisted in the stems, which had, as Miss Anne assured me, belonged to her grandfather’s grandfather. I was permitted once to hold one of these glasses. It was lighter than I had expected, and this circumstance, trifling as it is, remains among the material evidences which attach those remote evenings to actual life.

From the upper windows one looked across orchard and pasture to the lake, whose colour changed with every motion of the sky. On clear evenings it was a pale mirror, with the hills lying upside down within it; under rain it became black, fretted, and immeasurably old. Beyond a belt of alders rose the castle—not the inhabited house, but the older stronghold—a gaunt and much-battered pile, with one tower broken and a great hall still roofed in Miss Anne’s childhood, though scarcely so in mine. Its walls were immensely thick; the narrow arched doorways seemed made for men of another stature; and in the deep window recesses a child might sit entirely hidden from anyone crossing the chamber.

The country people called its principal apartment the Earl’s Hall. Whether an Earl of Desmond ever lodged there is, I believe, doubtful; but antiquarian caution has little authority over popular nomenclature, and the whole neighbourhood was haunted by that illustrious and unfortunate house. Every ruined wall had sheltered a Geraldine, every subterranean passage led to one of their castles, and every unusual rider seen by moonlight was, sooner or later, identified with the great magician earl.

It was from Miss Anne that I heard the following particulars. She had them partly from her grandmother, partly from an old herd named Brian Shea, and partly, I think, from a woman who had been servant to her mother. I do not pretend to reconcile every variation. In such histories the disagreement of witnesses is sometimes a proof, not of falsehood, but of antiquity.

## The Earl and His Young Countess

The Earl of Desmond, according to this tradition, was not merely learned beyond the ordinary measure of noblemen, but possessed those secret arts which are acquired at great hazard and seldom relinquished without a heavier one. He had travelled in foreign countries, conversed with astrologers, and read books whose letters moved upon the page when an unlicensed eye regarded them. He knew the virtues of stones, the speech of birds, the courses of hidden water, and the names by which spirits of the air may be constrained.

Late in life he married a lady of extraordinary beauty, scarcely seventeen years of age. She was proud, affectionate, curious, and accustomed to have every wish obeyed. For some time she heard rumours of her lord’s powers from servants and dependants, who would cease speaking whenever she entered. At first she laughed; afterward she questioned; and at last she became offended that her husband should conceal from her a part of his nature known, as she supposed, to every groom and kitchen-maid in the castle.

“Show me,” she said, “one proof of this famous learning.”

The earl refused with gentleness.

She renewed her request. He again refused, and told her there were appearances which the senses could endure, though the reason knew them to be harmless, and others which, being once admitted to the imagination, might trouble it until death.

This warning, as warnings commonly do with the young and imperious, increased her desire. She wept, accused him of distrust, and declared that, if his art were lawful, there could be no danger in beholding it, and, if unlawful, she had a wife’s right to know the peril in which she lived.

At length the earl consented.

He led her at midnight into a lofty chamber overlooking the lake. The walls were hung with dark arras; a lamp burned upon a stone table; and all doors were fastened. Before beginning, he required of her a solemn promise that, whatever shape she saw, whatever sound she heard, she would neither scream nor utter a word. Speech, he said, would break the governing charm and deliver them both to a power which he might invoke but could not then withstand.

She promised readily.

He removed his cloak, drew a circle upon the floor, and stepped within it. For a little while nothing occurred. Then the lamp sank to a blue spark, though there was no wind, and the earl appeared to diminish. His shoulders narrowed; his garments clung to him and became glossy; feathers sprang in a ghastly succession along his arms and breast; his nose curved to a hooked beak; and where the grave and courtly gentleman had stood, a great black bird beat its wings against the dusky air.

The countess clasped her hands, but remembered her oath.

The bird rose, circled the room, and descended within the ring. In an instant it was gone, and there crouched in its place a monstrous hound, long-bodied, yellow-eyed, and foam-lipped, with teeth that shone like wet ivory. It gathered itself and sprang toward her, but the invisible boundary checked it. Its paws struck the stones; its tongue quivered; its eyes, enormous and almost human in their malice, never left her face.

Still she was silent.

The hound melted downward, as wax sinks before heat, and became a serpent, green and gold, whose folds multiplied until they filled the circle. Its head rose to the height of a man’s breast. It swayed before her, opened a pale mouth, and hissed her Christian name.

She trembled, but did not speak.

Then, after several other transformations which Miss Anne enumerated—and among them a wolf, a skeleton wrapped in a friar’s habit, and a little child with the face of an aged man—the earl resumed his own appearance.

The countess laughed from relief, and would have run to him; but he raised his hand.

“One trial remains,” he said. “It is the shortest.”

He became a column of fire. It ascended almost to the roof, making the silver clasps of the hangings shine and reddening every stone in the chamber. In the heart of the flame his face appeared, calm and sorrowful. Then the fire fell suddenly inward; the room was dark; and from above came a rushing, leathery sound.

A creature dropped at her feet.

It was neither beast nor bird, though it possessed something of both: a grey, shrunken body, arms prolonged like wings, yellow skin hanging in folds, a round head without hair, and great lidless eyes. It came crawling over the stones, not quickly, but with a dreadful certainty, raising one hand after another. When it reached the hem of her gown, it looked up and grinned.

The countess screamed.

At that cry there arose beneath the castle a noise like thunder confined underground. The floors heaved; bells rang without hands; doors flew open; and through passage, gallery, kitchen, chapel, and stable ran one universal shriek. The earl stood again before his wife, but his face had changed.

“You have broken the spell,” he said.

Before she could answer, the chamber sank. Tower, battlement, hall, household, horses, hounds, men-at-arms, serving-women, chaplain, cook, and scullion descended together, not in ruin and confusion, but entire, as a ship settles beneath still water. The lake closed above them. By dawn nothing remained upon the shore but scattered stones and a long line of bubbles rising among the reeds.

This catastrophe, I need hardly observe, cannot be made consistent with the visible castle; but Miss Anne explained that the ruins we knew belonged to an outer building, while the enchanted residence stood farther toward the water. Indeed, in a dry season some foundations may yet be traced below the surface, although whether they are masonry or natural rock I shall not decide.

The earl and his household remain under the servitude of a strong spell. Once in seven years, upon a night which no almanac has precisely fixed, the castle rises—not visibly to all, but to those whom destiny or misfortune conducts near the place. Its doors open beneath the water; lights move in the depths; and the earl rides forth upon a milk-white horse, followed by his countess, his officers, his musicians, his hounds, and the entire retinue.

The horse is shod with silver. When those shoes are worn through, the enchantment will end; but the silver suffers little from earthly roads, and the earl must continue his nocturnal journeys until the last thin fragment falls away. Some say he rides round the lake; others, that he passes from Lough Guir to the Galtees and back before cockcrow. All agree that no mortal must speak to him. He desires above everything to obtain an answer, for a willing word addressed to him transfers part of the speaker’s remaining years to the spell and preserves the shoes from wear.

The manner of his solicitation is courteous. He does not threaten. He salutes the traveller, asks the road, inquires the hour, or lets fall some valuable object, which he begs the witness to restore. Courtesy itself becomes the snare.

## Teigue O’Neill and the Silver Shoes

Teigue O’Neill was a smith who lived near the old road, in a cabin with a forge at one end and sleeping-room at the other. He was a sober man, though fond of company on fair-days, and had the reputation of being fearless except where fairies were concerned. Upon those subjects he was prudent, which is perhaps a better quality.

One autumn night he remained at work long after his neighbours had gone to bed, mending the iron hoop of a car-wheel for a farmer who required it at sunrise. The fire burned red; the door stood open to cool the forge; and each stroke of his hammer sent an echo over the silent fields.

Between two blows he heard music.

At first he thought it came from a distant house, but it approached too rapidly. There were trumpets, small silver bells, and the measured beat of many horses. Teigue dropped his hammer. He remembered that it was the seventh year since old Nora Crone had seen lights moving under the lake.

He extinguished the forge-fire, drew the door nearly shut, and peered through the crack.

Along the road came a grand-looking company, every horse white, every rider richly dressed, and yet the moon shone through them faintly, as through breath upon glass. Their bridles glittered; plumes nodded without wind; and the hoofbeats, though numerous, were so light that the loose stones did not stir.

At their head rode the earl. His face was handsome and pale, his cloak black velvet, his gloves embroidered in silver. As he passed the forge he turned his eyes directly upon the hidden smith.

One of the white horse’s shoes came loose and rang upon the road.

“Good fellow,” said the earl, “you are of the trade. Will you set me this shoe?”

Teigue held his tongue.

The earl dismounted and led the horse to the door. One of its silver shoes was indeed hanging by a single nail. The animal looked gentle, but from its nostrils came a vapour cold enough to whiten the grass.

“Name your price,” said the earl.

Teigue pressed both hands upon his mouth.

The earl smiled, drew out a purse, and shook it. Gold pieces sounded within. Then he stooped, picked up the smith’s hammer from where it lay near the threshold, and held it toward him.

“Is this yours?”

Teigue afterward declared that the question almost surprised an answer from him. He felt the word rise to his lips, but at that instant the horse stamped. The loosened silver shoe turned, and on its inner surface he saw the print of five scorched fingers.

He crossed himself.

The whole retinue wheeled about. The music became a wild screaming; cloaks, plumes, and pale faces streamed backward; and the cavalcade departed at a hurry-scurry gallop toward the lake. The white horse went last, striking sparks of blue fire from the road. At the water’s edge riders and horses plunged together. For a moment their lights moved under the surface.

Then all was black.

In the morning Teigue found the grass before his forge rimed with frost, though the day was mild. The mark of the silver shoe remained upon the threshold for many years. Miss Anne had seen it as a girl, or believed she had; it disappeared when the stone was replaced.

## Moll Rial’s Ring

Moll Rial, who served in the Baily household during the time of Miss Anne’s mother, was a cheerful, round-faced girl, industrious, shy with strangers, and not at all disposed to tales of terror. She had been beetling linen by the water—a laborious operation, now nearly forgotten, in which the wetted cloth was laid upon a smooth stone and beaten with a wooden implement to improve its surface—and remained later than usual because several sheets were required for guests expected on the following day.

Twilight had faded when she gathered the linen into a basket. As she crossed the narrow field between the lake and the orchard, she saw a gentleman standing beside the path.

He was tall and gracious, dressed in an old fashion, with a short cloak and a hat looped by a jewel. Moll supposed him one of the visitors, though she wondered how he had come there without passing the house.

“You have worked late, my pretty maid,” said he.

She curtsied but made no answer.

“Your burden is heavy. Allow me.”

He lifted the basket before she could prevent him and walked at her side. His manner was so agreeable, and his conversation—of which Moll could afterward remember nothing—so diverting, that her alarm subsided. At the orchard gate he placed the basket upon the wall.

“You have lost a ring,” he said.

“No, sir,” answered Moll, forgetting all caution. “I never owned one.”

“Then you must take mine.”

He drew from his finger a broad gold ring, engraved with a coronet, and held it upon his palm. Moll, dazzled by its beauty and unwilling to appear ungrateful, extended her hand.

At that instant a red light touched his face.

She looked toward the lake.

The whole expanse, which a moment before had been dark, appeared crimson—an estuary of blood stretching from shore to shore. Black shapes moved beneath it; towers, pinnacles, and chimney-stacks rose upside down in the depths; and over the surface came a procession of drowned faces, all with open eyes.

Moll recoiled. The gentleman still smiled, but his hand had grown long and grey, the nails curving over the ring.

She invoked the sacred name.

There was a clap like the shutting of a vast door. The stranger vanished. The basket fell from the wall, and the clean linen was scattered in the mud. From the lake came one prolonged sigh, after which its natural colour returned.

Moll reached the house in such disorder that she was at first unable to speak. Miss Anne’s mother reproved her severely for the condition of the sheets; but when the girl described the coronet upon the ring, an old servant began to cry.

“Here had been the earl himself.”

## The Music at the Castle

Of the other occurrences related by Miss Anne, one belonged to her own experience, and she always recounted it without embellishment.

She and Susan had an elder sister, Margaret, who died while still a young woman. During the last nights of her illness the household took turns watching beside her. The sick-room became oppressive toward morning, and Anne and Susan, then little more than girls, went out for air.

The moon was hidden, but the night was clear enough to show the castle against the paler sky. They walked in the garden, speaking in whispers, when music arose from the direction of the Earl’s Hall.

It was not, Miss Anne said, like any rural instrument, nor like singing heard from a distance. It resembled an organ in a great cathedral, accompanied by voices so numerous and mournful that the sound seemed to fill earth and air. The melody rose slowly, paused, and recommenced, always approaching but never becoming louder.

The sisters stood hand in hand. Neither proposed going toward the castle. After some minutes the music ceased in the middle of a prolonged note.

There was no answer from the night.

When they returned, they found that Margaret had awakened and asked who was singing in the garden. Before sunrise she died.

The old people called the music the Baily banshee. Miss Anne, in later life, allowed that fatigue, grief, and the distant movement of wind through ruined chambers might account for it. Susan, who was less imaginative in ordinary matters, would never permit such an explanation.

“We both heard the tune,” she once said to me. “And Margaret heard it too.”

## The Governess’s Dream

The governess of the Baily girls was a Miss Ellinor Vane, the daughter of a clergyman in the north. She was a grave, conscientious woman, no longer young, with an excellent hand, a fondness for history, and, so far as anyone knew, no taste whatever for supernatural fancies.

For three nights she dreamed the same dream.

A man in an old brown coat came to her bedside and said: “There is treasure hidden in the castle. If it is not removed before the north wall falls, it will be lost forever.”

On the first occasion she attributed the vision to indigestion and mentioned it to nobody. On the second, the man added directions. She was to enter the Earl’s Hall, stand with her back to the western fireplace, proceed twelve paces toward the narrow window, turn to the left, measure seven feet along the wall, and raise the third flagstone from the angle.

On the third night he repeated all this, but angrily.

“You have delayed,” he said. “Take witnesses, if you distrust me; only dig.”

Miss Vane related the dream at breakfast. The elder Mr. Baily laughed, but, being of an antiquarian disposition and curious concerning every feature of the castle, consented to inspect the place.

The expedition was conducted with more solemnity than anyone had intended. Mr. Baily carried a measuring-line; his steward brought crowbars and a spade; Miss Vane held a lantern; and several servants followed at a respectful distance.

The Earl’s Hall was then in better preservation. A portion of its roof remained, though ivy had penetrated between the stones, and rain lay in green pools upon the floor. There were two fireplaces, one of great breadth and another apparently constructed at a later period. The western one could still be distinguished by its carved lintel, on which appeared an animal—lion, boar, or wolf; I forget which—worn nearly smooth.

Miss Vane placed herself with her back to it and repeated the directions. Twelve paces brought her near the narrow window. Seven feet to the left indicated a flagstone differing little from the others, except that one corner bore a shallow circular mark.

The servants set to work.

At first the stone resisted. Its joints were clogged with earth, and the crowbars slipped more than once; but after half an hour it rose with a cavernous sound, disclosing compact soil. They dug three feet and encountered masonry. Mr. Baily, now thoroughly interested, ordered the opening enlarged. Beneath the soil appeared a flat slab, carefully fitted. This also was raised.

A small stone chamber lay below.

It was perhaps four feet deep, three broad, and lined throughout with dressed limestone. In the dust at the bottom stood the perfect impression of a vessel—a round base, swelling sides, and two projecting handles. Green stains marked the place where metal had rested. There were also scraps of decayed sacking and one small link of a bronze chain.

Alas! it was empty.

No opening could be found except the one they had made. The walls were sound, the floor unbroken, and the covering slab did not appear to have been moved for a very long time. Yet something had unquestionably stood within the cavity and had as unquestionably been removed.

Mr. Baily suggested that workmen repairing the hall in the previous century had discovered the deposit and concealed the fact. The steward thought smugglers had entered through a subterranean passage, though none was found. The servants preferred to believe that the dead man of the dream had arrived too late: some other dreamer had received his instructions in an earlier generation and secured the treasure.

Miss Vane said little. The failure affected her strangely. She examined the empty chamber again and again, as if the absence of what she had expected were more disturbing than its presence could have been.

For several weeks nothing further occurred.

## The Little Man in Red

One afternoon in November, Miss Vane went alone to the castle. Her purpose, as she afterward stated, was to copy the device over the western fireplace for a book of family memoranda. The day was damp and windless. A yellow fog lay upon the lake, and the weak light entering the hall seemed unable to reach its farther extremity.

She seated herself in a deep window recess, with paper upon her knee. The place was very still. Drops fell at intervals from the broken roof, striking the stones with distinct, hollow taps.

While she worked, she became aware that someone was standing at the opposite end of the hall.

It was a little man dressed entirely in red.

His coat was scarlet, with narrow skirts; his stockings, shoes, and cap were of the same colour. His face was brown and withered, his mouth extremely wide, and his eyes small, black, and bright. He stood near the excavated flagstone, which had not then been replaced, and regarded her without movement.

Miss Vane supposed at first that he was some deformed countryman who had entered without her hearing him. She asked what he wanted.

There was no answer.

The little man raised one finger and beckoned.

She remained seated.

He beckoned again, and at the same time pointed toward the narrow arched doorway leading to the spiral stair. His gestures were deliberate, imperious, and accompanied by a smile that displayed a multitude of very small teeth.

Miss Vane rose. She afterward declared that she did not intend to obey him; yet she crossed the hall.

As she approached, he receded without turning. His red shoes made no sound. He reached the doorway and passed beneath the arch, still facing her, though the stair beyond was narrow and steep.

Miss Vane followed as far as the threshold.

From below came a cold current of air, carrying an odour of wet earth and something older and more oppressive—the exhausted air, she said, of a chamber long sealed. The little man stood three steps down. He beckoned once more.

Then she perceived that his arms, though folded close while he stood in the hall, were of an unnatural length. One hand rested upon the stair below his feet; the other reached upward and touched the crown of the arch. His smile widened. Behind him, in the darkness, something heavy began to breathe.

Miss Vane uttered a cry and tried to turn. The hall seemed to lengthen before her; the window became a remote point of grey light; and the figure sprang upward, not touching the intervening steps.

She remembered no more.

The servants, alarmed by her continued absence, found her at twilight lying across the doorway of the spiral stair. Her papers were scattered through the hall. On one sheet she had begun a careful drawing of the carved device; across it, in red chalk which no one could identify, was the rude outline of a little man with one arm stretched above his head.

When consciousness returned, Miss Vane insisted that she had received warning of her death.

The family endeavoured to reason with her. Mr. Baily maintained that a wandering beggar had frightened her; the physician spoke of vapours, solitude, and the consequences of prolonged nervous excitement over the dream and excavation. But she could not be persuaded. A fever declared itself that night. For several days she repeated the measurements—twelve paces, seven feet, the third stone—and begged them not to follow the red guide below the stair.

She died before the week was out.

Whether the apparition foretold her death, occasioned it through terror, or was itself the first image of a fever already upon her, I cannot say. Miss Anne offered all three possibilities, though her uplifted finger and dark side-long glance showed plainly which she favoured.

The spiral stair was afterward examined. It descended to a vaulted room, half-filled with fallen stone and wholly innocent of treasure. No subterranean passage to the lake was discovered; no red chalk lay among Miss Vane’s possessions; and no little man answering the description was known in the parish.

The castle has altered greatly since then. Part of the Earl’s Hall fell in a winter storm; the western fireplace is buried; and the narrow doorway, I am told, has been blocked for the safety of cattle. The Baily house also has passed into other hands. Its orchard is gone, the old glasses were dispersed at auction, and the shore where Moll Rial beetled the linen is now soft ground overgrown with rushes.

Yet Lough Guir preserves, even under the candid light of noon, an aspect favourable to ancient recollection. At evening the hills lean darkly over it; islands appear where none were visible by day; and beneath the quiet water one may sometimes discern long wavering lines, like walls and towers disturbed by an unfelt wind.

I once asked Miss Anne whether she believed that the silver shoes of the earl’s horse would ever be worn through.

“My dear,” she replied, “all shoes wear through at last.”

Then, after looking toward the lake, she added:

“But silver is a patient metal.”

I

The track from Ryan’s selection to Wylie’s place ran twelve miles through box country, then dipped suddenly between two black ridges into Deadman’s Hollow. There the stringy-barks grew close and crooked, as if they had crowded down to look at something hidden in the gully. In summer the creek was a chain of rocky waterholes, green at the edges and black in the middle. Cattle would drink from them; horses, unless driven, went thirsty.

Mary Wylie’s mare stopped at the first pool.

“Go on, Bess.”

The mare stretched her neck, sniffed, and turned one mild eye upon Mary. It was almost sympathy. Mary struck her lightly with the end of the reins.

“Don’t be foolish. It’s near dark.”

A wallaby broke from the bracken and went thumping uphill. Farther along, three kangaroos stood against the fading sky, their ears pointed, their bodies grey and still. Then they moved without sound among the saplings.

Mary was seventeen, though trouble had taught her the caution of an older woman. She was tall and slight, and had a face that might have been beautiful in a place where there were looking-glasses, clean dresses, and time for beauty. At Wylie’s there was a piece of cracked mirror no larger than a man’s palm, and Mary seldom looked into it.

She had been helping Mrs. Ryan with the washing. The Ryans paid her in flour, tea, and an old print dress altered from one of the daughters’. The flour was tied behind the saddle, the tea was in her pocket, and the dress lay across her knees beneath a sugar-bag lest the dust spoil it. She was proud of the dress. It was blue, with little white flowers—almost a town dress.

Beyond Deadman’s Gap the land opened into poor grazing country, stony and thinly grassed. Smoke hung above the Wylie homestead.

Mary sat straighter.

The chimney always smoked, but this was not chimney smoke. A red glow showed behind the yard. She put Bess to a canter, crossed the dry flat, and pulled up at the slip-rails.

The house consisted of two slab rooms, with a bark skillion leaning against the rear wall as if tired of standing. A sheet of flattened kerosene tin covered a hole in the roof. There was no garden now. The goats had eaten it during the drought, and Wylie had broken the fence for firewood.

Behind the hut a fire burned fiercely in a shallow pit. Wylie stood over it with a green branch, thrusting something down among the coals. His shirt was open; blood had dried black on his forearms. Jack, Mary’s younger brother, crouched beside the woodheap, watching.

Near the killing-pen lay the head and feet of a steer.

Mary slipped from the saddle.

“Whose beast is that?”

Wylie turned. His eyes were small and pale beneath his dirty brows.

“Mine.”

“It’s got Merton’s ear-mark.”

“Has it?” He looked at the head as though the discovery interested him. “Then Merton must’ve put his mark on my steer.”

Mary went closer to the pit. A rank smell of burning hair and hide came from it.

“You’re burning the brand.”

“Git inside.”

“Father—”

“Git inside, I tell yer!”

Mrs. Wylie appeared in the doorway, twisting her apron. She was a little, bent woman with colourless hair and hands swollen from work.

“Mary, don’t speak to him. Come in, dear. Come in now.”

“Where did he get it, Mother?”

“I don’t know. He says he bought it.”

“Bought it!” Mary looked from her mother to the burning hide.

Wylie threw down the branch. “Are yer callin’ me a liar?”

“No.”

“Yer are.”

“I only asked—”

He crossed the yard and caught her by the shoulder. His fingers went through the thin stuff of her blouse.

“You’re gettin’ too flash, miss. Ryan’s gals been puttin’ notions inter yer? Think yer too good for us?”

Mary’s face went white, but she did not cry out.

Mrs. Wylie fluttered between them. “Let her be, Bill. She’s brought flour. Look, Bill—flour and tea.”

He flung Mary aside. She struck the rails and would have fallen but for Bess, who stood at her back. The mare shifted and touched Mary’s sleeve with her nose.

“There,” said Wylie. “Even the blarsted horse reckons she ought ter mind her own business.”

Jack laughed because his father had laughed. He was thirteen, undersized, sly-eyed, and quick to side with whoever held the whip.

From within the hut came the crying of the two little girls.

“Shut them kids up!” Wylie roared.

Mrs. Wylie ran in. The crying ceased presently. A peaceful calm descended upon that happy home.

They ate boiled beef that night.

Mary would not touch it. Wylie noticed.

“Too proud fer meat?”

“I’m not hungry.”

“Then yer can go without.”

He and Jack fed the dogs under the table. The dogs swallowed greedily and fought over a piece of gristle. Mrs. Wylie chewed with difficulty and avoided Mary’s eyes. The children dipped crusts into the greasy liquor. On the clay floor by Mary’s foot lay a scrap of blue cloth with little white flowers.

After supper Wylie carried two quarters of beef into the skillion and covered them with ’possum skins. Mary lay awake on the bark bed she shared with the girls. Through the cracks between the slabs she saw the fire brighten, sink, and brighten again. Her father went out twice to stir it.

Always in trouble. They were always in trouble.

If she could only get away from the bush.

Dan Coyle had said he would take her when the next droving season ended. Dan was twenty-three and worked cattle from the western runs to the railway. He had a straight smile, clean hands when he came courting, and a red handkerchief that Mary had once washed and ironed beneath a hot pannikin. He spoke of Queensland, of towns where lamps burned along the streets, and once—even of the sea.

“You’ve never seen it, Mary?”

“No.”

“Blue clean to the sky. Looks like yer could ride over it.”

She had carried that sea in her mind ever since.

Near midnight a dog barked. Then another answered from the ridge.

Mary sat up.

Hoofbeats came along the track: steady, deliberate, several horses together.

Mrs. Wylie whispered from the other room, “Bill! Bill, wake up!”

Wylie was already awake. He stumbled into the kitchen, swearing, and dragged on his boots. Jack slipped from his bunk.

“Who is it?” Mrs. Wylie asked.

“How’d I know?”

The hoofbeats stopped at the slip-rails. A man called, “Police!”

Wylie stood perfectly still. Then he went to the back door.

Mary was there before him.

“You can’t run,” she said.

He struck her across the mouth.

By the time he reached the yard two mounted troopers had ridden round the hut. Their dark uniforms looked almost black in the firelight. Behind them was Mr. Merton and one of his stockmen.

The sergeant dismounted.

“William Wylie?”

“What d’yer want?”

“A steer was taken from Merton’s lower paddock yesterday.”

“Was it?”

Merton went straight to the killing-pen. “That’s the head.”

“Prove it,” Wylie said.

The stockman raked the embers from the pit. Beneath them lay a curled strip of hide. Fire had eaten most of it, but part of a brand remained.

The sergeant examined it.

“Clear skin, you said?” he asked Wylie.

“I bought the beast clear.”

“From whom?”

“A bloke.”

“What bloke?”

“How the hell should I know his name?”

The sergeant took out the handcuffs.

Mrs. Wylie began to moan. “Oh, Bill—oh, Bill, tell them. Tell them who you bought it from.”

“Hold yer noise.”

Jack had disappeared.

Mary noticed it while they were fastening her father’s wrists. She looked behind the shed, in the skillion, and towards the creek. Jack was nowhere. He had taken his blanket and Wylie’s old saddle.

Wylie was led to a trooper’s horse. He twisted round once.

“Mary!”

She thought he meant to say farewell.

Instead he shouted, “Feed them dogs, or I’ll skin yer when I git back!”

The troopers rode away with him between them. Their hoofbeats passed over the flat, climbed towards Deadman’s Gap, and died in the timber.

Mrs. Wylie sank down beside the slip-rails.

“What’ll we do?” she asked. “Mary, what’ll we do now?”

The fire in the pit gave a soft fall and sent up a cloud of sparks.

Mary watched them rise into the darkness.

“We’ll work,” she said.

She had plenty to moan about, but there was the morning to get through first.


II

Jack did not come back.

For three weeks Mrs. Wylie expected him at every sound. She set aside food for him, though there was scarcely enough for those at home, and each night she left the back door unfastened.

“He’s only frightened,” she said. “He’ll come when he knows.”

Mary knew better. Jack had inherited his father’s talent for leaving trouble to the women.

Wylie received eighteen months.

Mr. Merton allowed them to remain on the place, which belonged to him, and gave Mary occasional work at the homestead. This was called kindness. It was also cheaper than wages to a servant brought from town.

Winter came early. Rain entered through the bark roof and ran down the slabs. Mary stuffed the cracks with rags, patched the children’s clothes, cut wood, milked the cow, and walked twice a week to Ryan’s. Mrs. Wylie worked too, but fear had consumed what strength hardship had left her. She would stop with a dish in her hands and stare towards the road.

“Do you think they feed him proper in gaol?”

“Better than we eat here, likely,” Mary said.

“Oh, Mary, don’t speak hard of your father.”

Mary bit back the answer. Her mother’s loyalty was like an old tether: rotten, twisted, and still strong enough to choke her.

In August Tom returned.

He came at sunset carrying a bluey and leading a lame bay horse. He was twenty, broad-shouldered, dark, and handsome in the damaged Wylie fashion. He had been away nearly two years—shearing, fencing, fighting, and, by his own account, doing nothing a policeman could prove.

Mrs. Wylie cried against his coat.

“My boy! My poor boy!”

Tom looked embarrassed. “That’s all right, Mum. Don’t go on.”

For a while he was good to them. He repaired the roof, split posts, shot kangaroos, and brought flour from the township. He gave Mary a silver brooch shaped like a fern.

“Where did you get it?” she asked.

“Bought it.”

“With what?”

“Money.”

She looked at him.

Tom grinned. “Don’t start preachin’. I done a job drovin’ down Narrabri way.”

She wanted to believe him. Want was a poor witness, but often the only one available at Wylie’s.

For six weeks the place changed. There was meat in the safe, wood by the chimney, and sometimes laughter after supper. Tom played a cracked concertina he had carried home, and the little girls danced on the clay floor. Even Mrs. Wylie’s face seemed to fill out.

Then Tom began watching the road.

He slept with a revolver beneath his blanket. If a rider appeared on the ridge, he went into the scrub until the man had passed. He accused Mary of moving his saddle-bags and struck the younger child for touching his coat.

“Who’ve you been talkin’ to at Ryan’s?” he asked Mary one night.

“No one about you.”

“That ain’t what I asked.”

“Then ask what you mean.”

His expression altered.

“Getting clever, ain’t yer?”

“So Father used to say.”

Tom swept his pannikin from the table. Tea splashed across the floor.

“Don’t put me with him!”

Mrs. Wylie cried, “Tom, dear, she didn’t mean it.”

“She did.”

He went out and did not return until dawn.

The next week brought a letter for Mary. It had travelled through three stations and was dirty at the folds. She knew Dan’s hand before she opened it.

Her heart beat so hard that she went behind the cow-bail to read alone.

The letter was not from Dan. It was from his mate, Ned Parsons.

There had been a hold-up near Bourke. Two masked men had stopped the mail-coach. Shots were fired. Dan had been taken at a camp by the river, with a revolver and part of the money in his swag. Ned wrote that Dan said he was innocent. Ned hoped Mary would not take on.

Below the letter was a newspaper cutting.

Mary read the words again and again: ARMED ROBBERY—ARREST OF TWO DROVERS.

The world did not end. A magpie flew down and pecked in the cow-yard. The cow switched at flies. From the hut Mrs. Wylie called for kindling.

Mary folded the cutting.

She had dreamed herself beyond the ranges. Dan had held out his hand, and the sea had shone behind him, blue clean to the sky. Now a cruel hand had reached from the bush and dragged him back among the rest of them.

Perhaps he was innocent.

Her father had “bought” a clear-skin steer. Tom had “done a job drovin’.” Jack was only frightened. There were so many innocent men in the district that it seemed strange the gaols were full.

That evening Tom found her crying by the creek.

“What’s up?”

“Nothing.”

He snatched the letter from her.

She tried to recover it, but he held her off and read. Presently he gave a short laugh.

“Dan Coyle! Well, yer can pick ’em.”

Mary struck him.

Tom stared at her. For a moment she thought he would kill her. Then he thrust the letter into her face.

“Don’t go givin’ yerself airs over me again.”

He walked back towards the hut.

Mary remained by the dry creek until the moon rose. She wished Tom had hit her. Pain was clean; shame spread through the blood.

Dan received seven years.

After that Mary ceased speaking of leaving. Yet the thought remained, stripped of Dan and the sea and every tender foolishness.

Away from the bush.

Anywhere. With anyone. Only away.

In November two troopers rode up at noon.

Mrs. Wylie saw them first. She was plucking a fowl near the kitchen door. The bird dropped from her lap.

“Mary,” she whispered.

Tom was asleep in the skillion.

One of the troopers called, “Is Thomas Wylie here?”

Mrs. Wylie stood up. Her hands were covered with feathers and blood.

“What’s he done?”

“We only want to speak to him.”

“What’s he done? Oh, Mary, what’s he done?”

Tom came through the back of the skillion like a bullock breaking scrub. He had his revolver, but no boots. The trooper shouted. Tom fired once—not at them, perhaps, but near enough—and ran for the ridge.

Both troopers wheeled and galloped after him.

Mrs. Wylie clutched Mary’s arm.

“They’ll shoot him.”

“No.”

“They shot at him.”

“No, Mother.”

“They’ll hang him. They’ll take him like Bill. Oh, God, my boys! My poor boys!”

Her fingers tightened. Then her mouth opened and no sound came.

She fell beside the dead fowl.

Mary knelt and lifted her. Mrs. Wylie’s head rolled against her shoulder, light and loose. Her eyes were open, still fixed upon the ridge.

“Mother?”

There was no answer.

The children began screaming. The dogs barked at the confusion. Somewhere beyond the timber came another shot, flattened by distance.

Mrs. Wylie had feared death, the police, hunger, her husband, her sons, and the opinion of neighbours. In the end it was impossible to say which had killed her. They had all been working at it for years.


III

They buried Mrs. Wylie in the little cemetery near Ryan’s, between two children who had died before Mary was born. Mr. Ryan read from a prayer-book. Rain fell softly. The red earth clung to the spades and would not leave them.

Tom escaped.

For months there were stories. He had crossed into Queensland. He had drowned in the Macquarie. He was shearing under another name. He had joined Jack. He had been seen at every sly-grog shanty from the border to the ranges.

Mary listened to none of it.

The two little girls were taken by an aunt near Bathurst. The aunt could not take Mary; Mary was old enough to fend for herself. Thus charity knew its proper limits.

For a time Mary remained alone at the hut. She worked for the Ryans and slept with an axe beside the bed. The silence should have been a relief. Instead the house preserved every voice. Her mother whispered by the hearth. Tom moved in the skillion. Wylie shouted from the yard. At night Mary sometimes woke believing she heard Jack saddling the old horse.

She considered going to town. But town required money, references, decent clothes, and the courage to be looked at. Everyone within fifty miles knew the Wylies. Beyond fifty miles, the road was long.

Then her father came home.

His sentence had been shortened for good behaviour—a phrase which Mary found almost comic.

He arrived in a spring-cart driven by a red-faced woman in a feathered hat. A keg was tied beneath the seat. Wylie was thinner but otherwise unchanged. Gaol had not reformed him; it had merely interrupted him.

“Here she is,” he said, looking at Mary. “Still sulkin’.”

The woman laughed.

“This is Mrs. Pratt,” Wylie said.

Mary waited.

Mrs. Pratt was not his wife. Mrs. Wylie had been dead seven months.

He brought home a—another woman.

That was all.

Mrs. Pratt took possession of the kitchen, the bed, and the broken mirror. She had coarse yellow hair and a loud, pleasant manner when sober. She was seldom sober. Wylie and she drank from breakfast onward, quarrelled in the afternoon, and became affectionate at night.

Mary moved into the skillion.

“Yer ought ter be grateful,” Wylie told her. “Bella knows how ter make a place cheerful.”

Bella emptied ashes on the clay floor, broke Mary’s blue-flowered dress into rags, and fed the last of the tea to the kettle in one extravagant handful.

“Cheerful!” she cried, raising her pannikin. “Here’s to cheerful!”

Wylie drank.

Mary worked longer hours at Ryan’s. She returned after dark, cooked what food remained, and endured their talk. Mrs. Pratt asked questions about Dan Coyle.

“Fine sweetheart you picked. Seven years, was it?”

Mary said nothing.

“Runs in the family,” Wylie observed.

She turned on him. “You should know.”

The blow knocked her against the table.

Mrs. Pratt shrieked with laughter. “She’s got spirit, Bill!”

“I’ll knock it outer her.”

He tried. But spirit is not always destroyed by blows; sometimes it is driven inward, where it works against the heart.

Summer hardened the country. The grass whitened, the creek shrank, and the waterholes in Deadman’s Hollow became low and evil-looking beneath their rims of stone. Bushfires reddened the horizon. Smoke entered the hut and stayed there.

One Sunday Bob Bentley came along the track with his hawker’s wagon.

Bob was a round, dusty man whose beard seemed to have collected samples of every road he travelled. Pots, bridles, cloth, boots, tinware, patent medicines, and children’s toys hung from his wagon. Bells jingled over the patient backs of his two horses.

He pulled up when he saw Mary carrying water from the creek.

“Morning, miss. Any millionaires at home?”

“No.”

“Ah, then I’ve come to the right place. Millionaires always want credit.”

Despite herself, Mary smiled.

Bob’s eyes softened. He had known the Wylies for years, but had the decency not to show that he knew too much.

“Want a lift for the bucket?”

“I can manage.”

“So could a bullock, but we don’t admire him for it.”

He carried the bucket to the hut.

Wylie bought tobacco on credit and Mrs. Pratt bought a bottle of scent, which she poured on her dress to conceal the smell of rum. Bob gave Mary a ribbon.

“I can’t pay.”

“Old stock.”

“It isn’t.”

“Then it’s new stock I’m tired of looking at.”

The ribbon was green. Mary held it carefully.

“Do you go far?” she asked.

“Far enough. Across to Mudgee next, then south if the roads keep.”

“Do you ever go to the coast?”

“Once a year.”

“The sea?”

Bob nodded. “Big wet paddock. No fences.”

Wylie called from inside, “Stop yarnin’ to the girl!”

Bob’s expression changed, but only for an instant.

“You take care, miss,” he said.

Care! Mary had taken care all her life, and every disaster had found her.

After Bob left, Mrs. Pratt demanded the ribbon.

“It was given to me.”

“Yer don’t need ribbons in the skillion.”

“No.”

“Give it here.”

Mary put it inside her dress.

Mrs. Pratt slapped her. Mary caught the woman’s wrist.

For one second they stood so, surprised by Mary’s strength.

Then Wylie came from the hut.

He dragged Mary by the hair across the yard, kicked her when she fell, and threw her bundle after her.

“Git!”

She rose slowly. Blood ran from her temple.

“Where am I to go?”

“Ask yer gaol-bird sweetheart.”

Mrs. Pratt leaned against the doorway. “Or the hawker. She was makin’ eyes at him.”

“I wasn’t.”

“Liar!”

Wylie picked up a length of harness.

Mary backed towards the slip-rails.

“Git off my place,” he said. “If yer come back, I’ll flog yer till yer carnt stand.”

His place! The hut belonged to Merton. The table had been made by Tom. The curtains were Mary’s mother’s flour-bags. Mary had carried the water, cut the wood, patched the roof, buried the dead, and kept life crouching there long after it wanted to go. But it was his place.

She lifted her bundle.

Mrs. Pratt called after her, “Don’t forget yer ribbon!”

They laughed.

Mary walked towards the road.


IV

The sun went down in smoke.

Mary had no hat. Her bundle contained a petticoat, a comb with three teeth missing, Dan’s letter, and the newspaper cutting. She had neither money nor food.

At first she intended to go to Ryan’s. Then she imagined Mrs. Ryan’s tightened mouth, the daughters whispering, the careful question—What happened this time? Pity would be given, certainly. Pity, and a bed for one night, and advice about honouring her father.

She turned the other way.

Bob Bentley could not be far ahead. His horses travelled slowly. If she caught him before the Gap, she would ask him to take her on.

Not as his wife. Not as anything shameful. She would work. She could cook, mend, drive, reckon accounts. She would sit among the pots and bridles and ride beyond the district. Beyond the ranges. To the sea.

Away from the bush.

The track entered timber. Darkness gathered under the trees before it reached the sky. Mary walked faster.

A curlew cried from the gully.

She thought of her mother on the day of the arrest: What’ll we do now? They had worked. That was what women did when men brought home stolen cattle, warrants, fists, drink, bullets, disgrace. They worked until work wore through them. Then they died, and the men found other women.

Mary began to laugh.

The sound frightened her. She stopped and listened.

Hoofbeats came faintly from behind.

She turned.

Nothing showed on the road, but she heard them clearly: several horses together, steady and deliberate.

Troopers.

They were coming for Tom. Or Jack. Or Dan. Or Wylie. It did not matter whom they wanted; they always came through Mary to reach the guilty.

“No,” she said.

The hoofbeats grew louder.

She left the track and ran between the trees.

Branches caught her skirt. Loose stones rolled beneath her boots. She crossed a dry wash, climbed the far bank, and found herself on the road again where it bent towards Deadman’s Gap.

Ahead, a small light moved among the trunks.

Bob’s wagon!

“Wait!” she called.

The light vanished, then reappeared lower down. It might have been a lantern. It might have been the rising moon.

“Bob!”

Her voice went into the gully and returned thinly.

The hoofbeats were nearer now. She could hear harness, the clink of bits, a trooper calling her name.

Mary—Mary Wylie!

She ran downhill.

Deadman’s Hollow opened beneath her, deeper and darker than she remembered. The waterholes caught the moon in broken pieces. White stones lay along the creek like bones. A dead stringy-bark stretched one branch over the track, pointing.

At the first pool she stopped.

This was where Bess had refused to pass. She saw the mare’s mild eye, felt the warm nose at her sleeve.

“Go on, Bess,” she whispered.

There was no horse.

Mary stumbled forward. The green ribbon had worked loose from her dress and trailed over her shoulder. A branch snatched it away. She did not notice.

At the bend above the second pool, the wagon stood.

Bob Bentley had camped on a small flat. His horses were hobbled nearby; a fire burned between three stones. Bob was frying meat when Mary came out of the scrub.

He sprang up.

“Good Lord! Miss Wylie?”

“Take me with you.”

Her hair hung about her face. Blood had dried along her cheek. Her dress was torn, and her eyes had the fixed brightness of fever.

Bob put down the pan.

“What’s happened?”

“Take me away.”

“Sit down first.”

“No. We must go.”

“Where’s your father?”

“Behind me. They’re all behind me.”

Bob looked towards the road.

“Nobody’s there.”

“Can’t you hear them?”

He listened. The horses cropped dry grass. A night insect shrilled in the reeds.

“I don’t hear anything.”

“The troopers.”

“There ain’t any troopers.”

“They’re coming.”

Bob took a careful step towards her. “All right. Let ’em come. They won’t hurt you.”

Mary recoiled.

“They always hurt me.”

“Not you, miss.”

“You don’t know.”

“I know enough. Sit by the fire. I’ll make tea. Then we’ll talk.”

“Will you take me to the coast?”

“To the coast?”

“The sea. You said you go there.”

“When the season’s right, maybe. But tonight—”

“Tonight!”

Bob rubbed his beard. He was a decent man, but decency likes daylight, arrangements, and time to consider. A half-mad girl at a lonely camp was a different article from a ribbon given beside a homestead.

“I can take you to Ryan’s,” he said. “Or into the township. There’s Mrs. Bell keeps the hotel—”

“No!”

“Be sensible.”

Sensible. Careful. Patient. Good.

All the useful virtues had become instruments for fastening her to the place.

“You won’t take me.”

“I didn’t say that. I said we ought ter think.”

“They’re coming.”

“Who?”

She looked beyond him.

Three mounted figures stood among the trees.

Moonlight shone on their shoulders. Their horses’ nostrils smoked. The middle rider was her father with handcuffs on his wrists; on one side rode Tom barefoot, with the revolver; on the other was Dan Coyle in prison grey. Behind them walked her mother, wringing her apron.

Mary—Mary Wylie!

Mary screamed.

Bob turned. He saw only three kangaroos upright near the edge of the scrub.

“It’s all right!” he said. “Only roos.”

But Mary had already fled.

She ran down the creek.

“Come back!” Bob shouted.

He followed, crashing through the reeds, but he was heavy and the ground was broken. Mary moved with the speed of terror. Her bundle fell near the third pool. The comb, the letter, and the old newspaper cutting scattered among the stones.

Away from the bush.

The words beat with her feet.

Away—away—away—

The creek narrowed between walls of rock. She knew the place. All the children knew it and had been forbidden to come there. The last pool lay beneath a cliff at the head of the gully. No one had sounded its depth. In drought it remained black and full while the other holes soured to mud.

They called it Deadman’s Water.

Mary climbed the sloping rock beside it. Thorns tore her hands. Behind her Bob called again.

“Mary! Stop! There’s nobody after you!”

Nobody.

That was worse.

If no one followed, there was no one to flee from and nowhere to flee to. There was only herself: Mary Wylie, daughter of Bill Wylie, sister of thieves, promised to a robber, born in the bush and marked by it more surely than Merton’s steer had been marked by the iron.

She reached the ledge.

The moon had risen over the ridge. It spread a pale road across the water.

For one moment the pool was no longer ghastly. It was the sea Dan had told her of, blue clean to the sky. Beyond it were streets with lamps, clean rooms, music, ships with white sails. Her mother stood there young and straight. The little girls wore shoes. Bess waited in a green paddock. No troopers could cross that water. No name could follow.

Bob came into the open below.

“Mary, for God’s sake!”

She looked down at him.

“Will it take me away?”

He did not understand.

“Come off there. Give us yer hand.”

He began climbing.

From the ridge came the clatter of stones. The three grey shapes moved through the moonlight.

Mary heard bridles. She heard Wylie’s voice.

Git!

She raised both arms as if reaching towards someone on the other side.

“God help me,” she said.

Then she stepped from the cliff.

Bob saw the white of her face, the dark dress opening in the air, and one bare hand that seemed to wave farewell.

The pool received her with a single heavy splash.

For a little while circles widened beneath the moon. Bob scrambled to the edge, tore off his coat, and went into the water. He dived again and again. The pool was cold and deep, with ledges beneath and drowned branches that caught at his clothes. Once he thought he touched her hair. Once he came up calling her name as though she might answer from the rocks.

She did not rise.

At last Bob dragged himself onto the bank. He sat shivering, his hands bleeding, and stared at the black water.

Above him the bush was silent.

Then three kangaroos, startled by the splash, bounded across the ridge. For an instant they showed against the moon—three grey ghosts—and vanished beyond Deadman’s Gap.

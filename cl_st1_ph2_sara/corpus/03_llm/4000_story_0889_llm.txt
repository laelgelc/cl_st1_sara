In that portion of Long Island City where small cottages had broken out in rows like a municipal rash, Mr. Horace Braddy maintained a cellar.

He also maintained a wife, a furnace, a mortgage, seven geraniums, and a reputation for never having done anything sudden in his life.

The cellar was more useful than the geraniums and less influential than the wife. Into it descended all objects which Mrs. Braddy could neither employ nor endure: cracked preserve jars, a lawn mower with one philosophical wheel, two portraits of departed aunts, an army cot, a bust of Shakespeare with a chipped nose, and enough old magazines to educate a dentist’s waiting room for twenty years.

On a Saturday afternoon in October, Mr. Braddy went below to seek a storm window and found a bottle.

He stood looking at it.

Mr. Braddy was a large, soft, moon-faced man whose body appeared to have been designed for some more spacious planet. His pink cheeks, small blue eyes, and insignificant nose gave him the expression of a giant celluloid baby. At the rug counter of Hummidge & Beal’s Great Metropolitan Store, where for eleven years he had sold Axminsters, Wiltons, runners, mats, and fibre floor coverings, he inspired confidence in elderly ladies and amusement in elevator boys.

The bottle lay under a collapsed hamper. It was dark, cobwebbed, and sealed with red wax. From its neck hung a label on which could faintly be read:

**OLD HIGHLAND SPECIAL  
DISTILLED 1894**

Mr. Braddy’s heart performed a timid hop.

“Angelica!” he called.

Then he remembered that Angelica had gone to Brooklyn to visit her sister, who possessed three children, a Victrola, and opinions. He was alone.

This circumstance increased the bottle’s wickedness.

Prohibition had transformed liquor from a beverage into a branch of archaeology. Mr. Braddy had never approved of drinking, partly because Angelica did not approve of it, partly because his mother had once shown him a colored tract entitled *The Drunkard’s Children Cry in the Snow*, and chiefly because intoxication involved uncertainty. He distrusted uncertainty. He distrusted revolving doors, strange dogs, doctors, raises in rent, new soups, and any railroad train that did not stop at every station.

He carried the bottle upstairs with both hands, as though it were an infant of dubious parentage, and set it upon the kitchen table.

Then he telephoned Mr. Chauncey Lum.

Mr. Lum lived next door in a yellow cottage with green blinds, an architectural combination which he described as “snappy.” He was a shoe buyer for the Consolidated Family Boot Corporation and received eighty-five dollars a week, not counting Christmas considerations. He was narrow, alert, waspy, and apparently concealed in ambush behind a fierce pair of mustachios. In business he was said to possess judgment. At home he possessed Mrs. Lum.

Within four minutes he entered the Braddy kitchen without knocking.

“Whatcha got?” he demanded.

“I found this down cellar.”

Mr. Lum pounced upon the bottle, scraped the label with a thumbnail, held it to the light, shook it, sniffed the wax, and became solemn.

“Braddy,” he said, “d’you know what this is?”

“It says whisky.”

“Whisky!” cried Mr. Lum. “You call this whisky? You might as well call Niagara a leak. This, Braddy, is prewar Scotch. Private stock. Old Highland Special. Eighteen ninety-four! Why, Rockefeller couldn’t get this if he sent a vice-president after it.”

“You don’t tell me!”

“I do tell you. There’s a hotel on Forty-second Street where they’d pay forty dollars for that bottle and ask no questions.”

“Forty dollars!”

“Maybe fifty. Depends who’s thirsty.”

Mr. Braddy regarded the bottle with reverence and alarm. Fifty dollars was more than a week’s salary. He earned thirty dollars, which Hummidge & Beal’s gave him each Saturday in an envelope thin enough to be insulting.

“My Uncle Elmer might’ve put it there,” he said. “This used to be his house. He died in nineteen six.”

“Then he had foresight.”

“He was a quiet man.”

“All the smart ones are quiet till you open their cellars.”

Mr. Lum seated himself and drew from his pocket a long black cigar. It cost eleven cents, and he generally contrived to mention the price before the ash fell.

“Smoke?” he asked.

“No, thanks. Angelica says cigars make the curtains smell.”

“Braddy,” said Mr. Lum, “that’s your trouble.”

“The curtains?”

“Fear.”

Mr. Braddy blinked.

“You’re afraid of curtains. You’re afraid of your wife. You’re afraid of old Berger at the store. You’re afraid to take a chance. You’re afraid to ask for what’s yours. You go through life sayin’, ‘You don’t tell me,’ while other fellows do the tellin’ and collect the money.”

Mr. Braddy shifted uneasily. His chair creaked beneath the movement of his conscience and his avoirdupois.

“I wouldn’t say afraid.”

“What wouldja say?”

“Careful.”

“Careful!” Mr. Lum spat the word past his eleven-cent cigar. “A clam is careful. A tombstone is careful. Who ever heard of a careful captain of industry?”

“I never expected to be a captain.”

“Why not?”

“I get seasick.”

Mr. Lum dismissed this literal obstruction with a wave.

“Listen, Braddy. I been readin’ a booklet by W. Napoleon Griggs, the big efficiency expert. *The Man Who Acts*. Ever hear of it?”

“You don’t tell me!”

“There you go! First principle: Get busy. Second principle: Act. Third principle: Do something. Griggs says every man has a giant inside him.”

Mr. Lum surveyed Mr. Braddy.

“In your case there’s room.”

Mr. Braddy sighed. He had often suspected that something was wrong with his life. At forty-two he possessed a wife, a cottage, a job, and a digestion which objected to radishes. These were achievements, but none had been accomplished with a trumpet flourish. He had married Angelica because she had explained that he intended to. He had bought the cottage because the agent had already filled out the papers. He had entered Hummidge & Beal’s rug department as temporary Christmas help in 1911 and had lacked the aggressiveness to leave.

Eleven years later, he was still temporary in spirit.

“Take your salary,” continued Mr. Lum. “Thirty dollars! It’s a crime.”

“Mr. Berger says business is unsettled.”

“Business is always unsettled when you ask for money. They settle it when they pay dividends.”

“I did think of asking for a raise.”

“When?”

“In nineteen nineteen.”

“What happened?”

“There was a customer near his office.”

Mr. Lum slapped the table.

“Exactly! No nerve. No decision. You’ve let the demon Timidity sit on your neck till he’s made a sofa out of you.”

Mr. Braddy touched his neck protectively.

“I’ve always been that way,” he confessed. “When I was a boy I wanted to go off the high board. I never did. I wanted to make a speech at the Carpet Salesmen’s Benevolent Supper. I didn’t. And for twenty years I’ve wanted to take a Turkish bath.”

“Why haven’t you?”

“I don’t know where you put your clothes.”

Mr. Lum leaned forward. His mustachios pointed at Mr. Braddy like two accusing fingers.

“A man can do what he is a-mind to.”

“I suppose so.”

“Don’t suppose. Say it.”

Mr. Braddy looked at the bottle.

“A man can do what he is a-mind to,” he repeated weakly.

“Louder!”

“A man can do what he is a-mind to.”

“That’s the stuff! Now what are you a-mind to do?”

Mr. Braddy’s eyes remained on the red wax. The old liquor seemed to glow darkly within its glass prison. It represented sin, antiquity, fifty dollars, his dead Uncle Elmer, and a door upon unknown regions.

“I believe,” he said, “I’ll get drunk.”

Mr. Lum removed his cigar.

“What?”

“I said I believe I’ll get drunk.”

There are moments when empires alter their boundaries, when explorers sight continents, when timid nations cast aside treaties and advance beneath the banners of destiny. Mr. Braddy’s passage of the Rubicon occurred at fourteen minutes past four in a kitchen containing a cold gas stove, a calendar advertising linoleum, and three bananas.

Mr. Lum rose.

“Now you’re talking! Get glasses.”

“Angelica keeps the good ones—”

“Get jelly tumblers.”

Mr. Braddy got them.

The wax resisted. Mr. Lum attacked it with a bread knife. Chips flew.

Pat, pat, pat!

“Careful,” said Mr. Braddy.

“Careful nothing.”

The cork emerged with a moist, historic pop.

A fragrance, powerful and smoky, filled the kitchen. Mr. Lum inhaled with his eyes shut.

“Boy!” he whispered. “That’s the real article. That’s got authority.”

He poured two fingers into each tumbler. In Mr. Braddy’s glass, owing to the scale of the hand holding it, two fingers became a generous geological formation.

“What do we drink to?” asked Mr. Braddy.

“To nerve. To action. To the giant within.”

Mr. Braddy lifted his glass.

“I can and I will.”

They drank.

Mr. Braddy coughed. His eyes expanded. The whisky descended through him like a torch dropped down an elevator shaft.

“Zizzzzzz!” he said.

“Smooth,” declared Mr. Lum, whose eyes were watering.

“It burns.”

“That’s the age.”

“Does age burn?”

“Only the best age.”

They drank again to decision.

They drank to Mr. Braddy’s raise.

They drank to Mr. Lum’s success in footwear.

They drank to the memory of Uncle Elmer, who began as a quiet man and ended as a benefactor of mankind.

At five o’clock the bottle was half empty and Mr. Braddy had ceased to fear it. At five-thirty he had ceased to fear Angelica. At six he had ceased to fear anything except the possibility that the bottle might become empty before justice had been done to it.

Mr. Lum’s mustachios appeared to have relaxed. He stood with one foot on a chair and delivered a lecture upon Will Power, Great Men, and the Buying of Ladies’ Button Shoes.

“Napoleon,” he said, “wasn’t any bigger’n me.”

“Not sideways,” said Mr. Braddy, and laughed for three minutes.

“Whaddaya mean, sideways?”

“Nothin’. You’re a fine man, Chauncey.”

“You’re a fine man, Horace.”

“I always liked you.”

“Same here.”

“Even when you borrowed my stepladder.”

“Never had it.”

“You got it now.”

“Then why bring it up?”

“I’m not bringin’ it up. Can’t bring anything up. Fresh as a li’l’ daisy.”

“So’m I.”

At six-thirty they decided to go to Manhattan.

Why Manhattan was never clearly established. It lay across the river, glittering, sinful, and full of people who did not know Angelica. This was sufficient.

Mr. Braddy put on his derby at a conquering angle. Mr. Lum took the bottle, discovered it empty, and placed it tenderly beside the bananas.

“History,” he said.

“Uncle Elmer,” said Mr. Braddy.

They marched out.

The subway received them.

Mr. Lum paid two fares and attempted to enter through the exit. Mr. Braddy paid two more and became wedged in the turnstile. For thirty seconds his body was divided between civic jurisdictions. A guard approached.

“Back up!”

“Can’t,” said Mr. Braddy.

“Go ahead!”

“Can’t.”

“What’re ya doin’ in there?”

“Travelin’.”

“Didja enjoy the trip?”

Mr. Lum became bellicose.

“This gentleman is a taxpayer.”

“He looks like two.”

Mr. Braddy was eventually extracted by combined pressure from the guard behind and Mr. Lum in front.

Wop! Wop! Wop!

He shot through, recovered his derby, and shook hands with everybody.

On the train they sat beside an old woman with a parrot in a covered cage. The parrot said, “Shut up!” at intervals.

“A man can do what he is a-mind to,” Mr. Lum announced.

“Shut up!” said the parrot.

“Who asked you?”

“Shut up!”

Mr. Braddy leaned toward the cage.

“Bird,” he said confidentially, “you got no constructive outlook.”

At Grand Central they emerged into electric magnificence. Broadway blazed before them like a jeweler’s delirium. Signs crawled, flashed, spun, winked, exploded, vanished, and returned with improved selling arguments. Tooth powders twenty feet high promised romance. A lady in illuminated underwear advised economy. A river of motorcars honked its metallic chorus while newsboys cried scandal beneath the chariots of pleasure.

Mr. Braddy stopped on the sidewalk and spread his arms.

“N’Yawk!” he cried.

A messenger boy collided with him, rebounded, and used a phrase not found in W. Napoleon Griggs.

“Get busy!” commanded Mr. Lum. “Act! Do something!”

They did several things.

They bought cigars at a hotel counter. Mr. Braddy selected a fifteen-cent cigar because fear was dead and thrift temporarily stunned. He lit it at the wrong end, corrected the error, and thereafter carried it as a smoking baton.

They visited an Automat, where Mr. Braddy inserted a nickel into a slot marked **BEANS**, opened a neighboring compartment, and secured lemon pie. He regarded this as proof that machinery could be mastered by confidence.

They ate frankfurters, pie, beans, three kinds of pastry, and a dish which neither could identify but which Mr. Lum said was “probably Swiss.”

They rode upstairs in a hotel and downstairs in another hotel.

They asked a policeman where Broadway was while standing on it.

They applauded an electric sign for collar buttons.

Mr. Braddy purchased a newspaper from a boy and gave him a dollar.

“Change, mister?”

“Keep it. Buy a corporation.”

The boy examined him carefully.

“Gosh,” he said. “Didja escape alone?”

At ten o’clock Mr. Lum looked at a clock and became sober in one eye.

“Ten!” he exclaimed.

“Bright boy.”

“Mrs. Lum thinks I’m at the lodge.”

“Aren’t you?”

“No.”

“Then she’s mistaken.”

“You don’t know my wife.”

“I know wives.”

“You know one wife. I know Mrs. Lum.”

The distinction, though subtle, had weight. Mr. Lum’s self-help philosophy, capable of overthrowing business competitors and timid neighbors, melted before the approaching figure of domestic inquiry.

“I gotta go.”

“Go where?”

“Home.”

“Coward,” said Mr. Braddy.

Mr. Lum stiffened.

“Who’s a coward?”

“You are. Demon Timidity. Sofa on your neck.”

“That’s different.”

“All fear’s same.”

“Listen, Braddy, a man can be brave and still have sense.”

“Get busy. Act. Do something.”

Mr. Lum glared at the monstrous pupil who had swallowed his teachings, distorted them, and now returned them with interest.

“I’m goin’ home,” he said. “You better come.”

“I’m going to take a Turkish bath.”

Mr. Lum’s jaw descended beneath his mustachios.

“Now?”

“Now.”

“You’re in no shape.”

“Fresh as a li’l’ daisy.”

“You’ll get lost.”

“I can and I will.”

“You’ll boil.”

“A man can do what he is a-mind to.”

Mr. Lum took his arm.

“Horace, be reasonable.”

Mr. Braddy drew himself up. He seemed to rise in sections, like a soft pink office building under construction.

“For forty-two years,” he said, “I been reasonable. Where’s it got me? Thirty dollars a week and no Turkish bath. Good night.”

He turned and moved majestically down Forty-second Street.

Mr. Lum watched him go. For a moment the shoe buyer’s conscience struggled with his watch. Then the watch won.

“Don’t forget your clothes!” he shouted.

Mr. Braddy waved without turning.

The Imperial Turkish Bath occupied a basement beneath a billiard academy. Its sign showed a sultan reclining amid clouds, attended by three ladies whose costumes had been reduced by the management to the minimum necessary for advertising.

Mr. Braddy descended.

Behind a marble-patterned desk sat a young man with brilliant hair and the expression of one who had seen humanity without its collars.

“Bath?” asked Mr. Braddy.

“This ain’t the Aquarium.”

“I’d like a Turkish one.”

“Dollar twenty-five. Room’s extra. Rub’s extra. Shampoo’s extra. Sleep overnight, fifty cents.”

“Give me everything,” said Mr. Braddy. “I just bought a corporation.”

The clerk rang a bell.

A small attendant appeared. He had bowed legs, black hair flattened to a mirror, and a blue tattoo of an anchor upon one wrist.

“Al, take the gentleman to thirty-six.”

“Right this way, chief.”

Mr. Braddy followed Al into a corridor of curtained cubicles. Men in various stages of removal wandered about carrying towels. To Mr. Braddy, who had spent his life preserving the barriers between himself and the public, the spectacle was startling. A thin gentleman stood in nothing but spectacles, reading a racing sheet. A bald man brushed his teeth before a mirror with the sternness of a surgeon. Somewhere a shower hissed. Somewhere else a man sang “The Rosary” in a bass voice and an undetermined key.

Al pulled aside a curtain.

“Thoity-six. Take off everything. Wrap this around ya. Lock valuables in the box. Key goes on your wrist.”

Mr. Braddy entered.

The cubicle contained a narrow couch, a peg, a tin box, and an area of floor inadequate to his turning radius. He removed his derby and became uncertain.

“Everything?” he called.

“Everything.”

“Including shoes?”

“All of it, chief.”

Mr. Braddy undressed cautiously, folding each garment. His confidence had survived Broadway, but clothing was its armor, and with every piece removed the demon Timidity, believed slain, showed symptoms of resurrection.

He wrapped the sheet around himself.

The sheet did not go around.

It went partway around and stopped from exhaustion. Mr. Braddy pulled. The sheet resisted. He inhaled, diminished temporarily, and achieved an overlap of half an inch. When he exhaled, the arrangement opened like stage curtains.

“Al!”

The attendant appeared.

“This is too small.”

“That’s the biggest.”

“You must have something else.”

“I could give you two.”

“Do that.”

Mr. Braddy joined two sheets in an arrangement resembling the sails of a damaged yacht. He stepped into the corridor with his garments locked safely in the box and the key tied to his wrist.

“Where do I go?”

“Straight down, through the steam, into the hot room. Then shower, plunge, rub, rest. Can’t miss it.”

This assertion has been made concerning many roads which have subsequently become historic for the number of persons who missed them.

Mr. Braddy started down the corridor.

The Imperial Bath had been enlarged at various times by owners of differing ideals. Its passages curved, branched, narrowed, and returned upon themselves with the ingenuity of a rabbit warren designed by a committee. Curtains concealed cubicles. Doors opened into showers, toilets, linen closets, massage rooms, and one office in which a sleeping bookkeeper awoke to find Mr. Braddy in two sheets asking the way to Turkey.

“Other hall,” said the bookkeeper.

Mr. Braddy entered the other hall and found himself back at room thirty-six.

He tried again.

Pat, pat, pat went his bare feet.

He passed the spectacled gentleman, still reading the racing sheet.

“Steam room?” asked Mr. Braddy.

“Follow the arrows.”

Mr. Braddy found an arrow painted on the wall. It pointed left. He obeyed it, turned twice, and met another arrow pointing toward the place he had just left.

He obeyed that also.

Three minutes later he was back at room thirty-six.

Al saw him.

“What’sa matter, chief?”

“Nothing. Just looking over the place.”

“Steam’s that way.”

“I knew it.”

This time Al guided him to a heavy glass door.

“Through there.”

A white cloud pressed against the glass. From beyond came splashes, coughs, slaps, and voices. Mr. Braddy hesitated.

“How far through?”

“Twenty feet.”

“Can you see?”

“Sure, after a while.”

“After what while?”

But Al had departed.

Mr. Braddy opened the door.

Steam swallowed him.

It was not air. It was a hot, wet substance with hands. It seized his face, entered his nose, filled his eyes, and wrapped his body in a boiling blanket. He could see nothing. The tiled floor beneath his feet was slippery. Dim shapes shifted in the fog—pink mountains, pale islands, hairy promontories of humanity.

He advanced one step.

“Hot,” he said.

He advanced another.

His right foot struck a pail.

Clang!

“Hey!” cried a voice.

“Beg pardon.”

“Watch where you’re goin’!”

“I can’t see where I’m going.”

“Then don’t go there!”

Mr. Braddy turned to retreat, but the door had vanished. He moved sideways, collided with a seated bather, rebounded, trod upon another gentleman’s ankle, and sat abruptly in a basin.

“Ouch, me ankle!”

“Who pushed me?”

“Lay off me, guy!”

“Turn on that cold!”

“Who’s got the soap?”

Mr. Braddy rose like a flabby pink bear surprised in a geyser. His sheets had loosened. One dragged behind him. The other had climbed under his left arm and tied itself about his neck with malicious purpose.

He saw a blur which appeared to be the door and charged it.

Wop!

It was the wall.

He recoiled, changed direction, and found the door by falling against it. The door opened and discharged him into the corridor.

Al looked up.

“Through already?”

“I forgot something.”

“What?”

Mr. Braddy considered.

“My breath.”

He returned to room thirty-six and sat upon the couch. The couch protested in springs. His heart hammered. Steam rolled from him. He resembled a cauliflower removed from the kettle one minute before completion.

The demon Timidity sat beside him.

It was useless, the demon explained. Turkish baths were intended for men with narrow hips, geographical instinct, and no objection to appearing in public as nature’s rough draft. Mr. Braddy should dress, go home, and tell nobody. Angelica need never know. Mr. Lum could be informed that the bath had closed early.

This proposal was sensible.

For forty-two years Mr. Braddy had been sensible.

He looked at the key on his wrist.

“I can and I will,” he muttered.

He rose.

Pat, pat, pat.

Again he reached the glass door. Again the steam leaped upon him. This time he kept one hand on the wall and moved forward.

“Excuse me.”

“Watch it!”

“Coming through.”

“Not over me, you ain’t!”

He encountered a bench, followed it, and sat down accidentally between two enormous men. They were bald, scarlet, and motionless, like twin hams awaiting shipment.

“Crowded,” said one.

“Plenty room,” said the other, without moving.

Mr. Braddy tried to rise. His wet thigh adhered to the tile. He pulled.

Pock!

The release propelled him forward. He struck a man with a brush, who struck a man with a bucket, who dropped the bucket on a foot not his own.

Clang! Splash! Ouch!

“Who done that?”

“Big guy.”

“What big guy?”

“The biggest guy!”

Mr. Braddy crawled in what he believed to be the proper direction. Through the steam he glimpsed a brass handle. Salvation! He seized it, pulled the door open, and stepped through.

He was in a broom closet.

Two mops fell upon him.

He struggled free, emerged into the steam room, turned in confusion, and went back through the first door.

Al stared.

“Forget something else?”

Mr. Braddy clutched the remains of his dignity.

“Just testing the circulation.”

“Steam room’s got good circulation.”

“I noticed.”

Back in thirty-six, he sat again. Water ran from his hair, his nose, his elbows, and regions not commonly associated with drainage. His courage had become soluble and was washing away.

He could dress.

Yes.

There was no shame in abandoning a Turkish bath. Hannibal had turned back from Rome. Napoleon had withdrawn from Moscow. General Washington had occasionally gone in the opposite direction, and history called it strategy.

Mr. Braddy opened the tin box and reached for his undershirt.

Then he stopped.

The undershirt represented retreat. It was a white cotton flag of surrender.

He remembered Mr. Berger.

Mr. Berger was manager of the sixth floor at Hummidge & Beal’s. He was a dry, pointed man who wore gray suits and measured human value by sales slips. Twice a year he called Mr. Braddy into his office.

“Braddy,” he would say, “we must have more pep.”

“Yes, sir,” Mr. Braddy would reply.

“More initiative.”

“Yes, sir.”

“More aggressive contact.”

“Yes, sir.”

Then Mr. Braddy returned to the rug counter and waited quietly for customers to contact him.

Thirty dollars.

Thirty dollars after eleven years of Oriental patterns, lint, customers who wanted blue but not that blue, and women who ordered twelve rugs sent to Scarsdale so that they might reject thirteen.

He shut the box.

“No,” he told the undershirt. “Not this time.”

He gathered the sheets around his equator, tightened them with a square knot, and marched out.

Al whistled.

“Going back, chief?”

“Going through.”

“Attaboy.”

The words were not W. Napoleon Griggs, but they helped.

At the door Mr. Braddy paused only long enough to take a monumental breath. Then he entered.

Steam. Heat. Blindness.

He put his left hand to the wall.

Step.

“Coming through.”

Step.

“Look out!”

Step.

“You’re on my towel.”

“Take it with you.”

Step.

A body loomed. Mr. Braddy went around it.

A bench attacked his shin. He climbed over it.

Someone pushed him.

He pushed back.

The someone, unprepared for moral regeneration weighing two hundred and sixty-eight pounds, disappeared with a splash.

“Hey!”

“Get busy!” cried Mr. Braddy.

A wet brush struck his shoulder.

Pat!

A bucket grazed his knee.

Clang!

His sheets caught upon a faucet and flew behind him like the banners of a victorious army. Mr. Braddy did not stop. Modesty was a luxury of the defeated.

Ahead, faintly, appeared a square of darkness.

The far door!

Between him and it sprawled one of the bald, hamlike men, now reclining across the path.

“Excuse me,” said Mr. Braddy.

“Go around.”

“There isn’t room.”

“Make room.”

In former days Mr. Braddy would have retreated, waited, apologized, or settled permanently in the steam. Now he placed both hands upon the obstruction.

“A man,” he said, “can do what he is a-mind to.”

He rolled the hamlike gentleman six inches.

“Hey! Lay off!”

Mr. Braddy stepped over him, reached the door, and burst through.

Cooler air struck him.

He had crossed.

He stood in the hot room, which was hotter than the steam room but dry enough to be recognized as part of the world. Three men lay upon shelves. An attendant glanced up.

“First time?” he asked.

Mr. Braddy gathered the remnant of a sheet from his shoulder and wrapped it about himself with imperial carelessness.

“No,” he said. “Not anymore.”

There followed showers hot, showers cold, a plunge which received his bulk with a report like a safe dropped into a canal, and a rubbing administered by a muscular young man named Gawge.

“Easy or strong?” asked Gawge.

“Strong.”

Gawge went to work.

Wop, wop, wop!

Mr. Braddy vibrated upon the slab.

“Too much?”

“More.”

Wop! Wop! Wop!

Gawge kneaded, slapped, twisted, pounded, and rolled him. Under ordinary circumstances Mr. Braddy would have apologized for causing so much labor. Tonight he accepted treatment like a Roman emperor under repair.

When at last he was wrapped in fresh sheets and placed upon a couch in the cooling room, he felt boneless, purified, and enormous. A cup of black coffee stood beside him. He drank it.

He was sober now—not miserably sober, but magnificently so. His head was clear. His skin tingled. Every joint felt newly catalogued. He was a cauliflower cooled after boiling; a pink mountain after rain; a citizen returned from battle carrying no visible medal but certain that schoolchildren would eventually learn his route.

The spectacled gentleman lowered his racing sheet.

“Enjoy the bath?”

Mr. Braddy smiled.

“I did.”

At midnight he dressed.

Every garment seemed smaller, cleaner, and more military. He buttoned his vest, tied his shoes, set the derby upon his moonlike head, and looked into the mirror.

The face was the same. The eyes were not.

“Fresh as a li’l’ daisy,” he told the glass.

He went home.

Angelica had not yet returned from Brooklyn. Her sister’s youngest child had swallowed a button, delaying everybody. Mr. Braddy entered the silent cottage, saw the empty bottle beside the bananas, and put it in the ash can.

Then he slept.

At seven-thirty Sunday morning Angelica shook him.

“Horace! You smell like a barbershop and a boiler room.”

“Good morning.”

“When did you get in?”

“Twelve-forty.”

“Where were you?”

“Manhattan.”

“With that Lum?”

“For part of the evening.”

“What did you do?”

Mr. Braddy sat up.

He could have lied. Instead he looked calmly at his wife.

“I drank Uncle Elmer’s whisky, bought a fifteen-cent cigar, ate at an Automat, and took a Turkish bath.”

Angelica stared.

“You did what?”

“A Turkish bath.”

“You’ve been wanting to do that for twenty years,” she said.

“I know.”

There are limits beyond which astonishment becomes administration. Angelica inspected him, found him alive, and went downstairs to prepare oatmeal.

On Monday morning Mr. Braddy crossed the Queensboro subway with the briskness of a man who had defeated both alcohol and architecture. His derby was straight. His shoes shone. In his pocket rested an eleven-cent cigar purchased from his own funds.

Hummidge & Beal’s Great Metropolitan Store rose above the avenue in twenty acres of stone, glass, brass, merchandise, and management. Its doors revolved at eight-thirty, swallowing employees by the thousand. Buyers ascended. Cash girls scattered. Floorwalkers took up strategic positions. Elevators clanged. Somewhere high in the building, an executive pressed a button, and six departments trembled.

Mr. Braddy reached the rug floor at eight-forty-five.

Mr. Silas Berger stood near the Axminsters, examining his watch.

“You’re three minutes late, Braddy.”

“Yes,” said Mr. Braddy, “I am.”

Mr. Berger waited for apology. None arrived.

He was a thin man with a triangular bald head and a pencil mustache as precise as an accounting mark.

“See that it does not occur again.”

Mr. Braddy removed the eleven-cent cigar from his pocket, bit off the end, and lit it beneath a placard reading **NO SMOKING BEFORE OPENING**.

“I want to speak to you in your office.”

Mr. Berger’s eyebrows rose.

“At ten.”

“Now.”

The demon Timidity scratched faintly at Mr. Braddy’s ribs. Mr. Braddy inhaled smoke, coughed once, and continued looking at the manager.

Mr. Berger turned.

“Very well.”

The office had a glass front, a rolltop desk, two chairs of unequal dignity, and a framed motto: **THE CUSTOMER IS ALWAYS RIGHT**. Mr. Berger sat in the larger chair. Mr. Braddy sat in the smaller one, which disappeared beneath him.

“Well?”

Mr. Braddy had prepared his speech on the subway. In its first version it was modest, truthful, and doomed. In the second it became stronger. By Queens Plaza it had acquired figures. At Lexington Avenue it acquired a rival company.

“Mr. Berger,” he began, “I’ve been with Hummidge & Beal’s eleven years.”

“I’m aware of that.”

“My sales have averaged twenty-one hundred dollars a week for the past six months. My returns are the lowest on the floor. I know stock, I know customers, and I know rugs.”

Mr. Berger folded his hands.

“If this is about an increase, the condition of business—”

“It’s about an offer.”

“What offer?”

“The Interstate Carpet Company has offered me sixty dollars a week.”

This statement entered the office, sat down, and crossed its legs.

Mr. Braddy’s interior became ice.

He had never spoken to the Interstate Carpet Company. He knew only that it existed, that its store was on Thirty-fourth Street, and that one of its windows contained an unfortunate green carpet. Yet the lie, once born, possessed health and confidence.

“Sixty?” repeated Mr. Berger.

“Sixty.”

“That’s double what you receive here.”

“Yes.”

“In what capacity?”

“Head of domestic rugs.”

There was no reason for this additional falsehood, but courage, like liquor, develops momentum.

Mr. Berger studied him.

“Who made the offer?”

Mr. Braddy’s brain became a vacant warehouse. Names rushed through it and found no loading platform. Napoleon? Griggs? Uncle Elmer?

“Mr. Thompson,” he said.

There was almost certainly a Mr. Thompson somewhere in the Interstate Carpet Company. No commercial institution could entirely avoid Thompsons.

“Henry Thompson?”

Mr. Braddy saw before him once more the steam room: fog, heat, pink mountains, the dark square of the far door.

He stepped forward.

“I don’t discuss another firm’s private affairs.”

Mr. Berger leaned back.

Mr. Braddy leaned forward.

“I’ve told them I’d give Hummidge & Beal’s the first chance. I want sixty dollars a week. I’ll expect your answer by five o’clock.”

“Really, Braddy—”

“By five.”

He rose. The smaller chair clung briefly to him and then fell over.

Bang!

Mr. Braddy set it upright with dignity.

“Good morning,” he said.

He returned to the rug floor.

His knees were trembling.

At nine o’clock the doors opened and customers arrived. Mr. Braddy sold a hall runner to a woman from the Bronx before she had decided whether she wanted one. He sold three Chinese mats to a dentist. He sold an eight-by-ten Wilton to a gentleman who had merely asked for the washroom.

“Third aisle to the left,” said Mr. Braddy, “and while you’re here, notice the wear in this weave.”

The gentleman noticed it, admired it, and bought it for his mother.

Mr. Braddy moved with astonishing vigor. He strode where formerly he had waddled. He interrupted indecision. He rolled carpets as though hurling bridges across rivers. When a customer said, “I’ll think it over,” he replied, “Think with it in your home; we deliver Wednesday.”

At noon his sales slips formed a small blizzard.

Privately he was terrified.

Each time Mr. Berger’s office door opened, Mr. Braddy’s stomach contracted. The Interstate Carpet Company might telephone. Mr. Berger might know Henry Thompson. There might be no Henry Thompson. There might be seven. One of them might be Mr. Berger’s brother-in-law.

At twelve-thirty Mr. Braddy went to lunch.

Ordinarily he purchased ten cents’ worth of soup and consumed crackers without charge. Today he ordered the club breakfast, although it was no longer breakfast and cost forty-five cents.

“Fruit, cereal, eggs, bacon, rolls, coffee,” recited the waitress.

“All of it,” said Mr. Braddy.

“Orange or grapefruit?”

He nearly said, “Whichever is easiest.” Then he remembered.

“Grapefruit.”

“With sugar?”

“I’ll decide when it gets here.”

At one-fifteen he returned to the floor.

Unknown to Mr. Braddy, conferences were occurring above him.

Mr. Berger telephoned Personnel.

“What do we pay Braddy?”

“Thirty.”

“What’s his classification?”

“Senior salesman, temporary rating.”

“Temporary? He’s been here eleven years.”

“He was never reclassified.”

Mr. Berger asked for sales records. Sales records produced columns. Columns, unlike managers, have no prejudices. They showed that Horace Braddy sold steadily, made few errors, retained customers, and had missed only four days in eleven years, two for influenza and two for Angelica’s aunt’s funeral, an affair which had extended beyond reasonable expectations.

The buyer of rugs was summoned.

“Can we replace him?” asked Mr. Berger.

“Sure,” said the buyer. “In six months.”

“At what salary?”

“Forty-five, if we get a good man. Fifty if we want one before Christmas.”

“What’s this about the Interstate?”

“They’re expanding domestics.”

“Do they have a Henry Thompson?”

“Everybody has a Henry Thompson.”

By three o’clock the matter had reached Mr. Beal, whose name appeared on the building but whose person appeared only in emergencies involving money. Mr. Beal disliked doubling salaries. He also disliked trained employees walking into competing stores carrying eleven years of information in their hats.

“How much business does this Braddy do?”

The figures were placed before him.

“Why’s he only getting thirty?”

Nobody knew.

“Offer fifty.”

“He says sixty by five.”

Mr. Beal looked at the figures again.

“Then keep him waiting till five.”

Thus does commerce preserve its dignity.

On the rug floor, the hours marched with leaden shoes.

At three-thirty Mr. Braddy sold two stair carpets.

At four he knocked over a rack of samples.

Pat, pat, pat, pat, pat! The squares fell like giant playing cards.

At four-fifteen Mr. Berger crossed the department without looking at him.

At four-thirty Mr. Braddy’s new identity began to crack. He saw himself unemployed, exposed, and walking from store to store while managers telephoned one another about the lunatic who invented Henry Thompson. He saw Angelica’s face when he announced that courage had reduced their income from thirty dollars to nothing.

At four-forty he considered entering Mr. Berger’s office and saying there had been a misunderstanding.

At four-forty-five he picked up his cigar.

It had gone out.

He relit it.

At four-fifty Mr. Berger sent for him.

The office seemed smaller than it had in the morning. Mr. Berger remained seated. On his desk lay several sheets covered with sales totals.

“Braddy,” he said, “we’ve reviewed your record.”

Mr. Braddy waited.

“You’ve been a reliable employee.”

Mr. Braddy waited.

“Your conduct this morning was irregular.”

Mr. Braddy waited.

“And smoking before opening is contrary to regulations.”

Mr. Braddy’s heart struck his ribs.

Wop, wop, wop.

“Nevertheless,” continued Mr. Berger, “Hummidge & Beal’s does not wish to lose an experienced salesman at this time.”

Mr. Braddy’s right hand closed around the chair arm.

“We are prepared to meet the sixty-dollar figure, effective this week.”

For a moment Mr. Braddy saw steam.

Then he saw the far door.

“Very good,” he said.

“We shall expect corresponding results.”

“You’ll get them.”

“And, naturally, this is confidential.”

“Naturally.”

Mr. Berger cleared his throat.

“Was it Henry Thompson who approached you?”

Mr. Braddy stood.

“I don’t discuss another firm’s private affairs.”

He left the office.

At five o’clock the salary department altered a card. A clerk drew one line through **$30** and wrote **$60** above it. This modest movement of pencil accomplished what eleven years of loyalty, patience, and diffidence had failed to perform.

Mr. Braddy telephoned Mr. Lum from a booth near the employee entrance.

“Chauncey?”

“Yeah?”

“I got the raise.”

“What raise?”

“Sixty dollars.”

There was a silence.

“You don’t tell me!”

“I do tell you.”

“How’d you work it?”

“I acted.”

“You mean the Griggs system?”

“I got busy. I did something.”

Mr. Lum lowered his voice.

“Say, Horace—what happened after I left?”

“I took the Turkish bath.”

“All the way through?”

“All the way.”

“Was it hot?”

“Hot enough.”

“Didja get lost?”

“Three times.”

“Then what?”

“I went through anyway.”

Mr. Lum was silent again. In the background Mrs. Lum called sharply, “Chauncey! Who is that?”

Mr. Lum answered in a whisper.

“I gotta go.”

“Coward,” said Mr. Braddy pleasantly, and hung up.

He stepped into the avenue.

Manhattan roared around him—trains beneath, traffic before him, offices above, millions of citizens pushing, bargaining, bluffing, selling, buying, demanding, and pretending not to be afraid. Mr. Braddy joined them.

He bought a fifteen-cent cigar.

At home Angelica was waiting with supper.

“You’re late,” she said.

“I got a raise.”

“How much?”

“Sixty dollars a week.”

Angelica sat down.

“Horace!”

“Yes.”

“Sixty?”

“Yes.”

“How did you ever get up the courage to ask?”

Mr. Braddy removed his derby and placed it upon the peg. He looked vast, placid, pink, and faintly heroic. No bard would sing of him. No statue would preserve him in bronze. Yet he had crossed the steam room, routed the demon Timidity, and doubled the family income before supper.

“A man,” he said, “can do what he is a-mind to.”

Angelica regarded him with new respect.

Then she sniffed.

“You haven’t been drinking again?”

“No.”

“Smoking?”

“A little.”

“Well, wash your hands. We’re having cauliflower.”

Mr. Braddy smiled.

He had already had cauliflower enough for one week.

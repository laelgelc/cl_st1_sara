# Keo and the Oath of a Year and a Day

Keo was a young hippopotamus of an ancient and aristocratic family. His father had the broadest nose in the Upper River, his mother could remain under water longer than any lady of her rank, and his grandfather had once overturned a royal canoe merely by scratching his back against it. This was considered a notable achievement among the River People, although mankind knew it not.

Keo himself had not yet performed any great deed. He was round, smooth, grey, and exceedingly cheerful. He laughed when he woke in the mud, he laughed when the little fish tickled his toes, and he laughed when his aunt warned him that excessive laughter was unbecoming to a hippopotamus of noble birth.

“Guk-uk-uk-uk!” laughed Keo.

That is how a hippopotamus laughs. If you have never heard one, you may suppose the sound to be thunder caught in a rain barrel; but it is laughter nevertheless.

The River People lived in a wide, warm stream that wound between reeds, sandbanks, forests, fields, and villages. They knew every bend, every deep pool, every shallow crossing, every smooth stone, every sunken log, every patch of sweet grass and every pleasant wallow. The villagers, for their part, knew where the hippopotamuses came ashore, where they slept by day, where they fed by night, and where one might dig a pit large enough to catch one.

Thus each knew far too much about the other.

It was continual warfare.

Now in one of the villages lived a man named Gouie, who was both poor and clever. A poor man who is not clever may remain merely poor, but a poor man who is clever is often troublesome as well. Gouie wished to become rich, and after considering fishing, farming, trading, marrying a wealthy widow, discovering treasure, and being appointed a chief, he decided it would be easiest to capture a full-grown hippopotamus.

He therefore dug an enormous pit beside a path used by the River People. He dug it deep and wide, lined the sides with slippery clay, covered the mouth with poles and branches, and scattered dust and grass over the top until even a beetle might have crossed it without suspicion—though not without danger.

“There,” said Gouie. “By tomorrow I shall possess a beast as large as a house.”

But the next morning, when he approached the pit with ropes, spears, neighbours, and a very proud expression, he heard from below:

“Guk-uk-uk-uk!”

Gouie peered over the edge.

There sat Keo.

He had gone ashore after moonrise, wandering farther than his mother permitted because the grass tasted especially green and because young hippopotamuses, like young people, believe that rules become weaker after dark. He had stepped upon Gouie’s covering, fallen through with a crash, and landed upon his broadest and most convenient portion. Having discovered that he was neither broken nor buried, he had laughed.

Gouie did not laugh.

“This is no full-grown hippopotamus,” he said. “This is a baby.”

“I am not a baby,” replied Keo. “I am young.”

“You are too young to sell, too small to work, and too fat to feed cheaply.”

“That last part may be true,” said Keo, and laughed again.

Gouie sat beside the pit and thought. He had worked seven days digging it. He had borrowed three ropes, damaged two spades, quarrelled with four neighbours, and promised half the profit to five relatives. To find only Keo at the bottom seemed an insult.

At last he said, “Little hippopotamus, if I release you, will you return when you are fully grown?”

“When shall that be?”

“In a year and a day.”

Keo blinked up at him.

“And what will happen then?”

“You will become my prisoner. I shall do with you as I please.”

This was not an inviting arrangement. Keo considered it as solemnly as one can while sitting in a muddy pit with a twig upon one’s head.

“If I promise, will you let me go now?”

“I will.”

“Then I promise,” said Keo. “In a year and a day I shall return and become your prisoner.”

“Swear it.”

“I swear by the two tusks I do not yet possess.”

“That is not enough.”

“I swear by the River, by the Royal Mud, and by my grandfather’s honour.”

“That will do,” said Gouie.

He lowered a rope. Keo could not climb it, of course, for hippopotamuses are not made for climbing ropes, ladders, vines, stairs, steeples, or any other vertical object. So Gouie and his neighbours cut away one side of the pit and formed a slope. Keo scrambled out, shook mud over everyone, thanked Gouie politely, and trotted toward the river.

“Remember!” Gouie called. “A year and a day!”

Keo looked back.

“Guk-uk-uk-uk!”

Then he went home.

When Keo told his family what had occurred, a terrible commotion arose. His mother groaned. His father stamped. His aunts wailed. His uncles opened their mouths so wide that three birds flew into one of them by mistake and flew out again much alarmed. His grandmother declared that promises made in pits were not binding. His grandfather declared that all promises were binding, especially foolish ones, because no sensible creature needed laws to make him sensible.

“You must not return,” said Keo’s mother.

“But I promised.”

“You were frightened.”

“I was not frightened,” said Keo. “I was sitting.”

“You were too young to understand.”

“I understood that he would let me out.”

“He means to kill you!”

“Perhaps,” said Keo. “But a promise is a promise.”

At this his family wept. They wept into the river until it rose three inches, or perhaps only one inch; but tears are difficult to measure in a river.

For many days they argued. The uncles wished to attack Gouie’s village. The aunts wished to hide Keo in a distant swamp. His father wished to fill every pit in the country. His mother wished Keo had never learned to speak. But Keo continued to eat, grow, wallow, and laugh, and whenever anyone mentioned the approaching day, he said:

“I shall keep my word.”

The year passed in the usual fashion. The rains came, the river swelled, the reeds flowered, the sun returned, and Keo grew from a round young hippopotamus into a magnificent nearly grown one. His shoulders became broad, his mouth became vast, and two admirable tusks appeared, thus making his oath by the tusks he had not possessed particularly respectable.

But as the promised day drew near, the River People grew desperate.

At last Keo’s great-grandmother, who remembered matters forgotten by everybody else, said, “There is one who may help us.”

“Who?” asked the family.

“Glinkomok.”

At the name, even the oldest bulls became quiet.

Glinkomok lived in a cave beyond the Red Ridge, where the forest grew so thick that noon wandered about looking for a place to shine. No one knew exactly what Glinkomok was. Some said part beast, part man, part fowl and part fish. Others said part sorcerer, part wizard, part magician and part fairy. All agreed that he was ancient when the first river was merely a damp idea, and that he disliked visitors who arrived without good reason.

“We have a good reason,” said Keo’s mother.

So the royal family set out.

They travelled by river where they could, by marsh where they must, and by land where they had no choice. They crossed seven streams, went around six hills, pushed through five thickets, frightened four hunters, stepped over three sleeping crocodiles, disturbed two porcupines, and finally reached one black cave in the face of the Red Ridge.

There they stood in a dignified semicircle and called:

“O Glinkomok, Most Ancient, Most Mysterious, Lord of That Which Is Known and Several Things Which Are Not, we implore your mercy and friendly assistance!”

Nothing answered.

They called again.

From within the cave came a yawn so deep that stones rolled down the ridge.

Then two green eyes opened in the darkness.

Glinkomok emerged.

It would be improper to describe him exactly, because nobody saw the whole of him at once. Keo thought he had scales, feathers, fur, fins, horns, hands, claws, and a tail that occasionally became two tails when no one watched closely. His face was old and young together, his voice sounded both near and far, and moss grew upon one shoulder as if it had paid rent there for a thousand years.

“Who wakes Glinkomok?” he demanded.

The River People all began speaking at once. They told of Gouie, the pit, the bargain, the oath, the year, the day, the danger, the tears, and Keo’s unreasonable determination to be honourable. Because everyone told a different part, Glinkomok heard the end before the beginning, the middle twice, and the account of the birds flying into Uncle Boro’s mouth more often than was necessary.

At last he raised one claw.

Silence fell.

“Keo,” he said, “did you promise?”

“Yes.”

“Will you return?”

“Yes.”

“Are you afraid?”

“A little,” Keo admitted. “But not enough to break my word.”

“And why do you laugh?”

Keo smiled.

“Because if I did not laugh, I might worry.”

Then Glinkomok laughed, and his laughter caused a dead tree to put forth three leaves in surprise.

“Come here,” he said.

Keo stepped forward.

Glinkomok rubbed Keo’s hide with a blue powder that smelled of lightning. He drew circles around Keo’s feet with a silver claw. He blew once into Keo’s right ear and twice into his left. Then he struck him between the eyes with a little crooked stick.

Keo sneezed.

The sneeze shook the mountain.

“Your skin is now harder than iron,” said Glinkomok. “No spear shall pierce it, no knife shall cut it, no fire shall scorch it, and no club shall bruise it. You are stronger than ten elephants pulling eleven elephants uphill. You can run faster than the wind when the wind is late for an appointment.”

“Guk-uk-uk-uk!” said Keo.

“Do not waste the gifts,” Glinkomok warned. Then he bent and whispered into Keo’s ear.

What he said was secret. Keo’s mother tried to listen, his father pretended not to listen, the aunts listened openly, and the uncles pushed one another for a better position. Yet none heard a word.

Keo’s eyes grew wide. Then he laughed until the cave echoed.

On the morning of the year and the day, Keo bathed, rolled in fresh mud, ate a large breakfast, ate a smaller second breakfast, and said farewell to his family.

“Do exactly as Glinkomok commanded,” said his mother.

“And remember the dignity of our ancient house,” said his father.

“And if Gouie troubles you,” said Grandfather, “sit on him.”

Keo trotted away.

Gouie was waiting beside the old pit. He had counted every month, every week, and every day, but he did not truly expect Keo to return. Dishonest people are often astonished by honesty, since they regard it as an improbable sort of magic.

When he saw the enormous young hippopotamus coming down the path, he shouted with delight.

“You came!”

“I promised,” said Keo.

“And now you are my prisoner.”

“I promised that also.”

Gouie led Keo into the village and tied him to a thick post. Then he brought his sharpest spear and called all his neighbours to witness his good fortune.

“This beast belongs to me,” he announced. “Observe!”

He thrust the spear at Keo’s side.

The spearhead bent like a reed.

Gouie frowned. He tried a second spear, a knife, an axe, a heavy club, a sharpened stake, and a cooking fork belonging to his eldest sister. The spear broke, the knife curled, the axe chipped, the club split, the stake shattered, and the cooking fork flew through a window and lodged in a melon.

Keo did not so much as blink.

“Guk-uk-uk-uk!”

Gouie built a fire around him. Keo found it pleasantly warm. Gouie rolled a boulder from a hill. Keo stopped it with one foot. Gouie tied six ropes to Keo and ordered twenty villagers to pull. Keo walked in the opposite direction and took all twenty villagers, three goats, and a fence with him.

At last Gouie sat down to think.

He could not kill Keo, but he had no intention of wasting so remarkable a prisoner. Cleverness, you see, does not always depart merely because plans have failed; sometimes it changes hats.

That afternoon Gouie painted a sign:

**RIDE THE INVULNERABLE HIPPOPOTAMUS!  
ONE SHELL A SHORT JOURNEY.  
THREE SHELLS A LONG JOURNEY.  
NO REFUNDS IF FRIGHTENED.**

The villagers had never ridden a hippopotamus. Indeed, most sensible villagers had made a custom of being elsewhere whenever a hippopotamus appeared. But novelty is stronger than wisdom, and soon six young men paid Gouie one shell apiece.

“You will carry them,” Gouie told Keo.

“I am your prisoner,” said Keo. “Where shall I carry them?”

“To the Red Ridge and back.”

Keo knelt. The six climbed upon him, gripping ropes Gouie had fastened around his middle.

Then Keo ran.

He ran over the fields, through the grass, past the trees, across the streams, around the hills, and up the Red Ridge faster than the wind when the wind has overslept. The riders screamed so loudly that birds abandoned their nests, monkeys dropped fruit, and Gouie heard the noise from the village long after Keo had vanished.

At the black cave Keo stopped.

Two green eyes opened within.

“Welcome,” said Glinkomok.

The six riders slid from Keo’s back and stumbled into the darkness, though whether they walked, fell, were pulled, or merely found the cave more interesting than their homes, no one could afterwards say.

Keo returned alone.

“Where are my customers?” cried Gouie.

“They remained at the cave.”

“Why?”

“They did not tell me.”

Gouie was troubled, but he had earned six shells. Six shells can soothe a considerable amount of curiosity.

The next day eight villagers purchased rides. Keo carried them to Glinkomok’s cave. The green eyes opened, the cave gave a little sigh, and the eight disappeared.

Keo returned alone.

On the third day twelve villagers climbed upon him, having concluded that the missing riders must have discovered treasure, a feast, or a secret festival. People will believe almost anything when others have done it first.

Keo bore them to the cave.

They, too, vanished.

By the end of the week Gouie had earned more shells than he had seen in his whole life. He bought bracelets, cloth, spears, jars, two handsome stools, a red umbrella, and a hat too large for his head. Yet the village had become remarkably quiet, for nearly everyone bold enough to ride Keo had failed to return.

“No more customers,” Gouie complained.

“You might ride,” suggested Keo.

Gouie stared at him.

“Why should I?”

“To see where the others went.”

This was reasonable, and Gouie disliked it for that reason. Yet he had begun to suspect that the missing villagers had obtained riches and intended to keep them. Moreover, he owned the hippopotamus. Surely a man may ride his own property.

“Very well,” said Gouie. “Carry me to the cave.”

Keo knelt, and Gouie climbed upon his back with a spear, a bag of food, and his new red umbrella.

Then Keo ran.

But he did not run toward the Red Ridge.

He ran toward the Great River.

“Wrong way!” shouted Gouie.

Keo ran faster.

He bounded over bushes, smashed through fences, crossed dry gullies in a single stride, and sent Gouie’s hat sailing into a thorn tree. Gouie dropped his bag, lost his umbrella, broke his spear, and wrapped both arms around Keo’s neck.

“Stop!”

“Guk-uk-uk-uk!”

At last they reached the river. Keo plunged in and swam toward the middle.

Now the hippopotamuses of the eastern bank belonged to Keo’s family, and they knew what had happened. They rose from the water with their eyes shining and their vast mouths open.

“Bring Gouie to us!” they cried. “We will bite him into seven unequal pieces!”

Gouie looked east and trembled.

Keo turned toward the western bank.

But the hippopotamuses there were cousins of Keo’s family, and they too had heard the tale.

“Bring Gouie here!” they bellowed. “We will flatten him and use him to mark the path!”

Gouie looked west and trembled twice.

Keo stopped upon a sandbar in the very middle of the river and shook Gouie from his back. The water around the sandbar was deep, swift, and full of crocodiles who had not been consulted but were willing to participate.

Keo began to swim away.

“Do not leave me!” cried Gouie.

“Why not?”

“They will kill me!”

“You wished to kill me.”

“That was different.”

“How?”

“I had dug a pit.”

Keo considered this.

“No,” he said. “I do not think that makes it different.”

The eastern hippopotamuses moved nearer. The western hippopotamuses moved nearer. Several crocodiles arranged themselves in a patient circle. Gouie climbed to the highest point of the sandbar, which was not very high.

“Save me!” he begged. “Merciful Keo, Noble Keo, Swift Keo, Most Excellent and Valuable Keo!”

“I am your prisoner,” said Keo.

“No longer! You are free!”

“But what shall I receive for saving you?”

“Anything!”

“Then become my captive.”

Gouie’s mouth fell open.

“For how long?”

“That is for me to decide.”

“When must I come?”

“In a year and a day.”

Gouie looked at the hippopotamuses. He looked at the crocodiles. He looked at the river, which was rising about his ankles.

“I promise!”

“Swear it.”

“I swear by the sky, the earth, and this unfortunate sandbar!”

“That is not enough.”

“I swear by my house, my wealth, and my red umbrella!”

“The umbrella is in a tree.”

The water reached Gouie’s knees.

At last he cried, “I swear by my grandfather’s bones that in a year and a day I shall return and become your captive!”

“That will do,” said Keo.

He swam back, allowed Gouie to climb upon him, and carried him safely to an empty stretch of shore. Gouie crawled onto the bank, exhausted, muddy, hatless, umbrellaless, and greatly reduced in dignity.

“Remember,” said Keo. “A year and a day.”

“I shall remember,” said Gouie.

“Guk-uk-uk-uk!”

Keo returned to his family, who welcomed him with such splashing, snorting, embracing, stamping, and weeping that the river rose another three inches—or perhaps the rains began at that moment.

As for the villagers who had disappeared into Glinkomok’s cave, some say they were changed into bats, some say they became servants in an underground palace, and some say the cave opened at the far side of the mountain and they all went home by a longer road, too ashamed to mention what had happened. Glinkomok never explained, and ancient magical creatures are not improved by questioning.

Gouie did not wait for the year and the day.

He gathered the shells he had earned, packed his bracelets and cloth, abandoned his stools, and fled to a distant country where no one had heard of Keo. There he traded cleverly, married advantageously, defeated his rivals by promising each of them something different, and in time became a rich and powerful chief. He possessed cattle, servants, drums, umbrellas, and a chair so grand that four men were required to move it and six more to admire it.

But Gouie was not happy.

Each night, just as sleep crept near, he heard water flowing in the darkness.

Then came the snorts of hippopotamuses on the eastern bank, the bellows of hippopotamuses on the western bank, and the soft splash of crocodiles circling a sandbar. Last of all he heard Keo’s voice:

“A year and a day!”

Gouie would leap from bed, call his guards, light every lamp, and count how many years and days had passed. Yet no guard could defend him from an oath, and no lamp could show him where the laughter came from.

“Guk-uk-uk-uk!”

So Keo, who kept his promise, lived merrily among the River People and became famous for honour, courage, speed, strength, and a laugh that could be heard from one bend of the river to the next.

And Gouie, who broke his promise, became wealthy, powerful, honoured, and afraid.

It may seem strange that his oath should have troubled him, for he had sworn by his grandfather’s bones, and Gouie’s grandfather had been the most celebrated boneless sorcerer in all that country.

He had never possessed a bone in his life.

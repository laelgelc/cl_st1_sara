# The Roses of Henry Floyd

No two persons agreed concerning Henry Floyd, except upon the point that he was eccentric; and as eccentricity is commonly the name given by society to any mode of life which it does not understand and cannot profit by, the agreement signifies less than might at first appear.

The ladies who met him at receptions thought him distinguished until they learned that he had no carriage. Men of business admitted that he possessed uncommon abilities, but regretted that he had never applied them to the accumulation of money. Young authors called him a genius when he praised their manuscripts, and an embittered failure when he declined to praise them. His tailor respected him because he always paid, though late; his landlord distrusted him because he was a writer; children adored him because he never spoke to them as if childhood were an offense; and certain poor people, whose names were not in the directory, believed him to be one of the greatest men in the city.

Perhaps the poor were nearer the truth than the directory.

Floyd was tall, loosely built, and carelessly dressed. His coat was generally brushed, but rarely new; his hat appeared to have shared both his reflections and the weather; and his gray eyes, which could become fierce under injustice, were usually softened by an expression of absent kindness. He had the delicate hands of a musician and the shoulders of a laborer. His hair had begun to whiten at the temples before he was thirty-five, though he was not, as one charitable lady observed, “really old enough to look so unsuccessful.”

He wrote essays which a few thoughtful persons read and many editors admired too much to purchase. He wrote stories which were said to be beautiful but uncommercial, and verses which were occasionally copied without his name. In addition to these unremunerative employments, he invented useful things.

One was a simple attachment by which a workman might stop a certain dangerous machine without placing his arm between two rapidly moving wheels. A manufacturer offered Floyd five thousand dollars for the exclusive right to it.

“And afterward?” asked Floyd.

“Afterward?”

“What should the other manufacturers pay?”

“Whatever we chose to charge.”

“And the workmen?”

The manufacturer laughed.

“My dear sir, workmen do not buy machinery.”

“No,” said Floyd. “They merely lose their hands in it.”

The interview ended unsatisfactorily. Floyd afterward gave the design to an association of mechanics, who distributed it wherever they could. Several manufacturers adopted it, and one of them, in a speech before a commercial club, referred to “the humanitarian foresight of modern industry.” Floyd was not mentioned.

Another invention reduced the cost of warming schoolrooms. A company proposed to organize itself around the idea, issuing a quantity of stock which appeared to Floyd to bear no reasonable relation to the amount of heat produced.

“You do not understand finance,” the promoter told him.

“I have always suspected it,” replied Floyd. “Finance does not seem to understand fire.”

He placed the plans at the disposal of the public schools.

Thus he proceeded through life, producing advantages for other men and astonishment for his friends. He was poor, not because he despised money, as was frequently alleged, but because he could never persuade himself that the possession of an idea conferred a moral right to exact tribute from every person benefited by it. This distinction, clear enough to Floyd, was obscure to nearly everybody else.

His eccentricity had deeper roots than his inventions and his coat. He had suffered early, and suffering, though it is praised as a teacher, is often a savage instructor, destroying half the pupil in order to educate the remainder.

At twenty-three Floyd had loved a girl with the complete faith possible only to the very young and the very proud. She had accepted his devotion, encouraged his ambition, and allowed him to believe that poverty shared with him would be more agreeable than prosperity with another. At length she discovered that prosperity was not without attractions. She married a gentleman whose chief distinction was an inheritance and whose most serious thought concerned horses.

Floyd did not reproach her. He even attended the wedding, stood pale and erect beneath a chandelier, and wished her happiness in a voice so steady that she wept afterward in her dressing-room. There are women who can forgive themselves everything except the generosity of those they have injured.

For several years Floyd believed his heart dead. Then he met Clara Vane.

She was neither celebrated for beauty nor likely to become so. Fashionable people described her as “interesting,” which is sometimes the last kindness bestowed upon a woman whose face they cannot praise. But her presence had the repose of a quiet room after noise. She understood Floyd’s silences, laughed at his indignations, and possessed that rare intelligence which does not demand that another’s mind shall cease working in order to admire its own.

She restored him slowly. A tree struck by lightning may put forth green leaves on one side while the other remains charred; so Floyd’s nature, which he had thought ruined, became living again wherever her tenderness touched it. He told her at last that she had given him back the world.

“No,” she answered. “I have only persuaded you to look at it.”

They were to be married in the autumn.

In June she died.

There was no warning worthy of the name. She had complained of weariness; the physician had prescribed rest; Floyd had brought her books and a little basket of strawberries. Three days later she was gone.

After that, the lesser griefs of life ceased to trouble him. A man who has been shipwrecked does not complain because the rain has spoiled his coat. Rejections, debts, slanders, the coldness of acquaintances and the ingratitude of beneficiaries passed over Floyd without leaving visible marks. But compassion, which sorrow deepens in noble natures, sometimes becomes bitter at its edges. He could forgive pain more readily than cruelty; poverty more readily than the worship of wealth; weakness more readily than polite deceit.

He withdrew from ordinary society. He kept a room near the upper end of Willow Street, where the roofs crowded together as if for warmth. The furniture consisted of a bed, two chairs, a long table covered with books and mechanical fragments, and a portrait of Clara which stood above a shelf. Beside the portrait was a small earthen jar holding, according to the season, a branch of apple blossom, a spray of goldenrod, or nothing.

His neighbors knew him better than his acquaintances did.

There was a lame boy named Philip who waited each evening on the lower stair for the sound of Floyd’s step.

“What have you discovered to-day?” Floyd would ask.

“Nothing.”

“Impossible. Columbus discovered a continent, and he was not half so well situated as you. You have a window.”

One rainy afternoon Philip announced that the opposite chimney was a castle and the black smoke a dragon.

“An excellent beginning,” said Floyd. “But where is the princess?”

“She is shut inside.”

“Then we must release her.”

“How?”

“By intelligence. Swords are noisy and often injure the dragon.”

They constructed, from a spool, a piece of wire, and two bits of wood, a device which had no practical relation to imprisoned princesses but occupied Philip until supper. Floyd afterward carried the boy upstairs when his lame leg pained him.

Old Mrs. Bell, who sold apples on the corner, believed Floyd wealthy because he always bought the bruised fruit.

“It is sweeter,” he explained.

“It is cheaper,” she corrected.

“That also is a species of sweetness.”

When her daughter was ill, a physician came unexpectedly and refused to say who had sent him. Mrs. Bell knew. The physician knew that she knew. Floyd alone preserved the fiction.

A certain number of people sought him for assistance. He corrected the poems of clerks, designed a brace for a crippled seamstress, wrote letters for an Italian fruit seller, and spent three nights preparing calculations for an inventor who afterward patented the result as his own. Friends remonstrated.

“You allow yourself to be robbed,” said Martin Hale, a journalist who admired Floyd with intermittent impatience.

“My dear Hale, I was not using the calculations.”

“But the fellow will make money.”

“Then perhaps he will cease borrowing mine.”

“You have no sense.”

“None that can be negotiated.”

Hale groaned. “You ought to be rich.”

“Why?”

“Because you have ability.”

“An amiable theory. It would ruin several millionaires.”

Yet Floyd was not always melancholy. With children he became fanciful; with pedants he became mischievous; with injustice, formidable. Once, at a dinner, a prosperous lawyer remarked that poverty was commonly the consequence of improvidence.

“Undoubtedly,” said Floyd. “The improvidence of being born to poor parents is particularly disastrous.”

The lawyer stared.

“And sometimes,” Floyd continued, “a man imprudently becomes ill, or loses his employment, or trusts an honest-looking scoundrel. But these are faults against which respectability has always been severe.”

He was not invited again.

Years passed. His hair whitened further, his name occasionally appeared in small type beneath essays of uncommon beauty, and people continued to say that he might have done something had he chosen. Those who saw him only in public believed his life empty. Those who saw him carry Philip up the stairs, or sit beside Mrs. Bell when her daughter died, were not so certain.

Then Sarah Dangerlie came into it.

He met her at a reception given by Mrs. Danton, whose house was famous for bringing together persons who wished to be known and persons whom it was useful to know. Floyd belonged to neither class, but Mrs. Danton had read one of his essays and invited him as she might have purchased an unusual piece of furniture.

He came late. The rooms were brilliant with flowers, uniforms, jewels, and that cultivated animation which allows a hundred persons to speak without communicating anything. Floyd stood for some moments near a marble pillar, observing the company with an expression which Mrs. Danton, seeing it from afar, feared might be intelligent.

Then Sarah Dangerlie spoke to him.

She was very beautiful. This is easily said, because beauty, like wealth, saves description. Her hair was dark, her complexion clear and faintly warm, her figure slender without fragility. But the charm which most impressed Floyd lay in her eyes. They appeared grave even when she smiled, and seemed to promise depths of sympathy beneath her social ease.

“I have wished to meet you,” she said.

Floyd bowed. “That is generous. Most people wish to have met me. It requires less time.”

She laughed, but not loudly.

“I read your paper on the children in the factories.”

“Did you?”

“It made me ashamed.”

“Then I beg your pardon.”

“Why? We are not often ashamed enough.”

That answer surprised him. During the next quarter of an hour she spoke of books, public schools, the obligations of fortunate people, and the vulgarity of estimating every human effort in money. Floyd listened at first with courtesy, afterward with interest. She knew some of his obscure essays. She quoted a sentence which even he had forgotten. When a young man interrupted them to tell Miss Dangerlie that the waltz was beginning, she declined the dance.

“I would rather hear Mr. Floyd,” she said.

The young man withdrew with the injured expression of one who has seen literature preferred to his person.

“Who is that?” Floyd asked.

“Mr. Router.”

“Have I offended him?”

“Mr. Router is offended by competition.”

“Then in America he must lead an agitated life.”

John Router was the only son of a railway and mining magnate. He was handsome in a robust, finished manner, had been educated at three institutions without suffering noticeably from the experience, and was spoken of as a young man of great prospects, though the prospects lay chiefly behind him in the person of his father. His name appeared frequently in the social columns. He drove excellent horses, belonged to several clubs, and had once crossed the Atlantic in a yacht.

Floyd forgot him before the evening ended.

He did not forget Sarah.

Their second meeting occurred at an exhibition of paintings. She stood before a picture of a peasant woman holding a dead child, while well-dressed spectators passed behind her and discussed the frame.

“It is terrible,” she said when Floyd approached.

“Truth usually is, when one has paid admission to avoid it.”

She looked at him with reproach. “You make compassion sound bitter.”

“It becomes bitter when it is kept for ornament.”

“And do you think mine ornamental?”

“No.”

He answered too quickly. She lowered her eyes, and for a moment neither spoke.

Thereafter they met often, sometimes by accident and sometimes through those accidents which society carefully arranges. Mrs. Danton found that Floyd’s presence gave a distinction to her receptions, especially after a celebrated critic praised his latest book. Sarah would leave a circle of admirers to speak with him. She asked him questions which others did not ask; she remembered the lame boy Philip; she sent books for the children at the hospital after Floyd had mentioned their want of them.

He began to change.

Hale noticed it first.

“You have bought a new coat,” the journalist said suspiciously.

“The old one was tired.”

“You are attending receptions.”

“One should study civilization at its source.”

“You have had your hair cut.”

“An accident.”

Hale leaned across the table at the club. “Who is she?”

Floyd’s face became cold.

“My dear Henry,” Hale said more gently, “you look as if somebody had opened a window in a room you meant to keep shut.”

Floyd gazed into the empty fireplace.

“There is a woman,” he said at last.

“Of course.”

“No. Not ‘of course.’ Nothing about her is of course.”

Hale had heard that tone before—in young poets discussing bad sonnets, in politicians discussing themselves, and in men already beyond rescue discussing women. He sighed.

“Miss Dangerlie?”

Floyd looked up sharply.

“Yes.”

Hale folded his napkin. “Take care.”

“Of what?”

“There is a report concerning her and young Router.”

“There are reports concerning every unmarried woman who possesses beauty and every unmarried man who possesses money.”

“This one is definite.”

“Have you asked Miss Dangerlie?”

“No.”

“I have.”

It had happened a week earlier, upon the stairway at Mrs. Danton’s. The music below had ceased; guests were moving toward supper; Sarah and Floyd stood for a moment alone between the lighted landing and the shadow of the upper hall.

She held a cluster of white flowers. A broken petal had fallen upon her glove.

“Mr. Router seemed displeased that you did not drive with him yesterday,” Floyd said.

“He is often displeased.”

“People say—”

She looked at him. “Must we repeat what people say?”

“No,” Floyd answered. “Unless silence gives it authority.”

“What authority?”

“That of belief.”

She was very still.

Floyd continued with visible effort. “They say you are to marry him.”

“Do you believe them?”

“I have believed nothing about you which you did not permit me to believe.”

Her eyes softened. “Then do not begin now.”

It was not a denial in words. Yet the heart, when it desires certainty, can construct it from less. Her glance, her voice, the slight movement by which she turned from the brilliant rooms below toward the shadow where he stood—all spoke to Floyd more powerfully than formal speech.

He bent over her hand.

“I shall never distrust you,” he said.

She withdrew her hand slowly, as if the promise pained her.

“You should not say never.”

“It is the only word love understands.”

Someone called her name. She descended the stairway. Floyd remained where he was until the music began again.

This he did not tell Hale. He merely said, “I have her assurance.”

“An assurance that she is not engaged?”

“An assurance that satisfies me.”

Hale studied his face and dared go no further. Friendship has its cowardices, and they are not always ignoble.

Spring came early that year. The parks brightened; soot-blackened branches put forth a tender green which made even the city appear briefly innocent; children sailed sticks along the gutters; and Floyd, walking the same streets he had walked for years, saw them as if some kindly power had washed the windows of the world.

He had not forgotten Clara. The dead are not displaced in a faithful heart; they are given a quiet chamber into which the living may enter without profanation. He told Sarah of her one afternoon.

They were in Mrs. Danton’s conservatory. Beyond the glass, rain silvered the bare trees.

“She must have been very good,” Sarah said.

“She was.”

“And very beautiful?”

“To me.”

“That is the only beauty which matters.”

“Is it?”

Sarah touched the leaf of a fern. “A woman who is praised by everyone sometimes longs to be beautiful to one person.”

Floyd’s breath caught. “You could never doubt it.”

She looked at him, and the rain, the distant voices, the long years of loneliness seemed to fall away. Yet he did not speak the declaration rising within him. He had become chivalrous almost to folly. He wished to bring her no embarrassment, to demand no answer until he could offer something besides a name respected by the poor and ignored by bankers.

For the first time in many years he began to consider money.

A publisher had proposed a collected edition of his essays. Ordinarily Floyd would have accepted whatever sum seemed sufficient to pay his debts and purchase new apparatus. Now he bargained. The publisher, astonished, increased the offer.

Floyd also completed a device for regulating pressure in municipal water mains. The invention had lain unfinished because he disliked certain tedious calculations. He worked on it nightly. Hale found him asleep one morning at the table, his head beside a page of figures and Clara’s portrait above him.

“You will kill yourself,” Hale said.

“I have survived worse things.”

“Are you going to sell this one?”

“Yes.”

Hale whistled. “Then it is serious.”

“It is sacred.”

He meant to ask Sarah to marry him. There was no formal understanding between them; no words had passed which a court of law, or a fashionable mother, could construe into an obligation. Yet every meeting appeared to carry them nearer. She wore the violets he sent. She preserved a little note he had written. Once, when he corrected her quotation of a verse concerning trust, she smiled and said:

“You demand the exact word in poetry.”

“And in life.”

“Is life so exact?”

“It should be, where another heart supplies the meaning.”

She did not answer. But she gave him her hand at parting and allowed it to remain in his longer than custom required.

Everyone said that Henry Floyd was in love with Sarah Dangerlie. Everyone said that Sarah encouraged him. Everyone also said that she would marry Router. Society is able to maintain several contradictory convictions, provided each affords material for conversation.

Mrs. Pryde, a lady whose charity consisted chiefly in discovering misfortune before it became public, stopped Floyd after a musicale.

“My dear Mr. Floyd,” she said, “you know I am your friend.”

“I had not known it,” he replied. “But I accept the information gratefully.”

“I feel that someone should tell you—”

“Then that someone will enjoy an advantage over the rest of society, which merely hints.”

She lowered her voice. “Miss Dangerlie and Mr. Router have been understood for more than a year.”

“By whom?”

“The families.”

“Not by Miss Dangerlie.”

Mrs. Pryde raised her eyebrows. “She has not denied it.”

“To me she has.”

This was not literally true; but fidelity sometimes compels an honest man to become the advocate of his hope.

“Then,” said Mrs. Pryde, “of course I have nothing more to say.”

She proceeded immediately to say it elsewhere.

Sarah heard of the conversation and spoke to Floyd at the next reception.

“Why do you defend me so fiercely?”

“Because you are absent when they accuse you.”

“Of what do they accuse me?”

“Of allowing falsehood to wear your name.”

She became pale. “And you do not believe them?”

“I believe you.”

“Even against everyone?”

“Everyone is often wrong. It is the chief occupation of everyone.”

A shadow of distress crossed her face.

“Mr. Floyd, suppose someday you discovered—”

“What?”

“That I was not what you think.”

“I should first doubt the discovery.”

“And afterward?”

“There would be no afterward.”

She turned away. “You make trust a terrible thing.”

“Only to those who betray it.”

The words were spoken quietly and without suspicion. They struck her nevertheless. For the remainder of the evening she avoided him.

Three days passed before a note arrived asking him to call. It was brief and gracious. Her aunt, with whom she lived, would be at home on Thursday afternoon.

Floyd read the note many times. Its lines contained nothing that another person could interpret as tenderness; but love is a language in which punctuation may become eloquent. He decided that Thursday should end uncertainty.

His mother had been dead fifteen years. She had lived in a low, white house beyond the northern edge of the city, where Floyd had spent his childhood. The house had passed to strangers, but by an old agreement a corner of the garden remained his until the land should be sold. In that neglected corner grew several rosebushes planted by his mother.

They were old-fashioned roses, deep red, richly scented, opening not with the measured perfection of greenhouse flowers but in lavish, irregular folds. His mother had called them faithful roses because they bloomed after severe winters.

On Thursday morning Floyd went to the garden.

The city had reached almost to it. Rows of new houses stood where fields had been; a streetcar sounded beyond the hill; advertisements were painted upon a neighboring fence. But the roses remained behind a sagging wall, bright in the sun.

He cut twelve.

As he did so, he remembered his mother’s hands tying flowers for a sick neighbor; Clara walking beside him once through that same garden; Sarah’s face beneath the conservatory glass. Memory, grief, hope, and the fragrance of the roses mingled until he could not separate them.

He carried the flowers back to Willow Street and placed them in the earthen jar beneath Clara’s portrait.

“I am not forgetting,” he said aloud.

The dead did not answer, but the peace of the room seemed untroubled.

He dressed carefully. From a locked drawer he took two banknotes—the first payment from the publisher. One was to discharge an old debt; the other he intended to spend upon a ring after Sarah had consented. His trunk stood half packed near the bed, for he had planned, if she accepted him, to travel the next day to inspect the water device at a distant town and return with the contract completed. Poverty had taught him caution, but love had made the caution hopeful.

At three o’clock a messenger knocked.

The envelope bore Sarah’s hand.

Floyd smiled as he broke the seal. Then he read:

> My dear Mr. Floyd,—Circumstances which I have not had the courage to explain will, I fear, become known to you before I can speak of them. I beg you to believe that I have valued your friendship more than I can say, and that if I have permitted you to misunderstand me, it was from weakness and not from any wish to cause pain. I shall always remember your goodness and hope you will someday forgive me.
>
> Sarah Dangerlie.

He read it once.

He read it again.

The room remained unchanged. Sunlight lay across the books. A cart rattled in the street. From below came Philip’s laughter. The roses stood beneath Clara’s portrait, splendid and fragrant.

Floyd read the letter a third time.

A knock sounded, but he did not answer. The door opened and Hale entered, holding an evening newspaper.

“Henry—”

Floyd looked at the paper.

Hale stopped.

“You know?”

“I have this.”

He extended Sarah’s letter.

Hale read it and swore softly. Then, with the helpless cruelty of one who knows concealment is useless, he unfolded the newspaper. The announcement appeared among the social notices:

> Mr. and Mrs. Adrian Dangerlie announce the approaching marriage of their niece, Miss Sarah Dangerlie, to Mr. John Router, son of Mr. and Mrs. James K. Router. The engagement, which has been privately understood by the families for more than a year, will culminate in a wedding early next month.

Floyd did not take the paper. He read the paragraph where Hale held it.

“More than a year,” he said.

“Yes.”

“She knew.”

“Yes.”

“And when I asked her—”

Hale put the newspaper down. “Do not think of what she said.”

“She said nothing.”

His face had changed, not violently, but as a landscape changes when the sun is extinguished. The youth which love had restored disappeared. Even Hale, who knew the old wounds, was frightened by the stillness.

“I’ll go to Router,” he said. “Or to her aunt. There may be—”

“No.”

“She has behaved damnably.”

“No.”

“Henry, you cannot defend her.”

“I do not defend her.”

“Then what will you do?”

Floyd looked at the roses.

“Nothing.”

He sat at his table and drew paper toward him. For several moments his pen did not move. Then he wrote:

> My dear Miss Dangerlie,—Your letter has reached me, and I have also seen the public announcement. You owe me no explanation. Whatever I believed was my own responsibility, and whatever I suffer must therefore be mine. I shall remember only that you brought light for a little while into a life which had grown accustomed to darkness. For that I remain grateful.
>
> I wish you peace and happiness.
>
> Henry Floyd.

Hale read the letter over his shoulder.

“You cannot send that.”

“Why?”

“It absolves her.”

“I am not her judge.”

“She deceived you.”

“She was weak.”

“That is no excuse.”

“No,” said Floyd. “It is an explanation. Explanations are often the saddest things we possess.”

He sealed the letter.

Hale paced the narrow room. “You must not remain here alone.”

“I have been alone before.”

“That is what I fear.”

Floyd lifted the roses from the jar. A little water fell across the table and blurred one line of Sarah’s letter.

“You meant those for her,” Hale said.

“Yes.”

“What will you do with them?”

Floyd seemed not to hear.

He put on his coat. He placed Sarah’s letter in the drawer beside the publisher’s banknotes, hesitated, then took out the money and slipped it into his pocket. He gave his reply to Hale.

“Will you have this sent?”

“If you insist.”

“I do.”

“Where are you going?”

“Out.”

“Let me come.”

“No, Hale.”

There are refusals which friendship must obey. Hale remained in the room while Floyd descended the stairs carrying the roses.

Philip waited on the landing.

“Are those for a princess?” the boy asked.

Floyd stopped.

“I thought so.”

“Did the dragon get her?”

“No,” said Floyd. “She chose the castle.”

He passed on before the child could ask another question.

Evening had begun to descend. Lamps appeared one by one along Willow Street; shop windows brightened; clerks hurried home; carriages rolled toward the avenue where dinner waited behind polished doors. The city, indifferent and immense, pursued its bargains, pleasures, hungers, and griefs. A marriage announcement interested it. A broken heart did not.

Floyd walked without direction.

He passed the club where Router’s friends were doubtless congratulating him; passed a florist’s window filled with roses more perfect and less fragrant than those in his hand; passed Mrs. Danton’s lighted house, at whose doorway servants were laying a carpet for another reception. Once he saw a carriage resembling Sarah’s and stopped, but the woman within was a stranger.

At the corner of Mercer Street a thin rain began to fall.

Near the entrance of a closed shop stood a woman. She was not old, though misery had attempted to make her so. Her dress was torn at one sleeve, and a faded shawl covered her hair. She watched the passing men with an expression in which shame struggled against desperation. Most avoided her eyes. Respectability is particularly skilled at not seeing what it does not intend to relieve.

As Floyd approached, she stepped forward.

“Sir—”

He stopped.

She looked at the roses, then at his face, and the words she had prepared failed her.

“What is it?” he asked.

“I—I have nowhere to go.”

“Have you no home?”

“Not now.”

“That was not my question.”

She stared at him.

“Is there a place where someone would receive you?”

“My mother. In Millport. But I can’t go there.”

“Why not?”

“I’ve been away two years. She thinks I’m working respectable.”

“Are you not?”

The woman laughed once, without mirth.

Floyd’s expression did not alter.

“Would she close the door?”

“No. That’s worse.”

“Nothing is worse than a closed door.”

“I haven’t the fare. And I owe for my room. They put me out this morning.”

The rain darkened the shoulders of his coat. The roses glowed between them like a wound.

“What is your name?” he asked.

“Mary.”

“Mary what?”

“Does it matter?”

“It may matter to your mother.”

She covered her face. “Mary Lane.”

Floyd drew the banknotes from his pocket. He had intended one for an old debt and one for a ring. He looked at them briefly, as a man might look upon the remains of a vanished country.

Then he gave both to her.

She did not take them.

“What do you want?” she whispered.

“For you to go home.”

She searched his face for the accustomed bargain and found none.

“All that?”

“The fare, your debt, a room for to-night, and food.”

“I can’t take it.”

“Yes, you can.”

“Why?”

Floyd looked down at the roses.

“Because it was meant for happiness.”

He held the flowers toward her.

“Take these also.”

Her hands closed around them uncertainly. Their fragrance rose between the wet pavements and the darkened shops, carrying with it a forgotten garden, a mother’s kindness, the faith of a lonely man, and the tenderness which another woman had not known how to value.

“What shall I do with them?” Mary asked.

“Give them to your mother.”

She began to cry.

Floyd turned away.

“Sir!”

He looked back.

“Who are you?”

For a moment the old, dry humor touched his face.

“No one in the directory,” he said.

Then he walked on alone into the rain.

At half past five Bertha Young came running up the steps with her key already in her hand, though there was no reason to run, none at all. She was not late. Cook had promised the soufflé would behave; the flowers had arrived before luncheon; the wine had been sent up from the cellar; and Harry, if he came home when he said he would, had an hour in which to dress.

Still she ran.

The cold evening air entered with her and seemed to ring through the hall like a handful of little bells. She shut the door, leaned against it, and pressed both palms to her cheeks.

What could one do with happiness like this?

It was absurd. At thirty, one ought to know. One ought to be able to take off one’s coat, put down one’s bag, say, “Is everything ready?” and go upstairs without feeling that one’s body was a bright glass filled to the edge and trembling there—just trembling.

Instead, she wanted to laugh. Or dance. Or run out again and give the astonished cabman a sovereign. Or catch the first respectable elderly woman she saw by the wrists and cry, “Don’t you understand? Everything is marvellous!”

Everything!

The narrow hall, with its black-and-white floor; the blue bowl holding three yellow tulips; the small red lacquer table Harry disliked; the faint warm smell of beeswax and soup. Even the umbrella stand appeared charmingly intelligent, as if it had been waiting all afternoon to receive her wet green umbrella and now knew precisely what was expected of it.

Bertha bit her lip.

No. She must be calm. Calm and modern and competent.

“Mrs. Young?” called Mary from the dining room.

“Yes, Mary. It’s only me.”

Only me! That was good. As if she were not at that moment a whole orchestra.

She slipped out of her coat. Beneath it she wore a soft white blouse and a jade-green skirt, and the sight of the green folds falling straight to her shoes pleased her immensely. She had chosen the skirt without knowing why. But now—of course. It was the colour of the pear tree leaves in summer, though the tree had scarcely any leaves tonight, only flowers.

The pear tree.

She went quickly through the drawing room and stood before the long windows.

At the bottom of the garden, beyond the dark square of lawn, the tree rose against the pale evening sky. It was in full flower, every branch powdered with white. There was no wind. Not one blossom stirred. The slender trunk seemed to gather all the dimness of the garden into itself and send it upward, changed into light.

Bertha stopped breathing.

It was too beautiful. That was the trouble. The tree, the sky, the clear cold dusk, the first lamp shining in the house opposite—everything had gone a little too far. Beauty had become almost painful, as if a hand pressed gently but steadily beneath her heart.

And she thought: That is my life.

Not vainly. Not in the ordinary boastful way. She did not mean the house or the money or the dinner party. She meant the shape of it, the upwardness, the secret flowering. She was that tree. No—she and the tree had somehow agreed to be the same thing for one evening.

How ridiculous!

Yet how true.

“Madam?”

Mary stood behind her with a dish towel over one arm.

“The fruit has come, madam. Shall I arrange it now?”

“The fruit. Yes. Let me.”

Mary’s face, which had been composed for service, showed the faintest alarm.

“If you wish, madam.”

“I do. I particularly do.”

On the dining-room table stood a broad grey bowl and several paper bags. Bertha opened them eagerly. Oranges rolled out, polished and heavy; purple grapes lay close together with a smoky bloom upon them; pears, yellow-green and firm, smelled of cupboards and autumn sunlight. There were also small red apples, two pomegranates and a cluster of white grapes which looked almost unreal, as though made from tiny globes of cloudy glass.

She arranged them by colour. First the oranges, then the dark grapes, then the pale pears lifting from the centre. No, that was too deliberate. She displaced three pears, added the white grapes, turned one pomegranate so that its dry crown pointed outward.

“There,” she said.

The fruit seemed to float in the grey bowl, rich and cool and self-contained.

Mary waited.

“Lovely, isn’t it?”

“Very nice, madam.”

Nice! But of course Mary could not be expected to feel it. One could not demand from everybody this extraordinary—what was it? Illumination? Fever? The word bliss came to Bertha, but it was too round, too complacent. This feeling had edges. It flashed.

She laughed softly and touched one of the grapes.

“Has Nurse brought the baby down?”

“I don’t think so, madam.”

“I’ll go up.”

She took the stairs two at a time.

The nursery door was open, and there sat Nurse by the fire with Little B in her lap. The child wore a blue woollen jacket and had one sock half off. Her hair, soft as thistledown, stood upright at the crown. Nurse was feeding her from a silver spoon.

Bertha paused in the doorway.

The fire shone on the brass fender, on the white bars of the cot, on Nurse’s starched apron and the child’s solemn round cheeks. It was a complete little world, warm, rhythmic, enclosed. Spoon, mouth, swallow. Spoon, mouth—

“My treasure!” cried Bertha.

Little B turned, saw her and gave a sudden crow. Her whole face opened. She kicked so violently that porridge splashed on Nurse’s apron.

“Now look what Mummy has made you do,” said Nurse.

“Oh, I’m sorry.”

Bertha came closer, longing to lift the child, to bury her face in the warm hollow under the small chin.

“May I finish feeding her?”

Nurse hesitated. It was only an instant, but Bertha felt it.

“She’s nearly done, madam. And when her little routine is upset she doesn’t settle.”

“I shan’t upset her.”

“No, madam. Of course not.”

Still, Nurse held the spoon.

Bertha knelt by the chair and put out one finger. Little B seized it with porridge-sticky hands and pulled it toward her mouth.

“Isn’t she wonderful?” whispered Bertha.

Nurse’s expression suggested that wonderful was not a practical category.

“She’s been a good girl all afternoon.”

Little B smelled of milk, wool, soap and something warm that was simply herself. Bertha kissed her temple. A shiver of happiness ran through her again, so sudden that her eyes filled.

This baby was hers. This grave, greedy, shining creature. She and Harry had made her. It seemed impossible that people were allowed such things—to have a baby, a house, a pear tree in flower, friends coming to dinner. Surely someone would knock at the door and say there had been an error.

“Let me hold her for one minute,” Bertha said.

Nurse surrendered the child with the air of a banker releasing securities.

Little B came against Bertha’s breast, solid and alive. She patted Bertha’s cheek, then caught her necklace and tugged.

“You little savage,” Bertha murmured. “You perfect little savage.”

The child laughed. It was not so much a laugh as a shout of triumph.

At once Nurse stood.

“Her bath water will be cooling.”

“Yes. Of course.”

Bertha gave the baby back. There it was again: that slight dismissal, so correct that it could not be called a dismissal at all. The nursery belonged to Nurse. Bertha, in her green skirt, was a visitor.

But tonight even this could not hurt her. Nothing could. She kissed the baby’s bare foot and went downstairs with the warmth of it still upon her lips.

In her bedroom she found her evening dress laid across the bed: silver, with a narrow sash of deep violet. Beside it lay stockings fine as smoke and shoes with little crystal buckles. She switched on the lamps, then switched them off again. The room was better in the dusk.

She sat before the mirror and took the pins from her hair.

What was this strange happiness? She had been happy before, certainly. She was happy every day—mostly. She liked Harry’s quick way of entering a room, his appetite, his decisiveness. She liked their friends, even when they became exhausting. She loved Little B beyond sense. She loved the house. She loved buying cushions and ordering books and discovering a new kind of olive.

But this was different.

This seemed to come from inside her body, from somewhere beneath thought. A flame, but not hot. A spring, but not wet. Oh, hopeless! Why did language become idiotic just when one needed it?

Perhaps she was ill.

She studied her reflection. Bright eyes. Flushed cheeks. No sign of illness. She looked younger than thirty—almost absurdly young. And her face, which she had never thought beautiful, appeared transparent with expectation.

Expectation of what?

The bell rang downstairs.

Too early. It could not be anyone.

Then she remembered Pearl Fulton.

Pearl had said she might come before the others because she would be nearby. Or had Bertha asked her to? The conversation returned in fragments: Pearl’s lowered eyelids, a slow smile, a gloved hand resting on the edge of a table.

Pearl Fulton.

Bertha’s happiness rose sharply, like a bird startled from grass.

There was something about Pearl. Something reserved, something silver and remote. Other people called her cold. Harry called her “that frozen woman” and had objected when Bertha invited her.

“She sits there looking as if she’s swallowed a secret and means to charge admission,” he had said.

“You’re unfair.”

“I’m exact.”

“You hardly know her.”

“Don’t need to.”

That was Harry all over—quick, positive, brutal for the pleasure of hearing the blow land. Yet his dislike had secretly delighted Bertha. It gave Pearl an added mystery. For Bertha knew—knew without evidence—that beneath Pearl’s stillness lived a feeling as intense as her own.

Tonight, perhaps, they would recognise one another.

Not explain. Never that. Explanations spoiled everything. But recognise.

Bertha dressed rapidly. The silver gown slipped over her with a cool whisper. The violet sash fell from her waist like a dark petal. She pinned up her hair and fastened a small moonstone at her throat.

Downstairs, Mary had shown Pearl into the drawing room.

She stood by the fireplace, tall and pale in a dress the colour of dull silver. A black wrap hung from one arm. Her fair hair was drawn smoothly back, and her eyes, when she turned, seemed darker than Bertha remembered.

“Pearl! How lovely of you to come early.”

“Am I inconveniently early?”

“No. Perfectly early.”

They kissed the air beside each other’s cheeks.

Pearl’s hand was cold.

Bertha became aware of every object in the room: the amber lampshades, the blue cushions, the white chrysanthemums, the fire opening and closing along the logs. She had arranged all of it, and now Pearl stood at its centre as though the room had been waiting for her.

“Do sit down.”

“Thank you.”

Pearl sat. She did not remove her black wrap, though the room was warm.

For one alarming second Bertha could think of nothing to say. Yet this silence did not feel empty. It was full—almost embarrassingly full.

“You walked?” she asked.

“Part of the way.”

“Isn’t the evening extraordinary?”

“Is it?”

“The sky. The cold. Everything so clear.”

Pearl looked toward the window.

“Yes,” she said. “Very clear.”

That was all. But Bertha felt understood.

She wanted to say, Do you feel it too? Do you have days when the world seems to be giving off light from every surface? Do you sometimes think your body cannot contain what is happening to it?

Instead she asked, “Sherry?”

“Please.”

As she poured, the door opened and Harry came in.

He brought the street with him: cold air, noise, impatience. He was broad-shouldered, dark-haired, and handsome in a way that seemed less a matter of features than of movement. Everything he did was definite. Hat down. Gloves off. Door shut.

“Hullo, Ber.”

Then he saw Pearl.

“Oh. Miss Fulton.”

“Mr. Young.”

His voice changed. Only slightly. It became more clipped.

Bertha looked from one to the other. How absurdly childish Harry was when he disliked someone! Pearl had barely spoken and already he had put on that blunt expression.

“You’re home early,” Bertha said.

“Am I? Thought I was late.”

“You have ages.”

“I’ll need them. Tub’s hot, I hope.”

“Of course.”

He crossed to the drinks table and poured whisky.

Pearl’s eyes followed him—or perhaps they did not. Her eyelids were lowered. It was impossible to know exactly where she looked.

Harry swallowed, set down the glass and said, “Well, I’ll make myself civil upstairs.”

“Harry!”

But he had gone.

Bertha laughed too brightly.

“He’s impossible.”

Pearl’s mouth curved.

“I don’t mind.”

That made Bertha love her. Not personally—no, the word was wrong—but ardently, suddenly. Love could exist without history, couldn’t it? It could be a recognition of quality, like hearing one note and knowing the whole music.

The bell rang again.

The Norman Knights arrived together, making the hall appear too small for them. Mrs. Knight—Face, everyone called her, because she had once declared that a woman’s face should be treated as a public argument—wore an orange coat with a collar of green fur. Under it was a black dress embroidered with tiny red hands. Her husband, Norman, had a beard cut to a point and carried a cane although he had come by motorcar.

“My dears,” cried Face, “we have survived a committee.”

“Barely,” Norman said. “One never escapes a committee. One merely becomes absent from its minutes.”

They entered with the pleased exhaustion of actors after an opening night.

Face saw Pearl and extended both hands.

“The silent Miss Fulton. How exquisite. I’ve spent the whole day among women who use speech as upholstery.”

Pearl smiled.

Behind them came Eddie Warren, thin, fair and anxious, wearing an enormous white scarf. He looked as though the scarf had captured him and was slowly carrying him away.

“I nearly didn’t arrive,” he said.

“Why?” Bertha asked.

“The cab driver had a red patch on his neck. An ominous red patch. I became convinced he had murdered somebody in Camden Town.”

“Did you ask him?”

“My dear Bertha, one doesn’t question destiny at a traffic light.”

Face kissed Eddie’s forehead.

“You are the only man in London who can turn dermatitis into Greek tragedy.”

Mary appeared with the cocktails. Everyone accepted one except Pearl, who kept her sherry.

Then Harry returned in his dinner jacket, his hair damp and his face scrubbed pink. He greeted the Knights loudly, clapped Eddie on the shoulder and ignored Pearl with such care that Bertha nearly laughed.

How transparent men were! Harry thought himself subtle, but there was his dislike in every movement.

Dinner was announced.

They went in: Face rustling, Norman tapping his cane though the dining room was twelve steps away, Eddie trailing his white scarf until Mary rescued it from the door. Pearl walked beside Bertha.

At the table the candles burned steadily. The fruit glowed in the grey bowl. Glasses, silver and white plates floated upon the dark polished wood, and beyond the windows the garden pressed close, blue-black and secret.

Bertha sat at one end and Harry at the other. For an instant she saw the whole table as a painting: Face’s orange sleeves, Norman’s pointed beard, Eddie’s long hands, Pearl’s silver dress, Harry’s dark head. And herself, in white and green and violet, holding them all together.

Her party. Her life.

She wanted to lift her glass and drink to it, though no one had proposed anything.

The soup came, pale green with a swirl of cream.

Norman was explaining his new theatre.

“No stage,” he said. “The stage is tyranny. The audience must not know where to look.”

“Then why come?” Harry asked.

“Exactly!” cried Norman. “That is the first artistic question.”

Face leaned toward Bertha.

“We’re doing a piece entirely in staircases.”

“How wonderful.”

“There are no actors. Only people who have failed to arrive somewhere else.”

Eddie shuddered delicately.

“Staircases are dreadful. They always imply a body above you.”

“Or below,” said Harry.

“Below is too obvious.”

Pearl said nothing. She lifted her spoon, tasted the soup and set the spoon down again.

Was she unhappy? No. Not unhappy. Waiting.

Bertha knew she was waiting.

For what? For the moment when the two of them would be alone near the window, perhaps. They would look at the pear tree. That was it. Pearl must see the tree.

The idea came with such certainty that Bertha’s hand shook and her spoon struck the bowl.

Harry glanced at her.

“All right, Ber?”

“Perfectly.”

“Too much sherry?”

“I’ve had almost none.”

“Then have some.”

There was laughter. Bertha laughed too. But beneath the conversation another current had begun to run, silent and bright, between her and Pearl.

Surely Pearl felt it. Once, when Norman declared that furniture was more moral than people because it supported one without asking questions, Pearl looked up. Her eyes met Bertha’s.

Only for a second.

Yet in that second Bertha thought: Yes.

Yes, she knows.

The fish was served with lemon and herbs. Then a small roast, pink within, surrounded by glazed vegetables. The dishes appeared and vanished. Wine reddened the glasses. The candles shortened. Talk leapt from theatres to books, from books to a sculptor who had married his model’s mother, from the model’s mother to morality, from morality to whether a hat could be insincere.

“Of course a hat can lie,” said Face. “Most hats are alibis.”

“I once owned a hat that confessed everything,” Eddie said. “I had to burn it.”

Harry ate heartily and kept the wine moving. He was a good host, Bertha thought. He seemed impatient with the talk, yet his impatience gave it force, as a wall gives force to a thrown ball.

But he remained rude to Pearl.

When she asked him whether he had seen a certain exhibition, he said, “No,” and turned immediately to Norman. When Mary offered vegetables, Harry spoke across Pearl as if her chair were empty. Once he caught Bertha’s eye and made a tiny grimace.

Really! Like a schoolboy.

Pearl appeared not to notice. She sat with her shoulders relaxed, her long white fingers curved around the stem of her glass. Now and then a half-smile touched her lips and vanished before it could become amusement.

How beautiful she was. Not warm beauty, not the beauty of roses or firelight. She was like moonlight on metal. Like the pear tree under stars.

The pear tree.

Bertha could hardly bear to wait.

Dessert was a white cream in glass dishes, with crimson cherries. Eddie said it resembled a drowned duchess. Face announced that all white food was socially ambitious. Harry ate two helpings.

Then they returned to the drawing room.

Coffee arrived. Norman and Harry stood by the fire discussing some impossible business scheme of Norman’s involving a cinema, a farm and unemployed violinists. Face curled on the sofa and smoked through a long amber holder. Eddie bent over a portfolio of prints, murmuring, “Too fleshy, too fleshy,” though the prints were landscapes.

Pearl wandered toward the windows.

At once Bertha followed.

No one noticed them. Or if they did, it did not matter.

The curtains had not been drawn. Outside, the garden had changed. Night had deepened to a dark blue, and a thin moon hung above the wall. The pear tree stood in the centre of the window, white and motionless, each branch distinct.

Bertha reached for the curtain cord but did not pull it.

“There,” she said softly.

Pearl came beside her.

For a while neither spoke.

The room behind them continued: Norman’s voice rising, Face’s dry laughter, the clink of a coffee cup. Yet all of it seemed to recede, to become a faint machinery turning far away.

The tree filled the world.

Its blossoms held the moonlight. Its upper branches narrowed into a point, not sharp but aspiring, like a pale flame that had forgotten how to flicker. The grass beneath it was invisible; the trunk appeared to rise from pure darkness.

Bertha felt Pearl’s arm near hers. Not touching. Almost.

And suddenly the happiness within her took form. It was no longer a confusion of baby, house, food, friends, colour, cold air. It was this: herself and Pearl before the tree. Two beings recognising in silence the same hidden radiance.

They had not invented it. They had found it.

Bertha’s throat tightened.

Pearl turned her face a fraction. Her profile was luminous. Her eyelids lifted, and she looked not at the tree but at Bertha.

There it was again.

Yes.

A look could say everything. A look could cross all those years of ordinary speech and arrive immediately at the centre. Bertha felt known—completely, almost frighteningly known.

Then Pearl lowered her eyes.

“The blossoms seem lit from within,” she said.

Bertha’s fingers closed around the curtain cord.

“Yes.”

Nothing more was needed.

Behind them Harry called, “Bertha, where’ve you hidden the brandy?”

She turned, laughing.

“In the cabinet where it always is.”

“Then why does it move every time I want it?”

The spell broke, but not entirely. It entered Bertha instead. She carried it back into the room.

Harry was kneeling at the cabinet. The line of his back beneath the black cloth, the strength in his bent neck, struck her with astonishing force.

She stared.

Something new moved through her happiness—something warmer, darker, almost unbearable.

She wanted Harry.

The knowledge was so sudden that she caught the edge of a chair.

Of course she loved him. She had always loved him. They had kissed, slept together, had a child. But desire—this direct, bodily longing—had somehow remained outside their marriage, like a guest repeatedly invited who never arrived. She had accepted that. One could not have everything, after all. Affection, laughter, companionship, a baby—surely these were enough.

But now the guest had come.

Harry stood with the brandy bottle in his hand, arguing with Norman. His face was animated; his teeth showed when he laughed. Bertha imagined the guests gone, the house quiet, the lamps extinguished one by one. She imagined going upstairs with him.

Tonight.

She would tell him. Or perhaps she need not. Perhaps he would see it at once.

The thought made the room pulse.

Was this what the tree had meant? Was this the flowering? Her happiness, Pearl’s understanding, the pear tree, Harry—everything connected, everything moving toward one exquisite conclusion.

She looked at Pearl with gratitude.

Yes, gratitude. Somehow Pearl had opened the door. By recognising Bertha’s joy she had made it complete, and in completeness it had become desire.

Pearl stood alone near the window, pale and composed.

Harry gave her a brandy without asking.

Bertha noticed and smiled. So he had decided to behave at last.

The evening became very gay. Face performed an imitation of a famous actress ordering boiled eggs. Norman attempted to balance Eddie’s coffee cup on the point of his beard. Eddie read aloud from a new poem which began, “Rain is the aunt of mirrors,” and stopped when Harry said, “Whose aunt?”

Everyone laughed until Face’s cigarette fell into a flowerpot.

Even Pearl laughed, though silently, her head tilted back.

Bertha wanted the night to continue forever and to end at once.

At last Face rose.

“We must go. Norman has an appointment at midnight with a man who wishes to abolish scenery.”

“He has already abolished punctuality,” said Norman.

Coats were brought. The hall filled with farewells, silk, fur, dropped gloves and unfinished sentences.

Face seized Bertha’s hands.

“A perfect evening. The cream was indecently successful.”

“You’ll come Sunday?”

“If I am not arrested before then.”

Norman bowed over Bertha’s hand, then over Mary’s by mistake. Eddie could not find his scarf, although it was around his neck.

Harry opened the door. Cold air swept in.

“Who’s taking Eddie?” Bertha asked.

“I can walk,” Eddie said faintly.

“You cannot,” said Face. “You’d interpret every lamp-post as a warning.”

The Knights bundled him between them and departed noisily.

Their motorcar pulled away. The house became abruptly quiet.

Pearl was still in the drawing room.

Bertha’s heart gave a small leap.

She had forgotten Pearl must also leave. Or perhaps she had imagined—what? That Pearl would remain as a sort of silver witness while Bertha and Harry went upstairs? How absurd. Happiness had made her absurd all evening.

Mary brought Pearl’s black wrap.

“I’ll fetch a cab,” Harry said.

“My driver should be at the corner,” Pearl replied.

“I’ll see.”

He went into the hall.

Pearl slipped one arm into the wrap. Bertha helped with the other. The fabric was cold and soft, and for a moment Bertha’s fingers rested on Pearl’s shoulder.

“What a lovely evening,” Pearl said.

“Was it?”

“Very.”

Bertha longed to ask: You felt it, didn’t you? At the window? You saw what I saw?

But Pearl’s face was closed again, its secret light hidden.

She moved toward the hall. Bertha followed, then stopped because Mary called from the dining room.

“Madam, shall I leave the fruit?”

“The fruit? Oh—yes. Leave it.”

It took no more than a second.

When Bertha turned back, Pearl was gone from the drawing-room doorway.

Harry’s voice came from the hall, low and hurried.

Bertha smiled. He was probably making some last clumsy attempt at politeness. She went toward them, but before she reached the door she saw their reflections in the small oval mirror above the lacquer table.

At first the image made no sense.

Harry stood with his back partly toward her. Pearl was close to him—much too close. His hand was on the black wrap at her waist. No, beneath it. Her face, lifted to his, had changed completely. There was nothing cold in it.

Harry bent his head.

Pearl’s lips touched his.

Bertha did not move.

The mirror held them neatly, decoratively, inside its narrow gilt frame. Harry dark, Pearl pale. Black and silver. A finished arrangement.

They separated.

“Tomorrow,” Harry whispered.

Pearl looked at him.

“Same time?”

She nodded.

“At the flat?”

Another nod.

Harry’s fingers tightened at her waist. Pearl’s eyes closed. It was only a small closing of the eyelids, slow and intimate—the very gesture Bertha had believed belonged to their secret understanding.

Then Harry opened the front door.

Pearl stepped away from him.

Bertha remained in the drawing room, one hand resting on the back of a chair. She felt no blow, no cry rising in her, none of the things that ought to happen. Her body was extraordinarily calm. Even her heart seemed to have stopped not from shock but from attention.

She heard Pearl say, in her usual quiet voice, “Good night, Mrs. Young.”

Mrs. Young.

Bertha went into the hall.

Pearl stood on the threshold, wrapped in black. Beyond her, the street shone faintly under the lamps.

“Good night,” Bertha said.

Her voice sounded natural.

Pearl glanced past her toward the drawing-room windows.

“Your pear tree is magnificent,” she said.

For one instant their eyes met.

Did Pearl know that Bertha knew?

Yes. No. Her face revealed nothing. Or perhaps everything had always been there and Bertha had read the signs backward, turning concealment into sympathy, appetite into mystery, silence into communion.

Pearl smiled—barely.

Then she was gone.

Harry shut the door.

“Well,” he said, stretching his shoulders, “thank God that’s over.”

Bertha looked at him.

He picked up his gloves from the table. His face was ordinary. Slightly tired, slightly amused. There was a faint mark near his mouth where Pearl’s lipstick had touched him, though Pearl’s lipstick was very pale.

“Not a bad dinner,” he said. “The lamb was good.”

Bertha continued to look.

Harry frowned.

“What is it?”

What was it?

What could she say? The words stood somewhere beyond reach, like people on the opposite bank of a flooded river.

You kissed her.

Tomorrow.

The flat.

But if she said them, the house would hear. The blue bowl would hear; the red table, the staircase, the nursery, the sleeping baby. Every object would be forced to change its meaning. The rooms would split open.

Harry glanced toward the dining room.

“Any brandy left?”

Bertha’s hand slid from the chair.

“Harry—”

“Yes?”

He waited.

How direct he looked. How impatient. His body already angled away from her, toward the drink, toward tomorrow, toward some life that had been taking place beside hers while she arranged fruit and admired colours.

“Harry—what happens now?”

He stared.

“Now?”

She heard how strange the question sounded.

“Yes. Now.”

He gave a short laugh.

“Bed, I should think. Unless our theatrical friends have hidden under the sofa.”

He walked past her into the dining room.

Bertha did not follow.

The front hall, emptied of coats and voices, seemed larger than before. On the floor lay one white petal from Face’s buttonhole, bruised at the edge. The door chain swung slightly, though the door was shut.

Upstairs, faintly, the baby cried once and stopped.

Bertha went to the drawing-room window.

The fire had sunk low. Cups stood abandoned on the small tables. One cushion had fallen to the floor. Eddie’s cigarette smouldered in a saucer, giving off a narrow blue thread.

She parted the curtains.

The garden waited.

There was the pear tree.

Perfectly still. Perfectly flowered. White beneath the thin moon.

Nothing in it had altered. Not one branch had bent. Not one blossom had fallen. It rose from the darkness with the same serene, impossible beauty, as though beauty had never promised anything, as though it had never spoken at all.

Bertha pressed her forehead to the cold glass.

Behind her, Harry opened a cupboard and moved bottles. Above her, the child slept. Somewhere beyond the square, Pearl was being carried through the night toward another room, another door, tomorrow.

And the tree remained—luminous, flowering, unchanged.

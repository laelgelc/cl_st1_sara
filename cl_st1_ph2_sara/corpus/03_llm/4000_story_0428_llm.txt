# The Twelve Walnuts of Monsieur de Samblançay

In the year when the good city of Tours had more priests than chimneys, more lawsuits than loaves, and as many pretty women as there were loopholes in the walls—which is saying a great deal, for the walls were old and the women young—there entered by the gate of Saint-Éloi a certain Jacques de Beaune, carrying beneath his cloak all his possessions, namely: one shirt tolerably white at the collar, a sword without a scabbard, a purse without a coin, and a pedigree long enough to have bridged the Loire had pedigrees been made of timber.

He was then twenty-two years of age, or twenty-three according to a register burned later by the English, the Burgundians, the rats, or the canons of Saint-Martin, each of whom has been blamed according to the politics of the historian. He was tall, with shoulders such as tailors love and creditors distrust, because good shoulders fill velvet bravely though they do not pay for it. His hair was black, his eye lively, his nose venturesome, his teeth all present, and his legs so straight that an abbess, having once seen them at mass, forgot the response and said *Amen* in the middle of the Epistle. Hunger had hollowed his cheeks a little, but hunger in a young man is like frost upon a March field: it sharpens the green without killing the crop.

This Jacques was the son of a gentleman formerly employed in the household of the Lady of Beaujeu, daughter of King Louis XI and then Regent of France during the minority of her brother Charles VIII. His father, who had kept accounts, supervised estates, restrained purveyors, appeased tenants, fed hounds, bribed ushers, and performed the seven other labours necessary to the administration of great houses, had fallen into disgrace through a matter of money. Whether he had taken what was the Crown’s, or merely failed to take enough for those above him, is not now known. In courts these are opposite offences on parchment and the same offence in practice. He had been dismissed, mulcted, threatened, and sent home to brood over his innocence with the obstinacy peculiar to ruined men.

Before Jacques set out, the old man had laid both hands upon his son’s shoulders.

“Restore our name,” he said, “but do not sell your honour.”

“My honour,” replied Jacques, “is the only property left to us, and therefore the likeliest to find a purchaser.”

At this the father raised his hand to strike him, then lowered it, perceiving that wit was also among the family remnants.

“Go to Tours,” he said. “The court passes between Tours, Plessis, and Amboise as a cat passes among three kitchens. Find a protector.”

“With what shall I feed him?”

“With hope.”

“That is a thin sauce.”

“Then feed him flattery. Great persons digest it even in Lent.”

Thus instructed, Jacques had come to Tours, where during the first day he presented himself at three noble doors, two ecclesiastical doors, and the house of a financier whose knocker was shaped like a lion swallowing a sinner. From the first he was dismissed because his doublet was worn; from the second because his boots were muddy; from the third because the lord was at dinner; from the fourth because the bishop was at confession; from the fifth because the financier had heard his name. He finished the day with less hope than he had begun it, which seems impossible, but poverty is an arithmetician capable of subtracting from zero.

On the following morning he sold the silver buckle from his belt and breakfasted upon bread, onions, and a measure of wine so small that the tavern girl carried it in one hand without spilling. Thus fortified, he walked beneath the arcades near the great church, observing the passers-by and considering whether Fortune, who is painted blind, might not nevertheless be tempted by a handsome face.

At noon she appeared.

She came from a goldsmith’s shop accompanied by two ladies, a page, a chaplain, and a servant who bore a little box of chased silver. Her gown was of dark violet velvet, bordered with sable; its train moved behind her like a royal thought too weighty to be hurried. She was no longer in the first May of womanhood, nor even in the uncertain sunshine of June, but rather in that noble August when the grain is ripe, the fruit perfumed, and the wise husband locks the garden gate. Her complexion was clear, her mouth firm yet desirous, her bosom authoritative, and her eyes—those two councillors who betrayed the secrets of the sovereign—quick, grey, and accustomed to obedience.

She walked with measured majesty; but at the turn of the street, seeing Jacques planted beneath a carved saint, she looked at him once, then again, the second glance being shorter and therefore more dangerous.

Jacques uncovered.

The lady did not smile. A Regent may order a hanging before breakfast and a marriage after dinner, but she does not smile in public at an unknown gentleman whose sleeves shine at the elbows. Nevertheless, when she had passed, the page looked back, then one of the ladies, then the servant with the box. Last of all the chaplain looked back also, though whether at Jacques or at the tavern sign across the way no chronicler has decided.

Jacques followed.

Now, certain moralists have maintained that it is imprudent to follow women in strange cities, because one may meet a husband, a brother, or a ditch. This is true of ordinary women. But an extraordinary woman is a road laid by Providence, and the traveller who refuses it deserves to die at the tollgate. Jacques kept twenty paces behind, appearing to inspect windows, gargoyles, shop signs, and the calves of maidservants, all of which are common objects of antiquarian study.

The lady entered a mansion near the river, a house with a high wall, a turreted gate, and windows so stern that they seemed to collect taxes from the daylight. Her people went in after her. The gate closed.

Jacques remained outside.

He walked once around the house and learned nothing except that the wall was too high. He walked around it a second time and discovered a narrow lane beneath the rear windows. On his third circuit he found a cooper’s boy eating an apple and asked who lived there.

“A great lady,” said the boy.

“What name?”

“A very great lady.”

“Whose servants?”

“Her own.”

Jacques gave him the last crust from his pocket.

“The Lady of Beaujeu,” said the boy, and ran away before he could be questioned concerning affairs of state.

At this revelation Jacques leaned against the wall. Here indeed was Fortune, not blind but squinting wickedly at him. Madame de Beaujeu, called Anne of France, was the daughter of Louis XI, sister to the young king, wife to the Duke of Bourbon, guardian of the realm, terror of rebellious princes, netter of conspirators, collector of taxes, arranger of marriages, and, what mattered more to Jacques, former mistress of his father’s fate.

He had followed the Regent of France as a hungry dog follows a butcher’s wife.

“What is to be done?” he asked himself.

Had he possessed money, he might have bribed an usher. Had he possessed fine clothes, he might have presented a petition. Had he possessed patience, he might have waited three months in an antechamber. But having none of these, he possessed only his body and his wits, two currencies which pass everywhere though princes affect to despise them.

While he deliberated, a casement opened above him.

Jacques looked up.

A maidservant leaned out with a vessel which, by its shape, its steam, and the haste with which she held it at arm’s length, had not been filled at any holy fountain. She glanced left, glanced right, failed to glance down, and emptied it.

The contents descended upon Jacques.

There are moments when the destiny of kingdoms hangs upon a marriage, a battle, a signature, or a chamber pot. This was one of them.

Jacques could have cursed. He could have leaped aside. He could have announced that the blood of the Beaunes, though impoverished, was not to be watered from windows. Instead he uttered a tremendous cry, clapped both hands to his head, staggered three steps, spun like a weathercock in a storm, and fell full length upon the stones.

The maid screamed.

“A man! Sainte Vierge, I have killed a man!”

At once three female heads appeared, then two male ones, then the chaplain’s tonsure. The gate opened. Servants rushed out and gathered around the corpse.

“He is bleeding,” said one.

“That is not blood,” said another.

“He does not breathe.”

“Turn him over.”

“No, do not move him! A dead man moved unlawfully becomes the responsibility of whoever moves him. My cousin knew a notary—”

“Carry him inside,” commanded a woman’s voice.

It was the voice of the Regent.

Four servants lifted Jacques, who allowed his arms to swing and his head to loll with all the dignity of Saint Denis after decapitation. He was conveyed through the gate, across a paved court, and into a lower chamber. There he was laid upon a table ordinarily used for folding linen, which offended him slightly, since even in counterfeit death a gentleman prefers a bed.

Madame de Beaujeu entered.

“Which fool did this?” she asked.

The maid fell to her knees.

“Mercy, Madame! I looked both ways.”

“You neglected the third.”

“I did not know there was a third way.”

“There is always a third way,” said the Regent, thereby summarising the whole art of government.

The chaplain bent over Jacques and placed a hand upon his breast.

“I perceive no movement.”

Jacques held his breath.

“Give him absolution,” said one lady.

“If he is dead, it is late,” said the other.

“If alive, it may be early,” replied the chaplain.

The Regent stepped nearer. Jacques smelled amber, violet leaves, warm velvet, and that faint iron fragrance belonging to women who command men. She studied his face.

“I saw this young fellow in the street,” she said.

Although dead, Jacques was flattered.

“A handsome corpse,” murmured one lady.

“A poor gentleman, by his clothes.”

“Death is the tailor who dresses all ranks alike,” observed the chaplain, having saved this sentence for years.

The Regent touched Jacques’s cheek with two fingers.

“He is warm.”

“Recently killed,” said the chaplain.

At that moment Jacques opened his eyes.

The maid shrieked again, the chaplain dropped his holy-water sprinkler, one lady crossed herself, and the other seized a warming pan as a weapon. Jacques sat up, wiped his face with the edge of the linen cloth, and bowed to the Regent.

“Madame,” he said, “I beg pardon for entering without announcement. Your porter would not have admitted me, but your maid found a means.”

The Regent stared. She had been deceived by ambassadors, princes, bishops, rebels, and once by her own physician, who prescribed boiled lettuce for a fever; but never had she been imposed upon by a young man dripping upon her linen.

“You are not dead.”

“Not in your presence, Madame.”

“Then why this comedy?”

“To obtain an audience.”

“An audience may cost you a whipping.”

“I had rather be whipped within your house than ignored outside it.”

The ladies tittered. The Regent silenced them with a look, though the corner of her mouth had moved.

“Who are you?”

“Jacques de Beaune, son of the man who once served Your Highness faithfully, until his enemies proved that fidelity without friends is a crime.”

At the name, the Regent’s expression altered. She remembered the father, his precise accounts, his stubborn pride, and the accusation which had removed him. Great rulers forget a thousand petitions but remember the servants who know where the money went.

“Your father was condemned.”

“Then he has need of mercy.”

“He was accused of diverting revenues.”

“He says he diverted them from thieves into the Treasury.”

“All disgraced officers say the same.”

“True, Madame; but not all have figures to prove it.”

“Have you the figures?”

“No. The men who accused him kept those.”

This answer pleased her, because it was both insolent and probable.

“And you came to ask his pardon?”

“I came to lay my life at your feet.”

“You laid it upon my folding table.”

“The floor was wet.”

The Regent laughed. It was not a loud laugh, for rulers laugh as misers give alms, unwilling to encourage expectation; yet everyone in the chamber heard it, and the maid who had emptied the vessel knew herself saved.

Madame de Beaujeu dismissed the servants, retaining only her two ladies. She questioned Jacques regarding his father, his family, his education, and his means. He answered honestly whenever truth was advantageous, and otherwise with that higher honesty which expresses what ought to have happened. He had studied accounts, law, horsemanship, fortification, Latin enough to expose a false quotation, and theology enough not to argue with a bishop before supper. He could write three hands, cast columns of figures without moving his lips, estimate the yield of an estate by smelling its barns, and tell from a steward’s boots whether the tenants were being robbed.

“You claim many accomplishments,” said the Regent.

“Poverty examines a man in all of them.”

“And what office do you seek?”

“The one nearest Your Highness.”

One lady coughed. The other examined the ceiling.

Madame de Beaujeu’s eyes narrowed.

“Near my person or near my purse?”

“In wise government, Madame, are they not the same?”

Now you may be sure that this was dangerous speech. Another man might have been sent to the guardroom, there to learn from sticks the proper distance between a subject and the Regent. But Jacques had judged his woman as he would later judge a debtor: by the pause before the answer. She was amused, and because she was amused she became curious; and because she was curious she began to desire. These three movements are the drawbridge, gate, and inner stair of many a fortress.

She ordered that he be washed, fed, and given a serviceable suit from the wardrobe of a secretary recently dismissed for marrying without permission.

Within an hour Jacques returned transformed. Food had restored colour to his face, clean linen had declared his neck, and the secretary’s black doublet fitted him so closely that the Regent regretted the man’s dismissal only because he had not owned more clothes.

That evening she sent Jacques to carry a memorandum to the royal lodging. The memorandum contained nothing that could not have been entrusted to a page, but rulers begin their greatest experiments under the appearance of trifles. Jacques returned with the proper answer, the names of everyone in the antechamber, an account of which lord had whispered to which bishop, and the news that a clerk of the Treasury was selling information to a Breton agent.

“How did you learn this?” asked the Regent.

“By losing at dice to his servant.”

“But you had no money.”

“I promised to pay.”

“With what?”

“With the money I shall earn by exposing his master.”

The Regent looked at him for a long moment.

“You are either useful or intolerable.”

“Permit me to be useful first.”

For several days she employed him in small matters. He found mistakes in rent rolls, discovered that eight royal horses had been fed for two years after their deaths, and demonstrated that a supplier of candles had invoiced wax sufficient to illuminate Noah’s ark throughout the Flood. In each affair he showed neither reverence for thieves nor pity for fools, and the Regent, who considered both expensive, came to value him.

She also came to observe him.

If Jacques stood over a ledger, she noticed his hands; if he crossed the court, she noticed his gait; if he bowed, she noticed the recovery. Desire in a mature woman does not gallop noisily like a page’s horse. It advances under cover, sends scouts, purchases informers, studies the ground, and occupies the fortress before the garrison knows there is a war.

One evening the Regent asked, “Have you ever been to Amboise?”

“Never, Madame.”

“Would you like to see the château?”

“I should like to see whatever Your Highness sees.”

“You answer too quickly.”

“My heart speaks before my prudence can dress itself.”

“Then your prudence goes naked often.”

“In your company, always.”

She turned away, but he saw the colour rise beneath her ear.

Two nights later a covered litter left Tours by the river road. It was accompanied by six mounted servants wearing no badge and commanded by an officer who had been told that the Regent travelled upon urgent business of the Crown. This was true. All desire is urgent, and a Regent’s desire is necessarily business of the Crown.

Jacques rode behind until they had passed Montlouis. There the litter stopped beneath poplars silvered by the moon. One of the curtains lifted.

“Monsieur de Beaune,” said the Regent, “the road is cold. Sit within.”

The litter was wide enough for two persons who respected ceremony or four who did not. Jacques entered, and the procession resumed.

Madame de Beaujeu wore a hooded mantle lined with fur. She had left jewels behind, but not perfume. For some time they spoke of taxes, the ambitions of the Duke of Orléans, the young king’s fondness for tournaments, and the unreliability of Breton fortresses. This is how great ladies approach tender matters: by marching around them with artillery.

At last she said, “You are ambitious.”

“As a vine is ambitious to climb a wall.”

“And if the wall lifts the vine, what fruit does it offer?”

“Whatever fruit the wall commands.”

“Even out of season?”

“For Your Highness, I would make January blush like June.”

The Regent laughed softly.

“Young men are prodigal in promises.”

“They possess little else.”

“I have heard boasts from soldiers who fled the first cannon, poets who fainted after one stanza, and lovers who slept before midnight.”

“Those were not men of Touraine.”

“What distinction has Touraine?”

“Its wine strengthens the blood, its women teach gratitude, and its walnuts grow in dozens.”

“Walnuts?”

“Twelve to the branch, Madame, and all easily brought down by a stout stick.”

The Regent understood him perfectly, as she understood treaties written in cipher and petitions written in tears. Yet she preferred to prolong the reconnaissance.

“You imply that you could strike twelve from one tree?”

“In one night.”

“Pasques Dieu! Twelve?”

“If the tree were noble, the moon favourable, and the gardener merciful.”

“Suppose the stick broke?”

“It is young ash.”

“Suppose the arm tired?”

“It serves a faithful heart.”

“Suppose the walnuts resisted?”

“I have never known a walnut unwilling to fall when properly addressed.”

At this the Regent closed the curtain more tightly, lest the outriders hear her laughter.

They reached Amboise after midnight, entering through a postern where the captain had been ordered to ask no questions—which made him certain of the answer. The Regent led Jacques by a private stair into apartments overlooking the black Loire. Fires burned in both chambers, wine and sweetmeats stood upon a table, and the bed, with curtains of crimson damask, was large enough for a royal marriage, a council meeting, or the siege presently intended.

Madame de Beaujeu removed her gloves.

“Before we test the walnuts,” she said, “there must be terms.”

Jacques bowed as though before the Parlement.

“If you perform the promised dozen between now and sunrise, your father shall be pardoned.”

“That would be worth six.”

“The manor of Azay shall be granted to you.”

“Worth three more.”

“And I shall place you in an office where a man who knows figures may multiply his fortune without diminishing mine.”

“That accounts for the remaining three.”

“But if you fail—”

“I shall have served without wages.”

“You shall leave before dawn, speak of this to no one, and never again present yourself before me.”

Jacques considered the penalty. To lose patronage was dreadful; to lose the lady worse; to lose both after eleven victories would have broken a saint.

“Agreed,” he said. “But the count must be fair.”

“I shall count.”

“Women count kisses as misers count crowns: they often forget those received and remember those withheld.”

“Then I shall mark each upon this cord.”

She took from a sewing basket a length of blue silk and tied twelve loose knots in it.

“Each salute earns one knot undone.”

“Madame,” said Jacques, “France could not be more justly governed.”

The Regent rang a small bell. Her attendants withdrew from the outer chamber, the doors were barred, and the night began.

What happened thereafter has been related by ecclesiastics with indignation, by courtiers with envy, by physicians with disbelief, and by the women of Touraine with a degree of precision suggesting supernatural revelation. I shall preserve decency, not because the participants did, but because ink is costly and metaphor plentiful.

The first walnut fell promptly, with the ease of ripe fruit in September.

The second followed while the château clock was striking one.

The third required a draught of wine, not from weakness but from ceremony, as armies drink before an assault.

The fourth came down after the Regent declared that men always boast of what chance has granted them. Jacques, wounded in his professional honour, proved chance innocent.

At the fifth, Madame de Beaujeu laughed so loudly that a guard in the court below crossed himself and later swore that the ghost of Louis XI was pacing the gallery.

The sixth was an act of pure administration. It was conducted briskly, counted carefully, and entered in memory without ornament.

Here they paused to eat sugared pears, capon paste, and little cakes flavoured with aniseed. Jacques would have preferred beef, but no man wishes to explain to a Regent, in the midst of gallantry, that her supper lacks military substance.

“You are pale,” she said.

“It is the moon.”

“The curtains are closed.”

“Then it is devotion.”

“Would you stop?”

“At six? Madame, a half-dozen is fit for eggs, apostles sent two by two, or candles purchased by a poor curate. It is not a royal measure.”

Thus encouraged by his own rhetoric, he brought down the seventh.

The eighth cost him more. He afterwards maintained that a draught came under the door and chilled the artillery, but this is the excuse of all commanders whose powder has grown damp. Nevertheless, courage warmed what weather discouraged, and the eighth knot was loosened.

At the ninth the Regent, whose appetite increased with victory as certain generals grow bolder when their troops are exhausted, proposed a variation borrowed, she claimed, from an ancient manuscript concerning the customs of the Greeks. Jacques suspected there had been no manuscript, but scholarship must be respected. The ninth walnut fell into a field unknown to ordinary horticulture.

The tenth was achieved near three in the morning. Bells sounded faintly from the town below, calling monks to prayer and honest bakers to work. Jacques heard them as a mariner hears a buoy through fog. His limbs were heavy, his breath short, and the crimson curtains appeared to sway like banners above a battlefield littered with the slain. Yet the Regent’s eyes shone, and the tenth knot slipped free.

“My father’s pardon,” he murmured.

“Ten,” she said.

“The manor?”

“Not yet.”

“The office?”

“Two more.”

“By the rood, Madame, you collect service as strictly as tax.”

“That is why France remains mine.”

For the eleventh Jacques summoned his family honour, his hunger, his youth, the memory of unpaid tavern bills, the imagined towers of Azay, all the saints of Touraine, and certain pagan deities whom the Church has wisely left unemployed. These forces, united under one banner, prevailed.

The eleventh walnut struck the grass.

The Regent untied the eleventh knot.

Only one remained.

Jacques lay back like a horse which has carried dispatches from Paris to Rome without changing saddle. The room seemed immensely quiet. The fire collapsed into embers. Somewhere behind the wall a mouse gnawed with offensive vigour.

“One more,” said Madame de Beaujeu.

“One,” Jacques agreed.

“A little one.”

“There are no little acts in royal service.”

“You spoke differently at the first.”

“At the first I had not yet served the Crown eleven times.”

She gave him wine. He drank. She gave him cordial distilled from cinnamon, cloves, and substances imported by Venetians at prices proving their wickedness. He drank that also. She rubbed his shoulders, praised his valour, doubted his valour, insulted the men of Touraine, extolled the men of Burgundy, and finally threatened to send for one of her guards to finish the count.

This last device raised Jacques from the pillow as the trumpet raises a fallen knight.

He addressed the twelfth campaign.

The spirit was willing; the flesh drew up a petition. He argued, commanded, encouraged, and promised preferment. The flesh, having already performed beyond its ordinary feudal obligation, refused further levy. He changed tactics. He rested. He attacked from the left, from the right, and by a route whose engineering merits need not detain us. Nothing availed. The final walnut trembled upon its branch but contained, as events proved, no proper kernel of obedience.

At the first grey of dawn a cock crowed from the lower town.

Jacques collapsed.

The Regent sat upright, triumphant, flushed, and magnificent, with her loosened hair upon her shoulders and the blue cord in her hand. Eleven knots hung open. The twelfth remained tied.

“You have lost,” she said.

Jacques could not answer immediately, because breath is the coin with which speech must be purchased and his treasury was empty.

“You promised twelve,” she continued. “I count eleven.”

He raised one finger.

“That is not twelve.”

He raised the second, reconsidered, and let it fall.

“You have lost the pardon, Azay, the office, and access to my person.”

At this last penalty Jacques found voice.

“Madame, I appeal.”

“To whom?”

“To your council.”

The Regent stared, then laughed so heartily that she nearly fell from the bed.

“You would submit this matter to the council of France?”

“Why not? It concerns a contract with the Regent, a disputed performance, three promised considerations, and a question of natural deficiency. Such matters have founded wars.”

“And how will you state your case?”

“With respect for the dignity of all parties.”

“My councillors are old foxes.”

“Then they will understand walnuts.”

Her amusement overcame prudence. Moreover, she believed victory certain, for no man could publicly plead the facts without hanging himself upon his own evidence.

“Very well,” she said. “At council this morning you may present a hypothetical case. If they rule for you, I shall pay the wager. If they suspect the truth—”

“I shall return to the folding table and this time be dead in earnest.”

At nine o’clock the Regent’s council assembled in a vaulted chamber at Amboise. There were present the Chancellor, a bishop with a purple nose, two presidents of accounts, a captain of archers, an elderly duke, three secretaries, and Master Cotereau, a jurist so dry that flies died upon his opinions. The young king was away hunting, which was fortunate, because kings under eighteen transform all secret business into ballads.

Madame de Beaujeu took her seat beneath the canopy. She appeared composed, severe, and altogether incapable of having spent the night as reported above. Jacques stood near the door in the secretary’s black doublet, pale but upright. His knees trembled, though less from fear than from public service.

After ordinary matters—grain duties, a rebellious baron, repairs to a bridge, and a quarrel between two abbeys over the right to drown pigs in the same stream—the Regent said:

“A question of law has been proposed by this gentleman. Let him state it.”

Jacques bowed.

“My lords, imagine a poor but vigorous traveller who, passing through an orchard, sees a noble walnut tree. The lady who owns the tree says to him, ‘With one stick, before sunrise, knock down twelve walnuts, and I shall reward you with a pardon, an estate, and employment.’”

The Chancellor glanced at the Regent. The Regent inspected a document.

“The traveller agrees,” continued Jacques. “He strikes faithfully and brings down eleven sound walnuts. At the twelfth stroke, a final shell falls from the branch; but when examined, it proves empty, having no kernel within. The lady therefore refuses payment, alleging that only eleven true walnuts were gathered. The traveller maintains that twelve shells fell, and that the barrenness of the last cannot be charged against the stick. I ask this council: has he performed his obligation?”

The bishop’s purple nose deepened toward crimson.

The captain coughed into his gauntlet.

One president of accounts bent suddenly to search for a quill beneath the table and remained there longer than necessary.

Master Cotereau alone received the question with legal calm.

“Was the number twelve expressly stipulated?”

“It was.”

“And all twelve objects were attached to the tree before the operation?”

“So it appeared.”

“Did the traveller select the empty nut?”

“No, my lord. The lady offered the entire branch.”

“Was he warranted that each contained a kernel?”

“No warranty was spoken.”

“Did the twelfth shell become empty because of the blow?”

Jacques placed a hand upon his heart.

“My lord, the traveller is prepared to swear that he employed every reasonable force to discover a kernel.”

The duke sneezed explosively and turned it into a prayer.

The Chancellor said, “The question is whether performance relates to the fall of the nuts or to their interior quality.”

“Precisely,” replied Jacques.

The first president emerged from beneath the table.

“In accounts,” he said, “we reckon containers when the levy is imposed by measure, though some prove deficient.”

“But a walnut is valued for its meat,” argued the second.

“A manor is valued for its revenues,” said the first, “yet we still call barren land a manor.”

The bishop cleared his throat.

“Nature sometimes presents the appearance of fruit without the substance. The labourer cannot be blamed if, having shaken the tree with due zeal, he discovers the fault to be in generation.”

The captain nodded gravely.

“A bombardier ordered to fire twelve shots has obeyed if the twelfth charge makes smoke but no breach, provided the powder was supplied by the fortress.”

At this the Regent’s composure cracked. Her mouth twitched. Jacques lowered his eyes.

Master Cotereau folded his hands.

“I distinguish,” he said, which was his manner of beginning all judgments and most meals. “If the bargain required the gathering of twelve kernels, the claimant fails. If it required the knocking down of twelve walnuts, and if in common speech a walnut denotes both shell and contents, then the claimant succeeds upon the fall of the twelfth shell, unless fraud, concealment, substitution, witchcraft, or ecclesiastical privilege be proved.”

“None is alleged,” said Jacques.

“Then,” concluded Cotereau, “the twelfth walnut fell. Its emptiness is a defect of the tree, not a failure of the stick.”

The council murmured assent.

Madame de Beaujeu looked around the table.

“Is that the unanimous judgment?”

“It is,” said the Chancellor, whose eyes now shone with suppressed laughter.

“Then let it be recorded that the traveller has won.”

“Must the allegory be recorded?” asked a secretary.

“No,” said the Regent. “Burn your notes.”

Thus Jacques de Beaune obtained by legal decision what strength alone had failed to secure, a lesson proving that when the body has exhausted its remedies, it should retain counsel.

That same day the Regent signed a pardon restoring his father’s civil standing and releasing the family property from certain seizures. She also caused letters to be drawn granting Jacques the manor of Azay, subject to liege homage, annual dues, military obligations, river rights, mill customs, woodland restrictions, and enough other conditions to make a gift resemble a siege. Yet a manor remains a manor even when burdened, just as the council had ruled a walnut remained a walnut even when empty.

Jacques knelt to thank her.

“Rise,” she said. “You have been on your knees enough.”

The Chancellor turned toward the window. The bishop studied his ring. Master Cotereau, who never understood a jest until three days afterward, asked whether the allodial case of the property had been examined.

Jacques’s father received the news with tears. He thanked God first, the Regent second, and his son third, in proper theological order; but when he heard by what stratagem Jacques had entered the house at Tours, he laughed until his old ribs pained him.

“And how did you obtain Azay?” he asked.

“By winning a wager.”

“What wager?”

“One involving agricultural produce.”

“Was it honest?”

“It was judged so by the council.”

The father crossed himself, for he knew the honesty of councils.

From that time Jacques rose swiftly. Madame de Beaujeu placed him first among clerks dealing with extraordinary receipts, where he discovered that “extraordinary” meant money raised by methods not yet customary. He reformed collections, revised leases, compelled dead pensioners to cease drawing salaries, and required living ones to prove the distinction. He learned how royal money moved: reluctantly from the people, mysteriously through the Treasury, and magnificently out of it.

He was soon appointed to offices whose names grew longer as their duties became less intelligible. He became treasurer here, receiver there, superintendent elsewhere; he handled salt, taille, aides, domains, loans, ransoms, forfeitures, and gifts which were compulsory though called free. He could make ten thousand livres appear from a province without visibly removing more than nine, and the missing thousand always returned wearing the livery of necessity.

Do not suppose he merely stole, as vulgar chroniclers have alleged. Theft is the unskilled taking of money contrary to law. Finance is the learned movement of money through law until ownership becomes a matter of opinion. Jacques kept accounts, receipts, warrants, counter-warrants, duplicates, extracts, and memoranda. If a crown passed his fingers, three clerks recorded it, two councillors approved it, a secretary copied it, and a monk prayed for it. Such a crown cannot be said to vanish; it becomes historical.

His wit served him no less than his figures. When a prince complained that a new levy would strip the country bare, Jacques replied, “Monseigneur, a country is like a sheep: if wisely shorn, it grows another fleece.”

“And if skinned?” asked the prince.

“Then it belongs to the next reign.”

When a bishop protested that church lands were sacred, Jacques said, “So are church bells, my lord, yet they are rung whenever the king has a victory.”

When a nobleman refused payment on the ground that his ancestors had been exempt since Charlemagne, Jacques requested the original charter. The nobleman produced a copy dated two hundred years later.

“Your privilege,” said Jacques, “is like an old woman’s age: ancient in conversation, young upon examination.”

Thus he gained enemies, which is the surest proof of advancement. Yet he also gained wealth, lands, offices, houses, horses, plate, tapestries, and friends who loved him according to the number of courses at his table.

At Azay he determined to build a château worthy of his ascent. The old manor was a heavy feudal structure, good for resisting archers and imprisoning daughters but poorly suited to music, painted ceilings, Italian guests, or that pleasant circulation of air required after large dinners. Jacques summoned masons, sculptors, engineers, glaziers, gardeners, and architects who had travelled beyond the Alps and therefore despised every wall not pierced by symmetrical windows.

He planned a residence rising from the waters of the Indre, with pale stone façades, high roofs, carved dormers, a noble stair, galleries for walking in rain, chambers for love, closets for accounts, and kitchens vast enough to roast an ox while boiling its relatives. The river should embrace it; the trees should frame it; every tower should proclaim that the penniless young man once drenched beneath a window had become a lord whose gutters were ornamented with salamanders.

Some said he chose Azay because of the Regent’s promise. Others said the château’s many walnut carvings commemorated the council’s judgment. There are no such carvings now, but absence proves little. Zealous restorers remove truth faster than weather.

Jacques became Baron of Samblançay, and his name carried such authority that merchants bowed to his signature before examining the sum. Kings changed; ministers rose and fell; wars in Italy consumed men and money with equal appetite. Jacques survived because every monarch needs someone who can find funds after all honest sources have been exhausted.

Under King Francis I his splendour reached its summit. Francis was young, broad-shouldered, magnificent, fond of hunting, architecture, women, war, poetry, and expenditure—the six arts in which a king may most rapidly immortalise himself at the expense of his subjects. He liked Jacques, who could discuss a campaign, judge a building plan, praise a mistress without overpraising her, and produce money with the sorrowful air of a saint producing a relic.

One autumn evening, while the court stayed near Amboise, the king rode to inspect the works at Azay. The walls glowed in the setting sun; scaffolds clung to the stone; masons shouted from tower to tower; barges brought timber along the Indre. Francis gazed upon the rising château.

“Samblançay,” he said, “you build like a prince.”

“Sire, I spend like a king.”

“Take care. That is treason.”

“Then every creditor in France is a conspirator.”

The king laughed and dined with him.

After supper, when the wine had loosened ceremony, Francis asked how Jacques had first won the favour which made all this possible. Several courtiers leaned closer, for a great fortune’s beginning interests men more than its honest continuance.

Jacques looked into the fire.

“Sire,” he said, “my first office came through a fall from a walnut tree.”

“Did you break your neck?”

“No, Your Majesty. I nearly broke something more useful.”

He then related the adventure: the street in Tours, the descending vessel, the counterfeit death, the Regent’s chamber, the journey to Amboise, the wager, the eleven triumphs, the rebellious twelfth, and the judgment of the council. He spoke with delicacy where necessary and with arithmetic where delicacy failed.

Francis laughed until tears stood in his eyes.

“And my sister’s council judged for you?”

“Unanimously, Sire.”

“Cotereau also?”

“He distinguished.”

“Of course he did! He would distinguish between Adam and Eve if their marriage contract lacked a seal.”

The king called for walnuts. A dish was brought. Francis counted twelve, cracked eleven, and placed the last before Jacques.

“This one,” he said, “I grant you unopened. You have already litigated enough.”

The story passed through the court by morning, through Tours by noon, and through all Touraine before the end of the week. At every telling the Regent grew more beautiful, Jacques more tireless, the council more solemn, and the twelfth walnut emptier. By the next generation some asserted there had been twenty-four; but this is impossible, since even patriotic anatomy has limits.

Yet Fortune, having raised Jacques by a wet fall, prepared him a dry one. The great are never so close to the scaffold as when they believe themselves seated firmly at table. Wars demanded money; the Treasury lacked it; rivals envied him; courtiers borrowed and forgot; princes spent and remembered only the treasurer. A deficit appeared, vast enough to require a victim of suitable stature.

Jacques had advanced large sums to the Crown. He had obeyed orders not written, concealed necessities not acknowledged, and shifted funds according to commands later denied. A queen’s favour turned against him; an enemy supplied papers; another suppressed them; old transactions were reopened and made to confess under torture.

The king, who had laughed at the walnuts, listened now to accusations.

Such is royal friendship: warm at supper, cool at council, and frozen before a judge.

Jacques de Beaune, Baron of Samblançay, was arrested, tried, and condemned. He had once pleaded that an empty shell still counted as a nut; his enemies now pleaded that an empty Treasury still required a head. The law, which had served his youth, served his ruin with equal gravity.

On the day of execution he was old, white-haired, and erect. He passed through Paris toward the gallows with more composure than many men go to weddings. A former acquaintance, seeing him, lamented that so great a person should die shamefully.

Jacques replied, “My friend, I have known shame dressed in velvet and honour wearing rags. This ladder is only another stair at court.”

Some say he added that Fortune had first lifted him from the pavement and now meant to hang him above it. Others put into his mouth a Latin sentence. Chroniclers are generous with last words because dead men cannot revise them.

The Château of Azay was not completed according to all his designs. Another hand finished portions, altered others, erased emblems, added roofs, removed walls, planted trees, and called each change an improvement. Yet the pale château endured beside the Indre, reflected in waters smoother than courtly promises. Visitors admired its windows and turrets without knowing that its foundations had been paid for, in the first instance, by a maid’s carelessness, a young man’s counterfeit death, a Regent’s appetite, eleven sound walnuts, and one empty shell.

This, then, is the true origin of the greatness of Jacques de Beaune, afterwards Baron of Samblançay, financier of kings, builder of Azay, and ornament of Touraine—true, I say, in the manner in which all excellent histories are true: the dates may quarrel, the clerks may protest, the moralists may cover their ears, and the lawyers may demand instruments under seal; but the château stands, the council judged, and every old woman between Tours and Amboise knows that no man ever rose so high by falling so artfully.

Therefore let the proud remember the pavement, the poor remember the open window, the ambitious carry clean shirts, and all lovers who promise dozens take care to define in advance whether the bargain concerns the kernel or the shell. For strength may win eleven victories, wit may secure the twelfth, and Fortune—who keeps the only perfect set of accounts—will in the end charge interest upon both.

Thus has this memorable occurrence, corrupted by prudish copyists, envious financiers, Burgundian commentators, and persons incapable of counting beyond ten, been restored to its veritable, historical, juridical, patriotic, and admirable beauty.

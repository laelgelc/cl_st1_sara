### The Broken Circle

There are men whose happiness is so complete that they cannot resist tempting Providence by declaring it aloud. Joshua Considine was one of them.

He was young, prosperous, and sufficiently successful at the Bar to regard the briefs which had not yet come to him as merely delayed. He possessed a pleasant house upon the edge of the Surrey moors, a sound constitution, a cellar selected by a friend who understood such things, and a wife whom he loved with that mingling of devotion and comradeship which is rarer in marriage than either husband or wife commonly supposes. He had, moreover, an agreeable disposition, and was entirely without that melancholy instinct which warns some men against admitting that the sun is shining lest a cloud should overhear them.

His friend, Dr. Gerald Burleigh, had known him at Oxford, where they had shared rooms, creditors, and opinions, though seldom at the same time. Gerald was now a physician with a growing practice and the grave exterior which patients expect in one who knows how little is the matter with them. He had come down on a Saturday in July to spend two days with the Considines, and had found the household in so harmonious a condition that he professed himself depressed.

“It is unnatural,” he said at dinner. “No married people can remain so contented without becoming a public danger.”

Mary Considine laughed. She was a slender, fair-haired woman, with very gentle eyes and a mouth made for confidence. During the eighteen months of her marriage she had not yet learned that every fear is not lightened by being spoken, because Joshua had hitherto laughed away all her small anxieties and fulfilled all her larger hopes.

“You are envious, Gerald,” she said.

“Madam, I am a bachelor. Envy is the tribute which my order pays to matrimony from a safe distance.”

Joshua raised his glass.

“To the safe distance!”

“Amen!” said Gerald.

After dinner Mary played to them for a little while, and afterwards sent them out with their cigars, declaring that the summer night was too beautiful to be wasted under a ceiling. The two men strolled across the lawn and through the wicket beyond it, where a narrow path ran over the heath. The west was still luminous, though the sun had gone; long purple shadows lay between the furze bushes, and the warm air was full of the faint sweetness of heather. Joshua walked with his hat pushed back, whistling beneath his breath.

“You are offensively happy,” said Gerald.

“I am. I have always maintained that happiness is largely a matter of good management.”

“You married Mary.”

“Exactly. My best example of sound judgement.”

“Yet I remember,” said Gerald, “that before you married her you permitted yourself to be admired by several other young women.”

“My dear fellow, no man permits that. He submits to it.”

This is the universal kind of submission which a man endures with particular fortitude when the woman is pretty.

They had gone perhaps a mile when Gerald pointed with his cigar to a thin blue thread rising beyond a low ridge.

“There are your neighbours.”

“What neighbours?”

“The gipsies. I passed their camp this afternoon. Half a dozen vans, several dogs, innumerable children, and one patriarch who offered to sell me a horse whose age he described as ‘between seven and nine.’ The animal was old enough to remember both dates.”

Joshua laughed.

“I heard they had pitched by Blackthorn Hollow. Mary’s maid has been talking of having her fortune told.”

“And have you forbidden it?”

“Certainly not. I am a barrister, not an ecclesiastical court.”

“Then let us go and have ours told.”

Gerald spoke idly, but Joshua turned at once towards the ridge.

“An excellent experiment! We will test the occult faculty under proper conditions. You shall be the scientific observer, I the innocent subject.”

“You are never innocent where an argument is concerned.”

“All the better. We shall see whether the priestess discovers my past, reveals my present, and makes some parsimonious investment in my future.”

They crossed the heath as the light faded. The encampment lay in a hollow sheltered by thorn trees. Three painted vans stood in a rough crescent, their little windows glowing red; a fire burned in the centre, and over it hung a black pot from which proceeded a smell too complicated for analysis. Children ceased their play to stare at the strangers. Two lean dogs advanced with professional suspicion, but were recalled by an old man seated upon an upturned basket.

“We desire,” said Joshua, “to consult the oracle.”

The old man looked at him without amusement.

“Then ask for the Queen.”

Gerald murmured, “A constitutional settlement, evidently.”

From the darkest van there descended a tall woman. She was old, but not infirm; the years had sharpened rather than bent her. A crimson kerchief was drawn over her iron-grey hair, and about her neck hung several chains of tarnished silver. Her eyes were black and singularly steady. It was not beauty which made the camp fall silent when she appeared, but authority. She came towards the gentlemen as though they, and not she, had been summoned.

“Who seeks knowledge?” she asked.

“This gentleman,” said Gerald, indicating Joshua. “I seek only entertainment, being a medical man and consequently past hope.”

The Queen did not look at him. Her gaze rested on Joshua.

“Give me your hand.”

“Which one?” he asked. “I should dislike to invalidate the experiment through a technical irregularity.”

“The right.”

Joshua extended it, smiling. She took it between her dry, brown fingers and turned the palm towards the fire. For some moments she said nothing. Her face, severe from the first, gradually became rigid.

“You have a wife.”

Joshua glanced triumphantly at Gerald.

“Remarkable! I wear no wedding ring.”

“You love her.”

“Still more remarkable, though perhaps less unusual.”

“She loves you. She trusts you. Her heart has no locked chamber against you.”

His smile softened.

“That, Queen, is true.”

The woman released his hand abruptly.

“Then leave her.”

Joshua stared, and Gerald’s brows lifted.

“Leave my wife?”

“Go from her. Cross the sea. Let her never hear your step nor look upon your face again.”

“My good woman, your remedy seems extreme. For what disease?”

“For death.”

The whispering among the people about the fire ceased. A stick fell inward with a shower of sparks.

Joshua’s amusement returned, though not quite so freely.

“Whose death?”

“Be warned.”

“I am a lawyer. Warnings, like indictments, should contain particulars.”

The Queen caught his wrist and again opened his hand. Her voice rose, harsh and sonorous.

“Blood is written here.”

“We all possess a certain quantity.”

“Her blood!”

Gerald moved nearer.

“That will do,” he said quietly.

But Joshua, piqued by the interruption and conscious of the watching eyes, would not withdraw.

“No, no. We are arriving at the particulars. What am I to do with my wife’s blood?”

“You will shed it. She will lie at your feet, and your hands will be red. The blood will flow through the broken circle of a severed ring.”

There was a moment in which even Joshua made no answer. The fire cracked; somewhere behind the vans a horse stamped heavily.

Then he laughed.

“There is an error in grammar as well as manners. You speak as though I were a murderer.”

“You are.”

“I beg your pardon. At most you allege that I shall become one. The distinction between the present and future tenses has saved more than one man from an unjust verdict.”

“Mock if you will,” said the Queen. “On your own head be the doom.”

“Not upon my wife’s?” asked Joshua.

She let his hand fall as if it had become unclean.

Gerald placed a silver coin upon her palm.

“That is for the entertainment.”

She flung it into the dust.

“I take no silver for blood.”

Joshua stooped, picked up the coin, and offered her gold instead.

“For professional consistency?”

The Queen drew back, her eyes burning.

“Go!”

They went.

For the first quarter of a mile neither spoke. The moor had altered with the night. Its wide peace had become emptiness; the thorn bushes seemed to crouch, and the path appeared only where the pale sky gave it shape. Presently Joshua began to whistle again.

“Well?” he asked.

“Well what?”

“As a man of science, how do you explain her?”

“As a man of science, I explain nothing after dinner unless paid a fee.”

“She was impressive.”

“She was infernally mischievous.”

“You cannot deny that she knew I was married.”

“She may have seen you with Mary. She may have heard of you in the village. Your bootmaker may be in correspondence with her. There are fifty explanations.”

“And the fifty-first?”

“Coincidence.”

Joshua laughed.

“Yet she would not take the money.”

“A fine stroke of business. Refusing one sovereign in public may bring her twenty from frightened fools in private.”

“My dear Gerald, you are almost as cynical as a solicitor.”

“I am serious. Say nothing of this to Mary.”

Joshua stopped.

“Why not?”

“Because Mary is sensitive, and because an image, once lodged in a sensitive mind, may do harm though the words which conveyed it were nonsense.”

“You do not suppose she would believe it?”

“I suppose that nobody knows beforehand what he will believe when his happiness is threatened.”

“That is a very medical answer.”

“It is advice.”

Joshua put his arm through Gerald’s.

“Mary and I have no secrets.”

“There are secrets which are only undigested facts. Keep this one until it is harmless.”

“Then I must keep it for ever, since it was born harmless.”

Gerald sighed.

When they reached the house, Mary was waiting at an open window. The lamplight behind her made a golden mist of her hair.

“At last!” she cried. “Where have you been?”

“To seek my destiny,” said Joshua.

Gerald gave him a warning glance.

Mary came into the hall.

“You have seen the gipsies?”

“Their Queen has read my hand.”

“And told you that you would be Lord Chancellor?”

“Nothing so improbable.”

“What did she tell you?”

Joshua hesitated for one fraction of a second. That hesitation, which Mary noticed, did more mischief than any immediate jest could have done.

“Come,” she said. “You must tell me.”

“It was the usual catalogue—travel, peril, a dark woman—”

“A dark woman?” Mary smiled, but faintly.

“Of the imperial kind. In fact, she ordered me to desert you.”

Mary’s colour changed.

“To desert me?”

“My dear child, the woman was magnificent. She said I must flee across the sea and never see your face again.”

“Why?”

Gerald interposed.

“Because prophecy, like fashionable millinery, requires a startling shape.”

But Joshua, delighted with the phrase he had remembered, raised his hand theatrically.

“Because blood is written here! Her blood! She shall lie at my feet, while my hands—”

Mary uttered a low cry.

Joshua broke off.

“What is it?”

“The rest,” she whispered. “What was the rest?”

He should have lied then; Gerald knew it, and Joshua knew it afterwards. But a man who has begun to laugh at a thing is often loth to grant it even the dignity of concealment.

“The blood was to flow through the broken circle of a severed ring. There, you see! Quite a poetess. I pointed out that she had confused the tenses by calling me a murderer in advance.”

Mary looked at his open hand. Her eyes widened with a strange, fixed terror; then the light seemed to go out of them. She swayed.

“Mary!”

Joshua caught her before she struck the floor. Gerald was beside them instantly.

“Carry her to the sofa. Not like that—support her head. Ring for water.”

The comedy vanished from Joshua’s face. He obeyed with clumsy haste, kneeling beside his wife while Gerald loosened the lace at her throat.

“What have I done?”

“What I advised you not to do.”

“Is she ill?”

“She has fainted.”

“I can see that!”

“Then be useful and remain quiet.”

Mary recovered quickly, but her first conscious movement was to seize Joshua’s hands and examine them. Finding no stain there, she began to tremble.

“It was only a jest,” he said. “A camp-fire melodrama. Mary, look at me. You cannot suppose—”

“No. Oh, no!”

“You do not believe I could hurt you?”

“Never.”

Yet she clung to him as though there were danger in the room, and Gerald, watching her over Joshua’s shoulder, saw in her face not the passing confusion of a faint but a strange, settled look which he did not like.

For the remainder of his visit the matter was not mentioned. Joshua became extravagantly cheerful, Mary unusually gentle, and Gerald found both conditions disquieting. On Sunday afternoon he spoke to her alone.

“You are not troubling yourself about that old woman’s rubbish?”

Mary attempted a smile.

“Of course not.”

“Prophecies are generally safe because they are vague. If I say that you will suffer pain, meet a stranger and receive news, I may confidently await my reputation.”

“She was not vague.”

“No; she was theatrical. That is the other method.”

“Gerald, did she truly say that Joshua ought to leave me?”

“She did.”

“And you thought it best that he should not tell me.”

“I thought the words might frighten you.”

“Then you thought there was something to fear.”

“I thought there was fear to fear—which is a very different matter.”

Mary turned towards the garden. Joshua was cutting dead blooms from a standard rose, and whistling as he worked.

“I trust him more than I trust myself,” she said.

Gerald answered gently, “Then trust him enough to forget.”

But fear is not dismissed by an argument; and Mary, being ashamed of hers, gave it secrecy in which to grow.

Three days after Gerald’s departure she ordered the dog-cart and drove into the village. There she dismissed it, saying that she wished to visit a poor woman, and walked by the back road towards Blackthorn Hollow. The camp was still there. In the hard morning sunlight it looked meaner than it had beneath the moon: torn canvas, muddy wheels, cooking tins, thin poultry scratching in the dust. Yet the Queen, when she came from her van, had lost none of her authority.

“You are his wife,” she said.

Mary’s courage almost failed.

“You know me?”

“I know the ring.”

Mary covered her left hand with her right.

“You told my husband that he would kill me.”

“I told him to go.”

“He will not go. Nor would I let him.”

“Then why come?”

“To ask whether you lied.”

The Queen regarded her for a long time.

“Give me your hand.”

Mary obeyed. The old woman looked first at the palm and then at the wedding ring—a narrow band of gold which had belonged to Joshua’s mother.

“Can it be prevented?” Mary whispered.

“What is written is written.”

“Will he—will Joshua mean to do it?”

“I see the deed, not the heart. You lie before him. His hands are stained. Your blood runs beneath the broken circle.”

Mary pulled her hand away.

“No!”

“Leave him.”

“I cannot.”

“Send him from you.”

“I will not.”

“Then be warned.”

Mary placed money on the step of the van, but the Queen neither accepted nor refused it. She had already turned away.

The drive home passed like a dream. Mary remembered afterwards the white dust, a child waving from a cottage gate, and the monotonous rise and fall of the pony’s quarters. She also remembered that when she entered the hall Joshua came from his study and kissed her. She had started so violently that he asked what ailed her.

“Nothing,” she said. “I am tired.”

That evening, as the carving-knife lay beside his hand at dinner, she could not keep her eyes from it.

During the following week a curious affliction overtook the cutlery of the Considine household. The cook complained that no knife would divide a joint; the parlourmaid declared that the bread-knife bent upon a crust; Joshua, attacking a peach, found himself in possession of an implement with an edge like a paper-knife. He sent half a dozen blades to the ironmonger, and they returned sharp. Within two days they were blunt again.

At last he entered the morning-room unexpectedly and found Mary kneeling before a low cupboard. Around her lay the kitchen knives. She held one in her lap and was laboriously drawing its edge across a small whetstone—not to sharpen it, but to turn the edge.

For several seconds Joshua stood without speaking.

Mary looked up. The stone fell from her hand.

“What are you doing?”

She rose too quickly and caught at the chair.

“Nothing.”

“Nothing has ruined every knife in the house. Why?”

Her silence answered him.

“Is this because of that woman?”

“Joshua—”

“You believe her?”

“No!”

“You believe that I shall murder you, and therefore you are making the carving-knives safe?”

“I do not believe it. I only thought—if there were no sharp things—”

He turned away. Anger would have been easier for her to bear; but he was hurt, and made no attempt to conceal it.

“We have been married eighteen months,” he said, “and this is what your trust amounts to.”

She came to him, holding out her hands.

“It is not you I fear.”

“Then what?”

“Something that may happen through us—something we cannot help.”

“You think my will counts for nothing?”

“I think fate—”

“Fate!” He laughed, but there was no gaiety in it. “An old vagabond uses three ugly phrases, and you set them above everything you know of me.”

“I went to her,” Mary confessed.

He faced her.

“You went to the camp?”

“I had to know.”

“And she repeated it?”

Mary’s eyes filled.

“She said I should lie at your feet and that your hands would be stained with my blood.”

Joshua was silent. The accuracy of the repetition disturbed him for a moment, not because he credited it, but because he saw how completely it had mastered her imagination.

Then pride rose in him—the pride of a rational man challenged within his own household by nonsense.

“This ends now.”

“How?”

“By treating the whole business with the contempt it deserves. You will not blunt another knife.”

She caught his sleeve.

“Joshua, promise me that you will not use one.”

“I shall promise nothing so absurd.”

“For a little while—only for a little while.”

“Until when? Until the stars alter their arrangements? No, Mary. We shall live as reasonable creatures, not as frightened savages.”

He kissed her forehead, but she felt that the kiss was a dismissal of the subject, not a reconciliation.

The next afternoon Gerald arrived unexpectedly, having been called to a patient in the neighbourhood. He found Joshua alone in the library, smoking with unusual violence.

“Thank Heaven!” Joshua exclaimed. “A sane man.”

Gerald listened to the account of Mary’s visit and the blunted knives without interruption.

“You see what your silence would have prevented?” Joshua concluded.

“No. I see what your recital began.”

“You cannot blame me for her going to the camp.”

“I can blame neither of you profitably. What do you intend?”

“To cure her.”

Gerald looked suspicious.

“By what means?”

“Common sense.”

“That is not a medicine. It is a diet, and must be administered slowly.”

Joshua led him into the garden. Mary sat beneath the cedar with some sewing, but though she greeted Gerald warmly, he perceived at once that she was pale and watchful. The day was of extreme beauty. Every border was rich with colour, bees wandered heavily among the lavender, and the lawn lay smooth and bright in the afternoon sun.

Joshua paused before a great rose bush.

“Mary wants these for the drawing-room.”

“Then call the gardener,” she said quickly.

“He has gone to market.”

“There are shears in the potting-shed.”

“Blunt,” said Joshua. “By domestic decree.”

Mary’s sewing dropped into her lap.

Joshua walked towards the house. Gerald followed.

“What are you doing?”

“Cutting roses.”

“With what?”

“I have an old Ghurka knife in the hall cupboard. It was given to my father in India. It is sharp enough to satisfy even Providence.”

Gerald seized his arm.

“Don’t be a fool.”

“I am going to cut flowers, not throats.”

“You are going to wave the precise object of her terror before a disordered imagination.”

“Then her imagination must be put in order.”

“Not by shock.”

But Joshua had already entered the house. He returned carrying a curved knife in a leather sheath. The blade, when he drew it, flashed almost white in the sun. It was a formidable thing, heavy at the back and narrowing to an edge which had been maintained with military care.

Mary had risen.

“What have you there?”

“A perfectly innocent implement,” said Joshua lightly.

Her face became colourless.

“Put it away.”

“My dear Mary—”

“Put it away!”

He moved towards the rose bush.

“Observe. I take the murderous weapon. I approach the intended victim—a Maréchal Niel of irreproachable character—and thus—”

The blade passed through the stem with scarcely a sound.

Mary gave a strangled cry. Joshua turned. She was staring, not at his face, but at the knife in his hand.

“Mary, this is absurd.”

He stepped towards her.

She retreated. Her heel caught upon the edge of the rug beneath the cedar chair. At that instant her strength left her.

Gerald cried out.

Joshua flung down the knife and sprang forward to catch her. He was a fraction too late. Mary fell to her knees, then sideways; her left hand struck the grass where the blade lay hidden among the fallen roses.

There was a small metallic snap.

For one dreadful second nobody moved.

Then Mary screamed.

Joshua lifted her. Blood ran across her palm and down his wrist. The severed halves of her wedding ring fell upon the white petals at his feet.

Gerald reached them and caught her wounded hand.

“Hold still!”

Joshua stared at the blood upon his fingers. Mary lay against him, half fainting, her dress spread upon the ground, her face below his. The roses were scattered around them; the broken ring shone redly among them.

The prophecy stood complete before their eyes.

“She is killed,” Joshua said in a voice Gerald had never heard from him.

“Nonsense. She has cut her hand.”

“But the blood—the ring—”

“Press here. Hard! If you mean to become hysterical, wait until I have bandaged her.”

Professional authority restored movement. Joshua obeyed. Gerald examined the wound and found it long but not deep. The knife had sliced through the fleshy part of the palm and passed beneath the ring, cutting the gold cleanly and releasing the finger before worse injury could be done.

Mary opened her eyes.

“Joshua?”

“I am here.”

“You threw it down.”

“Yes.”

“To save me?”

“I tried to catch you.”

She looked at his hands, crimson with her blood. Then she looked at the roses, at the knife, and lastly at the two fragments of gold which Gerald had placed upon his handkerchief.

A singular change came over her face. The terror vanished, not gradually, but as darkness leaves a room when the shutters are opened.

“It has happened,” she whispered.

Joshua bent closer.

“What?”

“All of it. I lay at your feet. Your hands were stained. My blood flowed through the broken circle.”

Gerald tied the bandage firmly.

“You have lost very little blood, though quite enough for dramatic purposes.”

Mary began to laugh. It was weak laughter, and tears followed it, but there was sanity in both.

“Do you not see? That was what she saw. Nothing more. She called it murder, but it was only this.”

Joshua’s face remained stricken.

“My folly did it.”

“No,” she said. “My fear did it. If I had trusted you, I should not have fallen.”

“If I had not been a brute, you would not have been frightened.”

“Amen,” said Gerald. “I am glad that point is conceded.”

Neither attended to him. Joshua lifted Mary very carefully and carried her into the house. She rested her uninjured arm about his neck, and for the first time in many days there was no fear in the movement.

Later, when the cut had been properly dressed and Mary lay upon the sofa, Gerald examined the broken ring. The keen blade had divided it almost as neatly as a jeweller’s tool.

“It can be mended,” Joshua said.

“Of course.”

Mary shook her head.

“No. I shall keep it as it is.”

“My dear, you cannot wear a severed ring.”

“I will have the pieces joined upon a little chain. It shall remind me.”

“Of what?”

“That a prophecy may tell the truth and yet be a lie.”

Gerald, who was drinking a much-needed glass of brandy, considered this.

“A very respectable definition of most fortune-telling.”

Joshua knelt beside the sofa and took her bandaged hand.

“Can you forgive me?”

“There is nothing to forgive.”

“There is a great deal. I made a jest of your fear when I ought to have understood it.”

“And I doubted you.”

“You feared an accident.”

“I feared your hand.”

He winced.

Mary drew him nearer.

“But I do not fear it now.”

He kissed the bandage, precisely where the white linen concealed the cut. Gerald turned towards the window with the discretion of a bachelor who likes to have his delicacy observed.

Outside, evening gathered over the garden. The roses Joshua had cut lay forgotten beneath the cedar, their pale petals marked here and there with red. Beside them the Ghurka knife still glittered in the grass, harmless now that nobody feared it. Beyond the lawn the moor stretched quietly towards Blackthorn Hollow, where a little smoke rose in the distance and dissolved into the tranquil sky.

The gipsy camp was gone by morning.

Whether its Queen had foreseen a murder, an accident, or only the power of her own words, none of the three could ever determine. Gerald maintained that chance, assisted by nervous suggestion and masculine obstinacy, had provided the whole performance. Joshua accepted this judgement with unusual meekness. Mary did not dispute it; she needed no supernatural explanation, for she had obtained something better than one.

The ring was repaired, though not invisibly. At Mary’s wish the jeweller left a fine seam across the gold, and she wore it so until the end of her life. Sometimes, when the light caught that little scar, Joshua would take her hand and kiss it. On such occasions neither spoke of the blood, the knife, or the woman at the fire.

Yet the broken circle, being closed again, remained between them not as a token of doom, but as a reminder that love may survive both prophecy and fear—and that Fate, when invited into a happy house, may be satisfied with a very small fulfilment.

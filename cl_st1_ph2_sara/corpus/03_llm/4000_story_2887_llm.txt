### The Gray Masquerade

In the autumn of 1799, when the commerce of the young American republic had already carried its striped ensign into seas where older monarchies maintained only a jealous or decaying vigilance, Captain Amasa Delano, of Duxbury in Massachusetts, lay with his sealing vessel at anchor in a small harbor off the coast of Chile. The island which formed that harbor was uninhabited, though not wholly barren; for coarse grass clothed its upper slopes, sea-birds nested in the cliffs, and among the ravines grew certain twisted shrubs whose roots, rather than their branches, seemed to contend with the perpetual wind.

On the morning of the occurrence here related, there was no wind.

The sky had the color of wool long exposed to dust; the sea, unwrinkled and metallic, resembled a plate of cooled lead. Vapors hovered between water and heaven, obscuring the distinction of both, so that the American ship, though securely anchored, appeared to float in a medium less substantial than water and less transparent than air. Even the cries of the gulls were subdued. Nothing moved with freedom. The world seemed to have paused, not in peace, but in expectation.

Shortly after sunrise a sailor stationed forward reported a strange sail entering the outer bay.

Delano, who had risen early and was pacing the quarter-deck, took his glass and directed it toward the object. At first he could distinguish little beyond a high, dark hull, advancing without visible means of propulsion; then a rent in the fog disclosed three masts, the upper spars broken or bare, and sails hanging in strips as neglected banners hang over the gate of some plague-deserted fortress. The vessel yawed heavily, recovering herself with such slowness that she seemed less navigated than drawn onward by an invisible cable.

No colors flew from her peak.

“She has suffered,” said Delano.

The mate, a taciturn Nantucket man, observed that she might also have deceivers aboard.

But the captain, whose disposition combined readiness in action with an almost constitutional reluctance to suspect evil where misery offered a simpler explanation, lowered his glass and smiled. A distressed ship in those seas required assistance; and to debate whether suffering men deserved relief, merely because wicked men sometimes imitated suffering, appeared to him a species of prudence scarcely distinguishable from cowardice.

He ordered a whale-boat lowered, filled it with provisions, gourds of water, and several hands, and himself took the steering oar.

As they approached the stranger, her aspect became at once more intelligible and more perplexing. She was a Spanish merchant vessel of considerable burden, built in an antiquated fashion, with lofty bulwarks, a broad stern, and carved galleries whose mouldings, once gilt, were now green with weather and crusted with salt. Along her sides, streaks of rust descended from the chain-plates like old blood from iron wounds. Her figurehead had lost both arms and half its face. Sea-moss clung beneath the cat-heads; ropes trailed overside; the standing rigging sagged; and the few sails still set were patched in so many colors that they might have been stitched from the garments of a defeated army.

The name at her stern could be made out only with difficulty:

**SAN DOMINICK.**

Above it, under the quarter-gallery, a broad strip of canvas had been nailed over some carved device. Upon that canvas, in faded black letters, appeared the words:

**FOLLOW YOUR LEADER.**

Delano noticed the inscription, but, taking it for some devotional or dynastic motto, gave it no prolonged consideration.

The deck was crowded. Spaniards in torn shirts and soiled jackets leaned over the bulwarks, crying hoarsely for water. Among them moved a much larger number of Africans—men, women, and children—some nearly naked, others wrapped in remnants of sailcloth or wool. Their movements were disorderly; yet disorder aboard a famished ship is but order deprived of strength. The calls for water increased. Arms were extended. Children shrieked. Several men beat their hands against the rail with such violence that Delano feared they would throw themselves into the sea.

“Stand off,” his mate muttered. “Let them lower their own boat.”

“They may have none fit to lower,” replied Delano. “Give way.”

When the whale-boat came under the lee quarter, no proper side-ladder was sent down. A few knotted ropes dangled there instead, and by these the Americans climbed. Delano was scarcely over the bulwark before the crowd enclosed him. Hands seized the water vessels; a Spanish sailor and an African disputed a loaf until it tore between them; women pressed forward with bowls, caps, fragments of bottles, even cupped palms. The captain’s men, alarmed by the tumult, reached instinctively for their knives, but Delano called for order in a voice accustomed to carrying through weather.

His firmness, aided by the visible abundance remaining in the boat, achieved what threats might not have done. The water was distributed first to the sick and children. As they drank, the confusion diminished, though not the low, continuous murmur pervading the ship—a murmur made up of coughs, prayers, complaints, whispers, and the tap of metal upon stone.

The last sound drew Delano’s eye toward the main hatch.

There, seated cross-legged in a row, were six Africans of powerful build. Each held a small hatchet, which he polished upon a piece of rough stone. Their labor was conducted without conversation and apparently without haste. They lifted the blades, inspected the edges, breathed upon them, and resumed their measured rubbing. The steel flashed whenever the fog thinned overhead.

“Useful fellows,” Delano thought. “The ship has need of carpenters.”

Near them two younger men were engaged in winding rope, though neither rope nor men appeared attached to any general labor. Elsewhere, several Africans lounged on the windlass, and others passed freely between forecastle and quarter-deck. Such license was remarkable aboard a slaver, yet famine, fever, and diminished numbers might have dissolved the usual regulations. Necessity, that rough legislator, often repeals in an hour those ordinances which authority has maintained for years.

Presently a Black man of small stature but composed bearing approached Delano. His face was intelligent, his manner deferential, and his clothing, though poor, had been arranged with care.

“Master,” he said in Spanish, “the captain begs you to come aft. He is very sick.”

Delano followed.

Under the quarter-deck awning, which hung like a funeral canopy from broken stanchions, stood Don Benito Cereno, commander of the San Dominick. He was a slender man, perhaps not yet thirty, though suffering had placed upon him the pallor and lassitude of advanced age. His black coat, richly cut but stained by sea-water, hung loosely from his shoulders. One hand rested on a malacca cane; the other lay upon the shoulder of the servant who had summoned Delano. From time to time Don Benito’s lips trembled, and a nervous motion disturbed the muscles beneath one eye.

The servant announced the American captain.

Don Benito bowed. The courtesy was formal, yet the movement nearly overbalanced him. His attendant caught his arm.

“My master is weak,” the man explained. “But his heart thanks the generous captain.”

“You have suffered severely, sir,” said Delano.

Cereno looked toward the crowd forward. “Severely.”

“How long have you been in this condition?”

The Spaniard opened his mouth but did not immediately answer. The servant leaned close, as if solicitude alone had prompted the movement.

“One hundred and ninety days from Valparaiso,” Cereno said at last. “We were bound northward. Storms—calms—fever. Our water failed.”

At the word *failed*, his glance darted toward Delano and then away.

“And your crew?”

“Many dead.”

“The passengers?”

“Dead also. Many.”

His voice sank until the last words were scarcely audible.

The servant pressed the captain’s wrist between his fingers, apparently feeling the pulse. “Master must not exhaust himself.”

Delano regarded the pair with benevolent interest. Rarely, he reflected, did misfortune reveal attachment more affecting than that which united this infirm commander and his faithful domestic. The servant anticipated every need: he supported his master before the master knew himself to be falling; he interpreted unfinished sentences; he watched the slightest alteration in Cereno’s face. In households of greater comfort, where service becomes mechanical and command capricious, devotion of so pure a quality might have been sought in vain.

“What is your name?” Delano asked him.

“Babo, sir.”

“Your master is fortunate in you, Babo.”

The servant bowed, and a smile of modest gratitude appeared on his face. Don Benito shuddered.

The fog began to lift, though the calm persisted. Delano sent his boat back for more water, bread, pumpkins, and such medicines as his vessel possessed. He expected the San Dominick’s captain to display relief; but Cereno received the offer with an agitation strangely disproportionate to gratitude. He gripped his cane, glanced at Babo, and then expressed thanks in a hurried monotone, as though repeating words dictated from behind a curtain.

To account for this oddness was not difficult. Prolonged command under disaster may render a sensitive man suspicious of every promise; moreover, Spaniards, in Delano’s experience, preserved amid ruin a ceremonious pride which sometimes made assistance itself painful. He therefore changed the subject and asked for particulars of the voyage.

The account came slowly. The ship, said Cereno, had sailed from Buenos Aires with Spanish passengers, a crew of about fifty, and more than one hundred and fifty enslaved Africans belonging chiefly to Don Alexandro Aranda, a merchant of rank. Contrary winds delayed them; fever broke out; provisions spoiled; then storms carried away boats, spars, and men. Don Alexandro died with many others. Owing to sickness among the sailors, the Africans had necessarily been allowed unusual freedom, though, Cereno added, they had on the whole behaved peaceably.

At this Babo uttered a soft exclamation of grief and covered his face.

“What is it?” asked Delano.

“He remembers Don Alexandro,” said Cereno.

“Ah,” said the American. “A good master?”

Babo bowed lower. “He was our owner.”

Something in the answer—perhaps the absence of the word *good*, perhaps merely the uninflected tone—caused Delano to inspect him more closely. But Babo’s eyes were lowered, and his whole posture expressed submission.

Just then a disturbance arose near the mainmast. A young Spanish sailor had attempted to pass between two Africans; one of them struck him upon the head with a coil of rope. The sailor staggered and cried out, while those near him laughed. No officer intervened. Even the hatchet-polishers did not look up.

Delano turned sharply to Cereno. “Do you permit such insolence?”

The Spaniard’s pallor deepened.

Before he could answer, Babo said, “Master is too weak to enforce every rule. The men know this. Hunger makes children disobedient.”

“Children!” Delano repeated. The African who had delivered the blow was a giant with shoulders like a Hercules supporting some ruined entablature.

Don Benito murmured an order. Babo called to the offender, who approached without apparent fear. A rapid exchange followed in a language Delano did not understand. The man listened, smiled, and withdrew.

“What punishment did you impose?” Delano asked.

“He will be watched,” said Cereno.

The answer was inadequate, even absurd. Delano felt the first distinct pressure of suspicion.

He surveyed the deck anew. Several Spaniards stood in small groups, never far from Africans. Their faces were exhausted, but beneath the exhaustion there seemed another expression—one not easily named, because each time Delano attempted to define it, the face in question turned away. The Black women sat among the coils of rigging, murmuring together. Their children played beside the guns. The six men continued sharpening hatchets. Along the bulwarks others watched the American boat crossing the bay.

Could the crew have lost command of the slaves?

The thought presented itself plainly, almost grossly, like a shape revealed by lightning. Yet the next instant it seemed unreasonable. Had the Africans revolted, why permit an American captain to board? Why allow him to speak privately—or almost privately—with Don Benito? Why not seize the whale-boat and escape? Above all, why would Babo, whose affectionate vigilance never relaxed, remain so perfect an image of fidelity?

Suspicion is not always the child of discernment; sometimes it is only vanity offended by unfamiliar arrangements. Delano, ashamed of having entertained so uncharitable an idea toward persons whose distress he had come to relieve, dismissed it.

He walked forward to examine the damage. The forecastle looked as though wind, disease, and neglect had divided its possession among them. Planks were sprung; dead-lights broken; bedding lay mouldering in corners; cooking vessels, empty casks, fragments of chains, and devotional images were heaped together. A small wooden Madonna, blackened by smoke, had been tied upright against the mast. At her feet lay a dead bird.

An elderly Spanish sailor followed Delano into this confusion. The man’s cheeks were sunken, and one sleeve hung empty below the elbow.

“You need a carpenter,” Delano said.

The sailor glanced behind him. “We need—”

An African entered.

The Spaniard stooped at once and began sorting rope ends.

“What do you need?” Delano repeated.

“Everything, señor.”

The African smiled pleasantly and lifted one of the ropes as if offering to assist.

Again the American felt that obscure internal check which resembles memory more than fear: the conviction that a fact has been perceived but not acknowledged. He waited, hoping the Spaniard might say more. Nothing followed. From the deck came Babo’s voice, announcing that Don Benito desired his guest’s presence.

Cereno had seated himself beneath the awning. His servant stood behind him, combing the captain’s long, damp hair. The scene had the intimacy of a bedchamber and the publicity of a tribunal. When the comb encountered a knot, Don Benito flinched. Babo apologized in tones of tender reproach, as a nurse might address a restless invalid.

“You are troubled, Captain Delano,” said Cereno.

“Only by the condition of your ship.”

“Nothing else?”

The question was abrupt.

Delano laughed. “What else should trouble me?”

Don Benito looked at him fixedly. His lips moved. At that moment the comb slipped from Babo’s hand and fell. The servant bent to recover it. Cereno’s gaze followed him; whatever he had intended to say vanished.

Babo resumed his work.

There are moments when the understanding, like a compass near concealed iron, trembles toward truth without settling upon it. Delano’s mind now oscillated between uneasiness and confidence. A hundred details disturbed him; yet each possessed, when considered alone, an explanation sufficiently ordinary. The Africans were free because the crew was weak. The Spaniards were silent because they were sick. Cereno was evasive because suffering had impaired his faculties. Babo hovered near because devotion made him anxious. Even the hatchets, however unpleasantly bright their edges, were tools required by a damaged vessel.

Thus reason, which men praise as the enemy of delusion, may become delusion’s advocate, arranging evidence not according to its weight but according to the conclusion the heart has already chosen.

The American boat returned. During the distribution of supplies, Delano saw one of his sailors speaking with a Spanish boy near the main-chains. The boy appeared terrified. He thrust something toward the sailor, but before it could be received, a Black woman came between them, crying out that her child had been robbed. The accusation drew a crowd. The object fell and disappeared through a scupper.

“What was it?” Delano later asked his sailor.

“A bit of cloth, sir. Maybe paper sewn inside.”

“Why did you not pick it up?”

“Three of them stood over it.”

The man hesitated, then added, “I do not like this ship.”

Delano rebuked him for superstition, though less severely than he might have done an hour earlier.

At noon, the fog withdrew beyond the bay, leaving the San Dominick exposed beneath a pale sun. Illumination did not dispel her gloom. Rather, as light entering a charnel-house reveals what darkness mercifully confounds, the day disclosed new marks of ruin: dried stains on the deck, hacked railings, shot scars near the cabin, and places where the rigging had been cut, not broken.

On the forward part of the poop stood a carved pedestal covered by the inscribed canvas. Delano now perceived that the canvas concealed some bulky object. Its outline was irregular; beneath one corner protruded what looked like a yellowish branch or length of coral.

“What is under there?” he asked.

Don Benito’s cane struck the deck.

“A figure,” Babo answered. “The ship once carried a carving of Christopher Columbus.”

“Why cover it?”

“Master ordered it, after Don Alexandro died. A sign of mourning.”

Delano considered the words *FOLLOW YOUR LEADER*. He supposed the leader must be Columbus, though the association between the discoverer and mourning was not obvious. Perhaps the motto belonged to the original ornament; perhaps it referred to Christ; perhaps Spanish taste delighted in those obscure combinations of piety, heraldry, and admonition which northern merchants, content with a painted eagle or codfish, seldom attempted.

A bell sounded aft. It gave one cracked stroke, though no hand appeared upon the rope.

Cereno rose suddenly. “My cabin.”

Babo supported him. Delano accompanied them below.

The cabin was spacious but close, its stern windows sealed by shutters. Tarnished mirrors, cracked porcelain, charts spotted with mildew, and a silver crucifix testified to former elegance. A rack for swords stood empty. On the table lay a Spanish Bible, a shaving basin, and two loaded pistols.

Delano noticed the weapons.

“Yours?” he asked.

Cereno stared at them as if seeing them for the first time.

“Master keeps them for defense,” Babo said, and moved casually between Cereno and the table.

The captain suffered a coughing fit. Babo seated him and proposed that he be shaved, explaining that the service soothed his nerves and restored a sense of ordinary life. Delano, though surprised by the timing, consented to wait, believing that the familiar ritual might indeed steady the invalid.

Babo spread a white cloth around Cereno’s shoulders. Against that pallor the Spaniard’s face appeared waxen, while the servant’s hand, holding the bright razor, seemed detached in the dim cabin, an instrument floating before its appointed victim. Yet Babo’s touch was delicate. He lathered the cheeks, drew the blade in smooth strokes, wiped it, and smiled whenever Cereno started.

“Master is fearful today,” he said.

“The ship rolls,” Cereno whispered.

The sea was almost perfectly still.

A drop of blood appeared beneath the Spaniard’s jaw.

Babo recoiled with theatrical distress. “Ah! Babo has hurt his master.”

“It is nothing,” Delano said.

Cereno looked directly at him. The drop ran down his throat and disappeared beneath the cloth.

For one instant the cabin seemed to alter. Servant and master, black and white, blade and flesh, tenderness and terror—each relation remained outwardly the same, yet each might have reversed its meaning, as figures painted upon a turning screen exchange places without moving. Delano felt his hand drift toward one of the pistols.

Then Babo wiped away the blood, chiding himself so earnestly that suspicion appeared monstrous. The American removed his hand.

When the shaving ended, Cereno asked to speak with Delano alone.

Babo’s smile did not change. “Master may speak before Babo.”

“It concerns navigation,” Cereno said.

“Babo knows nothing of navigation.”

“Then leave us.”

The servant lowered his head but did not move.

A heavy step sounded outside. The door opened, and one of the hatchet-polishers looked in. He carried no hatchet now; his hands were folded upon his chest. Babo said something to him. The man withdrew.

Cereno had begun trembling so violently that the chair creaked.

“Another seizure,” Babo said. “The American captain must excuse him.”

Delano helped carry Cereno to a couch. While leaning over him, he felt the Spaniard’s fingers close upon his wrist. The grip, though feeble, was deliberate. Cereno’s mouth approached his ear.

“Stay—”

Babo bent over both men.

“Stay for food,” Cereno completed.

Delano straightened, uncertain whether he had heard one plea or two fragments accidentally united.

The meal was served on deck. It consisted chiefly of bread brought from the American vessel, a stew of pumpkins, and a little wine which Cereno had preserved. Spaniards and Africans ate in groups without regular separation. The six hatchet-men sat somewhat apart, their tools beside them. Babo remained behind his master’s chair.

During the meal, Delano proposed taking the San Dominick in tow as soon as a breeze arose.

The effect was curious. A murmur passed among the Africans. Several Spaniards dropped their eyes. Cereno raised his cup but failed to drink.

“To what port?” he asked.

“To the harbor yonder first. There we can repair your rigging and examine the hull.”

“No.”

The refusal was immediate.

“No?” Delano repeated.

Babo rested a hand upon the captain’s chair.

Cereno swallowed. “The anchorage is dangerous. We should proceed north.”

“With your foretopmast sprung and half your canvas useless?”

“We have survived worse.”

Delano’s patience began to fail. Benevolence may bear ingratitude more easily than absurdity. “Sir, your ship can scarcely answer her helm. Another night outside the bay may finish what fever began.”

Cereno’s expression became one of naked anguish. He looked toward the distant American vessel, then toward Babo, then at the hatchet-men.

“You are right,” he said faintly. “We will enter.”

The murmur ceased.

Afterward Delano walked alone as far as the weather gangway. Below him his boat waited, manned by six Americans. Beyond lay his own clean ship, her yards squared, her flag moving scarcely at all in the dying air. The sight produced in him a homesick affection for order: for commands plainly given and promptly obeyed, for ropes coiled where ropes belonged, for men whose duties could be named without ambiguity.

A Spanish sailor approached, carrying a coil. As he passed, he whispered in English, “Do not leave us.”

Delano turned.

The sailor had gone three paces farther. Beside him walked an African.

“What did you say?” Delano called.

The Spaniard looked back blankly. “Nothing, señor.”

“You spoke English.”

“No English.”

The African laughed. The sound was not loud, but others took it up, until scattered laughter moved across the deck like fire among dry grass. Delano’s face grew hot. He could not tell whether he was being mocked, warned, or merely observed by men whose amusement had another cause.

He returned to Cereno and demanded the truth.

“The truth?” said the Spaniard.

“Is there disorder aboard beyond what you have told me? Have these people threatened you? Are you master of your ship?”

At the final question, Don Benito’s face contracted as if struck. Babo stepped nearer.

“My master has explained everything,” he said.

“I asked your master.”

“And he is ill.”

“Illness does not prevent an honest answer.”

Cereno rose. For a moment dignity returned to him—not health, not courage, but the rigid ceremonial dignity of an ancestral portrait spared amid the burning of its palace.

“I am Benito Cereno,” he said, “captain of this vessel.”

The statement, intended perhaps as an answer, sounded instead like an appeal to a fact no longer acknowledged.

Delano regretted his severity. He offered his hand. Cereno looked at it but did not take it until Babo murmured, “The captain waits.”

Their hands met. Don Benito’s was cold.

Toward evening, cat’s-paws darkened the water. The Americans rigged a line to assist the San Dominick toward the inner harbor, but the breeze repeatedly failed, and the vessels made little progress. Delano remained aboard longer than he had intended, partly from duty and partly because the mystery of the ship, though he denied it the name of danger, held him with the fascination of a dream whose events are absurd yet whose atmosphere forbids awakening.

At last he resolved to return to his vessel, promising renewed assistance at dawn.

Babo appeared relieved. Cereno did not.

The whale-boat came alongside. Delano descended to the lower deck, accompanied by Cereno and his servant. Several Africans crowded near. The hatchet-men had risen and stood beneath the mainmast, each with his blade tucked into a belt or held loosely at his thigh.

“Keep them back,” Delano said.

Babo called an order. They withdrew a pace.

Don Benito swayed. Delano offered support, but Babo interposed his arm.

“Master cannot bear farewells,” he said.

Cereno gazed at the boat below. “You go now?”

“Only until morning.”

“Morning,” Cereno repeated.

The word seemed to contain an immeasurable distance.

Delano climbed over the side and descended the knotted rope. Seated in the stern, he looked up. Don Benito stood at the bulwark, Babo close behind him. The canvas on the poop stirred in the new breeze. For an instant the concealed object pressed outward, and the yellowish protrusion appeared again—not coral, Delano now thought, nor carved wood, but something shaped like the bones of a hand.

The boat shoved off.

At that moment Don Benito sprang.

No warning preceded the act. One instant he stood nearly helpless above; the next he had vaulted the bulwark, coat flaring behind him, and fallen among the astonished Americans. The boat lurched. Delano seized him, believing madness had completed what illness began.

Then Babo appeared at the rail with a dagger.

His face no longer wore solicitude. It was not distorted by rage; indeed, its composure was more dreadful than fury. He leapt after Cereno, aiming the blade as he fell.

“Down!” cried Delano.

An oarsman struck Babo’s wrist with his loom. The dagger glanced from Cereno’s shoulder and entered the gunwale. Babo clung to the side, one leg in the sea. Another American clubbed him across the head, but before he lost his hold, Cereno tore free and screamed—not in command, nor even in articulate explanation, but with the released terror of a man who has been forbidden to utter pain.

“Mutiny! They killed Aranda! The ship is theirs!”

The deck of the San Dominick erupted.

Africans rushed to the bulwarks. Hatchets lifted. Stones, knives, and pieces of iron struck the water around the retreating boat. A Spanish sailor attempted to jump after them, but a Black man seized him by the hair and dragged him backward. The women cried commands to the men. Children carried missiles. Above all sounded the sharp voice of Babo, who, having regained the deck, directed the attack with the authority of long possession.

The masquerade was ended.

Delano’s hesitation vanished with it. No man was more decisive once danger accepted a recognizable shape. He ordered his crew to pull for the American ship, firing pistols to summon aid. Cereno lay in the bottom of the boat, clutching Delano’s knees, unable to speak except in fragments: revolt—murder—eighty days—forced—Babo—bones.

Within minutes the American vessel was alive with preparation. Muskets were distributed; boarding pikes brought up; the anchor cable shortened. The mate, whose suspicions had earlier been dismissed, asked no triumph of vindication, but received his orders with grave efficiency.

The San Dominick, meanwhile, attempted to escape the bay. Her remaining sails were loosed, and Africans swarmed among the braces with a skill proving that their long freedom had not been idle. Yet the breeze was weak, the hull foul, and confusion followed the sudden destruction of the fiction by which they had hoped to obtain water and provisions without battle.

The American boats advanced under musket fire.

What followed was brief in duration, though not in violence. The first boat reached the starboard chains and was met by hatchets. Two Americans fell into the sea. A Black sailor, standing on the rail like a figure cast in iron, split a boarding pike and wounded its bearer before a musket ball turned him backward. From the quarter-deck, Babo called men to defend the helm. No weapon was in his hand. His weapon was arrangement.

The surviving Spaniards, understanding that rescue had come, attacked their guards. Some seized marline-spikes; others used broken bottles. The old one-armed sailor threw himself against a hatchet-man and carried both of them down the hatch. Women fought beside men, hurling blocks from the poop. The San Dominick, so recently a stage of hushed gestures, became a many-leveled theater of undisguised war.

Delano boarded near the main-chains. As he gained the deck, he saw the canvas torn from the structure on the poop.

There, wired upright to the carved pedestal, was a human skeleton.

The bones had been cleaned. Around the neck remained scraps of linen; upon one finger gleamed a ring. The skull faced forward over the ship it no longer owned, and beneath its feet the words **FOLLOW YOUR LEADER** were now horribly plain.

Delano stopped.

Only for an instant. Then the press of combat closed around him.

The Africans fought with the desperation of persons to whom defeat promised not mere imprisonment but the deliberate restoration of a condition they had overthrown. The Americans fought with the disciplined confidence of armed sailors, supported by their vessel’s guns. Between them struggled the Spaniards, some for rescue, some for revenge, and some perhaps merely because action, however fatal, was preferable to the long paralysis of compelled deception.

By sunset the San Dominick was taken.

Babo was found crouched behind the mizzenmast, blood running from his temple. He had discarded the servant’s jacket. When seized, he neither pleaded nor resisted. His eyes moved once toward Cereno, who stood under guard for his own protection, wrapped in an American coat. In that glance there was no affection, and no shame at affection counterfeited. There was judgment.

Cereno fainted.

In the weeks that followed, while the San Dominick underwent repairs and the colonial authorities assembled witnesses, the hidden history of the voyage emerged through depositions, examinations, and testimonies whose legal order could not wholly subdue the horror they recorded.

The revolt had occurred not after months of storm and fever, but only days after departure from port. At dawn, while the Spaniards slept, Babo and several others seized hatchets and knives, killed the guards, and broke open the arm chests. Don Alexandro Aranda, owner of most of the captives, begged for his life, promising liberty and safe return to Africa. Babo listened, then ordered him killed.

The ship’s officers were compelled to steer toward Senegal. When contrary winds and ignorance of navigation made that course impossible, Babo devised another. The Spaniards would remain alive so long as they served. Any signal, whisper, or attempt at escape would be punished. Guards attended them by day and slept across their doors at night.

Aranda’s body disappeared. Later his skeleton was displayed in place of the ship’s covered figurehead. The inscription was Babo’s command to the surviving Spaniards: follow your leader. If they resisted, they would join him.

When the American vessel appeared, the insurgents feared cannon fire but needed water desperately. Babo therefore designed the deception. Cereno was instructed in the tale of storms, fever, and shortage. Africans would imitate disorderly yet obedient captives. Spaniards would behave as surviving crew. Babo would remain constantly beside Cereno, both to prompt and to kill him at the first sign of betrayal.

Every cough had concealed a warning. Every act of service had been surveillance. The shaving razor had not been an emblem accidentally suggestive of death, but death itself held temporarily in abeyance. The hatchet-polishers had not been carpenters. The silent Spaniards had not been merely sick. The things Delano saw were true; only the names he gave them were false.

In court, Babo spoke little. The prosecutors called him savage, conspirator, murderer, and rebel. The surviving Spaniards called him demon. Sailors who had watched him command described his intelligence with unwilling admiration, as though competence, appearing where they believed nature had forbidden it, aggravated every crime.

Cereno testified from behind a screen.

His voice was weak, but his recollection, once compelled into sequence, proved exact. He recounted the threats, rehearsals, executions, and nights passed beneath guard. He described how Babo had squeezed his wrist whenever an answer displeased him; how a blade was always hidden in the servant’s clothing; how the Africans nearest Delano had understood enough Spanish to detect any disclosure; how each Spaniard knew that his companions would die if one attempted warning.

“And why,” asked the magistrate, “did you not trust Captain Delano sooner?”

There was a long silence.

“I tried.”

The magistrate pressed him. “At what moment?”

“At every moment.”

This answer, though perhaps unsatisfactory to law, approached nearer to the truth than a catalogue of gestures could have done. Cereno’s pallor had tried; his broken speech had tried; his refusals, contradictions, collapses, and staring silences had tried. But expression requires not only one who makes a sign; it requires another prepared to receive it. Delano had possessed courage, intelligence, and charity. What he lacked was the conception that would have assembled the evidence before him.

Babo was condemned to death.

On the morning appointed, he was brought into the square under guard. The crowd expected confession, curses, or some theatrical final appeal. He gave them none. He walked steadily, looked neither toward church nor tribunal, and said nothing upon the scaffold.

After execution, his head was placed upon a pole overlooking the harbor. From that elevation it faced the warehouses where men were bought and sold, the church where masters and servants knelt beneath one crucifix, and the road by which Don Alexandro Aranda had once traveled in dignity. The body was burned outside the city.

Captain Delano returned often to Cereno during the Spaniard’s convalescence. The American, restored to his native confidence, attempted to comfort him by contrasting the present with the danger escaped.

“You are saved,” he said. “Your enemies are dead or imprisoned. The sea is calm. Why look backward?”

Don Benito sat near a shuttered window. Though the room was warm, he wore a heavy cloak.

“You saw the sun that day,” he replied.

“Yes.”

“I saw only the shadow.”

Delano supposed he referred to Babo, and after some hesitation asked whether the servant’s image still haunted him.

Cereno looked up. His face had acquired the stillness of one who no longer expects language to cross the interval between human minds.

“The servant?” he said.

Then he turned away.

Within several months he entered a monastery. He took no vows, and the brothers recorded that he seldom attended their common meals. At night he was heard walking in the corridor, pausing outside closed doors as if listening for a guard. A fever came upon him near winter’s end. He died before spring.

As for Delano, he sailed again, and the habits of command, business, and open weather gradually reduced the San Dominick to an incident often related but never entirely comprehended. He would describe the gray morning, the strange captain, the devoted servant, and the leap into the boat; and his hearers, knowing the conclusion, marveled that he had not recognized the truth at once.

But conclusions illuminate backward with a dishonest clarity. Once the mask has fallen, every painted smile appears an obvious disguise; once the dagger is drawn, each former courtesy seems to have pointed toward its blade. Those who blamed Delano might themselves, under another arrangement of appearances, have mistaken cruelty for order, terror for obedience, or captivity for peace.

The sea keeps no testimony. It receives the tyrant and the insurgent, the merchant and the captive, erasing their separate wakes beneath one indifferent surface. Yet men are less forgetful, though not necessarily more just. They preserve names, verdicts, gallows, and inscriptions; they call one death murder and another punishment; they polish the instruments of authority until those instruments shine like reason itself.

Years afterward, sailors entering that Chilean harbor claimed that on certain windless mornings a dark ship could be seen beyond the fog, moving without canvas. No sound came from her except the measured scraping of steel upon stone. Her bulwarks were crowded, but no observer could determine who commanded and who obeyed, for every face seemed masked, every hand appeared both chained and armed, and upon the stern, where the name should have been, there remained only a strip of weathered canvas, stirring though the air was still.

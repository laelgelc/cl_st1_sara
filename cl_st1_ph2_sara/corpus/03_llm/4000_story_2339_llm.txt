### POISONOUS JIMMY’S SHOUT

I can tell you a yarn about me and Jim Bently and a publican they called Poisonous Jimmy, though his proper name was James Phelan, and he kept a shanty five miles the other side of Mulgatown, on the Old Stock Route.

It was a slab place with a bark roof and a verandah that leaned forward as if it was tired of standing, and there was nothing within twenty miles except mulga, dust, and Jimmy’s whisky. The whisky was the worst of the lot.

They called him Poisonous partly on account of the whisky and partly on account of his temper. He had one sharp little eye that looked into you and counted your money, and another pale, stony eye that seemed to be watching somebody behind you. He’d got that eye through being struck with a bottle in Bourke, or kicked by a horse at Cobar, according to whether he was talking to a magistrate or a horseman. His nose had been broken in two places and had set out to the west; and his beard went round his face like a bush fence round a stubble-field. When he smiled—which was mostly when a chequeman came along—the smile would have made anybody home-sick.

Me and Jim was hard up at the time. We’d been sinking tanks for a contractor who went insolvent the day before pay-day, and we had our swags, a plug of tobacco between us, and threepence. The threepence belonged to Jim, but I was helping him look after it.

We was tramping towards Mulgatown in the heat when we came on a mob of travelling cattle camped by a dry creek. There was six drovers and a cook, and they were waiting for the boss to ride in from town with their cheques. They’d been paid off from a Queensland trip and were going south after the cut-out.

One of them knew Jim.

“Any tucker?” sez Jim.

“A bit,” sez he. “Sit down.”

They gave us beef and johnny-cake and tea, and we heard all about the cheques. The boss had promised to be back by sundown. The men meant to pass Poisonous Jimmy’s next morning and go straight through Mulgatown, because they knowed Jimmy’s ways.

“He’ll be waitin’ for us,” said the cook.

“He generally is,” I said.

“He was outside sweeping the road when we come up,” said another chap. “Said he was cleanin’ the place.”

“That road ain’t been swept since the blacks had it,” said Jim.

After dinner we shouldered our swags and started on. We hadn’t gone half a mile when Jim stopped.

“It’s hot,” he said.

“It’s hot,” I said.

“There’ll be beer at Jimmy’s.”

“There generally is—if you’ve got money.”

Jim looked back at the drovers’ camp, and then at me.

“They’re waitin’ for their cheques,” he said.

“They are.”

“And Jimmy’s waitin’ for them.”

“He is.”

Jim shifted his swag to the other shoulder.

“We might as well tell him they’re comin’.”

“We’d be doin’ him a kindness,” I said.

So we went into the mulga and planted our swags under a fallen tree. You can generally tell a sundowner by his swag, and we didn’t want to be taken for anything poorer than drovers. Then we brushed the dust off each other, tightened our belts, and walked on with our hands loose and easy, like men with wages behind them.

Poisonous Jimmy was standing in the road when we came in sight. He was shading his good eye with one hand and pretending not to be looking past us. There was a new blackboard outside the shanty, and on it was written:

**GOOD STABLING. BEST BRANDS. CIVILITY.**

There wasn’t a stable, the brands was Jimmy’s, and civility depended on the amount of your cheque.

“Afternoon,” he said careless-like.

“Afternoon,” I said.

“Hot.”

“It’s hot.”

“Come far?”

“A bit.”

“Cattle travelling slow?”

“Pretty slow,” I said.

He glanced along the road behind us, but the dust hid the camp.

“Better have a drink,” he said. “It’s a dry stretch.”

“We ain’t got time,” said Jim.

That settled it. If we’d said we wanted one, he’d have charged double; but a man in a hurry is nearly always expected to have money.

“Oh, come in,” said Jimmy. “One won’t hurt you.”

We went in.

There was a barmaid called Rosie, a red-haired girl who had been in Mulgatown long enough to know what questions not to ask. She looked at our trousers and boots, then at Jimmy, and began polishing a glass that wasn’t worth it.

“What’ll it be?” said Jimmy.

“Anything,” I said, in the tone of a man who had drunk everything.

He drew three beers and put his own down without paying for it.

“Here’s luck,” he said.

“Here’s luck,” we said.

He drank and watched us over the top of the glass with his sharp eye. The cold eye appeared to be watching the door.

“Boss behind?” he asked after a while.

“He was behind when we left him,” said Jim.

“How many in the party?”

“Oh, the usual mob.”

“Good trip?”

“Fair.”

“Paid off?”

“He went in about the cheques,” I said.

Jimmy’s beard shifted with his smile.

“Have another.”

“We don’t mind.”

We had another, and Jimmy talked about rain, cattle prices, the Government, and the crookedness of publicans in general. He said a man on the road ought to be treated like a Christian. Then he took us through to the dining-room and made Rosie put cold mutton and bread on the table.

“Have a feed,” he said. “No charge.”

“That’s very kind,” said Jim, as if kindness worried him.

“Oh, we always look after the boys here,” said Jimmy.

Rosie turned her back and coughed.

After the feed we went to the bar again. Jimmy was becoming anxious because the drovers hadn’t come in sight, but he didn’t like to show it.

“They’ll be here before dark, I suppose?”

“Likely,” I said.

“Might camp this side?”

“They might.”

“Boss carrying the cheques?”

“I never looked in his pockets.”

Jimmy’s good eye narrowed. Then he smiled again, and it was like a parson smiling through a broken nose.

“Have another drink.”

We had it.

Presently I put my hand into my pocket and looked annoyed.

“What’s up?” said Jim.

“I left me purse in me swag.”

“You always do,” said Jim.

Jimmy heard him.

“Short of a few bob?” he asked.

“Only till the boss comes up,” I said. “I wanted to get some tobacco in Mulgatown.”

“How much?”

“Oh, it don’t matter.”

Jimmy took a sovereign from behind the bar and pushed it towards me. He did it slow, as if his fingers was attached to it by elastic.

“Take that,” he said. “You can square up when the others arrive.”

“I don’t like borrering,” I said.

“You’re all one party, ain’t you?”

Jim looked at me, and I looked at him.

“We’ve been together long enough,” I said.

That answer satisfied Jimmy, because it was true in more ways than he knew. I took the sovereign, and Jimmy wrote something in a book. I reckon he charged interest while he was writing.

“We’d better get back,” said Jim.

“Back?” Jimmy said quickly.

“To see where they’ve got to.”

Jimmy thought a moment, then drew a gallon can of beer and put the lid on it.

“Take this along to the boys,” he said. “Compliments of the house. Tell ’em there’s a good paddock and plenty of water here.”

The paddock was mostly stones and the water had drowned two goats, but we thanked him.

“And tell the boss,” Jimmy added, casual as a snake beside a rabbit-hole, “that I can cash the cheques.”

“We’ll mention it.”

We took the can and walked back along the road. Jimmy stood outside watching us until the bend hid us. Then we cut through the scrub, recovered our swags, and went to the drovers’ camp from the creek side.

“We brought you a present,” said Jim.

The cook opened the can.

“Beer!” he said. “Where’d you get it?”

“Poisonous Jimmy sent it,” I said.

The drovers looked suspicious.

“What for?”

“He’s taken a fancy to you.”

“He ain’t seen us.”

“That’s the beautiful part of it,” said Jim.

We told them enough to make them laugh, but not too much, because a joke can be spoilt by explaining the business side. We drank one pannikin each and left them the rest. Then, while Jimmy was watching the stock-route, me and Jim went across country, skirted the back of his selection, and reached Mulgatown by an old timber track.

We spent sixpence on tobacco, ninepence on tea and meat, and slept beyond the railway dam. Jim said we ought to put the balance aside against Jimmy’s sovereign.

So we put it aside in the Mulgatown Hotel.

That is my part of the yarn.

But I met one of those drovers years afterwards at a shed on the Bogan, and he told me what happened after we left. I’ll give it as near as I remember, because a yarn like this belongs to all hands once it gets started.

* * *

We got our cheques that evening, and the boss had heard things in Mulgatown that made him particular about Poisonous Jimmy. So he sez:

“We’ll start early and go straight through.”

No one objected. We’d drunk Jimmy’s beer and felt friendly towards him, but not friendly enough to let him cash our cheques.

We moved at daylight. When we got within a mile of the shanty, one of the boys sez:

“Think he’ll expect us?”

“Not unless them two sundowners told him,” I said.

“They weren’t sundowners,” said the cook. “They was commercial travellers in beer.”

We saw Jimmy out in the road. He’d got his good coat on and his hair greased, and Rosie had tied a bit of blue ribbon round the neck of a cracked water-bottle. There was fresh chalk on the blackboard, too:

**WELCOME, DROVERS.**

Jimmy waved.

We waved back.

Then we kept on.

He ran beside the lead horses for fifty yards.

“Pull up!” he called. “Breakfast ready!”

“We’ve had breakfast,” said the boss.

“Have a drink!”

“Too early.”

“It’s never too early!”

“It is for us.”

Jimmy stopped. We travelled on. After another hundred yards we heard a horse galloping behind, and Jimmy came up on a grey mare with one stirrup shorter than the other and no hat.

“Here!” he shouted. “What’s this game?”

“What game?” said the boss.

“My sovereign!”

“What sovereign?”

“The sovereign I lent your men!”

“What men?”

“The two men you sent ahead.”

“We sent no men ahead.”

Jimmy’s face went quiet, which looked worse than when it was angry.

“One tall, thin blanky in a blue shirt,” he said, “and another crimson liar with a red scarf.”

“That sounds like the two that brought us your beer,” said the cook.

“My beer?”

“Very good beer,” I said. “Considering.”

Jimmy got off his mare.

“Who’s payin’ for it?”

“You said it was compliments of the house.”

“I never said— They was your men!”

“They never said they was,” said the boss.

“They said they’d been with you!”

“They might have meant spiritually,” said the cook.

Jimmy’s sharp eye jumped from one face to another, while the stony eye kept a cold watch on the cattle.

“They had three drinks each,” he said, “and feeds, and a sovereign, and a gallon of beer!”

“You shouldn’t trust strangers,” said the boss.

That made Jimmy dangerous. He caught the boss’s horse by the bridle and demanded a sovereign, the price of the beer, six drinks, two feeds, use of a can, and something extra for being made a fool of.

“We’ll pay for the beer,” said the boss, “seeing we had it.”

“You’ll pay the lot!”

“We’ll pay for what we had.”

“I’ll take it out of your hide!”

The boss was a quiet man, and quiet men should be left alone. He got down, handed his reins to me, and said:

“Don’t damage him. He may want to serve travellers later.”

Jimmy swung first. His fist went past the boss and struck our cook, who wasn’t in the dispute but had always believed in settling things thoroughly. The cook hit Jimmy under the beard. Then three dogs joined in, the grey mare backed over a water-bag, and somebody’s horse started for Mulgatown with a frying-pan clattering under its belly.

It was hard to tell where the fight was for a minute. Jimmy’s cold eye misled us. You’d think he was looking at the cook when his good eye had picked out somebody else entirely. He planted me in the ribs without once appearing to notice me, and then he rushed the boss and they rolled into a patch of burr.

At last we got him on his feet. His coat was split, his beard was full of grass-seed, and his nose pointed a shade farther west.

The boss gave him the price of the gallon.

“That’s for the beer,” he said.

Jimmy threw the money in the dust.

“I want justice!”

“You can pick it up when you find some,” said the cook.

We moved on.

Jimmy went back to the shanty, and Rosie told us afterwards that he began drinking his own whisky and explaining the robbery. Each time he explained it, the sovereign grew larger. By dinner-time it was five pounds; by three o’clock Dave and Jim had held him at revolver-point and stolen the day’s takings; and by sundown they’d tried to burn the shanty with Jimmy and Rosie inside.

Then a carrier came through and told him he’d seen two men answering the description camped beyond the railway dam. Jimmy took a tomahawk, got on the grey mare, and rode into Mulgatown yelling that he was after two bushrangers.

He found a mounted constable instead.

The constable asked why he had a tomahawk. Jimmy said it was evidence. The constable asked why he was drunk. Jimmy said that was grief. Then Jimmy called the constable a Government loafer and tried to ride through the lock-up gate.

They fined him ten bob.

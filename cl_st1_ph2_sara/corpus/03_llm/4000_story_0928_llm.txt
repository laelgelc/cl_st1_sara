# The Oak Beyond the Orchard

## I

“Rain before sundown,” said the corporal.

The captain, who had been listening to the irregular mutter of rifles beyond the ridge, looked upward. The sky was a vast lid of tarnished silver. Along the west a black stain had spread beneath the clouds, and from it long veils descended toward the earth, though as yet no rain touched the dusty road.

“Maybe,” he said.

The corporal regarded this answer as a military opinion of some importance. He shifted his carbine and squinted again at the heavens.

“Derned if it don’t smell like rain.”

The company waited among a row of locust trees. Their blue coats, dimmed by dust, showed in broken strips between the gray trunks. The horses hung their heads and breathed heavily. A trooper’s canteen knocked with a hollow and persistent sound against his saddle; another man cursed in whispers at a buckle which had pinched his leg. Leather creaked. Bits jingled. Somewhere behind the trees a horse coughed with astounding human misery.

From beyond the ridge came three shots.

They sounded small and petulant, like sticks snapping in a distant wood. Then there was silence, during which the men gazed toward the ridge with that profound attention which soldiers give to a place where other men may be considering the advisability of killing them.

The captain sat motionless upon a bay mare. Dust had powdered his boots, his trousers, and the lower portion of his coat, but his shoulders remained straight, and his countenance possessed the calm severity proper to an officer whose decisions were expected to arrive complete and flawless. He was young. This was not apparent to the men when he shouted an order, but it was betrayed now by the smoothness at his temples and by the almost boyish impatience with which he twisted one end of his moustache.

A lieutenant came trotting from the right.

“They’ve got men in that grove, sir. Sharpshooters, I reckon. Sergeant Pell saw two of ’em move.”

He pointed beyond the ridge. There, on a low swell of land, stood a grove of cedars and hickories. It was an ordinary grove, dark at its base and ragged at the top, yet to the waiting cavalry it had assumed the malign intelligence of a fortress. Its leaves concealed eyes. Its branches appeared to bend together in whispered consultation.

“How many?” asked the captain.

“Couldn’t say.”

The corporal, who had overheard, gravely winked at a private. The private did not respond. He was studying the grove with an expression of personal resentment.

Another rifle cracked. A bullet went overhead, making the air utter a quick metallic complaint. One of the horses lurched sideways. The men straightened in their saddles.

The captain turned.

“Company—forward!”

The waiting line changed at once. Men who a moment before had drooped like garments hung in the sun became abrupt and rigid. Sabres slid from scabbards with a long hiss. Carbines came up. The flag, limp and reluctant beneath the heavy sky, was lifted from among the trees.

“At a trot!”

The company moved into the road. Hoofs struck the baked earth, first in scattered blows, then in a rolling measure. Dust rose about the horses’ knees and followed in a pale, boiling wall. The fences appeared to run backward. The flag awakened and streamed out, snapping angrily at the gloom.

The captain rode at the head. The bay mare fought the bit and tossed white foam against her breast. Behind him came blue sleeves, flashing steel, strained faces, red mouths, and the fierce eyes of horses transformed by noise into harnessed demons. The whole column bent and swayed as though a river of blue and steel had burst from its banks and was rushing, blind with foam, upon the grove.

“Gallop!”

The hoofbeats became thunder. Equipment clanked and hammered. A hat flew away and spun in the road, lonely and ridiculous, while its owner rushed onward bareheaded. Men shouted, though none could afterward have said what words they used. Their voices were snatched into the tumult and torn to pieces.

Smoke sprang from the grove. It came in small gray puffs that wriggled among the branches like stung phantoms. The rifles spoke together.

A horse went down in front, plowing the road with its shoulder. Its rider turned once in the air, showing the pale soles of his boots, and vanished beneath the dust. The line divided about them without pausing. Another man sagged slowly over his pommel as if overcome by sudden sleep.

The captain saw these things, and did not see them. The grove had become the sole object in the world. It swelled before him until every black interval between the trunks seemed the mouth of a cannon. He raised his sabre. His shout had no words in it.

The mare leaped the ditch.

For a moment there was only green darkness, torn smoke, branches striking faces, horses plunging over roots, men swearing, carbines exploding so near that their flame seemed to burn the leaves. A gray-coated figure ran from behind a cedar. One trooper fired and missed; another pursued with his sabre raised. The enemy did not await the arrival of the shining blade. They fled through the far edge of the grove in twos and threes, stooped nearly double, their rifles clutched across their bodies.

“Go in hard now!” cried the captain. “Drive ’em! Drive ’em!”

The company poured among the trees and out upon a weedy field. The Confederate sharpshooters were already scrambling over a stone wall. Two paused to fire. The cavalry answered with a ragged volley, and the wall became briefly veiled in smoke. When the wind tore the veil aside, one gray figure lay tangled on the stones; the others had disappeared down a wooded hollow.

The captain checked his mare. The men gathered by degrees, excited, dusty, and eager to give accounts of private combats in which each narrator had displayed an ingenuity bordering upon the supernatural. One corporal swore he had knocked a rifle from a man’s hand with the flat of his sabre. A private announced that he had been fired upon at a distance of four feet and was alive only because the rebel had shut both eyes. Two men argued loudly over which of them had shot the figure on the wall.

The captain silenced them.

“Lieutenant, take half the company and hold that road. Sergeant, see to the wounded.”

His voice was cool again. The charge, with its blood, thunder, and lunatic speed, had fallen behind him like a door slammed upon a noisy room.

The corporal who had predicted rain came near. There was a scratch across his cheek. He touched it several times with secret admiration.

“Three of our men down, sir. Wilkes is dead. Moore’s bad. Ketter’s leg may be broke.”

The captain looked back toward the grove. The dust was settling there in slow folds. A riderless horse, with reins trailing, walked in a circle and nuzzled the ground beside a motionless blue sleeve. Beyond it lay two gray bodies. In death, the colors seemed less hostile. Blue and gray had become merely patches of cloth in trampled grass.

“Send Moore to the rear,” said the captain. “And find that horse.”

The corporal saluted. Before leaving, he glanced toward the west.

“Rain,” he observed solemnly.

The captain gave him a hard look, but the corporal departed with the satisfaction of a prophet who has been consulted twice.

It was then that the lieutenant, from his post near the stone wall, called out and pointed southward.

Across the field, half hidden by an overgrown orchard, stood a house.

It was a square white edifice with green shutters and a narrow porch. Great boxwoods crowded before it, dark and rounded as crouching beasts. No smoke rose from the chimney. No person showed at a window. A lane wandered from its gate toward the road, but weeds grew high between the wheel tracks.

The house seemed to be watching them.

The men watched in return.

“Could be some of ’em went there,” said the lieutenant.

“Could be,” said the captain.

A private declared that he had seen movement at an upstairs window. Another immediately said that he, too, had seen it, though he had been facing the opposite direction. The orchard, with its twisted trees and burden of small green fruit, offered many excellent places for ambush. The silent shutters had the appearance of closed lips.

The captain called for six men.

They rode across the field at a walk.

This advance, being directed against a quiet house rather than a grove full of rifles, was marked by a peculiar caution. In the charge, men had galloped toward smoke with their mouths open. Now they hunched in their saddles and peered suspiciously at every bush. One trooper started when an apple dropped from a tree.

The captain noted this and felt no contempt. The house had advantages not possessed by the sharpshooters. It concealed its intentions.

At the gate he dismounted.

“Corporal, two men around back. Lieutenant, keep the others here. If firing starts—”

“We’ll come in.”

“No. Cover the doors and windows. I’ll call if I need you.”

The lieutenant appeared disappointed. He had an intense curiosity concerning houses inhabited by enemies.

The captain chose three men and walked up the path.

The porch steps complained under their boots. At the door he paused, listening. Behind him the troopers shifted their carbines. Their battle-stained faces had become grave and almost embarrassed, as though they were intruding upon a funeral.

The captain knocked.

No answer came.

He knocked again.

Within the house, something moved.

The captain placed his hand on the latch.

The door opened.

A young woman stood before him.

## II

She wore a plain brown dress and a white collar. Her hair, dark and lustrous despite the poor light of the hallway, was drawn back severely, but little strands had escaped near her ears. Her face was pale. One hand held the edge of the door; the other was hidden in the folds of her skirt.

For a moment no one spoke.

The captain had expected perhaps a gray uniform, a rifle barrel, an old woman with a broom, or nothing at all. He had not expected this girl. The omission in his calculations vexed him. His hand, which had been prepared to seize a pistol or signal an assault, now seemed large, red, and entirely without purpose.

Behind him, a trooper coughed.

The captain became aware of his coat. It was white with dust. One sleeve had been torn by a branch. There was a brown stain on the cuff which might have been mud, but might not. He also became conscious that his hair was wet with sweat beneath his hat and that his boots had recently passed through a ditch.

“Good afternoon,” he said.

It was not a military opening. He knew this at once.

The girl’s eyes moved from his face to the armed men behind him. They were gray eyes, but fear had darkened them.

“What do you want?”

Her voice trembled, though she tried to make it sharp.

The captain recovered a part of his official dignity.

“Madam, some Confederate soldiers were driven in this direction. I must know whether any have entered this house.”

“No.”

The answer came too quickly.

He looked at her hidden hand.

“Who is here?”

“No one.”

“No one at all?”

“My grandfather. He’s old. There’s no one else.”

The captain heard a small sound from above. It might have been a foot upon a board. It might have been the ancient house settling its bones. The men behind him heard it also. Their carbines rose a little.

The girl stepped forward.

“You cannot come in.”

“I’m afraid I must.”

“There are no soldiers here. Indeed there are not.”

The word “indeed” broke in the middle. Her eyes widened as the captain moved his hand toward the door.

“Please,” she said. “You must believe me.”

He wished to believe her. This wish arrived with such force that he instantly distrusted it. Duty, which during the charge had been a bright straight road, now divided into obscure paths among household shadows. He thought of the three men lying in the grove. He thought of a rifle thrust from an upstairs window. He thought also of the girl’s lips, which had lost their color.

“Madam,” he said, more gently, “I don’t intend harm to you or your family. But I have to search the house.”

At the word “search,” her concealed arm made a slight movement.

The captain saw a dark gleam between the folds of her skirt.

His manner changed.

“What have you there?”

“Nothing.”

“Show me your hand.”

She drew back. A dull flush passed over her face.

“It is nothing.”

“Show it.”

The abrupt command caused the men to close about the doorway. One of them took a step into the hall.

The girl looked at the captain as if he had struck her. Then, slowly, she brought out her hand.

It held a pistol.

The troopers shouted. Two carbines sprang forward. The captain knocked both barrels aside with his arm and stepped between them and the girl.

“Hold on!”

“She’s armed, sir!”

“I can see that. Hold on, I said!”

The girl stared at the weapons. The pistol hung from her fingers. It was an old heavy thing, brown at the barrel and worn smooth at the grip.

The captain extended his hand.

“Give it to me.”

She did not move.

“Please,” he said.

This second word, spoken before his men, cost him more than many commands under fire. She looked at him, bewildered. Then she surrendered the pistol.

He broke it open. The chambers were empty.

“It isn’t loaded,” she whispered.

The nearest trooper lowered his carbine and frowned at the weapon as though it had behaved dishonorably.

The captain turned to her.

“Why did you have it?”

She clasped her hands tightly at her breast.

“It belongs to my grandfather.”

“Then why—”

“He heard the firing. He said if your men came here, he would shoot the first one through the door. He would! He doesn’t care what happens. I took the pistol while he was at the window. I took out the loads. I was afraid he would try, and then you would kill him.”

The captain glanced at the ancient weapon.

“So you meant to protect us?”

“No!” she cried. “I meant to protect him.”

A smile touched the face of one trooper. The captain silenced it with a look.

From somewhere in the rear of the house came a furious voice.

“Clara! Who’s there? What thievin’ scoundrel’s at that door?”

The girl closed her eyes briefly.

“My grandfather,” she said.

A cane struck the floor in a rapid series of blows. An old man appeared at the far end of the hall. He was tall despite a stoop, with a white beard that spread over a black waistcoat. Rage had brought a magnificent color into his cheeks. He advanced as if leading an infantry attack, using his cane less as a support than as a sword.

“Bluebellies!” he shouted. “Cutthroats! Horse thieves!”

The three cavalrymen stiffened. The old man’s progress was slow, but his wrath arrived in advance of him and filled the hall.

“Grandfather, please!”

“Get away from ’em, Clara! I’ll settle this. Where’s my pistol?”

The captain held it up.

“Do you mean this?”

The old man stopped. His eyes flashed beneath white brows.

“You give me that, you damned Yankee burglar!”

“Grandfather!”

“Sir,” said the captain, “this pistol was in your granddaughter’s possession.”

“She stole it!”

“I took it so you wouldn’t be killed,” said Clara.

“Stole it from my desk! Robbery under my own roof! And now she hands it to Yankees!”

“I did not hand it—”

“Sir,” the captain interrupted, “the weapon is unloaded.”

The old man’s countenance altered. He looked at Clara with an astonishment so profound that for one instant it extinguished his rage.

“You unloaded it?”

“Yes.”

“Every chamber?”

“Yes.”

His fury returned with reinforcements.

“Treason! Treason in my own blood! My granddaughter disarms me before the invader!”

He swung his cane at the captain. Clara darted between them and caught his arm.

It was an extraordinary spectacle. Three armed cavalrymen, who less than half an hour earlier had entered a grove under rifle fire, retreated from an old gentleman whose principal weapons were a cane and a vocabulary of unusual bitterness. The captain alone held his place, chiefly because Clara’s shoulder was against his sleeve and movement in any direction seemed impossible.

“Grandfather, stop! They’ll kill you!”

“Let ’em try!”

“We ain’t tryin’, ma’am,” said a trooper earnestly.

The old man pointed his cane over Clara’s head.

“You’ve taken my horses!”

“We have not,” said the captain.

“You took four last month.”

“I wasn’t here last month.”

“Yankees were!”

“I don’t command all the Yankees.”

“You took my sorrel mare, six shoats, twelve hens, two silver candlesticks, and a new gate off the east pasture.”

“A gate?” asked one of the troopers.

The captain turned upon him. The man studied the floor.

“And now,” continued the old man, swelling with accumulated injury, “you come back for the furniture. I suppose the piano’s next. Then the roof. Why not take the roof? There’s good shingles on it.”

“We haven’t come to take anything.”

“You’ve come to search.”

“Yes.”

“That’s taking. Taking liberties!”

The old man’s phrase struck the air like shattered glass. Behind his indignation stood ruined fields, missing animals, fences burned for campfires, and the long impersonal hand of war reaching daily into cupboards and barns. The captain recognized the truth beneath the absurd inventory. He could not restore the sorrel mare or the gate. He represented the force which had eaten them.

His face hardened.

“I’m sorry for what has happened to your property. But enemy soldiers were seen near this house, and I must search it.”

“There are none here,” said Clara quickly.

The old man looked at her.

The glance was brief. It was enough.

The captain saw it. So did the corporal behind him, who raised his eyebrows with judicial solemnity.

“There is someone here,” said the captain.

“No!”

“Madam—”

“There is no one who can harm you.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

She stood before the stairway. Her arms spread slightly, not in a dramatic gesture, but with the instinctive motion of one who guards a door. The terror in her face had changed. It now possessed purpose.

The captain felt a weight settle within him.

“Who is upstairs?”

She said nothing.

The old man struck the floor once with his cane.

“No business of yours.”

The captain handed the empty pistol to the corporal.

“Keep this.”

Then he stepped toward the stairs.

Clara caught his arm.

Her fingers rested above his dusty cuff. The touch was light, yet he stopped as if seized by an authority greater than rank.

“Please don’t go up.”

He looked down at her hand. She withdrew it instantly.

“There’s a wounded man there,” he said.

Tears came to her eyes, but did not fall.

“He’s my brother.”

The troopers exchanged glances.

“Was he in that grove?” asked the captain.

“No. He came two nights ago. He was wounded at Miller’s Ford. He can’t ride. He can scarcely stand.”

“Is he armed?”

“No.”

The old man muttered something concerning liars and Northern tyrants.

The captain’s duty resumed its straight and pitiless shape. A Confederate soldier lay upstairs. Whether wounded or not, he was an enemy combatant, subject to capture. The situation was simple. Simplicity, however, had become hateful to him.

“What is his unit?” he asked.

“Twelfth Virginia.”

“His name?”

“Thomas Vale.”

The captain nodded to two men.

“Come with me.”

Clara moved again to block the stairs.

“You said you wouldn’t harm us.”

“I won’t harm him.”

“You’ll take him away.”

“If he’s able to be moved.”

“He isn’t! Indeed he isn’t. The fever only left yesterday. The wound has opened twice. If you put him on a horse, he’ll die.”

Her words rushed together. She clutched the banister, and the captain had the bewildering impression that if he ordered her aside, his command would be a form of violence more terrible than the charge through the grove.

“I need to see him,” he said.

She stared at him for a long moment. Then she turned and went up.

The captain followed. Two troopers came behind him, attempting to place their boots delicately upon the steps. Sabres knocked the rail. Spurs caught in the carpet. Their equipment made them sound like a collapsing hardware shop.

At the landing, Clara opened a door.

The room beyond was small and very warm. The curtains had been drawn. Medicine bottles stood on a table beside a basin stained pink with bloody water. The smell of fever, linen, and old plaster crowded the air.

A young man lay propped against pillows. His face was thin and yellowish, with a black beard along the jaw. One shoulder was wrapped in bandages. A gray military coat hung over a chair.

He looked first at Clara, then at the captain. His eyes flared.

“So,” he said. “You brought them.”

“I didn’t bring them, Tom.”

The wounded man tried to rise. Pain caught him. He sank back, breathing rapidly, but his look remained defiant.

The captain stood at the foot of the bed.

“Thomas Vale?”

“That’s my name. Captain Thomas Vale, if rank signifies anything to a Yankee.”

The captain observed the gray coat. Its captain’s bars were tarnished.

“Were you with the men in the grove?”

“No.”

“Tom,” Clara whispered, fearing his tone.

He ignored her.

“If I had been, you’d not have ridden out so handsomely.”

One of the troopers made an angry sound. The captain’s eyes narrowed.

“You’re in no condition to make threats.”

“And you’re in a fine condition to receive surrender from a man in bed.”

“I haven’t asked for your surrender.”

“Then get out.”

The insult came weakly, but its intention was strong. The room seemed to contract about it. The captain could feel his men waiting for him to vindicate the blue uniform, the army, the government, and perhaps the entire North. From the bed the wounded Confederate stared with the desperate arrogance of a man whose body had betrayed him and who possessed only words with which to defend the remains of his pride.

Clara stood between them in spirit, if not in place. Her gaze moved from her brother to the captain.

“Tom, be quiet.”

“I’ll not be quiet in my grandfather’s house.”

“You’ll bring them down on us!”

“They are already down on us. Look at them. Three armed men to arrest one invalid. A heroic campaign.”

The captain’s face reddened.

“I could have the house surrounded.”

“Do it.”

“I could take you prisoner.”

“Then do it.”

“I could—”

He stopped.

There was a silence.

Outside, a horse whinnied. The sound floated faintly through the shutters. In the hallway the old man’s cane struck the floor below, an intermittent signal of rebellion.

Clara came near the captain.

“He doesn’t mean it,” she murmured.

“I do mean it,” said her brother.

“He’s ill.”

“I am wounded, not witless.”

“Tom!”

The captain looked at the man’s bandaged shoulder. A dark spot had begun to spread through the linen. The effort of speaking had drawn sweat upon his forehead. One hand gripped the coverlet, and its trembling could not be concealed.

The captain remembered Moore lying in the grove, his blood soaking the dust. He remembered the two gray bodies by the wall. They had all looked astonished by the sudden destruction of the fierce arguments which had brought them there.

“What is your wound?” he asked.

“Ball through the shoulder.”

“Any bone broken?”

“No.”

Clara answered at the same time. “The surgeon said the bone was splintered.”

Her brother glared at her.

“What surgeon?”

“Dr. Reeves from Pine Hill,” she said. “He came at night.”

The captain turned to his men.

“Wait in the hall.”

They looked surprised.

“Sir?”

“In the hall.”

When the door closed behind them, he moved toward the window and pulled the curtain slightly aside. The orchard lay below, green and quiet. Beyond it, in the field, his company waited in loose formation. The men appeared small from this room. Their sabres were tiny lines of light.

He could arrest Thomas Vale. It would be proper. It would also require that the man be carried downstairs and jolted across miles of bad road. Perhaps he would live. Perhaps he would die beneath a Union guard before nightfall. The regulations offered clarity and no comfort.

The captain released the curtain.

“Do you give me your word that you will not take up arms until you can be properly exchanged or paroled?”

Vale laughed once. The sound became a cough.

“My word to you?”

“Yes.”

“No.”

Clara turned white.

The captain’s mouth tightened.

“Then I have no choice.”

“Wait,” she said.

She came between them. Her hands were clasped so tightly that the knuckles showed.

“Tom, give him your word.”

“No.”

“Please.”

“I won’t beg leave of him to remain in my own bed.”

“It isn’t begging.”

“It is worse.”

She bent over him.

“If they take you, you’ll die.”

He looked away.

“You heard Dr. Reeves. You mustn’t be moved. You know it. Grandfather knows it. I know it.” Her voice sank. “Must your pride kill you because the bullet did not?”

The wounded man closed his eyes. For a moment he seemed very young. The beard, the uniform, and the title of captain fell away, leaving only a brother exhausted beneath his sister’s fear.

At last he said, “How long?”

The Union captain answered, “Until formally exchanged, or until the armies have moved beyond this district. You remain in this house. You don’t carry information. You don’t aid troops.”

Vale opened his eyes.

“And if your men come robbing again?”

“They won’t be my men.”

“Fine distinction.”

“It’s the distinction I can give you.”

Another silence passed.

“You have my word,” Vale said at last.

The phrase seemed to wound him more severely than the ball.

The captain inclined his head.

“I accept it.”

Clara’s breath escaped in a sob. She covered her mouth.

Vale regarded the Union officer with renewed hostility.

“Don’t expect thanks.”

“I don’t.”

“If I meet you after this war—”

“Tom!”

“—I’ll tell you exactly what I think of your army.”

The captain’s expression altered by the smallest degree.

“If we meet after the war, you may tell me over a drink.”

Vale stared. Then, despite himself, a brief crooked smile appeared and vanished.

“Yankee drink?”

“Whichever side pays.”

“That will be yours. You’ve taken all our money.”

Clara laughed.

It was only a fragment of laughter, broken at once by alarm and weariness, but it changed the room. The medicine bottles became less grim. The closed curtains seemed to admit light. Even the wounded man’s drawn face lost some of its bitterness.

The captain looked at Clara. She looked at him.

Both immediately looked elsewhere.

He moved toward the door.

“Keep the bandage clean. If the bleeding worsens, send someone to our field hospital on the north road. Tell them Captain Mercer sent you.”

“Mercer?” she said.

He turned.

“That is your name?”

“Yes. Daniel Mercer.”

She repeated it softly, as if testing whether the name belonged to the dusty, armed figure before her.

“Thank you, Captain Mercer.”

He wished to answer with something memorable—something marked by graceful courage, compassion, and perhaps a delicate indication that he had not failed to notice her eyes. His mind, so fertile in commands while bullets passed, produced nothing.

“You’re welcome,” he said.

It sounded miserably common.

He went into the hall.

The two troopers straightened. Their curiosity shone nakedly upon their faces.

“He stays,” said the captain. “He’s paroled on his word. No one enters this room.”

The corporal waited at the top of the stairs, still holding the empty pistol.

“That all, sir?”

“That’s all.”

The corporal nodded with great seriousness and gave the captain a grave wink.

The captain regarded him with cold menace.

The corporal descended hurriedly.

## III

Downstairs, the old man had prepared a fresh campaign.

While the captain had been above, he had discovered two troopers near the pantry and concluded that the general plunder of the house had begun. In truth, the men had merely followed a black cat which had darted through the kitchen. This explanation made no impression.

“Caught ’em with their hands nearly on the silver!” he cried.

“We never touched nothin’,” protested one soldier.

“There isn’t any silver there,” said Clara, coming down behind the captain.

“They don’t know that!”

The old man brandished his cane.

The captain entered the dining room. One drawer stood open. The black cat sat beneath the table washing a paw with phlegmatic contempt.

“Who opened that?” he demanded.

The two men looked at each other.

“It was open, sir,” said one.

“Was it?”

“Well—Corporal Doss looked in, sir.”

“Looking ain’t stealing,” said Doss.

“No,” replied the captain, “but orders are orders. Both of you outside.”

They went with offended dignity.

The captain walked through the front hall and onto the porch.

“Lieutenant!”

The lieutenant came up the path.

“Sir?”

“Withdraw the men from the house and yard. No search. Nothing is to be taken. Not food, not animals, not a fence rail. Post two riders at the lane until the company moves.”

The lieutenant glanced toward the upstairs windows.

“Did you find anybody?”

“A wounded officer. He has given his parole.”

The lieutenant’s eyebrows rose.

“Can he travel?”

“No.”

The answer ended discussion.

The men were called away from the outbuildings. One emerged from the smokehouse carrying a ham, saw the captain, and returned it with the tender haste of a mother replacing an infant. Another had discovered three eggs in the barn and was compelled to put them back in a nest despite his claim that no reasonable hen could identify them.

The old man stood on the porch and supervised the withdrawal.

“Count the spoons, Clara!” he shouted. “Look to the linens! See whether they’ve pried up the floorboards!”

“No one has touched the floorboards,” she said.

“How do you know?”

“Because we are standing on them.”

This checked him briefly.

The captain descended the steps. His mare was brought from the gate. He gathered the reins, but did not mount.

Clara stood behind her grandfather. Sunless light lay softly across her face. The fear had gone from her eyes, leaving fatigue and a shy uncertainty which the captain found more dangerous than fear.

“Well,” he said.

“Well,” she replied.

The old man looked from one to the other.

“You Yankees have a curious way of leaving.”

The captain placed a boot in the stirrup.

“Good day, sir.”

“Return my pistol.”

The corporal, who had hoped the subject forgotten, produced the weapon. The captain took it and held it toward Clara rather than her grandfather.

“You may want to keep charge of this.”

She accepted it. Their fingers touched.

The contact was so slight that no witness could reasonably have called it an event. Yet to the captain it seemed that the company, the house, the orchard, and the wide darkening sky all paused to observe it. Clara drew back quickly, holding the pistol by its barrel.

“Load it!” said the old man.

“No,” she answered.

“You see?” he cried to the captain. “This is what your invasion has done. Destroyed discipline in the home!”

The corporal bent his head to conceal a smile.

The captain mounted.

He should have ridden away. His men had formed in the field. The lieutenant was watching. Duty pointed toward the road with an unmistakable finger.

Instead, Captain Mercer adjusted his reins, looked at the sky, looked at the orchard, and looked again at Clara.

“It may rain,” he said.

The corporal’s head snapped up.

Clara followed the captain’s gaze toward the west. “Yes.”

“Your roof appears sound,” said the captain.

“It is.”

“That’s fortunate.”

“Yes.”

The old man leaned on his cane, fascinated despite himself by the poverty of this conversation.

Captain Mercer had led men into rifle fire. He had made decisions while horses fell and smoke hid the ground. Now, confronted by a young woman on a porch, he found his faculties scattered like frightened chickens. He searched for a phrase and encountered only weather, roofs, and the condition of the lane.

Clara seemed afflicted by a similar helplessness. She turned the empty pistol in her hands.

The captain said, “Your brother should recover.”

“I hope so.”

“He has a strong constitution.”

“Yes.”

“And a strong temper.”

A smile appeared near her mouth.

“Yes.”

The captain’s courage revived a little.

“I’m glad you took the pistol.”

She looked at him.

“So am I.”

“Though, of course, my men were in no danger from it.”

“Of course.”

“It was unloaded.”

“I know.”

“And old.”

“Very old.”

“Still—”

“Yes.”

He had arrived nowhere.

The lieutenant called from the field, “Captain, the road’s clear!”

Mercer looked savagely in his direction.

The lieutenant pretended to inspect his saddle.

The captain turned back to Clara.

“We must move.”

“Yes.”

“Goodbye, then.”

“Goodbye.”

He touched his hat. She inclined her head. The old man muttered, “At last.”

The bay mare moved down the path.

At the gate the captain stopped.

He looked back.

Clara had descended one step from the porch. Whether this movement had been made to watch him or merely to escape her grandfather’s cane was impossible to determine. The captain chose the former explanation.

He wheeled the mare and rode back.

The corporal’s face became solemn with delight.

“There is one thing,” said Mercer.

Clara waited.

He dismounted again, because speaking from the saddle suddenly seemed arrogant. On the ground he discovered that this act had brought him nearer to her and therefore increased all difficulties.

“One thing,” he repeated.

“Yes?”

The old man groaned.

Captain Mercer removed his gloves. He folded them, unfolded them, and struck them once against his palm.

“This war,” he began, “will not continue forever.”

“No,” she said.

“At least, it is generally believed that it will end.”

“I hope it does.”

“As do I. Naturally.”

“Naturally.”

“When it ends, people may travel more freely.”

“I suppose so.”

“There will be fewer patrols.”

“Yes.”

“And no cause for—well—for houses to be searched.”

“No.”

He wiped his brow with the back of his hand. The day was not warm.

Clara’s eyes had lowered. A flush touched her cheeks.

The captain rushed forward as he had rushed the grove.

“If I were to come this way—afterward, I mean—would it be disagreeable to you?”

She looked up.

“To me?”

“Yes. To you. Or your family. Your grandfather perhaps would object. Your brother very likely would. I understand that. I don’t mean to presume upon anything that has happened here. I merely thought that if I passed nearby, I might inquire about your brother’s recovery. And about your grandfather’s property. The horses. The gate.”

“The gate?” she echoed.

“Yes.”

A tiny, traitorous smile trembled upon her lips.

“I don’t think Grandfather expects to recover the gate.”

“Still, one might inquire.”

“One might.”

The captain drew breath.

“Then I might?”

Before she could answer, the old man called from the porch.

“Clara! Don’t stand there in the damp talking to that horse thief.”

“It isn’t damp yet,” she replied.

“It will be!”

The corporal nodded, vindicated.

Clara stepped nearer the captain and spoke quietly.

“There is an old oak beyond the south orchard.”

He gazed at her.

She continued, her voice becoming hurried. “The lane runs past it toward the chapel road. Sometimes I walk there. Not now, of course. Not while troops are about. But when it’s quiet.”

The captain’s countenance brightened with such suddenness that she saw at once the full enormity of what she had said.

“I only mean,” she added, “that if you came to inquire, you need not come to the house.”

“I understand.”

“No, you don’t. I didn’t mean I would meet you.”

“Of course not.”

“I said I sometimes walk there.”

“Yes.”

“Sometimes may mean very seldom.”

“Certainly.”

“And after the war may be a long time.”

“It may.”

“You might not come.”

“I might.”

She looked alarmed by his obedience to every retreating qualification.

“I should not have mentioned it.”

The light left his face.

“No,” he said. “Perhaps not.”

He put on his gloves. The movement was performed with great care. His disappointment, wishing to preserve its dignity before her, clothed itself in military precision.

“I beg your pardon. I had no right to ask.”

“You had a right to ask about Tom.”

“Only about him.”

“And Grandfather’s gate.”

“Yes. The gate.”

The old man shouted, “It was a new gate!”

The captain bowed.

“Goodbye, Miss Vale.”

“Goodbye, Captain Mercer.”

He turned away before his expression could betray him. He mounted, gave a short command, and rode through the gate.

The company began to move. Horses wheeled into the lane. The flag lifted, drooped, then lifted again beneath the first breath of the approaching storm.

Clara remained by the path.

She was angry with herself. She had been foolish to speak of the oak. She had known him less than an hour. He was an enemy officer. His men wore the same blue coats as those who had taken the sorrel mare, the shoats, the hens, the candlesticks, and the gate. Upstairs her brother lay beneath a gray coat. The land itself seemed divided into hostile colors.

Yet Captain Mercer had stood between her and the carbines. He had spared Tom. He had ordered the ham returned.

These facts contended within her.

At the end of the lane, the captain looked back once.

She did not move.

He turned forward.

The predicted rain began.

It came first in great separate drops, striking the dust with dark round marks. One touched the captain’s hand. Another tapped upon his hat brim. Then the western stain opened, and a mournful patter spread across the orchard, the roof, the road, and the bowed backs of the horses.

The men hunched their shoulders. A trooper dragged his coat collar upward. The corporal allowed himself no surprise; he received the rain with the imperturbable gravity of its author.

“Called it,” he murmured.

The captain heard and did not answer.

The company rode on. Dust turned to paste beneath the hoofs. Water drew shining lines along sabre scabbards. The flag, which had been brave in wind and smoke, clung dejectedly to its staff.

At the porch the old man shouted for Clara to come inside.

She did not obey.

Captain Mercer’s figure was receding beyond the orchard, blue coat darkening beneath the rain. Soon the bend in the lane would take him from sight.

“Captain!” she called.

The rain swallowed the word.

She ran to the gate.

“Captain Mercer!”

He heard.

The whole column halted in confusion as he jerked his mare around. Men collided. Horses protested. The lieutenant, believing perhaps that the house had opened fire, drew his sabre halfway before observing Clara beneath the dripping trees.

The captain rode back alone.

Rain shone upon his face. His expression was guarded, almost stern, as though he feared another retreat.

Clara stood with one hand on the gate. Drops trembled in her hair and darkened the shoulders of her dress.

“I only wanted to say—”

She stopped.

He waited.

The house watched behind her. The orchard leaves whispered beneath the shower. Far away, from beyond the ridge, cannon spoke with the immense indifference of thunder.

“Yes?” he said.

She looked toward the south, where the orchard concealed the old oak.

“I was wrong.”

“About what?”

“About mentioning it.”

His hand tightened on the reins.

“You needn’t explain.”

“I mean I was wrong to take it back.”

The captain stared.

She lowered her eyes. “Sometimes is not always very seldom.”

“No?”

“No.”

“And after the war?”

“After the fighting leaves this county,” she said carefully. “That may be sooner.”

“It may.”

“The oak is easy to find. It was struck by lightning years ago. One limb is white.”

“I’ll remember.”

“But I make no promise.”

“None.”

“I may not be there.”

“Of course.”

“And if Tom is worse—”

“You must stay with him.”

“And if Grandfather needs me—”

“Naturally.”

“And if there are troops—”

“You said when there are no troops.”

She looked up then. Laughter and alarm struggled in her eyes.

“You remember very well.”

“I remember orders,” he said.

“This was not an order.”

“No. It was not.”

There was a silence. Rain pattered upon the gate between them, and from the waiting company came the subdued clink of harness and the impatient stamping of horses. The captain knew that thirty men were watching his back with ruthless attention. He no longer cared.

He held out his hand.

Clara hesitated, then placed hers in it.

This time the contact was not accidental.

“Goodbye, Captain Mercer.”

“Goodbye, Miss Vale.”

He released her hand, though not promptly.

She drew it back, though not quickly.

The old man’s voice came from the porch.

“Clara! If you mean to desert to the enemy, at least bring in my pistol first!”

She turned, startled. The captain laughed. It was not the controlled laugh of an officer, but the frank, relieved laugh of a young man who has escaped from peril by means he does not understand.

He mounted and saluted her.

Then he rode away.

The company moved after him down the wet lane, blue figures blurring behind the silver cords of rain. The corporal passed the gate last. He touched two fingers to his hat and bestowed upon Clara a wink of such profound official approval that she blushed in spite of herself.

Soon the cavalry vanished beyond the orchard.

Clara remained until the last sound of hoofs had dissolved into the weather. Then she turned toward the house, holding the empty pistol at her side.

Above, at his window, her brother had managed to pull back the curtain. His pale face watched her with a crooked, knowing expression. On the porch her grandfather waited with his cane, prepared to demand an exact accounting of spoons, eggs, firearms, loyalties, and conversations.

Behind her, unseen through the rain, the old oak stood beside the chapel road. Its lightning-blasted limb shone ghostly white against the dark fields, like an arm raised in warning—or in welcome.

### Mrs. Peet’s Journey

I first saw Mrs. Peet on the platform at East Wilby, though I had known her by reputation for many years. The morning train had already whistled beyond the alder swamp, and the station-master was waving us forward with that appearance of personal injury which railway men assume when travelers have not arranged their lives to suit the timetable.

Mrs. Peet came hurriedly round the corner of the freight shed, followed by a boy pushing a barrow. On the barrow stood a small hair trunk, a covered basket that rocked suspiciously, and a bundle tied in a red cotton handkerchief. She herself carried a green umbrella, a tin matchbox, and two battered books clasped against her breast. A black bonnet, too large in front and too scanty behind, trembled on her gray head.

“Land alive, don’t let it go without me!” she cried. “I ain’t be’n drove out o’ one place to be left behind in another!”

The conductor, who was kinder than his official countenance promised, caught her trunk and swung it aboard. The basket emitted an indignant howl.

“That’s only the cat,” said Mrs. Peet, as if the explanation repaired everything. “She ain’t traveled none, neither.”

Then the train began to move. I seized Mrs. Peet’s umbrella, the boy thrust the handkerchief bundle after us, and between the conductor and myself the old woman was lifted up the steps and deposited in the seat opposite mine. She sat for a moment with both hands spread upon her knees. Her face, pale with excitement, was narrow and weather-worn; but the eyes beneath the bonnet were quick, dark, and almost youthful.

“Well!” she exclaimed. “So this is ridin’ on the cars.”

She leaned toward the window just as the station slipped backward and the freight shed appeared to run away from us.

“I can’t catch holt of a thing,” she complained. “There goes old Trafton’s mill, an’ I ain’t more’n seen the roof. Why, we’re past the buryin’-ground a’ready! There’s where my folks is, all but me. Seems queer they should stay an’ I should be took.”

She looked about the car with pathetic amusement. Two young women across the aisle were laughing together; a drummer slept with his hat tipped over his face; at the farther end a mother fed crumbs of cake to a restless child. Mrs. Peet regarded them all frankly.

“Be they goin’ to Shrewsbury too?” she whispered.

“Some of them may be.”

“Everybody looks as if they knew where they was goin’. I expect I look like an old stray hen somebody’s flung over a wall.”

Before I could answer, she remembered the cat. Bending down, she pulled the basket close and thrust a finger through a gap in the cover.

“There, Mis’ Trimble, you keep your composure. You’ve got as good a chance as I have, an’ mebbe better. Cats can always take to a barn. Folks thinks an old woman’s got to explain herself.”

The cat struck at her finger, and Mrs. Peet gave a short laugh.

“She’s some put out. She caught three mice yesterday mornin’, the very day Isaiah come with the team. I says to him, ‘There’s them that earns their keep to the last minute,’ but he never took the meanin’. Isaiah don’t, unless the meanin’ has a dollar mark to it.”

At that name her face altered. The smouldering excitement which had sustained her through the confusion of boarding gave way to fatigue. She held the basket between her worn shoes and laid both hands upon its cover.

“You’ve left your farm, I understand,” I said gently.

“Left it!” she repeated. “I expect that’s one way to put it. The tree leaves the pasture when the axe is laid to the root. I ain’t got no more claim there than this cat has to the meetin’-house.”

She looked through the window, though there was now nothing to be seen but blurred woodland and the gray shine of flooded meadows.

“My husband’s be’n dead five weeks come Thursday,” she continued. “You knew that, I s’pose? Job Peet wa’n’t a forehanded man, but he was honest, an’ I’d rather had honesty with a patched roof than some folks’ prosperity. He’d be’n failin’ a good spell. Couldn’t remember whether the cows was milked, nor what day Sunday come on, but he trusted Isaiah. That was the mischief.”

“Isaiah is your nephew?”

“His sister’s boy. We brought him up from eleven year old till he went off lumberin’. He was a peaked little creatur’ then, with a way of lookin’ sorry enough to melt a stove door. Job set by him. ‘The boy’s got ambition,’ he’d say. I expect he had. So has a fox when he’s outside the henhouse.”

Mrs. Peet’s bonnet had slipped awry. She straightened it with an impatient movement and went on.

“Isaiah come last fall with papers. He said Job had better fix things while he was able. Said taxes was high, an’ there was a note over to the bank, an’ if the place was made to him he’d see we never wanted. Job says, ‘You hear that, Mary? The boy’ll stand by us.’ I was clean beat out that day. We’d killed the pig, an’ I’d rendered lard till midnight, an’ next mornin’ Job had one of his poor spells. Isaiah says I’d only got to put my name where he pointed. I asked if it took the farm away, an’ he laughed. ‘Aunt Mary,’ says he, ‘you don’t understand business.’ That was true enough. I understood butter an’ wool an’ sickness an’ savin’ oven-wood, but I wa’n’t schooled to see a trap under print.”

She opened one of the battered books she had carried. It was an old geography, its cover warped and its maps faded to the colors of dried flowers.

“I can read some,” she added defensively. “I wa’n’t brought up a heathen. But law print’s different. It looks you in the face an’ tells you nothin’.”

The train rounded a curve. Mrs. Peet clutched the seat, while from the basket rose another muffled protest.

“Mercy, what a gait! If Job could see me now! He never would ride. Said the Lord give folks feet, an’ if He’d meant ’em to go forty mile an hour He’d put wheels to their ankles. But I always wanted to. I used to hear the whistle nights when I was turnin’ the last pans in the dairy, an’ I’d think, ‘There’s folks goin’ somewhere.’”

For a moment curiosity brightened her face. Then memory returned.

“After Job died, Isaiah fetched Lawyer Pike. They said the place was Isaiah’s, all signed an’ witnessed. He’d allow me twenty dollars because I was his aunt, an’ he’d take no charge for fetchin’ me to the station. Take no charge! I told him he might as well charge a stump for pullin’ it out.”

“Had you no one to advise you?”

“Who’d I have? Job was gone. The minister said likely the paper was bindin’. He prayed with me, but prayer don’t alter a county record. Mis’ Fosdick said I could stay a week, only her daughter’s family is comin’. Everybody’s got their own troubles an’ their own feather beds filled. I won’t crowd in where I ain’t wanted.”

There was pride in the lift of her chin, but also fear. She untied one corner of the handkerchief bundle as if to reassure herself that her little property remained within it. A pair of quilted flatiron holders appeared, followed by an almanac, a skein of gray yarn, a goose-wing used for brushing the hearth, and a small china lamb with one ear gone.

“I had to choose quick,” she said. “Isaiah said the team couldn’t wait. I took them holders because I made ’em the winter Job had rheumatic fever. The almanac’s got the date our brindle cow calved, though I don’t know why I need remember now. That lamb stood on the parlor mantel thirty-two year. There was another, but Job knocked it down reachin’ for the camphor bottle.”

The bundle loosened suddenly, and its contents rolled upon the floor. The china lamb went under my seat, the matchbox slid into the aisle, and the goose-wing lodged against the drummer’s boot. He woke in alarm.

Mrs. Peet descended upon her possessions with more agility than I should have expected. I recovered the lamb; the young women rescued the yarn; even the drummer handed back the goose-wing with ceremony. For an instant our end of the car was united by the small disaster.

“There!” said Mrs. Peet when all was restored. “That’s the way my life’s goin’—everythin’ broke loose an’ strangers pickin’ it up.”

One of the young women smiled kindly. Mrs. Peet smiled back, but when they had turned away she examined the china lamb for injury.

“I ain’t always suspicious,” she told me. “But when you’ve lost the main thing, you take watch over trash.”

I asked about her destination.

“I’m goin’ to my nieces, Ellen an’ Martha Bell, in Shrewsbury. Their mother was my half-sister. I ain’t seen ’em since Martha was married, an’ that’s twelve years if it’s a day. Ellen wrote after Job died. She says, ‘Come right along, Aunt Mary; we can make room.’ But there’s a difference between makin’ room on paper an’ findin’ it in a house.”

“Do they have children?”

“Martha’s got three. That’s one reason I’m goin’. I can tend children, if they ain’t been raised too modern. I can cook, mend, wash, iron, nurse sick folks, set bread, make soap, an’ get blackberry stains out of anything but a conscience. I ain’t askin’ to be kep’. If my hands don’t earn the victuals for my mouth, I’d ruther shut the mouth.”

She held up those hands. They were knotted at every joint, bent by labor into the permanent semblance of grasping.

“They don’t look capable,” she acknowledged, “but they know how. That’s more’n can be said of many smooth ones.”

I tried to assure her that her nieces would value both her experience and her company. She listened, hungry for comfort but unwilling to accept it too readily.

“Martha used to be affectionate,” she conceded. “She’d follow me out to the hen-yard an’ ask a hundred questions. Ellen was quieter. Folks may change. Shrewsbury’s a sizable place, ain’t it?”

“It has nearly ten thousand people.”

“Ten thousand!” Mrs. Peet settled back in awe. “Where do they put ’em all nights?”

“In houses, I believe.”

She laughed heartily at this, and the drummer lowered his newspaper to look at us.

“I expect you think I’m a poor ignorant old piece,” she said. “But if them ten thousand was set down on our farm, half of ’em couldn’t tell timothy from witch-grass. Knowledge depends on where you stand.”

She turned once more to the flying landscape. A village appeared and vanished: three white houses, a store, a steeple, and washing spread upon a line.

“I saw the wash,” she said triumphantly. “I’m gettin’ so I can pick things out. Only I can’t keep ’em. It’s like rememberin’ when you’re old. You get a sight, an’ away it goes.”

The conductor entered for the tickets. Mrs. Peet searched the pocket of her dress, then the pocket of her cloak. She opened the geography, shook the almanac, untied the bundle again, and at last grew white.

“Isaiah bought it,” she said. “He stood there an’ bought it. I saw him.”

The conductor waited. Mrs. Peet looked at him as one already convicted by the law’s unanswerable print.

“I ain’t stealin’ no ride,” she protested. “I’ve got twenty dollars, less what I paid the boy, an’ you can take the fare over again, but I’d like folks to know I had a ticket.”

The ticket was found beneath the cat’s basket. It had adhered to a smear of butter on its wicker bottom.

“There,” said the conductor, punching it. “Your cat was traveling on it.”

“Then I expect she ought to pay half,” Mrs. Peet replied, with recovered spirit. “She takes more seat than I do.”

The small excitement relieved her. She began to speak of the farm, at first in scattered details and then with the full current of recollection. There were twelve acres of pasture beyond the brook and a rocky mowing field where the daisies came too thick. There were seven apple trees, each distinguished by taste and temper, and a pucker-pear tree near the kitchen door which bloomed bravely but bore fruit fit only to discipline children. She had planted sage beneath the south window, kept bees for twenty years, and knew from the sound in the chimney whether snow or rain was coming.

“I could walk that place blind,” she said. “From the bed to the stove, from the stove to the well, from the well to the barn. I’ve traveled a good many thousand mile, you might say, but all of it tied in a knot. Mondays I washed, Tuesdays I ironed, Wednesdays I churned, Thursdays I baked, Fridays I scrubbed, Saturdays I made ready for Sunday, an’ Sundays I set still an’ thought what had got to be done Monday.”

“Did you never take a holiday?”

“Once Job an’ me went to the county fair. The red heifer took a prize, but Job said the feed an’ tolls come to more’n the ribbon was worth. We went home before the balloon ascension. I always wished I’d stayed to see it.”

She was silent a little while.

“Maybe Shrewsbury has entertainments,” I suggested.

“Ellen wrote there was lectures. What do they lecture about?”

“Almost everything.”

“I’d like one about foreign parts. Or stars. I’ve seen stars all my life an’ don’t know a thing about ’em except they come out when chores is done. There’s music too, ain’t there?”

“Very often.”

Mrs. Peet drew a deep breath.

“Well, I don’t calculate to be runnin’ after pleasures at my age. Still, if a person’s transplanted, she might as well see what kind of weather there is.”

At that instant the cat contrived to lift the basket cover. A gray paw appeared, then a fierce old head, flattened by captivity. Mrs. Peet cried out and pressed the cover down, but the cat came through the gap with astonishing determination. She sprang to the seat, ran over Mrs. Peet’s lap, and launched herself into the aisle.

The child at the farther end screamed with delight. The drummer blocked one direction with his traveling case while the conductor approached from the other. Mis’ Trimble, finding civilization closed on both sides, climbed the back of a seat and clung there like a storm-driven sailor.

“Don’t scare her!” Mrs. Peet implored. “She’s be’n took from everything she understands!”

Perhaps no one in the car mistook the appeal as meant solely for the cat. The conductor caught the animal by the loose skin of her neck, receiving a scratch for his pains, and returned her to the basket. Mrs. Peet tied the cover with a strip torn from the handkerchief.

“She won’t forgive me,” she said. “She was born under the barn floor fourteen year ago. Knows every stone wall from our place to the Fosdicks’. What’s a Shrewsbury cellar goin’ to mean to her?”

“She will become accustomed to it.”

“Folks says that about every misfortune they ain’t sufferin’.”

The rebuke was just, though not unkindly offered. I accepted it, and after a moment she laid her hand upon my sleeve.

“I don’t mean you. You’ve be’n real good. It’s only I feel uprooteder every mile. Set an apple tree on the beach an’ tell it there’s plenty o’ room, but what’s it to take holt of?”

We were nearing Shrewsbury now. Farms gave place to villas, villas to rows of close-set houses; telegraph wires multiplied, factory chimneys rose, and at every crossing bells clanged with an urgency new to Mrs. Peet’s experience. She looked from side to side in open wonder.

“My soul!” she exclaimed. “There’s a woman with a red parasol! An’ see them windows—three stories straight up! That must be one o’ them street-cars. Why, there’s another! They’ll run into each other sure.”

Her terror and delight were so mingled that she seemed for a time released from grief. She asked the name of every building, read signs aloud, and marveled at a florist’s window flashing briefly beside the track.

“All them flowers, an’ snow hardly off the ground! Mebbe folks can contrive more’n I give ’em credit for.”

As the train slowed, anxiety returned. She fastened her bonnet, gathered her books, counted the bundle and umbrella twice, and peered down the platform.

“I don’t see ’em,” she said. “They’ve forgot. Or Ellen’s sick. Or Martha’s husband says there ain’t room.”

Then two women came hurrying beside the train. One was stout and rosy, with a little boy clinging to her hand; the other, taller and plainly dressed, searched every car window with a face sharpened by concern.

“There they be!” cried Mrs. Peet.

She rose too quickly and would have fallen if I had not steadied her. The younger woman saw her and began waving both arms.

“Aunt Mary! Aunt Mary!”

Mrs. Peet stood motionless. Her mouth trembled, though no tears came. When the train stopped, the women entered almost before the passengers could leave. Martha put both arms around her; Ellen took the bundle as if it were something precious; and the little boy, after staring at the basket, asked whether the cat could sleep in his bed.

“Not if you aim to keep your features,” said Mrs. Peet, with a broken laugh.

There was no mistaking her welcome. I saw the dreadful strain go out of her face, not wholly, but enough to show what it had cost her to maintain her courage.

“This lady’s kep’ me from losin’ myself entire,” she told her nieces. “We’ve be’n travelin’ faster’n sin, an’ Mis’ Trimble’s tried to leave the cars without waitin’ for a station.”

They thanked me warmly. The little procession moved away: Ellen carrying the household relics, Martha supporting the old woman, the boy guarding the cat, and a porter following with the trunk. Mrs. Peet looked back once.

“I’ll earn my keep!” she called, as if I must be assured of it.

That had been the phrase to which she clung. It was her last defense against the world.

Winter passed before I heard of her again. In April I met Ellen Bell in a Shrewsbury shop and asked at once for Mrs. Peet.

“You wouldn’t know Aunt Mary,” she said. “She thinks Isaiah did her a favor.”

The change, it appeared, had been as complete as it was unexpected. For two weeks Mrs. Peet had risen before dawn, polished an already shining stove, baked more bread than the family could eat, and pursued dust with an energy embarrassing to her nieces. Then Martha, with affectionate firmness, had forbidden work before six and insisted that Aunt Mary attend a church concert.

That evening altered the scale of existence. Mrs. Peet heard a violin for the first time. She sat forward through the whole performance with her hands clenched, and afterward declared that if King David’s harp was any better, heaven had privileges indeed.

She went next to a lecture on the Arctic regions, though she disapproved of explorers who left good homes merely to freeze. She attended a stereopticon exhibition of Italian cities, a temperance meeting, two school exercises, and a talk on household sanitation at which, according to Ellen, she contradicted the speaker concerning the proper airing of feather beds. The children adored her stories; the neighbors sought her recipes; and Mis’ Trimble took possession of a woodshed where she terrorized a prosperous race of town mice.

“She says she never knew there was so much going on,” Ellen told me. “She has made friends in every street. We couldn’t spare her now.”

I was glad, with that peculiar gladness which carries within it an accusation against the years that have gone before. Mrs. Peet had not required luxury. A little companionship, a little music, and the sight of other lives had been enough to quicken her. For more than seventy years her world had lain within the distance between well, barn, stove, and bed. Isaiah’s treachery had broken that narrow circle, though no good consequence could cleanse the act itself.

In early May I had occasion to pass through East Wilby and drove by the Peet farm. Isaiah had not improved it. A broken harrow stood in the yard, one shutter hung loose, and last year’s burdocks rattled beside the barn. The pucker-pear tree was in bloom, extravagant and white against the weather-darkened house. I thought how eagerly Mrs. Peet would have noted its blossoms and how little she would now have wanted its fruit.

A week later I saw Isaiah at the undertaker’s door in Shrewsbury. He wore a new black coat and carried his hat with the importance of a man conducting public business.

“You’ve heard about Aunt Mary?” he asked. “Stroke took her Tuesday. Never spoke afterward. Funeral’s tomorrow.”

I had not heard.

“She was gettin’ pretty old,” he continued. “Couldn’t expect much. I’m seein’ to things, bein’ the nearest man relative. The Bells wanted extras, but I told ’em a plain coffin was what Aunt Mary would favor. She never had expensive tastes.”

His smoothness filled me with a cold anger.

“She had a few happy months here,” I said.

“So I understand. Worked out better’n folks claimed it would.” He glanced toward the undertaker’s window. “There was considerable talk about that farm, but no sense to it. Land’s poor. Buildings all run down. Mortgage near eats the value. Uncle Job knew what he was signin’. I done more for them two than anybody knows.”

He spoke briskly, as men do who have repeated a lie until its edges no longer hurt the mouth.

“Did Mrs. Peet forgive you?” I asked.

He looked surprised.

“Nothing to forgive. Why, she told Ellen I’d done her a favor.”

That was his absolution, and he accepted it without perceiving the judgment hidden within.

I went to the funeral. The church was nearly full—not with Mrs. Peet’s old neighbors, who had known her chiefly as a tired woman passing from one task to another, but with the new friends of her brief freedom. The violinist from the concert played a simple hymn. Three children from Martha’s household cried without restraint. Ellen had placed the one-eared china lamb among the flowers, where it looked both foolish and faithful.

Isaiah sat in the front pew, respectable and dry-eyed.

Afterward, as the spring rain began softly over Shrewsbury, I found myself thinking less of the coffin than of the railway carriage: the old bonnet trembling with speed, the geography upon her lap, the goose-wing escaping down the aisle, and the gray cat clinging to the seat as the unknown world closed around her.

I wondered what became of Mis’ Trimble, though I might easily have asked. I wondered whether the flatiron holders were used, whether anyone kept the almanac with the brindle cow’s date, and whether the red handkerchief bundle was tied once more and put away. Such things survive us without explaining us. Yet sometimes they preserve, in their poor dumb fashion, the warmth of a hand and the shape of a life.

Mrs. Peet had been uprooted; but for one short season, in strange soil, she had flowered.

### The Black Chamber

I was eight-and-twenty when I went to Blackthorn Castle, and of that age when a man supposes the universe obliged to behave according to rules he has lately learned. I had read of apparitions, prophetic dreams, corpse-lights, rappings, and all the dreary furniture of superstition; I had even investigated two haunted houses, in one of which the ghost proved to be rats behind a wainscot, and in the other a consumptive gardener who wandered at night to conceal his drinking. These triumphs had left me intolerably confident.

Thus, when Sir Edmund Vale offered me the use of the Black Chamber for a night, I accepted with more amusement than courage. Courage, as I have since understood, presupposes fear; and I had none.

The castle stood upon a low eminence above marshy country, with no village nearer than five miles. I arrived after sunset in a hired chaise, under a sky the colour of tarnished silver. Blackthorn appeared first as an irregular darkness against the west, then as a multitude of steep roofs, chimneys, and broken battlements. The bare trees about it writhed in the wind, and the moat, long since drained, was full of nettles and pale reeds that whispered together.

My driver would not wait.

“You have told them I am expected?” said I.

He gathered the reins without looking at the gate.

“They know,” he muttered. “They always know when one is expected there.”

This rustic absurdity pleased me. I laughed, paid him, and watched his lamps dwindle down the avenue. Their disappearance left me in a silence so complete that I heard the soft descent of moisture from the trees.

The bell beside the gate hung from the mouth of a stone beast. I pulled it, and somewhere in the depths of the castle an iron clamour awoke, repeated itself, and died by slow degrees. Presently bolts were withdrawn. The door opened no wider than the length of a hand, and an old man peered at me through the interval.

His face was of a yellowish pallor, and one inflamed eye showed beneath a green shade. The other regarded me with a watery suspicion.

“You are the gentleman,” he said.

“I am the gentleman who is to sleep in the Black Chamber.”

He opened the door.

Behind him stood a second man, older even than the first, with a spine so bent that his head seemed thrust forward from his chest. His left arm was withered and drawn against his body, while the right held a candlestick. Beyond both, seated close to a low fire, was a woman in a black dress. She had thin white hair, a bloodless face, and hands like bundles of ivory twigs. None of them greeted me.

“This night,” said the bent man.

“Yes, this night,” I replied. “Unless the spectre keeps a calendar and would prefer Thursday.”

The woman raised her eyes. Their fixed pallor made them seem blind, though I knew at once that they saw me.

“You make light,” she said.

“I intend to make a great deal of light.”

The man with the shaded eye closed the door and shot the bolts home.

“It is your choosing,” said he.

“Entirely.”

“Your own choosing,” repeated the bent man.

The woman leaned towards the fire. “And this night of all nights.”

There was something mechanical in their repetition, as though long service in that decaying house had reduced speech to a few worn phrases. Yet their deformities, their silence, and the enormous shadows they cast upon the walls gave their warning an unpleasant gravity. The bent man’s shadow crouched above him like a black ape; the woman’s nodded upon the ceiling; the shaded eye of the other glittered red in the firelight.

I warmed my hands and spoke briskly.

“Sir Edmund informed me that candles would be provided, and that the chimney is sound.”

“The chimney is sound,” said the first man.

“And the door locks?”

“The door locks.”

“Then I require only the key and someone to show me the way.”

At this they exchanged looks. The old woman’s mouth opened, revealing two long yellow teeth.

“No one goes into the north wing after eleven.”

“It is not yet eleven.”

“No one returns from it after eleven,” said the bent man.

“Then you may leave me at the door.”

A log settled in the grate with a sigh, sending up a flight of sparks. All three watched them extinguish upon the hearth.

At last the man with the eye shade took a ring of keys from his belt. “The passage is long,” he said. “There are turns. Keep to the runner until you reach the gallery. Do not take the stair that descends.”

“Whither does it lead?”

“To the old chapel.”

“And from there?”

“Lower.”

His manner invited no further inquiry.

They gave me two candles and a little brass lamp. As I lit the latter, the bent man caught my sleeve with his sound hand. His fingers had astonishing strength.

“If you hear a child laughing,” he whispered, “do not open the inner door.”

“There is an inner door?”

“There are many doors there.”

The man with the eye shade struck his hand away.

“Do not fill his head,” he said. “The room needs no help from us.”

This was the most sensible remark any of them had made, and I repeated it as I left them. Their voices followed me from the hall.

“His own choosing.”

“This night of all nights.”

Then a door closed, and I was alone.

The corridor beyond the hall had a stone floor crossed by a threadbare red runner. My lamp lit oak panelling upon either side and the lower portions of ancestral portraits above it. Faces emerged as I passed: a powdered woman with a little cruel mouth; a soldier whose cuirass shone like wet bone; a judge in scarlet robes, one hand laid upon a skull. Their painted eyes, constructed according to the familiar trick of perspective, appeared to follow me.

At the first turning I mistook a dark figure for a crouching man. I stopped, and my heart gave one unnecessary beat. It was a bronze Moor holding an empty dish. A little farther on something white moved behind glass; this proved to be a porcelain shepherdess revolving slowly upon the bent spring of a clock. I smiled at these betrayals of the senses. The mind, I reflected, does not perceive the world; it guesses at it, and in darkness guesses badly.

Beyond the portraits a spiral stair wound upward. The steps were hollowed in their centres, and the walls sweated with cold. My footfalls ran ahead of me and came back altered, so that once I paused, fancying another person climbed below. When I ceased to move, the sound ceased also.

At the top was a gallery open on one side to a great hall. Moonlight entered through high windows and lay in long white shapes upon the floor. Torn banners hung motionless from the rafters. Suits of armour stood between the windows, their visors lowered, their iron hands resting upon swords. Here the wind made itself heard—not as a continuous sound, but in separate low breaths that entered I knew not where, travelled through the timber, and faded under the roof.

I crossed the gallery and found the north passage. Its carpet had rotted away, exposing black boards. The walls were covered with faded hunting scenes in which red-coated riders pursued a stag through a forest nearly as dark as the corridor itself. At the end, three shallow steps led to a door bound with iron.

The key turned heavily. The lock yielded with a report like a pistol shot.

I entered the Black Chamber and held my lamp high.

It was larger than I had expected, perhaps forty feet from end to end, and furnished in a sombre fashion that justified its name. The walls were panelled in black oak almost to the ceiling; above the panelling ran a frieze of faded crimson. Heavy red curtains enclosed the windows and the canopied bed. There were cabinets, a tall press, two chairs with carved arms, a broad table, and a cheval-glass so tarnished that my reflection appeared to stand far within it, dim and drowned.

An arched recess occupied the wall opposite the bed. It was covered by a curtain darker than the rest, and for a moment I thought the cloth stirred. I crossed at once and swept it aside. Behind was a shallow alcove containing nothing but a marble pedestal and a broken urn.

“So much for you,” said I.

My voice returned faintly from the ceiling.

I made a deliberate inspection. There were two windows, both barred outside and latched within. The chimney was wide but defended by an iron grating. I looked beneath the bed, behind the curtains, inside the press, and under every article of furniture large enough to conceal a person. The walls sounded solid when struck. A little door near the fireplace opened upon a narrow cabinet containing moth-eaten blankets and a child’s wooden horse, eyeless and grey with dust. I remembered the old man’s whisper, examined the cabinet nevertheless, and locked it.

The entrance door I locked also, leaving the key in place. I then arranged three chairs before it, not because I feared intrusion, but because a physical disturbance would thereby become easier to detect. My method, I thought, was admirable.

A fire had been laid. I put a match to it, and soon the logs burned strongly. Upon the table stood a box containing candles—no fewer than thirty—as well as two packets of matches. I distributed the candles throughout the room: on the mantel, the cabinets, the window ledges, the bedside table, the pedestal in the alcove, and even the floor in those corners where shadows collected most thickly. I lit them one after another until the chamber glowed.

The effect was almost cheerful. The crimson hangings brightened; brass fittings winked; the black panels reflected soft points of gold. What shadows remained were thin and orderly, anchored to familiar objects. I drew a chair to the hearth, poured a little brandy from my flask, and consulted my watch.

A quarter before midnight.

“Now, sir or madam,” said I to the reputed inhabitant, “you have an audience and excellent illumination.”

The fire cracked. No other answer came.

For some time I sat with perfect composure. I had brought a volume of essays, and I read several pages, though the silence made each turn of paper indecorously loud. Once an ember burst and I looked up. Once the curtain over the alcove swelled towards me; but I perceived a draught from the chimney and secured the cloth with a chair.

At midnight the clock in some distant part of the castle began to strike. The first stroke came muffled through walls; the second seemed nearer; the succeeding blows rolled solemnly through the fabric of the house. I counted twelve.

Before the final vibration had died, a candle upon the window ledge went out.

I watched its thread of smoke rise.

“Draught,” said I.

I took a match, crossed the room, and relit it. The flame caught readily and burned upright. As I turned away, a candle beside the bed was extinguished.

That was odd. There could be no common current of air between those points, for they stood upon opposite sides of the chamber. I examined the second candle. Its wick was sound, the wax newly pooled.

I relit it.

At once the candle upon the pedestal vanished.

There is a quality in the sudden extinction of a light which differs from its gradual failure. One instant there is a flame, complete and living; the next, nothing—only a black wick and a thread of smoke. The eye, still shaped to brightness, seems for a moment to preserve the lost flame and then surrenders it.

I went to the alcove and struck another match.

“This is childish,” I said aloud.

The candle flared. Behind me came the faintest sound—a soft puff, almost a breath.

Two lights on the mantel had gone out together.

I wheeled about. The fire burned well. Every curtain was still. I hurried to the mantel, relit one candle, then the other. While I did so there came another breath, another, from different quarters of the room.

Out went a light near the press.

Out went one by the mirror.

Out went three along the farther wall.

“Come!” I cried, with a laugh that was not quite natural. “One at a time!”

I carried the box of matches under my arm and moved rapidly from candle to candle. Each relit without difficulty. Yet no sooner had I restored one than another disappeared. The darkness did not merely increase; it altered its position, retreating before me and springing up behind. A corner would be bright, then, as I turned, sealed black like the shutting of an eye.

I reasoned that some change of pressure in the chimney must produce intermittent currents. I spoke the explanation aloud and found it convincing while the words lasted. Then four candles went out in a row, from left to right, as if an invisible hand had swept them.

“Odd!” said I.

The word sounded very small.

I seized two candles and set them upon the table beside the lamp. A fortress of light, I called it. The phrase was absurd, but I repeated it, and began lighting the extinguished candles from one I held in my hand.

A flame failed at my shoulder.

Another died close to my face.

I felt no breath.

The room had grown perceptibly darker. The red curtains were once more sombre; the bed’s canopy hung like a roof of blood; the carved chairs stretched long arms over the floor. My reflection in the glass appeared to dart independently as I hurried, a pale, agitated creature imprisoned behind tarnished silver.

“This won’t do,” I muttered.

The matches began to betray me. One snapped. Another produced only a red spark. A third lit, scorched my fingers, and fell. By the time I had found it, two more candles were gone.

Then the brass lamp went out.

I confess that I stopped dead.

The lamp was enclosed by glass. Its reservoir was full; its wick, when I touched the chimney, was hot. No draught that entered the room could have reached it.

For the first time I conceived not an explanation but a Presence.

It had no shape. I saw nothing stand before me. Yet the chamber seemed occupied—crowded, indeed—by an intention that was bent upon one simple end: darkness. The thought came upon me with such force that for several seconds I could not move.

Then reason returned in anger. I snatched up the lamp, opened it, and relit the wick. My hands shook so violently that the glass chimney rattled.

“Tricks!” I shouted. “Some damned trick of ventilation!”

My voice broke upon the last word.

The lamp died in my hand.

At the same instant half the remaining candles went out.

Darkness advanced from the window wall like a curtain flung across the room. The bed disappeared; the press became a towering blackness; the arch of the alcove widened into a mouth. Only the fire, six or seven candles, and the matches remained.

I ran now. There was no pretence of method. I dashed from one dwindling flame to another, dropping wax upon my cuffs, overturning a stool, striking my knee against the table. I burned my thumb and did not feel it. My breath came harsh and rapid.

A candle died before I reached it.

Another expired behind me.

Two upon the floor vanished together, and a shadow leapt up the wall.

“No!” I cried. “No, you don’t!”

It is shameful to remember that I spoke to the darkness as though it were a beast. Yet by then it was more real to me than any visible enemy. It watched without eyes, pursued without movement, and took each light with a noiseless certainty. The extinguished candles resembled stars swallowed one by one by a storm cloud.

I flung fresh wood upon the fire. Sparks rushed up the chimney. The flames rose, illuminating the whole room in a wild red glare, and for an instant my confidence returned.

“There!” I shouted. “Put that out!”

The fire sank.

Not naturally—not by degrees. The flames bent low together, turned blue, and collapsed between the logs. A dull red remained, then blackened. Cold seemed to pour from the hearth.

One candle survived on the mantel, one near the door, and the one clenched in my hand.

The mantel light went out.

The candle near the door followed.

I stood in a shrinking yellow circle, staring at the little flame I held. Beyond it there was no room, no furniture, no wall—only an immense black pressure. The flame leaned, though the air was still. It drew itself thin. I cupped both hands about it.

“Steady,” I whispered. “Steady.”

Something cold touched the back of my neck.

I cried out and turned. The candle slipped from my fingers and struck the floor.

Darkness closed over me.

For a moment I could neither breathe nor think. The absence of light was absolute. It had weight; it pressed upon my eyes, entered my mouth, wrapped my chest in a stifling embrace. The geography of the chamber vanished from my mind. I knew the door was somewhere to my left, yet left itself had become uncertain.

I tore at the matchbox. It fell, scattering matches across the carpet.

I dropped to my knees, sweeping both hands over the floor. My fingers found wax, dust, the sharp edge of a match—then another. I struck it against the box.

A spark showed my own white hand and nothing more.

The match failed.

I struck another. For half a second the room sprang into monstrous fragments: the bed like a hearse, the mirror blazing with a distorted face, the black panels rushing upward. Then darkness struck them all away.

I screamed.

After that reason left me altogether.

I sprang up and ran for the door. My shin collided with a chair, and I pitched forward across it. The carved wood caught my coat; I fought it as though hands restrained me, tore free, and rushed in another direction. My shoulder met the edge of the press. I rebounded, struck the table, and sent candlesticks clattering to the floor.

The noise was appalling. Every object I touched seemed to multiply and pursue me. A chair fell behind; the little stool rolled underfoot; shattered glass cracked beneath my heel. I put out my hands and encountered a curtain, heavy and soft. It folded about my head. I believed for one hideous instant that the darkness itself had seized me.

“Help!” I shouted. “Open the door! Open it!”

No answer came.

I dragged the curtain down and stumbled on. My fingers met cold glass. The mirror! Then the door must be opposite. I turned, ran three paces, and struck the bedpost with my forehead. White light exploded within my skull, but the outer darkness remained.

I remember laughing then—a high, idiotic laugh.

“What’s up?” I said. “What’s up with the room?”

I began to sing, perhaps to hear a human voice, but the words would not come. I shouted the names of the old people, though I had never learned them. I cursed the castle, Sir Edmund, the candles, and myself. Meanwhile I moved constantly, because stillness seemed equivalent to being caught.

My hands found a wall. I followed it, feeling panel after panel. Here was a seam—no, only carved moulding. Here a knob—no, the snout of some wooden beast. At last I touched iron and knew the door.

The key was gone.

I clawed at the lock, searched above and below it, and remembered that in my panic I must have turned the chairs over the threshold. Something obstructed the door. I pulled, pushed, beat upon the panels.

Behind me the room seemed to breathe.

I abandoned the handle and ran blindly backwards, as though I might escape the thing by crossing its path. My foot caught in the fallen curtain. I spun about, crashed against the corner of the table, and felt a violent blow above my ear.

The darkness flashed crimson.

Then even fear went out.

I awoke to daylight and the smell of vinegar.

I lay in a narrow bed in a chamber with white walls. My head was bandaged, my body bruised from shoulder to ankle, and each breath produced a pain in my ribs. The old woman sat beside me, holding a cloth. In daylight she appeared smaller and less terrible, though scarcely more human. The man with the withered arm stood at the window. The other, his green shade lifted, revealed an empty socket puckered with scar tissue.

No one spoke until I asked the hour.

“Near noon,” said the woman.

“How long?”

“Since dawn we found you.”

“In the Black Chamber?”

“Across the doorway,” said the one-eyed man. “The door was open. You had broken the key in the lock and thrown yourself against it until the chairs gave way.”

I closed my eyes. Images returned: the narrowing lights, the dead lamp, the cold touch, the room erased.

The bent man approached.

“You saw her, then?”

“I saw nothing.”

He frowned. “The woman in red?”

“There was no woman.”

“The little child?” whispered the old woman.

“No child.”

The one-eyed man sat near the foot of the bed. “What was it?”

I considered my answer. My earlier self would have spoken of deficient ventilation, exhausted oil, damp wicks, nervous suggestion, and concussion. Each might explain a fragment. Together they explained nothing—not because they were impossible, but because the thing I had encountered had not depended upon any object in the room.

“It was fear,” said I.

They were silent.

“Not fear caused by a ghost,” I continued. “Not fear of something seen or heard. Fear itself. Naked fear. The old fear that existed before men had words, before they had fire, when darkness meant teeth beyond the circle of light. It was in that room as truly as cold may be in a vault. It took the candle-flames because they were reason made visible. It took the fire. It took the shape of the walls from me, and then the order of my thoughts.”

The old woman nodded slowly, as though I had repeated a lesson she had long known.

“Yes,” she said. “It has no face.”

“No face,” I answered, “and therefore every face. No body, and therefore it may fill a chamber. A spectre can be questioned, denied, or fled from. But that power enters a man and uses his own heart against him. It needs no dead hand to strike, for it makes him strike himself. It needs no voice, for it turns silence into a threat.”

The bent man looked towards the window. Thin winter sunlight lay upon the floor.

“We warned you.”

“You did.”

“It was your own choosing,” said the one-eyed man, though without triumph.

“Yes,” said I. “And I would choose otherwise now.”

That evening they carried me to a carriage. As we passed beneath the north windows, I looked up at the castle. In the daylight it was only masonry: cracked stone, blind glass, rusted gutters, and rooks turning above the chimneys. Nothing watched from the Black Chamber. Nothing moved behind its curtains.

Yet when the carriage entered the avenue and the castle began to sink from sight, a cloud passed over the sun. Shadow flowed across the road, overtook us, and filled the carriage for a moment.

I shut my eyes like a child.

Even now, after many years, I cannot sleep in perfect darkness. I keep a lamp beside my bed and matches within reach, though I know how poor a defence they are. For light is not merely what the eye requires. It is the boundary we draw against the ancient world, the little golden law by which we assure ourselves that objects remain where we placed them, that doors lead outward, that walls stand still, and that the mind is sovereign within its narrow house.

But there are nights when a flame shortens without cause.

Then I remember the Black Chamber, and the power that needed no shape to follow me from it.

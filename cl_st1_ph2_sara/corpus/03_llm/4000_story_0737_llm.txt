At eighty-five, Amelia Bellowes had outlived three husbands, seven doctors, twelve canaries, and every religion except the next one.

The next one was always best.

She had sat beneath crystal pyramids while a woman from Detroit tuned the planets with a salad fork. She had worn magnetic stockings to attract angels. She had purchased a bottle of Sacred Jordan Water which, according to the label, had been bottled in New Jersey under heavenly supervision. She had fasted on Tuesdays, sung backward on Thursdays, and once spent an entire winter awaiting the reincarnation of Saint Paul in a parlor canary.

But Mr. Thirkell was different.

Mr. Thirkell had literature.

COME TO MARS! cried the silver pamphlets.

FROM MARS—TO GOD!

PERSONALLY CONDUCTED CELESTIAL PILGRIMAGE  
NO RETURN RESERVATION NECESSARY  
THE INFINITE AT MODERATE RATES

The pamphlets showed a polished rocket climbing a staircase of stars. At the top, a golden door stood open, and beyond it appeared a large welcoming brightness, tastefully blurred so as not to offend any denomination.

So Amelia sold her little house in Ohio, her dead mother’s teaspoons, her third husband’s watch, and a cabinet containing the bones of Saint Whoever-they-were, which later proved to be rabbit bones but brought a fair price. Then she took the transport to Mars.

There were eleven other women at the Thirkell Restorium, all between seventy-nine and ninety-three, all dressed for eternity.

They had brought lace collars, smelling salts, hymnals, photographs of ungrateful children, and enough pills to medicate a small republic. Their faces were white with powder and hope. Their hands fluttered like pale poultry. When they walked together down the Restorium corridors, they resembled a procession of mushrooms uprooted by a strong wind.

The Restorium stood on the edge of a Martian desert, a long pink building with fourteen windows, thirteen of them stuck shut. Above the entrance, an electric sign blinked:

THIRKELL’S GATEWAY TO HIS PRESENCE

The word PRESENCE worked only on alternate nights. On the others, the sign offered merely HIS, which Amelia found mysterious but satisfactory.

They waited.

For six weeks they ate cabbage soup in the dining room beneath murals of ascending saints. The saints had been painted in a hurry and looked as though they were fleeing a fire. Every morning Mr. Thirkell entered wearing white robes, silver sandals, and the expression of a man hearing distant trumpets.

“My dear pilgrims,” he would announce, raising both hands, “today we approach the Hour.”

“When?” Mrs. Pinner would ask.

“Soon.”

“How soon?” said Mrs. Feeney.

“Radiantly soon.”

Then he would smile.

It was a magnificent smile, broad and curved and fixed, like the grin painted on a theatrical moon. His dark eyes had the polished stillness of two Egyptian beetles. He smelled faintly of mothballs, incense, and hot electrical wiring.

During the seventh week, the women began counting their money.

During the eighth, they began counting Mr. Thirkell’s lies.

During the ninth, Amelia Bellowes woke at three in the morning with doubt sitting on her chest like a fat black cat.

She stared at the cracked ceiling.

The Rocket, she thought.

They had never seen it.

The brochures showed it, certainly. It was white as an archangel’s tooth, with golden fins and twelve observation windows. But Mr. Thirkell had always prevented inspection.

“The vessel is being sanctified.”

“The atomic choir requires adjustment.”

“The navigation cherub has developed a technical irregularity.”

At breakfast Amelia pushed away her porridge.

“We will see the Rocket today,” she said.

The spoons stopped.

Eleven floury faces lifted toward her.

Mrs. Pinner blinked. “Mr. Thirkell says—”

“I have heard Mr. Thirkell say enough to fill a pillow.”

“He is our guide,” whispered Mrs. Feeney.

“He is a pudding in sandals.”

This was strong language. Several women crossed themselves; one, being of no settled faith, crossed herself both ways.

Amelia rose. Her body unfolded in sections, squeaking like an old hotel lift climbing toward a condemned floor. She gathered her cane and struck the table with it.

“We paid for God,” she said. “At our age, we cannot afford delays.”

The twelve women found Mr. Thirkell in the Sun Chapel, rehearsing a sermon before an empty room.

“And when the great black curtain divides,” he proclaimed, “you shall behold—”

“Mr. Thirkell.”

He turned. For half a second his smile slipped, and Amelia saw the naked machinery behind it: panic, arithmetic, and a desire to run.

Then the smile sprang back.

“Mrs. Bellowes! My dear celestial child!”

“We are going to see the Rocket.”

“Ah.”

“Now.”

“Ye-es.”

He stretched the word like a piece of cheap elastic.

“The moment is not spiritually advantageous.”

“Twelve old ladies asking for their money is never spiritually advantageous.”

A murmur passed through the group.

“Our money!” cried Mrs. Pinner.

“My furniture!” said another.

“My wedding ring!”

“My nephew said I was a fool!”

“He was right,” said Mrs. Bellowes. “But that does not mean Mr. Thirkell may enjoy it.”

Mr. Thirkell drifted backward toward the altar. The ladies advanced. He looked like a magician being pursued by his own handkerchiefs.

“Ladies, faith is founded upon patience.”

“Open the hangar,” Amelia said.

“The vessel is undergoing—”

“Open it.”

“I must insist—”

Amelia lifted her fist. It was a tiny fist, knobbed and light, a toy mallet left out in winter. Yet at the sight of it Mr. Thirkell swallowed.

Five minutes later they crossed the desert behind him.

Mars lay red and empty beneath the afternoon sky. Dust hopped around their ankles. The women leaned into the wind, hats tied under their chins, canes tapping, bracelets jangling. They followed one another in a crooked line, twelve elderly mice pursuing a very nervous cheese.

The hangar stood half a mile from the Restorium.

Mr. Thirkell opened the door.

Darkness waited inside.

He found a switch.

One yellow bulb came on.

The Rocket stood beneath it.

Nobody spoke.

It was not white. It was not golden. It possessed no observation windows, unless one counted three holes covered with saucepan lids. It leaned slightly to the left. Its hull was green, brown, copper, and rust, as though it had been assembled from gutters, boilers, bath fixtures, and the discarded armor of a mechanical dragon.

A name had once been painted along the side. Only three letters remained:

RIP

Mrs. Feeney began to weep.

Mrs. Pinner sat down in the dust.

One woman laughed a single dreadful laugh and then put both hands over her mouth, as if she had accidentally swallowed a mouse.

Amelia walked toward the ship. She tapped its side with her cane.

Something fell off inside.

The noise traveled downward: clang, bang, rattle, crash, tinkle. It sounded like a pantry pushed down a flight of stairs.

“There is no expedition,” Amelia said.

Mr. Thirkell clasped his hands.

“My dear Mrs. Bellowes, words such as no are inappropriate in a universe of infinite possibilities.”

She turned.

“Our money?”

“Invested.”

“In what?”

“The development of the spiritual enterprise.”

“Cabbage?”

“Among other necessities.”

“Our return fares?”

His smile became very small.

The women made a sound then—not one sound, but twelve: cries, gasps, curses, prayers, and the dry little ticking of old hearts discovering fury. They closed around him. Chicken hands seized his robes. Canes prodded his ribs. Mrs. Pinner hit him with a handbag containing two hymnals, a thermos, and a plaster statue of the Virgin.

“You thief!”

“You devil!”

“You pudding!”

That last was Amelia, who had grown fond of the word.

Mr. Thirkell fled around the Rocket. The women pursued him, squealing, wheezing, losing hats and collecting speed. It was a circus chase performed at the bottom of the sea. They cornered him beneath the engine nozzles, where he crouched with his robe over his head.

“Stop!” he cried. “You’ll kill me!”

“We had an agreement,” said Amelia.

“There can be no voyage! Look at the ship!”

“We have looked.”

“It is unfit!”

“So are we.”

“It will never reach Earth.”

“We are not going to Earth.”

The women quieted.

Mr. Thirkell peered from beneath his robe.

Amelia looked at the ruined vessel. The years inside her shifted heavily. Eighty-five years: childhood prayers spoken into a pillow; husbands buried under wet grass; children gone away into their own crowded worlds; mornings when the mirror appeared to contain a stranger wearing her skin.

What waited on Earth? A rented room. A government pension. Nurses with brisk shoes. A bed with rails. Death arriving politely between lunch and supper.

They had bought a journey. Fraud or no fraud, there it stood: ugly, dented, impossible.

“We are going,” she said.

Mrs. Feeney stared at her. “In that?”

“Yes.”

“But it might explode!”

Amelia considered this.

“So might I.”

A nervous giggle moved through the women.

“We’ve got a rocket,” Amelia said. “We’ve got a navigator.”

“I am not a navigator!” cried Thirkell.

She regarded him.

“Then this will be educational for all of us.”

They dragged him aboard.

The interior smelled of mildew, kerosene, burned toast, and forgotten basements. Cobwebs trembled over the instrument panel. The seats were garden chairs bolted to the floor. Someone had labeled the controls with ink:

UP  
MORE UP  
SIDEWAYS?  
DO NOT TOUCH

Mr. Thirkell was strapped into the forward chair with belts, scarves, and Mrs. Pinner’s rosary. He protested until Mrs. Feeney put her hearing aid in his mouth.

The women settled themselves.

Amelia sat nearest the round forward port, which had been repaired with thick glass and pink sealing gum. Beyond it lay Mars, the Restorium, and the small human world of prices, timetables, disappointments, medical appointments, and cabbage.

“Start it,” she said.

Mr. Thirkell spat out the hearing aid.

“You are all mad!”

“Yes,” said Amelia. “But we are paid up.”

“I don’t know the firing sequence!”

“Guess.”

He stared at the panel. Sweat jeweled his forehead. Behind him the women began to sing a hymn, not all the same hymn and not in the same key.

Mr. Thirkell pressed UP.

The Rocket coughed.

He pressed MORE UP.

Somewhere below, an ancient engine awoke with the offended roar of a metal god kicked out of bed. The ship shuddered. Bolts leaped from the walls. A cupboard opened and released thirty-seven spoons into the cabin. The women screamed.

“Again!” Amelia shouted.

Mr. Thirkell struck three switches.

The floor hit them.

The hangar roof approached.

The Rocket went through it.

Mars dropped away in a red avalanche of bricks, dust, and torn metal. The Restorium sign flashed below them—HIS, PRESENCE, HIS, PRESENCE—then shrank to a spark.

The women laughed.

They laughed with their mouths open and their gray hair flying loose. Their old bodies shook in the chairs. Pills escaped from handbags and swarmed through the cabin like bright insects. A set of false teeth floated past Amelia’s nose, smiling independently.

They were going.

Out past the red world, out past the moons like two chipped plates, out where the Sun burned and the stars no longer twinkled but stared.

Amelia pressed her face to the glass.

How clean space was! How vast! Nothing here smelled of cabbage. No clock ticked. No nephew explained sensible investments. The dark opened in every direction, solemn and enormous.

Behind her, Mr. Thirkell whimpered.

A red light blinked.

Then another.

Then all the lights began blinking together, a cheap electric choir singing doom.

“What does that mean?” asked Mrs. Pinner.

Mr. Thirkell examined the gauges.

“It means,” he whispered, “that the fuel chambers are separating.”

“Is that bad?”

The Rocket answered.

It exploded.

For one instant there was a white flower, enormous and soundless.

The ship opened its metal petals.

Seats, spoons, women, scarves, prayer books, and Mr. Thirkell flew gently apart into the black.

Amelia turned head over heels. Mars spun past, then the Sun, then stars, then Mars again. Her skirts billowed around her knees. Frost glittered over her sleeves.

A figure tumbled nearby, wrapped in strips of white robe.

Mr. Thirkell.

He was traveling sunward, turning slowly, arms and legs spread like a badly made angel dropped from a church roof. His mouth opened and closed. Perhaps he was preaching. Perhaps he was apologizing. The Sun waited ahead of him, large, gold, and very warm.

There goes Mr. Thirkell, Amelia thought.

It seemed appropriate.

The women drifted the other way.

Mrs. Pinner sailed past with her plaster Virgin clasped to her chest. Mrs. Feeney revolved gracefully beside a cloud of peppermint drops. Farther out, the others floated among fragments of the ship, their white heads shining.

The air in Amelia’s suit would last only minutes. She knew this because a little gauge on her wrist was sinking toward EMPTY with admirable honesty.

She was not frightened.

Or rather, fear was there, but very far away, knocking at a door she no longer needed to open.

The darkness changed.

It became an immense church, larger than planets, older than sorrow. The scattered wreckage became silver incense burners. The stars stood in their thousands like candles set along an endless nave. The women drifted forward in their best dresses, hats gone, hands extended, a congregation without floor or roof.

Out, and out, and out.

Amelia saw something golden among the stars.

A warmth. A brightness. A vast hand, perhaps, though no hand could have been so gentle or so large. It reached through the dark toward the little company of foolish, faithful women.

Her breath grew thin.

Mrs. Bellowes straightened her collar. She smoothed her skirt with both hands and arranged her face as she had for weddings, funerals, afternoon callers, and every occasion at which life demanded good manners.

The golden Presence drew nearer.

Behind her the Sun received Mr. Thirkell.

Ahead, the doors of the great dark church opened.

Amelia smiled in her best company manner.

“Good evening,” she said. “We have arrived.”

### The Appointment at Dawn

The prisoner stood before General Clavering’s camp-table with his cap under his arm and a composure which, under circumstances less irregular, might have been mistaken for good breeding. His gray coat, darkened at the shoulders by rain, bore the tarnished insignia of a Confederate lieutenant; his boots were Confederate, his sword Confederate, and the mud upon him belonged impartially to Virginia. Only his allegiance was counterfeit.

Behind him stood two guards with fixed bayonets and expressions of so austere a vigilance that one might have supposed Parker Adderson capable of escaping through the pores of the canvas. At the entrance of the tent Captain Tassman, assistant provost-marshal, maintained an attitude of consequence. Having caught the spy, or having arrived immediately after others had caught him, Tassman was resolved that history should not lose sight of his connection with the affair.

The tent was lighted by two candles in brass sockets. Between them lay a map, weighted at its corners by a pistol, an inkstand, a field-glass, and a smooth black stone which General Clavering had carried from his boyhood home. Rain rattled upon the canvas; at intervals the wind pressed inward one side of the tent, causing the lights to lean and the shadows of the men to execute grotesque obeisances.

General Clavering looked at the prisoner.

“You admit your name is Parker Adderson?”

“I have found it convenient to do so, General.”

“And that you are a sergeant in the Federal service?”

“In a cavalry regiment which, I regret to observe, is not at present in a position to assist me.”

“You were taken inside our lines wearing this uniform.”

“Taken is the word. I did not surrender it.”

“You carried sketches of our defenses, memoranda of our strength, and a list of batteries.”

“The list is imperfect. Your people conceal their deficiencies with admirable discretion.”

Captain Tassman permitted himself a short laugh, then instantly suppressed it on perceiving that the general had not laughed. His face assumed the injured solemnity of a man whose mirth has been officially disapproved.

Clavering’s voice remained level.

“You know the penalty.”

“I know the custom.”

“The distinction?”

“A penalty is imposed by law. A custom is imposed by men who have forgotten why they began it.”

“You will be shot at dawn.”

“So I had understood. May I sit?”

The general indicated a camp-stool. Adderson seated himself, crossed one leg over the other, and examined a rent in his borrowed sleeve.

“You take the announcement lightly,” said Clavering.

“I take it as I must. If you had sentenced me to a month’s imprisonment I should have complained; a month is a considerable portion of one’s life. But you propose to take all of it, leaving me no remainder in which to feel the deprivation.”

Captain Tassman again showed symptoms of amusement, but checked them with a cough of military severity.

“You do not fear death?” asked the general.

“Not particularly.”

“Most men do.”

“Most men fear the expectation of it. Death itself has never frightened anybody. No dead man was ever known to complain.”

Clavering leaned back. He was a handsome man of forty-five, carefully bearded and punctilious in dress even under rain and canvas. His coat was buttoned to the throat, his sash correctly knotted, and his polished sword hung from a peg within reach. In battle he had a reputation for coldness; soldiers said that cannon fire affected him no more than weather. Soldiers make such observations with confidence because they cannot see inside a general.

“You reason like a philosopher,” he said.

“I reason like a prisoner. Philosophers commonly have better lodging.”

“You jest because you are afraid.”

“Possibly. Courage and wit are both devices for hiding discomfort. One is merely louder.”

“And when you stand before the rifles?”

“I shall endeavor to stand straight. It is the only courtesy demanded of me.”

“You expect to sleep?”

“Certainly. I have an appointment in the morning and should like to appear refreshed.”

“With death?”

“With your firing squad. Death may attend, if it chooses.”

The wind struck the tent with a broad hand. One of the candles went out, and for a moment the general’s face was lighted from below by the surviving flame. Its expression had changed. The prisoner’s levity, which should have appeared contemptible, possessed an obscure authority. Adderson spoke of extinction as an inconvenience belonging wholly to other people; and Clavering, this man of death, who had sent regiments into batteries and signed papers resulting in the punctual cessation of human hearts, felt again the private terror which had lately visited him in dreams.

For weeks he had awakened before reveille with the conviction that he had ceased to exist. The absurdity of being conscious of that condition did not console him. Sometimes, while dictating an order, he would imagine the world continuing without his participation: the clerk dipping his pen, the horses stamping at the picket-line, the earth turning beneath unregarding stars. The thought produced in him a kind of spiritual vertigo. He could face a projectile, for a projectile was visible and obeyed a trajectory; but nothingness had neither position nor discipline.

“Suppose,” he said, “that death is not the end.”

Adderson smiled. “Then your sentence is less severe than you intended.”

“Suppose there is judgment.”

“I shall present the facts. I was doing my duty; you were doing yours. Heaven, being accustomed to military explanations, will doubtless mislay the papers.”

“You are irreverent.”

“I am condemned. Respectability has no further service to render me.”

Clavering rose and walked to the entrance. Outside, camp-fires glowed redly through the rain. Men moved between them like shapes in a furnace; farther off, a horse neighed, and from the dark came the monotonous challenge of sentinels. The army slept in fragments, guarded by men who would not leave their beats though the skies should fall—unless properly relieved.

The general turned.

“Captain Tassman, when does the rain cease?”

Tassman, startled by this appeal to his meteorological authority, looked into the night.

“Toward morning, I should say, sir.”

“An augury in the prisoner’s favor,” said Adderson. “I dislike being shot in wet clothes.”

Clavering studied him. “What is the last thing a man feels?”

“The last pain, presumably.”

“And then?”

“Then he has completed the experiment.”

“You believe there is no soul?”

“I have never seen one on inspection.”

“Yet in another hour you might wish—”

“An hour?” Adderson uncrossed his legs. “I understood the execution was at dawn.”

“So it is ordered.”

“Then I have several hours.”

“You speak as if they were property.”

“They are at least in my possession.”

The general’s eyes narrowed. Until that instant Adderson’s courage had seemed invulnerable; now, in the slight emphasis upon possession, Clavering detected a seam. It was no more than a thread in polished armor, yet the discovery gave him a cruel and immediate relief. The prisoner did not despise life; he counted it.

Clavering resumed his seat and drew the paper before him.

“Captain Tassman.”

“Sir.”

“Take the prisoner to the west hollow and have him shot immediately.”

For several seconds no one moved. The rain continued its drumming, and the candle made a small sputtering noise.

Adderson rose slowly.

“Immediately?”

“That was the word.”

“There is some mistake. You said dawn.”

“I have amended the order.”

“But the usage—the established practice—”

“You preferred law to custom, I think.”

The prisoner’s face had become colorless. His hands, which had been quiet, opened and closed at his sides.

“General, this is unworthy. A condemned man is entitled to prepare himself.”

“You assured me that preparation was unnecessary.”

“For death, perhaps—not for murder in the dark.”

“The darkness will inconvenience the marksmen more than you.”

Adderson took a step toward the table. The guards lowered their bayonets.

“Listen to me. I was speaking idly. A man says things—one maintains an appearance. Give me until dawn. That is three hours, perhaps four. They can be nothing to you.”

“They appeared valuable when you possessed them.”

“General, I have a wife.”

“Most men have relations.”

“A child—two children. They do not know where I am.”

“They will learn.”

Adderson’s mouth trembled. The wit had fallen from him like a garment; beneath it stood a frightened animal wearing a man’s shape. He looked from Clavering to Tassman, from Tassman to the guards, seeking in each face some accidental mercy.

“I will tell you everything,” he said. “Our strength, our positions, the ford used by the cavalry—”

“You have already told us something,” replied Clavering. “You have shown us the value of philosophy.”

The prisoner’s breath came audibly.

“Do not do this. At dawn—I ask only what you promised. General, for God’s sake!”

Clavering signed the order.

Tassman approached and laid a hand upon Adderson’s arm. What followed occurred with the speed of an event that has long been waiting for permission.

Adderson twisted free, struck Tassman beneath the chin, and flung himself across the table. The candle toppled; the map caught fire at one corner. One guard thrust with his bayonet, but the prisoner seized the rifle-barrel and dragged the man forward into the table. The second guard fired. The ball passed through the canvas, and the whole camp seemed instantly to awaken in cries.

Tassman drew his sword. Adderson, stooping beneath the blade, snatched from the table a narrow camp-knife used for cutting meat and drove it into the captain’s breast. Tassman uttered a surprised oath, as if the wound were a breach of procedure, and fell upon the burning map.

General Clavering reached for his pistol. Adderson sprang at him. The pistol discharged between their bodies, filling the tent with smoke and a red flash. They went down together among the overturned stools, locked in a confusion of arms and cloth. The guards struck with rifle-butts; someone outside slashed open the tent-wall with a saber; rain, men, canvas, curses, boots, steel, blood, and fire became one crowded welter. The central pole snapped. The roof descended upon combatants and furniture alike, extinguishing the remaining flame and converting the headquarters of a division into a dark, heaving mound.

Bugles sounded. A drum began beating somewhere with frantic irrelevance. Men rushed from every quarter, some armed, some half dressed, all demanding explanations which nobody was sufficiently detached to provide.

When the canvas had been lifted, Captain Tassman was found dead, his face wearing an expression of official resentment. One guard had a bayonet wound in the thigh, inflicted by his companion. Sergeant Parker Adderson lay unconscious with his left arm broken above the elbow and a rifle-muzzle against his head. General Clavering was discovered beneath the fallen table. The pistol ball had entered his body below the ribs and emerged near the spine.

He was carried to a neighboring tent and placed upon a cot. The surgeon examined him, said nothing, and busied himself in that manner by which surgeons communicate the worst information to intelligent observers. Clavering’s face was white, but his breathing remained regular.

After perhaps twenty minutes he opened his eyes.

“The prisoner?” he asked.

“Alive, sir,” said an aide. “Under guard.”

“The order is to be carried out.”

The surgeon bent over him. “General, you must not speak.”

“I was not speaking to you. Major, have the prisoner shot.”

The major saluted and withdrew.

In the west hollow the rain had diminished to a thin, cold mist. A firing squad of eight men stood before a low bank, their uniforms dim in the moonlight that now broke between the fleeing clouds. Lanterns cast uncertain circles upon the grass. Beyond them the camp-fires burned with a dull crimson, and the wet barrels of the rifles shone.

Adderson was brought down between four soldiers. His broken arm had been bound to his chest, but every step jarred it, and he cried out continuously—not always from pain. His words came in fragments, prayers mixed with offers, denials, names, military secrets, and promises addressed indiscriminately to God, the Confederacy, and any private soldier who might be persuaded to disobey.

“I am not ready. Tell the general—I was mad. I can be exchanged. I know General Harlan’s plan. Men, you are Americans—you cannot—wait until morning—only until morning!”

The provost lieutenant read the order.

Adderson stared at the squad. His knees failed, and the guards held him upright.

“Do not bind my eyes,” he said; then immediately: “Bind them! No—wait. Let me sit down. I cannot stand. You have broken my arm. A wounded man cannot be executed. There is a regulation—there must be a regulation!”

There was, so far as anyone present knew, no regulation protecting a spy from death by reason of discomfort. The army had provided rules for nearly every human emergency, but mercy remained a discretionary article and was consequently scarce.

They placed him against the bank. A handkerchief was tied over his eyes. He tore it away with his free hand and was struck across the mouth by a guard. Blood showed black upon his chin.

The lieutenant raised his sword.

Adderson screamed.

The rifles made one flame and one report. His body struck the earth, shuddered with mechanical brevity, and was still. The echoes went among the hills, diminishing as if carried from world to world.

In the surgeon’s tent General Clavering heard the volley. By then his pulse had nearly vanished. Those about him stood silent: the surgeon with two fingers upon the failing wrist, an aide at the foot of the cot, an orderly holding a basin crimsoned with blood. Beyond the canvas the camp resumed its customary murmur. A bugle called from the cavalry lines; wheels creaked; rainwater fell from trees.

Clavering looked toward the sound of the rifles.

“He is dead?” he whispered.

No one answered, for the question required none.

A singular change came over the general’s face. The dread which had so long inhabited it secretly was gone. Perhaps the swift approach of death had made death too actual for terror; perhaps the mind, finding no further time in which to fear, had abandoned the occupation. He seemed to listen.

The bugle ceased. The wheels ceased. The dripping trees, the voices, the far stamping of horses—all sound withdrew from his perception, leaving a silence absolute and immeasurable.

“So this,” he said calmly, “is the last thing.”

He smiled with ineffable sweetness and died.

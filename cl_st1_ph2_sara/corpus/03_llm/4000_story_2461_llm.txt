It was finished.

Subienkow knew it with the calm certainty of a man who has watched the river ice break beneath his feet and has already heard the black water speaking below. There would be no rescue from the forest, no sudden quarrel among his captors, no rifle shot from beyond the spruce. The post at Nulato was ashes. The Russians who had ruled it were dead or dying. The people they had beaten, starved, robbed, and driven like dogs had risen at last, and now was come the harvest of that long sowing of blood.

Subienkow sat in the trampled snow with his hands bound behind him and watched Big Ivan die.

This would never do.

Ivan had been a mountain of a man. He could shoulder a bale of pelts that bent two lesser men. He had once lifted a sledge clear of a drift while the dogs screamed in their traces. At Yakutsk he had killed a soldier with one blow of his fist; on the Kolyma he had crossed open water between cakes of spring ice, swimming in boots and fur trousers; in the western mountains he had gone three days with an arrowhead between his ribs and had cursed only because the wound slowed him.

Yet now Ivan was no longer a mountain. He was scarcely a man.

They had worked on him since sunrise, and the short northern day was failing. He groaned in a weak, astonished fashion, as if some part of him still could not believe that Big Ivan had come to this. His strength, his oaths, his old boasts over raw spirits and greasy meat—none availed him. Strength belonged only to the hour in which it could be used. Bound hand and foot, the strongest ox and the gentlest dove were equal under the knife.

The people stood in a broad ring. Some watched without expression. Some laughed. Others remembered.

Subienkow understood their laughter, and because he understood it he feared it more. He had seen the floggings at the post. He had heard the overseer say that one village must bring twice its tribute of skins because the first tribute had not pleased him. He had watched women held as pledges for hunters who fled into the timber. He had seen old men struck because they could not haul logs quickly enough, boys beaten for hiding dried fish, families denied powder and lead until they surrendered fox, marten, and beaver taken through the whole black winter.

There had been warning enough. Warning in the averted eyes. Warning in the empty camps. Warning in the traps found broken, in the dogs poisoned, in the hunters who smiled too readily and spoke too softly. But the Russians had laughed. They had muskets, powder, walls, and the authority of a czar farther away than the moon.

Then, in one night, authority had vanished.

The first guard died without crying out. A storehouse burned. Shots came from the riverbank and from behind the dog sheds. Men rushed half clothed into snow reddened by firelight. Some fought; some begged; some ran. None ran far. By morning the palisade leaned in smoke, and those taken alive were reserved for remembrance.

Subienkow had been reserved.

He shifted his knees where they pressed into the crusted snow. The movement brought a grunt from the man guarding him. Subienkow stopped. He had no wish to be struck—not because the blow mattered, but because he desired to preserve every fragment of himself for the work of thinking.

Ivan cried again.

Why did he not die?

That was the horror of it. The body clung to breath after dignity had gone. It endured beyond courage, beyond speech, beyond prayer. Nerves and flesh had no honor. They existed only to carry fire to the brain. A man might command armies, cross continents, read books, love music, dream of white cities and warm rooms; but when the knife sought the proper place he became an animal twisting against rawhide.

Subienkow was loth to become that animal.

He was not deceived concerning courage. He had courage enough for bullets, for the quick rush of men, for a fall through river ice, for hunger that hollowed the belly through many days. Pain that came in battle was part of action. One grappled with it as with an enemy. But this deliberate dismantling, this slow stripping away of the human until only sensation remained—no. His imagination betrayed him. It showed too clearly what waited.

Ivan’s groans ceased for a space. A child ran forward to look, then skipped back to its mother. The woman drew the child close and whispered something. Both looked at Subienkow.

He lowered his eyes.

Across the clearing, under the black ribs of the burned storehouse, Chief Makamuk stood talking with Yakaga, the shaman. Makamuk wore a coat taken from the dead overseer, blue cloth with brass buttons, scorched at one shoulder. It was too narrow for him, yet he held himself proudly in it. About his neck hung a string of iron keys. They opened nothing now, for the locks and doors were burned, but keys were signs of possession, and Makamuk understood signs.

Yakaga was old and narrow, with a face dried by wind until it resembled smoked hide. Suspicion lived in him. He touched the earth before stepping; he smelled food before tasting it; he studied a man’s words as a fox studies a trap.

Subienkow studied them both.

He had lived because he studied men. All his life had been flight, and flight teaches attention.

He remembered Poland: green fields under rain, church bells, books, women whose hands smelled of lavender, young men whispering in rooms where curtains were drawn. He had been young then and rich in purposes. Freedom was a word that filled the mouth nobly. They had plotted, been betrayed, scattered. Some were hanged. Some knelt in courtyards before firing squads. Subienkow went east in chains.

East, always east.

Across Russia under guard, through mud and winter dust, past villages where peasants stared without curiosity; beyond the Urals into a land widening without end; through prison camps, mining settlements, frozen roads, fever huts; from punishment to escape and from escape to hunger; south for a season, north for two; hiding with traders, gambling with soldiers, stealing horses, changing names. He had fled toward Europe and awakened each year farther from it.

Again and again the wall closed before him.

At the Lena he worked for a merchant whose clerk disappeared beneath the spring flood. On the Aldan he hunted sable with men who had forgotten the sound of church bells. At Okhotsk he smelled the sea and thought the world open at last. But that sea led not west. It drove him farther east.

He crossed in a foul little vessel that leaked at every seam. They ate rancid flour and drank water green from casks. Storms tore the sail; scurvy loosened teeth; one sailor went mad and leaped overboard beneath a sky full of sleet. Then came islands buried in fog, coasts without names he knew, mountains like broken teeth, forests vast and cold, and peoples whom the traders measured only by the skins they could be forced to surrender.

East, always east, until there was no east left—only the immense white and the rumor of another ocean beyond.

He told himself each year that he was gathering strength for return. A little money, then return. A season of trade, then return. A share in an expedition, then return. But money was lost, partners died, ships failed to come, and the trail behind him filled with graves.

He had done things.

That also he knew.

He had not wielded the lash as often as others. He had argued against killing hostages. Once he had cut loose a hunter left tied naked in frost by a drunken agent. These memories had comforted him in better hours. They did not comfort him now. He had eaten the food purchased by violence. He had taken his share of skins. He had stood armed while frightened villagers brought tribute. He had gone with a punitive party after two traders vanished, and he had fired into brush where unseen people cried out.

It had been nothing but savagery.

The word did not belong to one people. It belonged to hunger armed with power. It belonged to the trader counting pelts, to the chief counting injuries, to the exile firing because others fired, to the crowd now waiting for Ivan to die. It was a wall around them all, and every man added stones to it.

Ivan breathed again, a thin whistle.

Subienkow felt sweat beneath his shirt despite the cold. His bound fingers had become numb. He flexed them carefully and forced himself to look.

He must look. To turn away would teach the crowd that he feared. And he did fear, but fear was still private. So long as it remained behind his face, he possessed it. Once shown, it would belong to them.

Makamuk turned from Yakaga and glanced toward him.

Their eyes met.

Subienkow yawned.

It was an excellent yawn, wide and lazy, the yawn of a man tired by a poor entertainment. He saw the chief’s brows draw together. Good. Pride was the cord by which Makamuk might be led. But led where?

There was no escape. The fort’s rifles had been taken. The surviving Russians were bound. Snow lay deep to the south, and every trail was watched. Even if his hands came free, even if he reached a dog team, the villages along the river would know. A lone man needed food, fire, dogs, snowshoes. Freedom in such a land was not the absence of bonds; it was possession of a thousand small necessities.

No, there was no life to be won.

Then what remained?

A clean death.

The thought came without grandeur. It was a practical thing, like finding dry moss before making fire. He could not change the journey’s end, but perhaps he could choose the final step. A bullet would suffice. An axe. Even a knife, if quick and rightly driven.

He looked at Makamuk’s belt, where a short broad axe hung by a loop.

An axe.

But why should Makamuk grant mercy? Mercy would cheat the people of vengeance. It would confess that Subienkow was not worth tormenting. No, mercy could not be asked. It must be purchased—or disguised as something else.

Ivan’s body gave a sudden convulsion. The watchers moved nearer. Their backs turned for an instant, and Subienkow saw the clearing beyond them: smoke lying low, red sun at the forest edge, dogs fighting over a strip of hide.

A memory stirred.

Years before, at a mining camp beyond Irkutsk, a wandering mountebank had sold charms against musket balls. He rubbed grease and soot upon a recruit’s chest, muttered nonsense, and fired a pistol loaded only with powder. The recruit swaggered for a month until a real bullet proved the charm insufficient.

Subienkow’s heart struck once, heavily.

Medicine.

These people had medicines of their own—herbs for fever, songs for hunting, carved tokens against bad luck. Yakaga guarded his knowledge because mystery was power. The Russians too possessed mysteries: powder that turned flame into thunder, compasses, letters, medicines in glass bottles. Makamuk had seen such things. He had also seen white men lie.

A simple lie would fail.

A great lie, perhaps.

Subienkow closed his eyes and began to build it. The structure formed piece by piece, each absurdity supporting the next. It must be valuable beyond ordinary price. It must touch Makamuk’s pride. It must invite a proof so dangerous that refusal would seem cowardice. It must require the axe.

Three blows, he thought.

Not one. If he offered one, suspicion might say trickery. Three carried the weight of ritual. Thrice struck, thrice safe. Yet one blow, if honest—

He opened his eyes.

Ivan was dead.

The crowd relaxed with disappointment. Some went to the fires. Others looked toward the remaining prisoners. Makamuk spoke, and two men dragged away what remained of Big Ivan. Another captive began praying loudly in Russian.

Subienkow laughed.

The sound surprised even him. It rang brittle and clear in the cold. Heads turned. Makamuk approached, Yakaga beside him.

“Why do you laugh?” Makamuk asked in the trade tongue.

“At foolishness.”

Makamuk’s face darkened. “Ivan was strong. Now he is dead.”

“He was strong in the arms and weak in the head.”

“You will be stronger?”

“I will not cry like him.”

“All cry.”

“Those who must feel pain cry.”

Yakaga peered at him. “And you do not feel pain?”

Subienkow smiled. “Not when I choose.”

A murmur moved among those close enough to hear. Makamuk crouched before him.

“You will feel,” the chief said.

“If I permit it.”

Makamuk struck him across the mouth. Subienkow’s head snapped aside. He tasted blood where a tooth cut his cheek. He turned back slowly and smiled again.

“That was a hand,” he said. “Hands are nothing.”

Yakaga’s eyes narrowed further. “What is not nothing?”

“Iron.”

Makamuk touched the axe at his belt.

Subienkow allowed his gaze to follow the hand, then looked away as if the matter bored him.

The chief said, “Iron will make you cry.”

“No.”

“It will open you.”

“No.”

Makamuk drew the axe. Firelight moved along its edge. “Then I open you now.”

Subienkow shrugged. “You may. Then the greatest medicine in the world dies with me.”

The axe stopped.

There it was—the tiny stoppage, the instant in which curiosity put its foot inside the door.

Yakaga saw it too. “He lies.”

“Of course I lie,” Subienkow said contemptuously. “All men lie when speaking with fools.”

Makamuk rose. “Tell the medicine.”

“Why?”

“So you die quickly.”

Subienkow laughed again. “I will die quickly in any case.”

Yakaga studied him. “He fears Ivan’s death.”

“Every man with sense fears clumsy work,” Subienkow replied. “But fear does not make knowledge cheap.”

Makamuk’s fingers tightened around the axe handle. “What medicine?”

“The medicine that turns skin against iron. Rub it here”—Subienkow nodded toward his neck—“and the sharpest axe leaps away as from stone.”

No one spoke.

The idea entered them one by one. Subienkow watched their faces. A young hunter glanced at the blade. An old woman touched the scar on her forearm. Makamuk looked toward the captured rifles stacked by the burned wall, and Subienkow knew the chief was imagining battle: himself advancing while knives failed, spear points broke, perhaps bullets flattened against his breast.

Yakaga spat into the snow. “No skin stops iron.”

“Yours does not.”

“Nor yours.”

“Untie me. I will show you.”

Makamuk made a short gesture. The guard cut the rawhide at Subienkow’s wrists. Pain returned to his hands in hot needles. He rubbed them while rising stiffly. The crowd widened around him.

“Make the medicine,” Makamuk commanded.

“First the price.”

At this, some laughed. Makamuk did not.

“You have no price. You are dead.”

“And the medicine lives only here.” Subienkow tapped his forehead. “Kill me, and it dies. Let me go, and you possess what no chief from the sea to the great mountains possesses.”

“What do you ask?”

Subienkow seemed to consider, though the demands were ready. They must be enormous. A cheap secret was a false secret.

“Three sledges,” he said. “The best dogs. Food for forty days. Two rifles, powder, lead, blankets, an axe, a knife. Six men to escort me south until I am beyond your enemies.”

Makamuk’s expression hardened. “Much.”

“The medicine is much.”

Yakaga said, “He asks things for escape because he has no medicine.”

Subienkow nodded approvingly. “The old one is almost clever. Yet he forgets the proof.”

“What proof?” Makamuk asked.

Subienkow pointed to the axe.

“I make the medicine. I rub it upon my neck. Then you strike me thrice with all your strength.”

Again the crowd fell silent.

The praying Russian stopped his prayer.

Makamuk stared at Subienkow’s throat. Yakaga came close and pinched the skin at the side of the neck, as if searching for hidden metal. Subienkow endured it without moving.

“Thrice?” Makamuk said.

“Thrice. But you must strike strongly. If you hold back, I keep the medicine and call you coward.”

A few smiles appeared. Makamuk saw them. Pride took the hook deeper.

“If the axe does not cut,” he said, “you go.”

“With the sledges, dogs, rifles, food, blankets, escort—and your daughter.”

This final demand produced a roar.

Makamuk leaped forward. The axe rose. Subienkow did not flinch, though every muscle in his body cried for movement. He looked into the chief’s face and let boredom return to his own.

“Your daughter,” he repeated, “or there is no bargain.”

“You die now!”

“Then die ignorant.”

The axe remained above them.

Subienkow could hear the river ice groaning beyond the trees. He could hear a dog gnawing bone. He could hear the blood in his ears.

Makamuk lowered the weapon.

“My daughter goes with you,” he said. “If the medicine is true.”

“It is true.”

“If false, your death lasts many days.”

Subienkow smiled. “If false, do what you can.”

Preparations began.

He asked for a wooden bowl, warm water, fish oil, ash from willow, the inner bark of spruce, and a certain yellow moss from the north side of a stone. None of these mattered. Their very commonness worried him; mystery required inconvenience. He rejected the first bowl because a woman had touched it. He sent back the fish oil because it came from the wrong species. He demanded that the moss be gathered by a boy whose father still lived and whose mother had never remarried.

There was confusion. That was good.

While people ran to obey, Subienkow walked about the clearing under guard, testing his legs. He made no attempt to flee. Flight would expose all. Instead he chose a place by the fire, ordered everyone back ten paces, and drew signs in the snow with a charred stick.

The signs were fragments of Polish letters, church symbols half remembered, merchants’ marks, and inventions of the moment. He drew a circle within a square, divided it thrice, then erased one line with his heel and cursed. At once he demanded fresh snow be brought in a clean basket.

Yakaga watched everything.

“Why do you erase?” the shaman asked.

“A shadow crossed the circle.”

“There was no shadow.”

“You did not see it.”

Yakaga grunted. He did not like this answer because it placed the failure in his own sight.

The ingredients arrived. Subienkow crumbled bark into the bowl, added ash, oil, moss, and water. He stirred nine times to the left and three to the right. The mixture became a foul gray paste smelling of fish and smoke.

He whispered over it in Polish.

He recited the names of streets from his childhood. He named men hanged in Warsaw, women long dead, inns in Siberia, rivers crossed and recrossed. He spoke the Latin of a prayer, then reversed the syllables. To the watchers it was one continuous incantation. To Subienkow it was the trail of his life called back in words.

Poland. Moscow. The Urals. Yakutsk. Okhotsk. The sea. The islands. Alaska. Nulato.

The trail ended here.

He felt suddenly weary, not afraid but immensely tired. All those miles, all that striving south, west, homeward; all the schemes, hungers, bargains, escapes. Fate had driven him like a leaf before wind, and now the leaf had lodged against a log in a clearing beyond the last boundaries of his world.

He stirred the paste again.

Makamuk approached. “It is ready?”

“No.”

He added a hair plucked from his own head.

“Now?”

“No.”

He asked for a strip of red cloth. When it came, he burned it and scattered the ash over the mixture.

“Now?”

Subienkow frowned into the bowl as if troubled.

Yakaga noticed. “Something is wrong.”

Subienkow looked up sharply. “Nothing.”

“You are afraid.”

“Of your stupidity, yes.”

But he allowed his hand to tremble when he lifted the bowl. That tremor was deliberate. Confidence alone might have awakened doubt; fear suggested the medicine had power and danger. He dipped two fingers into the paste, then hesitated before touching his neck.

Makamuk smiled.

“You fear.”

Subienkow drew back his hand. “There is a difficulty.”

Yakaga gave a dry chuckle. “The lie breaks.”

“The medicine must be rubbed fresh before each blow.”

This was unnecessary. One blow was all he wanted. Yet he had to account for the promised three, had to make them believe he expected to stand again after the first.

Makamuk said, “Then rub it again.”

“There may be no time. The virtue leaves the skin after the axe touches it. You must wait while I renew the circle and speak the words.”

“I will wait.”

“You must swear.”

Makamuk swore by his dead father, by the river, by the hunger of winter.

Subienkow pretended reluctance. “And no other man may strike. Only you.”

“I strike.”

“With both hands.”

“With both.”

“All your strength.”

“All.”

“If you weaken the blow, the medicine may turn inward and kill me. Then the fault is yours, and the secret is lost.”

Makamuk’s nostrils widened. “I do not strike weakly.”

“Many chiefs boast.”

A growl came from the crowd. Makamuk ripped off the dead overseer’s coat and flung it aside. Underneath, his shoulders were broad and heavy. He swung the axe once through the air. The blade made a low sound.

Subienkow’s stomach tightened.

That sound was death. Quick death, if luck remained. But an axe could glance. The edge could strike badly. Makamuk might, in the final instant, suspect and restrain himself. He might choose the flat side. He might test with a small blow.

No. The chief must be driven past caution.

Subienkow looked him up and down and smiled faintly.

“Perhaps Yakaga should do it.”

Laughter broke from several young men before they could stop themselves.

Makamuk’s face became stone. “I strike.”

“Then prove you have the arm.”

They brought a thick block of spruce used for splitting fish. Subienkow inspected it and rejected it because it was too low. A second log was rolled near the fire. He tested its steadiness with his foot. The bark had been chopped away on one side, leaving pale wood marked by old blows.

“Here,” he said.

He took off his fur coat and folded it carefully. Beneath it his shirt was ragged and dark with smoke. The cold seized him at once. He unfastened the collar, baring his neck and shoulders.

The crowd drew close again.

By the ruined wall, the other prisoners stared. One understood. Subienkow saw understanding rise in the man’s eyes, followed by horror and admiration. The man began to speak, perhaps to betray him, perhaps to bless him. A guard struck him silent.

Subienkow rubbed the paste onto his neck. It was warm from the water and oily against his skin. He spread it thickly from below the jaw to the shoulder, then drew three lines across it with his fingernail.

Yakaga bent close and smelled it.

“Fish,” he said.

“Do you think power must smell like flowers?”

“It is ash and grease.”

“And powder is charcoal, sulfur, and salt, yet it breaks a man at two hundred paces. A fool sees the pieces. A wise man knows how they are joined.”

This checked Yakaga, for gunpowder was a white man’s mystery and therefore proof that unlikely things, mingled properly, could create thunder.

Subienkow lifted the bowl and held it out to Makamuk. The chief reached for it.

“No.” Subienkow drew it back. “After the proof.”

Makamuk nodded.

“Remember the bargain.”

“I remember.”

“Three sledges.”

“Yes.”

“The best dogs.”

“Yes.”

“Rifles, food, blankets, escort.”

“Yes.”

“Your daughter.”

At that, anger flashed again.

Subienkow laughed softly. “Already you repent. Then stop. Admit before your people that you fear my medicine.”

Makamuk seized the axe.

Subienkow knew the time had come.

The world became sharply beautiful.

He saw each spark rise from the fire and die among the falling flakes. He saw frost shining in Makamuk’s hair. He saw a raven perched upon the blackened palisade, its claws curled around charred wood. Beyond the clearing stood the spruce, rank upon rank, burdened with snow, dark witnesses that had watched men come, kill, trade, boast, and vanish.

For an instant Poland returned—not as a country, but as sunlight on a window, the curve of a girl’s cheek, a loaf opened by hand, rain striking leaves in a garden. He had thought home a place one reached by traveling. Now he understood that it was a place carried until the carrier fell.

He knelt beside the log.

The snow wet his knees. He arranged his shirt so the cloth would not interfere with the blade. His hands were steady. That pleased him.

Yakaga said, “Let another test first.”

Subienkow looked up. “On whom?”

“On a dog.”

“The medicine is for men.”

“On another prisoner.”

“The words are bound to my blood.”

“Then let Makamuk strike lightly.”

There was the danger.

Subienkow sprang to his feet in rage so convincing that even he felt it.

“Lightly?” he cried. “You insult me with children’s games! You think I crossed the great sea to show tricks to an old woman? Keep your dogs, your daughter, your miserable skins. Do your worst. Cut slowly. Burn me. I will carry the medicine into death and laugh while your enemies open you from throat to belly.”

He kicked the bowl. Not hard enough to spill it, but hard enough to make those near it gasp.

Then he turned his back on the log.

Makamuk seized his shoulder and spun him around.

“You will show.”

“Not to cowards.”

“I strike with all strength.”

“Swear again.”

“I swear!”

“Thrice?”

“Thrice!”

“And between the blows I renew the medicine?”

“Yes!”

“Then strike so that all remember.”

Subienkow knelt again.

There was no laughter now. Even the children were silent.

He laid his throat across the pale face of the log. The posture was awkward. He shifted until the side of his neck rested flat and the spine was exposed to the path of the axe. A splinter pressed his cheek. He smelled spruce sap, old blood, fish smoke, and the rank medicine upon his skin.

The earth seemed very near.

He looked sideways and saw Makamuk’s moccasins plant themselves in the snow. He saw the chief’s hands slide apart on the haft. He saw the axe head lower once as Makamuk judged the distance.

Subienkow’s body revolted.

Every nerve demanded that he leap up, twist away, beg, bite, run. Life had its own mind, older than pride, older than language. His heart battered his ribs. The log beneath his cheek appeared to rise and fall.

He mastered it.

This was his last possession: not life, but the use to which he put its final instant.

“Wait,” he said.

Makamuk froze.

The crowd breathed.

Subienkow raised himself on one elbow and touched the paste. He examined his fingers anxiously.

“It dries too fast,” he muttered.

Yakaga hissed in triumph. “He delays.”

“I make certain,” Subienkow snapped. He scooped more paste from the bowl and rubbed it upon the back of his neck. “Now. But remember—you must not fail. If your axe turns aside, strike again at once. Do not let fear stop you.”

Makamuk’s lips pulled back from his teeth.

Subienkow lowered his head.

“Strike strongly,” he said. “Or your people will say Makamuk has the arm of a child.”

The axe went up.

He did not close his eyes. He watched the shadow lengthen across the snow.

It fell.

There was a hard, wooden sound, clean and final.

The axe passed through flesh and bone and bit deep into the log. Subienkow’s head rolled once upon the snow and stopped with the face turned toward the fire. His body remained against the block for the smallest part of a second, then settled sideways.

No cry had escaped him.

For a moment nobody moved.

Makamuk stood bent over the buried axe, both hands gripping the haft. Surprise held him more tightly than any bonds. He looked at the body, then at the head, then at the gray paste spattered along the bright edge.

Yakaga was first to understand.

He touched the severed neck, examined the axe, and dipped a finger into the bowl. He rubbed the mixture between thumb and forefinger. Fish oil. Ash. Bark. Moss. Nothing.

No iron skin. No hidden charm. No second or third blow.

The old man straightened. His mouth twitched.

A young hunter laughed.

He tried to swallow it, but another man caught the sound and understood. Then a woman laughed, and two boys, and the laughter ran outward through the ring like fire through dry grass.

Makamuk pulled at the axe. It was wedged deep. He set one foot on the log and heaved, but the blade held. This made the laughter louder.

“He struck strongly,” someone called.

“One blow!” cried another.

“Where are the three blows?”

“Give him the sledges!”

“And the dogs!”

“Do not forget the daughter!”

Makamuk wrenched the axe free and whirled on them. They scattered beyond his reach, still laughing. Even those who had hated Subienkow laughed, for the dead man had made the chief bargain with death and had taken the better price.

Yakaga looked down at Subienkow’s face. It was composed, almost contemptuous. Blood darkened the snow beneath it, but there had been no long crying, no twisting, no reduction of the man into the beast.

The shaman began to laugh as well.

Makamuk struck him, but that could not end it. Laughter once born belonged to everyone. It passed from fire to fire that night. It traveled to the villages upriver and downriver. Hunters carried it over the portages. Women repeated it while scraping hides. Children acted the scene with sticks for axes and bowls of mud for medicine.

Each telling sharpened the bargain. Each winter enlarged the sledges, multiplied the dogs, made the blade keener and the chief more proud. Yet the heart of the tale remained: Makamuk had wished to take a man’s face before killing him, and the condemned man had instead taken Makamuk’s.

The chief kept his life, his daughter, his rifles, and all his dogs. He kept the blue coat with brass buttons and the iron keys that opened burned doors.

But his face was gone.

From that day, at feasts, at councils, on long nights when storm pressed against the houses and old stories were summoned to warm the dark, men called him by the name Subienkow had purchased with a bowl of ash and a single stroke of iron.

Lost Face.

# The Crystal Within

When Margaret Lee was thirty-eight years old, and had long ceased to expect any of those events which the world, with its inveterate partiality for the young and shapely, calls romantic, she lost in a single winter the house in which her family had lived for five generations, the fortune which should have maintained her there in modest dignity, and almost the last protection between her sensitive spirit and public cruelty.

The Lee house stood a little beyond Greenhill, behind elms which had been old when Margaret’s grandfather was a child. Its red brick had mellowed to the color of autumn apples; its white pillars were cracked but stately; and within it lingered that fragrance of lavender, cedar, books, and clean linen which no upholsterer can purchase. The Spanish mahogany had grown dark under the hands of dead women. The silver bore the gentle scars of use. Even the lace curtains, though mended in many places, seemed to have inherited manners.

Margaret belonged to the house, as surely as the house belonged to her. She was gentle, cultivated, and painfully large.

Her obesity had begun in childhood, when a grave illness left behind it some disorder which physicians named differently and treated unsuccessfully. By twenty-five she had become immense; by thirty-five, prodigious. Her face, though almost submerged in softness, retained beautiful gray eyes and a small, finely shaped mouth. Her hands were white and delicate, but buried at the wrists in billowing flesh. She moved slowly, with an effort which she endeavored to conceal, and dressed always in dull silks, cut with severe simplicity.

People who met her only once remembered her size. Those who knew her remembered her voice.

Camille Desmond, Margaret’s stepsister, had a pretty figure, bright black eyes, a small head, and no more moral imagination than a canary. She was twelve years younger than Margaret and had been petted through girlhood because she was lively where Margaret was quiet, slender where Margaret was enormous, and demonstrative where Margaret’s affections, though profound, were restrained by delicacy. Camille intended, in a shallow fashion, to be kind; but since she could not imagine suffering which did not resemble her own, her kindness was often merely cruelty wearing a smile.

“I’m sure I never notice how stout you are, Margie,” she would say, looking at her with fascinated attention. “That is, not at home. Of course, in the street everybody turns around, but strangers are so rude.”

Margaret would answer, “Yes, dear,” and change the subject.

Camille married Jack Desmond because he drove a racing-gig, wore astonishing waistcoats, and knew how to make an entrance into any room where nobody of consequence had entered before him. He was a large-voiced, small-souled man, shrewd in trifles and credulous in great matters. He distrusted tradesmen over sixpence and trusted promoters with thousands. He chewed gum while smoking cigarettes, possessed a scarf-pin representing a horseshoe set with imitation rubies, and considered himself a sportsman because he lost money on horses selected by other men.

Jack came to the Lee house with schemes folded in his pockets and confidence shining upon his face.

“There’s no sense letting capital lie dead,” he told Margaret, sprawling in her father’s chair. “Property ought to work. Money ought to hustle. This ain’t our grandfathers’ day.”

“No,” said Margaret, glancing at the chair, “it is not.”

She understood very little of oil concessions, western land, mechanical harvesters, or the chain of popular cafés by which Jack proposed to double the Lee income. What she did understand was that Camille’s bills had become embarrassing, that Jack spoke of temporary difficulties, and that refusing him brought tears to Camille’s eyes.

“You’ve got such an awful lot, Margie,” Camille pleaded. “And we’re your only family.”

Margaret signed the first paper for Camille’s sake, the second because the first investment must be protected, the third because Jack assured her that all would otherwise be lost, and the fourth when nearly all had already been lost. There are gentle natures which yield, not because they are weak, but because resistance wounds them more sharply than surrender. Predatory persons, who are seldom troubled by such wounds, call this foolishness and profit by it.

Within two years the bank held the house. The Spanish mahogany was sold in lots. The silver went to a dealer who weighed it. Camille kept a small pearl brooch because she had always admired it, and Jack retained the racing-gig until the horse was seized for unpaid stable charges.

On the morning Margaret left, she passed alone through the empty drawing-room. Pale rectangles upon the walls marked the places where portraits had hung. A little lavender had fallen from a linen press and lay scattered on the bare floor. She bent to gather it, but her body would not permit her; after one effort she stood upright again, breathing painfully.

It seemed a trifling defeat, yet it broke the composure which foreclosure, betrayal, and farewell had not broken. She wept because she could not pick up the lavender.

Then she went out to the hired cab where Jack was shouting at the driver over the fare.

The city flat to which the Desmonds took her had three rooms, walls the color of mustard, gilt chairs, and a view of a brick shaft hung with washing. Camille called it “cozy.” Jack called it “a place to lay low till things begin moving.” Margaret, who paid the first three months’ rent with the money from her last piece of jewelry, called it nothing.

She attempted to support herself. Her handwriting was beautiful, her knowledge of French respectable, and her needlework exquisite; but employers who might have used these gifts could not look beyond the extraordinary body in which they were housed. One schoolmistress, after receiving her with shocked eyes, murmured that the pupils might be distracted. A fashionable dressmaker declared that there was no room for so large a person in the workroom. The manager of a correspondence bureau was civil, but his narrow staircase made employment there impossible.

Each failure diminished Margaret’s little store and increased Jack’s impatience.

“Book learning’s all right in its place,” he said, “but it don’t draw unless you show it off proper. You got to give the public what the public wants.”

Margaret was repairing a torn cuff of Camille’s. She did not look up.

“What does the public want of me, Jack?”

He exchanged a glance with Camille. That glance, half eager and half ashamed, told Margaret that the conversation had been arranged.

“Well now, don’t go getting huffy. There’s a feller I know—Mr. Bostock—runs the Imperial Congress of Marvels. High-class concern. Educating, you might say. No rough stuff. They’ve got a sword-swallower, a living skeleton, the Albanian twins, and Professor Milo, who lifts a pony.”

Margaret laid down the cuff.

Camille rushed into speech. “You’d wear lovely clothes, Margie. Pink satin, perhaps, and quantities of stage jewelry. And you’d only have to sit there. Really, it would be much easier than sewing.”

For several seconds Margaret did not comprehend. Her mind approached the meaning and drew back from it, as the body shrinks from flame before the nerves have admitted pain.

“You mean,” she said at last, “that I should be exhibited because of my size.”

“Not exhibited exactly,” said Camille. “Featured.”

Jack slapped his knee.

“That’s it! Featured. ‘Madame Marguerite, the Majestic Lady.’ Bostock says there ain’t another woman of your weight in the business. You’re a natural headliner.”

Margaret’s face became so still that Camille felt, for one uncomfortable instant, the presence of something finer than herself.

“No,” Margaret said.

But poverty has arguments which vulgarity need not invent. There was no money for rent. Jack had debts which, he hinted, might bring him into unpleasant relations with the police. Camille was expecting a child—or believed that she was—and lay upon the tawdry sofa with a handkerchief over her eyes, sobbing that Margaret had never loved her. Mr. Bostock advanced fifty dollars upon a contract whose provisions Margaret was not permitted to examine closely. Jack took forty-five of them before nightfall.

Thus the last Miss Lee of Greenhill entered the Imperial Congress of Marvels.

Mr. Bostock was not actively cruel. He was worse: he was commercial. Cruelty sometimes tires, sometimes repents, sometimes is ashamed; commerce, when divorced from conscience, consults only receipts. He gave Margaret a scarlet gown spangled with tin disks, a gilt crown, and a raised platform draped in purple. Behind her hung a painted banner which represented her consuming a banquet intended for twelve soldiers. In fact, Margaret ate little and with embarrassment, but truth which does not sell tickets has always been treated cavalierly by showmen.

A placard announced:

**MADAME MARGUERITE  
THE HUMAN MOUNTAIN  
WEIGHT, 712 POUNDS  
A MARVEL OF MERRY MAGNITUDE**

She weighed far less, but Mr. Bostock said the public liked a big round figure.

The first exhibition occurred in a river town on a wet Saturday evening. Margaret sat upon the platform beneath flaring gas-jets while men, women, and children filed past. Some stared silently. Some whispered. Some laughed with the grateful laughter of those who have been given permission to despise another human being. A youth asked whether she slept in a freight car. A woman wondered audibly how many yards it took to make her dress. Two little girls, prettily clothed and innocent until instructed, hid behind their mother; the mother drew them forward and said, “Don’t be silly. The lady can’t hurt you.”

At intervals Jack appeared before the curtain and delivered a speech.

“Walk right up, ladies and gents! No deception, no padding, no mirrors! Madame Marguerite, descended from one of the oldest families in America, is as good-natured as she is big!”

The allusion to her family was his own addition. It drew laughter, and therefore remained.

Margaret endured six hours. She sat erect, her hands folded upon the scarlet satin, and answered direct questions with courtesy. Her voice perplexed the crowd. It was too finely modulated for the booth, too serene for the grotesque crown and vulgar banner. Occasionally some coarse face changed as she spoke, and its owner passed on in silence. But most did not perceive the contradiction. The eye is a tyrant when the soul has not been trained to rebel.

That night, in the railway carriage assigned to the performers, Camille counted the money while Jack drank from a flask.

“You did splendidly,” she said. “Though you might smile more. Bostock says folks like jolly fat people.”

Margaret looked out at the darkness through which wet lamps moved and vanished.

“I shall try,” she answered.

Her submission did not spring from cowardice. She believed Camille dependent upon her, and Jack took care that she should continue to believe it. Their rooms, meals, clothes, and amusements were charged against Margaret’s earnings. When she asked how long the engagement must last, Jack spoke of advances, penalties, and legal obligations. When she requested the contract, it had been misplaced. Her ignorance of business, which had ruined her fortune, now made a chain of her conscience.

The show traveled north in spring. Margaret learned the smell of wet canvas, trampled grass, sawdust, hot metal, cheap perfume, animals, and crowds. She learned that ridicule differed from town to town but never ceased. Country boys tested one another’s courage by touching her dress. Young men offered mock proposals of marriage. Respectable matrons studied her with the grave interest they might have bestowed upon a diseased animal. Once a clergyman’s son threw a peanut into her lap, and his father, after suppressing a smile, reproved him mildly.

Yet there were mercies. The living skeleton, whose name was Amos Pike, placed a cushion behind her back without comment. One of the Albanian twins—both were from Hoboken and unrelated—brought her tea. Professor Milo addressed her as “Miss Lee” when Jack was absent. Humiliation had not brutalized these people; being themselves objects of curiosity, they sometimes remembered that an object may suffer.

Camille, on the contrary, grew cheerful. She bought hats, wore a jangling bracelet, and told strangers that she managed her sister’s career.

“It’s a gift, really,” she said. “Some people can sing, and Margaret is fat.”

Then the Imperial Congress of Marvels came to Greenhill.

For the first time Margaret rebelled with all the force left in her.

“Not there,” she said to Jack. “Anywhere but there.”

“Greenhill’s the best stand on the route.”

“I was born there.”

“So were plenty of folks. It ain’t a crime.”

“People knew my father.”

Jack’s expression hardened. He had lately acquired a checked suit and a cane with a silver-plated knob; prosperity made him less patient.

“That’s exactly why it’ll draw. ‘Local Society Lady Reduced to This!’ There’s human interest in it.”

Margaret rose. The effort took time, but when she stood, her great height and mass gave her a dignity which Jack, for all his swaggering, could not wholly resist.

“If you advertise me so, I shall not appear.”

“You signed a contract.”

“I did not sign away my father’s name.”

Camille intervened, intending peace and inflicting anguish.

“Now, Margie, don’t be grand. The house is gone. Everybody knows we haven’t any money. Pretending you’re still a big-bug only makes people laugh harder.”

Margaret looked at her. In that moment Camille almost understood what she had done. Almost. Then Jack began to whistle, and the moment passed.

Mr. Bostock compromised. The old family was not mentioned on the bills, but the town recognized Margaret before sunset.

Greenhill had changed less than she had. The courthouse clock still lost seven minutes. The druggist’s window still displayed the enormous blue bottle which had enchanted Camille as a child. Beyond the roofs, Margaret could see the tops of the Lee elms, though the house itself was hidden.

She sat beneath the gas-jets and watched familiar faces approach.

Some recoiled. Some greeted her with embarrassed kindness, which hurt more. Mrs. Vane, who had eaten from the Lee silver every Christmas for twenty years, raised her lorgnette and said, “Can it truly be she?” A former gardener removed his hat, tears standing in his old eyes. Margaret inclined her head to him as though she still received guests in the drawing-room.

Then Sydney Lord entered the booth.

Sydney was forty-five, unmarried, wealthy enough for comfort but not for magnificence, and nearly as remarkable in size as Margaret. He had been stout from boyhood and enormous since thirty. Greenhill, having known him always, no longer treated his body as a public occurrence. He owned a small printing establishment, served upon the library board, knew the financial affairs of half the farmers in the county, and could silence a disorderly town meeting by saying, “Gentlemen,” in a certain tone.

He was respected because he respected himself without demanding that others pretend him handsome. There was humor in him, but no self-contempt; authority, but no hardness. His sister Eleanor kept house for him in a square white dwelling whose garden was famous for late roses.

Sydney had heard that the show contained a woman of uncommon dimensions. He had not meant to attend. Curiosity concerning physical misfortune seemed to him indecent. But when Amos Pike, who had come to the printing office for handbills, mentioned that the lady was “somebody born here, and too good for the crew that’s got her,” Sydney put on his hat.

He stopped at the entrance to Margaret’s enclosure.

She saw first a massive silhouette against the yellow light, then a broad face of uncommon intelligence and dark eyes in which astonishment gave way to comprehension. He saw the scarlet dress, the tin ornaments, the painted banquet, and the small white hands folded with desperate propriety. He understood immediately that her agony did not arise chiefly from being looked at, but from being misread.

Their gaze met.

The noise of the booth continued—the barking voice outside, the giggle of girls, the wheeze of the mechanical organ—but between these two there fell a stillness.

She knew that the man understood. She knew that he had his fullest sympathy.

And Sydney, looking past the monstrous costume and the body which the public had converted into a jest, perceived a spirit clear as crystal, wounded but unclouded. He bowed, not deeply and not theatrically, but as he would have bowed to an honored woman in her own house.

“Miss Lee,” he said.

Her lips trembled.

“Mr. Lord.”

A boy behind him snickered. Sydney turned his head.

“You will leave,” he said.

The boy left.

Sydney remained only a few minutes. He asked whether Margaret remembered his sister. She did. He spoke of Eleanor’s roses and of the library’s new eastern window. He addressed no question to her condition and offered no facile condolence. Before going, he said, “My sister will call upon you tomorrow.”

Camille laughed when Margaret repeated this.

“Call on us where? In the costume wagon?”

Eleanor Lord came nevertheless. She was a small, tranquil woman with Sydney’s dark eyes and none of his physical magnitude. She entered the performers’ quarters carrying a basket covered with a linen cloth. Beneath it were chicken sandwiches, strawberries, a flask of coffee, and a spray of white lilac.

“I did not know what would be useful,” she said, as naturally as though Margaret had merely arrived upon a visit. “Sydney hopes you will dine with us Monday, when the exhibition is closed.”

Mr. Bostock objected; Sydney persuaded him. Jack objected; Eleanor did not appear to hear. On Monday Margaret entered the Lord house and found a wide armed chair placed near an open window, strong without being ugly and comfortable without advertisement. No one mentioned it. Such foresight is one of the subtlest forms of love.

The room contained books, old walnut, fresh flowers, and polished brass. It was not the Lee drawing-room, nor did it imitate one; but everything in it had been chosen for use, retained through affection, and cared for with piety. Margaret’s starved sensibilities opened painfully to its peace.

At dinner Sydney spoke of books. Eleanor spoke of neighbors. Neither urged Margaret to eat, neither watched her plate, neither manufactured gaiety. When Sydney asked her opinion of a biography recently published, he listened to her answer as if the answer mattered. She had not been listened to in years.

Afterward, in the garden, they walked slowly. Sydney’s pace matched hers without condescension, for it was naturally little quicker.

“There are,” he said, “advantages in being obliged to proceed deliberately. One sees what the agile miss.”

Margaret smiled. It was the first uncommanded smile he had seen upon her face.

“And what have you seen, Mr. Lord?”

“Many persons running rapidly in the wrong direction.”

She laughed then, softly. From the veranda Eleanor heard and turned away, because happiness, when it returns timidly to one long deprived of it, should not be stared at either.

The following week was a strange island of dignity in Margaret’s ignominy. Each morning Eleanor came for her. Mr. Bostock, influenced by Sydney’s commanding formality and by certain printing debts, permitted Margaret to spend the hours before the show at the Lord house. Sydney did not question her until the last afternoon.

They sat in the library, where rain moved gently against the windows.

“You wish to leave them,” he said.

It was not a question, and therefore required no painful confession.

“Yes.”

“Why do you not?”

She explained the contract, the advance, Camille’s dependence, Jack’s threats, and her own ignorance of what sum might release her. Sydney’s face remained composed, but anger gathered beneath the composure like fire under iron.

“May I examine the contract?”

“Jack says it is lost.”

“It is not lost.”

He found it that evening in Mr. Bostock’s iron box. Jack had signed as witness and unofficial manager; Margaret had bound herself for a full season, with penalties so outrageous that no honest court would have enforced them. But a lawsuit would require time, publicity, physical travel, and money. More dangerously, Jack had taken advances upon the next three months and spent them. Mr. Bostock, though not eager for legal scrutiny, knew precisely how helpless shame may make a gentlewoman.

Sydney offered to repay the advances and more. Bostock refused. Margaret was his principal attraction. The Greenhill engagement had surpassed every expectation.

“Buy out the skeleton if you want,” he said. “Madame’s business.”

Sydney named a larger sum, nearly all his ready capital.

“No.”

“Then I shall expose the circumstances under which that agreement was obtained.”

“Go ahead. Newspapers’ll love it. ‘Real Lady in Freak Show.’ Free advertising from here to Chicago.”

Sydney’s hand tightened upon the head of his cane. He could have struck the man. That would have relieved anger and accomplished nothing.

“Furthermore,” Bostock added, “Desmond owes me. If she walks, I have him jailed.”

Sydney cared very little whether Jack went to jail. Margaret cared because Camille would suffer. Her compassion was the lock upon her prison, and every scoundrel concerned possessed a key.

There was only one way.

Sydney Lord took it.

The plan, once conceived, had a grotesque simplicity. Bostock had advertised weight, not identity. At most performances Margaret’s face was whitened and rouged until its finer distinctions vanished beneath theatrical paint; a curled wig and small gilt mask had lately been added to increase the air of “oriental mystery.” Sydney was within a few pounds of her weight, and the scarlet draperies concealed the form rather than defined it. If the attraction remained, Bostock cared little who suffered inside it.

“You want to take her place?” he said, staring.

“Yes.”

“In skirts?”

“If necessary.”

Bostock began to laugh. Sydney waited. There was something in his waiting which gradually extinguished the laughter.

“You’re cracked.”

“That is possible. Will you agree?”

There were conditions. The substitution must continue for the remaining twelve weeks. Sydney must appear whenever required, preserve the deception, submit to weighing and advertisement, and make no public accusation against the management. In return Margaret’s contract would be canceled, the Desmond debt discharged, and a modest weekly sum paid to Camille—who, Bostock foresaw, might otherwise make noise.

Jack agreed eagerly.

“He’s got the size for it,” he said. “Face is a bit mannish, but paint’ll fix that. Folks don’t look at the face much anyhow.”

That sentence revealed the whole enormity.

Camille objected only because she doubted whether Sydney could imitate Margaret’s “classy way of talking.” When assured that the new Madame Marguerite would speak little, she became enthusiastic. She regarded Sydney’s act neither as sacrifice nor knight-errantry, but as an eccentric business arrangement by which everybody, particularly herself, would do well.

Margaret was told only that Sydney had discovered a legal irregularity and persuaded Mr. Bostock to release her. Jack warned her that discussion might renew the difficulty. Sydney, she learned, had gone away upon urgent business.

“You owe him a lot,” Camille said. “Though it didn’t cost him what you’d think.”

Margaret went to the Lord house. Eleanor received her with tenderness, but her eyes bore the sleepless brightness of one who has consented to a noble action while hating every pain it requires.

Sydney departed with the show that night.

His first appearance took place forty miles from Greenhill. Bostock’s dresser laced him into a bodice, powdered his face, adjusted the wig, hung imitation emeralds upon his breast, and drew the gilt mask across his eyes. The indignities were petty, numerous, and deliberate. The performers did not laugh. Jack did.

“Well, if you ain’t a beauty!”

Sydney looked at him through the mask.

“Mr. Desmond, your continued health depends upon your silence.”

Jack’s grin weakened.

Then the curtain opened.

“Walk right up, ladies and gents! Behold Madame Marguerite, the Human Mountain!”

Sydney sat beneath the gas-jets.

He had believed himself fortified. He had lived all his life under glances, had heard jokes muttered behind him, had seen chairs inspected anxiously at his approach. But the show was different. Here insult was organized, illuminated, and sold. He could neither rebuke nor withdraw. His dignity, which had always protected him, must be hidden with his sex beneath scarlet satin.

A man asked how much he ate.

A girl said, “Imagine being married to that!”

A laborer reached out and pinched the drapery over his arm.

Sydney’s first impulse was to rise and strike him. Then he remembered Margaret seated there, night after night, with no strength of anger to defend her and no secure home waiting in memory. His wrath changed its object. He no longer suffered merely for her. He began to understand what she had suffered.

This was the deeper substitution.

Meanwhile Margaret recovered slowly in Eleanor’s house. Rest could not immediately undo degradation. A burst of laughter in the street made her grow pale. If strangers visited, she instinctively looked for the merciless curiosity which had become familiar. Yet peace, applied daily and without fuss, performed its healing work.

She arranged flowers. She read aloud while Eleanor sewed. She helped catalog books for the library and conducted, by correspondence, a French class for three village girls. The first money she earned—small, clean, and wholly her own—she placed in a blue china dish and looked at for a long while.

Sydney wrote each week. His letters came from towns along the show route, but he said he was traveling on printing business. He described weather, architecture, ridiculous hotel wallpaper, and books found at railway stalls. Of his nights beneath the gas-jets he wrote nothing.

Margaret answered at length. She told him of Eleanor’s roses, the eastern light in the library, the progress of her pupils, and those movements of the spirit which she had never before trusted to another person. Gratitude deepened into recognition. She knew that he had delivered her; she did not know the price. Yet perhaps some finer faculty guessed what knowledge had not discovered. In his letters she felt fatigue restrained, pain transmuted into humor, and a solitude answering her own.

“You have made a home possible to me,” she wrote once. “Not merely a roof, nor even safety, but that inward home in which one is permitted to be oneself. I had almost forgotten that such a place existed.”

Sydney read the letter in a freight car while rain entered through a crack in the roof. Jack snored opposite him. The gilt crown rolled upon the floor with every motion of the train.

He pressed the page between his enormous hands and closed his eyes.

The season ended in September. Mr. Bostock kept his agreement because the receipts had been good and because Sydney possessed, even in petticoats, an authority which made treachery inadvisable. Camille and Jack departed for the city with the weekly money already pledged to a new scheme involving electric shoe polish. They did not return to Greenhill.

Sydney came home at dusk.

Margaret saw him from the Lord veranda. He moved slowly through the gate, broader and wearier than memory, carrying one valise. Eleanor ran to him and stopped before embracing him, as though tenderness itself feared to hurt. Margaret rose.

For a moment none of them spoke.

Sydney looked at her in her plain gray dress, with white lilac at her breast. The old humiliation had not vanished from her face, but it no longer possessed it. Her spirit shone through—clear crystal, giving out light.

“Your business detained you a long while,” she said.

“Yes.”

“I am glad you have come home.”

Home. The word passed between them with a meaning neither attempted to lessen by explanation.

She held out her hand. He took it carefully.

Margaret never learned all. Eleanor told her only that Sydney’s intervention had required personal inconvenience and grave sacrifice. The scarlet gown was burned; the gilt crown disappeared; Amos Pike alone sent a Christmas card addressed to “Mr. Lord, the finest gentleman in the profession.” Margaret puzzled over it, and Sydney said that Mr. Pike had an odd humor.

But in the deepest region of her nature, where facts are sometimes less exact than truth, she knew. She knew that he had gone down into her shame and had not despised her there. She knew that the compassion in his eyes had been purchased by suffering, not sentiment. And he knew that she saw within his ungainly form what he had once seen within hers: the person unaltered by the body, the immortal self which no booth, placard, laughter, or vulgar hand could make grotesque.

They did not marry. Whether this arose from habit, health, diffidence, or a mutual reluctance to give the village a coarse interpretation of something sacred, no one could say. Their lives nevertheless entered a harmony more intimate than many marriages. Margaret remained with Eleanor. Sydney came each evening to the library. They read, talked, worked, and sometimes sat without speech while the light faded beyond the elms.

Greenhill called them peculiar, as communities commonly call what they cannot classify. Yet children came to Margaret for lessons; troubled men came to Sydney for counsel; and persons who entered the Lord house with curiosity often left it ashamed of having been curious.

The world had looked upon two bodies and found comedy. Each had looked upon the other and found a soul.

This was their romance, if romance must be named: not the hunger of beauty for beauty, nor youth’s bright demand for happiness, but the grave recognition of two spirits disciplined in God’s difficult school. He had taken upon himself her ignominy; she, without fully knowing it, gave that sacrifice its meaning. Compassion spread over them its wings of healing. What was outward remained heavy, awkward, and mortal. What was inward grew light.

And if there is, beyond our noisy booths and painted banners, a country where all things are seen as they truly are, then surely these two entered it long before death: not hand in hand before an applauding multitude, but quietly, in the dusk of a Greenhill room, while one read and the other listened, and between them moved the eternal harmony of perfect understanding.

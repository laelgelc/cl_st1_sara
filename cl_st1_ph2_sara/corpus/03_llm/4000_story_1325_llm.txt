# The Yale-Blue Ribbon

From the sporting page of the Bellville *Clarion*:

> **COLLEGE STAR TO FACE SALISBURY**
>
> Bellville’s management has secured Wayne, Yale’s famous right-handed pitcher, for Saturday’s contest with Salisbury. This move should settle the county championship beyond dispute. Salisbury, whose nine is composed entirely of local amateurs, has protested against the employment of outside talent, but Bellville replies that no written rule forbids it. Wayne’s record needs no introduction. With proper support he is practically unbeatable.

Wayne read the paragraph twice while the little branch-line train rattled through the yellow August country.

At the second reading he folded the newspaper, put it into his pocket, and looked out at the dusty roads, the green cornfields, and the far blue haze above the hills. He did not care for the tone of the paragraph. A man who has pitched Yale to victory before twenty thousand persons ought not to mind what a county paper says of him; nevertheless, certain words have a way of sticking.

“Secured,” for example.

Also, “local amateurs.”

Most of all, “outside talent.”

He had understood that he was going to Bellville to pitch a game of baseball, receive two hundred dollars, and return to New Haven in time for the early football talk. Summer baseball was summer baseball. Many college men played under assumed names, and not all of them played for nothing. Wayne had not taken an assumed name. He had been frank enough about the matter, and had rather admired his own frankness.

Yet the word “ringer” lurked behind the newspaper’s polite phrases.

He dismissed the thought. Bellville had telegraphed for him because Salisbury had beaten it twice. Bellville wanted the championship. He wanted two hundred dollars. That was the straight goods, and there was no reason for pretending otherwise.

At Salisbury Junction he left the train to wait forty minutes for the Bellville connection. There were touring cars before the station, boys in baseball caps on the platform, and girls in white gowns carrying orange-and-black pennants. Wayne remembered that Salisbury’s colors were maroon and gray. The orange and black, then, must belong to Bellville, or perhaps to Princeton men summering in the vicinity.

He became aware that some one was studying him.

The observer was a tall young fellow in flannels, broad through the shoulders, brown from sun, and pleasant of eye. He advanced with hand outstretched.

“You’re Wayne of Yale, aren’t you?”

Wayne admitted it.

“My name’s Rex Huling. I captain the Salisbury nine.”

If Huling expected embarrassment, he showed none. His grip was hearty.

“I’m glad to meet you,” he went on. “Naturally, I wish Bellville hadn’t imported you to trim us, but since you’re here I hope you’ll like the place.”

There are greetings which compel a man to revise himself. Wayne had expected hostility, sarcasm, possibly a public scene. This cordial welcome from the captain of the team he had come to defeat annoyed him by being more sportsmanlike than anything he had prepared to answer.

“Much obliged,” he said. “I’m only engaged for one game, you know.”

“One may be plenty,” returned Huling, laughing. “We’ve heard how you put ’em over.”

A horn sounded. From a blue touring car a feminine voice called:

“Rex! The train will be in before you finish talking baseball.”

Huling turned.

“That’s my sister. I came down to meet Burns, our catcher. He’s been visiting in New York. Come over and be introduced. Dorothy will want to meet the man who is going to break all our hearts.”

Wayne followed him across the platform.

There are moments when the great, absorbing, mutable game of life alters its score without warning. One instant Wayne was thinking of two hundred dollars, a hostile crowd, and the probable weakness of Bellville’s catcher. The next he had forgotten all three.

Dorothy Huling sat at the wheel of the touring car.

She wore white, with a narrow maroon ribbon at her throat. Under a broad hat her hair showed golden—not the pale gold of straw, but warm gold touched by sunlight. Her eyes were blue, her face oval and clear, and her expression combined sweetness with that proud reserve which makes a man wish at once to win a girl’s favor and to deserve it.

Wayne had seen beautiful girls at New Haven, New York, Newport, and half a dozen football fields. He had even believed himself proof against the legend of “the girl” whom every imaginative man expects to meet some day. Now, with a simplicity almost ridiculous, he recognized her.

“This is Mr. Wayne,” said Rex. “He has come to strike us all out.”

Dorothy’s eyes met Wayne’s. For a second surprise shone in them. Then she smiled and held out her hand.

“We must be kind to him until the game begins.”

Her voice completed the disaster.

“I’m not so formidable off the field,” Wayne replied.

“No?” she asked, with an innocent upward glance. “Then perhaps there is hope for us.”

A train whistled down the line, and Huling went to find Burns. Wayne remained beside the car. He knew that he should say something easy and memorable. A Yale pitcher, accustomed to working with three men on bases, ought not to lose control before one girl in white.

He said, “Fine weather.”

“Excellent,” Dorothy agreed gravely.

That left him worse off than before.

Huling returned with a short, thick-set young man whose square jaw and scarred hands proclaimed the catcher. Burns greeted Wayne without resentment.

“I’ve caught some fast ones,” he said, “but I’m glad I don’t have to hold yours.”

“You may find Bellville’s man has the same opinion,” said Huling.

The Bellville train was announced. Wayne’s bag was carried across the platform, and the Bellville delegation—three officials in orange-and-black hatbands—came hurrying toward him. He shook hands all round.

As he turned away, Dorothy called, “Mr. Wayne!”

He looked back eagerly.

“You have forgotten your newspaper.”

She held out the folded *Clarion*. In taking it, his fingers touched hers.

“I shall see you at the game?” he asked.

“Oh, yes,” she replied. “I could not miss it now.”

The word “now” accompanied him all the way to Bellville.

---

Bellville proved to be a prosperous town with a brick hotel, a broad main street, and a baseball enthusiasm entirely disproportionate to its population. Orange-and-black streamers crossed the street. In shop windows were photographs of the team. One displayed a stuffed wildcat with a card beneath it:

> THIS IS WHAT WE WILL DO TO SALISBURY.

Wayne’s arrival became an event. The hotel proprietor assigned him the best room. The mayor shook his hand. Boys followed him to the ball grounds, where the Bellville players were practising.

They were a strong-looking lot. Two had played in the minor leagues, one was a Georgetown catcher, and another was said to have been “resting” under discipline from a professional club. Their amateur standing, like the famous straight line, depended upon the point of view.

Madden, the manager, introduced them.

“We’ve got the batting,” he told Wayne. “All we need is the pitching. Salisbury’s been lucky. Huling is clever, Burns is a crack catcher, and they’ve got a left-hander named Lane who keeps the ball down. But we’ll murder him Saturday. You hold ’em, and we’ll do the rest.”

Wayne warmed up easily. The Bellville catcher handled his speed, though not without puffing, and the players watched in open admiration as his curve broke across the plate.

“That’ll trim Salisbury!” shouted Madden.

Wayne should have been pleased. Instead he recalled Rex Huling’s honest grip and Dorothy’s eyes.

After practice he found an envelope in his room containing two hundred dollars in new bills. He put it into his traveling case. The money had been earned in advance, he told himself, by his reputation. On Saturday he would earn it again.

That evening the Bellville people gave a dinner in his honor. There were speeches, predictions, and humorous references to Salisbury’s funeral. Wayne smiled when required, but his attention wandered toward the hotel entrance.

At nine o’clock a group came in from the porch—Huling, Burns, two girls, and Dorothy.

She had changed the maroon ribbon for one of Yale blue.

Wayne saw nothing else in the room.

Huling brought the party over. Bellville men made space with the slightly elaborate courtesy of rivals constrained by social usage. Dorothy sat opposite Wayne. Once, while Madden was explaining how Bellville intended to score in the first inning, she looked at Wayne and smiled.

The Yale-blue ribbon disturbed him.

It was possible that she had worn it by accident. It was possible that she knew his college color. It was possible—indeed, the thought arrived with astonishing ease—that she had put it on for him.

After dinner the company drifted toward the veranda. Chinese lanterns glowed among the trees. Somewhere beyond the lawn a banjo sounded, and from the village street came the shouts of boys rehearsing Bellville cheers.

Dorothy stood alone near the railing.

Wayne joined her.

“You have come into the enemy’s camp,” he said.

“Rex says it is honorable to know one’s enemy.”

“Do you always obey Rex?”

“Only when I agree with him.”

He looked at the ribbon.

“And do you always wear Yale blue?”

Her fingers touched it lightly.

“No.”

That monosyllable, spoken with a faint color in her cheeks, completed the work begun at the station. Wayne’s heart rose with the foolish confidence of a man who has mistaken one pleasant signal for a whole system of communication.

“I’m glad you wore it,” he said.

“I thought you might be.”

They were silent. The pause was not awkward to Wayne. It was full of exquisite possibilities.

Then Dorothy turned to him with sudden earnestness.

“Mr. Wayne, will you think me very dreadful if I ask you something?”

“Nothing you could ask would make me think that.”

“Will you pitch for Salisbury Saturday?”

The banjo twanged on. A lantern stirred in the warm breeze. Wayne stared at her.

She went on quickly:

“We have never hired a player. Every man on our team lives in Salisbury or has friends there. Bellville has brought in professionals before, but this is different. You are—you. Rex would not ask you. He is too proud. And I suppose it is outrageous for me to do it.”

Wayne’s first emotion was amazement. His second was vanity. His third, unfortunately, was suspicion.

He had known girls who used smiles in behalf of football tickets, dance invitations, society elections, and brothers in trouble. He remembered a pretty sophomore’s sister who had once persuaded a rival athlete to withdraw from an event on the plea of injury. He remembered the blue ribbon, the look at dinner, the quiet meeting on the veranda.

So that was it!

Salisbury had sent its fairest advocate. Her interest had a purpose. The instant attraction he had dignified as destiny was merely strategy. He felt foolish, and, because he felt foolish, became cynical.

“What would Salisbury pay?” he asked.

Dorothy drew back.

“Pay?”

“I’m receiving two hundred from Bellville.”

“Oh! I did not mean—Rex could not— We do not hire players.”

“Then the inducement must take another form.”

Her eyes searched his face. He was smiling, and she may have thought the smile playful rather than bitter.

“What form?” she asked.

Wayne leaned against the railing.

“Two conditions.”

“I am listening.”

“First, you must wear that Yale-blue ribbon at the game.”

The color deepened in her cheeks, but she met his glance.

“And the second?”

“If I pitch for Salisbury and win, you must give me a kiss.”

The words were hardly spoken before the mounting influence of truth stripped them of all cleverness.

Dorothy did not start. She did not cry out. She merely became very still.

The blue eyes that had been warm turned cold—not angry, but wounded. Her hand left the ribbon.

“I see,” she said.

Wayne ceased to smile.

“Miss Huling—”

“You mistake me. I asked for Salisbury because the team means a great deal to Rex and to all of us. I had no authority to ask, and I am sorry I did so. Please forget it.”

“But you agreed to the first condition,” he said desperately, as if the wreck might be repaired by argument.

“I agreed to nothing.”

She turned. Huling was crossing the veranda.

“Dorothy,” he called, “we must go.”

She passed Wayne without another word.

For one confused moment he considered following her, stopping her before them all, explaining that he had thought—what had he thought? That she was acting? That her friendliness was bait? That because he believed her insincere he had felt entitled to insult her?

There was no explanation which did not deepen his offense.

He watched the Salisbury party descend the steps. Dorothy did not look back. The blue ribbon showed once in the lantern light, then vanished into the darkness.

Wayne went upstairs, took the two hundred dollars from his bag, and stared at it.

Half an hour later he found Madden in the billiard room.

“I can’t pitch for Bellville Saturday,” he said.

Madden lowered his cue.

“What’s that?”

“I’m returning the money.”

“Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Then what in thunder—”

“I’ve changed my mind.”

“You’ve changed your mind after taking our engagement?”

“Yes. That’s rotten business, and I’ll pay any reasonable expense. But I won’t pitch.”

Madden’s face hardened.

“Going over to Salisbury?”

Wayne hesitated only an instant.

“If they’ll have me.”

The billiard room grew quiet.

Madden laughed without mirth.

“Well! Salisbury kicks about ringers and then steals ours.”

“They haven’t stolen me. Huling knows nothing about this.”

“Then why?”

Wayne could not tell him.

“Personal reasons.”

“Personal fiddlesticks! Bellville will make you sorry.”

“Very likely.”

Madden pushed the envelope back across the table when Wayne offered it.

“Keep it till morning. You’ll come to your senses.”

Wayne laid it down.

“I’ve just come to them.”

---

Rex Huling received him next morning on the Salisbury field.

The turf was green as an emerald and level as a billiard table. Beyond center field rose a line of elms; behind the grand stand, white tents and refreshment booths were being erected for Saturday’s crowd. The Salisbury players, in maroon stockings and gray shirts, paused in practice to stare.

“You want to pitch for us?” repeated Huling.

“If you’ll let me.”

“But you came under contract to Bellville.”

“I returned the money.”

“Why?”

Wayne looked toward the grand stand. Dorothy was not there.

“I discovered I was on the wrong side.”

Huling studied him.

“Bellville will say we bought you.”

“They can say what they please. I won’t take a cent.”

“We can use you,” said Huling frankly. “Lane’s arm is sore. Still, I don’t like mysteries.”

“There is a personal reason I can’t explain. It concerns no member of your team unfairly. If you refuse me, I’ll leave town and pitch for nobody.”

Burns approached, pulling on his catcher’s mitt.

“Let him throw a few, Rex. We can decide whether his morals are bad after I find out if his curve is as good as they say.”

The tension broke.

Huling held out his hand.

“All right, Wayne. You pitch for Salisbury. But you’re one of us—no star privileges.”

“That’s what I want.”

Practice exposed the burden he had accepted. Salisbury’s players were willing and cheerful, but their fielding had holes large enough to drive a touring car through. The shortstop handled grounders as if they were hot stove lids. The left fielder judged a fly by running in three directions. The first baseman could stop anything directly against his chest and little else.

Burns alone was first-rate. He crouched close, gave signs intelligently, and held Wayne’s speed without flinching.

After ten minutes he tossed back the ball.

“You’ve got smoke,” he said. “But don’t fan everybody in practice. Save some for Bellville.”

Wayne liked him.

They worked for an hour on signals and on Bellville’s hitters. Burns knew them all.

“Carter, their lead-off man, crowds the plate and likes a low one. Jam him high. Dugan can hit a fast ball, but he’ll chase a slow curve outside. Slade’s the dangerous fellow. He bats fourth and guesses well.”

“What does he dislike?”

“Being struck out,” said Burns. “Nobody has found anything else.”

Wayne smiled for the first time since the veranda.

He asked no question about Dorothy. Late in the afternoon she appeared at the edge of the field with two other girls. Wayne saw her immediately. She wore white, and at her throat was a maroon ribbon.

She spoke to Rex, nodded to Burns, and looked once toward the pitcher’s box.

Her glance passed over Wayne as if he had been an umpire’s broom.

That evening the *Clarion* issued an extra:

> **WAYNE DESERTS BELLVILLE!**
>
> In an astonishing reversal, the Yale star has broken his engagement and joined Salisbury. Bellville citizens may draw their own conclusions regarding the amateur pretensions of our rival.

Wayne read it without resentment. He had earned worse.

He would pitch the game, win it if his arm held, and afterward apologize to Dorothy. Winning could not purchase forgiveness. He understood that now. But he wanted at least to approach her having done one unselfish thing—even if the unselfishness had come late and wore the suspicious appearance of a Yale pitcher’s pride.

Saturday brought a cloudless sky and half the county.

Touring cars lined the road for a mile. The grand stand fluttered with white gowns, maroon pennants, Princeton orange and black, and here and there the blue of Yale. Boys climbed trees beyond the fence. Brass bands competed until the air shook.

When Wayne came out to warm up, Bellville greeted him with hoots. Salisbury answered with cheers.

He looked toward the Huling box.

Dorothy sat beside her mother. She wore maroon and gray. No Yale blue was visible.

“Play ball!” called the umpire.

Bellville batted first.

Carter advanced, grinning, and crowded the plate. Burns signaled for a high fast ball inside. Wayne put it over like a bullet.

Carter jerked back.

“Strike one!”

He protested. The crowd roared.

The second pitch was faster and nearer. Carter swung late.

“Strike two!”

Burns called for a wide curve. Carter reached for it, and the ball dropped as though it had struck an invisible barrier.

“Strike three!”

Dugan came next. Wayne gave him speed, then more speed, and Dugan fouled twice into the stand. A slow curve outside brought the expected lunge.

Two out.

The Bellville cheering diminished.

The third hitter, Mason, tapped an easy roller to short. Salisbury’s shortstop charged, gathered it, and threw five feet over first baseman’s head.

Mason reached second while the crowd groaned.

Wayne felt the old irritation rise. At Yale that was an out. At Salisbury it was a crisis.

Burns walked to the box.

“Don’t mind it,” he said. “We promised you weak support and we’re giving it to you.”

Wayne laughed.

Slade strode up, heavy-shouldered and confident. Bellville found its voice.

“Hit him out, Slade!”

Wayne tried a curve low. Slade did not offer.

“Ball one!”

A fast ball cut the corner.

“Strike!”

Another curve missed.

“Two balls, one strike!”

Burns signaled for speed high and outside. Slade met it squarely. The crack brought the crowd to its feet.

The ball drove toward right field. Salisbury’s fielder ran back, turned, stumbled, recovered—and thrust up both hands. The ball struck one palm, bounced against the other, and stayed there.

Out!

The men bawled, the women screamed, the boys shrieked. Wayne walked to the bench with no runs against him.

For four innings the game became a duel.

Lane, Salisbury’s sore-armed left-hander, had been moved to first base, and Bellville used a lean professional named Kerr. Kerr possessed no blazing speed, but he kept the ball at the knees and mixed a sharp drop with a tantalizing slow one. Salisbury hit nothing solidly.

Wayne was better.

He struck out two in the second, three in the third, and two in the fourth. Yet Bellville threatened repeatedly because Salisbury’s defense appeared determined to manufacture danger.

In the second, a dropped fly put a man on first. In the third, Huling fumbled a bunt. In the fourth, the second baseman allowed a ground ball between his feet and looked after it with the stunned expression of a man whose dog has betrayed him.

Each time Wayne tightened.

“Put ’em over!” Burns snapped.

And Wayne put them over.

He used his curve sparingly, changing height, changing pace, studying hands and feet. To Carter he pitched inside. To Dugan he fed slow balls until the batter nearly screwed himself into the earth. To Mason he gave nothing good. Against Slade he shifted constantly, never repeating a sequence.

At the end of four innings the score was nothing to nothing.

Salisbury had made one hit, a scratch single by Burns. Bellville had made none.

In the fifth Huling reached first on an error. With one out, Burns bunted beautifully along the third-base line. Kerr pounced on it and threw to second, forcing Huling by half a step. Wayne came to bat amid a tremendous cheer.

He wanted a hit too much.

Kerr’s first pitch looked good. Wayne swung from the shoulders and missed.

“One strike!”

The next was low.

“One and one!”

Kerr tried the drop. Wayne struck over it.

“Two strikes!”

“Make him pitch!” shouted Burns from first.

Wayne shortened his grip. The next ball was outside, but close. He slapped it toward right. For an instant the crowd rose. Then the second baseman leaped, caught it, and came down grinning.

Side out.

As Wayne took the field, he heard Bellville’s third baseman call, “Yale can’t hit county pitching!”

In the Bellville fifth, trouble arrived in battalions.

The first hitter sent a slow roller toward third. Huling hurried his throw. Lane stretched, got both hands on the ball, and dropped it.

The next batter bunted. Wayne fielded and threw to second, but the shortstop reached the bag late. All hands were safe.

No out. Men on first and second.

The Bellville band thundered. Orange-and-black pennants whipped above the shouting section.

Carter came up.

Burns flashed the sign for a fast ball. Wayne shook his head. Burns repeated it. Wayne nodded.

Carter squared to bunt. The pitch climbed sharply. He popped it foul, and Burns, throwing off his mask, raced toward the stand.

He dove.

His big body struck the ground. One battered hand came up with the ball.

Out!

The crowd’s roar seemed to lift him. But when Burns rose, he held his right hand against his chest.

Wayne met him at the plate.

“Split finger,” Burns muttered. Blood showed through the glove.

“Can you go on?”

“Can you throw straight?”

“Yes.”

“Then I can catch.”

The trainer bound two fingers together. Burns crouched again.

Dugan took his place. The first pitch was a curve outside.

Dugan swung.

“One strike!”

Wayne fired high.

“Two strikes!”

Burns called for the slow curve. Wayne delivered it perfectly. Dugan checked his swing, and the umpire called a ball.

Again the slow curve.

This time Dugan struck, and missed by a foot.

Two out!

The runners moved with the pitch, then scrambled back.

“Two out—play the hitter!” cried Huling.

Mason stood in. Wayne got two strikes, then wasted one high. Burns’s injured hand was swelling. Every hard pitch made his mouth tighten, but he gave no sign of retreat.

Mason fouled three in succession.

The count went full.

Wayne looked toward the grand stand. For one fragment of a second he saw Dorothy standing, hands clasped against the rail, her face pale with excitement.

He turned back.

Burns signaled for the curve.

Wayne shook his head.

Fast ball.

Burns hesitated, then accepted.

Wayne wound up. The runners broke. The ball shot low over the inner corner. Mason swung.

“Strike three!”

Burns caught the pitch, dropped the ball, and fell to one knee.

Salisbury poured from the bench. Wayne reached him first.

“I’m all right,” Burns said between his teeth. “Quit looking like an undertaker.”

His hand was not all right. The trainer advised him to stop. Burns refused.

“Let somebody else catch those curves?” he demanded. “Not while I can see.”

In the sixth and seventh Wayne pitched chiefly fast balls to spare him. Bellville made its first clean hit, a single over second, but no runner reached third.

Salisbury remained helpless before Kerr.

Nothing to nothing after seven.

The tension spread over the field and into the crowd. Laughter ceased. Conversation became whispers between pitches. Every foul brought cries. Every decision produced a storm.

In the top of the eighth Bellville made its strongest assault.

Carter singled sharply to left. Dugan bunted, and Huling threw him out, Carter taking second. Mason hit a high fly to center. The fielder misjudged it, ran in, then back, and caught it against his shoulder.

Two out.

Carter remained at second.

Slade came up.

Wayne had struck him out once and retired him twice on flies. The big batter dug his spikes into the earth.

“Now, Slade!” roared Bellville. “One hit wins it!”

The first pitch missed outside.

“Ball one!”

Slade fouled the second straight back.

“One and one!”

A curve broke low.

“Two and one!”

Wayne tried speed. Slade swung viciously and fouled it over the Bellville bench.

“Two and two!”

Burns called for another fast ball. Wayne shook his head. The catcher signaled curve. Wayne shook again.

Burns stood and walked out.

“What have you got in mind?”

“A slow ball.”

“He’ll kill it if it hangs.”

“It won’t hang.”

Burns looked into his face.

“All right. Put it where I can reach it with this claw.”

He returned to his crouch.

Wayne held Carter close with a glance. Then he began his motion, using the same arm speed as for his fast ball.

Slade committed himself.

The ball floated, dipped, and vanished under the sweeping bat.

“Strike three!”

Slade flung the bat toward the bench. Carter stood marooned at second. Salisbury’s cheering rolled across the field like thunder among hills.

In the last of the eighth Huling led off with a single. Lane sacrificed him to second. Burns, pale from pain, grounded to short, and Huling held.

Two out.

Wayne came to bat.

He had failed twice, and Kerr looked confident.

“Nothing good,” Huling called from second. “Wait him out!”

Kerr’s first pitch was a strike at the knees.

The second was a ball outside.

The third was Kerr’s drop. Wayne watched it break and did not swing.

“Ball two!”

Bellville objected noisily.

Kerr scowled, wound up, and sent a fast ball inside. Wayne turned away.

“Three and one!”

Now Kerr had to pitch.

Wayne expected the straight ball. It came belt-high, angling over the plate.

He swung.

The bat met the ball with a hard, clean crack.

It sped between first and second, touched the grass, and raced into right field. Huling rounded third. The right fielder charged and threw home.

“Slide!” screamed the Salisbury bench.

Huling slid in a cloud of dust. Burns, acting as coach, waved both arms.

The catcher caught the throw and swept backward.

“Safe!”

One to nothing!

Wayne stood on first while the field dissolved into sound. Hats flew. Girls rose like a covey of ptarmigans. The Salisbury band attempted three tunes at once.

The next batter grounded out, but Wayne returned to the bench with the only run of the game driven in by his bat.

“Hold ’em one inning,” said Huling.

Wayne looked at Burns’s bandaged hand.

“We’ll hold them.”

Bellville’s ninth began with a single.

The next man struck out.

The seventh hitter chopped a ball toward second. It should have been a double play. Salisbury’s second baseman fielded it, wheeled, and threw past shortstop into left field.

Runners on first and third. One out.

The Bellville multitude became delirious.

A sacrifice fly would tie the score. A hit might win. The batter was Kerr, the opposing pitcher, who had hit hard twice.

Burns crouched and called for the curve.

Wayne threw. Kerr swung and missed.

“One strike!”

The next curve stayed high. Kerr drove it foul.

“Two strikes!”

Wayne tried to waste a fast ball outside. Kerr refused it.

“One and two!”

Burns called for the drop. Wayne delivered. Kerr struck downward and topped it toward first.

Lane charged. The runner on third broke for home.

“Plate!” shouted Burns.

Lane threw.

The ball came low. Burns blocked it with his body, caught it against his chest, and wheeled into the runner. They collided heavily.

“Out!”

Two out. Runners on first and second.

Burns rose slowly. His face was gray, but the ball remained in his glove.

Carter came to bat.

Wayne’s arm felt strong. His head was clear. He saw every detail: Carter’s fingers creeping up the bat, the runner’s lead, Burns’s blood-darkened bandage, the long shadows across the emerald field, and, beyond the screen, Dorothy standing among the maroon and gray.

He put the first pitch over.

“Strike one!”

Carter fouled the second.

“Strike two!”

The crowd rose as one body.

Wayne wanted the strikeout. Burns signaled for a curve outside.

The pitch broke too far.

“Ball one!”

Another curve missed low.

“Two and two!”

Carter stepped out, rubbed dirt upon his hands, and returned.

Wayne looked in.

Burns gave the sign for the fast ball.

This was the place where pitchers became heroes or merely human. Wayne drew a breath, swung his arms, and drove from the box.

The ball flashed toward the plate.

Carter swung.

He hit it.

A sharp grounder bounded straight at Wayne. He knocked it down with his bare hand, chased it two steps, and threw to first.

The throw was low.

Lane stretched until it seemed he must split in two. The ball struck his glove as Carter’s foot hit the bag.

“Out!”

The game was over.

For an instant Wayne heard nothing. Then the field vanished under an avalanche of Salisbury humanity. Players pounded him. Boys seized his cap. Huling embraced Burns and then Wayne. The men bawled, the women screamed, the boys shrieked, and maroon pennants streamed from every side.

Somewhere in that joyous confusion Wayne remembered Dorothy.

He looked toward the box.

She was gone.

---

The victory dance filled the Salisbury Hotel that night.

The ballroom shone with electric lights and flowers. Maroon and gray draped the balconies; college pennants covered the walls; a small orchestra played beneath palms borrowed from the hotel conservatory. The Salisbury players appeared in evening clothes, each bearing some visible damage from the game. Burns wore his right hand in a sling and received more attention than a victorious general.

Wayne arrived late.

He had dressed with unusual care and had rehearsed his apology in six forms. All six became worthless when he saw Dorothy across the room.

She wore a white gown. Her golden hair was arranged simply, and her expression, while not unhappy, gave him no encouragement. She danced first with Huling, then with Burns, who managed the operation one-handed. Wayne waited.

At last he found her alone near an open window.

“Miss Huling.”

She turned.

“Mr. Wayne.”

The formality chilled him, but he had earned it.

“May I speak with you?”

“Yes.”

“I owe you an apology. Not one made by winning a baseball game, because that has nothing to do with it. I insulted you on Thursday night.”

Dorothy looked down at her fan.

“I misunderstood your kindness,” he continued. “I thought you were pretending an interest in me to persuade me to change sides. That was contemptible enough. Then, because I believed you insincere, I behaved worse myself. There is no excuse.”

She was silent.

“I have met people who use friendship to get what they want,” he said. “I had grown vain enough to think I understood everybody. I did not understand you. I know now that your request was honest and generous—and that my answer was unworthy of a gentleman.”

Dorothy lifted her eyes.

“You did pitch for Salisbury.”

“I pitched because I wanted to put one thing right. But the game does not pay any debt to you.”

“No,” she said softly. “It does not.”

“I renounce the condition.”

A faint light appeared in her eyes.

“Both conditions?”

“The first was harmless. The second was unforgivable. I ask nothing under either.”

“You are very solemn for a man who struck out thirteen batters.”

“Fourteen,” he corrected automatically.

Then he caught the glimmer of mischief in her face, and hope stirred.

“Fourteen,” she repeated. “Of course.”

“Miss Huling, I want to make another offer. No conditions this time. I will pitch for Salisbury for the rest of the season, if Huling wants me. I’ll take no money. And in return—”

“You said there would be no conditions.”

“This is a request, not a condition. Will you forgive me and let me be your friend?”

She did not answer at once. The orchestra began a waltz. Couples swept past in white, black, maroon, and gray. Outside, beyond the veranda, the moonlight lay silver upon the road to the baseball field.

Dorothy’s hand moved to the folds of her gown.

“Did you notice what I wore at the game?” she asked.

“Maroon.”

“Only maroon?”

“I saw no other color.”

“I thought you were supposed to have excellent eyes.”

From beneath a fold of white chiffon she drew the end of a narrow ribbon.

It was Yale blue.

Wayne stared at it.

“I wore it under the maroon,” she said, and now her cheeks were pink. “I had promised nothing, you understand. But I had intended to wear it before you made your second condition, and I would not let your bad manners change my intention.”

“Dorothy!”

“You may call me Miss Huling until we are better acquainted.”

“But you forgive me?”

“I think I began to forgive you when you returned Bellville’s money.”

“How did you know?”

“Rex told me.”

“And the friendship?”

She smiled—the smile he had first seen at the station, but warmer now and meant wholly for him.

“You may pitch for Salisbury,” she said. “We shall see about the rest of the season.”

The waltz swelled.

Wayne offered his arm.

“Will you dance?”

Dorothy placed her hand upon it.

As they moved toward the floor, the Yale-blue ribbon showed for an instant against her white gown. It was only a strip of silk, hidden beneath the colors of Salisbury; but to Wayne, who had that day placed speed, curve, courage, and all the pride of a famous pitching arm against Bellville’s strongest nine, it seemed the fairest trophy he had ever won.

And, being somewhat wiser than he had been, he understood that he had not won it at all.

It had been freely given.

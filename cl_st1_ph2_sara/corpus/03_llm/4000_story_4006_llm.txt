At noon the oval bed burned quietly beneath the July air. Its flowers—crimson, ultramarine, sulphur-yellow—stood on their slender stalks as if each contained a separate, coloured weather, cool at the centre though the paths whitened in the heat and the lawns beyond them trembled. A breeze, too faint to stir a sleeve, passed through the upper leaves; the leaves turned their pale undersides and turned back. Between the flowers lay a country of brown clods, green spears, white stones and cavities black as wells.

Across this country a snail was travelling.

Its shell held a round stain of amber, then bronze, as light slipped through the petals above. Before it rose a ridge of earth divided by the root of a plant, and beyond the root a dead leaf leaned upon one point, making a roof, a wall, perhaps a bridge. The snail extended its horns. The leaf did not answer. Patiently, with the soft muscular certainty of a thought not yet expressed, it drew itself forward.

Along the path came a family of four.

The man walked half a pace ahead, broad-shouldered, his straw hat tilted back; the woman moved beside him in a pale dress, while the children—a boy who struck at seed-heads with a stick and a girl dragging one gloved hand along the rail—flowed and checked about them like two small currents disturbed by stones.

“Don’t do that, Robin,” the woman said.

The boy stopped striking the seed-heads and began immediately to strike his own shadow.

The man had seen the flower bed. Or rather, he had seen a different bed, fifteen years earlier, when the glasshouses shone with rain and every black branch dripped, though this same oval lay there, reduced by memory to three objects: a bench shining wetly, a dragonfly, and the silver buckle of a woman’s shoe.

“Do you remember,” he said to Eleanor, “the first time we came here?”

“With your aunt? When Robin fell into the pond?”

“No, no. Long before that.”

The boy, hearing his name, turned, decided the conversation was not about him, and ran on.

There had been another woman. Her name—Lily—opened in Simon’s mind without sound. Lily in a grey dress; Lily bending over the railing; Lily saying that dragonflies looked wicked because their bodies were too bright. He had carried the words he meant to say all afternoon. They had been heavy at first, then lighter, then almost ridiculous, like a parcel one has forgotten the contents of but cannot put down. At last, beside this flower bed, he had asked her.

A dragonfly had hung over the water beyond her shoulder, blue as a struck match. It moved once, returned, moved again. Simon had believed—why?—that if it settled on a certain broad leaf she would say yes. The creature hovered. Lily looked at the point of her silver shoe. The leaf rocked empty.

“It was here,” he said. “I proposed to someone.”

Eleanor turned her head, not sharply, but as a flower might turn when the sun disappears. “Someone?”

“Before I knew you. I thought I’d told you.”

“Perhaps you did.”

“She refused me.”

“Ah.”

The little girl had stopped. She was staring into the bed, where a red petal, loosened at its base, shivered without falling.

“Come along, May,” Eleanor called.

Simon laughed. “What a fool I was! I let a dragonfly decide my whole future.”

“Did it?”

“It seemed to.”

And the scene, which had risen so clear—the buckle, the bench, the rainy glass—clouded at once. Was the dress grey? Had there been rain? Perhaps the buckle belonged to somebody else. Only the waiting remained exact: that immense suspended second in which the dragonfly had chosen neither leaf nor air, and all possible lives had stood upright together, transparent and equally near.

Eleanor folded her parasol. She had her own bright relic, though she did not at once offer it. She saw a garden not this garden but her grandmother’s, small and square, with hollyhocks against a brick wall and the smell of hot currants coming from the kitchen. She had been six. Or seven. An old woman named Mrs. Plomer had come through the gate wearing a bonnet with purple ribbons. Everybody said Mrs. Plomer was severe; the servants laughed at her boots. Yet when Eleanor stood alone under the pear tree, sticky with stolen jam, the old woman had bent and kissed her on the nape of the neck.

Without warning. A dry, feather-light kiss.

For years afterwards Eleanor had believed that some hidden person might at any moment cross a lawn, bend through the leaves, and bestow upon her that inexplicable tenderness.

“My happiest memory,” she said, “is of a kiss.”

Simon looked at her.

“Not one of yours,” she added, smiling.

The smile restored the path, the children, the sensible white blaze of noon. He touched her arm. The four figures moved away—straw hat, pale dress, blue sailor suit, small red sash—breaking apart between the tree trunks, coming together again, and finally dwindling into the greenery as if the garden, having coloured them, had washed them gently from its brush.

Under the flowers, the snail reached the ridge. To go over meant exposure to the dropping light; to go around meant entering a ravine between two stones, where a thin, silver track, left by another traveller, vanished into moss. Above it the dead leaf rested at an angle. Its veins made a map of provinces without names.

The snail paused.

Voices approached. One rose brilliantly, broke, and rose again; the other remained low and even, laying down small verbal stepping-stones.

“Here, Mr. Armitage. This way. Mind the border.”

“I mind nothing,” said the elder man. He was tall and astonishingly thin, with a white beard divided into two points by the wind. His waistcoat was buttoned wrongly; one hand wore a yellow glove, the other was bare. Behind him came a young man in dark clothes, patient-faced, holding but not visibly restraining the elder’s arm.

“Nothing!” Mr. Armitage cried. “That is the system. Empty the mind and they enter by the electrical passages. Ceylon first. Then Patagonia. Then the moon, though the moon is inconvenient because of tides.”

“Yes,” said his companion. “Shall we sit?”

“Sit? When she is speaking?”

He stopped before the oval bed and inclined his head. The flowers gave no sign, yet the red blossoms seemed, under his attention, to deepen; one blue flower trembled on its stalk.

“She has changed her voice,” he whispered. “In Lisbon it was bells. In Cairo it was running water beneath stone. Here—listen!—a woman folding silk.”

The young man listened. He heard a child calling, the scrape of a gardener’s rake, wheels beyond the wall.

“Who is she today?” he asked gently.

“All of them.”

Mr. Armitage lifted his ungloved hand. His fingers plucked at the sun.

“Maria, with the black hair; poor Jane under the cedar; the Empress who sent me twelve emeralds and then denied it before Parliament. Women are doors, Stephen. Blue doors, green doors, doors painted with stars. One opens and there is a desert. Another, a nursery. Another opens on the sea, and you step through—”

He stepped into the border.

Stephen caught him by the sleeve before his shoe crushed the yellow flowers.

“Not there, sir.”

“Why not?”

“The plants.”

“Plants!” The word delighted him. He bent so low that his beard nearly brushed a blossom. “Exactly. They receive the dead at noon. Each cup is an ear, each root a wire. Your mother knows that.”

“My mother has been dead twelve years.”

“You see?”

Stephen’s face tightened, but only for a moment. Heat had made a dark crescent under his hatband. He guided the old man back to the path.

“We’ll find some shade.”

“In China,” Mr. Armitage continued, allowing himself to be led, “they put messages inside oranges. Never eat an orange until you’ve read it. Promise me.”

“I promise.”

“And send word to Maria that I have not forgotten the red balcony.”

“I will.”

Their voices passed behind the foliage. The younger man’s replies, quiet and repeated, accompanied the older man’s leaps from island to planet, queen to chambermaid, gunfire to violets, until both became merely a rise and fall among other sounds. Yet for a little while the word Maria remained, suspended over the bed like a bright scrap of cloth caught on a thorn.

The snail began to descend into the ravine.

Two women came next, walking with the determined slowness of people for whom an afternoon’s outing is both pleasure and expense. One was stout and wore a black hat ornamented with cherries; the other was narrow, brown-coated despite the heat, and carried a paper bag which had grown translucent at the bottom.

“So I said to Mrs. Bell,” the stout woman declared, “I said, if it’s good enough for Lady Harcourt it’s good enough for me, because what’s sauce for one—”

“Exactly,” said the narrow woman. “Though Ada says they’re not married.”

“Not married?”

“Not properly.”

“Well! And with the curtains new last March.”

“Green.”

“I heard mustard.”

“Green, Ada says. Then there’s the butcher’s account.”

They passed into the shadow of a plane tree, and their faces momentarily lost their features, becoming two pale ovals beneath black and brown shapes.

“Eight and six,” said the stout woman.

“Nine and four.”

“For chops?”

“For everything. Chops, suet, bones for the dog. Of course, she gets her tea direct.”

“Ceylon?”

“Indian. Two shillings.”

“Robbery.”

“Pure robbery. And Nell’s boy’s taken up with a girl in haberdashery. Ribbons, buttons, elastic, that sort of thing.”

“Nothing comes of that.”

“Nothing ever does.”

Names, prices, ailments, tram routes, stains, funerals and bargains clicked between them like beads shaken in a box: Ada, Nell, Mrs. Bell, George; sixpence off; poor liver; mauve ribbon; Tuesday week; no sugar; twins again. But when they reached the flower bed, the narrow woman stopped.

A purple flower had opened towards her. Its petals were streaked with white so delicately that the colour appeared to be breathing through them. At the bottom of the cup lay a darkness sprinkled with yellow dust.

“Well?” said her friend.

The narrow woman did not answer.

She had forgotten Ada. She had forgotten the damp paper bag and the price of tea. The flower’s purple entered her—not as a thought, not as a name, but as a passage down which she seemed to fall very slowly. There had once been a dress that colour. Or a sky. Or nothing she had ever possessed, only something awaited. The white streak on the petal widened into a road over hills; the yellow grains became lamps in windows seen at dusk; and she stood, with her gloved hands clasped over the greasy bag, gazing into a country no larger than a teacup and farther away than youth.

“Ethel?”

“Yes.”

“You’re staring.”

“It’s pretty.”

“That? They grow them by the cemetery.”

“Oh.”

The country closed. Purple became purple, dust became dust. Ethel shifted the bag to her other hand.

“Tea?” her friend suggested.

“Tea.”

They went on, discussing whether seed cake was filling, their words returning to the ordinary track with a little jolt, though Ethel once looked back. The flower had already turned to receive another angle of light.

The snail now confronted the dead leaf. Beneath its lifted edge ran a tunnel of greenish shade, cool but narrowed by a twig. Above, the leaf’s dry surface offered a steep road exposed to birds. To the left, the soil broke away around a pebble and formed a hollow where moisture gleamed. Three paths: danger, obstruction, delay. It touched the twig. It touched the shadow. Its horns withdrew and emerged again.

A young couple approached without quite walking side by side. The girl wore white gloves and carried a closed green parasol. The young man’s new collar seemed to trouble him; he inserted one finger beneath it and removed it whenever she looked.

They had been silent long enough for silence to become a third companion, stepping between them, magnifying the click of her shoe and the small cough with which he repeatedly prepared to speak.

“Hot,” he said at last.

“Very.”

“Extraordinary for July.”

“Is it?”

“I mean—perhaps not extraordinary. One expects it.”

“Yes.”

They smiled. Her smile vanished first; his remained, uncertain what to do, then faded also.

A butterfly crossed before them, white, then blue where shadow stained it, and another rose to meet it. Together they swerved above the bed, separated, touched, separated again, like two fragments of porcelain kept aloft by invisible jets of water.

“Would you like to sit down, Clara?”

“If you would.”

“I don’t mind.”

“Nor do I.”

They remained standing.

The young man saw her hand tighten on the parasol handle. The glove had split at one finger, showing a narrow crescent of skin. He wanted to touch that small exposed place. The desire seemed enormous, indecorous, and impossibly tender. It filled the glasshouses, lifted their shining roofs, rang against every pane. Yet what he said was, “They’ve done these beds rather well.”

Clara looked at the flowers. She knew he was not speaking of flowers. Or hoped he was not. Each commonplace phrase had acquired weight: hot, sit down, tea, flowers. They were little sealed envelopes passed between them, and neither dared tear one open.

“Yes,” she said. “Especially the blue.”

“Blue?”

“There.”

He bent towards her line of sight. Their shoulders touched.

Neither moved away.

For that second the heat ceased to press upon them. The garden drew in one immense green breath. Clara smelled soap, warm cloth, bruised grass. The young man saw nothing but the transparent hairs at her temple, each lit separately. A future—rooms, quarrels, a brass bedstead, rain on a window, her hat on a peg—flashed before him with the absurd completeness of a remembered life.

Then a child shouted nearby. Clara raised her parasol, though she did not open it.

“Shall we have tea?” she asked.

“Yes. Yes, let’s.”

“Ice, perhaps.”

“Or buns.”

“Tea first.”

They moved away more closely than they had come. His hand hovered near her elbow but did not touch it. Their words—tea, ice, buns, this way—floated behind them, light and foolish and full as bells.

Having deliberated, the snail entered the shade beneath the leaf. Its body narrowed around the twig. The shell tilted, caught, released itself with a minute, soundless effort, and passed into the green chamber beyond, where a drop of water held the whole inverted garden: sky, petal, moving whiteness, a dark wheel turning outside the wall.

Now no single group possessed the path. Figures came and went—nurses with prams, soldiers, clerks, widows, children carrying hoops—each dissolving at the edges in heat. Voices crossed without meeting. Maria. Nine and four. Don’t run. Tea first. I promise. A laugh leapt up and was lost.

Above, petals flashed red, blue, yellow; wings opened and closed; the glass roofs burned silver, then softened. Beyond the trees the city revolved in its steel compartments: wheels within wheels, streets inside streets, offices stacked over tunnels, each box holding a voice, each voice holding some dragonfly, some kiss, some purple country too large to utter. Motors coughed; an omnibus groaned around a corner; metal struck metal. The sounds entered the garden, where leaves sifted them with birdsong and human speech until machinery, memory, desire and weather became one continuous murmur.

Under the dead leaf, the snail travelled on.

## THE PRIVATE MEANS OF ART

I have always held that, if there is one department of human activity in which I stand practically alone, it is the giving of advice.

Lots of fellows can advise you about a horse, and quite a number will tell you what to do with your money, particularly if the thing they want you to do with it is to hand it over to them. But when the human heart ties itself into one of those complicated knots which look as if they had been designed by a sailor with insomnia, the ordinary man merely paws the carpet and says, “Well, old man, that’s rather rotten.”

I am different.

I examine the affair from every angle, bring the full force of a trained intelligence to bear, and generally devise some simple, straightforward scheme which puts everything right.

It is only fair to add that now and then circumstances, displaying a low cunning which would disgrace a professional card-sharper, interfere with the working of the scheme. But that, naturally, is not my fault.

The business of Archie Ferguson and his wife Eunice is a case in point.

I had known Archie for years. We had been at school together, where he had once saved my life by removing a plate of the house custard from in front of me before I could eat it. Since then our paths had diverged. I had become a man about town, well spoken of in circles where a fellow’s ability to identify the correct fork is considered a solid virtue; while Archie had gone in for Art.

I spell Art with a capital letter because that was the way Archie talked about it. Art to him was not a thing you hung in the passage because the wallpaper had faded. It was a sacred flame, a stern mistress, a trumpet-call, and several other things which made it sound thoroughly uncomfortable.

His pictures were what are termed advanced. I never found out what they had advanced from, but they had certainly gone a long way. He painted square clouds, green horses, and women with both eyes on the same side of the nose. A portrait by Archie did not so much preserve a likeness as settle an old score.

His masterpiece was called *The Coming of Summer*.

I saw it once in his studio and had to sit down.

It showed a red sun exploding over a purple field, while in the foreground something resembling a lady who had been assembled in a hurry out of spare parts appeared to be wrestling with a blue tree. Archie explained that the blue tree symbolized aspiration and that the lady was Humanity awakening to the rhythm of the soil.

To me it looked as if summer had stubbed its toe on a bomb.

Still, there is no arguing about Art. If a chappie says a blue tree is aspiration, the only civil course is to accept his statement and hope that aspiration does not drop a branch on you.

Archie was engaged at this time to Eunice Nugent.

Eunice was a poetess. She wrote slender little volumes with white covers and titles like *Mists of the Unborn Dawn* and *Petals on the Brink*. There was one poem about a skylark which I tried hard to read, but after the bird had become the echo of a dream and the dream had turned into a silver footstep, I lost the thread.

She was a striking girl—dark, intense, and almost painfully soulful. When she looked at you, you had the uncomfortable feeling that she was examining your spirit and finding the upholstery vulgar. She believed that the artist must remain pure from commerce. According to Eunice, money was dust upon the wings of inspiration.

This seemed to me severe on money, which I had always found extremely useful.

One afternoon Archie came to my rooms and sank into a chair with the air of a man who has just discovered a tiger in his bath.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’m in the soup.”

“Then take off your hat, old scout. Nothing looks sillier than a man wearing a hat in soup.”

“This is serious.”

“So I gathered. Your left eyebrow is doing that funny thing.”

He leaned forward and clutched his knees.

“You know I’m marrying Eunice next month?”

“I had heard rumours.”

“And you know what she thinks about commercial art?”

“Dimly. She seems to regard it as a cross between burglary and putting water in milk.”

“Exactly.” He swallowed. “Reggie, I am a commercial artist.”

I looked at him.

“You?”

“Yes.”

“But nobody buys your pictures.”

“I don’t mean those pictures. I mean—I draw something else.”

There was a pause. It was one of those solid, chunky pauses which seem to occupy more room than the furniture.

“What do you draw?” I asked.

Archie glanced over his shoulder, though the only other person present was a small bronze statuette which had been looking the wrong way since I bought it.

“The Doughnut Family.”

I stared.

Everybody knew the Doughnut Family. You could not open an evening paper without finding them in the act of falling downstairs, swallowing a firework, or putting glue in a bishop’s hat.

The family consisted of Papa Doughnut, a stout chappie with a checked waistcoat; Mrs. Doughnut, a determined woman with a rolling-pin; Uncle Oswald, who slept in the coal-cellar; little Percy, whose hobby was exploding things; and a dog named Cuthbert, who bit tax-collectors.

They were enormously popular. Station bookstalls displayed albums of their adventures. Children loved them, grown men read them behind financial papers, and I had myself followed their fortunes with no little interest. Only that morning I had seen Papa Doughnut attempt to steal a sausage from Cuthbert and become involved with a steam-roller.

“You draw the Doughnuts?”

“Every line.”

“By Jove! That bit where Uncle Oswald paints the parrot—”

“That was mine.”

“And Percy’s exploding pudding?”

“Mine.”

I shook his hand warmly.

“Archie, old top, this is magnificent. I have always respected you, of course, but until this moment I never knew why.”

He recoiled as if I had offered him a dead fish.

“Don’t say that!”

“Why not? The thing’s genius.”

“It’s degrading hack-work. Eunice would be horrified.”

“Hasn’t she ever read it?”

“She thinks newspapers are the graveyards of the soul.”

“Very likely. But one can often pick up useful information in a graveyard.”

He groaned.

“You don’t understand. Eunice believes I live for my painting. She thinks I starve for it.”

“Do you?”

“Good heavens, no. The Doughnuts bring me nearly fifteen hundred a year.”

I was impressed. Fifteen hundred a year was not wealth beyond the dreams of avarice, but it was a dashed sight more than blue trees generally fetched.

“What does Eunice think you live on?”

“She supposes I have investments.”

“Well, there you are.”

“But she wants to know what investments.”

I reflected. The straightforward path was obvious, and, as usual, I saw it before he did.

“Railways,” I said.

“Railways?”

“Railways are always good for this sort of thing. Solid. Respectable. Nobody asks to see them. Tell her your money is in railway shares.”

He stared at me with dawning hope.

“Would that work?”

“Of course it would. You receive your Doughnut money, call it dividends, and all is to the velvet.”

“I hate deceiving her.”

“Naturally. Shows a good heart. But one must use common sense. After you are married, you can break the news gradually.”

“How gradually?”

“Very gradually. Say, in about fifty years. By then her views may have mellowed.”

He rose and paced the room.

“It seems cowardly.”

“Not cowardly, old scout. Considerate. Why batter a sensitive soul with facts when a small, well-chosen fiction will keep it comfortable?”

He stopped pacing.

“You really think so?”

“I am certain of it.”

That settled the matter.

Looking back, I perceive that there may have been some microscopic flaw in the counsel. But at the time it seemed one of those masterly strokes which statesmen remember with pride and their countries with bank holidays.

---

Archie and Eunice were married, and for three months everything purred like a well-fed cat.

They took a flat in Chelsea with long windows, low chairs, and a brass bowl in the hall which I was always tripping over. Archie painted in the larger room. Eunice wrote poetry in the smaller. In the evenings they sat together and discussed Beauty, Truth, and whether the dining-room curtains were sufficiently Byzantine.

Meanwhile Papa Doughnut continued to fall into ornamental ponds, and the money rolled in.

Whenever the cheque arrived Archie concealed it with the feverish care of a man handling stolen diamonds. He had opened a special banking account under the name of A. F. Dodd, Dodd being his middle name and also the name under which he drew the strip. Eunice saw none of the correspondence. She merely understood that the Great Midland and Scottish Permanent Railway, or some such organization, sent dividends with gratifying regularity.

Then the Great Midland and Scottish Permanent Railway collapsed.

It did not merely sag or show signs of fatigue. It went down with a report like a grand piano dropped from a sixth-floor window. The papers were full of it. Directors resigned, shareholders held meetings, and financial experts explained that the disaster had been inevitable ever since it happened.

I was breakfasting when the headline jumped out and bit me.

GREAT MIDLAND CRASH  
SHARES PRACTICALLY WORTHLESS

I spilled coffee down my waistcoat.

A moment later the telephone rang.

“Halloa?” I said.

“Is that Reggie?”

It was Archie, and his voice sounded like a mouse calling from inside a boa-constrictor.

“Yes, old thing.”

“Have you seen the paper?”

“I was just admiring it.”

“I’m ruined.”

“Nonsense. You haven’t got a penny in the railway.”

“Eunice thinks I have.”

This was true. I applied the brain to the problem, but before it could get properly under way Archie continued.

“She’s here.”

“Where?”

“Here, beside me.”

“Then why are you telephoning?”

“She told me to ring you and ask if you’d seen the news.”

“Tell her I haven’t.”

“I just told her you always read the financial page at breakfast.”

This was a scandalous slur, but there was no time to resent it.

“Couldn’t you say I’ve gone blind?”

“She wants to speak to you.”

There was a rustle, and Eunice came on the line.

“Reggie?”

“Good morning.”

“You know what has happened?”

“My dear girl, nothing is ever as bad as it looks in print.”

“Archie has lost everything.”

“Well—not necessarily everything. He still has his health. Fine constitution. I’ve seen him eat lobster at two in the morning.”

“This is no time for levity.”

“No, no. Of course not.”

“We must be brave.”

“Absolutely.”

“We must accept poverty as a cleansing wind.”

I did not like the sound of this. Cleansing winds generally mean giving up the second bottle at dinner.

“I have told Archie,” she continued, “that our happiness does not depend upon money.”

“Splendid.”

“We shall leave this expensive flat.”

“Steady on.”

“We shall take simple lodgings. We shall live close to the earth.”

“In Chelsea?”

“Somewhere cheaper.”

There was another rustle, and Archie’s voice returned.

“You see?” he whispered.

“Perfectly.”

“What am I to do?”

“Leave it to me.”

“You said that before.”

“And observe how well everything went until the railway people interfered.”

He made a hollow noise and rang off.

Within a week they had moved from the flat to two rooms in a back street off the King’s Road. The house had a landlady with a moustache and a staircase which appeared to have been built as an exercise in mountaineering. The sitting-room faced a brick wall. If you leaned far enough out of the window, dislocating two ribs in the process, you could see a chimney-pot.

Eunice was magnificent about it.

“Poverty,” she said, when I called, “is the guardian of the artist.”

Archie, who had just discovered that the gas-ring smoked, looked doubtful.

“The soul expands when material luxury is stripped away,” she continued.

At that moment a spring emerged from the armchair and prodded me in the spine. My soul may have expanded, but the rest of me contracted sharply.

“How are you managing?” I asked.

“We have my private means.”

Archie looked up.

“Your what?”

“My private means, darling.”

“You never told me you had private means.”

“I did not wish money to come between us. It is not much, but it will keep us from actual want.”

She said this as if actual want were a small dog which could be prevented from entering by holding the door shut with one foot.

Archie blinked.

“Where does it come from?”

“A family source,” she replied with dignity.

There was something in her manner which discouraged inquiry. Archie, who had already been discouraged by the gas-ring, let the matter drop.

I took him aside before leaving.

“This simplifies things,” I said. “You live on her private means and bank the Doughnut money.”

“That’s just it. I’ve got nearly a thousand pounds sitting in the bank already, and I can’t touch it.”

“Why not?”

“How can I? If I suddenly start producing money, Eunice will ask where it came from.”

“Say you found it.”

“Found a thousand pounds?”

“People find things.”

“Not in this neighbourhood.”

He had a point. The street did not look as if it had mislaid a thousand pounds since the reign of Henry the Eighth.

“What do you spend the Doughnut money on now?”

“Nothing. It accumulates. And we’re living on tinned tongue.”

“Tinned tongue is nourishing.”

“You haven’t tried our landlady’s method of preparing it. She boils the tin.”

He seized my lapel.

“Reggie, I can’t stand this much longer. Last night Eunice suggested that we should save money by giving up tobacco. This morning she talked of making our own soap.”

I patted his shoulder.

“Courage, old top. I’ll think of something.”

“You thought of the railway.”

“That was a sound scheme ruined by outside interference.”

“I want a scheme without outside interference.”

“One cannot legislate for the universe being unreasonable.”

He released me and sank onto the sofa, where a second spring promptly attacked him.

---

For some weeks no solution presented itself.

Archie’s bank balance became more and more congested, while his personal appearance went steadily downhill. He continued drawing the Doughnuts in secret at night, but the strain was telling on him. Papa Doughnut grew thinner. Uncle Oswald’s smile had a hunted look. Cuthbert bit people without his old animation.

Then, one evening at the club, inspiration arrived.

It was after dinner. I had consumed one of those admirable meals which cause the mind to rise above mundane things like buttons and waistcoats. I was considering the ceiling in a broad, statesmanlike way when I remembered J. Bellingwood Brackett.

You must know about Brackett. He was an American millionaire who bought modern art by the acre. Whenever a painter produced something which caused the public to ring for the police, Brackett bought it. His collection was said to contain pictures that had frightened hardened customs officials.

Only that week I had read that he had purchased a canvas consisting of a black triangle, two orange circles, and a real boot nailed to the frame. The work was called *Motherhood*.

The whole thing became clear in a flash.

I hurried to Archie’s lodgings.

He was sitting before *The Coming of Summer*, staring at it with loathing. The picture filled most of the room and all of the atmosphere.

“I’ve got it,” I said.

“Take it away.”

“I mean the solution.”

He turned slowly.

“If it involves a railway, I shall become violent.”

“No railways. Art.”

“Go on.”

“You sell *The Coming of Summer*.”

“To whom? A blind asylum?”

“To J. Bellingwood Brackett.”

Archie goggled.

“But he’s never seen it.”

“Not necessary. The man bought *Motherhood*.”

“He might have liked it.”

“Archie, it had a boot nailed to it.”

He considered this.

“How much does Brackett pay?”

“Thousands. We’ll say two thousand pounds.”

“We?”

“You announce that Brackett has bought your picture for two thousand. Then you transfer two thousand from the Doughnut account into your ordinary account. Your money becomes legitimate. You can smoke, buy soap, and eat tongue which has not been boiled in its armour.”

Archie’s face brightened, then clouded.

“It’s another lie.”

“A temporary adjustment of fact.”

“What if Brackett hears?”

“He’s in America.”

“People travel.”

“Millionaires don’t read every little item in English papers. They have secretaries to prevent that sort of thing.”

He rose and approached the picture.

“Would anyone believe Brackett had bought it?”

“Anyone who has seen *Motherhood* would believe he had bought the National Gallery and turned it upside down.”

Archie began to breathe more freely.

“Two thousand pounds.”

“Exactly. You can tell Eunice that prosperity has returned through the front door carrying a bouquet.”

“She would be so proud.”

“That is the idea.”

He extended his hand.

“Reggie, you’re a genius.”

I accepted the tribute modestly. After all, there are moments when false humility becomes mere affectation.

The scheme was simplicity itself. Archie drew a cheque on the Dodd account and paid it into his own, describing it as proceeds from the sale of the picture. He then went home to tell Eunice.

What happened next showed once again that no scheme, however perfectly designed, can survive contact with a poetess in a state of enthusiasm.

Eunice did not merely clap her hands and say, “How splendid.” She became radiant. She walked about the room speaking of vindication, recognition, and the inevitable triumph of the authentic vision. She kissed Archie. She kissed the frame of *The Coming of Summer*. She may even have kissed the blue tree.

Then she wrote to the newspapers.

I learned this the following morning when Archie burst into my rooms at nine o’clock, looking as if he had been dressed by an earthquake.

“She’s sent it to the papers!”

“Sent what?”

“The news. About Brackett buying the picture.”

I put down my teacup.

“All the papers?”

“Every one she could think of. She says the world must know that true art cannot be suppressed.”

“Couldn’t you suppress the letters?”

“She posted them last night.”

I remained calm. That is the advantage of having a first-class temperament.

“It may be all right.”

“How?”

“The papers may not print it.”

At that moment my man entered with the morning journals.

On the front page of one was a photograph of Archie standing beside *The Coming of Summer*. He looked pale and the picture looked dangerous.

AMERICAN MILLIONAIRE BUYS BRITISH MASTERPIECE

Another paper had the headline:

BRACKETT PAYS £2,000 FOR “SUMMER”

A third preferred:

GENIUS IN A GARRET  
ROMANTIC TRIUMPH OF YOUNG PAINTER

Archie picked up the last and read in a numb voice.

“‘Mr. Archibald Ferguson, whose fearless work has long been admired by the advanced school, has received striking recognition from Mr. J. Bellingwood Brackett, the celebrated American collector. Mr. Brackett has purchased for two thousand pounds the remarkable composition *The Coming of Summer*, described by Mrs. Ferguson, herself a poet of distinction, as a synthesis of elemental awakening and post-geometrical passion.’”

“Post-geometrical,” I said. “That’s good.”

Archie threw the paper across the room.

“We’re done for.”

“Not at all. Publicity is a wonderful thing.”

“So is influenza. You don’t want it in the house.”

“Brackett is three thousand miles away.”

“Suppose he cables?”

“Why should he? Millionaires are busy.”

I had scarcely uttered the words when the telephone rang.

Archie and I looked at it.

It rang again with a crisp, official note. There are telephones which merely tinkle, and telephones which seem to know your solicitor’s address.

I lifted the receiver.

“Halloa?”

“Mr. Reginald Pepper?” said a voice.

“Yes.”

“My name is Renshaw Liggett.”

“Congratulations.”

“I represent the British legal interests of Mr. J. Bellingwood Brackett.”

Archie closed his eyes.

“I understand,” continued the voice, “that Mr. Archibald Ferguson may be found at your residence.”

“Not at present.”

Archie opened his eyes and shook his head violently.

“I have reason to believe he is.”

“You have been misinformed.”

Archie sneezed.

There was a pause at the other end.

“Was that Mr. Ferguson?”

“No. A chair.”

“A chair?”

“An emotional chair.”

“I shall call upon Mr. Ferguson at his home at eleven-thirty.”

The line clicked.

I replaced the receiver.

“Outside interference,” I said.

Archie made a low, bubbling sound.

---

We reached the lodgings shortly before eleven-thirty.

Eunice received us in a glow of spiritual triumph. The room had been transformed. Flowers stood in jars. Congratulatory telegrams covered the mantelpiece. *The Coming of Summer* had been placed in the best light, though no light could do much for it except possibly lightning.

“Archie,” she cried, “the *New Dawn Review* wishes to publish an appreciation of your work.”

Archie sat down abruptly.

“How nice.”

“And three dealers have written.”

“Have they?”

“One offers to exhibit your early period.”

“My what?”

“Your early period.”

“I didn’t know I had one.”

“Every great artist has an early period.”

I tried to help.

“Quite. It is the bit before people start paying.”

She turned to me.

“Reggie, are you not proud?”

“Immensely.”

“You believed in him when others doubted.”

Archie looked at me. It was not a grateful look.

“I did my best,” I said.

The bell rang.

The sound entered the room, put on black gloves, and measured us for coffins.

A moment later the landlady announced Mr. Renshaw Liggett.

He was a long, dry man with rimless spectacles and a mouth like a letterbox which had been closed for a public holiday. He carried a leather case. Everything about him suggested documents, affidavits, and lunches consisting of a biscuit eaten over a wastepaper basket.

“Mr. Ferguson?”

Archie rose.

“Yes.”

“My name is Renshaw Liggett. I am a solicitor attached to the firm of Liggett, Liggett, Mowbray and Liggett.”

“You seem well supplied with Liggetts,” I said, trying to put him at his ease.

He looked at me as if noting the remark for future legal action.

“I act,” he continued, “for Mr. J. Bellingwood Brackett.”

Eunice beamed.

“How delightful. Has Mr. Brackett sent instructions about the picture?”

“In a manner of speaking.”

“Archie,” she whispered, “perhaps he wishes you to paint a companion work.”

Mr. Liggett’s mouth tightened.

“I sincerely trust not.”

Eunice stiffened.

“What do you mean?”

“I mean, madam, that I have come in consequence of certain statements published this morning in the press, statements alleging that my client has purchased the work now before me.”

He turned to inspect *The Coming of Summer*.

For perhaps ten seconds nobody spoke.

The picture seemed to inspect him back. The blue tree reached for his left ear. Humanity, awakening to the rhythm of the soil, appeared to be preparing to kick him in the waistcoat.

Mr. Liggett removed his spectacles, cleaned them, replaced them, and looked again. It did not help.

“My client,” he said at last, “has not purchased this picture.”

Archie swallowed.

“Not exactly.”

Eunice stared at him.

“Archie?”

“I was coming to that.”

Mr. Liggett opened his case.

“The position is one of unusual gravity.”

I saw a chance to soothe the fellow.

“There has been a slight misunderstanding, old top.”

“I am not your old top.”

“Old scout, then.”

“I am not your old scout.”

“There’s no need to be narrow about it.”

He ignored me.

“My client is at present engaged in resisting an application brought by two of his nephews in the Supreme Court of the State of New York.”

“Family life,” I said sympathetically. “Always tricky.”

“The application seeks to have Mr. Brackett declared mentally incapable of managing his affairs.”

Eunice gave a small gasp.

Mr. Liggett continued with gathering majesty.

“The plaintiffs allege eccentricity, delusion, impaired judgment, and a persistent tendency to squander large sums upon objects devoid of utility or aesthetic merit.”

His eyes moved toward the picture.

We all looked at it.

For the first time I saw *The Coming of Summer* not merely as a bad picture, but as legal evidence. In that role it was terrific. It could have got a bishop locked up.

“You perceive,” said Mr. Liggett, “the injury which this report may do. My client’s nephews have already cabled from New York requesting confirmation. They propose, I understand, to introduce the newspaper cuttings in support of their contention.”

“But Mr. Brackett buys modern pictures,” said Eunice.

“He does.”

“Then why should this particular purchase—”

She stopped.

Mr. Liggett waited.

There was another of those chunky silences. Somewhere upstairs a child dropped what sounded like a wardrobe.

Eunice drew herself up.

“Are you suggesting that my husband’s picture is so extraordinary that buying it would prove insanity?”

“I am suggesting nothing. I am stating the view likely to be urged by hostile counsel.”

“You have seen the picture.”

“I have.”

“And?”

Mr. Liggett took refuge in legal language.

“I am not prepared, without fuller instructions, to commit myself to an opinion concerning the work’s artistic status. I will say only that if my client had paid two thousand pounds for it, his nephews would undoubtedly be dancing in the streets.”

Archie covered his face.

“What do you require?” I asked.

“A signed statement from Mr. Ferguson admitting that Mr. Brackett did not purchase the picture, together with an immediate public denial sent to every newspaper which printed the allegation.”

Eunice turned to Archie.

“Why would you say that Mr. Brackett bought it if he did not?”

Archie removed his hands from his face.

“Well—”

“Yes?”

“The fact is—”

“Yes, darling?”

He looked at me with the despairing expression of a man who has fallen overboard and sees his best friend on deck explaining knots.

I tried to rally him.

“Nothing to it, Archie. Just tell the simple story.”

“What simple story?”

“The—well, the comparatively simple story.”

Mr. Liggett consulted his watch.

“I have another appointment at twelve-fifteen.”

That did it. There are moments when a solicitor’s watch can be more compelling than conscience.

Archie stood up.

“Eunice,” he said, “I lied.”

She went white about the gills.

“You lied?”

“I never sold the picture.”

“Then where did the two thousand pounds come from?”

Archie drew a deep breath.

“Papa Doughnut.”

Mr. Liggett looked up sharply.

“Who is Papa Doughnut?”

“Not who,” said Archie. “What.”

Eunice stared.

Archie hurried to a drawer and pulled out a bundle of newspaper cuttings. His hands trembled as he spread them on the table.

There was Papa Doughnut attempting to shave with a lawn-mower. There was Mrs. Doughnut pursuing Uncle Oswald with a stuffed haddock. There was Cuthbert fastening his teeth into a rent-collector at the precise moment when Percy ignited a rocket beneath the sofa.

Eunice looked at the drawings.

“What have these to do with you?”

“I draw them.”

“You—draw *The Doughnut Family*?”

“Yes.”

“All of them?”

“Every one.”

She picked up a cutting in which Papa Doughnut, mistaking a jar of glue for hair tonic, had attached himself to a police constable.

“You invented this?”

“Yes.”

“And this little dog?”

“Cuthbert.”

“He bites the policeman in the next picture?”

“No, the policeman bites him. It was a special Christmas reversal.”

Mr. Liggett cleared his throat.

“I fail to see the relevance.”

Archie swung toward him.

“The Doughnuts are where the money came from. I’ve drawn them for five years. They pay me. They pay me dashed well. I told Eunice my income came from railway shares because I was ashamed. When the railway collapsed, I couldn’t spend my real income without telling her the truth. So Reggie suggested—”

I coughed.

“Details, old man. No need to overload the narrative.”

“So Reggie suggested that I should pretend Brackett had bought my painting. I transferred two thousand pounds from my Doughnut account and bought the picture from myself.”

Mr. Liggett stared at me.

“You devised this arrangement?”

“It seemed sound at the time.”

“Did it?”

“One cannot foresee nephews.”

He took a document from his case.

“Mr. Ferguson, you will sign a denial.”

“Yes.”

“You will state explicitly that Mr. Brackett neither bought the picture, contemplated buying it, authorized anyone to buy it, nor had any knowledge of its existence.”

“Yes.”

“And you will not associate my client’s name with this work again.”

“Never.”

Mr. Liggett placed the paper on the table. Archie signed.

The solicitor examined the signature, put the paper away, and looked once more at the painting.

“I shall communicate with the press immediately,” he said. “It may be possible to limit the damage.”

At the door he paused.

“On a purely personal note, Mr. Ferguson—”

“Yes?”

“My youngest son is an admirer of the Doughnut Family.”

Archie blinked.

“Really?”

“He has collected the albums since infancy. I believe he would value your autograph.”

“Gladly.”

Mr. Liggett produced an album from his case.

It was a complete edition of *Doughnuts Down the Ages*.

Solicitors are rummy birds.

Archie signed it. Mr. Liggett thanked him, gave *The Coming of Summer* a final look of deep professional suspicion, and departed.

---

The door closed.

Archie stood in the middle of the room, limp but honest.

Eunice had not moved. She was still holding the cutting with the glued policeman.

“Eunice,” he said.

She looked up.

“I suppose,” he continued, “that this is the end.”

“The end?”

“You despise me.”

She did not answer.

“I know what you think of commercial art. I’ve betrayed everything you believed in. I’ve taken what talent I have and sold it to make people laugh at fat men falling into baths.”

Still she said nothing.

Archie’s voice became husky.

“I should have told you before we married. I was a coward. I thought you loved the artist in me, and I was afraid you wouldn’t love the fellow who drew Papa Doughnut. I don’t blame you. I’ll go away, if you want. I’ll sign over the money. I’ll—”

A strange sound interrupted him.

Eunice had put her hand over her mouth. Her shoulders shook.

Archie gazed at her.

“Don’t cry.”

She made the sound again.

It was not crying.

It was a snort.

A moment later she sat down abruptly and began to laugh.

I do not mean that she smiled wanly or permitted a silvery ripple to escape, as poetesses do beside woodland pools. She laughed like a schoolgirl who has seen the headmaster sit on a bun. She rocked to and fro. She gasped. She pointed at the cutting.

“The policeman!” she said faintly. “Oh, the policeman!”

Archie looked at me.

I looked at Archie.

“Halloa?” he said.

Eunice wiped her eyes.

“I’m sorry. But it is so funny. Look at his helmet!”

“You mean—you like it?”

“I love it.”

“The Doughnuts?”

“They’re glorious.”

“But you said commercial art was a betrayal of the soul.”

“Yes.”

“And newspapers were graveyards.”

“I know.”

“And that an artist who worked for money had sold his birthright for a mess of pottage.”

“I know, darling.”

Archie’s face began to assume the expression of a chappie who has walked into a wall and found it made of cream.

“Then why—”

Eunice rose.

“Because I am just as bad.”

She crossed to the desk and unlocked a drawer.

Archie stared.

“What do you mean?”

“I mean that my private means are not private means.”

She took out a thick packet of papers and handed it to him.

He read the top sheet.

“‘Wake to Vimco, the Coffee that Isn’t Coffee but Knows What Coffee Ought to Be.’”

Eunice covered her face.

“I wrote that.”

Archie turned to the next.

“‘Baby Bumbles Grow While Mother Slumbers.’”

“I wrote that too.”

Another sheet appeared.

“‘Higginbotham’s Sausages—Every Link a Little Holiday.’”

“Yes.”

“‘Do Your Teeth Feel Lonely? Give Them Grippo Gum Tonic.’”

“Yes.”

He dug deeper into the packet with mounting amazement.

“‘A Spoonful of Nutri-Malt at Night Keeps Morning Bright.’”

“Yes.”

“‘If It’s a Blot, Use Spotnot.’”

“Yes.”

“‘No Husband Roams When There’s Blenkinsop Foam.’ What on earth is Blenkinsop Foam?”

“A shaving soap.”

Archie lowered the papers.

“You write advertisements?”

Eunice nodded.

“For years?”

“Since before I met you.”

“And that’s where the private income comes from?”

“Yes.”

“But your poems—”

“Earn practically nothing. *Petals on the Brink* sold thirty-seven copies, and Aunt Julia bought twelve.”

Archie sat down.

Eunice rushed on, words pouring out now like peas from a split bag.

“I was ashamed. I thought you would despise me. You talked so nobly about the artist’s duty to reject the market. You said popularity was the applause of the spiritually underfed. How could I tell you that I spent my mornings finding rhymes for a patent infant food?”

Archie blinked.

“Does baby food need rhymes?”

“Apparently. ‘Little cheeks will bloom and glow, fed on Bimbo as they grow.’ That campaign paid for our wedding trip.”

There was a pause.

Then Archie began to laugh.

He laughed more quietly than Eunice had, but with feeling. Soon they were both at it, and I joined in, for the whole thing seemed to me distinctly entertaining and I am never a fellow to stand aloof when mirth is going about.

“So,” said Archie at last, “while I thought I was keeping you on railway dividends—”

“You were keeping me on Doughnuts.”

“And while I thought your private means came from a family source—”

“You were eating Higginbotham’s Sausages.”

“They were not Higginbotham’s. They were the landlady’s.”

“The money was Higginbotham’s.”

Archie examined one of the sheets.

“‘Every Link a Little Holiday’ is good.”

“Do you really think so?”

“Brilliant. It makes you see the sausage.”

“I was rather proud of it.”

He picked up another.

“Grippo Gum Tonic is weak.”

“The client insisted on ‘lonely.’”

“Clients,” said Archie bitterly.

They exchanged a look.

It was the look of two veterans discovering that they had served in the same disreputable regiment.

“You don’t mind Cuthbert?” he asked.

“I adore Cuthbert.”

“You don’t think Papa Doughnut is degrading?”

“I think he is immortal.”

“And you won’t mind if I go on drawing them?”

“Only if you let me help with the captions.”

Archie sprang up.

“My darling!”

He moved toward her with the speed of a man who has heard that soup is being served at a railway buffet. She met him halfway, and they folded together among the advertisements and comic strips.

It was touching.

I stood there for a moment, smiling in that tolerant, almost paternal way which comes naturally to the man whose foresight and diplomacy have steered two human beings through the rocks into the harbour of happiness.

Then I noticed that neither of them was paying the slightest attention to me.

I coughed.

They did not stir.

I coughed again, with more attack.

Archie murmured something about Grippo Gum Tonic being the greatest achievement of modern literature. Eunice replied that Cuthbert’s Christmas reversal had changed her whole conception of form.

Clearly, the moment had come for a tactful withdrawal.

I put on my hat and went out.

As I reached the pavement, two newspaper boys came racing along the street.

“Special!” shouted one. “Brackett Art Mystery!”

“American Millionaire Denies Purchase!” yelled the other.

I bought a copy.

The paragraph was brief but adequate. Mr. J. Bellingwood Brackett had never bought *The Coming of Summer*. The report had arisen from a misunderstanding. Mr. Archibald Ferguson regretted the error. Mr. Brackett’s legal representatives wished it to be known that their client remained in full possession of his faculties.

There was also a small reproduction of the picture.

I looked at it.

Even reduced to three inches by two, it seemed capable of putting the average millionaire straight into the nut factory.

Still, all had ended well. Archie had his money, Eunice had her sausages, Brackett had his sanity, and the public had an extra edition. The only person who had received no practical benefit from the affair was myself.

That is often the way when one devotes one’s life to helping others.

Nobody thinks of the adviser. Nobody embraces the adviser. Nobody even offers him a sausage.

I went to the club and ordered lunch.

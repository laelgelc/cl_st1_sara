## THE EDUCATION OF BOBBIE CARDEW

There are men who forget an umbrella, and there are men who forget a name. Bobbie Cardew forgot everything.

I don’t mean that he occasionally left his gloves in a taxi or posted a letter without the stamp. We all do that sort of thing. Only last Thursday I went into the club library to fetch a book and came out with a siphon of soda-water, and nobody thought the worse of me except the committee. Bobbie’s forgetfulness was on a larger, almost imperial scale. If he had been present at the Battle of Waterloo, he would have forgotten which side he was on and gone home to change his socks.

He had a face which suggested perfect innocence and a memory which confirmed it. Information entered his mind with every appearance of intending to settle down, took one look round, and left by the nearest exit. You could tell Bobbie at luncheon that you were sailing for Africa at four, and at five he would ring up to ask if you cared to dine.

Once, when he had promised to meet me at the Savoy at eight, I took precautions. At ten in the morning I telephoned him. At noon I sent a note. At three I sent a telegram. At six I telephoned again. At seven-thirty I dispatched my man, Wilberforce, in a taxi with instructions to remain at Bobbie’s side till he was dressed and to accompany him to the restaurant.

Bobbie arrived at nine-fifteen.

“Sorry, old lad,” he said cheerfully. “I met Wilberforce.”

“I know. I sent him.”

“Did you? That explains something. I’ve been wondering all evening why he was in my bedroom.”

“And why didn’t he bring you here?”

“I sent him away.”

“Why?”

“I thought he had come to borrow money.”

This was Bobbie Cardew.

Nature, mind you, has a soft corner for men like Bobbie. She constructs them with the loving care of an armourer turning out a tank. She gives them pleasant smiles, private incomes, good shoulders, and an air of being incapable of deliberately hurting a fly. The result is that, though they spread ruin on every side, people continue to feed them and ask them to stay for the week-end.

Bobbie had reached the age of twenty-eight without being seriously damaged by life. Then he met Mary Anthony.

He married her.

I remember receiving the news in the smoking-room. Bobbie came in at about three in the afternoon, upset a small table, apologized to a bowl of flowers, and dropped into the chair opposite mine.

“Reggie,” he said, “I’m married.”

I lowered the paper. One likes to preserve calm in a club, but there are limits.

“You’re what?”

“Married.”

“When?”

“This morning.”

“To whom?”

He looked wounded.

“To Mary, of course.”

“Of course,” I said. “Naturally. Who is Mary?”

It appeared that she was a girl whom he had met six weeks before while staying with his aunt in Hampshire. Bobbie had fallen off a horse, and Mary, who had been a nurse during the war and possessed the competent hands of one accustomed to tying up shattered humanity before breakfast, had attended to him. She had set his wrist, washed his forehead, and told him not to be an idiot when he attempted to smoke under the influence of chloroform. Bobbie, waking to find a beautiful girl bending over him and giving orders, had decided that this was love.

Mary was undoubtedly beautiful. She was tall and fair, with a determined chin and a quantity of hair which looked as if it ought to have been entered separately in the census. When she turned her head, the whole mass swung round like cargo in a gale. Her eyes were grey and level, and gave a fellow the uncomfortable impression that she knew exactly how often he had overdrawn his account.

She was fond of Bobbie. That was plain. But she was also a reformer.

There is something about Bobbie that brings out the reforming instinct in women. They look at him as an engineer might look at a swamp—regretfully, but with visions of drainage.

“His memory only needs training,” Mary told me a few days after the honeymoon.

We were taking tea at their flat. Bobbie had gone out to buy cigarettes and had been absent two hours.

“You think so?” I said.

“I know so.”

“Some memories are built in rather inaccessible country.”

“It is merely a question of concentration.”

“Possibly. But concentration on what? Bobbie can concentrate magnificently on a golf ball. He once remembered every stroke of a round for three months.”

“He must learn to concentrate on people.”

At this point Bobbie came in carrying a stuffed owl.

Mary looked at it.

“What is that?”

“An owl.”

“I can see it is an owl. Why have you bought it?”

Bobbie’s face changed.

“Bought it?”

“You went out for cigarettes.”

“So I did.”

“Where are they?”

He felt his pockets, encountered nothing but a handkerchief and a small brass hinge, and looked surprised.

“Good Lord!”

The owl, it appeared, had attracted him from a shop window. He had thought it might brighten the hall.

Mary’s lips tightened, but she said nothing. She was still in the early days of marriage, when a wife sees her husband’s defects through a sort of golden mist and calls them funny little ways. It is later that the mist lifts and she discovers that the funny little ways have sharp edges.

For a time things went smoothly. Mary instituted a system. Bobbie was provided with a pocket diary, a desk calendar, a slate in the bedroom, and a series of coloured cards. Red cards meant urgent engagements, blue cards meant social duties, green cards meant household matters, and yellow cards meant things which, if forgotten, would cause Mary to stop speaking to him.

Within a week he had lost the diary, written a racing tip on the calendar, used the slate to keep the score at billiards, and sent a yellow card to his tailor with a cheque.

Still, Mary persevered. The woman had courage. If she had been in command at Thermopylae, history would have had a different ending and Xerxes would have gone home early.

Then came the anniversary.

It was their first wedding anniversary, and Mary had arranged everything with care. She had obtained two stalls for a new opera. She had ordered dinner at a restaurant where the waiters moved as silently as guilty consciences and the prices caused strong men to grip the table. She had bought a new dress. She had even sent Bobbie a note in the morning reminding him to be home at seven.

At seven Bobbie was playing squash at the Bath Club.

At seven-thirty he was having a bath.

At eight he was explaining to a fellow named Phipps that he could not dine because he had a vague feeling there was something he ought to be doing.

At eight-fifteen Phipps persuaded him that the feeling was probably indigestion.

At eight-thirty they went to dinner.

I entered the thing at nine, when the telephone rang beside my chair.

“Is that you, Reggie?” said Mary.

The voice was calm. Too calm. It had the quality of a sheet of ice which has not yet decided whom to drown.

“Yes.”

“Do you know where Bobbie is?”

“No. Why?”

“He was to have been here at seven.”

“What for?”

There was a pause.

“Our wedding anniversary.”

I sat upright. One does not need to be an expert in domestic affairs to recognize a situation when it leaps at one’s throat.

“Ah,” I said.

“Is that all you have to say?”

“No. No, indeed. Many happy returns.”

“It is not my birthday.”

“Of course not. I meant—well, you know what I meant.”

“I know exactly what you mean. Will you find him?”

I found him at the Berkeley, eating asparagus and discussing putting-greens.

When I broke the news, he dropped his fork.

“My anniversary!”

“Your first.”

“I knew there was something.”

“You ass.”

“What time is it?”

“Nearly ten.”

He stared at me as if I had personally accelerated the clock.

“But the opera!”

“Half over.”

“The dinner!”

“Cold.”

“Mary!”

I shook my head.

This was not because I had anything useful to communicate. It merely seemed the right gesture.

Bobbie sprang up.

“Come with me.”

“No.”

“Reggie, old man—”

“Nothing doing. I have always felt that a husband should face his wife alone on these great ceremonial occasions. It builds character.”

“You’re afraid.”

“Certainly I’m afraid. I’m not ashamed of it. Fear is the instinct which has preserved civilized man from extinction.”

But he clung to my sleeve, and in the end I accompanied him. Friendship is a wonderful thing. It causes a man to enter houses from which common sense is shouting to him to remain at least eight hundred miles distant.

Mary was sitting in the drawing-room in her opera dress. It was a striking dress, silver or grey or something of that nature, and she looked magnificent. Her hair seemed to have increased in weight since tea-time and now resembled a golden thundercloud.

Bobbie began at once.

“Darling—”

“Good evening.”

“I can explain.”

“Where have you been?”

“At the club.”

“You received my note?”

“Yes. That is to say, I remember receiving a note. I thought it was from my dentist.”

“Did you read it?”

“I was just going to when I met Wilmot. He has bought a new putter.”

Mary’s eyes travelled to me.

“And you?”

“I wasn’t there,” I said quickly. “I knew nothing about it till nine.”

“You might have reminded him.”

“How?”

“You are his friend.”

This seemed unreasonable, but it was no moment for constitutional argument.

Bobbie advanced with the expression of a dog approaching a cook who has discovered the mutton missing.

“Mary, I’m frightfully sorry.”

“You are always sorry.”

“Yes, but this time I’m particularly sorry.”

“That makes it quite different.”

There are occasions when irony is wasted on Bobbie. He brightened.

“I knew you would understand.”

Mary rose.

“I do understand. I understand perfectly.”

She passed out of the room.

Bobbie looked at me.

“That went better than I expected.”

At that moment the bedroom door slammed with such force that a photograph of Bobbie’s mother fell from the mantelpiece.

I picked it up.

“Much better,” I said.

The anniversary was not the end. It was merely the opening gun.

During the next few months Bobbie forgot to post Mary’s letters, forgot to meet her aunt at Waterloo, forgot that they had guests coming to dinner, and once forgot Mary herself at a country railway station. He had driven there to fetch her, saw a man whose face reminded him of someone, followed the man to discover who it was, and returned home three hours later with a rabbit.

The rabbit had been part of a raffle.

Mary did her best. She spoke gently. She spoke severely. She appealed to his love, his pride, and his common decency. She devised tests. She made him repeat things. She stood him in front of her and said, “Now, Bobbie, what are you doing at half-past four?” until the words seemed to be printed on the atmosphere.

At half-past four Bobbie would be somewhere else.

The crash came in April.

I had been lunching with Bobbie, and he had seemed unusually subdued. He pushed a cutlet about his plate and stared at it as though it had betrayed him.

“What’s wrong?” I said.

“Mary’s left me.”

I nearly swallowed an olive whole.

“Left you?”

“Gone to her mother.”

“What did you forget this time?”

“I don’t know.”

“Think.”

“I have thought. I may have forgotten to pay the gas bill.”

“She wouldn’t leave you for that.”

“I forgot her cousin’s wedding.”

“Nearer.”

“And I called her Margaret twice yesterday.”

“Warmer.”

“And there was something about a vase.”

“What vase?”

“I broke it.”

“Bad?”

“It had been in her family since 1760.”

“Very warm indeed.”

He took a letter from his pocket and handed it to me. Mary had written with her usual clearness. She said that she loved him, but could no longer endure being treated as an item which might or might not occur to him between golf and dinner. She was going away. She would return on one condition.

On her next birthday, he must send her his good wishes without assistance or reminder.

That was all.

“Well,” I said, “there you are. Quite simple.”

Bobbie stared at me.

“Simple?”

“You wish her happy birthday.”

“When is her birthday?”

I lowered the letter.

“You don’t know?”

“No.”

“You have been married nearly a year and you don’t know your wife’s birthday?”

“I knew it once.”

“When?”

“Before we were married.”

“And then?”

“It slipped.”

I walked to the window and looked out. A milk cart was passing. A policeman was regulating the traffic. London moved calmly beneath me, unaware that in a room above Piccadilly a man had been asked to name his wife’s birthday and could not even get into the preliminary heats.

“Have you any idea of the month?”

Bobbie frowned till his forehead looked like a ploughed field.

“It wasn’t winter.”

“Good. That disposes of three months.”

“Or autumn.”

“Excellent. We are narrowing it down.”

“It may have been autumn.”

I sat again.

“Did she ever mention the date?”

“Often.”

“Did you make a note of it?”

“Yes.”

“Where?”

“In my diary.”

“Where is the diary?”

“I lost it.”

I had forgotten the diary.

“Ask her mother.”

“She won’t tell me. Mary has warned everybody.”

“Her friends?”

“Warned.”

“Servants?”

“Warned.”

“Family Bible?”

“At her mother’s house, under guard.”

The thing began to interest me. I have always fancied that I possess something of the detective mind. Give me a cigar, an arm-chair, and a problem involving no physical exertion, and I am as keen as any bloodhound.

“Let us proceed methodically,” I said. “What year was she born?”

Bobbie looked hopeful.

“Nineteen hundred and—”

He stopped.

“Yes?”

“I’m not certain.”

“You don’t need the year, fortunately. Only the day and month.”

“That’s what makes it difficult. There are so many.”

“Three hundred and sixty-five.”

“Exactly.”

“You might begin at January and work forward.”

“I have begun. But every date looks possible.”

“Did anything happen on her birthday? A dinner? A dance?”

“We went to the theatre.”

“Ha!”

I leaned forward. The quarry was moving in the undergrowth.

“What theatre?”

“I don’t remember.”

“What did you see?”

“A play.”

“What play?”

“I don’t remember.”

“Who acted in it?”

“I don’t remember.”

I controlled myself. The detective mind must remain cool even when the witness is doing his best to turn it into a poached egg.

“Was it a comedy?”

“There was a cat.”

“A cat?”

“A real cat. It came on in the second act.”

This was something. Not much, perhaps, but when a man is drowning he does not criticize the colour of the life-belt.

“Anything else?”

“There was a French maid.”

“There is always a French maid.”

“And somebody hid behind a screen.”

“There is always somebody behind a screen.”

“And in the last act they all went to Scotland.”

I stared at him.

“Bobbie, that describes practically every play produced in London since 1890.”

He sighed and covered his face.

For the next week he worked.

I use the word advisedly. Before this affair, Bobbie’s idea of work had been to write his name on a score-card without help. Now he haunted libraries, theatrical agencies, newspaper offices, and the reading-room of the British Museum. He bought files of old dramatic journals. He interviewed actors. He wrote to managers. He became a pale, feverish creature with ink on his cuffs and dates in his eyes.

It was impressive.

At the club one afternoon I found him surrounded by books.

“What are these?” I asked.

“Astrology.”

I picked up a volume. It contained a picture of a goat with a fish’s tail.

“How does this help?”

“Mary once said she was a typical something.”

“Something?”

“A typical sign. A ram or a crab or a pair of scales.”

“That seems broad.”

“I thought I might recognize the description.”

He read from a pamphlet.

“‘Persons born under Aries are energetic, courageous, and impatient of stupidity.’ That might be Mary.”

“Very possibly.”

“‘Those born under Taurus are firm, practical, and slow to forgive.’ That’s Mary too.”

He turned a page.

“‘Gemini subjects possess quick minds and may have fine hair.’”

“Definitely Mary.”

“Cancer people are domestic but resent neglect.”

“Mary again.”

He threw down the pamphlet.

“They all sound like her.”

“That is astrology’s strong point.”

He had purchased twelve pamphlets at sixpence each, and every one proved that Mary might have been born at any time between New Year’s Day and Christmas Eve.

Still, he did not give up.

This was the remarkable thing. The old Bobbie would have tired of the search in ten minutes and gone to watch polo. The new Bobbie pursued the date as a terrier pursues a rat beneath a tool-shed. He stopped playing golf. He declined dinners. He remembered appointments. Once he arranged to meet me at eleven and arrived at five minutes to the hour. I thought for a moment that the clock had stopped.

Love had entered his system like a powerful drug.

One evening he burst into my flat waving a newspaper.

“I’ve got it!”

“The birthday?”

“The play.”

He had traced the cat.

This sounds simple, but it was not. London managers apparently kept a surprising number of cats employed. There had been cats in drawing-room comedies, cats in melodramas, cats in children’s spectacles, and one cat which had appeared as the ghost of a murdered baronet. Bobbie had followed every animal through the records with the concentration of a racing man studying pedigrees.

At last he had found an interview with an actress who complained that the cat in a comedy called *The Marriage Market* had scratched her nightly.

“That’s the piece,” he said. “I remember the name now.”

“When did we—you—see it?”

“That’s the trouble. It ran for fourteen months.”

His triumph deflated like a punctured football.

“Could you have gone on the first night?”

“No. Mary hates first nights.”

“The last?”

“No.”

“Did you keep the programme?”

“I keep nothing.”

“Did you book the seats?”

“Yes.”

“Then the theatre may have a record.”

He raced out before I had finished the sentence.

The theatre did have records, but not the kind Bobbie wanted. The booking clerk remembered neither him nor Mary. Why should he? Bobbie is not a man who fixes himself in the official mind unless he is knocking over its inkpot. The accounts showed hundreds of pairs of stalls sold over fourteen months, and no note existed of which particular pair had witnessed Mary’s birthday.

Bobbie returned looking as merry as a damp biscuit at a funeral breakfast.

“We shall have to approach from another direction,” I said.

“There isn’t another direction.”

“There is always another direction. Sometimes it leads over a cliff, but it is there.”

We reviewed the evidence.

On Mary’s birthday, Bobbie had taken her to *The Marriage Market*. Before the theatre they had dined somewhere. Afterward they had gone to supper with friends—or possibly they had not. Bobbie could not remember. There had been rain, unless that had been another night. Mary had worn a blue dress, unless it was green. Bobbie had given her flowers, and this, at least, seemed certain because she had complained that the orchids were too expensive.

“Where did you buy the orchids?”

“I don’t know.”

“How did you pay?”

“Cheque.”

I sat very still.

“Cheque?”

“Yes.”

“Do you keep your old cheque stubs?”

“My bank does.”

For perhaps five seconds we looked at one another. Then Bobbie made a noise like a cavalry regiment receiving orders to charge and shot from the room.

The bank manager was not helpful at first. Bank managers dislike romance. Their attitude toward human emotion is that it ought to be entered in the correct column and initialled. But Bobbie persisted. He explained. He pleaded. He may even have concentrated. At last the manager allowed an assistant to examine the paid cheques from the relevant period.

The next difficulty was that Bobbie had written cheques to four florists during the run of the play.

“Four?” I said.

“I’m generous with flowers.”

“You are reckless with cheques.”

“One was for Mary. One was for my aunt. One was for a hospital fête. And one—I can’t remember.”

We inspected the list.

March 17: Pomfret & Sons, three guineas.

May 8: Lucullus Flowers Ltd., five pounds ten.

June 29: Miss E. Pringle, Florist, one pound.

September 2: The Orchid House, seven pounds seven.

“Which florist did you use for Mary?” I asked.

Bobbie groaned.

“Must you always ask impossible questions?”

“The amount may help. You said the orchids were expensive.”

“They were.”

“Seven pounds seven?”

“Possibly.”

“That would make the birthday September second.”

“But we had already met before March.”

“Then it may be March seventeenth.”

“Three guineas isn’t expensive.”

“It is to some people.”

“Not for orchids.”

“May eighth, five pounds ten?”

He pressed his hands to his temples.

“I can almost see the shop.”

“Which shop?”

“It had a gold sign.”

“They all have gold signs.”

“And a girl with black hair.”

“All florists contain a girl with black hair. It is compulsory.”

“And there was a parrot.”

I rang for a restorative.

The parrot proved decisive.

After two days of telephoning, Bobbie found that Lucullus Flowers Ltd. had once possessed a parrot called Captain, removed after it had learned to imitate the proprietor asking customers if they were absolutely sure their cheques would be honoured.

The cheque had been written on May eighth.

Bobbie still refused to trust it. His mind, awakened after twenty-eight years of slumber, was now working with the feverish suspicion of a detective in the final chapter. He went to Lucullus Flowers. The black-haired girl had married a dentist and emigrated. The proprietor remembered the parrot but not Bobbie. The old order books had been stored in a warehouse at Battersea.

Bobbie went to Battersea.

The warehouse was unheated. He spent six hours among ledgers, dust, and the remains of a mechanical Father Christmas. When he emerged, his feet were frozen and his suit looked as if he had been rolled in flour, but he carried a slip of paper.

Order 6417. Mr. R. Cardew. One dozen orchids. To Miss Mary Anthony. “With love on your birthday.” Delivery, May 8.

He came to me directly.

I was dining quietly when the door burst open and Bobbie entered, white with exhaustion and radiant with victory.

“May eighth!” he shouted.

The waiter dropped the soup.

“Steady,” I said. “This is a respectable restaurant.”

“May eighth, Reggie! I’ve got it. I’ve proved it. There can’t be any mistake.”

He laid the order slip beside my plate.

I examined it.

“My dear old bloodhound, you have treed the date.”

He sat down and laughed. Then, to my embarrassment, he nearly cried. He covered the movement by drinking my wine.

“When is May eighth?” he asked.

“Three weeks from tomorrow.”

“I shall send the letter now.”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“A birthday greeting delivered three weeks early suggests panic rather than affection.”

“I am in a panic.”

“Conceal it. Write the letter, address it, stamp it, and place it in the hands of a responsible person.”

“You?”

I considered.

“No. Someone responsible.”

In the end we deposited it with his solicitor, who promised to post it on May seventh. Bobbie also ordered flowers, chocolates, books, scent, and a small diamond object which looked to me like a brooch but may have been a piece of agricultural machinery. He set three alarm clocks. He wrote MAY 8 on his walls. He memorized it forward, backward, and in French.

On May eighth he was awake at six.

At seven he telephoned the solicitor.

At eight he telephoned the florist.

At nine he telephoned Mary’s mother’s house and asked if the post had arrived. The maid refused to say.

At ten he walked there and stood on the opposite pavement.

At eleven a messenger brought him a note.

It contained four words:

**Thank you. I’m coming home.**

I found him at noon. He had the expression of a man reprieved while actually on the scaffold.

“She’s coming back,” he said.

“I knew she would.”

“You didn’t.”

“No, but one should always claim foresight after the event. It gives tone.”

“She’s coming this afternoon.”

“Splendid.”

He gripped my arm.

“Reggie, I owe it all to you.”

This should have warned me. Whenever a man says he owes everything to you, trouble is usually circling overhead like a vulture waiting for the luncheon gong.

I left him to prepare the flat. Then I went to my club and telephoned Mary.

This requires explanation.

From the beginning, I had known the date.

Mary had told me before she left. She had rung me up and said that Bobbie needed a shock.

“He doesn’t mean to be selfish,” she said. “That is what makes it worse. He thinks affection is something that can be kept in a drawer and taken out when convenient.”

“Very true,” I said, though I was not sure that affection fitted comfortably in drawers.

“I want him to understand what it feels like to be forgotten.”

“Quite.”

“You must promise not to tell him my birthday.”

“But suppose he asks?”

“He will ask.”

“Suppose he suffers?”

“He must suffer a little.”

I had hesitated. I am not hard. The sight of an old friend in anguish is unpleasant, especially when he keeps coming to one’s flat and drinking one’s best brandy.

But Mary had fixed me with those grey eyes.

“Promise.”

So I promised.

And I had kept the promise like a Roman sentinel. Through astrology, cats, theatre records, bank managers, parrots, Battersea warehouses, and frozen feet, I had said nothing. Mind you, I had helped. I had encouraged. I had supplied ideas and refreshments. But I had not betrayed Mary’s confidence.

I felt rather fine about it.

There are moments when a fellow sees himself as a strong, silent figure standing between conflicting duties. I had been loyal to Mary, helpful to Bobbie, and generally a model of what the well-dressed mediator should be. If medals were awarded for tact in private life, I felt that the King might reasonably send for me.

So I telephoned Mary in a glow of conscious virtue.

“Well,” I said, “the experiment has succeeded.”

“What experiment?”

“Bobbie. He found the date.”

“I know.”

“Not a hint from me.”

There was a silence.

“What?”

“I said I never told him.”

“You let him go through all that?”

“But you made me promise.”

“Didn’t you see how miserable he was?”

“Constantly.”

“And you said nothing?”

“Not a syllable. You may rely on Reggie Pepper.”

Her voice changed.

It became the sort of voice in which an iceberg might inform a ship that there had been a slight misunderstanding.

“You heartless brute.”

I nearly dropped the receiver.

“I beg your pardon?”

“You knew how unhappy he was.”

“Naturally. That was the idea.”

“My idea was to teach him a lesson, not to torture him.”

“I merely kept my promise.”

“You might have broken it.”

“One cannot go about breaking promises.”

“Why not?”

“Because—well, because one has promised.”

“You should have used your judgment.”

“My judgment told me to keep it.”

“Then your judgment is idiotic.”

This was unjust. No other word. Unjust.

“But, Mary—”

“He has lost weight.”

“Only a little.”

“He has been unable to sleep.”

“It has sharpened his mind wonderfully.”

“He caught cold in a warehouse.”

“He found the date.”

“You stood by and allowed it.”

“I did more than stand by. I went with him to the bank.”

“I shall never forgive you.”

The line went dead.

I remained holding the receiver.

It is in moments like this that one begins to understand martyrs. Not the grand martyrs, perhaps, with flames and lions and stained-glass windows, but the smaller domestic sort who keep promises and then discover that they have selected the one promise in the world which nobody wished them to keep.

I went round to Bobbie’s flat that evening. I did not particularly want to, but I had left a cigarette-case there and it was a favourite.

Mary had returned. I heard voices in the drawing-room—soft voices, affectionate voices, the sort of voices which indicate that a reconciliation is proceeding without need of outside assistance.

I rang.

Mary opened the door.

She looked beautiful. Happiness had softened her expression, though not, I noticed, in my direction.

“Oh,” she said. “It’s you.”

“Good evening.”

Bobbie appeared behind her.

For one hopeful instant I expected gratitude. After all, I had been with him through the whole campaign. I had shared the deductions. I had found the theatrical records. I had suggested the cheque. I had lent him money for taxis.

Then Mary said, “Bobbie, he knew all the time.”

Bobbie stared at me.

“Knew what?”

“My birthday.”

“Knew?”

“From the beginning.”

Bobbie’s face altered slowly.

“You knew it was May eighth?”

“Yes, but—”

“And you let me read those astrology books?”

“I was under oath.”

“You let me interview twenty-three actors?”

“Twenty-one. Two were stage-managers.”

“You let me spend six hours in that warehouse?”

“I did advise you to wear thicker socks.”

He looked at me with horror.

“My feet turned blue.”

“Temporarily.”

“You are a low hound, Reggie.”

Mary touched his arm.

“Don’t excite yourself, darling.”

“I won’t. But I never want to speak to him again.”

“Nor do I.”

This was solidarity. Marriage had done its work. A few hours earlier they had been separated by a gulf; now they stood shoulder to shoulder, united by the wholesome desire to kick me down the stairs.

I retrieved my cigarette-case and withdrew.

Since then Bobbie has improved astonishingly. He remembers birthdays, anniversaries, dinners, trains, and even the names of Mary’s relations. He keeps a diary and consults it. He sends flowers before he is reminded. Marriage, assisted by emotional dynamite, has awakened the sleeping machinery of his brain.

Mary is happy. Bobbie is happy. Their home is a model of domestic peace.

I alone am under a cloud.

When I meet them, Mary bows as if I were a tradesman whose account is disputed. Bobbie says “Ah” in a chilly voice and moves to the other side of the room. Last week they gave a dinner and invited every man in London except me, including Phipps, who eats asparagus with his fingers.

And all because I acted from the best motives.

That is the lesson. A man may lie, cheat at cards, forget to repay loans, and tell a woman that her new hat suits her, and society will continue to shake his hand. But let him act from the best motives, and sooner or later he will find himself dining alone while his friends explain to one another that they never trusted him.

There is probably one born every minute.

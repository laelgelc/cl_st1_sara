## The Bay Colt Learns to Work

The Bay Colt stood in his stall with his forefeet planted wide apart, his ears laid back, and his strong white teeth fastened upon the edge of the manger.

Gr-r-r-r! went his teeth upon the wood.

“Don’t do that,” said the Dappled Gray from the next stall. “Cribbing is a bad habit, and a Horse who begins it may keep on until his teeth are worn and his manger ruined.”

The Bay Colt stopped long enough to turn one eye toward him.

“I can stop whenever I choose,” he said.

Then he began again.

Gr-r-r-r! Gr-r-r-r!

The Dappled Gray sighed. He was an old Horse, and the black pits had long since disappeared from his front teeth. He considered this excellent proof that he had learned a thing or two.

“That is what young Horses always say,” he remarked. “They think being able to stop and being willing to stop are the same thing.”

“Pooh!” said the Bay Colt. “You old Horses are forever giving advice. When I am as old as you, perhaps I shall give advice too.”

“It is likely,” answered the Dappled Gray. “And the young Horses will think you know nothing.”

The Blind Horse, who stood beyond the Dappled Gray, gave a soft whinny. “I used to think my dam knew very little,” he said. “Afterward I found that she knew nearly everything I had to learn.”

The Bay Colt pulled a splinter from the manger and dropped it on the floor.

“My mother worries too much,” he said. “She worries if I run, and she worries if I stand still. She worries if I eat quickly, and she worries if I leave a wisp of hay. It is a wonder her mane has not turned white.”

“Perhaps,” said the Dappled Gray, “you have helped her worry.”

At that moment the Bay Colt’s mother looked over her stall door. She was a handsome Bay Mare, broad across the chest and marked upon one shoulder by the smooth place where the harness had rubbed through many seasons.

“Son,” she said, “leave the manger alone.”

“I wasn’t hurting it.”

“You were hurting yourself.”

“My teeth are strong.”

“They may not always be.”

“There! Everybody is at it again!” cried the Bay Colt. He tossed his head so quickly that a stem of hay caught in his forelock. “I wish people would mind their own affairs.”

The Gray Colt, his cousin, was eating quietly across the aisle. She was nearly his own age, but she had a gentle way of listening before she spoke.

“They do it because they care for you,” she said.

“I did not ask them to care for me.”

The barn became very still. Even the Hens scratching in the loose straw near the doorway seemed to pause, although Hens seldom pause when there is any possibility of finding grain.

The Bay Mare lowered her head and said nothing.

Now the Bay Colt was not wicked. You must understand that. He loved his mother very much, and if anything had frightened her, he would have run to her at once. But young creatures often say sharp things merely because good advice has touched the sore place in their pride. Then, when the sharp thing has been said, they are ashamed to take it back.

So the Bay Colt nibbled at his hay and pretended not to notice that his mother had turned away.

The Dappled Gray changed the subject.

“I remember,” he said, “when this young fellow would not stay in the pasture.”

The Bay Colt’s ears came forward.

“That was different.”

“It generally is,” said the Dappled Gray.

The pasture lay south of the barn, where the grass grew sweet and thick beside a willow-bordered stream. It was a very good pasture, with water, shade, and enough room for the Colts to gallop until their tails streamed behind them. Yet the Bay Colt had decided that the field beyond the fence must be better.

The first time he jumped out, his mother called after him.

“Come back! There are young apple trees in that field, and the Farmer does not want them trampled.”

“I shall not trample them,” said the Colt. “I am only looking.”

He arched his neck and trotted between the rows, taking great care to set one hoof directly beside a tender root and another upon a little mound of freshly turned earth.

Soon the Farmer came and led him back by the halter.

The next morning the Bay Colt jumped out again.

“Son,” said his mother, “the Farmer has mended the fence. You know he does not wish you to cross it.”

“Then why does he put such interesting things on the other side?”

“What interesting things?”

“Things I have not seen.”

His mother might have answered that nearly everything in the world was something he had not seen, and that this was no reason for breaking fences. But she knew he would not listen, so she only switched her tail sadly.

That afternoon he jumped into the lane and trotted as far as the bridge. A wagon rattled around the bend, and the driver had to pull his team sharply aside.

“Whoa there, you foolish young rascal!” called the man.

The Bay Colt sprang into the ditch, splashed through the mud, and returned to the pasture looking as though the adventure had been planned exactly that way.

“Were you frightened?” asked the Gray Colt.

“Not at all.”

“Your knees are shaking.”

“I like to shake them.”

The next day, and the next day, and the next, he went over the fence. If the Farmer raised one rail, the Bay Colt jumped higher. If he strengthened a weak corner, the Bay Colt chose another. Every escape made him more proud of his cleverness, although it was the sort of cleverness that brings everyone trouble and does no one good.

At last the Farmer said, “We must keep that fellow safe in spite of himself.”

He brought out a stout wooden bar, fastened it by a strap to the Colt’s neck, and let it hang before his knees. Farmers call such a contrivance a poke. It does not hurt a Horse who walks properly, but it makes jumping a fence exceedingly inconvenient.

The Bay Colt looked down.

“What is this?”

“A reminder,” said the Dappled Gray.

“I don’t need a reminder.”

The first time he tried to leap, the wooden bar struck the fence. The Colt landed in a heap on the pasture side, very much surprised and not at all dignified.

The Cows lifted their heads and chewed thoughtfully. The Sheep crowded near one another. The Hogs, who consider nearly every mishap amusing if it happens to somebody else, grunted together by the trough.

The Gray Colt hurried to him.

“Are you hurt?”

“Certainly not,” said the Bay Colt, scrambling up. “I meant to do that.”

He tried again at another place. Again the poke caught, and again he came down where he had begun.

For several days he was forced to carry the clumsy thing. It bumped his knees when he hurried and reminded him of his folly whenever he lowered his head to graze. Before long he began to understand that it was not the Farmer who had made his life troublesome. The Farmer had only met one inconvenience with another. The real trouble had begun when the Bay Colt refused to remain where he belonged.

“If I had stayed in the pasture,” he thought, “I should not be wearing this miserable stick.”

This was a sensible thought, and sensible thoughts are the first signs of improvement. When the poke was removed, the Colt did not jump the fence again.

For a time he was modest about it. Then he began to tell the other animals that he had never truly cared to run away.

“I was merely testing the fence,” he said. “Now that I know it is sound, there is no reason to jump it.”

The Dappled Gray said nothing. Older Horses sometimes know that a lesson which has entered only through the knees has not yet reached the heart.

Summer passed, and the Colts grew. Their legs no longer seemed quite too long for them, their chests widened, and their soft baby coats gave place to sleek hair that shone in the sun. The Bay Colt was particularly proud of himself. He had four unsplit hoofs, a thick mane, and a white star in the middle of his forehead. He thought the star gave him a distinguished appearance.

Two-year-old Horses often look more important than ten-year-old Horses and feel more so.

One morning the Farmer came into the pasture carrying a halter and a light snaffle-bit.

The Gray Colt walked to meet him.

“What do you suppose he wants?” whispered the Bay Colt.

“To begin our training, perhaps,” she answered.

“I won’t have a bit in my mouth.”

“You have never had one.”

“That is how I know I won’t like it.”

The Farmer rubbed the Gray Colt’s forehead, slipped on the halter, and led her to the barnyard. Her mother watched closely but did not call out.

The Farmer let the Gray Colt smell the bit. It was a smooth metal bar with rings at either end, much milder than a curb-bit and suited to a young Horse. He touched it to her lips. She drew back at first, for metal tastes strange to a Colt who has known only grass, grain, wood, and the occasional stolen apple.

“Steady, girl,” said the Farmer.

The Gray Colt trembled. She did not understand what was expected, and the straps hanging beside her face seemed alarming. Still, she heard the quietness in his voice. When he placed a thumb gently in the corner of her mouth, where a Horse has no teeth, she opened her jaws, and the bit slipped in.

It felt cold and awkward. She rolled it under her tongue and tossed her head once.

“That’s right,” said the Farmer.

He did not ask much of her that day. He led her around the barnyard, taught her to stop when the line tightened, and praised her when she followed. Afterward he removed the bit and gave her an apple core.

The Bay Colt had watched from behind the fence.

“Well?” he asked when she returned.

“It was strange,” said the Gray Colt, “but not terrible.”

“You submitted.”

“I learned.”

“Those are the same thing.”

“No,” she said. “Sometimes one must submit before one can learn, but learning is what matters.”

The Bay Colt snorted. “I shall never allow anyone to put iron in my mouth.”

His turn came the following day.

When the Farmer approached, the Bay Colt swung away. The Farmer followed without haste. The Colt trotted to the far corner, then wheeled and trotted back, hoping to show that catching him was impossible.

It was not impossible. The pasture had corners, the Farmer had patience, and the Bay Colt had not yet learned that the creature who wastes the most temper is usually the first to become tired.

At last the halter was buckled around his head.

“I won’t go!” cried the Colt, setting his forefeet.

The Farmer pulled steadily.

“I won’t go! I won’t go!”

His feet slid through the dust. Then the Farmer slackened the rope, stepped to one side, and clucked softly. The Colt, surprised by the release, took a step forward. The Farmer praised him.

The Bay Colt had meant to resist every step, but resisting properly requires almost as much attention as obeying, and presently he found himself in the barnyard.

When the bit touched his lips, he flung up his head. The Farmer waited. When it touched them again, the Colt struck at the ground.

“Steady,” said his mother from her stall.

“I am steady!”

“You are standing on three legs and waving the fourth.”

The Farmer stroked his nose and tried again. The Bay Colt clamped his jaws. Then, because he was busy planning how tightly to keep them closed, he forgot the toothless space at the side of his mouth. The Farmer placed his thumb there, and in another moment the bit was between the Colt’s teeth.

The Bay Colt threw himself backward.

The reins tightened, and the bit pressed against the corners of his mouth. He sprang forward, and the pressure ceased.

Instead of noticing this, he jerked backward again. The pressure returned.

Thus the Farmer was trying to teach him a very simple thing: yield, and the discomfort ends. But the Colt insisted upon teaching himself a much harder one: fight, and the discomfort continues.

He shook his head, pulled, backed, and tried to rub the bridle against the gatepost. The Farmer remained firm and calm. He neither struck the Colt nor shouted at him, for anger teaches fear more readily than wisdom.

After a short lesson the bridle was removed.

The Bay Colt’s mouth was sore.

“I knew it would hurt,” he grumbled that evening.

“You made it hurt,” said the Dappled Gray.

“The Farmer pulled.”

“You pulled against him.”

“That is the same thing.”

“It is not,” said the Blind Horse. “A closed door and a Colt pushing against a closed door may be in the same place, but only one of them is wasting strength.”

On the next day the Gray Colt learned to turn. When the left line tightened, she bent her neck and stepped left. When the right line tightened, she stepped right. She made mistakes, of course. Once she turned all the way around and faced the Farmer, looking pleased.

“That was too much,” he said, laughing.

“I thought perhaps it was,” she told the Bay Colt afterward.

“Then you made a fool of yourself.”

“Yes,” she answered cheerfully. “But I know better now.”

There is a great advantage in being willing to make a small mistake while learning. Those who are too proud to do a thing imperfectly often never learn to do it at all.

Soon the Farmer put a surcingle around the Gray Colt’s body and ran long lines through the rings. Then came the breast collar, the traces, and the light drag that bumped softly behind her. She started when she heard it, but the Farmer spoke to her, and she went on.

The Bay Colt watched through the rails.

“I suppose you enjoy being ordered about,” he said later.

“No,” replied the Gray Colt. “Sometimes I ache to kick up my heels and run away. Yesterday, when the gate stood open, I wanted to gallop down the lane more than I can tell you.”

“Why didn’t you?”

“Because I knew the Farmer trusted me.”

The Bay Colt had no answer to that, so he bit the manger.

Gr-r-r-r!

His sore mouth hurt at once, and he let go.

His own lessons grew more difficult because he made them so. When asked to turn, he stiffened his neck. When asked to stop, he rushed forward. When asked to go, he backed. The Farmer fitted no cruel curb-bit to conquer him, but continued with the mild snaffle and steady hands.

After each lesson the Colt returned sweating and angry. The corners of his mouth became tender, not because the Farmer sawed upon them, but because the Colt dragged against the bit.

Meanwhile the Gray Colt was learning to pull a light cart. She stepped between the shafts, stood while the harness was fastened, and moved when the Farmer spoke. Her ears flicked backward to catch his voice, and she carried her head with quiet attention.

One afternoon she passed the pasture gate drawing the little cart. The wheels rolled smoothly, the harness shone, and the Farmer sat behind her with loose lines.

The Bay Colt called, “Are you tired?”

“A little.”

“Are you afraid?”

“Not now.”

“Would you rather be playing?”

“Perhaps for an hour,” she said. “But I am proud to know how to do this.”

The Bay Colt watched until the cart disappeared beyond the willows.

That evening he stood over his manger, but he did not gnaw it. The barn was quiet. Dusty bars of sunset slanted through the windows, and the Cows breathed slowly in their stanchions. Somewhere in the loft a Mouse rustled under the hay.

The Bay Colt thought of the poke. He thought of the bruises he had earned while trying to jump with it, and of the sore places in his mouth. He thought of the Gray Colt stepping freely under the harness, with no tight line and no angry tossing of the head.

“It happened before,” he said to himself. “I blamed the poke, but I had caused it by jumping. Now I blame the bit, but I am the one pulling against it.”

That thought was not pleasant. The most useful thoughts are sometimes unpleasant at first, just as a bit is cold when it first touches the tongue.

His mother looked over the partition.

“Your mouth troubles you,” she said.

“A little.”

She waited.

“I suppose,” he continued, “that I have been foolish.”

“A little,” said his mother.

He gave a short whinny which was almost a laugh.

“Why did you not make me behave?”

“You were made to stay in the pasture,” she replied, “but that did not make you wise. Wisdom begins when you choose what is right.”

The following morning, when the Farmer brought the bridle, the Bay Colt stood still.

He opened his mouth.

The Farmer’s eyebrows rose, but he said only, “Good fellow.”

At the first tightening of the left line, the Colt’s old pride told him to pull right. He felt his neck stiffen. Then he remembered his sore mouth and the Gray Colt’s quiet steps. He bent left.

At once the line softened.

“Why,” thought the Colt, “that is much easier.”

The next signal asked him to stop. He stopped. The pressure vanished, and the Farmer rubbed his neck.

There was no miracle about his improvement. He still forgot. He still became impatient, and once, when a Pheasant burst from the grass, he jumped so far sideways that he nearly stepped upon a trace. But instead of turning each mistake into a battle, he listened and tried again.

He also stopped cribbing.

“I thought you could stop whenever you chose,” said the Dappled Gray.

“I can,” answered the Bay Colt.

The old Horse moved his ears mischievously.

“And when did you choose?”

“After I found out that saying I could was not the same as doing it.”

The Dappled Gray nodded. “Your teeth still have their black pits, but there may be hope for you.”

By autumn the Bay Colt could pull a cart, back between shafts, and stand quietly at a gate. In the spring the Farmer harnessed him beside his mother to the plow.

The field smelled of damp earth. Larks sang overhead, and beyond the fence the Colts of that year raced through the pasture. The Bay Colt looked at them and felt the old desire to toss his head and gallop away.

Then the Farmer called, “Get up!”

His mother leaned into her collar. The Bay Colt leaned with her. The traces tightened, the plowshare entered the ground, and a dark furrow opened behind them.

At first he pulled too fast. Then he matched his mother’s steady stride. Down the field they went, and back again, while furrow followed furrow, straight and even.

“Well done,” said the Farmer at noon.

The Bay Colt looked across the plowed field. He had helped make those long brown lines. Soon seed would be dropped there, and rain and sunshine would turn it green. His shoulders were tired, but it was a good tiredness, very different from the weariness of fighting.

His mother touched noses with him.

“You are becoming a useful Horse,” she said.

“Were you ashamed of me before?”

“I was worried.”

“And now?”

“Now I am proud.”

He glanced toward the young Colts frisking beyond the fence.

“I behaved very badly.”

His mother switched away a Fly. “Colts will be Colts,” she said. “But they need not remain Colts forever.”

So the Bay Colt learned that guidance is not an enemy to freedom, and that work willingly done brings a pleasure which idleness never gives. He had not lost his spirit; he had learned to govern it. And that, you know, is one of the hardest lessons for young Horses—and for young people too.

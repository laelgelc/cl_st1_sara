# THE TRUE AND COMPENDIOUS HISTORY  
## OF JOHN LONGBOWE, YEOMAN, NAVIGATOR, COURTIER, AND SCOURGE OF THE SPANIARD

*Compiled from the Testimony of the Author, who was present at most of these Matters, excepting such as he hath forgotten, together with several eminent Sources that disagree with him.*

### WHEREIN ARE SET FORTH A PLAYHOUSE, A QUEEN UPON ICE, A MISTAKEN CLOAK, PIRATES, TREASURE, LOVE (BRIEFLY), AND OTHER THINGS OF LESS CONSEQUENCE

I was born in the reign of Good Queen Bess, though whether upon a Tuesday or a Thursday I will not positively swear, there being already too much falsehood in Histories, and I, being a plain, blunt man, shall add none where it may be avoided. The year was fifteen hundred and—no matter. A date is but a nail whereon a pedant hangeth his cap; and I have known many a scrivener who could reckon backward unto Adam, yet not forward unto dinner.

My father called me John Longbowe, not because I shot with that weapon, as certain malapert antiquaries have printed, but because Longbowe was our name. I set this down to prevent controversy. The same persons have complayned that in old writing “ye” should be pronounced “the.” If so, why write a y? Letters are not geese, to go masquerading in one another’s feathers. I have ever pronounced a word as it looketh, save “colonel,” “boatswain,” and certain French terms which no Christian mouth can pronounce at all.

In my youth I entered the service of Henry Wriothesley, Earl of Southampton, a nobleman of great spirit, fine stockings, and uncommon patience toward poets. I accompanied him to London in that winter when the Thames froze from bank to bank, which some place in the year 1607, though Queen Elizabeth was then alive, as I know because I saw her. Therefore the calendars must be wrong.

London was at that time a noble city, composed chiefly of mud, steeples, apprentices, open sewers, foreign ambassadors, and men crying pies. Every second house was an inn and every third a prison, the inhabitants moving daily between the two according to their fortunes. We rode over London Bridge, beneath the heads of traitors, which were set upon spikes for the discouragement of political discussion; and my lord, looking up at them, said he hoped never to attain so high a place at Court.

“Your lordship hath not the head for it,” said I, comforting him.

He cuffed me, which was his familiar manner of expressing agreement.

Our business was at the Globe Theatre, a round wooden edifice upon Bankside, built like a barrel and containing much the same quality of wit. My lord had composed three tragedies, two comedies, a pastoral, and an entertainment wherein Neptune descended in a shell and praised the Earl of Essex for nine hundred and forty lines. The players had kept these manuscripts near six months, and Southampton, who desired only an honest judgement, had sworn to burn the theatre if they were rejected.

At the door we found a young Warwickshire lout holding horses. He had a smooth brow, a pointed beard, and that look of sleepy impudence which country lads acquire when they have learned to spell their names.

“Here, Will,” said one of the players, thrusting a bundle upon him, “give these papers back to his lordship.”

The fellow glanced at the first page.

“‘The Most Lamentable Tragedy of Prince Hamnet of Denmark,’” he read. “There is somewhat in the title, my lord, but your ghost talketh too long, your prince thinketh too much, and the fencing cometh late.”

“Who asked thee?” Southampton replyed.

“No man, and therefore my opinion is unbribed.”

My lord would have run him through, but I caught his arm, being ever merciful to the ignorant.

“Let the boy live,” said I. “Mayhap he will one day hold horses tolerably.”

The Warwickshire varlet bowed. “And mayhap, Master Longbowe, another man will one day write thy adventures tolerably.”

This was the first and last I heard of William Shakespeare, who, I am told, afterward became a wool merchant, poacher, actor, lawyer, philosopher, or all five. Some ascribe plays to him, but they are plainly Southampton’s, improved by being shortened.

Coming away from the Globe we heard music, bells, cannon, shouting, and the crash of crockery. The whole city was upon the frozen Thames, where booths had been set up selling hot ale, ribbons, roast ox, Dutch clocks, Italian gloves, Turkish sweetmeats and relics of saints not yet dead. An elephant drew a sledge past a company of skaters dressed as ancient Romans, while a printer struck off broadsheets entitled *A True Account of the Frost*, though the frost was still occurring and he had not looked outside.

In the midst of this revel came Queen Elizabeth herself, masked as Diana, with silver moons upon her stomacher and six courtiers dressed as nymphs, three of whom had beards. A Spanish ambassador, attempting a bow upon skates, lost his hat; Her Majesty kicked it the length of the river and said England would do the like to Spain. This pleased everybody except the ambassador, who had lately purchased the hat.

Then, whether by treachery, enchantment, or a piece of orange-peel, the Queen slipped. Southampton sprang forward, cast his cloak beneath her, and received Her Majesty upon it with a noise like a cupboard falling downstairs.

“Who art thou?” she demanded.

“Southampton, madam.”

“Then thy cloak is wetter than thy wit.”

She rose unharmed, gave him her hand to kiss, and passed on, while all cried that it was the noblest deed since Saint George had slain the dragon. Sir Walter Raleigh, who stood nearby carrying a new velvet mantle, looked extremely vexed, as though someone had stolen an idea from him.

Southampton’s cloak was soaked. Being of a delicate constitution where damp embroidery was concerned, he demanded mine, which was dry and bore upon the shoulder his silver badge.

“You may wear mine,” said he.

Thus I put on the Queen-wetted garment, and he departed in my sober cloak to flirt with a bearded nymph. This exchange, trivial in itself, altered my whole destiny; whereby we learn never to lend apparel to noblemen, particularly when kidnappers are abroad.

I had gone no more than fifty paces when a sack descended over my head. Four men seized me, trussed my arms and carried me away, saying, “We have the earl!” I could easily have overthrown them all, but, being a plain, blunt man, I wished first to discover their purpose. It is cowardice to flee danger before one hath ascertained how dangerous it is.

They bore me through lanes, down steps, into a boat, and thence aboard a ship anchored below the bridge. When the sack was removed, I stood before a lean fellow in black, with one eye, two daggers and no agreeable features.

“This is not Southampton,” said he.

The four kidnappers examined me.

“Art thou not the earl?” one asked.

“I have never said so.”

“He wore the cloak.”

“A cloak,” said I, “is not a peerage, though many at Court have little else.”

They debated throwing me ashore. Unfortunately, I had heard them mention Spain, Essex, gunpowder, the succession, and a secret list of names.

“He knoweth too much,” said the one-eyed man.

“I know nothing,” I assured him. “Moreover, I remember less.”

This honest declaration failed to satisfy them. As the tide had turned, they sent me to sea, intending that wind, water, foreign prisons, pirates, hunger or boredom should silence me. In this they were nearly successful, for no sooner did the ship pass Gravesend than I became sick.

Certain enemies have alleged that I lay three days beneath a gun, embracing a bucket and begging the sailors to kill me. The truth is that I was studying the motion of the vessel at close quarters. I ate nothing because provisions were bad, spoke little because conversation is vanity, and groaned only to encourage the men at the pumps. Upon the fourth day I arose, pale but terrible, and asked where England was.

“Behind us,” said a sailor.

“Then sail backward.”

He laughed, and I knocked him down, after which discipline improved greatly.

The vessel was the *Merry Defiance*, commanded by Captain Rafe Bloodworthy, who traded in spices, prisoners and things found unattended. His flag displayed a red grinning skull upon black cloth. I told him the design lacked gaiety, took up white paint, and added two crossed bones and a broad smile. I called it the Jolly Roger after Roger, our cook, who laughed whilst boiling a Spaniard’s boot for soup. All subsequent pirates copied me, though few paid the customary fee.

### CHAPTERS I TO XX  
### CONTAINING WARS, STORMS, MONSTERS, MUTINIES, AND OTHER ORDINARY BUSINESS

We pursued the Spanish Main, though it seldom remained where our charts placed it. We captured a caravel by approaching under French colours, an offence excused by the fact that Frenchmen would have done the same had they thought of it. We boarded another ship at midnight; I leapt first upon her deck, or third, according to whether one counts the two men who fell before reaching it. At Porto Bello I received a sword-cut, at Cartagena a musket-ball, and at Hispaniola a coconut, the last being by far the most severe.

Captain Bloodworthy was brave, as cruel men frequently are when surrounded by persons compelled to protect them. He flogged the crew for sleeping, waking, swearing, praying, eating too much, eating too little and looking at him with the left eye. At length the men came to me.

“Master Longbowe,” said they, “we mean to kill the captain.”

“Nay,” I replyed. “Mutiny is a bloody and unlawful remedy.”

“What then?”

“Leave it to me. I shall prevent the shedding of precious Christian blood.”

That night I entered Bloodworthy’s cabin and found him asleep beside an open window, if a round hole in a ship may be called a window. Waking him would have caused dispute, and dispute might have led to violence. I therefore lifted him gently, conveyed him through the hole, and dropped him into the Atlantic.

He struck the sea without spilling a drop of blood.

Some say he cried my name as the ship passed on. This merely showeth his gratitude. Others contend sharks consumed him; but I saw no sharks, having gone below to search his papers.

The crew elected me captain unanimously after I reminded them that the powder-room key was in my pocket. As captain I abolished unnecessary flogging, retaining only such as was needful for order, amusement and Sunday observance. I divided plunder equally: one half for the ship, one quarter for the captain, one quarter for repairs, and the remainder among the men. Certain arithmeticians objected there was no remainder. Sailors ought not meddle with higher mathematics.

Among Bloodworthy’s possessions I discovered a map drawn upon goatskin. It showed a crooked island, three palms, a hill shaped like a bishop’s nose and a cross marked TREASURE. Wrapped with it was a letter from the Reverend Obadiah Pinch, a clergyman whom Bloodworthy had transported from Plymouth. Pinch declared that the treasure belonged to a pagan king and should be recovered for holy purposes, by which I understood that he desired a portion.

I swore that, if Providence brought me to the island indicated, the Reverend Pinch should receive his just share after deduction of expenses, wages, victuals, powder, sailcloth, interest, hazard, management and postage.

We sailed nine months. There were storms wherein mountains of water swept the deck; calms wherein the sea lay like melted pewter; serpents longer than churches; fish with wings; birds without feet; and a mermaid who sang outside my cabin until I threw a bottle at her. All these I omit for brevity, having no love of boasting.

At last our lookout sighted an island answering the map in every particular save that it was round instead of crooked, had five palms instead of three, and its hill resembled a turnip rather than a bishop’s nose. Our navigator said it lay seven hundred leagues from the position marked.

“Maps shrink with age,” I said. “This is the place.”

The islanders came down to the beach in canoes, wearing feathers and gold ornaments and displaying no weapons. They appeared friendly, which made me suspect treachery. I had our masts cut halfway through, our sails torn, and the ship smeared with soot and pig’s blood, so that she seemed a wreck. The men lay upon deck pretending death, an art in which Roger the cook excelled because he had been drunk since Barbados.

I alone stood at the rail, supported by a bandage about my head.

“Help!” I cried. “We are harmless merchants afflicted by storm, plague, famine and low prices!”

The islanders, being innocent of commercial practice, believed me. Some fifty came aboard bearing fruit, water and baskets of bright stones. Once they had laid aside their paddles, my crew rose.

A melancholy misunderstanding followed. I had intended only to detain our visitors while we negotiated, but they objected to being bound; the men mistook their cries for war songs; somebody discharged a pistol; and within a short space all were dead. Thus was a greater battle prevented, and the shedding of precious blood kept within moderate limits.

I grieved, naturally. Yet sorrow should not make us neglect opportunity. The stones in their baskets were emeralds, rubies, diamonds and nuggets of gold, any one of which might have supported a bishop for several weeks. We went ashore and found, beneath an idol of solid silver, a chamber piled with treasure: crowns, cups, chains, pearls, plates, idols, ingots and coins stamped with the head of Alexander the Great, Julius Caesar or Prester John—the faces were much alike.

We loaded until the *Merry Defiance* sat so low that fishes looked down upon her deck. As to the surviving inhabitants, they withdrew inland, no doubt overcome by our manners.

On returning to sea my mate asked what share belonged to Reverend Pinch.

“None,” said I.

“But the map was his.”

“The map showeth a crooked island with three palms.”

“This island had five.”

“And was round. Plainly it is not the reverend gentleman’s island. Would you have me cheat him by offering treasure to which his map gave no title?”

The mate confessed himself answered, though with a sour expression common among men defeated by reason.

We had been absent from England either four years or eleven; I cannot be exact, for our almanac was eaten during a famine and the ship’s bell had been pawned in Jamaica. During that time I fought the Spaniard upon seven seas, though geographers name but five; escaped hanging at Lisbon by proving I was another man; ruled an island for a fortnight; converted forty pirates to temperance after the rum was exhausted; and buried treasure in three places, one of which I afterward remembered.

These exploits would fill fifty volumes. Being a plain, blunt man, I pass them over.

We reached Plymouth beneath a sky of English grey, never so beautiful because nowhere else possesseth so many shades of it. The harbour officials boarded us and asked whether we carried goods subject to duty.

“Nothing,” said I, standing before a hold full of gold.

They looked at the broken masts, patched sails and starving crew, and believed me. Honesty is the best disguise.

I hastened to London to find Southampton, wearing pearls in my hat and six rings upon each hand, not from vanity but that wealth should be kept where one may observe it. London seemed changed. The mud was deeper, the prisons fuller, and smoking tobacco had become fashionable among persons who desired to smell like chimneys.

At Southampton House a porter told me the earl was dead.

“Dead?” I said. “He was alive when last I saw him.”

“So are most men.”

I further learned that Queen Elizabeth was dead, Essex was dead, Raleigh imprisoned or dead depending upon the day, several of my lord’s companions executed, and a Scot sat upon the throne. This last report I judged the least credible, but publickly accepted it after seeing the new King’s portrait.

Thus doth Time ravage greatness. Men who had strutted in silk now fed worms; courtiers who had owned a hundred cloaks had not one winding-sheet to spare; and Will Shakespeare, the horse-boy, had become celebrated, which proved the world entirely overturned.

I made discreet enquiry concerning the conspiracy that had caused my abduction. Every conspirator had either been hanged, pardoned, knighted, or made a government adviser. There was therefore no advantage in revealing what I knew. Silence, when profitable, is the crown of patriotism.

Here I should conclude, but my wife Marian insisteth that I mention her.

Marian Straitways was the daughter of a Kentish gentleman and had known me before my voyage. It seemeth—though I paid little attention—that after my disappearance she disguised herself as a shipboy, crossed half the world, served aboard the *Merry Defiance* under the name of Martin, and several times saved my life. She claims she warned me of Bloodworthy’s knife, steered through the hurricane, and prevented the crew from roasting me when provisions failed. Such domestic embellishments are natural in a woman.

I do remember a slight lad who followed me everywhere, fought neatly with a rapier, and wept when I proposed marriage to a governor’s widow. I supposed the boy suffered from fever.

Later a Portuguese villain named Don Alonzo de Vile abducted Marian from our ship, having discovered her sex before I did. I pursued him to a fortress, climbed a wall, crossed a moat full of crocodiles, fought twelve guards, swung from a tapestry, broke through a window and found Marian already free, having struck Don Alonzo with a stool. I then rescued her by leading her back to the boat.

We were married upon my return, chiefly because she had retained the key to one chest of emeralds. She is a good wife, though inclined to correct my dates, my navigation and my account of our courtship. Romance, however, is a trifling matter fit for minstrels, serving-women and persons unequal to real adventure. I shall say no more of it.

I settled at Longbowe Hall, which I purchased from a noble family ruined by honesty. Over the gate I placed the Jolly Roger, my own invention. In the great chamber hang Southampton’s wet cloak, Bloodworthy’s sword, the goatskin map and the Spanish ambassador’s hat, which came into my possession by means too dull to relate.

Now, in my age, scholars accuse me of confusion, pirates accuse me of cheating, Reverend Pinch’s heirs accuse me of theft, and Marian accuseth me of leaving out all the best parts. Let them write their own histories. I have set down nothing but truth, except where truth was uncertain, unbecoming, tedious, dangerous to my reputation, or likely to provoke an action at law.

This much I shall say: I served my Queen, though for a short time I did not know she had died; I spared blood whenever a quieter method offered; I enriched England by bringing home wealth that had previously enriched foreigners; and I rose from yeoman to gentleman without surrendering that plain bluntness which hath ever been my chief ornament.

If any man doubt these deeds, let him seek the witnesses. Most are dead, lost, transported, hostile, married to me, or unable to write.

What stronger proof could an honest chronicler desire?

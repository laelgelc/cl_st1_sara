### The Maddox Apple Tree

The village of West Dunham was tidy even for New England, where tidiness is not merely a condition of cupboards and door-yards, but frequently an article of faith. Its white houses, though most of them were small, had the air of having been washed behind the ears. Their green blinds hung straight; their doorsteps were scrubbed to a pallor; their fences retained every picket and seemed conscious of the fact. Lilacs flowered where lilacs had been appointed to flower, syringas kept decently to the corners, and the currant bushes stood in disciplined ranks behind the kitchens. Even the elms, which could not be held altogether responsible for their manner of growth, arched over the road with a certain sober conformity.

There was only one serious offense to the general eye, and that was the Maddox place.

It stood at the western end of the village, where the road dipped toward the brook, directly opposite the neat gray house of Sarah and Edison Blake. The Maddox house had never been painted within the memory of anybody under seventy, and the few scraps of ancient yellow which remained upon it served only to expose its nakedness. The roof sagged, the chimney leaned, and a broken gutter hung from the eaves like an arm which had long ago relinquished hope of assistance. One front window was stopped with a quilt in winter and with nothing in particular in summer. The door-yard was raw earth, scratched into hollows by hens and children; burdocks besieged the back step; a rusty stove, put out for temporary disposal eleven years before, supported morning-glories and two cracked milk pans.

In West Dunham there were poor people whose poverty was treated with respect, because they struggled against it in a becoming manner. The Maddoxes did not struggle. They accepted poverty with a spacious, almost hospitable calm which affronted those who regarded misfortune as a refractory substance to be scrubbed, stitched, salted, or put down in the cellar.

Sam Maddox worked when work was brought very near him and explained with unusual clearness. He could saw wood, mend a wall, load hay, dig potatoes, or drive a team; but between the knowledge that a thing ought to be done and the performance of it there extended in his nature a pleasant interval, generally occupied by sitting down. His wife, Adeline, possessed the same gentleness in a smaller form. She was thin and faded, with soft brown eyes and a patient smile. She could cook when there was anything to cook, sew when the rent became socially embarrassing, and wash when the children ceased to resemble members of the family; but she had no genius for pursuing dirt into its private retreats.

The children—there were six, though at certain hours there appeared to be nine—grew as burdocks grew, sturdily and without supervision. They were earth-stained, sun-browned, ragged at knee and elbow, and as peaceable as young animals after feeding. They quarreled seldom, perhaps because there was so little property to divide. They played in the road, under the house, behind the stove, and in the branches of the great apple tree which redeemed the Maddox premises every May.

That tree was a miracle which the village found it difficult to reconcile with justice. It rose at the right of the house, enormous yet symmetrical, its trunk sound, its boughs curving outward and upward with the ease of perfect strength. When it blossomed, the ruin beneath it became subordinate. The sagging roof, the rags at the window, the hens in the burdocks, and the children in garments of no ascertainable color were all covered, if not actually, at least in the merciful judgment of the eye, by a dome of rose and pearl.

The inflorescence was so profuse that the tree seemed less to wear blossoms than to have been transmuted into blossom. It panted and sang and shouted with perfume. Bees hung about it in a golden confusion. Petals drifted upon the bare ground and made of that neglected soil a carpet which no housewife in West Dunham, however industrious, could have woven or kept clean.

People paused to look at the tree and then remembered whose tree it was.

“A sight o’ waste,” said Sarah Blake every spring. “Never sprayed, never pruned, never banked proper. Half the apples wormy, and them Maddox young ones eat the rest green.”

Sarah had a pear tree of her own. It stood behind the kitchen, neatly limed and pruned into a shape resembling a determined hand. It produced seven pears one year and eleven the next, all hard, sound, and capable of remaining so until required for Thanksgiving pies. Sarah valued fruit according to the length of time it could be prevented from giving pleasure.

Sarah herself was a small woman, spare and alert, with bright eyes and a sharp nose. She suggested a bird which had found the world sadly behind with its nest-building. Her hair, still dark though she was past sixty, was drawn tightly from her forehead; her skirts cleared her ankles for action; and her hands, thin and knotted, were rarely without a cloth, broom, knife, pan, needle, or some other implement of correction.

Her husband, Edison, was seventy-two, stout, mild, and slow. He had once been a moderately active man, but years of marriage to Sarah had reduced activity in him to a covert and defensive form. He could remain motionless with remarkable intention. His pauses were not empty; they were barricades. When Sarah told him to bring wood, he sat while he considered what pieces she meant, whether the basket would hold them, and whether the weather did not threaten rain. Often, during these meditations, Sarah brought the wood herself.

This did not soften her opinion of him.

“Edison Blake,” she would say, standing in a doorway with both hands occupied, “if you ain’t the beatin’est man ever was created, I’d like to know who is.”

Edison never offered a rival candidate. He had learned that agreement was useless and contradiction unsafe.

One bright morning in May, Sarah began her spring housecleaning in earnest. She had been housecleaning in a preliminary manner for ten days, but preliminaries, like most virtues, have their limit. The sun shone warmly, the village yards glittered, and the Maddox apple tree across the road billowed against a tender blue sky; but Sarah saw chiefly that the sitting-room windows were clouded at the corners and that winter had persisted under the braided mat.

“Edison,” she called, “you fetch down them chamber curtains.”

Edison, who was sitting on the back step, looked at the pear tree.

“Hey?” he said, although his hearing was good.

“The chamber curtains. Fetch ’em down. I’m goin’ to bile ’em.”

“Ain’t they clean?”

“Clean! They’ve hung there since October.”

“Well,” said Edison, “they ain’t been doin’ much.”

Sarah appeared in the doorway.

“Ain’t been doin’ much! You think dirt has to work its way in with a crowbar? You think dust sends notice afore it settles? Fetch them curtains.”

Edison rose. He climbed the stairs slowly, not because he was incapable of climbing them faster, but because every step taken slowly was one step less in the unknown labors ahead. He unpinned the curtains and brought them down trailing.

“Hold ’em up!” cried Sarah. “Don’t drag ’em over every stair edge from here to creation.”

“I’m holdin’ ’em.”

“You’re holdin’ one end and cleanin’ the stairs with the other.”

She seized the curtains, carried them to the kitchen, and plunged them into a tub with the air of drowning an enemy. Edison returned to the sitting-room and lowered himself into a cane-seated chair by the window.

From there he could see Sam Maddox lying on the bare ground under the apple tree. Sam was not asleep. He had one knee raised and his broad hands clasped behind his shaggy head, and he was looking straight upward into the blossoms. Three children lay near him in similar attitudes. The smallest, a girl in a red dress faded to the color of dried sumac, was opening and closing her mouth as petals fell upon her face.

Edison sighed.

Sarah entered carrying a feather bed doubled over her arms.

“Get up,” she said.

“I jest set down.”

“I know it. You generally do. Get up and help me beat this.”

“Ain’t it been beat?”

“It was beat last fall.”

“Seems to me things don’t stay done no time.”

“They don’t in this house.”

They dragged the feather bed to the back porch and attacked it. Sarah beat it with two carpet sticks, rapidly and with personal feeling. Edison struck at intervals. Feathers swelled and rolled within the ticking like a live thing attempting escape.

“Harder!” said Sarah.

“I’m hittin’.”

“You’re pattin’ it. I ain’t askin’ you to put a baby to sleep.”

Edison struck harder. A small white feather worked through the seam and sailed into the sunshine. He watched it cross the yard with an attention which Sarah considered morbid.

“Other side,” she ordered.

They turned the bed. Edison breathed heavily.

“You pooty near tired out?” Sarah asked.

“I be tired out.”

“You ain’t begun.”

This information removed something essential from Edison. He followed her back into the sitting room, where chairs stood upside down, tables had migrated from their customary places, and the carpet, half loosened, disclosed a floor which Sarah regarded with ascetic disapproval.

“Stand on that chair,” she said, “and take down the curtain pole.”

Edison looked at the chair. It was an old maple chair inherited from Sarah’s mother, with a cane seat whose central depression had deepened through several generations of use.

“I dunno as that seat’s safe.”

“It held your father when he weighed two hundred and eighteen.”

“My father’s been dead thirty year.”

“That ain’t weakened the chair.”

Edison wished to say that it had not strengthened it, but Sarah’s expression was compelling. He put one foot upon the cane.

“It’s crackin’.”

“It’s always cracked.”

He placed his weight upon it and reached for the pole. At that instant the cane seat, which had long maintained a negative opposition to the demands of human life, gave way completely. Edison descended through the chair until his arms caught beneath the back and his knees doubled above the front rung. The curtain pole came down with him, struck the mantel, and rolled under the sofa.

For a second there was silence.

Then Edison said, “Sarah.”

Sarah, who had been gathering tacks from the carpet, turned.

“Well, of all the performances!”

“I fell through.”

“I can see you did.”

“The chair broke.”

“You broke the chair. That chair was my mother’s.”

Edison attempted to rise, but the broken cane gripped his coat tails and the chair rose with him. He sat down again abruptly.

“I’m ketched,” he said.

Sarah tugged at the chair. Edison groaned.

“Don’t haul me that way.”

“I’ve got to haul you some way.”

“I feel dretful queer.”

“You look ridiculous.”

“Head’s goin’ round.”

Sarah ceased pulling. Her sharp face changed, though not so much that alarm entirely displaced irritation. She put a hand on his forehead, then opened the front door and looked across the road.

Sam still lay beneath the apple tree.

“Sam Maddox!” she called.

Sam did not move. Distance had always affected his hearing unfavorably when work might be involved.

“Sam! Sam Maddox!”

One of the children sat up and poked him.

“Mis’ Blake’s hollerin’, Pa.”

“Hey?” said Sam.

“She wants ye.”

Sam rolled to one elbow and looked across the road. Sarah stood in her doorway waving both arms.

“Come over here this minute!” she cried.

Sam rose by gradual stages. He was a large man, loosely constructed, with long arms, stooping shoulders, and a brown beard which appeared never to have been fully introduced to a comb. His clothes sagged upon him sympathetically. He crossed the road with a gait which might have been called a shuffle if it had not covered the ground so effectively.

“What ye want, Mis’ Blake?”

“Edison’s fell through a chair.”

Sam looked toward the house.

“Fell clear through?”

“Come and get him out. He says he’s dizzy.”

Sam’s face became attentive. Idleness in him was profound, but it was not unkind. He entered the sitting room and saw Edison imprisoned amid the disordered furniture.

“Wal,” he said, “you done a thorough job.”

“I didn’t aim to,” said Edison faintly.

“Quit talkin’ and get him out,” said Sarah.

Sam knelt and examined the chair. He did not pull at once. He broke two splintered strips of cane, freed the cloth caught beneath Edison, and then lifted the old man bodily, chair and all, with surprising gentleness. Edison was stout, but Sam handled him as he handled the smallest of his children when they fell asleep outdoors.

“Easy,” Edison whispered.

“Sartain.”

Sam set him on his feet. Edison swayed. His face had lost color, and perspiration stood upon his forehead.

“He’s goin’ to faint,” said Sam.

“He never fainted in his life,” Sarah replied, as if previous inexperience were a prohibition.

Edison’s knees bent.

Sam caught him under the arms and carried him to the sofa. He swept two curtain rings and a dust cloth from it with one hand, laid Edison down, and put a cushion beneath his head.

“Fetch him some water,” he said.

Sarah fetched it, because there are tones which even the habitually compelling obey. Edison drank, shut his eyes, and continued to breathe.

“He’s all right,” said Sarah after a moment. “Only scairt.”

“I be dizzy,” Edison murmured.

“You set too long on the back step in the sun.”

“Wa’n’t in the sun.”

“You know what I mean.”

Sam stood beside the sofa, looking down at him. The old man opened one eye. In that furtive glance passed a complete account of thirty-seven years of housecleanings, wood boxes, curtain poles, feather beds, braided mats, Thanksgiving preparations, preserving seasons, and morally unavoidable errands.

“Better lay still,” said Sam.

“I can’t have him layin’ there all day,” Sarah protested. “I’ve got the carpet to take up.”

“Then you better not take it up over him.”

Sarah compressed her lips. She looked at Edison, at the disemboweled chair, at the loosened carpet, and finally at Sam.

“You can help me shake the sitting-room rug.”

Sam blinked.

“Hey?”

“You heard me. Edison ain’t fit, if he’s goin’ to act this way. It won’t take ten minutes with two men.”

“There’s only me,” said Sam.

“I know there’s only you. I said Edison ain’t fit. You take one end and I’ll take the other.”

Sam glanced through the open door toward his apple tree. The children had resumed their places beneath it. Adeline had come out and was sitting on the back step shelling peas into a yellow bowl. There were not enough peas to require attention, but she was giving it cheerfully.

“I dunno,” said Sam.

“I’ll pay you.”

He looked offended, though mildly.

“I don’t take pay for helpin’ folks when they’re hurt.”

“Edison ain’t hurt. He’s dizzy.”

“Same family of trouble.”

“I ain’t askin’ you to make him dizzy. I’m askin’ you to shake a rug.”

Sam rubbed his beard.

“I’ll shake it. Don’t want no pay.”

Sarah’s respect for him rose a reluctant inch and was immediately checked by the certainty that no respectable man should have leisure to give away in the middle of a weekday.

They rolled the carpet and dragged it into the yard. Sarah backed through the door, issuing directions at every step.

“Not that corner. This corner. Don’t scrape it. Lift! Lift, Sam! There’s a tack in it. Stop steppin’. Wait till I say. Now.”

Sam, who could carry nearly twice what Sarah could, submitted to being instructed in the management of every ounce.

They bore the carpet to the line between two trees. One was Sarah’s pruned pear tree; the other was an apple tree of exemplary uprightness which bore little fruit and less shade. The carpet sagged between them.

“Take hold there,” Sarah ordered.

Sam took hold.

“Higher.”

He raised his hands.

“Not so high.”

He lowered them.

“You ain’t got no medium in you?”

“Dunno,” said Sam.

They shook. A pale cloud rose from the carpet.

“See that?” cried Sarah triumphantly. “And Edison said it wa’n’t dirty.”

“He didn’t say that.”

“He would have.”

Dust poured down. Sam sneezed.

“Don’t let go!” Sarah cried.

“I didn’t.”

“You come nigh it.”

They shook until the rug appeared exhausted and Sarah was not. Then she spread it on the grass and beat it with a broom. Sam looked toward the house. Edison’s face was visible above the sofa back.

“Think I’d better see how he is,” Sam said.

“He’s restin’.”

“Could be worse.”

“He could be helpin’.”

Nevertheless, Sam went inside. Edison opened both eyes when he approached.

“She want me?” he whispered.

“Nope.”

“I ain’t dizzy now.”

“You lay low.”

Edison stared at him.

“Hey?”

“Lay low,” Sam repeated. “You’re pooty bad off yet.”

A faint, almost youthful intelligence came into Edison’s face.

“How bad?”

“Bad enough so’s you can’t lift nothin’ nor stand on chairs.”

“I might be bad till supper.”

“Sartain.”

Sarah entered behind them.

“How is he?”

Sam turned with a solemnity which sat strangely upon his shaggy face.

“Dizzy.”

“I ain’t heard him say so.”

“Dizzy folks don’t always feel like talkin’.”

Edison closed his eyes.

Sarah looked from one man to the other. She suspected the existence of masculine treachery, but the suspicion lacked a handle. Sam’s countenance was as innocent and unenlightened as a pasture rock.

“Well,” she said, “if he’s got to lay there, come help me move the bureau.”

The bureau had not been included in Sam’s original understanding of the rug. After the bureau came a washstand. After the washstand came the chamber mattress, which Sarah wished turned, beaten, aired, inspected, and turned again. A clock had to be lifted from a shelf so that she might wash behind it; a stovepipe had to be carried to the shed; two boxes of winter apples had to be moved from one cellar corner to another because the first corner had become damp in March.

All things have a bay toward which they drift, and Sarah Blake’s tasks drifted toward Sam.

He obeyed with a patience which was partly kindness, partly inability to frame a decisive refusal, and partly the vast indifference of a nature which did not measure time in useful divisions. At noon Sarah gave him bread, cheese, doughnuts, and coffee. He ate on the back step while she stood over him discussing the disgraceful condition of his own premises.

“You could have a decent place if you’d stir yourself,” she said.

“Could, mebbe.”

“That house wants shinglin’.”

“Wants it dretful.”

“And paint.”

“Yep.”

“And them burdocks dug.”

“They ain’t spread much this year.”

“They’re all over the yard!”

“Then they won’t need to spread.”

Sarah stared at him. Sam drank his coffee.

“You’ve got no ambition,” she said.

“Mebbe not.”

“What do you expect them children of yours to come to?”

“Supper, mostly. They generally come to that.”

“I mean when they grow up.”

Sam considered. Beyond the yard, the crown of his apple tree rose in sunlit blossom over the road. Two of his boys had climbed high among the boughs. Their bare feet and brown legs showed between clusters of bloom.

“Growin’ pooty good now,” he said.

“You let ’em run like wild Injuns. Ain’t one of ’em got a whole suit of clothes.”

“They’d outgrow it.”

“Other folks’ children outgrow clothes.”

“Then we’re all even.”

Sarah gave up, not because she recognized defeat, but because she remembered the cellar shelves.

During the afternoon she washed those shelves, and Sam held the lamp. She scoured stone jars and made him carry them into the yard. She discovered a loose hinge, a warped board, a nest of mice, and a stain upon the pantry ceiling. Each discovery created labor as naturally as the apple tree created perfume.

Edison remained on the sofa.

At intervals Sarah approached him.

“You dizzy now?”

“A mite,” he answered, following Sam’s instruction with a delicacy unusual in him.

“Where?”

“In my head.”

“Where do you expect to be dizzy?”

Edison considered whether an answer existed and wisely rejected the inquiry.

Once, when Sarah had gone to the shed for soft soap, he sat up.

“You’re a good man, Sam,” he said.

Sam was stretching his back near the window.

“Hey?”

“You’re a good man.”

“Dunno ’bout that.”

“You’ve done more work today than you’ve done in a month.”

“Mebbe that proves I ain’t used to it.”

Edison looked across the road. Adeline had set a pot upon a little outdoor fire, either because the kitchen stove smoked or because no one had thought to bring in wood. The children gathered around her. A baby sat in a wooden box and played with a spoon. Over them the apple tree moved softly in the afternoon wind.

“Pooty over there,” Edison said.

“Yep.”

“Sarah’s pear tree don’t blossom like that.”

“Nope.”

“She says yours ought to be pruned.”

“Likely.”

“You goin’ to prune it?”

Sam looked alarmed.

“Wouldn’t tech it.”

“Why not?”

“Might stop doin’ that.”

He nodded toward the bloom.

Edison sighed and lay down quickly as Sarah returned.

Near four o’clock she led Sam to the braided mat. It was an old mat, oval and prodigious, composed of garments worn by several generations of Blakes and therefore invested with ancestry as well as dust. Sarah had taken it from beneath the dining-room table and decided that it must be shaken before the day’s work could properly close.

Sam regarded it with fatigue.

“That’s a heavy one,” he said.

“Rags don’t weigh much.”

“Enough rags do.”

“Take that end.”

They carried it outside. Sam’s arms ached from the bureau, his back from the mattress, and his shoulders from the carpet. Labor, which regular persons encounter gradually and become hardened against, had descended upon him in its entirety, like weather upon a traveler without shelter.

They shook the mat.

“Harder,” Sarah said.

Sam shook harder.

“Snap it.”

He snapped it.

“Together! You pull when I push.”

“I’m watchin’.”

“You’re lookin’ at that tree.”

Sam had indeed turned his eyes toward the Maddox apple tree. The descending sun shone level through it, kindling every blossom edge. The tree appeared to contain light rather than merely receive it.

“Never saw so much bloom,” he said.

“Bloom ain’t apples.”

“Nope.”

“And your apples don’t keep.”

“Young ones eat ’em.”

“They eat ’em before they’re fit.”

“Fit them.”

“You could gather ten bushel and sell ’em if you’d spray and sort.”

“Mebbe.”

“You could put away the sound ones for winter.”

“Never seem to last till winter.”

“Because you don’t take care of ’em.”

Sam smiled slowly.

“They taste good in August.”

Sarah snapped the rug with unnecessary violence.

“Anybody can eat a thing the minute they see it. The point is to save.”

“What for?”

“Thanksgiving, for one thing.”

Sam pondered this. Thanksgiving lay six months away, with snow, hard ground, empty trees, and a great deal of preparation between.

“Long wait,” he said.

“You don’t expect to have anything worth havin’ unless you wait and work for it, do you?”

Sam looked at the apple tree again.

“Mebbe not,” he said, “but there’s that.”

The argument could proceed no further because the braided mat, perhaps resenting the strain of opposed philosophies, slipped from Sarah’s hands, wrapped itself around Sam’s legs, and brought him to the ground.

Sarah cried out. Sam sat in the middle of the mat, blinking.

“You hurt?” she asked.

“Nope.”

“You tore it?”

He examined the rug.

“Nope.”

“Well, get up. We ain’t done.”

Sam rose, slower than he had risen that morning. They shook the mat until Sarah admitted no further dust could reasonably be expected from it. Then they carried it inside. The sitting-room carpet had to be laid, the chairs returned, and the broken cane chair removed to the shed. Sarah mourned the chair with a severity she had not shown over Edison.

“My mother set in that chair,” she said.

“So did Edison,” Sam replied.

“He needn’t have stood in it.”

“You told him.”

“I didn’t tell him to go through it.”

At last the rooms were restored. They appeared precisely as they had appeared before, except to Sarah, who could perceive their resurrection. The windows shone, the curtains hung damp and immaculate, the braided mat lay subdued beneath the dining table, and the house smelled of soap, wet cloth, dust, coffee, and moral accomplishment.

Edison sat up cautiously.

“How you feel?” Sarah demanded.

“Some better.”

“You’ve had rest enough.”

Sam met his eye.

“Better not push it,” he said. “Dizzy spells come back.”

Edison reclined with gratitude.

Sarah brought her purse from the pantry and took out a silver half-dollar.

“There,” she said to Sam. “You earned it.”

Sam shook his head.

“I said I didn’t want pay.”

“You’ve worked all day.”

“Didn’t come to work. Come ’cause Edison fell through.”

“You done work.”

“Couldn’t help it, the way things kept turnin’ up.”

“Take it.”

“Nope.”

“I don’t owe folks.”

“You don’t.”

“I do if you won’t take it.”

Sam’s face became troubled. Money was useful to the Maddox household, when it arrived, and Adeline could have named twenty needs for half a dollar without leaving the kitchen. But Sam had attached his day’s labor to Edison’s white face and helpless position in the chair; payment would separate it from kindness and place it in the category of business, where he felt it did not belong.

“I’ll shake mats for old women for no pay,” he said at last.

Sarah stiffened.

“Old women!”

“Or young ones,” Sam added peaceably.

“I ain’t askin’ charity.”

“Wa’n’t charity. You asked.”

“That’s just what I mean.”

Sam backed toward the door.

“Much obliged for the dinner.”

“You’re the most aggravatin’—Sam Maddox, you come back and take this!”

But he was already across the yard. Sarah followed him to the gate and stood there with the coin in her hand.

“You need it more’n I do!” she called.

Sam turned. In the evening light he seemed larger and shabbier than ever, his beard dusty, his shoulders bent, his sleeves marked with soap and cellar mold.

“Sartain,” he called back.

Then he went home without it.

Sarah remained at the gate, offended by an act which she could neither classify nor repay. She looked at the half-dollar, then at the Maddox house. Adeline was stirring the pot over the outdoor fire. The children ran to meet Sam, climbing upon him and hanging from his hands. He endured them with weary pleasure.

“A man that won’t work and won’t take money when he does,” Sarah said. “There ain’t any sense in it.”

Edison, standing behind the screen door, felt sufficiently recovered to answer.

“Mebbe sense ain’t all there is.”

Sarah turned sharply.

“What did you say?”

“I said I guess I’ll eat a good supper.”

“After layin’ all day, I should think you could.”

Edison said no more. Liberty had visited him briefly and departed; but, like some rare bird seen once in an orchard, it left the air altered.

Across the road, Sam lowered himself onto the back step. The children reported the day’s events in chorus. The baby had eaten dirt, a hen had laid under the stove, Ben had torn his trousers, the fire had gone out twice, and somebody’s brindled cow had entered the yard and departed of her own accord. Adeline listened to all with equal serenity.

“You been helpin’ Mis’ Blake?” she asked.

“Yep.”

“All day?”

“Pooty much.”

“You must be tired.”

“Never knowed I had so many places to ache.”

Adeline served supper: potatoes, a little salt pork, peas, and bread which had risen more on one side than the other. Nobody criticized it. The children ate with the direct gratitude of appetite, and Sam ate slowly, his arms resting heavily beside his plate.

After supper the light lingered. The children scattered again, though less rapidly, and the baby fell asleep in the wooden box. Adeline washed the few dishes in a basin and left them upside down on the step. She did not scour the basin afterward. She did not sweep beneath the table. She did not examine the corners for dust which might have settled there during supper. The house, having been untidy before the meal, remained untidy after it with no violent change in the moral universe.

Sam went beneath the apple tree and sat with his back against the trunk. Adeline joined him. She folded her hands in her lap, and for a while neither spoke.

The evening was very still. From the Blake house came the faint rattle of pans and Sarah’s voice reminding Edison to fasten the cellar door. Farther down the road a dog barked twice. The brook, invisible beyond the meadow, made a low, continuous sound as if the earth were breathing in sleep.

Above Sam and Adeline, the apple blossoms had lost their daylight colors. They were pale now, multitudinous and soft, floating against the deepening sky. Their fragrance descended as the air cooled. It could be tasted, almost drunk; it entered the mouth and nostrils and seemed to pass into the blood.

Adeline leaned her head against the trunk.

“Dretful sweet,” she said.

“Yep.”

“Tree never done so well.”

“Nope.”

“You think there’ll be apples?”

“Some. Young ones’ll get most.”

“They do set by ’em.”

Sam stretched his legs carefully.

“Mis’ Blake says we ought to save ’em for Thanksgivin’.”

“Would they keep?”

“She says they would if we took care.”

Adeline considered the question without anxiety.

“Children like ’em fresh,” she said.

“Yep.”

A petal fell upon Sam’s beard. Adeline reached over and brushed it away.

“What happened to Mr. Blake?” she asked.

“Fell through a chair.”

“Land.”

“Got ketched. Dizzy, too.”

“He all right?”

“Pooty much. Told him to lay low.”

Adeline smiled.

“Mis’ Blake keep you workin’?”

“Never saw things breed like work in that house. Every time I carried one thing, there was somethin’ under it wanted movin’. Every time I moved that, there was dirt wanted scrubbin’. Then what I moved had to go back.”

“Seems discouragin’.”

“It is.”

“House look nice?”

“Looks jest like a house.”

They sat quietly again. A child laughed from somewhere in the branches; another hushed him with importance. Presently two small figures descended and ran toward the brook.

Adeline watched them go.

“Mis’ Blake works dretful hard,” she said.

“Yep.”

“Everything she’s got is nice.”

“Yep.”

“She don’t seem to enjoy it much.”

Sam rubbed one sore shoulder.

“Too busy keepin’ it nice.”

“Mebbe.”

There was no condemnation in Adeline’s voice. She understood Sarah no better than Sarah understood her, but her failures of comprehension were soft and made no demands upon their objects. She could imagine that Sarah’s clean rooms gave Sarah pleasure, even if it was a thin and breathless pleasure which immediately perceived another room.

“Half a dollar’d come handy,” Adeline observed after a moment.

Sam looked guilty.

“She tried to pay me.”

“You didn’t take it.”

“Nope.”

Adeline nodded. She might have wished for sugar, lamp oil, thread, or shoes for the baby, but she did not reproach him. She knew the structure of his mind as one knows the slope of familiar ground. A wagon could not climb a bank beyond its strength, and Sam could not accept money for having seen an old man in trouble.

“You done right, likely,” she said.

“Dunno.”

“You feel right?”

“Pooty much.”

“Then likely you done so.”

This was not a principle which would have been safe in West Dunham generally. Many persons felt right while doing things which their neighbors regarded with reasoned alarm. Yet, applied to Sam, whose conscience was slow but warm, it served better than several more exact systems.

The last sunlight withdrew from the upper branches. A star appeared through the bloom. The ruined house behind them darkened mercifully; its broken window, leaning chimney, and unpainted boards passed into a common dusk. The raw door-yard disappeared. The rusty stove became no uglier than a stone. Even the burdocks lost their coarse identity and stood in the shadows with a certain primitive dignity.

The tree remained.

It gathered the darkness into itself and gave back perfume. It had not been pruned to usefulness, disciplined to symmetry, sprayed against corruption, or exhorted to bear. Worms would enter some of its fruit. Winds would cast down branches. Children would devour its apples before maturity. Nothing about it promised thrift. Yet it flowered with an extravagance beyond calculation, pouring sweetness upon the unpainted house, the ragged children, the tired idle man, the faded woman, the hens, the burdocks, and the common road.

“Makes ye feel rich,” Adeline said softly.

Sam looked around. There was scarcely a possession within sight which a prudent person would have accepted as a gift.

“Yep,” he said.

“I s’pose Mis’ Blake’s gettin’ ready for Thanksgivin’ already.”

“Talked about it.”

“That’s a long ways off.”

“Said so.”

Adeline drew a deep breath of the apple fragrance.

“Seems as if a body could be thankful now.”

Sam turned his broad, tired face upward. Through the rose-white multitude he saw the first stars, remote and tranquil. He heard the children near the brook, the soft clucking of a hen settling under the step, the faint pans across the road, Adeline’s breathing beside him. His muscles hurt; his roof leaked; his boots were cracked; there might not be meat again before Sunday. He knew all this without being mastered by it.

“Any time,” he said. “Any time right along.”

Adeline slipped her hand into his great earth-stained one.

The village would not have accepted their conclusion without amendment. Gratitude, in West Dunham, properly followed preparation; thanksgiving belonged after harvest, preserving, cleaning, cooking, and the payment of whatever debts could be paid. It required sound apples, white tablecloths, polished stoves, and chairs which did not give way beneath the deserving. The Maddoxes had none of these qualifications.

Nevertheless, beneath the apple tree, they sat in contentment.

The fragrance descended upon them like an invisible abundance. Beauty entered where counsel had failed, and from those two unregenerate hearts, which had neglected so many duties and escaped so many improvements, there arose a praise as natural as bloom—unplanned, unprofitable, and sweet.

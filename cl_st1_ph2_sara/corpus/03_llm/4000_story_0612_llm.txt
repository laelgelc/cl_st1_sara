## The Balcony

### I

There was snow on the roofs of Bursley; there was frost upon the black roads; there were stars in a clear dark velvet sky; and beneath the stars the furnaces of the Five Towns flamed and expired, flamed and expired, as though the district itself breathed fire. There were also forty-three persons in the house of Stephen and Vera Cheswardine, drinking coffee, eating marrons glacés, discussing the latest musical comedy, and asserting that Christmas parties were not what they had been.

The Cheswardines’ house stood high above the town, on the edge of a new road inhabited by manufacturers who had begun life with no view whatever and ended it with views extending, on a favourable day, to three counties. From the drawing-room windows could be seen chimney-stacks, the distant kilns, a church spire, and part of the golf links. The house contained hot-water pipes, electric bells, two bathrooms, and a boudoir in pale blue for Vera.

Vera’s boudoir was the despair of women who possessed no boudoir, and the secret disappointment of Vera. It had silver-grey paper, a white bearskin, a writing-table at which she never wrote, French novels which she could not read without a dictionary, and a low chair expressly designed to make rising from it almost impossible. But its two ordinary sash windows remained obstinately English.

Charlie Draycott discovered this defect at a quarter past eleven on Christmas night.

Charlie was a young widower, elegant without being dangerous, romantic without being poor, and sufficiently idle to notice things which busy men overlook. He admired Vera. Everybody knew it, including Stephen, who was not disturbed. Stephen regarded admiration as one of those luxuries that other men might safely provide for his wife.

Charlie and Vera stood at a boudoir window. Behind them the party made a rich, agreeable murmur; before them the town lay darkly magnificent, its smoke hidden, its fires exposed.

“What a night!” said Charlie.

“Yes,” said Vera. “And what an ugly window!”

Charlie examined it. He had the talent of accepting a woman’s change of subject as though it were an inspiration.

“You ought to have a French window here,” he said, “opening on to a marble balcony.”

Vera turned to him.

“A marble balcony?”

“White marble. With a balustrade. You would come out in summer before breakfast, in something floating and Parisian, and look across the valley.”

“At the bottle works?”

“Beyond the bottle works.”

“And the golf links?”

“Far beyond the golf links. At the horizon.”

The scheme was complete. Vera saw it immediately: the window thrown wide, curtains swelling in a June wind, roses climbing the balustrade, herself in a garment so exquisite that no Staffordshire dressmaker had ever imagined it. She saw Charlie below, perhaps, lifting his hat. Stephen did not occur in the picture, except as the person who would pay the builder.

“Stephen!” she called.

Stephen was in the doorway. He was broad, healthy, thirty-eight, and emphatic. His evening clothes fitted him with the severe correctness of a commercial contract. He manufactured glazed ware and distrusted poetry.

“What?”

“Charlie says I ought to have a French window and a marble balcony.”

“No.”

He spoke before seeing Charlie. Then he saw Charlie.

“Hullo, Draycott.”

“Hullo.”

“Why no?” Vera demanded.

“Because the wall would have to be knocked about.”

“Well?”

“And the room would be draughty.”

“Not with proper glass.”

“And a balcony would cost a ridiculous sum.”

“Not compared with the house.”

“And it would overlook the works.”

“It overlooks them now.”

“And you’d never use it.”

“I should use it every morning.”

“You don’t come down to breakfast before ten.”

“I should, if I had a balcony.”

Stephen glanced at Charlie, but Charlie’s face had the innocence of moonlight.

“Besides,” Stephen concluded, “marble doesn’t suit this climate.”

“Palaces have marble balconies.”

“This is not a palace.”

“It might be.”

“It won’t.”

No romance in Stephen!

Vera compressed her lips. Her soul, which was small, bright, restless and bird-like, fluttered violently against the bars of marriage. Yet she smiled. There are smiles which mean surrender, and smiles which mean that hostilities will recommence as soon as the guests have gone. Stephen knew the latter sort.

At that instant Mrs. Cynon, the doctor’s wife, hurried into the boudoir with news.

“The fortune-teller has come!”

The news stopped the consumption of coffee. It stopped a dispute on Wagner. It stopped three married women from explaining the proper management of husbands to a bride of six weeks. It even stopped the Mayor from telling how he had once missed a train at Crewe.

“A real fortune-teller?” cried Vera.

“My dear, perfectly real! Mrs. Machin’s laundress knows her. She reads tea leaves, and she told a girl at Longshaw the very day she would receive a letter from abroad.”

“Did the letter come?”

“No. But her young man prevented it.”

This explanation was accepted.

The fortune-teller had been intended merely as one item among many; but the conjurer had disappointed them, the tenor had a cold, and dancing had been hindered by an inequality in the drawing-room floor. Thus destiny, aided by a cracked teapot and Mrs. Cynon’s laundress, took possession of the festival.

### II

Madame Tarot was not called Madame Tarot. She was called Mrs. Pollett, and she lived in two rooms behind a sweetshop. She wore a black bonnet, a sealskin cape of uncertain ancestry, and cotton gloves. Her face had been browned by weather and sharpened by domestic experience. She knew nothing of psycho-analysis, bridge, the new dances, Bernard Shaw, calories, or the proper fork for asparagus. But she knew that the rich, when assembled after dinner, desire above all things to be deceived respectfully.

A small morning-room was given to her. A kettle, cups and a lamp with a red shade were provided. One by one the guests went thither; one by one they came back altered. The jocular returned solemn; the solemn returned offended; the sceptical returned eager to compare results.

Of course nobody believed.

“Oh no!” said Mrs. Cynon. “But she described my brother in Canada exactly.”

“Pure chance,” said the Mayor. “Still, she knew I had enemies.”

“Every public man has enemies,” Stephen observed.

“Precisely. That proves nothing. Yet she said one of mine had a grey moustache, and Councillor Brough has a grey moustache.”

Charlie returned smiling.

“Well?” Vera asked.

“I am to make a journey over water.”

“You cross the canal every day.”

“That may be it.”

“And romance?”

Charlie looked at Vera.

“She was discreet.”

Vera laughed, but not easily. She had a terror of fortune-telling. This terror was founded upon complete disbelief. She did not believe that tea leaves could reveal the future; nevertheless, if they happened to reveal something unpleasant, the unpleasant thing might occur. Logic has never yet slain a superstition; it merely wounds it, and the creature becomes more savage.

“You must go,” said Charlie.

“I wouldn’t for worlds.”

“Then you are afraid.”

“I am not afraid. I think it’s vulgar.”

“Mrs. Cynon has been.”

“Mrs. Cynon is afraid of nothing because her husband can prescribe for it.”

“Stephen has been.”

Vera stared.

Stephen was indeed emerging from the morning-room, folding a scrap of paper. His expression was one of indulgent amusement.

“Well, darling?” Vera called.

“All rot,” he said.

“What did she tell you?”

“Nothing.”

“Then why have you got that paper?”

“A memorandum.”

“Of what?”

“Nothing important.”

He put it into his waistcoat pocket.

This secrecy decided Vera. There are women who can resist fear and women who can resist curiosity; the woman who can resist both has not yet been discovered in the Five Towns.

Five minutes later Vera sat opposite Mrs. Pollett. Between them stood a cup containing a brown confusion of tea leaves. The red lamp made the ordinary morning-room ominous. Outside, distant laughter could be heard; but it seemed to belong to persons already safe in another world.

Mrs. Pollett rotated the cup, inverted it, waited, and examined the leaves with a gaze whose power would have embarrassed them had they possessed consciousness.

“Yo’ve got a good ’eart,” she began.

Vera knew this.

“But yo’re masterful.”

Vera was surprised.

“There’s money around ye. Money earned, not left.”

This was incontestable.

“I see a fair gentleman.”

Vera thought of Charlie and blushed, though there was no necessity.

“And a dark obstacle.”

She thought of Stephen.

“There’s summat yo’ want in bricks and stone.”

“The balcony!” Vera exclaimed.

Mrs. Pollett had never heard the word “balcony” pronounced with such emotion. She peered again.

“Ah! But there’s a shadow afore it.”

“What shadow?”

“A man’s shadow.”

“My husband’s?”

“I canna say.”

“You must.”

Mrs. Pollett sighed. Prophets have always found that precision increases both their influence and their danger.

“There’s black by the ring,” she murmured. “A break. A great change. Yo’ll wear widow’s weeds.”

Vera’s fingers tightened upon her handkerchief.

“You mean—Stephen will die?”

“There’s death in the cup.”

“When?”

“Time’s hidden.”

“Soon?”

“I canna say, missis. But yo’ll be a widow.”

The word rang in Vera’s head like a bell heard across snow. Widow! She saw Stephen’s empty chair, Stephen’s clothes hanging uselessly in a wardrobe, Stephen’s boots outside a bedroom door that no servant need enter. She saw the marble balcony too, but now it was cold, moonlit and intolerable.

When she rejoined the party she was pale.

“What did she say?” cried six voices.

“Nothing,” Vera replied.

And for once this word, which at such parties usually means everything, meant more than everything.

### III

During the following fortnight Stephen had reason to wonder whether Christmas had miraculously reformed his wife.

Vera ceased to mention the balcony. She ceased also to mention a motor-car of her own, a month in Cannes, the enlargement of the dining-room, and the injustice of his objection to Pekingese dogs. She agreed with him about the household accounts. She allowed him to finish sentences. Once, when he contradicted her before company, she merely smiled.

Stephen became uneasy.

Her solicitude for his body was impressive. Before the prophecy, she had trusted his constitution as she trusted the Bank of England—that is to say, without thinking of it. Now she watched him drink cold water and shuddered. She removed the cigar from his hand after dinner. She sent back underdone beef. She purchased a clinical thermometer, a chest-protector, malt extract, disinfectant lozenges, and a patent electrical belt whose advertisements promised vigour to the exhausted systems of both sexes.

“What’s all this?” he demanded.

“It’s for your own good, dear.”

“My own good is becoming a nuisance.”

“You work too hard.”

“I’ve always worked.”

“You must rest.”

“I rested on Sunday.”

“You read the trade papers.”

“That is rest.”

“It is not rest for the brain.”

“My brain is accustomed to trade papers.”

She persuaded him to consult Dr. Cynon about a cough which Stephen denied possessing. Dr. Cynon examined him, laughed, and prescribed nothing. Vera was dissatisfied. A doctor who prescribed nothing appeared to her almost immoral.

She prohibited golf in east winds, motoring after dark, meetings that involved late suppers, and all discussion of labour disputes. When Stephen sneezed she turned white. When he complained that the bedroom was too hot she burst into tears.

I regret to have to say that Stephen enjoyed this change. At first he protested; then he yielded; finally he exploited it. He discovered that a slight pressure of the hand upon the forehead would produce tea, cushions, silence, and the immediate abandonment of any argument. He had merely to remark, “I’ve had rather a trying day,” and Vera became an angel in pale silk.

A husband is only human.

For three weeks peace brooded over the Cheswardine household. It was a peace founded upon the imagined decease of its master, but many excellent institutions have rested on foundations no sounder.

Charlie Draycott noticed the transformation.

“You are becoming a model wife,” he said to Vera one afternoon.

They were in the boudoir. Snow had vanished from the roofs; the factories emerged again in all their detail, and a damp wind troubled the smoke.

“I always was,” Vera answered.

“You have not spoken of the balcony since Christmas.”

“I don’t want it.”

Charlie looked at the sash window, then at her.

“My dear Vera, that is serious.”

“I have outgrown such foolishness.”

“In three weeks?”

“One can change in a moment.”

“Yes,” said Charlie. “But usually one changes back.”

She turned away. Charlie’s amused gentleness irritated her. Stephen’s possible death had become a private monster that followed her through the house. At night she listened to his breathing. By day she examined his face for signs of destiny. She loved him; she had never known how much until a strange woman in cotton gloves had announced that he would be removed.

That evening Stephen refused a second helping of pudding.

Vera dropped her spoon.

“What is it?”

“Nothing. I don’t want any more.”

“You always take two helpings.”

“I don’t tonight.”

“Are you ill?”

“No.”

“You look pale.”

“I am not pale.”

“You do. Parkins, telephone Dr. Cynon.”

“Parkins, don’t.”

The maid stood suspended between life and death, or, more accurately, between master and mistress.

“Vera,” said Stephen, “what in heaven’s name is the matter with you?”

“Nothing.”

“You’ve been behaving like a hospital nurse for weeks. You count my cigars. You smell my overcoat when I come in out of the fog. Yesterday you hid my golf shoes.”

“It was wet.”

“I am thirty-eight, not eighty-eight.”

At the second figure Vera gave a little cry.

Stephen stared at her.

“Now what?”

She began to weep. Stephen sent Parkins away, moved round the table and put an arm about his wife. His manner lost its commercial hardness.

“Tell me,” he said.

“You’ll laugh.”

“I won’t.”

“You always do.”

“Not if it’s serious.”

“It’s terribly serious.”

He waited.

“That woman—the fortune-teller—said I should be a widow.”

Stephen blinked.

“Is that all?”

“All!”

“My dear girl, did you suppose I had heart disease?”

“She said there was death in the cup.”

“There generally is, if the tea is bad enough.”

“Stephen!”

“Sorry. But you don’t believe that rubbish?”

“No. Of course not.”

“Then why—”

“One never knows.”

This argument, being unanswerable, silenced him for a moment.

Then Stephen laughed. He laughed until Vera withdrew from his arm and regarded him with offended misery.

“You promised!”

“I know. Forgive me. But it’s too absurd. She told me I should live to ninety.”

Vera’s tears stopped.

“What?”

“Ninety. I’ve got it in writing.”

He drew from his pocketbook the scrap of paper he had preserved as a curiosity. Mrs. Pollett’s laborious pencil declared: “Long life. Great success. Ninety year.”

Vera seized it and read it twice.

“Did she really say that?”

“Distinctly.”

“Then she contradicted herself.”

“Fortune-tellers must live.”

“But if you live to ninety, I can’t be widowed—unless—” She paused.

“Unless what?”

“Unless I live longer.”

Stephen looked at her. Vera had already begun to calculate. He could see hope returning to her features like sunlight to a landscape after rain.

“You may live to a hundred,” he said rashly.

“Yes,” she breathed.

In that instant Stephen perceived his tactical error. While Vera feared widowhood, he had been wrapped, fed, protected and obeyed. By proving himself immortal, he had restored her natural character.

It was too late.

### IV

The next morning Vera entered Stephen’s dressing-room while he was shaving.

“About the balcony,” she began.

Stephen turned, one cheek white with lather.

“What balcony?”

“My balcony.”

“You said you didn’t want it.”

“I was overwrought.”

“You said you had outgrown such foolishness.”

“I have grown back.”

“No.”

“It needn’t be marble.”

“No.”

“Stone would do.”

“No.”

“Artificial stone.”

“No.”

“A small iron one, then.”

“No.”

“Stephen, you are unreasonable.”

“I am consistent.”

“Consistency is merely obstinacy in men.”

“And determination in women, I suppose?”

“Exactly.”

He resumed shaving. Vera sat on the edge of a chair and watched him with the calm of a general who has selected the battlefield.

“It would improve the house.”

“No.”

“It would increase the value.”

“No.”

“It would give me air.”

“Open the window.”

“It sticks.”

“I’ll have it mended.”

“I want to step outside.”

“Go downstairs.”

“In my boudoir slippers?”

“Put on shoes.”

“You have no imagination.”

“None.”

“Charlie says—”

“Charlie doesn’t own the house.”

“He has taste.”

“He can put balconies on his own house.”

“He hasn’t the view.”

“Neither have we. We have three potbanks and a gasometer.”

“There are hills beyond them.”

“There are always hills beyond things.”

Vera rose.

“Very well.”

Stephen knew this phrase. It did not mean very well.

“I shall have my balcony when I am a widow,” she said.

“You won’t be a widow. I am to live to ninety.”

“I may live to a hundred.”

“You will be ninety-two.”

“I shall still enjoy a view.”

“At ninety-two?”

“More than ever. I shall have time to look at it.”

Stephen wiped his razor.

“And where shall I be during these architectural improvements?”

“Dead,” said Vera sweetly. “At ninety.”

For a moment they contemplated one another: Stephen, vigorous, soapy and practical; Vera, delicate, obstinate and radiant. Between them stretched fifty-two years of married life, fortified on one side by common sense and on the other by a balcony that did not yet exist.

“Bosh!” said Stephen.

But Vera kissed the top of his head. She was entirely happy again.

A week later the gardener received instructions to plant three young copper beeches below the boudoir window.

“Not there, ma’am,” he objected. “They’ll block the light when they grow.”

“Not if they are properly spaced.”

“They’ll take forty year to make much show.”

“That will do beautifully,” said Vera. “I am arranging for the future view.”

## The Parable of the Wells

And it came to pass in a certain land that the heavens were shut up, and no rain fell upon the fields; and the rivers diminished, and the grass withered upon the hills, and dust lay upon the highways like a garment.

Now beneath the ground there were many springs, and the water thereof was sweet and abundant. But the mouths of the springs, and the wells that had been digged unto them, were possessed by a few men, who called themselves the Owners; for their fathers had set stones about the wells in former times and had written their names upon the stones.

And the Owners said one unto another, Behold, the wells are ours, because our names are written upon them; and the water also is ours, because it cometh forth within our walls.

But the multitude possessed neither wells nor springs, neither had they wherewithal to quench their thirst. And they came unto the Owners, saying, Sirs, give us water, lest we and our little ones perish.

Then the Owners answered, saying, We are not hard men, neither do we desire that any should perish, for dead men are unprofitable customers. Bring ye therefore the water from the springs and pour it into the great tank in the midst of the city, and we will give unto every man one penny for every bucket that he bringeth.

Now the tank was called the Market.

And the people rejoiced greatly, saying, Surely the Owners are benefactors, for they permit us to labor, and without labor no man can live. So they descended into the wells and drew up the water, and carried it beneath the burning sun, and poured it into the tank which was the Market.

For every bucket that a man brought, the keepers of the Owners gave him one penny. And when the people thirsted they came unto the other side of the tank, where the merchants sat beneath awnings, and said, Give us to drink of the water which we have brought.

And the merchants answered, Gladly; for the price of a bucket is two pennies.

Then the people marvelled and said, How is this? For ye have given us but one penny for bringing the bucket, and now ye demand two pennies before we may bear it away.

But the merchants replied, Ye are dull of understanding. The first penny repayeth you for your labor, but the second penny is the profit of the Owners. Without profit the Owners would have no cause to own the wells; and if none owned the wells, who would give you employment? Therefore pay thankfully, and dispute not concerning the mysteries of Business.

And the people held their peace, for they feared to be accounted ignorant. They joined themselves two by two, and one gave his penny unto the other, so that one might buy a bucket. Then they divided the water between them, and each drank half a bucket, although each had brought a whole bucket unto the Market.

Thus the children cried in the night because their tongues clave unto their mouths, and the women searched the jars for one drop, and strong men dreamed of rivers. Nevertheless, the tank rose higher day by day, because the people put into it twice as much water as they could buy therefrom.

And the Owners walked upon a gallery above the Market and beheld the tank filling, and they were pleased. They said, Trade is brisk, production increaseth, and the prosperity of the land is established upon a sound foundation.

Yet after many days the water stood near unto the brim, while the people were consumed with thirst.

Then the superintendent came hastily unto the Owners and said, My lords, there is no more room in the Market. If the workers bring other buckets, the tank will overflow, and the water will run into the street.

And the Owners answered, Then command them to cease from bringing water until the Market be emptied.

So the superintendent went forth and cried, Let no man draw from the wells, neither let any woman carry a bucket, for there is overproduction of water.

When the people heard this, they were astonished, and one said, What manner of calamity is overproduction?

And the superintendent said, It is a grievous visitation, wherein there is too much of that which men need.

But the people answered, How can there be too much water while we thirst? Behold our vessels are empty, our lips are broken, and our infants faint in our arms.

Then the superintendent rebuked them, saying, Ye confuse need with demand. Your need is great, as any fool may perceive; but your demand is small, for ye have no pennies. The Market taketh heed not of empty vessels, but of full purses. Go hence, therefore, and trouble not the Owners with contradictions.

Thus were the people cast out from their labor. And because they brought no buckets they received no pennies, and because they received no pennies they could buy no water. Yet because they bought no water the Owners would not hire them to bring more, for the tank which was the Market was already full.

And the people assembled before the gates and cried, Hire us, that we may earn pennies and buy water.

But the Owners answered from the gallery, We cannot hire you unless the water in the Market be bought.

Then said the people, How shall we buy it unless ye hire us?

And the Owners said, How shall we hire you unless ye buy it?

And they repeated these words many times, and every repetition seemed unto them a proof of wisdom.

Now the Owners were troubled, not because the people thirsted, but because no profit came unto them. Therefore they summoned the economists, who were soothsayers learned in figures, and interpreters of dark sayings.

And the economists put on long robes, and carried tablets covered with signs, and looked first at the full tank and afterward at the empty jars of the people.

After they had taken counsel for seven days, the chief economist said, This affliction is plainly caused by overproduction.

Another said, Nay, it ariseth from underconsumption.

And a third said, It is a lack of confidence; for if the people confidently believed that they possessed pennies, they would go forth and purchase water, whereupon employment would revive.

A fourth shook his head and said, The trouble is spots upon the sun.

A fifth declared, It is the natural operation of an immutable law, concerning which nothing can be done without injuring Business.

Then the Owners asked, What remedy prescribe ye?

And the economists answered, Wait until the Market correcteth itself.

But when the Owners inquired how the Market would accomplish this thing, the economists spake in words which no man could understand. And the Owners rewarded them greatly, saying, Surely these are learned men, for their speech is deeper than a well.

Nevertheless, the people continued to thirst, and their murmuring increased.

Therefore the Owners called unto them the advertisers, who were men skilful in painting signs. And the advertisers covered the walls with pictures of fountains and green valleys and vessels beaded with coolness. Beneath the pictures they wrote, DRINK MORE WATER, FOR PROSPERITY DEPENDETH UPON THE CONSUMER.

And musicians went through the streets singing, Happy is he who buyeth two buckets where one sufficeth.

But the people said, Give us the pennies and we will obey.

Then the advertisers were offended, saying, These people respond poorly to education.

Now there were among the priests certain false shepherds who ate at the tables of the Owners and dipped their bread in water while the multitude looked on. These went forth among the thirsty, saying, Murmur not against them whom Providence hath set over the wells. The order of society is ordained from above. Some are born to own the springs, and others to carry the buckets; and if ye bear thirst meekly in this world, ye shall drink without price in the world to come.

A woman whose child lay faint upon her breast answered, Shall the Owners also thirst in the world to come?

The priest said, The judgments of heaven are secret, and such questions tend unto disorder.

Another among the people asked, Did God make the springs, or did the Owners make them?

The priest replied, God made them.

And the man said, For whom made he them?

Then the priest became angry and cried, Blasphemy! For although God made the water, he hath committed the title deeds unto the Owners, whose handwriting is witnessed by lawyers.

And the false shepherds exhorted the people to patience, saying, Blessed are the thirsty, if only they disturb not property.

Yet there were other priests, few in number, who came forth from among the poor and said, Woe unto them that join well to well, until there be no place left for the people. The water of life was not created for merchandise, neither did the Lord write the law of profit upon the clouds. And these were driven from the temples and called dangerous men.

Now when the cries before the Market grew loud, the Owners said, We must practise charity, lest the people question justice.

So they ordered that one bucket in every thousand be set aside for the relief of the deserving thirsty. And committees were appointed to inquire who deserved to drink.

The applicants stood many hours beneath the sun. Each was asked whether his poverty came from idleness, whether he had spoken disrespectfully of the Owners, whether he attended the sermons of patience, and whether his grandfather had ever been accused of agitation.

Those who survived the inquiry received each a spoonful. And the wives of the Owners came in fine garments and dipped their fingers into the water and sprinkled drops upon the children, while scribes recorded their compassion.

Then the Owners said, Behold how much we sacrifice for the poor.

But the people answered, Ye give us drops from the water we brought in buckets.

And the Owners called this ingratitude.

Afterward hunger was added unto thirst, because no labor was done in the fields, and the people began to gather in secret places. They said one to another, The tank is full and our vessels are empty. We made the tank full, yet we are forbidden to touch it. Surely this is madness.

When report thereof came unto the Owners, they were afraid, and said, The sacred right of the wells is in danger.

Therefore they sent recruiters among the strongest of the people, saying, Take ye spears and staves, and guard the Market against thieves. We will pay unto each of you a penny daily.

And certain men, being sorely thirsty, accepted the penny and stood between the tank and their brethren. With the penny two soldiers together bought one bucket, and divided it between them.

The Owners said unto these armed men, If the multitude enter the Market, they will steal your water.

But one soldier looked upon the overflowing tank and asked, Which part thereof is ours?

And the captain answered, Ask not foolish questions. Your duty is to protect all of it.

Thus brother was set against brother, and the thirsty guarded water from the thirsty. And whenever the multitude approached, the captains cried, Law and Order! and the staves descended.

Now the Owners possessed gardens beyond the city, and fountains played therein by day and night. They made lakes whereon painted boats sailed, and fishponds for fishes which they did not eat. They washed their marble courts with water and cooled the roofs of their houses. Their children sported beneath jets while the children of the workers licked dew, when dew could be found.

And because the Market remained full and trade moved not, the Owners devised means to consume the water.

One built a cascade over steps of silver. Another dug a moat about his dwelling. A third commanded that water be poured upon the road before his chariot, lest dust offend him. And the chief among them made a fountain in the likeness of Profit, being a golden man who held a bucket upside down forever.

The people beheld these things through the gates and groaned.

But the economists rejoiced, saying, Luxury createth demand, and waste is the mother of employment.

After many days the level of the Market fell. Then the superintendent sounded a trumpet and cried, The crisis is ended! Confidence is restored! Let the people return unto the wells.

And the people, being near unto death, hastened again to labor. They drew the water and bore it beneath the sun; and for each bucket they received one penny, and for each bucket they desired they were charged two.

Again the tank filled, and again the jars remained half empty. Again came overproduction, and dismissal, and sermons, and charity, and staves. Again the Owners wasted the water until the Market had room, and again they hired the people.

Thus the years went round like a wheel that crusheth those who turn it. In the days of labor the people thirsted somewhat less; in the days of crisis they thirsted unto death. Yet whether they labored or labored not, the Owners prospered, for they owned the wells, the tank, the buckets, and the law concerning all three.

Now there arose among the people certain agitators, whom the Owners called pestilent fellows, disturbers of confidence, and enemies of water. These went from house to house by night, and spoke in the workshops and beside the empty jars.

And one of them stood upon a stone and said, Hearken, ye bearers of buckets. The drought is real, yet it is not the cause of your thirst, for water aboundeth in the Market. Neither is your misery caused by overproduction, for plenty cannot of itself breed famine. Ye thirst because that which all require is held by a few for profit.

The people asked, But do not the Owners give us work?

And the agitator answered, Who draweth the water?

They said, We draw it.

Who carrieth it?

We carry it.

Who buildeth and mendeth the tank?

We do.

Who maketh the buckets, keepeth the pipes, and guardeth the springs?

We do all these things.

Then said he, What do the Owners?

And the people considered. At length one replied, They own.

The agitator said, And what is it to own?

Another answered, It is to forbid others to use.

Then the agitator cried, Behold the whole mystery! They permit you to fill the Market, on condition that ye surrender unto them half the fruit of your labor. They call the surrendered half profit; and because ye cannot buy back all that ye produce, the tank overfloweth. Then they dismiss you because ye are too poor to buy, although they themselves have made you poor.

And the people said, These words are plain, unlike the words of the economists.

Another agitator arose, a woman grey with labor, and she said, The Owners tell you that without them none would direct the work. But when a bridge is built, do the stones command the mason? When bread is baked, doth the title deed knead the dough? Ye have skill among you. Choose coordinators from your own number, and if they serve badly, remove them. Let those who understand the springs tend the springs, and those who know the tank oversee the tank; but let none become master of the water.

Then certain cautious men said, If there be no profit, why shall any man work?

She answered, Because without labor all thirst, and because by labor all may drink. Is life itself a smaller inducement than the enrichment of an Owner? Ye now labor that one may have a fountain and another a spoonful. Labor rather that every vessel be filled.

And the people asked, How shall the water be divided?

The agitators answered, According unto need. Let every household receive enough, and let the sick, the young, and they who labor under the fiercest sun receive first care. Keep account, not that one may gain against another, but that none be forgotten and nothing be wasted.

Then said the people, But the wells belong unto the Owners.

And the agitators replied, The springs were in the earth before the Owners were born. Their fathers enclosed them by force, their lawyers blessed the enclosure with ink, and their priests perfumed the ink with incense. Shall ink bind the living forever?

These sayings passed among the people, and understanding spread as fire among dry reeds.

The Owners heard thereof and commanded the armed men to seize the agitators. But many soldiers cast down their staves, saying, We also are thirsty, and our mothers stand in the charity line.

Then the Owners themselves came upon the gallery and cried, The agitators would destroy civilization! They would divide your cups, abolish freedom, and bring chaos upon the wells.

The people answered, What freedom have we, save freedom to thirst? And what order is this, wherein abundance standeth guarded while children die?

The chief Owner stretched forth his hands and said, Without us the Market will perish.

But the workers opened the gates, and the Market did not perish.

They took possession of the wells, not each man for himself, lest the strong seize more than the weak, but all together for the common good. They chose overseers from among the diggers, carriers, builders, healers, and householders; and these rendered account before the assembly. If any grew proud or sought privilege, the people removed him, saying, An office is a duty and not a well to be owned.

They abolished the penny at one gate and the two pennies at the other. No merchant sat between the thirsty and the water. The tank was filled according unto what the people required, and water was drawn according unto need.

Neither did they cease from foresight. They dug new wells, repaired old channels, covered the tank against the sun, stored water against evil seasons, and planted trees about the springs. Because no man gained by waste, waste diminished; and because no man gained by withholding, none withheld.

The economists came and warned that prosperity must soon collapse, since profit had vanished. But the children drank and did not collapse. The fields became green and did not collapse. The workshops made sound buckets and did not collapse.

The false priests lamented that the lower orders had forgotten humility. But the true priests blessed the common wells and said, Let justice roll down as waters, and let no man sell unto his brother the gift of life.

As for the former Owners, the people did not leave them to thirst. They received water as did all others, and they were invited to share in useful labor. Some learned to mend pipes, some planted orchards, and some complained for many years that equality was persecution. Yet even these drank their fill.

And when rain returned at last, the people built cisterns in every quarter, and no man was permitted to enclose the clouds.

They applied also the wisdom of the wells unto bread and raiment, unto houses and healing, unto learning and the instruments of labor. They said, That which all create and all require shall not be made the private throne of any.

Thus there remained among them neither masters nor servants, neither profit nor enforced want, but coordinators and workers, brothers and sisters, each giving according unto strength and receiving according unto need.

And the old tank, which had been called the Market, they preserved in the midst of the city as a memorial. Upon one side they carved a full vessel, and upon the other an empty cup; and beneath them they wrote:

**WHEN WATER WAS MADE FOR PROFIT, PLENTY BROUGHT THIRST.  
WHEN WATER WAS HELD IN COMMON, THE DESERT BLOSSOMED.**

And it was so.

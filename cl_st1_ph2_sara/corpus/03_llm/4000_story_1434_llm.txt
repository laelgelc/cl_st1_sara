# WESTWARD GLIMPSES

## I.

It is one of the privileges of a traveler in this country that he may sail westward in the morning and find, before sunset, not merely a new landscape, but a new order of society; for the towns of America, like the visions of an uneasy sleeper, rise, enlarge, and change their character while one is yet rubbing his eyes to examine them. A European city, if it has once seated itself comfortably upon its foundations, seems resolved to remain there until the day of judgment, gathering moss, monuments, and municipal abuses with equal composure; but an American settlement has no sooner put up a sign-board and counted its chimneys than it begins to speculate upon becoming a metropolis.

We came to Burlington in a skiff, a conveyance which possesses this advantage over a larger vessel, that the passenger is left in no doubt as to his nearness to the water. Lake Champlain lay about us in one of those serene moods which almost persuade a man that Nature has no temper. The sky, faintly clouded toward the north, bent over a sheet of soft silver; the distant mountains, blue at their bases and melting upward into a violet haze, appeared to gaze over one another’s shoulders at the little commerce of mankind; and a green island, with a few white dwellings visible among its trees, rested upon the bosom of the lake as tranquilly as though no surveyor had ever measured it, no lawyer disputed it, and no legislator thought of taxing it.

Our boatman was an inland mariner of considerable confidence and very little salt. He wore an air which might have become an admiral entering a hostile harbor; though, to speak truth, the handling of a skiff upon a quiet lake resembles the navigation of the Atlantic about as closely as a perch resembles a Newfoundland cod. Yet he had his nautical words, his contempt for landsmen, his weather wisdom, and a certain habit of looking into the wind with half-closed eyes, which supplied, at a cheap rate, all that was wanting of the quarter-deck. He brought us alongside the wharf with a flourish that seemed intended for the admiration of the town, and I stepped ashore prepared to behold Burlington.

The place, viewed from the water, had assumed something of the consequence of a seaport. Masts rose above the roofs; sloops and schooners lay beside the wharves; barrels, boxes, planks, bags of grain, and coils of rope were scattered with that studied confusion which everywhere attends profitable business; and the air was filled with hammering, shouting, creaking, and the loud remonstrance of cart-wheels against inequalities in the road. Had a whale displayed his back beyond the breakwater, I believe the illusion would have been complete. The lake itself performed the part of an inland ocean with commendable gravity, and the mountains, like theatrical spectators who knew the trick but did not wish to spoil it, looked on without betraying a smile.

Burlington’s foreign trade was spoken of with becoming pride. Foreign trade! The phrase brings before the imagination spicy islands, turbaned merchants, long voyages, coral reefs, and bills of exchange signed beneath equatorial suns. Here its visible representatives were a Canadian sloop, two shaggy horses attached to a wagon of potash, a French coin that nobody wished to take, and several kegs whose contents, judging by the reverence paid them, possessed both spirit and nationality. Yet the trade was foreign beyond dispute. A line upon the map divided one shore from another, and transformed flour into an export, lumber into international commerce, and a man who had rowed twenty miles into a person lately arrived from abroad.

In the shops, money from both sides of the boundary circulated with a sociability not always found among the governments whose images it bore. Kings, republics, eagles, and royal heads met in the same pocket, rubbed their distinctions smooth against one another, and emerged upon the counter with no more quarrel than if monarchy and democracy had been invented merely to make change. A British sovereign and an American goddess might lie cheek to cheek in a merchant’s drawer, while their respective nations disputed over fisheries, frontiers, and the proper spelling of political virtue. Mammon, whatever else may be charged against him, has generally been tolerant in matters of heraldry.

On closer inspection, the town showed those contrasts which belong to a community advancing rapidly, but not yet marching in one uniform. Brick stores, broad in front and deep with merchandise, stood beside wooden houses that appeared to have stepped in from the country and to be waiting until somebody directed them home. New tin roofs glittered with a brilliancy painful to the eye and flattering to the proprietor; narrow lanes descended abruptly toward the water; gardens lingered behind warehouses; cows contemplated commerce from vacant lots; and here and there a church-spire rose with a delicate propriety above signs announcing dry goods, salt fish, insurance, pills, and accommodations for man and horse. Religion had the highest position, but trade occupied the greater space.

The population was scarcely less various than the currency. There were Yankees, upright, inquisitive, and sharpened at the edges by traffic; Canadians in homespun; boatmen browned by sun and spray; farmers who had brought the odor of hay and stable into the counting-room; women newly landed, keeping anxious watch over children and bundles; and emigrants from Ireland, whose numbers seemed to increase with every turn toward the waterfront. They rolled barrels, shouldered timber, dug foundations, dragged ropes, drove carts, and performed the thousand heavy offices by which a young town exhibits its prosperity without over-fatiguing its richer inhabitants.

These laborers had come from green fields to a greener continent, though the resemblance, I fear, ended principally in color. Their coats were patched, their faces weather-marked, and their worldly possessions often compressed into a bundle which a prosperous merchant would have considered insufficient for a night’s lodging. Yet they moved with a rough vivacity amid the burdens of the wharf; and if their gestures were broad, their laughter loud, and their acquaintance with American geography imperfect, they were already helping to alter the map more effectually than many a gentleman who could have drawn it. One lifted the stones of a warehouse in which another man’s name would be painted; another cut a road over which carriages would presently roll too fast to notice him; a third carried merchandise whose owner might, in a public speech, attribute all success to native enterprise.

I have often observed that civilization, when represented upon paper, advances in a robe, bearing a lamp; but when encountered upon a new road it is commonly found in coarse trousers, swinging a pickaxe. The emblem is less graceful, but perhaps more trustworthy.

Burlington, with its white houses ascending from the lake, its busy wharves, its sparkling roofs and many-colored crowd, pleased me greatly. There was something comic in its imitation of maritime grandeur, yet something admirable too; for imitation is often the first rehearsal of reality. The little port played at being a seaport with such industry that one could not doubt its eventual success. Its schooners might never smell the ocean; its sailors might grow old without seeing a tide; but its flour went north, its merchandise came south, its warehouses enlarged, and its citizens calculated the future in square feet and compound interest.

When we left it, the afternoon sun struck full upon the windows, and the whole town flashed for an instant between the blue mountains and the silvery lake. Then an island intervened; the last spire sank behind the trees; and Burlington, with its foreign trade, mixed money, inland admirals, and laboring multitude, disappeared as completely as if it had been no more than one of those speculative cities which projectors build in pamphlets and sell in corner lots.

## II.

Our next movement carried us through a country in which improvement had been so active that even the scenery appeared to have entered into business. Rivers were measured by their power, hills by the roads that might cross them, forests by the boards concealed within their trunks, and cataracts by the number of mills they could be persuaded to turn. A waterfall, in this practical region, was no longer permitted to waste its sublimity in mere noise. It must grind, saw, pump, spin, or submit to being regarded as an idle vagabond among industrious citizens.

Thus I came to the Falls of the Genesee with expectations formed partly by geography and partly by imagination, those two authorities which seldom agree when called upon to describe the same place. The evening was gray and singularly transparent. A thin vapor hung above the chasm; the rocks descended in broad, dark masses; and the river, robbed by races, dams, channels, and other ingenious contrivances, made its plunge with diminished pomp. There was still height enough to command respect, foam enough to whiten the depths, and thunder enough to awaken echoes; but the genius of utility had laid hands upon the torrent and taught it to earn its living.

I confess that my first sensation was disappointment. I had prepared myself for an unprofitable sublimity—for a vast body of water rushing down in majestic idleness, producing nothing but astonishment. Instead, the stream had been divided like an estate among industrious heirs. A portion turned wheels; a portion crept along mill-races; another portion, perhaps ashamed of its leisure, hurried toward some mechanical engagement below. What remained fell over the precipice and represented Nature as well as circumstances allowed.

But the place possessed an association which restored to it more than the engineers had taken away. Here Sam Patch, the celebrated jumper, made his final leap.

There are men whose road to distinction lies through senates, battlefields, books, or discoveries; and there are others who, finding all these avenues crowded, go over a precipice. Sam chose the latter path. He had leaped from masts, bridges, rocks, and various elevations which ordinary persons consider useful chiefly for staying upon. Each successful descent raised him higher in public estimation, though it brought him, in a physical sense, invariably downward. The multitude applauded; newspapers announced him; boys imitated him from fences; prudent men condemned him at length; and his fame, like the perfume of certain fruit, became strongest just before decay.

His last exhibition was arranged at the Genesee. The water below waited without curiosity; the rocks maintained their ancient composure; but the banks were crowded with spectators, for mankind will neglect a sunrise that occurs every morning and travel twenty miles to see a man risk breaking his neck. Sam appeared upon his platform, elevated above the cataract and, for the moment, above criticism. He had calculated the distance, the depth, the posture, and whatever other particulars belong to the science of arriving alive at the bottom. Yet there remains in every calculation a small blank reserved for Providence.

He sprang.

For one brief instant he was not a mechanic, nor an exhibitor, nor a poor adventurer earning money from the dangerous curiosity of his fellow-creatures. Suspended against the sky, he became an emblem of human ambition—detached from safety, applauded by folly, and descending rapidly toward truth. Then the river received him. He did not rise.

The crowd, which had assembled to be astonished, was gratified beyond its wishes. The leap became memorable because it was final; failure accomplished what success could never have done; and the man who had repeatedly descended into water at once alighted in the other world and entered posthumous renown. Had he survived, he might have jumped again, grown old, lost his agility, and been forgotten. Death, more considerate of his reputation than he had been of his life, fixed him forever in the act.

It is easy, while standing securely behind a railing, to condemn Sam Patch. I condemned him myself, with much eloquence and complete safety. What madness, I reflected, to purchase a little noise in the newspapers by an appointment with eternity! What miserable glory, to make one’s name depend upon the exact angle at which one meets a river! What blindness, to mistake a crowd’s open mouths for the trumpet of Fame!

Yet, after arranging these observations to my satisfaction, I began to suspect that Sam’s folly differed in degree rather than in kind from that of wiser persons. One man leaps from a platform because five thousand spectators are watching; another hazards fortune, peace, conscience, and digestion because five hundred guests may admire his house. The statesman jumps into an opinion from which he cannot swim; the merchant springs after a speculation and is drowned in figures; the author casts himself from a lofty preface into a shallow book. We all have our precipices, and most of us perform before a crowd, though the crowd may consist only of neighbors, relations, or that most troublesome spectator—ourselves.

I was wise in theory. Practically, I have more than once taken a leap because somebody might observe that I had refused it; and though the fall was less than Sam’s, the motive would scarcely have supported a better man. Perhaps civilization consists not in abolishing such jumps, but in placing them nearer the ground.

Thus musing, I turned from the cataract. Above the gorge, where the thin mist moved like the departing ghost of the river’s ancient grandeur, Rochester spread itself before me—a city so new, so active, and so confident, that it seemed to have sprung up in the night and to be already complaining that the surrounding country did not keep pace with it.

## III.

Rochester has the air of a place which has never been young. It possesses novelty without infancy; it was born, one might say, in boots and a broadcloth coat, with a deed in one pocket and a price-current in the other. The forest had scarcely withdrawn before streets took possession; mills shouted from the river; bridges stitched the banks together; hotels raised their many windows; church-spires pointed toward heaven; and lottery offices, with equal confidence, proposed shorter roads to felicity.

From an eminence the city presented a spectacle of extraordinary animation. The evening light, subdued by a pale haze, gave softness to objects which, at noon, might have appeared raw and angular. Roof after roof stretched along the river; smoke rose in separate columns and then united above the town in a commercial cloud; mill-windows glimmered; newly painted houses shone with an almost embarrassing whiteness; and, as twilight deepened, lamps began to twinkle in the streets, till the whole settlement resembled a constellation lately discovered and already divided into building lots.

There was no repose in it. Burlington had bustled, but Burlington sometimes paused to look at the lake. Rochester seemed to regard a pause as a species of insolvency. Men hurried along as if pursued by appointments; horses trotted as if conscious of the value of time; wagons rattled, coaches rolled, ox-teams groaned, wheelbarrows squeaked, saws rasped, hammers rang, bells summoned, doors slammed, and voices rose through all the confusion, offering, demanding, promising, disputing, and calculating. The very dust appeared in haste.

In one street a line of wagons stood loaded with produce, each attended by a farmer whose countenance combined patience with suspicion. In another, clerks darted between stores and warehouses carrying papers of an importance evident from the speed with which they were carried. A coach arrived at a hotel and discharged ladies, children, hat-boxes, cloaks, umbrellas, and one bewildered gentleman, who seemed to have been packed among the luggage by mistake. Horsemen threaded the throng; drovers urged cattle forward; pigs, maintaining republican independence, went where they pleased; and an ox-team, moving with primeval deliberation through the center of all, opposed to the fever of the city the majestic slowness of the ancient world.

The shops overflowed with temptations. Broad windows displayed cloth, tools, books, china, boots, bonnets, clocks, and medicines capable, according to their labels, of repairing every defect in the human frame except credulity. Sign-boards projected over the pavement with such profusion that a careless rider risked being instructed, insured, shaved, lodged, or legally advised by collision. The newspapers announced farms, fugitives, lectures, auctions, marriages, bankruptcies, patent devices, political meetings, and remedies for disorders produced chiefly by reading advertisements.

The hotels were enormous republics in miniature. Their doors never rested; stages drew up before them with a blast and a tumult; travelers entered wearing the dust of three counties and emerged with information concerning six. In the public rooms, legislators without office settled national affairs; merchants compared prices; lawyers attached themselves to controversies as naturally as burrs to a coat; land-agents unfolded maps upon which future cities lay in pink and yellow squares; and young men, recently arrived from the East, listened with shining eyes to accounts of fortunes that required only courage, capital, judgment, influence, luck, and the immediate purchase of a particular corner lot.

Servants moved through this commotion with trays, boots, messages, and that practiced indifference by which those who labor in hotels defend themselves against the temporary importance of guests. Some were Black men whose alertness was relied upon by persons unwilling to grant them any quality but service; they carried burdens, remembered names, found missing trunks, anticipated demands, and received, in exchange, commands delivered with the expansive dignity that travel often bestows upon small minds. A man who is insignificant at home may become a tyrant for the price of a room.

Near the court-house another crowd had assembled. Justice, like commerce, was doing a brisk business. Plaintiffs, defendants, witnesses, constables, loungers, and gentlemen who had no connection with any case but hoped for entertainment, moved in and out beneath a portico designed to give the law a classical appearance. Within, I was told, property was disputed, character examined, liberty weighed, and fees accumulated. The court-house was substantial and grave; yet opposite it a lottery office displayed a brilliant scheme in red and gold, promising sudden abundance to every purchaser of a ticket.

The neighborhood seemed allegorical. On one side stood Justice, blindfolded and deliberate, distributing what men deserved after long argument; on the other Fortune, still more thoroughly blind, offering what they had not earned without argument at all. The latter appeared to have the larger congregation. Her window contained pictures of overflowing cornucopias, ships under full sail, and piles of coin so lofty that a reasonable man might have distrusted the table supporting them. But reason is a poor engraver. Hope, when printed in scarlet, sells rapidly.

Land speculation furnished the city with a mythology of its own. Every tavern had its prophet; every map its promised land. Swamps were future harbors, fields intended court-house squares, lanes magnificent avenues in disguise. A hill two miles from any dwelling was praised for its central situation, and an orchard became valuable because posterity might require a theater upon it. The buyer was rarely asked to consider what the property was; his attention was directed to what it must inevitably become, provided population, trade, canals, railroads, government patronage, and Providence performed their respective parts.

Yet beneath the exaggeration there was solid wonder. The mills did turn; the grain did arrive; the flour did depart; houses multiplied; roads converged; families settled; institutions formed; and a city, however loudly it advertised itself, actually stood where wilderness had lately held undisputed possession. If the citizens boasted of tomorrow, it was because yesterday had justified boasting beyond all sober expectation.

Walking toward the outskirts, I encountered a figure who supplied the best commentary upon this instantaneous growth. He was a tall, sunburnt man, dressed partly as a hunter and partly as a soldier, with a long rifle upon his shoulder, a powder-horn at his side, and boots bearing evidence of roads less civilized than those beneath him. He had perhaps followed the frontier westward, expecting to find it always before him, and had now been overtaken by brickwork.

He stood at a crowded crossing while coaches, carts, riders, and wagons divided and reunited around him. His expression was not fear, but a grave bewilderment. He looked at the hotel, at the signs, at the mill-smoke, at the glittering shops; then at his rifle, as if that old counselor had failed to explain the difficulty. The weapon which had been an argument in the woods was here merely an inconvenience. No hostile beast confronted him, unless the omnibus could be classed as one; no path needed blazing; no fire required protection. Civilization had surrounded him so rapidly that he seemed not to have been conquered by it, but ambushed.

For a moment he remained motionless, while the town hurried, trotted, rattled, and rumbled past. Then he crossed the street with great caution and disappeared behind a coach bound westward.

The incident impressed me more than the mills. There went the older America, armed, solitary, suspicious, carrying its necessities upon its back; around it surged the newer America, incorporated, advertised, litigated, insured, and in debt. The hunter had subdued the wilderness; the speculator had purchased it from him; the surveyor had divided it; the lawyer had described it; the merchant had stocked it; and the hotel-keeper was ready to charge them all for dinner.

Nor was the change ended. Beyond Rochester stretched the West—that spacious word which continually retreats as we advance, drawing roads, towns, ambition, and disappointment after it. Every coach carried recruits; every boat bore furniture, tools, children, hopes, and family Bibles; every newspaper widened the horizon with reports of land still cheaper and fortune still farther away. Rochester itself, though boasting like an established capital, was in some sense only a camp upon the march.

When night settled fully, I looked back from the road. The falls muttered below, robbed but not silenced; the mills shone beside them; lamps marked the principal streets; and from the town arose that confused, unceasing murmur in which wheels, waters, hammers, and human voices had become one sound. Nature and enterprise were quarreling in the dark, yet each had borrowed the other’s strength. The river moved machinery; the machinery enlarged the city; the city diminished the cataract; and the diminished cataract, by serving men, became the cause of a spectacle scarcely less astonishing than its former grandeur.

Sam Patch had leaped there for fame. Rochester had made the greater leap—for fortune—and thus far had reached the surface alive.

## IV.

From Rochester I continued toward Detroit, joining a company of westward passengers whose variety would have furnished a census-taker with occupation and a philosopher with despair. There were emigrant families surrounded by bedding, kettles, baskets, children, and the melancholy remnants of household order; merchants guarding small trunks with great vigilance; young adventurers guarding nothing because they possessed nothing; elderly men traveling for health and discussing their diseases; ladies who endured discomfort with more composure than their protectors; speculators carrying maps; mechanics carrying tools; and several persons whose business remained a mystery even after they had explained it.

Our steamboat labored along with much panting, trembling, and smoky breath. The vessel combined the appearance of a floating hotel with the internal anxieties of a factory. Above, passengers walked, ate, slept, complained, played cards, admired sunsets, and inquired when we should arrive; below, unseen powers clanged, hissed, revolved, and threatened. The deck offered landscape and society; the engine-room supplied fate.

Lake Erie had been smooth during the afternoon, though not with the smiling tranquillity of Champlain. Its broad surface wore a harder color; the horizon seemed more remote; and the low shores, appearing and vanishing through haze, gave little encouragement to the imagination. Toward evening clouds gathered in the west, not stormily, but in long dusky ranges, among which the sunset burned like a red furnace closing for the night. One by one the colors withdrew. The passengers descended; the wind freshened; and darkness, having no mountain or island to define it, spread over the lake with an immensity that made our vessel seem no larger than the spark from its own chimney.

At some uncertain hour I was awakened by a change in the accustomed motion. A traveler soon learns the pulse of a steamboat, and starts when it falters as a mother does beside a sleeping child. The regular throb had ceased. Feet moved rapidly overhead; the machinery uttered detached groans; then came silence, followed by the lowering of a boat and the confused sounds of orders carefully stripped of alarming words.

We had suffered some mechanical injury—small, as all such injuries are officially described, though sufficiently large to stop us. The boat had approached a wooded shore, and preparations were made for repair. Fuel also was required; for our iron monster, whatever might be wrong with its limbs, retained an excellent appetite. A party of laborers, chiefly Irishmen employed aboard or taken on during the journey, went ashore to collect wood. Soon axes rang among the trees, and a fire was kindled near the margin.

At first it was no more than a useful blaze. A few sticks crackled; smoke drove low before the wind; men passed between the boat and the trees carrying branches. But the fire grew rapidly. Dead limbs were thrown upon it, then split logs, then heaps of brush, until a broad red flame leaped upward and bent beneath the night air. The nearest trunks flashed into view, each leaf and strip of bark distinct for an instant; beyond them the forest remained solid black. The lake caught a broken path of crimson, shattered it with small waves, and sent the fragments trembling toward us.

I stood upon the deck, wrapped against the cold, and watched.

The men entered and left the light in a ceaseless procession. At one moment the shore seemed deserted; then a figure burst suddenly from the darkness, black against the flames, stooping under a load of branches. His hair, blown loose, took a fiery edge; his arms lengthened in monstrous shadow; sparks whirled about his feet. He flung his burden down, sprang aside as the blaze mounted, and vanished again among the trees. Another appeared dragging a log; two more crossed behind him; a fourth stood with an axe uplifted, and for a second his shadow climbed the illuminated trunks until it seemed some giant of the wood had raised a weapon against the stars.

Their ordinary garments were transformed by the uncertain glare. Coats became shaggy hides; caps sharpened into horns; red shirts glowed like wounds; faces, visible only in portions, showed an eye, a cheek, a row of teeth, and were instantly abolished by darkness. The labor itself acquired the gestures of incantation. Men bent, rushed, lifted, cast fuel upon the heap, and retreated before the answering flame. The fire roared approval.

I knew them for tired laborers gathering wood because machinery had failed and passengers wished to proceed. Reason supplied the axes, the wages, the engineer, the damaged part, and our position upon Lake Erie. Fancy rejected these poor materials. Under her management the shore became the boundary of another world, and the men no longer served a steamboat, but tended some enormous subterranean furnace whose chimney was the night.

What if the old tales were not false, but merely ill-lighted? Perhaps the supernatural requires nothing more than darkness, distance, fatigue, and a fire seen from water. By day we classify a man according to his occupation and the cut of his coat; at midnight, when flame tears him for an instant from the immense void and darkness swallows him again, we remember that all flesh is mysterious.

The blaze now towered above the bank. Smoke rolled through it in dense folds, reddening from within; sparks ascended by thousands, like a reversed shower of stars; the trees leaned and sprang back as the illumination shifted; and the lake, black everywhere else, writhed beneath the reflection. Had the final conflagration begun upon that lonely shore, it could scarcely have announced itself with more theatrical propriety.

One figure, standing near the center, directed the work by gestures. He was broad-shouldered and bareheaded; the wind tossed his hair; and a long pole in his hands became, by the simple operation of firelight, a trident. Around him the others came laden from the invisible forest, delivered their offerings, and disappeared. I could almost believe them condemned spirits compelled to feed the element that punished them; yet there was in their vigor something more triumphant than miserable, as though the flames were their own and they rejoiced in the approaching ruin of the world.

Then the wind shifted. Smoke swept over the entire scene, hiding men, trees, and fire beneath a red-brown cloud. For several seconds nothing remained but a dull glow; afterward the vapor tore open, and the figures reappeared in new positions, without visible passage from the old. One stood upon a log; another crouched close to the earth; three emerged together bearing a heavy branch, their bodies moving with a single rhythm. They seemed not men but thoughts of fire—formed, distorted, extinguished, and formed again.

The passengers had gathered quietly along the rail. Even those who had spent the day disputing over berths and baggage were subdued. A child, awakened and brought on deck, stared without speaking. An elderly gentleman who had previously explained every feature of the engine now offered no explanation of the spectacle. The practical cause was too plain to need one; the imaginative effect was too complete to permit it.

Meanwhile, aboard the vessel, mechanics worked by lantern-light. Now and then a hammer struck metal, producing a sharp note unlike the muffled axes ashore. Steam whispered from a valve. Men carried tools below. The captain passed with the compressed look of one upon whom a hundred private anxieties have suddenly claimed public authority. Thus two dramas proceeded side by side: one of bolts, fuel, pressure, and repair; the other of demons, flame, darkness, and the end of created things. Both were equally visible. Only one was entered in the log.

At length the wood was sufficient. The men came down to the water, their supernatural offices concluded, and resumed, as they entered the boat, their common shapes. Horns became caps; hides became jackets; the trident dwindled to a pole. One stumbled upon the plank, another rubbed his hands against the cold, a third bent wearily beneath the final load. The devils were laborers again—the transformation proving, perhaps, that many terrors would become objects of sympathy if they were allowed to come near enough.

The engine gave a preliminary shudder. A wheel turned, stopped, and turned once more. The familiar beat resumed slowly, then strengthened into regularity. We moved from the shore.

For a time the great fire followed us. It shone between the trees, disappeared behind a projecting bank, returned as a wavering redness above the woods, and finally contracted to a single spark. The lake closed over its reflection; darkness reclaimed the men’s brief theater; and the shoreline vanished so utterly that the whole spectacle might have been a vision produced by smoke, fatigue, and the uneasy conscience of a traveler.

Yet it remained with me more vividly than many a monument viewed at noon. Burlington had shown me commerce masquerading as the sea; Rochester, a wilderness masquerading as an ancient city; the Genesee, grandeur put to work; and here common labor had worn for half an hour the aspect of infernal ceremony. In each case reality had crossed some invisible border without ceasing to be real.

We resumed our westward course, bearing emigrants, merchandise, ambitions, ailments, maps, tools, and repaired machinery into the darkness. Behind us the fire sank; before us lay Detroit and the farther country, where new towns were already preparing to call themselves old, and forests waited to become streets. I remained on deck until the cold compelled me below, listening to the steady pulse of the engine and watching the sparks from our chimney fly upward, burn brilliantly, and expire.

They resembled reputations, cities, and men: a sudden brightness against the immense void of night—then darkness; yet, for the traveler who has seen them, not altogether forgotten.

## The Boy Who Knew

There was once a woman who appeared to possess every necessary blessing. She was beautiful in a pale, finished way; she had married for love, or for what she had believed to be love; and she had three fine children, two girls and a boy. People who met her in drawing-rooms said that she was charming. People who saw her with her children said that she was devoted.

Nevertheless, at the centre of her there remained a little chamber where no warmth entered.

She knew it. She had known it almost from the birth of her first child. When the children ran to her, she bent and kissed them. When they were ill, she sat beside them. She chose their clothes with care and remembered their birthdays. Yet, behind her tenderness, she felt herself watching, as though some colder woman looked through her eyes and wondered why love had not come.

The children did not know this in words. Children seldom know the names of the forces governing them. But they watched their mother’s face. They became quiet when she entered unexpectedly. They sensed that her kisses were accurate and that her embraces ended before they were complete.

The mother herself suffered from the secret. Because she believed she ought to love them, she behaved as though she loved them greatly. And because the performance was almost perfect, everyone praised her.

Only the children and the mother knew that something was wanting.

The family lived in a pleasant house in a fashionable quarter of London. There was a narrow garden behind, a nursery high above the street, a motor-car that was not quite new, and servants enough to suggest comfort without ever securing it. The father went each morning to the City in a well-cut coat. The mother entertained, shopped, and made clever attempts to earn money by advising a draper about new fabrics and arrangements of colour.

Yet neither the father nor the mother possessed the power of money.

They had an income. They had also tastes, obligations, acquaintances, expectations and debts. The income entered politely at the front door, took off its hat, and departed at once by six windows and a servants’ passage. There was always something unpaid. School fees approached. A bill from the dressmaker waited beneath the silver tray. The motor required repair. The father’s club account grew. A man from the bank wrote restrained letters, which were more alarming than rude ones would have been.

Outwardly, the house was serene. There were flowers in the hall, iridescent cushions in the drawing-room, good prints upon the walls and fruit in a blue glass bowl. But through the rooms moved a pressure that no servant could dust away.

It seemed to say:

More is wanted.

The words were never spoken aloud at first. They came from the unopened envelopes upon the escritoire. They trembled in the fur collar of the mother’s winter coat and glittered in the sequins of her evening dress. They rustled beneath the children’s clothes, which were good but must last another season. They hid in the shining motor and in the polished piano.

More is wanted. More. More.

At Christmas the nursery was crowded with gifts. There were dolls in silk dresses, a miniature theatre, boxes of soldiers, a train that ran on metal rails, and a great wooden rocking-horse with a red mouth and one fixed, glassy eye. Even then, amid the tissue paper and coloured ribbons, the whisper entered.

These things have cost money.

More is wanted.

The dolls appeared to hear it. Their painted faces stared. The soldiers listened from their box. The rocking-horse leaned forward upon its curved bow, always galloping and never advancing, as though he too knew that effort could be endless and arrival impossible.

Of the three children, Paul heard the whisper most distinctly.

He was a slender boy with light hair and large blue eyes. At times his eyes were soft and uncertain. At other times they became so still and brilliant that adults hesitated beneath his gaze. He was not the eldest by much, but he felt responsible for things that no one had asked him to understand.

One afternoon he found his mother arranging white flowers in the drawing-room. She wore a pale grey dress, and the flowers seemed to belong to her hands. Outside, rain made a delicate smoke against the windows.

“Mother,” Paul said, “why don’t we keep a car of our own?”

“We do keep one.”

“No. I mean a good one. A new one.”

His mother moved a stem to the other side of the bowl.

“Because we are not rich.”

“Why aren’t we rich?”

She smiled faintly. “Most people aren’t.”

“But we look rich.”

“That is quite a different matter.”

Paul considered this. The difference troubled him more than the answer.

“Why haven’t we enough money?”

“Because your father has no luck.”

She spoke lightly, as though the words were merely another stem placed among the flowers. But a bitterness escaped with them.

“Is luck money?” Paul asked.

“No.”

“Then what is luck?”

“Luck is what causes money to come to you.”

“I thought people earned money.”

“They do. But unless they are lucky, they earn too little, or lose what they earn, or make the wrong decision at the proper moment. Your father works. He has prospects. He is always going to do well. Yet somehow it does not happen.”

“Why?”

“Because he is unlucky.”

The boy looked down at the carpet.

“Was he always unlucky?”

“I did not know it when I married him.”

The white flowers gave off a cool, expensive scent. His mother stepped back and examined them, dissatisfied.

“Can luck change?” he asked.

“Perhaps. But it rarely does.”

“Are you lucky?”

She gave a short laugh.

“I cannot be very lucky, can I?”

“Because you married Father?”

Her face altered. Only for an instant. Then it was smooth again.

“You ask too many questions, Paul.”

“But is God lucky?”

“God does not require luck.”

“Does God give it?”

“I suppose so, if He gives anything.”

“Then He might give it to me.”

His mother turned and looked at him. The boy’s face was grave. There was nothing playful in it.

“Why should He?”

“Because I’m lucky.”

“Are you?”

“Yes,” said Paul. “I’m sure.”

She laughed again, not cruelly, but without belief. She kissed the top of his head and went out to dress for dinner.

Paul remained beside the flowers.

“I’m lucky,” he whispered.

The room did not answer. But from behind the curtains and beneath the carpet, from the silver photograph frames and the cream-coloured walls, came the soft insistence:

More is wanted.

Paul went upstairs.

The rocking-horse stood in the nursery, rigid in full career. Its mane was real horsehair, rubbed thin by years of grasping hands. Its nostrils were scarlet; its wooden teeth showed in a permanent effort. The glass eye stared beyond the wallpaper, beyond the house, beyond London perhaps, toward some destination invisible to everyone else.

Paul mounted.

He had grown almost too large for the horse. His knees rose high, and his feet nearly touched the floor. Nevertheless, he seized the handles and began to rock.

At first the motion was ordinary. Forward and back. Forward and back. The curved rails thudded softly upon the rug.

“Take me where luck is,” he said.

The horse sprang nowhere.

Paul rocked harder.

“Come on! You know. You must know.”

The nursery shook with the rhythm. His hair fell over his forehead. The horse’s red mouth leapt toward the wall and retreated. Its one bright eye flashed beneath the lamp.

“Take me there!”

Forward, backward. Forward, backward. The boy urged, struck, flung himself into the movement. The wooden body charged beneath him. The walls drew away. The toys blurred. The floor seemed to lift and fall like dark water.

Then, sometimes, something happened.

A name entered him.

He did not invent it. He did not remember hearing it. The name appeared whole and hard, like a stone uncovered when a tide withdrew. For a second he knew it with the certainty of cold, fire or pain.

Then the horse slowed, the nursery returned, and he sat trembling above the rug.

The first name was Daffodil.

He repeated it several times.

“Daffodil. Daffodil. Daffodil.”

The rocking-horse looked past him.

Paul knew nothing about Daffodil. But Bassett, the young gardener, knew about horses.

Bassett had been wounded in the war and walked with a stiffness in one leg. He was solemn, narrow-faced and respectful. He clipped the lawn, trained the roses, and spoke of racing as some men speak of weather and others of religion. Paul’s father had once told him not to fill the boy’s head with betting nonsense. Bassett had accepted the rebuke with lowered eyes. But he and Paul continued to discuss the races beside the potting shed, where the damp smell of earth made secrets seem secure.

“Bassett,” Paul said the next morning, “is there a horse called Daffodil?”

Bassett stopped winding a length of twine.

“There is, sir.”

“Is it running soon?”

“Lincoln, sir.”

“Can it win?”

Bassett’s expression became cautious.

“It can, sir. Anything in the field can win, in a manner of speaking.”

“Do you think it will?”

“I wouldn’t go as far as that.”

“I know it will.”

Bassett looked at him.

The boy’s eyes had the stony brightness that made grown people uneasy.

“You know, sir?”

“I’m sure.”

“Where did you hear?”

“I didn’t hear.”

“Then how—?”

“I know. Will you put something on it for me?”

Bassett moved the twine from one hand to the other.

“It isn’t proper, sir.”

“Why not?”

“You being a young gentleman.”

“I have five shillings.”

“That don’t alter it.”

“Please, Bassett. Honour bright, no one will know.”

The phrase belonged to adults, and for that reason it sounded strange in the child’s mouth.

Bassett studied him. He had known Paul since the boy could barely walk. He believed in rank, racing form, divine Providence, and the mysterious appearance of certainty in unexpected persons. He did not set these beliefs in opposition. They rested side by side in his mind.

“At what odds?” Paul asked.

Bassett’s eyebrows rose slightly.

“You’ve been learning, sir.”

“At what odds?”

“Could be twelve to one by race day.”

“Put my five shillings on Daffodil.”

Bassett hesitated.

“Honour bright,” Paul said.

“All right, sir. Just this once.”

Daffodil won.

Afterward Bassett brought Paul three pounds and five shillings in a tobacco tin. He explained the figures carefully. Paul counted the coins twice, not because he distrusted Bassett but because the money seemed to prove something.

“I told you,” he said. “I’m lucky.”

“You were right, sir.”

“I knew.”

Bassett said nothing.

That evening the house whispered as before. Three pounds and five shillings made no impression on it. The curtains still breathed, More. The lamps glowed, More. Somewhere below, Paul heard his parents’ voices rise and then become controlled again.

He went to the nursery and mounted the horse.

“Where next?” he demanded. “Where next? Take me there.”

The wooden animal plunged.

Sometimes the knowledge came, and sometimes it did not.

When it came, Paul wagered. When it did not, he refused.

Bassett soon understood this distinction. The boy might say, “I fancy Lively Spark,” and Bassett knew that meant nothing more than an opinion. But if Paul’s face became blank and brilliant, if he said, “I’m sure,” then Bassett felt a movement in his own blood.

They won on Lancelot. They lost a little on Merry Andrew, about whom Paul had not been sure. They won heavily on Sansovino.

The tobacco tin became insufficient.

Bassett kept an account in a small notebook wrapped in oilcloth. Every stake and return was recorded in his careful hand. He treated Paul’s money with the seriousness of a trust fund. Before long there were hundreds of pounds lodged secretly in Bassett’s savings account and later with a bookmaker who asked few questions.

Paul told no one. His sisters sometimes watched him riding, but children will accept the most extraordinary behaviour if it has always been present.

“You’re too big for that horse,” Joan said.

“I’m not.”

“You look silly.”

“I don’t.”

“Where do you ride to?”

“Nowhere.”

But that was false. He rode there.

There was a place beyond speed and noise, a place reached only when his body had become an instrument and his will had narrowed to a blade. At first there were many names, many colours, the remembered voices of Bassett and the scraps of sporting news heard at breakfast. Then everything was beaten away by the rocking. One name remained.

He could not say who spoke it.

Perhaps the horse spoke. Perhaps the house did. Perhaps his mother’s cold distress entered him and turned itself into knowledge. Perhaps he guessed as gamblers guess and remembered the victories more fiercely than the losses.

Yet there were victories.

And the money increased.

Uncle Oscar discovered the secret by accident.

Oscar was the mother’s elder brother, a large, agreeable man with a red-brown moustache and the careless confidence of one whose financial position was solid enough to make gambling amusing. He called at the house one Saturday and found Paul in the garden talking to Bassett.

“What’s the conspiracy?” Oscar asked.

Bassett straightened.

“No conspiracy, sir.”

“We were discussing the Ascot card,” said Paul.

“Oh, were you? And what does this young oracle fancy?”

Paul looked at Bassett. Bassett looked at Paul.

“Well?” said Oscar.

“Singhalese,” Paul replied at last.

“Singhalese? Nonsense. It hasn’t the pace.”

“I think it has.”

“Think, do you?”

“I’m not sure.”

Oscar laughed. “A careful tipster. And when are you sure?”

Paul flushed.

Bassett said quietly, “The young gentleman has been remarkably correct once or twice, sir.”

“Has he indeed?”

Uncle Oscar’s amusement sharpened into interest.

“How much have you lost, Paul?”

“I haven’t lost.”

“Everybody loses.”

“I have over a thousand pounds.”

Oscar’s laugh stopped.

“A thousand shillings?”

“Pounds.”

“Nonsense.”

“It’s true, sir,” said Bassett.

The gardener’s solemnity made the matter more absurd and also more credible.

Oscar took them into the potting shed and shut the door. Sunlight entered through dusty panes. Pots stood in brown ranks upon the shelves.

“Now,” he said, “let’s have the whole sporting romance.”

Paul explained. He did not mention how he obtained the names. He said only that he sometimes knew.

“And Bassett acts as your commissioner?”

“Yes.”

“Without your father’s knowledge?”

“Yes.”

“Without your mother’s?”

“Yes.”

“Good Lord.”

“It’s our secret,” Paul said. “Honour bright.”

Uncle Oscar looked from the boy to the gardener.

“You appreciate, Bassett, that this is irregular?”

“Yes, sir.”

“And possibly outrageous?”

“Yes, sir.”

“And that you have nevertheless continued?”

Bassett lowered his voice. “When Master Paul is sure, sir, it seems to come from beyond ordinary judgement.”

“From beyond ordinary judgement!”

“That is how it seems, sir.”

“Show me the accounts.”

Bassett produced the notebook. Oscar read it twice. The entries were exact. The bookmaker’s name was reputable enough. The balance stood at one thousand four hundred and twenty pounds.

Oscar whistled.

“Well, Paul, you’re a queer little fish.”

“I’m lucky,” said Paul.

“So it appears.”

“Mother says Father is unlucky. I’m not.”

Oscar’s face became less amused.

“Did she tell you that?”

“Yes. But I’m lucky. I knew I was.”

The boy’s insistence struck Oscar as both comic and painful. He had no wish to examine the pain, so he returned to the comedy.

“Very well. We’ll test this revelation. What wins the Leger?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“When will you?”

“When I get there.”

“Get where?”

Paul’s lips closed.

“He’ll not say, sir,” Bassett murmured. “And I don’t press him.”

Oscar drove Paul to the race-meeting. The boy sat beside him in a neat coat and cap, outwardly an ordinary child enjoying a masculine expedition. Yet his eyes moved continually, taking in the names, odds and colours. The stands blazed with dresses and polished glasses. Bookmakers cried their prices. Men with grave faces debated chance as though the nation’s fate depended on a length of turf.

“What about Singhalese?” Oscar asked.

“I’m not sure.”

“A hundred to win?”

“No. Twenty.”

“Very prudent.”

Singhalese lost.

Paul accepted it without surprise.

“You see,” he said. “I wasn’t sure.”

Before the next race, he touched his uncle’s sleeve.

“Put three hundred on Mirza.”

Oscar stared at him.

“Three hundred! Your own money?”

“Yes.”

“Are you sure?”

“I’m sure.”

The words were quiet, but there was something beneath them that did not belong to ordinary conversation.

Oscar put on one hundred for Paul and twenty for himself.

Mirza won at ten to one.

After the race, amid shouting and jostling, Oscar looked down at his nephew. Paul was pale. His eyes burned as though victory had not relieved him but fed a fever.

“You knew,” Oscar said.

“Yes.”

“How?”

“I knew.”

“From Bassett?”

“No.”

“From anyone?”

“No.”

“Then where does it come from?”

Paul looked toward the course, where the horses were being led away, dark and wet beneath the bright sky.

“It comes when I’m sure.”

Oscar might have stopped the business then. He sometimes remembered this later. He might have spoken to Paul’s mother, dismissed Bassett from the arrangement, and insisted that the boy be kept from racing news. But the thing had the charm of a private miracle, and Oscar enjoyed miracles when they yielded ten to one.

Instead he became a partner.

The fortune grew.

There were setbacks, but Paul’s certainties were astonishing. He knew when not to risk much. He knew when to stake heavily. Bassett continued to manage the accounts with reverence, while Oscar arranged the larger bets and protected the boy from suspicious enquiries.

Soon Paul had ten thousand pounds.

The sum seemed tremendous to him, large enough to change the climate of the house. Yet the whisper continued.

More is wanted.

At breakfast, his mother opened a letter and went pale. At luncheon, she spoke sharply to the maid because the fish was overcooked. In the evening, she sat among silver cushions correcting fashion sketches for the draper, her beautiful mouth compressed.

Paul watched her.

If the house could be satisfied, his mother might cease to be anxious. If she ceased to be anxious, perhaps she would look at him without that secret distance. He did not reason this plainly, but the hope was alive within him. Money, luck and love had become three faces of the same power.

One day he spoke to Uncle Oscar.

“I want to give Mother five thousand pounds.”

Oscar lowered the newspaper.

“Do you?”

“Yes. But she mustn’t know it comes from me.”

“Why not?”

“She’d stop me.”

“A sensible argument in her favour.”

“She says she has no luck. I want her to have some.”

Oscar tapped the paper against his knee.

“Five thousand is a great deal of money.”

“I have more.”

“You might lose the rest.”

“I’ll win more.”

“You’re confident.”

“I’m sure.”

Oscar disliked the way the boy said it. Still, five thousand pounds would unquestionably ease his sister’s affairs.

“How do you propose to present it?”

“You arrange things.”

Eventually they formed a plan with the family solicitor. The money would be transferred as though it came from a forgotten relative’s investment. Paul’s mother would receive one thousand pounds each year for five years, on her birthday. Thus, Oscar thought, the gift would bring security without encouraging extravagance.

On the morning of her birthday, the letter arrived.

Paul watched her open it at breakfast. The winter sun lay weakly across the tablecloth. His sisters talked about a governess. His father had already gone out.

The mother read the solicitor’s message. Her face did not brighten. It became intent.

“Well, Mother?” Paul asked. “Have you had anything nice?”

“Nothing of consequence.”

Her voice was casual, but her fingers held the paper tightly.

“Was it good news?”

“A small legacy.”

“Are you pleased?”

“It is always useful to have money.”

That afternoon she went to the solicitor.

Paul knew because Uncle Oscar told him later. She had asked whether the entire five thousand could be paid at once. There were pressing obligations, she said. It was inconvenient to receive the sum by instalments. The solicitor consulted Oscar. Oscar consulted Paul.

“She wants the whole lot,” said Oscar. “A bird in the hand, you know.”

“Give it to her.”

“All five thousand?”

“Yes.”

“You understand that once she has it, it is gone?”

“She needs it.”

“Very well.”

The money was paid.

For several days the house seemed to pause. Bills were settled. The mother ordered new furnishings. Paul was promised a tutor so that he might prepare for Eton. His sisters received fresh dresses. There were flowers every evening and talk of replacing the car.

Then the whisper returned.

Not softly.

It ran through the passages and climbed the stairs. It shook in the new curtains and rang beneath the polished plates. The walls seemed to stretch their mouths.

More! Now there can be more! More than before! More, more, more!

Paul lay awake and heard it.

The five thousand had not silenced the house. It had taught the house to cry.

His mother worked harder at her drawings. She earned more from the draper and immediately required more. The family’s standard of comfort rose like water around them, covering each new sum. Nothing was enough because enough was not a figure. It was a feeling, and no one in the house possessed it.

Paul’s face grew thinner.

He studied racing papers in secret. Names marched through his thoughts: Cinders, Green Mantle, Silver Bell, Larkspur. At meals he seemed absent. His sisters complained that he stared. His mother noticed that his eyes had an unnatural fixity, but she attributed it to growth and nerves.

“You mustn’t overwork,” she said.

“I don’t.”

“You look feverish.”

“I’m all right.”

“Are you worried about school?”

“No.”

“What are you worried about?”

“Nothing.”

She touched his forehead. Her hand was cool and elegant.

For one instant he longed to press his face against it and tell her everything: the horse, the names, the money, the terrible place of certainty. But he feared she would take the rocking-horse away. Worse, she might laugh as she had laughed when he first said he was lucky.

So he stood still beneath her hand.

“You are a strange child,” she said.

“Do you love me?” he asked.

The question surprised her.

“Of course I do.”

“As much as you love the girls?”

“What a ridiculous thing to ask.”

“As much as you love anyone?”

Her hand dropped.

“You are overtired.”

“But do you?”

“I have answered you.”

She kissed him and left. The kiss was soft. The little chamber at the centre of her remained locked.

Spring came.

Paul had a bad season. He was not sure. He rode and rode, yet names broke apart before he could seize them. He fancied two horses that lost. He refused to bet heavily, but each uncertainty increased his desperation.

Bassett urged caution.

“Best to wait, sir. There’s always another race.”

“There isn’t always.”

“There’s the Derby ahead.”

“I know.”

“We’ve money enough laid by to last many years.”

“It isn’t enough.”

Bassett looked troubled. “By most standards, sir, it is a fortune.”

“The house says it isn’t.”

“The house, sir?”

Paul turned away.

Uncle Oscar also noticed the change.

“You’re taking this too seriously, my boy.”

“I must know the Derby.”

“No one must know anything of the kind.”

“I must.”

“What’s wrong with taking a modest interest?”

Paul’s eyes flashed. “It has to be certain.”

“Certainty is not part of racing.”

“It is for me.”

Oscar felt a cold uneasiness. The secret game no longer appeared genial. He saw the boy’s narrow shoulders and the blue veins in his temples. But the Derby approached, and Oscar himself had begun to wonder what name would come. Concern struggled with appetite. Appetite was quieter, but it endured.

Paul’s mother decided that the boy ought to go to the seaside before the race.

“You look ill,” she said. “We shall send you with Miss Wilmot and the girls.”

“I can’t go.”

“You can and will.”

“Not before the Derby.”

She stared at him.

“What has the Derby to do with you?”

“Uncle Oscar is taking me.”

“He certainly is not.”

“He said he might.”

“Then he was joking.”

“I must stay here.”

The force in his voice startled her.

“Why, Paul?”

“I just must.”

“Are you interested in racing?”

“A little.”

“Your father has always considered it a vulgar waste.”

Paul said nothing. His father had no luck. His father’s opinions therefore carried no power.

“You will go to the seaside on Monday,” she said.

“No!”

It was the first time he had shouted at her.

The word remained between them. His mother’s face became expressionless.

“I beg your pardon?”

Paul trembled.

“I’m sorry. But please let me stay. Please, Mother. Until after the Derby.”

“Why?”

He searched for an answer she would accept.

“Because I’m comfortable in my own room.”

“Your room?”

“The nursery.”

“You scarcely use it now.”

“I like my things there.”

She watched him. A peculiar suspicion entered her mind, though she could not have said what she suspected. The nursery, with its outgrown toys, represented a part of the children’s lives from which she had always felt excluded. Perhaps Paul kept some childish secret there. Perhaps it was no more than fear of change.

“You may stay,” she said at last. “But only if you rest.”

“I will.”

He meant it as he spoke. He always meant his promises at the moment of making them. Then the whisper came.

More is wanted.

At night he went to the nursery.

The moonlight made the horse pale. Its red mouth was black in the darkness. Paul climbed upon it and rocked.

“Derby,” he whispered. “Give me the Derby.”

The horse creaked.

“Take me there.”

He rode until sweat chilled upon his skin. Nothing came.

The next night he rode again.

“Derby. Derby. I must know.”

Names flickered and vanished. Praetor. Bolingbroke. Malabar. Torchlight. They meant nothing. He could not discover which was the one.

At breakfast he could barely eat.

His mother said, “Did you sleep?”

“Yes.”

“You look dreadful.”

“I’m all right.”

The house murmured beneath the clink of cups:

More, more, more.

As the race drew nearer, Paul ceased to hear ordinary conversation. His tutor’s voice became a drone. The street sounded far away. Every pulse in his body asked the same question.

Which horse?

Bassett came by the kitchen garden, but Paul had no certainty for him.

“What about Malabar?” Bassett asked.

“I don’t know.”

“There’s quiet support for him.”

“I don’t know!”

Bassett lowered his eyes.

“Very good, sir.”

“Don’t bet yet.”

“No, sir.”

“If I know, I’ll send for you.”

“Yes, sir.”

“Honour bright.”

“Honour bright, sir.”

The words had once bound a game. Now they sounded like vows made at an altar.

Three nights before the Derby, Paul’s mother went out to dinner with friends. She wore a gown the colour of ivory and a narrow band of diamonds at her throat. Her husband was away in Scotland. Before leaving, she looked in on the children. The girls slept. Paul appeared to be asleep, his face turned toward the wall.

At dinner she was admired. A man on her right told her that she looked younger than she had in years. She smiled, answered cleverly, and felt nothing.

Yet beneath the conversation a thread of anxiety tightened in her.

She thought of Paul.

She saw his bright, fixed eyes. She remembered the violence of his refusal to leave before the Derby. She heard him asking, Do you love me? The memory made her uncomfortable, almost angry. Children should not demand an accounting of love. Love ought to be accepted in the forms provided.

At eleven she went to the telephone and called home.

The governess answered.

“Is everything well?”

“Quite well, madam.”

“And Master Paul?”

“He went to bed at the usual time.”

“Was he asleep when you looked in?”

“I believe so, madam.”

“Go and see now.”

There was a pause. The mother listened to the faint noises of connection, the distant life of the house transmitted through wire.

The governess returned.

“He isn’t in his bed, madam.”

“What do you mean?”

“He may be in the nursery.”

“At this hour?”

“I’ll look.”

Another pause.

Then the governess said, “The nursery door is locked.”

The mother’s hand tightened around the receiver.

“Locked?”

“Yes, madam. I knocked, but there was no answer.”

“What is he doing?”

“I don’t know.”

“Open it.”

“I haven’t the key.”

The mother felt something cold move beneath her ribs.

“I am coming home.”

She left at once.

During the drive, London passed in black windows and wet lamps. She told herself that Paul was playing. He had fallen asleep on the nursery sofa. He was hiding some silly mechanical toy. Nothing had happened.

Nevertheless, urgency beat in her mind.

The motor stopped. She entered the house without removing her cloak and ran upstairs.

The upper passage was dim. The governess waited outside the nursery.

“Listen,” she whispered.

At first the mother heard nothing.

Then there came a faint rhythmic blow.

Thud—creak.

Thud—creak.

Thud—creak.

It was rapid and regular, like a machine working behind the locked door.

“What is it?” the mother said.

The governess shook her head.

The mother called, “Paul!”

The sound continued.

Thud—creak. Thud—creak.

“Paul, open this door!”

No answer.

The noise accelerated.

What was it? What in heaven’s name was it?

The mother seized the handle and shook it. The lock gave suddenly; it had not been fully turned. The door opened.

Moonlight flooded the nursery.

At first she saw only a violent shape surging against the pale wall. Something charged forward and recoiled. A head tossed. A body rose and plunged.

Then she saw the rocking-horse.

Paul was mounted upon it in his nightclothes. His hair was dark with sweat. He slashed the wooden flanks with a small riding whip, though the horse could feel nothing. His bare feet struck the rails. His face was turned upward; his eyes were wide and blind.

“Now!” he cried. “Now take me there! Take me there!”

“Paul!”

He did not hear.

The horse flung him forward and back, forward and back. The glass eye flashed. The red mouth opened. The wooden runners hammered the floor with a noise like approaching hooves.

“Paul, stop!”

The boy gave a last convulsive thrust.

Then his body stiffened.

“Malabar!” he screamed. “It’s Malabar! Malabar! I’m sure!”

He saw his mother in the doorway. For a second triumph shone in his face.

Then he fell.

The horse rocked on without him.

For two days Paul lay unconscious with brain fever.

Doctors came and went. Curtains were drawn. Ice was placed upon his head. His mother sat beside him, still in the pale composure with which she met disaster. But the chamber at the centre of her was no longer quiet. Something struck against its locked door.

Paul muttered.

Sometimes he spoke of money. Sometimes he commanded the horse. Often he said the name.

“Malabar.”

His mother bent near.

“What is Malabar?”

He did not answer.

Bassett came to the servants’ entrance on the day before the Derby and begged to know how Master Paul was. The message reached Uncle Oscar, who arrived that afternoon.

Oscar stood at the foot of the bed. The boy’s face had become small and remote upon the pillow. His hands moved restlessly over the sheet.

“Malabar,” Paul whispered.

Oscar looked at his sister.

“What does it mean?” she asked.

He hesitated too long.

“You know,” she said.

“It’s a horse.”

“A horse in the Derby?”

“Yes.”

Her face whitened.

“What has Paul to do with it?”

“Very little, perhaps.”

“Don’t lie to me.”

Oscar had never before heard her speak with such naked force.

He told her part of it: Bassett, the tips, the winnings. He did not describe every frenzied certainty, nor confess the pleasure he had taken in the game. Still, as he spoke, the ugliness of the arrangement emerged.

“You allowed my child to gamble?”

“He was astonishingly successful.”

“He is a little boy.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Oscar turned away.

From the bed came the thin voice:

“Malabar. Malabar.”

The mother looked at her son. Ten thousand pounds, Oscar had said. Perhaps more. The notion was monstrous. It was also real. Even beside the bed, with death perhaps in the room, the figure entered her mind and stood there.

She hated herself for seeing it.

The next day was the Derby.

Bassett sent no message in the morning. At noon, however, he appeared at the door. Oscar received him in the library.

“Well?” Oscar asked.

Bassett’s face was grey.

“I’ve done it, sir.”

“How much?”

“All that Master Paul said should go on when he was certain.”

“How much?”

“Eight thousand, sir.”

Oscar stared.

“On Malabar?”

“Yes, sir.”

“At fourteen to one for most of it.”

“Are you mad?”

“Master Paul was sure.”

“The boy is delirious!”

“He named Malabar before the fever took him, sir. You heard him.”

Oscar crossed the room and returned.

“We should stop it.”

“Too late, sir.”

“Good God.”

They waited.

The race was run while Paul lay between life and death.

Far from the darkened bedroom, a crowd roared beneath a bright sky. Horses broke forward in a mass of colour. Men shouted names and odds. Whips rose. Hooves struck turf. Malabar, a dark colt dismissed by many, held back, found an opening, and came through with a terrible late strength.

Malabar won.

Bassett received confirmation by telephone. For several minutes he sat without moving. The figures in his notebook seemed indecently calm.

When all obligations were settled, Paul’s fortune exceeded eighty thousand pounds.

Bassett insisted on seeing him.

The mother did not wish to permit it, but Oscar said the man had been faithful in his way. So Bassett entered the bedroom on softened feet.

Paul’s eyes were closed. The heat had left his face unnaturally pale.

Bassett stood beside the bed, twisting his cap in both hands.

“Master Paul,” he whispered. “Sir.”

The boy’s eyelids moved.

“Malabar came in, sir. He won. Just as you said.”

Paul opened his eyes.

For a moment they were clear.

“I knew,” he breathed.

“Yes, sir. You knew.”

“How much?”

“More than eighty thousand pounds, sir.”

A weak brightness crossed Paul’s face. He looked toward his mother, who sat on the other side of the bed.

“You see?” he said.

She leaned nearer.

“What, darling?”

“I’m lucky.”

“Yes,” she said, because she could say nothing else.

“I told you. I told you I was lucky.”

“Yes, Paul.”

“Now there’s enough.”

His mother’s lips parted, but no answer came.

The house was silent. Or perhaps it only gathered breath.

“Do you believe me?” Paul asked.

“I believe you.”

“Do you love me now?”

The question entered her like a blade.

“I have always loved you.”

But he was already slipping away from her. His eyes turned toward something beyond the bed, beyond the room.

“Malabar,” he murmured. “I’m sure.”

Before dawn, he died.

The rocking-horse was removed from the nursery and taken to a storage room beneath the roof. Two servants carried it between them. Its curved rails knocked against the stairs. Its glass eye continued to stare ahead, and its red mouth retained the expression of furious effort.

The house became very quiet after the funeral.

Black-edged letters stood upon the silver tray. Flowers filled the rooms, white and heavy-scented. Visitors came with lowered voices and left relieved that grief had not chosen them. Paul’s sisters moved cautiously, bewildered by the disappearance of their brother and by the solemn importance suddenly granted to everything he had touched.

The mother wore black. It suited her beauty.

She inherited her son’s money.

After fees, settlements and careful investment, it remained a fortune. The bank ceased writing restrained letters. The father’s debts were paid. The family could afford a new motor, better schools, longer journeys and a country place if they wished.

No one could say they were poor.

Yet sometimes, late at night, the mother woke and listened.

At first there was nothing but the ticking clock and the remote sound of wheels in the street. Then, from above, came a faint imagined rhythm.

Thud—creak.

Thud—creak.

She knew the rocking-horse had been taken apart and burned. She herself had ordered it. Nevertheless, she heard it.

Forward and back. Forward and back.

Going nowhere.

And through the gracious rooms, through the new silk curtains and over the deep carpets purchased with Paul’s winnings, another sound returned. It was softer than grief and more enduring.

More is wanted.

More.

More.

Uncle Oscar heard it too, in his fashion. One evening, after the accounts had been concluded, he stood with his sister beside the drawing-room fire. Neither had spoken of Paul for some time.

“At least,” Oscar said, and then stopped.

She looked at him.

“At least what?”

He studied the fire.

“The money is secure.”

Her face did not move.

“Yes.”

“It is a ghastly thing to say.”

“Then why say it?”

“Because it is what everyone has been thinking and refusing to say.”

She turned toward the window. Her reflection floated in the dark glass, pale above her black dress.

Oscar continued, more quietly.

“The boy won what he set out to win. He proved his luck.”

“No.”

“He did.”

“No,” she repeated. “He lost.”

Oscar had no reply.

For the first time, perhaps, the little hard chamber within her opened. But it opened upon emptiness. Love, arriving too late, found no child there to receive it.

Behind her, the fire settled with a small collapse. Somewhere in the walls, the house counted its gains.

Eighty thousand pounds acquired.

One son spent.

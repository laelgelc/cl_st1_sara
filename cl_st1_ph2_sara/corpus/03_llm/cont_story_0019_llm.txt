By the time Bibi Haldar was twenty-nine, her illness had acquired more names than she had saris. The doctors called it epilepsy, hysteria, nervous collapse, uterine agitation, hereditary weakness, and, on one memorable occasion, a vaporous disposition. The rest of us called it Bibi’s trouble.

It announced itself without courtesy. In the ration queue she would stiffen with a packet of lentils pressed to her chest. On the roof, among our bleaching sheets and petticoats, she would drop the bundle of Haldar’s invoices and begin clawing the air. Once she fell beside the fish seller and sent a basin of carp sliding into the gutter. Another time, during the evening prayer, she rolled beneath the hibiscus shrubs and remained there until the mosquitoes had gathered on her arms.

We learned the signs. Her left eyelid flickered. Her breath shortened. She complained of a penny taste in her mouth and a hammering behind her ears. Then she cried out.

“Hold me. It is coming. Do not let me die alone.”

We held her when we could. We loosened her blouse, removed the pins from her hair, wedged a folded handkerchief between her teeth. She shook. She spat. Her heels beat the floor. Afterward she slept as if punished, her face gray, her hair pasted to her cheeks.

Nobody knew what to do.

For years every known remedy had been tried. Her late father, a clerk in the Land Records Office, took her to charitable hospitals, private chambers, homeopathic dispensaries, saints’ tombs, riverside shrines, and a Swiss specialist staying three weeks at the Grand Hotel. She swallowed powders of asafoetida and pearl, tonics containing iron and arsenic, syrups advertised for female debility, sheep’s-milk curd, boiled neem, powdered coral, and the bitter scrapings of a tree struck by lightning. Leeches were applied to her ankles. A magnet was tied to her waist. An astrologer advised that she sleep facing northwest with seven grains of rice beneath her tongue.

Nothing helped for long.

One physician prescribed cold baths before dawn. Another forbade bathing altogether. One demanded a diet of mutton and eggs; another permitted only pumpkin, plantain, and rock salt. She was instructed to avoid red cloth, sour fruit, fried food, window seats, thunderstorms, funerals, weddings, and moonlight. For six months she wore an amulet containing the whisker of a black cat. When the attacks continued, the man who sold it explained that the cat must not have been entirely black.

Her father sold his watch, then her mother’s brass cooking pots, then a narrow strip of land outside Krishnanagar. He kept a notebook of her paroxysms—their hour, duration, severity, and possible cause—but died before discovering a pattern. Her mother had died years earlier, and so Bibi came to live with her cousin Haldar, who occupied two rooms and ran a cosmetics business on the ground floor of our building.

Haldar’s shop was called Fancy Palace, though there was nothing palatial about it. It was a dim cubicle between a bicycle repair stall and a man who sharpened knives. From iron shelves Haldar sold cold cream, hair oil, shaving soap, talcum powder, bindis, combs, nail enamel, cheap cologne, skin-lightening lotion, packets of henna, glass bangles, imitation sandalwood paste, and lipstick in colors called Imperial Rose, Bombay Flame, and Paris Evening. A naked bulb burned above the counter. In summer, the jars sweated.

Bibi kept the accounts.

From ten in the morning until seven at night she sat in the storeroom upstairs, entering stock into ledgers: six dozen eyebrow pencils, three dozen tins of jasmine powder, twenty-four bottles of coconut oil, less two broken in transit. She pasted labels, folded cartons, and separated cracked bangles from sound ones. She was not paid. Haldar said her food and lodging were payment enough.

“What use would money be to her?” he asked us. “She cannot cross a street alone.”

This was not entirely true. Bibi crossed streets when she wished, though she did so with her arms extended and blamed buses for targeting her personally. She could read, add columns without error, sew on a button, grind spices, rinse rice, and identify by smell which brand of hair oil had gone rancid. She could not cook a complete meal, because Haldar’s wife, whom we called simply the wife, did not allow her near the stove.

“If she collapses into the flame, who will answer?” the wife said.

Nor was Bibi permitted to carry the baby of the woman downstairs, fetch water from the pump, slice vegetables, light incense, or be left with a pressure cooker. She ate after Haldar and his wife, slept behind a curtain in their front room, and spent her evenings on the landing, listening to our families through the doors.

Her deepest complaint was not the seizures.

“No man will take me,” she told us. “I shall grow old smelling other people’s suppers.”

At first we laughed. Bibi was known for pronouncements. She said the grocer adulterated sugar with ground glass, that the moon caused her hair to fall, that the radio announcer had once proposed to her through the weather report. But the marriage question persisted.

“Look at me,” she said, tugging the loose skin beneath her eyes. “My beauty is being squandered.”

She was tall and narrow, with a long, unsettled face. Her hair, when oiled and combed, reached her waist. During attacks she bit her lips, leaving scars. She preferred blouses too tight across the chest and draped her saris so high above the ankle that older women objected.

We advised modest colors, a center parting, a trace of powder, fewer exclamations.

“A husband wants calm,” we said.

“How can I be calm before I have one?” she replied.

Then, in the month when the heat thickened the drains and crows stood with their beaks open, a new doctor came to the neighborhood. He had studied in Patna, displayed a framed certificate in English, and possessed an instrument for auscultation that he wore even while drinking tea. Haldar took Bibi only because she had suffered three attacks in one week and frightened customers by overturning a display of vermilion.

The doctor examined her pupils, struck her knees with a rubber hammer, listened to her chest, and asked questions Haldar considered indecent.

At last he said, “The patient requires a settled conjugal life.”

“What medicine is that?” Haldar asked.

“Marriage.”

The word traveled through our building before Bibi returned from the consultation. By evening it had reached the sweet shop, the tailor’s veranda, and the woman who sold flowers beside the tram stop. We heard that marriage would regulate her blood, quiet her nerves, correct certain deficiencies, and provide an outlet for energies improperly contained. Whether the doctor actually said all this we cannot be sure. Bibi said he had.

“A husband will cure me,” she announced from the landing. “A qualified man has confirmed it.”

For the first time, her longing carried scientific authority.

She began preparations immediately. She borrowed our bridal magazines and studied photographs of women beneath veils of gold thread. She practiced lowering her eyes. She asked how much turmeric was required for the bathing ceremony, whether the groom’s relatives should receive sweet yogurt or rose sherbet, and which fish looked most auspicious when decorated with betel leaves.

“I shall wear red,” she told us. “Not maroon, not rust. Red like the inside of a pomegranate. My palms will be painted. There will be sandalwood at my throat and milk boiling in silver. Invitations with scarlet borders. A procession. Music. Two kinds of meat.”

“You have not met the man,” we reminded her.

“He will want two kinds.”

We took an interest. It was difficult not to. In our building no confidence remained private past lunchtime, and Bibi discussed her future marriage from the roof. We taught her to fold a husband’s shirts, to judge hilsa by its gills, to season lentils without burning cumin, to keep household money in three separate envelopes. One of us showed her how to braid her hair more softly. Another lent her pearl earrings. We told her not to mention seizures before the second meeting.

“After the engagement,” someone suggested.

“After the wedding,” said someone else.

“A husband has a right to know,” a widow objected.

“A wife has a right to be married,” we answered.

This was how we loved Bibi: with combs, cautions, recipes, and dishonest advice.

Haldar refused to participate.

“Who will pay?” he demanded. “The doctor?”

He calculated the cost of a wedding aloud: priest, canopy, musicians, jewelry, transport, rice, fish, meat, sweets, gifts for the groom, gifts for the groom’s mother, tips for the groom’s cousins, tips for the men who carried chairs. Even a modest event, he declared, would ruin a prudent man.

“You are her nearest relative,” we said.

“I am her only relative. That is my misfortune.”

Bibi heard him. She was on the other side of the curtain.

The next morning she suffered an attack in the storeroom. Boxes of face powder burst beneath her elbows. For days afterward the ledgers smelled of violets.

Since Haldar would neither engage a matchmaker nor write to distant families, we composed an advertisement. It required five evenings and produced several quarrels. Bibi insisted on describing herself as a “cultivated beauty of distinguished temperament.” Haldar objected to the price per word. His wife suggested “orphan, no dowry,” on the grounds that honesty saved postage.

The final notice read:

BRIDE, 29, TALL, LITERATE, DOMESTICALLY TRAINABLE, SEEKS RESPONSIBLE HINDU GENTLEMAN, EMPLOYED, PATIENT NATURE. MINOR NERVOUS COMPLAINT, CURABLE BY MARRIAGE. NO EXCESSIVE DEMANDS.

It appeared in a Sunday newspaper between an advertisement for a widower with two sons and another for a wheat-complexioned stenographer.

For three weeks Bibi waited beside the letter box.

She changed her sari before the postman came. She rubbed almond cream into her elbows and asked us whether her profile looked educated. The postman, a thin man tormented by sinus trouble, began using the back stair.

No replies came.

A rumor spread that a railway guard from Durgapur had inquired at the tea stall. He had not. Someone proposed the lame brother of a pharmacist. The brother was already engaged. A widower nearly twice Bibi’s age expressed interest until Haldar mentioned the illness, at which point the man said he required a wife strong enough to massage his mother’s legs.

Bibi’s hope did not vanish all at once. It frayed.

She stopped practicing her lowered gaze. She returned the pearl earrings. She accused us of hiding eligible cousins. At night she stood on the landing and described the life being denied her.

“No one will call me from the market to ask whether we need coriander. No one will leave wet towels for me to scold about. I shall never pack food for a journey. I shall never quarrel over curtains. Never have keys tied in the end of my sari. Never hear a child cry from the next room and know it belongs to me.”

Her voice traveled down the stairwell.

Even Haldar’s customers heard.

Then his wife became pregnant.

She had been married to Haldar for eight years, and until then there had been no child. The news altered her at once. She refused papaya, lifted nothing heavier than a teacup, and consulted three almanacs to determine the most favorable hour for sleep. A mosquito net was purchased before her belly showed. Haldar brought home almonds, pomegranates, clarified butter, calcium tablets, and a book illustrating fetal development in alarming colors.

Bibi celebrated as if the pregnancy were her own.

“I shall be the aunt,” she cried. “I shall teach it songs. Let it sleep beside me.”

The wife stepped back.

“Do not touch.”

“What?”

“My food. My clothes. Anything.”

The wife had decided that Bibi’s condition might pass to the unborn child. No physician had suggested this, but a sister-in-law in Cuttack had heard of a woman whose baby developed a squint after watching a servant faint. From that day Bibi’s cup and plate were separated. She was forbidden to enter the bedroom. If she sneezed, windows were opened. If she descended the stairs while the wife was ascending, she had to flatten herself against the wall.

“Your shadow falls too heavily,” the wife said.

Bibi came to us outraged.

“Can a seizure climb into a womb?”

We said no.

“Can it travel on a spoon?”

No.

“Then tell her.”

We told her. She listened, thanked us, and afterward boiled the spoons.

As the pregnancy advanced, the wife’s fear grew inventive. Bibi’s hair was blamed for nausea, her footsteps for cramping, her muttering for dreams in which the infant was born with teeth. Haldar supported every prohibition. He said a first pregnancy was delicate, costly, not to be endangered by sentiment.

At the beginning of the rains, they moved Bibi to the roof.

The room there had once housed sacks of grain and was now used for surplus merchandise. It had a tin roof, one small window, and a door swollen by damp. Shelves leaned beneath cartons of soap, combs, tissue-wrapped perfume, and bottles whose labels had faded in the heat. Pigeons nested above the water tank. Rain entered beneath the sill and collected in a metal tray.

Haldar put a rope cot between two crates.

“It is temporary,” he said.

Bibi looked at the room.

“There is no fan.”

“You have air.”

“There is no bathroom.”

“Use the one downstairs.”

“At night?”

“Do not drink after nine.”

We objected. We had objections enough to fill the stairwell. We told Haldar the tin roof would roast her, the monsoon would flood her, an attack could throw her against the shelves. He said the wife needed quiet. He said the business was suffering. He said we should take Bibi into our own homes if we felt so strongly.

We did not.

Our rooms were small. Our children had exams. Our husbands disliked commotion. One mother-in-law said illness attracted illness. Another household had no extra bed. We brought Bibi a mosquito coil, an earthen water jug, two clean sheets, and a bell she could ring in an emergency.

From below, the bell could not be heard.

Each night she climbed to the room with her lamp. Each morning she descended before the wife woke. She continued keeping Haldar’s accounts, though now she worked in the same room where she slept. The shop’s stock surrounded her: towers of lotion, cartons of kajal, tins of shaving cream, strings of bangles clicking whenever the trains passed.

She suffered fewer public attacks because she appeared less often in public. We mistook this for improvement.

In late August the wife gave birth to a daughter at a private nursing home. Haldar distributed sweets and complained of the bill. The infant was brought home wrapped in flannel despite the heat. Bibi watched from the landing.

“Let me see her face,” she begged.

The wife covered the child.

For the first month Bibi was forbidden downstairs except to use the latrine. She ate meals sent up on an enamel plate. Sometimes the rice had hardened. Sometimes the vegetables were only skins and stems. Once, during a storm, no food was sent at all. We learned this the next morning and scolded Haldar.

“She could have reminded us,” he said.

“She rang the bell.”

“We heard nothing.”

The child remained healthy. Nevertheless the wife insisted that Bibi had caused a rash beneath its chin. Haldar began speaking of sending Bibi to an institution. He obtained the address of a charitable home two districts away, though he never paid for the necessary medical certificate.

By then Fancy Palace had begun to fail.

Some of us stopped buying there. The boycott was not declared at first. We simply crossed the lane for hairpins, purchased soap near the post office, asked visiting relatives to bring powder from other neighborhoods. Then we grew bolder. We told customers that Haldar diluted shampoo, sold expired cream, and charged extra for imported labels he pasted on himself. The last accusation was true only twice.

The knife sharpener joined us because Haldar owed him money. The fish seller joined because of Bibi’s overturned basin years before, which he had decided, retrospectively, was Haldar’s fault. Schoolgirls scratched the gold lettering from the sign.

Haldar stood at his doorway and watched us pass.

“Women,” he said, “are destroying commerce.”

His stock stagnated. Colognes turned sour. Cream separated in the jars. Monsoon damp curled the cartons and bloomed across the powder puffs. By autumn the shop opened at noon and closed before dusk. The naked bulb went out.

Then, without warning, Haldar announced he was leaving town.

He said his wife’s family had found him an opportunity in Ranchi. We suspected debts. A truck came one morning and carried away the bed, the child’s mosquito net, the cash box, the brass utensils, two trunks, and the best merchandise. Haldar left behind damaged cartons, unpaid bills, and most of the old inventory upstairs.

He also left Bibi.

“You cannot abandon her,” we said.

“She refuses to come.”

This was false. Bibi had not been asked.

Haldar placed three hundred rupees in a tin under her cot and claimed it was sufficient for six months if used sensibly. He surrendered the building key to the landlord, who had no interest in the roof room and agreed, after our insistence, not to evict her immediately.

The wife carried the baby to the taxi. Bibi stood behind the rooftop parapet, visible only from the shoulders upward.

“Say goodbye,” one of us called.

No one did.

For several days Bibi spoke of taking legal action. She had no idea where to begin. She threatened to write the prime minister, the chief minister, the police commissioner, the physician who had prescribed marriage. We brought her paper. She wrote nothing.

Then she withdrew.

She stopped coming to our kitchens. She no longer waited at the pump or complained on the stairs. If we knocked, she said she was sleeping. The storeroom remained bolted. At times we heard her moving cartons. At times we heard nothing.

Winter entered the city through cracked windows. Wool blankets came down from trunks smelling of camphor. Vendors sold oranges and peanuts at crossings. Wedding bands played in the lanes, their trumpets flattening every melody into the same triumphant noise. From the roof Bibi could see processions—grooms lifted on white horses, electric garlands powered by coughing generators, boys dancing beneath strings of lights.

We expected these sights to provoke her.

She did not comment.

We carried up food: spinach, rice, lentils, fried eggplant, fish in mustard gravy. Some days she opened the door only wide enough to accept the bowl. Her hair was uncombed. Her nails were dark. Once we saw bruising near her wrist and asked what had happened.

“I struck a shelf.”

“During an attack?”

“I don’t remember.”

This answer did not trouble us as it should have. Forgetfulness belonged to her illness. Bruises belonged to the storeroom. We had children to bathe, sweaters to mend, guests to feed, accounts of our own to settle before the year ended.

It is impossible now to say when the man came.

We argued about it for years afterward. One of us recalled footsteps on the roof during a December power cut. Another remembered finding the stairwell door unlatched after midnight. The widow across the courtyard claimed she saw a figure in a brown shawl, but she also claimed to recognize, from three floors away, the brand of cigarettes he smoked. There were deliverymen, repairmen, meter readers, boys searching for lost kites, laborers sent by the landlord to inspect the water tank. Any one of them might have known that Bibi slept alone.

Or perhaps she knew him.

Perhaps she let him in.

Perhaps he promised marriage. Perhaps he gave a false name. Perhaps she fought. Perhaps an attack came first, or afterward, or not at all.

We asked every question later. None produced an answer.

By February, Bibi began appearing outside again. Her face had changed. It seemed both fuller and more tired. She washed at the pump before sunrise. She purchased tamarind, roasted chickpeas, and green mangoes out of season. She complained that the smell of hair oil sickened her.

“You are pale,” we said.

“I am always pale.”

“You have gained weight.”

“It is the cold.”

In March the heat returned abruptly. The tin roof clicked all afternoon. Bibi stopped wearing her tight blouses and wrapped herself in loose cotton. She suffered no seizures that we witnessed, but we had become accustomed to not witnessing them.

One morning in April she fainted beside the water pump.

This was not her usual collapse. There was no cry, no thrashing, no bitten lip. She folded quietly, one hand beneath her belly.

We carried her to the room of a retired midwife named Mrs. Dutta, who pressed Bibi’s abdomen, counted dates, and looked at us without surprise.

“Four months,” she said.

We thought she meant four months of treatment.

“Four months what?”

“Pregnant.”

The word emptied the room.

Bibi opened her eyes.

Mrs. Dutta asked when her monthly bleeding had stopped. Bibi turned her face to the wall. We asked who had visited the roof, who had touched her, who had promised what, whether she remembered a night, a voice, a smell, a name.

She said nothing.

“Was it someone from the building?”

Nothing.

“Did he hurt you?”

Her shoulders tightened.

“Tell us.”

“I cannot.”

“Cannot remember?”

“I cannot tell.”

We sent for the police, then decided not to. We sent for the landlord, then decided he would evict her. We considered the charitable hospital, an orphanage, a lawyer, a priest. Advice arrived from every direction. One woman said the pregnancy should be ended at once. Another said four months was too late. A third said no child was guilty of its father. A fourth asked how a woman with seizures could raise an infant in a storeroom.

We also asked the ugliest question.

“Are you sure no one came willingly?”

Bibi looked at us then.

“You wanted me married,” she said.

No one answered.

The room itself disclosed little. The cot was neatly made. The cartons had been stacked against the wall. A broken bangle lay beneath the window, but it might have been there for years. There were no letters, no man’s clothing, no gifts except a red plastic comb Bibi claimed came from Haldar’s stock. The bolt could be secured from inside, though the window latch was loose and the roof accessible from the adjoining building.

We searched as if an orderly room were evidence of innocence or guilt.

For two weeks Bibi would not leave her cot. Then the child moved.

She summoned us one evening after the call to prayer, when incense from the downstairs shrine had thickened the air and film songs blared from a loudspeaker tied to a neem tree.

“Here,” she said, taking one of our hands.

Beneath her sari, something tapped.

Again.

Bibi stared at her belly. Her expression was not joyful. It was astonished and severe.

“He is alive.”

“It may be a girl.”

“He is alive,” she repeated.

After that, she ate.

She accepted milk, eggs, lentils, guava, and the iron tablets Mrs. Dutta brought. She swept the storeroom. We helped her move the heavier cartons, scrub pigeon droppings from the parapet, patch the window with tin, and hang a curtain around the cot. Someone donated a fan. Someone else found a secondhand cradle. The landlord, discovering that an infant was expected, demanded extra rent. We reminded him that his illegal extension blocked the fire stairs. The rent remained unchanged.

Bibi prepared without ceremony. No turmeric was rubbed into her skin. No scarlet invitations were printed. There was no silver pot of milk, no groom, no mother-in-law to receive sweets. She cut old saris into squares, boiled them, dried them in the sun, and folded them in stacks. She learned to sterilize a bottle, though she hoped not to need one. She listened when we explained fever, colic, rashes, bathing, burping, and the dangerous softness on a newborn’s head.

“You must sleep when the baby sleeps,” we told her.

“Who will mind the shop?”

“What shop?”

She pointed to the cartons.

During her isolation she had counted everything Haldar left: seventy-two cakes of sandal soap, forty-one bottles of hair oil, nineteen jars of cold cream, twelve packets of vermilion, nine boxes of bindis, six intact mirrors, five dozen combs, three dozen bangles, four bottles of cologne still fit for use, and one hundred and eight eyebrow pencils nobody wanted.

“It belongs to me,” she said.

We did not disagree.

With part of the money under the cot, she bought a wooden board and paid a sign painter to write BIBI’S GOODS in blue letters. The name disappointed us.

“Beauty Corner,” we proposed.

“Ladies’ Choice.”

“New Fancy Palace.”

“No,” Bibi said. “Bibi’s Goods.”

The sign was hung beside the rooftop door.

At first her customers were only us. We purchased soap we did not need, combs of the wrong size, bangles that pinched. Bibi refused charity disguised as commerce. She knew the old prices and charged them. She marked each sale in a ledger, subtracted the item, counted the cash twice, and stored it in three envelopes: stock, rent, child.

By August her belly prevented her from sitting comfortably at the desk. The rains returned. Water drummed on the repaired roof and washed soot from the parapets. Bibi continued working. She had no attacks.

“Pregnancy has cured you,” we said.

She frowned.

“The doctor said marriage.”

“Doctors are often approximate.”

In September, during a night of relentless rain, labor began. Bibi knocked on the floor with a length of wood until the family below woke. Mrs. Dutta was fetched. We boiled water, tore cloth, lit lamps when the electricity failed, and crowded the storeroom until the midwife expelled half of us.

Bibi did not perform her usual theatrics. She did not call upon heaven, curse the absent father, or demand that Haldar be dragged back from Ranchi. She gripped the cot. She sweated. She obeyed.

Near dawn she gave birth to a son.

He was small, furious, and healthy. His fists opened and shut beneath the lamp. Bibi held him with both arms and studied his face as though checking an invoice for errors.

“Whose nose?” someone whispered.

Mrs. Dutta ordered silence.

For the first weeks we took turns. We cooked, washed cloths, swept the shop, watched the child while Bibi bathed. Some of us were more generous than others. Some came only to inspect the baby’s features. Theories multiplied. His ears resembled the landlord’s nephew’s. His chin resembled the knife sharpener’s. His complexion proved nothing in infancy. A birthmark behind his knee was said to match that of a former delivery boy until the delivery boy’s mother confirmed that his mark was on the opposite leg.

Bibi named the child Amal.

Motherhood did not transform her into a saint. She snapped when advice contradicted itself. She accused us of waking the baby, then complained when no one visited. She refused to discard spoiled milk until we poured it away. But she learned quickly.

She recognized hunger before crying began. She could change a cloth in darkness, balance Amal on one hip while totaling a column, and distinguish the cough of dust from the cough of fever. She sang badly and without embarrassment. When he slept, she watched his chest rise.

Business improved.

The sign was visible from neighboring roofs. Women came for bindis, soap, combs, hairpins, henna, powder, and gossip. Bibi ordered new stock from a wholesaler who had once supplied Haldar. She negotiated credit. She discarded the rank colognes and repackaged loose bangles in newspaper. When Imperial Rose lipstick returned to fashion, she sold twelve tubes in a week.

She installed shelves. She bought a lock. She paid rent on time.

The storeroom ceased to look like a place where a dependent relative had been hidden. It became a shop with a home fitted into it: cradle beside ledger, kettle beneath counter, diapers drying above mirrors, jars aligned in rows. Amal learned to crawl between cartons and later to stand by gripping the shelf of talcum powder. Customers carried him while Bibi wrapped purchases.

Once Haldar wrote from Ranchi. He claimed the remaining merchandise legally belonged to him and requested an accounting. Bibi read the letter, folded it into a square, and placed it beneath the leg of a wobbling table.

No reply was sent.

As for the seizures, they did not return.

At first we expected one whenever Amal developed a fever, whenever business was slow, whenever thunder shook the tin roof. We kept the folded handkerchief ready. We warned her not to lock the door at night. Months passed. Then a year.

Bibi crossed streets alone. She carried cash to the bank. She traveled by bus to the wholesale market with Amal asleep against her shoulder. She no longer extended her arms before traffic or accused buses of conspiracy. Her hair, once the subject of elaborate care, was twisted into a practical knot.

We asked whether she wished to marry now.

“For what treatment?” she said.

We stopped asking.

The father of her son was never discovered. Perhaps Bibi knew and protected him. Perhaps she remembered nothing. Perhaps the knowledge was a room within her more securely bolted than the one on the roof. Our questions, vigorous in the beginning, became quieter, then shameful, then rare.

What remained visible was easier to describe. Amal grew. The shop prospered. Bibi’s ledgers were exact. Each autumn she stocked vermilion and sandal paste; each winter, cold cream; each summer, prickly-heat powder and cheap rose water. Brides came to buy red bangles, and Bibi measured their wrists without speaking of the wedding once prescribed for her health.

We had wanted a ceremony to save her. What she received instead had no music, no witness, no agreed name. From it came a child, a livelihood, and a silence we could neither diagnose nor forgive.

Still, in the years that followed, no one saw Bibi Haldar fall again.

A man alone is one sort of creature; a man joined to his work may be quite another. He may be timid for himself and bold for his duty, careless of his own honour and jealous of his craft, a weakling under insult and a giant under responsibility. Labour sometimes merely fills a belly. Sometimes it supplies the missing pieces of a soul.

Take the case of Richard Darrell.

Before you can understand Richard Darrell, called Roaring Dick throughout three counties of pine, you must understand something of a winter lumber-camp. It is not enough to picture a collection of low roofs smoking among snow-buried firs, nor a company of red-shirted fellows cutting trees to the rhythm of axes. That is the appearance of the thing. The life of it is organization.

A camp may contain sixty men, twelve teams, a blacksmith, a cook, a clerk, and a foreman; but it possesses only one purpose. The sawyers must keep ahead of the swampers, who clear the roads; the swampers must keep ahead of the teamsters, who haul the logs; the teamsters must feed the skidways and rollways; and all must labour before thaw turns the roads to porridge and releases the streams. A delay of three days in January may become a jam in April, and a jam in April may swallow the profit of a year.

The foreman carries this delicate machine in his head. He knows which team will stand a hard pull, which road will drift overnight, which sawyer has a grievance, which cook is losing his temper, and which man has begun to talk too lovingly of town. He must calculate frost, snow, horseflesh, human vanity, and the temper of rivers not yet awake. If one part fails, all fail. For this he receives somewhat more money, very much less sleep, and the privilege of being hated whenever things go wrong.

Richard Darrell was an excellent foreman.

He was not large. His narrow, triangular face came to a short brown mustache, and his little brown eyes had the quick brightness of a chipmunk’s. His shoulders were heavy and hung forward, as though they had been attached too far in front; his arms dangled loose; his hands remained half open. In repose he seemed awkward, almost unfinished. In motion he was astonishingly exact. He wasted neither step nor glance.

His voice had earned him his name. Across a rollway in a storm it could crack like a splitting pine.

“Ease th’ nigh horse! Hold her! Now—together!”

Men heard that voice through wind, falling timber, and the shriek of runners on frozen roads. They obeyed it because it was generally right.

In town, however, Richard Darrell was not right at all.

Town loosened the arrangement of him. Without a road to mend, a team to save, or a dangerous tree to watch, his nervous force wandered like a dog without a master. Whiskey made it worse. Two glasses rendered him boastful; four made him suspicious; six took the courage from his knees while persuading his tongue that he was a conqueror.

It was after six glasses that he encountered Silver Jack.

Silver Jack kept the Imperial Saloon, though keeping a saloon was merely the legal centre of his activities. He gambled, traded horses, arranged prize-fights, advanced money to improvident men, and recovered it by methods not always described in the note. He was tall, deep-chested, bronze-red of face, with pale yellow hair and a long mustache which he trained outward between finger and thumb. He feared fists, knives, cold, and constables less than most men. This was material courage, and he possessed it abundantly. Of imagination, which tells a man what another man may become, he had very little.

Silver Jack found Roaring Dick in a corner of the Imperial, explaining to three uninterested teamsters how he had once broken a rollway with a peavey after everybody else had given up.

“You?” said Jack.

Darrell turned his bright eyes upon him. “Me.”

Jack looked him over deliberately.

“You couldn’t break a biscuit ’thout somebody soaked it fer you.”

The loungers laughed. Darrell pushed back his chair. He wished to rise with majesty, but his boot caught the rung, and he sat again.

“I’m th’ best foreman north of Saginaw,” he announced.

“Mebbe north of th’ stove,” said Jack. “Not much farther.”

Darrell’s face twitched. In the woods that twitch warned men to attend closely. Here it merely amused them.

“You shut your mouth,” he said.

Silver Jack leaned across the table and slapped him.

It was not a heavy blow. Its lightness made it contemptuous. Darrell got to his feet at last, his hands hanging open. For an instant his chipmunk eyes struck Jack’s face with the physical shock of an impact. Then uncertainty entered them. There was no cant-hook to seize, no team in danger, no order of labour to defend. There was only Richard Darrell, drunk, insulted, and afraid.

Jack slapped him again.

“Hit me,” he invited.

Darrell’s shoulders drew farther forward.

“I don’t want no trouble.”

This pleased Silver Jack. He took Darrell by the collar and belt, walked him to the door, and threw him into a bank of street snow. Darrell arose slowly. Muddy flakes clung to his mustache. The Imperial roared behind him.

He made no attempt to return.

Silver Jack had learned a fact, but not its boundaries. That is among the most expensive forms of ignorance.

By the middle of the following February Darrell’s camp on the Upper Cass was eleven days behind. Snow had come early and deep. A thaw had softened the tote-road; then a sudden freeze had covered it with ridges hard as iron. One team had been lamed. Two teamsters had quit. A falling limb broke a sawyer’s collarbone, and the cook, who considered this an insult to his bill of fare, chose the same evening to leave.

Darrell slept four hours a night and ate wherever he happened to remember food. His cheekbones sharpened. His eyes grew brighter. He moved from road to skidway, from skidway to rollway, carrying in his mind the season’s shrinking margin.

The men were tired, and tired lumbermen think of town.

They thought of hot baths they would not take, feather beds they could not afford, women who would spend their money, and saloons where a winter’s pay might be converted into noise in two evenings. At night they lay in the bunkhouse while socks steamed over the stove and spoke of these delights with the solemnity of pilgrims discussing heaven.

The camp clerk reported it.

“They’re talkin’ jump,” he said.

Darrell glanced up from the scale book.

“Who?”

“Baptiste. Nolan. Them two new swampers. More’ll go if they start.”

“When?”

“First whiskey that comes along.”

Darrell closed the book.

Whiskey in camp is not merely drink. It is mutiny bottled by the gallon. A man who has been swinging an axe ten hours daily for three months does not sip judiciously. He drinks because he is weary, because another man drinks, because the cook burned the beans, because the snow is deep, because spring is far away. In an hour the bunkhouse becomes a court in which every grievance is proved. By morning half the crew may be walking toward town.

Silver Jack knew this much. He did not know the rest.

Business at the Imperial had been dull, and somebody told him Darrell’s men had three months’ wages coming. Jack therefore loaded six stone jugs and two cases of bottled whiskey into a low freight sleigh. With him went a wide-jawed individual called Scotty Burns, who acted as Jack’s assistant when argument required more than one pair of hands.

“You’ll never git it past Dick,” warned the stableman.

Jack spread his pale mustache.

“That little rabbit? I chucked him through my door.”

“This ain’t town.”

“A rabbit’s a rabbit anywheres.”

The stableman had worked in the woods. He said nothing further. A man should be permitted to purchase his own enlightenment, especially when he insists on paying cash.

Jack and Scotty travelled before dawn, hoping to reach the camp at supper. The cold was intense. Harness buckles burned the fingers; bacon in the lunch-box could have been used for shingles; the runners screamed upon the packed road. Spruce and balsam stood black above the snow, and the low sun touched their tops without warming them.

At noon they passed the abandoned No. 2 skidway, five miles from headquarters. A mile beyond, the tote-road bent around a cedar swamp.

Richard Darrell stood in the bend.

He had been examining a break in the road. Snowshoes were strapped to his back, and an axe rested on his shoulder. His cap was pulled low. In that immensity of white he appeared a small, dark, forward-hanging figure.

Jack reined in.

“Well!” he cried. “If it ain’t Roarin’ Dick!”

Darrell approached the sleigh. His gaze went first to the team, then to the load, then to Scotty, and finally to Jack. It arrived at each like a thrown object.

“What you carryin’?”

“Supplies.”

Darrell lifted the canvas. He saw the straw-packed bottles and stone jugs. He replaced the cover carefully.

“Turn round.”

Jack laughed.

“We’re goin’ to your camp.”

“No.”

“We got business with the boys.”

“You got no business there.”

“Who says?”

“I do.”

Scotty grinned and thrust his mittened hands under his armpits. Jack climbed out of the sleigh. He seemed enormous beside Darrell.

“Look here, Dick. I ain’t askin’ leave. The boys can buy what they want.”

“They can buy it in town after the drive. You can’t take that whiskey into camp.”

The words contained no anger. Darrell stated them as he might have stated that a rotten bridge would not carry a load. Behind him, five miles away, stood a camp already strained to the breaking point. In front of him stood the thing that could break it. The geography was simple.

Silver Jack remembered the snowbank outside the Imperial.

“Git out of my road.”

“No.”

Jack struck him open-handed across the mouth.

Darrell’s head moved with the blow and came back. Blood appeared on his lower lip. His little eyes did not become uncertain. This affair did not concern Richard Darrell’s pride. It concerned the camp.

“You can’t take that whiskey in,” he repeated.

Jack struck with his fist.

Darrell went backward into the snow. Before he could rise, Jack kicked him in the ribs. Scotty jumped down, laughing. Together they dragged him to the roadside and flung him beyond the packed track.

Jack returned to the sleigh.

Darrell came out of the snow.

He walked bent over, one arm held against his side. His lips were red, and powdery snow whitened his eyebrows. He placed himself before the horses.

Jack stared.

“Want some more?”

“Turn round.”

Jack hit him again. This time Darrell caught the blow on his forehead, stepped inside it, and fastened both half-open hands in the front of Jack’s coat. The movement was extraordinarily quick. He twisted, thrust with his hip, and sent the big man staggering against the sleigh.

Scotty sprang upon Darrell from behind.

All three went down between the runners.

There is little art in a winter fight among men wearing heavy clothing. Coats prevent clean blows; snow destroys footing; mittens turn fists into clubs. For half a minute there was only a confusion of boots, curses, steaming breath, and flying crystals.

Then Scotty rolled clear, clutching his throat.

Darrell was on top of Jack. He struck twice—short, chopping blows. Jack threw him off by main strength and arose with blood running from his nose.

“You little devil!” he gasped.

Darrell crouched before him. The familiar posture had become terrible: shoulders forward, arms loose, hands half open, brown eyes blazing from the triangular face.

“Turn round,” he said.

Jack rushed.

Darrell did not oppose force to force. He slipped aside, caught Jack’s sleeve and collar, and drove him face first against the rear standard of the sleigh. The oak cracked. Jack sagged, recovered, wheeled, and seized a neck-yoke from the load.

The change from fists to weapons is quicker than civilization likes to admit. Anger first desires victory, then pain, then extinction. The forest receives these stages without comment.

Jack swung the yoke.

Darrell ducked. The wood struck his shoulder and knocked him to one knee. Jack raised it again.

Darrell sprang under the stroke. His head struck Jack’s chest; his arms locked about Jack’s waist. They crashed against the load. Bottles clinked musically, preserving for one absurd instant the manners of a dining-room. Then the sleigh overturned.

Cases, jugs, robes, men, and straw poured into the road.

Scotty had recovered. He snatched the axe from where Darrell had dropped it.

“Hold him!” he shouted.

Jack held Darrell by both arms.

Scotty came on with the axe raised—not as a woodsman carries an axe, but as a murderer carries one.

Darrell saw him. Fear entered his face then, unmistakably. His pupils widened; his mouth opened; his body strained away. Courage does not consist in failing to perceive an axe. Often it consists in perceiving the axe exactly and finding that retreat is nevertheless impossible.

If Darrell escaped into the timber, Silver Jack would gather his whiskey, right the sleigh, and continue to camp. The men would jump. The teams would stand idle. The rollways would remain short. When thaw came, the company’s logs would be scattered above the landing, and the winter’s labour would be lost.

He could not retreat.

He stamped down upon Jack’s instep. Jack’s hold loosened. Darrell tore one arm free, swung by the other, and kicked Scotty squarely on the knee.

The axe descended sideways. Its blade split the overturned sleigh-box.

Scotty screamed and fell.

Darrell wrenched loose. He seized the axe handle, but Scotty clung to it. They struggled in silence, face to face. Then Darrell bent Scotty’s wrist backward until the fingers opened one by one.

Jack had found a broken sleigh stake. He struck Darrell behind the ear.

Darrell dropped.

For a time nobody moved except the horses, which plunged against their traces, filling the cold air with bells and vapour. The sun had descended behind the spruces. Long blue shadows crossed the road. From somewhere in the timber came the dry tapping of a woodpecker.

Two ravens appeared above the swamp. They circled once, interested.

Scotty sat in the snow nursing his knee.

“Kill him,” he said hoarsely. “He near broke my arm.”

Jack stood over Darrell with the stake.

Perhaps he intended it. Murder had entered the road and breathed there like a pestilential air. Jack lifted the club.

Darrell moved.

His hand closed around Jack’s ankle. He pulled and twisted. Jack fell heavily. Before the big man could turn, Darrell was upon him.

No science remained. No economy of force remained. Darrell struck until Jack ceased trying to rise. He struck as the foreman who had watched snow bury his roads, frost lame his horses, and discontent creep through his bunkhouse. He struck for every delayed load, every weakened team, every whispered plan to jump. Silver Jack had brought not six jugs of liquor but the ruin of a season, and the whole concentrated resistance of the season descended upon him.

Scotty crawled toward the axe.

Darrell saw him.

He left Jack, snatched the weapon first, and stood astride it.

Scotty stopped.

“Git up,” said Darrell.

Scotty attempted to obey and fell.

“Git up!”

The roar travelled through the forest. Snow loosened from a cedar bough and fell with a soft rush. Scotty struggled upright on one leg.

Jack also stirred. Darrell kicked the sleigh stake away.

“On your feet.”

Jack looked at him without comprehension. His face was swollen and dark. One pale strand of mustache hung wet against his chin.

Darrell waited, axe in hand.

At last Jack rose.

For several seconds the three men stood among the wreckage. Blood spotted the snow. The horses trembled, their flanks smoking. The ravens settled in the top of a dead pine and expressed disappointment.

Darrell turned to the whiskey.

He knocked the neck from the first jug. The sharp odour rose richly. Amber liquid gurgled into the snow, staining it the colour of old varnish.

Jack made a sound.

“That’s my property.”

Darrell smashed the second jug.

“Stop!”

The third broke under the axe.

Jack took one step forward. Darrell looked at him. He stopped.

Bottle after bottle shattered. The reports were small and crisp. Whiskey flowed beneath the overturned sleigh, soaked the straw, and ran in golden threads along the runner grooves. The cold forest smelled suddenly of saloons, varnished bars, wet sawdust, and wasted wages.

When the last bottle was broken, Darrell attacked the sleigh. He chopped through the cracked box, split the runners at the bends, and hewed the tongue in two. Each stroke was placed with a woodsman’s accuracy. Five minutes reduced a useful vehicle to kindling.

Then he cut the harness.

Jack found speech.

“You’ll pay fer that.”

“Mebbe.”

“How we goin’ to git back?”

“Walk.”

“It’s twelve mile!”

Darrell severed the last tug. “Road’s plain.”

“The horses—”

“Lead ’em.”

Scotty swore.

Darrell drove the axe into the chopping block of a stump and leaned upon the handle. Weariness showed all at once in his body. One eye had closed. Blood ran behind his ear and froze along his collar. His left arm hung useless. Yet his posture remained forward, expectant, immovable.

“Git started,” he said.

Jack looked down the road toward town. Twilight had thickened among the trees. Twelve miles on foot in bitter cold, leading frightened horses with ruined harness, was no comic journey. He looked the other way, toward Darrell’s camp. Five miles only.

Darrell read the glance.

“You can’t take that whiskey into camp.”

There was no whiskey left. The words had passed beyond literal meaning. They were the boundary itself.

Jack understood at last—not everything, for understanding is seldom given in such generous quantities, but enough. The man he had thrown out of the Imperial and the man before him were housed in the same skin, yet they were not identical. In town Darrell had defended only himself, a duty for which he possessed no especial talent. Here he defended an organization of men, horses, roads, tools, timber, wages, and time. Behind him stood the season’s work. Responsibility braced his narrow back and armed his dangling hands.

Silver Jack was fearless regarding pain. He had not supposed a man might be fearless by proxy.

He bent, gathered a length of cut rein, and looped it through a horse’s bridle.

“Come on, Scotty.”

Scotty limped after him. Neither spoke as they led the team westward. The bells, hanging from severed straps, made irregular sounds. Soon the two men and the horses disappeared around the cedar bend.

Darrell remained beside the wreck until darkness almost covered it. Then he pulled on his snowshoes and started toward camp.

He travelled slowly. Twice he rested against a tree. Once he sank to his knees and remained there so long that frost began to silver his coat. But presently he rose. Headquarters lay ahead, and there were matters to settle before morning: a drift on the north road, a lame mare in Team Seven, and four men whose thoughts had wandered toward town.

At supper he entered the cookhouse without explanation.

Conversation stopped.

He stood in the doorway battered, frozen, and blood-marked, his shoulders hanging forward and his little brown eyes travelling from face to face.

“Baptiste,” he said, “you take Nolan an’ clear that north turn before daylight. Pete, hitch th’ blacks at four. We got to make up six loads tomorrow.”

Nobody mentioned jumping.

Darrell ate standing beside the stove. Then he went out to inspect the horses.

So it is not always profitable to judge a man apart from the thing to which he has given his allegiance. Alone, Richard Darrell might be drunk, uncertain, even cowardly. Add the winter road beneath him, the camp behind him, the threatened work before him, and he became something larger than his private virtues.

He became the Man defending his Work.

He became the Foreman.

## Mrs. Gratz’s Christmas Stocking

Christmas morning came to Mrs. Gratz’s little yellow house in a drizzle.

It was not a proper Christmas drizzle, if there is such a thing. It had none of the fine, silver quality that poets give to winter rain. It was a common gray drizzle that softened the snow left beside the fences, loosened the mud in the lanes, and made the bare trees look as if they regretted getting up.

Mrs. Gratz did not regret getting up, because she did not get up until half past eight.

She was a placid widow, and she was fat. These two facts had been friends for many years. A thin person may hurry out of bed because the thin have sharp elbows, sharp appetites, and sharp suspicions that life is getting ahead of them. Mrs. Gratz had no such fears. Life had never got far ahead of her. If it did, she sat down and waited for it to come back.

She opened one eye at eight, the other at a quarter past, and at half past she put both feet upon the rag rug. Then she washed, dressed, twisted her gray hair into its comfortable knot, and went downstairs to make breakfast.

There were Christmas cards on the mantel, a sprig of holly over the kitchen clock, and a red paper bell hanging from the lamp. Her daughter in Milwaukee had sent the bell, folded flat in an envelope, with instructions for pulling it open. Mrs. Gratz had pulled too hard, so one side of the bell was longer than the other; but a bell that does not ring may as well hang crooked.

She fried three strips of bacon, two eggs, and four slices of bread. She made coffee strong enough to carry a spoon upright, and while she ate she looked through the wet window toward the back yard.

The chicken coop stood there, a low, whitewashed building beyond the pump. Ordinarily ten red and white heads would have appeared behind the wire at the first kitchen sound. Chickens know bacon when they smell it, and they do not allow the fact that it is another animal’s bacon to lessen their interest.

This morning no heads appeared.

Mrs. Gratz drank her coffee.

“Dey sleeps late for Christmas,” she said.

She ate one egg.

Still no heads appeared.

“Maybe dey celebrates.”

She ate the second egg and wiped the plate with bread. Only then did she put on her heavy coat, tie a scarf over her head, and step into the drizzle.

There was no reason to hurry. If the chickens were asleep, haste would only wake them. If they were awake, they would remain so. And if they were dead, they were beyond complaining about delay.

The mud received Mrs. Gratz’s shoes with soft, affectionate sounds. She crossed the yard slowly, holding her skirt clear with one hand. When she reached the coop, she stopped.

The padlock was broken.

One half hung from the staple; the other lay in the mud. The door stood open by three inches. Inside there were feathers, an overturned feed pan, and silence.

Mrs. Gratz pushed the door wider.

“Here, chick-chick-chick.”

Nothing came.

She counted the nests, because they were all that remained to count. There had been nine hens and one rooster the night before: red hens, speckled hens, one white hen with a bad disposition, and Herman, the rooster, who believed that dawn was his personal invention.

Now there were none.

Mrs. Gratz looked at the broken lock. She looked at the feathers. She looked behind the coop, under the coop, and into a wooden box where no sensible chicken could have hidden. Then she looked at the sky.

“So,” she said. “Santy Claus comes, after all.”

It was not the conclusion another woman might have reached, but Mrs. Gratz was not another woman.

She began to walk back toward the house, considering whether Santa Claus had taken a fancy to poultry. Perhaps reindeer liked fresh eggs. Perhaps the old gentleman had grown tired of cookies. Perhaps, after delivering toys all night, he had decided upon chicken soup.

Halfway across the yard she saw something dark in the mud near the gate.

She bent down, slowly, because bending was a journey with Mrs. Gratz and required proper preparations. The object was a black leather purse, spattered but not soaked. Its clasp was brass. Its handle was worn. One side was pressed into the print of a long, narrow shoe.

Mrs. Gratz picked it up.

“Ach,” she said. “He drops his pocketbook.”

Inside were no calling cards, no letters, and no photographs. There was a comb with three teeth missing, a railway timetable, two cigar coupons, a small key, and a packet of money.

Mrs. Gratz carried the purse into the kitchen. She removed her coat, wiped the leather with a towel, and sat in her rocking chair. Then she counted the bills.

She counted once.

She counted twice.

She counted a third time, not because she was uncertain, but because certain pleasures improve upon repetition.

There were nine hundred dollars.

Mrs. Gratz laid the money across her lap in nine even piles.

Nine hundred dollars was a generous price for nine hens and a rooster, especially when Herman had begun to limp and the white hen had lately eaten more than she laid. Even allowing for the broken lock, the transaction showed a profit that no honest poultry dealer would have proposed.

Her round face broadened into a smile.

“Dot Santy Claus,” she said. “He is maybe careless, but he pays good.”

At ten o’clock Mrs. Flannery came through the front door without knocking. Mrs. Flannery always entered without knocking because knocking used up the time in which she might have announced why she had come.

“A merry Christmas to ye, Mrs. Gratz, though what’s merry in rain I don’t know, and me shoes that wet I could raise goldfish in ’em! I brought ye a bit of cake, but don’t be eatin’ too much, for there’s brandy in it, and Mrs. Corcoran took two pieces last year and sang ‘The Wearing of the Green’ in the coal cellar.”

“Merry Christmas,” said Mrs. Gratz. “Sit down. I got a present.”

“A present, is it? From your Emma?”

“From Santy Claus.”

Mrs. Flannery stopped removing her hat.

“Santa Claus?”

“He comes in der night. He breaks open my chicken house, takes nine hens and Herman, und leaves me nine hundred dollars.”

Mrs. Flannery sat down suddenly.

Mrs. Gratz showed her the money.

For half a minute Mrs. Flannery was speechless. It was the longest rest her tongue had enjoyed since childhood.

Then she cried, “The police!”

“No.”

“The constable!”

“No.”

“The newspapers!”

“No.”

“But woman alive, you’ve been robbed!”

Mrs. Gratz touched the bills affectionately. “If dis is robbery, Mrs. Flannery, I hope I get robbed every Thursday.”

“It’s stolen money, that’s what it is. There’s blood on it, likely.”

“Only mud on der pocketbook.”

“There’ll be a desperate criminal prowling the neighborhood. A murderer, maybe. A strangler. A man with one eye and a hook where his hand should be.”

“The shoe mark got two feet,” said Mrs. Gratz.

“Then there’s two of them!”

Mrs. Gratz smiled. “Santy Claus got two feet, too.”

Mrs. Flannery leaned close. “You must report it. The law says lost property belongs to whoever lost it.”

“Den maybe der law says my chickens belongs to whoever finds dem?”

“That’s different.”

“Why?”

“Because it is.”

Mrs. Gratz rocked gently. “If I tells der police, maybe dey takes der nine hundred. If I tells nobody, maybe I puts it in der bank.”

“Your conscience, woman!”

“My conscience does not pay interest.”

Mrs. Flannery looked shocked, then thoughtful. She had a conscience herself, but she also had seventeen dollars in a savings account and respected interest.

Before she could settle the moral question, there came a quick tapping at the back door.

Mrs. Gratz gathered the bills, returned them to the purse, and slipped the purse under a cushion.

A tall, thin man stood outside. He had a long neck, a narrow brown coat, and a face on which anxiety had written several paragraphs. His hat brim dripped. His eyes moved past Mrs. Gratz toward the yard.

“Mornin’, ma’am,” he said. “Merry Christmas. You keep chickens?”

“I did.”

The man swallowed. “Did?”

“Last night I keeps chickens. Dis morning I keeps feathers.”

“That so?” His surprise was very active but not very convincing. “Too bad. Mighty bad. I’m in the poultry line myself.”

Mrs. Flannery appeared behind Mrs. Gratz. “On Christmas morning?”

“Poultry don’t observe holidays, lady.”

“You buying?” asked Mrs. Gratz.

“Buying and selling. Mostly buying. I was passing and saw your coop, so I thought maybe you had birds to dispose of.”

“I dispose of dem already.”

“Who to?”

“Santy Claus.”

The man blinked.

Mrs. Flannery folded her arms. “And he paid nine—”

Mrs. Gratz’s broad foot came down upon Mrs. Flannery’s wet shoe.

“—nine visits!” Mrs. Flannery finished with a squeak. “Nine visits in the night, the shameless creature.”

The thin man tried to see around Mrs. Gratz. “Mind if I look at the coop? Being in the business, I might tell what happened.”

“Dey was took.”

“Sure, sure, but there might be tracks. Signs. Clues.”

“Clues?” Mrs. Gratz repeated.

“Things left behind. A button, say. A scrap of cloth. A pocketknife. Maybe a—well, maybe a pocketbook.”

He said the last word carelessly, but his eyes said it with both hands.

Mrs. Gratz considered him. He was too thin to be Santa Claus. Yet a man who had spent nine hundred dollars for chickens might naturally become thin.

“What kind pocketbook?” she asked.

“Oh, any kind. Black, brown. They come in different colors.”

“Wid money?”

“Could be.”

“How much?”

“How would I know?” he snapped, and immediately smiled. “I mean, there’s no telling. Ten dollars. Twenty. Five hundred. Nine—anything.”

Mrs. Flannery made a noise like a kettle preparing to boil.

Mrs. Gratz said, “You may look from der door.”

The thin man stepped into the kitchen, bringing rain and nervousness with him. He crossed to the back entrance and stared into the yard. His gaze went straight to the patch of mud by the gate.

Then he went into the yard without waiting for permission.

He searched beside the path, beneath the pump, around the woodpile, and along the fence. He entered the coop and came out with feathers on his coat. Twice he crouched at the place where Mrs. Gratz had found the purse. Once he scratched the mud with his fingers.

Mrs. Flannery watched through the window.

“There’s your Santa Claus,” she whispered. “A poor, underfed one.”

“No beard,” said Mrs. Gratz.

“He may have shaved to avoid identification.”

“No reindeers, neither.”

“He’s after the money. Send for the police.”

“Not yet. I like his come-agains.”

“His what?”

“He comes, he goes, maybe he comes again. Come-agains.”

The thin man returned to the kitchen, wiping mud on his trousers.

“No good,” he said.

“What you loses?” asked Mrs. Gratz.

“I didn’t lose anything.”

“Den you finds all of it.”

He stared at her. Mrs. Gratz stared back pleasantly.

“Maybe the fellow that took your birds dropped something,” he said.

“He drops feathers.”

“Anything else?”

“A broken lock.”

“That ain’t what I mean.”

“What you mean?”

The man opened his mouth, discovered no safe word inside it, and shut it again.

“Well,” he said, “if you hear of anybody wanting to sell chickens, let me know.”

“Red ones, speckled ones, one white one, und a rooster named Herman?”

“Any kind.”

He hurried away through the front door.

Mrs. Flannery rushed to the window. “He’s running!”

“Thin people do,” said Mrs. Gratz.

“You’ve got him! He all but confessed.”

“No. He is a poultry buyer.”

“He’s a thief.”

“If he is a thief, why he buys poultry?”

“He doesn’t buy it!”

“Den he is bad at his business.”

Mrs. Flannery seized her hat. “I’m going straight to Officer Rafferty.”

Mrs. Gratz caught the tail of her coat.

“If you goes to der police, I tells Mr. Flannery about der brandy cake.”

Mrs. Flannery turned pale. Mr. Flannery was a temperance man except during influenza, which he had twice each winter.

“You wouldn’t.”

“I might.”

Mrs. Flannery sat down.

At eleven there came another knock, this one important and official. The thin man stood at the back door again. He had turned his coat inside out, revealing a gray lining. He had also put on a pair of spectacles, though one lens was missing.

Mrs. Gratz opened the door.

“Good morning,” he said in a deep voice. “Municipal inspection.”

“You was taller before,” said Mrs. Gratz.

He coughed. “I never been here before.”

“Your face was.”

“I got a common face.”

“It is not so common. It is very thin.”

He straightened. “I’m an inspector from the city department of animal sanitation, chicken division.”

Mrs. Flannery covered her mouth with both hands.

Mrs. Gratz nodded gravely. “What you inspects?”

“Chicken houses. There’s a dangerous outbreak going around.”

“What breaks out?”

“Bugs.”

“What bugs?”

“Tuberculosis bugs.”

“Toober-chlosis bugs?”

“That’s right. Very ravenous. Eats a chicken clean up—bones and feathers and everything.”

Mrs. Gratz glanced toward the yard. “Mine leaves feathers.”

“Then it’s the early stage.”

“How many chickens dese bugs eats?”

“It depends on how hungry they are.”

“Nine hens und one rooster?”

“That sounds like a medium-sized colony.”

Mrs. Flannery sat upon the rocking chair and rocked violently, though whether from suppressed laughter or moral agitation was difficult to say.

The inspector produced a pencil stub and a folded envelope. “Name?”

“Mrs. Gratz.”

“Number of birds?”

“Before or after der bugs?”

“Before.”

“Nine hens und Herman.”

“Breed?”

“Hungry.”

“I mean what kind?”

“Red, speckled, white, und Herman kind.”

He made several marks on the envelope. “I’ll have to inspect the premises.”

“You inspects from here.”

“Regulations require me to go over the entire yard.”

“Why?”

“To find bug traces.”

“What dey looks like?”

“Small.”

“How small?”

“So small you can’t see ’em.”

“Den how you finds dem?”

“With scientific methods.”

“What methods?”

“Searching.”

He attempted to pass her, but Mrs. Gratz filled the doorway comfortably. A placid fat woman, when she chooses to remain in a doorway, is nearly as useful as a wall and less easy to argue with.

“Do dese bugs eat pocketbooks?” she asked.

The inspector’s pencil snapped.

“Pocketbooks?”

“Black ones, brown ones. Dey comes in different colors.”

Mrs. Flannery gave a choking gasp and hid it in her handkerchief.

The man looked from one woman to the other.

“Sometimes,” he said cautiously. “These bugs are very destructive. Leather especially. If any leather article was left in an infected area, the department must take charge of it.”

“Wid money inside?”

“Money can carry germs.”

“How many germs on nine hundred dollars?”

His face sharpened. “Who said nine hundred?”

“Nobody. I asks.”

“Why that amount?”

“Why not?”

He rubbed his jaw. “If you found anything in that yard, ma’am, you’d better hand it over. For public safety.”

“I found mud.”

“Anything else?”

“Rain.”

“Anything else?”

“Feathers.”

He leaned near her. “Listen. This ain’t a joke.”

“Toober-chlosis bugs sounds serious.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Ja,” said Mrs. Gratz, and for one small moment her eyes ceased to be sleepy. “I knows.”

The man stepped back.

Mrs. Gratz smiled again, and the moment passed.

“I look for your bugs myself,” she said. “If I sees one, I catches him in a jar.”

“You can’t see ’em!”

“Den I cannot catch dem.”

“That’s why an inspector—”

“Good-bye, Mr. Inspector.”

She closed the door.

Through the window they watched him stalk down the path. At the gate he stopped, turned, and stared at the muddy ground once more. Then he went away rapidly, his inside-out coat flapping.

Mrs. Flannery collapsed into laughter.

“Tuberculosis bugs! Oh, save us! Bugs that eat chickens on the hoof!”

“Chickens do not got hoofs,” Mrs. Gratz said.

“Neither do bugs eat pocketbooks.”

“Maybe city bugs is educated.”

“You’re playing with a dangerous criminal.”

“He plays first.”

“And when he comes again?”

“I hears what he is next.”

Mrs. Flannery wiped her eyes. “He’ll be the mayor.”

“Too thin.”

“A bishop.”

“Too nervous.”

“A traveling dentist come to examine the rooster’s teeth.”

“Herman got no teeth.”

“That won’t stop him.”

They ate the brandy cake at noon. Mrs. Flannery took only one piece, then another piece too small to count. Mrs. Gratz made tea. The rain thickened and rattled softly against the windows.

At half past twelve, Mrs. Flannery left by the front door after promising, reluctantly and three times, not to call the police. She had crossed no farther than the hedge when a man came up the street.

It was the thin man.

He now wore a black derby instead of his soft hat. His coat was right side out again, and a tin badge shone upon his lapel. The badge appeared to have come from a child’s box of holiday candy.

Mrs. Flannery retreated behind the hedge.

The man marched to the back door and knocked with authority.

Mrs. Gratz admitted him to the kitchen.

“Police department,” he said.

“Chicken division?”

“Detective bureau.”

“You changes jobs quick.”

“I told you, lady, I ain’t been here before.”

“Your coat comes often.”

He ignored this. “We received a report of poultry theft in this neighborhood.”

“Who reports it?”

“Anonymous party.”

“What is his name?”

“If he’s anonymous, he ain’t got one.”

“Poor man.”

The detective took out a small notebook—the railway timetable from the purse would have suited him better, had he still possessed it—and wet his pencil.

“You are Mrs. Gratz?”

“Still.”

“Sometime during the night, unknown persons entered your premises and removed ten head of poultry.”

“They got heads, bodies, und feathers when dey goes.”

“That’s what ‘head of poultry’ means.”

“Not ten heads by demselves?”

“No.”

“Good. Dat would be messy.”

He frowned and continued. “I am investigating. Valuable clues may have been dropped by the criminal.”

“Mr. Poultry Buyer says dat.”

“Who?”

“Mr. Toober-chlosis says it, too.”

The detective’s eyes narrowed. “There’s impostors around. You can’t trust everybody.”

“Can I trust you?”

“Certainly. I got a badge.”

Mrs. Gratz peered at it. Around the edge were the words DELICIOUS CREAMS—A CHRISTMAS ASSORTMENT.

“Very official,” she said.

He covered the badge with his hand. “Now, did you find anything in the yard?”

“Ja.”

His whole narrow body leaned toward her. “What?”

“A broken padlock.”

“What else?”

“Feathers.”

“What else?”

“Mud.”

“What else?”

“The yard.”

He struck the notebook with his pencil. “Anything that didn’t belong there?”

“Somebody’s shoe marks.”

“What size?”

“I do not measure.”

“Long and narrow?”

“Like you.”

He put one foot behind the other.

“The thief may have dropped personal property,” he said. “A wallet, purse, pocketbook, something of that nature.”

“Why?”

“Criminals often do.”

“Dey should sew up der pockets.”

“This is serious police business. If you conceal evidence, you can be arrested.”

“For what?”

“Obstructing justice.”

“Is justice in der pocketbook?”

“Maybe.”

“How much justice?”

His patience, which had never been fat, grew thinner.

“Look here,” he said. “A pocketbook was lost in this yard.”

Mrs. Gratz folded her hands over her apron. “How you knows?”

“Because—I mean, information came to headquarters.”

“From der anonymous man wid no name?”

“Yes.”

“What kind pocketbook?”

“Black leather.”

“Black?”

“Yes.”

“Wid brass on it?”

“Yes.”

Mrs. Gratz nodded slowly. “Und what is inside?”

“A comb, timetable, key, some coupons—”

He stopped.

Mrs. Gratz’s smile did not change, but it seemed to become wider without moving.

The detective swallowed. “That’s the description supplied to us.”

“Anything else inside?”

“Some currency.”

“How much?”

“Maybe nine hundred dollars.”

“Exactly?”

“Exactly.”

“A very exact maybe.”

He thrust the notebook into his pocket. “All right. You found it. Hand it over.”

“Why?”

“Because it belongs to the party that lost it.”

“Der anonymous party?”

“Yes.”

“Who is he?”

“That’s confidential.”

“Maybe he is Santy Claus.”

“He ain’t Santa Claus!”

“How you knows?”

“Because there ain’t any Santa Claus!”

Mrs. Gratz looked mildly offended. “Den who takes my chickens und leaves nine hundred dollars?”

“Nobody left it! It was dropped by accid—”

Again he stopped, but this time the stop came too late.

Outside the window, Mrs. Flannery rose from behind the hedge like a red-faced judgment angel.

“Ha!” she cried through the glass. “Caught by his own villainous tongue!”

The thin man spun around.

Mrs. Flannery hurried to the back door and entered, dripping hedge water.

“You stole them chickens!” she declared. “You broke the lock, you dragged off nine innocent hens and one poor rooster, and you dropped your wicked gains in the mud!”

“They weren’t wicked gains,” he said. “I earned that money.”

“Earned it stealing chickens?”

“I didn’t say I stole anything.”

“You said you dropped it.”

“I said somebody dropped it.”

“You said by accident.”

“Somebody did!”

Mrs. Gratz poured herself another cup of tea.

The man turned to her. “Lady, let’s quit fooling. That money’s mine.”

“Maybe.”

“There’s nine hundred dollars in that purse.”

“Maybe.”

“There’s a key, a comb, a timetable, and cigar coupons.”

“Maybe.”

“It fell out of my pocket right by your gate.”

“Maybe.”

His hands trembled. “Then give it back.”

“Why was you in my yard?”

“I wasn’t.”

“Den how it falls out?”

“I was passing.”

“Inside der locked gate?”

“The gate wasn’t locked.”

“Der chicken house was.”

“I don’t know nothing about that.”

“Mr. Poultry Buyer knows.”

“He was mistaken.”

“Mr. Inspector knows.”

“He was a fake!”

“Mr. Detective knows.”

He tore the candy badge from his coat and threw it on the table.

“All right!” he shouted. “I took the chickens.”

Mrs. Flannery clapped both hands together. “Confession!”

“But they ain’t worth nine hundred dollars.”

“Herman was a fine rooster,” said Mrs. Gratz.

“He was a crow-footed old noise box.”

“You knows him good.”

“And them hens—half of ’em was scrawny.”

“You inspects close.”

“I was going to sell ’em for maybe twelve dollars. Fifteen at most. You can keep twenty. Keep fifty! But give me the rest.”

Mrs. Gratz stirred her tea.

“Where is der chickens?”

“I don’t know.”

“You takes dem.”

“I sold ’em.”

“To who?”

“A fellow.”

“Anonymous?”

“I don’t know his name.”

“Everybody you knows got no name.”

The thief ran both hands through his hair. “Listen, ma’am. I need that money. It ain’t mine exactly.”

Mrs. Flannery gasped. “More robbery!”

“I collected it for somebody.”

“Who?”

“A man.”

“Anonymous again,” said Mrs. Gratz.

“He’ll kill me if I don’t turn it in.”

Mrs. Gratz regarded him over her cup. For the first time, the thief seemed less comic than frightened. His coat was too thin for the weather. Rain darkened his shoulders, and his cheeks had the gray look of a man who had not eaten breakfast.

Mrs. Gratz was placid, but she was not made of dough. She could feel pity without allowing it to run away with her pocketbook.

“You steals chickens because you carries somebody’s money?” she asked.

“I was short of cash.”

“Nine hundred dollars short?”

“No, I had the nine hundred. I wanted a little extra before I left town.”

“So you steals ten chickens.”

“It looked easy.”

“Easy is sometimes expensive.”

He sat down hard in a kitchen chair. “I never had luck.”

“You got my chickens.”

“And lost nine hundred dollars.”

“Dat is what I means.”

Mrs. Flannery pointed toward the street. “March him to the station.”

The thief jumped up. “No police.”

“I’m not frightened of you,” Mrs. Flannery said, immediately moving behind Mrs. Gratz.

Mrs. Gratz rocked once, twice, three times.

“No police,” she decided.

The man stared at her. “You mean it?”

Mrs. Flannery stared harder. “Have ye lost your reason?”

“If I calls der police,” said Mrs. Gratz, “dey comes in. Dey asks questions. Dey writes papers. Dey takes der pocketbook for clues. Maybe dey gives it to him. Maybe dey keeps it until Easter. Maybe dey tells me lost money must go to der man who loses it.”

“That’s the law,” said the thief eagerly.

“But der law also tells what happens to chicken thieves.”

His eagerness departed.

Mrs. Gratz went on rocking. “I do not need der police. I knows what happens. Santy Claus comes in der night. Toober-chlosis bugs eats my chickens.”

The thief gaped at her.

Mrs. Flannery began to smile.

“Bugs?”

“Ravenous bugs,” said Mrs. Gratz. “A medium colony. Dey eats nine hens und one rooster on der hoof.”

“Chickens don’t have hoofs,” he said automatically.

“Dat is what I tells Mrs. Flannery. But city bugs is educated.”

“And the lock?” Mrs. Flannery asked, entering joyfully into the arrangement.

Mrs. Gratz considered. “Reindeers breaks it.”

“Unruly beasts,” said Mrs. Flannery.

“Then what about the purse?” demanded the man.

“Santy Claus leaves it.”

“I ain’t Santa Claus!”

“No,” Mrs. Gratz agreed. “You are too thin.”

He paced the kitchen. “You can’t just keep nine hundred dollars.”

“I can put it in der bank.”

“It’s mine!”

“Den you tells der police.”

He stopped pacing.

Mrs. Gratz’s round face was peaceful. Her hands lay quiet upon her apron. She seemed to have no weapon except comfort, and yet the thin man looked at her as if she had built a stone wall around the house.

“You’re robbing me,” he said.

“I do not break your lock.”

“You know that purse wasn’t a Christmas present.”

“I believes what is convenient.”

“That ain’t belief.”

“It is very comfortable belief.”

He pointed at her. “You’re a sly old woman.”

“Und you are a bad poultry buyer, a worse inspector, und a detective wid a candy badge.”

Mrs. Flannery laughed so loudly that the crooked paper bell trembled above the lamp.

The thief looked toward the door. “Suppose I come back with the real police?”

“Come again,” said Mrs. Gratz. “I like come-agains.”

“And tell them you’ve got my money?”

“Den I tells dem about Herman.”

He stood for a moment, calculating. Thin men calculate rapidly because figures do not have to travel far around them. The calculation gave him no pleasure.

“Couldn’t you give me some of it?” he asked at last.

Mrs. Gratz looked at his wet coat and gray face.

She rose, went to the pantry, and returned with a paper parcel.

“Here.”

He took it suspiciously. “What’s this?”

“Bacon sandwiches. Also two pieces cake.”

“I mean money.”

“No.”

“I got to get out of town.”

“Walk fast. You are practiced.”

Mrs. Flannery said, “The cake has brandy in it. It may restore your criminal circulation.”

The man tucked the parcel under his arm. At the door he turned.

“You ought to be ashamed.”

“Maybe tomorrow,” said Mrs. Gratz. “Today is Christmas.”

He went out into the rain, crossed the yard without searching it, and disappeared through the gate.

Mrs. Flannery watched him go.

“You gave a chicken thief your best bacon.”

“He was hungry.”

“You kept his nine hundred dollars.”

“I was hungry for dat.”

Mrs. Flannery sat down, slowly this time. “Mrs. Gratz, I don’t know whether you’re a saint or the cleverest sinner in the county.”

“I am a widow.”

“That’s no answer.”

“It answers many things.”

They remained quiet for nearly a minute. The drizzle softened. Somewhere in the neighborhood a church bell began to ring, not crookedly like the paper bell, but deep and solemn, calling respectable people to respectable Christmas duties.

Mrs. Flannery sighed.

“What will you do with it?”

“Der bacon?”

“The money!”

“Tomorrow I puts it in der bank.”

“All of it?”

Mrs. Gratz thought of the empty coop, the broken lock, and Herman’s silenced dawn.

“Maybe eight hundred fifty. Fifty buys a new lock und new chickens.”

Mrs. Flannery nodded. “And a rooster?”

“No.”

“No rooster?”

“I buys an alarm clock. It eats less.”

The next morning the sky cleared. Sunlight lay pale upon the wet fences, and the yard shone with puddles. Mrs. Gratz dressed early—at eight instead of half past—and placed the black purse in her shopping bag.

Before leaving, she went to the chicken coop.

Nothing had changed. Feathers still clung to the wire. The broken padlock still lay in the mud. Long, narrow footprints led from the gate to the coop and back again, crossing and recrossing where a nervous man had searched for his lost fortune.

Mrs. Gratz looked at the prints, then up at the washed blue sky.

“Santy Claus,” she said, “next year you should wear boots dat fit.”

At the bank, the clerk counted the money twice.

“Nine hundred dollars, Mrs. Gratz?”

“Ja.”

“Christmas gift?”

“Exactly.”

“Your family was generous.”

“Very.”

The clerk pushed a deposit slip toward her. “Name of donor?”

Mrs. Gratz considered the blank line. She thought of poultry buyers, city inspectors, detectives, tuberculosis bugs, anonymous men without names, and reindeer with a reckless attitude toward padlocks.

Then she wrote, in her slow, heavy hand:

SANTY CLAUS.

The clerk looked at it, smiled, and said nothing. Bank clerks encounter many kinds of faith, especially when accompanied by cash.

Mrs. Gratz tucked the deposit book into her bag. It was a satisfying little book, neat, official, and much easier to feed than ten chickens. On the way home she bought a new padlock, eight hens, and an alarm clock.

The hens cost rather more than she liked, but Christmas presents are meant to spread happiness, and the poultry dealer looked extremely happy when he took her money. The alarm clock was guaranteed to ring every morning. The padlock was guaranteed against ordinary thieves, though the shopkeeper could not promise anything about reindeer.

By noon the new hens were scratching in the yard. The clock sat upon the kitchen shelf. The new padlock shone upon the coop door.

Mrs. Flannery came to inspect all three.

“Do you think he’ll return?” she asked.

“The clock salesman?”

“The thief.”

“Maybe. He got come-agains.”

“And if he does?”

“I tells him der money is gone.”

“To the bank?”

“To Santy Claus.”

Mrs. Flannery shook her head. “You’ll have to answer for this someday.”

“Maybe.”

“What will you say?”

Mrs. Gratz settled into her rocking chair. The new hens clucked outside, the clock ticked inside, and her bankbook rested warmly in her pocket.

“I says,” she replied, “I loses nine hens und a rooster, und I finds nine hundred dollars. I does not make der world. I only counts what is in it.”

Mrs. Flannery attempted to object, but the sentence gave her very little room. She took a piece of leftover cake instead.

Mrs. Gratz rocked.

The hens scratched.

The clock ticked.

And if somewhere beyond the town a tall, thin man ate two bacon sandwiches while regretting that he had ever entered the poultry business, that also was part of Christmas. For Christmas, as anyone can tell you, is a season of giving and receiving.

It is only the bookkeeping that sometimes becomes confused.

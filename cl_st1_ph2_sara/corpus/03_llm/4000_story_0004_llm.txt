## THE JATAKA OF THE ELEVEN-LINKED CHAIN

This is the tale of the chain that bound more surely when it was broken, and of the cage whose door stood open though its prisoners could not pass through it. The wise man Narada of the Nine Memories told it beneath a tamarind tree when the evening parrots were flying home like green leaves blown backward through the golden air.

At that time Prince Chandraketu, who was young enough to believe that generosity purchased gratitude and gratitude ripened inevitably into love, sat beside the wise man upon a carpet from Bokhara. The prince had asked:

“Master, if a man gives all that he possesses to a woman—liberty, treasure, honour, and the unguarded citadel of his heart—must she not at last love him?”

The wise man laughed softly, as water laughs beneath lotus leaves.

“Listen, O moon among questioners. A pearl may be given, a province may be given, a life may be given. Love alone cannot be given by him who desires it, but only by the heart in which it awakens. HEAR, therefore, the jataka of Vasantasena, Vikramavati, and Madusadan, the captain of horse.”

And thus began the tale.

* * *

In the age when elephants wore silver castles, when the snows of Kailasa were believed to be the white robes of meditating gods, and when kings engraved their boasts upon pillars lest the wind forget them, Vikramavati ruled in the city of Jayapura.

He was young, high-hearted, and beautiful after the fashion of a sword newly drawn from its sheath. His skin was bronze touched with morning; his eyes were black almonds set beneath an impatient brow; his hands were narrow and strong; and upon his head he wore a turban of purple silk fastened by an emerald large enough to have ransomed seven caravans. He could split a mango at sixty paces with an arrow, tame a stallion which had killed its groom, compose a tolerable verse upon the moon, and hear petitions for three hours without yawning. Therefore the people praised him exceedingly, as people praise a king when taxes are light and soldiers remain beyond the horizon.

Yet Vikramavati possessed one error, and because it wore the garments of virtue he did not recognise it. He believed that a gift created a debt in the invisible world. When he forgave, he waited for devotion; when he bestowed, he expected remembrance; when he loved, he looked for love to return like a trained falcon to the jewelled glove.

Now to the north of Jayapura lay the hill-kingdom of Ratnapura, governed by King Vasantapala, whose banners bore a white peacock upon crimson cloth. Vasantapala rebelled against Vikramavati, or Vikramavati rebelled against Vasantapala—the scribes disagree, for the pen commonly sleeps beside the victor’s pillow. There was war. Conches cried beneath a copper sunrise; horses foamed; drums rolled like caged thunder; arrows crossed the sky in black flights; and by sunset the rice fields were redder than the hibiscus gardens of Kama.

King Vasantapala fell beside his shattered chariot. His palace was taken. His elephants, treasure, wives, dancers, astrologers, parrots, ivory couches, sandalwood chests, and hereditary enemies passed into the possession of Vikramavati.

Among the captives was Vasantapala’s daughter, the Princess Vasantasena.

She had hidden in a ruined pavilion while soldiers searched the palace, and there she might have perished beneath a fallen cedar beam, for flames moved through the painted chambers with the golden tongues of hungry demons. But Madusadan, captain of Vikramavati’s horse, heard a cry which was scarcely louder than the crackling lacquer screens. He entered through fire.

Madusadan was no perfumed youth of the women’s courtyard. Forty years had the monsoon written upon him. His face was browned and marked by campaigns; a white scar crossed his chin; his beard showed three threads of silver; and his shoulders had learned the burdens of armour, command, defeat, and survival. He found Vasantasena pinned beneath the beam, her ankle bleeding, her hair loosened, her father’s dagger clenched in both hands.

“Stand back,” she cried, “or I shall kill thee.”

“If thou canst kill me,” he answered, lifting the blazing beam, “do so after I have carried thee out.”

He wrapped her in his cloak and bore her through the falling embers. A burning tile struck his shoulder, yet he did not loosen his hold. Outside, when she had swooned, he placed her beneath a neem tree, gave his water flask to the physician, and returned to battle without looking again upon her face.

This restraint entered Vasantasena’s memory more deeply than fire.

For desire is not always born from possession. Sometimes it is born from the hand withdrawn.

LISTEN!

When Surya climbs the eastern stairs, the closed lotus hears him; when Vayu crosses the jasmine garden, the sleeping fragrance follows him; when Varuna troubles the sea, buried shells remember the moon. So did the princess, waking in defeat, remember the man who had saved her and had not asked even the payment of her eyes.

* * *

Three days later Vasantasena was brought before King Vikramavati.

The Hall of a Hundred Columns had been prepared for triumph. Blue silk canopies floated overhead; bronze lamps hung in chains from beams painted with geese and clouds; sandalwood smoke curled among the courtiers; and the floor, washed in rose water, reflected anklets, sword points, and the crimson soles of dancing girls. On trays of hammered gold lay the spoils of Ratnapura: rubies from Badakhshan, sapphires from Serendib, turquoise from Nishapur, pearls from the Gulf, moonstones pale as the tears of widows, cat’s-eyes striped with secret fire, coral branches from southern seas, diamonds from Golconda, lapis from beyond the Oxus, amber holding dead insects older than dynasties, and jade cups green as the first leaf of spring.

Then Vasantasena entered, wearing the plain white cloth of a captive.

She was not adorned; therefore all adornments appeared vulgar beside her. Her hair descended in a black river almost to her knees. Her face was oval and still, like a moon seen in a well at midnight. Her eyes were long, dark, and grave; her mouth resembled the cut side of a pomegranate; her throat was a pale column upon which grief had set no jewel; and the wound upon her ankle was bound with coarse linen. She looked neither to right nor left.

Vikramavati forgot the proclamation in his hand.

“Who is this?” he asked, though he knew.

“Vasantasena,” replied Deo Singh, the elderly prime minister, “daughter of the lately deceased and formerly inconvenient Vasantapala, once princess of Ratnapura, now by the intelligible customs of victory a slave of Your Majesty.”

Deo Singh was lean, bald, and yellow-robed, with a beard like a white broom and a jewelled thumb which he wagged whenever delivering a truth nobody wished to hear. He had read the Vedas, half the commentaries upon the Vedas, and several books which contradicted both. For this reason he was esteemed wise.

Vikramavati descended from his throne.

“Princess,” he said, “look upon me.”

She looked.

The court watched the king receive that gaze as a garden receives hail.

“Thy father opposed me and is dead,” said Vikramavati. “Thy kingdom opposed me and is mine. By the law of conquest thou art my slave.”

“So I have been informed,” she replied.

“But I abolish that law concerning thee. Before these nobles, these priests, these captains, and the gods who dwell invisibly above our heads, I declare thee free.”

He took the chain of servitude from her wrists and flung it upon the floor.

The courtiers applauded. The priests murmured blessings. Deo Singh’s jewelled thumb made one slow circuit in the air.

Vasantasena rubbed the pale marks upon her skin.

“Thou art free,” repeated the king.

“To go where?”

Vikramavati hesitated.

“To any pavilion in my palace. To any garden within my walls. To command servants, musicians, litter-bearers, horses—”

“To leave Jayapura?”

The king’s smile became thinner.

“The roads are unsafe.”

“A road forbidden by another name remains forbidden.”

“Thou judgest me harshly. I have restored thy dignity.”

“My dignity was not thine to restore.”

A silence opened in the hall, deep enough to drown an elephant.

But Vikramavati was captivated; and a captivated man often calls the lock upon his own ankle a garland. He smiled again and ordered that Vasantasena be conducted, not to the prisoners’ court, but to the Pavilion of Blue Lilies.

Thereafter his gifts descended upon her like an extravagant monsoon.

He sent silks from China, so delicate that twenty lengths could be drawn through a signet ring; shawls from Kashmir, woven with red poppies and golden deer; muslins from Dacca, fine as mist; brocades from Persia; belts of linked emeralds; anklets chiming with silver bells; combs of ivory; mirrors of polished steel; alabaster jars filled with musk, myrrh, attar of roses, civet, saffron, henna, and powdered sandalwood.

He gave her twelve maidservants, four singing women, two dwarfs accomplished in mimicry, a physician, a reader of poems, a white mare from Arabia, a green parrot which recited obscenities in three languages, and a garden where fountains fell through marble cups among champaka, jasmine, magnolia, oleander, asoka, tuberose, and blue lotus.

She thanked him with perfect courtesy.

She did not love him.

He released thirty prisoners because she had once looked sadly toward the prison tower. She thanked him.

He remitted a village tax because she spoke of hungry children. She thanked him.

He sat through a night of rain outside her pavilion because she would not admit him. In the morning she sent a servant with dry clothes. She thanked him.

At last he said, “Vasantasena, I have laid my kingdom beneath thy feet.”

“Nay,” she answered, “thy kingdom remains beneath thine own feet. Thou hast merely invited me to stand beside thee.”

“I have made thy wishes law.”

“Only those wishes which do not contradict thine.”

“I would die for thee.”

“That is easily said by one who expects the saying to be rewarded.”

Then Vikramavati pressed both hands against his heart.

“Cruel one! What more can love perform? I have humbled pride, scattered treasure, neglected sleep, pardoned enemies, and placed my happiness in thy keeping.”

Vasantasena regarded him with that grave gaze which neither wounded nor comforted, but measured.

“Thou hast placed thy happiness in my keeping without asking whether I desired the burden. Thou callest thyself selfless, O king, yet all thy roads return to thee. Thou freest me because thou wouldst be loved for freeing me. Thou givest because thou wouldst be loved for giving. Thou sufferest because thou wouldst be loved for suffering. Thy sacrifice is a merchant dressed as a pilgrim.”

“Then teach me to love without desire.”

“Can the river be taught not to seek the sea?”

“Give me hope.”

“I will not give false coin to a king.”

“Is there another?” he whispered.

At this Vasantasena turned away.

Thus the king learned the answer.

* * *

Madusadan had avoided the Pavilion of Blue Lilies. If duty brought him through the eastern garden, he chose the western arcade; if Vasantasena rode at dawn, he inspected stables at dusk. Yet avoidance is also a road, and every road, if faithfully travelled, acknowledges its destination.

One evening Vasantasena found him beneath a flowering kadamba tree, tightening the girth of his black stallion. The sunset had dyed the palace walls apricot and purple; peacocks screamed from the roofs; and rain waited in the swollen clouds like a confession behind closed lips.

“Captain,” she said.

Madusadan bowed.

“Princess.”

“Do not call me princess. My father is ashes, my kingdom another man’s province, and my crown perhaps melted into drinking cups.”

“What shall I call thee?”

“My name would suffice.”

“That is more dangerous.”

“Thou hast faced cavalry.”

“Cavalry commonly announces its charge.”

She came nearer. “Why hast thou avoided me?”

“Because the king loves thee.”

“That answers a question I did not ask.”

“Because thou art lonely, and gratitude in loneliness may wear the mask of love.”

“Still thou answerest another question.”

“Because I am not young enough to mistake every flame for the household lamp.”

“Nor am I foolish enough to mistake the household lamp for the sun.”

Madusadan smiled, though sorrow stood behind the smile like a soldier behind a curtain.

Vasantasena said, “When thou carriedst me from the fire, thou didst not look back.”

“If I had looked back, I might not have gone forward.”

“And since then?”

“Since then I have looked back every hour.”

The rain began—not violently, but in broad, warm drops that darkened the dust and jewelled the kadamba leaves. Vasantasena stood so near that he could smell wet jasmine in her hair.

“Then look now,” she said.

He looked.

Yet he did not touch her.

This patience was the second key. The first had been restraint; the second was understanding. Desire awoke within her not as a storm that conquers a valley, but as a lamp uncovered in a shrine: small, unwavering, and suddenly sufficient to illuminate the whole darkness.

“Why dost thou not kiss me?” she asked.

“Because thou hast lost a father, a throne, a country, and freedom within one month. I will not become another conqueror taking tribute from thy confusion.”

“And if I command thee?”

“I obey the king.”

“And if I ask thee?”

“I ask thee to ask again when grief is not speaking through thy mouth.”

She struck him then, not hard, but with all the pride of Ratnapura. Madusadan accepted the blow. He took her hand, kissed the palm once, and released it.

For seven days she hated him.

On the eighth she loved him.

Weeks passed. Madusadan waited. He spoke with her in gardens where servants could see; he rode beside her but did not brush her stirrup; he listened when she spoke of her father’s foolish courage, her mother’s death, the hill winds of Ratnapura, the little temple where she had fed sugared rice to monkeys. He did not praise her beauty when she wished to be heard. He did not use her sorrow as a bridge to her body. When she was unjust, he contradicted her; when she was proud, he laughed; when nightmares woke her, he sat outside the screen until dawn, telling ridiculous tales of army mules and dishonest quartermasters.

He taught her that tenderness need not kneel, that patience need not be cold, and that service without freedom is servitude, but service freely given is love’s most secret crown.

Love’s play is worship.

Love’s fulfilment is a rite.

Love’s mystery is never wholly read.

At last, beneath the same kadamba tree, Vasantasena said, “I ask again.”

Madusadan touched her cheek.

“Is grief speaking?”

“No.”

“Is gratitude speaking?”

“No.”

“Is loneliness speaking?”

“It has gone away.”

“What remains?”

She drew his face down to hers.

“This.”

Their mouths met while thunder walked among the hills. The black stallion turned his head, observed them, and returned with philosophical indifference to the grass.

* * *

Now secrets are birds with bright feathers: even when caged, they leave colour upon the bars.

The king noticed Vasantasena’s altered silence. Before, it had been a shut door; now it was a curtain trembling after someone had passed behind it. He saw that her eyes no longer rested upon nothing, but waited. He saw Madusadan depart from court too quickly. Jealousy, that green serpent which sleeps beneath the pillow of possession, lifted its head.

One night Vikramavati crossed the garden alone.

The moon was full. White lilies floated upon black water; the marble paths shone like bones; and night-blooming flowers poured forth such fragrance that the air itself seemed intoxicated. At the far end of the cypress walk, beside a fountain shaped like a silver heron, he saw two figures.

Vasantasena stood in Madusadan’s arms.

There was no haste in their embrace, no shame, no stolen trembling. Her cheek rested against the captain’s breast; his chin rested upon her hair. They seemed not two flames devouring each other, but one lamp enclosed by two hands against the wind.

The king drew his sword.

Madusadan heard the blade leave its sheath and turned, placing Vasantasena behind him.

“Do not,” she said, stepping forward again. “I will not be hidden now.”

Vikramavati’s face had become calm—terribly calm, for passion which ceases to shout has entered the chamber where judgments are forged.

“Captain of horse,” he said, “thou hast betrayed thy king.”

“I have disobeyed his desire,” replied Madusadan. “Whether I have betrayed him depends upon whether he appointed me keeper of a woman’s heart.”

“And thou,” said Vikramavati to Vasantasena, “hast answered mercy with deceit.”

“I promised thee no love.”

“I gave thee freedom!”

“Freedom which forbade the choice thou disliked.”

“I gave thee honour!”

“Honour cannot be given like a bracelet.”

“I would have given thee my life.”

“But never the right to refuse it.”

Vikramavati raised the sword; then, perhaps because he loved her, perhaps because he loved himself as the man who loved her, he lowered it.

“Guards!” he called.

Men came running with spears and lanterns. The king ordered that the lovers be seized.

At dawn the court assembled. Vasantasena and Madusadan stood in chains before the throne. The hall that had once glittered for triumph now seemed made of iron and shadow.

Vikramavati spoke without anger.

“Madusadan, thou shalt be tied beneath the war elephants’ path, and Vasantasena shall be placed where she may watch. Thereafter she shall be bound upon the northern tower beneath the sun, and thou, if life remains in thee, shalt hear her cries.”

Even Deo Singh ceased wagging his jewelled thumb.

Madusadan laughed.

“Why dost thou laugh?” asked the king.

“Because punishment has become extravagant where argument failed.”

“Dost thou believe love will protect thee from death?”

“No. Love can accomplish much, but it cannot turn flesh to stone, command an elephant’s foot to hover, or persuade the sun to cool its fire. Love can accomplish almost anything except the one miracle thou demandest of it: it cannot create itself in a heart where it does not live.”

“Death will end thy triumph.”

“What triumph?”

“That she chose thee.”

Madusadan looked at Vasantasena. She smiled, and there was in that smile neither bravado nor despair, but remembrance.

“O wise king,” said the captain, “death may prevent tomorrow, but it cannot abolish yesterday. Thou mayst crush my bones and dry her blood upon thy tower. Yet we have known fulfilment. We have been patient, foolish, tender, jealous, merry, angry, reconciled, and awake together. What has existed cannot be made not to have existed. Even the gods cannot unring a bell.”

Vasantasena added, “If thou slayest us, thou wilt possess two corpses and lose even the dream of being loved.”

The king’s fingers tightened upon the arms of his throne.

Then Deo Singh advanced. His yellow robe rustled; his white beard trembled; his jewelled thumb rose.

“Majesty,” said he, “elephants are expensive, tower executions disorder the pigeons, and cries beneath the sun disturb the accountants. Moreover, death is an unreliable instructor. Its pupils never report what they have learned.”

“Speak plainly, old man.”

“I propose a punishment more subtle than pain and more certain than execution.”

“What punishment?”

“Proximity.”

The court murmured.

Deo Singh continued: “Love thrives upon absence, secrecy, moonlight, danger, perfume, music, interrupted meetings, forbidden doors, and the conviction that nobody has ever loved so magnificently before. Remove these spices and the dish will reveal its true taste. Chain the lovers together for three days and three nights in one chamber. Give them every luxury but no separation: one couch, one table, one window, one bathing room, one privy, one another. Let them eat together, sleep together, wake together, stumble together, quarrel over cushions together. By the third day desire will become weariness; tenderness will become irritation; and each will perceive the other not as moon or lotus, but as an inconvenient human creature who breathes, snores, complains, and occupies too much space.”

Vikramavati leaned forward.

“And then?”

“Then release the chain and offer each freedom from the other. They will bless Your Majesty with sincerity.”

The king looked at Vasantasena.

“Will thy love survive this?”

She answered, “A chain may test our ankles. It cannot instruct our hearts.”

Madusadan said, “Three days are short, Majesty. Make it thirty years.”

The king’s face darkened. “Three days will suffice.”

Thus was the ordeal decreed.

* * *

They were taken to the Chamber of the Painted Heavens, the most luxurious room in the palace.

Its ceiling was midnight-blue and covered with golden stars. Its walls showed gardens where painted lovers wandered among impossible birds. There were carpets thick as moss, cushions of crimson velvet, a low cedar couch spread with white silk, lamps of perfumed oil, trays of figs, peaches, pistachios, sweetmeats, rice, spiced lamb, and sherbet cooled with mountain snow. A fountain whispered in an alcove. Through a latticed window one could see half the city and a narrow blade of sky.

The blacksmith brought a chain ten feet in length. One iron cuff was fastened about Madusadan’s left ankle, the other about Vasantasena’s right. The links were heavy, polished, and strong enough to hold a bull elephant.

Deo Singh inspected them.

“Observe,” he said, “ten feet: sufficient for hope, insufficient for escape.”

“Thou hast forgotten something,” said Madusadan.

“What?”

“To provide us with a topic of conversation.”

“You will discover several,” replied the minister. “The first will concern who has taken the blankets.”

The door was locked.

During the first hour, the lovers laughed whenever the chain caught against a chair. During the second, Vasantasena moved suddenly toward the window while Madusadan moved toward the sherbet, and both nearly fell.

“Thou pullest like an artillery mule,” she said.

“And thou changest direction like a courtier.”

They ate. When Vasantasena reached for a peach, Madusadan took the same one. She surrendered it; he divided it. The juice ran over his thumb, and she licked it away.

“Deo Singh would call that appetite,” he said.

“Deo Singh calls ignorance philosophy after adding footnotes.”

At night they discovered that the chain lay cold between them. Madusadan wrapped it in silk. Vasantasena dreamed of the burning palace and woke crying her father’s name. Madusadan held her until her breathing quieted. Neither spoke of the trial.

On the second morning he snored.

“Captain,” she said, shaking him, “thou hast routed armies more quietly.”

“I was dreaming of cavalry.”

“The cavalry was in thy nose.”

Later she wished to bathe and ordered him to face the wall.

“I have seen thee without armour,” she said, “but not yet without obedience.”

He faced the wall. She splashed him deliberately. He retaliated with a cup of water. Soon the painted heavens witnessed a battle more cheerful than that of Ratnapura.

But confinement is a patient file. By noon the room had grown smaller. The perfumes cloyed; the fountain repeated one silver sentence; the same patch of sky remained blue; the delicacies lost savour. Vasantasena tried to walk while Madusadan sat. The chain tightened.

“Must thou remain there?” she asked.

“My knee pains me.”

“Thy knee has not pained thee during any moonlit meeting.”

“Moonlight is a celebrated physician.”

She frowned and turned away.

There it was—the first irritation, small as a grain of sand.

Madusadan did not plead. He rubbed his knee in silence.

After a time Vasantasena knelt beside him.

“Does it pain much?”

“Enough to make me disagreeable.”

“Thou wert disagreeable before.”

“Then it has made me consistent.”

She laughed and massaged the scar beside his kneecap. The grain of sand became a pearl.

That evening they quarrelled concerning the king.

“Thou shouldst not have laughed at him,” she said.

“He had condemned us to be trampled and burned.”

“He was wounded.”

“So were we.”

“His wound was not wholly ignoble.”

“No,” Madusadan conceded. “He loves according to his nature.”

“And we according to ours.”

“He wished to possess thee.”

“He also wished to be worthy of me.”

“He confused worthiness with reward.”

“And hast thou never expected reward?”

Madusadan was silent.

The chain lay between them, black upon the white carpet.

“At first,” he admitted, “I expected the reward of believing myself honourable. I avoided thee partly because restraint pleased my pride.”

Vasantasena looked sharply at him.

“Why didst thou never say so?”

“I did not know it until now.”

“Then the king is not the only merchant dressed as a pilgrim.”

“No.”

“And I,” she said slowly, “loved thy restraint partly because it allowed me to conquer the one man who would not come when summoned.”

“Then we are a bazaar of disguised merchants.”

They sat apart as far as the ten-foot chain permitted.

After sunset Madusadan said, “Forgive me.”

“For what?”

“For enjoying thy love as proof of my virtue.”

She considered.

“Forgive me for enjoying thy surrender as proof of my power.”

They drew nearer, link by link.

Love without forgiveness is a cup with a crack.

Love without confession is a lamp without oil.

Love without laughter is a kingdom without windows.

On the third day monotony entered openly and sat with them. They had told childhood histories, campaign tales, court gossip, dreams, fears, obscenities, poems, and lies once believed. They had eaten the sweetmeats, rearranged the cushions, counted the painted stars, invented names for the impossible birds, and composed a hymn to the privy door. Nothing remained to be said.

Therefore they were silent.

Vasantasena rested her head upon Madusadan’s lap. He combed her hair with his fingers. The chain curved beside them like a sleeping serpent.

Beyond the locked door, Vikramavati arrived with Deo Singh. A hidden panel, built long ago by a suspicious queen, permitted them to listen unseen.

The king whispered, “They are silent. Thou wert right.”

Deo Singh lifted his jewelled thumb. “Wait. Silence may be emptiness, Majesty, but it may also be a room where two persons no longer fear one another.”

Inside, Vasantasena said, “Madusadan.”

“Yes, little flower?”

“I have discovered a misfortune.”

“What misfortune?”

“When this chain is removed, thou mayst walk away without taking me with thee.”

“I shall demand another.”

“I am serious.”

“So am I.”

“Do we love because we are confined?”

“No. We endure confinement because we love.”

“And if we were chained for years?”

“I would sometimes hate thee.”

Vikramavati drew a sharp breath beyond the panel.

Vasantasena asked, “Only sometimes?”

“Frequently. Thou art proud, imperious, untidy with fruit skins, and incapable of admitting that the window is closed because thou closed it.”

“And thou art vain, stubborn, elderly, noisy in sleep, and affectionate toward boots which should be burned.”

“Then we understand one another.”

“Not entirely.”

“May it never be entirely.”

“Why?”

“Because complete certainty is a room with no unopened door. If I believed thee wholly known, I would cease to look. If I believed thy love secured forever, I might cease to deserve it. Let there remain some country in thee where I travel as a stranger.”

Vasantasena raised herself upon one elbow.

“And if one day I do not love thee?”

“Then no chain, vow, sacrifice, or memory should make thee counterfeit it.”

“Wouldst thou let me go?”

“I would break my heart opening the door.”

She touched his face.

“And if one day thou dost not love me?”

“Remind me to look again.”

“Look where?”

“At thee. Not at my memory of thee, nor my expectation, nor my comfort, nor the service thou hast rendered, nor the promise thou hast made. At thee.”

Then Vasantasena smiled.

“I love thee now.”

“And I thee.”

“After three days?”

“After every hour of them.”

“After the snoring?”

“In spite of the cavalry.”

They laughed softly and kissed.

Outside, Deo Singh’s jewelled thumb drooped.

The king stood motionless. Tears did not fall from his eyes; they remained there, making his gaze brighter and more terrible.

“At noon,” he said, “release them.”

“Majesty—”

“Release them. Give Madusadan command of the northern frontier. Restore to Vasantasena the revenues of three districts of Ratnapura. Let them marry, if marriage does not frighten them more than death.”

Deo Singh bowed.

“And what of Your Majesty?”

Vikramavati looked through the secret aperture at the lovers, who sat linked ankle to ankle and yet appeared freer than any sovereign.

“I shall learn,” he said, “that an open hand is not a hook.”

* * *

Thus the iron chain was removed. Yet, says the tale, another chain remained—not forged by smiths, not closed by locks, not imposed by king, priest, custom, fear, gratitude, or need.

Its first link was Passion, for without flame the lamp is cold.

Its second was Patience, for flame untended consumes its own house.

Its third was Fulfilment, for hunger forever praised becomes merely hunger.

Its fourth was Friendship, for lovers who cannot speak must one day become strangers in the same bed.

Its fifth was Tolerance, for every beloved creature casts a shadow.

Its sixth was Understanding, which listens beneath words.

Its seventh was Tenderness, which knows where strength must kneel.

Its eighth was Forgiveness, which repairs what pride declares irreparable.

Its ninth was Service, freely offered and freely refused.

Its tenth was Humor, without which even paradise acquires the gravity of a tomb.

And there was an eleventh link, invisible and unnamed, lighter than breath, stronger than iron. Some call it Uncertainty. Some call it Vigilance. Some call it the little fear which keeps love wakeful beside the lamp. For security is love’s house, but complacency is the worm in its foundation; trust is love’s garden, but possession is the wall that starves it of sun.

Madusadan and Vasantasena grew old together, though not without quarrels. He continued to snore. She continued to close windows and deny it. They buried friends, survived fevers, lost a child, welcomed grandchildren, forgave offences, repeated stories, and discovered in each other provinces unmapped by youth. Sometimes they slept back to back. Sometimes they woke holding hands.

As for Vikramavati, he became a just king, though never a merry one. In later years, when petitioners praised his generosity, he would look at his empty hand before answering. Deo Singh lived to the age of ninety-three and maintained until his final breath that his experiment had succeeded, since it had destroyed not the lovers’ affection but the king’s illusion.

Here the wise man Narada ended his recitation.

Prince Chandraketu, seated beneath the tamarind tree, was silent. Evening had become night; the carpet from Bokhara was silvered by moonlight; and the green parrots had long since vanished into sleep.

At last the prince asked, “Master, if love cannot be purchased by sacrifice, why should a lover sacrifice anything?”

The wise man smiled.

“To purchase nothing.”

“And if love is never secure, why trust it?”

“Because trust is not certainty.”

“And what was the true chain?”

Narada pointed first to the prince’s heart, then to the road disappearing beneath the dark trees.

“Walk,” he said. “Watch. Forgive. Ask again.”

This is the tale of the chain that bound more surely when it was broken, and of the cage whose door stood open though its prisoners could not pass through it.

THIS IS THE TALE OF THE CHAIN THAT WAS LIGHTER THAN JASMINE AND STRONGER THAN IRON.

LISTEN.

In the reign of the Peacock Emperor, when the roads of Hindustan were white with the dust of armies and the moon herself seemed a silver coin stamped with the face of conquest, there came at evening to the pavilion of Jehan Tugluk Khan a young warrior whose name has been forgotten, though his folly remains immortal.

The young warrior wore a coat of crimson mail, and his sword-hilt was crusted with turquoise. His beard was black and curled like smoke above a sacrificial lamp; his arms were strong enough to bend a bow of horn; and in battle he had ridden laughing beneath arrows as another man rides beneath the rain. Yet now he trembled.

Jehan Tugluk Khan sat beneath an ancient fig tree where green parrots quarreled among the leaves. He was old—so old that the snow upon his beard seemed to have fallen from the first winter of the world. Before him stood a bronze bowl filled with rosewater, and beside the bowl burned a slender candle whose flame bowed whenever the warm night wind came wandering from the gardens.

The young warrior saluted and said:

“O Father of Counsel, Pearl of the Understanding, physician of wounds which bleed inwardly, I desire from thee a potion.”

“Is thy liver troubled?” asked Jehan.

“My liver is a fortress.”

“Thy stomach?”

“A lion’s.”

“Thy sleep?”

“I have none.”

“Ah,” said Jehan Tugluk Khan. “Then the disease is lower than wisdom and higher than hunger. What is the maiden’s name?”

The warrior looked about him, though there were none to hear save the parrots, the candle, the old man, and the moon.

“Her name is Chandrabala,” he whispered. “Her hair is night braided with perfume. Her eyes are two dark antelopes drinking at the same pool. Her voice makes the vina ashamed of its strings. Yet when I praise her, she smiles as one smiles upon a child who has mistaken a firefly for a star. Give me, therefore, a draught which shall compel her heart.”

Jehan Tugluk Khan stirred the rosewater with one finger.

“What wilt thou give her if she loves thee?”

“My palace.”

“And if she desires it not?”

“My horses.”

“And if horses weary her?”

“My emeralds, my rubies, my estates, my slaves, my victories, my body, my soul, my next seven lives.”

“And if she does not love thee?”

The young warrior struck his breast.

“Then she is ungrateful.”

At this, Jehan laughed softly. A fig dropped from the tree and burst upon the earth like a little purple heart.

“O Foolish Virgin,” said the old man—for so he called all young men who knew warfare but not love—“thou wouldst purchase sunrise with copper, bind fragrance with rope, and command the wild swan to nest in thy helmet. There is no potion which creates love where love has chosen not to dwell.”

“There must be one.”

“There is none.”

“I have heard that in Kashmir grow blue flowers whose juice changes hatred to desire.”

“Men who sell such flowers live upon the hatred of fools.”

“I have heard that beneath Mount Meru a serpent guards a jewel which makes every woman faithful.”

“The serpent guards only the jewel; as for faithfulness, he has been thrice deserted by his wives.”

“Then teach me some charm.”

“I shall teach thee a tale.”

“I asked for a potion.”

“And I offer thee a mirror. Drink from it if thou canst.”

Then Jehan Tugluk Khan lifted his hand, and even the parrots ceased their green disputation.

LET ALL THE WISE CHILDREN LISTEN TO MY JATAKA!

There was once, in the country watered by the seven silver branches of the river Saras, a king named Vikramavati. He was young, victorious, generous, impatient, beautiful, and accustomed to obedience. His face shone like bronze warmed by the sun. He possessed nine hundred elephants with bells of beaten gold, six thousand mares whose nostrils were red as pomegranate flowers, three palaces roofed with copper, and a treasury in which pearls lay heaped like hailstones after a celestial storm.

When Vikramavati frowned, governors lost provinces. When he laughed, poets acquired villages. When he desired war, drums spoke from mountain to mountain; and when he desired peace, ambassadors came carrying sandalwood boxes filled with tribute.

Because all things yielded to him, he imagined that the heart also was a province.

In the ninth year of his reign he went against Raja Surasena, lord of the northern hills, whose banners bore the white boar and whose fort stood upon a cliff like an eagle’s nest built of black stone. For forty days the armies contended. Spears ripened in the fields. Blood made the dust into red clay. Elephants screamed beneath towers of painted wood, and arrows crossed in the sky until day appeared to wear a veil.

On the forty-first morning Madusadan, captain of the royal horse, found a narrow path behind the fortress, a goat track hidden by thorns and yellow blossoms. He ascended with two hundred riders before dawn. At sunrise the gates were opened from within, Raja Surasena’s army broke like a clay vessel beneath a hammer, and the victorious soldiers entered the palace.

Madusadan was not young. There were silver threads in his beard, scars upon his cheek, and a stiffness in his left knee when rain approached. His shoulders were broad, his hands hairy, his mouth unexpectedly red. He spoke little, observed much, and had learned from horses, kings, women, and war that whatever is pulled too fiercely either breaks or bites.

In a pavilion behind the burning palace he found Princess Vasantasena, daughter of the defeated raja.

She stood among six dead guards with a curved dagger in her hand. Her robe was white, but smoke had written gray verses upon it; one sleeve was torn, and from the wound beneath it blood fell slowly, each drop bright as a ruby released from a miser’s fist. Her hair streamed to her knees. Her face was pale with fury, but her eyes—dark, opaque, and powdered with golden specks—held neither tears nor surrender.

A soldier ran toward her.

Madusadan struck him down.

Another reached for her necklace.

Madusadan broke the man’s wrist.

Then he removed his cloak, placed it upon the ground between himself and the princess, and stepped backward.

“Lady,” he said, “thy father is defeated, thy palace taken, and thy life ringed by swords. These things I cannot deny. Yet no man shall touch thee while my command endures. Stand upon my cloak, and it shall be a little kingdom.”

Vasantasena looked upon him with disdain.

“Is the kingdom mine?”

“Until thou leavest it.”

“And if I use thy cloak to strangle thee?”

“Then I shall regret having chosen so strong a clasp.”

Despite herself, the corner of her mouth moved.

Madusadan called for women, water, and a covered litter. He did not seize the dagger. He did not gaze upon the torn sleeve. He did not speak to her of gratitude. When she stumbled crossing the threshold, he offered his arm but did not touch her until she took it.

Thus desire, which often enters with trumpets, came to Vasantasena barefoot.

She was brought with the spoils before King Vikramavati. Around the throne rose heaps of conquered wealth: ivory tusks, bronze shields, carpets woven with hunting leopards, chests of saffron, sapphires blue as fragments of midnight, images of gods with almond eyes, and cups carved from crystal. Yet when Vasantasena entered, all these treasures became as pebbles beside the jewel of her living presence.

She wore no crown. Dust lay upon her feet. Her wrists bore the dull iron rings of captivity. Nevertheless, she moved as the moon moves through clouds—veiled but sovereign.

Vikramavati leaned forward.

“Who is she?”

“The daughter of Surasena,” said Deo Singh, his prime minister.

Deo Singh was a narrow Brahmin with a brow like a closed door. His wisdom had preserved three kings, ruined seven enemies, and made him distrusted by every honest fool at court.

“She is my slave?” asked Vikramavati.

“By war, she is.”

The king descended from his throne. With his own hands he struck the iron rings from Vasantasena’s wrists. Then he took the chain, coiled it at his feet, and said:

“By my will, thou art free.”

“Can the hand which enslaves confer freedom?” she asked.

The court gasped. Deo Singh hid a smile in his beard.

Vikramavati answered, “If freedom lies beyond my power, take what freedom thou canst and forgive the remainder.”

This reply pleased her less than beauty but more than arrogance.

The king gave her a palace in the Garden of Green Fountains. Its walls were of rose-colored stone; its lattices of sandalwood; its floors of polished marble where black and white flowers had been set in mosaic. Twenty maidens attended her. Musicians played behind screens at twilight. In the court grew champak, orange, and pomegranate trees, and a channel of clear water ran through the chambers over tiles painted with silver fish.

He restored her father’s jewels. He sent physicians to the surviving people of her country. He forbade songs celebrating his victory. He placed no guard at her door.

Each morning came a gift: anklets strung with tiny bells; a shawl light as breath; a cup of jade; a white falcon; a manuscript painted in lapis and gold; seven black pearls; a mechanical bird which sang when water was poured through its silver throat. Each evening came a letter.

Vasantasena returned the anklets, for she disliked their sound. She kept the shawl because an old attendant was cold. She freed the falcon. She admired the manuscript. She laughed at the bird. She did not answer the letters.

Yet Vikramavati did not grow angry. He came to walk with her under the flowering trees and spoke no word of conquest. He listened when she remembered her father. He rebuilt a shrine burned by his soldiers. When she was ill, he waited outside her chamber three nights, neither eating meat nor hearing music.

The court said, “Behold how greatly he loves.”

But Vasantasena’s heart remained silent.

For gratitude is a lamp, admiration is incense, pity is rain upon hot dust, and tenderness is a white cloth laid over a wound; but none of these is necessarily love. The heart may receive every gift and still keep one chamber locked. It may kneel and yet not surrender. It may forgive and yet not desire.

Vikramavati offered her his hand, his crown, and the half of his kingdom.

She answered, “Thou hast given me everything except that which I might return.”

“What is that?”

“Freedom from the burden of thy hope.”

At these words the king’s face became pale.

“Do I displease thee?”

“No.”

“Am I cruel?”

“No.”

“Am I ugly?”

“No.”

“Is there another?”

She turned away.

Then Vikramavati knew.

LET ALL THE WISE CHILDREN LISTEN TO MY JATAKA!

Madusadan had avoided the Garden of Green Fountains. He rode the western marches, inspected stables, punished thieves, and found a hundred honorable duties by which a man may flee one dangerous happiness. But duty is a wall through whose smallest crack longing grows like a vine.

One evening he returned from the frontier. Rain clouds towered purple above the city. The earth smelled of wet leaves, horses, and distant thunder. Passing the garden, he saw a white falcon circling free above the palace and knew it for the bird Vasantasena had released.

He entered by neither stealth nor command. He sent his name through an old woman and waited beside the fountain.

Vasantasena came wearing a robe the color of young mango leaves. No jewels adorned her except drops of rain in her hair.

“Captain,” she said, “thou hast taken long to inquire whether thy little kingdom survived.”

“My cloak?”

“Thy cloak.”

“I feared to claim rent.”

“Coward.”

“Yes.”

“Thou faced five hundred spears at the northern gate.”

“They wished only to kill me.”

“And I?”

“Thou mightest have wished me to live.”

She looked into the flowing water. Silver fish passed over her reflection and broke it into trembling fragments.

“Why didst thou not come?”

“Because the king loves thee.”

“Is his love a wall around my garden?”

“No. It is a fire near dry grass.”

“Why didst thou not come?”

“Because thou wert wounded, captive, and alone. A man who takes advantage of helplessness is not a lover but a second conqueror.”

“Why didst thou not come?”

Madusadan’s restraint broke then, not like glass, which shatters loudly, but like snow beneath sunlight.

“Because,” he said, “I desired thee beyond wisdom, beyond loyalty, beyond the peace of my remaining years. I desired the anger in thy eyes, the courage in thy torn sleeve, the hand which kept hold of its dagger when all else was lost. I desired thy voice when it mocked me. I desired even thy grief, though I was ashamed to stand near it. Therefore I stayed away.”

Vasantasena raised her golden-flecked eyes.

“Thou givest nothing.”

“I give thee distance.”

“Thou askest nothing.”

“I ask that thou choose without debt.”

“Thou promisest nothing.”

“I promise to hear the answer.”

She came closer.

“Then hear it.”

The rain descended.

It fell upon the marble court, upon the champak flowers, upon the bronze lamps, upon the white walls and green leaves; it filled the garden with whispering, as if ten thousand hidden lovers repeated one secret. Vasantasena put her hands into Madusadan’s rough hands, and the captain who had not feared war began to tremble.

“Hadst thou seized me,” she said, “I should have hated thee. Hadst thou begged, I should have pitied thee. Hadst thou praised thyself, I should have laughed. But thou didst wait outside my sorrow until I opened its gate.”

“And now?”

“Now enter.”

Love’s invitation is a kingdom. Love’s threshold is a sword. Love’s play is worship, and its worship is peril.

They met afterward in secrecy, though secrecy is a veil woven from smoke. The old women knew. The parrots knew. The fountain knew and carried their laughter from court to court. Perhaps even Deo Singh knew, for ministers know everything except when to leave happiness uncounted.

At last Vikramavati came at midnight to Vasantasena’s palace, carrying in his own hand a garland of moon-white flowers. He had resolved to release her from his court forever if she again refused him, since even a young king may learn nobility after exhausting hope.

The guards were absent by her command. The lamps burned low. From the terrace came two voices.

He heard Vasantasena say, “When I am old, will thy patience remain?”

Madusadan answered, “No. I shall become impatient for thee to walk more slowly.”

“And when my beauty is gone?”

“I shall ask where it has gone, and thy mirror will be embarrassed.”

“And when we quarrel?”

“I shall remind thee that thou once threatened to strangle me with my own cloak.”

Vasantasena laughed.

The sound entered Vikramavati’s heart more cruelly than any dagger, for she had never laughed so with him.

He stepped onto the terrace.

The moonlight lay upon the lovers. Vasantasena’s head rested against Madusadan’s shoulder. Their hands were joined. Between them stood neither fear nor apology.

The king dropped the garland. Its white blossoms scattered upon the black floor like fallen stars.

“Captain,” he said, “thou hast betrayed thy king.”

Madusadan released Vasantasena’s hand and bowed.

“I have.”

“Princess, thou hast betrayed my mercy.”

Vasantasena stood erect.

“Mercy which demands love in payment is commerce.”

“I demanded nothing.”

“Thy silence demanded. Thy gifts demanded. Thy patience stood forever at my door with an empty bowl.”

Vikramavati drew his sword. The blade made a bright arc in the moonlight.

Madusadan did not draw his own.

“O king,” he said, “if blood will heal thee, take mine. But do not call thy wound justice.”

“What name should I give it?”

“The pain of loving where thou art not loved.”

“Silence!”

“That pain is terrible. Yet it grants no throne over another heart.”

“I gave her freedom.”

“Aye,” said Vasantasena. “And now thou wouldst punish me for using it.”

The king’s sword trembled. For one instant greatness and misery contended within him. Misery prevailed.

“At dawn,” he said, “ye shall both die.”

Thus jealousy took the crown from love and wore it crookedly.

At sunrise the court assembled in the Hall of a Hundred Pillars. Vasantasena and Madusadan stood unbound before the throne. Through high windows streamed bars of golden light in which dust turned and glittered like the souls of forgotten insects.

Vikramavati said, “Captain, thou hast one speech before death. Make it brief.”

Madusadan answered, “Majesty, love can persuade fear, outwait grief, soften pride, endure poverty, mock distance, and make an old soldier climb a garden wall in the rain. It can perform a thousand impossibilities. But it cannot create itself where it does not exist. Thou couldst give Vasantasena a continent and still not purchase one beat of her heart. Thou canst kill us both, yet not destroy the hour we have shared. Death may close the cup; it cannot make the wine undrunk.”

“Fine words,” said the king, “are feathers against the axe.”

“Even so, a feather may outlive the hand that swings it.”

Then Deo Singh rose.

“Majesty,” he said, “death is swift, and swiftness is mercy. Why give mercy to those who have mocked thee?”

“What punishment dost thou advise?”

The Brahmin’s eyes narrowed.

“Give them what they profess to desire. Give them one another—entirely.”

The court murmured.

“Speak plainly,” commanded Vikramavati.

“In the eastern tower is a chamber of pleasure: a bed soft as cloud, lamps perfumed with aloes, food in golden vessels, wine cooled in snow, music-boxes, flowers, painted walls, warm baths, and no window except one too narrow for escape. Take an iron chain ten feet in length. Fasten one end to the captain’s ankle and the other to the princess’s. Shut them within for three days and three nights. Let no servant enter. Let them eat together, sleep together, wake together, cross and recross the same narrow room, hear the same breathing, endure the same gestures, the same silence, the same flesh, the same face.”

Vikramavati stared.

Deo Singh continued:

“Passion feeds upon absence, uncertainty, danger, forbidden doors, hurried meetings, imagined perfection. Remove these spices, and desire becomes stale rice. Lovers praise union because they have not swallowed enough of it. Give them excess. Let nearness do what the executioner cannot.”

The courtiers nodded, for cruelty disguised as philosophy is always admired.

Vasantasena said, “Thou art very certain, Brahmin.”

“I have studied men.”

“Women also?”

“From sufficient distance.”

“Then thy education lacks its most troublesome manuscript.”

Madusadan smiled.

The king gave the order.

A chain was brought, black and heavy, each link broad as a man’s thumb. One end was locked around Vasantasena’s left ankle, the other around Madusadan’s right. When they walked, the iron dragged over the marble with the hiss of a sleeping serpent.

They were led to the chamber in the eastern tower.

The door closed.

One day passed.

Then one night.

Then another day, another night, and yet another day, while Vikramavati neither slept nor heard petitions. Sometimes he imagined them quarreling. Sometimes he imagined them silent. Sometimes, which was worse, he imagined them happy.

On the third night, when the last moon rose round and red above the palace roofs, Deo Singh came to the king.

“The lesson is complete,” said the minister. “Come and hear how love speaks when stripped of distance.”

They climbed the winding stair. At the door was a keyhole covered by a little brass plate. Deo Singh bent and listened. Vikramavati stood behind him.

Within, Vasantasena was speaking.

“Beloved, the chain has bruised thee.”

“It is jealous because thy ankle is prettier than mine.”

“Everything becomes a jest to thee.”

“Not everything. Thy singing remains a tragedy.”

“Monster! I sang to comfort thee.”

“Then I was comforted that songs must end.”

There was a pause, and afterward their laughter—tired, tender, unguarded—came through the keyhole.

Deo Singh frowned.

Madusadan said, “These have been the shortest three days of my life.”

“And mine,” said Vasantasena. “Yet I have learned terrible truths.”

“What truths?”

“That thou scatterest bread upon the bed.”

“And thou stealest the blankets.”

“That thy left knee predicts rain and complains before it.”

“That thou art vain enough to arrange thy hair though no mirror praises thee.”

“That thou dost snore.”

“I was reciting a cavalry prayer.”

“Then the cavalry should surrender.”

Again they laughed.

After a little silence, Vasantasena said:

“When the door opens, they may kill us.”

“Yes.”

“Art thou afraid?”

“Yes.”

“I am glad.”

“Glad?”

“Courage without fear is merely stone. Thy fear proves that life with me has value.”

Madusadan’s voice softened.

“I am afraid of losing mornings I have not yet seen: thy gray hair upon the pillow, thy anger at careless servants, thy hands teaching a child to hold a cup, thy voice calling me deaf when I pretend not to hear. I am afraid of the unworn years.”

“Then listen, my captain. Whether there be one hour or sixty years, I am chained to thee.”

“No,” he answered. “Chains are forced upon captives.”

“Then I choose the chain.”

“And I.”

Vikramavati drew back from the door as though struck. Deo Singh remained bent, his learned ear pressed uselessly against the keyhole.

At length the king whispered, “Open it.”

The locks were withdrawn.

Within the chamber, flowers lay withered in their bowls. Empty dishes stood upon the floor. A lamp burned low beside the bed. Vasantasena and Madusadan sat together against the wall, pale from confinement, their garments rumpled, the iron chain crossing their joined feet.

Yet peace was upon them.

Not the blazing peace of saints, nor the frozen peace of tombs, but the warm and imperfect peace of two who have quarreled, forgiven, eaten, laughed, wearied, awakened, and chosen once more.

Vikramavati entered.

“Did ye not hate the chain?”

Vasantasena touched the iron.

“This chain? It was troublesome.”

“Did ye not weary of one another?”

“Frequently,” said Madusadan.

Deo Singh brightened.

The captain continued, “But weariness is not hatred. A traveler may tire upon the road without cursing the destination.”

“Did closeness not destroy desire?” asked the Brahmin.

Vasantasena answered, “Closeness destroyed illusion. We are grateful to thee. I now know he scatters crumbs, snores cavalry prayers, and grows solemn when his knee aches. He knows I steal blankets, sing badly, and become unreasonable when hungry. Before, we loved in gardens. Now we have also loved in a room.”

The minister had no reply.

Then Vikramavati knelt and unlocked the chain.

“Go,” he said.

Madusadan bowed his head.

Vasantasena looked long at the king. Her eyes no longer held disdain, but a grave compassion more painful than anger.

“Thou hast done a kingly thing,” she said.

“Nay,” replied Vikramavati. “I have ceased for one moment to do an unkingly one.”

He restored Madusadan’s rank, though the captain would not keep it. He gave them horses, gold, and a house beyond the southern hills. Of the gold they took little. Of the horses they took two. The black chain Vasantasena carried with her.

In later years it hung above their hearth. Their children swung from it until the links shone smooth. When Madusadan’s knee stiffened, Vasantasena mocked his cavalry prayers; when Vasantasena sang, Madusadan complained until she sang louder. They quarreled over journeys, money, guests, spices, wet cloaks, open doors, and the raising of daughters. They forgave. They served. They watched one another age.

As for Vikramavati, he became a wiser king, which is not the same as a happy man but is often purchased at the same price. He married in time, though never again did he confuse devotion with tribute or generosity with a key. Deo Singh added a chapter to his books entitled “Concerning Exceptions,” and because he was a Brahmin and a minister, he placed all humanity within it.

THIS WAS THE TALE.

Jehan Tugluk Khan fell silent. The candle beside him had shortened; dawn stood pale behind the trees, and the parrots were beginning to mutter in their sleep.

The young warrior said, “Then love survives confinement?”

“Sometimes.”

“And gifts are useless?”

“Not if they are gifts.”

“And patience wins the beloved?”

“Not always.”

“And restraint?”

“Not always.”

“Then what is the charm? What was the secret of Vasantasena and Madusadan?”

Jehan took a reed and drew in the dust a chain of ten links.

“The iron chain was ten feet long,” he said. “The chain of their love had ten links.

“The first was passion, for without flame the lamp is merely decorated metal.

“The second was patience, for fruit torn green from the bough sets the teeth on edge.

“The third was fulfillment, for a promise forever postponed becomes a beggar at the gate.

“The fourth was friendship, which remains at the table when desire has gone to sleep.

“The fifth was tolerance, because every beloved creature possesses at least one habit designed by a demon.

“The sixth was understanding—not agreement, O Foolish Virgin, but the knowledge of where another’s sorrow keeps its knife.

“The seventh was tenderness, which touches strength without shaming it.

“The eighth was forgiveness, since two hearts dwelling near must sometimes tread upon one another’s dreams.

“The ninth was service, because love that will not carry water is thirsty rhetoric.

“And the tenth was humor, brightest and humblest of the links, by which pride is made small enough to pass through the door of reconciliation.”

The warrior counted the marks in the dust.

“If I forge these ten links, will Chandrabala love me?”

Jehan sighed.

“Thou hast heard the tale and remained deaf. These virtues may sustain love; they cannot compel its birth. Thou mayest prepare a garden, but no command makes the wild bird descend.”

“Then I must do nothing?”

“Thou must ask. Thou must listen. Thou must believe the answer.”

The warrior lowered his head.

After a time he said, “And if she loves me?”

“Then rejoice carefully.”

“Carefully?”

“Love has one enemy not named in the ten links.”

“Who is it?”

“Certainty.”

The young warrior looked puzzled.

Jehan Tugluk Khan scattered the chain drawn in the dust with his bare foot.

“When lovers become certain that love cannot depart, they cease to greet it at dawn. They neglect the lamp because yesterday it burned. They leave kindness undone, laughter unanswered, forgiveness unasked. They imagine the chain needs no smith. Rust is patient, O warrior. Rust keeps eternal vigil.”

“So enduring love is insecure?”

“Not fearful—watchful. The gardener trusts spring, yet still waters the rose. The sailor trusts his vessel, yet still studies the sky. The lover trusts the beloved, yet does not treat the beloved as furniture already possessed.”

The sun rose then, setting fire to the eastern clouds. The bronze bowl flashed. The old fig tree became full of birds, leaves, dew, and golden clamor.

Jehan Tugluk Khan lifted the rosewater and poured it upon the earth.

“This is the only potion,” he said. “Freedom, offered daily. Choice, welcomed daily. Love, earned never, tended always.”

The young warrior departed without spell, flower, jewel, or serpent’s charm. Whether Chandrabala loved him, the sages do not say. Perhaps she did. Perhaps she did not. But it is recorded that he asked her once, listened twice, and never called her answer ingratitude.

Therefore, O children of wisdom and children of folly, remember:

Iron may bind the ankle and leave the spirit free.

Gold may cover the body and never touch the heart.

Passion may open the gate, but patience must keep the road; friendship must furnish the house; tolerance must sweep it; understanding must light it; tenderness must warm it; forgiveness must mend it; service must feed it; and humor must open its windows when the air grows proud and stale.

Love is not a prison built by one for another.

Love is a chain forged by two, worn by choice, and polished by use.

And because even the strongest chain may rust when forgotten, let every lover become its vigilant smith.

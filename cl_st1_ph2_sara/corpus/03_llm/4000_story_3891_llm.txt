For seventeen years Mrs. Manstey had occupied the back room on the third floor of Mrs. Sampson’s boardinghouse.

The room was not large, nor, according to Mrs. Sampson, was it small. It possessed one window, a marble-topped washstand cracked through the middle, a narrow bedstead, and a horsehair armchair whose antimacassar had yellowed in the service of successive occupants. The carpet, once patterned with wreaths, retained its roses chiefly under the bed; and the wallpaper, of a watery blue, was traversed near the ceiling by stains resembling the coast-lines of imaginary continents. Yet Mrs. Manstey had never complained of these deficiencies. She had brought with her a mahogany work-table, two framed photographs, a few books, and a shelf of medicine bottles; and, having disposed these possessions with care, she had accepted the rest as one accepts the weather.

Her window made amends for everything.

It looked not upon the street, whose processions had long ceased to interest her, but upon the backs of a dozen houses and the neglected plots enclosed between them. To Mrs. Sampson, who came in once a week to collect the rent and glance reproachfully at the coal-scuttle, the prospect was a disorder of wash-lines, ash-barrels, kitchen roofs and partition fences. Mrs. Manstey saw it otherwise. She had observed it so long and with such fidelity that every yard had acquired a physiognomy, every tree a history, and every household an obscure but intelligible share in the general drama.

Immediately below her lay Mrs. Sampson’s yard, a paved oblong containing three refuse barrels, a broken chair, and a strip of earth in which nothing grew except plantain and one courageous stalk of goldenrod. Beyond the fence was a garden belonging to an old brick house in the next street. Here stood an ailanthus, despised by gardeners but dear to Mrs. Manstey because its branches rose almost to her window and divided the western light into green transparencies. Under it flourished a syringa, two lilac bushes, and a bed of dicentra whose rosy pendants appeared in May. Farther on, behind Mrs. Black’s house, a magnolia lifted its smooth gray limbs above a wooden balcony. In April its flowers opened before the leaves, white and firm as ornaments cut from ivory; in June the balcony was hidden by a Virginia creeper; and in October the same creeper burned against the weathered boards with a color richer than any curtain in Mrs. Sampson’s front parlor.

Beyond the gardens rose roofs, chimneys, water-tanks, and a brown-stone church spire, which, though hemmed in by warehouses, still caught the earliest light. On clear mornings its cross kindled before the rooms below were awake. At sunset the western sky, seen between two tenements, changed from fluid gold to a cold sulphur, and then to a thin green over which the smoke drifted in violet bands. In winter the snow gathered on shed roofs and balcony rails, softening their meanness; at the first thaw it withdrew in irregular stains, like ink spreading through damp paper. The city, which from the pavement pressed upon Mrs. Manstey in harsh surfaces and bewildering noises, became from her coign of vantage a succession of pictures, composed and recomposed by season, cloud and hour.

Her observation of the human figures in this landscape was not less exact. She knew when the German servant in the corner house had been scolded, because the girl afterward beat the rugs with a vigor unrelated to dust. She knew that the gray cat belonging to the people with the green shutters was fed irregularly, and sometimes lowered scraps of meat to it in a paper parcel tied with thread. She knew that the lame cook at Mrs. Black’s stole five minutes each afternoon to sit under the magnolia, though Mrs. Black believed her to be shelling peas in the kitchen. She had watched a red-haired child grow from a perambulator into a blue-sashed schoolgirl; had inferred an illness from a blind lowered at unusual hours; had rejoiced when a neglected wistaria was finally given wires to climb upon; and had suffered, with an indignation for which she found no audience, over the fortunes of a parrot left through two raw March days on an exposed balcony.

These interests filled spaces which had once been occupied by nearer obligations. Mrs. Manstey had a daughter in California, married to a prosperous man who dealt, as far as her mother understood, in irrigation. Their correspondence was punctual and without intimacy. At Christmas came a box containing crystallized fruit, a calendar, and sometimes a garment unsuited to the New York climate. Mrs. Manstey answered with thanks, in a careful hand, and reported that she was comfortable.

Comfort had become the word by which others measured her condition. She was warm enough, except when the wind came through the window-frame; she had her meals carried up for an additional charge; and Mrs. Sampson, who considered stairs beneficial to old people so long as they could climb them, often declared that no one in the house was more comfortably situated.

Mrs. Manstey herself had learned to use the same phrase.

“Very comfortable, thank you,” she said when the doctor called after an attack of rheumatism.

“So comfortable,” she wrote to her daughter.

Yet on certain winter afternoons, when daylight failed early and the gardens sank into a gray phantasmagoria of fences and bare branches, she would sit motionless in the horsehair chair and wonder whether comfort was what she had expected from life.

She was sixty-nine, a small woman with colorless hair and eyes still singularly clear. Her movements were slow, not from indecision but from the economy of one who knows that every exertion must be paid for. She seldom descended before noon. In bad weather she did not descend at all. The boarders therefore saw little of her and had concluded that she was proud. Mrs. Sampson corrected this judgment by saying that Mrs. Manstey had “always been peculiar,” a phrase which both explained her solitude and relieved the household of responsibility for it.

With the first mild days of March, Mrs. Manstey’s year began. She detected signs invisible to less attentive eyes: a deepening red in the maple twigs, a loosened scale on the lilac buds, the polished point of a crocus pushing through soot-darkened earth. The wash fluttering in the yards no longer froze stiff upon the lines; kitchen windows stood open for an hour at midday; cats chose sunny ledges; and the church spire, released from winter haze, showed sharply against heavens warming from pearl to blue.

That spring was late. Snow remained in the angles of the fences after the pavements had dried, and the magnolia kept its buds folded through the first week of April. Mrs. Manstey waited for it with the mild impatience she had once reserved for letters. Every morning, before dressing, she drew aside her mustard-colored curtain and examined the tree. When at last the outer sheaths loosened and showed white beneath, she felt a happiness so immediate that she turned toward the room as if someone might be there to share it.

There was no one. The tray from breakfast stood on the washstand; a fly, prematurely awakened, moved along the window-pane. Mrs. Manstey looked back at the magnolia.

“How beautiful it will be tomorrow,” she said aloud.

That afternoon Mrs. Sampson entered with the weekly bill. She wore a black silk dress which creaked when she moved and carried with her the odor of the cabbage being prepared below.

“Well, Mrs. Manstey, we’re having a change at last,” she remarked, placing the bill upon the table.

“The magnolia is opening,” said Mrs. Manstey.

Mrs. Sampson looked toward the window, though not far enough to see the tree.

“I guess everything’ll be opening, if this weather keeps up. And there’ll be changes next door too. Mrs. Black’s going to build.”

Mrs. Manstey’s attention, which had already returned to the white buds, came slowly back.

“To build?”

“An extension. Three stories, right out at the back. She’s been talking of it since Christmas, but now the plans are passed. She says she can get six more rooms that way, and bathrooms too. Bathrooms are what people ask for nowadays.”

Mrs. Manstey remained silent. The words seemed to arrange themselves before her without yielding their meaning.

“How far will it come?”

“Oh, pretty near the end of her yard, I should say. It’ll improve the property wonderfully. Those old back lots are a sight.”

“The end of the yard,” Mrs. Manstey repeated.

Mrs. Sampson had turned to inspect the washstand. “You want to be careful of that faucet. Water’s dearer every year.”

“But the magnolia?”

“The tree? I suppose that’ll have to go. You can’t put up a wall with a tree in the way.”

Mrs. Manstey rose and went to the window. She held the sill with both hands and looked down at Mrs. Black’s garden. Its shabby breadth lay in the afternoon light: the balcony, the tree, the cook’s chair, a row of bottles set upside down along the border, the lilac bushes beyond, and, farther still, the successive gardens mounting toward the church spire. She pictured a brick wall advancing across them, not only hiding Mrs. Black’s yard but cutting off everything behind it—the ailanthus, the distant roofs, the changing strip of sky. There would be a wall a few feet from her window, pierced perhaps by other windows in which strangers would appear. No depth, no weather, no seasons; only brick.

“I shall have no view,” she said.

Mrs. Sampson smoothed the bill with one gloved finger. “It isn’t what most people would call a view.”

“No,” Mrs. Manstey answered, after a pause. “Perhaps not.”

When the landlady had gone she continued to stand at the window. The magnolia, which that morning had seemed to promise a whole season of delight, now had the doomed aspect of something already remembered. The cook came out and lowered herself into the chair under it. A blackbird hopped twice across the grass. On the green-shuttered house a blind was raised, revealing a bowl of daffodils.

Could all these things disappear because Mrs. Black wanted bathrooms?

The question did not present itself to Mrs. Manstey as an argument against bathrooms, the utility of which she was ready to admit; it merely revealed the monstrous disproportion between causes and effects. Somewhere a pencil had moved across paper; a contractor had named a price; Mrs. Black had calculated six additional rents—and the sky was to be abolished.

That evening Mrs. Manstey ate no supper. The next morning she rang for the chambermaid and asked at what hour Mrs. Black received visitors.

“Mrs. Black next door? I couldn’t say, ma’am.”

“I wish to speak to her.”

The girl stared. Mrs. Manstey had not paid a call within her memory.

“You’d better try after ten. She don’t get through breakfast early.”

At half past ten Mrs. Manstey put on her bonnet and black cloth mantle. The descent of three flights fatigued her, and in the vestibule she rested against the umbrella stand. Mrs. Sampson, hearing the door, came from the dining room.

“Why, Mrs. Manstey! Going out?”

“I am calling on Mrs. Black.”

Mrs. Sampson’s eyebrows rose. “About the building?”

“Yes.”

“Well, I wouldn’t excite myself. It’s her own property.”

The morning street was full of a hard, untempered brightness. Wheels struck sparks from the rails; delivery carts blocked the crossings; a man pushing a barrow of oranges cried his wares with an insistence that seemed directed personally at Mrs. Manstey. She walked only to the corner and around it, yet the journey appeared longer than all the distances she habitually travelled with her eyes.

Mrs. Black’s front steps were newly scrubbed. The maid who answered the bell showed Mrs. Manstey into a parlor where plush chairs stood at militant angles and an artificial palm occupied the window. The shades were lowered to preserve the carpet. After several minutes Mrs. Black entered, fastening a brooch at her collar.

She was a broad, active woman whose face expressed hospitality until hospitality threatened inconvenience.

“Mrs. Manstey! Well, this is a surprise. Do sit down. Is anybody sick?”

“No. I came about your extension.”

A slight caution replaced the welcome in Mrs. Black’s eyes.

“Mrs. Sampson’s been telling you, has she? I hope the work won’t disturb her boarders. There’ll be some hammering, naturally, but the contractor says it won’t last.”

“I came to ask you not to build it.”

Mrs. Black sat down.

“Not build it?”

“It will cover the entire outlook from my window.”

For a moment Mrs. Black seemed uncertain whether this was intended as humor. “But, my dear Mrs. Manstey, I have to consider my house. The improvement will pay me twelve per cent, if it pays a cent.”

“I understand that it will benefit you. I have thought of that.” Mrs. Manstey’s hands tightened over the handle of her umbrella. “I have some savings. Not much, but more than I need. I could give you a thousand dollars if you would abandon the extension.”

Mrs. Black’s face changed. She knew Mrs. Manstey to be poor, as people with limited incomes know the precise degrees of poverty beneath their own.

“A thousand dollars!” she exclaimed. “You mustn’t talk that way. Why, you’ll need your money.”

“I shall not need it long.”

“Oh, come now! You’re good for years yet. And you can’t throw away your savings over a back window.”

“It is not the window,” said Mrs. Manstey. “It is what I see from it.”

Her voice, though low, had acquired an earnestness which embarrassed Mrs. Black more than tears would have done. Tears belonged to recognizable transactions; they could be met with smelling salts, concessions, or firmness. This dry, deliberate offer suggested some disorder beyond the reach of ordinary methods.

“Well,” Mrs. Black said, “don’t you worry. Nothing’s settled so finally as all that. I’ll speak to the contractor. Maybe we can alter the plans.”

Mrs. Manstey leaned forward. “You will not build?”

“I said I’d see what could be done. You go home and rest easy. We’ll talk again before anything starts.”

“May I rely on you?”

“Of course you may. I wouldn’t have you fretting yourself sick.”

Mrs. Manstey rose. The relief in her face was so complete that Mrs. Black accompanied her to the door with unusual gentleness. On the steps they parted, the one sustained by trust, the other by the comfortable reflection that old people often forgot what had agitated them.

For three days nothing happened.

Those days were among the happiest Mrs. Manstey had known in years. The magnolia opened magnificently. Its blossoms, touched at evening by a roseate light, seemed to float among the bare branches; below them the dicentra lifted its little pierced hearts, and a scatter of violets showed near the fence. Mrs. Manstey watched with gratitude sharpened by escape. She had not merely observed this beauty; she had saved it. Her useless years, her hoarded money, even her long solitude appeared to have found their purpose.

She began a letter to her daughter, describing the magnolia, but stopped after the first page. The account would require explanations which, transferred to paper, might look absurd. Instead she wrote that the spring was pleasant and that she was very comfortable.

On the fourth morning she woke to the sound of wood striking pavement.

At first she supposed that Mrs. Sampson’s barrels were being moved. Then came a man’s call, the groan of wheels, and a prolonged crashing in Mrs. Black’s yard. Mrs. Manstey reached the window.

A wagon stood in the alley. Two laborers were unloading planks; another rolled barrels beneath the balcony. Stacks of brick already occupied the far border. The cook’s chair had been thrown on its side. Against the trunk of the magnolia leaned a pickaxe.

Mrs. Manstey opened the window.

“What are you doing?” she called.

The men did not hear her. One of them spat upon his palms and lifted another plank. Presently a foreman emerged from the kitchen doorway, consulted a paper, and pointed toward the tree.

She closed the window and stood trembling in the cold draught. Mrs. Black had deceived her. The discovery contained, even for Mrs. Manstey, something more terrible than the threatened wall. She had lived so far from active dealings that she had retained a primitive faith in the direct meaning of words. Mrs. Black’s “rely on me” had seemed as solid as a signature. Now the phrases spoken in the plush parlor returned to her hollowed of sense, like scenery viewed from behind.

She sent a note by the chambermaid. It was returned with the message that Mrs. Black had gone downtown.

At noon the first blows fell upon the wooden balcony. Each stroke passed through the intervening walls and struck in Mrs. Manstey’s room with an almost physical vibration. The parrot in the corner house screamed; the gray cat fled over a fence; the lame cook appeared briefly at the kitchen door and was ordered inside.

All day the hammering continued.

Mrs. Manstey did not leave the window. She tried to calculate the height of the extension. Three stories: it would rise above her, then. Even if she moved her chair close to the glass and looked upward, she would see only a narrow wedge of sky. The church spire would vanish first, then the distant roofs, then the ailanthus. Last of all the magnolia would be cut down, unless they destroyed it at the beginning.

No view—no view! The words beat in time with the hammers.

Toward evening the laborers departed. They left the yard in confusion: planks under the balcony, shavings scattered on the paving, sacks of cement covered by tarpaulin. The magnolia still stood, but a white scar showed where a branch had been lopped away.

Mrs. Manstey remained by the window after darkness had obscured the gardens. Lights appeared in kitchens and upper rooms; the church spire dissolved into the sky; the yards became a black gulf crossed by clotheslines. From below came the clatter of dinner and the nasal rise and fall of the boarders’ conversation.

She knew now that persuasion was exhausted. The law belonged to Mrs. Black, money belonged to Mrs. Black, the men and their hammers belonged to Mrs. Black. Mrs. Manstey possessed only the view, and that possession, being written nowhere, was no possession at all.

Her gaze settled upon the lumber beneath the balcony.

The balcony was old and dry. Virginia creeper, dead from winter, clung about its supports. Near the lumber stood the barrels delivered that morning. One had a dark stain down its side. She had seen enough repairs in adjoining yards to know the smell of kerosene, the use of shavings, the quick appetite of seasoned wood.

The thought came without violence. At first she put it from her as one puts aside an object accidentally touched in darkness. But it returned, not as temptation, still less as revenge; it returned in the form of a practical possibility. Fire delayed building. Burned materials must be replaced. Plans might be reconsidered. Insurance men might object. A few days—perhaps a week—would preserve the magnolia in flower.

She rose, lighted her lamp, and took from the cupboard a small can of kerosene used for the stove. It was nearly full.

Her movements now were deliberate. She waited until the household had gone to bed. At eleven Mrs. Sampson’s door closed. At twelve the young clerk on the floor below returned, stumbled against the hall table, and spent several minutes attempting to fit his key into the wrong lock. After that the house settled into intermittent creaks and breathing.

Mrs. Manstey put on her mantle. She wrapped a woollen scarf twice about her throat and took the can and a box of matches. On the first landing she paused, not from fear but because her heart was beating with painful rapidity. The passage smelled of extinguished gas. A thread of light showed under Mrs. Sampson’s door; then it disappeared.

The back entrance opened upon the paved yard. The night air struck Mrs. Manstey with such force that she caught at the wall. April had withdrawn its mildness; the sky was clear and bitter, and the stars above the roofs appeared frozen in place.

She crossed Mrs. Sampson’s yard. The fence between the properties was low where two boards had rotted away. In daylight she could not have climbed it, but desperation discovers in the frail a temporary and ruinous strength. She set down the can, moved a barrel, and drew herself over. Her skirt caught on a nail. For several seconds she hung there, breathing in short gasps, before the cloth tore and released her.

Mrs. Black’s yard seemed unfamiliar from the ground. The gardens which had spread beneath her gaze like a map were narrowed into walls and looming shapes. She could not see the church or the distant sky; she saw only the magnolia trunk, the underside of the balcony, and the pale ranks of lumber.

She poured the kerosene upon the shavings and along the driest boards. The odor rose sharply. Her hands were numb, and the first match broke. The second flared, illuminating her fingers, the torn edge of her skirt, and a cluster of magnolia petals fallen upon the dirt.

For an instant she hesitated. Then she placed the flame among the shavings.

A blue tongue ran over them, sank, and sprang up yellow. Mrs. Manstey did not wait to see more. She recrossed the fence with greater difficulty, leaving the empty can beneath the balcony, and reached the back door. On the stairs her strength failed. She climbed by pulling herself along the banister, one step at a time.

In her room she removed her mantle and sat by the open window.

At first there was nothing to see. Then a reddish wavering appeared below, followed by a thin thread of smoke. The flame moved along the lumber with a stealth almost intelligent; it brightened the balcony rails and cast the magnolia’s branches in black arabesques against Mrs. Black’s wall.

A cry rose from the yard.

Windows opened. Someone shouted “Fire!” with an exultation disproportionate to the danger. Bells rang, doors slammed, and the boardinghouse erupted into stairway commotion. Mrs. Sampson, in a flannel wrapper, ran through the halls calling that everyone must remain calm. The young clerk, disregarding this instruction, rushed outside carrying the china water pitcher. In the adjoining houses women leaned from windows, their nightcaps brilliant in the growing light.

The engines arrived before the fire had taken firm hold. Water flooded the yard; axes struck the balcony; smoke rolled upward and entered Mrs. Manstey’s window. She watched until the flames diminished under a white torrent and the magnolia reappeared, dripping, scorched on one side but still erect.

Then she smiled.

When Mrs. Sampson entered, indignant at finding the window open, Mrs. Manstey was still in the chair.

“You’ll catch your death!” the landlady cried, dragging down the sash. “Sitting in that draught at your age! There wasn’t any danger—not a bit. They’ve got it out.”

“Yes,” Mrs. Manstey said. “They have stopped it.”

By morning she was feverish. The doctor came at noon and pronounced the lungs affected. Mrs. Sampson attributed the illness entirely to excitement and repeated throughout the house that she had always known something would happen if Mrs. Manstey persisted in staring out of windows at all hours.

For several days the old woman drifted between sleep and confused awakenings. She heard voices in the room, the clink of a spoon against glass, and the doctor’s guarded phrases in the passage. Sometimes the magnolia seemed to bend over her bed; sometimes a brick wall rose at its back, advancing steadily while she struggled to call for help.

On the third morning consciousness returned with the light. The chambermaid sat beside the bed, asleep, her head against the horsehair chair. Mrs. Manstey looked toward the window, but from the pillow she could see only a square of pale sky.

“Mary,” she whispered.

The girl woke.

“Put me by the window.”

“Oh, ma’am, the doctor said—”

“Please.”

There was something in Mrs. Manstey’s voice which made the girl obey. She wrapped the old woman in blankets and, with Mrs. Sampson’s reluctant assistance, moved her into the armchair.

The dawn was entering the gardens. Above the roofs the heavens warmed from gray to a tender blue; the wet branches caught the light, and every magnolia flower wore a shining edge. The balcony was blackened, the lumber scattered, and a portion of the fence had been broken by the firemen. But no workmen had come. The bricks remained under tarpaulin; the tools lay idle. Beyond them the ailanthus unfolded its first green leaves, and the brown-stone spire rose clear into the spring morning.

Mrs. Manstey looked long at the whole recovered world. Her breathing was shallow, yet the anxious contraction of her face had disappeared.

“No wall,” she murmured. “They did not build it.”

Mrs. Sampson, standing behind the chair, exchanged a glance with the chambermaid.

“No, no,” she said soothingly. “Don’t you fret about walls.”

“So comfortable,” Mrs. Manstey whispered. “So comfortable—”

The sun touched the upper branches of the magnolia. A blackbird descended to the scorched railing and shook the water from its wings. Mrs. Manstey’s eyes followed it; then they rested upon the distant spire, which had begun to glow.

She died before the breakfast bell rang.

At nine Mrs. Black conferred with the contractor in the yard. The damage, he assured her, was trifling, and the delay need not exceed a few hours. By noon fresh lumber had been delivered, and the men resumed the extension.

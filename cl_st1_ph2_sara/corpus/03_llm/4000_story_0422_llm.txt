The panther had just concluded her performance.

At a sign from the trainer she had leaped through a hoop of blue fire, walked with solemn delicacy between a double row of naked sabres, and laid her tawny head upon the man’s breast while he stood with folded arms, exposing his throat to the caprice of those formidable jaws. Then, amid the applause of the spectators, she had retired backward to her cage, not without turning twice toward her master, as a queen, banished by etiquette from her own drawing-room, might look reproachfully at the chamberlain who dismissed her.

“You believe that creature loves him?” asked Madame de Montsoreau, as we left the exhibition.

“I believe,” I answered, “that she loves him more sincerely than many women love their husbands.”

My companion struck my arm with her fan.

“There speaks masculine gratitude! Whenever a woman is constant, you compare her to a dog; whenever she is passionate, to a tigress; whenever she is prudent, to a cat. We are permitted no virtue which has not first belonged to the menagerie.”

“You mistake me. I paid the panther a compliment.”

“And insulted woman by implication.”

“The animal,” I continued, “has never read a romance, never danced beneath chandeliers, never learned the utility of tears, and never been told that a broken heart becomes charming when worn with diamonds. Her affection, if she has any, is innocent of literature.”

“Then you do believe in the affection of wild beasts?”

“I once knew a man who had no doubt of it.”

“A naturalist?”

“A grenadier.”

“That is better. Naturalists observe; soldiers experience. Tell me.”

The crowd had carried us from the heated hall into the cold brilliance of a winter afternoon. Carriages rolled along the boulevard; horses steamed in the pale air; vendors cried their wares; and Paris, that vast workshop in which passions are polished, numbered, sold, and forgotten, surrounded us with its industrious tumult. The contrast recalled to me, with singular force, a man whom I had encountered many years before, in a little town of Provence, seated beneath a plane-tree whose leaves were white with dust.

He was then nearly seventy. His right cheek bore a sabre scar; two fingers were wanting from his left hand; his shoulders, still broad, had that forward inclination peculiar to old infantrymen who have carried the knapsack over half the roads of Europe. Yet his eyes remained young. They were black, restless, and occasionally illuminated by a tenderness so unexpected in that weather-beaten face that one looked again, suspecting tears where battle had apparently dried every softer spring.

We had fallen into conversation concerning Egypt. At the name of the desert he became silent.

“You saw the Pyramids?” I asked.

“Bah! Stones,” he replied.

“The Nile?”

“Water.”

“The battles?”

“Smoke.”

“What, then, do you remember?”

He lifted his head and looked at me with almost hostile attention.

“Mignonne.”

I supposed he spoke of some Egyptian woman, and smiled with the discretion by which civilized persons invite indiscretion. The veteran understood me.

“No,” said he. “She had yellow eyes, a black velvet muzzle, and a tail as long as a lancer’s sash.”

Then, after a pause, he added:

“She was a panther.”

Madame de Montsoreau stopped upon the pavement.

“A panther!”

“Precisely.”

“And the soldier loved her?”

“Listen,” said I. “You may afterward decide which of the two possessed the more human heart.”

The veteran had told his history by fragments, with those digressions, contradictions, oaths, and abrupt silences through which old men reveal the truth more surely than an advocate reveals it by order. What follows is his tale, though I have been forced to lend some grammar to his recollections and some sequence to events which, in his mind, had long ceased to obey time.

He was a young conscript from the neighborhood of Tarascon when he went to Egypt—a compact, sun-browned fellow, quick of hand, light of conscience, and endowed with the gaiety of those southern natures which find a jest even beneath the cannon’s mouth. He could mend a shoe, cook a fowl, sing six couplets without knowing the seventh, sleep on stones, and march barefoot rather than confess fatigue. At twenty-two he had already loved three women, fought four duels, and believed himself acquainted with mankind.

The desert corrected that vanity.

During one of those confused movements in which an army extends its limbs beyond the reach of its own intelligence, he was surprised with several comrades by a band of Arab horsemen. Two Frenchmen were killed, one escaped, and the young Provençal, after using his musket as a club, found himself thrown down, bound, and dragged across the sands at the tail of a mare.

His captors carried him southward throughout the day. They treated him neither cruelly nor kindly. To them he was booty—a red-trousered object furnished with buttons, boots, and perhaps a ransom. At sunset they halted near a cluster of rocks. They ate dates, drank sparingly, prayed with their faces toward the horizon, and placed their prisoner between two sleeping men, fastening his wrists with a leather thong and his ankles with a cord.

The Frenchman appeared resigned. He even yawned, complained of the cookery, and allowed his head to fall upon the saddle which served him as a pillow. But while his guards slept he began that patient labor of deliverance which, among soldiers, is less a calculation than an instinct. By rubbing the thong against a sharp buckle, he fretted it strand by strand. Each movement cost him an hour; each hour seemed watched by the stars. Twice an Arab stirred, and the prisoner instantly snored with such sincerity that the man turned over, satisfied.

At last the thong parted.

He remained motionless for several minutes, scarcely trusting his freedom. Then he loosened his ankles, crawled to the stacked weapons, chose a musket, a poignard, powder, balls, a skin of water, and a small sack containing dates and barley meal. The horses, tethered beyond the fire, lifted their heads as he approached. He selected a gray mare whose legs promised speed, muffled the jingle of her bridle with a strip torn from his shirt, mounted without stirrups, and rode away at a walk.

The darkness received him.

When the camp-fire had vanished he urged the mare forward. She sprang into the night with the silent violence of an arrow. Behind him, nothing moved; before him, nothing existed save an immeasurable blackness in which the stars appeared lower and larger than in France. He had no compass. He knew only that the French army lay toward the north, and he guided himself by a star which one of the grenadiers had once pointed out to him between two jokes.

For two hours he heard no pursuit. Then, very far away, there came a cry.

Another answered it.

Presently the desert began to beat like a drum beneath galloping hoofs.

“Good,” he muttered to the mare; “we have company. Show these gentlemen that a Frenchman knows how to borrow a horse.”

The animal stretched herself. Her long neck sank, her flanks labored, and the loose sand flew behind her in pale clouds. The cries grew fainter, then stronger, then fainter once more. Several shots were fired. One ball passed with that sharp, contemptuous whisper which makes a soldier bow after it has gone by. Another struck the sack of meal, scattering a white trail upon the darkness.

Dawn found him alone.

The sun did not rise; it invaded. First a livid band appeared at the edge of the earth, definite as the cut of a sword; then the whole eastern sky turned yellow, and suddenly an enormous disk sprang above the horizon, flooding the plain with a light so naked that the smallest pebble acquired a shadow. Night, which had hidden the desert’s extent, had at least permitted imagination to people it. Day revealed naught.

Sand. Sand before, behind, on every side.

It was an ocean arrested in the moment of its greatest anger, its waves changed to tawny ridges, its foam to salt, its abysses to burning hollows. Here and there black rocks rose like wrecked ships. The heavens, of a hard and pitiless blue, seemed not a roof but an infinite depth into which the world might fall. No tree, no bird, no insect, no blade of grass offered that minute contradiction by which life protests against emptiness.

The mare continued bravely, though foam whitened the bit. At noon she began to stumble. The soldier dismounted and walked beside her, speaking in the coaxing language of the stable.

“Come, my beauty. Courage! There will be oats in Cairo, sugar in Alexandria, and a general’s saddle for you if I live to recommend you.”

The mare turned upon him one dark, exhausted eye. Her breath came in groans. He gave her a mouthful from the water-skin, though each drop was his own blood measured in advance. She drank, shivered, and moved on.

Toward evening she fell upon her knees.

He unloaded her, waited, rubbed her legs, cursed the Arabs, the Republic, Bonaparte, the sand, and himself. The animal struggled upright, made ten uncertain steps, and collapsed upon her side. Her ribs rose twice. A convulsion passed from her shoulders to her hoofs; then her head sank, and the great eye, still reflecting the sun, became a piece of black glass.

The young man stood beside her.

He had seen comrades die without interrupting his dinner. Death in battle has drums, orders, smoke, excitement; it takes a man publicly and leaves the survivors no leisure for tenderness. But this solitary death, purchased by his escape, accused him. He laid his hand upon the mare’s neck and felt the warmth departing beneath the skin.

“Poor comrade,” said he. “I have ridden you into the next world, and you did not even know my name.”

Then he took the remaining provisions and went on foot.

The day declined, but the heat seemed to rise from the ground rather than descend from the sky. His boots filled with sand. His tongue swelled; a bitter crust formed upon his lips. Distances mocked him. A dark line which appeared half a league away retreated as he advanced; a sheet of water trembled suddenly before him, bordered by green trees and white houses, then broke into glittering dust. Once he saw, with perfect distinctness, the Rhône flowing between familiar banks, and his mother beating linen at the water’s edge. He ran toward her, laughing. The river became a slope of salt.

Night restored coolness but not hope. He walked until the stars reeled, then dropped behind a dune and slept. At dawn he awoke with his face in the sand and the certainty that he would die before evening.

Curiously, this conviction calmed him. So long as danger leaves a choice, the soul is tortured by arithmetic; when every road ends at the same grave, a man may whistle as he walks. He counted his cartridges, ate three dates, took one swallow of water, and addressed the sun.

“You will cook me, old copper-pot; but I shall make you work for your breakfast.”

He climbed the highest ridge near him. From its summit he surveyed the immense circle of the horizon. Nothing. He turned slowly, examining each quarter with the trained patience of a sentry.

Then, toward the southwest, he saw a stain of green.

He rubbed his eyes. It remained.

The stain was so slight that it might have been a flaw in the blazing atmosphere; yet as he watched, he distinguished several upright forms, thin and motionless, surmounted by plumes. Palm trees!

He descended the dune at a run, fell, rose, lost sight of the place, found it again from another height, and marched toward it with the frantic economy of strength which hope inspires. The oasis seemed near, then distant, then near again. For two hours it floated before him like a promise made by a liar. At last the sand grew firmer. A dead reed cracked beneath his boot. He smelled damp earth.

That odor nearly made him faint.

The oasis occupied a hollow guarded on one side by a steep mass of granite. About thirty palms surrounded a spring which escaped from beneath the rock, spread into a clear basin, and disappeared again under reeds. A few thorn bushes grew in the shade; fallen dates lay among the stones; tracks of gazelles, jackals, and larger beasts marked the mud.

The soldier flung himself down to drink.

Military discipline, which survives where reason perishes, checked him. He rinsed his mouth, swallowed cautiously, waited, and drank again. Then he plunged his head and arms into the basin, and laughed aloud at the delicious coldness. His laughter returned from the rock with so strange a voice that he stopped. Solitude had answered.

He gathered dates, chose those least spoiled by birds, and explored his kingdom.

Halfway up the granite wall a narrow shelf led to a shallow cavern, or rather a chamber formed by the fall of two enormous blocks. It commanded the spring and could be reached only by a sloping path. The floor was dry. From the entrance he could survey the oasis and a vast tract of desert beyond. The place offered shade, shelter, and a position defensible against men.

Against beasts—that question remained.

He found bones near the back of the cave.

Some were old and white, some recent. One retained shreds of hide.

“Our landlord keeps a table,” he said.

Nevertheless, fatigue overcame prudence. He dragged thorn branches across the entrance, arranged his musket, sabre, and poignard within reach, ate his dates, and slept.

The next day passed almost pleasantly. Having repaired the water-skin and taken stock of his powder, he cut a young palm, stripped its leaves, and fastened his red neckcloth to the top. The improvised flag, planted upon the highest accessible rock, might be seen from afar. French scouts could pass. A caravan might approach. Even Arabs offered a less certain death than thirst.

He cleaned the cave, collected dates, enlarged the barrier, and discovered that the spring contained several small fish. By eventide he had begun to consider himself proprietor of the oasis. Man adapts himself to circumstances with a speed which proves that civilization is chiefly furniture. Give a Parisian a cave at noon, and before sunset he will distinguish the salon from the bedchamber.

The second night was windless. The desert lay beneath the moon like an immense plate of silver, polished here, tarnished there by the shadows of the dunes. The palms scarcely stirred. Their long leaves, touched by the light, resembled green sabres suspended above the water. The soldier watched until his thoughts lost their form in that luminous infinity; then he retired behind his branches and slept.

Near midnight he awoke.

He did not know what had awakened him. No sound followed. Yet his body, warned by that obscure sentinel which watches when the mind is absent, became rigid before his eyes opened.

A warm odor filled the cavern—an odor powerful, musky, and wild.

He heard breathing.

Not the quick rustle of a rat, nor the stealthy panting of a jackal, but a deep, regular respiration which seemed to proceed from some large chest close beside him. He extended his hand imperceptibly toward the musket. His fingers touched the stock.

Then the moon, passing beyond the edge of the rock, sent a pale beam into the cave.

The soldier saw the panther.

She lay scarcely three feet away, stretched upon her side, with her head resting on her forepaws. Her body, supple and prodigious, filled the narrow space. The fur of her belly shone almost white; her back was clothed in gold, marked with black rosettes; the tail curved toward him, ending near his knee. Each breath lifted her flanks, and from time to time the claws emerged softly from their sheaths, scratched the stone, and disappeared.

He felt his hair stir beneath his cap.

The beast had entered despite the thorn barrier, probably while he slept, and had chosen the cave as her habitual refuge. The bones explained themselves. He was not the proprietor. He was the guest—or the supper.

His musket was within reach, but the muzzle pointed away. To turn it he must move both arms. The panther might wake before he could fire; and if the ball failed to pierce the brain or heart, the cavern would become a slaughter-house in which there was room for only one executioner.

His sabre lay beneath his leg. The poignard was at his belt.

“Very well,” he thought. “We will wait. Mademoiselle sleeps; it is impolite to disturb a lady.”

This pleasantry, though addressed only to himself, restored a portion of his courage. He examined her with the desperate attention of a gambler reading the face of the man who holds his fortune. Blood darkened the fur around her muzzle. She had fed. That circumstance seemed favorable. A full stomach, he reasoned, makes even a creditor humane.

Hours passed.

Never had time possessed such weight. Each minute descended upon him like a stone laid upon a tomb. His muscles ached from immobility; sweat gathered at his temples and rolled slowly down his cheeks. Once the panther moved. Her tail struck his boot. He nearly cried out. She opened her eyes.

Two yellow lamps shone in the darkness.

They rested upon him without surprise. The animal yawned, displaying a rose-colored mouth armed with white fangs; then she lowered her head and slept again.

The soldier breathed.

Toward dawn the gray light entered the cave. The desert became visible by degrees: first the black ridge, then the palms, then the water, at last the whole plain, washed of color and waiting for the sun. The panther awoke, rolled upon her back, stretched her paws with a voluptuous slowness, and licked the blood from her chest.

Her toilet lasted several minutes.

She passed a broad red tongue over her forelegs; washed her face; polished each shoulder; paused to bite delicately at some irritation in her fur; then sat upright, composed and magnificent, as though prepared to receive ambassadors.

The soldier had by this time formed a plan. It was absurd, but all plans in the presence of death contain absurdity. He would caress her. Men strike what they fear; children touch it. Perhaps an animal, accustomed to combat, would be disarmed by a gesture which resembled neither flight nor attack.

He slowly raised his hand.

The panther’s eyes followed it.

He stopped.

She did not growl.

His fingers approached her head, traveled through the final inch of air, and touched the fur between her ears.

It was softer than velvet.

The panther closed her eyes.

Encouraged, he stroked her forehead, then the neck, always in the direction of the hair. A tremor ran through the animal. From her chest issued a low vibration, so profound that the stone beneath them seemed to participate in it. The sound enlarged, rolled around the cave, and became like the pedal note of an organ heard beneath a cathedral floor.

“She purrs!” he thought, astonished.

He scratched behind one ear.

The panther thrust her head against his hand with such force that he nearly fell. Then she turned, presented her back, and looked over her shoulder.

“Oh, you are never satisfied!” said he aloud.

At the sound of his voice she ceased purring. Her ears flattened.

The soldier continued rubbing her spine. The ears rose again.

In this manner the treaty was concluded.

When he attempted to leave the cave, the panther preceded him. She descended to the spring, drank, and returned to rub her flank against his legs. Her tail encircled him for an instant like a living rope. The soldier stood ready to draw his poignard; but she passed on, sprang upon a flat stone, and exposed her golden robe to the morning sun.

He could now admire her fully.

She was smaller than a lioness, but more graceful; not built for open war, but for sudden, irresistible murder. Her shoulders moved beneath the skin with the smooth play of machinery concealed under silk. The head was broad, the ears short, the muzzle black and delicately whiskered. On the inner side of one foreleg the fur formed a pale crescent. The rosettes upon her back were unequal, and near the tail a patch resembled a cluster of grapes.

She looked at him; he looked at her.

“You need a name,” he said. “Every respectable woman has one, if only to permit men to complain of her.”

At once there returned to his memory a girl of Avignon, blonde, plump, capricious, and jealous, who had once broken a pitcher upon his head because he danced with her cousin. She had been called Mignonne.

“You are Mignonne,” he told the panther. “But take warning, ma petite blonde: the last lady of that name had claws.”

The animal blinked, stretched, and laid her chin upon her paws.

For the remainder of the day she remained near the oasis. Sometimes she vanished among the rocks and returned noiselessly, appearing where he least expected her. Once she crouched beside the spring and watched the small fish with an attention almost comic. Again she pursued her own tail, wheeling with the intoxicated gravity of a dervish. Toward evening she climbed the rock, surveyed the desert, and uttered a cry.

That cry had nothing in common with the roar heard in menageries. It began as a hoarse cough, deepened into a savage lament, and traveled across the plain until distance made it supernatural. The palms seemed to listen. The sunset itself, crimson and purple above the black dunes, acquired something bloody from the sound.

The soldier’s tenderness cooled.

“She calls her husband,” he thought. “If monsieur arrives, the household will be crowded.”

He resolved to kill her while she slept.

All night the resolution occupied him. His hand closed repeatedly upon the poignard. He calculated the exact place where the blade must enter behind the shoulder. Yet each time he raised himself, Mignonne opened an eye, not suspiciously, but with a confidence which embarrassed him. Once she laid a paw upon his arm. The claws remained hidden. Its weight was heavy, warm, almost affectionate.

“Tomorrow,” he whispered.

Tomorrow came, and he did not kill her.

Perhaps gratitude restrained him, for her presence kept jackals away; perhaps curiosity, because the animal was a perpetual spectacle; perhaps loneliness, which, more powerful than fear, already demanded that this living creature should remain. A man abandoned among crowds becomes a misanthrope; abandoned among sands, he embraces the first being that returns his gaze.

The panther went hunting before noon. She disappeared toward the northern rocks and returned at sunset with blood upon her muzzle, her belly full, her movements languid. The soldier received her as a wife receives a husband who has dined elsewhere.

“So,” he said, “Mademoiselle has eaten without inviting me. Arabs, I hope? You will find Frenchmen less digestible.”

Mignonne rubbed her head under his arm.

On the third morning he discovered that she understood certain inflections of his voice. A sharp command made her stop; a coaxing tone brought her nearer. She soon responded to her name, though whether she recognized the word or merely the affection contained in it he could not determine. He called her from the spring; she came. He ordered her from the cave while he arranged his bed of leaves; she withdrew, sat at the entrance, and watched him with the offended dignity of a sultana expelled from her apartments by a servant.

Their amusements developed naturally. He rolled dates across the sand, and she struck them aside with her paw. He dangled his neckcloth from a branch, and she leaped for it, sometimes twelve feet at a bound. When he hid behind a palm, she stalked him, belly close to the ground, the tip of her tail twitching; then sprang, stopping so near that her whiskers brushed his cheek. At these moments he laughed, but his laughter was not entirely free. The spectacle of death at play has a peculiar gaiety.

He studied her eyes. Their pupils widened with pleasure, contracted with anger, clouded with fatigue. Her tail spoke even more plainly. It questioned, threatened, mocked, complained, and forgave. A certain rapid beat against the ground warned him to be prudent; a slow curve signified contentment. When she was displeased, the skin above her nose wrinkled before any sound escaped her throat.

Mignonne, in turn, studied him.

She watched him clean his musket, prepare food, climb to the signal flag, and scan the horizon. If he remained long upon the rock, she followed and sat behind him. If he descended without speaking to her, she pushed against his knees. Once, when he sang a camp song, she listened through two verses, then gave a short cough and walked away.

“You have taste,” he said. “The sergeant also disliked that song.”

Day succeeded day. No French patrol appeared. No caravan crossed the plain. The red neckcloth hung motionless above the rock, a minute human stain between the blue sky and the yellow earth.

Yet despair no longer returned with its first violence. Before Mignonne, the desert had been emptiness; with her, it became a country. Her departures gave direction to distance; her returns divided time. The ledge where she slept, the stone upon which she sharpened her claws, the path by which she descended to drink—all acquired history. Even silence changed. It was no longer the silence of annihilation, but the repose of two creatures who understood that speech was unnecessary.

The soldier began to perceive beauties which terror had concealed from him.

At sunrise the dunes wore rose and violet shadows finer than the colors upon a dove’s breast. At noon the air trembled above the plain, building palaces of crystal which collapsed and rose elsewhere. In eventide the granite cliffs became red fortresses; the palms spread black lace against a sky of greenish gold; and the first star appeared in the spring before it appeared in heaven. Sometimes a wind came from the south, hot as the breath of a furnace, driving a thin veil of sand before it. Sometimes the night brought odors from plants flowering leagues away—delicate messages from invisible gardens.

Infinity, which had at first crushed him, now opened around his soul. There are moments when solitude reveals to the simplest man thoughts which philosophers seek by extinguishing lamps and filling books. The soldier, who could scarcely write his name without placing the letters under military command, felt that this enormous world did not require mankind in order to exist. The stars had shone before the first bivouac fire; the desert would retain no print of Napoleon’s armies; the panther possessed joys, fears, and perhaps memories which no human language would ever enter.

These reflections did not prevent him from quarreling with Mignonne.

One afternoon she refused to let him approach the spring. She stood across the path, ears flattened, and gave a warning growl whenever he advanced.

“Ah! You wish to keep the water?” he exclaimed. “This is tyranny. We made no such article in our treaty.”

He moved to the left. She barred him.

He moved to the right. She sprang before him.

Angered, he seized a dry branch and struck the sand.

“Out of the way!”

Mignonne crouched. For an instant the ancient wilderness blazed in her eyes. He saw the muscles gather beneath her shoulders and understood that another gesture might end their friendship—and him.

Then the ground near the spring moved.

A patch of pale sand trembled like jelly. A lizard attempting to cross it sank, struggled, and vanished. The wind had covered a bed of treacherous mud with dust.

The soldier lowered his branch.

“So that was it,” he said gently. “You are wiser than your master.”

He went around by the rocks. Mignonne followed, purring, and accepted his apologies with the generosity of one who knows herself to have been right.

Several days later she saved him more directly.

He had wandered beyond the palms in pursuit of a flock of birds which he hoped to shoot. Seeing them settle near a low hollow, he descended without observing that the sand changed color beneath his feet. At the third step he sank to the ankle; at the fourth, to the knee.

He tried to withdraw his leg. The surface yielded with a soft sucking noise.

Quicksand.

He threw himself backward, but the crust broke under his weight. In a few seconds he was buried to the thighs. Every violent movement drew him lower. The musket, flung aside, lay beyond reach.

Mignonne appeared at the rim of the hollow.

“Go away!” he shouted. “You will be caught too.”

The panther paced along the edge, growling. Then she descended upon firmer ground, approached until her paws began to sink, and stopped. The soldier leaned toward her and grasped her tail.

She whirled with a scream, showing her teeth.

“Pull, Mignonne! Pull, ma petite maîtresse!”

Whether she understood his words, his peril, or merely the pain in her tail, she bounded forward. His body moved several inches. He clung desperately. She bounded again, claws tearing furrows in the sand. He emerged to the knees, then to the ankles, and at last rolled upon solid ground.

For some moments he could do nothing but breathe. Mignonne stood apart, lashing her offended tail.

He crawled toward her.

“You may eat me when you please,” he said. “I owe you the right.”

She allowed him to embrace her neck. Her fur smelled of sun and wild herbs. He pressed his face against it, laughing and weeping with that entire surrender of emotion which men permit themselves only when no other man is present.

After that, the idea of killing her seemed infamous.

Their intimacy became almost domestic. He removed thorns from her paw; she licked the salt sweat from his hands. He counted her whiskers, examined her teeth, and once, emboldened by success, placed his cap upon her head. Mignonne endured it for three seconds, then shook it off and struck it into the spring.

“Coquette!” he cried. “You will wear nothing which does not become you.”

She had jealous moods. A small desert fox began to visit the oasis at dusk, tempted by scraps of food. The soldier amused himself by tossing it dates. Mignonne watched from the rock, apparently asleep. On the third evening she descended, caught the fox with one paw, and killed it before he could rise.

He reproached her sternly.

She withdrew to the far end of the cave and refused his caresses. Her back remained turned, but one ear listened. At last he said, “Come, Mignonne,” in the voice he used when sharing food. She approached slowly, rubbed against him, and placed her paw upon the dead fox as though maintaining her case.

“You are exactly like the blonde at Avignon,” he told her. “She also murdered everything I admired, though she used her tongue.”

At other times Mignonne displayed fears which astonished him. Thunder, rolling far beyond the horizon, made her restless. She disliked the smoke of his little fire and sneezed with an expression of personal insult. A beetle crawling over her flank caused her to leap upright and examine the ground suspiciously. Thus the terrible and the ridiculous met in her, as in all sovereigns.

Nearly three weeks had passed since his escape. The provisions taken from the Arabs were gone, his clothing was torn, and his face had become so dark and thin that he scarcely recognized its reflection in the spring. He lived upon dates, roots, fish, and occasionally a bird. Mignonne hunted for herself. Once she brought a young gazelle to the oasis and, instead of devouring it at once, dropped it near him.

The gazelle was dead. He cut meat from it, cooked his portion, and pushed the remainder toward her.

“Mess is served, Mademoiselle.”

The panther ate beside him. The sun went down while man and beast shared their banquet beneath the palms; and had some traveler beheld them from afar, he might have taken the scene for a fable sculptured upon an Egyptian tomb.

Yet the soldier’s mind, nourished again by meat, returned to France. He dreamed of white roads bordered by poplars, of vineyards, bells, kitchens, quarrels, human faces. He imagined the camp: the clatter of tin plates, the smell of tobacco, the corporal’s curses, the laughter after roll-call. Longing entered him like a sickness.

Each morning he climbed to the flag.

Each morning the horizon remained empty.

Mignonne seemed to dislike these vigils. She came between him and the distance, pushed her head under his hand, or caught at his sash. One day she seized the end of the red neckcloth which served as a signal and pulled until the knot loosened.

“Leave that!” he cried, striking her lightly across the muzzle.

She recoiled.

The change was instantaneous and dreadful. Her body lowered; her ears vanished against her skull; a growl vibrated in her throat. The soldier gripped his poignard.

For several seconds they faced one another—civilization and the wilderness, each armed, each offended, each awaiting the other’s crime.

Then Mignonne turned away.

She descended slowly, without looking back, and disappeared among the rocks. She did not return at sunset.

The cave seemed enormous. The soldier arranged the branches, cleaned his weapon, sang, spoke aloud, and listened after every sentence. Night fell. Still she did not come.

He discovered then how completely she had occupied his existence. Every shadow took her form and mocked him. The silence, formerly divine, became hostile again. He feared that she had abandoned him; feared that she had been wounded; feared, with a shame he would not have confessed, that she would return as an enemy.

Near dawn he heard a soft step.

Mignonne entered. Blood stained her shoulder, where some horn or claw had opened the skin. She walked past him and lay down.

The soldier approached, murmuring endearments. She permitted him to wash the wound. When the cold water touched it she trembled, but did not move. He tore a strip from his shirt and bound her shoulder, though the bandage slipped almost immediately from the sleek fur.

“Peace?” he asked.

She licked his wrist.

Peace.

Two days afterward the weather changed. A veil covered the sky, and the horizon vanished in a tawny mist. The palms bent northward. Sand whispered along the ground in swift, narrow streams.

The soldier secured his flag and retreated to the cave. Mignonne paced uneasily. By noon the wind howled among the rocks; the oasis disappeared behind a moving wall of dust; the daylight became copper-colored, then brown. Sand entered through every crevice, stung the skin, filled the mouth, and buried the spring beneath a shifting sheet.

Man and panther crouched together in the deepest part of the cavern. Mignonne pressed so close that he felt the rapid beating of her heart. Her eyes, ordinarily luminous, were dull with fear.

“Courage, ma petite blonde,” he said. “The cannon makes more noise and is less clean, but I have slept beneath it.”

He covered her head with his jacket. She remained still, breathing against his breast, while the tempest labored outside like an army destroying a city.

Toward evening the wind ceased as suddenly as it had risen.

They emerged.

The oasis was transformed. One palm had fallen. Reeds lay buried; dunes had changed their shape; the path to the spring no longer existed. But the red signal, torn nearly to rags, still fluttered from the rock.

The soldier climbed to restore it. In the newly cleansed air he could see farther than before. The desert rolled away beneath the setting sun, every ridge edged with flame.

Something flashed on the northern horizon.

He stared.

Again—a minute spark.

Metal reflecting light.

He fired his musket.

The report crossed the plain and returned faintly from the rocks. Mignonne bounded to his side, alarmed. He reloaded and fired again.

No answer came.

“It may be cavalry,” he said. “It may be Arabs. It may be the devil wearing a cuirass. Whoever it is, we shall receive him politely.”

All night he watched. Nothing appeared. At dawn the horizon was once more empty, and he wondered whether the flash had existed only in his desire.

That morning Mignonne was unusually playful. She followed him around the spring, struck at his sash, and sprang upon the rocks above him. Perhaps the storm had excited her; perhaps she sensed his agitation. Animals, living nearer than we to the secret movements of the body, often read the heart before it has translated itself into thought.

The soldier sat beneath a palm and began repairing his sandal. Mignonne lay near him, rolling in the sand. Presently she rose, came behind him, and rested her head upon his shoulder.

“You are heavy, Madame,” he said.

She purred.

He pushed her muzzle aside and resumed his work. She returned, rubbed her cheek against his neck, and caught the cloth of his trousers with her teeth.

“Enough!”

He pulled away.

The panther, excited by the game, sprang forward and closed her jaws gently around his leg, just below the knee.

There was no pain—only pressure.

But in that instant all the tenderness of three weeks disappeared beneath one primitive terror. He saw not Mignonne, companion, savior, jealous mistress, but the carnivorous beast; not play, but attack; not affection, but appetite. The warnings accumulated in his memory—the bones in the cave, the bloody muzzle, the murdered fox, the yellow eyes opening at midnight. Fear, which had slept beneath love without dying, awoke armed.

He drew his poignard and struck.

The blade entered the throat.

Mignonne released him. She leaped backward, gave a cry unlike any sound he had heard from her, and fell upon her side.

Blood poured across the pale sand.

The soldier remained kneeling, the poignard in his hand. The entire action had occupied less than a second. He stared first at the blade, then at his uninjured leg, where the cloth bore only the damp print of her mouth.

“Mignonne?”

The panther tried to rise. Her forepaws moved, but the hindquarters failed her. She looked at him.

There was pain in her eyes, and astonishment; but no anger.

He crawled to her, pressed both hands against the wound, called her name, cursed himself, promised absurd remedies, begged forgiveness as though she could grant it. The blood escaped between his fingers. Mignonne’s great head sank upon his knee. Her tail moved once across the sand. She licked his hand—perhaps by habit, perhaps in farewell.

Then her eyes remained open without seeing him.

At that moment a musket-shot sounded beyond the oasis.

Voices followed.

French voices.

A patrol had seen the rag of his signal after the storm and marched toward the rocks. The soldiers entered among the palms cautiously, expecting Arabs. They found the Provençal seated beneath the fallen tree, drenched in blood, holding the dead panther’s head against his breast.

“Are you wounded?” cried the sergeant.

He did not answer.

“Who killed the beast?”

“I did.”

The men congratulated him. One examined the panther’s teeth; another praised the beauty of the skin; a third proposed that they flay her before the heat spoiled it.

The young soldier sprang up with such a face that they recoiled.

“Touch her,” he said, “and I will kill you.”

They thought the sun had maddened him. Perhaps it had. He would permit no one to approach until the officer gave his word that the body should remain entire. Before leaving, he dug a grave beneath the palm. The sand collapsed continually, as though the desert refused to hold memory; nevertheless he dug until his hands bled.

He placed Mignonne in the hollow, wrapped in the remnant of his jacket.

When the first sand fell upon her golden fur, he fainted.

Here the old veteran had stopped. Beneath the plane-tree in Provence, village sounds came to us softened by the hot afternoon: a cart creaked, cicadas cried among the branches, and from a neighboring house a woman called to a child. The old man rubbed his mutilated hand across his brow.

“You have seen much since then,” I said after a long silence.

He shrugged.

“I saw men freeze while standing in Russia. I saw horses eat one another. I saw comrades crushed by cannon, villages burned, kings run away, and brave fellows shot for stealing a loaf. At Waterloo the mud was red enough to plant vineyards in. All that”—he waved his hand—“all that is war. It belongs to men, and men explain everything badly but easily. Orders, ambition, hunger, glory—choose a word.”

“And the panther?”

“That was different.”

“Did she intend to hurt you?”

“No.”

“You are certain?”

“I have had sixty years to feel her teeth. She was playing.”

He looked toward the dusty road, though it was plain that he saw another country.

“In battle,” he continued, “you kill a man because he wears another coat. He would kill you for the same reason, and afterward, if both survive, you may drink together. But she trusted me. I struck because, for one second, I remembered that she was a beast and forgot that I was her friend.”

He rose with difficulty and took several steps. Then he turned.

“You ask what the desert was like? Before her, it was emptiness. With her, it was a world. After her—”

He removed his cap.

“It was God without mankind.”

Madame de Montsoreau had ceased walking. Around us Paris continued its uproar; the pavements trembled beneath wheels, whips cracked, bells rang, and a newspaper boy proclaimed some new quarrel of nations. She looked back toward the exhibition hall, where the trainer was doubtless compelling his panther to repeat her submission for another audience.

“And do you believe,” she asked softly, “that the animal forgave him?”

“My dear friend,” I replied, “forgiveness is the name we give to a love which survives our knowledge of an injury. Her last look contained no accusation. Whether that was forgiveness or merely the innocence of a creature incapable of hatred, I cannot say.”

“Then the beast was nobler than the man.”

“At that moment.”

She closed her fan.

“And at most moments?”

I offered her my arm.

“At most moments,” said I, “man has the advantage of speech, by which he proves whatever pleases him.”

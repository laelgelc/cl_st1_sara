### Ti Démon’s Last Journey

The road through the pine hills was scarcely a road at all, being two pale tracks with a green ridge between, and so thickly cushioned with needles that Ti Démon’s small hoofs made no sound upon it. Here and there the morning sun descended in long yellow bars; ants crossed importantly; a mockingbird repeated, with additions, the complaints of every feathered neighbor. The forest was full of those drowsy noises which persuade mankind that nature is at peace, though Ti Démon knew better.

He carried Herminia Broussard, two baskets of eggs, a sack of butter-beans, six cucumbers, three young cabbages, and more grievances than could conveniently be counted.

Herminia wore a red calico dress and a white sunbonnet. Her brown hands were strong, her legs were sturdy as steel, and her black eyes, when anything pleased her, were as quick as a squirrel’s. That morning they looked often down the road, for the road led to Belle-Rivière, the planter Ducros’s summer house; and Prospere Labatier, who was staying there, had once told Herminia at the church fair that red became her.

A foolish girl might remember such a remark for a week. Herminia had remembered it since Easter.

Ti Démon remembered other things. He remembered a country where the sky was larger, where grass rolled farther than sight, and where a lean man called Buck Talley had taught him several expressions unsuitable for the pine woods of Louisiana. His coat, once a respectable bay, had faded in patches; his mane stood out like an old broom; and his tail, through age and accident, had become no more than a courteous suggestion. Yet he considered himself superior to every mule in the parish, especially Raymond Cormier’s gray mule, whose intelligence had been praised in his hearing.

“Mules!” he thought. “A thousand dollars to a doughnut that long-eared washboard couldn’t find Texas with a compass.”

Herminia shifted in the saddle.

“Allons, Ti Démon. We late already.”

He knew they were late. He knew also that the left forefoot, which had troubled him during damp weather, was beginning to burn. He shortened his step. Herminia urged him with her heel.

“Come, grosse bête. Don’ make yo’ foolishness to-day.”

Ti Démon’s indignation carried him twenty yards. Then a sharp pain struck upward from the hoof to the shoulder, and he stopped.

Herminia dismounted and lifted the foot. There was no stone, no thorn, nothing which she could see. She led him a little way. He limped piteously.

“Mais w’at I goin’ do wit’ you?”

Ti Démon lowered his head, not without satisfaction. Sympathy, though delayed, was his right.

They were four miles from home and less than two from Belle-Rivière. Herminia looked toward the distant opening in the pines. Her eggs must be sold. The cucumbers would not improve by returning to the garden. Moreover, Prospere might ride along that road. He might find her walking beneath a heavy basket, inquire tenderly into her misfortune, and perhaps insist upon carrying her to the house before him on his spirited chestnut.

This picture, once admitted, possessed every appearance of probability.

She tied Ti Démon to a young pine, loosened the girth, set the baskets beneath a bush, and divided the produce into such burdens as she could carry. The eggs she balanced upon one arm, the vegetables upon the other.

“Stay good, Ti Démon. I come back fo’ you. Don’ you pull an’ don’ you kick.”

He watched her red dress flash between the trunks and disappear.

“Stay good!” he repeated inwardly. “Whole shootin’ match breaks down on my back, then she tells me stay good.”

Nevertheless, he remained. The foot throbbed less after a while. A patch of sunlight moved from his shoulder to the pine roots. Flies settled upon him. He switched the remnant of his tail with philosophic resignation and discovered that the knot securing his rein had been tied by a young woman whose thoughts were elsewhere.

At Belle-Rivière, Herminia entered by the kitchen yard. There were carriages beneath the live-oaks, children in white dresses on the gallery, and gentlemen sitting with their coats unbuttoned while a servant brought iced drinks. Her red calico, so brave among the pines, became suddenly too red. Her sunbonnet seemed larger than a sail.

Madame Ducros’s housekeeper bought the eggs after examining each one against the light. She objected that the cabbages were loose, that the beans were stringy, and that the cucumbers had lain too long upon the vine. Herminia received three dimes less than she had counted upon.

While she waited at the kitchen door, laughter came from the gallery. Prospere Labatier stood there in a light coat, talking to a tall young lady whose dress was blue and narrow and who held a little yellow fan. Prospere looked down, saw Herminia, and lifted his hat.

“Bonjou’, Herminia. You walk all dis way?”

“My ho’se, he hurt his foot,” she answered.

“That bad,” he said. Then the tall lady asked him something, and he turned from the railing before Herminia could explain where Ti Démon stood or how heavy the empty baskets would feel on the road home.

It was nothing. A greeting was a greeting. Yet when she went out by the kitchen yard, the red dress burned upon her like a public confession.

She had nearly reached the first pines when she heard hoofs behind her. Her heart rose in one swift, disobedient motion. She stepped aside and arranged her sunbonnet.

The rider was old Monsieur Ducros’s overseer, who passed without seeing her.

Herminia continued on foot.

Ti Démon, meanwhile, had come to the conclusion that he was cured. Not entirely cured, perhaps, but sufficiently recovered to go home, where cotton seed, corn, and shade could be obtained without conversation. He drew back once. The knot slipped. He drew back again, and the rein came free.

The saddle annoyed him. He shook himself violently. It slid sidewise. He kicked, twisted, and backed into the pine, whereupon the saddle slipped under his belly and remained there, held by the loosened girth. One stirrup struck his knees at every step.

“A fine arrangement,” he thought. “Buck Talley would’ve shot the man that rigged a horse this way.”

He started toward home, limping only when he remembered to do so.

The Broussard cabin stood beyond a field of corn, with a vegetable patch behind it and pumpkins spreading broad leaves between the tobacco rows. No one was there. Herminia’s mother had gone to visit a sick cousin, and her two brothers were cutting timber south of the bayou. Ti Démon pushed the gate, found it fastened, and regarded the empty trough with bitterness.

From Solistan Guidry’s place, across the narrow lane, came the agreeable smell of roasting corn. Ti Démon passed through a break in the fence and entered Solistan’s yard with the saddle still beneath him.

Solistan was repairing a plow. He wore a blue checked shirt, and his boots were heavy with black earth. At sight of the pony he dropped his wrench.

“Ti Démon! Bon Dieu—w’ere Herminia?”

Ti Démon approached the corn barrel.

Solistan saw the hanging saddle, the broken sprigs caught in the bridle, and the dark stain of sweat upon the pony’s back. In one instant he beheld Herminia thrown beneath a pine, Herminia lying senseless in a ditch, Herminia calling while no one heard.

He seized Ti Démon by the bridle.

“W’ere you lef’ her, vieille bête?”

Ti Démon pulled toward the barrel.

“Look at him,” Solistan cried. “He don’ care!”

This accusation was unjust. Ti Démon cared deeply about the corn.

Solistan stripped away the saddle, ran for his own horse, and mounted without changing his earth-heavy boots. He rode first toward the Broussard garden, then toward the bayou, then out upon the Belle-Rivière road. Every scrap of red among the trees stopped his heart. A sumac leaf became her dress; a pale stump became her face.

Until that hour, Solistan had believed his feeling for Herminia to be no more than a neighborly concern, strengthened by the pleasant way she laughed when he passed and weakened by her habit of mentioning Prospere Labatier. But the menace of some dreadful and unknown fate removed him beyond further hesitancy. If Herminia were hurt, all the tobacco in his field might wither. If she were dead, the field itself could sink into the bayou.

He had reached this conclusion when he saw her walking toward him, carrying two empty baskets.

“Herminia!”

She stopped. Solistan reined so sharply that his horse sat back upon its haunches.

“You hurt?”

“No.”

“You fall?”

“I walk.”

“W’ere Ti Démon?”

“Tied yonder. His foot, it hurt him.”

“He ain’t tied. He come home wit’ de saddle hangin’ under like he been drag t’rough all de pine hill.”

Herminia’s face changed. “Ti Démon come home?”

“He come by me. I t’ought—” Solistan swallowed. “I t’ought you was kill.”

Her humiliation at Belle-Rivière, which she had borne with some dignity, yielded suddenly to alarm.

“Maybe he hurt bad! Maybe de girth cut him! Oh, Solistan, I shouldn’ lef’ him.”

“He ain’t hurt bad enough fo’ keep away from my corn. Come. I take you.”

He dismounted and helped her into the saddle. There was room before him, but propriety and embarrassment placed her sideways behind. She held the back of the saddle at first. When the horse began to trot, she caught Solistan around the waist.

Prospere Labatier, had he witnessed the return, might have supposed them an established couple. No one witnessed it except two mockingbirds and a yellow dog, and these possessed no social influence.

Ti Démon stood beneath Solistan’s shed, eating corn with such energy that his recent martyrdom seemed doubtful.

“You!” Herminia cried, running to him. “Bad ole rascal! I been scare fo’ you.”

Ti Démon received the reproach calmly. She examined his foot, his knees, his back, and the place where the girth had rubbed away a little hair. Then she put both arms around his neck.

The pony ceased chewing. There were attentions which embarrassed a horse of experience, yet he did not move.

Solistan leaned against the post.

“Herminia, dat ho’se too ole fo’ carry you all over. Some day he break down fo’ true.”

“He been good to me.”

“I got de bay mare. She safe. She got a mouth soft like butter, an’ she don’ scare from wagon nor gun. You take her.”

“I can’t take yo’ mare.”

“You can take her if you take me wit’ her.”

Herminia released Ti Démon and turned. Solistan studied the packed earth between his boots.

“W’at you mean?”

“I mean de mare mine. If you marry me, she be yourn. Me, too.”

The proposal lacked those decorations which Herminia had sometimes associated with marriage. There was no gallery, no moon, no gentleman in a light coat. Solistan had mud on his boots, a tear in his sleeve, and fear still visible in his face. He looked up at last, and she understood that he had ridden through the woods believing her dead.

Her own eyes softened, but coquetry, being hardier than romance, did not immediately expire.

“I got to t’ink.”

“Take all de time,” he said. “Only don’ take Prospere.”

She gave him a quick glance. “Nobody ax you ’bout Prospere.”

“No. He don’ ax you neither.”

This was cruel enough to be accurate. Herminia lifted her chin and walked home.

Ti Démon watched her go. “Smooth work,” he thought. “A thousand dollars to a doughnut she makes him sweat till Christmas.”

She made Solistan wait three weeks.

During those weeks he repaired the Broussard fence without invitation, carried two sacks of meal from the landing, and sent over a watermelon of extraordinary size. Herminia accepted these proofs without committing herself. At the end of the third week, she came to Solistan’s field while he was hoeing between the tobacco plants.

“I t’ink ’bout dat mare,” she said.

Solistan rested upon his hoe. “Only de mare?”

“Maybe de man, little bit.”

They were married in October, after the corn had been gathered and the pumpkins lay yellow beneath the shed. At the wedding feast there were chickens, rice, couche-couche, preserves, coffee, and a fiddler who played until the youngest children slept on folded quilts. Prospere Labatier sent no message and was not missed.

Ti Démon spent the evening tied near the crib. Several persons brought him crusts and praised his part in the courtship. He had no objection to praise, but resented the assertion, made repeatedly by Raymond Cormier, that any mule would have shown equal intelligence.

“Any mule!” Ti Démon thought. “That gray scarecrow of yours would’ve gone to sleep in the road and spoiled the whole shootin’ match.”

After the marriage, Ti Démon’s circumstances improved. Solistan kept his promise. The old pony was required to do almost nothing. He grazed in a small pasture, slept under a sound roof, and received cotton seed on cold mornings. Herminia rode the bay mare to market and returned without incident. Sometimes she brought Ti Démon an apple and rubbed the white star upon his forehead.

He ought to have been happy.

But on the day after his arrival in the corn yard, when Solistan had believed Herminia injured, Ti Démon had overheard a conversation which he could not dismiss. Old Raymond, examining the lame foot, had said, “Dat pony ain’t wuth de feed no mo’.”

Solistan, laughing in his relief, had answered, “Maybe I shoot him an’ save de corn.”

Herminia had cried, “You do dat, I shoot you!”

Everyone laughed. Everyone except Ti Démon.

A horse understands a laugh less reliably than a word. The word remained.

Shoot him.

Through the winter, Ti Démon watched Solistan. The young man brought feed, mended the shed, and spoke kindly enough; yet this conduct could be explained as the deceitful preparation of a man awaiting a convenient hour. When Solistan cleaned his gun before the duck season, Ti Démon stood trembling behind the fence.

“Not yet,” he thought. “He’s fattenin’ me first. That’s their game.”

Spring came fragrant with wet earth. Corn showed green in the furrows, mockingbirds quarreled in the mulberries, and Herminia planted beans beside the cabin. Ti Démon grew thinner. His old foot ached. One afternoon Solistan remarked that the pony might not last another winter.

That settled the matter.

“No man puts daylight through Ti Démon,” the pony resolved. “I’m pullin’ my freight.”

The west had remained in his memory, confused but splendid. Somewhere beyond the pine hills, beyond fields and bayous and towns without number, lay the Indian Nation where he had been foaled. He remembered wide grass, cold stars, and Buck Talley singing beside a fire. Age had polished the memory until no hunger, rope, storm, or whip remained in it.

That night the gate was carelessly fastened.

Ti Démon lifted the latch with his nose and walked out.

In the morning Herminia found the pasture empty.

They searched the neighborhood, then the parish road. Solistan rode south to the bayou and north as far as the rail station. Notices were left at stores and cotton gins: OLD BAY PONY, WHITE STAR, SCANT TAIL, ANSWERS TO TI DÉMON. For several days travelers reported seeing him—near a cane field, beside a creek, upon the western road—but each report came too late.

“He goin’ back where he come from,” old Raymond said.

Herminia pressed her lips together. “He too ole.”

“Old don’ stop some folks.”

Summer passed. The empty shed remained. Solistan proposed using it for tools, but Herminia said no, and he understood.

Nearly a year later, a cattle buyer from Texas stopped at their farm. Hearing the pony’s description, he remembered a small dead horse found beside a road west of Nacogdoches, the saddle marks long faded from his back and a white star upon his forehead. There had been no wound. The horse had simply lain down beneath an oak and failed to rise.

The Indian Nation was still a great distance away.

Herminia went to the shed after the cattle buyer had departed. In a corner hung Ti Démon’s old bridle, stiff with dust. She took it down and wiped the bit with her apron.

Solistan stood at the door.

“I never would shot him,” he said softly. “You know dat, Herminia.”

“I know.”

“Maybe he know, too.”

She shook her head. “No. Ti Démon, he remember every bad word anybody say. Good word, sometime he forget.”

This judgment was so exact that, had the pony heard it, he would have considered himself insulted.

Herminia hung the bridle again.

“He was a good ho’se,” she said, “an’ faithful.”

Solistan made no correction. Outside, the bay mare called from the pasture, and beyond the corn the pine woods held the last gold of evening. Herminia remained a moment in the empty shed, remembering the noiseless road, the red dress, the eggs, the foolish hopes of a younger girl, and the little old pony who had carried them all without complaint—or, at least, without any complaint that human ears could understand.

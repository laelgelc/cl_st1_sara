# THE SECOND GRAND CONGRESS OF UNIVERSAL SCIENCE

## From Our Own Commissioner

The Public will learn with emotions which we shall not attempt to regulate, because we could not do so without calling in the police, that the Grand Amalgamated Association for the Advancement, Retardation, Diffusion, Concentration, Application, Misapplication, and General Encouragement of Universal Science has selected Oldcastle as the scene of its second annual meeting.

When we mention that the first meeting was held last year at Newborough, and that the inhabitants of that town have not yet entirely recovered from it, nothing further need be said respecting the magnitude of the present occasion.

Nevertheless, something further shall be said.

It became the duty of this Journal, which has never shrunk from any duty unless the weather was wet, the road inconvenient, or the remuneration inadequate, to provide an account of the proceedings distinguished alike by courage, punctuality, impartiality, exactness, literary elegance, scientific discernment, and that intimate acquaintance with every branch of human knowledge which can only be acquired by never allowing ignorance to interfere with confidence. For this purpose we selected a Commissioner whose powers of observation are so remarkable that he has frequently observed circumstances which did not occur, and whose memory is of that superior order which retains with singular fidelity whatever it finds most convenient.

Our Commissioner left London yesterday by the *Royal Porpoise* steam-packet. We publish his correspondence entire. We have suppressed nothing except a private demand for money, three complaints concerning his cabin, an insinuation against the steward, and a passage which our legal adviser, after turning pale, requested us to burn.

## THE DEPARTURE

### London Wharf, Wednesday, Half-past Five in the Morning

I am here.

The words are few, but they contain a history.

Through the exertions of a driver who mistook the wharf twice, contradicted me four times, and demanded eighteenpence beyond his lawful fare, I have reached the vessel upon whose deck so many hopes, carpet-bags, scientific instruments, sandwiches, and men of European reputation are at this moment collected.

The morning is cold. A yellow vapour hangs over the river. Through it the masts of shipping appear like the blackened bones of extinct animals—an observation which I communicate for the benefit of the Geological Section. The water is of a deep brown colour, and bears upon its surface chips, corks, cabbage-leaves, and other evidences of our national prosperity.

At the extreme edge of the wharf lies an object wrapped in sacking.

Its shape is mysterious.

It may be a corpse. It may be a machine. It may be the luggage of Professor Pumpkincrust, whose investigations into the probable square-ness of circles have created so much alarm in continental universities. A boy has kicked it. I await developments.

### Twenty Minutes to Six

The object is cheese.

It was dropped by a porter belonging to the *Royal Porpoise*, who has now placed it upon a trunk labelled “APPARATUS—WITH GREAT CARE.” I mentioned the circumstance to the owner of the trunk, a small gentleman in spectacles. He replied that it did not matter, as the apparatus was only his shirts.

This coolness, under circumstances calculated to shake the strongest intellect, marks the true philosopher.

The vessel herself is a noble specimen of naval architecture. Her funnel leans slightly to the left; her paddle-box on the right has recently been painted; the intermediate portions preserve a dignified neutrality. She is commanded by Captain Gulp, a man of few words, nearly all of which are addressed to the engineer and unfit for publication.

The captain has already fallen over a coil of rope.

### Six o’Clock

We have not started.

The scene baffles description; I shall therefore describe it with particular minuteness.

Professors hurry to and fro without knowing why. Porters carry boxes from the wharf to the vessel, and, discovering mistakes, carry them back again. Ladies inquire after their husbands. Husbands avoid being found. A dog belonging to Doctor Splayfoot has bitten the second steward, who maintains that the animal ought to be charged as a passenger. Doctor Splayfoot replies that no fare can be demanded from a creature travelling in the interests of Comparative Anatomy. The dog supports this view by biting the first steward.

A tall, loose-jointed man, wearing a blue coat, a white comforter, and one boot cleaner than the other, has attracted universal attention by carrying beneath his arm a brass tube three feet long. He is Mr. Septimus Slug, honorary corresponding member of seventeen societies, including two which have ceased to exist and one which has not yet been formed. He refuses to disclose the contents of the tube. There is a rumour that it contains a newly discovered explosive capable of removing mountains, governments, and small warts.

I shall keep near Mr. Slug.

### Ten Minutes Past Six

The rope has been cast off, but remains attached to the wharf.

### A Quarter Past Six

The rope has been cast off again.

We move! London recedes! The wharf dwindles! A porter runs beside us, crying that somebody has taken his basket! The basket is discovered round his own neck! He stops! The metropolis fades from view!

No, it does not.

We have struck a barge.

### Half-past Six

We are now actually proceeding down the river, bearing with us an intellectual freight such as has seldom floated upon any water, and never, I should hope, upon this particular portion of it.

I have commenced a survey of my fellow-passengers.

Professor Woodensconce, President-elect of the Section for Moral Machinery, is a broad gentleman with a bald head, a fur collar, and an expression of habitual victory over persons who have not contradicted him. His chief luggage consists of a hat-box, a case of bottled stout, and a roll of diagrams illustrating the influence of hinges upon the domestic affections.

Doctor Muddlebranes is pale, long-haired, and wrapped in a plaid. He has brought three microscopes but no handkerchief.

Professor Coppernose, whose remarkable organ has furnished Science with a feature and caricaturists with a livelihood, is eating cold mutton from a newspaper.

Mr. Flummery, Parliamentary Statistician, has counted the passengers and announces that there are forty-six. The steward says thirty-nine. Mr. Flummery observes that this apparent discrepancy is highly interesting and will form the subject of a paper.

Among the humbler devotees of knowledge I perceive Mr. Crinkles, antiquary; the Reverend Doctor Pulpitbox, meteorologist; Sir Jingle Dangle, amateur chemist and hereditary legislator; and young Lord Rattletrap, who has attended because he was told that Oldcastle contained a racecourse.

I am surrounded by Intellect.

The smell is chiefly cheese.

## INCIDENTS OF THE VOYAGE

### Seven o’Clock

The cabin arrangements have been explained to us by the steward with a brevity bordering upon insolence.

My berth is situated in a cupboard, six feet below the deck, between the pantry and a pipe which emits hot vapour whenever I touch it. Entrance is effected by placing the left foot upon a box, the right knee upon Professor Coppernose’s pillow, the chin against a shelf, and the remainder of the person wherever Providence may direct.

Professor Coppernose, discovering my boot in his bed, spoke with warmth. I replied that the accommodation was public, that the advancement of Science demanded sacrifices, and that his pillow had been placed by the Company in the path of Journalism. He was not convinced.

The beds are narrow boards covered with blankets of an antiquity which Doctor Crinkles pronounces mediæval. He has found upon one of them a stain resembling the plan of Oldcastle Cathedral, and wishes the entire mattress conveyed to the Antiquarian Section.

### A Quarter Past Seven

A serious occurrence.

The chief engineer, Mr. Boilall, came down the companion-ladder carrying a mug in one hand and a spanner in the other. At the third step he sat down unexpectedly, drank from the spanner, attempted to tighten the mug, and informed us that the engines were “as sober as judges.”

This statement, coming from the responsible officer of the vessel, has caused uneasiness.

Captain Gulp says there is no danger. I have yet to learn whether British passengers are to be reassured by the unsupported assertions of a man who, less than two hours ago, fell over his own rope. I have yet to learn whether mechanical power may safely be entrusted to an engineer incapable of distinguishing iron from earthenware. I have yet to learn whether the Thames is to become the grave of learning, merely because a Steam Navigation Company considers sobriety an optional accomplishment.

I have put on my best waistcoat in case of a national catastrophe.

### Eight o’Clock

Breakfast.

The company assembled round two tables secured to the floor. The meal consisted of coffee, hard biscuits, fried fish, cold beef, hotter plates than anything placed upon them, and a substance announced as butter, but resisting the knife with geological firmness.

Professor Woodensconce proposed that we should determine by lot who was to have the last clean cup.

Doctor Muddlebranes suggested a coin. A halfpenny was produced and tossed. It struck the ceiling, lodged between two beams, and could not be recovered.

A second halfpenny was tossed. Professor Woodensconce cried “Heads,” and won.

I confess that I had supported him. There is something in the comprehensive outline of his skull which inspires attachment. Yet I am bound, in justice to the Public, to state that his behaviour in victory was not generous. He seized the cup, rinsed it in the coffee, and winked at Doctor Muddlebranes. That wink revealed an abyss. The man who can triumph over a defeated colleague in the matter of crockery may, under favourable conditions, overturn a constitution.

Doctor Muddlebranes drank from the milk-jug.

### Half-past Eight

We have reached rougher water.

Sir Jingle Dangle, who had been explaining that seasickness is a superstition encouraged by apothecaries, has retired abruptly.

Professor Coppernose maintains his position beside the beef. His courage is sublime, though intermittent. He eats a slice, turns green, condemns the construction of the vessel, eats another slice, and lies down.

Mr. Flummery has begun to calculate the proportion of passengers who may be ill without impairing the scientific character of the expedition. He first puts it at one in seven; then, finding six persons ill, alters the basis of calculation and calls the result “substantially identical.”

The Reverend Doctor Pulpitbox attributes the motion to electricity.

Captain Gulp attributes it to the sea.

A controversy is expected.

### Nine o’Clock

The sufferings of the learned are painful beyond expression, though not, I regret to say, beyond hearing.

Doctor Splayfoot’s dog is the only passenger entirely unaffected. He moves from sufferer to sufferer with benevolent curiosity, carrying away handkerchiefs, biscuits, gloves, and, in one instance, a set of artificial teeth.

Professor Woodensconce has stretched himself upon a bench, preserving in the contraction of his features an expression of severe thought. He assures me that his present condition is voluntary and undertaken to demonstrate the supremacy of mind over matter.

A moment later he requested a basin.

I brought him a soup plate, there being no basin. He rejected it on scientific grounds.

Mr. Septimus Slug still clings to his brass tube. I asked whether it contained an instrument for measuring marine oscillation. He replied, with reserve, that it was intended to meet emergencies arising from such oscillation. This confirms my suspicion of its national importance.

### Twenty Minutes Past Nine

The Reverend Doctor Pulpitbox has delivered a brief address from the floor, where he is lying face downward.

He maintains that the storm—there is scarcely enough wind to move a handkerchief—has been occasioned by the simultaneous operation of our forty-six nervous systems upon the magnetic fluid surrounding the vessel. The steward repeated that there are thirty-nine passengers. Mr. Flummery explained that the difference consists of “unregistered intellectual forces.”

A deck-chair then moved across the cabin and struck Doctor Pulpitbox behind the knees.

The magnetic fluid is violent.

### Ten o’Clock

An old gentleman in a brown greatcoat is missing.

His hat was discovered near the rail. His umbrella leans against the funnel. A report has spread that he has fallen overboard.

The captain declines to stop the vessel unless somebody can prove that the old gentleman came aboard. Mr. Flummery’s list contains three old gentlemen, two brown greatcoats, and no umbrellas; it is therefore useless.

Professor Crinkles says the hat is certainly of the reign of George the Fourth and may have been left on board for years.

I have examined the water, which reveals nothing.

The incident is wrapped in gloom.

### Five Minutes Past Ten

The old gentleman has been found asleep in the ladies’ cabin.

How he entered that sacred apartment no person can explain. He declares that he was projected through the door by a sudden lurch of the vessel. The ladies insist there was no sudden lurch. Captain Gulp states that there ought to have been one, and will speak to the engineer.

The rescued gentleman gives his name as Doctor Feeblewit, authority upon the migratory habits of oysters. He regrets the alarm, but thinks the event illustrates a principle.

It does.

### Half-past Ten

Professor Coppernose has lost his carpet-bag.

It was last seen beside the cabin stairs. It contains two shirts, a speech on Public Cleanliness, nine specimens of street mud, and the professor’s boots. Suspicion rests upon no one, but everybody has searched everybody else’s luggage.

My own bag has also disappeared.

In the interests of authentic reporting I suppress my private emotion. Property is a social arrangement; shirts are accidents; the naked truth alone concerns the journalist. Nevertheless, if the thief is discovered, I shall strike him with Mr. Slug’s tube.

### Eleven o’Clock

The lost bags have been found.

Mine is beneath Professor Coppernose’s berth. His is beneath mine. The steward says we exchanged them ourselves. This is impossible, because I distinctly remember taking the black bag with brass letters, and both bags are black with brass letters.

Professor Coppernose has been wearing one of my stockings round his neck under the impression that it was a comforter. He refuses to return it until it is dry.

### Noon

A crisis of the first magnitude has developed between Professor Woodensconce and Doctor Muddlebranes.

The former asserts that the motion of the ship is perpendicular. The latter maintains that it is horizontal. To settle the question they placed a knob of cheese upon the table and watched it move.

It rolled first towards Professor Woodensconce, then towards Doctor Muddlebranes, and finally fell into Mr. Flummery’s hat.

Both disputants claim victory.

Mr. Flummery, who had been asleep beneath the hat, claims damages.

The cheese has been reserved for exhibition at the meeting.

### One o’Clock

Luncheon was announced, but did not meet with enthusiasm.

Mr. Slug has at last opened the brass tube.

The disclosure must disappoint those readers who had hoped, as I did, for a weapon, a telescope, a preserved serpent, or the charter of a secret society. It contains six rolls of adhesive plaster, a corkscrew, two lemons, and a printed paper entitled *Immediate Relief for Persons of Delicate Stomach Travelling by Water*.

Mr. Slug has distributed the plaster among the company. Professor Woodensconce applied a piece to his forehead, Doctor Muddlebranes to the table, and Sir Jingle Dangle to the dog. No relief has followed, except to the dog, who ate it.

Such is the frequent fate of discovery when prematurely entrusted to the public.

### Two o’Clock

Oldcastle is visible.

Its towers, chimneys, roofs, monuments, factory smoke, church spires, warehouse cranes, and innumerable flags rise before us in confused magnificence. The whole town appears to have rushed down to the quay. Windows are filled, roofs occupied, lamp-posts climbed, and boats crowded with spectators. Whether they have come to welcome the Association or to see the vessel strike the pier cannot yet be determined.

Professor Woodensconce has recovered sufficiently to stand and wave his handkerchief, which belongs to Doctor Muddlebranes.

Doctor Muddlebranes is searching for it.

Captain Gulp has shouted an order. The engineer has shouted a different order. The vessel approaches the quay broadside.

### Ten Minutes Past Two

We have arrived.

Not elegantly.

The *Royal Porpoise* came against the landing-stage with a force which threw Professor Crinkles into a hamper of poultry, displaced the band, and caused the mayor’s address of welcome to fall into the river. The band, displaying that firmness which has so often supported this country in moments of peril, continued to play, although the trombone had lost his instrument and was performing upon an umbrella.

The mayor, Alderman Butterbun, stood beneath a decorated arch bearing the inscription:

**WELCOME, SCEINCE!**

I am informed that the error is intentional and illustrates the freedom of English spelling.

## OLDCASTLE IN A STATE OF INTELLECTUAL EXCITEMENT

### Oldcastle, Three o’Clock

The invasion is complete.

Every inn is full. Every lodging-house displays cards announcing “Apartments for Scientific Gentlemen,” “Beds for Philosophers,” “Hot Water for Geologists,” or “No Experiments Permitted Indoors.” Professors occupy attics, cellars, dining-rooms, cupboards, warehouses, and, in the case of the distinguished Professor Bristles, a stable already occupied by a horse, with whom he is expected to discuss Instinct.

I have secured a room at the Boot-jack and Countenance, an ancient hostelry situated opposite the Black Boy and Stomach-ache. My chamber contains two beds, four professors, a washstand without a basin, and a basin without a bottom. The landlord states that, owing to the demand created by Science, six more gentlemen may be introduced before night.

My luggage has again disappeared.

Oldcastle is decorated beyond belief. Blue flags mark the Botanical Section; red flags the Mechanical; green flags the Geological; yellow flags the Statistical; and a black flag, mistakenly placed over the refreshment room, has caused a report that several members are dead.

The inhabitants gaze with reverence upon every stranger who wears spectacles. A commercial traveller from Leeds has already been requested to lecture on the rotation of crops. He accepted, and spoke for forty minutes.

### Half-past Three

I have discovered a proceeding of so grave and startling a character that duty compels me to lay it instantly before the nation.

The meetings are to be held in the Assembly Rooms. Near the side entrance stands Beadle Bumblethump, wearing a cocked hat, a blue coat, a staff, and an expression which no Englishman can contemplate without asking who pays his wages.

I approached this official and inquired his business.

“To keep them boys out,” he replied.

“What boys?”

“Any boys.”

“By whose authority?”

“Committee’s.”

“What committee?”

“Them as told me.”

Here, then, is the whole system of secret despotism exposed in four syllables—“Them as told me.” A nameless Committee, acting through a uniformed instrument, proposes to exclude from the temple of knowledge the Youth of Britain! No charge is made against these boys. They are not accused, tried, convicted, or even individually identified. They are Boys; and that is enough.

I have yet to learn that age is a crime.

I have yet to learn that buttons, a staff, and a cocked hat confer the right to separate the rising generation from Natural Philosophy.

I have yet to learn that an English boy may not whistle under the windows of Science, look through its keyholes, hang upon its railings, and call its most eminent representatives “old buffer,” without being struck down by the armed hand of municipal tyranny.

Bumblethump has just pushed away a small lad carrying three dead rats.

The Constitution trembles.

### Four o’Clock

I demanded an explanation from the Local Secretary, Mr. Ledgerly, a thin gentleman whose fingers are permanently stained with ink.

Mr. Ledgerly says the beadle was stationed at the door because, during the morning rehearsal, several boys entered the hall, crawled beneath the platform, imitated the lecturers, and tied a kettle to Professor Bristles.

This explanation aggravates rather than removes the offence. Were the imitations inaccurate? Was the kettle tied without scientific method? If so, instruction, not exclusion, was required. A generous institution would have appointed a subcommittee on Kettles and encouraged the boys to repeat the experiment under supervision.

Mr. Ledgerly says the matter is too ridiculous to discuss.

Such is ever the language of Power when confronted by a Free Press.

### Five o’Clock

The Mayor has held a reception.

Alderman Butterbun is a large man whose waistcoat presents a surface favourable to civic decoration. He welcomed the Association in the name of Oldcastle, commerce, civilisation, posterity, municipal institutions, the ladies, the rates, and himself.

Professor Woodensconce replied. He observed that Science was a mighty river which, descending from the mountains of Inquiry, irrigated the plains of Knowledge, turned the mills of Improvement, floated the vessels of Progress, and occasionally overflowed its banks, to the inconvenience of householders. He then forgot the remainder of his speech and sat down upon the mayor’s hat.

Refreshments were served. The struggle for sandwiches was severe but brief. Doctor Muddlebranes obtained seven. Sir Jingle Dangle concealed two in his sleeve. The Reverend Doctor Pulpitbox, while discoursing on moderation, backed into the trifle.

I have secured a tart for future reference.

### Seven o’Clock

The town is illuminated. The word SCIENCE, in coloured lamps, burns above the Assembly Rooms, though the final E has gone out and the public are at present honouring “SCIENC.”

Young Lord Rattletrap and three companions have driven a hired carriage through the market, upsetting a vegetable stall and removing the stone head from a public fountain. They describe this as “seeing the place.” The constable bowed to them.

A poor man who laughed was told to move on.

Oldcastle understands rank.

# THE GREAT MEETING

## OFFICIAL AND EXCLUSIVE REPORT

### Thursday, Ten o’Clock in the Morning

Long before the appointed hour the Assembly Rooms were filled by a brilliant audience comprising professors, doctors, noblemen, aldermen, clergymen, manufacturers, ladies of strong understanding, gentlemen of weak digestion, reporters, servants, and one boy who had entered through a ventilator in defiance of Bumblethump.

The platform was furnished with a table, chairs, water-bottles, hammers, diagrams, stuffed animals, mineral specimens, a mechanical hand, a glass jar containing something brown, and the celebrated knob of travelling cheese.

At precisely twenty minutes past ten Professor Woodensconce took the chair.

The applause was tremendous. It was renewed when the chairman dropped his papers. It increased when he went under the table to recover them. It became uncontrollable when he rose and struck his head.

The proceedings then commenced.

---

## THE PRESIDENT’S ADDRESS

PROFESSOR WOODENSCONCE said that he stood before them oppressed by the grandeur, magnitude, profundity, longitude, latitude, and general importance of the occasion. It had been alleged by ignorant persons that Science was remote from daily life. He rejected the accusation with contempt. Science entered the kitchen, descended the chimney, sat in the railway carriage, looked into the teapot, accompanied the citizen to bed, and sometimes, owing to imperfect apparatus, blew him out of it.

The Association acknowledged no narrow limits. If an object existed, they would investigate it. If it did not exist, they would investigate it more freely, because facts could not then interfere with conclusions. The humblest circumstance might contain the loftiest principle. A button might reveal the laws of empire; a turnip, properly considered, might reform taxation; the motions of a flea might explain the British Constitution.

He concluded by calling upon every member to pursue Truth without fear, favour, common sense, or unnecessary reference to consequences.

Immense applause followed. A gentleman in the gallery cried, “Hear, hear!” and was immediately appointed to a committee.

---

## ON THE NATIONAL RESTORATION OF DANCING BEARS

PROFESSOR BRISTLES read a paper “On the Decline of the Dancing Bear, Considered in Relation to Public Cheerfulness, International Commerce, and the Muscular Education of the Working Classes.”

The learned Professor began by observing that in former times no English fair, market, bridge, quay, festival, execution, or respectable public gathering had been complete without a bear standing upon its hind legs while a man played a pipe. Modern refinement had banished this spectacle. What had been the result? Melancholy, political agitation, railway accidents, cheap literature, and an alarming increase in the consumption of pickles.

The bear, he contended, was naturally musical. Its apparent reluctance to dance arose from defective teaching and the injudicious application of heated plates. He had himself played “God Save the Queen” to a bear at Rotterdam. The animal had climbed a post. Superficial observers might suppose this indicated dislike; but the scientific mind perceived an attempt to obtain a better view of the performer.

The author proposed that the Government should purchase five hundred bears and distribute them among populous districts. Each bear should receive a salaried instructor, a brass collar, two meals daily, and a certificate of proficiency. Public dancing should take place on Saturdays, election days, and whenever Parliament had nothing more useful to offer.

The expense, he calculated, would not exceed two hundred thousand pounds, exclusive of bears.

SIR JINGLE DANGLE asked whether foreign bears would be admitted.

PROFESSOR BRISTLES replied that British bears should have preference, but regretted to say that few had presented themselves.

MR. FLUMMERY produced statistics showing that the dancing-bear population of England had declined by 137 per cent. On being asked how a decline greater than the whole could occur, he explained that the missing 37 per cent represented bears which ought to have existed, but did not.

DOCTOR SPLAYFOOT objected that the proposal ignored the rights of the bear.

THE CHAIRMAN observed that rights were a difficult subject. If once granted to bears, they might be demanded by horses, dogs, donkeys, and eventually ratepayers.

The paper was received with marked approval, and referred to a committee consisting chiefly of gentlemen who had never seen a bear.

---

## ON ORGAN-GRINDER MONKEYS AS AN IMPERIAL NECESSITY

DOCTOR MUDDLEBRANES followed with “A Few Suggestions for the Preservation and Endowment of the Organ-Grinder’s Monkey.”

The author said it was impossible to walk through the streets without perceiving that the monkey was disappearing. A few still remained, wearing red jackets and caps, but their number, morals, and tailoring had deteriorated. This was a national calamity.

What, he asked, had the monkey done for civilisation? It had collected halfpence, saluted windows, frightened horses, scratched children, and maintained a dignified silence under musical provocation sufficient to overthrow the reason of mankind. In patience, industry, and abstention from political speeches it surpassed many human beings supported at the public expense.

He proposed the establishment of a Royal College of Monkeys. Candidates should be examined in climbing, cap-removal, chain management, facial expression, and the distinction between a halfpenny and a trouser button. Advanced pupils might study banking, from their natural aptitude for receiving deposits.

A pension should be granted after fifteen years’ public service. Widows and orphans presented difficulty, but not, he trusted, insuperable difficulty. The children of deceased monkeys might be apprenticed to barrel-organists under municipal supervision.

ALDERMAN BUTTERBUN asked who would pay.

DOCTOR MUDDLEBRANES replied that posterity would pay.

The Mayor expressed satisfaction.

MR. LEDGERLY wished to know whether the monkey or the organ-grinder was to receive the pension.

The author said the money should be paid to the monkey, thereby preventing misapplication.

PROFESSOR COPPERNOSE doubted whether monkeys understood currency.

DOCTOR MUDDLEBRANES indignantly referred to numerous persons who received public money without understanding it.

Loud applause greeted this unanswerable observation.

The proposition was adopted in principle, details being reserved until it could be discovered where the monkeys had gone.

---

## THE PHYSIOLOGICAL EFFECTS OF BEARS’ GREASE

DOCTOR SPLAYFOOT then exhibited the glass jar containing the brown substance and read a paper entitled “Does the External Application of Bears’ Grease Communicate the Moral and Physical Qualities of the Bear?”

The learned Doctor stated that bears’ grease was extensively used by young gentlemen desirous of improving their hair. But had society considered the consequences? Could the substance of a bear be rubbed upon the head without affecting the brain beneath? Could a youth anoint himself nightly with a savage animal and rise each morning a perfectly peaceable member of society? He thought not.

The conduct of fashionable young men furnished abundant proof. They growled when contradicted, hugged persons without invitation, prowled after dark, consumed large quantities of food, scratched their names upon public property, and retired during winter mornings to places from which no ordinary effort could extract them. All these were ursine characteristics.

He had conducted an experiment upon his assistant, Mr. Noddles. For three weeks he rubbed the left side of Mr. Noddles’s head with genuine bears’ grease and the right side with ordinary dripping. The results were remarkable. Hair grew on neither side; but Mr. Noddles demanded higher wages and bit a tax collector. Whether these effects proceeded from the grease or from natural development remained to be established.

The author urged the appointment of inspectors empowered to enter bedrooms, examine pomade-pots, and seize all unlicensed animal fats.

A LADY inquired whether scented bears’ grease was equally dangerous.

DOCTOR SPLAYFOOT replied that perfume might render the bear more agreeable, but not less a bear.

SIR JINGLE DANGLE asked whether bald men were exempt.

The author said baldness afforded no security. Grease might penetrate invisibly.

PROFESSOR WOODENSCONCE considered the investigation of the utmost importance. Several recent disturbances at theatres had been attributed to drink, bad manners, and stupidity. Science now suggested grease.

MR. FLUMMERY stated that, of one hundred young men arrested for disorderly conduct, ninety-eight had hair. This proved an intimate connection.

The paper was ordered to be printed, circulated among hairdressers, and sent to the Home Secretary, who, it was believed, had a nephew.

---

## THE SHAKESPEAREAN TWIG

MR. CRINKLES, Antiquary, rose amid expectation and produced from a velvet case a short crooked twig bearing two leaves, one dead caterpillar, and a label.

He said that, during recent excavations behind the kitchen of an inn at Stratford-on-Avon, a workman had discovered an ancient bottle. Within the bottle lay a paper containing the words, “Wood—Shaks—rear wall.” Behind the rear wall stood a tree. From that tree the present twig had been taken.

Could anyone doubt its connection with Shakespeare?

The name of the immortal dramatist, he observed, had been spelt in many ways. “Shaks” was one. “Wood” plainly signified wood. “Rear wall” proved that the relic had been found behind a wall. The convergence of testimony was overwhelming.

Moreover, upon one leaf appeared a small hole. Shakespeare had written of “the slings and arrows of outrageous fortune.” Might not this aperture have been caused by an arrow? Upon the bark were two projecting points resembling horns. Shakespeare mentioned horns repeatedly. The twig was crooked. Shakespeare’s character Richard the Third was also crooked. The deduction was irresistible.

Mr. Crinkles submitted that the twig had either grown from Shakespeare’s own tree, been seen by Shakespeare, resembled something seen by Shakespeare, or belonged to the class of objects which Shakespeare might have seen had he looked at them.

He valued it at five thousand pounds, though he would accept four thousand seven hundred and fifty for immediate national purchase.

PROFESSOR COPPERNOSE examined the twig through a lens and said it was elm.

MR. CRINKLES replied that Shakespeare had nowhere denied possessing an elm.

DOCTOR MUDDLEBRANES asked the age of the tree.

MR. CRINKLES answered that the landlord thought it was planted seven years ago, but landlords were notoriously hostile to antiquarian research.

MR. LEDGERLY wished to inspect the paper found in the bottle.

The antiquary regretted that it had been thrown away by the workman’s wife, who used it to light a fire. The bottle had contained pickled onions and was therefore equally unavailable.

PROFESSOR WOODENSCONCE observed that the absence of evidence was often the most affecting evidence of antiquity. Genuine relics possessed a delicacy which caused them to disappear when closely questioned.

A MEMBER asked whether the twig might not be what vulgar persons called a plant.

The Chairman replied that it was unquestionably a decided plant, but he trusted the expression would not be misunderstood.

After animated discussion the relic was placed under a glass shade. Doctor Splayfoot’s dog immediately knocked it down and ate the caterpillar. Mr. Crinkles announced that the value had risen.

## THE AUTOMATIC POCKET-RELIEVING APPARATUS

MR. GRABBLE, Mechanical Inventor, advanced to the table carrying a compact arrangement of wires, hooks, springs, pincers, straps, and a small bell.

His paper was entitled “The Substitution of Machinery for Manual Dexterity in the Removal of Portable Property.”

The inventor began by deploring the waste of skill involved in ordinary pocket-picking. The operator required long practice, delicate fingers, courage, observation, and quickness. Even then he was liable to interruption by the police, resentment from the owner, and irregular earnings. Machinery had transformed agriculture, weaving, printing, mining, and baking. Why should the pocket remain the last fortress of unassisted labour?

His apparatus could be worn under the coat. Upon approaching a gentleman, the operator pressed a button. A jointed arm entered the nearest pocket, selected its contents, rejected handkerchiefs below a predetermined quality, transferred money and watches to an internal bag, and rang a bell if it encountered a pistol.

The advantages were enormous. Children, elderly persons, and men with rheumatism could enter the profession. The work could be performed more rapidly and with less personal contact. The machine never drank, betrayed accomplices, or kept more than its allotted share.

Mr. Grabble admitted that the model had defects. In a trial upon an omnibus it had removed the inventor’s own purse, torn a lady’s dress, seized a conductor by the waistcoat, and extracted a small dog from a basket. These were the unavoidable difficulties attending every great improvement.

To demonstrate the mechanism, he requested the loan of a gentleman.

No gentleman volunteered.

The inventor then approached Mr. Flummery, who protested that he had not consented. Mr. Grabble replied that Science could not wait upon individual timidity, fastened the apparatus around his body, and pressed the button.

The mechanical arm entered Professor Coppernose’s coat, removed a spectacle-case, struck Alderman Butterbun in the stomach, seized the Chairman’s chain, and wound itself around the leg of the table. The bell rang furiously. Mr. Flummery revolved twice and fell among the water-bottles.

The experiment was declared substantially successful.

SIR JINGLE DANGLE said the invention would reduce employment.

MR. GRABBLE replied that displaced pickpockets could manufacture machines.

DOCTOR PULPITBOX feared that dishonesty might increase.

The inventor asked whether that objection had ever arrested mechanical progress.

THE CHAIRMAN said it had not.

MR. LEDGERLY suggested that the machine might be adapted to Parliamentary use.

This produced excitement. Several members observed that an apparatus capable of entering pockets without the owner’s consent, removing money by concealed means, and ringing a bell when challenged possessed obvious qualifications for public finance.

Mr. Grabble offered a larger model to the Treasury.

The paper was accepted, and the inventor left the hall hurriedly after discovering three watches in his experimental bag.

## A PLAYGROUND FOR PERSONS OF HIGH RANK

LORD RATTLETRAP next presented “A Scheme for the Protection of the Public from the Recreations of the Aristocracy.”

His Lordship, who bore a small cut over one eye and carried the handle of the Oldcastle fountain in his coat, said that much misunderstanding existed between the nobility and the people. A young man of rank, after dining, naturally wished to drive rapidly, break lamps, remove knockers, repaint statues, release animals, push barrows into canals, or introduce a constable into a horse-trough. The public, unable to appreciate hereditary vivacity, complained.

This was unjust to both classes. The aristocrat suffered interruption; the citizen suffered damage. The remedy was separation.

His Lordship proposed the construction, near every large town, of an enclosed Noblemen’s Recreation Ground surrounded by a wall thirty feet high. Within it should be provided imitation streets, breakable windows, removable signs, wooden policemen, ornamental fountains, harmless pedestrians stuffed with straw, doors fitted with knockers, market stalls, lamp-posts, and a short canal containing waterproof boatmen.

Young gentlemen could enter after dinner and destroy whatever they pleased. The ground would be restored each morning by workmen. Special evenings might be reserved for members of Parliament.

To preserve reality, a mock magistrate should sit at midnight, hear charges, impose fines paid from public funds, and address offenders as ornaments of their station.

ALDERMAN BUTTERBUN warmly approved the scheme. Oldcastle had lost seventeen windows, two signs, one fountain-head, and a live pig during the previous night.

LORD RATTLETRAP said the pig had followed voluntarily.

PROFESSOR WOODENSCONCE asked whether the walls would keep noblemen in.

The author replied that this was the chief engineering difficulty. Walls ordinarily kept poor persons out; their application to the governing classes had not been sufficiently studied.

MR. FLUMMERY estimated that each ground would save the nation fourteen thousand panes of glass annually, besides hats, vegetables, and police dignity.

DOCTOR MUDDLEBRANES proposed that the enclosed area should contain a newspaper office, so that young noblemen might smash the windows of the Press without injuring independent journalism.

I rose from the reporters’ table to object. No man reveres the nobility more profoundly than I do when it is at a distance; but I have yet to learn that the Press is to be supplied, even in imitation, as a target for titled violence. I have yet to learn that an English journalist, engaged in exaggerating the truth for the protection of liberty, may be represented by a stuffed figure and beaten with a wooden bottle. I have yet to learn—but here the Chairman requested silence and the beadle approached me.

I sat down in defence of constitutional order.

SIR JINGLE DANGLE proposed that the playgrounds be paid for by a tax upon damaged householders, since they would receive the greatest benefit.

The motion was carried unanimously by persons who owned no houses in the affected districts.

## ON THE SCIENTIFIC VALUE OF FALLING DOWNSTAIRS

The Reverend Doctor Pulpitbox submitted a paper suggested by his descent that morning from the staircase of the Boot-jack and Countenance.

Falling, he said, had been treated with undeserved levity. Yet the human body in motion offered problems involving gravity, velocity, anatomy, architecture, theology, and carpet manufacture. Every staircase was a laboratory; every loose stair-rod an invitation to knowledge.

The author divided falls into three classes: the Forward, the Backward, and the Complicated. In the Forward, the subject perceived his destination but could not avoid it. In the Backward, he avoided perception but not destination. In the Complicated, another person was involved.

His own fall had begun as a Backward, developed into a Complicated upon meeting the chambermaid, and ended as a Forward beside the boots.

He proposed that trained observers should be stationed at the top and bottom of public staircases, with clocks, notebooks, measuring tapes, and medical assistance. Persons falling should be required to answer questions before recovering, as later testimony was often contaminated by anger.

PROFESSOR BRISTLES inquired whether stairs could be abolished.

DOCTOR PULPITBOX replied that this would destroy a large department of inquiry.

MR. FLUMMERY said statistics showed most staircase accidents happened upon staircases. The connection could no longer be denied.

The Association voted fifty pounds for investigation, provided no member of the committee was required to fall.

## THE CHEESE EXPERIMENT

At this stage Professor Woodensconce directed that the knob of cheese preserved from the voyage should be brought forward.

The Chairman explained that the cheese had rolled in contrary directions, thereby supporting two opposing theories at once. It represented, in his view, the highest ideal of scientific evidence.

The specimen was placed upon an inclined board.

PROFESSOR COPPERNOSE predicted that it would descend.

DOCTOR MUDDLEBRANES predicted that it would remain where it was, unless it moved.

MR. FLUMMERY predicted that fifty-one per cent of it would descend while forty-nine per cent remained stationary.

The board was raised.

The cheese disappeared.

A search followed. It was eventually discovered in the President’s pocket, where the mechanical apparatus had deposited it during the previous experiment.

Professor Woodensconce said the result demonstrated Personal Attraction.

Professor Coppernose called it theft.

The Chairman ruled the latter expression unscientific.

# THE BANQUET AND ITS CONSEQUENCES

### Thursday, Eight o’Clock in the Evening

A grand banquet was held in the Corn Exchange.

The hall glittered with lamps, banners, plate, glass, civic chains, artificial flowers, and a transparency representing Newton receiving an apple from a committee. Three hundred guests sat down to soup, salmon, beef, mutton, fowls, pies, jellies, and speeches.

The Mayor proposed “Science,” observing that without Science there would be no steam, gas, drainage, electricity, commerce, civilisation, or scientific associations. Professor Woodensconce replied that without municipal hospitality there would be no banquet, and the truth of this was acknowledged with enthusiasm.

Mr. Flummery proposed “Statistics,” but was persuaded to withdraw it when the company learned that he intended to quote them.

Sir Jingle Dangle proposed “The Ladies” in a speech of forty-seven minutes, during which most of the ladies left.

The Reverend Doctor Pulpitbox proposed “Moderation” and drank to it repeatedly.

At eleven o’clock Professor Bristles attempted to demonstrate the gait of a dancing bear upon the table. Doctor Splayfoot followed with his theory of transmitted animal qualities. Sir Jingle Dangle produced a jar of hair-grease. Lord Rattletrap anointed Professor Coppernose while the latter slept.

The effect, whether physiological or accidental, was immediate. The Professor awoke, growled, embraced the Mayor with crushing force, swept three plates from the table, and pursued Mr. Flummery beneath a curtain.

Doctor Splayfoot cried that his theory was established.

Professor Coppernose cried something else.

The band played.

### Midnight

A disturbance has taken place outside the Assembly Rooms.

Several boys, excluded during the day, assembled to hear the banquet speeches through an open window. Beadle Bumblethump ordered them away. They replied with observations concerning his hat. One boy threw an apple. The apple struck the transparency of Newton, passed through the philosopher’s head, and lodged in Professor Woodensconce’s soup.

The beadle gave chase, tripped over Lord Rattletrap’s fountain-handle, and fell into a tub.

I witnessed the whole affair.

Here is the inevitable result of repression. Denied access to Science, Youth attacks Newton. Driven from the lecture-room, it takes refuge in fruit. Opposed by authority, it places authority in water.

I sought the boy who threw the apple, intending to obtain an exclusive statement. He demanded sixpence. I offered twopence. He declined.

Corruption has reached the young.

# SECOND DAY OF THE CONGRESS

### Friday, Nine o’Clock

Oldcastle bears marks of intellectual occupation.

A stuffed crocodile has been left in the market. The hotel omnibus contains three geological hammers and no cushions. A chemical experiment conducted at the Black Boy and Stomach-ache has removed the eyebrows of the landlord. Professor Crinkles has purchased an old boot under the impression that it belonged to Shakespeare, because it was found near the Shakespearean twig.

The local newspaper announces that the Association has brought distinction, prosperity, inconvenience, broken glass, and nineteen disputed bills to the town.

My luggage is missing for the third time.

I now regard its disappearance as a natural phenomenon.

### Ten o’Clock

The General Committee met privately to consider resolutions arising from yesterday’s papers.

I obtained admission by wearing a card belonging to Professor Pumpkincrust, who has not arrived and is believed to be in Hull.

The Committee resolved:

1. That dancing bears are essential to national gravity.
2. That monkeys possess claims upon the Treasury, provided those claims are presented through respectable human intermediaries.
3. That hair-grease is a subject requiring vigilance, inspection, and a salaried department.
4. That the Shakespearean twig is authentic until disproved by evidence acceptable to its owner.
5. That machinery should relieve labour wherever labour is illegal, difficult, or liable to detection.
6. That aristocratic recreation deserves protection from the consequences of aristocratic recreation.
7. That the exclusion of boys is regrettable in theory and indispensable in practice.
8. That the Association has rendered important service to mankind.

The last resolution was carried three times.

### Eleven o’Clock

A deputation of Oldcastle tradesmen waited upon the Committee.

Mr. Pane, glazier, expressed gratitude to Lord Rattletrap and supported the noble playground scheme, provided ordinary windows were used and replaced frequently.

Mr. Lockitt, locksmith, opposed any arrangement which might reduce the removal of knockers.

Mrs. Crust, lodging-house keeper, complained that Professor Bristles had kept a live goat in her best bedroom to test whether it could be educated by geological maps.

Professor Bristles explained that the goat had eaten Derbyshire.

Mrs. Crust said it had also eaten a curtain.

The Committee recommended further observation.

### Noon

The closing General Assembly commenced.

Professor Woodensconce appeared with a strip of plaster upon his forehead, a bearskin travelling rug over his shoulders, and the Mayor’s spectacles in his pocket. He declared the meeting an unparalleled triumph.

He reviewed the achievements of the Association. They had restored the bear in principle, pensioned the monkey in anticipation, traced disorder to pomade, added a twig to Shakespeare, mechanised theft, enclosed privilege, classified falling, and established the attraction of cheese.

What former assembly, he asked, had done more in two days?

No one answered.

The President then announced that the next annual meeting would be held at Little Pedlington, unless the inhabitants prevented it.

The members rose and sang a hymn to Progress, composed for the occasion by Doctor Muddlebranes. The first verse described Knowledge as a soaring eagle; the second as a railway engine; the third as a watering-pot; and the fourth could not be sung because the author had written it on the back of his luncheon voucher and exchanged it for a pork pie.

The meeting dissolved in emotion.

# FINAL DISPATCH

### Oldcastle Station, Friday, Four o’Clock

The return journey is about to begin.

The platform presents a scene of desolation and greatness. Professors count parcels; porters contradict labels; ladies search for umbrellas; scientific instruments protrude from baskets; the stuffed crocodile has been registered as a child; Doctor Splayfoot’s dog has entered a first-class carriage without a ticket and refuses discussion.

Professor Crinkles is carrying the Shakespearean twig in a locked iron case. He has insured it for six thousand pounds. The twig itself is now somewhat shorter, Doctor Splayfoot’s dog having eaten one end.

Mr. Grabble’s pocket-relieving machine has been confiscated by the police, but not before it removed the warrant from the constable who seized it. The legal consequences promise to be profound.

Lord Rattletrap has subscribed five shillings towards the restoration of the fountain and considers the incident closed.

Beadle Bumblethump stands at the gate keeping boys from the platform. I reminded him that the Association had expressed regret at their exclusion.

He replied, “Regret ’em outside.”

There is a brutal completeness in the man’s despotism.

### Twenty Minutes Past Four

My luggage has been found in the parcels office under the label “MINERALS—NO VALUE.”

The description is offensive but serviceable.

### Half-past Four

We are moving.

Oldcastle recedes beneath a cloud of smoke. Its towers and chimneys fade; its flags droop; its broken windows shine in the declining sun. Upon the platform the Mayor waves his hat. Professor Woodensconce waves the Mayor’s spectacles. The boys wave portions of the beadle’s staff, which has somehow been divided among them.

Thus ends a convocation which must exercise an enduring influence upon Science, Government, Commerce, Morality, Machinery, Hair, Bears, Monkeys, Shakespeare, Cheese, and the future destination of public money.

It would be premature to estimate the full value of its discoveries. Great ideas require time. The dancing bears have not yet been purchased; the monkeys have not yet applied for pensions; the inspectors of grease have not yet entered our bedrooms; the automatic hand has not yet relieved the nation’s pockets; and the noble playground exists only upon paper. But principles have been asserted. Committees have been appointed. Resolutions have been passed. Expenses have been incurred.

The work of civilisation is therefore secure.

As for myself, I return exhausted but conscious of duty performed. I have faced water, vapour, bad coffee, uncertain butter, cramped bedding, public speaking, constitutional outrage, scientific grease, mechanical robbery, and the persistent disappearance of my shirts. I have observed all things, misunderstood nothing of importance, and transmitted facts with that severe fidelity which permits no vulgar probability to stand in the way of a magnificent conclusion.

The train gathers speed.

Professor Coppernose has discovered that he is travelling with my carpet-bag.

I have his.

We shall not exchange them.

The advancement of knowledge demands sacrifice.

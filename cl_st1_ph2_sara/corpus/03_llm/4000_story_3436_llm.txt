# The Economies of Clover Cottage

I had long entertained the conviction that Providence intended every man of moderate income to live in the country. The proofs appeared to me numerous and conclusive. Peaches grew upon trees; strawberries covered the ground; cows manufactured milk without wages; hens laid eggs from motives of disinterested benevolence; and a seed, which might be purchased for the hundredth part of a cent, would, if treated with nothing more than ordinary civility, return cabbages, turnips, melons, cucumbers, beans, and perhaps a pumpkin of such dimensions that the family might dine in it.

Why, then, should a sensible person remain in town, paying eighteen cents for a quart of milk which a cow stood ready to furnish for nothing?

This question became acute one February evening when my wife, Clara, discovered that our grocer had charged us for three dozen eggs in a week during which, as she declared, we had eaten but eleven.

“The country,” said she, laying the bill before me, “is the only place where one can live economically.”

I had reached precisely the same conclusion from an entirely different examination of the account.

“Undoubtedly,” I replied. “Consider butter.”

“And cream!”

“And fruit!”

“And flowers!”

“And air,” said I. “Air costs nothing in the country.”

“It ought not to cost anything here,” Clara observed, glancing at the smoke beyond the window, “for what we get.”

This was the beginning of our domestic duet. Before we rose from the table we had acquired, in imagination, a white cottage with green blinds, a brook, six apple trees, two cows, a flock of hens, a kitchen garden, an arbor, a bee-hive, and a view extending to the Catskills. I was to labor for an hour before breakfast, return rosy from the bean rows, bathe in cold spring-water, and apply myself to literature with the tranquil vigor of Virgil. Clara was to gather roses in a broad hat and receive our city friends under a grape-covered porch. Our children—then represented only by a small daughter named Lucy—were to acquire health, innocence, and a useful knowledge of botany by eating currants from the bush.

“Jeremiah says country living isn’t cheap unless you know how,” Clara remarked.

Uncle Jeremiah was my mother’s elder brother, a farmer from habit, appearance, and conviction. He possessed acres, gray whiskers, a slow horse, and that most irritating of human faculties, the ability to be right without appearing clever.

“He means,” said I, “that it isn’t cheap for everybody.”

“He said it particularly about us.”

“Your uncle,” I replied with dignity—though he was equally mine—“undervalues intelligence.”

As if summoned by this sentence to expose it, Uncle Jeremiah entered the next morning. He was in town to buy a plowshare and had called, as he said, to “see how we stood the winter.”

We showed him the egg bill.

Then we showed him an advertisement of Clover Cottage, a rural paradise situated but twenty-seven miles from the city, containing six rooms, a stable, a well, an orchard, a stream, and three acres of productive land, all obtainable for a sum so small that prudence itself appeared to command the purchase.

He read the advertisement twice.

“Been to see it?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

“Roof sound?”

“It is described as picturesque.”

“Cellar dry?”

“It has rustic stonework.”

“Fence good?”

“There is a beautiful natural boundary on the east.”

“Swamp?”

“Brook,” said Clara.

“Humph.”

We waited for enthusiasm. It did not arrive.

“I can borrow the purchase-money,” I explained. “The seller will take my note for part, and Mr. Plummer will advance the rest. The saving in household expenses will pay the interest and leave a surplus.”

“Maybe,” said Uncle Jeremiah. “If you know how.”

This phrase offended me by its brevity. A long objection offers handles for refutation; a short one sits down like a stone.

“Knowledge,” I said, “is readily acquired.”

“Some kinds.”

“We shall learn by experience.”

“Experience charges tuition.”

Clara laughed, and I looked upon her laughter as treason; but three days afterward we visited Clover Cottage, and even she forgot Jeremiah.

It was April weather in February—a day lent by spring and afterward collected with interest. The cottage stood beneath two elms, which concealed the defective chimney. A brook glittered through the meadow, which concealed the swamp. A vine climbed over the porch, concealing the absence of several boards. The orchard consisted, indeed, of six trees; three leaned east, two leaned west, and the sixth had reached that calm condition in which a tree is no longer disturbed by sap.

The agent was a cheerful man who called every defect an opportunity.

“The place needs a little touching up,” he admitted.

I liked the phrase. It implied a paintbrush, an idle Saturday, and perhaps a ladder.

“The soil,” he continued, taking up a handful of something dark beside the stable, “is as rich as chocolate.”

It afterward proved to be chiefly stable refuse, but at that moment it had the fragrance of property.

Clara stood under the arbor, through which an old grapevine wandered with the exhausted air of a traveler who has lost his road.

“We can have tea here,” she whispered.

“We can grow everything,” I answered.

The agent heard me.

“Everything, sir! The former owner raised all his own vegetables.”

I learned later that the former owner had been a professional market gardener with four sons.

We bought Clover Cottage.

There is no sensation more agreeable than purchasing what one cannot pay for. Money, when actually handed over, gives acquisition a coarse commercial character; but a signature!—a neat flourish, a blot, a courteous exchange of papers—and behold, a house, an orchard, a stable, a brook, three acres, and a mortgage descend upon a man with the delicacy of fairy gifts.

Uncle Jeremiah examined the papers.

“When’s the note due?”

“In December.”

“Expect to have the money?”

“We expect to save it.”

He folded the paper very slowly.

“Learn quick,” he said.

We moved in April.

The cottage required, as the agent had foretold, a little touching up. The first carpenter touched it for eighty-seven dollars. The mason touched the chimney for forty-three. A plumber touched the pump, the drains, and my confidence for sixty-nine. Then came the glazier, the painter, the paperhanger, the man who repaired the range, the man who repaired what the first man had done to the range, and a person with a cart who removed the rubbish left by all the others.

The green blinds, which had appeared poetic at a distance, refused either to open or to close. To leave them as they were excluded both light and air; to remove them injured the picturesque effect; to repair them cost twenty-six dollars. We repaired them.

“The place must be put in order once for all,” I said.

This sentence was our financial anthem. Every new expenditure was to occur once for all. The roof was shingled once for all; the well was cleaned once for all; the brook was bridged once for all; the kitchen floor, whose central board rose with the solemn persistence of Banquo’s ghost, was taken up once for all. By June we had done so many things once for all that there was hardly any money left with which to do them again.

Still, the improvements were beautiful. Clara designed a crescent flower-bed before the house, a circular bed near the gate, and a star at the intersection of two paths. The local laborer, Patrick Doolan, regarded her plans with respectful melancholy.

“Ye’ll be wantin’ the grass took up in all thim shapes?”

“Exactly.”

“And flowers put where the grass was?”

“Yes.”

“And then watered to keep them alive?”

“Of course.”

“It’s a powerful deal of trouble to prevent a bit of ground from doin’ what it wants,” said Patrick.

Yet he performed the work, slowly and at a daily wage. We bought roses, geraniums, verbenas, fuchsias, heliotropes, petunias, two hydrangeas, one deutzia with a Latin surname, and a small evergreen which the nurseryman assured us was cheap at seven dollars. The bill for making the cottage look natural was one hundred and nineteen dollars.

Meanwhile I addressed myself to the useful garden.

Never was agriculture entered upon with nobler intentions. I purchased a spade, a fork, two hoes, three rakes, a wheelbarrow, a watering-pot, pruning-shears, garden line, stakes, labels, netting, twine, a seed-drill, and an illustrated volume entitled *The Amateur’s Complete Rural Companion*. The author stated that the proper preparation of soil was the foundation of success. I therefore hired two men to prepare it.

“You might do some of that yourself,” said Uncle Jeremiah, who had driven over.

“I intend to direct the operation.”

He looked at my gloves. They were of yellow leather and had cost two dollars.

“Planning to dig in those?”

“They protect the hands.”

“From what?”

I ignored the inquiry and unfolded my map of the garden. I had arranged the vegetables according to color and literary association. The beans were near the arbor because beans climb; the melons occupied the sunny middle ground; the lettuces bordered the path in alternating shades; and the corn stood at the farther end, where its tassels would compose admirably with the horizon.

“Where’s north?” asked Jeremiah.

I pointed toward the stable.

He turned the map around.

“Your cucumbers are under the apple trees.”

“They prefer partial shade,” I said, though this fact had not previously occurred to me.

“Your corn’s in the wettest patch.”

“It will save watering.”

He shook his head.

“You can live cheap in the country.”

“Ah!” I exclaimed.

“If you know how.”

There it was again.

We planted abundantly. Ten packages of peas, eight of beans, twelve of lettuce, six of radishes, four of beets, three of parsnips, two of carrots, six of sweet corn, and a miscellaneous company of herbs went into the earth. We planted enough tomatoes to feed an orphan asylum and enough cucumbers to pickle the population of a small republic. I dropped seeds with the magnanimity of Deucalion repopulating the world.

The cow came next.

No pastoral establishment is complete without a cow. A cottage without one is merely a residence; with one, it becomes a farm.

Patrick knew a man whose cousin had a cow to sell “amazin’ cheap.” She was a large brindled animal named Daphne, with liquid eyes, a modest manner, and horns curved like Turkish scimitars. The owner praised her butter, her disposition, her ancestry, and the peculiar tenderness of her heart.

“She’s that gentle,” he said, “a child could milk her.”

“Has a child milked her?” I asked.

“Not lately.”

“Why are you selling?”

“Family reasons.”

This answer should have alarmed me. A cow’s family reasons are commonly expressed through the hind legs. But Daphne looked at Clara with such meditative sweetness that we paid sixty-five dollars and led her home.

For twenty-four hours she remained a model of rustic decorum. She grazed in the meadow, drank from the brook, and completed the landscape. Clara sketched her from the porch. Lucy carried her a bunch of grass, which Daphne accepted with the grave condescension of a duchess receiving tribute.

On the second morning I undertook to milk her.

Patrick placed the stool, handed me the pail, and offered certain instructions whose simplicity encouraged me.

“Set close,” he said. “Keep your head agin her side, and don’t be nervous.”

“Why should I be nervous?”

“No reason at all—if she’s in the humor.”

Daphne turned her head and regarded me. There was nothing openly hostile in her expression; it suggested only that she had not previously known how low a man might descend.

I seated myself.

Daphne moved forward.

I moved forward.

She moved backward.

I moved backward.

We thus traversed a considerable portion of the stall with the slow solemnity of a minuet. At last Patrick held her by a rope, and I again approached the source of our future butter.

My first efforts produced nothing. My next produced a thin, silvery line which struck the pail with a sound more delightful than music. Milk! Our own milk! Free milk! I saw cream rising, butter forming, bills diminishing. I had obtained perhaps three tablespoonfuls when Daphne lifted her left foot and placed it accurately in the pail.

“Shoo!” cried Patrick.

I removed the foot, washed the pail, and began again.

This time she waited until I had collected nearly a pint. Then, with the graceful ease of a lady arranging her train, she swept the pail across the stable, kicked over the stool, and sent me backward into a basket of turnip seed.

“Is she always like this?” I demanded.

“Sure, no,” said Patrick. “Sometimes she’s worse.”

I determined that firmness alone was needed. Job had endured; Hercules had labored; and I was not to be defeated by an animal whose intelligence, according to naturalists, stood considerably below my own. I tied Daphne’s tail to a post.

She kicked with both feet.

I tied one hind leg.

She leaned against me and compressed me between herself and the partition.

I approached from the other side.

She introduced a horn beneath my coat and removed me from the stool with an upward flourish which tore the garment from pocket to collar.

Clara, hearing the disturbance, appeared at the stable door.

“My dear!” she cried. “What are you doing?”

“Milking.”

“You look as if the cow were milking you.”

Daphne turned her innocent eyes upon Clara, then upon me, and gave a low, gentle moo. It was the most sarcastic sound I have ever heard.

During the following week we hired Patrick to milk her. This cost nearly as much as buying milk in town, but I pointed out that we also obtained manure. Then Daphne broke the fence, ate half the crescent flower-bed, chased the laundress into the kitchen, upset the evergreen, and walked through the lettuce.

We sold her for forty dollars to a drover who said he understood her.

“She has genius,” I told him, unwilling to confess total defeat.

“She has horns,” he replied.

The bees arrived in May.

I had read that apiculture united profit with poetry. The bees worked; the owner contemplated. They required no wages, asked no holidays, and converted the idle fragrance of flowers into a marketable commodity. It was industry organized according to the dreams of Fourier.

A traveling lecturer sold us a patent hive equipped with glass observation windows, ventilating tubes, adjustable honey chambers, anti-moth entrances, and a device by which the honey could be removed without disturbing either bees or operator. He assured us that one healthy swarm might yield a hundred pounds in a season.

“At market price,” I whispered to Clara, “the hive will pay for itself by August.”

We bought the hive, the swarm, a veil, gloves, a smoker, a bee manual, and six small boxes intended for comb honey.

The lecturer installed the colony beneath the apple tree. For two days all went well. The bees flew out, the bees flew in, and I watched them through the patent window. Their industry shamed me. I began an essay comparing the hive to a well-governed commonwealth.

On the third day they departed in a body.

Clara called to me from the lawn. I ran out and beheld our investment suspended from the branch of an elm in the shape of a large brown beard.

“Get a ladder!” I cried.

Patrick brought one. I put on the veil, mounted with a basket, and approached the swarm. The manual directed the operator to shake the branch sharply.

I shook it.

The bees declined the basket and accepted me.

They settled upon my veil, shoulders, sleeves, and gloves; several entered at the wrists, where the gloves, though admirable in price, proved deficient in architecture. I descended the ladder with more rapidity than dignity, overturned the hive, collided with Patrick, and ran toward the brook pursued by an excited delegation.

“Don’t jump in!” Clara called. “The book says not to jump in!”

The book was in the house. The bees were in my trousers.

I jumped.

When I emerged, wet, muddy, and amplified about the face, the swarm had risen from the elm and was traveling westward. We followed across two fields, carrying the hive, the basket, the smoker, and a sheet. At sunset they selected a hollow tree on a neighbor’s property and disappeared into it with the resolution of emigrants who had escaped political persecution.

The lecturer, when informed by letter, replied that we had probably failed to make the patent residence sufficiently attractive.

“Ungrateful conservatives!” I said. “They preferred a rotten monarchy in a tree to every modern improvement.”

The hive remained under the apple tree all summer, inhabited by spiders.

But the garden promised compensation. In June it was green—prodigiously, triumphantly green. Vegetation rose everywhere. I walked among the beds like Adam before the necessity of distinguishing weeds from vegetables.

Unfortunately, the necessity soon arose.

The seed labels had vanished. Patrick confessed that he had pulled them up while raking because he thought them “little bits of kindlin’.” The English gardener whom Clara had hired for two days regarded my map with professional scorn.

“Plants must have breadth, sir,” he said. “Masses. Effects.”

“We desire food,” I replied.

“Certainly, sir; but food may be composed.”

He rearranged the beds. He moved the lettuce for effect, transplanted the beets for balance, set the tomatoes in a semicircle, and pruned the currant bushes into what he called “airy forms.” He also dug up the parsnips, mistaking them for weeds, and carefully trained a vigorous bindweed over the arbor because he supposed it to be a rare convolvulus.

When he departed, the garden resembled a geometric problem.

Then came rain.

The weeds rose by battalions, brigades, and empires. They occupied the paths, stormed the beds, embraced the tomatoes, strangled the peas, concealed the beets, and disputed possession of the corn. I attacked them with a hoe. For one heroic hour I cut, hacked, uprooted, and perspired. At the end of it I had cleared a patch about the size of a dinner napkin, broken the hoe, blistered both hands, and developed a pain in the back which assumed the permanent dignity of rheumatism.

“We must have help,” said Clara.

Help arrived in the shape of Ambrose Tibb, a boy of fourteen with an appetite of twenty-eight and the memory of a goldfish. He had been recommended as willing.

Willingness, I discovered, is not action. Ambrose was willing to weed, willing to feed the hens, willing to shut gates, willing to carry water, and willing to do each at some indefinite period following the Day of Judgment. He possessed, however, an immediate genius for eating.

“Ambrose,” I said one morning, “weed the onion bed, but be careful not to pull the onions.”

“Yes, sir.”

He pulled the onions and left the weeds.

“How did you distinguish them?”

“The weeds came up harder.”

“That should have suggested leaving them.”

“Yes, sir.”

The next day I directed him to thin the turnips.

“How many shall I leave?” he asked.

“One every six inches.”

At noon I found he had left six inches of turnips at each end of the row and removed everything between.

He hoed down the beans, stepped on the cucumbers, cut the garden line into small pieces, lost the pruning-shears, and watered the cabbages at midday by pouring a pailful into the heart of each plant. Three died of inundation, while the others appeared to have lost interest in life.

Yet we retained Ambrose because labor was scarce and because each dismissal seemed to waste the instruction already invested in him. He became, in this manner, an educational establishment of increasing cost and no graduates.

The hens were Clara’s enterprise.

“They require almost no care,” she said. “They pick up their own living.”

We purchased twelve laying hens and a rooster of commanding appearance. The poultry dealer guaranteed ten eggs daily.

On the first day we obtained two.

On the second, one.

For the remainder of the week the hens abstained from production on grounds known only to themselves. They consumed corn, meal, scraps, grit, shells, tonics, and a prepared food advertised to stimulate laying. The rooster consumed everything first.

“Perhaps they lay where we don’t find the eggs,” said Clara.

We searched the barn, stable, hedges, hayloft, woodpile, and meadow. Ambrose found seven eggs under the porch, but four were of uncertain antiquity and expressed their objection to examination with great force.

The hens also acquired an enthusiasm for the garden. They scratched up the flower seeds, dusted themselves among the radishes, pecked the tomatoes, and pursued insects by standing on the lettuce. We fenced them out. They flew over. We clipped their wings. They climbed through. We shut them in the henhouse, where they quarreled, ceased laying entirely, and developed ailments described in the poultry book with such breadth that every hen could be diagnosed with every disease.

Then one became broody.

Here, at last, was increase! We placed thirteen eggs beneath her and awaited the miracle of maternity. She sat with admirable constancy for nine days; then she abandoned the eggs and appropriated another hen’s nest containing three china nest-eggs and a doorknob which Ambrose had put there “to encourage ’em.”

A second hen proved more faithful. Eleven chicks appeared, small, golden, and eloquent. Lucy named them after the months of the year, omitting November because there were only eleven.

Within a fortnight one had drowned in a saucer, two had been carried off by a cat, one had vanished through the fence, three had succumbed to a cold rain, one had been stepped upon by Ambrose, and another had disappeared under circumstances in which the rooster’s expression was, to my mind, compromising. Three survived, at a cost which, divided among them, would have purchased as many full-grown turkeys.

The pigs came in July.

I opposed pigs on aesthetic grounds, but Patrick argued that they would eat kitchen refuse.

“No waste at all then, sir,” he said. “They’ll turn it into pork.”

This was economy in its purest form—transmutation without loss. We bought two small pigs.

They disliked kitchen refuse.

They preferred purchased meal, garden vegetables, soap, shoes, gateposts, and the lower portion of the pigpen. They escaped the first evening by lifting a loose board and entered the parlor through the open French window. Clara was receiving two city friends at the time.

There are situations which no rules of etiquette adequately cover. One may present an acquaintance to a bishop, a poet, or even a creditor; but the introduction of two muddy pigs to ladies at tea must be improvised.

The first pig passed beneath the sofa. The second upset the cake stand. Lucy screamed with joy; the ladies screamed without it; Clara stood upon a chair; and I pursued the animals around the table with a broom. The pig beneath the sofa emerged behind me, seized the cuff of my trousers, and accompanied me for several revolutions like a grotesque train.

Patrick finally captured one with a coal scuttle. The other escaped into the flower garden and rooted up the hydrangea.

“They’re young yet,” Patrick said. “They’ll improve.”

They did. They improved in weight, appetite, ingenuity, and speed.

By August our establishment contained no cow, no bees, uncertain hens, three expensive chicks, two enterprising pigs, one incompetent boy, and a garden of weeds. Yet visitors envied us.

“How charming!” they exclaimed. “How peaceful! And you must save so much!”

“Enormously,” I said.

Pride is a stout plaster; it will cover almost any crack until the wall falls.

We served our guests raspberries from the market because our canes had produced fourteen berries, of which the birds ate nine and Lucy five. We bought cream because Daphne had departed and no successor could be obtained at a figure I considered economical. We bought eggs because the hens were molting, meditating, brooding, ill, offended, or otherwise engaged. We bought vegetables because ours were not ready. We bought flowers because the crescent bed had been eaten, the circular bed had washed out, and the star had become a green blur resembling no constellation recognized by astronomy.

Meanwhile, the carriage demanded repairs.

I have neglected to mention the carriage because, at the time of purchasing the cottage, we had neglected to anticipate it. The station was two miles away. A rural paradise within easy reach of the railway is not necessarily within easy reach of the train. For the first week we hired a wagon. At the end of the week, finding the charges extravagant, we bought a secondhand carriage and an elderly horse named Socrates.

Socrates was economical in motion. He never wasted energy by starting promptly, climbing rapidly, or passing another vehicle. At the station, however, when a locomotive appeared, he became a disciple of speed and attempted to return home by the shortest route, whether or not a fence intervened.

His harness broke twice, the carriage spring once, and the shaft on three occasions. The blacksmith greeted me like a relative.

“A little weak there,” he would say, touching some new portion. “Might as well fix it now.”

“Once for all?” I asked bitterly.

“Well,” he said, “once for now.”

Autumn approached, and with it the harvest upon which we depended for our winter comfort.

The corn produced nine ears, five of them philosophical rather than material. The potatoes, dug with ceremony, filled half a basket and included several stones which at first encouraged us greatly. The tomatoes remained green until frost, when they blackened with unanimity. The beans had climbed well but bore sparingly. The cucumbers, planted in shade, accepted the shade and declined the cucumbers. Of the melons, one reached maturity; Ambrose sat upon it while resting from his duties.

The cabbages were abundant in leaf and empty in head. The carrots had divided themselves underground into grotesque fingers. The beets were no larger than buttons. The onion bed, replanted after Ambrose’s first exertion, yielded six onions possessing collectively less substance than the tears shed in cutting one.

We did have radishes. They were strong enough to have voted.

I stood in the garden one October afternoon amid withered vines, broken stakes, abandoned tools, and weeds which seemed to bow pleasantly as I passed.

“Good season,” they appeared to say. “Do come again.”

Clara joined me with a basket.

“What can we put away for winter?” she asked.

I surveyed the domain.

“We might preserve the weeds.”

The next morning we drove to market and purchased potatoes, turnips, cabbages, onions, carrots, apples, and canned tomatoes. The grocer, who had heard of our farming, was tactful.

“Garden didn’t quite come up to expectations?” he asked.

“On the contrary,” I said. “A great deal came up. We had difficulty determining what it was.”

Then came the bills.

They arrived first singly, then in pairs, then in flocks. They entered by mail, by hand, beneath the door, and in the pockets of workmen who “happened to be passing.” The carpenter had omitted extras; the plumber had discovered further items; the nurseryman had not been paid; the feed merchant sent a statement whose total suggested that our hens had dined at Delmonico’s. There were bills for seed, tools, lime, lumber, fencing, veterinary advice, carriage repairs, horse-shoeing, pig meal, chicken remedies, labor, cartage, and the removal of things which we had paid other persons to bring.

Clara and I sat at the dining-room table in November and arranged the accounts.

“This cannot be right,” she said.

“Certainly not.”

We added them again.

The total increased by twelve dollars.

We added them separately.

Our answers differed by forty-three dollars, but neither was encouraging.

“Think of what we have saved in rent,” I said.

“We paid interest.”

“In food, then.”

She produced the grocer’s book.

“In health?”

I rubbed my back.

“In experience,” I concluded.

Experience had indeed been lavish. If it charged tuition, as Jeremiah had said, it had enrolled us in every course and added laboratory fees.

The note on Clover Cottage fell due on the fifteenth of December.

I had not the money.

There are men who meet financial difficulty with calmness, frankness, and courage. I met it by placing the notice beneath a railway timetable and spending three days pretending that mail had ceased to exist.

At last Clara said, “You must tell Uncle Jeremiah.”

“Why?”

“Because he knows.”

“How could he?”

“He asked yesterday whether the note was due.”

This was intolerable. To be rescued is humbling; to be rescued by a man who predicted the shipwreck is almost beyond Christian endurance.

I drove to his farm. Snow lay on the fields, simplifying the landscape and concealing its labor. Jeremiah was mending a harness by the kitchen stove.

“Well?” he said.

I explained the matter eloquently. I described temporary expenses, unusual weather, dishonest tradesmen, defective seeds, migratory bees, eccentric livestock, insufficient labor, and the general hostility of nature toward the educated beginner. I demonstrated that our failure arose from a conjunction of circumstances unlikely to recur in ten generations.

He listened without interruption.

“How much short?” he asked.

I told him.

“Humph.”

“I do not ask you to pay it,” I said. “I merely desire advice.”

“Sell?”

“We should lose heavily.”

“Stay?”

“We cannot meet the note.”

He threaded the needle through the leather and pulled it tight.

“Got to town to-day,” he said.

I returned home offended by the inadequacy of his sympathy.

Two days later the holder of the note informed me that it had been paid.

Jeremiah came to supper that evening. He said nothing of the debt. He praised Clara’s biscuits, asked Lucy about her lessons, and advised me to bank manure before the ground froze. His silence became unbearable.

“You paid that note,” I said.

“Had some money.”

“I shall repay you.”

“When you can.”

“You warned me.”

“Yes.”

“I suppose you consider this the proper occasion to repeat your maxim.”

He buttered another biscuit.

“No use shutting the barn after the cow’s sold,” he said.

Clara laughed. I did also, though not immediately.

We kept Clover Cottage two more years. Under Jeremiah’s direction we reduced the garden by half, dispensed with ornamental geometry, bought fewer seeds, hired one competent man instead of three picturesque incompetents, repaired tools rather than purchasing new inventions, and learned that a hen is not a feathered annuity, a cow is not a milk fountain, and a pig will convert refuse into pork only if supported by a surprising quantity of meal.

I learned to dig without gloves, to distinguish an onion from a weed, to plant corn on dry ground, to close gates, to sharpen a hoe, to examine a roof before admiring the vine upon it, and to ask why a gentle cow was being sold.

In time the garden actually fed us. The hens laid when they chose, which was more often after we ceased calculating their yield. The orchard, pruned by Jeremiah, produced apples. Clara grew flowers in straight beds and found them no less beautiful. We had peaches, strawberries, beans, butter, eggs, and air—excellent air, still free of charge.

And we lived cheaply.

But whenever a city friend came out, praised the brook, envied the arbor, and declared that he too meant to buy a little place where everything could be had for nothing, I felt Uncle Jeremiah’s wisdom rising within me.

“My dear fellow,” I would say, “country living is the cheapest thing in the world.”

Then I would pause, as Jeremiah used to pause, and add:

“If you know how.”

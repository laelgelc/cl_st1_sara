### The Speech of Her Eyes

Banikantha had three daughters. The elder two, Sukeshini and Suhasini, had been named with that hopeful extravagance by which parents seek to bribe destiny. The youngest was called Subhashini—she who speaks sweetly. But destiny, having accepted the bribe, did not deliver the goods. The child was born mute.

In time the household shortened her name to Subha. Thus, with one syllable, they dismissed both the promise and its failure.

Her sisters were married with the proper music, feasting, debts, tears, and discussion of ornaments. Their mother then turned her anxious attention to Subha. A daughter is already regarded by many as a sealed jar that must be carried carefully into another man’s house; if the jar be cracked, though it contain the nectar of heaven, who will accept it? Such arithmetic passes in society for wisdom.

Subha understood that she was a burden. No one told her so plainly. Human beings seldom vouchsafe such mercy. Her mother’s sigh when she entered, her father’s silence when neighbours spoke of marriage, the servants’ pity, the village women’s curious looks—these were sentences, and Subha read them all.

Yet she herself possessed a language. Her large dark eyes spoke it without grammar or disguise. Joy passed through them like sunlight across deep water; fear darkened them in an instant; sorrow gathered there slowly, as rain gathers on the edge of a leaf. Spoken words must travel from the heart through many crooked chambers before they reach the lips, and on the journey they often change their clothing. But what rose in Subha’s eyes arrived naked.

Unfortunately, those around her preferred clothing.

Banikantha’s house stood in Chandipur beside a little river. It was not one of those mighty rivers whose fame is printed in books and whose floods ruin districts with imperial grandeur. It was a slender village stream, diligent and intimate, keeping within its banks, serving fishermen, boatmen, cattle, children, and women with brass water-pots. Like a well-bred daughter of a middle-class home, it completed its daily duties without noise and seemed content to be forgotten by the world.

But Subha did not forget it.

At noon, when the men had eaten and the women rested, when the boatmen’s songs ceased and even the birds withdrew into shadow, Subha came to the bank. The village sounds fell away one by one—the thud of the husking-pestle, the call of the hawker, the quarrel at the bathing steps—until there remained only the whisper of current against reeds, the drowsy complaint of insects, the occasional plop of a fish, and the long breathing of the earth beneath the sun.

In that great quiet she was not defective. She was complete.

Nature never asked her a question and waited impatiently for an answer. The bamboo leaves did not mock her; the clouds required no explanation; the river received every look she gave it and returned a hundred shifting glances. The wind touched her forehead as a mother might touch a child who had awakened from a bad dream. I know not what conversations passed between them. Perhaps they spoke of matters too ancient for words.

The cows in Banikantha’s shed understood her also. Sarbashi was broad-flanked and mild, with a white patch upon her brow; Panguli was smaller, brown, and somewhat jealous. Subha visited them at appointed hours, and they knew her footsteps. She would wind one arm round Sarbashi’s neck and press her cheek against the warm hide, while Panguli nudged her shoulder, demanding equal worship.

When she had been scolded at home, she went straight to the cows. They needed no account of the offence. Sarbashi turned her large tranquil eyes upon her; Panguli licked her hand with a rough tongue; their tails flicked away flies with the solemnity of priests performing a rite. A little goat and a striped kitten also belonged to her silent congregation. The kitten, being of a practical temperament, chiefly loved her lap; but affection founded upon comfort is still affection, and many marriages have rested on less.

There was, besides, Pratap.

Pratap was the son of a village shopkeeper and had established a firm reputation for uselessness. Every village requires one or two such persons. Since they have no urgent business of their own, they are available for everyone else’s: they carry news, watch games, assist at festivals, accompany travellers halfway down the road, and offer advice where wiser men have prudently withheld it.

Pratap’s chief occupation was fishing. With rod in hand he sat for hours beneath a tamarind tree near the river, exercising that patience which, had it been applied to any profitable trade, might have ruined his character as an idler.

Subha often sat near him.

A talkative companion disturbs the fish; wherefore Pratap valued her presence. He called her “Su,” a privilege he had invented for himself. She would bring him betel folded in a leaf, then sit with her knees drawn up, watching the float tremble on the water. He spoke from time to time—of a large fish that had escaped yesterday, of a boat newly painted, of a ghost seen near the mango grove. Subha listened, and now and then her eyes brightened.

She longed to perform some astonishing service for him. If only she could become a water-spirit, dive beneath the current, drive every silver fish toward his hook, and rise crowned with lilies! Then Pratap would stare and say, “Why, Su, who knew you possessed such power?”

But miracles are distributed carelessly. Those who need them receive none; those who do not recognise them meet them daily.

Gradually she grew older. Gradually the peaceful world about her altered its expression.

Her limbs lengthened, her face lost its childish roundness, and a grave beauty came upon her. With this beauty came fear. The village women began asking questions. Her mother’s irritation sharpened into alarm. Banikantha, who loved Subha more tenderly than he admitted, smoked his hookah in troubled silence while relations calculated, advised, reproached, and shook their heads.

“A girl cannot remain forever in her father’s house.”

“What will people say?”

“You should have arranged something earlier.”

As though Subha’s life were a neglected lawsuit!

One evening Pratap, attending to his float, said lightly, “Su, they say a bridegroom has been found for you in Calcutta. You will marry and forget us all.”

He smiled at his own wit.

Subha looked at him.

Only think: the river was flowing as before; a kingfisher flashed blue across the opposite bank; the hook waited beneath the water; the evening cattle returned through dust. Yet in the midst of these familiar things his words opened a foreign road and sent her alone upon it.

Pratap did not look up. Had he done so, even he might have understood something. But at that moment the float sank, and a small fish claimed the whole attention of man.

The arrangements were made secretly. Banikantha had been threatened with loss of caste, that peculiar thunderbolt which respectable society keeps ready for the timid. He therefore announced that the family would visit Calcutta. Subha knew this was false. The room in which her mother sorted clothes, the whispered consultation over jewels, the measuring glance cast upon her hair and complexion—all declared the truth.

On the night before their departure, she went to the cowshed. She fed Sarbashi with her own hands and stroked Panguli’s neck again and again. The cows, disturbed by her grief, pressed close to her. She clung to them, and her tears darkened their hides.

Then she went to the riverbank and threw herself upon the grass.

“Mother earth,” her whole body seemed to cry, “hold me. Do not let them take me away.”

The earth remained strong beneath her, but it could not close its arms. The stars watched. The river went on carrying moonlight toward places unknown.

Calcutta received Subha with walls, wheels, cries, smoke, and strangers. The house where they lodged had no open riverbank, no familiar cowshed, no afternoon vast enough for silence. Its courtyard was hemmed in by buildings; its strip of sky seemed a blue cloth stretched over a cage.

Her mother became busy with her transformation. Subha’s hair was twisted and pinned; ornaments were fastened upon her; a stiff sari was arranged to display her figure. Her tears threatened the powder upon her face, and this produced severe rebuke.

“Do not cry so! Your eyes will swell.”

But the tears came still.

The prospective bridegroom arrived with relatives. He had employment in the west and wished to marry before returning. He inspected the girl according to custom. Nay, let us not quarrel with the word. When a horse is bought, its teeth are examined; when a brass vessel is bought, it is struck to test its sound; when a bride is selected, the methods are gentler, but the principle has venerable consistency.

Subha entered with lowered head. Her mother had warned her not to speak—which was perhaps the easiest command ever given to the child. Yet silence compelled by deception was different from her natural silence. She sat trembling while unknown eyes measured her face, her hands, her hair, her modesty, and the probable amount of her father’s property.

Then someone asked her a question.

Subha raised her eyes in terror. She looked toward her mother. That lady answered quickly on her behalf and laughed. The others laughed also. Subha’s lips quivered like a leaf under the first touch of storm, and tears rolled down her cheeks.

The bridegroom saw those tears and was pleased. He took them for tokens of tenderness, maidenly shame, and deep attachment to parents. Pearls are valuable; tears, properly exhibited, may be more so. They made the girl appear soft-hearted and obedient. The bargain was approved.

Banikantha turned away to wipe his eyes. He had consented to the concealment, yet love and shame fought within him. He told himself that marriage would secure her future, that a husband’s house was a woman’s rightful refuge, that all fathers must endure separation. Human beings can build a bridge of reasons over the darkest pit, provided they do not look down while crossing.

The wedding took place on an auspicious day. Conch shells sounded; priests chanted; guests ate fish-curry and sweetmeats; lamps shone upon silk and gold. Amid all that noise Subha’s silence passed unnoticed.

Before leaving, she caught her father’s hand and gazed into his face. He could not bear the appeal. He blessed her hurriedly.

Her mother adjusted the edge of her veil.

The palanquin moved.

Thus Subha departed—not merely from a house, but from the entire nation of beings who had understood her: from Sarbashi’s patient eyes and Panguli’s warm breath; from the kitten’s sleep-heavy trust; from the tamarind shade and Pratap’s idle chatter; from the murmuring current, the trembling reeds, the open sky. None of these had signed the marriage agreement. None was consulted.

Her husband took her westward. For several days the new household praised her modesty. She neither contradicted her elders nor gossiped with servants; she did not complain, argue, or answer back. What a well-bred bride! Her silence was admired so long as it could be mistaken for obedience.

But truth, though born mute, does not remain hidden forever.

Within ten days they discovered that she could not speak.

A servant first suspected; a sister-in-law tested her with sudden questions; the mother-in-law demanded an explanation; the husband stared. Messages went to Calcutta. Indignation filled the house. They had been deceived! The bride’s beauty, her tears, her lowered eyes—all were re-entered in the account-book and found insufficient payment for the defect.

No one asked what terror she had endured. No one considered that she too had been deceived: shown a husband as though he were a refuge, carried among strangers as though affection waited there, sacrificed to respectability and told it was happiness.

Her husband’s family spoke around her, over her, about her. Their words struck like pebbles against a shuttered window. She tried to answer with her eyes, but these people knew only the alphabet of the tongue. Her gestures seemed foolish; her tears became tiresome; her stillness, once praised, appeared stubborn.

Had Sarbashi been there, one touch would have sufficed. Had the river been there, it would have taken her grief and borne it away upon its ceaseless breast. Even Pratap, I think, might at last have understood if he had seen her then.

But there was no river near, no cow’s patient flank, no strip of grass where she might lie against the earth. There were only strange rooms, strange faces, and doors that opened upon further strangeness.

At night Subha sat beside the shutter and looked toward the darkness. Somewhere beyond those walls lay Chandipur. Perhaps Panguli turned at the sound of footsteps and found another hand. Perhaps Pratap sat beneath the tamarind tree, telling some new companion of the fish that had escaped. Perhaps the little river carried the moon past the empty place where she had once sat.

Her eyes filled. No one read them.

And in the house of those who could speak, explain, accuse, bargain, and judge, the mute girl remained the only soul without a listener. Her heart called continually toward the lost river and the strong silent earth; but no answer reached her, and her grief flowed on in endless, voiceless weeping.

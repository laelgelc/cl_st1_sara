**SAN FRANCISCO, April 3.**

My dearest Irene:

You may put away your smelling-salts and cease composing my obituary. I survived the Alborough reception, though Mrs. Alborough placed me between a mining superintendent who ate asparagus with his fingers and a poet who had written an Ode to a Dead Canary. There are forms of death which leave the body walking about in satin slippers.

But I saw **Him**.

Do not ask “whom?” in that aggravating way of yours. Dr. Barritz, naturally—the Dr. Barritz whom everybody has been talking about; the man who has crossed Tibet, breakfasted with princes, attended a rajah in some place no Christian can spell, and acquired Eyes.

Yes, Eyes. All men have eyes, you say? My dear, oysters have pearls, but one does not therefore confuse an oyster-bed with the crown jewels.

He is tall, dark, and carries his head as if it belonged to him by divine right. He speaks little. When Mrs. Alborough informed him that California possessed the finest climate in the world, he looked at her until she added, “except, perhaps, certain portions of India.” That woman has never before surrendered an inch of territory. I watched the miracle from behind a palm.

Jack Raynor says Barritz has learned things in the East—strange things. Jack heard from a bell-boy, who had it from a courier, who was told by a gentleman’s gentleman that the Doctor once fixed his gaze upon a cobra and caused it to die of remorse. I do not believe this, of course. Cobras have no moral nature. Still, there is something in the man that makes one feel transparent.

He was presented to me.

“I have long wished to meet Miss Dement,” he said.

Long wished! Consider that. We have never met. Unless he saw my photograph in Paris—which is possible, for that horrid little portrait with the pearls has been shamefully circulated—or unless he possesses the faculty of seeing at a distance.

I replied, with perfect composure, “The wish is now gratified, Doctor.”

Then I caught my heel in the fringe of a rug and sat down upon a dwarf palm.

Do not laugh, Irene. The palm was more injured than I.

He assisted me to rise. His hand was cool and strong, and when he said, “You are not hurt?” I had the sensation of having known that voice in another existence—perhaps when I was an Egyptian princess and he an astrologer, or when we were both frogs. The doctrine of transmigration has always seemed confused to me.

Jack says Barritz never looks twice at a woman.

He looked twice at me.

Burn this letter.

Your distracted but uninjured  
**MARY JANE**


**April 7.**

Irene:

You are a wormwood-hearted creature. No, I am not “in love with a pair of eyes.” I am interested in an unusual psychological phenomenon. If the phenomenon happens to be six feet high, beautifully dressed, and able to silence Mrs. Alborough, that is not my fault.

Jack dined here last night and poured out information like a broken hydraulic pipe. According to him, Barritz has wandered through India, Persia, Arabia, China, and several countries which Jack invented on the spot. He can sleep at will, remain awake at will, stop his pulse, read sealed letters, and compel people to tell the truth.

That last accomplishment is indecent.

Suppose he should look at me and say, “Miss Dement, where were you born?”

What should I answer?

You, who were born in a brick house with a brass knocker and had a nurse who pronounced her h’s, cannot understand. There is Redhorse, of course, but there is also the *idea* of Redhorse—the red dust, the tin roofs, the mules, the men shooting at bottles behind the hotel, and Mother taking in washing until Father’s claim began to pay. I am not ashamed of Mother. I am not! But society makes such exquisite distinctions. Poverty at a distance is picturesque; poverty remembered by people who can tell anecdotes is dynamite.

And there are people who remember.

Jack remembers everything. He recalls when I wore two flour-sacks sewed together and a sunbonnet large enough to shelter a family. He recalls the freckles. He recalls my nickname.

No, I shall not write it.

At Redhorse there were three of us: Jack, a long-legged imp called Giggles because he never smiled; a ragged little boy called Dumps because he lived near the ore-dump; and myself. We formed a secret society, robbed Chinese plum-trees, prospected for gold with a kitchen spoon, and swore eternal fidelity over a deceased horned toad. I was president because I could lick both boys.

Dumps was a thin, black-eyed child with one suspender and astonishing pride. Nobody knew precisely to whom he belonged. He slept anywhere, ate when opportunity and I agreed, and followed me with the fidelity of a dog and the temper of a wildcat. When Father struck the Comet Ledge and sent us away, Dumps disappeared. Years afterward we heard that road agents had killed him. Jack said there had been a boy’s cap found near the trail.

Why do I tell you this? Because Dr. Barritz’s voice sometimes gives me that absurd sensation of a hand reaching through years of red dust. There! Now you will say I am bewitched.

I am not bewitched.

I am merely uneasy.

And if you write that nickname on a postcard again, I shall cross the continent and destroy you.

**M. J. D.**


**April 12.**

Beloved Fiend:

He called.

At four o’clock precisely Dr. Barritz entered my drawing-room, and at four minutes after four I forgot everything I had intended to say. I had prepared conversation on India, psychic force, Schopenhauer, and the probable future of decorative art. What I actually said was, “Do you find the fog damp?”

He replied that fog was generally so regarded.

Then he smiled.

Irene, the room brightened. You may call that an optical effect; you were always strong in the sciences when they could be used to insult your friends.

We spoke of travel. He has crossed deserts where men die of thirst, slept in ruined temples, and seen the sunrise on mountains higher than our ambitions. Yet when I mentioned Redhorse—merely as one might mention Nineveh or some other extinct centre of civilisation—his face altered.

“You know Redhorse?” he asked.

“I have visited it,” I said.

Visited it! I, who was born behind McGinty’s livery stable during a cloudburst!

He looked at me for a long time. I felt the flour-sack dress descend upon my shoulders. I felt every freckle return to its original station. I heard, in memory, Jack shouting that name across the gulch.

Then the Doctor said quietly, “It must have been an interesting place.”

“Picturesque,” I answered, and changed the subject with such violence that we were discussing Venetian glass before either of us drew breath.

He stayed an hour. As he rose to go, he took my hand—not improperly, not even warmly, but as if it were something he had lost and found.

“I hope you will not always be afraid of me,” he said.

Afraid! I laughed. It was a cultivated laugh, Irene, silver bells shaken in a marble temple. Unfortunately, one of my feet had gone to sleep. When I attempted to accompany him to the door, I lurched against a table and upset the Venetian glass.

He caught me about the waist.

For one celestial instant I rested upon his heart.

For the next three minutes I hopped privately behind the curtains and stamped life into my leg.

He has asked permission to call again.

You may begin the obituary.

**MARY JANE**


**April 18.**

Dearest:

The situation is grave. Jack says the Doctor is in love with me.

I told him he had the delicacy of a stamp-mill and the judgment of a burro, whereupon he demanded twenty dollars that he had lent me at Redhorse when we were ten years old. It was not twenty dollars; it was two bits, and I spent it on licorice. Wealth develops the predatory instinct in one’s early associates.

“How do you know?” I asked.

“He told me,” said Jack.

I nearly died, recovered, and called Jack a liar.

He then admitted that Barritz had not told him in words, but that any fool could see it. As Jack is undoubtedly qualified under that definition, his evidence has weight.

But why should a man like Dr. Barritz love me? You will answer, with offensive loyalty, that I am handsome, rich, and occasionally tolerable. So are fifty women in this town, and three of them have grandfathers whose portraits do not show pickaxes.

He looks at me as if waiting for something—as if he knows there is a locked room in my life and possesses the key. Last evening at the opera, during the most divine passage, he leaned near and whispered:

“Did you ever believe the dead could return?”

I dropped my fan.

“What dead?” I asked.

“Any one greatly loved.”

Was he speaking of Dumps? Could he—by some abominable Oriental process—have drawn that poor child’s history out of my mind? Jack insists that adepts in India can recover the image of every event from the universal ether. I told him the universal ether ought to mind its own business.

After the opera Barritz drove with me. There was moonlight on the bay, and the whole city looked washed clean of trade, gossip, and relatives. He spoke of loneliness; not dramatically, but in that restrained voice which makes every word seem carved upon one’s ribs. I wished to say something noble. Instead I asked whether he had found San Francisco agreeable.

He said, “Since I found you.”

Irene, I blubbered.

Not openly. One tear escaped and ran down the side of my nose. I pretended the wind had struck my eye and used my handkerchief. It was the wrong handkerchief—one Jack had handed me after spilling claret—and it was wet.

Thus does Romance clothe herself in glory.

**M.**


**April 23.**

My Own Irene:

You advise confession. Confession! Shall I sit before this accomplished being and say:

“Doctor, the lady you see was once known as Gunny Dement because she wore garments manufactured from grain-sacks, and because the finer poetry of childhood is expressed by yelling the first cruel thing that occurs to it”?

There. I have written it. **Gunny.**

The name lies upon the page like a toad.

Suppose he despises pretence? Suppose he despises poverty? Worse—suppose he pities it? I could endure scorn better than that soft, uplifted expression cultivated people assume when told one’s mother washed shirts.

Yet I love him.

There! I have written that too, and it looks less like a toad than a thunderbolt.

I love his silences, his grave courtesy, the small scar above his left eyebrow, his hands, and the way he says my Christian name when no one is near. I love him when he looks like an exiled prince and when, thinking himself unobserved, he shakes sand out of his shoe. Yesterday we walked on the beach, and I fancied the wet sand grew brighter beneath his feet. Then he stepped upon a piece of kelp and said something in Hindustani—or in plain American, rapidly suppressed.

He has asked me to walk there again tomorrow at sunset.

Jack predicts a declaration. Jack also predicts earthquakes, marriages, failures, and rain, so that time must occasionally justify him.

If Barritz speaks, I shall tell him everything.

No, I shall not.

Yes, I shall.

Irene, what if his occult training enables him to know already? What if the whole courtship is some majestic experiment in psychology? What if he brings me to the highest pinnacle of adoration and then says, “Gunny, I knew you all along”?

I should strike him with my parasol.

Afterward, perhaps, I should faint.

Pray for me—though not in that superior tone.

**MARY JANE DEMENT**


**April 24, midnight.**

Irene! Irene!

I have been living in a world constructed by an idiot angel.

We walked beyond the last houses where the beach lies wide and lonely under the cliffs. The sun was setting with unnecessary magnificence—gold, crimson, purple, the entire celestial paint-box. Dr. Barritz said almost nothing. I heard the surf, my heart, and a small dog barking half a mile away. All three seemed equally ominous.

At last he said, “Mary Jane.”

No one but Mother ever made both names sound beautiful.

He took my hand. Mine was icy. His was warm. This contradicted every novel I have read, but there was no time to correct it.

“I have loved you,” he said, “for years and years.”

I snatched my hand away.

“That is impossible.”

“It is true.”

“You did not know me.”

“I knew you before you wore diamonds.”

The cliffs revolved. The Pacific Ocean rose upon its hind legs. Every secret society, plum-tree, dead horned toad, flour-sack, and freckle of Redhorse marched before me in military order.

“Who are you?” I whispered.

He answered, “Dumps.”

I did not faint. Heroines are forever fainting when consciousness is their only source of information. I stood and stared at him.

Then I said, “You are not.”

“I am.”

“You were killed by road agents.”

“They robbed the stage. I hid under the bridge. Later I went with a freighter to Nevada.”

“You had one suspender.”

“I subsequently acquired another.”

That broke the spell. I struck him on the breast with both hands and demanded proofs.

He gave them. The scar above his eyebrow came from the day I threw a tomato-can at Jack and hit Dumps. He remembered the horned toad’s funeral; the spoon with which we prospected; the nugget we found, which proved to be brass; the oath that whoever first became rich should share with the others; and the time I pushed him into McGinty’s trough because he said I could not lick him.

“And you called me—”

“Gunny,” he said.

I tried to kick him. He embraced me before I could get the proper distance.

I kicked anyway, but without conviction.

He had survived, worked, studied, wandered, and somehow made himself into the man before me. He had returned to Redhorse and found it changed; heard of Father’s fortune; traced Jack; and from Jack learned where I was.

“Why didn’t you tell me?” I demanded from the neighbourhood of his coat-collar.

“Because you looked at me as if I were a god, and I was weak enough to enjoy it.”

That honesty deserved another kick, but my feet were occupied in keeping me upright.

“And if I had told you that I was poor?” I asked.

“I knew.”

“That Mother took in washing?”

“I carried the baskets.”

“That I wore flour-sacks?”

“One of them was marked ‘Extra Fine.’”

I began to laugh. Then I cried. Then I laughed while crying, which is a moist and unbecoming compromise. He kissed me, sand blew into my hair, and a wave ran up the beach and soaked both our shoes.

Thus, Irene, did the veiled Adept of India, the lord of invisible forces, the man who kills cobras by moral disapproval, reveal himself as the ragged boy who once ate half my supper and put a lizard down my back.

We are to be married in June.

I am happy beyond grammar.

Your ever-loving  
**GUNNY**


**April 26.**

Dearest Irene:

Jack has confessed the last villainy.

Dr. Barritz never studied black magic, never belonged to an Indian brotherhood, never read sealed letters, never killed a cobra, and cannot even stop his pulse without dying in the ordinary manner.

Jack invented the entire Oriental mystery because, he says, I would never have looked twice at a plain American physician, however handsome, unless some foreign wickedness were attached to him.

The little wretch was right.

**P.S.** Dumps says Jack was always the brains of our society. I have postponed the wedding until June only because it will take me that long to forgive them both.
